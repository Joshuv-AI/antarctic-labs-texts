An Estrogen–IL-33 Circuit in Aortic Fibroblasts Underlies Sex Differences in Tregs in Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm
Authors: Wangling Hu, Jingyong Li, Luna Zhai, Zhuo Chen, Linwei Ye, Ni Xia, Tingting Tang, Shaofang Nie, Min Zhang, Jiao Jiao, Bingjie Lv, Fen Yang, Yuzhi Lu, Lingfeng Zha, Muyang Gu, Xiajun Hu, Shuang Wen, Dan Li, Desheng Hu, Weimin Wang, Chen Chen, Qing K Wang, Zhilei Shan, Yong Ji, Xiang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey words: abdominal aortic aneurysm, IL-33, NF-κB, regulatory T cell, sex

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: This study advances our understanding of sex-dependent immune regulation in AAA, especially Tregs. We identify an estrogen/ERα/NF-κB/IL-33 axis driving Treg sex dimorphism. It highlights sex as a critical biological variable in vascular inflammation and aneurysm pathogenesis.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: These findings support sex-stratified AAA risk assessment and targeted therapy. Circulating Tregs/IL-33/ST2 may serve as sex-biased biomarkers. Future work should validate this axis in human tissues and test sex-specific NF-κB/ERα modulators in preclinical models.

Inflammation represents the core pathophysiological mechanism driving abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) progression, wherein it promotes smooth muscle cell (SMC) apoptosis and upregulates matrix metalloproteinase, leading to progressive extracellular matrix degradation and loss of aortic wall structural integrity and sustained dilatation.1, 2, 3, 4 Regulatory T cells (Tregs), as key mediators of immune tolerance, are critical components of the AAA vascular immune microenvironment.5, 6, 7, 8 Multiple studies have demonstrated that Tregs maintain aortic homeostasis by releasing suppressive cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-10 and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, as well as tissue repair factors like trefoil factor 1 (Tff1).6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 After puberty, men exhibit a significantly higher abundance of peripheral blood Tregs than age-matched women,12 and visceral adipose tissue in men harbors more tissue-specific Tregs.13 AAA contains a subset of tissue-resident Tregs in AAA lesions,9 but most previous studies were based on male models or ignored sexual dimorphism. Whether Tregs exhibit sex differences in AAA remains largely unknown.

Estrogen critically governs sex-based differences in immune cells by directly modulating their activation, differentiation, and function via nuclear receptor (estrogen receptor [ERα/Erβ]) or membrane receptor (GPER [G protein‑coupled estrogen receptor]) signaling pathways,14,15 and indirectly shapes the immune microenvironment through regulating the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α) and chemokines from stromal cells such as vascular endothelial cells and SMCs.16 However, whether and how estrogen regulates the behavior of Tregs, a pivotal player in adaptive immunity, and whether this process contributes to the sex differences in AAA Tregs remains a critical question.

IL-33, an alarmin released after tissue injury, exhibits significant sexual dimorphism in its expression across different immune microenvironments.13 In the lung, intestine, muscle, and heart, IL-33 promotes Treg proliferation and survival via ST2 signaling, enhances IL-10/amphiregulin secretion by Tregs, and is critical for maintaining immune homeostasis and tissue repair.17, 18, 19, 20 The regulation of IL-33 by estrogen is complicated. On 1 hand, estrogen can inhibit the production of proinflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α and IL-6) in macrophages via ERα,21 and because inflammation is a key inducer of IL-33 expression,22 estrogen may indirectly downregulate IL-33 by suppressing inflammation. On the other hand, in airway epithelial cells and endometriotic stromal cells, estrogen instead promotes IL-33 release.23,24 Therefore, the interaction between estrogen and IL-33 needs to be further clarified in combination with cell types.

In the present study, we established 2 murine models of AAA. Flow cytometry, transcriptomic sequencing, and gene knockout techniques were used to systematically elucidate the sex-dimorphic phenotype of Tregs in AAA. We uncovered a novel estrogen-driven regulatory axis wherein estrogen constrained Treg function by suppressing fibroblast-derived IL-33.

Materials and Methods

Experimental animals

This study used specific-pathogen-free C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) mice, ERα-knockout mice (ERα−/−, C57BL/6 background, with littermate WT controls), and Foxp3-green fluorescent protein (Foxp3‑GFP) reporter mice. All animals were 8-12-week-old adults of both sexes, procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co, Ltd Rodents were housed in barrier facilities maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, 50% ± 5% humidity, under a 12-hour light-dark cycle with unrestricted access to standard chow and water. All experimental protocols received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology ([2023] IACUC Number 5085), adhering strictly to the Guidelines for Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare with efforts to minimize suffering and reduce animal usage.

Mice were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation using a precision vaporizer and scavenging system. Induction was achieved at 3%-4% isoflurane in 100% oxygen (1-2 L/min flow) within an acrylic chamber until loss of righting reflex; anesthesia was maintained at 1.5%-2% isoflurane delivered through a nose cone mask. For terminal tissue harvest, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (4%-5% until cessation of spontaneous respiration), followed by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) as a secondary agent to ensure complete cardiac arrest. Depth of anesthesia was confirmed by absence of corneal reflex and unresponsiveness to noxious stimuli. After euthanasia confirmation via bilateral thoracotomy and direct observation of cardiac standstill, tissues were promptly excised. All procedures strictly adhered to AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition) and institutional regulations to ensure humane endpoints.

AAA Model induction

Elastase model

Mice were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation (induction at 3%-4% in oxygen, maintenance at 1.5%-2%) and positioned supine on a thermoregulated heating pad. A midline laparotomy was performed to expose the abdominal aorta. The infrarenal segment between the renal arteries and iliac bifurcation was carefully isolated. A sterile cotton patch saturated with porcine pancreatic elastase (PPE, Sigma-Aldrich, E1250) was applied to the infrarenal aorta and wrapped with perivascular adipose tissue. After 40 minutes, the patch was removed, and the peritoneal cavity was irrigated twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Sham-operated animals underwent identical procedures using heat-inactivated enzyme. Aortic specimens were harvested at postoperative days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 for subsequent analyses.

CaPO4 model

After isoflurane anesthesia and abdominal exposure as described earlier, the aortic adventitial adipose tissue was gently dissected. The exposed infrarenal aorta was wrapped with a sterile cotton ball soaked in 0.5 mol/L calcium chloride (CaCl2) solution for 10 minutes to ensure adequate stimulation. Subsequently, the CaCl2 cotton was replaced with a PBS-soaked cotton ball (Procell, Cat. No. PB180327) for an additional 5 minutes to terminate the reaction. After saline peritoneal lavage, the incision was sutured in layers. Sham controls received sterile saline application only. Animals were euthanized on day 14 postsurgery for aortic tissue collection.

Cell isolation and flow cytometry

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

For aortic tissue

Harvested aortas were cleared of adipose and connective tissue, minced, and digested in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 1 mg/mL collagenase XI, 1.5 mg/mL hyaluronidase, 1.5 mg/mL DNase I, and 36 mg/mL collagenase I (Sigma) at 37 °C with agitation for 60 minutes. Digests were filtered through 70 μm cell strainers, quenched, and pelleted at 1,600 rpm for 6 minutes. Cells were resuspended in PBS.

For lymphoid tissue

Spleens were mechanically dissociated, filtered through 70 μm strainers, and centrifuged at 1,600 rpm for 6 minutes. Erythrocytes were lysed with 1 mL red blood cell lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 minutes before neutralization with 5 mL PBS and final cell collection.

Staining and acquisition

Treg surface marker staining

Single-cell suspensions were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (4 °C, dark, 30 minutes) including anti-CD45-allophycocyanin (APC)-Cy7 or fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), fixable viability dye-BV510, F4/80-BV421, CD11b-PerCP-Cy5.5, CD4-BV711, ST2-phycoerythrin (PE)-Cy7, and KLRG1-APC.

Intracellular Treg markers

After surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 00-5523-00) and stained with anti-Foxp3-PE, Ki-67-BV605, IL-10-FITC, or Tff1-FITC (4 °C, dark, 30 minutes). For IL-10 and Tff1 detection, cells were stimulated with 50 ng/mL phorbol 12‑myristate 13‑acetate (PMA), 1 μg/mL ionomycin, and 10 μg/mL brefeldin A at 37 °C for 4 hours before intracellular staining.

Tregs were identified as live CD45+F4/80−CD11b−CD4+Foxp3+ cells. Percentages among CD4+ T cells and positive rates for target molecules (ST2, KLRG1, Ki-67, IL-10, Tff1) were quantified.

IL-33-expressing cell analysis

Fibroblasts were identified as CD45−PDGFRα+, vascular SMCs as α-SMA+, and endothelial cells as CD31+. Among CD45+ cells, B cells were CD19+, T cells CD3+, and macrophages F4/80+CD11b+. After fixation/permeabilization and the aforementioned stimulation protocol, cells were stained with anti-IL-33-PE.

Data were acquired on a Sony ID7000 spectral flow cytometer (Sony Biotechnology) and analyzed using FlowJo V10 software.

Transcriptomic sequencing (RNA-seq) and data analysis

Treg collection and RNA extraction

CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs were purified by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (BD FACSAria III) from aortas or splenocytes of Foxp3-GFP mice of both sexes postsurgery. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, 9109), with purity confirmed by an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8-2.0 (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher) and integrity verified by RNA integrity number >8.0 (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer). Complementary deoxyribonucleic acid libraries were constructed using the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (Takara, 634894) and subjected to paired-end sequencing (10 Gb/sample) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Fibroblast collection and RNA extraction

Cultured fibroblasts were washed twice with PBS before TRIzol extraction. RNA quality control standards were identical to those for Tregs. Libraries were prepared using the SMARTer Stranded RNA-Seq Kit (Takara, 634413) and sequenced as mentioned previously.

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw reads were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic (Q < 20) and aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) via STAR. Gene expression quantification was performed with FeatureCounts. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (|log2 fold change [log2FC]|>1 and adjusted P < 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg method) with R statistical software (version 4.1.3; R Core Team, 2022). Functional annotation (Gene Ontology: biological process, cellular component, molecular function) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were conducted using clusterProfiler. Visualization included volcano plots, heatmaps, and principal component analysis (PCA).

Molecular Biology Assays

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA from aortas or purified cells was reverse-transcribed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara). Amplifications were performed with SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR036 B) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 seconds and 60 °C for 30 seconds. Target genes included Il33, Esr1, Esr2, GPER, normalized to Gapdh (glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate dehydrogenase). Relative expression was calculated using the 2ˆ(-ΔΔCt) method. Primer sequences are provided in the Supplemental Appendix.

Western blotting

Tissues or fibroblasts were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche) on ice for 30 minutes. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes. Protein concentrations were determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Beyotime). A total of 20 μg protein was separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‑polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk for 1 hour, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-IL-33 (1:5,000), antiphospho-P65 (1:500), anti-P65 (1:500), anti-β-actin (1:5,000), and anti-GAPDH (1:5,000). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were applied for 1 hour at room temperature. Bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Millipore) and quantified using ImageJ.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood was collected via retro-orbital bleeding, and plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 minutes. Tissue homogenates were prepared using standard protocols. Estrogen concentrations were determined using a mouse estrogen ELISA kit (R&D Systems, KGE014) according to the manufacturer’s instructions: Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours, followed by detection antibody (1 hour), substrate development (15 minutes), and OD450 measurement on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher). Concentrations were extrapolated from standard curves.

Fibroblast isolation and in vitro culture

Aortic single-cell suspensions were prepared as mentioned previously and plated in DMEM supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). After a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, nonadherent cells were removed by PBS washing, and cultures were maintained in 10% FBS medium with medium changes every 2-3 days.

For AAA-derived fibroblasts

Primary (P0) cells were cultured for 7 days postisolation, then treated with vehicle or 500 nM 17β-estradiol (Sigma-Aldrich, HY-B0141) for 24 hours before IL-33 expression analysis.

For WT fibroblasts: After 2-3 passages, cells were allocated to vehicle, 200 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Sigma-Aldrich, L2630), or LPS plus 500 nM estradiol groups for 24-hour stimulation before IL-33 assessment.

NF-κB activation assay

Passaged fibroblasts were pretreated with estradiol (500 nM) for 1 hour, followed by stimulation with LPS (50 ng/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, T7539) for 30 minutes (Western blot) or 1 hour (immunofluorescence) before harvest.

NF-κB inhibition assay

Cells were pretreated with estradiol (500 nM) for 1 hour, followed by LPS stimulation in the presence or absence of JSH23 (50 μM, Sigma-Aldrich, HY-13982). Incubation times were 30 minutes for Western blot and 1 hour for immunofluorescence.

Functional interventions

Ovariectomy (OVX) and estrogen supplementation

Four-week-old female WT mice underwent bilateral OVX via dorsal incision (OVX group) or sham surgery (Sham-OVX). After a 2-week recovery, OVX + E2 mice received subcutaneous injections of 17β-estradiol (0.1 mg/kg in corn oil) every 2 days; OVX controls received vehicle. After 2 weeks of hormone modulation, PPE-induced AAA was established and analyzed on day 14.

ST2 neutralization

WT mice of both sexes were administered anti-ST2 neutralizing antibody (50 μg/mouse, BioLegend) intraperitoneally daily beginning immediately after PPE modeling; control cohorts received isotype IgG. Analysis was performed on day 14.

Treg depletion

Anti-CD25-mediated depletion

WT mice received intraperitoneal injections of anti-CD25 antibody (200 μg/mouse, BioLegend, PC61) on days −3, −2, 7, and 8 relative to PPE surgery; isotype controls received IgG. Aortic diameter was measured on day 14.

DEREG mice

Depletion of regulatory T cell (DEREG) mice were injected intraperitoneally with diphtheria toxin (DT, 1 μg/mouse, Sigma-Aldrich, D0564) on days −2, −1, 7, and 8 post-PPE; DT-naive DEREG littermates served as controls. Aneurysm size was quantified on day 14.

Histologic analysis

Aortic diameter measurement

Postmortem infrarenal aortas (renal artery to iliac bifurcation) were excised and photographed in PBS. Maximal external diameter was quantified using ImageJ software, with aneurysm defined as dilation >50% of normal aortic width.

Immunofluorescence

Frozen sections were rehydrated at 37 °C for 30 minutes, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, subjected to antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0), and blocked with 10% serum before overnight incubation with anti-P65 primary antibody. Sections were then labeled with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody and counterstained with 4′,6‑diamidino‑2‑phenylindole (DAPI). Immunofluorescent colocalization was visualized using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope, with quantification performed via ImageJ.

Statistical Analysis

All data were derived from 2-5 independent replicate experiments and expressed as the mean {plus minus} standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, 2-group comparisons used unpaired Student's t-test, whereas multigroup comparisons used 1-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's post hoc test or 2-way analysis of variance followed by Sidak's post hoc test. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test for 2 groups or the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at a P value <0.05. GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software) was used for all analyses.

Results

Sexual dimorphism of Tregs in AAA

The tissue microenvironment can shape Treg phenotype.25 To investigate sex differences in the phenotypic characteristics of Tregs within AAA, we first detected the abundance of Tregs in the aortas of AAA mice using flow cytometry. After the induction of PPE-induced AAA, female mice had fewer aortic Tregs compared with male mice (Figure 1A, Supplemental Figures 1A to 1C). This sex difference in Treg abundance was specific to AAA, as no sex-specific differences were observed in sham-operated aortas (Supplemental Figure 1D) or spleens (Supplemental Figure 1E).

Tregs in AAA also exhibited significant sex-dependent phenotypic differences. Female Tregs expressed lower levels of the IL-33 receptor ST2 (Figure 1B, Supplemental Figure 1C), the activation marker KLRG1 (Figure 1C, Supplemental Figure 1C), and the proliferation marker Ki-67 (Figure 1D, Supplemental Figure 1C). Similarly, AAAs in female mice showed a lower proportion and number of both IL-10+ Tregs (Figure 1E, Supplemental Figure 1C) and Tff1+ Tregs (Figure 1F, Supplemental Figure 1C). The immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 has recently been shown to exert reparative functions by maintaining endothelial integrity.6,26 Furthermore, our previous work demonstrated that AAA Tregs enhance aortic repair by inhibiting SMC apoptosis via Tff1.9

To confirm whether the sex difference in Tregs is widespread, we established calcium phosphate (CaPO4)-induced AAA to evaluate Tregs. Consistently, in this model, we observed reduced abundance of Tregs in the aorta of female mice (Figure 1G). We detected the expression levels of ST2, KLRG1, Ki-67, IL-10, and Tff1 on Tregs and found that the expression levels of these molecules were lower in Tregs from female AAAs (Figure 1H). Together, these results demonstrate that female AAA mice harbor fewer aortic Tregs with an attenuated functional phenotype compared with male mice.

Transcriptomic sequencing revealed functional heterogeneity of Tregs between male and female mice with AAA

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the sex difference in Tregs in AAA, we used Smart-seq technology to compare the transcriptional profiles of Tregs isolated from the spleens and aortas of male and female mice with AAA. The results showed significant differences between Tregs isolated from male AAAs and those isolated from the spleens, with 1,366 DEGs identified (Figure 2A). Notably, these DEGs included Prdm1 (encoding Blimp-1, associated with Treg differentiation), tissue-specific Treg precursor markers (Il1rl1 and Klrg1), and function-related markers (Tff1, Il10, and Il10ra) (Figure 2B). In contrast, aortic Tregs from female mice with AAAs showed minor differences compared with splenic Tregs, with only 552 DEGs identified (Figure 2A). Although Il1rl1, Klrg1, and Tff1 were still differentially expressed, the differences in Prdm1, Il10, and Il10ra expression were abolished (Figure 2C).

Comparison of Tregs from male and female AAAs revealed significant differences in transcriptional profiles, characterized by 741 sex-specific DEGs (Figures 2A and 2D). This difference was significantly more evident than the differences observed between splenic Tregs of the 2 sexes (Figures 2A and 2E). The results of PCA showed that although individual data points of splenic Tregs from female mice exhibited a certain degree of dispersion, the overall grouping pattern still clearly demonstrated a significant distinction between male aortic Tregs and the other 3 cell populations (Figure 2F).

Consistent with the results of flow cytometry, Tregs from male AAAs expressed higher levels of Il1rl1, Klrg1, Mki67 (encoding Ki-67), and Il10 (Supplemental Figure 2). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the FoxO, Hippo, and MAPK pathways were upregulated in Tregs from male AAAs (Figure 2G). In addition, male Tregs showed higher expression levels of FoxO4, Mapk11, and Prkaa1 and lower levels of Pik3r5, suggesting enhanced function and superior survival of male Tregs (Figures 2E and 2H). Similarly, Tregs from male AAAs, but not female AAAs, exhibited increased expression levels of TGFb1, Arg2, Atg7, and Bcl2l1 (Figures 2E and 2H), indicating functional characteristics associated with suppressing effector T cell function, maintaining tissue integrity, and inhibiting apoptosis. Interestingly, male Tregs also showed higher expression levels of Ccr5 and Tbx21 (Figures 2E and 2H), which may reflect the adaptability and plasticity of male AAA Tregs in a proinflammatory environment.27 Collectively, our study revealed significant functional heterogeneity of AAA Tregs between male and female mice with AAA.

Estrogen-controlled sex differences in aortic Tregs

As a female sex hormone, estrogen may affect immune responses and interact with Tregs.28, 29, 30 To investigate whether estrogen regulates aortic Tregs, we reduced estrogen levels in female mice via OVX and supplemented estrogen to restore its abundance (Figure 3A, Supplemental Figure 3A). Female mice with OVX exhibited a higher proportion and number of Tregs (Figure 3B, Supplemental Figure 3B). These Tregs had a phenotype similar to that found in male AAA mice, characterized by increased expression of ST2 and KLRG1. Estrogen supplementation after OVX reversed these changes (Figures 3B to 3D, Supplemental Figure 3B).

ELISA indicated that subcutaneous injection of estrogen in male mice increased the levels of estrogen (Supplemental Figure 3C). Treatment with estrogen reduced Treg infiltration and decreased the abundance of ST2+ Tregs and KLRG1+ Tregs in male AAA lesions (Figures 3E to 3G, Supplemental Figure 3D).

Among estrogen receptors, ERα exhibited the highest expression level in AAA tissues and showed a significant sex difference.31 Compared with male mice, female mice showed a 10-fold increase in the messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expression level of ERα in the aorta on day 3 after AAA induction and a 100% increase in the protein levels of ERα31 on day 14. In human AAA tissues, the protein levels of ERα were 80% higher in women than in men.31 To confirm our findings, we induced AAA in ERα knockout (ERα−/−) mice. Compared with littermate female controls, ERα−/− female mice exhibited enhanced Treg proportion and number in AAA lesions (Figure 3H, Supplemental Figure 3E), accompanied by an elevated proportion and number of ST2+ Tregs and KLRG1+ Tregs, a phenotype similar to that of aortic Tregs in WT male mice (Figures 3I to 3J, Supplemental Figure 3E). After ERα knockout, no significant sex differences were observed in Tregs in AAA, and the abundance of ST2+ Tregs and KLRG1+ Tregs was comparable (Figures 3K to 3M, Supplemental Figure 3F).

These results confirm that estrogen regulates sex differences in Tregs, and this regulatory effect is mediated via ERα.

Fibroblast-derived IL-33 mediates sex differences in Tregs

ERs are widely expressed in various cell types, including immune cells.21 Previous studies have shown that estrogen can directly promote Treg proliferation.29,32 Next, we investigated whether the effect of estrogen on Tregs in AAA is cell-intrinsic or cell-extrinsic. We used male and female DEREG mice (in which Tregs are ablated by diphtheria toxin) as recipients, transferred Tregs from male ERα+/+ or ERα−/− mice to male and female DEREG mice, and induced AAA in the recipient mice (Figure 4A). In this condition, sex differences between male and female mice persisted, encompassing the abundance and functional phenotype of AAA Tregs (Figure 4B, Supplemental Figures 4A to 4C). Neither Tregs transferred from ERα+/+ nor ERα−/− mice could alter the proportion and number of splenic Tregs, indicating that this method is not affected by host rejection (Supplemental Figure 4D). Adoptive transfer of Tregs from both ERα+/+ and ERα−/− mice alleviated the severity of AAA (Supplemental Figure 4E). The results as discussed earlier indicated that the sex difference in AAA Tregs was cell-extrinsic.

Studies have shown that the IL-33-ST2 axis plays a crucial role in the proliferation and maintenance of Tregs in AAA.9,33 Our earlier results indicated that estrogen attenuated the abundance of ST2+ Tregs in AAA (Figure 3F). We first detected IL-33 expression. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction showed that the mRNA levels of IL-33 increased first and then decreased regardless of sex (Figure 4C), with a sex difference appearing on day 10 after AAA induction. Subsequently, we detected the protein levels of IL-33 in AAA lesions. The protein levels of IL-33 increased first and then decreased, peaking on day 10 with the emergence of a sex difference (Figures 4D to 4E).

We next identified the source of IL-33. Parenchymal cells are known to be a major source of IL-33 across multiple disease models.22,34 Consistent with previous studies,33 IL-33 was mainly expressed by CD45- cells, and its expression was higher in male mice (Supplemental Figure 5A). We sorted CD45- cells and demonstrated that fibroblasts expressed the highest levels of IL-33 (Figure 4F). Flow cytometry confirmed the sex difference in the proportion and number of IL-33+ cells in AAA lesions (Supplemental Figures 5B-5C). Subsequent analysis of IL-33 expression in fibroblasts also revealed a sex difference (Figure 4G). Analysis of other IL-33-expressing cells in the aorta showed no significant sex differences (Supplemental Figures 5D to 5H).

Male and female mice with AAA were treated with ST2-neutralizing antibody to attenuate IL-33 signaling and determine whether IL-33 mediates the observed sex difference in Tregs in AAA. The sex difference in Tregs disappeared after ST2 neutralization (Figure 4H, Supplemental Figure 6A). Blockade of the ST2 signaling axis only exacerbated AAA severity in male mice, without significantly altering AAA in female mice (Supplemental Figure 6B).

These results indicated that IL-33 in AAA is mainly produced by fibroblasts and mediates the sex difference in Tregs.

Estrogen inhibited IL-33 secretion by fibroblasts via NF-κB

To verify whether estrogen suppresses IL-33 secretion by fibroblasts, we manipulated the circulating estrogen levels of female mice via OVX and supplemented estrogen to restore its levels. Then, we detected changes in IL-33 expression in aortic fibroblasts. The results showed that low levels of estrogen increased IL-33 expression in aortic fibroblasts, whereas estrogen supplementation decreased IL-33 expression in fibroblasts (Figure 5A). On day 10 post-AAA induction, when IL-33 expression peaked (as shown in Figures 4C to 4D), we treated male mice with subcutaneous estrogen and quantified the abundance of IL-33-expressing fibroblasts using flow cytometry. Furthermore, we measured the effects of ERα deletion on IL-33 production in the fibroblasts of female mice. Subcutaneous estrogen supplementation in male mice reduced the proportion and number of IL-33-expressing fibroblasts (Figure 5B), whereas ERα knockout in female mice increased the proportion and number of IL-33-expressing fibroblasts (Figure 5C). Subsequently, aortic fibroblasts from male mice with AAA were isolated and cultured in vitro, after which estrogen stimulation inhibited IL-33 expression in fibroblasts (Figure 5D).

We next measured the expression levels of 3 subtypes of ER in fibroblasts to identify the key factors mediating the effects of estrogen. We also investigated whether estrogen can regulate the expression of this dominant receptor. We detected the expression levels of 3 ERs in fibroblasts from male mice and found that ESR1 (ERα) had the highest expression levels (Figure 5E). We also found that estrogen upregulated ESR1 expression (Figure 5E). To confirm the role of ERα in estrogen-mediated IL-33 suppression, we isolated fibroblasts from male ERα−/− mice and assessed their IL-33 expression in response to estrogen supplementation. Fibroblasts isolated from ERα−/− mice showed no changes in IL-33 expression after estrogen supplementation (Figure 5F).

To dissect the molecular pathway underlying the inhibitory effect of estrogen on IL-33 secretion by fibroblasts, we isolated aortic fibroblasts from male WT mice, stimulated them with LPS,35,36 and treated them with estrogen. The results indicated that estrogen inhibited IL-33 expression (Figure 5G). Similar results were also observed under TNFα stimulation conditions (Supplemental Figure 7). Next, we conducted transcriptomic sequencing on the treated cells and found that LPS stimulation upregulated the expression of a series of inflammation-related genes, such as Cxcl1, Nos2, Il1b, Csf2, Il6, Ccl2, and matrix metalloproteinase-9, whereas estrogen downregulated the expression of these genes (Supplemental Figure 8A). Gene Ontology pathway analysis showed that LPS stimulation upregulated inflammation- and stress-related pathways in fibroblasts, such as defense response to virus, inflammatory response, immune response, and cytokine activity. Estrogen downregulated the enrichment of such genes, suggesting the classical anti-inflammatory properties of estrogen (Supplemental Figure 8B).

Building on available evidence linking estrogen to NF-κB inhibition37 (a pathway potentially tied to IL-33 regulation), we analyzed KEGG enrichment to explore how LPS and estrogen modulate NF-κB-associated signaling cascades. LPS stimulation enhanced cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway in fibroblasts (Figure 5H). In contrast, estrogen inhibited the enrichment of these pathways (Figure 5I). We also focused on changes in the NF-κB pathway through gene set enrichment analysis and found that LPS stimulation upregulated the NF-κB signaling pathway and promoted IκB phosphorylation (Figure 5J), which were reversed after treatment with estrogen (Figure 5K).

We conducted western blotting to quantify changes in P65 (a core component of NF-κB signaling) levels and phosphorylation in fibroblasts treated with LPS and/or estrogen, and to confirm that estrogen can suppress the NF-κB pathway. The results showed that LPS stimulation upregulated P65 phosphorylation. Correspondingly, estrogen downregulated P65 phosphorylation (Figure 6A). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that LPS stimulation promoted the nuclear translocation of P65, which was reversed after treatment with estrogen (Figure 6B). These results indicate that estrogen inhibited phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of P65, thereby inhibiting the NF-κB pathway.

Using the NF-κB inhibitor JSH23 to block this pathway, we found that estrogen could no longer suppress NF-κB activity (Figures 6C to 6D). We subsequently measured IL-33 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in fibroblasts. Inhibition of the NF-κB pathway led to a reduction in IL-33, and estrogen treatment failed to produce a further decrease under these conditions (Figure 6E), suggesting that NF-κB is a critical mediator involved in the regulatory effects of estrogen on IL-33 expression.

These results demonstrated that estrogen can inhibit the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of P65, thereby reducing IL-33 secretion by fibroblasts.

Tregs are dispensable in the pathogenesis of female AAA

The diminished functional phenotype of aortic Tregs in female mice prompted us to question their necessity in AAA progression. We hypothesized that, unlike their established protective role in male mice, Tregs might be dispensable in female mice because of compensatory mechanisms. To test this, we selectively depleted Tregs in female AAA mice using 2 independent strategies: administration of anti-CD25 antibody (Supplemental Figure 9A) and the DEREG/DT system (Supplemental Figure 9B). Remarkably, neither Treg depletion approach exacerbated AAA severity in female mice, as assessed by maximal aortic diameter (Figures 7A and 7B). This stands in stark contrast to the protective role of Tregs in male mice with AAA (Figures 7A and 7B), demonstrating that the Treg population in female mice is largely redundant for disease control. Thus, we propose a unified model based on these data (Figure 7C). The more robust inflammatory response in male mice promotes IL-33 production in fibroblasts, which in turn expands and activates ST2+ Tregs, maintaining their higher number and intact functional phenotype. In female mice, estrogen-mediated suppression of IL-33 in fibroblasts via ERα establishes a low-IL-33 milieu, resulting in fewer Tregs as well as functionally attenuated Tregs.

Discussion

In male murine AAA models, Tregs are generally considered key components in regulating the vascular immune microenvironment. However, whether sex differences in Tregs exist in AAA and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Therefore, our study addressed 1 critical question: the biological basis of Treg sex dimorphism and the underlying mechanisms in AAA. Through comprehensive analyses of 2 murine AAA models, transcriptomic sequencing, and gene knockout techniques, we identified a novel regulatory axis. The estrogen-ERα-NF-κB-fibroblast-IL-33-Treg axis governed Treg functional heterogeneity between male and female mice, providing a theoretical foundation for sex-specific understanding of AAA Tregs.

Notably, the epidemiology of human AAA exhibits striking sexual dimorphism: the incidence in men is approximately 4 times that in women, yet women face a higher risk of aortic dissection or rupture.1 This seemingly paradoxical phenomenon suggests that sex not only influences the occurrence of AAA but also affects its pathologic progression and clinical outcomes. AAA predominantly affects the elderly population, particularly postmenopausal women, who had experienced a marked reduction in estrogen levels. We speculate that the decline in estrogen levels after menopause leads to attenuated anti-inflammatory effects, which may partially explain the increased inflammatory activity and higher risk of rupture observed in postmenopausal women.38 It should be noted that the model used in this study reflects immune regulatory mechanisms under relatively stable sex hormone conditions and does not fully capture the complex process of natural postmenopausal estrogen withdrawal. Therefore, future studies are warranted to further validate the translational relevance of the estrogen-mediated protective mechanism using aged animal models and human AAA samples across different sexes and hormonal states, thereby providing a theoretical basis for sex-specific therapeutic strategies.

A key finding of our study was the pronounced sex dimorphism in aortic Tregs that was specific to the pathologic microenvironment of AAA. Transcriptomic sequencing also confirmed that Tregs from male AAA showed significant differences compared with splenic Tregs, whereas Tregs from female AAA exhibited minor differences compared with splenic Tregs. This difference partially reflects the constraint of Treg activation imposed by the lower inflammatory burden in female AAA, suggesting that their transcriptional profile is shaped by the tissue microenvironment rather than fully representing intrinsic functional potential. DEGs between aortic Tregs from the 2 sexes were enriched in pathways associated with immunosuppression (Il10, TGFb1), tissue repair (Tff1), cell survival (Bcl2l1, Atg7), and inflammatory adaptation (Ccr5, Tbx21), suggesting that male Tregs possess stronger immunosuppressive functions, tissue repair capacity, and inflammatory adaptability, whereas female Tregs exhibit relatively impaired functions. KEGG pathway analysis showed that the Hippo, MAPK, and FoxO pathways were upregulated in male AAAs. The synergistic effect of these pathways may explain the enhanced function and superior survival of Tregs in male mice.39, 40, 41

The sex difference in Tregs was observed only in the AAA lesions and not in sham-operated aortas or other lymphoid tissues, confirming that this difference is specific to the pathologic microenvironment of AAA rather than a systemic immune difference.

We also clarified that estrogen, acting via ERα, is the central regulator of Treg sex dimorphism. OVX transformed the Treg phenotype of female mice to that of male mice (characterized by increased expression levels of ST2 and KLRG1), and estrogen supplementation reversed these changes. Conversely, estrogen supplementation in male mice reduced the number of aortic Tregs. ERα knockout in female mice recapitulated the phenotype of male Tregs, with increased Treg abundance and an elevated proportion of ST2+/KLRG1+ subsets. ERα knockout also abolished Treg sex differences, confirming the role of ERα as the critical mediator involved in the effects of estrogen.

Estrogen can directly enhance the immunosuppressive function of Tregs and promote their expansion via ERα.28,29 Our work revealed that estrogen shaped Treg sex dimorphism through a cell-extrinsic mechanism by modulating IL-33 production from fibroblasts, rather than the direct action on Tregs. First, adoptive transfer of Tregs from ERα−/− donors into DEREG recipients did not alter the sex differences in Treg number or phenotype, demonstrating that cell-intrinsic ERα signaling in Tregs was dispensable. Second, blockade of IL-33 signaling using an ST2-neutralizing antibody eliminated Treg sex differences, establishing IL-33 as the essential downstream effector.

Mechanistically, estrogen targets aortic fibroblasts to suppress IL-33 production. We identified fibroblasts as the primary source of IL-33 in AAA lesions33 (expressed by CD45- cells), with male fibroblasts showing significantly higher expression levels of IL-33 compared with female counterparts, an effect relying on estrogen-ERα signaling. As a ligand for ST2, IL-33 is essential for Treg proliferation, activation, and functional maintenance;18,42 thus, reduced IL-33 secretion in female fibroblasts impairs Treg phenotype. Further experiments revealed that estrogen inhibits IL-33 production by suppressing the NF-κB pathway. Treatment with estrogen reduced P65 phosphorylation and blocked nuclear translocation of P65 in fibroblasts.37 LPS increased the IL-33 expression level and P65 nuclear translocation in fibroblasts, whereas NF-κB inhibition with JSH23 attenuated estrogen-mediated IL-33 down-regulation. These findings confirm the role of NF-κB as a downstream mediator of estrogen.43, 44, 45 Estrogen suppresses inflammatory responses in the vascular wall.37,46,47 Inflammation not only serves as a critical inducer of IL-33 production by fibroblasts but also acts as a key driver of Treg infiltration into the lesion site.13,48 Thus, the down-regulation of IL-33 expression and decreased Treg infiltration in the aortic tissues of female mice represent a manifestation of preserved vascular homeostasis rather than an initiating factor for AAA development.

Although the present study systematically clarified the novel mechanism by which estrogen regulates the sex dimorphism of Tregs via the fibroblast-IL-33 axis, there are still several limitations. First, although we confirmed estrogen-mediated inhibition of IL-33 production in fibroblasts in murine models, the specific upstream molecular mechanisms, especially the crosstalk between estrogen signaling and NF-κB, need further in-depth studies. Second, the present study mainly focused on Tregs. The sex differences in other immune cell subsets (such as Th17 cells, B cells, and so on) in the same microenvironment and their interaction with the Treg network can help construct a more complete immune map. Third, whether the same regulatory mechanism exists in human AAA and whether this sex-dependent therapeutic response can be translated into clinical practice represent important directions for future studies. Finally, the role of estrogen in AAA is not limited to immune regulation. In addition to suppressing local inflammatory responses, estrogen may exert systemic effects on major risk factors for AAA by lowering blood pressure and inhibiting angiotensin II type 1 receptor expression.49 Although this study primarily focuses on the immunoregulatory axis of estrogen-fibroblast-IL-33-Treg, we fully recognize that the protective effects of estrogen in AAA may involve synergistic actions across multiple systems and pathways. Future studies using models that better recapitulate human pathophysiology (eg, hypertension combined with AAA) are warranted to systematically evaluate the multifaceted mechanisms of estrogen and to elucidate its overall contribution to sex-dependent AAA initiation and progression.

In the natural course of the disease, a heavier local inflammatory load is associated with more pronounced recruitment and expansion of Tregs, which can indeed be regarded as a bystander effect accompanying differences in the overall inflammatory response. However, the key to establishing causality lies in the intervention outcomes: when Treg numbers are artificially reduced, AAA is significantly exacerbated in men but remains largely unaffected in women. This disparity indicates that Tregs play a protective role in men, whereas their protective function in women is relatively limited. Therefore, although Treg numbers are themselves influenced by the inflammatory load, their functional contribution to disease progression differs fundamentally between the sexes.

In summary, this study unveils sexual dimorphism in Tregs within AAA and elucidates its upstream mechanism. Estrogen suppresses IL-33 production by aortic fibroblasts via ERα/NF-κB signaling, thereby shaping a functionally impaired female Treg population. These findings not only illuminate a key mechanism underlying sex differences in AAA Tregs but also provide a novel theoretical framework for understanding sex-specific immune regulation.
