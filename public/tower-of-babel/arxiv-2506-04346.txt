On the Physics of Higher Condensation Defects
Ibrahima Bah, Enoch Leung, and Thomas Waddleton

Abstract
We study the structure of topological defects for finite Abelian symmetries in quantum field theories, and argue on physical grounds that they satisfy the definition of a higher fusion category proposed by Johnson-Freyd. Our primary focus is on the requirement of Karoubi completeness, i.e. the factorization conditions on higher condensation defects. We demonstrate this on a tree of such defects, constructed by successive higher gauging, explicitly using Lagrangian techniques in a concrete four-dimensional example, before turning to more general field theories. Along the way we also comment on the phenomenon where decoupled topological field theories appear as fusion coefficients. We further discuss the categorical role of anomalies, and how they may affect the properties of (higher) condensation defects.

Starting with a concrete example of a 4d Euclidean QFT

\mathcal{C}_{K}^{[1]}(M^{3})=\int\mathcal{D}a_{1}\mathcal{D}c_{1}\,\exp\left(2\pi i\int_{M^{3}}Ka_{1}\wedge dc_{1}+a_{1}\wedge B_{2}\right)\,,

Recall that at the quantum level, an (ordinary) gauging is not simply a quotienting of the degrees of freedom in the 4d QFT. It also gives rise to new twisted sectors labeled by a (1-form) quantum symmetry corresponding to the Pontryagin dual, i.e. the group of continuous maps from

(1.3)

Before turning our attention to the construction of (higher) condensation operators, we can already deduce some non-trivial structure in the symmetry operators of

Monoidality:

Given two symmetry generators

\mathbb{C}
-linearity:

Let us now focus on the topological operators supported on points. In our theory

(2.10)

Physically, this amounts to considering superpositions of (local) operators. Mathematically, this is the statement that the set of topological local operators has the structure of a complex vector space.

Additivity:

We can consider the superposition of operators beyond that of the topological local operators. For example, given two generators
\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Sigma^{2})
and
\mathcal{U}_{n^{\prime}}(\Sigma^{2})
of the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(1)}

(\mathcal{U}_{n}\oplus\mathcal{U}_{n^{\prime}})(\Sigma^{2})
whose correlation functions satisfy

\langle\cdots\left(\mathcal{U}_{n}\oplus\mathcal{U}_{n^{\prime}}\right)(\Sigma^{2})\cdots\rangle\coloneqq\langle\cdots\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Sigma^{2})\cdots\rangle+\langle\cdots\mathcal{U}_{n^{\prime}}(\Sigma^{2})\cdots\rangle\,.

(2.11)

Similarly, we can consider this notion of addition for operators of all codimensions. For two operators
\mathcal{O}_{1}(M^{4-p-1})
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}(M^{4-p-1})
supported on a codimension-

\langle\cdots\left(\mathcal{O}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{2}\right)(M^{4-p-1})\cdots\rangle\coloneqq\langle\cdots\mathcal{O}_{1}(M^{4-p-1})\cdots\rangle+\langle\cdots\mathcal{O}_{2}(M^{4-p-1})\cdots\rangle\,.

(2.12)

Strictly speaking, there is a distinct

The expressions above defining the operations
\oplus
are very similar to the addition of topological local operators as in (
2.10
), but there is an important subtlety when considering the “addition” of higher-dimensional operators in a Euclidean QFT. If an extended operator is truly topological, then it can be placed along any submanifold of the spacetime

including the timelike direction. Consequently, the interpretation of such an extended object is no longer a symmetry

determining the boundary conditions of states in a

is a topological defect with one dimension along the time direction, a correlation function with an insertion of
\mathcal{O}_{1}

\langle\cdots\mathcal{O}_{1}(M^{4-p-1})\cdots\rangle:=\langle\cdots\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\,,

(2.13)

where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is the twisted Hilbert space associated with the operator
\mathcal{O}_{1}(M^{4-p-1})
. One may also say that the presence of a defect specializes to a twisted sector of the overall Hilbert space for the QFT
\mathcal{T}
.

Now let us consider two extended topological defects
\mathcal{O}_{1,2}(M^{4-p-1})
corresponding respectively to the twisted Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1,2}
. The direct sum
\mathcal{O}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{2}
is characterized by a decomposition into different twisted sectors as

\langle\cdots\left(\mathcal{O}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{2}\right)(M^{4-p-1})\cdots\rangle=\langle\cdots\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\langle\cdots\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\,.

(2.14)

In general, one can consider multiple copies of each twisted sector, which is equivalent to taking linear combinations of defects
\mathcal{O}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}
with non-negative integer coefficients,

(2.15)

or simply as

n\mathcal{O}_{1}\oplus m\mathcal{O}_{2}:=\underbrace{\mathcal{O}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{O}_{1}}_{n\text{ copies}}\oplus\underbrace{\mathcal{O}_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{O}_{2}}_{m\text{ copies}}\,.

(2.16)

Here we see that the coefficients

and

and

between the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and the twisted sectors

. This justifies the requirement that

and

The fact that the topological extended operators

A tale of two additions:

The special case of topological local operators/defects deserves special attention. In general, the interpretations as operators or defects are distinguished by whether they are supported on spacelike or timelike submanifolds, but there is no well-defined notion for a point to be spacelike or timelike. Nonetheless, we can effectively differentiate between these two notions (i.e. operators vs. defects) by studying how they behave under the two inequivalent “additions,”
+
and
\oplus
, respectively describing superposition of operators and decomposition of Hilbert spaces.

If we regard two local operators as linear transformations, say, some
n\times n
matrices
A,B
, acting on states
|\varphi\rangle
in a vector space
V
, then we define the local operator

A+B:V\to V

(2.17)

as the
n\times n
matrix given by the entry-wise addition of
A
and
B
. At the level of correlators, we obtain

\langle A+B\rangle:V^{\ast}\otimes V\to\mathbb{C}

(2.18)

as a single complex number. Here
V^{\ast}
denotes the vector space dual to
V
, in which the dual vectors
\langle\varphi|
live. Therefore,
A+B
should be viewed as a linear transformation acting on a
single
Hilbert space.

On the contrary, given a state
|\varphi\rangle
as a column vector (with
n
entries) in
V
, there is a diagonal map sending
|\varphi\rangle
to a column vector in
V\oplus V
, by populating the first and second
n
entries respectively with
|\varphi\rangle
. We then define the direct sum of
A
and
B
to be

A\oplus B:V\oplus V\to V\oplus V\,,

(2.19)

where
A\oplus B=\text{diag}(A,B)
is a block-diagonal
2n\times 2n
matrix. The corresponding correlator

\langle A\oplus B\rangle:(V^{\ast}\oplus V^{\ast})\otimes(V\oplus V)\to\mathbb{C}

(2.20)

gives a complex number again since
V\oplus V
is itself a vector space. In other words,
A\oplus B
should be viewed as a linear transformation acting on a “total Hilbert space” consisting of two
identical
copies of the original Hilbert space.

The construction above clarifies the statement that non-trivial topological local operators in a
d
-dimensional QFT imply a
(d-1)
-form symmetry, in which case the theory decomposes into its constituent
universes
. The total Hilbert space of the theory can be written as a disjoint union of Hilbert spaces of the individual universes
Hellerman:2006zs
;
Tanizaki:2019rbk
;
Cherman:2020cvw
;
Komargodski:2020mxz
;
Sharpe:2022ene
. This is exactly the analogue of (
2.14
) but for topological local operators, so that the RHS decomposes into universes rather than sectors of a single given Hilbert space, which is sharply different from the standard addition in (
2.10
).

Similarly to before, the argument above shows that it only makes sense to consider direct sums of “universes” with non-negative integer coefficients, as reflected in the number of copies of
V
in (
2.19
). It is worth noting that the operators
A+B
and
A\oplus B
are technically distinct in nature, but from a practical perspective, the correlators (which are simply
numbers
) computed using the two notions of additions agree with each other, at least when the coefficients are non-negative integers.

Systematically, for a given (co)dimension, it is convenient for one to fix a basis of symmetry defects, in which arbitrary

can be expressed as non-negative integer linear combinations of.

However, one may also define

The relations between correlators involving defects and operators can be deduced using (analogues of) the aforementioned maps. For simplicity, we will henceforth restrict our attention only to topological defects, i.e. those constructed as non-negative integer linear combinations, but use the terms “operators” and “defects” synonymously unless otherwise specified.

3.1
Interfaces between Condensation Defects

Before analyzing the factorization of condensation defects, we first discuss the possible interfaces between them. In the following, we focus on two kinds of “minimal” interfaces, between a pair of condensation defects (of the same dimension): “soft” interfaces permeable to a select subset of symmetry operators, and “hard” interfaces that act as boundary conditions between two theories.

Soft interface:

Consider two 1-condensation defects
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K^{+}}(M^{3}_{+})
and
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K^{-}}(M^{3}_{-})
obtained respectively by 1-gauging a
\mathbb{Z}_{K^{+}}^{(1)}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{K^{-}}^{(1)}
symmetry in
\mathcal{T}
. The supports of these operators are such that
\partial M^{3}_{+}=-\partial M^{3}_{-}:=M^{2}
, with the minus sign signifying a difference in orientation. See Figure
9
for an illustration. The composite system has a 3d worldvolume action given by

S_{\rm 3d}=\int_{M^{3}_{+}}K^{+}a_{1}^{+}\wedge dc_{1}^{+}+a_{1}^{+}\wedge B_{2}^{+}+\int_{M^{3}_{-}}K^{-}a_{1}^{-}\wedge dc_{1}^{-}+a_{1}^{-}\wedge B_{2}^{-}\,,

(3.1)

where
a_{1}^{\pm}
and
c_{1}^{\pm}
are dynamical fields and
B_{2}^{\pm}
are background data from the point of view of the worldvolume. If we consider the gauge transformation

a_{1}^{\pm}\to a_{1}^{\pm}+d\lambda_{0}\,,\qquad c_{1}^{\pm}\to c_{1}^{\pm}+d\omega_{0}^{\pm}\,,

(3.2)

for scalar functions
\lambda_{0},\omega_{0}^{\pm}
, one finds the variation in the action (
3.1
) to be

\delta S_{\rm 3d}=\int_{M^{2}}\lambda_{0}\left(K^{+}dc_{1}^{+}+B_{2}^{+}-K^{-}dc_{1}^{-}-B_{2}^{-}\right)\,.

(3.3)

Let
\bar{K}=\mathop{\mathrm{gcd}}(K^{+},K^{-})
. By Bézout’s identity, there exists a properly quantized gauge field
\bar{c}_{1}
such that

\bar{K}d\bar{c}_{1}=\left.(K^{+}dc_{1}^{+}-K^{-}dc_{1}^{-})\right|_{M^{2}}\,,

(3.4)

Hence, a 2d interface theory on
M^{2}
, such as

S_{\rm 2d}=\int_{M^{2}}\varphi_{0}\left(\bar{K}d\bar{c}_{1}+B_{2}^{+}-B_{2}^{-}\right)\,,

(3.5)

preserves gauge invariance of the composite system, where
\varphi_{0}
is a dynamical scalar field that transforms as
\varphi_{0}\to\varphi_{0}-\lambda_{0}
under the variation (
3.2
), while all other fields are background. In terms of the discrete cocycles
\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\pm}
associated with the continuum fields
B_{2}^{\pm}
, the scalar acts as a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the identification

\mathcal{B}_{2}^{+}|_{M^{2}}=\mathcal{B}_{2}^{-}|_{M^{2}}\mod\bar{K}\,,

(3.6)

and similarly for the dual fields
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\pm}|_{M^{2}}\text{ mod }\bar{K}
. Therefore, the interface identifies the defects generating the common subgroup
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bar{K}}^{(1)}\subset\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{K^{\pm}}^{(1)}
on the condensation defects
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K^{\pm}}(M^{3}_{\pm})
.

The fate of the remaining subsets of the dual defects is subtler. If
\mathop{\mathrm{gcd}}(\bar{K},K^{\pm}/\bar{K})=1
, then the short exact sequence

0\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bar{K}}\xrightarrow{\times K^{\pm}/\bar{K}}\mathbb{Z}_{K^{\pm}}\xrightarrow{\text{mod}\,K^{\pm}/\bar{K}}{\mathbb{Z}}_{K^{\pm}/\bar{K}}\to 0

(3.7)

splits, so the
\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{K}}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{K^{\pm}/\bar{K}}
components of
\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\pm}
are independent, i.e.
\mathcal{B}_{2}
admits a decomposition

\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\pm}=\frac{K^{\pm}}{\bar{K}}\,\mathcal{B}_{2,{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bar{K}}}^{\pm}+\bar{K}\mathcal{B}_{2,{\mathbb{Z}}_{K^{\pm}/\bar{K}}}^{\pm}\,.

(3.8)

Consequently, we are only left with the option to impose the Dirichlet boundary condition,

\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\pm}|_{M^{2}}\,\text{mod}\,K^{\pm}/\bar{K}=0\,,

(3.9)

such that the defects generating the
\mathbb{Z}_{K^{\pm}/\bar{K}}^{(1)}
symmetry get trivialized on the interface.

On the other hand, if
\mathop{\mathrm{gcd}}(\bar{K},K^{\pm}/\bar{K})\neq 1
, then the aforementioned short exact sequence no longer splits, in which case certain non-trivial combinations of defects generating the
\mathbb{Z}_{K^{\pm}/\bar{K}}^{(1)}
subgroup may fuse into a defect belonging to the other
\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{K}}^{(1)}
subgroup in
\mathcal{T}
.
14
14

14

For instance, two charge-1 defects generating a
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
symmetry fuse into a charge-1 defect generating a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{4}
symmetry.
Hence, these combinations of defects are identified with their counterparts from the opposite side of the interface.

The defects generating the residual
\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{N/K^{\pm}}^{(0)}
symmetry on
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K^{\pm}}(M^{3})
follow parallel arguments, i.e. defects generating the common subgroup of the symmetries can move freely across the interface at
M^{2}
, while others are trivialized. We will refer to the anomaly inflow interface described by (

) as a “soft interface,” and denote it as

1^{[2]}_{K^{+},K^{-}}(M^{2})=\int\mathcal{D}\varphi_{0}\exp\bigg(2\pi i\int_{M^{2}}\varphi_{0}\left(\bar{K}d\bar{c}_{1}+B_{2}^{+}-B_{2}^{-}\right)\bigg)\,.

(3.10)

The notation merely reflects the fact that the interface acts
almost

15

15

As with the

Specifically, in the case where

, we recover the identity defect on
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K^{\pm}}(M^{3})
.

While we focused above on an interface between two codimension-1 condensation defects, the construction is readily generalized to interfaces between two condensation defects of any codimension. Note that the notation has an implicit directionality, where
1^{[2]}_{K^{+},K^{-}}(M^{2})
is defined such that it mediates from
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K^{+}}(M^{3}_{+})
on the “left” to
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K^{-}}(M^{3}_{-})
on the “right.” This direction and choice of left/right is arbitrary, but important to keep consistent when we discuss the fusion of these interfaces in Section

below.

Hard interface:

One can also construct a different interface by considering a slightly modified set up. We start with the same composite system (
3.1
), but now consider the more general gauge transformation

a_{1}^{\pm}\to a_{1}^{\pm}+d\lambda_{0}^{\pm}\,,\qquad c_{1}^{\pm}\to c_{1}^{\pm}+d\omega_{0}^{\pm}\,,

(3.11)

where we have allowed for different gauge parameters
\lambda_{0}^{\pm}
on the two sides of the interface. Demanding this configuration to still be gauge invariant yields the inflow action,

S_{\rm 2d}=\int_{M^{2}}\varphi^{+}_{0}\left(K^{+}dc_{1}^{+}+B_{2}^{+}\right)-\varphi^{-}_{0}\left(K^{-}dc_{1}^{-}+B_{2}^{-}\right)\,,

(3.12)

where
\varphi_{0}^{\pm}
are dynamical scalar fields that transform as
\varphi_{0}^{\pm}\to\varphi_{0}^{\pm}-\lambda_{0}^{\pm}
under (
3.11
).

On this interface, the Lagrange multiplier fields
\varphi_{0}^{\pm}
impose Dirichlet boundary conditions,

(B_{2}^{+}+K^{+}dc_{1}^{+})|_{M^{2}}=0\,,\qquad(B_{2}^{-}+K^{-}dc_{1}^{-})|_{M^{2}}=0\,,

(3.13)

which effectively trivializes
all
defects for both the
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{K^{\pm}}^{(1)}
and
\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{N/K^{\pm}}^{(0)}
symmetries when crossing the interface worldvolume. As this acts as a “hard wall” that kills all symmetry defects, we will refer to such an interface as the “hard interface,” and denote it
0^{[2]}_{K^{+},K^{-}}
. Similarly to before, the construction is generalizes to interfaces between two condensation defects of any codimension.

More general interfaces between two given condensation defects can be obtained from either a soft or hard interface by decorating with additional topological defects, or by stacking with a symmetry-protected topological (SPT) order etc. The constructions of these interfaces are qualitatively straightforward and will not play important roles in the rest of this paper, so we are not going to pursue the details here.

3.2
Condensation Completeness

Condensation defects have two crucial properties which we are going to examine below. For concreteness, we will focus on the 1-condensation defect
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}(M^{3})
, obtained by 1-gauging a
\mathbb{Z}_{K}^{(1)}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(1)}
symmetry in
\mathcal{T}
, along with the higher condensation defect
\mathcal{C}^{[2]}_{\dot{K}}(M^{2})
obtained by 1-gauging a
\mathbb{Z}_{\dot{K}}^{(1)}\subseteq\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{K}^{(1)}
symmetry in the
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}(M^{3})
worldvolume theory. However, the resulting discussion is applicable to all other condensation defects in a straightforward manner.

Idempotence:

Our first observation is that the condensation defect absorbs any symmetry defect used in its construction. Let
\mathcal{U}_{k}(\Sigma^{2})
be a symmetry generator in the
\mathbb{Z}_{K}^{(1)}
subgroup with support
\Sigma^{2}\in H_{2}(M^{3};\mathbb{Z}_{K})
, then using the product defined in (
2.5
), we have the fusion rule,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k}(\Sigma^{2})\otimes\mathcal{C}_{K}^{[1]}(M^{3})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{k}(\Sigma^{2})\otimes\bigoplus_{\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}\in H_{2}(M^{3};\mathbb{Z}_{K})}\mathcal{U}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}\in H_{2}(M^{3};\mathbb{Z}_{K})}\mathcal{U}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}(M^{3})\,,

(3.14)

where in the second line we see that the defect
\mathcal{U}_{k}(\Sigma^{2})
simply rearranges the summands of the condensation defect.
16
16

16

Strictly speaking, to compute the monoidal product of these operators we should consider
\mathcal{U}_{k}
as dressing a trivial codimension-1 defect, i.e. the fusion is given by
((\mathcal{U}_{k}\,\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\circlearrowright$}\,1^{[1]})\otimes\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K})(M^{3})
. We will not be careful to distinguish such mathematical subtleties in what follows, though such structure can be reinstated if needed.
This fusion rule follows directly from the physical construction of
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}(M^{3})
as a 1-gauging of the
\mathbb{Z}_{K}^{(1)}
symmetry. The absorption of
\mathcal{U}_{k}
by the condensation defect is the statement that the global symmetry generators become trivialized when the symmetry is gauged.

Next we shall consider the more involved product coming from the parallel fusion
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}(M^{3})\otimes\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}(M^{3})
of a condensation defect with itself. From (
3.2
), we can immediately deduce that this product must be proportional to
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}(M^{3})
. Carrying out the computation more explicitly using (
2.22

;
Bah:2023ymy

. We denote such a fusion rule schematically as

\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}=|T_{K}|\,\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}\,,

(3.15)

where
T_{K}(M^{3})
is the decoupled Dijkgraaf-Witten theory,

\int\mathcal{D}a_{1}^{\prime}\mathcal{D}c_{1}^{\prime}\exp\left(2\pi i\int_{M^{3}}Ka_{1}^{\prime}\wedge dc_{1}^{\prime}\right)\,.

(3.16)

The exact interpretation of the coefficient
\left|T_{K}(M^{3})\right|
is subtle, and we will return to it later in Section
4.5
. For now it is important to note that the fusion rule implies the existence of a 2-dimensional interface between the product
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{[1]}\otimes\mathcal{C}_{K}^{[1]}
and the original defect
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{[1]}
.

For our present purposes, the above tells us that fusing two condensation defects yields again a condensation defect, up to some coefficient. In this sense, condensation defects are
idempotent
operators, provided that the decoupled TQFT does not act on any bulk operators. We will give a precise definition of idempotence later in a categorical context.

We can repeat the argument for the higher condensation defect
\mathcal{C}^{[2]}_{\dot{K}}
living on the
\mathcal{C}^{[1]}_{K}
worldvolume and find that it too is idempotent, i.e.

\mathcal{C}^{[2]}_{\dot{K}}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{[2]}_{\dot{K}}=|\mathfrak{T}_{\dot{K}}|\,\mathcal{C}^{[2]}_{\dot{K}}\,,

(3.17)

where now
\mathfrak{T}_{\dot{K}}(M^{2})
is a decoupled 2-dimensional
\mathbb{Z}_{\dot{K}}
gauge theory, with continuum action given by

\int\mathcal{D}c_{0}^{\prime}\mathcal{D}a_{0}^{\prime}\exp\left(2\pi i\int_{M^{2}}\dot{K}c_{0}^{\prime}da_{0}^{\prime}\right)\,.

(3.18)

By the same token, one finds that all
n
-condensation defects in

are idempotent for

, with fusion rules similar to (
3.15
) and (
3.17

Higher-categorical Structures

In Section
2

n
-condensation defects, in the 4d QFT
\mathcal{T}
are naturally
\mathbb{C}
-linear, additive, and monoidal on purely physical grounds. The same conclusion applies to generic
d
-dimensional QFTs as well. Consequently, we would like to assemble a higher category
\mathfrak{C}
encoding the data of the symmetry of
\mathcal{T}
.

To be more specific,
\mathfrak{C}
will be a
d

\mathcal{T}
and their fusion rules, such that it forms a fusion

18

Strictly speaking, the definition we are using in this paper is that for a

-category. For our purposes, we will refer to it loosely as a fusion
(d-1)
-category.
The main focus in the rest of this paper is to argue that
\mathfrak{C}
is indeed also Karoubi-complete, and to explore its physical implications. In order to do so, we will first need to introduce some (higher) categorical notions and terminologies.

As a warm-up, let us briefly review the inductive definition of a higher category. A
0-category
is simply a
set
of objects. For our purposes, we always want this set to be finite. A
1-category
contains the following data:

•

a collection of objects (or 0-morphisms),

•

for any two objects
X
and
Y
, a collection
{\rm Hom}(X,Y)
of 1-morphisms
f:X\to Y
, such that
{\rm Hom}(X,Y)
forms a set, i.e. a 0-category,

•

for any two maps
f\in{\rm Hom}(X,Y)
and
g\in{\rm Hom}(Y,Z)
, there is a well-defined composition
g\circ f\in{\rm Hom}(X,Z)
,

•

for any object
X
, there is an identity 1-morphism
1_{X}\in{\rm Hom}(X,X)
.

The collection
{\rm Hom}(X,X)
of
endomorphisms
of an object
X
, i.e. 1-morphisms
f:X\to X
, is conveniently denoted instead as
{\rm End}(X)
. Moreover, the composition must be defined such that it is associative and unital, with the appropriate identity 1-morphisms acting as units. In what follows, we refer to such data (and higher generalizations) as the
coherence data
of a category.

To define a 2-category, we need the following data:

•

a collection of objects and their 1-morphisms as above,

•

for any two 1-morphisms
f_{1},f_{2}\in{\rm Hom}(X,Y)
, a collection
{\rm Hom}(f_{1},f_{2})
of 2-morphisms
\alpha:f_{1}\to f_{2}
satisfying suitable coherence relations, such that
{\rm Hom}(f_{1},f_{2})
forms a 1-category.

Furthermore, the corresponding coherence data may be weakened to hold only up to invertible 2-morphisms.
19
19

19

In this work, we do not demand any strictness on our categories, so that any morphisms need only hold up to a higher isomorphism. As such any time the word “category” is used it should be implicitly understood to be weak.

An
n
-category

-category. We will henceforth use the notation

to denote the collection of all

, in an

-category.

4.5
Decoupled TQFT Fusion Coefficients

In Section
3.2
, we saw the presence of “fusion coefficients” described by decoupled TQFTs such as in (
3.15
). We previously made sense of similar coefficients by saying they should count something, as in (
2.14
), and therefore are non-negative integers. However, the decoupled TQFTs do not a priori lend themselves to such an interpretation as integers.

Here we analyze in what precise sense the TQFTs are “coefficients,” discuss the values these coefficients take, and lay some groundwork to discuss idempotent defects in greater generality in the next section. Importantly, the fact that fusion coefficients are inherently subtler than just being integers is strictly a higher-categorical phenomenon. Before investigating this claim in general spacetime dimensions, let us first review some known results of 2-dimensional theories.

If
\mathcal{T}
is only 2-dimensional, then the only symmetry defects described in
{\rm End}(\mathcal{T})
are topological lines and points. As in (
2.5

\mathcal{O}_{a}(M^{1})
and
\mathcal{O}_{b}(M^{1})

(\mathcal{O}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{b})(M^{1})

(\mathcal{O}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{b})(M^{1})
can itself be expressed as a linear combination of other line operators
\mathcal{O}_{c}(M^{1})

fusion rule
as follows,

(\mathcal{O}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{b})(M^{1})\cong\bigoplus_{i}N_{a,b}^{c}\mathcal{O}_{c}(M^{1})\,,

(4.11)

where

counts the number of times each line

Recall that, for a 2-dimensional theory,
{\rm End}(\mathcal{T})
constitutes a 1-category. By definition, the collection of 1-morphisms,

\mathcal{O}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{b}
and
\mathcal{O}_{c}

(4.12)

measure precisely the dimension of (finite-dimensional) vector spaces, and so they are necessarily non-negative integers in the case of a 2-dimensional QFT

;

;
Moore:1988qv

If we now consider the case of a

6
Anomalous Finite Abelian Symmetries

Previously, we have always specified that all the symmetries we were dealing with were strictly anomaly-free. Nonetheless, we regularly saw hints of how the presence of anomalies could modify the discussion and introduce subtleties. In the current section, we will address additional details arising when anomalies are present. We begin with a general discussion of (higher) anomalies and their relation to the categorical structure of
\mathfrak{C}
, and follow with three examples relating to its Karoubi completeness.

Consider again a general

with a global

A^{(p)}\subseteq G^{(p)}

A^{(p)}
to be self-anomalous. This is not restrictive; if there were some anomaly-free subgroup
H^{(p)}\subseteq A^{(p)}
, one could repeat the analysis of the previous sections on
H^{(p)}
and apply the subsequent discussion on the remaining quotient
A^{(p)}/H^{(p)}
.

Despite being independent coherence data,

In the assumptions of the previous sections, we have implicitly taken both

If desired, one could construct more non-trivial higher groups by iterating this process. In particular, one could start with

Examples

Let us now see how the presence of non-trivial

Non-trivial

The simplest case to consider is when a given

Trivial 1-anomaly, non-trivial self 0-anomaly:

We thus see that one cannot gauge the

Once we have written the 1-condensation defects of interest as products of

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p},\,0}^{[1]}=\iota_{p_{1}}\otimes\rho_{p_{1}}^{\prime}\,,\qquad\rho_{p_{1}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\rho_{p_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{C}_{p_{2}}^{[1]}\,,\\
\hskip-9.0pt\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p},\,p-1}^{[1]}=\iota_{p_{1}}^{\prime}\otimes\rho_{p_{1}}\,,\qquad\iota_{p_{1}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}_{p_{2}}^{[1]}\otimes\iota_{p_{1}}\,,\end{gathered}

where

It should be noted that this choice of splitting is not unique, and we have only chosen one such possibility. However, as discussed in Section

This further justifies the claim that the the two 1-condensation defects (
6.23

\mathcal{C}_{p_{2}}^{[1]}
into another set of gauging and ungauging interfaces. We therefore see the only idempotent 1-condensation defects can indeed be split without issue, and the symmetry category

More general constructions:

We have so far given three specific examples for how the presence of non-trivial anomalies can affect the
m
-condensation defects in a theory, and hence how Karoubi completeness manifests in the associated
d
-category
\mathfrak{C}
. Let us now take a step back and outline a general procedure for such an analysis.

The first step in studying the effect of anomalies it to have the anomalies in the first place. Either by fiat or by computation, one can find the 0-anomalies
\omega
and
\sigma
and thus their remnants as
q
-anomalies
\omega^{\rm tr}
and
\sigma^{\rm tr}
. If the latter are trivial, then
m
-condensation defects of appropriate dimension can be constructed out of the relevant symmetry operators. This also requires the possible data of SPTs as labeled by
\alpha
in (
6.22
). Once the
m
-condensation defects are constructed, the question becomes which, if any,
m
-morphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
are idempotent. Any that are should then be able to be factored into gauging/ungauging interfaces. These may only come from products of other
m
-condensation defects as in the final example above, in which case the possible splittings are inherited from the individual factors. If any of these steps fail, the process truncates without any bearing on
\mathfrak{C}
being Karoubi-complete.

7
Discussion and Outlook

It is important for us to emphasize that what we have done in this work is not meant to be a rigorous mathematical proof that all QFTs have symmetry structures described by higher fusion categories.
47
47

47

In fact, there are known counterexamples which are not described by fusion categories
Gaiotto:2019xmp
.
Rather, we have illustrated that the defining features of a (separable weak) higher fusion category
Gaiotto:2019xmp
;
Johnson-Freyd:2020usu
have natural physical origins. Particularly, the
n
-condensation defects for
1\leq n\leq d
in a
d
-dimensional QFT are necessarily arranged in a hierarchical manner that respects the nested structure of a higher category, due to the fact that Pontryagin dual/quantum symmetries are localized to the worldvolume in which the original symmetry is (higher) gauged.

Among the properties of a higher fusion category, the notion of Karoubi completeness provides a crucial organizing principle for us to investigate condensation defects, which constitute a cornerstone in the modern study of generalized symmetries in QFTs. It reveals the precise relation between
n
-condensation defects (which have codimension
n
) and gauging with respect to a codimension-
(n-1)
submanifold of spacetime. For anomaly-free (finite Abelian) symmetries, an
n
-condensation defect is idempotent (in a suitably categorified sense), and so it always factorizes into a pair of gauging interfaces sandwiching a “bulk theory” of dimension
d-n+1
. The spectrum of operators present in such a bulk theory matches with that in the original
n
-condensation defect. For anomalous (finite Abelian) symmetries, an
n
-condensation defect may or may not be idempotent. We argued that the condition for it to be idempotent, hence splittable, corresponds precisely to the anomaly being trivializable, such that a corresponding “bulk” exists.

In this paper, although we only focused on finite Abelian (group-like) symmetries, we saw that there is already a rich interplay between the symmetry generators, charged operators, and condensation defects. An immediate question one may ask is whether our analysis of higher condensation defects can be generalized to finite non-Abelian symmetries. In this case, the quantum symmetry one obtains by (higher) gauging a group
G
is no longer the Pontryagin dual
\widehat{G}=\text{Hom}(G,U(1))
, which is still Abelian by definition. Instead, it should be replaced with a generalization known as the Tannaka dual, i.e. the category
\text{Rep}(G)
of
G
-representations, or potentially higher-categorical analogues depending on the dimensions involved (e.g. see
Bartsch:2022mpm
;
Bartsch:2022ytj
;
Bartsch:2023pzl
;
Bartsch:2023wvv
). Assuming Karoubi completeness always holds as a general property of the symmetry
d
-category
\mathfrak{C}
for a
d
-dimensional QFT, we expect the corresponding “non-Abelian” condensation defects to be splittable into gauging interfaces sandwiching a “bulk theory” with
\text{Rep}(G)
symmetry. On top of that, analogously to the construction of the “dual” higher condensation defects in Section
2.2
by 1-gauging the Pontryagin-dual symmetry
\widehat{G}
, here one will need to study the gauging of the Tannaka-dual symmetry
\text{Rep}(G)
in a higher-categorical setting for generic spacetime dimensions.

More generally, it will be interesting to apply the same philosophy to study non-invertible symmetries which are not group-like in nature. However, to the best of our knowledge, it is somewhat unclear how to unambiguously define the notions of “gauging” and “anomaly” of a non-invertible symmetry in generic spacetime dimensions (see
Kaidi:2023maf
;
Choi:2023xjw
;
Sun:2023xxv
;
Cordova:2023bja
;
Antinucci:2023ezl
;
Choi:2023vgk
;
Antinucci:2024ltv
;
DelZotto:2025yoy
for some partial results). We anticipate that a good definition of condensation defects for non-invertible symmetries should nevertheless have appropriate idempotence and splitting properties that are compatible with these notions.

Another key aspect in the study of generalized symmetries is the construction of the Symmetry Topological Field Theory (SymTFT)
Apruzzi:2021nmk
;
Freed:2022qnc
, which lives in a
(d+1)
-dimensional bulk. In terms of the fusion category encoding the symmetries of our
d
-dimensional QFT, the SymTFT roughly corresponds to the notion of its Drinfeld center
2002math……3060E
, or rather its higher-categorical equivalent (e.g. see
Johnson-Freyd:2020usu
). Simply put, the
(d+1)
-dimensional SymTFT bulk is supported on the manifold
W^{d}\times I
, whose boundary consists of two copies of the spacetime manifold
W^{d}
. These two copies respectively accommodate the gapped and gapless degrees of freedom, such that upon the interval compactification of the SymTFT, one recovers the full QFT. For finite group-like symmetries, a choice of global form of the QFT amounts to a choice of boundary condition on the gapped boundary of the SymTFT. As we learned in this paper, if we insert a 1-condensation defect in the QFT
\mathcal{T}
, then it splits into a pair of gauging interfaces sandwiching a gauged theory in the middle, say,
\mathcal{T}/K^{(p)}
. From the perspective of the SymTFT bulk, such a configuration has to result from a hybrid choice of boundary conditions imposed on the gapped boundary. Moreover, one may even insert condensation defects within condensation defects, which unfold into a non-trivial, nested, network of sub-theories of various codimensions. It will then be instructive to systematically characterize the corresponding boundary conditions, incorporating the entire hierarchy of higher condensation defects, and establish concrete physical realizations in terms of the SymTFT.

A related question is the holographic realization of higher condensation defects. As pointed out by
Apruzzi:2022rei
;
GarciaEtxebarria:2022vzq
, followed by a series of subsequent work, non-invertible symmetry defects, including condensation defects, have a holographic dual description in terms of D-branes in string theory. Particularly, it was shown in
Bah:2023ymy
that 1-condensation defects can be understood as the consequence of pairs of D-branes and anti-D-branes undergoing tachyon condensation
Sen:1998ii
;
Sen:1998sm
;
Sen:1999mg
, at least in the examples studied therein. In this scenario, the top-level D
p
-brane charge cancels between the D
p
-brane and the
\overline{\text{D}p}
-brane, but the lower-level D
(p-2)
-brane charge generically does not vanish, and is interpreted quantum mechanically as a superposition of D
(p-2)
-branes localized within the worlvolume of the
\text{D}p
-
\overline{\text{D}p}
-brane pair, which is the defining characteristic of a 1-condensation defect. Furthermore, the dual gauge field on the 1-condensation defect, whose holonomies are the symmetry generators of the Pontryagin dual symmetry, admits a natural stringy interpretation as the Chan-Paton gauge field on the D-branes. It is therefore possible for one to reconstruct the hierarchy of higher condensation defects in terms of D-brane physics. Doing so will hopefully also shed light on the precise relation between two parallel mathematical descriptions, namely, generalized symmetries in QFTs using higher fusion categories, and D-branes in string theory using K-theory.

-categories are additive. We have discussed these properties in the case of our symmetry operators in
\mathcal{T}
surrounding (
2.12
), namely, there is a zero operator at every level of
{\rm End}(\mathcal{T})
, as well as a notion of addition
\oplus
at every level, thus making
{\rm End}(\mathcal{T})
additive.

Composition of condensations:

A useful property of categorical condensations is that they can be composed. This is crucial for us to realize sequentially gauging in a physical theory as compositions of categorical condensations. More precisely, suppose there exist two
n
-condensations of objects in an
n
-category, i.e.

\rho_{X,Y}:X\rightleftarrows Y:\iota_{X,Y}\,,\qquad\rho_{Y,Z}:Y\rightleftarrows Z:\iota_{Y,Z}\,,

(A.2)

such that the morphisms above satisfy

e_{X,Y}\circ e_{X,Y}\mathrel{\,\hskip 3.22916pt\joinrel\rhook\joinrel\hskip-3.22916pt\joinrel\rightarrow}e_{X,Y}\,,\quad\rho_{X,Y}\circ\iota_{X,Y}\mathrel{\,\hskip 3.22916pt\joinrel\rhook\joinrel\hskip-3.22916pt\joinrel\rightarrow}1_{Y}\,,\quad e_{Y,Z}\circ e_{Y,Z}\mathrel{\,\hskip 3.22916pt\joinrel\rhook\joinrel\hskip-3.22916pt\joinrel\rightarrow}e_{Y,Z}\,,\quad\rho_{Y,Z}\circ\iota_{Y,Z}\mathrel{\,\hskip 3.22916pt\joinrel\rhook\joinrel\hskip-3.22916pt\joinrel\rightarrow}1_{Z}\,.

(A.3)

If we define the compositions of these morphisms as (up to isomorphisms)

\rho_{X,Z}\cong\rho_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{X,Y}\,,\qquad\iota_{X,Z}\cong\iota_{X,Y}\circ\iota_{Y,Z}\,,\qquad e_{X,Z}\cong\iota_{X,Z}\circ\rho_{X,Z}\,,

(A.4)

then one obtains

\displaystyle e_{X,Z}\circ e_{X,Z}

\displaystyle\cong(\iota_{X,Y}\circ\iota_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{X,Y})\circ(\iota_{X,Y}\circ\iota_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{X,Y})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\,\hskip 3.22916pt\joinrel\rhook\joinrel\hskip-3.22916pt\joinrel\rightarrow}(\iota_{X,Y}\circ\iota_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{Y,Z})\circ 1_{Y}\circ(\iota_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{X,Y})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\,\hskip 3.22916pt\joinrel\rhook\joinrel\hskip-3.22916pt\joinrel\rightarrow}(\iota_{X,Y}\circ\iota_{Y,Z})\circ 1_{Z}\circ(\rho_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{X,Y})

\displaystyle\cong\iota_{X,Z}\circ\rho_{X,Z}

\displaystyle\cong e_{X,Z}\,,

(A.5)

and also

\rho_{X,Z}\circ\iota_{X,Z}\cong(\rho_{Y,Z}\circ\rho_{X,Y})\circ(\iota_{X,Y}\circ\iota_{Y,Z})\mathrel{\,\hskip 3.22916pt\joinrel\rhook\joinrel\hskip-3.22916pt\joinrel\rightarrow}\rho_{Y,Z}\circ 1_{Y}\circ\iota_{Y,Z}\mathrel{\,\hskip 3.22916pt\joinrel\rhook\joinrel\hskip-3.22916pt\joinrel\rightarrow}1_{Z}\,,

(A.6)

which implies that

\rho_{X,Z}:X\rightleftarrows Z:\iota_{X,Z}

(A.7)

is an
n
-condensation. Therefore, the composition of two
n
-condensations is also an
n
-condensation.

Semisimplicity:

In an additive
n
-category
\mathfrak{C}
, an object
X
is said to be
simple
if it is indecomposable under the direct sum, i.e. there is no way it can be written as
X=\bigoplus_{i}X_{i}
for
X_{i}
simple. From here, we can define a semisimple
n
-category recursively.

An
n
-category
\mathfrak{C}
is said to be
semisimple
if it is locally semisimple, and every object is either simple or can be written as a finite sum of simple objects. By locally semisimple, we mean that
{\rm Hom}(X,Y)
is a semisimple
(n-1)
-category for any two objects
X
and
Y
in
\mathfrak{C}
. This compiles recursively for every categorical level in
\mathfrak{C}
.

Finiteness:

For an
n
-category
\mathfrak{C}
to be finite it must have a finite set of connected components of simple objects and the
(n-1)
-category
{\rm Hom}(X,Y)
must also be finite for any two objects
X
and
Y
in
\mathfrak{C}
.

The final property for a monoidal

A.2
Monoidal Product Coherence Relations

In Section
4.2
, we briefly reviewed the definition of a monoidal category and quickly stated the necessary coherence relations that such a structure must satisfy. Here we expand on this description and offer some proof of additional claims. We do not claim any of the material in this subsection is new. Most of it can indeed be found in standard textbooks such as
etingof2015tensor
. We simply include it for completeness and clarity.

A 1-category
\mathfrak{D}
with functor
\otimes:\mathfrak{D}\times\mathfrak{D}\to\mathfrak{D}
forms a monoidal 1-category
(\mathfrak{D},\otimes)
if the following is true.

•

There is a distinguished object
1^{[1]}
in
\mathfrak{D}
known as the monoidal unit of
\mathfrak{D}
.

•

There is a natural isomorphism defined for any
X,Y,
and
Z
in
\mathfrak{D}
,

a_{X,Y,Z}:(X\otimes Y)\otimes Z\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.959pt}{$\sim$}}X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)\,,

(A.11)

called the associator.

•

There are natural isomorphisms

L_{X}:1^{[1]}\otimes X\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.959pt}{$\sim$}}X\,,\quad\text{ and }R_{X}:X\otimes 1^{[1]}\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.959pt}{$\sim$}}X\,,

(A.12)

respectively called left and right unitors.

In order for
(\mathfrak{D},\otimes)
to be consistently defined, so that the operation
\otimes
behaves as an appropriate monoidal product, there must be additional coherence relations imposed on the above data.

The first of such data is known as the
pentagon axiom
or
pentagon identity
, and demands that the associator satisfies the following commutative diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(W\otimes X)\otimes(Y\otimes Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((W\otimes X)\otimes Y)\otimes Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W\otimes(X\otimes(Y\otimes Z))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(W\otimes(X\otimes Y))\otimes Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W\otimes((X\otimes Y)\otimes Z)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{W,X,Y\otimes Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{W\otimes X,Y,Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{W,X,Y}\otimes 1^{[2]}_{Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{W,X\otimes Y,Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1^{[2]}_{W}\otimes a_{X,Y,Z}}

(A.13)

The diagram must hold for any
W,X,Y,
and
Z
in
\mathfrak{D}
. This gives consistency between the associator and identity morphisms for each object.

The second demand is similarly known as the
triangle axiom
or
triangle identity
, and is such that the unitors satisfy the following diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\otimes(1^{[1]}\otimes Y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(X\otimes 1^{[1]})\otimes Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\otimes Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1^{[2]}_{X}\otimes L_{Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{X,1^{[1]},Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{X}\otimes 1^{[2]}_{Y}}

(A.14)

This ensures consistency between the associator and the monoidal unit of
(\mathfrak{D},\otimes)
. As long as both (
A.13
) and (
A.14
) are satisfied, then
(\mathfrak{D},\otimes)
forms a consistent monoidal 1-category.

The monoidal operation
\otimes
in a monoidal category
(\mathfrak{D},\otimes)
may be equipped with additional structure. Such structure is a categorified analogue of commutativity, and is known as a
braiding
. A braiding structure is given by an isomorphism,

c_{X,Y}:X\otimes Y\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.959pt}{$\sim$}}Y\otimes X\,,

(A.15)

for any two objects
X
and
Y
in
\mathfrak{D}
. In order to be consistent with the rest of the data in
(\mathfrak{D},\otimes)
, the braiding structure must satisfy additional coherence data on top of that already demanded of a monoidal category. In particular, the braiding structure must be consistent with the associator morphisms, through what is known as the
hexagon axiom
or the
hexagon identity
. This is the statement that
c_{X,Y}
must satisfy the following commutative diagram for any
X,Y,
and
Z
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(Y\otimes Z)\otimes X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(X\otimes Y)\otimes Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y\otimes(Z\otimes X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(Y\otimes X)\otimes Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y\otimes(X\otimes Z)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{X,Y\otimes Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{Y,Z,X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{X,Y,Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{X,Y}\otimes 1^{[2]}_{Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a_{Y,X,Z}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1^{[2]}_{Y}\otimes c_{X,Z}}

(A.16)

Once this is satisfied, it follows without additional assumptions that the braiding is consistent with the unitor isomorphisms as well. If the operation
\otimes
is coherent with the existence of
c_{X,Y}
in this way, we say
(\mathfrak{D},\otimes)
is a
braided
monoidal category.

A.3
Homotopy Cardinality of Discrete Gauge Theory

Here we will further explain the equivalence of the two expressions (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) in the case of a
d
-dimensional QFT
\mathcal{T}
with a finite
p
-form symmetry
G^{(p)}
. After constructing
\mathfrak{C}
as in the main body, let us focus on the operators in
{\rm End}(\mathcal{T})
, which will form a
(d-1)
-subcategory of
\mathfrak{C}
. For notational brevity, let us denote
\mathfrak{T}\coloneqq{\rm End}(\mathcal{T})
. In considering the “size” of
\mathfrak{T}
, we compute the homotopy cardinality as in (
4.15
),

\big|\mathfrak{T}\big|=\sum_{x\in\pi_{0}(\mathfrak{T})}\prod_{k=1}^{d-1}\left|\pi_{k}(\mathfrak{T},x)\right|^{(-1)^{k}}=\sum_{x\in\pi_{0}(\mathfrak{T})}\frac{\big|\pi_{2}(\mathfrak{T},x)\big|\big|\pi_{4}(\mathfrak{T},x)\big|\cdots}{\big|\pi_{1}(\mathfrak{T},x)\big|\big|\pi_{3}(\mathfrak{T},x)\big|\cdots}\,.

(A.17)

In order to proceed in evaluating this expression, we need to take a closer look at the category
\mathfrak{T}
. The objects (and higher morphisms) are given by extended operators in our field theory. Such operators can equivalently be viewed as maps from the spacetime
M^{d}
to the classifying space of our symmetry group
B^{p+1}G=K(G,p+1)
. In other words, we can view our category
\mathfrak{T}
as the mapping space
{\rm Map}(M^{d},B^{p+1}G)
.

Once we have reframed our understanding of
\mathfrak{T}
in this way, the computation becomes almost automatic. We simply need to use one more result, originally from
fbc52825-a403-38b8-87e5-64427df9db62
,

\pi_{n}{\rm Map}(M^{d},B^{p+1}G)\cong H^{p+1-n}(M^{d};G)\,.

(A.18)

Applying this relation to (
A.17
), we thus find

\big|\mathfrak{T}\big|=\big|H^{p+1}(M^{d};G)\big|\frac{\big|H^{p-1}(M^{d};G)\big|\big|H^{p-3}(M^{d};G)\big|\cdots}{\big|H^{p}(M^{d};G)\big|\big|H^{p-2}(M^{d};G)\big|\cdots}\,,

(A.19)

where the first factor comes from the sum over connected components of
{\rm Map}(M^{d},B^{p+1}G)
in (
A.17
). Meanwhile, the partition function for a
(p+1)
-form gauge theory with gauge group
G
can be computed as

Z[M^{d},G]=\frac{|H^{p+1}(M^{d};G)||H^{p-1}(M^{d};G)|\cdots}{|H^{p}(M^{d};G)||H^{p-2}(M^{d};G)|\cdots}\,,

(A.20)

by counting the number of inequivalent gauge configurations, accounting for all (higher) gauge transformations. Hence, we see the desired equivalence between (
A.17
) and (
A.20
).
