Two algebraic proofs of the transcendence of e \mathrm{e} based on formal power series
Martin Klazar

Abstract
We remind the classical analytical proof of the transcendence of e \mathrm{e} due to Hilbert. Then, using formal power series, we give two algebraic semiformal proofs of this result. The first proof is a specialization of the proof of the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem found by Beukers, Bézivin and Robba [ 2 ] . The second proof uses improper integrals of formal power series and is due to this author. We explain what “semiformal” means.



1
Hilbert’s proof that
\mathrm{e}
is transcendental. Why this proof is not completely satisfactory. How to fix it

The transcendence of the number
\mathrm{e}
was first proven by Hermite
[
4
]
.
Hilbert’s proof
[
5
]
goes as
follows. We suppose for the contradiction that there exists
n+1
,

n\in\mathbb{N}
(
=\{1,2,\dots\}
), integers
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
,
\dots
,
a_{n}
, not all of
them
0
, such that

P:=a_{0}+a_{1}\mathrm{e}+a_{2}\mathrm{e}^{2}+\dots+a_{n}\mathrm{e}^{n}=0\,.

We may assume that
a_{0}\neq 0
. For
r\in\mathbb{N}
we define integral polynomials

p_{r}(x)=x^{r}((x-1)(x-2)\dots(x-n))^{r+1}\,,

with
n
as in
P
. We have the expansions

p_{r}(x)=(-1)^{n(r+1)}(n!)^{r+1}\cdot x^{r}+ax^{r+1}+\dots

and

p_{r}(x+j)=bx^{r+1}+cx^{r+2}+\dots\,,

where
j=1,2,\dots,n
and
a,b,c,\dots
are in
\mathbb{Z}
(the integers). We consider improper integrals

{\textstyle I_{r}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.}

Using the additivity of improper integrals, we express
the product
PI_{r}
as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle PI_{r}=A_{r}+B_{r}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{j}\int_{0}^{j}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x+\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{j}\int_{j}^{+\infty}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x}\,.

As for the quantity
A_{r}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{j}\int_{0}^{j}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x
, it is easy to
estimate it by means of M-L bounds on integrals as

|A_{r}|\leq c^{r},\ r\in\mathbb{N}\,,

where
c\geq 1
is a constant (depending only on the coefficients
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
,
\dots
,
a_{n}
).

We determine the second quantity

{\textstyle B_{r}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{j}\int_{j}^{+\infty}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.}

We claim that
B_{r}
is an integral multiple of
r!
and that
B_{r}\neq 0

for infinitely many
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
c^{r}/r!\to 0
as
r\to\infty
for any
constant
c\geq 1
, the above equalities

A_{r}+B_{r}=0,\ r\in\mathbb{N}\,,

are contradictory and we are done. We prove the claim by means of the integral identity that

{\textstyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}x^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x=n!}

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(
=\{0,1,\dots\}
).
The identity is due to Euler and is easy to
establish by induction on
n
via integration by parts. Using the linearity of improper integrals, we obtain for any integers
b_{0}
,
b_{1}
,
\dots
,
b_{m}

with
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the more general identity that

{\textstyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big(\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}x^{j}\big)\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}\cdot j!\,.}

Thus

\displaystyle B_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\mathrm{e}^{j}\int_{j}^{+\infty}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\int_{j}^{+\infty}p_{r}((x-j)+j)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-(x-j)}\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{r}(y+j)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-y}\,\mathrm{d}y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pm a_{0}(n!)^{r+1}\cdot r!+m_{r}\cdot(r+1)!\,,

for some
m_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}
. In the second line, we used the identity that

\mathrm{e}^{a+b}=\mathrm{e}^{a}\mathrm{e}^{b}
. In the third line, we changed the variable to
y=x-j
.
In the fourth line, we used the above generalization of Euler’s identity and
the expansions of
p_{r}(x+j)
,
j=0,1,\dots,n
. It is clear that

B_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}
and is divisible by
r!
. If
B_{r}=0
then

(r+1)\,|\,a_{0}(n!)^{r+1}
. This divisibility is excluded when
r+1
is
coprime to the number
a_{0}\cdot n!
. Hence
B_{r}\neq 0
for infinitely many

r\in\mathbb{N}
.

\Box

Baker
[
1
, Theorem 1.2 on p. 4]

gives a variant of the previous proof using only proper integrals

\int_{a}^{b}
. A similar proof is given in Conrad
[
3
]
.

Hilbert’s proof is elegant and short, but it has the
unsatisfactory feature that it employs uncountable sets, for example, the integrands in
Euler’s identity. We explain how to improve upon it by proving the transcendence of
\mathrm{e}
in a way satisfying two conditions.

(A)

Only hereditarily at most countable (HMC) sets are directly used in the proof.

(B)

No technical notion of mathematical logic such as,
for example, the notion of a formula, is used in the proof.

We call proofs satisfying these two conditions
AB proofs
.
A set
x
is HMC
if for every index
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and every chain of sets

x_{n}\in x_{n-1}\in\dots\in x_{0}=x\,,

the set
x_{n}
is at most countable
(finite or countable). In a related result, Kaibkhanov and Skopenkov
[
6
]
gave an AB proof of
the transcendence of the Mahler number
\sum_{n\geq 0}2^{-2^{n}}
based on binary expansions.

There are at least four ways to an AB proof of the transcendence of

\mathrm{e}
. For the first two see
[
7
]
. The other two ways are
the topic of the present article. They are two AB proofs of the
transcendence of
\mathrm{e}
based on formal power
series. The first proof in Section
2
is
a specialization of the proof in
[
2
]
. The second proof in
Section
3
is due to this author. We presented it already
in early versions of

[
7
]
, and we rework it here.

The word “directly” in condition (A)
means that non-HMC sets may appear in the proof only as
supersets of the main, directly used sets, which have to be HMC. We may
say, for example, that for every two sequences
(a_{n}),(b_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
we
have
\dots\;
. It is clear that the set
\mathbb{R}
is mentioned only for
convenience and can be easily
formulated away. We allow such mentions of non-HMC sets in AB proofs for the
sake of natural and legible formulations.

In conclusion of the introduction, we review formal power series and
explain the meaning of the adjective “semiformal”. If
R

is an (integral) domain, we write
R[[x]]
for the uncountable
set of maps
f\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\to R
. They are written as infinite
formal sums

{\textstyle f=f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}}

with
a_{n}=f(n)
. We denote the coefficient
a_{n}
also as
[x^{n}]f
.
We work with functionals on
R[[x]]
, which are maps from

R[[x]]
to
R
. For example, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have the
functional
f\mapsto[x^{n}]f
.

Besides functionals, the set
R[[x]]
is endowed with the operations of addition and multiplication

\displaystyle{\textstyle f(x)+g(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}+\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}x^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}(a_{n}+b_{n})x^{n}\,\text{ and}}

\displaystyle{\textstyle f(x)\cdot g(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}x^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}b_{n-k}\big)x^{n}}

which have neutral elements
0=0+0x+0x^{2}+\dots

and
1=1+0x+0x^{2}+\dots\;
. For any
c,d\in R
and
f,g\in R[[x]]
we form the linear combination

{\textstyle cf(x)+dg(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(ca_{n}+db_{n})x^{n}\,.}

This is in fact a family of binary operations on
R[[x]]
, one for every pair
c,d
. The structure

R[[x]]=\langle R[[x]],\,0,\,1,\,+,\,\cdot\rangle

is a domain, in which
f
is an
R[[x]]
-unit iff
[x^{0}]f
is an
R
-unit. An
important unary operation on
R[[x]]

is the (formal) derivative,

{\textstyle\big(\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}\big)^{\prime}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)_{R}\cdot a_{n+1}x^{n}\,.}

Less often one uses the (formal) primitive

{\textstyle\int\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{n+1}x^{n+1}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n-1}}{n}x^{n}\,.}

In this article,
\int
is more important than the derivative. If
K
is the normed field
K=\mathbb{R}

or
K=\mathbb{C}
(complex numbers), we denote by
K\{x\}
the subdomain of

K[[x]]
formed by the
convergent
formal power series
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x_{n}
. They satisfy for every
c\in K
that

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}c^{n}=0_{K}\,.

In other words,

f(x)\in K\{x\}
iff
f(x)
has infinite radius of convergence.

The functionals and operations on
R[[x]]
we have listed so far,
f\mapsto[x^{n}]f
,

+
,
\cdot
,
(\cdot)^{\prime}
,
\int(\cdot)
and linear combinations, are
formal
in the sense
that, for example, every coefficient in the product

f\cdot g
,

{\textstyle[x^{n}](f\cdot g)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}[x^{j}]f\cdot[x^{n-j}]g\,,}

is determined by only finitely many
coefficients in
f
and
g
, and the same holds for the other functionals
and operations. We say that a proof involving formal power series

f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}
in
K[[x]]
is
formal
if it employs only
formal functionals and operations, and if it does not use any values of
f(x)
(
c\in K
)

{\textstyle f(c)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}c^{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}c^{k}\,.}

It is an open problem to give
a completely formal AB proof of the transcendence of
\mathrm{e}
based
on formal power series. No such proof is known to this
author.

However, we can present two such proofs in the next two sections
if formality is relaxed to semiformality.
Semiformal
functionals
are defined
on
K\{x\}
, may use limit transitions, and may use at most
countably many values
f(c)
for
f\in K[[x]]
and
c\in K
. The same
holds for
semiformal
operations
. For examples of semiformal functionals and operations on
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
see Definitions
3.7
and
3.3
, respectively.
We say that a proof involving elements in
K[[x]]
is

semiformal
if it satisfies the following condition.

(SF)

Only formal and semiformal functionals and operations on
K[[x]]
are
used in the proof. For every formal power series involved in the proof, only at most
countably many of its values are used.

In Sections
2
and
3
we give two AB
\&
SF
proofs of the transcendence
of
\mathrm{e}
.

2
Specializing the proof of Beukers, Bézivin and Robba

The Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem says that

b_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{1}}+b_{2}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{2}}+\dots+b_{t}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{t}}\neq 0

whenever
b_{j}
and
\alpha_{j}
are
2t
, with
t\in\mathbb{N}
, algebraic numbers in
\mathbb{C}
such that
every
b_{j}\neq 0
and the
\alpha_{j}
are mutually distinct. See
Baker
[
1
, Theorem 1.4 on pp. 6–8]
for an analytic proof. Using
formal power series, Beukers, Bézivin, and Robba
[
2
]
obtained another proof of the LW theorem. In this section, we
specialize their proof to
b_{j},\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
, which
gives the transcendence of
\mathrm{e}
. In order to clearly
present the logical structure of their proof, we reorganize
the exposition in
[
2
]
.

We first recall that a formal
power series
f(x)
in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
is rational if there exist two
polynomials
p(x),q(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
such that
q(0)=1
and
q(x)f(x)=p(x)
; we
may assume that
p(x)
and
q(x)
are coprime. If
f(x)
and
g(x)
in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
are
rational, then so are
f(x)+g(x)
,
f(x)g(x)
, and
f^{\prime}(x)
. Any rational formal power series

f(x)\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]
has a unique decomposition into partial fractions

{\textstyle f(x)=r(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{j}}\frac{\beta_{j,i}}{(1-\alpha_{j}x)^{i}}\,,}

where
r(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
,
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
m_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\beta_{j,i},\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\beta_{j,m_{j}}\neq 0
, and
the
\alpha_{j}
are nonzero and mutually distinct. For
t=0
, the double sum is interpreted as
0
, and the numbers
m_{j}
and
\alpha_{j}
come from the factorization

{\textstyle q(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}x)^{m_{j}}\,.}

The partial fractions are the formal power series

{\textstyle\frac{\beta_{j,i}}{(1-\alpha_{j}x)^{i}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{-i}{n}\beta_{j,i}\alpha_{j}^{n}\cdot x^{n}\,.}

We say that
1/\alpha_{j}
is a pole of
f(x)
with order
m_{j}
.

Proposition 2.1

If
p(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
c\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
(
=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
) and if
A(x)
in
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
is rational, then the
rational formal power series

p(x)A(x)+cx^{m}A^{\prime}(x)

either has no pole (is a polynomial) or all its poles have orders at least
2
.

Proof.
The product

p(x)A(x)
only possibly decreases the orders of the poles of
A(x)

or cancels them and does not create any new poles. The product and derivative

cx^{m}A^{\prime}(x)
preserve all poles of
A(x)
, increase their orders by

1
, and do not create any new poles. The sum
p(x)A(x)+cx^{m}A^{\prime}(x)
merges the
two sets of poles. It follows that the resulting set of poles with their
orders is that of
cx^{m}A^{\prime}(x)
.

\Box

We extracted the following theorem on rational formal power series from the
arguments in
[
2
]
.

Theorem 2.2

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
,
\dots
,
b_{t}
be nonzero integers and
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
,
\dots
,
\alpha_{t}
be distinct natural numbers. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we define
integers

u_{n}=b_{1}\alpha_{1}^{n}+\dots+b_{t}\alpha_{t}^{n}\,\text{ and }\,v_{n}=n!\cdot{\textstyle\sum_{r=0}^{n}u_{r}/r!}\,.

Suppose that for some constant
A\geq 1
we have bound

v_{n}=O(A^{n})\,\text{ for }\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,.

Then the formal power series
\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}
is rational, because

{\textstyle\big(1-a_{1}x-a_{2}x^{2}-\dots-a_{t}x^{t}\big)^{k}\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]}

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where the integers
a_{j}
are the coefficients in

{\textstyle\prod_{j=1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}x)=1-a_{1}x-a_{2}x^{2}-\dots-a_{t}x^{t}\,.}

Proof.
For
k,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we define integers
v_{n}(k)
by the relations

{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}(k)x^{n}=\big(1-a_{1}x-a_{2}x^{2}-\dots-a_{t}x^{t}\big)^{k}\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}\,.}

Then for every
n\geq t
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have the recurrence

v_{n}(k+1)=v_{n}(k)-a_{1}v_{n-1}(k)-\dots-a_{t}v_{n-t}(k)\,.

We establish for every
n,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
n\geq tk
two properties of the numbers
v_{n}(k)
.

1.

We have the bound
|v_{n}(k)|\leq cA^{n}C^{k}
where
C=1+|a_{1}|+\dots+|a_{t}|
and
c\geq 0
is a constant.

2.

k!\,|\,v_{n}(k)
.

The bound in item 1 follows by induction on
k

from the assumed bound on
v_{n}=v_{n}(0)
. For
k=0
the bound
holds because it is the assumed bound on
v_{n}
. Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
n\geq t(k+1)
. Then
n-t\geq tk
, and
by the above recurrence and induction we have

\displaystyle|v_{n}(k+1)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|v_{n}(k)-a_{1}v_{n-1}(k)-\dots-a_{t}v_{n-t}(k)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\big(A^{n}C^{k}+A^{n-1}C^{k}|a_{1}|+\dots+A^{n-t}C^{k}|a_{t}|\big)\leq cA^{n}C^{k+1}\,.

In order to prove the divisibility in item 2, we note that
v_{0}=u_{0}
and that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

{\textstyle\frac{v_{n}}{n!}-\frac{v_{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\frac{u_{n}}{n!}\,\text{ (hence $v_{n}-nv_{n-1}=u_{n}$)}\,.}

For
k=0
the divisibility trivially holds. So let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We
set
(0)_{0}=(n)_{0}=1
and
(n)_{m}=n(n-1)\dots(n-m+1)
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we
combine
\min(n,k)
displayed differences, multiply the result by
n!
, and get that

{\textstyle v_{n}=(n)_{0}u_{n}+(n)_{1}u_{n-1}+\dots+(n)_{k-1}u_{n-k+1}+(n)_{k}v_{n-k}}\,,

where the
u_{\dots}
and
v_{\dots}
with negative indices are defined
arbitrarily, for example as
0
.
We denote the last summand
(n)_{k}v_{n-k}
(
\in\mathbb{Z}
) by
w_{n}
. Note that always
k!\,|\,w_{n}
because

w_{n}=k!\cdot\binom{n}{k}v_{n-k}
for
n\geq k
, and
w_{n}=0
for
0\leq n<k
. The difference of the formal power series

V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}
and
W(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}w_{n}x^{n}
is

{\textstyle V(x)-W(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\big((n)_{0}u_{n}+(n)_{1}u_{n-1}+\dots+(n)_{k-1}u_{n-k+1}\big)x^{n}\,.}

We claim that

{\textstyle V(x)-W(x)=\frac{p(x)}{(1-a_{1}x-\dots-a_{t}x^{t})^{k}}=:\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}}

for some polynomial
p(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
with degree less than
tk
. Then for
every
n\geq tk
we have

v_{n}(k)=[x^{n}]q(x)V(x)=[x^{n}]q(x)W(x)

and
k!\,|\,v_{n}(k)
because every coefficient in
W(x)
is divisible by
k!
. We prove the claim: for every
r

with
0\leq r\leq k-1
we indeed have,
since
\binom{n}{r}=(-1)^{n-r}\binom{-r-1}{n-r}
for
n\geq r
, that

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}(n)_{r}u_{n-r}x^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}r!\cdot b_{j}x^{r}\sum_{n\geq r}\binom{n}{r}\alpha_{j}^{n-r}x^{n-r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}r!\cdot b_{j}x^{r}(1-\alpha_{j}x)^{-r-1}=\frac{p_{r}(x)}{(1-a_{1}x-\dots-a_{t}x^{t})^{r+1}}}

for some polynomial
p_{r}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
with degree less than
t(r+1)
.

Now the two properties of numbers
v_{n}(k)
imply that if
n\geq tk
and

v_{n}(k)\neq 0
, then

k!\leq|v_{n}(k)|\leq cA^{n}C^{k}\,.

In other words, if
n\geq tk
and if

k!>cA^{n}C^{k}
, then
v_{n}(k)=0
. Using this implication and the modification

v_{n}(k)=v_{n}(k+1)+a_{1}v_{n-1}(k)+\dots+a_{t}v_{n-t}(k),\ n\geq t\,,

of the above recurrence, we show that there exists a
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

v_{n}(k)=0
for every large
n
.
This will prove that the formal power series
V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}
is
rational. So let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
k!>cA^{2tk}C^{k}
for every
k\geq k_{0}
, and let

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\langle k,\,n\rangle\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\colon\;k\geq k_{0}\wedge tk\leq n\leq 2tk\}\,\text{ and}

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\langle k,\,n\rangle\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\colon\;k\geq k_{0}\wedge n\geq 2tk\}\,.

Thus
\langle k,n\rangle\in N

\Rightarrow

v_{n}(k)=0
. For
\langle k,n\rangle\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
we consider the norm

\|\langle k,n\rangle\|=n-2tk
(
\in\mathbb{Z}
). We name the ordered pairs in the modified recurrence:

\gamma=\langle k,\,n\rangle,\,\beta_{0}=\langle k+1,\,n\rangle,\,\beta_{1}=\langle k,\,n-1\rangle,\,\dots,\,\beta_{t}=\langle k,\,n-t\rangle\,.

The following three properties of the norm
\|\dots\|
and
the pairs
\gamma
,
\beta_{0}
,
\dots
,
\beta_{t}
are easy to prove.

1.

The norm
\|\dots\|
attains a minimum value on the set
M
(namely, zero).

2.

If
\gamma\in M
then
\beta_{i}\in M\cup N
for every
i=0,1,\dots,t
.

3.

For every
i=0,1,\dots,t
, the norm
\|\beta_{i}\|<\|\gamma\|
.

From these three properties, from the property of the set
N
and from the modified recurrence, it follows by
induction on the norm that
v_{n}(k)=0
for every
\langle k,n\rangle\in M
.
This concludes the proof because we see that every number
k\geq k_{0}
has the
required property.

\Box

We prove, with the help of Proposition
2.1
and
Theorem
2.2
, that the number
\mathrm{e}
is transcendental.
We assume, for the contradiction, that
b_{j},\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
j=1,2,\dots,t
,
are
2t\geq 2
integers such that
every
b_{j}\neq 0
, the
\alpha_{j}
are mutually distinct, and that

{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{j}}=0\,.}

We may assume that
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
. We set
A:=\max(\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{t}\})
and define
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
as in Theorem
2.2
. Then

{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{u_{n}}{n!}x^{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{j}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\alpha_{j}^{n}}{n!}x^{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{j}x}}

and

{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{u_{n}}{n!}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{j}}=0\,.}

From the definition of
u_{n}
and
A
it easily follows that
u_{n}=O(A^{n})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Since by the definition of
v_{n}
we have

|v_{n}|={\textstyle n!\cdot\big|\sum_{r=0}^{n}u_{r}/r!\big|=n!\cdot\big|\sum_{r=n+1}^{\infty}u_{r}/r!\big|}

— now we invoke the contradictory assumption — we get the
bound

\displaystyle|v_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{|u_{n+1}|}{n+1}+\frac{|u_{n+2}|}{(n+1)(n+2)}+\dots\leq cA^{n}\big(\frac{A}{n+1}+\frac{A^{2}}{(n+1)(n+2)}+\dots\big)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\textstyle cA^{n}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{A^{j}}{j!}=c\,\mathrm{e}^{A}\cdot A^{n}\,,}

where
c\geq 0
is an absolute constant.
Thus
v_{n}=O(A^{n})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Let
V(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}x^{n}
(
\in\mathbb{Z}[[x]]
). As we know from the previous proof,
v_{n}-nv_{n-1}=u_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
v_{0}=u_{0}
. In terms of formal power series we get

{\textstyle(1-x)V(x)-x^{2}V^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(v_{n}-nv_{n-1})x^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}u_{n}x^{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{b_{j}}{1-\alpha_{j}x}}

(with
v_{-1}:=0
). However, the equality

{\textstyle(1-x)V(x)-x^{2}V^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{b_{j}}{1-\alpha_{j}x}}

contradicts Proposition
2.1
because
by Theorem
2.2
the formal power series
V(x)

is rational.

\Box

We repeat that the proof in
[
2
]
is more general as it
establishes the full LW theorem. In that proof, one can
only achieve that
u_{n},v_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}
, and denominators have to be taken care of.
The previous proof works with formal power series, but it is semiformal and not formal
because the key player is the infinite sum

{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{u_{n}}{n!}=0}

which is the value
f(1)
of the formal power series
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{u_{n}}{n!}x^{n}
.
In fact, this is the only non-formal component in an otherwise formal
proof.

It is clear that the previous proof satisfies condition (A), which states
that all directly used sets are HMC. It works directly only with complex formal power series, which are maps from
\mathbb{N}_{0}
to
\mathbb{C}
. As explained in Section
1
, non-HMC sets
like
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
are not really needed in the proof.
Condition (B), which states that no formalism of mathematical logic is
used, is satisfied as well. Although the article
[
2
]

of Beukers, Bézivin, and Robba is a pioneering one, as it is the first AB
proof of the transcendence of the number
\mathrm{e}
(in fact, of course, of the full LW theorem), it is little known; in the database of
Mathematical Reviews
, it currently has only
1

citation (January 2026).

3
The proof by semiformal integration

We present the second AB
\&
SF proof of the transcendence of

\mathrm{e}
. It is due to this author and first appeared in early
versions of
[
7
]
. The idea is to translate Hilbert’s proof,
described in Section
1
, into the language of formal power
series.

We work in the domains of formal power series
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
and
\mathbb{R}\{x\}
.
We introduce further functionals and operations on
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
besides
those mentioned in Section
1
. For any
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}
in
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
and any
a\in\mathbb{R}
we define the formal power series

{\textstyle f(ax)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a^{n}a_{n}x^{n}\,.}

The family of operations

\{f(x)\mapsto f(ax)\colon\;a\in\mathbb{R}\}\,,

which we call
constant inner products
, consists of formal unary
operations. For every

a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
and every
f(x)
and
g(x)
in
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
we have

f(1x)=f(x)
,

(af+bg)(cx)=af(cx)+bg(cx)\,\text{ and }\,(fg)(cx)=f(cx)g(cx)\,.

Also,
f(0x)=[x^{0}]f
. If
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}

then also
f(cx)\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
. By

f(-x)
we mean
f((-1)x)
.

In the
next proposition, whose straightforward proof we omit, we
collect properties of the
operations
f\mapsto f^{\prime}
and
f\mapsto\int f
.

Proposition 3.1

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{R}[[x]]
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. The following holds.

1.

(af+bg)^{\prime}=af^{\prime}+bg^{\prime}
,
(fg)^{\prime}=f^{\prime}g+fg^{\prime}
and
f(ax)^{\prime}=af^{\prime}(ax)
.

2.

\int(af+bg)=a\int f+b\int g
and
\int f(ax)=\frac{1}{a}(\int f)(ax)
if
a\neq 0
.

3.

If
f\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
then
f^{\prime},\int f\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
.

4.

\big(\int f\big)^{\prime}=f
and
\int(f^{\prime})=f-[x^{0}]f
.

We define the (formal)
exponential
.

Definition 3.2

The formal power series

{\textstyle\mathrm{Exp}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}x^{n}=1+x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{6}x^{3}+\dots\ \ (\in\mathbb{Q}[[x]])}

is called the (formal) exponential.

Clearly,

\mathrm{Exp}\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
,
\mathrm{Exp}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Exp}
and

\int\mathrm{Exp}=\mathrm{Exp}-1
.

We introduce a family of
semiformal unary operations.

Definition 3.3
(shifts)

Let
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}
be in

\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and let
b\in\mathbb{R}
. We define the formal power series
f(x+b)
in
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
by

{\textstyle f(x+b)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{m\geq n}\binom{m}{n}a_{m}b^{m-n}\big)x^{n}\;.}

The coefficients in
f(x+b)
are correctly defined because
f(x)
is
convergent. It is easy to show that
f(x+b)\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
. Clearly,

f(x+0)=f(x)
and if

f(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
then
f(x)\mapsto f(x+b)
is the usual polynomial
substitution. We combine the operations of constant inner product
and shift by means of brackets. For example,
f((x+a)+b)
is
g(x+b)
where
g(x)=f(x+a)
or

f((ax)+b)
is
g(x+b)
where
g(x)=f(ax)
or
f(a(x+b))
is
g(ax)
where
g(x)=f(x+b)
and so on.

We show that shifts behave in
desirable ways.

Proposition 3.4

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and
c,d\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

f((x+c)+d)=f(x+(c+d))\,.

Proof.
Let
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}
and
c,d
be as stated, and let
f(x+c)=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}x^{n}
. Then

{\textstyle b_{n}=\sum_{m\geq n}\binom{m}{n}a_{m}c^{m-n}\,\text{ and }\,f((x+c)+d)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{m\geq n}\binom{m}{n}b_{m}d^{m-n}\big)x^{n}\,.}

Thus

\displaystyle{\textstyle[x^{n}]f((x+c)+d)=\sum_{m\geq n}\binom{m}{n}\big(\sum_{l\geq m}\binom{l}{m}a_{l}c^{l-m}\big)d^{m-n}=}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\sum_{m\geq n}\sum_{l\geq m}\frac{l!}{n!(m-n)!(l-m)!}a_{l}c^{l-m}d^{m-n}=}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{l\geq n}\binom{l}{n}a_{l}\sum_{m=n}^{l}\binom{l-n}{l-m}c^{l-m}d^{m-n}=}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{l\geq n}\binom{l}{n}a_{l}\sum_{j=0}^{l-n}\binom{l-n}{j}c^{j}d^{l-n-j}=}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{l\geq n}\binom{l}{n}a_{l}(c+d)^{l-n}=[x^{n}]f(x+(c+d))\,.}

In the third
line, we changed the order of summation. This is allowed because

f(x)
is convergent. In the fourth line, we used the binomial theorem.

\Box

Proposition 3.5

Let
f(x),g(x)\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

(f\cdot g)(x+c)=f(x+c)\cdot g(x+c)\;.

Proof.
Let
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}
,
g(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}x^{n}
and
c
be
as stated. Then the coefficients
[x^{n}]f(x+c)
and
[x^{n}]g(x+c)
equal to

{\textstyle\sum_{m\geq n}\binom{m}{n}a_{m}c^{m-n}\,\text{ and }\,\sum_{l\geq n}\binom{l}{n}b_{l}c^{l-n}\,,}

respectively. Thus

\displaystyle{\textstyle[x^{n}]f(x+c)g(x+c)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\big(\sum_{m\geq k}\binom{m}{k}a_{m}c^{m-k}\sum_{l\geq n-k}\binom{l}{n-k}b_{l}c^{l+k-n}\big)}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{m\geq k}\sum_{l\geq n-k}\binom{m}{k}\binom{l}{n-k}a_{m}b_{l}c^{l+m-n}=}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,\,l\geq 0\\
m+l\geq n\end{subarray}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{m}{k}\binom{l}{n-k}a_{m}b_{l}c^{l+m-n}=}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,\,l\geq 0\\
m+l\geq n\end{subarray}}\binom{m+l}{n}a_{m}b_{l}c^{l+m-n}}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{j\geq n}\binom{j}{n}\big(\sum_{m=0}^{j}a_{m}b_{j-m}\big)c^{j-n}=[x^{n}](fg)(x+c)\,.}

In the third line,
we changed the order of summation. This is allowed since

f(x)
is convergent. In the fourth line, we used Vandermonde’s identity
(
[
8
]
).

\Box

The following semiformal exponential identity is perhaps counter-intuitive; at first we got it wrong.

Proposition 3.6

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a\neq 0
. Then

{\textstyle\mathrm{Exp}\big((ax)+\frac{b}{a}\big)=\mathrm{e}^{b}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(ax)\,.}

Proof.
Let
a
,
b
be as stated, and let
f(x)=\mathrm{Exp}(ax)
. Then

\displaystyle{\textstyle f\big(x+\frac{b}{a}\big)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{m\geq n}\binom{m}{n}(a^{m}/m!)(b/a)^{m-n}\big)x^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\geq n}\frac{b^{m-n}}{(m-n)!}(a^{n}/n!)x^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{b^{l}}{l!}\big)(a^{n}/n!)x^{n}}=\mathrm{e}^{b}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(ax)\,.

\Box

We use this identity only for
a=-1
.

We introduce a family of semiformal functionals on
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
.

Definition 3.7
(Newton integral of fps)

Let
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and let
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
. We define the Newton integral of

f(x)
from
u
to
v
as the difference

\displaystyle{\textstyle\int_{u}^{v}f=\int_{u}^{v}f(x)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\big(\int f\big)(v)-\big(\int f\big)(u)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\cdot\frac{v^{n+1}}{n+1}-\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\cdot\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}}

of the values of the formal primitive of
f
at the numbers
v
and
u
.

By item 3 of Proposition
3.1
,

\int f\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and the integral is always defined. We obtain three transformations for it: linear combination,
additive decomposition, and shift of
the integration interval. The first two transformations are
straightforward.

Proposition 3.8

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and
u,v,a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

{\textstyle\int_{u}^{v}(af+bg)=a\int_{u}^{v}f+b\int_{u}^{v}g\,.}

Proof.
This follows from the equality
\int(af+bg)=a\int f+b\int g
in item 2 of Proposition
3.1
.

\Box

Proposition 3.9

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and
u,v,w\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

{\textstyle\int_{u}^{w}f=\int_{u}^{v}f+\int_{v}^{w}f\,.}

Proof.
Indeed,

{\textstyle\big(\int f\big)(w)-\big(\int f\big)(u)=\big(\int f\big)(v)-\big(\int f\big)(u)+\big(\int f\big)(w)-\big(\int f\big)(v)\,.}

\Box

The third transformation needs some work.

Proposition 3.10

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and
u,v,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

{\textstyle\int_{u}^{v}f(x+b)=\int_{u+b}^{v+b}f(x)\,.}

Proof.
Let
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n}
,
u
,
v
and
b
be as stated. Then

\displaystyle{\textstyle\int_{u}^{v}f(x+b)=}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{m\geq n}\binom{m}{n}a_{m}b^{m-n}\big)\frac{v^{n+1}}{n+1}-\sum_{n\geq 0}\big(\sum_{m\geq n}\binom{m}{n}a_{m}b^{m-n}\big)\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{a_{m}}{m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{m}\binom{m+1}{n+1}b^{m-n}v^{n+1}-\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{a_{m}}{m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{m}\binom{m+1}{n+1}b^{m-n}u^{n+1}}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{a_{m}}{m+1}\sum_{l=0}^{m+1}\binom{m+1}{l}b^{m+1-l}v^{l}-\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{a_{m}}{m+1}\sum_{l=0}^{m+1}\binom{m+1}{l}b^{m+1-l}u^{l}}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}a_{m}\frac{(v+b)^{m+1}}{m+1}-\sum_{m\geq 0}a_{m}\frac{(u+b)^{m+1}}{m+1}=\int_{u+b}^{v+b}f(x)\,.}

In the third line, we used the identity
\binom{m+1}{n+1}=\frac{m+1}{n+1}\binom{m}{n}
and we exchanged the order of summation. This is
allowed since the involved series absolutely converge.
In the fourth line, we added

{\textstyle 0=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{a_{m}}{m+1}\binom{m+1}{0}b^{m+1}v^{0}-\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{a_{m}}{m+1}\binom{m+1}{0}b^{m+1}u^{0}\,.}

In the fifth line, we used the binomial theorem.

\Box

We introduce another family of semiformal functionals on
\mathbb{R}[[x]]
.

Definition 3.11
(improper Newton
\int
of fps)

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}
and
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
. If for every real sequence
(b_{n})
going to
+\infty
the proper limit

{\textstyle L=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{u}^{b_{n}}f\ \ (\in\mathbb{R})}

exists, then
L
does not depend on the sequence
(b_{n})
and we define

{\textstyle\int_{u}^{+\infty}f=\int_{u}^{+\infty}f(x):=L\,.}

We call
L
the improper Newton integral of
f(x)
from
u
to
+\infty
.

We leave the proof of
the independence of the limit
L
on the sequence
(b_{n})
to the reader as an exercise.

We obtain for the improper integral the same three integral transformations as before.

Proposition 3.12

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and let
u,a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the equality

{\textstyle\int_{u}^{+\infty}(af+bg)=a\int_{u}^{+\infty}f+b\int_{u}^{+\infty}g}

holds whenever the last two improper Newton integrals exist.

Proof.
This follows from Proposition
3.8
and
the arithmetic of limits of real sequences.

\Box

Proposition 3.13

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
.
The equality

{\textstyle\int_{u}^{+\infty}f=\int_{u}^{v}f+\int_{v}^{+\infty}f}

holds whenever one of the two improper Newton integrals exists.

Proof.
Let
f
,
u
and
v
be as stated, let
(b_{n})
be any real sequence
going to
+\infty
and let us suppose that the former improper integral
exists. Thus the limit

L=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(\int f\big)(b_{n})
(
\in\mathbb{R}
) exists. But then

\displaystyle{\textstyle\int_{u}^{+\infty}f=L-\big(\int f\big)(u)=\big(\int f\big)(v)-\big(\int f\big)(u)+L-\big(\int f\big)(v)=}

\displaystyle{\textstyle=\int_{u}^{v}f+\int_{v}^{+\infty}f\,.}

In the other way we argue similarly.

\Box

The third transformation is immediate as well.

Proposition 3.14

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}\{x\}
and
u,b\in\mathbb{R}
. The equality

{\textstyle\int_{u}^{+\infty}f(x+b)=\int_{u+b}^{+\infty}f(x)}

holds whenever one of the two improper Newton integrals exists.

Proof.
Let
f(x)
and
u,b
be as stated.
Let
(c_{n})
be any real sequence going to
+\infty
.
We assume that the integral
I
on the
left-hand side exists, and prove that the integral on the right-hand side
exists too and equals
I
. Indeed,

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\int_{u+b}^{c_{n}}f(x)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Prop.~\ref{prop_shifInte3}}}}{{=}}\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\int_{u}^{c_{n}-b}f(x+b)=\int_{u}^{+\infty}f(x+b)=I\,.}

Similarly, if the integral on the right-hand side exists, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\int_{u}^{c_{n}}f(x+b)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Prop.~\ref{prop_shifInte3}}}}{{=}}\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\int_{u+b}^{c_{n}+b}f(x)=\int_{u+b}^{+\infty}f(x)\,.}

\Box

We need bounds, obtained in an AB way, on integrals

\int_{0}^{i}p(x)\mathrm{Exp}(-x)

where
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
p(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. We obtain them via the next bound which is based on two values of the primitive.

Proposition 3.15

For every numbers
i,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

{\textstyle\big|\int_{0}^{i}x^{k}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)\big|\leq i^{k+1}\mathrm{e}^{i}\,.}

Proof.
The integrand

{\textstyle\text{$\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}x^{n+k}$ has the primitive $\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!(n+k+1)}x^{n+k+1}$}\,.}

Thus

{\textstyle\big|\int_{0}^{i}x^{k}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)\big|\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}i^{n+k+1}+0=i^{k+1}\mathrm{e}^{i}\,.}

\Box

As we know, it is crucial that the bound is exponential and not factorial in
k
.

The key step in Hilbert’s proof is Euler’s identity. We prove it now in an AB
\&
SF way.

Proposition 3.16
(Euler’s identity)

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the integral

{\textstyle I_{k}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}x^{k}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sum_{n\geq k}\frac{(-1)^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}x^{n}=k!\,.}

Proof.
We proceed by induction on
k
. Let
(a_{n})
be any real sequence going to
+\infty
. Since

{\textstyle\int\mathrm{Exp}(-x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+1)!}x^{n+1}=1-\mathrm{Exp}(-x)\,,}

we have

I_{0}=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle(1-\mathrm{Exp}(-a_{n}))-(1-\mathrm{Exp}(-0))=1-0=1\,.}

Let

F_{k}=x^{k}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. By item 1 of Proposition
3.1
and the derivative of
\mathrm{Exp}(-x)
,

F_{k}^{\prime}=\big(x^{k}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)\big)^{\prime}=kx^{k-1}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)-x^{k}\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)=k\cdot F_{k-1}-F_{k}

and
F_{k}=k\cdot F_{k-1}-F_{k}^{\prime}
. By item 4 of
Proposition
3.1
,

\int F_{k}^{\prime}=F_{k}-[x^{0}]F_{k}=F_{k}
.
By Proposition
3.12
and induction,

\displaystyle I_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\int_{0}^{a_{n}}F_{k}}=k\cdot\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\int_{0}^{a_{n}}F_{k-1}-}\lim_{n\to\infty}{\textstyle\int_{0}^{a_{n}}F_{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\cdot I_{k-1}-\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(F_{k}(a_{n})-F_{k}(0)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\cdot(k-1)!-0=k!\;.

\Box

We are ready to state Hilbert’s proof in an AB
\&
SF way. We again assume for the contradiction that

a_{0}+a_{1}\mathrm{e}+a_{2}\mathrm{e}^{2}+\dots+a_{n}\mathrm{e}^{n}=0

where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
a_{0}\neq 0
. We multiply this equality
by the improper Newton integral

{\textstyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)\,,}

where
p_{r}(x)=x^{r}((x-1)(x-2)\dots(x-n))^{r+1}
is as in
Section
1
. This integral exists by
Propositions
3.12

and
3.16
. Using
Proposition
3.13
we get that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{r}+B_{r}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\mathrm{e}^{i}\cdot\int_{0}^{i}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)+\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\mathrm{e}^{i}\cdot\int_{i}^{+\infty}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)}

for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
. The numbers
A_{r},B_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
are the same as before, only now they are defined in an AB
\&
SF way.
We get the same contradiction as in Section
1
: every number

B_{r}
is an integral multiple of
r!
,
B_{r}\neq 0
for
infinitely many
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and numbers
A_{r}
are exponentially bounded. The
computation of
B_{r}

It remains to bound, in an AB

{\textstyle A_{r}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\mathrm{e}^{i}\int_{0}^{i}p_{r}(x)\cdot\mathrm{Exp}(-x)\,.}

The polynomial

p_{r}(x)=x^{r}((x-1)(x-2)\dots(x-n))^{r+1}

has degree less than
(n+1)(r+1)
and each coefficient in it is in absolute
value at most
l^{r+1}
where
l\in\mathbb{N}
depends only on
n
. By
Propositions
3.8

3.15

n+1
Newton integrals in
A_{r}
is in absolute value at most

(n+1)(r+1)\cdot l^{r+1}\cdot n^{(n+1)(r+1)}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{n}\,.

Thus
|A_{r}|\leq c^{r}
for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
and some constant
c\geq 1
. We get a contradiction as before.
