Beyond the Autoimmune Spectrum, Evaluating Ovarian Health in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Patients: A Case‐Control Study
Authors: Shaghayegh Moradi Alamdarloo, Hoda Naseri, Zahra Habibagahi, Ameneh Keshavarzi, Atefe Hashemi, Sara Haseli, Sepide Javankiani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease, and recent findings reveal that fertility and ovarian reserve are reduced even in moderate illness, showing that SLE has a direct impact on ovarian function. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate ovarian reserve using Anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH) and antral follicle count (AFC) in SLE patients compared to healthy controls.MethodsThis case‐control study was performed on 300 cases (150 SLE patients and 150 healthy controls). For evaluating ovarian reserve, AMH and AFC were evaluated. Furthermore, SLE activity was determined through the employment of the SLEDAI‐2k questionnaire.ResultsFindings revealed that the mean serum levels of AMH were significantly lower in the SLE group as compared to healthy controls (1.34 ± 1.16 vs. 2.47 ± 1.48 ng/mL, p < 0.001). Moreover, left AFC (2.47 ± 1.57 vs. 5.3 ± 1.94, p < 0.001), right AFC (2.77 ± 1.83 vs. 5.04 ± 2.36, p < 0.001), and mean AFC (2.62 ± 1.56 vs. 5.17 ± 1.96, p < 0.001) were significantly lower in the SLE group compared to healthy controls. Moreover, the results of examining the relationship between controlled (SLEDAI‐2K ≤ 4) and uncontrolled (SLEDAI‐2K > 4) patients were significant in terms of AMH serum levels, which were significantly lower in uncontrolled patients (p < 0.001).ConclusionsAccording to the results of the present study, young women with SLE should check their ovarian reserve regularly, and both AMH and AFC should be evaluated periodically because ovarian reserve may be reduced faster in SLE cases.Keywords: anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH), ovarian antral follicles count (AFC), ovarian reserve, systemic lupus erythematosus

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune rheumatic disease [1] that has a 9:1 female‐to‐male incidence ratio [2] and primarily affects women of reproductive age (20‐40 years old) [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Recent findings reveal that fertility and ovarian reserve are reduced even in moderate illness, showing that SLE has a direct impact on ovarian function and reserve [10].

Ovarian reserve is a critical factor in reproductive‐aged women's fertility preservation and endocrine function. Age, luteinizing hormone (LH), antral follicle count (AFC), estradiol (E2), follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH), especially in Days 2–5 (early follicular phase), inhibin B, and Anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH) are currently possible predictors of ovarian reserve. For instance, serum levels of AMH can assist in assessing the course of ovarian senescence due to its reduction to undetectable levels at menopause and independence from the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐gonadal axis [11].

SLE patients' ovarian function can be affected by the activity of the disease and the treatment medicines. Cytotoxic medicines, such as cyclophosphamide, can impair ovarian function, resulting in menstrual abnormalities [12, 13, 14, 15, 16], primary ovarian insufficiency [16, 17, 18, 19], and infertility [20]. Menstrual irregularities induced by cyclophosphamide therapy are related to the dosage and the age of the patient [13, 15, 21]. Furthermore, the use of high‐dose corticosteroids and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) may lead to menstrual irregularities and infertility in SLE patients [17]. In general, immunosuppression due to medication may expose SLE cases to an increased risk of infection [22]. To the best of our knowledge, there aren't enough prospective studies about the changes in ovarian reserve in SLE patients in Iran; therefore, this study was designed to evaluate ovarian reserve in terms of AMH and AFC, compared to healthy controls.

Methods and Materials

Target Group

This case‐control study was conducted in Obstetrics and Gynecology clinics and the Rheumatology Department of Shiraz University of Medical Science, the center of Iran, from November 2021 to May 2022. The ovarian reserve in terms of AMH and AFC of patients with SLE was compared with a control group (healthy group without SLE). Inclusion criteria consisted of 18–37‐year‐old SLE cases (diagnosed based on American Rheumatology Association criteria) referred to the Rheumatology Department for check‐up, and healthy subjects were selected from cases without current or history of SLE who signed a consent form to participate in the study. Moreover, exclusion criteria consisted of cases having history of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), other rheumatological diseases at the same time, chronic liver disease or end‐stage renal disease, any kind of disorder in the menstrual cycle, history of taking oral contraceptive pill (OCP) or hormone replacement therapy in the last 12 months, history of oophorectomy, hysterectomy, or pelvic irradiation, or those who had inadequate visualisation of ovaries on ultrasonography, age below 18 years or more than 37 years, and lack of satisfaction to continue participation in study in each level of study. We also excluded patients with missing data.

Study Design

The flowchart of the study is shown in Figure 1. Three hundred cases based on inclusion and exclusion criteria and clinical and paraclinical findings were included (150 in the SLE group and 150 in the healthy control group). The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1401.059), and all participants gave written informed consent. All data were coded with strict confidentiality, and participants were informed of the potential psychological impact of the study; also, in cases of psychological distress, appropriate support was provided with no cost as needed. After completing informed consent, eligible individuals were enrolled. The diagnosis of a patient with SLE was based on observing the presence of four of the following 11 criteria by a rheumatology specialist:

Study flowchart.

(1) butterfly rash, (2) discoid rash, (3) arthritis, (4) serositis, (5) oral ulcers, (6) photosensitivity, (7) nervous disorders (psychosis, convulsions), (8) renal disorders (excretion of protein more than 5 g) per day or more than +3 or cell casts in urine, (9) blood disorders (hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia), (10) immune system disorders, anti‐dsDNA, anti Sm, anticardiolipin antibody, (11) antinuclear antibody (ANA) [23].

In the following, all relevant information, including age, weight, blood pressure, medications, number of pregnancies, cases of abortion and stillbirth, intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD), and evaluation of kidney function, coagulation tests, blood count formula, were extracted from the medical records. All patients were followed up and examined by gynecologists, nephrologists, and rheumatologists.

The measures of lupus activity were based on the SLEDAI‐2k questionnaire. In the next step, 2–3 cc of blood samples were taken from both groups to measure the level of AMH using an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) test (Human anti‐Müllerian hormone ELISA Kit, CSB‐E12756h, Biotechnologies Inc., CHN). AMH normal range was 1–8 ng/mL, with a value of 1 ng/mL considered low. To reduce assay variance from day to day, all serum samples were evaluated at the same time. The coefficients of variation within and between assays were reduced to 3.0% and 6.0%, respectively. Also, to determine the amount of AFC, both groups were referred to the infertility department of Hazrat Zainab Hospital, and one operator checked and recorded the ovaries for all AFC patients on the second day of the menstrual cycle. AMH measurements were performed concurrently with AFC assessments on the second day of the menstrual cycle to ensure consistency and minimize variability. Finally, recorded findings such as AMH, AFC, and other clinical and demographic characteristics were compared between the group of patients with SLE and healthy controls.

SLEDAI‐2k Questionnaire

SLEDAI‐2K is based on the presence of 24 descriptors in nine organ systems throughout a 30‐day period. SLEDAI‐2K descriptors are reported as present or absent. Each descriptor gets a weighted score, and the final SLEDAI‐2K score is the sum of all 24 descriptor ratings. The overall SLEDAI‐2K score ranges from 0 to 105, with higher values indicating more disease activity. In SLEDAI‐2K questionnaire seizure, psychosis, organic brain syndrome, vision disorders, brain nerve disorders, lupus headache, cerebrovascular accident (CVA) and vasculitis get 8 points, arthritis, myositis, urinary casts, hematuria, proteinuria, pyuria get 4 points and rash, alopecia, mucosal ulcers, pleurisy, pericarditis and low level of complement and increased DNA binding have 2 points and fever, thrombocytopenia and leukopenia have 1 point [24, 25, 26, 27].

Data Analysis

Only patients with complete data were included for analysis and reporting. The data were statistically analysed using SPSS version 24 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) following SAMPL guidelines for statistical reporting [28]. The study design adhered to CONSORT guidelines for observational studies [29]. To assess qualitative characteristics between groups, the χ 2 test was performed. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed to assess the normal distribution of all quantitative parameters. For variables with normal distribution, the Student t‐test was used; for variables with non‐normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U‐test was employed. All analyses were pre‐specified, used a two‐sided p value threshold of 0.05 for significance.

Results

In this study, 150 SLE cases and 150 healthy controls were included. There was no significant difference in terms of age (p = 0.1) and BMI (p = 0.08) between the two groups. The results showed that the mean serum levels of AMH were significantly lower in the SLE group as compared to healthy controls (1.34 ± 1.16 vs. 2.47 ± 1.48 ng/mL, p < 0.001). Additionally, due to the skewed distribution of AMH values, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were calculated (SLE: median 1.20, IQR 0.49–1.92; controls: median 2.23, IQR 1.30–3.20 ng/mL), as shown with a box plot illustrating the distribution in Supporting Information S1: Figure 1.

Moreover, left AFC (2.47 ± 1.57 vs. 5.3 ± 1.94, p < 0.001), right AFC (2.77 ± 1.83 vs. 5.04 ± 2.36, p < 0.001), and mean AFC (2.62 ± 1.56 vs. 5.17 ± 1.96, p < 0.001) were significantly lower in SLE group as compared to healthy controls (Table 1).

Furthermore, SLE cases were divided into two groups based on the age cutoff point of 27 years, and the relationship between disease duration and ovarian reserve factors in each age group was investigated separately. The effect of the age factor on the investigated groups was somewhat neutralized, and the relationship between the duration of the disease and these factors was evaluated in a less biased way. Thus, 127 cases of patients were over 27 years old, and 23 cases were less than or equal to 27 years old. The results showed that in cases with age less than or equal to 27 years, ovarian reserve parameters (AMH, left, right, and mean AFC), have a high and significant correlation with the length of the disease (r = −0.52, p = 0.01 for AMH, r = −0.79, p < 0.001 for right AFC, r = −0.8, p < 0.001 for mean AFC), while in the age group over 27 years, there was no significant correlation between the mentioned factors and the length of the disease (p > 0.05).

Finally, multivariable regression analysis revealed that, after adjusting for age and BMI, patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) had significantly lower AMH levels (β = −1.191, p < 0.001) and a lower mean AFC (β = −2.529, p < 0.001) compared to controls, with adjusted R² values of 0.186 and 0.339, respectively (Supporting Information S1).

Discussion

The results of our study have demonstrated that SLE patients had impaired ovarian reserve in terms of evaluating AMH and AFC, especially in cases with uncontrolled disease. Furthermore, serum levels of AMH had an inverse and significant correlation with the duration of the disease and the patient's age. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to conduct a comprehensive hormonal profile alongside ovarian ultrasonography in SLE patients compared to healthy subjects in a large sample size in Iran.

Ovarian reserve, which predicts women's fertility potential, is represented by the quantity and quality of remaining oocytes [30]. AMH, AFC, and FSH are other biomarkers used to evaluate ovarian reserve. Meanwhile, AMH is highly sensitive and specific in predicting ovarian response [31]. Compared to the FSH level, which begins to increase only with complete ovarian failure, the AMH level has the advantage that it decreases rapidly after exposure to traumatic events and, therefore, is a more sensitive marker of ovarian status [32].

Similar to our results, previous reports have shown that serum AMH levels are lower in SLE patients [33, 34, 35]. However, other studies demonstrated that SLE cases did not show significant changes in ovarian reserve [36, 37]. Contradictory results have been found in recent research, which may be due to different sample sizes, the severity of SLE, the duration of disease, and inclusion and exclusion criteria. Moreover, serum AMH levels can be measured using a variety of methods, which could contribute to heterogeneity. For this purpose, three studies based on Chinese populations appeared to be the primary source of the considerably high variability in a comparison of serum levels of AHM between SLE individuals and healthy controls [38, 39, 40].

A study showed that menstrual cycle problems were observed in 54% of SLE cases and were linked to disease activity, showing that SLE patients are more likely to have ovarian dysfunction [41]. By comparing serum levels of AMH between SLE cases and healthy controls, a meta‐analysis supported the aforementioned finding. Ovary involvement in SLE, similar to autoimmune oophoritis, may promote systemic inflammation and reduce ovarian function. The hypothalamic‐pituitary ovarian (HPO) axis becomes dysfunctional as a result of persistent inflammation. Further indications of a hormone imbalance include ovarian malfunction leading to infertility, irregular menstruation, and ovarian failure [18].

As mentioned, based on the findings of the present study, age showed an inverse correlation with AMH levels in SLE patients. Aging naturally reduces ovarian function, especially ovarian reserve. Although several factors in addition to age can decrease ovarian reserve [42]. In one study, the ovarian reserve of SLE cases was decreased in the interquartile range of 23.25–34.50 years [43]. It is noteworthy that in our study, younger SLE patients (≤ 27 years old) show no significant AMH difference versus age‐matched controls. This outcome can be explained by several factors. First, small sample sizes in subgroup analyses limit statistical power, leading to nonsignificant findings despite a true effect. Second, younger patients typically have shorter disease duration and lower cumulative damage; many have milder disease activity and fewer gonadotoxic treatments, so their ovarian reserve remains relatively preserved.

Today, AMH and AFC are widely used to determine ovarian reserve, predict hormonal stimulation‐ovarian response in assisted reproductive techniques, and estimate menopause [44]. In this way, ovarian reserve is used to assess the ovary's ability to produce egg cells capable of fertilization, resulting in a healthy and successful pregnancy. This metric is regarded as significant in assisted reproductive clinics when evaluating women's fertility potential [45]. In our study, the mean AMH level in SLE patients (1.34 ng/mL) approaches the threshold for low ovarian reserve (< 1 ng/mL), which is associated with reduced fertility potential in assisted reproductive techniques [46]. This suggests that SLE patients, particularly those with uncontrolled disease (mean AMH 0.33 ng/mL), may face significant fertility challenges, necessitating early fertility preservation strategies including cryopreservation or the use of gonadotropin‐releasing hormone agonists [47].

In the present study, the medication and dosage of the patients were not investigated. However, there are many studies regarding the effectiveness or lack of effectiveness of treatments prescribed for patients with SLE. Studies have shown that immunosuppressive drugs are frequently utilized in the treatment of moderate to severe lupus nephritis, central nervous system involvement, and other disorders. Cyclophosphamide is the most damaging to the ovarian reserve of these medications [18]. Some other studies have shown that ovarian function failure is common during cyclophosphamide treatment, which is associated with dosage, longer duration of treatment, and older age at initiation of treatment [48, 49].

This study had some limitations. First, we used only two parameters (AFC and AMH) to predict ovarian reserve, even though AMH is a noninvasive and sensitive ovarian reserve marker that outperforms FSH, E2, and LH. However, these hormones play crucial roles in ovarian function, and their inclusion might have provided a more comprehensive understanding of ovarian reserve in SLE patients. Second, when comparing serum levels of AMH between SLE cases and healthy controls, drug treatment was not taken into account. According to studies, drug treatment has a significant effect on AMH levels. Full control of important confounders such as concurrent medications (e.g., corticosteroids, hydroxychloroquine, immunosuppressants) and exogenous hormonal exposure was not possible. These unmeasured variables could have directly or indirectly affected ovarian reserve markers, particularly AMH and AFC, by altering gonadotropin secretion, follicular recruitment, or ovarian blood flow.

Although the condition of the patients was evaluated, it can be considered an important alternative factor for the mentioned parameter. Additionally, we did not collect data on parity or socioeconomic status, which could influence ovarian reserve. Unmeasured confounders like parity and socioeconomic status may influence ovarian reserve, as higher parity could reduce ovarian reserve due to repeated ovulatory cycles, and socioeconomic factors may affect access to healthcare or nutrition, potentially impacting fertility outcomes [50, 51]. In addition, the statistical depth of the analysis was limited by the cross‐sectional design and sample size. While multivariable models adjusted for age and BMI, additional covariates such as treatment duration, disease subtype, and hormonal milieu could not be incorporated, which may have introduced residual confounding. The absence of these data limits our ability to fully adjust for their effects, which may reduce the generalizability of our findings. Future longitudinal studies addressing the aforementioned limitations would more comprehensively clarify the complex effect of SLE on ovarian reserve.

Conclusions

According to the results of the present study, young women with SLE should check their ovarian reserve regularly, and both AMH and AFC should be evaluated periodically because ovarian reserve may decrease faster in SLE patients. They can become pregnant during a disease‐free interval before illness progression, and the requirement for cytotoxic treatment reduces ovarian reserve and fertility even more. If cytotoxic therapy is required, the patient should be provided with fertility preservation techniques to safeguard ovarian function, and if a decrease in ovarian reserve is detected early in these patients, egg cells can be frozen for future use or assisted reproductive treatment.
