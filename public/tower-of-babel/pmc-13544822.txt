Delayed presentation of left-sided penetrating diaphragmatic injury with multivisceral herniation, gastric fundal perforation, and splenic laceration leading to fatal septic shock: a case report from Ethiopia
Authors: Amanuel Mesfin Oljira, Berhanu Nigusse Bikila, Desalegn Abdisa Hordofa, Solomon Guteta Beyene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Traumatic diaphragmatic injury following penetrating trauma is uncommon and easily overlooked; delayed diagnosis may lead to visceral herniation, perforation, contamination, and sepsis.Case presentation:A 21-year-old man presented 6 days after a posterior stab wound, referred from a primary hospital where a chest tube had been inserted for suspected hemopneumothorax. He was hypotensive, febrile, and hypoxic, with reduced left air entry, bowel sounds in the chest, and gastrointestinal contents draining through the tube. A chest radiograph showed a large left intrathoracic air-fluid level with mediastinal shift. Emergency laparotomy revealed a 12 cm left posterior diaphragmatic defect; herniation of the stomach, jejunum, transverse colon, and spleen; two gastric fundal perforations; and a grade I splenic laceration. The viscera were reduced; the gastric perforations were repaired in two layers with omental reinforcement; the diaphragm was closed primarily; and lavage was performed. Re-look laparotomy on postoperative day 2 confirmed intact repairs and drained a left subphrenic collection. Despite norepinephrine support and escalation from ceftriaxone/metronidazole to vancomycin/meropenem, he died on postoperative day 11 from refractory septic shock and multiorgan failure.Clinical discussion:This case highlights diagnostic pitfalls of delayed penetrating diaphragmatic injury, the significance of enteric chest-tube drainage, and the impact of referral delay and limited CT availability.Conclusion:Early suspicion, rapid transfer, and prompt operative source control are essential after thoracoabdominal stab wounds with suspected diaphragmatic injury.

Introduction

Traumatic diaphragmatic injury (TDI) is an uncommon but serious consequence of thoracoabdominal trauma, occurring in approximately 0.8–5% of major injuries[1,2]. Penetrating injuries, particularly stab wounds, may be missed during initial assessment because the signs are often subtle or attributed to hemopneumothorax[3,4]. Left-sided injuries are more frequently recognized because the liver partially protects the right hemidiaphragm, while negative intrathoracic pressure promotes abdominal visceral herniation[2,5].

Delayed TDI may progress to incarceration, strangulation, perforation, pleural contamination, sepsis, and death[6,7]. Gastric perforation within a traumatic diaphragmatic hernia is rare and especially dangerous when accompanied by other visceral injuries and by limited access to advanced imaging or critical care. Although related cases have been reported in sub-Saharan Africa[8–12], the combined presentation of delayed penetrating TDI, multivisceral herniation, double gastric perforation, splenic laceration, and fatal septic shock remains sparsely documented. This case is reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 criteria[13].

Case presentation

A 21-year-old man presented to a tertiary hospital 6 days after sustaining a stab wound to the left posterior chest. He had been referred from a primary hospital, where an intercostal chest tube had been inserted for a suspected hemopneumothorax before a definitive diagnosis was established. On arrival, he was disoriented and critically ill: blood pressure 80/55 mmHg, heart rate 120/min, respiratory rate 38/min, temperature 38.6°C, and oxygen saturation 85% on 5 L/min of oxygen. Examination showed a 4 cm wound in the left sixth intercostal space, markedly reduced left basal air entry, audible bowel sounds in the chest, a rightward displacement of the cardiac impulse, a scaphoid abdomen with generalized peritonitis, and blood-stained gastrointestinal effluent from the chest tube (Fig. 1). The initial differential diagnosis included left hemopneumothorax with bowel injury, empyema with bronchopleural fistula, esophageal rupture with mediastinal contamination, and subphrenic abscess with reactive pleural effusion. However, enteric chest-tube drainage, bowel sounds in the thorax, and plain radiographic findings were pathognomonic for diaphragmatic rupture with hollow viscus herniation. Computed tomography of the chest and abdomen was unavailable at the treating hospital and was not required for operative decision-making. Laboratory tests showed a WBC count of 24 × 109/L with 89% neutrophils, hemoglobin of 10.2 g/dL, and platelets of 122 × 109/L. After brief resuscitation, an emergency midline laparotomy was performed. Approximately 200 mL of hemorrhagic, gastrointestinally contaminated fluid was found in the peritoneal cavity, and 300 mL was found in the left pleural cavity. A 12 cm posterior left diaphragmatic defect contained herniated stomach, jejunum, transverse colon, and spleen (Fig. 3A). A Grade I superior-pole splenic laceration measuring 1 cm was present. Two through-and-through gastric fundal perforations, measuring 1 × 1 cm and 1 × 2 cm, were identified 7 cm distal to the esophagogastric junction (Fig. 3B). Herniated viscera were reduced, both gastric perforations were repaired in two layers with absorbable sutures and omental reinforcement, and the diaphragmatic defect was closed using interrupted non-absorbable sutures. The pleural and peritoneal cavities were thoroughly irrigated, and the chest tube was repositioned. Figure 3.(A) Intraoperative view of the left diaphragmatic defect with multivisceral herniation. Postoperatively, he was admitted to the intensive care unit on norepinephrine, ceftriaxone 1 g IV twice daily, and metronidazole 500 mg IV three times daily. A planned re-look laparotomy on postoperative day (POD) 2 found 50 mL of reactive left subphrenic fluid, which was drained and irrigated; gastric and diaphragmatic repairs were intact; no leak or missed injury was identified; and the chest tube was functioning. Because of persistent sepsis, antibiotics were escalated on POD 4–5 to vancomycin 1 g IV twice daily and meropenem 1 g IV three times daily. He had hypotension-related cardiac arrests on POD 3 and POD 6, both of which were resuscitated successfully, but he developed progressive multiorgan failure and died on POD 11 after a third irreversible arrest.

Discussion

Penetrating TDI remains difficult to diagnose, particularly after posterior thoracoabdominal stab wounds and in settings without CT access[1–4,9,10]. In this patient, gastrointestinal drainage through the chest tube and bowel sounds in the chest were decisive clues. Such findings are not compatible with uncomplicated hemopneumothorax and should prompt urgent transfer or exploration when definitive surgical care is unavailable[2–4].

The chest tube placed at the referring hospital deserves a balanced interpretation. In a resource-limited primary-care setting, insertion of a chest tube for presumed hemopneumothorax may be clinically defensible. However, atypical enteric drainage should immediately change the diagnosis to diaphragmatic injury with hollow viscus involvement because tube thoracostomy in an unrecognized diaphragmatic hernia may also risk causing visceral injury. This diagnostic transition is a key teaching point.

The fatal septic course was probably driven by delayed source control rather than technical failure. Six days elapsed before definitive surgery, allowing gastric contents to contaminate both the pleural and peritoneal cavities. At re-look laparotomy, the gastric and diaphragmatic repairs were intact, and no missed injury was found, supporting adequate operative source control. Nevertheless, the established infectious burden, shock physiology, and limited critical-care resources likely contributed to refractory septic shock despite norepinephrine support and escalation to broad-spectrum antibiotics.

This report adds practical value by documenting the full sequence from referral delay and diagnostic pitfall to operative repair, planned re-look, antibiotic escalation, and death. Its main lesson is not novelty alone but the need for early suspicion, rapid referral, and immediate exploration when clinical findings strongly indicate diaphragmatic rupture with hollow viscus herniation.

Conclusion

Delayed penetrating TDI with multivisceral herniation, gastric perforation, and splenic injury carries a grave prognosis. Early recognition, prompt transfer, and timely operative source control are essential, particularly in settings where CT imaging and critical-care resources are limited.
