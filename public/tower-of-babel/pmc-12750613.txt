METTL3 promotes chondrocyte injury in osteoarthritis by increasing CTSB expression
Authors: Wei Wei, Xiangyu Yao, Wanli Duan, Jiang Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is one of the most frequent joint diseases and its incidence is increasing. However, its pathogenesis remains poorly understood. The study aims to analyze the role and mechanism of methyltransferase 3, N6-adenosine-methyltransferase complex catalytic subunit (METTL3) in OA.MethodsHuman chondrocytes (CHON-001 and C28/I2) were stimulated using interleukin-1β (IL-1β) to mimic OA-like cell injury. mRNA expression levels were measured by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction. Protein expression was detected by Western blotting. Cell Counting Kit-8 assay was used to detect cell viability. Flow cytometry analysis and TUNEL assay were performed to analyze cell apoptosis. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays were used to measure the levels of TNF-α and IL-6. Cellular ROS Assay kit was used to detect ROS level. Lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit was utilized to detect MDA level. Fe2+ level was assessed using a Fe2+ colorimetric assay kit. The interaction between METTL3 and CTSB was confirmed by m6A RNA immunoprecipitation assay and dual-luciferase reporter assay.ResultsMETTL3 and CTSB expression were upregulated in human osteoarthritic cartilage in comparison with healthy cartilage tissues. Treatment with IL-1β up-regulated the expression of both METTL3 and CTSB in human chondrocytes. Knockdown of METTL3 or CTSB attenuated the pro-apoptotic, pro-inflammatory, oxidative, and ferroptotic effects induced by IL-1β in these cells. In addition, METTL3 upregulated CTSB expression by regulating its m6A methylation. Moreover, the protective effects of METTL3 silencing against IL-1β-induced injury were dependent on the downregulation of CTSB.ConclusionMETTL3 promoted chondrocyte injury by upregulating CTSB expression in OA, thereby highlighting METTL3-CTSB axis as a promising target for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06515-3.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), also known as degenerative joint disease, is a common form of arthritis that affects more than 500 million people worldwide [1]. OA primarily affects older adults, as well as individuals who are overweight, have a family history of the disease, or have had joint injuries in the past [2, 3]. The disease most commonly occurs in the knees, hips, hands, and spine [4, 5]. OA significantly impacts patients, causing pain, stiffness, swelling, and reduced mobility in the affected joints [6, 7]. In severe cases, it can substantially impair the ability to perform daily activities and diminish quality of life. Currently, the treatment options for osteoarthritis include pain management medications, physical therapy, lifestyle modifications, and in some cases, joint replacement surgery [8, 9]. However, these treatments often provide only symptomatic relief and fail to address the underlying disease mechanism. Understanding the molecular mechanisms of OA is crucial for developing more effective and targeted treatment options.

M6A methylation is a post-transcriptional modification known to regulate various cellular processes, including gene expression, protein translation, and RNA stability [10]. M6A methylation is dynamically regulated in cells and is involved in the development of various bone-related diseases including OA, osteoporosis, and fracture healing [11–13]. One of the proteins involved in m6A methylation is methyltransferase 3, N6-adenosine-methyltransferase complex catalytic subunit (METTL3). METTL3 is a key catalytic enzyme responsible for installing m6A modifications on mRNA molecules. It has been shown to regulate various cellular processes, including proliferation, migration, and survival [14]. In the context of OA, METTL3 has been shown to promote disease progression in experimental models [15], however, its precise molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Cathepsin B (CTSB) is a lysosomal cysteine protease enzyme primarily located within lysosomes, where it facilitates intracellular protein degradation [16]. CTSB plays a crucial role in various physiological processes, including protein turnover, antigen processing, and hormone activation [17]. However, dysregulation of CTSB activity has been linked to several pathological conditions, including cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and inflammatory disorders [18–20]. In the context of OA, CTSB has been implicated in the progression of the disease. In OA, hydroxyapatite crystals deposit in the cartilage and are phagocytosed by resident chondrocytes. Subsequent processing of these crystals within lysosomes can lead to the leakage of CTSB into the cytoplasm. Cytosolic CTSB then triggers chondrocyte pyroptosis [21]. However, whether CTSB is involved in METTL3-mediated regulation in OA progression has not been reported.

Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) is a proinflammatory cytokine that can induce several pathological processes in OA, such as cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, and bone resorption [22]. It activates chondrocytes to produce enzymes like matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and aggrecanases, which degrade the extracellular matrix of cartilage [23]. Additionally, IL-1β upregulates the expression of adhesive molecules on chondrocytes, contributing to the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the joint [24]. Therefore, IL-1β is commonly used to establish an in vitro OA cell model [25, 26]. This study investigates the METTL3-CTSB axis in OA progression using IL-1β-stimulated human chondrocytes, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation for developing novel OA therapies.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

This experimental study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Shangyu People’s Hospital of Shaoxing [No. SYRY-REC-2024(A005)]. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to specimen collection. The specimens were obtained from discarded articular cartilage after orthopedic surgery at Shangyu People’s Hospital of Shaoxing. Normal osteochondral blocks were collected from the femoral condyles of amputated limbs (N = 7), whereas OA specimens (N = 22) were obtained from the central region of the femoral condyles of OA patients.

Cell culture and treatment

Human chondrocytes C-28/I2 (Otwo Biotech, Shenzhen, China) and CHON-001 (Huzhen Industrial Co., LTD, Shanghai, China) were respectively maintained in complete DMEM/F12 (Otwo Biotech) and DMEM (Otwo Biotech) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To simulate osteoarthritic damage, the study added IL-1β-containing culture medium with different final concentrations (5, 10, and 20 ng/mL, MedChemExpress, Princeton, NJ, USA) to the cells and placed them in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. The control group was treated with an equal volume of sterile deionized water.

Cell transfection

Small hairpin RNAs of METTL3 (sh-METTL3), METTL3 overexpression plasmid, shRNA of CTSB, CTSB overexpression plasmid, and respective controls including sh-NC and pcDNA 3.1( +) vector (named pcDNA in the study) were provided by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 12-well plates 24 h prior to transfection, allowing them to reach 70–80% confluence. Transfection was initiated when the cells reached 50% confluence. The culture medium was replaced with Opti-MEM medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Gibco) was added to each tube. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min before being added to the cells. After 4 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and the cells were cultured for the designated experimental period.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Following treatments, the culture medium was aspirated from chondrocytes. For cartilage tissues, samples were pulverized in liquid nitrogen. The cell and tissue samples were placed in Trizol (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on ice for 5 min, followed by the addition of an equal volume of chloroform (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). After vortexing and centrifugation, the upper aqueous phase was transferred to new EP tubes. Isopropanol (Sigma) was added, and the supernatant was removed after centrifugation. The pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, followed by centrifugation and removal of the supernatant. RNase-free ddH2O was added to dissolve the RNA. Genomic DNA was extracted using a gDNA wiper Mix (Vazyme), and reverse transcription was carried out using HiScript III qRT SuperMix (Vazyme). Subsequently, quantitative analysis was performed using Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), and gene expression was quantified according to the 2−∆∆Ct method with normalization to β-actin. Western blotting

Cells were digested with 0.25% EDTA (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and lysed with RIPA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Concurrently, tissue samples were minced with scissors and ground before being added to protein lysis buffer RIPA. Protein samples were sequentially loaded into the gel wells and subjected to electrophoresis at 80 V until bromophenol blue had migrated past the concentration gels. The voltage was then increased to 120 V, and electrophoresis was stopped when the bromophenol blue was approximately 0.3 cm from the bottom of the glass plate. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes at a constant current of 300 mA. The membrane was incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, including the METTL3 antibody (Catalog DF12020, 1:1000, Affinity, Nanjing, China), the CTSB antibody (Catalog AF5189, 1:1000, Affinity), the MMP13 antibody (Catalog AF5355, 1:1000, Affinity), the MMP3 antibody (Catalog AF0217, 1:1000, Affinity), the ADAMTS-5 antibody (Catalog DF13268, 1:1000, Affinity), the COL2A1 antibody (Catalog AF0135, 1:1000, Affinity) and the ACAN antibody (Catalog DF7561, 1:1000, Affinity), the β-actin antibody (Catalog AF7018, 1:10,000, Affinity) and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG antibody (Catalog S0001, 1:5000, Affinity). The blots were exposed and developed in a darkroom using highly sensitive ECL chemiluminescent reagent (Beyotime). The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Cell viability analysis

Chondrocytes in the logarithmic growth phase and good condition were digested using trypsin to prepare cell suspensions. The cell suspensions were pipetted and mixed well, and then plated in 96-well plates. Phosphate Buffer Solution (PBS) was added to the outer wells of the culture plates, and the plates were placed in an incubator for cell culture. When the cell adhesion and fusion reached over 60%, the culture medium was removed by centrifugation. The cells were then transfected and treated with IL-1β. The cells were exposed to CCK-8 solution (Beyotime) for approximately 2 h for color reaction. Cell viability was measured using a microplate reader. The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Cell apoptosis analysis

Trypsin was used to digest the cells, and the cells were resuspended in fresh complete culture medium. Binding Buffer (Solarbio) was added to resuspend the cells, and PI staining solution (Solarbio) and Annexin V-FITC staining solution (Solarbio) were used to incubate cells. The cells were washed once with pre-cooled Binding Buffer in EP tubes, and the supernatant was carefully discarded. Apoptotic cells were quantified using flow cytometry. The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

For the TUNEL assay, cells were seeded on sterile glass slides placed in 6-well plates. Cells were digested with trypsin, centrifuged, and prepared into cell suspensions. The cells were evenly seeded on the glass slides, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. TUNEL detection solution (Beyotime) was dropped on the coverslip, and the glass slides were covered facing down on the detection solution, and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 60 min. After incubation, DAPI staining solution (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) was added, and the slides were washed 3 times with PBS. Anti-fade mounting medium was dropped on the coverslip, and the glass slides were covered and observed under a fluorescence microscope. The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs)

The levels of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) were measured as per the manufacturer’s instructions of commercial kits including TNF-α detection kit (#ml077385, Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and IL-6 detection kit (#ml058097, Enzyme-linked Biotechnology). Samples were detected using a microplate reader. The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and Malondialdehyde (MDA) detection

Cellular ROS Assay kit (#ab113851, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) was used to detect ROS level, and Lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit (#ab118970, Abcam) was utilized to detect MDA level. All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Fe2+ level analysis

Cells from each group were digested with trypsin and counted. Equal numbers of cells were then collected and resuspended in lysis buffer, followed by incubation on ice for 2 h to complete lysis. The supernatant was collected and the Fe2+ concentration was determined according to the instructions of a Fe2+ concentration analysis kit (#E1042, Applygen, Beijing, China). The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

m6A RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assay

Magna MeRIP™ m6A kit (Millipore, Bradford, MA, USA) was used for the study. Total RNA was extracted from the cells and quantified. A suspension of magnetic beads was added to enzyme-free tubes, followed by the addition of RIP wash buffer. Magnetic beads were collected on the magnetic stand, and the supernatant was discarded. RIP wash buffer was added to each tube of magnetic beads to resuspend them. The pre-incubated RNA-antibody complexes were then added to the magnetic beads and allowed to incubate overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The magnetic beads were collected on the magnetic stand, and the supernatant was discarded. The enrichment of CTSB mRNA in the immunoprecipitates was analyzed by qRT-PCR, as described above. The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The online m6A site predictor SRAMP (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp) was used to predict potential m6A modification sites on CTSB mRNA. DNA fragments of the wild-type CTSB 3’UTR containing the predicted m6A sites were synthesized by GenePharma Co., Ltd and subsequently used to insert into the pmirGLO vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) to generate wild-type CTSB reporter plasmid (CTSB-wt). Site-directed mutagenesis was performed to mutate the predicted m6A sites by GenScript Biotech Corporation (Nanjing, China), and the resulting mutant fragment was similarly cloned into the pmirGLO vector to create the mutant reporter (CTSB-mut). CHON-001 and C28/I2 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected with reporter plasmids with sh-METTL3 or sh-NC according to the above methods. After 48 h, the cells were harvested and subjected to analysis of the Dual-Lucy Assay Kit (Solarbio). The assay was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism, and results were shown as means ± standard deviations (SD). Differences between groups were analyzed using Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney test, Student’s t-tests, or one-way analysis of variance. P < 0.05 was indicative of statistical significance.

The study then analyzed the effects of METTL3 on IL-1β-induced human chondrocyte injury by transfecting shRNA of METTL3 into CHON-001 and C28/I2 cells before IL-1β stimulation. The results showed that IL-1β treatment increased the protein levels of matrix-degrading enzymes (MMP13, MMP3, and ADAMTS-5) and decreased the levels of cartilage matrix components (COL2A1 and ACAN) (Figure S1A). IL-1β stimulation-induced increase in METTL3 protein expression was attenuated after METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, the results revealed that IL-1β treatment inhibited cell viability and promoted cell apoptosis, whereas these effects were attenuated when METTL3 expression was downregulated (Fig. 2B–D). Similarly, IL-1β treatment increased the levels of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) as well as oxidative stress indicators (ROS and MDA), however, these effects were counteracted after transfection with sh-METTL3 (Fig. 2E–H). Further, the increased level of Fe2+ by IL-1β treatment was reversed after METTL3 knockdown in CHON-001 and C28/I2 cells (Fig. 2I). The results showed that IL-1β treatment downregulated the mRNA and protein levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 and promoted the mRNA and protein levels of ACSL4 (Figure S1B and C). METTL3 upregulated CTSB expression through m6A modification

The study continued to explore the mechanism by which METTL3 regulated IL-1β-induced human chondrocyte injury. The online m6A site predictor SRAMP revealed that there were potential m6A modification sites on CTSB mRNA (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, the MeRIP assay showed that the enrichment of m6A antibody to CTSB m6A was significantly weakened after METTL3 knockdown in CHON-001 and C28/I2 cells (Fig. 3B). Comparatively, METTL3 knockdown inhibited the luciferase activity of the wild-type reporter plasmid of CTSB but not the mutant reporter (CTSB-mut) (Fig. 3C). In addition, the result showed that CTSB expression at mRNA and protein levels was decreased after METTL3 silencing but increased after METTL3 overexpression (Fig. 3D and E). The mRNA and protein expression of CTSB were upregulated in human osteoarthritic cartilages in comparison with healthy cartilage tissues (Fig. 3F and G). Moreover, CTSB mRNA expression was positively correlated with METTL3 mRNA expression in human osteoarthritic cartilages (Figure S1D). Further, the study discovered that IL-1β treatment promoted CTSB protein expression in a dose-dependent manner in CHON-001 and C28/I2 cells (Fig. 3H). Discussion

The RNA methyltransferase METTL3 has emerged as a significant player in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including osteoarthritis. Its regulatory role in the development of OA has been observed. Studies have suggested that METTL3 may alter the expression of genes involved in cartilage degradation, inflammation, and cell metabolism, potentially contributing to the progression of osteoarthritis [27]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms and pathways through which METTL3 operates in OA remain incompletely understood. Further investigation is necessary to uncover the specific role of METTL3 in osteoarthritis pathogenesis and to explore its potential as a therapeutic target.

While OA pathogenesis has traditionally been attributed to inflammatory cascades, growing evidence underscores the critical role of non-inflammatory pathways in driving chondrocyte injury and joint degeneration. For instance, a recent landmark study in Bone Research highlighted that small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) act as pivotal non-inflammatory mediators of chondrocyte injury in arthritis, revealing a distinct paradigm that operates parallel to classical inflammation [28]. This emerging perspective suggests that chondrocyte dysfunction can be initiated and propagated through mechanisms independent of overt inflammation, such as dysregulated intercellular communication or intrinsic cellular stress. Our current study, focusing on the intracellular epigenetic regulator METTL3, adds another compelling layer to this evolving concept of non-inflammatory OA pathogenesis. Our results showed that METTL3 mediated m6A modification of CTSB to promote chondrocyte injury in osteoarthritis.

Other m6A regulators also participate in OA pathogenesis through distinct mechanisms. For instance, during limb development, METTL14 promoted chondrogenesis by regulating the GDF5-RUNX2-extracellular matrix gene axis [29], suggesting its potential role in cartilage homeostasis. In OA, WTAP-mediated m6A modification of FRZB activated the Wnt pathway, driving inflammatory responses [30]. In contrast, the demethylase FTO appears to be protective, attenuating meniscus degeneration and OA by coordinating autophagy and energy metabolism [31], highlighting the protective role of m6A erasers. Furthermore, in rheumatoid arthritis, m6A modification has a pathogenic role, as IGF2BP3 stabilizes RASGRF1 mRNA to promote joint injury [32], indicating shared mechanisms across different arthritic diseases. These findings collectively emphasize the complex and multifaceted roles of m6A modifications in joint disorders.

Accumulating evidence has revealed a critical role of METTL3 in OA. For example, METTL3-mediated m6A modification of autophagy related 7 promoted cellular senescence and cartilage destruction in OA progression [33]. METTL3-mediated m6A modification of long non-coding RNA IGFBP7-OT contributed to chondrocyte apoptosis and reduced extracellular matrix synthesis in an OA cell model [34]. Another paper revealed that METTL3 expression was upregulated in OA cartilage tissue and IL-1β-induced OA cellular model, and METTL3 upregulated LINC00680 by m6A modification to inhibit IL-1β-induced chondrocyte proliferation and promote extracellular matrix degradation [35]. Similarly, the study discovered METTL3 upregulation in human osteoarthritic cartilages and IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes and its promoting effect on IL-1β-induced human chondrocyte apoptosis. IL-1β stimulation elevated Fe2+/ROS/MDA levels in rat chondrocytes, and these effects were associated with METTL3 upregulation [36]. Our data showed that METTL3 knockdown neutralized IL-1β treatment-induced promoting effects on ferroptosis in human chondrocytes. In addition, METTL3 knockdown rescued IL-1β stimulation-induced elevation of TNF-α and IL-6 levels, aligning with the reported data [15]. These data demonstrate that METTL3 contributes to OA progression by promoting chondrocyte apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress and ferroptosis.

Our mechanistic investigations further identified that METTL3 upregulated CTSB expression by regulating its m6A methylation modification. A previous study has shown that CTSB expression was upregulated in human osteoarthritic cartilages and IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes, and its overexpression promoted IL-1β-induced chondrocyte apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress [37]. Similarly, the study also discovered its upregulation in human osteoarthritic cartilages and IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes and its promoting effects on IL-1β-induced chondrocyte apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress. In addition, our data revealed that CTSB promoted ferroptosis of IL-1β-induced chondrocytes. Importantly, the regulation of METTL3 knockdown in IL-1β-induced human chondrocyte injury was dependent on the downregulation of CTSB. Previous evidence has suggested that CTSB can activate the NLRP3 inflammasone in disease progression [38]. Thus, CTSB may activate the NLRP3 inflammasone to promote the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, leading to the recruitment of immune cells and exacerbating the inflammatory response. CTSB also determines the ferroptotic response [39], and it may disrupt iron homeostasis in chondrocytes, leading to iron accumulation and triggering iron-dependent cell death pathways in OA progression.

The identification of the METTL3-CTSB axis as a driver of chondrocyte injury presents an intriguing convergence with the aforementioned non-inflammatory paradigm involving sEVs. While the Bone Research study implicates extracellular vesicles in non-inflammatory cell-to-cell communication [28], our findings highlight intracellular epigenetic reprogramming (via m6A modification) as a fundamental mechanism that directly triggers chondrocyte dysfunction. Despite the distinct molecular players—sEVs acting as extracellular messengers versus METTL3 acting as an intracellular epigenetic writer—both pathways converge on the same critical outcome: chondrocyte injury in the absence of a primary inflammatory trigger. This comparison not only reinforces the significance of non-inflammatory mechanisms in joint diseases but also broadens the therapeutic landscape, suggesting that targeting either extracellular communication (e.g., sEV release/uptake) or intracellular epigenetic regulators (e.g., METTL3) could be viable strategies for halting OA progression.

In summary, our study demonstrates that METTL3 promotes chondrocyte injury in OA through m6A-dependent upregulation of CTSB. A limitation of this study is its reliance on in vitro models using IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes, which may not fully recapitulate the complex microenvironment of the human joint in vivo. Furthermore, while the study focused on specific injury phenotypes, the broader pathophysiological implications of METTL3 and CTSB dysregulation in OA warrant further investigation. Future clinical studies are needed to validate the therapeutic potential of targeting the METTL3-CTSB axis in OA. Despite these limitations, the study provides a novel therapeutic approach for managing OA.
