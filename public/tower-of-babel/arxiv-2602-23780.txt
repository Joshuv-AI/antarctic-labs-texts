Explicit Formulas for the Inversion of the Convolution of Polynomials and Arbitrary Functions with Schwartz Kernels
Alfredo González-Calvin

Abstract
Convolution serves as a powerful operation for the regularization of functions. While polynomials inherently possess smoothness, it is particularly interesting to investigate their behavior under convolution. This interest stems from the fact that numerous engineering and physical phenomena can be modeled through such operations, including weighted averages, blurring effects and convolutional integral equations. In this work, we show that under certain mild conditions, the convolution with any even Schwartz function acts as an automorphism on the vector space of finite-order polynomials. We derive explicit equations for the inverse operation of this convolution, which are numerically simple to implement. In addition, we extend the deconvolution with (not necessarily even) Schwartz functions to a broader class of functions, including L 1 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{1}(\mathbb{R}) , L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}) , the Schwartz space and tempered distributions. Specifically, we establish a explicit rigorous formula for the deconvolution of a function or distribution that has been convolved with a Schwartz function, being a particular example the Weierstrass Transform. For the latter, we show that any Schwartz function and tempered distribution that has been transformed, can be recovered, in their respective topologies, by the limit of a sequence of linear combination of recursive convolutions. This provides a new formula for the inverse of the Weierstrass Transform that can be numerically implemented.

Introduction

The Weierstrass transform with
parameter

\varepsilon>0
of a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, is
given, when it exists, by

F_{\varepsilon}(x):=(f*\varphi_{\varepsilon})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x-s)\varphi_{\varepsilon}(s)ds,

where
x\mapsto\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{4\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}}
is the so called

Gaussian Kernel
. The Gaussian Kernel is an even Schwartz function that
satisfies the following properties. It has unit area, i.e.,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}=1
, it converges to the Dirac delta distribution as

\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, and for any
f\in L^{p}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})
,
F_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

and
F_{\varepsilon}\to f
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
in several types of convergence. In
particular it
f\in L^{p}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})
then
F_{\varepsilon}\to f
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

pointwise and in
L^{p}
. It has been studied extensively in

19
, where its inversion is presented as a differential
operator, but also in
3
where it is shown the
relationship between the transform and the Hermite polynomials.

Observing the inversion formula in the aforementioned references, and how the
Weierstrass transform acts on polynomials, it is natural to ask how the
polynomials behave under a more general operation, and if there is an inverse
for this operation which is simple to implement and compute That is, instead of
focusing on the convolution with the Gaussian Kernel, we focus on the
convolution with Schwartz convolution kernels. These questions, apart from its
theoretical interest have interesting applications, since convolution with a
function of the above characteristics can be thought as a weighted average
operation, or equivalently, as a low pass filter, and so the process of

deconvolution
, i.e., the process of restoring an original signal from a
filtered one, is of great application and theoretical interest. The theoretical
importance of deconvolution is well-established. Classical treatments such as

2
and
11
provide systematic
inversion formulas for several convolution transforms,

13
provides explicit formulas for the solution of
convolution integral equations by means of differentiation and

16
presents a formula for inverting the convolution
when the convolution kernel has a Fourier transform with compact support.
Moreover, in
1
is presented an inverse convolution
approach by the limit of a finite sequence of linear combinations of exponential
functions. More recently, in

14
deconvolution
formulas for arbitrary distribution functions and densities using characteristic
functions and geometric series methods are developed. Beyond theory,
deconvolution has several practical applications and implementations. Numerical
approaches are deeply treated in
10
, while

15
provides a comparative analysis of different
deconvolution techniques. In
18
the deconvolution with
Gaussian Kernels is treated and applied to radiotherapy problems, and in

6
it is discussed several physical and engineering
applications where (de)convolution is necessary. If the convolution kernel is
unknown, blind deconvolution techniques become useful, as presented in

4
and
20
. Convolutions are also applied
in more mathematical abstract situations, as in

5
. More recently,

12
provides a novel framework for
deconvolution techniques.

In this work we contribute both theoretically and practically to the
deconvolution field with Theorem

2
and, particularly,
with Theorem
3
. In the
former we present that the convolution with an even Schwartz function is an
invertible operator on the space of finite order polynomials. In the latter we
show that the inverse of the convolution with a Schwartz function can
be
explicitly computed as a limit of a sequence of linear combinations of recursive
convolutions. More specifically, in Section
2
we
show that, if the convolution kernel is an even Schwartz function, the
convolution operator is an automorphism of polynomial vector
spaces of finite degree. Moreover, we explicitly present the
formula (Equations (
7
) and
(
8
)) of the inverse of the convolution, and
validate the results numerically. The inverse, curiously enough, consists on a
linear combination of iterative convolutions of the convolved
polynomial (see Theorem
2
), from
which it follows that any polynomial can be written as a linear combination of
its iterative convolutions; that is, any polynomial is, in essence, a
linear combination of its weighted averages, see Remark

4
. We end the section discussing the
main result (Theorem

3
), which we consider
the main contribution to the field of deconvolution, either
theoretically and numerically speaking. It happens that, by passing to the
limit in Theorem
2
,
we obtain Theorem
3
,
that is, we can recover as a limiting sequence of linear combinations of
recursive convolutions the original function which has been convolved,
obtaining, particularly, a new formula for the inverse of the Weierstrass
Transform. For this special case of the Weierstrass transform, we
also prove that any Schwartz function or tempered distribution can be recovered
by passing to the limit in Equation (
9
), in
their respective topologies. Clearly, several assumptions must be made clear
for the original function and the convolution kernel, which do not
contradict the traditional assumptions for the
inverse of the convolution to exist in the Fourier transformed space. In
Section
3
we present some applications of these results,
validating our approach and providing some insight for
new signal processing methods. We end this work with some conclusions in
Section
4
.

2
Convolution and deconvolution of polynomials and arbitrary
functions with Schwartz convolution kernels

Given
\varepsilon>0
and even
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
, it is easy to check that if
p

is an affine function, then
p*\varphi_{\varepsilon}=p
. This property does not
hold in general for polynomials
p
with a degree greater than one, that is, the
fixed points for the (even) convolution operator are constants and affine
functions. However, it is interesting to study if the convolution of a
polynomial results in a polynomial, if this operation can be reversed, and if
the inverse, if it exists, can be extended to more function spaces.

In this section we get the following results for a convolution kernel
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\neq 0
and any polynomial
p
of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
defined in the real numbers.

•

If
\varphi
is even, the convolution of
p
with
\varphi
results
in a polynomial of the same degree (Theorem

1
). In particular, if the
original degree of the polynomial is
n\geq 2
, the convolution is the sum
of the original polynomial with another polynomial of degree
n-2
, one or
zero, depending on the parity of
n
(Corollaries

1
and
2
). In
particular, the convolution with an even Schwartz function of a polynomial
is another polynomial with the same asymptotic growth, and in addition the
first and second leading coefficients are shared between
p
and its
convolution.

•

If
\varphi
is even, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the convolution operator
p\mapsto p*\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is an automorphism between the vector space of
polynomials of the same degree, for any value of
\varepsilon>0
(Theorem

2
). This implies that the
convolution with an even Schwartz kernel in the vector space of polynomials
is invertible. The inverse can be computed as in Equations
(
7
) and (
8
), and
we provide an insight of its numerical simplicity in Corollary

3
.

•

We prove in Theorem

3
, that these
results can be generalized by passing to the limit in Equation
(
7
) of Theorem

2
, for a certain class of
functions, and without the need for the Schwartz convolution kernel to be
even. This results are remarkable, since by an iterative process the
original function can be restored via convolutions.

•

Some results can be extrapolated to polynomials in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
, see Section
2.3
.

Refer to Section
3
to several applications of the previous
results.

2.1
Polynomials in the real line

Let
p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, expressed in the
form

p(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\dots+a_{n}x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k},\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}),

(1)

where
\{a_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R},a_{n}\neq 0
. The vector space of all
polynomials of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
will be denoted as
\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. We will say
that
a_{n}
is the leading coefficient, and
\{a_{n-k}\}_{k=0}^{r}
are the
r

leading coefficients for
0\leq r\leq n
. For a matter of convenience we will
always exclude the case that
p\equiv 0
, i.e., the zero constant polynomial.

We will show that for any convolution kernel
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
it holds that

P_{\varepsilon}:=p*\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is a polynomial of the same degree for
\varepsilon>0
.
That is, no matter how
\varphi
is constructed, that polynomials are, in some
sense, invariant under the convolution with Schwartz kernels, i.e., the mapping

p\mapsto P_{\varepsilon}
maps
\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
into
\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. But we will also
prove that it is an automorphism if
\varphi
is
even
. But first, we
need a preliminary definition. We are going to restrict ourselves to the set

\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
. But note that, being the convolution a linear mapping, and given
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
, we can define
f/\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
as long as
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\neq 0
. So we restrict ourselves from Schwartz functions with null integral.

Definition 1
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
be even. Define for
each
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the constant

c_{m}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{m}\varphi(x)dx.

(2)

It is clear that
c_{0}=1
and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{m}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx=\begin{cases}c_{m}\varepsilon^{m}&m\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}\\
0&m\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+1\end{cases}.

Note that the integral is well defined since
\id^{m}\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})

for any
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Remark 1
.

Note that if
p\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\int_{\mathbb{R}}p\varphi_{\varepsilon}
can
be considered as a polynomial with even powers in
\varepsilon
for any even

\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed if
n>2
is even

\varepsilon\mapsto h(\varepsilon):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)dx=a_{0}+a_{2}c_{2}\varepsilon^{2}+\dots+a_{n}c_{n}\varepsilon^{n},

while the result is clear if
n
is odd. Moreover, it is clear
that if
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
is neither even nor odd, we can still
define
c_{m}
in the same way.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
be
even and
\varepsilon>0
.

It holds that

P_{\varepsilon}(x)=(p*\varphi_{\varepsilon})(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}^{j}\binom{j}{k}a_{j}x^{j-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k},\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}).

(3)

Therefore, the mapping
p\mapsto P_{\varepsilon}
is a mapping of
\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})

into itself.

Proof.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We know by the Binomial theorem that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(x-y)^{m}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{k}x^{m-k}y^{k}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{k}x^{m-k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}y^{k}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}^{m}\binom{m}{k}x^{m-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k},

where the last equality comes from Definition

1
and the fact that
(-1)^{k}=1

for even
k
. Now note that since the convolution is a
bilinear operation

\displaystyle P_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(x-y)^{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)dy=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}^{j}\binom{j}{k}x^{j-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k},\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}),

and since the sums are finite, we can use Fubini’s Theorem to
end the proof of Equation (
3
).
Note that the right hand side of Equation
(
3
) is again a polynomial of at most degree

n
, proving the theorem.
∎

It is clear and immediate that if
\varphi
is not even we still get
a polynomial, the fact that we are restricting to even convolution kernels
will become apparent in Theorem

2

Corollary 1
.

Let

(4)

That is, the convolution of a polynomial results in another polynomial

P_{\varepsilon}
is the same as

.

Proof.

We proceed for the even case by induction over

that

and proceed to

\displaystyle Q_{\varepsilon}(x)=(q*\varphi_{\varepsilon})(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}^{j}\binom{j}{k}a_{j}x^{j-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}^{j}\binom{j}{k}a_{j}x^{j-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}+\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}a_{n+1}x^{n+1-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}

\displaystyle=(r*\varphi_{\varepsilon})(x)+\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}a_{n+1}x^{n+1-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}

\displaystyle=\left[r(x)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}x^{j}\left(\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}}^{n-j}\binom{k+j}{k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}a_{k+j}\right)\right]+\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}a_{n+1}x^{n+1-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}

\displaystyle=q(x)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}x^{j}\left(\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}}^{n-j}\binom{k+j}{k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}a_{k+j}\right)+\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}a_{n+1}x^{n+1-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}.

Suppose that
n
is even, then

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}a_{n+1}x^{n+1-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}=x

{}^{n-1}\binom{n+1}{2}c_{2}\varepsilon^{2}a_{n+1}+x^{n-3}\binom{n+1}{4}c_{4}\varepsilon^{4}a_{n+1}

\displaystyle+\dots+x\binom{n+1}{n}c_{n}\varepsilon^{n}a_{n+1},

which implies

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}x^{j}\left(\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}}^{n-j}\binom{k+j}{k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}a_{k+j}\right)+\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}a_{j}x^{n+1-k}c_{k}\varepsilon^{k}

Remark 2
.

Given
p\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
n\geq 2
. We will denote by

T_{\varepsilon}^{k+1}(p)=T_{\varepsilon}(T_{\varepsilon}^{k}(p))=T_{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}p_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}T_{\varepsilon}(p_{j})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(p_{j}+p_{j+1}),

and then

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}^{k+1}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\binom{k}{j}+\binom{k}{j-1}\right)p_{j}+p_{0}+p_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k+1}{j}p_{j}+p_{0}+p_{k+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\binom{k+1}{j}p_{j},

where we used the fact that
\binom{k}{j}+\binom{k}{j-1}=\binom{k+1}{j}
.
Moreover,
p_{k}\in\mathscr{P}_{n-2k}(\mathbb{R})
, which implies by
Corollary
1
and Remark

2
that
p_{k+1}\in\mathscr{P}_{n-2(k+1)}(\mathbb{R})
. This proves Equation
(
5
). Note that the formula
is always valid, independently if

is even or odd,
so what we need now is to prove it for
when
2k>n

1.

Suppose

is even and
2k\leq n
, then the previous
discussion is valid, and so
p_{j}\in\mathscr{P}_{n-2j}(\mathbb{R})

for
j\in\{0,\dots,k\}
. Suppose that
2k>n
, then

k>\frac{n}{2}
and for
j=n/2
it holds by the
previous proof that
p_{j}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e.,
it is a constant. Since constant functions are invariant
under convolution with
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
, it holds that

for

j\in\{\frac{n}{2},\dots,k\}
, and this proves
the first assertion.

2.

Suppose
n
is odd and
2k<n
, then
2k\leq n-1
, so the previous discussion is valid, that is

p_{j}\in\mathscr{P}_{n-2j}(\mathbb{R})
for
j\in\{0,\dots,k\}
.
Now, if
2k>n

2k\geq n+1
, and for

∎

It will be useful to compute the polynomials

in Corollary

Lemma 1
.

Let
p\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
with
n\geq 2
,
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})

be even and
\varepsilon>0
. Defining
T_{\varepsilon}:\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})

as in Corollary
2
as well as
p_{j}

for

p_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}(-1)^{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j-k}(p),

(6)

where
T_{\varepsilon}^{0}:=\id
for all
\varepsilon>0
and
p_{0}:=p
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction over

. By definition it is true for
j=0
,
and for
j=1
we know by Theorem

1
and Corollary

2
that
T_{\varepsilon}(p_{0})=p_{0}+p_{1}
and
therefore

p_{1}=T_{\varepsilon}(p_{0})-p_{0}
for which Equation
(

)
clearly holds. Suppose now that it is true for

T_{\varepsilon}(p_{j})=p_{j}+p_{j+1}
and so

\displaystyle p_{j+1}=T_{\varepsilon}(p_{j})-p_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}(-1)^{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j+1-k}(p_{0})+\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}(-1)^{k+1}T_{\varepsilon}^{j-k}(p_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}(-1)^{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j+1-k}(p_{0})+\sum_{k=1}^{j+1}\binom{j}{k-1}(-1)^{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j+1-k}(p_{0})

\displaystyle=T_{\varepsilon}^{j+1}(p_{0})+(-1)^{j+1}T_{\varepsilon}(p_{0})+\sum_{k=1}^{j}\binom{j+1}{k}(-1)^{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j+1-k}(p_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j+1}\binom{j+1}{k}(-1)^{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j+1-k}(p_{0}),

where the fact

∎

We now prove that the mapping
T_{\varepsilon}
defined in Corollary

\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})

in a recursive manner. This result has some
remarkable properties: in

the convolution with an even
convolution Schwartz kernel is
always a reversible operation
.
Therefore, we can consider this to be an extension of the Weierstrass Transform
of polynomials, in which

is the Gaussian Kernel, and so
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
is even. See

for a discussion of
Hermite polynomials and the Weierstrass function. Finally, note in the proof of

then

with

p_{1}
two degrees
below

.
This is what will allow us to prove the automorphism and the computation of the

Theorem 2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
be even and

\varepsilon>0
.
Then

Moreover, given

with

even

T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(p)=\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}(-1)^{j}p_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j+k}\binom{j}{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j-k}(p)=\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}(-1)^{n/2-j}\binom{n/2+1}{j}T_{\varepsilon}^{n/2-j}(p),

(7)

where
p_{0}:=p
, and
T_{\varepsilon}^{0}:=\id

while if

(8)

The fact that

is linear follows directly from the definition
of the convolution. We proceed to prove that it is bijective. Suppose
first that

. We know by
Corollary
2
that
T_{\varepsilon}(p)=p+p_{1}

where

.
If

p_{1}(x)=a_{2}\varepsilon^{2}c_{2}
and
q_{1}(x)=b_{2}\varepsilon^{2}c_{2}
. Thus

T_{\varepsilon}(p)=T_{\varepsilon}(q)\iff p+p_{1}=q+q_{1},

but since
p+p_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and

with the same leading coefficients as
p
and
q
by Corollary

1
,
and two polynomials of the same degree are equal if and only if all of
its coefficients
are equal, it follows that

b_{2}=a_{2},b_{1}=a_{1},b_{0}+b_{2}\varepsilon^{2}c_{2}=a_{0}+a_{2}\varepsilon^{2}c_{2},

from which it follows that
b_{0}=a_{0}
, that is

, proving
that
T_{\varepsilon}
is injective. Clearly given

the polynomial

is an isomorphism of
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto itself.
Therefore, we can know generalize this concepts by induction.

•

T_{\varepsilon}
is injective. Let

. Suppose
T(p)=T(q)
, then

that

and
a_{n-1}=b_{n-1}

2

and therefore

for all

•

\mathscr{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R})
onto

.

Suppose now that

. Then
T(p)=p+p_{1}
with

, which implies that

q=p-p_{1}

We proceed now to prove Equation (

For

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{(n+2)/2}\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j+k}\binom{j}{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j-k}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j+k}\binom{j}{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{j-k}(p)+\sum_{k=0}^{n/2+1}(-1)^{n/2+1+k}\binom{n/2+1}{k}T_{\varepsilon}^{n/2+1-k}(p)

Corollary 3
.

Equation (
7
) can also be computed as

T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}(-1)^{j}\binom{n/2+1}{j+1}T_{\varepsilon}^{j}

which in turn implies that in

, with even

,

T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\circ T_{\varepsilon}=\id=-\sum_{j=1}^{n/2+1}\binom{n/2+1}{j}(-T_{\varepsilon})^{j}=\id-(\id-T_{\varepsilon})^{n/2+1},

.

Proof.

The first result follows from a change of indexes in the
sum. The second follows noting that, if
f:V\to V
is a linear
mapping, where
V
is any vector space,
then, by the binomial theorem for linear functions,

\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}f^{k}=(\id-f)^{n}
where
\id:V\to V

is the identity on

.
∎

Note from Corollary
3
it is only needed
to compute
n/2
convolutions numerically speaking, because each time
the index of the sum advances, it is only needed to convolve the
previously convolved function.

Remark 3
.

Theorem
2
states that, for
polynomials, convolution is invertible as long as the kernel is an even
(Schwartz) function. Note that convolution is, practically speaking, the
filtering of the function with a smooth filter in
\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})
. Note that since

\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
the filter has finite energy. What
the Theorem states is that, even after having (for example) low-filtered a
polynomial via convolution with an even convolution kernel (e.g., Gaussian
Kernel, or the compactly supported convolution kernel
x\mapsto\varphi(x)=\ind_{(-1,1)}\exp(-1/(1-x^{2}))
), we can recover the original function
uniquely. This powerful fact can also be numerically implemented easily by
Equations (
7
) and
(
8
). See Section
3
for
an application related to this remark.

Remark 4
.

Suppose
n
is even. Note that
T_{\varepsilon}(T^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(p))=p
and
T_{\varepsilon}

is linear, which implies

p=\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}(-1)^{n/2-j}\binom{n/2+1}{j}T_{\varepsilon}^{n/2+1-j}(p),

that is, any polynomial can be expressed as a linear combination of its
convolutions with even Schwartz convolution kernels, no matter the
\varepsilon>0

nor the (even) convolution kernel. This is an interesting result: the sum of
the convolved versions of the polynomial generate the same polynomial. For
example, for
n=4
,

p=T_{\varepsilon}^{3}(p)-3T_{\varepsilon}^{2}(p)+3T_{\varepsilon}(p)=p*(3\varphi_{\varepsilon}-3\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\varphi_{\varepsilon}+\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\varphi_{\varepsilon}).

An in general, for even
n
we have that

\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}(-1)^{n/2-j}\binom{n/2+1}{j}T_{\varepsilon}^{n/2-j}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})

is an identity operator for the convolution of polynomials in

\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
with even Schwartz kernels. Clearly, the identity operator
is not a polynomial, and thus they do not form a group. Moreover, note that
being
T_{\varepsilon}
an automorphism in
\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
then it cannot hold that

T_{\varepsilon}(p)
is identically zero for any
p\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
except for
p\equiv 0
. This is congruent with information theory, we can
always

recover the original polynomial after convolution because there is

always
information about it after the convolution, i.e.,

T_{\varepsilon}(p)\not\equiv 0
for any
p\not\equiv 0
.

We end this section by noting the following. There is nothing special
about
\varphi
being a Schwartz function, since as it can be seen
in the proofs of the previous results, the rapidly decreasing property
of
\varphi
is
only
needed so that the convolution is well posed.
Therefore, any other even function for which
p*\varphi
, as
well as its recursive convolutions, make sense, is equally valid. Nevertheless,
in the following section we will show that the requirement for
\varphi

to be a Schwartz function is essential to carry the generalization to more
function spaces.

2.2
The extension to a bigger space of functions: The deconvolution
of arbitrary functions

The natural question now is to ask if, for a given function
f
, it holds (under
certain assumptions about
f
) that, by passing to the limit in Equation
(
7
), we can recover
f
from
T_{\varepsilon}(f)
. That is,
if
T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}
is the inverse of the automorphism
T_{\varepsilon}
in

for some even
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
(see Theorem

) does it hold that

\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(f))=f,

in some sense of convergence? The answer is affirmative under some
conditions on the Fourier transform of
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}
and different
assumptions for
f
. We prove these results in the next theorem, in which the
assumption that
\varphi
is even is not always required. As mention in the
introduction, we will denote
\mathcal{F}\left(f\right)
or
\widehat{f}
the Fourier
transform of a function
f
whenever it exists, and as
\mathcal{F}
the Fourier
transform as an operator. For its properties refer to

9
and
8
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Suppose that
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
it holds that
0<\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}(\xi)<2
. Let the operator
f\mapsto T_{\varepsilon}(f)=f*\varphi_{\varepsilon}
, when
it makes sense for

f
,
and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}

be the operator

f\mapsto T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n+1}{k+1}T_{\varepsilon}^{k}(f),

(9)

when it makes sense for
f
, where
T_{\varepsilon}^{k}
is the
k
-th folding
composition of
T_{\varepsilon}
. That is, the convolution applied repetitively

k
times.
If
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
denote by
f*\varphi_{\varepsilon}

the tempered distribution
\langle f*\varphi_{\varepsilon},\psi\rangle=\langle f,\check{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}*\psi\rangle
for all
\psi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
where

\check{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}(x)=\varphi_{\varepsilon}(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Extend the definition of the operators
T_{\varepsilon}
and
T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}

to
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
. Then the following statements are true

1.

If
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
then

\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(f))=f
in

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

2.

If
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\widehat{f}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
then

\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(f))=f

pointwise.

3.

Let
\mathds{1}\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
be the one tempered distribution (i.e.,

\langle\mathds{1},\psi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi
for all
\psi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}))
, let
\delta\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
be the Dirac delta distribution,
let
\mathrm{pv}\frac{1}{x}\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
be the Cauchy Principal Value distribution,
and for
y\in\mathbb{R}

let
e^{2\pi iy\id}
the distribution that arises from the
(slowly increasing) function
x\mapsto e^{2\pi ixy}
. Then,
for any of these distributions
u\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
it holds that

T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(u))\to u
as
n\to\infty
in the
distributional topology. The same holds for any linear combination
or/and translations of these distributions.

4.

Suppose now that
\varphi
is the Gaussian Kernel, i.e.,

\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}e^{-(x/2)^{2}}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, given
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
it holds that

T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(f))\to f
as
n\to\infty
in the
Schwartz topology, and
for any
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, it holds
T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(f))\to f

as
n\to\infty
in the tempered distribution sense.

Proof.

We prove each statement separately.

1.

First note that since
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
then
T_{\varepsilon}(f)=f*\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by
Young’s inequality for convolution
7
, which
implies that

T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(f))\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and so the operator makes
sense. By
the Fourier transform properties, it holds that

\mathcal{F}\left(T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(f))\right)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all

f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover
\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
, and
since
\mathcal{F}\left(T_{\varepsilon}(f)\right)=\widehat{f}\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}
,
by induction it is easily seen that
\mathcal{F}\left(T^{k}_{\varepsilon}(f)\right)=\widehat{f}(\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\dots\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}})=\widehat{f}\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, by
the
linearity
of the Fourier transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(T_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}(T_{\varepsilon}(f))\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n+1}{k+1}\widehat{f}\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}^{k+1}=\hat{f}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n+1}{k+1}\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}^{k+1}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n+1}{k+1}\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}^{k+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{k-1}\binom{n+1}{k}\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}^{k}=-\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}\left(-\widehat{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{k},

but by the binomial theorem

That

even convolution kernel is an isomorphism of
\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
onto itself
for all

n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and we can compute the inverse of the convolution similar to
equations
(
7
) and (
8
).
Indeed, if
n
is even the inverse of
p\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mapsto T_{\varepsilon}(p)=p*\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
even
is

T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(p)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq n/2}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha+\beta|}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}T_{\varepsilon}^{|\alpha-\beta|}(p),\quad(p\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),

while if
n
is odd

T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(p)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq\frac{n-1}{2}}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha+\beta|}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}T_{\varepsilon}^{|\alpha-\beta|}(p),\quad(p\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

4
Conclusions

In this work we have shown that even Schwartz convolution (i.e., convolution
with even Schwartz convolution kernels) is an automorphism between polynomial
spaces of finite order. In particular, we obtained an explicit formula for the
inversion of the convolution, and numerically validated it with the Taylor
polynomial of a sinusoidal function. Moreover, we have shown how these results
can be extended to a bigger class of functions and tempered distributions,
having a new iterative formula for the inverse of the convolution of these
functions and distributions with Schwartz kernels. We have numerically
validated this extension with an application to signal processing in which a
sinusoidal function has been recovered after convolution, either in a noiseless
scenario and in a less than unity signal to noise ratio situation.
