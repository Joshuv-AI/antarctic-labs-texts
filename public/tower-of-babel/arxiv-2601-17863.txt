A PDE Derivation of the Schrödinger–Bass Bridge
Alexandre Alouadi, Pierre Henry-Labordère, Grégoire Loeper, Othmane Mazhar, Huyên Pham, Nizar Touzi

Abstract
This short paper announces the main results of [ 1 ] , where the Schrödinger–Bass Bridge (SBB) problem is introduced and studied in full generality. Here we provide a direct PDE derivation of the SBB system in dimension one, showing how the optimal coupling problem that interpolates between the classical Schrödinger bridge and the Bass martingale transport can be solved explicitly via Legendre transforms and the heat equation. A key insight is that the optimal SBB process is a Stretched Schrödinger Bridge : the composition of a monotone transport map with a Schrödinger bridge. This extends the stretched Brownian motion representation of Bass martingales to the semimartingale setting and provides a unified framework that recovers both the Sinkhorn algorithm (in the limit β → ∞ \beta\to\infty ) and the Bass construction (as β → 0 \beta\to 0 ). We refer to [ 1 ] for complete proofs, the multidimensional setting, strong duality, dual attainment, and further developments.



1
Introduction

The Schrödinger bridge problem and martingale optimal transport provide two fundamental approaches to constructing optimal couplings of probability measures via continuous-time stochastic processes.

The Schrödinger bridge.

Given a reference Brownian motion and prescribed initial and terminal distributions
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{T}
, the Schrödinger problem seeks the path measure that minimizes relative entropy with respect to the Brownian reference while satisfying the marginal constraints. This problem has deep connections to large deviation theory and has recently found applications in machine learning and generative modeling; see
[
13
,
8
]
and the references therein.

The Bass martingale.

In contrast, the Bass problem (or stretched Brownian motion) prescribes the marginal laws of a continuous martingale and minimizes a pathwise cost measuring deviation from Brownian behavior. The solution can be represented as a monotone transport of Brownian motion, leading to elegant probabilistic constructions; see
[
4
,
5
,
10
,
12
]
.

The Schrödinger–Bass Bridge.

The work
[
1
]
introduces a one-parameter family of optimal semimartingale couplings that interpolates between these two classical problems. For a parameter
\beta>0
and probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one considers continuous semimartingales
X=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
with
X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
,
X_{T}\sim\mu_{T}
, and minimizes a functional that penalizes both the drift and the volatility of
X
:

\text{SBB}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T}):=\inf_{(X_{t})}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(|\alpha_{t}|^{2}+\beta|\sigma_{t}-\text{Id}|^{2}\right)dt\right],

(1)

where
\alpha_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
denote the drift and volatility of the semimartingale
X
.

The key insight is that:

•

As
\beta\to\infty
, the volatility is constrained to be close to the identity, and one recovers the classical Schrödinger bridge.

•

As
\beta\to 0
, the volatility penalization vanishes; the optimal process becomes a martingale, and one recovers the Bass problem.

Contribution.

In this note, we provide a direct PDE derivation of the main structural results of
[
1
]
in dimension one. By working through the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation and exploiting Legendre duality, we show that the optimal time-marginals admit a representation involving:

1.

A function
h
solving the backward heat equation,

2.

A measure
\nu
evolving by the forward heat equation,

3.

A pair of inverse transport maps
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}^{-1}
given by gradients of convex functions.

This yields the
SBB system
, which generalizes both the classical Sinkhorn iterations and the martingale Sinkhorn algorithm of
[
12
]
.

A key conceptual insight is that the process
Y_{t}:=\mathcal{Y}(t,X_{t})
is itself a
Schrödinger bridge
, and the optimal SBB process
X_{t}=\mathcal{X}(t,Y_{t})
is obtained by applying a monotone transport map to it. We call this a
Stretched Schrödinger Bridge
, by analogy with the stretched Brownian motion representation of Bass martingales. This provides a unified perspective:

Bass martingale = stretched Brownian motion

SBB solution = stretched Schrödinger bridge
.

2
Setup and Dual Problem

We work in dimension
d=1
for notational simplicity; the derivation extends verbatim to any dimension
d\geq 1
. Consider the SDE

dX_{t}=\alpha_{t}\,dt+\sigma_{t}\,dW_{t},\hskip 10.00002ptX_{0}\sim\mu_{0},

(2)

where
(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
are adapted processes. The
primal problem
is

\text{SBB}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T}):=\inf_{(\alpha,\sigma)\text{ adapted}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta(\sigma_{t}-1)^{2}\right)dt\right]

(P)

subject to the marginal constraints
X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
and
X_{T}\sim\mu_{T}
.

2.1
The dual problem

By standard arguments in stochastic control (see
[
16
]
), the
dual problem
takes the form

\mathcal{V}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T}):=\sup_{v}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{T}}[v(T,X_{T})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}}[v(0,X_{0})]\right\}

(D)

over functions
v
satisfying the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation

\partial_{t}v+\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{x}v|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial_{xx}v}{1-\partial_{xx}v/\beta}=0,

(3)

with the constraint that
\partial_{xx}v<\beta
(equivalently, the function
u:=x^{2}/2-v/\beta
is strictly convex).

Remark 1

(Strong duality and attainment)
.

The proof of strong duality
\text{SBB}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T})=\mathcal{V}(\mu_{0},\mu_{T})
and the attainment of the infimum in the primal problem (
P
) are studied in detail in
[
1
]
. This is a delicate matter: standard results from semimartingale optimal transport (e.g.,
[
16
]
) do not immediately apply because the running cost
(\sigma-1)^{2}
lacks coercivity in
\sigma
. The analysis in
[
1
]
overcomes this difficulty through careful a priori estimates and a reduction to a static dual formulation involving
\beta
-convex potentials.

3
PDE Derivation of the SBB System

3.1
Change of variables

Define the convex function

u:=\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\frac{v}{\beta}.

Substituting into (
3
), the function
u
satisfies

\partial_{t}u-\frac{\beta}{2}|\partial_{x}u-x|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\partial_{xx}u}\right)=0.

(4)

3.2
Legendre transform

Let
u^{*}
denote the Legendre transform of
u
in the spatial variable:

u^{*}(t,y):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{xy-u(t,x)\right\}.

Since
u
is convex in
x
, a standard computation shows that
u^{*}
satisfies

\partial_{t}u^{*}+\frac{\beta}{2}|\partial_{y}u^{*}-y|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(1-\partial_{yy}u^{*})=0.

(5)

3.3
Reduction to the heat equation

Define

w:=u^{*}-\frac{y^{2}}{2}.

Substituting into (
5
), the function
w
satisfies

\partial_{t}w+\frac{\beta}{2}|\partial_{y}w|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{yy}v^{*}=0.

(6)

Remarkably, if we set

h:=\exp(\beta w),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{equivalently}\hskip 10.00002ptw=\frac{1}{\beta}\log h,

(7)

then
h
solves the
heat equation
:

\partial_{t}h+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{yy}h=0.

(8)

This is the key observation: the nonlinear HJB structure reduces to the linear heat equation after the appropriate sequence of transforms.

4
The Transport Maps
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}

A key structural feature of the SBB problem is that the optimal solution can be encoded via a pair of inverse transport maps
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}
.

4.1
Definition of the maps

Since
u
is strictly convex in
x
, the function
y\mapsto\log h(t,y)+\frac{\beta}{2}|y|^{2}
is strictly convex (recall
h=e^{\beta w}
and
w=u^{*}-y^{2}/2
). For each
(t,x)
, define

\boxed{\mathcal{Y}(t,x):=\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\log h(t,y)+\frac{\beta}{2}|x-y|^{2}\right\}.}

(9)

The first-order condition for this minimization gives

\partial_{y}\log h(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x))+\beta(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)-x)=0,

which can be rewritten as

\mathcal{Y}(t,x)=x-\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{y}\log h(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)).

(10)

Define the map
\mathcal{X}
by

\boxed{\mathcal{X}(t,y):=y+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{y}\log h(t,y).}

(11)

One readily verifies that
\mathcal{X}(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x))=x
and
\mathcal{Y}(t,\mathcal{X}(t,y))=y
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}^{-1}
is the inverse map (w.r.t. the spatial variable) of
\mathcal{Y}
.

Remark 2

(Gradient structure)
.

The map
\mathcal{X}
is the gradient of a convex function:

\mathcal{X}(t,y)=\partial_{y}\mathcal{G}(t,y),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{G}(t,y):=\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\beta}\log h(t,y)=u^{*}(t,y).

4.2
Connection with the dual variables

The maps
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}
are directly related to the gradient of the dual value function
v
:

\mathcal{Y}(t,x)=x-\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{x}v(t,x).

(12)

Indeed, from the Legendre transform relations
v=\beta(x^{2}/2-u)
and
u^{*}=y^{2}/2+w
, one can verify that the minimizer in (
9
) satisfies (
12
).

5
The Optimal Process and the SBB System

5.1
Dynamics of the optimal process

Let
X_{t}
be the process induced by the optimal control from the solution
u
to the HJB equation, i.e.,

dX_{t}=\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t})\,dt+\frac{1}{1-\partial_{xx}v(t,X_{t})/\beta}\,dW_{t}.

(13)

A standard verification argument shows that
\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t})
is a martingale.

Using the transport maps, the optimal drift and volatility can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\alpha(t,x)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}v(t,x)=\beta\big(x-\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\big)=\partial_{y}\log h\big(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\big),

(14)

\displaystyle\sigma(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\partial_{xx}v(t,x)/\beta}=1+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{yy}\log h\big(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\big).

(15)

5.2
The transformed process

Define the process

Y_{t}:=\mathcal{Y}(t,X_{t})=X_{t}-\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t}).

(16)

By Itô’s formula, one can show that

dY_{t}=\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t})\,dt+dW_{t}.

Moreover, by the chain of Legendre transforms, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\beta(x-\partial_{x}u)\;=\;\beta(\partial_{y}u^{*}-y)

\displaystyle=\;\beta\partial_{y}w\;=\;\partial_{y}\log h.

Therefore,

dY_{t}=\partial_{y}\log h(t,Y_{t})\,dt+dW_{t}.

(17)

5.3
Inverse map and stretched Brownian motion

The inverse relationship, using the map
\mathcal{X}
, is

X_{t}=\mathcal{X}(t,Y_{t})=Y_{t}+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{y}\log h(t,Y_{t}).

(18)

This shows that the optimal process
X
is obtained by applying the time-dependent gradient map
\mathcal{X}
to the process
Y
.

6
Stretched Schrödinger Bridge

We now make a key observation: the process
Y_{t}
is itself a
Schrödinger bridge
, and the optimal SBB process
X_{t}
is obtained by applying a monotone transport to it. We first recall a standard characterization of Schrödinger bridges (see
[
13
,
8
]
for detailed treatments).

Definition 3

(Schrödinger Bridge)
.

Let
h:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
be a positive solution of the backward heat equation
\partial_{t}h+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{yy}h=0
. A process
(Y_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is called a
Schrödinger bridge
(with potential
h
) if it satisfies

dY_{t}=\partial_{y}\log h(t,Y_{t})\,dt+dW_{t}.

(19)

Equivalently, there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
under which
Y
is a standard Brownian motion, and
\mathbb{P}
is related to
\mathbb{Q}
by

\frac{d\mathbb{P}}{d\mathbb{Q}}=h(T,Y_{T}).

The drift
\partial_{y}\log h
in (
19
) is the classical
Nelson drift
or
osmotic velocity
that characterizes Schrödinger bridges. The function
h
is space-time harmonic for the backward heat operator.

Definition 4

(Stretched Schrödinger Bridge)
.

Let
(Y_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
be a Schrödinger bridge with potential
h
, and let
\mathcal{X}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a family of monotone increasing maps such that
\mathcal{X}(t,\cdot)=\partial_{y}\mathcal{G}(t,\cdot)
for some convex function
\mathcal{G}(t,\cdot)
. The process

X_{t}:=\mathcal{X}(t,Y_{t})

(20)

is called a
Stretched Schrödinger Bridge
.

Remark 5

(The stretching potential
\mathcal{G}
)
.

In Definition
4
, the stretching map
\mathcal{X}(t,\cdot)=\partial_{y}\mathcal{G}(t,\cdot)
is the gradient of a convex potential
\mathcal{G}
. A key feature of the Stretched Schrödinger Bridge is that
the stretching potential
\mathcal{G}
is directly determined by the Schrödinger potential
h
of the underlying Schrödinger bridge:

\mathcal{G}(t,y)=\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\beta}\log h(t,y).

(21)

The first term
\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}
is the identity potential, and the second term
\frac{1}{\beta}\log h
is a perturbation controlled by:

•

the Schrödinger potential
h
, which encodes the drift of the base Schrödinger bridge
Y
;

•

the parameter
\beta
, which controls the strength of the perturbation.

Thus, the stretching map

\mathcal{X}(t,y)=\partial_{y}\mathcal{G}(t,y)=y+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{y}\log h(t,y)=y+\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial_{y}h(t,y)}{h(t,y)}

combines the identity with the normalized gradient of the Schrödinger potential
h
.

Remark 7

(Connection to optimal transport calibration)
.

The Stretched Schrödinger Bridge framework has direct applications to
model calibration
in mathematical finance. In the calibration problem, one seeks to construct a diffusion process
X
whose marginal distributions match observed option prices (via the Breeden–Litzenberger formula), while staying close to some reference model.

The Bass martingale approach to calibration was developed in
[
10
]
, where the stretched Brownian motion construction provides a local volatility model consistent with given marginals. The path-dependent optimal transport perspective on calibration was studied in
[
11
]
, connecting optimal transport maps to model calibration on exotic derivatives. See also
[
2
]
for calibration of the Bass local volatility model.

The SBB framework extends these approaches by:

1.

allowing a non-trivial reference model (the Schrödinger bridge component);

2.

providing a principled way to balance fit to marginals (via the stretching map
\mathcal{X}
) against proximity to the reference (via the parameter
\beta
);

3.

yielding explicit formulas for the optimal drift and volatility in terms of the Schrödinger potential
h
.

Theorem 8

(SBB solution as Stretched Schrödinger Bridge)
.

The optimal process
X_{t}
solving the SBB problem (
P
) is a Stretched Schrödinger Bridge. Specifically:

1.

The process
Y_{t}=\mathcal{Y}(t,X_{t})
is a Schrödinger bridge with potential
h=e^{\beta w}
, where
h
solves the backward heat equation
\partial_{t}h+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{yy}h=0
with terminal condition determined by the marginal constraints.

2.

The optimal process is recovered as

X_{t}=\mathcal{X}(t,Y_{t})=Y_{t}+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{y}\log h(t,Y_{t}),

where
\mathcal{X}(t,\cdot)=\partial_{y}\mathcal{G}(t,\cdot)
with the convex potential

\mathcal{G}(t,y)=\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\beta}\log h(t,y)=u^{*}(t,y).

3.

There exists a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}\sim\mathbb{P}
with
\frac{d\mathbb{P}}{d\mathbb{Q}}=h(T,Y_{T})
under which
Y
is a standard Brownian motion.

Remark 9

(Interpolation via the stretching map)
.

The parameter
\beta
controls the “amount of stretching”:

•

As
\beta\to\infty
:
\mathcal{X}(t,y)\to y
, so
X_{t}\to Y_{t}
. The stretched Schrödinger bridge reduces to an ordinary Schrödinger bridge.

•

As
\beta\to 0
:
h\to\text{const}
, so
Y_{t}
becomes a Brownian motion. The stretched Schrödinger bridge reduces to a stretched Brownian motion (Bass martingale).

Thus, the Stretched Schrödinger Bridge provides a unified framework that interpolates between the Schrödinger bridge (
\beta=\infty
) and the Bass martingale (
\beta=0
).

7
The SBB System

Combining the above, we obtain the
Schrödinger–Bass Bridge system
. The key observation is that the time-marginals
\mu_{t}
of the optimal process
X
can be expressed using the transport map
\mathcal{X}
:

\boxed{\begin{aligned} &X_{t}\sim\mu_{t},\\
&Y_{t}\sim h(t,\cdot)\nu_{t},\\
&\mu_{t}=\mathcal{X}(t,\cdot)_{\#}(h(t,\cdot)\nu_{t}),\\
&\partial_{t}h+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}h=0,\\
&\partial_{t}\nu-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}\nu=0,\end{aligned}}

(22)

where
\mathcal{X}(t,y)=y+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{y}\log h(t,y)
is the transport map in (
11
), and
\nu
is the density of the Brownian motion starting from some initial distribution
\nu_{0}
, and satisfying the Fokker-Planck equation.

Equivalently, using the inverse map
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}^{-1}
, the system can be written as

\begin{cases}\mathcal{Y}(T,\cdot)_{\#}\mu_{T}=h(T,\cdot)\nu_{T},\\
\mathcal{Y}(0,\cdot)_{\#}\mu_{0}=h(0,\cdot)\nu_{0}.\end{cases}

(23)

This is the
Schrödinger–Bass system
of
[
1
]
, which characterizes the optimal solution through the boundary conditions at times
0
and
T
.

Remark 10

(Interpolation between Sinkhorn and Bass)
.

The SBB system interpolates between two classical algorithms:

Sinkhorn (
\beta\to\infty
):
As
\beta\to\infty
, the maps
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
both converge to the identity:

\mathcal{X}(t,y)=y+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{y}\log h(t,y)\to y,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\to x.

We recover the classical Schrödinger system:

X_{t}\sim\mu_{t},\hskip 10.00002ptY_{t}\sim h(t,\cdot)\nu_{t},\hskip 10.00002pt\mu_{t}=h(t,\cdot)\nu_{t},

with
X_{t}=Y_{t}
.

Bass (
\beta\to 0
):
As
\beta\to 0
, the function
h
tends to a constant (say
h\equiv 1
), and the map
\mathcal{X}
becomes purely determined by the convex potential
u^{*}
:

\mathcal{X}(t,y)=\partial_{y}u^{*}(t,y).

We recover the Bass martingale construction:

X_{t}\sim\mu_{t},\hskip 10.00002ptY_{t}\sim\nu_{t},\hskip 10.00002pt\mu_{t}=(\partial_{y}u^{*}(t,\cdot))_{\#}\nu_{t},

where
X=\partial_{y}u^{*}(Y)
for
Y
Brownian.

8
Sinkhorn-Type Iterations

To solve the SBB system numerically, one needs to enforce the marginal constraints at times
0
and
T
. Using the map
\mathcal{Y}
, the terminal constraint (
23
) becomes

\mathcal{Y}(T,\cdot)_{\#}\mu_{T}=h(T,\cdot)\nu_{T}=e^{\beta w(T,\cdot)}\nu_{T},

(24)

recalling that
h=e^{\beta w}
where
w=u^{*}-y^{2}/2
.

Equivalently, using the inverse map
\mathcal{X}
, we need

\mu_{T}=\mathcal{X}(T,\cdot)_{\#}(e^{\beta w(T,\cdot)}\nu_{T}).

With
u^{*}=y^{2}/2+w
at time
T
, this becomes

\mu_{T}=(\partial_{y}u^{*}(T,\cdot))_{\#}\left(\exp\left(\beta\left(u^{*}(T,\cdot)-\frac{y^{2}}{2}\right)\right)\nu_{T}\right).

This leads to the Monge–Ampère equation

\det D^{2}u^{*}(T,\cdot)=\frac{\nu}{\mu(\partial_{y}u^{*}(T,\cdot))}\exp\left(\beta\left(u^{*}(T,\cdot)-\frac{y^{2}}{2}\right)\right).

(25)

Remark 11

(Structure of the Monge–Ampère equation)
.

The equation (
25
) exhibits a remarkable structure that blends two classical Monge–Ampère theories:

(i) Optimal transport.
The standard Monge–Ampère equation arising in optimal transport with quadratic cost takes the form

\det D^{2}\upsilon=\frac{f}{g(\nabla\upsilon)},

where
f
and
g
are the source and target densities, and
\nabla\upsilon
is the optimal transport map. This equation, studied extensively by Brenier
[
6
]
and Caffarelli
[
7
]
, characterizes the convex potential whose gradient pushes forward
f
to
g
. Regularity theory for such equations, including the Ma–Trudinger–Wang (MTW) curvature conditions, was developed in
[
15
,
14
]
.

(ii) Kähler geometry.
In Kähler geometry and the study of canonical metrics, one encounters Monge–Ampère equations of the form

\det D^{2}\upsilon=e^{f+\lambda\upsilon},

where the right-hand side depends exponentially on the potential
u
itself. The case
\lambda>0
arises in Kähler–Einstein metrics with positive Ricci curvature (Aubin
[
3
]
, Yau
[
17
]
), while the affine sphere equation
\det D^{2}\upsilon=e^{\pm\upsilon}
was studied by Calabi and Cheng–Yau
[
9
]
.

(iii) The SBB equation as a blend.
The Monge–Ampère equation (
25
) combines both structures:

\det D^{2}u^{*}(T,\cdot)=\underbrace{\frac{\nu}{\mu(\partial_{y}u^{*}(T,\cdot))}}_{\text{OT Jacobian}}\times\underbrace{\exp\left(\beta\left(u^{*}(T,\cdot)-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}\right)\right)}_{\text{Entropic/K\"{a}hler weight}}.

The first factor is the classical optimal transport Jacobian, enforcing the pushforward constraint
(\partial_{y}u^{*}(T,\cdot))_{\#}\nu=\mu
. The second factor is an exponential weight depending on the potential
u^{*}(T,\cdot)
itself, akin to the Kähler–Einstein and affine sphere equations. The parameter
\beta
interpolates between these two regimes:

•

As
\beta\to 0
: the exponential weight becomes constant, and we recover the pure optimal transport Monge–Ampère equation.

•

As
\beta\to\infty
: the exponential weight dominates, enforcing
u^{*}(T,\cdot)\approx|y|^{2}/2
(i.e.,
\partial_{y}u^{*}(T,\cdot)\approx\text{Id}
), and the equation degenerates to the Schrödinger system.

This blend suggests that regularity and existence theory for (
25
) may require techniques from both optimal transport (MTW conditions, Caffarelli’s regularity) and Kähler geometry (a priori estimates, continuity methods). We leave the detailed analysis of this equation to future work.

An iterative scheme alternating between:

1.

Updating the spatial map
\mathcal{Y}(0,x)=\arg\min_{y}\left[\log h(0,y)+\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{2}\right]
,

2.

Updating
w(T,\cdot)
via the log-density ratio
w^{k+1}(T,\cdot)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\frac{d(\mathcal{Y}^{k}(T,\cdot)_{\#}\mu_{T})}{d\nu_{T}^{k}}
,

provides a Sinkhorn-like algorithm for computing the SBB solution. The maps
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}
are then recovered from the converged potential via (
9
) and (
11
).
