Glucose Management in Children Living with Type 1 Diabetes: Results from the BETTER Registry
Authors: Virginie Messier, Mélanie Henderson, Meranda Nakhla, Judith Simoneau-Roy, Meryem Talbo, Domitille Dervaux, Nathalie Kinnard, Andréane Vanasse, Monia Rekik, Rémi Rabasa-Lhoret, Anne-Sophie Brazeau, on behalf of the BETTER research group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Behaviors, Therapies, Technologies, and Hypoglycemic Risk in Type 1 Diabetes (BETTER) Registry collects patient-reported outcomes and experiences on individuals living with type 1 diabetes in Canada. The objective of our study was to describe the management of type 1 diabetes reported by parents of children and young adolescents (<14 years of age) enrolled in the BETTER registry.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional evaluation using self-reported data collected from April 2019 to October 2024. Parents of children and adolescents <14 years self-registered and provided online informed consent. As of October 2024, 699 children and adolescents <14 years of age and living with type 1 diabetes were enrolled.ResultsChildren had a mean age of 8.8 ± 3.1 years old with a diabetes duration of 2.3 ± 2.7 years, 44.9% were female, and 85.0% were of European ancestry. More than 80% used a continuous glucose monitoring system and 38.3% used an insulin pump. Most recent HbA1c ≤7% was reported by 15.9% of participants. At least 1 episode of level 2 hypoglycemia (glucose levels <3.0 mmol/L, with ability to self-treat) in the last month was reported by 72.5% of participants, with a median number of episodes (interquartile range) of 5 (2, 10). The occurrence of level 3 hypoglycemia (low glucose, requiring help from another person, use of glucagon, hospitalization, or loss of consciousness) in the last 12 months was reported by 8.6% of participants. Among these, the median number of episodes was 1 (1, 3).ConclusionOur results indicate that, despite a wide use of continuous glucose monitoring, the occurrence of hypoglycemia is still high and the proportion of children and adolescents reaching the HbA1c target is low.

Introduction

In 2025, around 1.8 million children and adolescents were living with type 1 diabetes worldwide and more than 50% were under 15 years of age [1]. Management of type 1 diabetes in children poses many challenges as they are generally dependent on parents or caregivers for their diabetes care, have unpredictable diet/appetite and changing food preferences, irregular physical activity patterns and have difficulty in describing and communicating their hypoglycemic symptoms [2, 3]. Adolescence can also be a challenging time for youth living with type 1 diabetes due to hormonal changes, progressive decrease in parental supervision/involvement, multiple competing responsibilities, and peer interactions [4]. While achieving blood glucose targets is important to reduce long-term complications, hypoglycemia is the most common complication of insulin therapy and is an important barrier to good glycemic management [5]. It was previously reported that children and adolescents experienced level 1 or 2 hypoglycemia daily with a mean of 1.5 hypoglycemic episodes per day [6]. Level 3 hypoglycemia, defined as a hypoglycemic event requiring third-party assistance, may cause permanent brain damage, cognitive impairment and brain-structural abnormalities particularly in young children living with type 1 diabetes [7–10]. A systematic review revealed that in 2011–2016, the level 3 hypoglycemia incidence range was 1.2–30 episodes per 100 patient-year [11]. Nocturnal hypoglycemia, defined as a hypoglycemic episode happening while the individual is asleep at night, occurs in 15–25% of nights [12, 13]. Nocturnal hypoglycemia can lead to anxiety and emotional stress, interfere with sleep and decrease quality of life in family members [14].

To reduce chronic complications, Diabetes Canada and the International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes recommends that the target HbA1c should be ≤7.0% for children and adolescents [15, 16]. In recent decades, steady improvements in HbA1c levels have been shown, yet it was previously reported that only 18% of children attained glycemic targets [17]. Observed improvements can be attributed to many factors such as healthcare teams dedicated to type 1 diabetes, improved treatments (insulin analogs) and technologies (continuous glucose monitoring, insulin pumps), and more recently, automated insulin delivery (AID) systems [18].

Over the last 30 years, many pediatric diabetes registries were established [19] such as the Diabetes prospective follow-up registry (DPV) [20], the T1D Exchange registry [21] and the SWEET initiative [22]. These registries are mainly using data collected by healthcare teams through medical records. On our end, a patient-oriented research approach was used for the development of the Behaviors, Therapies, Technologies and Hypoglycemic Risk in Type 1 Diabetes (BETTER) Registry. Recognizing the value of patients lived experiences and conducting research that focuses on patient-identified priorities were key principles in the development of the BETTER registry. A group of patients-partners, composed of people living with type 1 diabetes and parents of child/children living with type 1 diabetes, guided the research team in determining the research objectives of the BETTER registry and in the development and revision of patient-reported outcomes collected. The BETTER registry, launched in 2019, is the first Canadian registry to prospectively collect patient-reported outcomes on the management of type 1 diabetes and hypoglycemia episodes [23]. The objective of our study was to describe the management of type 1 diabetes and the occurrence of hypoglycemia in children and young adolescents (<14 years of age) enrolled in the BETTER registry, according to different development ages (0–5, 6–11, 12–13 years), as of October 2024.

Methods

Registry

To enroll in the BETTER registry, individuals must self-report a clinical diagnosis of type 1 diabetes, provide a valid address in Canada and be able to read French or English. Parents of children and adolescents <14 years self-registered and provided online informed consent. Recruitment relies on various means. In partner hospital centers or medical clinics, (1) potential participants may be approached by a research assistant during their follow-up appointment and/or (2) flyers available in waiting rooms, and/or (3) letters or emails sent as invitations to enroll in the registry. Recruitment also relies on social media, (4) by collaborating with different associations such as Diabète Québec, Diabetes Canada and Breakthrough T1D Canada to promote enrollment or (5) directly on Facebook groups for people living with type 1 diabetes. Other means used include (6) networks of healthcare professionals, (7) presentation of the BETTER registry during conferences/seminars for healthcare professionals to incite them to invite their patients to participate, (8) promotion during events for patients (e.g., BT1D Walk to Cure Diabetes), (9) study website (https://type1better.com/en/home/), (10) Connect1d platform (connect1d.ca), and (11) patient-partners advocacy within the community. Recruitment is still ongoing across Canada.

Variables

The socio-economic status of the parent completing the questionnaire was evaluated using questions on the highest degree of education completed, the household income, the employment status and type of health insurance coverage. Socio-demographic characteristics specific to children and adolescents included age, biological sex, gender identity, and ethnicity. Diabetes duration, most recent HbA1c range level (≤7.0%, 7.1–8.0%, 8.1–9.0%, ≥9.1%), insulin therapy, glucose monitoring and type of healthcare providers consulted for diabetes were reported. History of hypoglycemia included (1) level 2 hypoglycemia (glucose levels <3.0 mmol/L, with ability to self-treat), (2) level 3 hypoglycemia, defined as low glucose, requiring help from another person, use of glucagon, hospitalization, or loss of consciousness. Parents were asked about glucagon usage (injectable and nasal) over the last 12 months and reasons to use an insulin pump and/or continuous glucose monitoring system. Parent’s self-perceived confidence to calculate carbohydrates and to administer insulin and glucagon was assessed on a scale from 0 (not confident at all) to 10 (totally confident).

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were tabulated according to age groups (0–5 years, 6–11 years, 12–13 years). Continuous variables are described with mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) whereas categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. A nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis H test with multiple pairwise comparisons using Dunn's procedure was used to compare continuous variables between age groups. For categorical variables, chi-squared (χ2) test of homogeneity was used to determine the differences in characteristics (socioeconomic data, diabetes related characteristics, reasons to use an insulin pump and/or continuous glucose monitoring system) compared across the age groups, followed by a post hoc pairwise comparison using a z-test of two proportions with a Bonferroni correction to correct for multiple comparisons. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL).

Results

The data presented in this study were collected between February 2019 and October 2024. Sociodemographic characteristics of the 699 children living with type 1 diabetes enrolled in the BETTER registry during that period are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the children was 8.8 ± 3.1 years, with 60.2% being aged between 6 and 11 years; 44.9% identified as female and 85.0% were of European ancestry. More than half of the parents had a university degree; 70.1% worked full time, 52.1% had an annual household income above 100,000 CAN$; 74.8% had private medical insurance. About 11% of the parents reported that one parent was diagnosed with depression in the last year.

Parents’ confidence to perform carbohydrate counting, administer insulin and glucagon is shown in Table 2 and Figure 1. Detailed scores are presented in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/hrp/adaag016). Mean scores of 8.9 ± 1.4 and 8.0 ± 1.8 were observed for parent’s confidence to administer insulin and to perform carbohydrate counting, respectively, with no significant difference between age groups. However, parents of all age groups were less confident to administer injectable glucagon (6.0 ± 3.0). As for administration of nasal glucagon, only scores of parents of children aged ≥6 years old were considered as nasal glucagon is approved for children aged ≥4 years old. Mean scores were 7.6 ± 2.7 in the 6–11 age group and 7.7 ± 2.6 in the 12–13 age group with no significant difference between the groups.

Parent’s confidence to administer insulin (a), to perform carbohydrate counting (b), to administer injectable glucagon (c), and to administer nasal glucagon (d).

The use of insulin pump therapy increased with age; 31.1% of children aged 0–5 years, 38.7% of children aged 6–11 and 42.8% of children aged 12–13, but there was no significant difference between age groups. Tubeless insulin pump was used by 59.8% of the children (online suppl. Fig. 1). Main reasons to choose insulin pump therapy were to have more flexibility in treatment (75.0–87.9% of children), manage meals more easily (75.0–81.8% of children) and simplify glucose management during physical activity (54.5–70.2% of children) (Fig. 2). AID use was assessed in a subsample of 120 children using insulin pump therapy. Among those, 63 (52.5%) used an AID system. Real time continuous glucose monitoring (rtCGM) or intermittently scanned continuous glucose monitoring (isCGM) in the last year was used by more than 80% of the children in all age groups. Main reasons to choose continuous glucose monitoring were to have the possibility to track glucose levels easily (78.2–91.8% of children), to reduce the number of capillary blood glucose measurements or to not have to check capillary blood glucose (71.8–80.4% of children) and to simplify glucose management during physical activity (65.5–76.6% of children) (Fig. 3). We also observed that continuous glucose monitoring was chosen in a greater proportion of children aged 0–5 years old because of nocturnal hypoglycemia (62.5%) or difficulties to express hypoglycemia symptoms and possible impaired awareness of hypoglycemia (53.1%) compared to other age groups (p < 0.05). Among children not using a CGM, almost all of them reported measuring capillary blood glucose 4 times and more per day. Carbohydrate counting was used by more than 75% of the participants; we observed that the use of carbohydrate counting was less reported in children aged 0–5 years (68.1%) as compared to other age groups, but this difference was not statistically significant. Only 38.7% of children in the 0–5 age group wore a medical identification for diabetes. This was significantly different from the 6–11 (53.4%) and 12–13 (55.3%) age groups (p = 0.022). Between 83.0% and 84.6% of children had seen an endocrinologist in the last year for a diabetes follow-up appointment with no significant difference between age groups.

Discussion

In this first of its kind registry-based analysis describing lived experience of Canadian children living with type 1 diabetes and their caregivers, our results show that management of type 1 diabetes continues to be challenging for this population as only 16% of the youth reached the recommended glycemic targets, 4 in 5 report hypoglycemia, and the use of insulin pump/AID is low. Noteworthy, depression was reported in 11% of the parents in the last year which is higher than what is observed in the Canadian population [24].

In the past decade, there have been considerable advances in the management of type 1 diabetes, including the increased use of diabetes technologies such as CGM systems and insulin pumps, and more recently, the development of AID systems. Numerous studies have shown that the use of diabetes technologies is associated with improved glucose management and increased quality of life [25–29]. For example, a systematic review and meta-analysis showed that use of AID was associated with increased time spent in the target range, a reduced time spent in hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia and a reduction in HbA1c [30]. In our study, we observed that only 16% of children (age <14 years old) achieved the recommended A1c level of ≤7% even though close to 40% of children used insulin pump therapy and more than 80% used a continuous glucose monitoring system. Similarly, Demeterco-Berggren et al. [28] showed that among 25,383 children and adolescents (up to 18 years of age) included in the T1D Exchange Quality Improvement Collaborative (T1DX-QI) between 2017 and 2022, only 18% had an HbA1c below 7%. More recently, using data from eight national and one international pediatric diabetes registries (<18 years old), Zimmermann et al. [31] reported that, in 2022, 38.8% of participants achieved the target HbA1c of less than 7% while 13.1% of participants had an HbA1c higher than 9%. The relatively high proportion of participants achieving the recommended HbA1c target in the Zimmerman study (nearly 40%) may be partly explained by the high prevalence of insulin pump use, reported in 60% of participants. In addition, other factors such as the socioeconomic status and the structure of diabetes care (hospital-based vs. private practice) could explain the observed variation in the proportion of participants with an HbA1c ≤7% [32, 33].

Even though not statistically significant, we observed that the lowest proportion of children with a HbA1c ≤7% were aged 12–13 years. This aligns with previous reports from the T1D Exchange registry showing that only 17% of adolescents 13–17 years of age met the ADA recommendation [34]. Clements et al. [35] observed that HbA1c levels remained relatively stable from age 8–10 and increased over time until 16–17 years of age. These results highlight the challenges associated with increased autonomy in diabetes management during adolescence as well as the psychosocial and hormonal changes occurring during this period of life [36, 37].

Continuous glucose monitoring systems have advanced and become the standard of care for type 1 diabetes, particularly for children, adolescents, and young adults [26]. We observed across all age groups that more than 80% of children reported to use a CGM. Similarly, Zimmermann et al. [31] reported that, in 2022, more than 80% of participants were using a CGM in several pediatric diabetes registries: Norwegian Childhood Diabetes Registry, the Danish Registry of Childhood and Adolescent Diabetes, the Swedish Childhood Diabetes Registry, the Czech National Childhood Diabetes Register and Diabetes Prospective Follow-up Registry. With growing insurance coverage and ongoing technological improvements, CGM systems are expected to be used by the vast majority of children and adolescents with T1D in Canada.

Both Diabetes Canada and ISPAD state that AID systems should be the preferred treatment method for all individuals living with type 1 diabetes [16, 25]. In our cohort, use of AID was only assessed for a subgroup of children using insulin pump therapy, and we observed that 52.5% of them were using AID. In addition, we were surprised to find that only 38.3% of children in our cohort reported using insulin pump therapy, with no significant difference between age groups. We would await a greater proportion of participants using insulin pump since insulin pump and supplies are in most cases covered by the Canadian public insurance for children and adolescents across the country [38]. The T1D Exchange Quality Improvement Collaborative reported that, in 2021, insulin pump was used by 66% of their participants [39]. However, this proportion is probably not representative of the overall population living with type 1 diabetes in the United States as this initiative primarily consists of clinics in academic settings in large urban areas [39]. Even though diabetes registry data has demonstrated increased uptake of insulin pump therapy in youth, there is still potential barriers to its use such as concerns regarding the pump’s visibility on the body, concerns about having a device attached to the body, interference of the pump in some activities [40, 41].

In our study, level 3 hypoglycemia was defined as low blood glucose levels requiring help from another person or use of glucagon or hospitalization or loss of consciousness. Our finding that overall frequency of level 3 hypoglycemia in the last 12 months was 8.4% is difficult to compare to previous studies. This is mostly due to (a) the population studied – in some studies, data from children, adolescents and adults were not presented separately. For example, using a similar level 3 hypoglycemia definition as ours, data from the Diabetes Prospective Follow-up registry indicate that the frequency of level 3 hypoglycemia was 6.0% in 96,547 patients aged ≥1.5 years [42]; (b) how the data is reported – other studies have reported either the level 3 hypoglycemia rate as number of events per 100 person-years or report frequency of events over a different time period (e.g., over a year vs. the last 3 months). For example, Foster et al. [36] reported that, in 2016–2018, 5% of T1D Exchange participants aged 6–12 years experienced seizure or loss of consciousness due to hypoglycemia in the last 3 months while Zimmermann et al. [31] reported that the event rate in 2022 was 1.7 events per 100 person-years. Limited data is available regarding the frequency of level 2 hypoglycemia. We observed that a median number of 5 episodes occurs each month. According to ISPAD, mild hypoglycemia is common, and symptomatic hypoglycemia happens on an average of two episodes per week [43]. Hill et al. [6] used CGM data to identify hypoglycemic episodes in 214 children and adolescents. They reported that, on average, there were 1.5 hypoglycemic events per day out of which 1.2 were level 1 and 0.3 were level 2.

A diagnosis of type 1 diabetes in a child can be highly stressful for parents, who often have no prior knowledge of the disease. Effective management requires parents to quickly acquire skills in insulin administration, carbohydrate counting, and the recognition and treatment of glycemic events, including severe hypoglycemia requiring glucagon. We observed that parents across all groups were confident to administer insulin and to perform carbohydrate counting. Among all parents, the confidence to administer injectable glucagon averaged a score of 6 out of 10. In a study by Muradoglu et al. [44], parents evaluated their competence in injectable glucagon administration as 6 out of 10. In addition, anxiety related to glucagon administration was negatively associated to confidence to administer glucagon. We have previously shown that parents and school workers administered intranasal glucagon faster than injectable glucagon and that nasal glucagon was preferred for ease of use and acceptability [45]. As injectable glucagon still needs to be used for children aged <4 years, it may be beneficial to review glucagon administration on an annual basis until intranasal glucagon can be used.

We acknowledge that due to the voluntary participation and self-initiated nature of data collection, parents included in this study might not be representative of all Canadian parents of a child living with type 1 diabetes. In addition, our study population is composed of families with high educational attainment and household income which limit the generalizability of our results. Efforts to diversity recruitment strategies to reach ethnic minorities and families with a lower socio-economic status are ongoing. For example, the first questionnaire of the BETTER registry was translated into 3 languages in addition to French and English. We are also opening new recruitment sites in hospital centers located in areas where more diversity at various levels is expected. Finally, the Canadian population is not represented equally across provinces, and it should be noted that access to diabetes technologies varies from a province to another.

Our results indicate that, despite a wide use of continuous glucose monitoring, the occurrence of hypoglycemia is still high and the proportion of children and adolescents reaching the HbA1c target is low. It suggests that additional resources, such as use of insulin pump but also means of increasing confidence with diabetes management, are needed to help patients, caregivers and healthcare professionals to lower HbA1c level closer to the target recommended.
