Brockett cost function for symplectic eigenvalues
Nguyen Thanh Son

Abstract
The symplectic eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors of symmetric positive-definite matrices in the sense of Williamson’s theorem can be computed via minimization of a trace cost function under the symplecticity constraint. The optimal solution to this problem only offers a symplectic basis for a symplectic eigenspace corresponding to the sought symplectic eigenvalues. In this paper, we introduce a Brockett cost function and investigate the connection between its properties and the symplectic eigenvalues and eigenvectors, specifically prove that any critical point consists of symplectic eigenvectors. Surprisingly, the trace minimization theorem for the symplectic eigenvalues can be deduced from our results.



keywords

Brockett cost function, symplectic eigenpair, Williamson’s diagonal form, trace minimization

Introduction

In his classic work
[
32
]
, Williamson discovered that for any symmetric positive-definite (spd) matrix
M\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}}
, there exists a symplectic matrix
S\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}}
which diagonalizes
M
in the sense

S^{T}MS=\begin{bmatrix}D&0\\
0&D\end{bmatrix},

(1)

where
D=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
with
d_{j}>0,j=1,\ldots,n.
The decomposition (
1
) looks quite like the standard eigenvalue decomposition for real symmetric matrices, see, e.g.,
[
16
, Thm. 8.1.1]
, except for the special form on the right-hand side, which will be referred to as
Williamson’s diagonal form
, and the fact that
S
is, instead of an orthogonal matrix, a
symplectic matrix
, i.e., it verifies

S^{T}J_{2n}S=J_{2n},

(2)

where
J_{2n}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. Note that the matrix
J_{2n}
is itself a symplectic matrix and the following equalities additionally hold:

J_{2n}^{T}=-J_{2n},\ J_{2n}^{T}J_{2n}=I_{2n},\ J_{2n}^{2}=-I_{2n},\ J_{2n}^{-1}=J_{2n}^{T}.

It is straightforward from (
2
) that
S^{-T}=J_{2n}SJ_{2n}^{T}
and by multiplying this matrix to the left of (
1
), we obtain

MS=J_{2n}S\begin{bmatrix}0&-D\\
D&0\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

Let us write the columns of
S
as
S=[s_{1},\ldots,s_{n},s_{n+1},\ldots,s_{2n}]
. The relation (
3
) implies that for any ordered vector pair
[s_{j},s_{n+j}]
,

M[s_{j},s_{n+j}]=J_{2n}[s_{j},s_{n+j}]\begin{bmatrix}0&-d_{j}\\
d_{j}&0\end{bmatrix},\ j=1,\ldots,n

which most probably suggests the name
symplectic eigenvalue
for
d_{j}
and
symplectic eigenvectors
associated with
d_{j}
for the vector pair
[s_{j},s_{n+j}]
(c.f.
Mx=I_{2n}x\lambda
when
(\lambda,x)
is an eigenpair of
M
). More generally, denote by
\mathcal{I}_{k}=[i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}]
a
k
-tuple of indices in the index set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}=\mathrm{diag}(d_{i_{1}},\ldots,d_{i_{k}})
,
S_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}=[s_{i_{1}},\ldots,s_{i_{k}},s_{n+i_{1}},\ldots,s_{n+i_{k}}]
, a symplectic eigenvalue set,
we derive from (
3
) the relation

MS_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}=J_{2n}S_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}\begin{bmatrix}0&-D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}\\
D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

It is worth to note that the symplectic eigenvalues and eigenvectors are not the conventional eigenvalues and eigenvectors, although some connections between them can be established
[
12
,
19
,
29
]
.

Symplectic eigenvalues are involved in the study of optics
[
13
]
, quantum mechanics
[
22
]
, quantum computing
[
4
]
, quantum information/communication
[
2
,
31
,
11
]
, and learning linear port-Hamiltonian systems through their normal forms
[
28
]
. Using the connection between the symplectic eigenvalues of
M
and the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian matrix
J_{2n}M
, see, e.g.,
[
29
, Prop. 3.2]
, they can be used for stability analysis of weakly damped gyroscopic systems
[
5
]
.

In principle, to compute the symplectic eigenvalues and their associated eigenvectors of
M
, one can compute the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian matrix
J_{2n}M

[
3
]
or apply a standard eigensolver to the Hermitian pencil
M-\lambda\mathrm{i}J_{2n}
. However, the Hamiltonian structure, which results in special properties of its eigenvalues
[
26
]
, requires careful treatment
[
5
]
or the computation error might lead to complex values for the Hermitian pencil. Moreover, in some cases, the solver does not fully reveal repeated eigenvalues. In
[
29
]
,
inspired by the trace minimization theorem established in
[
18
,
7
]
,
the authors addressed computing the smallest
k
symplectic eigenvalues of given a
2n\times 2n
spd matrix
M
via minimizing a trace cost function under the symplecticity constraint,
1\leq k\leq n
. Namely, it seeks a solution to

\min\limits_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}\mathrm{tr}(X^{T}MX)\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad X^{T}J_{2n}X=J_{2k}.

(5)

Here, the rectangular matrix
X
satisfying the constraint is, similar to (
2
), also referred to as a symplectic matrix. Based on the achieved properties of the minimization problem (
5
) and the trace minimization theorem, which states that its global minimal value is twice the sum of the smallest
k
symplectic eigenvalues of
M
, they proposed a two-step algorithm to compute the symplectic eigenpairs. In the first step, a minimizer
X_{\min}
of (
5
) is sought. In the second step,
X_{\min}^{T}MX_{\min}
is diagonalized in which the obtained diagonal elements are the expected symplectic eigenvalues and the matrix product
X_{\min}X_{\mathrm{diag}}
, where
X_{\mathrm{diag}}
diagonalizes
X_{\min}^{T}MX_{\min}
, is the associated symplectic eigenvectors. More steps most probably result in a larger error. One wishes to have the symplectic eigenvectors and eigenvalues right after the optimization step. Unfortunately, the trace minimization solution only offers a symplectic basis as the columns of
X_{\min}
for the subspace corresponding to the smallest
k
symplectic eigenvalues.

For eigenvalues, this issue is dissolved by replacing the cost function
\mathrm{tr}(X^{T}MX)
by the Brockett cost function
\mathrm{tr}(NX^{T}MX)
on the set of matrices with orthonormal columns, where
N
is a square diagonal matrix
[
9
,
10
,
1
,
8
]
. This function can appear in a more general form. For instance, the case that
N
is a symmetric matrix was considered in
[
25
]
as a typical quadratic function. In
[
24
,
23
]
, the authors even went further by investigating the case when the orthogonality constraint
X^{T}X=I
was replaced by
X^{T}BX=J
with both
N
and
B
can be indefinite and
J=\mathrm{diag}(\pm 1,\ldots,\pm 1)
. Building on these results, in this paper, we will construct a Brockett cost function and investigate its optimization under the symplecticity constraint for the purpose of computing symplectic eigenpairs. With this in mind, we will not manage to find the most general function but construct a simple one that enables us to reach the goal. After the construction of the cost function, our main results include (i) a proof that any critical point of the optimization consists of symplectic eigenvalues, (ii) an explicit formula for the global minimal value of the Brockett cost function which interestingly allows us to re-establish the trace minimization theorem for symplectic eigenvalues mentioned above, and (iii) a sufficient condition for being a saddle point.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We first state some notations and recall the necessary background on the topic in section
2
. The main result will be presented in section
3
. Some final remarks are given in section
4
.

2
Notations and preliminaries

In this section, after stating some conventions for notation, we present related materials for later discussion.

For given integers
k,n,1\leq k\leq n
, we denote by
{\mathrm{Sp}(2k,2n)}
(
{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}
in case of
k=n
),
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
,
\mathrm{Skew}(n)
,
\mathrm{SPD}(n)
the set of
2n\times 2k
symplectic,
n\times n
symmetric,
n\times n
skew-symmetric, and
n\times n
spd matrices, respectively. Meanwhile,
\mathrm{tr}(\cdot)
,
\cdot^{T}
, and
\mbox{null}(\cdot)
stand for the trace, the transpose, and the kernel of a matrix or linear operator, respectively. Moreover, we write
\mathrm{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to mean the diagonal matrix with the entries
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
on the diagonal.
This notation is also used for block diagonal matrices, where each
a_{i}
is a submatrix block. For a twice continuously differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\nabla f(X)
and
\nabla^{2}f(X)
, respectively, the Euclidean gradient and the Hessian of
f
at
X
. Moreover,
\mathrm{D}h(X)
stands for the Fréchet derivative at
X
of a mapping
h
between Banach spaces. Conventionally,
{\rm i}=\sqrt{-1}
denotes the imaginary unit.

The diagonalizing matrix
S
in Williamson’s decomposition (
1
) is not unique. One is referred to
[
29
, Thm. 3.5]
for a full description of the family of diagonalizers. Thus, an argument involving
S
or a part of it holds for any element of this family.

A well-known result on the trace minimization for symplectic eigenvalues of an spd matrix is given in the following.

Theorem 1
.

[
18
,
7
]

Let a matrix
M\in\mathrm{SPD}(2n)
have symplectic eigenvalues
0<d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}
. Then for any integer
k
,
1\leq k\leq n
, it holds

2\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}=\min\limits_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}\mathrm{tr}(X^{T}MX)\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad X^{T}J_{2n}X=J_{2k}.

(6)

Next, we collect some technical facts.

Lemma 2
.

The following statements hold.

i)

Let
W\in\mathrm{Skew}(2k)
and
R=\mathrm{diag}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{2k}),r_{j}\not=0,
for
j=1,\ldots,2k
, such that

WR=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-\Lambda\\
\Lambda&\hskip 4.09723pt0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
,
where
\Lambda
is a nonsingular diagonal matrix.
Then
r_{j}=r_{j+k}
for all
j=1,\ldots,k
.

ii)

If a matrix
A
commutes with a diagonal matrix
R
with mutually distinct diagonal entries, then
A
is also diagonal.

Proof.

By assumption, if we write
W=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}W_{1}&W_{2}\\
-W_{2}^{T}&W_{3}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
,
W_{1}^{T}=-W_{1},W_{3}^{T}=-W_{3}
,
R_{1}=\mathrm{diag}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})
, and
R_{2}=\mathrm{diag}(r_{k+1},\ldots,r_{2k})
, then we obtain immediately
W_{2}R_{2}=R_{1}W_{2}=-\Lambda
. Together with the nonsingularity of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, these equalities first imply that
W_{2}
is also a diagonal matrix. And finally, if
\Lambda
is nonsingular, then
R_{1}=R_{2}
.

For the second statement, let us write
A=(a_{i,j})
and
R=\mathrm{diag}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
with
r_{i}\not=r_{j}
for
i\not=j,i,j=1,\ldots,m
. The assumption in the second statement implies that
a_{i,j}(r_{i}-r_{j})=0
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,m
. The conclusion is deduced from the mutual distinctness of the diagonal elements of
R
.

3
Main results

In this section, we first construct the Brockett cost function for symplectic eigenvalues and then study in detail its critical points, minimizers, and saddle points.

It has been pointed out that a solution to the minimization problem (
6
) provides a basis for the subspace associated with the smallest
k
symplectic eigenvalues
[
29
, Thm. 4.6]
, not the symplectic eigenvectors themselves. The main reason is that all terms in the trace in (
6
) are equally involved.
As a remedy to this, we consider minimizing the cost function

\min\limits_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}f(X):=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{N}X^{T}MX)\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad h(X):=X^{T}J_{2n}X-J_{2k}=0,

(7)

where
\tilde{N}
is a nonsingular diagonal matrix whose nonzero entries will be specified now. To ease the presentation, the diagonal entries of
\tilde{N}
are restricted to be positive. As (
7
) is an optimization problem under equality constraint of which the linear independence constraint qualification holds at any point in the feasible set
[
14
]
, we proceed with the associated Lagrangian function

{\cal L}(X,L)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\tilde{N}X^{T}MX\right)-\mathrm{tr}\left(L\left(X^{T}J_{2n}X-J_{2k}\right)\right),

where
L\in\mathrm{Skew}(2k)
is the associated Lagrange multiplier. The gradient of
{\cal L}
with respect to the variable
X
at
(X,L)
takes the form

\nabla_{X}{\cal L}(X,L)=2MX\tilde{N}-2J_{2n}XL.

Furthermore, the action of the Hessian of
{\cal L}
with respect to the first argument on
(Z,Z)\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}
reads

\nabla^{2}_{XX}{\cal L}(X,L)[Z,Z]=2\,\mathrm{tr}\left(Z^{T}(MZ\tilde{N}-J_{2n}ZL)\right).

Next, let us recall the first- and second-order necessary optimality conditions
[
6
, Prop. 3.1.1]
for the constrained optimization problem (
7
). A point
X_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}
is said to satisfy the
first-order necessary optimality condition
, also referred to as
a
critical point
of the problem (
7
), if
h(X_{*})=0
and there exists
a Lagrange multiplier
L_{*}\in\mathrm{Skew}(2k)
such that
\nabla_{X}{\cal L}(X_{*},L_{*})=0
or equivalently
X_{*}^{T}J_{2n}X_{*}=J_{2k}
and

MX_{*}\tilde{N}=J_{2n}X_{*}L_{*}.

(8)

The critical point
X_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}
with the associated Lagrange multiplier
L_{*}
is said to satisfy the
second-order necessary optimality condition
if

\nabla^{2}_{XX}{\cal L}(X_{*},L_{*})[Z,Z]=2\,\mathrm{tr}\bigl(Z^{T}(MZ\tilde{N}-J_{2n}ZL_{*})\bigr)\geq 0

(9)

for all
Z\!\in\mbox{null}\bigl(\mathrm{D}h(X_{*})\bigr)\!:=\!\{Y\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}:\mathrm{D}h(X_{*})[Y]=Y^{T}\!J_{2n}X_{*}\!+\!X_{*}^{T}J_{2n}Y\!=0\}
.

The condition (
8
) can be
equivalently written as

MX_{*}=J_{2n}X_{*}L_{*}\tilde{N}^{-1}.

(10)

We want that each critical point is also a symplectic eigenvector set, i.e., (
4
) holds for
X_{*}
for some index subset
\mathcal{I}_{k}
. Equating its right-hand side with that of (
10
) and multiplying the derived equality from the left with
J_{2k}^{T}X_{*}^{T}
,
we arrive at the condition

L_{*}\tilde{N}^{-1}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}\\
D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right].
In view of Lemma
2
(i),
\tilde{N}^{-1}
and therefore
\tilde{N}
must take the form

\tilde{N}=\mathrm{diag}(N,N),\ N=\mathrm{diag}(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{k}),\ \nu_{i}\not=\nu_{j}\mbox{ for }i\not=j,\ i,j=1,\ldots,k.

(11)

This observation suggests that (
11
) is a necessary condition for a critical point of (
7
) to be a symplectic eigenvector set. We will show in Theorem
6
that it is indeed a sufficient condition, too. Therefore, from now on, we consider the cost function

\min\limits_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}f(X):=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{N}X^{T}MX)\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad h(X):=X^{T}J_{2n}X-J_{2k}=0,

(12)

where
\tilde{N}
is of the form (
11
), and mention it as the
Brockett cost function
for the symplectic eigenvalues. Certainly, the order of the diagonal entries in
N
can be arbitrary. For the sake of convenience, we will arrange them in increasing order, i.e.,

0<\nu_{1}<\cdots<\nu_{k}.

In
[
29
]
, orthosymplectic matrices–those are both orthogonal and symplectic–play an important role in computing the symplectic eigenvalues in the optimization framework (
6
). In this paper, to treat the Brockett cost function, we introduce a specialized type of orthosymplectic matrices. Indeed, we define

\mathrm{OrSp}_{Q}(2k)=\{K\in{\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}}\ :\ K^{T}J_{2k}K=J_{2k},\ K^{T}QK=Q\}.

For the special case
Q=\tilde{N}
defined in (
11
), we characterize the elements of
\mathrm{OrSp}_{\tilde{N}}(2k)
in the following.

Lemma 3
.

It holds that

\mathrm{OrSp}_{\tilde{N}}(2k)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(\cos(\mathbf{\Phi}))&\mathrm{diag}(\sin(\mathbf{\Phi}))\\
-\mathrm{diag}(\sin(\mathbf{\Phi}))&\mathrm{diag}(\cos(\mathbf{\Phi}))\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{\Phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\right\}.

(13)

Proof.

From
K^{T}\tilde{N}K=\tilde{N}
, it holds that
\tilde{N}K\tilde{N}^{-1}=K^{-T}
which, with the symplecticity of
K
, implies that

J_{2k}K=\tilde{N}K\tilde{N}^{-1}J_{2k}.

(14)

Let
K
be decomposed into four
k\times k
blocks as
K=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}K_{1}&K_{2}\\
K_{3}&K_{4}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Taking (
14
) and (
11
) into account, we deduce that

K_{3}=-NK_{2}N^{-1},\ K_{4}=NK_{1}N^{-1},\ K_{1}=NK_{4}N^{-1},\ \mbox{and }K_{2}=-NK_{3}N^{-1}.

The second and third equalities yield that
K_{4}N^{2}=N^{2}K_{4}
. In view of Lemma
2
(ii),
K_{4}
is diagonal which immediately implies the same thing for
K_{1}
. Similar arguments lead to the same fact for
K_{2}
and
K_{3}
. Finally, this diagonal pattern together with the symplecticity and weighted orthogonality leads to the form announced in (
13
). Conversely, it is direct to show that each matrix
K
of the form (
13
) verifies the definition of
\mathrm{OrSp}_{\tilde{N}}(2k)
and therefore the proof is complete.

Remark 4
.

Using the explicit form (
13
), it is obvious that
K\in\mathrm{OrSp}_{\tilde{N}}(2k)
if and only if
K^{T}\in\mathrm{OrSp}_{\tilde{N}}(2k)
;
specifically
K\tilde{N}K^{T}=\tilde{N}
. Hence, it holds for any
X\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2k,2n)},K\in\mathrm{OrSp}_{\tilde{N}}(2k)
that

f(XK)=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{N}K^{T}X^{T}MXK)=\mathrm{tr}(K\tilde{N}K^{T}X^{T}MX)=\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{N}X^{T}MX)=f(X).

That is to say, the Brockett cost function
f
in (
12
) is homogeneous on the set
\mathrm{OrSp}_{\tilde{N}}(2k)
.

Next is a property of critical points.

Proposition 5
.

If
X_{*}
is a critical point of the minimization problem (
7
). Then, for any
K\in\mathrm{OrSp}_{\tilde{N}}(2k)
,

X_{*}K
is also a critical point.

Proof.

First, it is straightforward to check that
X_{*}K\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2k,2n)}
if
X_{*}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2k,2n)}
and
K\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2k)}
. Then, by assumption and Remark
4
, it holds that

MX_{*}K\tilde{N}K^{T}=MX_{*}\tilde{N}=J_{2n}X_{*}L_{*}=J_{2n}X_{*}KK^{-1}L_{*}

for some
L_{*}\in\mathrm{Skew}(2k)
. This leads to
MX_{*}K\tilde{N}=J_{2n}X_{*}KK^{-1}L_{*}K^{-T}
which means that
X_{*}K
is a critical point associated with the Lagrange multiplier
K^{-1}L_{*}K^{-T}
.

Now, we state the main result.

Theorem 6
.

The matrix
X\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}
is a critical point of the minimization problem (
12
) if and only if its columns is a symplectic eigenvector set of
M
.

Proof.

Assume
X_{*}
is any critical point of (
12
) with the associated Lagrange multiplier
L_{*}
. From
(
8
), we deduce that

L_{*}=J_{2k}^{T}X_{*}^{T}MX_{*}\tilde{N}.

(15)

The skew-symmetry of
L_{*}
immediately leads to

J_{2k}X_{*}^{T}MX_{*}\tilde{N}=\tilde{N}X_{*}^{T}MX_{*}J_{2k}.

(16)

Since
X_{*}^{T}MX_{*}
is symmetric, it can be expressed as

X_{*}^{T}MX_{*}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}\\
S_{2}^{T}&S_{3}\end{bmatrix}\quad\mbox{with}\quad S_{1},S_{3}\in\mathrm{Sym}(k).

(17)

It follows from (
11
), (
16
), and (
17
) that

\begin{bmatrix}S_{2}^{T}N&S_{3}N\\
-S_{1}N&-S_{2}N\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-NS_{2}&NS_{1}\\
-NS_{3}&NS_{2}^{T}\end{bmatrix}.

This equality is exploited as follows. On the one hand,
S_{2}N=-NS_{2}^{T}
and
S_{2}^{T}N=-NS_{2}
together result in
S_{2}N^{2}=N^{2}S_{2}
. From Lemma
2
(ii),
S_{2}
is diagonal which additionally yields
S_{2}=0
. Similarly,
S_{1}
and
S_{3}
are diagonal and additionally
S_{1}=S_{3}
. Eventually, we have

X_{*}^{T}MX_{*}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&0\\
0&S_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(18)

Moreover, as
M
is spd and
X_{*}
is of full rank, the diagonal elements of
S_{1}
are positive. Inserting
X_{*}^{T}MX_{*}
in (
18
) back into (
15
) and put the resulting form of
L_{*}
there in (
10
), we obtain the quality

MX_{*}=J_{2n}X_{*}\begin{bmatrix}0&-S_{1}\\
S_{1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

This says
X_{*}
is a symplectic eigenvector set of
M
associated with the symplectic eigenvalues on the diagonal of
S_{1}
.

Conversely, assume
X_{*}
is any symplectic eigenvector set associated with the symplectic
eigenvalues
[d_{i_{1}},\ldots,d_{i_{k}}]
. In this case (
4
) holds for
X_{*}
. Multiplying this equality with
\tilde{N}
on the right leads to

MX_{*}\tilde{N}=J_{2n}X_{*}\begin{bmatrix}0&-D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}\\
D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}&0\end{bmatrix}\tilde{N}=J_{2n}X_{*}\begin{bmatrix}0&-D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}N\\
D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}N&0\end{bmatrix}.

That is,
X_{*}
is critical point of the problem (
12
) associated with the Lagrange multiplier
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}N\\
D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}N&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. The sufficiency is then justified.

A similar result for the Brockett cost function with orthogonality constraint can be found in
[
1
, subsect. 4.8]
,
[
8
, sect. 3]
whose proofs were mainly based on the differentiable geometry of the feasible set.

Based on Theorem
6
, we will compute the global minimal value and determine the global minimizers of the cost function (
12
).

Theorem 7
.

Given an spd matrix
M
with the symplectic eigenvalues
d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}
and
\tilde{N}=\mathrm{diag}(N,N)
, where
N=\mathrm{diag}(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{k})
with
0<\nu_{1}<\nu_{2}<\cdots<\nu_{k}
. For any integer
k
with
1\leq k\leq n
, it holds that

2(d_{1}\nu_{k}+d_{2}\nu_{k-1}+\cdots+d_{k}\nu_{1})=\min\limits_{X\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}}\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{N}X^{T}MX)\mbox{ s.t. }X^{T}J_{2n}X=J_{2k}.

(19)

Moreover, the columns of a minimizer
X_{*}
consist of
k
symplectic eigenvector pairs associated with the smallest
k
symplectic eigenvalues of
M
arranged in nonincreasing order, i.e.,
X_{*}^{T}MX_{*}=\mathrm{diag}(d_{k},\ldots,d_{1})
.

Proof.

Let us denote the columns of
X
by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k}
and the diagonal elements of
\tilde{N}
generally by
\tilde{\nu}_{j},j=1,\ldots,2k
.
It holds that

\mathrm{tr}\left(\tilde{N}X^{T}MX\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{2k}\tilde{\nu}_{j}\left(x_{j}^{T}Mx_{j}\right)>\nu_{1}\lambda_{\min}(M)\sum_{j=1}^{2k}\|x_{j}\|^{2}=\nu_{1}\lambda_{\min}(M)\|X||^{2}_{F}

(20)

for any
X\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}
, where
\lambda_{\min}(M)>0
is the smallest eigenvalue of
M
,
\|\cdot\|
and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
are respectively the Euclidean and Frobenius norms. This estimate, together with the unboundedness of the set
{\mathrm{Sp}(2k,2n)}
, implies that the Brockett cost function (
12
) is coercive on the closed set
{\mathrm{Sp}(2k,2n)}
in
{\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}}
. In view of Weierstraß’s theorem, see, e.g.,
[
6
, Prop. A.8]
, the function (
12
) attains a global minimizer
X_{\min}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2k,2n)}
.
Moreover, because of the smoothness,
X_{\min}
must be one of the critical points determined in Theorem
6
which verifies
X_{\min}^{T}MX_{\min}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}&0\\
0&D_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
for some index subset
\mathcal{I}_{k}
. Accordingly, a critical value of (
12
) can only be of the form
2\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}\nu_{j}
where each
d_{i_{j}},j=1,\ldots,k
, is a symplectic eigenvalue of
M
and
\nu_{j}
is a diagonal element of
N
. It is the classical rearrangement inequality, see, e.g.,
[
17
, chap. 10]
, that the smallest value among all possibilities is the left-hand side of (
19
).

Obviously, the equality holds when
[x_{j},x_{k+j}]
is a symplectic eigenvector pair associated with the symplectic eigenvalue
d_{k-j+1}
for
j=1,\ldots,k
.
The proof is complete.

Remark 8

Unlike the trace function considered in

4
Concluding remarks

In

The notion of symplectic eigenvalue has been extended to the case of symmetric positive-semidefinite matrices

30
]
and real symmetric ones

]
