Spatiotemporal patterns and forecasted trends of dengue in Saudi Arabia: a national surveillance study, 2012–2024
Authors: Awadh Alanazi, Shandana Riaz, Hasan Ejaz, Intisar Alruwaili, Maha Munir, Mashael W Alruways, Lienda Bashier Eltayeb, Bi Bi Zainab Mazhari, Muhammad Umer Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue fever remains a significant public health issue in Saudi Arabia, with the majority of the infections occurring in the western urban centers. This study investigates the long-term trends from 2012 to 2024. Using the surveillance data from the Saudi Ministry of Health, the authors employed descriptive statistics, segmented log-linear regression, a spatial analysis across 20 health regions, Seasonal Trend Decomposition by Loess (STL) decomposition, and Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA) models to forecast up to 2030. From 2012 to 2024, the total reported infections were 63,312. Dengue fever incidence increased from 5.99 to 17.01 per 100,000 population, with considerable annual variation and a peak in 2023. Dengue fever infections were concentrated in the western coastal regions, among males, non-Saudi nationals, and those aged 15 to below 45 years. Between March and May, a regular seasonal peak took place. Forecasts indicate that seasonal variation will persist through 2030 within previously noted ranges. Saudi Arabia’s dengue epidemiology is marked by episodic amplification rather than structural transformation, steady seasonality, and ongoing regional concentration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13495-0.Keywords: Dengue, Saudi Arabia, Epidemiology, Surveillance, Seasonal trends, Spatial heterogeneity, Segmented regression

Introduction

Dengue is a mosquito-borne viral disease caused by the dengue virus (DENV), a member of the genus Flavivirus, which exists as four antigenically distinct serotypes: DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4 [1]. The primary vectors of dengue transmission is Aedes aegypti, while Aedes albopictus serves as a secondary vector with a more limited but regionally important role in transmission. The disease is a major public health problem in the tropics and subtropics [2]. The global burden of dengue has increased substantially over recent decades [3]. The major risk factors include urbanization, change in climate, increased international travel, and the spread of competent vectors, all of which contribute to increased vector density and human-vector contact, thereby facilitating dengue transmission [4]. Despite improvements in surveillance and control, mortality associated with dengue has declined. However, recurring outbreaks and geographic expansion remain defining features of its global epidemiology [5].

According to recent study focusing on South Asia, dengue incidence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) have increased in several countries within the region, indicating continued disease expansion despite existing control measures [6]. In addition, transmission risk modeling, particularly climate-based and statistical models, emphasizes the importance of surveillance-based alert systems and ecological risk analyses in assessing the potential for dengue outbreaks [7, 8]. As a result, these studies highlight the importance of surveillance systems capable of distinguishing between long-term changes in transmission trends and short-term, outbreak-related increases in case counts [9].

The dynamics of dengue transmission are governed by a variety of factors, including the migration patterns of humans, the immunological response and environmental conditions where the mosquito lives [10]. Seasonal variations in rainfall, temperature, and humidity directly influence dengue transmission by affecting larval habitat availability, mosquito population dynamics, and the extrinsic incubation period of the virus [11]. On the other hand, the extent and severity of dengue transmission are influenced by variations in host immunological response, including age-related immunity and mechanisms such as antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), which can exacerbate disease severity during secondary infections [12]. Despite advancements in vaccine development and implementation, maintaining population-level protection remains challenging due to factors such as serotype diversity, variable vaccine efficacy and uneven vaccination coverage, particularly in regions with fluctuating transmission [13].

Dengue fever in the Middle East demonstrates notable geographic heterogeneity, with transmission concentrated in specific regions where environmental conditions favor mosquito breeding [14]. In Saudi Arabia, national surveillance data indicate that the number of dengue cases varies from year to year [15]. Most of these cases are found along the western coast of Saudi Arabia, where the environment is favorable for Aedes mosquito breeding and people are densely populated [16]. Key contributing factors include rapid urbanization, construction and human movement [13].

Saudi Arabia, particularly its western metropolitan centers, provides favorable environmental conditions such as high temperatures, humidity, and urban water storage, that support established populations of Aedes aegypti [17]. In Saudi Arabia, dengue transmission demonstrates seasonal variation influenced by climatic conditions, with increased activity during favorable periods, and is characterized by urban-centered transmission patterns with episodic outbreak amplification rather than continuous nationwide endemicity [14].

Although dengue activity in specific regions of Saudi Arabia has been reported in outbreak investigations and localized studies, comprehensive national-level assessments integrating long-term temporal trends, demographic patterns, spatial variation, seasonal dynamics, and predictive forecasting remain limited [14, 18, 19]. Even though the Ministry of Health (MOH) surveillance record gives critical descriptive information, have not been widely applied to assess variations in underlying trends, seasonality patterns, or predict future trends of outbreaks within one framework [20, 21]. In a community with diverse demographic characteristics that are climate-sensitive, it is important to distinguish between spikes in transmission rates that are short-term versus long-term [4]. Long-term spatiotemporal analysis using segmented regression and forecasting algorithms provides more robust insights than basic statistical approaches by enabling the detection of underlying trends, seasonal patterns, and future projections of disease dynamics [22, 23].

The present investigation is retrospective and focuses on publicly available national dengue surveillance data in Saudi Arabia from 2012 to 2024 to provide a comprehensive assessment of long-term temporal, spatial, and demographic epidemiological trends. This study aims to characterize the spatiotemporal patterns of dengue transmission in Saudi Arabia and to inform geographically targeted and seasonally coordinated control strategies. To achieve this, the study integrated a descriptive analytical approach, join point (segmented log-linear) regression for temporal trend analysis, spatial heterogeneity assessment across 20 MOH health regions, seasonal decomposition of monthly case counts, and time-series forecasting up to 2030.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

The research employed a retrospective longitudinal design, which utilized the publicly accessible national dengue surveillance data in Saudi Arabia from the period 2012 to 2024. The data was retrieved from official annual reports issued by the MOH in Saudi Arabia, which collects dengue case notifications as part of national infectious disease surveillance activities [24]. These reports are based on standardized surveillance definitions. However, potential variations in reporting practices over time cannot be fully excluded. The data includes the number of laboratory-confirmed dengue cases reported annually in the country, based on diagnostic methods such as PCR, NS1 antigen detection, or serology, along with incidence rates per population where available; in years where incidence data were not reported, analyses were based on case counts only. In addition, the data was collected at both the national and regional levels, and no individual information was included in the data set.

Study area and regional classification

Although Saudi Arabia is administratively divided into 13 regions, the MOH organizes disease reporting and public health surveillance into 20 health areas, which correspond to major cities, and these regions were used as defined in the official MOH reports for each year of analysis. To ensure consistency with official surveillance reporting and comparability of regional data across years, all analyses were conducted using the MOH health-region classification employed in routine dengue surveillance. All regional epidemiological assessments were conducted using the same geographic framework for health-region classification, which is depicted in Fig. 1. The 20 officially designated health regions are shown on the map [24].

The 20 officially designated health regions

Case definition and classification

The Saudi MOH’s nationwide surveillance reporting system, which is included in the official Statistical Yearbooks, was used to identify dengue fever cases. According to the MOH surveillance system, reported cases are dengue infections that have been clinically and laboratory-confirmed, typically based on diagnostic methods such as PCR, NS1 antigen detection, or serological testing. All reported dengue cases were examined as a single disease, as the statistical yearbooks did not provide subclassification by serotype or clinical severity. This limitation restricts the ability to assess differences in transmission dynamics, disease severity, and serotype-specific patterns. Demographic information obtained from surveillance data included information on sex, nationality (Saudi or non-Saudi), and age groups (< 1, 1-<5, 5-<15, 15-<45, and ≥ 45 years), although the completeness of these variables varied across years. For subnational data on demographics and regions, the window for analysis was 2012–2024, corresponding to the availability and improved completeness of reporting in official MOH records. The term non-Saudi refers to both non-Saudi nationals residing in the country and visitors, as the data on nationality do not discriminate between these groups; this may introduce some bias in interpreting transmission patterns between resident and transient populations.

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to examine temporal trends in dengue cases at the national level, as well as the demographic distribution of affected populations. In terms of regional patterns, analyses were conducted using health regions data as reported by the MOH; however, some regions had incomplete reporting in certain years, which may affect comparability across regions and time. In cases where the population data were accessible, incidence rates were computed on a per 100,000 population basis. Population denominators for subgroup-specific incidence estimates were derived from publicly available GASTAT population data, as exact annual denominators stratified by nationality and sex were not available. To examine variation in dengue cases over time, across regions, and among population groups, descriptive analyses were performed using graphical and tabular representations.

The variables analyzed in this study included annual dengue case counts, incidence rates per 100,000 population (where available), sex (male, female), nationality (Saudi, non-Saudi), and age groups (< 1, 1-<5, 5-<15, 15-<45, and ≥ 45 years). In addition, regional classification based on 20 MOH health regions and temporal variables, including year and month (for seasonal and time-series analyses), were included.

Temporal trend analysis

To assess long-term changes in national dengue case counts, join point (segmented log-linear) regression analysis was performed using R software (RStudio version 2026.01.1 + 403) with the ‘segmented’ package. The objective of this model was to determine if there were any structural changes to yearly trends. Annual dengue case counts were used as a function of years and a single structural change or breakpoint was estimated. In cases where the test was not statistically significant (p ≥ 0.05), the segmented model was not interpreted as having a confirmed breakpoint, and overall temporal trends were assessed using the fitted regression line.

Spatial and regional analysis

The regional burden of dengue was examined using aggregated annual data from 2012 to 2024 for each of the MOH’s health regions. While this approach ensures consistency in comparisons across regions and years, it may mask seasonal variability and short-term outbreak dynamics. Region-by-year matrices were constructed by organizing annual dengue case counts for each MOH health region into a structured dataset, and heatmaps were generated to visualize spatial variation in case distribution over time, with a focus on identifying high-burden areas. The overall burden of the disease was examined by calculating the total number of cases for each region and identifying patterns of geographic concentrations. All spatial calculations were performed in R software (RStudio version 2026.01.1 + 403).

Seasonal pattern analysis

The monthly national dengue cases from 2012 to 2024 were studied to observe the pattern of transmission in the country throughout the year. We created distribution plots to observe the repeating patterns of peak cases in different years. Seasonal Trend decomposition by Loess (STL) was performed to observe the trend of cases over the years, separating the cases into trend, seasonal, and residuals components. To prepare the time series model, the months where the cases were structurally missing were filled using interpolation techniques for time series modeling to prepare the SARIMA model, but the summary statistics were not imputed.

Time-series forecasting

In time series analysis, the data consisted of the nationwide monthly dengue cases. Since seasonality was involved in the data, Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA) modeling was applied. The order of the model was obtained by checking the autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation plots (ACF/PACF plots), as well as the values of the Akaike information criterion (AIC). The forecast was done up to 2030, with 80% and 95% prediction intervals included. In some areas, annual Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) modeling was done for the trends. Residual plots were used to check the goodness of the model [25, 26].

A sensitivity analysis was performed by fitting the SARIMA model using pre-pandemic data (2012–2019) and generating forecasts for the 2020–2024 period. These forecasts were compared with observed values to assess the stability of the seasonal structure.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, a combination of descriptive and joinpoint (segmented) regression for temporal trends, seasonal decomposition, spatial visualization through heatmaps, and time-series forecasting was applied. In the study, descriptive statistics were employed to obtain the number of yearly cases, incidence rates, the distribution of the cases in terms of demographics and regions, and were presented as counts, proportions, and rates appropriately. Segmented log-linear regression was used to investigate long-term trends, and the Davies test to check if the trend had changed significantly. For the detection of seasonal patterns in monthly data, STL decomposition was applied, which separates the overall long-term trend from the components occurring within the year. The time series model used for the forecasting was the SARIMA model. The steps involved in determining the model were based on the ACF and PACF plots, along with the AICc method. For the evaluation of reliability of the model, the residuals analysis was conducted, including the Ljung-Box tests for checking the presence of any autocorrelation. Lacking data on regional subgroups, such as 2019, was not imputed and is therefore represented as unavailable. For the study, the level of significance was set at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Data management was done using Microsoft Excel. The entire statistical analysis and visualization was done using R (version 2026.01.1 + 403) in the RStudio environment using packages including ggplot2, forecast, segmented, tseries, dplyr and tidyr [26].

Ethical considerations

This study used collected anonymized secondary surveillance data in the form of accessible records obtained from the MOH. No individual identifiers were obtained in this study. As the analysis was based on publicly available, aggregated, and non-identifiable surveillance data from the Saudi Ministry of Health, ethical approval was not required, in line with international guidelines on the use of anonymized public health data [27].

Results

National dengue trends in Saudi Arabia (2012–2024)

Between 2012 and 2024, the total number of dengue cases in Saudi Arabia was recorded to be 63,312, with an average of approximately 4,870 cases per year. Substantial variation was observed in both annual case counts and incidence rates across successive years (Table 1). It is important to note that subgroup data for 2019 were unavailable in the source reports, limiting year-specific demographic comparisons for that period. The annual incidence rate increased from 5.99 per 100,000 in 2012 to 17.01 in 2024, representing an overall ~2.8-fold increase over the study period, despite substantial interannual variability. A pronounced spike in cases was observed in 2023, along with a general increase in incidence after 2020, indicating a fluctuating but overall rising trend. Across all years, males and non-Saudi individuals accounted for a higher proportion of cases, with the majority occurring in the 15-<45 years age group.

Temporal trend and joinpoint analysis

Figure. 2 shows national temporal patterns in dengue cases recorded between 2012 and 2024. Significant interannual variability was seen in the annual case counts, which showed a long-term increasing pattern with sporadic peaks and troughs.

Temporal trend of annual reported dengue cases in Saudi Arabia (2012–2024). Points represent observed annual case counts and the solid line indicates fitted values from regression based trend analysis, illustrating overall interannual variability with a gradual upward trajectory

To evaluate potential structural shifts in the trend, joinpoint (segmented log-linear) regression was used. Statistical testing did not indicate a significant change in slope, and no confirmed breakpoint was identified. This indicates that the observed increase cases after 2020 reflects an intensification of existing variability in dengue transmission rather than a statistically significant change in trend (Davies test: p = 0.926).

However, before the year 2020, the cases remained relatively stable, meaning that there was no trend of increasing at a rapid, exponential rate. After a dip in the year 2020, the numbers began increasing again from the year 2021, culminating in the highest point in the year 2023. Overall, rather than a statistically supported structural change, the national pattern shows episodic variability within a long-term trend that is progressively growing.

Demographic distribution by gender and nationality

As shown in Fig. 3, males consistently accounted for a higher proportion of reported dengue cases compared to females throughout the study period. Across all years with available data (excluding 2019), males contributed approximately 72–80% of total cases, corresponding to a male-to-female ratio ranging from approximately 2.5:1 to 4:1. For example, in 2023, males accounted for 78.5% (11,789/15,010) of cases (ratio ≈ 3.7:1), while in 2024, males represented 79.7% (4,788/6,003) (ratio ≈ 3.9:1). Despite differences in magnitude, both genders exhibited similar temporal patterns, with concurrent increases during peak years (e.g., 2013, 2016, 2018, and 2023), indicating parallel fluctuations in case counts over time.

In high-incidence years such as 2013, 2016, 2018, and 2023, non-Saudi males represented the largest number of reported cases. These observations are based on absolute case counts and proportions.

Regional dengue burden by health region

Over the course of the study, there was substantial regional variability in reported dengue cases across the 20 health regions. The Holy Capital and Jeddah, which had the greatest annual case loads between 2012 and 2024, were the western coastal locations where absolute case counts were predominantly concentrated (Fig. 4). Increased transmission was visible from 2013 to 2018, then in 2019 there was a noticeable national reduction. The number of cases rose once more starting in 2020, with higher numbers observed in regions such as Jeddah, the Holy Capital, Jazan, and Medina.

Saudi Arabia’s annual dengue case numbers per health region from 2012 to 2024. The degree of color illustrates the varied geographic distribution and distribution of transmission in western coastal areas by representing the total number of cases identified

In contrast, northern regions such as Qurayyat, Northern Borders, Hafr Al-Baten, Ha’il, and Al-Jouf consistently reported fewer cases across most years. In central regions, including Riyadh, increases in reported case counts were relatively limited and occurred intermittently, particularly in later years. These comparisons are based on reported case counts and proportions, as population-adjusted incidence rates were not available.

When expressed as a proportion of the national total, a clear pattern of regional dominance was observed (Fig. 5). Jeddah accounted for the largest proportion of reported dengue cases across most years, followed by the Holy Capital. In high-incidence years, these two regions together contributed a substantial share of the national total; for example, in 2023, Jeddah accounted for approximately 68% of cases and the Holy Capital for 22%, together comprising over 90% of reported cases. Similarly, in 2024, these regions jointly contributed nearly 80% of the national total. In contrast, other regions contributed relatively small proportions, with occasional increases during epidemic years. Overall, most reported cases originated from a limited number of Western regions throughout the study period.

Age group distribution and risk concentration (2012–2024)

Throughout the study period, the 15-45-year age group accounted for the largest proportion of reported dengue cases in most years (Fig. 6). In high-incidence years, this group contributed a substantial share of total cases; for example, approximately 71% in 2013 (4,622/6,512), 75% in 2016 (4,070/5,428), and 68% in 2023 (10,197/15,010). However, variation in age-group distribution was observed across years, particularly in 2020, when the proportion of cases in the 15–45 age group was markedly lower.

Stacked bar chart showing annual dengue case counts stratified by age group (< 1 year, 1-<5 years, 5-<15 years, 15-<45 years, ≥ 45 years). Total bar height represents overall annual cases and colored segments indicate the contribution of each age group

Children below 15 years accounted for a smaller proportion of reported cases in most years. However, in 2020, a distinct deviation was observed, with the 5-<15-year age group contributing 1,660 cases, exceeding the number reported in the 15-<45-year group. This represents a notable shift in the age distribution for that year.

During the investigation, infants (less than one year old) and children between the ages of one and five years old consistently accounted for the smallest percentage of reported cases. The official statistics yearbook did not contain age-disaggregated data for 2019.

The workforce-aged population (15–45 years) accounted for the majority of reported dengue cases at the national level, with the overall age distribution remaining broadly consistent over time. However, these observations are based on reported case counts, and information on differential exposure or risk factors across age groups was not available.

To better assess temporal changes in age-group distribution, a proportion-based visualization was generated (Supplementary Figure S2). The age composition remained broadly stable across most years, with the 15-<45-year group contributing the largest share of cases in most years. A notable deviation was observed in 2020, when the 5-<15-year group accounted for a substantially higher proportion of cases compared to other years.

Seasonal and monthly pattern analysis

The monthly cases of dengue infection also show a clear pattern of repetition each year from 2012 to 2024. If we examine the pattern of the combined years, the numbers continue to build throughout the late winter and early spring months, peaking from March to May (Fig. 7). On the other hand, troughs occur from August to October.

Monthly distribution of reported dengue cases in Saudi Arabia (2012–2024). Lines represent individual years, demonstrating recurrent seasonal peaks during spring months

This recurrent seasonal structure is further demonstrated by year-specific monthly panels (Fig. 8). In most years, the incidence started to increase in early spring, peaked in the middle of the year, and then started to decrease in late summer and early autumn. While maintaining the same seasonal date, epidemic years such as 2013, 2016, 2018, 2023, and 2024 showed increased peak magnitudes. There was very little activity reported in any of the months of 2019, which is in line with the national drop that was seen at that time.

Monthly dengue cases by year (2012–2024). Each panel represents one year, illustrating consistent intra-annual seasonal peaks with variable epidemic magnitude

The existence of a steady and enduring yearly seasonal component was verified using STL decomposition (Fig. 9). Over the course of the investigation, the seasonal signal’s amplitude remained largely constant, indicating a relatively stable seasonal pattern in dengue case counts. While the remaining component captured sporadic disease-associated spikes that could not be explained by seasonal or trend effects, the long-term trend component displayed moderate oscillations with brief elevations during epidemic years.

The findings indicate a consistent seasonal pattern in dengue case counts, superimposed on a long-term trend that varies over time.

Forecasted national dengue trend

SARIMA model was used to create national monthly dengue forecasts. The best specification was ARIMA(1,0,2)(1,0,1), which had a non-zero mean (AICc = 2324.95) and a 12-month seasonal component in addition to non-seasonal autoregressive and moving average terms.

Residual diagnostics were used to assess the model’s suitability (Fig. 10). The assumption that residuals approximate white noise was supported by the Ljung-Box test, which found no significant residual autocorrelation (Q* = 10.267, p = 0.946; lag = 24, p = 0.9934) and residual ACF values that stayed within the 95% confidence ranges. With a few extreme deviations corresponding to past outbreak episodes, the residual distribution was approximately symmetric.

Residual diagnostics for ARIMA(1,0,2)(1,0,1) [12] with non-zero mean. Upper panel shows residual time series; lower left panel shows ACF of residuals with 95% confidence limits; lower right panel shows residual distribution. Residuals approximate white noise

Forecasts for 2025–2030 show that dengue incidence would continue to fluctuate seasonally (Fig. 11). In line with past transmission patterns, the predicted mean shows recurrent annual peaks. Model-based projections suggest that the seasonal pattern of dengue cases is likely to persist, without clear evidence of substantial deviation from historical trends. These forecasts should be interpreted with caution, as long-term predictions are subject to uncertainty. Overall, forecasts show that endemic seasonal transmission will continue until 2030.

National SARIMA forecast of monthly dengue cases for 2025–2030. Blue line represents forecast mean; shaded regions indicate 95% prediction intervals

The sensitivity analysis indicated that most post-pandemic observations fell within the 95% prediction intervals of the pre-pandemic model, with deviations primarily observed during 2020 and the 2023 peak period (Supplementary Figure S3).

Regional ARIMA forecasts

According to historical transmission patterns, regional yearly ARIMA estimates showed significant variation (Fig. 12).

Regional annual ARIMA forecasts (2012–2030). Black lines represent observed annual cases; blue lines indicate forecast mean; shaded regions denote 95% prediction intervals

Jeddah, the Holy Capital, Jazan, Medina, and Riyadh, regions with historically larger burdens, show higher projected means and broader 95% prediction intervals, reflecting greater uncertainty in the forecasts. The Holy Capital and Jeddah continue to have the greatest anticipated central trajectories.

On the other hand, the areas with lower incidence rates, namely Al-Jouf, Ha’il, Northern Borders, Qaseem, and Qurayyat, have predicted averages close to zero with narrow confidence bands, indicating lower levels of transmission.

For all the sites, the forecasted trends are contained within the bands discussed earlier. None of the regions showed a consistent increasing trend over the projected period. It suggests maintaining the status quo with respect to the region-wise transmission rates.

Discussion

This nationwide longitudinal study characterizes dengue dynamics in Saudi Arabia from 2012 to 2024. It revealed that dengue transmission remained largely confined to previously affected regions, with fluctuations in case counts reflecting episodic increases rather than consistent geographic expansion. Although the pattern of the spread of dengue fever has not changed, the yearly pattern of the spread of the fever has varied significantly, with a general increase over time. This indicates that the observed pattern reflects persistence of established transmission dynamics, including stable seasonality, consistent regional concentration in western coastal areas, and recurring interannual variability, rather than emergence of a new epidemiological phase [28].

In a nutshell, the segmented log-linear regression analysis did not indicate a structural change point even though there were indications of growth after the year 2020. This outcome reveal that the steep increase from 2021 to 2023 seems to be a continuation of the pre-existing variations rather than a change of regime. The decline observed in 2020 should be interpreted with caution, as surveillance-based data do not allow direct attribution to specific factors, and multiple influences, including changes in healthcare utilization and reporting, may have contributed. The observed increase likely reflects a resurgence of pre-existing endemic transmission patterns within favorable ecological and environmental conditions [16, 29].

The spatial distribution of cases indicates a strong and sustained concentration in the western coastal health regions, particularly in the Holy Capital, Jazan, and Jeddah. This regional dominance persisted across both outbreak (high-incidence) and non-outbreak (lower-incidence) years. This suggests that dengue transmission remains consistently concentrated within specific urban corridors where ecological conditions favor vector persistence, rather than indicating widespread geographic expansion. The persistence of high case concentration in these regions over the 13-year period indicates stable, long-term transmission within these areas [15, 16, 23, 30].

The age distribution was essentially constant throughout the research, with most cases continuously falling between the ages of 15 and 45. The predominance of cases in the 15-45 year age group contrasts with patterns observed in hyperendemic settings, where a higher burden is typically observed among children. The transient disparity observed in 2020 most likely indicates modifications to healthcare provision brought on by the pandemic rather than a long-term shift in age-related risk [31].

Seasonal research revealed a predictable intra-annual transmission cycle, with the number of cases rising in late winter and peaked between March and May. STL decomposition revealed a consistent and long-lasting annual seasonal component with a uniform amplitude across the 13-year timeframe. It is noteworthy that during epidemic years, the peak intensity increased, but the seasonal timing remained the same. This indicates that increased case counts during epidemic years occur within an existing seasonal pattern, without evidence of a shift in overall timing. This consistent seasonality suggests the influence of predictable environmental factors; however, these drivers were not directly analyzed in this study [32, 33].

According to the time series forecasts with SARIMA, the seasonal fluctuations can be expected to continue to increase up to the year 2030 under the same conditions. Model diagnostics indicated adequate fit, with no evidence of a sustained increasing trend in either national or regional estimates. The areas with low incidence in the northern regions remain close to zero values, while the high-incidence areas of the west remain with higher average values but larger uncertainty ranges. The areas with low incidence in the northern regions remain close to zero values, while western regions show higher projected means with wider uncertainty ranges. These projections are consistent with previously observed regional patterns rather than indicating a shift in geographic distribution. The relatively stable trend suggests persistence of transmission under current conditions, without clear evidence of substantial change over time [20, 23].

The study period includes the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022), during which public health interventions such as movement restrictions, social distancing, disruptions to vector control activities, and changes in healthcare-seeking behavior may have influenced both dengue transmission dynamics and case reporting. These factors likely contributed to the observed decline in reported cases in 2020 and may have affected the magnitude and temporal distribution of subsequent outbreaks. In addition, inspection of the monthly patterns suggests that the timing of peak transmission was not entirely stable across years, with some post-pandemic periods showing shifts away from the typical April-May peak. While the STL decomposition provides a useful summary of overall seasonality, it aggregates year-to-year variation into a single seasonal component and may therefore mask subtle deviations in peak timing during the pandemic and post-pandemic periods. A sensitivity analysis using pre-pandemic data (2012–2019) further indicated that most post-pandemic observations fell within the model’s prediction intervals, suggesting that the underlying seasonal structure remained broadly consistent. However, deviations observed during 2020 and the 2023 outbreak period reflect the influence of external disruptions and extreme events that are not fully captured by the model [34–37].

The findings of this study are broadly consistent with prior investigations of dengue epidemiology in Saudi Arabia, which have reported persistent concentration of transmission in western regions, particularly Jeddah, Makkah, Al-Madinah, and Jazan. The present spatiotemporal analysis across 20 MOH health regions over 13 years supports this sustained regional concentration across both high and lower incidence years, indicating stable transmission within these areas rather than geographic expansion. The observed male predominance and higher burden in the 15–45 year age group align with previous reports. However, underlying exposure-related factors were not directly assessed. The March-May seasonal peak identified in this study is consistent with prior modeling studies. That indicate stable peak timing across years, suggesting amplification within an established seasonal pattern. The 2023 outbreak, the largest observed during the study period, has been discussed in relation to environmental and mass gathering factors, although these were not directly evaluated in this analysis. Consistent with this, sensitivity analysis also indicated that most post-pandemic observations fell within the prediction intervals of the pre-pandemic model, that supporting the interpretation of amplification with in an existing transmission pattern [15, 17, 30].

From a public health perspective, dengue transmission in Saudi Arabia appears consistently concentrated in specific urban regions, characterized by stable seasonal patterns, persistent regional burden, and a higher proportion of cases among the working-age population. Therefore, reactive outbreak response alone may not have as much of an impact as spatially planned and seasonally coordinated measures. These findings support consideration of enhanced surveillance in high-burden areas and pre-seasonal vector control before the March-May peak as potential strategies for improving early control [16, 23, 38].

From a public health perspective, long-term reduction may require sustained system-level strategies addressing housing density, urban environmental management, and water retention practices in high-receptivity areas. In order to stop repeated amplification, it will be crucial to maintain vector tracking capability in previously impacted areas as fast urbanization and population mobility continue. Overall, the findings indicate a stable and persistent pattern of dengue transmission rather than short-term or fluctuating changes.

It’s important to recognize a few constraints. Routine national reporting systems, which may be prone to underreporting or differences in diagnosis and reporting techniques between areas and years, were the source of the aggregated surveillance data. Due to the lack of individual-level contact, severity of illness, and travel histories, it was difficult to distinguish between illnesses that were acquired locally and those that were imported, as well as to evaluate certain risk factors. In several years, regional subgroup reporting was insufficient, which limited in-depth disaggregated studies. Data for 2019 were unavailable and were excluded from trend analyses, with interpolation applied for time-series modeling. Subgroup-specific population denominators were not available; incidence rates were therefore estimated using GASTAT-derived proportions, introducing approximation. The SARIMA model assumes stable seasonality; although supported by sensitivity analysis, extreme values in 2023 and COVID-era disruptions may not be fully captured. The absence of serotype, climatic, and entomological data limited assessment of transmission drivers.

However, the consistency of findings across seasonal, temporal, geographic, and forecasting analyses provides supportive evidence for the overall conclusions. Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It provides a comprehensive 13-year national analysis (2012–2024), capturing both pre- and post-pandemic periods, including the 2023 outbreak. The integration of segmented regression, STL decomposition, spatial analysis across 20 MOH regions, and SARIMA forecasting offers a multidimensional assessment of dengue dynamics. Sensitivity analysis supports the robustness of the time-series model, and the use of official MOH and GASTAT data enhances reproducibility and policy relevance.

Conclusion

Dengue remains an endemic in Saudi Arabia, as evidenced by the disease’s stable seasonality, periodic outbreak amplification, and persistent regional concentration in western coastal towns between 2012 and 2024. The presence of working-age people and communities associated with the workforce indicates exposure-related risk factors rather than childhood hyperendemicity. According to projections, seasonal variability in dengue transmission is expected to persist until 2030, with no clear evidence of a sustained structural increase or decrease. Long-term reduction in disease burden may require aggressive, timely and regionally tailored management measures.
