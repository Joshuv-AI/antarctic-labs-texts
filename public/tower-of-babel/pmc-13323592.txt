Survival With β‐Adrenergic Suppression After Lung Transplantation
Authors: Thomas Konecny, Cyrus K Khalily, Alex Nusbickel, Gretchen Konecny, Bradley Anair, Allan S Petty, Hiroko Beck‐Fernandez, Zubin Agarwal, Abbas Shahmohammadi, Amir Emtiazjoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurvival following lung transplant (LT) surgery presents an ongoing challenge, and adverse LT outcomes are linked to cardiac arrhythmias. Current guidelines indicate that the role of β‐adrenergic suppression (BAS) after LT remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included adult patients who underwent LT at the University of Florida between 2016 and 2021. Their pre‐ and post‐LT antiarrhythmic medication use was correlated with long‐term survival after a 10‐day post‐LT blanking window.ResultsThe LT recipients (N=299) had a mean age of 60±11 years, 45% were women, and mean body mass index was 26±6 kg/m2. Available mean follow‐up was 4.5±1.9 years. The primary analysis included 299 patients, of whom 209 (70%) were prescribed BAS. Prescription of BAS after LT correlated with prolonged survival, even after adjusting for potential confounders (P=0.008). A comparable survival benefit was observed in patients who continued BAS from pre‐LT to those who were newly initiated on BAS after LT, relative to patients not receiving BAS (P=0.03 and P=0.03, respectively). No survival benefit was observed for other classes of antiarrhythmic drugs used after LT.ConclusionsThis large, retrospective, single‐center study identified a correlation between BAS prescriptions and prolonged survival following LT. This association applied to both patients initiating BAS after transplant and those continuing preexisting BAS therapy. These findings underscore the promise of BAS in this population, and further prospective studies are warranted to confirm the beneficial role of BAS after LT.Keywords: adrenergic suppression, arrhythmia, β blockers, death, lung transplantationSubject Categories: Arrhythmias, Pharmacology, Transplantation

Severe pulmonary disorders are one of the few common causes of death whose mortality rate continues to climb in the United States, currently contributing to over 10% of deaths in individuals aged ≥65 years. 1 Although the number of lung transplantations (LTs) performed annually has grown 6‐fold in recent decades, surpassing 80 000 procedures since 1992, substantial long‐term morbidity and death remain key limitations to even more widespread adoption (with an estimated 5‐year survival rate of 55%). 2 , 3 Patients with cardiovascular comorbidities after LT have a significant reduction in survival, with the link between arrhythmias and post‐LT death being particularly strong. 4 , 5

The proarrhythmic influence of respiratory dysfunction on atrial automaticity has been historically well recognized, including electrocardiographic rhythms characteristic of severe lung impairment like multifocal atrial tachycardia. More recent reports, however, have also highlighted ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, noting markedly increased burdens of atrial and ventricular proarrhythmic automaticity in patients with severely impaired lung function. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 β‐Adrenergic suppression (BAS) medications are among the most central treatments available for the management of arrhythmias arising from increased myocardial automaticity. While our understanding of BAS in patients with lung impairments has remarkably evolved over the past 3 decades, shifting from a perceived contraindication to a recommended benefit, 12 the efficacy of BAS in LT recipients remains unclear, a point highlighted even in recent prominent review articles and guidelines. 3 We aimed to investigate the long‐term survival of LT recipients treated with BAS, whether newly initiated or continued from pretransplant use, compared with those without BAS treatment. Our goal was to determine if this readily available and cost‐efficient treatment is associated with longer survival in real‐world patients receiving LT.

METHODS

This retrospective observational cohort study was approved by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. We identified unique adult patients who received LT at the University of Florida between 2016 and 2021. Relevant demographic variables and comorbid conditions (listed in TableTable) were obtained from the electronic medical record at the time of LT by manually reviewing all records from the index LT hospitalization as well as clinical visits preceding the LT.

The cardiac‐active medications were recorded according to the Vaughan Williams classification (I, sodium channel suppression; II, BAS; III, potassium channel suppression; and IV, calcium channel suppression) at the time of LT admission and at the discharge after the LT hospitalization. The time of initiation of the medication relevant to the LT was noted, as well as the dosing and potential discontinuation during the LT hospitalization. The pre‐LT list of active medications was obtained from the documentation during the clinic visit preceding the LT hospitalization or if listed on the admission note for the LT hospitalization. The post‐LT list of medications was determined from the discharge note during the LT hospitalization and confirmed with the post‐LT clinic visit. The last available encounter recorded in the medical record after the LT (before death or before loss of follow‐up) was searched for the evidence of continued use or discontinuation of the BAS.

The pre‐LT ECG was recorded at the admission for the LT hospitalization or at the nearest prior clinical visit. The post‐LT ECG was recorded in the closest temporal proximity to the 3‐month post‐LT visit recorded in the medical record.

The primary outcome was the survival status and last available follow‐up date as established from any available source in the medical record, including visits to any medical provider and any available recorded communication from the patient, including pharmacy communications indicating that the patient was alive as well as from telemedicine visits. The survival was analyzed after a 10‐day blanking period following the LT, with additional analyses using no blanking window, and using a 30‐day blanking window, respectively. The causes of death were extracted from the electronic medical record and subsequently characterized as primarily (1) pulmonary, including rejection; (2) infectious; or (3) other (including cardiac, oncologic, neurologic, and failure to thrive).

Statistical analyses included calculation of means±SDs for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. The Student t test and Wilcoxon test were used for normally distributed and skewed variables, respectively. The χ2 test or Fisher exact test was used to compare the difference between proportions. The Kaplan–Meier, log‐rank test, and multivariable Cox hazard regression were used for the analysis of the survival in univariable and multivariable models. Significance was defined as P<0.05. The analyses were performed using JMP Version 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

RESULTS

The primary analysis included 299 LT recipients (age at transplant, 59.9±11.8 years; 45% women) with the following primary indications for LT according to the United Network for Organ Sharing classification 13 : Group A was represented by 91 patients, group B was represented by 1 patient, group C was represented by 16 patients, and group D was represented by 191 patients. The distribution of these indications was not statistically different between the studied cohorts (TableTable).

The mean follow‐up interval after LT in this study was 4.5 years (median, 4.75 years).

Baseline Characteristics

BAS was prescribed to 209 (70%) patients included in our primary analysis after LT. Medical records reflected the following reasoning for prescribing BAS after LT: hypertension control in 30 patients, sinus tachycardia in 38 patients, supraventricular tachycardia prophylaxis in 55 patients, treatment for actual supraventricular tachycardia (including atrial fibrillation or flutter) in 68 patients, coronary artery disease treatment in 9 patients, heart failure treatment in 2 patients, treatment of ventricular tachycardia in 1 patient, treatment of tremor in 2 patients, and unclear reason in 4 patients.

The TableTable depicts the baseline characteristics of patients prescribed BAS versus those not prescribed BAS. Age, sex, body mass index, and all recorded baseline comorbidities at the time of LT were not statistically different between the BAS and non‐BAS groups. Class I/III antiarrhythmic medications were prescribed to 60 (20%) patients, with amiodarone accounting for 59 (20%) patients. The proportion of patients using these agents after LT was not significantly different between both groups. Calcium channel suppression was used in 30 (10%) patients overall, with the distribution between the BAS and non‐BAS cohorts not being statistically different. Although the proportion of patients using BAS before LT was higher among those with subsequent post‐LT BAS use, this difference was not statistically significant (TableTable). The presence of sinus rhythm was not statistically different between the BAS and non‐BAS groups both before LT and at the 3‐month post‐LT electrocardiographic encounter.

Decreased Death Associated With BAS

During long‐term follow‐up after LT, patients prescribed BAS exhibited decreased death compared with patients who were not prescribed BAS (Figure 1A). This difference was statistically significant (crude hazard ratio, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.038–0.46), and it remained significant in a multivariable Cox proportional hazard model that adjusted for sex, age, body mass index, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, obstructive sleep apnea, coronary artery disease, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, heart failure history, and smoking history (Figure 2). In our model, the only variables exhibiting an independent effect on survival after LT were usage of BAS and body mass index. The results remained consistent whether death was assessed without a blanking window, with a 10‐day blanking window following LT, or with a 30‐day blanking window following LT.

Among the 209 patients prescribed BAS after the LT, 157 (75%) were recorded as still using BAS at the last available long‐term follow‐up or at the last available clinic appointment before their death. Prescribed BAS was selective for the β1‐adrenergic receptor in 195 patients (93% of the patients on BAS) while nonselective BAS was used in 14 patients (7% of the patients on BAS).

Separate analysis of patients who were on BAS before LT and those who initiated BAS after LT revealed that both cohorts manifested improved long‐term survival after LT when compared with patients not using BAS (Figure 1B).

The causes of death identified during the follow‐up were characterized as pulmonary (including rejection) in 60 patients, infectious in 17 patients, and other/unknown in 43 patients. A subgroup analysis of the patients who died during the long‐term follow‐up after LT identified that the patients who did not use BAS after LT were significantly more likely to die from nonpulmonary and noninfectious pathogeneses compared with patients on BAS (24% of the non‐BAS cohort versus 10% of the BAS cohort; P=0.001; depicted as “other” causes of death in Figure 3).

Survival With Class I/III and IV Antiarrhythmic Medications

Longer survival after LT was not associated with using class I/III antiarrhythmic medications (Figure 1C). Similarly, there was no statistically significant association of a longer survival and the use of class IV medications (calcium channel blockers).

DISCUSSION

This study provides a first retrospective assessment of long‐term survival in patients who were prescribed BAS after LT, with a mean long‐term follow‐up of 4.5± 1.9 years. The key findings were: (1) LT recipients for whom BAS was prescribed exhibited longer survival compared with those who were not prescribed BAS, and this difference remained significant after adjusting for known confounders (P=0.008); (2) correlation with longer survival was observed in both patients who continued BAS from before LT and those newly initiated on BAS after LT, relative to patients not receiving BAS (P=0.03 and P=0.03, respectively); and (3) no survival benefit was observed when analyzing other classes of antiarrhythmic drugs after LT.

Association of Longer Survival and BAS After LT

There has been a surprising paucity of data on the use of BAS in LT recipients, possibly because BAS was historically approached with caution in patients with severe lung pathologies. More recent meta‐analyses have demonstrated the overall beneficial effect of selective BAS in pulmonary patients. 12 This line of investigation has gained increasing prominence as multiple studies have highlighted the association between arrhythmias and adverse outcomes after LT, including increased death. 5 , 14 , 15 , 16 Arrhythmias encountered in the 30‐day period after LT carry a particularly high risk of adverse events. 17 Such previously unforeseen associations led to suggestions for routinely using BAS in LT recipients, supported by emerging data on the beneficial role of BAS in decreasing arrhythmias after LT. 18 However, given the historical reservations about using BAS in patients with severe lung disease states, even recent state‐of‐the‐art guidance statements have called the role of BAS after LT “uncertain.” 3

The present large, single‐center study offers real‐world insights into the promising use of BAS after LT. While this retrospective study cannot attribute causality, it does support the notion that the patients for whom BAS was prescribed could have benefited from its use. Future large randomized trials would be needed to fully define the potentially causal relationship between BAS and improved mortality rate, but given the complexity of conducting such trials in the unique population of LT recipients, we believe that our data support the need for a future prospectively conducted observational study, which could build on our findings and attempt to better account for the many potential confounders that stem from the patient selection and variation in the time of initiation of the BAS therapy. Such endeavor would be highly warranted given the prominent difference in the 5‐year mortality rate detected in our study: 25.3% mortality rate in patients on BAS after LT, compared with 39.4% in those for whom treating physicians did not prescribe BAS after LT.

The fact that 75% of patients receiving LT in our study remained on the BAS regimen since it was prescribed immediately after the LT suggests that BAS was relatively well tolerated. Future prospective studies should better delineate the optimal duration and dosing of BAS as well as provide more robust monitoring for possible side effects of BAS.

The observation that patients who received BAS both before and after LT benefited similarly to those newly initiated on BAS after LT (Figure 1B) further supports the promise of BAS use after LT in selected patients. This is because patients on BAS before LT would be expected to have clinical indications for these medications, stemming from additional nonpulmonary comorbidities, even if these were potentially unaccounted for from our review of the medical record. This notion may gain further relevance as the average age of LT recipients gradually increases in the United States, and the number of patients with pre‐LT indications for BAS may become more frequent.

Possible Mechanisms for Mortality Reduction

Our study identified association of longer survival in patients using BAS but not those using class I or III antiarrhythmic medications, but our data could not comment reliably on the mechanisms of mortality reduction directly, given its retrospective nature. One potential mechanism, enhanced myocardial automaticity, is a well‐documented phenomenon in patients with pulmonary disease, contributing to an increased prevalence of cardiac arrhythmias independent of cardiac muscle dysfunction. 6 , 7 , 19 If that were the case, BAS therapy may have been uniquely positioned to preempt arrhythmias and myocardial dysfunction stemming from this heightened automaticity. On the other hand, the surgical techniques during LT also introduce marked predisposition for reentry around the grafted pulmonary veins. While pulmonary vein isolation is a recognized sequela of LT, it may not be primarily the proarrhythmic activity arising from the pulmonary veins that could be improved by the application of BAS after LT. The survival benefit correlating with the use of BAS rather than the class I or III antiarrhythmic medications may invite future studies with more robust arrhythmia detection capabilities to investigate which arrhythmic or even nonarrhythmic mechanisms may be involved. 15 , 20

Accumulating evidence supports the existence of proarrhythmic states in the ventricular myocardium of patients with severe pulmonary pathologies. Given the markedly higher incidence of nonpulmonary deaths among patients not using BAS in our study, future studies are warranted to delineate whether BAS may be playing a role in preserving ventricular function or averting sudden cardiac death in LT recipients. 7 , 10 , 11

We focused on delineating as best as possible all confounders that could be gathered retrospectively from the medical record, including the occurrence of sustained supraventricular arrhythmias (including atrial fibrillation and flutter), which appeared to not have statistically different frequency in the perioperative period after LT. As previously noted, future prospective trials would be needed to better delineate whether BAS exhibits any beneficial effect via reduction of the high incidence of arrhythmias seen after LT, potentially following precedence from other thoracic surgical procedures. 16 , 21 , 22

Clinical Implications

While the use of BAS in certain patient populations with pulmonary disease is theoretically constrained by the potential for bronchospasm and reduced tolerability in severe lung disease, these limitations seem generally attenuated following LT as the majority (75%) of the patients included in our study tolerated BAS during the long‐term follow‐up. Consequently, the risk–benefit profile may open the possibility for prospective studies evaluating a routine long‐term use of BAS in LT recipients.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of our study cohort are consistent with those of typical LT populations (per distribution of United Network for Organ Sharing categories). This congruence suggests that our findings may reflect real‐world clinical practice in LT centers. A notable observation from our study concerns the lack of survival benefit associated with clinical use of amiodarone, thus advocating for careful consideration of the most applicable antiarrhythmic approaches in this unique patient population. Further prospective research is warranted to directly compare the long‐term efficacy and safety profiles of BAS versus amiodarone and other antiarrhythmic agents in patients receiving LT.

The unforeseen, markedly higher incidence of nonpulmonary and noninfectious (“other”) causes of death among patients who were not started on BAS (Figure 3) invites the consideration of novel and potentially long‐term screening methods in LT recipients, such as implantable loop recorders and event monitors, which may better identify whether arrhythmias could play a role in the survival differences between patients on BAS and patients not on BAS.

Limitations and Future Directions

While we observed a favorable correlation between BAS and improved survival, and we strove to retrospectively analyze potential influences from concomitant antiarrhythmic medications, we acknowledge that the complex polypharmacy often used in LT recipients presents inherent challenges, including the limited number of patients using calcium channel antagonists in this study. As with all retrospective observational studies, the potential for unrecognized confounders is a significant concern. The continuation or de novo initiation of BAS after LT were independent of any clinical guideline or clinical practice change during the time period of this study, and there was no known confounder we could identify in prescribing BAS other than clinical preference of the treating physicians. We attempted to collect from the medical record the stated reasons for initiation of BAS during the post‐LT stay, and while the retrospective design has obvious limitations in this regard, we noted comparable rates of sustained atrial arrhythmias among both BAS and non‐BAS groups. In the absence of randomized studies on this topic, we propose that our study supports the consideration of routine use of BAS after LT in appropriate patients, and these findings seem to be consistent with the direction many LT centers are taking around the world despite the paucity of conclusive evidence on this topic.

While we endeavored to control for known confounding variables, there may be unmeasured factors that influenced both the decision to prescribe BAS and the long‐term survival outcomes. However, given that there was no across‐the‐board recommendation to prescribe BAS to the patients in our study, the presence of such unmeasured confounders would generally be expected to bias results against our hypothesis. The observation of significantly improved survival in the BAS group that was using BAS even before LT may strengthen the likelihood of a true beneficial effect.
