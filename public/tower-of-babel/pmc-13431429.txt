Single-Arm Pre-Post Evaluation of a Six-Week Mindset-Integrated Stress Regulation Course Among Postgraduate Students at a Chinese Medical University
Authors: Zhuoyong Cheng, Rifeng Tan, Yimei Lian, Guodong Wang, Chengzhao Du, Xiaoqing Zhang, Yan Qiu, Xiaoyu Hao, Jianghui Dong, Xuanzi Li, Chanjuan Yang, Biyu Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate delivery and short-term pre-post changes associated with a single-arm six-week mindset-integrated stress regulation course for postgraduate students at a Chinese medical university.MethodsThis single-arm pre-post evaluation was conducted at Guangzhou Medical University among postgraduate students aged 18–35 years who provided electronic informed consent and completed baseline and immediate post-course assessments. The course comprised six weekly 90-minute face-to-face group sessions integrating emotion and stress awareness, mindset-oriented psychoeducation, mindfulness-based practice, and transfer exercises. Primary outcomes were the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21) and Chinese Perceived Stress Scale-10 (CPSS-10); secondary outcomes were the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), and Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS). Pre-post differences were assessed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with exploratory baseline-adjusted models.ResultsAmong 496 complete-case participants, attendance and assignment completion were high (overall attendance, 99.4%; assignment completion, 98.18%). DASS-21 depression, anxiety, and stress scores decreased, and CPSS-10 showed a statistically significant decrease, although the CPSS-10 median was unchanged (15.00 at both time points). Statistically significant pre-post effect sizes were small (r = 0.127–0.269). PHQ-9 scores increased modestly (r = 0.197), GAD-7 did not change significantly, and AIS showed a small statistical difference without a median shift. In adjusted exploratory models, baseline scores were the most consistent predictors, whereas mindset category did not predict any post-intervention outcome.ConclusionThe course was deliverable in this setting and was associated with mixed short-term changes: selected broad distress measures improved, CPSS-10 and AIS medians were unchanged, and PHQ-9 worsened modestly. These preliminary findings do not establish effectiveness or causal benefit. Controlled studies with follow-up and direct assessment of mindset change are needed.

Introduction

Postgraduate students at medical universities are increasingly recognized as a population at elevated risk for psychological distress amid the expansion of postgraduate education and intensifying academic and societal competition. Surveys and institutional reports suggest that symptoms of anxiety, depression, heightened perceived stress, and sleep problems may be more common among graduate students than among undergraduates and age-matched peers in the general population.1,2 In some university samples, more than 40% of graduate students have been reported to experience at least moderate psychological distress during training,3 often manifesting as depressive and anxiety symptoms and sleep problems.4,5 Common stressors include heavy coursework, pressure for research productivity, interpersonal difficulties, uncertainty about career prospects, and financial burden, yet these pressures are not always addressed through timely or adequate support.6 At the same time, university mental health service systems may face limitations in capacity and in the availability of structured, evidence-informed programs, creating a gap between growing needs and feasible support delivery.7,8 The postgraduate training period may also be an important stage for shaping stress appraisals, emotion regulation, and psychological adaptation.

Evidence-based approaches such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and mindfulness-based interventions have been widely studied for reducing emotional distress and improving sleep-related outcomes in student populations.9,10 However, their routine implementation in university settings may be constrained by requirements for specialized providers, training, time, and other resources, which can limit scalability and accessibility at the population level.11,12 These practical barriers have increased interest in lower-intensity psychoeducational interventions that target modifiable cognitive processes implicated in stress responses, emotion regulation, and sleep-relevant self-management. Within this context, mindsets - beliefs about the malleability of personal attributes and the meaning of challenging experiences - have been proposed as modifiable cognitive targets. Growth mindset, originally articulated by Carol S. Dweck, refers to the belief that abilities and related psychological characteristics can be developed rather than being fixed traits.13 Research suggests that a growth mindset is associated with more adaptive interpretations of setbacks and stressors, as well as more constructive coping and emotion-regulation responses.14 Brief growth-mindset interventions have been associated with reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms among adolescents over follow-up,15 and stress-is-enhancing mindset interventions have examined whether reframing stress responses can support learning, growth, and performance.16,17 Some studies further suggest that integrating growth mindset with stress-related mindset content may be associated with more favorable outcomes across psychological, functional, and biological domains.18,19

Despite increasing international interest in growth mindset and stress-related mindset interventions, empirical evidence in Chinese graduate student populations remains limited. Existing domestic studies incorporating mindset constructs have often emphasized single-session formats or focused on one mindset orientation at a time, whereas evaluations of course-based programs integrating mindset education with stress-regulation skills remain scarce.20 The present course differs from mindfulness-only or CBT-oriented psychoeducation in that mindset content was used to frame stress appraisal and academic setbacks, while mindfulness practice and structured reflection were used as practical skills for noticing, regulating, and transferring these appraisals to daily academic contexts. Because the synergistic mindsets framework was developed mainly in brief online adolescent interventions,18 its translation to a six-week face-to-face group course for postgraduate students requires cautious evaluation rather than assumptions of efficacy.

Guided by these considerations, and informed by existing frameworks for postgraduate mental health education in China,21 we developed a six-week Structured Psychological Stress Regulation Course integrating stress and emotion awareness, mindset-oriented psychoeducation aligned with a synergistic mindsets approach (growth mindset and stress-is-enhancing mindset), and mindfulness-based skills practice.22,23 Conceptually, the course was expected to act first on stress awareness, appraisal, and broad distress regulation, which justified DASS-21 and CPSS-10 as primary outcomes; symptom-focused depression, anxiety, and insomnia measures were treated as secondary outcomes because these domains may require more targeted or longer interventions. The present study reports a single-arm pre-post evaluation of this course in a university setting. Our primary objective was to examine whether participation in the course was associated with pre-post changes in postgraduate students’ self-reported psychological distress and perceived stress. Our secondary objective was to explore whether baseline psychological risk levels, mindset category, and selected individual characteristics were associated with post-intervention outcomes after adjustment for baseline values. These analyses were intended to inform future controlled and longitudinal evaluations.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was a single-arm pre–post evaluation of the Structured Psychological Stress Regulation Course conducted at Guangzhou Medical University. Baseline assessment served as the pre-intervention measurement, and the same cohort completed an immediate post-intervention assessment after the final session of the course. Because no comparison group was included, findings are reported as within-participant pre–post changes and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal effects.

Recruitment and baseline assessment began in September 2025. The course was delivered in multiple cohorts beginning in September 2025, and post-intervention assessment was administered immediately after the final session for each cohort.

Participants

Participants were recruited from Guangzhou Medical University beginning in September 2025 through university-wide announcements and postings on the university mental health education platform. Participation in the course evaluation was voluntary. All participants were postgraduate students who provided electronic informed consent before completing the electronic baseline questionnaire. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) current enrollment as a postgraduate student; (2) age 18–35 years; (3) voluntary participation; and (4) ability to understand the study information and complete the electronic questionnaires. Participants with a score of 1 or higher on item 9 of the PHQ-9 during baseline screening were flagged for further risk assessment. Further evaluation was conducted by trained mental health professionals in accordance with university psychological crisis management procedures. Those considered to have high psychological crisis risk after this evaluation were not enrolled in the course evaluation and were referred for appropriate support. In the present sample, no participant met the exclusion criterion on the basis of psychological crisis risk.

At baseline, 549 postgraduate students completed the questionnaire. After data-quality checks, 53 questionnaires were excluded because of clearly contradictory responses, implausible personal information, or missing data on key items. The remaining 496 participants were enrolled in the course evaluation and constituted the analytic cohort. No participant in this cohort withdrew from the course or was lost to post-intervention assessment; therefore, all 496 participants were retained in the complete-case analysis.

Measures

All measures were self-report questionnaires widely used in psychological and educational research to assess emotional distress, perceived stress, sleep problems, and cognitive mindsets relevant to adaptation in academic settings. A study-developed sociodemographic form collected information on sex, postgraduate program type (academic vs professional), and only-child status. If identifying information was collected for administrative linkage, it was stored separately from the survey data.

Growth mindset was assessed using a 3-item Growth Mindset Scale derived from Dweck’s framework. The items were phrased as fixed-mindset statements and rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 6 = strongly disagree), with higher total scores indicating stronger growth mindset beliefs (range, 3–18). For exploratory subgroup analyses, participants were categorized as having a growth mindset (total score >9) or a fixed mindset (total score ≤9).24 This dichotomization was used for exploratory subgroup analyses only, and findings based on mindset category should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Psychological distress was measured using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21), including the depression, anxiety, and stress subscales,25 and perceived stress was measured using the Chinese Perceived Stress Scale-10 (CPSS-10).26 Depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and insomnia-related difficulties were additionally assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9),27 the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7),28 and the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS),29 respectively. These measures were retained because they assess related but distinct constructs and time windows: DASS-21 captures broad recent distress across depression, anxiety, and stress domains; CPSS-10 assesses perceived stress appraisals; PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are symptom-specific screening measures for depressive and anxiety symptoms over the previous two weeks; and AIS focuses on insomnia-related symptoms and impairment.

For descriptive severity categorization, PHQ-9 scores were classified as none/minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27); GAD-7 scores as none/minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (15–21); and AIS scores as no insomnia symptoms (<4), mild insomnia (4–7), moderate insomnia (8–15), and severe insomnia (≥16). For DASS-21, subscale scores were multiplied by 2 before severity classification using conventional cutoffs for the depression, anxiety, and stress subscales. CPSS-10 scores were categorized descriptively as low (0–13), moderate (14–26), and high (27–40) perceived stress, in line with commonly used PSS-10 score interpretations. Consistent with the course’s emphasis on stress appraisal, coping-related beliefs, and emotion-related adaptation, DASS-21 and CPSS-10 were prespecified as primary outcomes because they assess broad domains of psychological distress and perceived stress that were considered most proximal to the course content. PHQ-9, GAD-7, and AIS were prespecified as secondary outcomes to complement the primary outcomes with symptom-focused assessments of depression, anxiety, and sleep. Growth mindset was measured only at baseline; therefore, the present study could not determine whether the course changed mindset beliefs.

Intervention: Structured Psychological Stress Regulation Course

The Structured Psychological Stress Regulation Course was developed as a psychoeducational program informed by positive psychology and mindset theory, with particular emphasis on growth mindset and stress-is-enhancing mindset frameworks. The course was positioned as mental health education and skills development rather than clinical psychotherapy and aimed to support postgraduate students in reflecting on stress appraisals, practicing emotion-regulation strategies, and strengthening self-management skills in academic contexts.

Format and Delivery

The intervention was delivered at Guangzhou Medical University in a face-to-face, group-based format. Using frequency, intensity/contact dose, time, and type descriptors, the course comprised one 90-minute session per week for six consecutive weeks (total planned contact time, 9 hours). The type of intervention was a psychoeducational group course combining brief didactic content, guided practice, structured reflection and discussion, and platform-supported practice assignments. The university mental health education platform supported scheduling notifications, distribution of materials, and submission of practice assignments. The post-intervention assessment was administered immediately after the final session for each cohort.

Core Components and Session Structure

The course integrated three core modules - emotion recognition, mindset-oriented psychoeducation, and mindfulness-based practice - and followed a progressive learning pathway from emotion awareness and cognitive understanding to stress reappraisal, skills practice, and transfer to real-life academic situations. A session-level summary and FITT descriptors are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Emotion recognition and stress awareness (Sessions 1–2): Participants were guided to identify and label their emotional and stress responses and to reflect on personal stress–emotion patterns through structured exercises and self-assessment activities. This component was designed to strengthen emotion recognition as a foundational self-regulation skill.30

Mindset-oriented psychoeducation (Session 3): Instruction and discussion focused on reframing beliefs about the malleability of abilities and the meaning of stress, with practice exercises applying these concepts to common academic setbacks and high-pressure situations.

Mindfulness-based skills practice (Session 4): Instructor-led mindfulness practice emphasized present-moment awareness and self-regulation under stress, consistent with evidence supporting mindfulness-based approaches for academic-related mental health outcomes.31

Integration and transfer (Sessions 5–6): Key concepts and skills were consolidated, and participants developed individualized plans for applying emotion recognition, mindset reframing, and mindfulness skills to daily academic and interpersonal contexts.

After the final session, participants submitted a brief practice artifact (eg, a mindfulness practice video) and an individualized stress coping plan via the university platform to reinforce learning and facilitate transfer.

Facilitators, Training, and Quality Assurance

Sessions were delivered by five trained facilitators, including three associate chief psychiatrists and two intermediate-level psychotherapists, all of whom had experience in university mental health education and psychological intervention. Before implementation, facilitators completed standardized training on session flow, core content, group-practice facilitation, and strategies for responding to varied emotional states. To support intervention consistency and participant engagement, the course incorporated formative feedback throughout delivery, such as check-ins on learning progress and targeted suggestions for adjustment.32 Where feasible, facilitators documented session delivery and participant engagement using brief checklists or post-session notes. Participants completed standardized questionnaires at the pre- and post-intervention time points. Attendance was recorded by the facilitators at each session. Adherence was operationalized as the number of sessions attended (0–6), the proportion of participants attending at least five sessions, and the proportion completing the required homework and practice assignments. Homework and practice assignments were submitted via the university platform as part of the course requirements.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25.0. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were assessed for distributional assumptions prior to analysis. Because most outcomes were not normally distributed, continuous variables are summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]) unless otherwise specified. For between-group comparisons of continuous outcomes (eg, only-child vs non-only-child, academic vs professional programs, and growth vs fixed mindset groups), Mann–Whitney U-tests were used Pre-post within-participant changes were evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Effect size r was calculated as |Z|/sqrt(N). For the seven pre-post outcome tests, unadjusted p values are reported; as a sensitivity benchmark for multiple testing, a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of p < 0.007 (0.05/7) was also considered when interpreting the pattern of findings. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

To explore whether post-intervention outcomes varied by selected participant characteristics while accounting for baseline levels, exploratory general linear models were fitted with the post-intervention score for each outcome entered as the dependent variable, the corresponding baseline score entered as a covariate, and participant characteristics entered as fixed factors. Because most outcomes were non-normally distributed and these analyses were model-based, they were treated as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared. For risk-category analyses, post-intervention PHQ-9, GAD-7, AIS, and CPSS-10 scores were dichotomized using instrument-specific thresholds (<5 vs ≥5, <5 vs ≥5, <4 vs ≥4, and <14 vs ≥14, respectively), whereas DASS-21 depression, anxiety, and stress categories were dichotomized as normal or mild versus moderate or higher. Multivariable binary logistic regression models were then fitted with demographic variables, mindset category, and baseline scores entered as predictors. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses beyond the primary outcomes, including model-based and dichotomized risk analyses, were considered exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously.

Intervention Exposure and Adherence

All 496 participants completed the post-intervention assessment. Attendance was high among enrolled participants: participants attended a mean of 5.97 sessions (SD = 0.18) out of six (median = 6). All participants (100%) attended at least five sessions, and 479 (96.6%) attended all six sessions. Across the program, 17 session absences were recorded out of 2976 planned attendances, corresponding to an overall attendance rate of 99.4%. Completion of homework and practice assignments was also high: 487 of 496 participants (98.18%) submitted the required assignments via the university platform. Because enrolled participants were expected to complete course activities, these adherence indicators should be interpreted as implementation data for this cohort rather than as evidence of general acceptability.

Discussion

This study evaluated baseline psychological profiles, implementation indicators, and short-term pre-post changes associated with a structured psychoeducational course among postgraduate students at a medical university. In baseline cross-sectional comparisons, participants classified as having a fixed mindset reported significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, stress-related distress, and perceived stress than those classified as having a growth mindset, suggesting a less favorable baseline psychological profile in this subgroup. Because these baseline comparisons were observational and cross-sectional, they should be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than evidence that mindset category caused baseline mental health differences.

In the uncontrolled pre-post analyses, outcome patterns were mixed. DASS-21 depression, anxiety, and stress subscales and CPSS-10 scores decreased over the intervention period, although the CPSS-10 median was unchanged. PHQ-9 scores increased, GAD-7 scores did not change significantly, and AIS showed only a small statistical difference without an evident shift in median scores. Accordingly, the present findings support that the course was deliverable in this setting and was associated with improvement in selected broad distress measures, but they do not establish effectiveness for symptom-specific mental health outcomes. Exploratory baseline-adjusted models further suggested that baseline symptom levels were consistently associated with post-intervention outcomes, while only-child status and postgraduate program type were associated with several selected post-intervention measures. In the risk-category analyses, only-child status and professional-degree status were also associated with lower post-intervention risk on several DASS-21 outcomes in addition to selected symptom-specific measures. Mindset category did not predict any post-intervention outcome in the adjusted models, which is important given the theoretical basis of the course.

Growth Mindset, Fixed Mindset, and Baseline Risk Profiles

Prior work suggests that individuals with a growth mindset often demonstrate stronger social adaptation and report fewer persistent negative emotional experiences.33 Cheng et al proposed that growth mindset may indirectly promote more adaptive coping by enhancing emotion regulation self-efficacy and cognitive flexibility, thereby reducing the duration and intensity of negative affect.34 Similarly, Burnette et al’s systematic review indicated that fixed mindset tendencies are more frequently associated with avoidant or maladaptive coping patterns and reduced emotional flexibility, which may confer greater risk for psychological difficulties.35 The baseline cross-sectional findings in the present study align with this literature, as participants classified as having a fixed mindset showed a less favorable baseline profile on depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and perceived stress than those classified as having a growth mindset.

Interpretation of Pre-Post Changes and Links to Mindset-Oriented Intervention Evidence

The observed pre-post reductions in DASS-21 subscales and perceived stress can be interpreted only as preliminary within-participant changes. Prior mindset-oriented intervention research provides a rationale for targeting stress appraisal and related beliefs in student populations,36 but the present single-arm design cannot determine whether the observed changes were attributable to the course, to nonspecific participation effects, or to natural fluctuation. Therefore, comparisons with controlled intervention studies are used here only to contextualize the theoretical basis of the course, not to claim efficacy.

Notably, growth mindset did not reach statistical significance in any adjusted model. This finding may reflect the relatively low baseline symptom severity in the overall sample, the brief intervention period, the limited dimensionality of the 3-item baseline mindset measure, or the possibility that mindset category was not a meaningful short-term predictor of outcomes in this implementation. Because mindset was not reassessed after the course, the present study could not test whether mindset beliefs changed or whether any change mediated psychological outcomes. Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up, additional measurement time points, and more comprehensive mindset assessments to evaluate whether mindset functions as a mediator or moderator of change.

Rationale for the Course Framework and Convergence with Synergistic Mindset Models

The intervention integrated growth mindset and stress-is-enhancing mindset education and complemented these cognitive targets with stress reappraisal and mindfulness-based strategies. This framework maps conceptually onto the synergistic mindset approach proposed by Yeager et al, in which cultivating both growth beliefs and stress-enhancing interpretations may optimize stress responses, reduce negative affect, and support functioning.16 However, the framework was originally tested in different populations and delivery formats, and the current six-week face-to-face group course should therefore be regarded as an adapted psychoeducational implementation rather than a direct replication of prior synergistic mindset trials. The present reductions in DASS-21 and CPSS-10 scores are compatible with the rationale for targeting stress appraisal, but the absence of post-intervention mindset measurement and the null adjusted findings for mindset category prevent mechanistic conclusions.

Potential Moderators: Demographics and Baseline Severity

Exploratory analyses suggested that only-child status and professional-degree status were associated with lower post-intervention scores on selected outcomes after adjustment for baseline values. Although mechanisms cannot be determined here, prior research in Chinese medical student samples has reported lower depression and anxiety levels among only-child students compared with non-only-child students, with less consistent differences in perceived stress.37 Similar subgroup differences may be related to variation in psychosocial resources or emotion-processing styles, although this remains speculative in the present study. In addition, the strong association between baseline symptom levels and post-intervention outcomes is consistent with prior intervention research showing that initial symptom burden can substantially shape short-term outcome patterns.38,39 These subgroup associations should be interpreted as exploratory and do not imply causality; given the single-arm, nonrandomized design and limited covariate adjustment, they may reflect residual confounding (eg, differences in training demands and stressor structure, time constraints, and access to academic or psychosocial resources across student subgroups and program types).

Heterogeneity Across Measures and Time Windows

A key observation is that not all measures shifted in the same direction. DASS-21 and CPSS-10 decreased, suggesting reductions in overall distress intensity and perceived stress appraisals over the intervention period, although the CPSS-10 group median remained unchanged. The secondary measures showed mixed patterns: PHQ-9 increased, GAD-7 did not show a statistically significant change despite a slight upward shift in median score, and AIS showed only a small statistical difference without a change in median score. Such divergence may have several explanations. First, when baseline symptom levels are generally low, screening-oriented instruments such as PHQ-9, GAD-7, and AIS can be sensitive to minor fluctuations in day-to-day mood and sleep rather than stable symptom change.40,41 Second, because the course emphasized emotion awareness, stress recognition, and self-monitoring of sleep-related states, participants may have experienced increased insight and attentional focus on symptoms, which can temporarily elevate self-reported symptom endorsement.42 Third, the measures differ in constructs and time frames: PHQ-9 and GAD-7 capture symptom frequency over the past two weeks, AIS focuses on core insomnia symptoms and impairment, whereas DASS-21 reflects current distress severity and CPSS-10 captures perceived stress over the past month. Stress and insomnia are likely interrelated in postgraduate students, but the unchanged AIS median indicates that this course did not provide clear short-term evidence of sleep improvement. More sustained improvements in depression, generalized anxiety, or insomnia may require longer intervention duration, follow-up support, or integration with individualized clinical services.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the single-arm pre-post design without a comparison group precludes causal inference and does not rule out alternative explanations such as regression to the mean, natural fluctuation over time, or nonspecific participation effects. Second, participants self-selected into the course evaluation, enrolled participants were expected to complete course activities, and the analytic sample was based on complete-case analysis; these features may limit generalizability and make the very high attendance and assignment-completion rates difficult to interpret as general acceptability. Third, baseline symptom levels were generally low across several measures, raising the possibility of floor effects and limiting the extent of observable change in some symptom-specific outcomes. Fourth, outcomes relied primarily on self-report measures; future research could incorporate behavioral or physiological indicators and objective sleep metrics to better characterize mechanisms and multi-level responses. Fifth, multiple exploratory subgroup, model-based, and risk-category analyses were conducted, and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The exploratory baseline-adjusted general linear models using post-intervention outcomes should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, particularly because outcome distributions were non-normal, cohort effects were not modeled, and dichotomized risk analyses discarded information from continuous scales. Sixth, growth mindset was measured only at baseline and dichotomized for exploratory analyses, so the study could not test whether the course changed mindset beliefs or whether mindset mediated outcomes. Seventh, fidelity monitoring relied on facilitator documentation and formative feedback where feasible; no independent fidelity rating, formal content-validity index, or cohort-level fidelity comparison was conducted. Finally, outcome heterogeneity - particularly the PHQ-9 increase and the lack of consistent improvements on GAD-7 and AIS - suggests that future controlled studies should specify primary outcomes narrowly, include longer follow-up, model cohort or facilitator effects when possible, and test growth mindset and stress-is-enhancing mindset as mediators or moderators using richer measurement models.

Conclusions

In this single-arm pre-post evaluation, participation in the Structured Psychological Stress Regulation Course was associated with mixed short-term changes among postgraduate students at a medical university. Some broad distress measures improved with small effect sizes, but CPSS-10 and AIS medians were unchanged, PHQ-9 scores increased modestly, and mindset category did not predict any adjusted post-intervention outcome. The course was delivered with high attendance and assignment completion among enrolled participants, although these implementation indicators should be interpreted in the context of course expectations. These findings provide preliminary information for designing future controlled and longitudinal evaluations, but they should not be interpreted as evidence of effectiveness, causal benefit, or a confirmed mindset mechanism.
