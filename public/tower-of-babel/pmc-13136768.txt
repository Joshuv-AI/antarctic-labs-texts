RMC-6272, a selective third-generation bi-steric mTORC1 inhibitor, improves cardiac function in pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy
Authors: Mariam Rogge, Johannes Fischer, Tobias Jakobi, Zoe Loewenthal, Parul Gupta, Lonny Jürgensen, Norbert Frey, Christoph Sandmann, Mirko Völkers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pathological remodeling in cardiomyocytes during heart failure is driven by excessive activation of mTORC1. Preclinical studies in mice demonstrated that reducing mTORC1 activity protects against cardiac dysfunction and hypertrophic remodeling. However, clinical application of current mTOR inhibitors is limited by incomplete mTORC1 inhibition and off-target effects, including suppression of mTORC2-mediated pro-survival signaling. To address these challenges, the therapeutic effects of the third-generation bi-steric mTORC1 inhibitor RMC-6272 was tested in pressure overload induced heart failure models. The potency and selectivity of RMC-6272 were evaluated in isolated cardiomyocytes. Hypertrophy was induced by phenylephrine in vitro and aortic banding in vivo. Cardiomyocyte-specific mRNA translation changes were assessed in αMHC-Cre Ribo-tag mice. RMC-6272 demonstrated superior potency and specificity for mTORC1 compared to rapamycin and Torin1. RMC-6272 prevented pathological hypertrophic growth in vitro and suppressed mTORC1-dependent mRNA translation, particularly those encoding components of the translational machinery. Preventive RMC-6272 treatment preserved cardiac function under pressure overload, maintaining contractile dysfunction in mice. Therapeutic treatment improved function in established hypertrophy, demonstrating therapeutic potential even after disease onset.

Introduction

The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a master central regulator of cell size, metabolism, and survival, and plays a critical role in cardiac growth and function [1], [2], [3], [4]. mTOR forms the core of two protein complexes: mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2) [1]. mTORC1 promotes anabolic processes, including protein synthesis, through phosphorylation of downstream targets such as ribosomal S6 kinase 1 and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4EBP1) [1], [2]. In contrast, mTORC2 regulates distinct cellular processes, particularly those involved in cell survival, through phosphorylation and activation of protein kinase B (Akt) [1], [5].

In response to increased cardiac workload or chronic neurohormonal stimulation, mTORC1 becomes activated and drives the hypertrophic growth response [2], [6]. Pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of mTORC1 has been shown to inhibit maladaptive hypertrophic remodeling and protect against cardiac dysfunction [6], [7], [8], [9], while the inhibition of mTORC2 has been associated with increased cell death [5], [10]. Collectively, these findings suggest that selective inhibition of mTORC1 represents a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate pathological hypertrophy and to preserve cardiac function. However, first-generation mTORC1 inhibitors, such as rapamycin, do not fully suppress all branches of the mTORC1 pathway [11]. Notably, they incompletely inhibit the mTORC1-4EBP1 axis, which plays a central role in mTORC1-dependent protein synthesis and growth regulation [1], [11].

Second-generation mTOR inhibitors, such as Torin1, were developed to achieve complete suppression of both mTORC1 and mTORC2 by targeting the mTOR kinase domain [5]. Although these agents effectively block mTOR activity, they also inhibit mTORC2, resulting in cardiac dysfunction [5]. Moreover, long-term treatment with first- and second-generation mTOR inhibitors is associated with significant side effects [12]. Such limitations underscore the need for more selective approaches that specifically target mTORC1 while sparing mTORC2 and minimizing off-targets, thereby improving clinical applicability [6].

To overcome these limitations, third-generation bi-steric inhibitors such as RMC-6272 have been developed [13], [14], [15], [16]. These compounds combine the selective allosteric binding properties of first-generation mTORC1 inhibitors with the potent kinase inhibition of second-generation mTOR inhibitors, thereby achieving high selectivity and potency for mTORC1 [13], [14], [15], [16].

In this study, we investigated whether the third-generation, mTORC1-selective inhibitor tool compound RMC-6272 exhibits improved potency and specificity compared with previous mTOR inhibitors in cardiomyocytes. Our data show that RMC-6272 is well tolerated and induces selective, complete, and durable suppression of mTORC1 in cardiomyocytes, effectively inhibiting mTORC1-dependent translational targets in the mouse heart in vivo. Using a mouse model of pathological cardiac hypertrophy induced by O-ring aortic banding (ORAB), we demonstrate that RMC-6272 treatment improves cardiac dysfunction in established cardiac hypertrophy. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the bi-steric mTORC1 inhibitor RMC-6272 exhibits improved selectivity and potency in cardiac applications, indicating the potential of clinical compound RMC-5552 as an effective therapeutic approach for treating pathological cardiac hypertrophy.

Material and methods

Animals and surgical procedures

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty Heidelberg. Male C57BL/6N mice (7–9 weeks old, 23–29 g) were housed under standard laboratory conditions in a temperature- and humidity-controlled facility with a 12-h light-dark cycle and free access to food and water. Cardiomyocyte-specific ribosome-tagged (Ribo-tag) mice were described previously [17]. Briefly, Ribo-tag mice (JAX ID 011029) were bred to αMHC (α myosin heavy chain)–Cre mice to obtain Rpl22 (ribosomal protein L22)-HA–expressing homozygous mice in cardiomyocytes. Pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy was induced in mice using an O-ring–induced aortic banding (ORAB) model. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, intubated, and ventilated using a small-animal ventilator. Following a midline cervical incision, the upper thoracic cavity was exposed. The ascending aorta was carefully isolated from the surrounding tissue, and an O-ring with a fixed inner diameter of 0.457 mm was placed around the ascending aorta to induce pressure overload. For sham-operated mice, the surgical procedure was identical, except that no O-ring was used. To assess the development of cardiac hypertrophy and monitor surgical outcomes, echocardiographic measurements were performed weekly using the Vevo 2100 Imaging System (VisualSonics). Anesthesia was administered via a facial mask and maintained by a minimum dose of isoflurane (1.0–2.0%). Echocardiography was performed at a heart rate of 450–550 bpm. Cardiac function parameters, including ejection fraction (EF), were measured at days 14, 21, and 28 post-surgeries. To evaluate the effects of RMC-6272 under different treatment conditions, two experimental cohorts were established. In the first cohort, mice underwent treatment with 10 mg/kg RMC-6272 or vehicle, administered intraperitoneally on days 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21 after surgery. 20 mg/ml RMC-6272 was diluted in Solutol/Transcutol to obtain a stock solution (maximum time of durability according to the manufacturer is 8 days at 4 °C). At the day of i.p. injection, respective amounts of RMC-6272 stock solution were further diluted in NaCL 0.9% to obtain a vehicle solution of Solutol:Transcutol:NaCl 0.9% in proportions 5:5:90 (%). A similar vehicle solution without RMC-6272 was used in similar amounts as the vehicle injections in the control groups. In the second cohort mice received 10 mg/kg RMC-6272 or vehicle starting on day 7 and again on day 14 after surgery. Throughout the study, mice were monitored daily for signs of discomfort, including changes in weight and activity. At the endpoint (28 days post-surgery), mice were weighed, euthanized, and hearts were excised, weighed, and processed for histological and biochemical analyses. The heart-to-body weight ratio was calculated as a marker of cardiac hypertrophy. For Ribo-Seq experiments mice were injected once with 10 mg/kg RMC-6272 or vehicle and left ventricles were isolated and further processed 12 h after drug administration.

For in vivo pharmacodynamic analyses, an additional cohort of ORAB mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of RMC-6272 (20 mg/kg) or vehicle on postoperative day 1. Left ventricles were isolated 5 h after treatment and processed for immunoblot analysis.

Rat cardiomyocyte isolation and treatment

Neonatal rat ventricular cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) were isolated and cultured for in vitro experiments as described previously [18]. Briefly, cardiomyocytes were isolated from 1- to 2-day-old animals through enzymatic digestion and purified by Percoll density gradient centrifugation. Cardiomyocytes were then plated at a density of 0.5 × 106 cells per well on 34.8-mm plastic plates that had been pretreated with 0.1% gelatin for 1 h at 37 °C (cat. No. F1141, Sigma-Aldrich) and then cultured in DMEM/F12 1:1 (cat. No. 11330032, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/ml of penicillin, 100 μg/ml of streptomycin, and 292 μg/ml of glutamine (cat. No. 10378016, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Hypertrophy was induced with 50 μM phenylephrine (PE) for 24 h. RMC-6272, rapamycin and Torin1 were diluted in DMSO. DMSO was used as vehicle for control conditions. Cell size was analyzed in vitro using an automated InCell Analyzer imaging system.

Adult rat ventricular cardiomyocytes (ARCMs) were isolated from adult Wistar rats by enzymatic digestion and cultured for in vitro experiments as described previously [18].

Cells were plated on laminin-coated culture dishes (10 μg/ml laminin, cat. No. L2020, Sigma-Aldrich). Laminin was applied in coating medium and removed prior to cell plating. After attachment, cells were maintained in Medium 199 (cat. No. M7528, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 100 U/ml of penicillin, 100 μg/ml of streptomycin.

Immunocytofluorescence

Cardiomyocytes were isolated as described above. Cardiomyocytes were then plated on glass chamber slides that had been pre-treated with gelatine for 1 h. The cells were washed twice in PBS and then fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min. Slides were washed three times for 5–10 min in PBS and then incubated for 10 min in permeabilization buffer consisting of PBS and 0.3% Triton X-100. Then slides were washed once with PBS and then incubated with blocking buffer containing 10% horse serum in PBS for 30 min. Next, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody diluted in blocking buffer. Primary antibodies used were α-Sarcomeric Actin (cat# A2172, Sigma-Aldrich; 1:100) and Phospho-4EBP1 Thr37/46 (cat# 236B4, 2855, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:100). On the next day slides were washed three times for 5–10 min in PBS and then incubated with secondary antibody in blocking buffer for 1 h. Secondary antibody used were Fluorescein (FITC)-conjugated AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG (cat# 715-095-151, Jackson Immuno Research; 1:100) and Cy3-conjugated AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG (cat# 711-165-152, Jackson Immuno Research; 1:100). The slides were then washed three times for 5–10 min in PBS and counterstained with DAPI. Then slides were washed with PBS and covered with VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium (cat# H-1000, Vector Laboratories) and a glass plate and visualized by microscopy. Images were obtained using a Leica DMi8 confocal laser scanning microscope.

IonOptix

Cardiac myocyte contractile and relaxation function were measured using the IonOptix Calcium and Contractility System (Westwood, MA, Ionoptix.com). Glass coverslips containing isolated myocytes were mounted onto a stimulation chamber and bathed in Tyrode's solution warmed to 30 °C. Length and width were measured with the Edge Detection feature in quiescent myocytes. Myocytes were stimulated with 1 Hz pacing frequency and sarcomere Length was measured. Data were analyzed using IonWizard Software (IonOptix, Westwood, MA, Ionoptix.com). For contractility experiments, the biological replicates (n) represent independent cardiomyocyte isolations from different animals. Multiple cells were analyzed per isolation. They were isolated and seeded onto imaging dishes in modified M199 medium and treated as follows: RMC-6272 5 nM for 6 h, isoprenaline 100 nM for 3 min, Fura-2AM 1.5 μM for 15 min in the absence of light. Cells were washed three times with modified M199 medium before the measurements.

Preparation of tissue lysates

Mice were sacrificed and left ventricles were rapidly excised, washed in PBS and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. Tissue used for Ribo-Seq was washed in PBS containing 100 μg/ml cycloheximide. Left ventricles were homogenized using a tissue homogenizer in 5 volumes of ice-cold polysome buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 7.4, 10 mM MgCl2, 200 mM KCl and 1% Triton X-100. Tissue used for Ribo-Seq was homogenized in polysome buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 7.4, 10 mM MgCl2, 200 mM KCl, 2 mM DTT, 1% Triton X-100, 1U DNase/μl and 100 μg/ml CHX. For library construction lysates were processed as previously described [18]. RNA was isolated from tissue using TRIzol (cat# 15596026, Invitrogen).

Immunoblotting

Western blotting was performed to analyze mTORC1 and mTORC2 activity by evaluating the phosphorylation of complex-specific downstream targets as described previously [18]. Cultured cells were lysed in RIPA Buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100 and 1% SDS, which was supplemented with protease inhibitor cOmplete ULTRA (cat#05892791001, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor PhosSTOP (cat# 04906837001, Roche). Tissue lysates were prepared as described above. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 4 °C for 10 min at 20.000 rcf. Lysate protein concentration was determined using the DC Protein Assay Kit II (cat# 5000112, Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equivalent amounts of protein, were brought up to similar volume, mixed with Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad; 161-0747) and 2-Mercapoethanol (cat# M6250, Sigma-Aldrich) and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were separated on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to Immobilon-P transfer membranes (cat# IPVH00010, Merck Millipore). The following antibodies were used to probe the membranes: Phospho-Akt Ser473 (cat# 9271, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), Phospho-Ribosomal S6 Ser235/236 (cat# D57.2.2E,4858, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:5000), Ribosomal S6 Ser235/236 (cat# 54D2, 2317, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), Phospho-4EBP1 Thr37/46 (cat# 236B4, 2855, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:5000), 4EBP1 (cat# 9452, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:5000), Phospho-Troponin I Ser23/24 (cat#4004, 1:1000), α-Tubulin, (cat# # 2144, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000). All immunoblots were normalized to a loading control.

Histology analysis

Hearts were retroperfused with PBS at 70 mmHg, arrested in diastole with 60 mM KCl, removed, immediately frozen in an optimal cutting temperature compound (Tissue-Tek) and stored at −80 °C. Cryosections of 8 μm thickness were prepared using a cryotome and directly placed on glass slides. Sections were stained with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) to evaluate cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area and imaged using the Axio Scan Z1 Slide Scanner (Zeiss). Quantification of cardiomyocyte size was performed using ImageJ software. For histological visualization, Sirius Red staining was used on cryosections to assess fibrotic remodeling.

Generation of Ribo-seq and RNA-seq libraries

Libraries were generated as previously described [18]. For each animal, the heart was lysed in 700 μl polysome buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.4, 10 mM MgCl2, 200 mM KCl, 2 mM DTT, 1% Triton X-100, 1U DNAse/μl and 100 μg/ml CHX) using a tissue homogenizer (Bullet Blender, NextAdvance). The tissue was homogenized further by passing the lysate through a 23-gauge syringe needle ten times. Homogenates were centrifuged at 4 °C and 18,000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was immediately used in the further steps. For complete lysis, the samples were kept on ice for 10 min and subsequently centrifuged at 20,000g to precipitate cell debris. Ribosome footprints were generated after immunoprecipitation of cardiomyocyte-specific polysomes with Anti-HA magnetic beads after treating the lysate with RNAse I (Ambion). Libraries were generated according to the D-Plex small RNA-seq kit (Diagenode). Sample pools were sequenced on the Next550 platform using 75-bp sequencing chemistry. Sequencing data processing and quality control sequencing data processing and quality control was performed as previously described. Adapters removal was done with Flexbar v3.0.350 using standard filtering parameters (no prior trimming). Reads with more than 1 uncalled base were not included in the output: flexbar --may-uncalled 1 --pre-trim-left 0. Reads aligning to a custom bowtie2 v2.3.051 index including mouse rRNA, mtRNA, tRNA, snRNA and other ncRNA (Ensembl mus muculus release 97 – ncrna) were discarded. Remaining reads were then aligned in genomic coordinates to the mouse genome (GRCm38.p6) with STAR, v.2.5.3a52, inserting annotations on the fly: STAR --quantMode TranscriptomeSAM --alignIntronMin 20 –alignIntronMax 100000 --outFilterMismatchNmax 1 --outFilterIntronMotifs RemoveNoncanonicalUnannotated --outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.04 --sjdbOverhang 50. Only uniquely mapping reads were kept for analysis. For Ribo-seq data, only periodic fragment lengths were kept that showed a distinctive triplet periodicity. We used the automatic Bayesian selection of read lengths and ribosome P-site offsets (BPPS) method to select and shift aligned reads to properly account for P-site of the ribosome. Finally, abundance estimates and read count to coding sequences were obtained using HTSeq-count, taking into account the strand-specific protocols. edgeR with standard parameters was used for differential gene expression analysis. We used a logFC >1 and an FDR < 0.05 as cutoff.

Gene ontology analysis

For GO term analysis, genes with FDR <0.05 were considered for further analysis. GOTERM_BP_DIRECT in DAVID 2021 [19] was used with the subset of expressed protein-coding genes as background set. Only enriched GO terms with at least five significantly changed genes were kept for further analysis and ranked by p-value. Top enriched terms were retained and visualized with a custom plotting routine showing p-value of enrichment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical differences were assessed using student's t-test for two-group comparisons or one- or two-way ANOVA where appropriate and the significance was set at P < 0.05 followed by Tukey's post hoc test.

Significance levels were indicated as follows: p < 0.05 *, p < 0.01 **, p < 0.001 ***, and p < 0.0001 ****.

Results

RMC-6272 is a full mTORC1-selective inhibitor in cardiomyocytes

To evaluate the potency and selectivity of RMC-6272 for mTORC1, western blot analyses were conducted to assess the phosphorylation of complex-specific downstream targets of mTORC1 and mTORC2 in isolated neonatal rat ventricular cardiomyocytes (NRCMs). Phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 at S235/236 (p-S6S235/236) and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 at T37/46 (p-4EBP1T37/46) served as readouts of mTORC1 kinase activity, whereas phosphorylation of protein kinase B at S473 (p-AktS473) was used to evaluate mTORC2 activity (Fig. 1A). RMC-6272 induced complete inhibition of both p-S6S235/236 and p-4EBP1T37/46 phosphorylation in NRCMs within 6 h of treatment initiation, even at the lowest concentration tested (1 nM) (Fig. 1B). In contrast, low concentrations of RMC-6272 (1 nM to 10 nM) increased p-AktS473 phosphorylation levels. At higher concentrations (100 nM), RMC-6272 began to exhibit off-target inhibition of AktS473 phosphorylation (Fig. 1B). In direct comparison, RMC-6272 (5 nM) showed superior potency in inhibiting mTORC1 signaling, compared to rapamycin (5 nM) and Torin1 (100 nM). RMC-6272 inhibits mTORC1 activation and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro

To further explore the effects of RMC-6272 on cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro, we analyzed its impact on mTORC1 signaling and cell growth in cardiomyocytes subjected to α1-adrenergic stimulation with 50 μM phenylephrine (PE) for 24 h. As anticipated, PE treatment increased p-S6S235/236 and p-4EBP1T37/46 phosphorylation levels (Fig. 2A–D), reflecting the upregulation of mTORC1 signaling that drives increased protein synthesis and anabolism during cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. RMC-6272 treatment efficiently suppressed the phosphorylation of both mTORC1 targets, demonstrating robust inhibition of mTORC1 activity even in the presence of hypertrophic stimuli. Elevated p-AktS473 phosphorylation by PE was not inhibited by RMC-6272. p-AktS473 levels were higher in RMC-6272-treated cells (Fig. 2A and D), suggesting that RMC-6272 not only preserves mTORC2 signaling but even enhance it. This effect may be explained by relief of mTORC1-dependent feedback loops to mTORC2, as previously described [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. Cell size was quantified 24 h post-treatment using an automated InCell Analyzer imaging system. Suppression of mTORC1-dependent translation by RMC-6272 prevents increased protein synthesis

mTORC1-dependent cell growth is largely mediated by its regulation of protein synthesis, primarily through its control of translation initiation [2], [6], [18], [25]. To gain mechanistic insights into the inhibition of pathological cardiomyocyte growth by RMC-6272, we analyzed the effects of mTORC1-selective inhibition on mRNA translation in cardiomyocytes in vivo using a cell-type specific Ribo-tag mouse model [17], [18]. The Ribo-tag mouse carries a floxed Rpl22 (a component of the large ribosomal subunit) allele with a wild-type exon followed by an identical C-terminal exon with three copies of a hemagglutinin (HA) epitope inserted before a stop codon. After crossing with a cell type-specific Cre recombinase-expressing mouse line, Cre will activate recombination of the floxed exon, leading to the synthesis of HA-Rpl22-tagged ribosomes in the desired cell type. Rpl22HA-tag mice were crossed with αMHC-Cre mice to achieve cardiomyocyte-specific ribosome tagging [17]. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 10 mg/kg RMC-6272, and left ventricles were harvested 12 h post-treatment (Fig. 4A). By combining ribosome-tagging with RMC-6272 treatment and ribosome profiling, we were able to assess the effects of selective mTORC1 inhibition on mRNA-translation in cardiomyocytes in vivo (Fig. 4A and B). RMC-6272 treatment specifically suppressed mRNAs enriched for encoded components of the translational machinery, consistent with previous findings in non-cardiac tissues (Fig. 4C, full list in Supplemental File 2) [26], [27]. In total, 463 genes showed reduced translation, strongly enriched for GO-term related to ribosome function (Fig. 4C). This reduction in the translation of ribosomal proteins and translation factors effectively reduced the translational capacity of cardiomyocytes in vivo, which was confirmed by puromycin incorporation assays (Fig. 4D). To evaluate whether RMC-6272 could mitigate cardiac dysfunction and hypertrophy in vivo, we employed a pressure-overload model using O-ring aortic banding (ORAB) [28]. Mice underwent ORAB or sham surgery using O-rings with a fixed inner diameter of 0.457 mm and a cross-section of 0.508 mm. Mice were monitored over 28 days post-surgery and were either treated with 10 mg/kg RMC-6272 or vehicle via intraperitoneal injection on post-surgical days 1, 3, 7, 14 and 21 (Fig. 5D). This dosing regimen was informed by recent preclinical cancer studies, where weekly injections of 8–10 mg/kg RMC-6272 effectively suppressed mTORC1 activity and tumor growth in models with hyperactivated mTORC1 [14], [15], [16], [29]. As expected, ORAB surgery induced cardiac hypertrophy (Fig. 5E). While RMC-6272 treatment showed a trend toward reduced heart weight-to-body weight ratios, the reduction did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5E). Similar, quantification of cardiomyocyte size using wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining revealed no significant differences between treatment groups (Fig. 5F and G). Echocardiographic measurements taken at days 14, 21, and 28 demonstrated that RMC-6272 treatment attenuated the decline of cardiac contractility over time, indicating its potential to preserve cardiac function under pressure-overload (Fig. 5H to K). Histological analysis, however, revealed no appreciable differences in fibrotic remodeling between RMC-6272- and vehicle-treated hearts (Fig. 5L), and expression of the fibrotic marker gene Col1a1 remained unchanged, suggesting that the observed preservation of cardiac contractility is mediated by mechanisms other than the inhibition of hypertrophic and fibrotic remodeling.

Overall, these findings suggest that RMC-6272 effectively suppresses cardiac mTORC1 activity in vivo and preserves cardiac function during pressure overload but does not significantly reduce hypertrophic growth or fibrosis at the dosage used in this study.

Improvement of cardiac function by RMC-6272 in established cardiac hypertrophy

While the results above demonstrate that RMC-6272 can prevent cardiac dysfunction in response to pressure overload, preventive intervention does not fully reflect clinical scenarios where patients are primarily identified after cardiac hypertrophy and pathological remodeling has already developed. To evaluate whether RMC-6272 can improve cardiac function after hypertrophy has already developed, we examined an additional cohort in which treatment was delayed until day 7 post-surgery - a time point when cardiac hypertrophic growth is nearly fully established in mice in response to pressure overload or adrenergic stimulation [17], [30]. In this treatment protocol, injections were administered on days 7 and 14 post-surgery to mimic a more clinically relevant scenario where therapeutic intervention is initiated after disease develops (Fig. 6A). Like the previous treatment protocol (Fig. 5), treatment with RMC-6272 showed a trend toward reducing heart weight-to-body weight ratio, which did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6B). Comparable findings were observed in cardiomyocyte cell area quantification in vivo (Fig. 6C and D). Despite the lack of significant structural changes, delayed RMC-6272 treatment effectively preserved cardiac contractility, preventing further decline in cardiac function observed in vehicle-treated animals after ORAB surgery. This preservation was maintained until the experimental endpoint at 4 weeks post-surgery (Fig. 6E to H). These findings suggest that RMC-6272 can improve cardiac function even after pathological hypertrophic remodeling has been established, indicating that its cardioprotective effects involve mechanisms other than the control of cardiac growth. In summary, this study reveals that the novel third-generation mTORC1 inhibitor tool compound RMC-6272 is superior to rapamycin and Torin1 with regards to potency and specificity in cardiomyocytes. RMC-6272 prevents cardiac dysfunction in both developing and established pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy in mice and regulates a translational network that controls protein synthesis, proteostasis, mRNA transcription and metabolism in cardiomyocytes in vivo. These findings suggest that a bi-steric mTORC1 selective inhibitor such as the clinical compound RMC-5552 has promising potential for the treatment of pathological cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of the third-generation mTORC1-selective inhibitor RMC-6272 on cardiac hypertrophy. RMC-6272 is a bi-steric mTORC1 inhibitor with high potency and selectivity for mTORC1 over mTORC2 [14], [15]. Our results demonstrate that RMC-6272 effectively suppresses mTORC1 activity in cardiomyocytes. Compared to the first-generation mTORC1 inhibitor rapamycin and the second-generation mTOR inhibitor Torin1, RMC-6272 exhibited superior efficacy and specificity. Notably, RMC-6272 did not suppress mTORC2 signaling, which is consistent with the finding that the clinical compound RMC-5552 resulted in less hyperglycemia as compared to other mTOR inhibitors [5], [12], [31]. No impairment of cardiomyocyte contractility was noted in isolated cardiomyocytes. Adult cardiomyocytes were studied under serum-free conditions, which may be associated with low basal mTORC1 activity. Therefore, the absence of detectable functional effects should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that pharmacological mTORC1 inhibition has no effect on cardiomyocyte contractility in conditions which increased mTORC1 activity. In an ORAB mouse model of pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy, RMC-6272 preserved cardiac function both when treatment was initiated immediately after surgery and when started after hypertrophy had already developed. This suggests that selective mTORC1 inhibition may help maintain cardiac function even in more advanced stages of disease. Differences in the functional outcome between the protective and reversible dosing schedules may reflect differences in both treatment timing and duration of mTORC1 inhibition. While mTORC1 activation contributes to pathological remodeling, sustained or repeated inhibition during the early adaptive phase may interfere with compensatory metabolic and stress-response mechanisms required to maintain cardiac function. In this context, prolonged pathway suppression during early remodeling does not necessarily result in greater functional protection. In contrast, a delayed and limited dosing approach may attenuate maladaptive signaling once dysfunction is established, without inducing extended suppression of mTOR activity. These findings align with previous studies showing that pharmacological or genetic mTORC1 inhibition improves cardiac function in preclinical models of cardiac hypertrophy [2], [6], [7], [8], [9]. However, despite the functional protection observed, histological analysis revealed no significant reduction in cardiomyocyte size or fibrosis. Prior studies using rapamycin or the genetic mTORC1 inhibitor PRAS40 have demonstrated attenuation of hypertrophic growth, in vivo [6], [7], [8]. While RMC-6272 effectively prevents cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro, achieving similar effects in vivo may require higher doses or more frequent applications than those used in this study.

Our results suggest that mTORC1 inhibition is a promising approach for treating pathological hypertrophy and cardiac dysfunction. mTORC1 hyperactivation is commonly observed in various forms of heart disease, including metabolic cardiomyopathy and heart failure with reduced or preserved ejection fraction [2], [32], [33], [34]. Future studies should explore whether selective mTORC1 inhibition improves cardiac function in other forms of heart disease. The observed disconnect between functional improvements and structural remodeling in this study suggests that the cardioprotective effects of selective mTORC1 inhibition may involve mechanisms beyond cell size regulation, such as modulation of metabolism, proteostasis, inflammation or specific downstream effectors, as suggested previously [2], [3], [5], [18], [32], [35], [36], [37]. As mTORC1 is a central regulator of cardiac metabolism, targeting it in metabolic heart diseases like HFpEF may hold particular promise, given the current lack of effective treatment options. For this, it will be critical to identify the optimal dosing, timing, and duration of treatment to achieve both functional and structural benefits. mTORC1 primarily regulates gene expression at the level of translation rather than transcription. Consequently, altered ribosome association of specific transcripts does not necessarily require changes in total mRNA abundance. TOP-mRNAs were the predominant transcripts showing reduced translation in cardiomyocytes following RMC-6272 treatment, suggesting that mTORC1 regulates metabolism primarily through selective control of specific transcripts rather than through a global suppression of metabolic mRNA translation or through posttranslational mechanisms such as phosphorylation of specific metabolic effectors. Metabolic regulation by mTORC1 may also involve post-transcriptional control of key metabolic enzymes and transcription factors, which does not necessarily manifest as altered translation of distinct metabolic mRNAs under the experimental conditions analyzed here.

Conclusion

In conclusion, RMC-6272 is a potent and highly selective mTORC1 inhibitor that preserves cardiac function in a mouse model of pressure overload induced pathological cardiac hypertrophy. Further studies are now needed to demonstrate significant potential for clinical application of the corresponding clinical compound, RMC-5552, in heart failure and related conditions.
