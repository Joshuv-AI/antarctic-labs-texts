On the Injective Norm of Sums of Random Tensors and the Moments of Gaussian Chaoses
Ishaq Aden-Ali

Abstract
We prove an upper bound on the expected ℓ p \ell_{p} injective norm of sums of subgaussian random tensors. Our proof is simple and does not rely on any explicit geometric or chaining arguments. Instead, it follows from a simple application of the PAC-Bayesian lemma , a tool that has proven effective at controlling the suprema of certain “smooth” empirical processes in recent years. Our bound strictly improves a very recent result of Bandeira, Gopi, Jiang, Lucca, and Rothvoss. In the Euclidean case ( p = 2 p=2 ), our bound sharpens a result of Latała that was central to proving his estimates on the moments of Gaussian chaoses. As a consequence, we obtain an elementary proof of this fundamental result.

We are now ready to define the variance parameters.

Definition 1.1
.

(Variance parameters.) Let
T\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d_{r}}
be a subgaussian random tensor. For any
p\geqslant 2

{\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{2}={\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{2}(T)=\sup_{x_{1}\in B_{p}^{d_{1}},\dots,x_{r}\in B_{p}^{d_{r}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=r-\ell\end{subarray}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|T_{k}^{\pi_{I}}(x_{\pi_{I}(1)},\dots,x_{\pi_{I}(r-\ell)},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}.

Our main result is the following clean bound on the expected
\ell_{p}
injective norm of subgaussian random tensors in terms of these variance parameters.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d_{r}}
be a subgaussian random tensor.
For any
p\geqslant 2

\beta>0

\E\left\|T\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{p}}\leqslant\sqrt{\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{1-\ell}{{\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{2}}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{r}d_{t}^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}.

(2)

Approximately optimizing the parameter

Corollary 1.3

Let
T\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d_{r}}

For any

\E\left\|T\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{p}}\leqslant\sqrt{\left({\sigma}_{(1,p)}^{2}+r\max_{2\leqslant\ell\leqslant r}{\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{\frac{2}{\ell}}{\sigma}_{(0,p)}^{\frac{2\ell-2}{\ell}}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{r}d_{t}^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}.

(3)

Corollary 1.4

Let

d^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\E\left\|T\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{p}}\lesssim_{r}\sigma_{(1,\mathcal{I}_{p})}+\max_{2\leqslant\ell\leqslant r}\sigma_{(\ell,\mathcal{I}_{p})}^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\sigma_{(0,\mathcal{I}_{p})}^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}.

The proof of the main result of
[
4
]

A classic result in the theory of Gaussian processes implies that this supremum can be bounded by Dudley’s entropy integral (see
[
19

).
The main technical contribution of
[
4
]

In this work, we completely avoid using any explicit geometric or chaining arguments.
Instead, we control the relevant (sub)gaussian process by appealing to the so-called
PAC-Bayesian lemma
.
At a high level, the PAC-Bayesian lemma provides a way to control the supremum of certain “smoothed” processes, including the multilinear processes we study in this paper.
We defer a formal statement and discussion of this lemma to
Section

2.2
.

Taking a step back, we should ask ourselves: How sharp are these bounds?
A natural setting in which to consider this question is that of Euclidean matrices.
It is easy to see that
Theorem

1.2
implies the bound

\E\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}\lesssim\max\left\{\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}^{\top}T_{k}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{\frac{1}{2}},\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}T_{k}^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}+\left(\sup_{x\in B_{2}^{d_{1}},y\in B_{2}^{d_{2}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x^{\top}T_{k}y)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|T_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
denotes the operator norm of a matrix.
The first term on the right-hand side of the above is precisely the term that appears in the (non-commutative) matrix Khintchine inequality
[
12
,
13
]
, but without the usual
\sqrt{\log d}
factor that is necessary in general.
There is no contradiction, however, since the sum of squared Frobenius norms in the second term generally yields
much larger
upper bounds than the matrix Khintchine inequality in regimes where the multiplicative
\sqrt{\log d}
factor is necessary.
For random tensors of order
r\geqslant 3
, the very recent work of Boedihardjo
[
5
]
provides a bound on the expected

injective norm of Gaussian random tensors in the independent entry model which is a special case of (

).
His bound is sharper than the bound implied by

1.2

While it is unfortunate that our bounds do not recover the matrix Khintchine inequality, this is somewhat expected since, as we will see, our proof is agnostic to the underlying matrix structure.
Loosely speaking, the proof of our main result is “volumetric” in nature.
We should briefly remark that in the Euclidean matrix setting, there is a way to use a very different kind of PAC-Bayesian argument that utilizes the underlying matrix structure
[
3
]
.
This approach ever-so-slightly generalizes Tropp’s celebrated matrix moment generating function (MGF) approach
[

22
]
and is able to recover the matrix Khintchine inequality (among other things).
Still, our
dimension-free

injective norm bounds for

A few applications of the form (1) are investigated in
[
4
]
.
An application of the form (2) that we will consider is that of estimating the moments of
Gaussian chaoses
.

1.1
Moments of Gaussian Chaoses

An order
r
(decoupled) Gaussian chaos is a random variable of the following form:

\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}=1}^{d}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}(g_{t})_{i_{t}}

where
A\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r}
is a deterministic tensor and
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\in\bR^{d}
are independent standard Gaussian random vectors.
Recall that the
p
th moment of a random variable
X\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as
\|X\|_{p}=(\E|X|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}
.
A fundamental result in probability theory due to Latała
[
11
]
is a sharp upper bound on the moments of decoupled Gaussian chaoses of all orders.
4
4

4

When the tensor
A
contains certain symmetries, a classic decoupling result
[
8
]
allows one to apply Latała’s result to
coupled
Gaussian Choases
\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\in[d]}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}g_{i_{t}}
that are defined by a single Gaussian vector
g\in\bR^{d}
.

This generalizes a well known result due to Hanson and Wright for the case
r=2

[
10
]
.
To formally state Latała’s result, we unfortunately need some more notation.
Let
S(r)
be the set of all non-empty partitions of
[r]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,r\}
.
Given a tensor
A\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r}
and a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{I_{1},\dots,I_{m}\}\in S(r)
, let
A^{\mathcal{P}}
be the tensor
A
when viewed as an element of the space
\bR^{d^{|I_{1}|}}\otimes\dots\otimes\bR^{d^{|I_{m}|}}
in the natural way: the
j
th index of
A^{\mathcal{P}}
is defined by a tuple
(i_{t})_{t\in I_{j}}
which represents a subset of indices of the original tensor
A\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r}
.
For example, if
A\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes 3}
and
\mathcal{P}=\{\{1\},\{2,3\}\}
, the
(i_{1},(i_{2},i_{3}))
th entry of
A^{\mathcal{P}}\in\bR^{d}\otimes\bR^{d^{2}}
is the
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})
th entry of
A
.
The following theorem is Latała’s celebrated result
[
11
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

There is a constant
C_{r}
that only depends on the order
r
such that for every
p\geqslant 2
we have

\left\|\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\in[d]}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}(g_{t})_{i_{t}}\right\|_{p}\leqslant C_{r}\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in S(r)}p^{\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{2}}\left\|A^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}.

Latała also proved a lower bound that matches the above up to constants that only depend on
r
.
The centerpiece of Latała’s proof of
Theorem

1.5
is a bound on the expected
\ell_{2}
injective norm of Gaussian random tensors following the model (
1
).
His bound is much larger than the one implied by
Theorem

1.2
.
The proof of his estimate is based on bounding the relevant Gaussian process via a somewhat ad-hoc chaining argument that seems to be distinct from both Dudley’s integral and Talagrand’s celebrated generic chaining
[
19
]
.
Moreover, his proof is very technical and is quite difficult to follow.
We refer the curious reader to Talagrand’s book
[
19
, Section 15.2]
for a textbook level treatment of Latała’s proof for the case
r=3
that seems to contain all the ideas needed for the general result.

Since our main result recovers Latała’s estimate for the expected
\ell_{2}
injective norm of Gaussian tensors via a simple argument, we also obtain an elementary proof of
Theorem

1.5
.
The hardest part of the proof (and this paper in general) is digesting the tensor notation.
We include the proof in
Section

4
for completeness.

2.1
Relationship with existing variance parameters

In this subsection we discuss the relationship between our variance parameters and other parameters that have already appeared in the literature.
In
[
4
]
, the following variance parameters were defined for symmetric subgaussian random tensors
T\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r}
for
p\geqslant 2
and
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,r\}
:

\sigma_{(\ell,\mathcal{I}_{p})}^{2}=\sup_{x_{1}\dots,x_{r-\ell}\in B_{p}^{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|T_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{r-\ell},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}.

It is not hard to see that we can relate our variance parameters to these variance parameters.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
T\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r}
be a symmetric subgaussian random tensor.
For any
p\geqslant 2
and
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,r\}
we have

{\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{2}\leqslant\binom{r}{r-\ell}\sigma_{(\ell,\mathcal{I}_{p})}^{2}

Proof.

Using the symmetry of
T_{k}
we have

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=r-\ell\end{subarray}}\sup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\in B_{p}^{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|T_{k}^{\pi_{I}}(x_{\pi_{I}(1)},\dots,x_{\pi_{I}(r-\ell)},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=r-\ell\end{subarray}}\sup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{r-\ell}\in B_{p}^{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|T_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{r-\ell},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}=\binom{r}{r-\ell}\sigma_{(\ell,\mathcal{I}_{p})}^{2},

as claimed.
∎

The relevant variance parameters that are implicit in Latała’s work are based on the order
r+1
tensor
A=A(\{T_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n})\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r}\otimes\bR^{n}
that is defined by stacking the tensors
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{n}
along a new index
i_{r+1}
.
For the the sake of proving
Theorem

1.5
,
it will be useful to consider the relationship between the variance parameters when we consider subgaussian random tensors
T\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r-1}
“re-indexed” by some partition
\mathcal{P}\in S(r-1)
.
The following observation relates our variance parameters to the variance parameters implicitly defined in Latała’s work.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
T\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r-1}
be a subgaussian random tensor.
For any partition
\mathcal{P}\in S(r-1)
and
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,|\mathcal{P}|\}
we have

{\sigma}_{\ell,2}^{2}\left(T^{\mathcal{P}}\right)\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{Q}\subseteq S(r)\\
|\mathcal{Q}|=|\mathcal{P}|-\ell+1\end{subarray}}\left\|A^{\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
m
be the size of the partition
\mathcal{P}=\{I_{1},\dots,I_{m}\}
and let
d_{j}=d^{|I_{j}|}
.
Using the simple identity
\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}^{2}=\sup_{x\in B_{2}^{N}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}x_{i}\right)^{2}
we get

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{\ell,2}^{2}\left(T^{\mathcal{P}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[m]\\
|I|=m-\ell\end{subarray}}\sup_{x_{1}\in B_{2}^{d_{1}},\dots,x_{m}\in B_{2}^{d_{m}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\|\left(T_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}\right)^{\pi_{I}}(x_{\pi_{I}(1)},\dots,x_{\pi_{I}(m-\ell)},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[m]\\
|I|=m-\ell\end{subarray}}\sup_{x_{1}\in B_{2}^{d_{1}},\dots,x_{m}\in B_{2}^{d_{m}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{t^{\prime}}\in[d_{t^{\prime}}]\\
t^{\prime}\in I^{c}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{t}\in[d_{t}]\\
t\in I\end{subarray}}\left(T_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}\right)_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\prod_{t\in I}(x_{t})_{i_{t}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\\
|\mathcal{Q}|=m-\ell\end{subarray}}\left\|A^{\mathcal{Q}\cup\{i_{r}\cup\{i\in I:I\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{Q}\}\}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{Q}\subseteq S(r)\\
|\mathcal{Q}|=m-\ell+1\end{subarray}}\left\|A^{\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}^{2},

as desired.
∎

2.2
The PAC-Bayesian lemma

The following result is one incarnation of the PAC-Bayesian lemma.
It is a simple consequence of the Donsker-Varadhan variational formula.
We include a proof in
Appendix

A
for completeness.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
be independent random variables on a common measurable space
\mathcal{Z}
.
Let
\Theta
(the parameter space) be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\pi
be a probability measure (the prior) on
\Theta
.
Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}:\mathcal{Z}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable functions such that
\E_{Z_{i}}[\exp(f_{i}(Z_{i},\theta))]<\infty

\pi
-almost surely for all
i\in[n]
.
Then

\displaystyle\E_{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}}\left[\sup_{\rho\ll\pi}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left(\E_{\theta\sim\rho}f_{i}(Z_{i},\theta)-\E_{\theta\sim\rho}\log\left(\E_{Z_{i}}\exp(f_{i}(Z_{i},\theta))\right)\right)-\operatorname{KL}(\rho\|\pi)\right]\leqslant 0.

(5)

We call the probability measures
\rho
that appear above
posteriors
.
We should mention that there is a deviation version of the above lemma, but we will not use it here.

The terminology “parameter space”, “prior”, and “posterior” used in
Lemma

2.3
are inherited from the original statistical learning context where this lemma was first used.
Lemma

2.3
is at the heart of the
PAC-Bayesian approach
.
The PAC-Bayesian approach
[
18
,
14
,
15
]

Proofs of main results

We will need the following simple but clunky identity.
It states that the second moments of certain non-centered chaoses are determined by the contractions of their associated tensors.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X_{1}\in\bR^{d_{1}},\dots,X_{r}\in\bR^{d_{r}}
be independent zero mean vectors such that each
X_{i}
has covariance matrix
\Sigma_{i}=\beta^{-1}I_{d_{i}}
and let
A\in\bR^{d_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\bR^{d_{r}}
be a deterministic tensor.
For any fixed vectors
x_{1}\in\bR^{d_{1}},\dots,x_{r}\in\bR^{d_{r}}
we have

\E\left(\sum_{i_{1}\in[d_{1}],\dots,i_{r}\in[d_{r}]}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}(x_{t}+X_{t})_{i_{t}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-r+\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=\ell\end{subarray}}\|A^{\pi_{I}}(x_{\pi_{I}(1)},\dots,x_{\pi_{I}(\ell)},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof.

Expanding the quadratic yields

\displaystyle\E\left(\sum_{i_{1}\in[d_{1}],\dots,i_{r}\in[d_{r}]}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}(x_{t}+X_{t})_{i_{t}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},i_{r+1}\in[d_{1}],\dots,i_{r},i_{2r}\in[d_{r}]}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}A_{i_{r+1},\dots,i_{2r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}\E\left[(x_{t}+X_{t})_{i_{t}}(x_{t}+X_{t})_{i_{r+t}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},i_{r+1}\in[d_{1}],\dots,i_{r},i_{2r}\in[d_{r}]}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}A_{i_{r+1},\dots,i_{2r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}\left((x_{t})_{i_{t}}(x_{t})_{i_{r+t}}+(\Sigma_{t})_{i_{t},i_{r+t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},i_{r+1}\in[d_{1}],\dots,i_{r},i_{2r}\in[d_{r}]}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}A_{i_{r+1},\dots,i_{2r}}\sum_{I\subseteq[r]}\prod_{t\in I}(x_{t})_{i_{t}}(x_{t})_{i_{r+t}}\prod_{t^{\prime}\in I^{c}}(\Sigma_{t^{\prime}})_{i_{t^{\prime}},i_{r+t^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq[r]}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{t^{\prime}}=i_{r+t^{\prime}}\in[d_{t^{\prime}}]\\
t^{\prime}\in I^{c}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{t},i_{r+t}\in[d_{t}]\\
t\in I\end{subarray}}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}A_{i_{r+1},\dots,i_{2r}}\prod_{t\in I}(x_{t})_{i_{t}}(x_{t})_{i_{r+t}}\beta^{-r+|I|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-r+\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=\ell\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{t^{\prime}}=i_{r+t^{\prime}}\in[d_{t^{\prime}}]\\
t^{\prime}\in I^{c}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{t},i_{r+t}\in[d_{t}]\\
t\in I\end{subarray}}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}A_{i_{r+1},\dots,i_{2r}}\prod_{t\in I}(x_{t})_{i_{t}}(x_{t})_{i_{r+t}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-r+\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=\ell\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{t^{\prime}}\in[d_{t^{\prime}}]\\
t^{\prime}\in I^{c}\end{subarray}}\;\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{t}\in[d_{t}]\\
t\in I\end{subarray}}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\prod_{t\in I}(x_{t})_{i_{t}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-r+\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=\ell\end{subarray}}\|A^{\pi_{I}}(x_{\pi_{I}(1)},\dots,x_{\pi_{I}(\ell)},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\|_{F}^{2},

as desired.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result.

Proof of

.

We will use the PAC-Bayesian lemma (

) to bound the following quantity:

(6)

To do so, we need to make some choices.
We choose our parameter space to be
\Theta=\bR^{d_{1}}\times\dots\times\bR^{d_{r}}
and pick our prior

\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{-1}I_{d_{1}})\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{-1}I_{d_{r}})

over
\Theta

\beta>0
is arbitrary.
Every parameter
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r})\in\Theta
has a corresponding posterior
\rho_{\theta}=\mathcal{N}(\theta_{1},\beta^{-1}I_{d_{1}})\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{N}(\theta_{r},\beta^{-1}I_{d_{r}})

Define the set

As we will see below, these probability measures play a crucial role in how we apply the PAC-Bayesian lemma.
For

define the function

where the parameter
\lambda>0
will be set below.
We can now reformulate
Eq.

6
as a smooth process by exploiting the linearity of expectation.
Since
T_{k}
is multi-linear in
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
, we can re-write
Eq.

6
as

\E_{\xi_{1}\dots,\xi_{n}}\left[\sup_{x_{1}\in B_{p}^{d_{1}},\dots,x_{r}\in B_{p}^{d_{r}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\xi_{k}T_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\right]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\E_{\xi_{1}\dots,\xi_{n}}\left[\sup_{\rho_{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\E_{\theta\sim\rho_{\theta}}f_{k}(\xi_{k},\theta)\right].

(7)

Applying
Lemma

2.3
with
Z_{i}=\xi_{i}
to the smooth process on the right-hand side of
Eq.

7
implies
5
5

5

It is not hard to see that every
f_{i}
has a bounded MGF
\pi

\displaystyle\E_{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}}\left[\sup_{\rho_{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}}\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\E_{\theta\sim\rho_{\theta}}f_{k}(\xi_{k},\theta)-\E_{\theta\sim\rho_{\theta}}\log\left(\E_{\xi_{k}}\exp(f_{k}(\xi_{k},\theta))\right)\right)-\frac{\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{\theta}\|\pi)}{\lambda}\right]\leqslant 0.

(8)

Notice that we have restricted our attention to the set of probability measure
\mathcal{M}_{p}
.
This is crucial for our goal of computing explicit bounds on
Eq.

6
.
All that remains is to bound both the logarithm of the MGF terms and the KL divergence term
uniformly
for all
\rho_{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}

We begin with the former.
Using the sub-gaussianity of
\xi_{k}
(conditioned on
\theta\sim\rho_{\theta}
) followed by

3.1
gives us, for any fixed
\rho_{\theta}
, that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\E_{\theta\sim\rho_{\theta}}\log\left(\E_{\xi_{k}}\exp(f_{k}(\xi_{k},\theta))\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\E_{\theta\sim\rho_{\theta}}\left(\sum_{i_{1}\in[d_{1}],\dots,i_{r}\in[d_{r}]}(T_{k})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}(\theta_{t})_{i_{t}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-r+\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=\ell\end{subarray}}\|T_{k}^{\pi_{I}}(\theta_{\pi_{I}(1)},\dots,\theta_{\pi_{I}(\ell)},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-r+\ell}\sup_{\theta_{1}\in B_{p}^{d_{1}},\dots,\theta_{r}\in B_{p}^{d_{r}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[r]\\
|I|=\ell\end{subarray}}\|T_{k}^{\pi_{I}}(\theta_{\pi_{I}(1)},\dots,\theta_{\pi_{I}(\ell)},\cdot,\dots,\cdot)\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-r+\ell}\;{\sigma}_{(r-\ell,p)}^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-\ell}\;{\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{2}.

(9)

We now bound the KL divergence term.
From the standard equation for the KL divergence between two univariate Gaussians together with the tensorization of KL for product measures we can conclude the bound

\operatorname{KL}(\rho_{\theta}\|\pi)=\frac{\beta\sum_{t=1}^{r}\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\leqslant\frac{\beta\sum_{t=1}^{r}d_{t}^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}{2}.

(10)

We can use the uniform bounds in

which, after rearranging, yields

\displaystyle\E_{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}}\left[\sup_{\rho_{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}}\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\E_{\theta\sim\rho_{\theta}}f_{k}(\xi_{k},\theta)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{-\ell}\;{\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{2}+\frac{\beta\sum_{t=1}^{r}d_{t}^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}{2\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\beta^{1-\ell}{\sigma}_{(\ell,p)}^{2}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{r}d_{t}^{1-\frac{2}{p}}}.

The equality above follows from an explicit optimization of the parameter
\lambda>0

Since

In this section we provide a short proof of

We would like to emphasize once more that the proof follows Latała’s original argument, up to the use of

This difference drastically shortens and simplifies the

We will need the following corollary of
Theorem

1.2
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
T\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes r-1}
be a subgaussian random tensor.
For any partition
\mathcal{P}\in S(r-1)
and
p\geqslant 2
we have

\left(\E\left\|{T^{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant C_{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\in S(r)}p^{\frac{-|\mathcal{P}|+|\mathcal{Q}|}{2}}\left\|A(\{T_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n})^{\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}.

Proof.

Let
m\leqslant r-1
be the cardinality of the partition
\mathcal{P}=\{I_{1},\dots,I_{m}\}\in S(r-1)
.
For
i\in[m]
define
d_{i}=d^{|I_{i}|}
and notice that

\left\|T^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}=\sup_{x_{1}\in B_{2}^{d_{1}},\dots,x_{m}\in B_{2}^{d_{m}}}\sum_{i_{r}=1}^{d}g_{i_{r}}A_{i_{r}}^{\mathcal{P}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

is the supremum of a (complicated) Gaussian process.
Using the well known bound on the tails of the supremum of a gaussian process
[
19
, Lemma 2.10.6]
together with the equivalence of tails and moments
[
19
, Exercise 2.3.8]
gives us the following:

\left(\E\left\|T^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant\E\left\|T^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}+C\sqrt{p}\sigma

(11)

where the variance
\sigma^{2}
is defined as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{1}\in B_{2}^{d_{1}},\dots,x_{m}\in B_{2}^{d_{m}}}\E T^{\mathcal{P}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})^{2}=\sup_{x_{1}\in B_{2}^{d_{1}},\dots,x_{m}\in B_{2}^{d_{m}}}\sum_{i_{r}=1}^{d}A_{i_{r}}^{\mathcal{P}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})^{2}=\left\|A^{\mathcal{P}\cup\{\{r\}\}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}^{2}.

Applying
Theorem

1.2
with
\beta=p
to the right-hand side of
Eq.

11
and recalling
Proposition

2.2
yields

\left(\E\left\|T^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant C_{m}\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\in S(r)}p^{\frac{-m+|\mathcal{Q}|}{2}}\left\|A^{\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}+C\sqrt{p}\left\|A^{\mathcal{P}\cup\{\{r\}\}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\leqslant C_{m}^{\prime}\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\in S(r)}p^{\frac{-m+|\mathcal{Q}|}{2}}\left\|A^{\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}},

as desired.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

We will prove the result via induction on the order
r
.
For
r=1
standard bounds on the moments of a Gaussian random variable imply that

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}g_{i}\right\|_{p}\leqslant C\sqrt{p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=C\sqrt{p}\left\|A^{\{\{1\}\}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}.

Assume that the claim is true for chaoses of order
r-1
.
Let
T=A(\cdot,\dots,\cdot,g_{r})\in(\bR^{d})^{\otimes r-1}
.
We can write

X\coloneqq\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\in[d]}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\prod_{t=1}^{r}(g_{t})_{i_{t}}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots i_{r-1}\in[d]}T_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r-1}}\prod_{t=1}^{r-1}(g_{t})_{i_{t}}

and from our inductive hypothesis we have, conditioned on
g_{r}
, that

\left(\E\left(|X|^{p}\mid g_{r}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant C_{r-1}\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in S(r-1)}p^{\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{2}}\left\|T^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}.

Minkowski’s (triangle) inequality for
L^{p}
norms yields

\|X\|_{p}\leqslant C_{r-1}\left(\E\bigg(\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in S(r-1)}p^{\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{2}}\left\|T^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\bigg)^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant C_{r-1}\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in S(r-1)}p^{\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{2}}\left(\E\left\|T^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Applying
Corollary

4.1
to the right-hand side of the above gives us

\|X\|_{p}\leqslant C_{r-1}\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in S(r-1)}p^{\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{2}}C_{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\in S(r)}p^{\frac{-|\mathcal{P}|+|\mathcal{Q}|}{2}}\left\|A^{\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\leqslant C_{r}\sum_{\mathcal{P}\in S(r)}p^{\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{2}}\left\|A^{\mathcal{P}}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{2}},

as desired.

Appendix A
Proof of the PAC-Bayesian lemma

In this subsection we will prove
Lemma

2.3
.
The proof is a direct consequence of the following result, known as the Donsker-Varadhan variational formula.

Lemma A.1
.

Fix a probability space
(\Theta,\pi)
.
For any measurable function
g
such that
\E\limits_{\theta\sim\pi}\exp(g(\theta))<\infty
we have

\log\left(\E_{\theta\sim\pi}\exp(g(\theta))\right)=\sup_{\rho\ll\pi}\left\{\E_{\theta\sim\rho}g(\theta)-\operatorname{KL}(\rho\|\mu)\right\}.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{g}
be the probability measure with density
\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_{g}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta)=\frac{\exp(g(\theta))}{\E_{\theta\sim\pi}\exp(g(\theta))}.

For any
\rho\ll\pi
we have

\operatorname{KL}(\rho\|\pi_{g})=\int_{\Theta}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{g}}\right)\mathrm{d}\rho=\operatorname{KL}(\rho\|\pi)+\log\left(\E_{\theta\sim\pi_{g}}\exp(g(\theta))\right)-\E_{\theta\sim\rho}g(\theta).

Using the non-negativity of the KL divergence yields

\E_{\theta\sim\rho}g(\theta)-\operatorname{KL}(\rho\|\pi)\leqslant\log\left(\E_{\theta\sim\pi}\exp(g(\theta))\right).

(12)

Noticing that
\rho\ll\pi
was chosen arbitrarily together with the fact that
Eq.

12
is an equality when
\rho=\pi_{g}
yields the claim.
∎

We are now ready to prove the PAC-Bayesian lemma.
Define the function
g:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\times\theta\to\mathbb{R}
as

g(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(f_{k}(Z_{k},\theta)-\log\left(\E_{Z_{k}}\exp(f(Z_{k},\theta))\right)\right).

Using
Lemma

A.1
(conditioned on
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
), Jensen’s inequality, and the independence of
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
yields

\displaystyle\E_{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}}\left[\sup_{\rho\ll\pi}\E_{\theta\sim\rho}g(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},\theta)-\operatorname{KL}(\rho\|\pi)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\log\left(\E_{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}}\E_{\theta\sim\pi}\exp(g(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},\theta))\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\E_{\theta\sim\pi}\E_{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}(Z_{k},\theta)-\log\E_{Z_{k}}\exp(f_{k}(Z_{k},\theta))\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\E_{\theta\sim\pi}\frac{\E_{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}(Z_{k},\theta)\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}\E_{Z_{k}}\exp(f_{k}(Z_{k},\theta))}\right)=\log(1)=0,

as desired.

Appendix B
Proof of
Proposition

3.2

We begin with the lower bound.
Using the trivial inequality to lower bound the infimum gives us

\displaystyle\inf_{x>0}\left\{a_{0}x+\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}a_{\ell}x^{1-\ell}\right\}\geqslant\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\inf_{x_{\ell}>0}\left\{\frac{a_{0}x_{\ell}}{r-1}+a_{\ell}x_{\ell}^{1-\ell}\right\}\geqslant 2\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}(r-1)^{\frac{1-\ell}{\ell}}a_{0}^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}a_{\ell}^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\geqslant 2(r-1)^{\frac{1-r}{r}}\max_{2\leqslant\ell\leqslant r}a_{0}^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}a_{\ell}^{\frac{1}{\ell}},

where the second inequality follows from an explicit optimization and the final inequality follows from the fact that the function
\ell\to(r-1)^{\frac{1-\ell}{\ell}}
is non-increasing for
\ell,r\geqslant 2
.

To prove the upper bound, we will relax our optimization problem.
We can upper bound our original optimization problem as follows:

\inf_{x>0}a_{0}x+\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}a_{\ell}x^{1-\ell}\leqslant\inf_{x>0}\max_{\ell\in\{0,2,\dots,r\}}ra_{\ell}x^{1-\ell}.

(13)

It will be convenient to apply a change of variables.
Let
y=\ln(x)
and
b_{\ell}=\ln(a_{\ell})
for
\ell\in I
.
Define the functions
f(y)=b_{0}+y
and
g(y)=\max\limits_{\ell\in I}b_{\ell}+(1-\ell)y
.
We can re-write the right-hand side of the above as

\inf_{x>0}\max_{\ell\in\{0,2,\dots,r\}}ra_{\ell}x^{1-\ell}=\inf_{y\in\bR}\max_{\ell\in\{0,2,\dots,r\}}r\exp(b_{\ell}+(1-\ell)y)=\inf_{y\in\bR}r\exp\left(\max\left\{f(y),g(y)\right\}\right).

(14)

We will prove that there exists a
y^{\star}\in\bR
such that
g(y^{\star})=f(y^{\star})
, i.e.

b_{0}+y^{\star}=\max_{2\leqslant\ell\leqslant r}b_{\ell}+(1-\ell)y^{\star}.

(15)

Before we prove this, we will show that such a
y^{\star}
suffices to prove our desired upper bound.
Let
\ell^{\star}\in\{2,\dots,r\}
be any index achieving the maximum on the right-hand side of
Eq.

15
.
Solving for
y^{\star}
in terms of
b_{0},\ell^{\star}
and
b_{\ell^{\star}}
and plugging this back into
Eq.

14
yields

\inf_{y\in\bR}r\exp\left(\max\left\{f(y),g(y)\right\}\right)\leqslant r\exp\left(\max\left\{f(y^{\star}),g(y^{\star})\right\}\right)=r\exp\left(\left(\frac{\ell^{\star}-1}{\ell^{\star}}\right)b_{0}+\frac{1}{\ell^{\star}}b_{\ell^{\star}}\right).

Plugging the above back into our original optimization problem via
Eq.

13
yields

\inf_{x>0}a_{0}x+\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}a_{\ell}x^{1-\ell}\leqslant r\exp\left(\left(\frac{\ell^{\star}-1}{\ell^{\star}}\right)b_{0}+\frac{1}{\ell^{\star}}b_{\ell^{\star}}\right)=ra_{0}^{\frac{\ell^{\star}-1}{\ell^{\star}}}a_{\ell^{\star}}^{\frac{1}{\ell^{\star}}}\leqslant r\max_{2\leqslant\ell\leqslant r}a_{0}^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}a_{\ell}^{\frac{1}{\ell}},

as claimed.

We will now prove that such a
y^{\star}\in\bR
exists.
Notice that the functions
f(y)
and
g(y)
are continuous, so the function
h(y)=f(y)-g(y)
is continuous.
Furthermore, for a large enough
y_{1}
, we have
f(y_{1})>g(y_{1})
which implies
h(y_{1})>0
.
For a small enough
y_{2}
, we have
f(y_{2})<g(y_{2})
which implies
h(y_{2})<0
.
The intermediate value theorem implies that a
y^{\star}
satisfying
h(y^{\star})=0
exists.
This completes the proof.
