Abstract
. There exist Lakes of Wada in 𝕊 n , n ≥ 3 , \mathbb{S}^{n},n\geq 3, which are quasiconformally equivalent to a Euclidean ball and are John domains.

1.
Introduction

Classically, Lakes of Wada are three or more open sets in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
which have a common boundary. The common boundary is necessarily a continuum and it is called a Wada continuum. This definition generalizes immediately to higher dimensional spheres and further to connected manifolds (possibly with boundary):

A continuum
X
on a connected manifold
M
is a
Wada continuum
if
M\setminus X
has at least three connected components and each point in
X
is a common boundary point of all connected components of
M\setminus X
. In this case, the connected components of
M\setminus X
are called
Lakes of Wada
.

In
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, Lakes of Wada were constructed by Yoneyama
[
24
]
in 1917, who credited the idea to Takeo Wada. They were also known previously by Brouwer
[
3
]
in 1910. In 1951, Lubański
[
12
]
proved the existence of Lakes of Wada in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
for
n\geq 3
.

While Lakes of Wada in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
are conformally equivalent to the unit disk, by the Riemann Mapping Theorem,
they do not have the internal geometry of the unit disk. For example, Lakes of Wada are never John domains (Proposition
8.3
). This changes when we pass from two to higher dimensions.

In this article, we construct Wada continua in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
for
n\geq 3
, whose complementary components are both quasiconformally equivalent to the unit ball and John domains.

Theorem 1.1

(Lakes of Wada)
.

For
n\geq 3
and
m\geq 3
, there exists a Wada continuum in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
having exactly
m
complementary components, each of which is both quasiconformal to the Euclidean ball
B^{n}(0,1)
and a John domain.

This result on Wada continua on spheres is an immediate consequence of the following existence result for Wada continua on compact Riemannian manifolds with boundary.

In what follows, for an
n
-manifold
M
with boundary, we denote
\mathrm{int}M
the set of manifold points of
M
, that is, the set of points having neighborhoods homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and denote
\partial M
the manifold boundary of
M
, that is, non-manifold points of
M
; recall that
\partial M
is an
(n-1)
-manifold in its relative topology. The sets
\mathrm{int}M
and
\partial M
are called, respectively, the manifold interior and manifold boundary of
M
. To distinguish, we denote by
\partial_{\mathrm{top}}E
the topological boundary of a subset
E
of a topological space.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 3
,
m\geq 3
, and
M
be a compact connected Riemannian
n
-manifold with
m
boundary components. Then there exist a constant
\mathsf{K}=\mathsf{K}(n,M)>1
and a Wada continuum
X\subset\mathrm{int}M
having the following properties:

(1)

M\setminus X
has
m
connected components, each of which contains a connected component of
\partial M
; and

(2)

each component
\Omega
of
M\setminus X
is a John domain in
M
and is
\mathsf{K}
-quasiconformal to the product
(\Omega\cap\partial M)\times[0,1)
.

Remark 1.3
.

In the case of two boundary components, the proof of Theorem
1.2
yields a continuum, which is the the common boundary of two complementary domains, satisfying properties (
1
) and (
2
); see Theorem
8.1
for a statement.
We follow here the tradition and do not call continua having less than three complementary components Wada continua.

Continua
X\subset\mathrm{int}M
having no interior points and satisfying properties (
1
) and (
2
), exist also on compact manifolds
M
without boundary or having only one boundary component. In particular, for a closed and connected Riemannian
n
-manifold
M
, there exists a continuum
X
, having no interior points, for which
M\setminus X
is a John domain quasiconformal to an open Euclidean unit
n
-ball. Recall that a compact manifold without boundary is called closed. We refer to Corollary
4.7
for the constructions of these continua.

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to fix
m
mutually disjoint closed PL balls
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and apply Theorem
1.2
to manifold
M=\mathbb{S}^{n}\setminus\mathrm{int}(B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{m})
.

We recall now the definitions in these statements. A homeomorphism
f\colon M\to N
between two oriented Riemannian
n
-manifolds without boundary (
n\geq 2
) is
\mathsf{K}
-quasiconformal
for
\mathsf{K}\geq 1
, if
f
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,n}_{\operatorname{loc}}(M;N)
and satisfies the distortion inequality

(1)

\lVert Df\rVert^{n}\leq\mathsf{K}J_{f}\quad\text{a.e.}\ M,

where
\lVert Df\rVert
is the norm of the weak differential
Df
of
f
and
J_{f}
is the Jacobian determinant of
f
.
We say that a homeomorphism
f\colon M\to N
between Riemannian manifolds with boundary is
quasiconformal
if there exist open Riemannian manifolds
M^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}
containing manifolds
M
and
N
as smooth submanifolds with boundary, respectively, and
f
extends to a quasiconformal homeomorphism
f^{\prime}\colon M^{\prime}\to N^{\prime}
.

Remark
.

This definition of quasiconformal homeomorphism between Riemannian manifolds with boundary stems from the corresponding definition of diffeomorphisms. Note that, a diffeomorphism between compact Riemannian manifolds with boundary is a quasiconformal homeomorphism.
Note also that a homeomorphism between two compact Riemannian manifolds with boundary, if quasiconformal with respect to some Riemannian metrics, is quasiconformal with respect to all Riemannian metrics.
The definition in the case of manifolds with boundary given here is equivalent to the requirement that, for a homeomorphism
M\to N
between manifolds with boundary, the restriction
\mathrm{int}M\to\mathrm{int}N
is quasiconformal. This follows from classical boundary extension of quasiconformal homeomorphis and theorems of Tukia and Väisälä
[
20
,
21
]
.
There are also other equivalent definitions, in particular, a metric definition and a geometric definition using modulus of curve families; we refer to monographs of Ahlfors
[
1
]
and Väisälä
[
22
]
for the quasiconformal theory.

The concept of John domains is due to F. John
[
11
]
, and the term John domain was coined by Martio and Sarvas in
[
14
]
.
John domains occur frequently in the study of elasticity and geometric analysis.
A proper subdomain
D
of
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 2
is a
C
-
John domain for
C\geq 1
, if any two points
a,b\in D
can be joined by a rectifiable curve
\gamma\subset D
satisfying the length-distance estimate

(2)

\min\{s(\gamma(a,x)),s(\gamma(b,x))\}\leq C\,{\operatorname{dist}\,}(x,\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus D)\quad\text{for all}\,\,x\in\gamma,

where
\gamma(a,x)
(resp.
\gamma(b,x)
) is the part of
\gamma
between
a
and
x
(resp. between
b
and
x
) and
s(\cdot)
is the length.

An open and connected proper subset
D
of a Riemannian manifold
M
(possibly with boundary) is a
C
-John domain in
M
if it satisfies an length-distance estimate (
2
) for rectifiable curves with respect to
{\operatorname{dist}\,}(x,M\setminus D)
.

Roughly speaking any two points in a John domain
D
can be connected by a twisted double-cone in
D
having vertices at these two points.
If
f\colon B^{n}(0,r)\to M
is a quasiconformal embedding of a Euclidean ball
B^{n}(0,r)
into a Riemannian manifold, then the image
f(B^{n}(0,r/2))
remains somewhat round and has diameter roughly the distance to the complement
of the image. While many quasiconformal images of Euclidean balls are John domains, these two classes are not the same.
For example, for
n\geq 3
, a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that contains an inward-directed spike can not be mapped to a ball by a quasiconformal map, but it can be a John domain. On the other hand, a domain, which contains an outward-directed spike, is not a John domain, but it can be mapped by a quasiconformal map to a ball. We refer to
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
for the explanation.

Apart from the intrinsic geometry of higher dimensional Lakes of Wada, our interest in the Wada continua stems from their roles in the construction of quasiregular mappings
and in complex dynamics. Recall that a continuous mapping
f\colon M\to N
between oriented Riemannian manifolds is
quasiregular
if
f
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,n}_{\operatorname{loc}}(M;N)
and satisfies the distortion inequality (
1
) for some
\mathsf{K}\geq 1
.

Rickman
[
17
]
used two dimensional complexes – called map complexes – in his construction of a quasiregular map
\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{S}^{3}
omitting a given finite set in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Analogous
2
-dimensional complexes were later used by Heinonen and Rickman
[
9
,
10
]
– first under the name of separating polyhedra in
[
9
]
and then under the name separating complexes in
[
10
]
– to construct, for example, quasiregular maps
\mathbb{S}^{3}\to\mathbb{S}^{3}
, whose branch sets contain Antoine’s necklaces, and quasiregular maps
B^{3}\to B^{3}
with no radial limits in the limit set of certain Kleinian groups.
The common property of these constructions is that the distortion of the map is controlled by a quantity depending only on the dimension
and topological data associated to the construction. In particular, in these constructions the distortion does not put restriction on the (global) degree of the mappings.
We refer to
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
, and
[
17
]
for the terminology and precise statements.
In higher dimensions
n\geq 4
, Drasin and the first named author used a cubical version of separating complexes (with respect to standard cubical structure of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) in
[
5
]
to construct a quasiregular map
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
omitting a given finite set in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
; see the end of the introduction in
[
5
]
for discussion.

In complex dynamics, Martí–Pete, Rempe, and Waterman recently gave a positive answer in
[
13
]
to a question of Fatou from 1926 on the existence of a Wada continuum in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
which is the common boundary of infinitely many Fatou components of a transcendental entire function
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
. In dimensions
n\geq 3
, an analog of Fatou’s question asks:
Does there exist, for
n\geq 3
, a Wada continuum in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
which is a common boundary of Fatou components of a uniformly quasiregular mapping
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
?
We refer to
[
13
]
for the history of Fatou’s problem and examples of Wada continua in dynamics.

Outline of the proof

The Wada continuum
X
in Theorem
1.2
is found by an iterative subdivision process starting from a cubical structure
K
on the manifold
M
.

We begin by passing from the Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
to a cubical
n
-complex
K
having space
|K|=M
. The cubical complex carries a natural polyhedral metric
d_{K}

for which the metric space
(|K|,d_{K})
is quasisimilar to
(M,d_{g})
, where
d_{g}
is the length metric induced by the Riemannian metric
g
; see Proposition
2.1
.

We take next a monotone sequence
K_{0}=K,K_{1},K_{2},\ldots
of cubical subdivisions of
K
in which each complex
K_{\ell+1}
is obtained by subdividing the cubes in
K_{\ell}
with a fixed rule. This subdivision respects the metric
d_{K}
in the sense that cubes in
K_{\ell}
have side length
3^{-\ell}
with respect to metric
d_{K}
.

The crux of the proof is to construct
a sequence
Z_{0}\subset K_{0},Z_{1}\subset K_{1},Z_{2}\subset K_{2},\ldots
of
(n-1)
-dimensional (separating) subcomplexes in the interior of
|K|

having the property that each connected component of
|K|\setminus|Z_{\ell}|
contains exactly one boundary component of
|K|
and is quasiconformal to a collar of that boundary component.
The Wada continuum
X
is obtained as the Hausdorff limit of the spaces
|Z_{\ell}|\subset M
of complexes
Z_{\ell}
.

Heuristically, the inductive step
from
Z_{\ell}
to
Z_{\ell+1}
may be viewed as a controlled way of trading
n
-dimensional cubes in
K_{\ell+1}
between
components of
|K|\setminus|Z_{\ell}|
near
Z_{\ell}
. The aims of this trading are threefold:

(1)

at each step, components of
|K|\setminus|Z_{\ell+1}|
remain in one-to-one correspondence with respect to the components of
|\partial K|
,

(2)

the maximum distance from each cube in
Z_{\ell}
to any of the components of
|K|\setminus|Z_{\ell}|
tends to zero as
\ell\to\infty
, and

(3)

each
|K|\setminus|Z_{\ell+1}|
is
\mathsf{K}
-quasiconformal to a fixed collar of
|\partial K|
for a distortion constant
\mathsf{K}
independent of
\ell
.

One difficulty of the construction stems from condition (2), which may be viewed as a Wada-type condition for complexes
Z_{\ell}
.
More precisely, each
(n-1)
-cube
q
in
Z_{\ell}
is a face of exacty two
n
-cubes in
K_{\ell}
. Thus
q
is
on the boundary of at most two of the components of
|K|\setminus|Z_{\ell}|
. By (2), however,
q
is required to be close to all components of
|K|\setminus|Z_{\ell}|
.

Organization of the article

We discuss, in Section
2
, cubical structures on Riemannian manifolds and the polyhedral metrics on cubical complexes. We also introduce some terminology related to cubical complexes.

In Section
3
, we discuss first adjacency graphs, realizations of subgraphs of adjacency graphs as complexes, and cut-graphs. We define next a special class of complexes, called tunnels, and prove a tunnel contracting lemma which is applied repeatedly in our proof. Finally, we introduce the notion of a good complex, which comprises all essential properties of a cubical complex that are needed in the construction.

Separating complexes are defined in Section
4
, along with the statement of a theorem on the evolution of separating complexes, which is used to guide our construction.

In Section
5
, we introduce the notion of indentation and prove a flattening theorem for indentations, which is used to control the quasiconformality constant of the domains.

The iterative
construction of the sequence
Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots
of separating complexes is given in Sections
6
and
7
.
In Section
6
we discuss the building blocks – reservoir-canal systems – for our construction and the method of channeling. In Section
7
we discuss the iterative processes.
Quasiconformal stability of the sequence
Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots
is proved in Section
7.5
. Finally Theorem
1.2
is proved in Section
8
. In this final section, we also show that two dimensional Lakes of Wada are not John-domains.

2.
Cubical structures on Riemannian manifolds

In this section, we discuss cubical structures on Riemannian manifolds. Recall that every Riemannian
n
-manifold
(M,g)
admits a triangulation
T
in which every
n
-simplex
(\sigma,g|_{\sigma})
is bilipschitz to an affine Euclidean
n
-simplex. We refer to Cairns
[
4
]
, Whitehead
[
23
]
, and Munkres
[
15
]
for constructions; see also e.g. Dyer, Vegter, and Wintraecken
[
6
]
, Peltonen
[
16
]
, or Saucan
[
18
]
for triangulations with controlled geometry.

In what follows, we call a simplicial complex
T
having space
M
a triangulation of
M
. We also denote
d_{T}
the simplicial metric on
M
under which simplices of
T
are similar to the standard simplices
\Delta^{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
, for
k=0,\ldots,n
, in the Euclidean space.

Barycentric subdivision of a triangulation induces a cubical structure on
M
(see e.g. Shtan’ko and Shtogrin
[
19
]
), which carries a natural polyhedral metric. Although this reduction of a Riemannian manifold to a metric cubical complex is known to the experts,
we devote
this section to establish this connection. We discuss this in three parts: cubical complexes and related notations, polyhedral metrics on cubical complexes, and finally the change of metric on barycentric subdivisions from cubical to simplicial complexes, then from simplicial to cubical complexes.

The following proposition allows us to pass from compact Riemannian manifolds with boundary to finite cubical complexes in our main theorem (Theorem
1.2
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Associated to each compact Riemannian manifold with boundary
(M,g)
, there exist a cubical complex
K
with a metric
d_{K}
, in which each cube of
K
is isometric a Euclidean unit cube, and a quasisimilar homeomorphism
(M,g)\to(K,d_{K})
.

Recall that a map
f\colon X\to Y
is
L
-bilipschitz for
L\geq 1
, if

L^{-1}\,d(x,y)\leq d(f(x),f(y))\leq L\,d(x,y)\quad\text{for all}\,\,\,x,x^{\prime}\in X.

A map
f\colon X\to Y
is
L
-quasisimilar for
L\geq 1
,
if there exists
\lambda>0
for which

L^{-1}\lambda\,d(x,y)\leq d(f(x),f(y))\leq L\lambda\,d(x,y)\quad\text{for all}\,\,\,x,x^{\prime}\in X.

The proof of this proposition is concluded in Section
2.2
. We first introduce the polyhedral metric; see e.g. Bridson and Haefliger
[
2
, Chapter I.7]
for an analogous discussion. After the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we introduce the terminology of refinements of a cubical complex in Section
2.1.3
, and the notion of good complexes in Section
3.3
.

2.1.
Preliminaries on cubical complexes

As usual, a
simplicial complex
is a complex whose elements are cells with the standard simplicial structure.

Definition 2.2
.

A collection
K
of cells is a
cell complex
if, for
C,C^{\prime}\in K
,
C\cap C^{\prime}\in K\cup\{\varnothing\}
.

It is immediate that the restriction
K|_{C}=\{C^{\prime}\subset C\colon C^{\prime}\in K\}
of a cell complex
K
to its cell
C\in K
is a cell-complex.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we call the complex
\mathsf{C}_{k}=\{\{0\},\{1\},[0,1]\}^{k}
a
standard cubical structure on
[0,1]^{k}
; we also set
\mathsf{C}_{0}=\{\{0\},\{1\},[0,1]\}^{0}=\{\{0\}\}
and
\mathsf{C}_{-1}=\{\{0\},\{1\},[0,1]\}^{-1}=\varnothing
.

Given a
k
-cell
Q
and a homeomorphism
\phi\colon Q\to[0,1]^{k}
, we call the complex

\mathsf{C}_{\phi}(Q)=\{\phi^{-1}q\colon q\in\mathsf{C}_{k}\}

a
standard cubical structure on
Q
(induced by
\phi
)
. If there is no ambiguity, we simply write
\mathsf{C}(Q)=\mathsf{C}_{\phi}(Q)
. The
(k-1)
-cubes and
(k-2)
-cubes in
\mathsf{C}_{\phi}(Q)
are called
faces and edges of
Q
, respectively.

Cubical complexes are cell complexes, whose cells have cubical structure. In what follows, we use the following slightly more restrictive definition.

Definition 2.3
.

A cell complex
K
is a
cubical
n
-complex for
n\geq 0
if

(1)

for each
Q\in K
, the restriction
K|_{Q}=\mathsf{C}(Q)
is a standard cubical structure on
Q
,

(2)

for each pair
Q,Q^{\prime}\in K
of cells,
\mathsf{C}(Q)|_{Q\cap Q^{\prime}}=\mathsf{C}(Q^{\prime})|_{Q\cap Q^{\prime}}
and the composition
(\phi_{Q}|_{Q\cap Q^{\prime}})\circ(\phi_{Q^{\prime}}|{Q\cap Q^{\prime}})^{-1}\colon\phi_{Q^{\prime}}(Q\cap Q^{\prime})\to\phi_{Q}(Q\cap Q^{\prime})
is an isometry,
and

(3)

each cell in
K
is contained in an
n
-cell in
K
.

The
k
-cells in a cubical
n
-complex
K
are called
k
-cubes. A collection of cells
P\subset K
is a
cubical
k
-subcomplex of
K
if
P
is a cubical
k
-complex.

In this definition, we tacitly assume that, for each
n
-cell
Q
in
K
, we have fixed a homeomorphism
\phi_{Q}\colon Q\to[0,1]^{n}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Property (
3
) is not typically part of the definition of a complex, and complexes satisfying (
3
) are called in the literature either homogeneous or pure complexes. The stronger condition holds for cubical complexes whose spaces are manifolds.
We emphasize that, in what follows, we consider cubical complexes and their subcomplexes satisfying this additional assumption.

Also the second part of property (
2
) is not typically part of the definition of a cubical complex. This condition, however, yields a well-defined polyhedral metric
d_{K}
on a cubical
n
-complex
K
having connected space
|K|
. Before discussing construction of polyhedral metrics on cubical complexes, we finish this general discussion with two definitions.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a homeomorphism
\phi\colon X\to|K|
, where
K
is a cubical
n
-complex, we call

\phi^{*}K=\{\phi^{-1}Q\colon Q\in K\}

the
pull-back of the complex
K
under
\phi
; clearly
|\phi^{*}K|=X
. A homeomorphism
\phi\colon|K|\to|K^{\prime}|
, between spaces of cubical complexes
K
and
K^{\prime}
, is called a
cubical isomorphism
if
\phi^{*}(K^{\prime})=K
.

Definition 2.6
.

We say that
n
-subcomplexes
K_{1},\ldots,K_{\ell}
of a cubical
n
-complex
K
are
essentially disjoint
if
K_{i}
and
K_{j}
do not have
n
-cubes in common for
i\neq j
.
We say that
\{K_{1},\ldots,K_{\ell}\}
is an
essential partition of a cubical
n
-complex
K
if
K=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{\ell}
, and
K_{1},\ldots,K_{\ell}
are essentially disjoint.

2.1.1.
Polyhedral metric of a cubical complex

We define first, for each
n
-cube
Q
in
K
, a metric
d_{Q}\colon Q\times Q\to[0,\infty)
by the formula
d_{Q}(x,y)=|\phi_{Q}(x)-\phi_{Q}(y)|
for
x,y\in Q
. By (
2
) in Definition
2.3
,
d_{Q}(x,y)=d_{Q^{\prime}}(x,y)
for
x,y\in|Q\cap Q^{\prime}|
.

We call a sequence
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})
in
|K|
a
K
-chain if
x_{i}
and
x_{i-1}
belong to the same
n
-cube in
K
. Since
|K|
is connected, any two points
x
and
y
in
|K|
may be connected by a
K
-chain. Thus we may define the polyhedral metric
d_{K}\colon|K|\times|K|\to[0,\infty)
of
|K|
by the formula

d_{K}(x,y)=\inf_{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})}\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{Q_{i}}(x_{i-1},x_{i}),

where the infimum is taken over
K
-chains connecting
x
and
y
and each
Q_{i}
is an
n
-cube containing
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i}
.

Remark 2.7
.

With respect to the polyhedral metric
d_{K}
on a cubical
n
-complex
K
, each
n
-cube
Q
in
K
is isometric to
[0,1]^{n}
and
d_{Q}(x,y)=d_{K}(x,y)
for
x,y\in|Q|
.

2.1.2.
Subcomplexes of cubical complexes

Let
K
be a cubical
n
-complex and
0\leq k\leq n
. We denote

K^{[k]}=\{q\in K\colon q\text{ is a $k$-cube}\}
the collection of all
k
-cubes in
K
and
K^{(k)}=\bigcup_{\ell\leq k}K^{[\ell]}
the
k
-skeleton of
K
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
S
be a subset of a cubical
n
-complex
K
. The subcomplex
\mathrm{Span}_{K}(S)

spanned by
S
is the smallest subcomplex of
K
that contains
S
.

We say that an
(n-1)
-cube
q
in a cubical
n
-complex
K
is a
one-sided
if there exists only one
n
-cube in
K
containing
q
.
The boundary
\partial K
of a cubical
n
-complex is the subcomplex of
K
spanned by all one-sided cubes, that is,

\partial K=\mathrm{Span}_{K}\left(\{q\in K^{[n-1]}\colon q\text{ is one-sided}\}\right).

A subcomplex
\Sigma\subset K
is a
boundary component of
K
if
|\Sigma|
is a component of
|\partial K|
. Note that, for a cubical complex
K
having a manifold with boundary as its space
|K|

is the manifold boundary of

.

Let

and

be subcomplexes of

the

and

Our definition of star is more restrictive than the usual definition; in the usual definition, a star is spanned by all cubes meeting

Definition 2.9
.

The

\mathrm{St}_{K}(q)
of a

The

.

The star of a subcomplex yields the notion of locally Euclidean complexes. For the definition, let
\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the standard cubical structure on

in which every cube is of unit size and corners in the integer lattice

Definition 2.10
.

Let

.

Remark 2.11
.

Let

be an

-cube,

-cube, and

-cube in

\mathrm{St}_{\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(Q)

2.1.3.
Refinement of cubical complexes

The standard cubical structure
\mathsf{C}_{n}
of the Euclidean
n
-cube
[0,1]^{n}
admits a natural subdivision into
3^{n}
congruent
n
-cubes.

For each
v\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}
, let
q_{v}=\frac{1}{3}\left(v+[0,1]^{n}\right)
and
\iota_{v}\colon q_{v}\to[0,1]^{n}
be the congruence
x\mapsto 3^{n}(x-v)
; we denote
\mathsf{C}(q_{v})=\mathsf{C}_{\iota_{v}}(q_{v})
. Then cubes
q_{v}
,
v\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}
, are congruent Euclidean cubes which cover
[0,1]^{n}
, have mutually disjoint interiors, and satisfy
q_{v}\cap q_{v^{\prime}}\in\mathsf{C}(q_{v})\cap\mathsf{C}(q_{v^{\prime}})
for
v,v^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}
. Then

\mathsf{R}_{n}=\bigcup_{v\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}}\mathsf{C}(q_{v})

is a cubical
n
-complex with space
[0,1]^{n}
, and is called the
standard refinement of
\mathsf{C}_{n}
.

Let now
K
be a cubical
n
-complex and, for each
Q\in K^{[n]}
, let
\phi_{Q}\colon Q\to[0,1]^{n}
be the fixed cubical isomorphism in the definition of cubical
n
-complex.
For each
Q\in K^{[n]}
, we take

\mathrm{Ref}(Q)=\phi_{Q}^{*}(\mathsf{R}_{n}).

Since the transition maps for
n
-cubes having a common face
q=Q\cap Q^{\prime}\in K^{[n-1]}
are isometries, we have that
\mathrm{Ref}(Q)|_{q}=\mathrm{Ref}(Q^{\prime})|_{q}
. Further, since each
n
-cube
Q^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Ref}(K)^{[n]}
is contained in an
n
-cube
Q\in K^{[n]}
, we have
\phi_{Q}(Q^{\prime})=q_{v}
for some
v\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}
and we may define
\phi_{Q^{\prime}}\colon Q^{\prime}\to[0,1]^{n}
to be the map
\phi_{Q^{\prime}}=\iota_{v}\circ\phi_{Q}|_{Q^{\prime}}
.
Hence

\mathrm{Ref}(K)=\bigcup_{Q\in K^{[n]}}\mathrm{Ref}(Q)

is a well-defined cubical
n
-complex. We call
\mathrm{Ref}(K)
is the
standard refinement of
K
. For
k\geq 1
, we call the cubical
n
-complex
\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)=\mathrm{Ref}(\mathrm{Ref}^{k-1}(K))
the
k
th iterated refinement of
K
.

For
x,x^{\prime}\in|\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)|=|K|
, we have

d_{\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)}(x,x^{\prime})=3^{k}d_{K}(x,x^{\prime})

under the two different metrics. To be consistent in all refinement scales, we equip all refinements
\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)
,
k\geq 1
, with the metric
d_{K}
.

Convention 2.12

(Standard metric on
\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)
)
.

Let
K
be a cubical
n
-complex and
d_{K}
the polyhedral metric of
K
. We equip
\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)
, for
k\geq 1
, with the metric
d_{K}=3^{-k}d_{\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)}
. We call
d_{K}
the standard metric on
\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)
.

2.2.
From simplicial to cubical structure

We construct now, using the barycentric subdivision, a cubical complex from a barycentric subdivision of a simplicial complex. The general case stems from the case of a single simplex.
Let

\Delta_{n}=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

be the standard
n
-simplex.

In the following statement, an
n
-cube
Q
is a polyhedron with a cubical structure
\mathsf{C}(Q)
isomorphic to
\mathsf{C}_{n}
, and two
n
-cubes
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
are
congruent
if there exists a cubical isomorphism
\psi\colon Q\to Q^{\prime}
which is an isometry.

Lemma 2.13
.

There exists a cubical complex
\Delta_{n}^{\square}
on
\Delta_{n}
consisting of
(n+1)
congruent
n
-cubes
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{n}
, for which the restriction
\Delta_{n}^{\square}|_{\sigma}
to each face
\sigma
of
\Delta_{n}
is a well-defined cubical
(n-1)
-complex consisting of
n
congruent
(n-1)
-cubes.

Proof.

We denote
\Delta_{n}=[v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}]
and let
X
be the barycentric subdivision of
\Delta_{n}
for which the simplices of the same dimension are congruent. For each vertex
v_{i}
of
\Delta_{n}
, let
\mathrm{St}_{X}(v_{i})
be the star of
v_{i}
in the complex
X
and let
Q_{i}=|\mathrm{St}(v_{i})|
.

We claim that each
Q_{i}
admits a cubical structure
\mathsf{C}(Q_{i})
isomorphic to
\mathsf{C}_{n}
on
[0,1]^{n}
for which each
k
-cube in
\mathsf{C}(Q_{i})
,
0\leq k\leq n
,
is a union of
k
-simplices in
X
. The argument is an induction on dimension.
The claim clearly holds for
n=1
.

Suppose that the claim holds for dimension
n-1
. For dimension
n
, it suffices to prove the claim for the vertex
v_{0}
.

By the induction assumption, for each
i\neq 0
, the restriction of
\mathrm{St}_{X}(v_{0})
to the face
[v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1},\hat{v}_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n}]
admits a cubical structure
K_{0\,i}
which has one
(n-1)
-cube and whose
k
-cubes,
k\in[0,n-1],
are unions of
k
-simplices in
X|_{Q_{0}}
. Thus
K_{0}=K_{0\,1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{0\,n}
is the cubical
(n-1)
-complex
\mathrm{St}_{X^{(n-1)}}(v_{0})
.

Let
v
be the unique vertex of
X
in the interior of
\Delta_{n}
. Let
L_{0}
be the link of vertex
v_{0}
in
X
and
\tau_{0}
be the face of
\Delta_{n}
opposite to
v_{0}
. Since
X
is the barycentric subdivision of
\Delta_{n}
, link
L_{0}
is isomorphic to the barycentric subdivision
X|_{\tau_{0}}
of
\tau_{0}
.
By the induction assumption,
X|_{\tau_{0}}
admits a cubical
(n-1)
-complex consisting of
n
cubes of dimension
(n-1)
and is the star of the unique vertex in the interior of
\tau_{0}
.
Thus the same holds true for
L_{0}
and
v
. We denote the corresponding cubical
(n-1)
-complex on
|L_{0}|
by
K^{\prime}_{0}
.

Then the union
K_{0}\cup K^{\prime}_{0}
is a cubical complex on the boundary of
\mathrm{St}_{X}(v_{0})
, obtained by taking unions of simplices in
X
. Moreover,
|K_{0}|
and
|K^{\prime}_{0}|
are
(n-1)
-cells and
\partial Q_{0}=|K_{0}\cup K^{\prime}_{0}|
is an
(n-1)
-sphere. Thus
Q_{0}
is an
n
-cell and is the space of the cubical complex
\mathsf{C}(Q_{0})=K_{0}\cup K^{\prime}_{0}\cup\{Q_{0}\}
which is isomorphic to
\mathsf{C}_{n}
.

We fix, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, an isometry
\rho_{i}\colon\Delta_{n}\to\Delta_{n}
which maps
v_{i}
to
v_{0}
. Then
\rho_{i}
fixes the barycenter
v
of
\Delta_{n}
and satisfies
\rho_{i}(Q_{i})=Q_{0}
. Thus
Q_{i}
admits a standard cubical structure
\mathsf{C}(Q_{i})
for which
\rho_{i}^{*}(\mathsf{C}(Q_{i}))=\mathsf{C}(Q_{0})
.
The
n
-cubes
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{n}
are congruent by definition. The fact that
\mathsf{C}(Q_{i})|_{Q_{i}\cap Q_{j}}=\mathsf{C}(Q_{j})|_{Q_{i}\cap Q_{j}}
for

follows from the induction assumption and the Euclidean barycentric subdivision of
\Delta_{n}
.

We set
\Delta_{n}^{\square}
to be the union of complexes
\mathsf{C}(Q_{0}),\ldots,\mathsf{C}(Q_{n})
and for brevity, denote complex
\mathsf{C}(Q_{i})
by

.

It remains to show the compatibility of the standard cubical structures.
Let
h\colon Q_{0}\to[0,1]^{n}
be a cubical isomorphism for which
h(v_{0})=0
,
h(v)=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{n}
, and
h
conjugates
each isometry of
Q_{0}
that fixes
v_{0}
and
v
to an isometry of
[0,1]^{n}
.
Set
h_{0}=h\colon Q_{0}\to[0,1]^{n}
and, for

,
h_{i}=h\circ\rho_{i}\colon Q_{i}\to[0,1]^{n}
.

Since
\rho_{j}(Q_{j}\cap Q_{i})
and
\rho_{i}(Q_{j}\cap Q_{i})
are unions of faces of
Q_{0}
in
K_{0}^{\prime}
, which differ by an isometry,
maps
h_{i}\cap h_{j}^{-1}|_{h_{j}(Q_{j}\cap Q_{i})}\colon h_{j}(Q_{j}\cap Q_{i})\to h_{i}(Q_{j}\cap Q_{i})
are Euclidean isometries. ∎

An immediate consequence of the previous lemma is the existence of a cubical complex
T^{\square}
on the space
|T|
of a simplicial complex

.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
T
be a simplicial
n
-complex for which
(|T|,d_{T})
is a Riemannian manifold. Then there exists a cubical complex
K=T^{\square}
on
|T|
for which the restriction
T^{\square}|_{\sigma}
to each
n
-simplex
\sigma\in T
is isomorphic to
\Delta_{n}^{\square}
.
Moreover, the polyhedral metric

and the Riemannian metric

are

-quasisimilar.

Proof.

We fix, for each
n
-simplex
\sigma\in T
, an affine simplicial homeomorphism
\psi_{\sigma}\colon\sigma\to\Delta_{n}
. Since the transition maps
\psi_{\sigma^{\prime}}\circ\psi_{\sigma}^{-1}
are Euclidean isometries on common faces, the pull-back structures
\psi_{\sigma}^{*}(\Delta_{n}^{\square})
and
\psi_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{*}(\Delta_{n}^{\square})
agree on
\sigma\cap\sigma^{\prime}
. We define

T^{\square}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in T^{[n]}}\,\psi_{\sigma}^{*}(\Delta_{n}^{\square}).

Recall that, by the proof of Lemma
2.13
,
all
n
-cubes
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{n}
in
\Delta_{n}^{\square}
are congruent and that maps

are cubical isomorphisms.

Let
\sigma
be an
n
-simplex in

and

be an
n
-cube in

. Let

be the index for which
\psi_{\sigma}(Q)=Q_{i_{Q}}
. Set
\phi_{Q}=h_{i_{Q}}\circ\psi_{\sigma}|_{Q}\colon Q\to[0,1]^{n}
for each

. It is now straightforward to check that transition maps

are Euclidean isometries on common faces.

Note that, for each
n
-simplex
\sigma
in

and
n
-cube

,
\psi_{\sigma}
is a quasisimilarity with a constant depending on

and

is a bilipschitz map in Euclidean metric with a constant depending on the dimension

.
Thus the map
\phi_{Q}\colon(Q,d_{T}|_{Q})\to[0,1]^{n}
is an

-quasisimilarity.
From this, the

-quasisimilarity between the two metric follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

A Riemannian
n
-manifold

admits a simplicial triangulation

in which every

∎

3.
Adjacency graphs

In this section, we discuss adjacency graphs of cubical complexes and their subgraphs. We study cubical complexes, called realizations, whose adjacency graphs are given subgraphs. Of particular interest is the class of cut-graphs and their realizations.
Using adjacency graphs, we introduce at the end of this section notions of tunnels and good complexes. This section may be viewed as a preliminary for the study of separating complexes in Section
4
.

We begin by recalling that a complex
K
is
connected
if its space
|K|
is connected. Similarly, a subcomplex
P\subset K
is a
component
of
K
if
|P|
is connected component of
|K|

In what follows, we use a stronger notion of connectedness for cubical complexes based on adjacency.

Definition 3.1
.

Two
n
-cubes
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
in a cubical
n
-complex
K
are said to be
adjacent
if they have a common face
Q\cap Q^{\prime}
in
K^{[n-1]}
.

The pair

\Gamma(K)=\left(K^{[n]},\left\{\{Q,Q^{\prime}\}\colon Q\neq Q^{\prime},\ Q\cap Q^{\prime}\in K^{[n-1]}\right\}\right)

is the
adjacency graph of
K
. We call cubes in
K^{[n]}
vertices and adjacent pairs
\{Q,Q^{\prime}\}
edges.

A cubical
n
-complex
K
is said to be
adjacently-connected
if its adjacency graph
\Gamma(K)
is connected.

Clearly, two
n
-cubes
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
of
K
may have non-empty intersection without being adjacent. Thus a cubical
n
-complex may have a topologically connected space without being adjacently-connected.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
K
be a cubical
n
-complex in which every
(n-1)
-cube is the face of at most two
n
-cubes. Then each edge
\{Q,Q^{\prime}\}
of
\Gamma(K)
may be identified with the face
Q\cap Q^{\prime}\in K^{[n-1]}
, hence the set of edges of
\Gamma(K)
with a subset of
K^{[n-1]}
.

3.1.
Realization of a subgraph

Let
K
be a cubical
n
-complex. Let

be a subgraph of the adjacency graph
\Gamma(K)
, and let

\mathrm{Span}_{K}(G)=\mathrm{Span}_{K}\left(\{Q\in K^{[n]}\colon Q\in V_{G}\}\right)

be the

.

We define a cubical

-complex

.

For the definition, let

S_{G}=\bigsqcup_{Q\in V_{G}}Q\quad\text{and}\quad F_{G}=\bigsqcup_{Q\in V_{G}}\mathsf{C}(Q)

be
the disjoint union of the

-cubes

By the definition,

Remark 3.6

The

Lemma 3.7

Let

Since

Q=Q_{0}
and
Q^{\prime}=Q_{1}
.

Thus there exists a PL homeomorphism
h_{Q}\colon|\mathcal{R}_{K}(G)|\to|R^{\prime}_{G}|
for which

for all

-cubes
q\in\mathcal{R}_{K}(G)

q\neq Q,Q^{\prime}
. By removing leaves of

to an

-cube, which is an

-cell.
Hence

is an
n
-cell.
∎

3.1.1.
Cut-graphs

A particular class of subgraphs of
\Gamma(K)
is obtained by cutting
\Gamma(K)
with a codimension one subcomplex

of

Definition 3.8
.

Let

be a cubical

-complex and

be a cubical
(n-1)
-subcomplex. The

\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z)=\left(K^{[n]},\left\{\{Q\cap Q^{\prime}\}\in\Gamma(K)\colon Q\cap Q^{\prime}\not\in Z\right\}\right).

We call the realization
\mathcal{R}_{K}(\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z))

the

relative to

, and the

\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z)
-quotient map

A component

does not meet

if it is not an inner component.

3.1.2.
\Sigma
-components of cut-graphs and their lifts

In this article, we are mainly interested in the case when

is in the interior of

.

Suppose that

is in the interior of
K
and
\Sigma\subset\partial K
is a boundary component.
We denote by
\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z;\Sigma)
the unique connected component of
\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z)
for which

\Sigma\subset\mathrm{Span}_{K}(\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z;\Sigma))\subset K

and call the span

\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)=\mathrm{Span}_{K}(\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z;\Sigma)),

the
\Sigma
-component of

.

Remark 3.9
.

In general,
Z
need not separate the components of
\partial K
. Thus it can happen that
\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z;\Sigma)=\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z;\Sigma^{\prime})
, hence
\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)=\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma^{\prime})\supset\Sigma\cup\Sigma^{\prime}
, for two distinct boundary components
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Note also that, if the cut-graph

\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z)
has an inner component,
then the union
\bigcup_{\Sigma}\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)
over all boundary components
\Sigma
is strictly contained in
K
. These two observations lead us to the discussion of separating complexes in Section
4
.

When
Z
is in the interior of
K
, we denote

\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)=\mathcal{R}_{K}(\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z;\Sigma)).

Since
\pi_{(K;Z)}
projects
\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)
onto
\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)
, we may also write

\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)=\pi^{-1}_{(K;Z)}(\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)),

and call it the

\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)
in
\mathcal{R}_{K}(\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z))
.

Remark 3.10
.

The
\Sigma
-component
\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)
, being a subcomplex of

, inherits a polyhedral metric from
K
.
Similarly, cubical
n
-complex
\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)
has a metric in which each
n
-cube

is isomorphically isometric to its projection
\pi_{(K;Z)}(Q)
in
\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)
.
Under these metrics, the restriction of

\pi_{(K;Z)}|_{\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)}\colon|\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)|\to|\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)|

is a length preserving map, that is, for each path
\gamma
in
|\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)|
,
we have

\ell(\pi_{(K;Z)}\circ\gamma)=\ell(\gamma)
.

Since interiors of
|\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)|
and
|\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)|
are locally Euclidean metric spaces with respect to the inherited metrics, the previous remark immediately yields that map
\pi_{(K;Z)}
is conformal in the interior of
|\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)|
. We record this fact as follows.

Lemma 3.11
.

With respect to the metrics above, interiors
\mathrm{int}(|\mathsf{Lift}(Z;\Sigma_{i})|)
and
\mathrm{int}(|\mathsf{Comp}(Z;\Sigma_{i})|\setminus|Z|)
are conformally equivalent.

We end this section with some more notations. Let
P
be an
n
-subcomplex of
K
, we may denote for simplicity

\pi_{(K;Z)}^{-1}(P)=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathcal{R}_{K}(\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z))}(\pi_{(K;Z)}^{-1}(P^{[n]})).

Clearly the
\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z)
-quotient map
\pi_{(K;Z)}
induces a natural isomorphism
\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(\mathcal{R}_{K}(\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z)))\to\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)
between refinements.
Thus, given an
n
-subcomplex
R
of
\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)
, we may also denote

\pi_{(K;Z)}^{-1}(R)=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(\mathcal{R}_{K}(\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(K;Z)))}(\pi_{(K;Z)}^{-1}(R^{[n]})).

Proposition 3.14

(Tunnel-contracting)
.

Let
K\cup T
be a cubical
n
-complex for which
T
is a tunnel and the intersection
q_{T}=K\cap T
is the common face of exactly two
n
-cubes.
Then there exist a constant
L=L(n,\#T^{[n]})\geq 1
and a piecewise linear
L
-bilipschitz homeomorphism
\phi_{T}\colon|K\cup T|\to|K|
for which
\phi_{T}
is the identity on
|K-Q_{T}|
, where
Q_{T}
is the unique
n
-cube in
K
having
q_{T}
as a face.

Proof.

Let
T^{\prime}=T\cup Q_{T}
. Then
\Gamma(T^{\prime})
is a tree.

Let
Q\in T^{\prime},Q\neq Q_{T},
be a leaf in
\Gamma(T^{\prime})
and
Q^{\prime}
be the unique
n
-cube in
T^{\prime}
sharing a face
q=Q\cap Q^{\prime}
with
Q
. Since
T^{\prime}
is isomorphic to the realization of
\Gamma(T^{\prime})
, we have that
Q\cap(T^{\prime}-Q)=Q\cap Q^{\prime}
. Thus there exists a piecewise linear homeomorphism
\varphi_{Q}\colon|Q\cup Q^{\prime}|\to|Q^{\prime}|
, which is the identity on
|\partial Q^{\prime}|\setminus|Q|

and whose bilipschitz constant depends only on
n
. We extend
\varphi_{Q}
, by identity, to a homeomorphism

\varphi_{Q}\colon|K\cup T|\to|(K\cup T)-Q|
.
The mapping
\phi_{T}
in the proposition is obtained as a composition of such homeomorphisms by contracting one leaf at a time. Thus the bilipschitz constant for

depends only on the dimension

and the size of the tree
\Gamma(T)
, which is
\#T^{[n]}
.
∎

The cubical complex

(2)

We show next that by

is

if it is isomorphic to either

(\{1,\ldots,m\},\{\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\ldots,\{m-1,m\}\})

In the first case, we say also that

Lemma 3.16
.

Let

-complex. Then the adjacency graph

-cube

Proof.

Let

. By condition (

) in Definition

, the adjacency graph

is connected.
Let

is adjacent to at most two other

The refinement of a good complex remains to be good; we omit the simple argument.

4.
Separating complexes

At the center of our construction is the notion of separating complexes.
Roughly, a separating complex

in a good cubical

(n-1)
-dimensional subcomplex whose complement in
|K|
is homeomorphic to
|\partial K|\times[0,1)
. Typically the space of a separating complex

is not a manifold. A formal definition stronger than the topological description above uses the notion of lifts.
We refer to Section
3
for definitions.

Definition 4.1

(Separating complex)
.

Let
K
be a good cubical
n
-complex for which
\mathrm{St}_{K}(\partial K)
is isomorphic to
\partial K\times[0,1]
. An
(n-1)
-subcomplex

is a

if

(1)

Z\cap\mathrm{St}_{K}(\partial K)=\varnothing
;

(2)

\Gamma(Z)
is connected;

(3)

for each boundary component
\Sigma\subset\partial K
,
\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)\cap\partial K=\Sigma
, and the union of all
\Sigma
-components is

, i.e.
\bigcup_{\Sigma}\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)=K

(4)

for each boundary component
\Sigma\subset\partial K
, the space of lift
\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)
is homeomorphic to
|\Sigma|\times[0,1]
.

Remark 4.2
.

In general, spaces
|\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)|
and
|\mathsf{Lift}_{K}(Z;\Sigma)|
are not homeomorphic, even the interiors of

has two boundary components. The subcomplex

in the figure, consisting of four

but not in

. Thus the cubical complex
\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma_{1})
has three boundary components.

Condition (

), however, implies a weaker property

\displaystyle\approx\pi_{(K;Z)}^{-1}(|\mathsf{Comp}_{K}(Z;\Sigma_{1})|\setminus|Z|)

\displaystyle=|\mathsf{Lift}_{k}(Z;\Sigma_{1})|\setminus\pi_{(K,Z)}^{-1}(|Z|)\approx|\Sigma_{1}|\times[0,1).

Remark 4.3

By (

Let

If

4.1.
Existence of separating complexes

The following theorem shows that, after refinement, every good complex
K
admits a separating complex of particular form. Indeed, there exists a separating complex
Z
in
\mathrm{Ref}(K)
having the properties that
Z\subset\mathrm{Ref}(K-\mathrm{St}_{K}(\partial K))
and
\mathrm{Ref}(K-\mathrm{St}_{K}(\partial K))
is tunnel-line with respect to
Z
. We return to the structures of the subcomplexes separated by a separating complexe and their lifts in Section
7
and, in anticipating that discussion, we formulate the statement in terms of the complementary components of
Z
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
K
be a good cubical
n
-complex for which
\mathrm{St}_{K}(\partial K)
is isomorphic to
\partial K\times[0,1]
, and
\Sigma^{\prime}\subset\partial K
a boundary component.
Then there exists
a separating complex
Z
of
\mathrm{Ref}(K)
having the following properties:

(1)

for each boundary component
\Sigma\subset\partial K
,
\Sigma\neq\Sigma^{\prime}
, the
\Sigma
-component
\mathsf{Comp}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(Z;\Sigma)=\mathrm{Ref}(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma))
, and the lift
\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(Z;\Sigma)
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{Ref}(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma))
.

(2)

for the boundary component
\Sigma^{\prime}
,

\mathsf{Comp}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(Z;\Sigma^{\prime})=\mathrm{Ref}(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma^{\prime}))\,\bigcup\,(\mathrm{Ref}(K)-\mathrm{St}_{K}(\partial K)),

and

\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(Z;\Sigma^{\prime})=\mathrm{Ref}(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma^{\prime}))\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(\tau),

where
\tau
is a spanning tree in
\Gamma(\mathrm{Ref}(K-\mathrm{St}_{K}(\partial K)))
,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(\tau)
is the realization of
\tau
and
\mathrm{Ref}(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma^{\prime}))\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(\tau)
is an
(n-1)
-cube.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{m}
be the boundary components of
\partial K
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma_{1}
. From the assumption, stars
\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{i})
are necessarily mutually disjoint and each
\Gamma(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{i}))
is connected.

Let
A^{\prime}
be the subcomplex of
K
obtained by removing all stars
\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{i})
, that is,

A^{\prime}=K-\left(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{m})\right),

recalling the difference of two complexes defined in Section
2
. Since
\Gamma(K)
is connected and the stars
\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{i})
are mutually disjoint, the complex
A^{\prime}
is adjacently-connected.
Let
B^{\prime}=\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{1})
and

K^{\prime}=B^{\prime}\cup A^{\prime}.

Then
K^{\prime}
is adjacently-connected and
B^{\prime}\cap A^{\prime}=\partial(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{1}))\setminus\Sigma_{1}
.

Let
T\subset\Gamma(A^{\prime})
be a spanning tree and let

\Omega=\{c(q)\in\mathrm{Ref}(q)\colon q\in(A^{\prime})^{[n-1]}\,\,\,\text{is an edge in}\,\,T\},

where
c(q)
is the center cube in
\mathrm{Ref}(q)
, be the collection of the center cubes of the edges in
T
. Let

Y=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}\left(\mathrm{Ref}((A^{\prime})^{(n-1)})\setminus\,\Omega\right).

Fix next a spanning tree
\tau
in
\Gamma(\mathrm{Ref}(A^{\prime}))
which is contained in the cut-graph
\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(\mathrm{Ref}(A^{\prime});Y)
and contains all faces of the center cube
c(Q)
of
\mathrm{Ref}(Q)
for all
Q\in(A^{\prime})^{[n]}
.
Fix also an
n
-cube
Q^{\prime}\in(A^{\prime})^{[n]}
having a face
q^{\prime}
in
\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{1})
and denote by
\omega=c(q^{\prime})
the center cube in
\mathrm{Ref}(q^{\prime})
.

We claim that

Z=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}\left((\mathrm{Ref}(A^{\prime}))^{[n-1]}\setminus(\tau\cup\{\omega\})\right)

is a separating complex in
\mathrm{Ref}(K)
.

Since
Z\subset\mathrm{Ref}(A^{\prime})
, the intersection
Z\cap\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(\mathrm{Ref}(\partial K))=\varnothing
. Condition (
1
) in the definition of separating complex holds.

Since the
(n-1)
-complex
(A^{\prime})^{(n-1)}
is adjacently-connected, the refinement
\mathrm{Ref}((A^{\prime})^{(n-1)})
is adjacently-connected. After the collection
\Omega
of
(n-1)
-dimensional center cubes is removed from
\mathrm{Ref}((A^{\prime})^{(n-1)})
, the complex
Y
remains adjacently-connected. From the selection of
\tau
, any
(n-1)
-cube in
Z
which does not belong to
Y
is adjacent to an
(n-1)
-cube in
Y
. Hence
Z
is adjacently-connected and Condition
(
2
) holds.

From the construction, the cut-graph

\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(\mathrm{Ref}(K^{\prime});Z)=\Gamma(\mathrm{Ref}(B^{\prime}))\cup\tau\cup\{\omega\}

is connected. Thus the connected components of
\Gamma(\mathrm{Ref}(K);Z)
are in one-to-one correspondence with the components of
\partial K
. Hence Condition (
3
) holds.

Since
\Gamma(\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(Z;\Sigma_{1}))
is isomorphic to
\Gamma^{\mathsf{cut}}(\mathrm{Ref}(K^{\prime});Z)
,
the lift has an essential partition

\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(Z;\Sigma_{1})=B\cup A,

where
B=\pi^{-1}_{(K;Z)}(B^{\prime})
is isomorphic to, hence identified with,
\mathrm{Ref}(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{1}))
,
A=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(\tau)
is a realization of a tree, hence a tunnel having an
n
-cell as its space, and

B\cap A=\{\omega\}
.
Finally, for
i=2,\ldots,m
, the adjacency graph of
\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathrm{Ref}(K)}(Z;\Sigma_{i})
is isomorphic to
\Gamma(\mathrm{Ref}(\mathrm{St}_{K}(\Sigma_{i})))
.
Thus Condition (
4
) holds also.

Hence
Z
is a separating complex in
\mathrm{Ref}(K)
having the properties stated in the theorem.
∎

for manifolds, whose boundary has

4.2.
Statement of the Evolution Theorem

In this section we state an Evolution Theorem for separating complexes (Theorem

We begin by associating two quantities to the good cubical complex

Topological Lakes of Wada

The first two conditions in Theorem

yield the existence of topological Lakes of Wada.

Proposition 4.17

Let

n
-complex with boundary components

Proof.

Let

The core-expanding property yields the fact that

Since the cores

We check next that

for each

Fix an index

is contained in the union

of two adjacent

5.2.
Cube and star indentations

We define

Loosely stated, a cube-indentation is an

-subcomplex in
\mathrm{Ref}^{k}(K)

The following formal definition is more restrictive and imposes some additional regularity conditions on the sizes and adjacency of spectral cubes.

Definition 5.5

Let

be a good cubical

-complex, and let

be an
(n-1)
-cube in

-subcomplex

(1)

.

(2)

has a face contained in the interior of

.

(3)

The intersection

of spectral cubes

and

j\leq j^{\prime}

; in the case

meets only two faces of

We record two simple observations on cube-indentations.

Lemma 5.6

Let

be a cube-indentation in

over

and let

be a spectral cube in

meets at most two spectral cubes of the same or larger side length, out of which at most one has larger side length than
Q
. Furthermore, if

meets two spectral cubes

and

of the same side length
or larger side length,
then

and

are opposite faces of

Note that there is no restriction on the number of spectral cubes in

Proof of Lemma
5.6

For the first claim, suppose that a spectral cube

of
\mathcal{S}_{*}(D;K)^{[n]}
meets spectral cubes

and

in
\mathcal{S}_{*}(D;K)^{[n]}

are opposite faces of

Retaining the previous notations, we may assume that the side length of

Q^{\prime\prime}
has side length at most the side length of

n

Proof of the Main Theorem

Evolution of separating complexes repeated indefinitely yields a continuum whose complement consists of Lakes of Wada. The following theorem, including manifolds with two boundary components, is an extension of the main theorem (Theorem

Theorem 8.1
