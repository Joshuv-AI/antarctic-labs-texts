Distinct clinical phenotypes of pediatric Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder with and without neurodevelopmental disorders: evidence for a neurodevelopmental continuum
Authors: Barbara D’Aiello, Maria Pontillo, Francesco Demaria, Michelangelo Di Luzio, Cristina Di Vincenzo, Ilaria Bertoncini, Valeria Villani, Deny Menghini, Stefano Vicari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate clinical, demographic, and psychopathological differences between pediatric patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) with and without comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), focusing on symptom presentation, comorbidities, and functional outcomes. A total of 121 drug-naïve children and adolescents with recent-onset OCD (66 with comorbid NDDs and 55 without) were assessed at the Child and Adolescent Neuropsychiatry Unit of Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital. Participants underwent multidisciplinary clinical evaluations, including structured diagnostic interviews (Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Aged Children – Present and Lifetime Version for DSM-5 K-SADS-PL DSM-5), symptom-specific scales (Children’s Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale [CY-BOCS], Children’s Depression Inventory 2 [CDI-2], Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children 2 [MASC-2]), and adaptive functioning assessments (Children’s Global Assessment Scale [CGAS]). The OCD + NDDs group was significantly younger, had lower IQ, and showed a male predominance, but did not differ in global functioning. No significant differences were found in anxiety or depressive symptoms across groups. However, distinct OCD symptom profiles emerged: sexual and forbidden thought obsessions as derived by CY-BOCS were more frequent in the OCD-NDDs group, while hoarding compulsions were more common in OCD + NDDs. Comorbid tics and ADHD were significantly more prevalent in the OCD + NDDs group, whereas PTSD was more frequent in OCD-NDDs. Findings support the presence of distinct phenotypic and comorbidity profiles in pediatric OCD with and without NDDs, emphasizing the need for tailored diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. While internalizing symptoms may remain comparable, the variation in obsessional content and neurodevelopmental burden highlights the clinical importance of early, individualized assessment strategies.Keywords: Pediatric Obsessive-Compulsive disorder, Neurodevelopmental disorders, Autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, Tic disorders, Symptom profiles

Introduction

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is a prevalent and debilitating neuropsychiatric condition characterized by intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors (compulsions) that significantly impair daily functioning. OCD has an estimated global prevalence ranging from 0.25% to 3.0% [1], with rates among children and adolescents approximating 1–3% [2]. In pediatric populations, untreated OCD tends to follow a chronic and persistent course [3–5], often leading to substantial impairments in quality of life, as well as in social, academic, and family functioning [5–8]. Among adults diagnosed with OCD, approximately 30–50% report the onset of symptoms before the age of 18 years [9].

The disorder can arise at various points across the lifespan, with wide variability in symptom presentation, comorbidities, and age of onset.

The late-onset OCD, typically manifesting in late adolescents and early adulthood, tends to exhibit a balanced gender distribution and may be precipitated by environmental stressors or traumatic events. The clinical presentation in these cases often differs from early-onset OCD, with variations in symptom dimensions and comorbidity profiles [10]. Studies have implicated stressful life events as significant contributors to the emergence of OCD symptoms in later life. For example, a study [11] found that patients who experienced stressful life events prior to OCD onset exhibited a distinct clinical pattern, including later onset of the disorder, less family history of OCD, a higher prevalence of contamination obsessions and cleaning compulsions. These findings highlight the role of environmental stressors in triggering OCD symptoms [12, 13].

In contrast, early-onset OCD, typically defined by a mean age of onset of 10, is often associated with a higher prevalence in males [14] and increased severity of compulsions [15], particularly those related to symmetry and ordering and a higher likelihood of comorbid conditions.

The onset of OCD symptoms may occur following the presence of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) [16–18]. NDDs are a group of conditions with onset in the developmental period, typically characterized by impairments in personal, social, academic, or occupational functioning [19]. These disorders include, but are not limited to, intellectual disability, communication disorders, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), specific learning disorders, and motor disorders, such as tic disorders. Notably, research indicates that up to 30% of individuals with ASD also meet diagnostic criteria for OCD, suggesting potential shared etiological pathways and neurobiological mechanisms [20].

Advancements in neuroimaging have provided insights into the shared neurobiological substrates among OCD, ASD, and ADHD. A study [21] involved brain imaging of white matter in 200 children with ASD, ADHD, OCD or no diagnosis revealed common white matter abnormalities in children with these disorders, particularly in the corpus callosum, which is crucial for interhemispheric communication. The study identified alteration of interhemispheric circuits as a common feature of ASD, ADHD and OCD. However, these alterations appear to be more widespread and severe in ASD and ADHD than OCD. These findings suggest that disruptions in neural connectivity may contribute to the pathophysiology of these conditions and that these neurobiological overlaps may, at least in part, account for the substantial symptom overlap observed among these disorders, which poses significant challenges in clinical diagnostics and increases the risk of misdiagnosis.

The association between premorbid OCD (i.e., characteristics or conditions present prior to OCD onset) and NDDs may reflect a shared developmental trajectory, as conceptualized by the “neurodevelopmental continuum model” [22–24]. This model proposes that neurodevelopmental conditions and psychiatric disorders—traditionally viewed as separate diagnostic categories—may instead lie along a continuous spectrum, underpinned by overlapping genetic, neurobiological, and developmental mechanisms. Genomic evidence, particularly from studies in schizophrenia, has demonstrated that common and rare genetic variants associated with NDDs (e.g., ASD, intellectual disability) also confer risk for psychiatric conditions, including OCD and schizophrenia [22]. In line with this perspective, a previous work by our research group [24] emphasized the clinical heterogeneity and developmental variability observed in early- and very-early-onset schizophrenia, further illustrating how early neurodevelopmental alterations may manifest along diverse psychiatric pathways. Such findings support a dimensional understanding of psychopathology, where phenotypic expression is shaped by the severity, timing, and interaction of neurodevelopmental disruptions across early life.

This reconceptualization is critical in clinical practice, as it encourages a developmental and transdiagnostic approach to diagnosis and intervention.

Within this framework, the presence of OCD symptoms in individuals with co-occurring NDDs—such as ASD or ADHD—may reflect a shared neurodevelopmental liability rather than mere comorbidity. This view underscores the necessity of comprehensive developmental assessments when evaluating children and adolescents with OCD, particularly in cases where atypical symptom profiles or poor insight are observed.

Given this heterogeneity, the clinical management of OCD may need to be adapted to account for differences in onset patterns and comorbid NDDs. For instance, the use of stimulant medications in individuals with comorbid ADHD and OCD requires careful consideration, as these agents may exacerbate OCD symptoms. Behavioral interventions, particularly Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) with Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP), remain the gold standard for OCD treatment, however, adaptations may be necessary to address the specific needs of individuals with comorbid NDDs, such as incorporating strategies to enhance cognitive flexibility and social understanding in those with ASD [25].

This study aims to examine the association between the onset of OCD and the presence of NDDs, with a particular focus on delineating the differential clinical phenotypes of OCD emerging in individuals with versus without comorbid NDDs. By characterizing the developmental trajectories and symptom profiles, distinguishing these subgroups, the study seeks to advance the nosographic understanding of early-onset OCD within neurodevelopmental contexts.

This represents a novel line of investigation compared to previous research, which, in attempting to characterize the clinical profiles of early-onset OCD, has predominantly focused on age-related differences in OCD symptomatology, comparing adults (> 18 years) with children and adolescents (< 18 years), often without adequately addressing the role of NDDs [10, 14, 26]. While some studies have examined psychiatric comorbidities associated with OCD [11, 20, 27, 28], others have specifically investigated how the presence of NDDs contributes to distinct clinical trajectories of OCD, including the occurrence of tics, and their influence on long-term outcomes [29].

Moreover, the existing literature is often limited by methodological constraints. Many studies include heterogeneous samples, combining medicated and unmedicated individuals or patients at various stages of illness, which can obscure early clinical markers and neurodevelopmental contributions [30, 31]. To address these limitations, our study focused on a drug-naïve pediatric population, allowing for a more accurate characterization of early OCD and minimizing confounding variables. Furthermore, we directly compared children with OCD and co-occurring NDDs to those without, revealing significant differences in both demographic and clinical profiles. This comparison contributes to refining the nosographic understanding of OCD within neurodevelopmental frameworks, emphasizing the need for personalized and developmentally sensitive diagnostic strategies.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study included 121 children who admitted for the first time to the Day Hospital of the Child and Adolescent Neuropsychiatry Unit at Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital. These children sought treatment for OCD symptoms that were significantly impairing their functioning. All participants were discharged with a recent-onset diagnosis of OCD, with data collected between 2022 and 2024. No participant required hospitalisation for the reported symptoms. Inclusion criteria were age 6–17 years, recent-onset OCD, drug-naïve. Exclusion criteria were prior psychiatric treatment for OCD, severe medical conditions, or IQ < 70. All eligible patients within this period meeting these criteria were invited to participate, and no selective enrolment was applied All participants were drug-naïve patients at the time of clinical assessment and did not receive psychosocial interventions. We included children and adolescents with pediatric OCD (66 with NDDS and 55 without NDDs see Tables 1 and 2, 3 for more details) who did not require hospitalization. In this study, no patients were found with NDD diagnoses other than ASD, ADHD, tic disorders, and/or specific learning disorders, which were identified through standardized diagnostic assessment. We acknowledge that this broad categorization may introduce heterogeneity, as specific NDDs—particularly ASD—could differentially influence clinical profiles. Therefore, comparisons with the OCD-NDD group should be interpreted with caution. The study protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee. All assessments were part of the standard clinical evaluation upon admission, and written informed consent was obtained from parents/legal guardians.

Clinical assessment

Participants were evaluated by a multidisciplinary team composed of neuropsychiatrists (i.e. physician specialized in diagnosing and treating neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents) and clinical psychologists, all of whom were trained in the administration of structured and semi-structured diagnostic instruments for the assessment of neuropsychiatric and psychopathological conditions. These clinicians also had specialized expertise in identifying OCD symptoms in children and adolescents, both in the presence and absence of neurodevelopmental comorbidities.

Each participant, along with their parent or legal guardian, underwent a comprehensive clinical interview, which included a review of medical, developmental, and psychiatric history family psychiatric history, academic functioning, and prior treatments. The presence of any neurodevelopmental or psychiatric disorder prior to the onset of OCD was determined through clinical evaluation and retrospective history.

Demographic and clinical information—such as age, sex, family psychiatric history, current or previous pharmacological treatments, and the use of psychoactive substances—was systematically collected for all participants.

Non-verbal Intelligence Quotient was assessed with the Perceptual Reasoning Index of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children Fourth Edition or Coloured Progressive Matrices or Standard Progressive Matrices (CPM/SPM [32, 33]).

Psychiatric diagnoses were made using the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Aged Children – Present and Lifetime Version for DSM-5 (K-SADS-PL DSM-5) [34]. This semi-structured diagnostic interview evaluates the presence of current and lifetime psychopathological disorders according to DSM-5 criteria, including disorders within the schizophrenia spectrum. In accordance with the administration guidelines proposed, information was obtained from both the child or adolescent and their parent.

Obsessive-compulsive symptoms were assessed using the Children’s Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (CY-BOCS) [35], a clinician-administered, semi-structured interview designed to evaluate the presence and severity of obsessive and compulsive symptoms. Consistent with previous studies, the CY-BOCS is considered the gold-standard instrument for the assessment of OCD symptomatology in pediatric populations [36].

Global functioning was measured with the Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS) [37], which provides a numeric estimate of overall psychological, social, and academic functioning in children and adolescents.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Children’s Depression Inventory 2 (CDI 2) [38], a widely used self-report questionnaire. The total score is derived from two main scales: the Emotional Problems scale and the Functional Problems scale. The Emotional Problems scale includes two subscales: the Negative Mood/Physical Symptoms subscale, which evaluates depressive affect and neurovegetative symptoms; and the Negative Self-Esteem subscale, which assesses self-critical and negative self-evaluative thoughts. The Functional Problems scale comprises the Ineffectiveness subscale, reflecting perceived inefficacy in academic and general functioning; and the Interpersonal Problems subscale, which measures difficulties in social interactions related to depressive mood.

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children 2 (MASC 2) [39], a self-report instrument designed to capture various dimensions of anxiety in youth. The total score is derived from six primary indices: Separation Anxiety/Phobias, Generalized Anxiety Disorder, Social Anxiety (including the Humiliation/Rejection and Performance Fears subscales), Obsessions and Compulsions, Physical Symptoms (composed of Panic and Tense/Restless subscales), and Harm Avoidance.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square analysis was conducted to verify that two groups (Group 1 = OCD with a diagnosed NDDs, Group 2 = OCD without a diagnosed NDDs) did not differ by gender. Associations between groups, diagnoses, sex and familiarity were examined using Chi-square analysis. Student’s t-tests were used to compare the two groups on demographic variables (age, IQ), functional impairment (Children Global Assessment Scale, C-GAS). Bonferroni’s corrected p-value for 3 multiple comparisons (p =.05/3).

Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to compare the two group on adaptive measures (Conceptual, Social, Practical Domain of Adaptive Behavior Assessment System-Second Edition), emotional and behavioural symptoms (MASC2, CDI2).

Considering that the two groups differed for age and IQ, Multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) on adaptive measures scales was conducted controlling for age and IQ.

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the clinical, demographic, and psychopathological profiles of children and adolescents with OCD, comparing patients with and without co-occurring NDDs.

Our findings provide further insight into the distinct phenotypic presentation of pediatric OCD in the presence of co-occurring NDDs and contribute to the growing body of literature advocating for more individualized approaches to diagnosis and treatment.

Our results showed that the OCD + NDDs group presented a significantly lower mean age, lower IQ, and a predominantly male distribution compared to the OCD-NDDs group. The group with OCD + NDDs was significantly younger than the OCD-NDDs group, suggesting that comorbid neurodevelopmental conditions may be associated with an earlier onset or referral for clinical assessment. These findings are consistent with previous literature indicating that early-onset OCD is more frequently associated with NDDs, particularly ADHD, tics and ASD [28, 30, 40]. The elevated comorbidity rates in early-onset cases support the hypothesis that these conditions may share overlapping developmental pathways, likely rooted in early neurobiological dysregulation. For instance, a study [30] found that children with early-onset OCD had significantly higher rates of comorbid tic disorders compared to those with later-onset OCD, and this association was further strengthened by the presence of ADHD. This constellation of symptoms reflects a potential subtype of OCD with a strong neurodevelopmental component, characterized by more insidious onset, higher familial loading, and differential response to pharmacological and behavioral treatments.

Our findings showed that the OCD + NDD group had a lower mean IQ compared to the OCD-NDD group. However, this difference should be interpreted with caution, as it may be influenced by the specific type of comorbid NDD rather than the presence of NDDs in general. For example, while ASD and ADHD are often associated with lower cognitive scores, individuals with tic disorders may present with normative or even elevated IQ, suggesting heterogeneity within the OCD + NDD group [41]. Consequently, the observed associations between lower IQ, reduced metacognition, and behaviorally driven compulsions should not be generalized to all children with OCD + NDD. Instead, these patterns may reflect the combined effects of IQ and the particular neurodevelopmental profile of each child. However, given the limitations of our sample and the cross-sectional nature of the analysis, these findings should be considered preliminary, and replication in larger samples is needed to confirm these neurocognitive profiles.

Notably, our findings also revealed a significant gender imbalance in the OCD + NDDs group, with a higher prevalence of males, in contrast to the more balanced distribution observed in the OCD-only group. This pattern is consistent with epidemiological data indicating male predominance in both NDDs—especially ASD and ADHD—and in early-onset OCD [42–44]. For example, ASD is diagnosed up to four times more frequently in males, and similar trends are observed in ADHD, where males are two to three times more likely to receive a diagnosis [44, 45].

These sex differences may reflect underlying neurobiological and genetic mechanisms that modulate susceptibility to neurodevelopmental psychopathology. Several theories have been put forward without finding confirmation [46–48]. Additionally, Martel [49] highlighted that sex-specific gene–environment interactions—particularly involving dopaminergic and serotonergic systems—might differentially influence the development of regulatory control and compulsive behavior in males versus females. These mechanisms may help explain not only the higher prevalence of OCD + NDD comorbidity in males but also potential differences in clinical presentation, severity, and treatment response. Taken together, these findings suggest a potential neurodevelopmental subtype of OCD, more common in males, marked by early onset [50], and associated with higher rates of comorbidity with ADHD and tic disorders. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the sample size and the need for replication in larger, more diverse cohorts.

Previous studies have demonstrated that males with early-onset OCD exhibited more symmetry and ordering compulsions and had higher rates of comorbid tics, further reinforcing the link between male sex and neurodevelopmentally influenced OCD phenotypes [42]. Recognizing this subtype has important clinical implications, as it may benefit from earlier and more targeted interventions, including modifications to CBT protocols to account for attentional deficits, motor disinhibition, and reduced cognitive flexibility often seen in this population.

Interestingly, our results showed that no group differences emerged in global functioning as measured by CGAS, suggesting that although children with OCD and comorbid NDDs present with lower cognitive functioning and earlier symptom onset, their day-to-day adaptive functioning in structured clinical environments may remain comparable to those without NDDs. These findings echo prior research suggesting that adaptive deficits in OCD populations are more closely related to symptom severity and insight than to comorbid conditions per se [51–53].

Contrary to expectations, no significant differences were observed in in anxiety and depressive symptoms (as measured by MASC-2 and CDI-2) suggesting that the presence of NDDs may not exacerbate general internalizing symptoms in children with OCD. This finding is notable given the high rates of internalizing symptoms in both OCD and NDD populations [54, 55]. It supports the hypothesis that while OCD presentations may vary depending on neurodevelopmental status, the burden of mood and anxiety symptoms may remain similar, consistent with previous pediatric studies [53]. It should be noted that the absence of significant differences in anxiety and depressive symptoms between groups may reflect the relatively young age of our participants, who may not yet have passed through the full developmental window of clinical risk for these internalizing disorders. Longitudinal follow-up would be necessary to determine whether differences emerge over time.

Concerning OCD symptomatology, the two groups significantly differed in certain obsessive and compulsive symptom domains, according to the CY-BOCS. Unlike existing literature [56], it is not the symptoms of contamination that differentiate the two groups. Notably, sexual obsessions and forbidden thoughts were more prevalent in the OCD-NDDs group, while accumulation compulsions were more common in the OCD + NDDs group. These differences appear to reflect underlying disparities in cognitive development, insight, and particularly metacognitive and mentalizing capacities, which are often compromised in individuals with NDDs [46, 57]. Interestingly, our findings partially diverge from prior studies suggesting that youth with OCD and tics are more likely to exhibit aggressive or forbidden thought obsessions and symmetry/checking compulsions [58–60]. This discrepancy may be related to the younger age of our sample, the inclusion of a broader set of neurodevelopmental comorbidities beyond tics (e.g., ADHD, ASD), and methodological differences such as assessment instruments and the selection of drug-naïve participants. These factors may affect symptom expression and reporting, emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation and further research on OCD profiles across specific NDD subtypes. Patients without NDDs, particularly those with normative or above-average intellectual functioning, tend to exhibit more complex forms of obsessions, including abstract, egodystonic, and morally or sexually charged intrusive thoughts. These obsessions typically require a certain level of metacognitive awareness—such as the capacity to reflect on one’s own thoughts, distinguish between thought and action, and appraise internal mental states as separate from the self—which is frequently impaired in individuals with ASD or other neurodevelopmental conditions [61]. In contrast, individuals with NDDs, especially those with ASD and ADHD, often show deficits in Theory of Mind (ToM) and mentalization, limiting their ability to engage in complex moral reasoning or to experience intense distress in response to intrusive taboo thoughts [62]. Consequently, their OCD symptoms may be more behaviorally driven—focused on symmetry, order, or accumulation—and less characterized by abstract, symbolic obsessions. Furthermore, evidence suggests that mentalizing and metacognitive abilities develop with age and cognitive maturation and are particularly sensitive to neurodevelopmental deviations. Studies in non-clinical and clinical populations have shown that reduced metacognition is associated with less insight into the irrationality of obsessions, reduced internal conflict, and an increased tendency to act on compulsions without engaging in reflective evaluation [63, 64]. Coherently, it was observed that patients with greater insight and cognitive flexibility were more likely to report obsessions involving sexual, religious, or aggressive content—suggesting that the emotional salience and egodystonic nature of such obsessions may be contingent on intact reflective functioning [42].

These results are consistent with neurocognitive models of OCD that link symptom expression to individual differences in executive functioning, self-monitoring, and internal state attribution [65]. Thus, the higher prevalence of sexual and forbidden thoughts in the OCD-NDDs group may be indicative of a higher-order cognitive subtype of OCD, in which intrusive thoughts arise from internal conflicts, guilt, or heightened moral sensitivity—processes that presuppose preserved or enhanced mentalization capacities.

Our findings showed that contamination fears and washing compulsions were prominent in both groups but statistically distinguished them, indicating that while these are hallmark symptoms of pediatric OCD, they may differ subtly in frequency or expression depending on developmental profile. These findings underscore the dimensional and heterogeneous nature of OCD and the necessity to move beyond categorical diagnostic frameworks [66].

Our results showed that patients with OCD + NDD exhibit prevalent hoarding compulsions, consistent with previous studies in which hoarding symptoms were observed to be associated with earlier onset of OCD, greater symptom severity, and greater impairment in youth with NDD [67]. This is likely due to the fact that pediatric patients who exhibit hoarding behaviors often have poorer insight [26], so hoarding has been identified as a significant predictor of treatment resistance in children with OCD, particularly those with NDD in comorbidity [28]. Moreover, this appears to be particularly associated with comorbid ADHD and related executive dysfunction, such as deficits in planning, attention, and inhibitory control, rather than with NDDs broadly [67, 68]. These cognitive deficits may contribute to poorer insight and greater difficulty in organizing and discarding items, explaining the elevated severity and treatment resistance observed in these patients.

These findings underscore the importance of considering hoarding compulsions as a distinct disorder [67, 69], associated with specific comorbidity profiles and treatment outcomes such that it is necessary to assess and manage pediatric OCD patients, particularly those with concomitant NDD.

Interestingly, it is helpful to distinguish between symptoms driven by internal cognitive-affective conflict, such as egodystonic obsessions involving moral, sexual, or aggressive content, and those rooted in sensory or behavioral compulsivity, such as ordering, symmetry, or accumulation. However, this distinction should be understood as reflecting individual differences rather than being specific to the presence or absence of NDDs. Both children with and without comorbid NDDs may exhibit either type of symptom depending on their personal cognitive, metacognitive, and developmental profile. For example, children with greater metacognitive awareness and cognitive flexibility may experience more distress from abstract or morally salient obsessions, whereas those with lower reflective capacities may present with compulsions that are more behaviorally or sensorily driven. Recognizing these differences at the individual level is crucial for tailoring assessment and intervention strategies, including the adaptation of cognitive-behavioral therapies to meet each child’s specific cognitive and emotional profile. Regarding additional psychopathological comorbidities, as expected, comorbid tic disorders and ADHD were markedly more prevalent in the OCD + NDDs group. This is consistent with prior studies highlighting a shared genetic and neurobiological underpinning between OCD, tic disorders, and ADHD, particularly involving fronto-striatal circuitry dysregulation [70]. The presence of these comorbidities can substantially alter the clinical course and treatment response, with evidence suggesting that children with both OCD and tics may respond differently to pharmacological or behavioral therapies.

Interestingly, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) was more prevalent in the OCD-NDDs group, potentially reflecting distinct vulnerability mechanisms. Children without NDDs may be more susceptible to the effects of psychosocial stressors or trauma, whereas those with NDDs might manifest more biologically rooted psychopathology [71]. The presence of complex PTSD in individuals with OCD was associated with more severe OC and anxiety symptoms, suggesting that trauma history may exacerbate the clinical presentation of OCD [72] and that trauma exposure may contribute to the development of comorbid conditions in OCD patients [73]. Similarly, it was found that traumatic experiences may contribute to the emergence of additional psychopathological conditions in OCD patients. Moreover, it was observed that individuals with both OCD and PTSD demonstrated poorer treatment outcomes, underscoring the clinical relevance of this comorbidity [74]. It should be noted that the number of participants with PTSD in our sample was small, and thus conclusions regarding group differences and clinical implications are preliminary. Larger studies are needed to confirm these findings and to better understand the relationship between trauma exposure, OCD, and neurodevelopmental comorbidity in youth.

Neurobiological evidence further reinforces these clinical observations. A meta-analysis [75] demonstrated that pediatric patients with PTSD show structural and functional brain alterations, including volume reductions in regions such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex—areas critical for emotion regulation and executive functioning. These neural changes may account for the long-term developmental and psychological impact of trauma in youth. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of systematically assessing trauma history in pediatric OCD populations, particularly among those without comorbid NDDs. Integrating trauma-informed approaches into diagnostic assessments and therapeutic planning may be essential to optimizing treatment outcomes in this subgroup.

Although not reaching statistical significance, Anorexia Nervosa appeared exclusively in the OCD-NDDs. This trend reflects literature suggesting shared etiological factors between restrictive eating disorders and OCD, such as cognitive rigidity and perfectionism [76].

However, the small sample size in this group limits interpretation.

In conclusion our results showed that the OCD + NDD group was significantly younger, had lower IQs and showed male dominance, but did not differ in global or adaptive functioning from the group without NDD. No significant differences in anxiety or depressive symptoms were found between the groups. However, distinct profiles of OCD symptoms emerged: sexual obsessions and for forbidden thoughts were more frequent in the OCD-NDD group, while compulsions for hoarding were more common in the OCD + NDD group. Tics and ADHD in comorbidity were significantly more frequent in the OCD + NDD group, while PTSD was more frequent in OCD-NDD.

Our findings emphasize the importance of early screening for NDDs in youth with OCD, particularly in males presenting at a younger age with lower cognitive functioning. The differing profiles of obsessions and compulsions across groups may also warrant tailored cognitive-behavioral interventions, as traditional exposure and response prevention (ERP) may require adaptation for patients with cognitive or social communication deficits.

Limitations and future directions

While our study provides valuable insights into the clinical and psychopathological profiles of children and adolescents with OCD, there are several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the sample size, particularly within certain subgroups, may limit the generalizability of our findings. The relatively small number of participants with comorbid conditions, such as anorexia nervosa or PTSD, restricts the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the interplay of these conditions with pediatric OCD and neurodevelopmental disorders. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples are needed to validate and expand upon our findings, particularly in examining fewer common comorbidities.

Second, the cross-sectional design of our study limits the ability to infer causal relationships between neurodevelopmental disorders and OCD symptomatology. Longitudinal studies would be invaluable in understanding how OCD develops over time in children with and without NDDs, and how these developmental trajectories may be influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. Additionally, future research could benefit from incorporating neuroimaging or genetic data to explore the underlying biological mechanisms that contribute to the observed differences in symptom expression between the two groups.

Another limitation is the reliance on parent and caregiver reports for some clinical and psychopathological assessments, which could introduce reporting biases. While structured and semi-structured diagnostic tools were used to minimize this issue, future studies could incorporate more objective measures.

Finally, while our study suggests the potential importance of gender differences in OCD presentations, we acknowledge that our sample was predominantly male, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to females. Future research should aim to examine sex differences in larger, more balanced cohorts to better understand how gender influences OCD symptomatology and treatment outcomes.

In terms of future directions, it would be beneficial to investigate the efficacy of tailored interventions that account for the neurodevelopmental status of children with OCD. Given the distinct profiles of obsessive-compulsive symptoms observed in children with and without NDDs, it is crucial to explore how CBT, especially exposure and response prevention (ERP), can be adapted for these different subgroups. Furthermore, integrating trauma-informed approaches into therapeutic models may be particularly important for children with OCD who also have PTSD, as our study highlighted the potential exacerbation of symptoms in this group.

In conclusion, while our study offers important insights into the clinical presentation and neurocognitive functioning of children and adolescents with OCD and NDDs, continued research with larger, longitudinal cohorts and more diverse methodologies will help to further clarify the complexities of this comorbidity and inform more effective, individualized treatment strategies.
