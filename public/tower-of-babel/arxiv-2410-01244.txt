Robustness and Structure Preservation in Flow-Based Generative Models via Wasserstein Path-Space Divergences
Ziyu Chen, Markos A. Katsoulakis, Benjamin J. Zhang

Abstract
We introduce a novel Wasserstein-1 ( 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} ) path-space divergence for stochastic and deterministic dynamics and establish a Wasserstein Uncertainty Propagation (WUP) theorem that bounds the 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} distance between terminal distributions by the proposed divergence, equivalently characterized by a weighted L 2 L^{2} discrepancy between the underlying drifts and the 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} distance between their initial measures. A key ingredient is a probabilistic framework combining adjoint Feynman–Kac representations with synchronous coupling (and reflection coupling on bounded domains), yielding Wasserstein stability estimates beyond existing PDE- and Girsanov-based approaches. The framework accommodates time-varying and possibly degenerate diffusion coefficients, empirical and singular measures, and remains valid in the deterministic limit of flow matching. Unlike KL-based uncertainty quantification bounds, it does not require absolute continuity of path measures and therefore remains well-defined in singular settings. As consequences of the WUP theorem, we derive 𝒲 1 \mathcal{W}_{1} robustness and generalization bounds for score-based generative models and flow matching at both population and finite-sample levels. We further specialize the framework to group-symmetric targets, providing the first error analysis of equivariant flow-based models and the first quantitative comparison between data augmentation and equivariant inductive bias. Our analysis identifies a symmetry-aware Wasserstein path-space divergence that quantifies the model-form error induced by non-equivariant parametrizations. We prove that this error cannot be removed by additional data or training and vanishes only under equivariant architectures, establishing a precise theoretical advantage of equivariant inductive bias over data augmentation. Numerical experiments on group-symmetric Gaussian mixtures corroborate the theory.

Introduction

Flow-based generative models, including score-based diffusion models (SGMs)
[
36
,
22
,
38
,
35
]
and flow matching
[
29
]
, are now the dominant paradigm for high-dimensional generative modeling. By learning a drift or velocity field that transports a simple reference distribution to a complex target, these models achieve state-of-the-art performance across image, audio, molecular, and scientific data generation. Their training reduces to a tractable regression problem, score matching for SGMs and velocity matching for flow matching, which is one of the principal reasons for their practical success. Despite this empirical progress, a rigorous understanding of when and how well they generalize, and how their generalization error depends on the discrepancies between the learned drift or velocity field and its target, remains incomplete. This question becomes especially important in settings that lie beyond the scope of many existing analyses, including singular target distributions, low-dimensional data manifolds, degenerate or vanishing diffusion coefficients, finite-sample training, and targets possessing additional structure such as symmetries or geometric constraints.

A key observation motivating this work is that flow-based generative models are fundamentally dynamical objects. The generated distribution at terminal time is the endpoint of an entire stochastic or deterministic trajectory, and therefore robustness should naturally be studied at the level of path measures rather than solely through terminal-time marginals. This perspective has a long history in uncertainty quantification and sensitivity analysis, where path-space relative entropy and relative entropy rate provide powerful tools for comparing stochastic processes and controlling errors in quantities of interest
[
14
,
21
,
24
,
42
]
. However, these information-theoretic divergences rely on absolute continuity of path measures and may become infinite or undefined when the underlying processes are singular. Such situations arise naturally in modern generative modeling, including deterministic flow matching, degenerate diffusions, empirical target distributions, and dynamics constrained to lower-dimensional manifolds.

To address these limitations, we introduce a Wasserstein path-space divergence that provides a geometric measure of discrepancy between trajectory laws and remains meaningful even when the corresponding path measures are singular. Building on this notion, we establish a Wasserstein Uncertainty Propagation (WUP) theorem that propagates trajectory-level discrepancies to terminal-time distributional errors. Specifically, the theorem bounds the Wasserstein-1 (
\mathcal{W}_{1}
) distance between generated and target distributions in terms of the proposed path-space divergence that includes discrepancies in the underlying dynamics. The resulting framework unifies deterministic and stochastic generative models, accommodates unbounded domains and degenerate noise, and naturally incorporates structured targets.

Our approach differs fundamentally from existing theoretical analyses of flow-based generative models. Most current results proceed through Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence and Girsanov-type arguments, deriving bounds in total variation,
\chi^{2}
, or Wasserstein distance as downstream consequences
[
8
,
28
,
7
,
12
,
32
]
. While powerful, these methods require absolute continuity with respect to a reference process and typically assume nondegenerate diffusion, excluding precisely the singular and deterministic regimes that motivate flow matching and many structure-preserving generative models. A separate line of work studies convergence directly in Wasserstein distance, but currently relies on restrictive assumptions such as compact domains, prescribed noise schedules, or particular discretization schemes
[
13
,
31
]
. In contrast, our path-space Wasserstein framework yields robustness and generalization guarantees under substantially weaker assumptions and applies uniformly across both diffusion-based and deterministic flow-based settings.

This paper develops a unified
\mathcal{W}_{1}
robustness and generalization theory. The central technical contribution is the introduction of a Wasserstein path-space divergence together with the Wasserstein Uncertainty Propagation theorem on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As applications, we derive
\mathcal{W}_{1}
generalization bounds for score-based diffusion models and flow matching, establish robustness guarantees that also apply for singular and structured targets, and develop a quantitative theory of symmetry-informed generative modeling. These results provide a common framework for analyzing robustness, generalization, and structure preservation across a broad class of modern generative models. We summarize our main contributions below.

When the target distribution is invariant under the action of a group

\mathcal{W}_{1}

9

1.1
Related work

Path-space UQ bounds for stochastic dynamics.

A substantial body of work has developed uncertainty quantification and sensitivity analysis directly at the level of path measures. In particular,
[
14
,
24
,
42
]
introduce goal-oriented uncertainty quantification bounds based on relative entropy or the KL divergence, establishing quantitative control of observable discrepancies between stochastic processes and scalable sensitivity estimates for high-dimensional systems. A central theme of this literature is that divergences defined on trajectory space provide a natural mechanism for propagating model perturbations to errors in quantities of interest. Our work adopts a similar path-space viewpoint but replaces the information-theoretic framework with a geometric one. We introduce a Wasserstein path-space divergence that remains meaningful when path measures are mutually singular, a situation that commonly arises in deterministic flow models and generative modeling with singular targets such as empirical observations and those with low-dimensional supports. Moreover, our Wasserstein UQ bound also applies to deterministic dynamics without noise when the KL bound can fail.

Convergence and generalization of SGMs and flow matching.

The quality of a generated distribution is typically measured by a probability divergence or distance. For SGMs, a first line of work bounds the KL divergence between the target and the generated distribution via Girsanov’s theorem and then derives weaker bounds in TV,
\chi^{2}
, or
\mathcal{W}_{1}
as downstream consequences
[
8
,
28
,
7
,
12
,
32
]
. These bounds, however, require the target to be absolutely continuous with respect to a reference measure, which excludes singular targets supported on low-dimensional manifolds and the noiseless flow matching regime. A second line works directly in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
:
[
13
]
establishes convergence under the manifold hypothesis but relies on a specific discretization, and
[
31
]
introduces an uncertainty quantification perspective using Bernstein-type PDE estimates that are restricted to compact domains and require the presence of a Laplacian term. For flow matching models,
[
4
,
41
]
provide
\mathcal{W}_{2}
error bounds when the target has a bounded support, and
[
19
]
establishes

on
[-1,1]^{d}
under smoothness assumptions. Closer to our setting,
[

]
derives a bound similar to our

3
, but under the additional assumptions that the target is sufficiently smooth and that its covariance has controlled decay; these assumptions exclude singular measures and ensure the velocity field is uniformly Lipschitz, making their result a special case of our framework. Proposition 3 in
[
2
]
establishes a similar bound for flow matching to our
Corollary

3
, albeit under the Wasserstein-2 metric. However, their argument does not appear to extend directly to a Wasserstein-2 bound for SGMs. Our
\mathcal{W}_{1}
bounds for SGMs build on the UQ perspective of

[

provides a related Girsanov-type KL discrepancy estimate using properties of Fokker-Planck equations, which require a nondegenerate diffusion coefficient; by contrast, our WUP theorem accommodates both the noiseless setting and singular initial measures.

Symmetry-preserving generative models.

A growing body of empirical work has demonstrated the benefits of incorporating group symmetry directly into generative models, including structure-preserving GANs
[
6
]
, equivariant normalizing flows

,

Our work bridges this gap by providing the first rigorous error analysis for symmetry-aware SGMs and flow matching, and by analytically demonstrating that equivariant parametrization strictly outperforms data augmentation.

\mathbb{R}^{d}

To see how the Wasserstein path-space divergence defined in (
7

\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\equiv 0

\partial_{t}\rho_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\rho_{i})-\Div(\rho_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i})=0,\,\,\rho_{i}(0)=m_{i}.

(8)

Then we have

1.

If

, then

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\rho_{2}(T),\rho_{1}(T))\leq e^{cT}\cdot\Xi\left(\rho_{1}^{[0,T]}\|\rho_{2}^{[0,T]}\right);

(9)

2.

If the negation of the drift term
\mathbf{b}_{1}(x,t)=\nabla U(x,t)+\mathbf{f}(x,t)
, where
U\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
, and both

and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla\mathbf{f}(x,t)\right\rVert}_{2}\leq A

and

for some

, then

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\rho_{2}(T),\rho_{1}(T))\leq e^{(-c+A)T}\cdot\Xi\left(\rho_{1}^{[0,T]}\|\rho_{2}^{[0,T]}\right).

(10)

Note that the right-hand side of (
9
) and (
10
) are exactly the Wasserstein path-space divergence in (

), up to multiplicative factors in the time horizon

.

Proof.

We begin by proving the first part. Taking the difference of (
8
) between
i=1,2
, the measure

satisfies the PDE

\partial_{t}\lambda-\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\lambda)-\Div(\lambda\mathbf{b}_{1}+\rho_{2}(\mathbf{b}_{1}-\mathbf{b}_{2}))=0\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T),\quad\lambda(\cdot,0)=m_{2}-m_{1}.

(11)

Let
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be a test function that evolves in time. We integrate in space and time against (
11
) and apply integration by parts to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda(x,T)\phi(x,T)-\lambda(x,0)\phi(x,0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda(-\partial_{t}\phi-\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\phi)+\mathbf{b}_{1}\cdot\nabla\phi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}

(12)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{2}\nabla\phi\cdot(\mathbf{b}_{1}-\mathbf{b}_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}=0.

Notice that if we choose the test function

to satisfy the Kolmogorov backward equation

-\partial_{t}\phi-\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\phi)+\mathbf{b}_{1}\cdot\nabla\phi=0\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T),\quad\phi(x,T)=\psi(x)\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}

(13)

with terminal condition

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\rho_{2}(T),\rho_{1}(T))\leq\sup_{\psi\in\mathcal{F}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda(x,0)\phi(x,0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\right\rvert}+\sup_{\psi\in\mathcal{F}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{2}\nabla\phi\cdot(\mathbf{b}_{2}-\mathbf{b}_{1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\right\rvert}.

The first term on the right-hand side of (

since we can split

For the second part of the theorem, note that in this case we can apply

Remark 1

Remark 2

Remark 3

When the domain is compact, for example, a

Remark 4
.

Theorem

2
offers a different perspective from Lemma 22 in the work on stochastic interpolants
[
1
]
for quantifying discrepancies between ground truth and observables via the Girsanov-type result and KL divergence. In particular, our framework accounts not only for differences in the drift terms, but also for discrepancies between the initial measures. More importantly,

remains applicable in the noiseless setting, such as flow matching with

and

requires a nondegenerate, albeit small, diffusion coefficient in front of the Laplacian. Furthermore, the

We provide estimations of the Lipschitz constant of the test function that appears in the proof of

. Instead of applying the logarithm transform and performing Bernstein-type gradient estimations as in
[

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
\phi
is the solution of the backward equation

\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\phi)+\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi-a\phi=0\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T),\quad\phi(x,T)=\psi(x)\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d},

(16)

where

is a constant,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla\mathbf{b}(x,t)\right\rVert}_{2}\leq c
uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in[0,T)
, and
\psi
is
L
-Lipschitz. Then
\phi(x,t)
is
e^{(c-a)(T-t)}L
-Lipschitz for any
t\in[0,T)

\phi(x,t)
is also
L
-Lipschitz for
t\in[0,T)
when
a\geq c
.

Proof.

Using the Feynman-Kac formula, the solution of (
95
) can be expressed as

\phi(x,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\int_{t}^{T}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau}}\psi(x_{T})\mid x_{t}=x\right]=e^{-a(T-t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(x_{T})\mid x_{t}=x\right],

(17)

where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x_{t}}=\mathbf{b}(x_{t},t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}}
. Let
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y_{t}}=\mathbf{b}(y_{t},t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}}
, where the increment of Brownian motion
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}}
is coupled with that of
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x_{t}}
, and we have

\phi(x,t)-\phi(y,t)=e^{-a(T-t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(x_{T})-\psi(y_{T})\mid x_{t}=x,y_{t}=y\right],

(18)

where the conditional expectation is taken jointly on
x_{T}
and
y_{T}
. Since
\psi
is
L
-Lipschitz,
\psi(x_{T})-\psi(y_{T})\leq L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{T}-y_{T}\right\rVert}_{2}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{t}-y_{t}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\langle x_{t}-y_{t},\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{(x_{t}-y_{t})}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle x_{t}-y_{t},\mathbf{b}(x_{t},t)-\mathbf{b}(y_{t},t)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2c\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{t}-y_{t}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}.

Then, we have by the Grönwall’s inequality,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{T}-y_{T}\right\rVert}^{2}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}e^{\int_{t}^{T}2c\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau}}=e^{2c(T-t)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}.

(19)

Therefore,

\psi(x_{T})-\psi(y_{T})\leq L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{T}-y_{T}\right\rVert}\leq e^{c(T-t)}L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert},

(20)

and returning to (
18
), we have

\phi(x,t)-\phi(y,t)\leq e^{-a(T-t)}e^{c(T-t)}L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}=e^{(c-a)(T-t)}L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert},

(21)

whenever
T\geq t
. Switching
x
and
y
, we can also show that
\phi(y,t)-\phi(x,t)\leq e^{(c-a)(T-t)}L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}
, and therefore
\phi(x,t)
is
e^{(c-a)(T-t)}L
-Lipschitz for
t\leq T
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
\phi
is the solution of the backward equation

\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\phi)+\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi-a\phi=0\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T),\quad\phi(x,T)=\psi(x)\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d},

(22)

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant, and
\mathbf{b}(x,t)=-\nabla U(x,t)+\mathbf{f}(x,t)
, where
U\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
, and both
\nabla^{2}U\succeq cI_{d}
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla\mathbf{f}(x,t)\right\rVert}_{2}\leq A
hold uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in[0,T]
for some
A,c>0
, and
\psi
is an
L
-Lipschitz function. Then
\phi(\cdot,t)
is
e^{-(a+c-A)(T-t)}L
-Lipschitz, and in particular, it is
L
-Lipschitz for
t\in[0,T)
if
a+c-A\geq 0
.

Proof.

Using the Feynman-Kac formula, the solution of (
22
) can be expressed as

\phi(x,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\int_{t}^{T}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau}}\psi(x_{T})\mid x_{t}=x\right]=e^{-a(T-t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(x_{T})\mid x_{t}=x\right],

(23)

where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x_{t}}=\mathbf{b}(x_{t},t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}}
. For
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y_{t}}=\mathbf{b}(y_{t},t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}}
, where the increment of Brownian motion
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}}
is coupled with that of
x_{t}
, we have

\phi(x,t)-\phi(y,t)=e^{-a(T-t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(x_{T})-\psi(y_{T})\mid x_{t}=x,y_{t}=y\right],

(24)

where the conditional expectation is taken jointly on
x_{T}
and
y_{T}
. Since
\psi
is
L
-Lipschitz,
\psi(x_{T})-\psi(y_{T})\leq L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{T}-y_{T}\right\rVert}

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{(x_{t}-y_{t})}=\left[-\left(\nabla U(x_{t})-\nabla U(y_{t})\right)+\left(\mathbf{f}(x_{t},t)-\mathbf{f}(y_{t},t)\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{t}-y_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\langle x_{t}-y_{t},\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{(x_{t}-y_{t})}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle x_{t}-y_{t},-\left(\nabla U(x_{t})-\nabla U(y_{t})\right)\rangle+2\langle x_{t}-y_{t},\mathbf{f}(x_{t},t)-\mathbf{f}(y_{t},t)\rangle

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{T}-y_{T}\right\rVert}^{2}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}e^{\int_{t}^{T}2(A-c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\tau}}=e^{2(A-c)(T-t)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}.

Therefore,

\psi(x_{T})-\psi(y_{T})\leq L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{T}-y_{T}\right\rVert}\leq e^{(A-c)(T-t)}L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert},

(26)

and using (

\phi(x,t)-\phi(y,t)\leq e^{-a(T-t)}e^{(A-c)(T-t)}L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}=e^{-(a+c-A)(T-t)}L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert},

(27)

whenever
T\geq t
. Switching

and
y
, we can also show that
\phi(y,t)-\phi(x,t)\leq e^{-(a+c-A)(T-t)}L\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}

.

Corollary 1

(Exponential decay of

.

. If

(28)

where

is a constant, then we have

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the class of 1-Lipschitz functions on

. Then for any

5
Population-level
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error bounds for flow-based models on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

In this section, we apply
Theorem

2
to derive population-level
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error bounds for both SGMs and flow matching, quantifying the discrepancy between the target data distribution and the corresponding generated distributions. We always denote by
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the target data measure that the generative model seeks to approximate.

5.1
Population-level bounds for score-based generative models

In SGMs, the generated measure is
m(T)
, where
m(t)
follows the time-reversal (denoising) diffusion process (
5
) with the score function
\nabla\log\eta^{\pi}
replaced by its score approximation
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
. In particular, we consider the forward process in (
4
) as the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x_{t}}=-x_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\sqrt{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}}.

(32)

Then
m(t)
satisfies the following backward PDE

\partial_{t}m=-\Div\left((x+2\mathbf{b}_{\theta})\cdot m\right)+\Delta m\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T),\quad m(0)=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d},

(33)

where
\mathbf{b}_{\theta}(x,t)=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x,T-t)
, and in practice,
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
is often parametrized by neural networks.

The score approximation
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
is typically obtained via score matching, which is commonly achieved by optimizing parameterized vector fields with respect to a chosen score matching objective function. For example, the
denoising score matching
(DSM)
[
40
]
objective is defined as:

\mathcal{J}_{D}(\eta^{\pi},\mathbf{s}_{\theta})=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\nabla\log\eta^{x^{\prime}}\right\rVert}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\eta^{x^{\prime}}}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi}(x^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s},

(34)

where
\eta^{x^{\prime}}(s)
denotes the conditional probability from
x^{\prime}
at time 0 to
x
of (
4
) at time
s
. In addition, we also introduce two other variants of score matching objectives. The
explicit score matching
(ESM) objective
[
38
]
is defined as:

\mathcal{J}_{E}(\eta^{\pi},\mathbf{s}_{\theta})=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\nabla\log\eta^{\pi}\right\rVert}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\eta^{\pi}}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s},

(35)

and it is obvious that
\mathcal{J}_{E}(\eta^{\pi},\mathbf{s}_{\theta})=\mathcal{J}_{D}(\eta^{\pi},\mathbf{s}_{\theta})
by the law of total expectation. The
implicit score matching
(ISM) objective
[
37
]
is defined as:

\mathcal{J}_{I}(\eta^{\pi},\mathbf{s}_{\theta})\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\right\rVert}^{2}+2\Div(\mathbf{s}_{\theta})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\eta^{\pi}(s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s},

(36)

which may be more practical for score matching. By an expansion of the square of the norm, it is easy to verify that
\mathcal{J}_{D}(\rho,\mathbf{s}_{\theta})=\mathcal{J}_{E}(\rho,\mathbf{s}_{\theta})=\mathcal{J}_{I}(\rho,\mathbf{s}_{\theta})+4\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla\sqrt{\rho}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T])}^{2}
for any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]
. This suggests that the optimal solutions to the DSM, ESM and ISM coincide for the same target probability measure.

We provide a
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error analysis for SGMs at the population level.

Theorem 3

(
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error bound for SGMs, population level)
.

Let
\pi
be the target data measure and assume the noising process follows (
32
). Let
A>0
, and suppose the generated distribution
m(T)
satisfies (
33
). Moreover, assume
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\in[0,T]}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x,t)\right\rVert}_{2}\leq A
and let
e_{nn}=\mathcal{J}_{D}(\eta^{\pi},\mathbf{s}_{\theta})
be the DSM error. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,m(T))\leq e^{(1+2A)T}\left(e^{-T}\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\pi,\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})\right)+\sqrt{T\cdot e_{nn}}\right).

(37)

The
\mathcal{W}_{1}
bound in
Theorem

3
remains well-defined and meaningful even when the target distribution
\pi
does not have a density; for example,
\pi
is supported on a low-dimensional manifold. Moreover,
\mathcal{W}_{1}
naturally yields a sample complexity bound that we will prove in

5
.

Proof.

Recall that
\eta^{\pi}
satisfies

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\eta^{\pi}-\Delta\eta^{\pi}-\Div(x\cdot\eta^{\pi})=0\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T],\\
\eta^{\pi}(0)=\pi\,\,\text{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

(38)

We define the drift

\mathbf{b}^{\pi}(x,t)\coloneq\nabla\log\eta^{\pi}(x,T-t)

and let
m^{\pi}(x,t)=\eta^{\pi}(x,T-t)
that satisfies the backward equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}m^{\pi}=\Delta m^{\pi}-\Div\left(m^{\pi}(x+2\mathbf{b}^{\pi})\right),\\
m^{\pi}(0)=\eta^{\pi}(T).\end{cases}

(39)

Applying the first part of
Theorem

2
to (
33
) and (
39
), we have

\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\pi,m(T)\right)=\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(m^{\pi}(T),m(T)\right)\leq e^{(1+2A)T}\left(\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(m^{\pi}(0),\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})\right)+\sqrt{T}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{b}^{\pi}-\mathbf{b}_{\theta}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(m^{\pi})}\right).

(40)

By
Corollary

1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(m^{\pi}(0),\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}))=\mathcal{W}_{1}(\eta^{\pi}(T),\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}))\leq e^{-T}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})),

since the standard Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is the stationary measure of the forward OU process.
∎

Remark 5
.

The exponential factor
e^{(1+2A)T}
originates from the first part of
Theorem

2
, which is established on the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In general, the reverse-time dynamics may amplify small perturbations of the terminal distribution. Consequently, without additional structural assumptions on
\pi
(e.g., log-concavity), the initialization error can only be controlled through a Lipschitz stability estimate, resulting in the factor
e^{(1+2A)T}
. By contrast, on a bounded domain, this growth can be replaced by an exponentially decaying factor in
T
; see
Remark

3
.

5.2
Population-level bounds for flow matching

In flow matching generative models
[
29
]
, the forward and backward processes are deterministic ODEs rather than SDEs and, in most cases, we wish to approximate the velocity field
\mathbf{v}(x,t)
, such that at the particle level, we have

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}}=\mathbf{v}(x,t),\quad x(0)\sim\pi,\quad x(1)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

(41)

Hence, in order to generate data, we would run the ODE backward in time from the standard Gaussian using the velocity
-\mathbf{v}
. The choice of
\mathbf{v}
can vary, such as the average velocity over
x(1)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
of the linear interpolation with a constant speed; see
[
29
,
1
]
for examples.

The particle movement (
41
) corresponds to the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v})=0,\quad\rho(0)=\pi,\quad\rho(1)=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

(42)

The flow matching objective for a velocity approximation
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
, similar to the ESM for SGMs, is defined as

\mathcal{J}_{FM}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}_{\theta},\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\right\rVert}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s},

(43)

where
\mathbf{v}
and
\rho
satisfy (
42
).
Using the approximation
\mathbf{b}_{\theta}(x,t)=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x,1-t)
, the generation follows the equation

\partial_{t}m-\nabla\cdot\left(m\mathbf{b}_{\theta}\right)=0,\quad m(0)=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),

(44)

and
m(1)
is the generated distribution of the flow matching model. Then we have the following error analysis for flow matching generative models at the population level.

Theorem 4

(
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error bound for flow matching, population level)
.

Assume
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
satisfies
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x,t)\right\rVert}_{2}\leq A
uniformly over
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]
for some constant
A>0
and let
e_{nn}=\mathcal{J}_{FM}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}_{\theta},\rho)
. Then we have

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,m(1))\leq e^{A}\sqrt{e_{nn}}.

(45)

Proof.

Note that
\pi
is the solution of the time reversal of (
42
) at
t=1
. Thus, by a direct application of the first part of
Theorem

2
, we readily have

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,m(1))\leq e^{A}\sqrt{e_{nn}},

(46)

since there is no mismatch for the initial measures, both of which are the standard Gaussian.
∎

Remark 6
.

The bound in
Theorem

4
for flow matching is derived using the new Wasserstein path-space divergence, and it cannot be derived via Girsanov’s theorem, since the dynamics are deterministic and contain no diffusion term. While
[
2
, Proposition 3]
establishes a related guarantee in the Wasserstein-2 metric for deterministic flows, their analysis does not directly extend to SGMs. In contrast, our adjoint formulation provides a unified framework for both stochastic and deterministic flows. In particular, this perspective highlights that generative flows can learn low-dimensional manifold structures, on which the Wasserstein distance remains well-defined whereas the KL divergence may fail to be finite, in both stochastic and deterministic settings under a common theoretical framework.

Remark 7
.

[
27
]
constructs a velocity field
\mathbf{v}
satisfying
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathbf{v}\right\rVert}_{2}\leq A^{\star}
uniformly over
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]
for some
A^{\star}>0
. Therefore, by choosing a neural network architecture for
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
with
A\geq A^{\star}
,
e_{nn}
can, in principle, be made arbitrarily small by the universal approximation property of neural networks.

6
Finite-sample
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error bounds for flow-based models on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

In this section, we investigate the finite-sample performance of SGMs and flow matching on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. While the results of the previous section characterize the population-level
\mathcal{W}_{1}
approximation error relative to the target distribution
\pi
, practical training is based only on finitely many samples from
\pi
. We therefore analyze the additional statistical error introduced by replacing population objectives with their empirical counterparts and derive finite-sample
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error bounds for the resulting learned generative models.

6.1
Finite-sample bounds for score-based generative models

We assume that, as in practical applications, the target distribution
\pi
is observed only through
N
i.i.d. samples
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
drawn from
\pi
. The score matching or the DSM objective (
34
) is then approximated such that
\eta^{\pi}(x,t)
is replaced by its kernel density estimator
\eta^{N}(x,t)=\int P_{t}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi^{N}}(y)
, where
\eta^{N}(x,0)=\pi^{N}\coloneq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{z_{i}}
is the empirical measure, and
P_{t}(x,y)
is the transition kernel of the OU process defined in (
54
). Since the kernel estimator does not have a well-defined score function at
t=0
, the DSM objective is often integrated over
t\in[\epsilon,\epsilon+T]
rather than starting from
t=0
, an example of early stopping in SGMs
[
38
]
. Specifically, this is equivalent to score matching for the mollified distribution
\eta^{N,\epsilon}(x,t)\coloneq\int P_{t}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\eta^{N}(y,\epsilon)}
for
0\leq t\leq T
.

For the error analysis for SGMs in the finite-sample regime, we assume that the target data measure
\pi
is sub-Gaussian; that is,
\pi
satisfies the following lemma.

Assumption 1
.

There exists
a>0
, such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{a\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(x)}<\infty.

(47)

For the proof of
Theorem

5
, we define the following quantity for
\pi
:

R_{\pi}(\epsilon)\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}}{2\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

(48)

Then, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
\pi
satisfies
Assumption

1
, then there exists
\epsilon(\pi)>0
, such that
R_{\pi}(\epsilon)<\infty
for any
\epsilon\geq\epsilon(\pi)
. Moreover, we have
\lim_{\epsilon\to+\infty}\epsilon^{-d/2}R_{\pi}(\epsilon)=(2\pi)^{d/2}
.

Proof.

Let
M_{1}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}
and
M_{2}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}}{2\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}
be the numerator and the denominator of the integrand. Then we have

\displaystyle M_{1}(x)=e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\frac{2x\cdot y}{\epsilon}-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\frac{2x\cdot y}{\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\frac{2x\cdot y}{\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}+\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{a\epsilon^{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{a\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)},

where in the last inequality we use the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality
\frac{2x\cdot y}{\epsilon}=\leq a\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}+\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{a\epsilon^{2}}
.

To provide a lower bound for
M_{2}(x)
, we can pick
R>0
such that
p_{R}\coloneqq\pi\left(B(0,R)\right)>0
. Then, we have

\displaystyle M_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}\leq R}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}}{2\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}\leq R}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{2\epsilon}-\frac{R\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}}{\epsilon}-\frac{R^{2}}{2\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}

\displaystyle=p_{R}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{2\epsilon}-\frac{R\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}}{\epsilon}-\frac{R^{2}}{2\epsilon}},

where in the last inequality we use the fact that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert}^{2}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}+2R\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}+R^{2}
for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}\leq R
.

Therefore,

\frac{M_{1}(x)}{M_{2}(x)}\leq\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{a\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}}{p_{R}}e^{\frac{R^{2}}{2\epsilon}}\cdot e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{2\epsilon}+\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}}{a\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{R\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}}{\epsilon}},

(49)

and the integral of the right-hand side in
x
is finite whenever
2\epsilon<a\epsilon^{2}
; that is,
\epsilon>\frac{2}{a}
, where
a
is the constant in
Assumption

1
.

For the second part, we can write

R_{\pi}(\epsilon)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{M_{1}(x)}{M_{2}(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}=\epsilon^{d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{M_{1}(\sqrt{\epsilon}z)}{M_{2}(\sqrt{\epsilon}z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{z}\coloneqq\epsilon^{d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\epsilon}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{z},

(50)

and
\epsilon^{-d/2}R_{\pi}(\epsilon)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\epsilon}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{z}
. Note that

\displaystyle M_{1}(\sqrt{\epsilon}z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sqrt{\epsilon}z-y\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}=e^{-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\frac{2z\cdot y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)},

the integrand of which can be bounded by

e^{\frac{2z\cdot y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}{\epsilon}}\leq e^{\frac{2z\cdot y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}}\leq a\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}+\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}{a\epsilon}\leq a\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}+\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}{a}

(51)

by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality we used for the first part for
\epsilon>1
. Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem,
\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}M_{1}(\sqrt{\epsilon}z)=e^{-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}
. Similarly, we can show that
\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}M_{2}(\sqrt{\epsilon}z)=e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}{2}}
, and so that we have
\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}F_{\epsilon}(z)=e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}{2}}
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}F_{\epsilon}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{z}=(2\pi)^{d/2}
.
Moreover, using (
49
), we have

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon}(z)=\frac{M_{1}(\sqrt{\epsilon}z)}{M_{2}(\sqrt{\epsilon}z)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{a\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}}{p_{R}}e^{\frac{R^{2}}{2\epsilon}}\cdot e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}{a\epsilon}+\frac{R\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{a\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}}{p_{R}}e^{\frac{R^{2}}{2\epsilon}}\cdot e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{2}}{4}+R\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}}

when
\epsilon>\max\{4a^{-1},1\}
, and the right-hand side is integrable in
z
. Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem,
\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}\epsilon^{-d/2}R_{\pi}(\epsilon)=\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\epsilon}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{z}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}F_{\epsilon}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{z}=(2\pi)^{d/2}
.
∎

The following lemma provides a bound on the population-level error resulting from early stopping.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}(\pi)
be the first moment of
\pi
, and
\pi^{\epsilon}
be the law of running the process (
4
) for
\epsilon
time with
\mathbf{f}(x_{t},t)=x_{t}
and
\sigma(t)=\sqrt{2}
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi^{\epsilon},\pi)\leq C_{d}\sqrt{(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}+(1-e^{-\epsilon})\Gamma_{1}(\pi),

(52)

where
C_{d}>0
is a dimensional constant.

Proof.

We can formulate
\pi^{\epsilon}
as

\pi^{\epsilon}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{\epsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)},

(53)

where

P_{\epsilon}(x,y)=[2\pi(1-e^{-2\epsilon})]^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-e^{-\epsilon}y\right\rVert}^{2}}{2(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}}

(54)

is the transition probability of the OU process.
Then, for any 1-Lipschitz function
g
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{(\pi^{\epsilon}-\pi)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi^{\epsilon}(x)}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)P_{\epsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(y)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)P_{\epsilon}(y,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(x)}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)P_{\epsilon}(y,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}-g(x)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(x)},

where in the third equality we apply a change of variables
x\mapsto y
and
y\mapsto x
, and Fubini’s theorem guarantees the switch of the order of integration. To bound the integrand, we have

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)P_{\epsilon}(y,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}-g(x)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[2\pi(1-e^{-2\epsilon})]^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y-e^{-\epsilon}x\right\rVert}^{2}}{2(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}}g(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}-g(x)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[2\pi(1-e^{-2\epsilon})]^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}{2(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}}g(y+e^{-\epsilon}x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}-g(x)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[2\pi(1-e^{-2\epsilon})]^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}{2(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}}\left[g(y+e^{-\epsilon}x)-g(x)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[2\pi(1-e^{-2\epsilon})]^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}{2(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert g(y+e^{-\epsilon}x)-g(x)\right\rvert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[2\pi(1-e^{-2\epsilon})]^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}^{2}}{2(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}+(1-e^{-\epsilon})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=C_{d}\sqrt{(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}+(1-e^{-\epsilon})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{(\pi^{\epsilon}-\pi)}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)P_{\epsilon}(y,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}-g(x)\right\rvert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}C_{d}\sqrt{(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}+(1-e^{-\epsilon})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle=C_{d}\sqrt{(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}+(1-e^{-\epsilon})\Gamma_{1}(\pi).

∎

We now derive an average
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error bound for SGMs when score matching is performed using empirical measures with early stopping
\epsilon>0
; that is, the DSM objective is
\mathcal{J}_{D}(\eta^{N,\epsilon},\mathbf{s}_{\theta})
instead of

. Compared with the bound in
Theorem

3
, additional error terms arise due to finite-sample effects and early stopping.

Theorem 5

(
\mathcal{W}_{1}
error bound for SGMs, finite-sample regime)
.

Assume that the target data measure
\pi
satisfies
Assumption

1
and that the noising process follows (
32
), and the early stopping
\epsilon>\epsilon(\pi)
defined in
Lemma

3
. Let
A>0
, and suppose the generated distribution

satisfies (
33
). Moreover, assume
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\in[0,T]}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x,t)\right\rVert}_{2}\leq A
and let
e_{nn}=\mathcal{J}_{D}(\eta^{N,\epsilon},\mathbf{s}_{\theta})
be the DSM error with early stopping
\epsilon
for any

i.i.d. samples
\pi^{N}
drawn from
\pi
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,m(T))\right]\leq C_{d}\sqrt{1-e^{-2\epsilon}}

\displaystyle+(1-e^{-\epsilon})\Gamma_{1}(\pi)

(55)

\displaystyle+e^{(1+2A)T}\left(e^{-(T+\epsilon)}\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\pi,\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})\right)+\sqrt{T}\sqrt{e_{nn}^{\prime}}\right),

\displaystyle e_{nn}^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}[e_{nn}]

\displaystyle+C_{d}Te^{-\epsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi^{N},\pi)\right]\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\right\rVert}^{2}_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T])}

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{N}\left([2\pi(e^{2(\epsilon+T)}-1)]^{-\frac{d}{2}}R_{\pi}(e^{2(\epsilon+T)}-1)-1\right),

Before we prove the theorem, we interpret each term in the upper bound:

•

The term
C_{d}\sqrt{(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}+(1-e^{-\epsilon})\Gamma_{1}(\pi)
is due to early stopping
\epsilon
;

•

and
e_{nn}

N

\epsilon
. Moreover, by

\mathbb{E}[e_{nn}]\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\log\eta^{N,\epsilon}\right\rVert}^{2}\eta^{N,\epsilon}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{4e^{-2(\epsilon+t)}}{(1-e^{-2(\epsilon+t)})^{2}}\Gamma_{2}(\pi)+\frac{2d}{1-e^{-2(\epsilon+t)}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}=2(\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\epsilon}}-\frac{1}{1-e^{-2(\epsilon+T)}})\Gamma_{2}(\pi)+d\log\frac{e^{2(\epsilon+T)}-1}{e^{2\epsilon}-1}

•

Within

•

When the domain is
R\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the

-dimensional torus endowed with periodic boundary conditions, the WUP result from

[

.

Proof.

We decompose
\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,m(T))
as

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,m(T))\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{\epsilon})+\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi^{\epsilon},m(T)).

(57)

By
Lemma

4
, we have
\mathcal{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{\epsilon})\leq C_{d}\sqrt{(1-e^{-2\epsilon})}+(1-e^{-\epsilon})\Gamma_{1}(\pi)
.
To bound the second term on the right-hand side of (
57
), we define
\eta^{\pi,\epsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
as the solution of

First, we have

Remark 8

(Lipschitzness of vector fields)
.

In contrast to
Theorem

5
for SGMs, the velocity field in flow matching is not intrinsically determined by the underlying distribution through a score function. Consequently, the quantity
\mathbb{E}[e_{nn}]
depends not only on the neural network architecture but also on the particular choice of interpolation flow
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
. Moreover, unlike the empirical score field, which admits the population counterpart, an empirical velocity field does not generally possess a canonical population-level analogue. As a result, the behavior of
\mathbb{E}[e_{nn}]
may be substantially more sensitive to finite-sample effects. Furthermore, for many flow matching constructions, the target velocity field associated with the empirical measure
\pi^{N}
may become increasingly irregular near the endpoints of the time interval. Therefore, under the architectural constraint
\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}\|\nabla_{x}\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x,t)\|_{2}\leq A
, the universal approximation theorem does not necessarily imply that
e_{nn}
can be made arbitrarily small. Indeed, if the target velocity field cannot be well approximated within the prescribed Lipschitz class, then a nontrivial approximation error may remain. From this perspective, imposing a uniform Lipschitz bound can be viewed as an implicit regularization that may prevent exact memorization of the empirical measure while improving stability and generalization. It is also applicable in the SGM case
Theorem

5
, and it becomes even more obvious in (
64
); in that sense, the parametrization (
64
) takes out the singularity at
t=0,\epsilon=0
and makes a more meaningful regularization assumption in
Corollary

2
.

7
Provable efficiency of equivariant SGMs and equivariant flow matching

In this section, building on
Section

6
, we consider generative modeling when the target distribution is invariant under a given group action. After introducing the necessary notions of group actions and symmetrization operators, we apply
Theorem

5
to obtain a Wasserstein-1 error bound for SGMs with data augmentation. We then develop a theory of equivariant vector fields for SGMs, establishing their key structural properties and demonstrating that equivariant parameterizations are provably superior to standard data augmentation schemes. Finally, we connect these results to equivariant flow matching, revealing a unified perspective on symmetry exploitation in flow-based generative models.

7.1
Group actions and symmetrization operators

Let
\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the space of bounded measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
group
is a set
G
equipped with a group product satisfying the axioms of associativity, identity, and invertibility. Given a group
G
and a set
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a map
\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a
group action on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if
\theta_{g}\coloneqq\theta(g,\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an automorphism on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
g\in G
, and
\theta_{g_{2}}\circ\theta_{g_{1}}=\theta_{g_{2}\cdot g_{1}}
,
\forall g_{1},g_{2}\in G
. By convention, we will abbreviate
\theta(g,x)
as
gx
throughout the paper.

A function
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
G
-invariant
if
\gamma\circ\theta_{g}=\gamma,\forall g\in G
. On the other hand, a probability measure
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is called
G
-invariant
if
P=(\theta_{g})_{*}P,\forall g\in G
, where
(\theta_{g})_{*}P\coloneqq P\circ(\theta_{g})^{-1}
is the push-forward measure of
P
under
\theta_{g}
. We denote the set of all
G
-invariant probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}P~\text{is}~G\text{-invariant}\}
. We make the following assumption on
G
.

Assumption 2
.

G
is a group such that the mapping
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be written as
g(x)\mapsto A_{g}x
for some orthogonal matrix
A_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
for any
g\in G,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. That is, each
g\in G
induces a linear isometry.

For example,
G
can include rotations and reflections. Given a group action induced by a group
G
, we introduce two symmetrization operators from
[
6
]
, that are useful for our theoretical analysis.

Symmetrization of functions:

S_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

S_{G}[\gamma](x)\coloneq\int_{G}\gamma(gx)\mu_{G}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{g})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{G}}[\gamma\circ g(x)],

(71)

where
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mu_{G}
is the unique Haar probability measure of
G
. Under
Assumption

2
,
S_{G}
is also well-defined for any continuous functions that may be unbounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, since
G
is compact.

Symmetrization of probability measures (dual operator of
S_{G}
):

S^{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, defined for
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\mathbb{E}_{S^{G}[P]}\gamma\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}S_{G}[\gamma]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{P(x)}=\mathbb{E}_{P}S_{G}[\gamma].

(72)

The operator
S^{G}
is commonly understood as
data augmentation
when
P
is an empirical measure of finite training samples. It has been shown in
[
6
]
that both
S_{G}
and
S^{G}
are projections. We also abuse the notation that if
P
evolves with time, then
S^{G}[P]
means the symmetrization of
P
at each time.

Definition 2
.

Given a group action induced by a group
G
that satisfies
Assumption

2
, we say a vector field
\mathbf{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
G
-equivariant if

\mathbf{s}(gx,t)=A_{g}\cdot\mathbf{s}(x,t)

(73)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
g\in G
.

It can be easily verified that if
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then its score
\nabla\log\rho
is
G
-equivariant. In addition to
S_{G}
and
S^{G}
, we propose the following symmetrization operator for vector fields.

Symmetrization of vector fields:

S_{G}^{E}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d})\to(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

S_{G}^{E}[\mathbf{s}](x,t)\coloneq\int_{G}A_{g}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{s}(gx,t)\mu_{G}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{g})

(74)

for any vector field
\mathbf{s}
, which is an extension of formula (12) in
[
30
]
for finite groups. It can be shown that
S_{G}^{E}[\mathbf{s}]
is
G
-equivariant for any vector field
\mathbf{s}
. The proof can be found in
Section

9
. By the definition of equivariance, we immediately have
S^{E}_{G}[\mathbf{s}]=\mathbf{s}
if
\mathbf{s}
is
G
-equivariant.

7.2

We apply

In contrast to score matching with

7.3
Properties of score matching with equivariant vector fields

Corollary

4
does not explicitly convey the significance of equivariant vector fields in score matching. To that end, we highlight the role of
G
-equivariant vector fields (typically parameterized by neural networks) in score matching, an aspect that has only been addressed experimentally in previous studies. Our rigorous results indicate that it suffices to perform score matching with
G
-equivariant vector fields with respect to an unsymmetrized empirical distribution. This approach will be particularly beneficial when we only have a finite set of
unaugmented
samples, i.e., a non-symmetric empirical distribution drawn from an invariant distribution. Proofs of results in this section can be found in
Section

9
.

First, we show that for any distribution
\rho
, the ISM objective when restricted to
G
-equivariant vector fields, is equivalent to the ISM objective with respect to its symmetrized counterpart.

Theorem 6
.

Consider the ISM problem in (
36
), in which
\rho
is not necessarily
G
-invariant. Then for any
G
-equivariant vector field
\mathbf{s}
, we have

\mathcal{J}_{I}(\rho,\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{J}_{I}(S^{G}[\rho],\mathbf{s}).

Remark 10
.

Theorem

6
is important for practical implementations, in the sense that the optimal equivariant vector field can be obtained by score matching for raw data without data augmentation. We will demonstrate this point in our numerical simulations in
Section

8
.

Moreover, for the ESM (or equivalently, the DSM) problem of a generic probability measure, the
G
-equivariant minimizer is exactly the score of the symmetrized probability measure, namely:

Proposition 1
.

Consider the ESM problem in (
35
) (or its DSM equivalent), in which
\rho
is not necessarily
G
-invariant. Denote by
V_{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the subspace of
G
-equivariant vector fields. Then we have

\argmin_{\mathbf{s}\in V_{G}}\mathcal{J}_{D}(\rho,\mathbf{s})=\argmin_{\mathbf{s}\in V_{G}}\mathcal{J}_{E}(\rho,\mathbf{s})=\nabla_{x}\left[\log\left(S^{G}[\rho]\right)\right].

We propose the following definition as an error quantification for the non-equivariance of a vector field with respect to a
G
-invariant measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]
.

Definition 3

(Deviation from equivariance)
.

The deviation from equivariance (DFE) of a vector field
\mathbf{s}
with respect to
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]
is defined as

\mathrm{DFE}(\rho,\mathbf{s})\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{s}-S_{G}^{E}[\mathbf{s}]\right\rvert}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\rho(s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{s}.

(79)

It is evident that
\mathrm{DFE}(\rho,\mathbf{s})=0
if
\mathbf{s}
is
G
-equivariant. Given this definition, we obtain the following decomposition of the ESM and DSM objectives.

Theorem 7
.

For any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]
and any vector field
\mathbf{s}
, we have

\mathcal{J}_{E}(\rho,\mathbf{s})=\mathrm{DFE}(\rho,\mathbf{s})+\mathcal{J}_{E}(\rho,S^{E}_{G}[\mathbf{s}]).

(80)

As DSM and ESM are equivalent objectives, we readily have

\mathcal{J}_{D}(\rho,\mathbf{s})=\mathrm{DFE}(\rho,\mathbf{s})+\mathcal{J}_{D}(\rho,S^{E}_{G}[\mathbf{s}])\,,\,\,\text{for any}\,\,\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times[0,T]\,.

(81)

Theorem

7
suggests that using
G
-equivariant vector fields can reduce the DSM error for
G
-invariant distributions, at least the DSM error can be reduced if
\mathbf{s}
is replaced by
S^{E}_{G}[\mathbf{s}]
for score matching.

The following proposition indicates that for any learned distribution
\eta
, its symmetrized counterpart
S^{G}[\eta]
is always closer to the
G
-invariant target distribution
\pi
in the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
sense. The
G
-invariance of the generated distribution is guaranteed by the
G
-equivariant vector field
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
and the
G
-invariant initial distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
under
Assumption

2
, as the solution of the backward equation (
33
) is unique.

Proposition 2
.

For any
\eta,\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
\pi
is
G
-invariant, we have

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\eta,\pi)\geq\mathcal{W}_{1}(S^{G}[\eta],\pi).

The significance of equivariant vector fields in SGMs

With the theoretical results established in

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{D}(\eta_{G}^{N,\epsilon},\mathbf{s}_{\theta_{1}})

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{J}_{D}(\eta^{N,\epsilon}_{G},S_{G}^{E}[\mathbf{s}_{\theta_{2}}]),

and so the gap

7.5
A symmetry-aware Wasserstein path-space divergence and equivariant flow matching

Since the flow matching objective in (
43
) shares the same
L^{2}
-regression structure as the DSM or ESM objectives for SGMs, inspired by the Wasserstein path-space divergence and the notion of DFE, we propose the following
symmetry-aware Wasserstein path-space divergence
, in which we assume one of the dynamics always has
G
-invariant marginals.

Proposition 3

(Symmetry-aware Wasserstein path-space divergence)
.

Suppose
\rho_{1}^{[0,T]}
and
\rho_{2}^{[0,T]}
are the path laws of the following SDEs

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{1}(x_{t},t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}},\quad x_{0}\sim m_{1};

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{2}(y_{t},t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W_{t}},\quad y_{0}\sim m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

from
t=0
to
t=T
respectively. Assume that
\mathbf{b}_{2}(\cdot,t)
is always
G
-equivariant for
0\leq t\leq T
and
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\in[0,T]}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla\mathbf{b}_{1}(x,t)\right\rVert}_{2}<\infty
, and we define the symmetry-aware Wasserstein path-space divergence as

\Xi_{G}\left(\rho_{1}^{[0,T]}\|\rho_{2}^{[0,T]}\right)\coloneqq\mathcal{W}_{1}(m_{1},m_{2})+\sqrt{T}\left(\left\|\mathbf{b}_{2}-S_{G}^{E}[\mathbf{b}_{1}]\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{2})}^{2}+\mathrm{DFE}(\rho_{2},\mathbf{b}_{1})\right)^{1/2},

(84)

where
\rho_{2}(x,t)
is the
G
-invariant marginal law of
\rho_{2}^{[0,T]}
at time
t
. Then we have
\Xi_{G}\left(\rho_{1}^{[0,T]}\|\rho_{2}^{[0,T]}\right)=\Xi\left(\rho_{1}^{[0,T]}\|\rho_{2}^{[0,T]}\right)
.

The result is a direct consequence of
Definition

1
and
Theorem

7
. In
Proposition

3
, one may reduce the value of the divergence, given
m_{1}
and
\mathbf{b}_{1}
, through replacing
m_{1}
by
S^{G}[m_{1}]
(due to

2
), and replacing
\mathbf{b}_{1}
by
S_{G}^{E}[\mathbf{b}_{1}]
. The DFE may also be viewed as a model-dependent regularization term and can therefore be weighted by an additional hyperparameter. More importantly, the decomposition reveals a fundamental limitation of data- and training-based improvements for learning
G
-invariant path laws: while increasing the sample size or training longer can reduce the first two error terms, neither can decrease the DFE. Similarly, data augmentation may improve the sample-complexity contribution, but it leaves the DFE unchanged. In contrast, an equivariant parametrization eliminates the DFE.

When
\pi
is
G
-invariant,
[
25
]
proposes an equivariant OT flow matching where the cost function of the OT is

without increasing the Lipschitz constant

.

Corollary 5

Suppose
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Assume
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
satisfies
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\in[0,1]}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(x,t)\right\rVert}_{2}\leq A
for some

\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
is

\pi^{N}

(85)

when we generate samples using
S_{G}^{E}[\mathbf{s}_{\theta}]
, where the expectation is taken over
N
i.i.d. samples drawn from
\pi
.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{\rho}(1)

\tilde{\mathbf{v}}

10
Conclusion

We have proposed a new Wasserstein path-space divergence and developed a unified
\mathcal{W}_{1}
robustness and generalization theory for flow-based generative models, built on a probabilistic WUP theorem that applies to unbounded domains, accommodates time-varying and possibly degenerate diffusion coefficients, and remains valid in the noiseless setting. Based on the WUP, we obtain
\mathcal{W}_{1}
generalization bounds for both score-based diffusion models and flow matching. Specializing the framework to group-invariant targets, we provide the first rigorous error analysis of equivariant SGMs and flow matching, identify a model-form DFE error that quantifies the cost of a non-equivariant parametrization, and show analytically that equivariant inductive bias strictly dominates data augmentation.
Several directions remain open. First, some real-world data distributions, such as image distributions on square domains, may not exhibit exact invariance under group actions. It would therefore be worthwhile to extend our analysis to distributions possessing only approximate symmetries, for instance, when the target distribution is close to a
G
-invariant distribution
[
5
]
under a metric such as
\mathcal{W}_{1}
. On the numerical side, our analysis does not account for the time discretization of SGMs or flow matching; incorporating discretization error, or designing symmetry-preserving numerical integrators within our theoretical framework, would close an important gap between theory and practice. Beyond the OU noising process considered here, extending the framework to nonlinear forward processes such as those in
[
5
,
34
]
is a natural next step. Finally, the symmetrization operator under group symmetry can equivalently be viewed as a conditional expectation on the
\sigma
-algebra generated by group orbits; see some preliminary discussion in
[
6
]
.
This perspective suggests an extension of our symmetry framework to more general coarse-graining generative models in which only a smaller
\sigma
-algebra is observable, a direction we find particularly promising for scientific applications where partial observability is the norm. In a different direction, while our primary motivation comes from generative modeling, the Wasserstein path-space divergence and the associated uncertainty propagation theorem are formulated at the level of dynamical systems and are not tied to any particular learning architecture. This opens the possibility of applying the framework to model calibration, sensitivity analysis, uncertainty quantification, and operator learning for stochastic and deterministic systems, as previously performed in the KL framework
[
14
,
24
,
42
]
. We expect that extending the path-space UQ paradigm beyond relative entropy to Wasserstein metrics and the symmetry-aware variants will prove fruitful in a broad range of applications where singular measures, deterministic dynamics, or geometric structure play a central role.
