Waves, structures, and the Riemann problem for a system of hyperbolic conservation laws
A. P. Chugainova, D. V. Treschev

Abstract




Abstract

A system of hyperbolic conservation laws

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\partial_{u}Q=0,\quad Q=u_{1}^{3}/3+u_{1}u_{2}^{2},\qquad u=u(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

as well as its viscous regularization

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\partial_{u}Q={\cal M}\partial_{x}^{2}u,\qquad{\cal M}=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),\quad\mu_{1}>0,\,\mu_{2}>0,

are studied. It is assumed that admissible shocks are those that
satisfy the requirement of existence of a structure (the traveling
wave criterion). A solution of the Riemann problem is constructed
that consists of rarefaction waves and shocks with structure.
Depending on the conditions imposed at
\pm\infty
, the solution
also contains undercompressive shocks and Jouguet waves.

1
Introduction

Consider a system of two nonlinear partial differential equations

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\partial_{u}Q=0,\quad Q=Q(u),\qquad u=u(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(1.1)

The eigenvalues
c_{1}\leq c_{2}
of the
2\times 2
matrix

\partial^{2}_{u}Q(u)
are the characteristic velocities at the point

u
. Since the matrix
\partial^{2}_{u}Q
is symmetric, the
eigenvalues are always real; therefore, system (
1.1
) is
hyperbolic. The strict hyperbolicity condition
c_{1}\neq c_{2}
may
not be satisfied at some points.

Discontinuous solutions of the form

u=u(\xi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}u^{+}&\mbox{if }\xi>0,\\
u^{-}&\mbox{if }\xi<0,\end{array},\right.\qquad\xi=x-Wt,

(1.2)

where
W
is the shock velocity, are called shocks.

The existence of solution (
1.2
) requires that the constants

u^{\pm}
lie on the same Hugoniot locus (Rankine–Hugoniot locus).
The Hugoniot locus is defined by the point
u^{+}
and is given by
the conservation laws at the discontinuity:

\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:W(u-u^{+})=\partial_{u}Q(u)-\partial_{u}Q(u^{+})\}.

(1.3)

However, not all the points
u=u^{-}
of the Hugoniot locus define a
shock. We should also require that the evolutionary condition (the
Lax condition
[
1
]
) be satisfied. For the
2\times 2
system,
these conditions are given by the following inequalities:

\displaystyle c_{2}^{+}<W,\quad c_{1}^{-}<W<c_{2}^{-}\quad\mbox{fast compressive shocks},

(1.4)

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}<W<c_{2}^{+},\quad W<c_{1}^{-}\quad\mbox{slow compressive shocks}.

(1.5)

Here
c_{1,2}^{\pm}
are the characteristic velocities ahead of

(+)
and behind
(-)
the shock.

A special role is played by undercompressive and overcompressive
shocks
[
2
]
(nonclassical shocks). The velocity of
undercompressive shocks satisfies inequalities (
1.6
),
while the velocity of overcompressive shocks satisfies
inequalities (
1.7
):

\displaystyle c_{1}^{+}<W<c_{2}^{+},\quad c_{1}^{-}<W<c_{2}^{-},

(1.6)

\displaystyle c_{2}^{+}<W<c_{1}^{-}.

(1.7)

The functions (
1.2
) are solutions of equations (
1.1
)
only in the generalized sense. It is well known (see, for example,

[
2
]
) that classical solutions of nonlinear hyperbolic
equations exist in general only on a finite time interval (the
gradient catastrophe). Therefore, along with system (
1.1
),
we also consider it regularization

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\partial_{u}Q={\cal M}\partial_{x}^{2}u,\qquad{\cal M}=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}).

(1.8)

Here
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are positive constants. The right-hand
side of (
1.8
) has the physical meaning of dissipative terms.
In applications these terms are often considered to be small,
although the key role in qualitative analysis is played not by the
values of the parameters
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, but by their ratio,
since the system (
1.8
) is invariant under rescaling

t\mapsto st,\quad x\mapsto sx,\quad\mu_{1}\mapsto s\mu_{1},\quad\mu_{2}\mapsto s\mu_{2}.

For system (
1.8
) we can consider traveling wave solutions

u=u(\xi),\qquad\xi=x-Wt,\quad\lim_{\xi\to\pm\infty}u(\xi)=u^{\pm}.

(1.9)

As
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\to 0
, these solutions turn into discontinuous
wave solutions of equation (
1.1
), and the quantities
u^{-}

and
u^{+}
are the limiting values of
u
at
x=-\infty
and

x=\infty
, respectively. In this case, the discontinuous solution
(
1.2
) is said to have a structure defined by the family of
solutions (
1.9
)
u(\xi)=u(\xi;\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
.

It is natural to take the above-mentioned limit as
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\searrow 0
along some preselected ray on which
\mu_{1}/\mu_{2}=
const. Then the presence or absence of structure of a shock
depends on the ratio
\mu_{1}/\mu_{2}
. Undercompressive and
overcompressive shocks may also have a structure. In the case of
undercompressive shocks, this occurs for a special choice of the
parameters
u^{+},W,\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
; more precisely, for the
values of parameters from some subset of codimension 1.

The dynamic stability of a shock is an important aspect
determining its observability. By stability we mean spectral
stability, or in other words, stability in linear approximation.

As a rule, the calculation of the spectral characteristics of the
wave solution (
1.9
) is a difficult problem. One of popular
methods for analyzing spectral stability is the application of the
argument principle to the Evans function
[
3
,
4
]
;
however, this usually can be done only numerically.

In a number of publications
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
, the authors studied the solutions of a

2\times 2
nonstrictly hyperbolic system of conservation laws. In

Q=pu_{1}^{3}/3+qu_{1}^{2}u_{2}+u_{1}u_{2}^{2}
. In
[
6

7
]
, the authors studied the Riemann problem for equations
(
1.1
) and (
1.8
) with such

in the case of

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
.

Let
q=0
. The study of such a system is motivated by the problem
of long small-amplitude longitudinal-torsional waves propagating
in the positive direction of the

rods
[

. The variables

Next, we consider the case of

(1.10)

Note that system (

) with potential (

However, the problem becomes nontrivial in the context of viscous

1.8

{\cal M}

(
\mu_{1}\neq\mu_{2}

In this paper we carry out the most comprehensive study of

2
The Hugoniot locus

Solutions of the form (
1.9
) satisfy the equation

(-Wu+\partial_{u}Q)^{\prime}={\cal M}u^{\prime\prime},\qquad(\cdot)^{\prime}=d/d\xi,

or after integration with respect to
\xi
,

u^{\prime}=-{\cal M}^{-1}\partial_{u}Z(u),\qquad Z(u)=-Q+W(u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2})/2+D_{1}u_{1}+D_{2}u_{2}.

(2.1)

The ordinary differential equation (
2.1
) is a gradient-like
system (in renormalized coordinates, gradient system) in the
plane. The coefficients
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are constants. The
function
Z
decreases along integral curves. In the Morse–Smale
theory, functions of the type
Z
are called energy functions
(see, for example,
[
12
]
).

The singular points (equilibrium states) of system (
2.1
)
satisfy the equations
\partial_{u}Z=0
:

-u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}+Wu_{1}+D_{1}=0,\quad-2u_{1}u_{2}+Wu_{2}+D_{2}=0.

(2.2)

Hence we obtain

D_{1}={u_{1}^{+}}^{2}+{u_{2}^{+}}^{2}-Wu_{1}^{+},\quad D_{2}=2u_{1}^{+}u_{2}^{+}-Wu_{2}^{+}.

(2.3)

If the determinant of the Hesse matrix

\det\partial^{2}_{u}Z=(-2u_{1}+W)^{2}-4u_{2}^{2},

(2.4)

is positive at a singular point
u
, then
u
is a node, while, if
it is negative, then
u
is a saddle.

Henceforth we assume that, for any
W
, the quantities

D_{1}=D_{1}(W,u^{+})
and
D_{2}=D_{2}(W,u^{+})
satisfy equalities
(
2.3
), so that for all
W
the point
u^{+}
is an equilibrium
state of system (
2.1
).

For any
W\in\mathbb{R}
, this system (
2.2
), (
2.3
) has four
solutions with respect to
u
(including multiplicities):

\begin{array}[]{cllrcl}u^{+}&=&(u_{1}^{+},u_{2}^{+}),&\quad u^{\times}&=&(W-u_{1}^{+},-u_{2}^{+}),\\
u^{a}&=&(u_{2}^{+}+W/2,u_{1}^{+}-W/2),&\quad u^{b}&=&(W/2-u_{2}^{+},W/2-u_{1}^{+}).\end{array}

(2.5)

Proposition 2.1

If all points (
2.5
) are different, then

u^{+}
and
u^{\times}
are of the same type (an extremum or a
saddle). The points
u^{a}
and
u^{b}
are also of the same
(opposite) type (a saddle or an extremum).

Proof
. It suffices to calculate (
2.4
) at the points
(
2.5
).

The curve

\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\partial_{u}Z(u)=\partial_{u}Z(u^{+}),\;W\in\mathbb{R}\}

is called the Hugoniot locus (the Rankine–Hugoniot locus). It
consists of the equilibrium states of system
(
2.1
)
|_{D=D(W,u^{+})}
.

There is the following equivalent definition of the Hugoniot locus
in the theory of hyperbolic equations. The system of equations
(
1.1
) expresses conservation laws, which correspond to the
relations at the discontinuities of the solutions
u(\xi)
:

W(u^{+}-u^{-})=\partial_{u}Q(u^{+})-\partial_{u}Q(u^{-}).

(2.6)

Here
W
is the velocity of the discontinuity. If we fix a value
of
u^{+}
and take
u=u^{-}
, then (
2.6
) gives the equation

W(u_{1}^{+}-u_{1})={u_{1}^{+}}^{2}+{u_{2}^{+}}^{2}-u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2},\quad W(u_{2}^{+}-u_{2})=2u_{1}^{+}u_{2}^{+}-2u_{1}u_{2},

(2.7)

which is equivalent to (
2.2
).

Eliminating the velocity
W
in equalities (
2.7
), we obtain
another equation of the Hugoniot locus:

(u_{2}+u_{2}^{+})(u_{2}-u_{1}+u_{1}^{+}-u^{+}_{2})(u_{2}+u_{1}-u_{1}^{+}-u_{2}^{+})=0.

(2.8)

Therefore the Hugoniot locus represents three straight lines (see
Fig.
1
)

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-u_{2}^{+},\qquad\qquad\quad\;W=u_{1}+u_{1}^{+},

(2.9)

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{1}-u_{1}^{+}+u_{2}^{+},\quad\;\;W=2(u_{1}+u_{2}^{+}),

(2.10)

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-u_{1}+u_{1}^{+}+u_{2}^{+},\quad W=2(u_{1}-u_{2}^{+}).

(2.11)

Figure 1:
Hugoniot
locus for the parameters
u_{1}^{+}=2
,
u_{2}^{+}=3
, and
p=1
.

The point

. The points of intersection

3
Evolutionary conditions for discontinuities. Shocks

Among the set of discontinuities in the solutions of the system of
equations (
1.1
), we distinguish evolutionary discontinuities
(shocks), which satisfy the Lax conditions
[
1
]
. The Lax
conditions are expressed as inequalities
(
1.4
)–(
1.5
) between the shock velocity
W
and the
characteristic velocities
c_{1}^{+}<c_{2}^{+}
ahead of and
c_{1}^{-}<c_{2}^{-}
behind the shock. The evolutionary conditions
(
1.4
)–(
1.5
) are equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{+}<0,\quad\lambda_{1}^{-}<0<\lambda_{2}^{-}\quad\mbox{fast shocks},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{+}<0<\lambda_{2}^{+},\quad 0<\lambda_{1}^{-}\quad\mbox{slow shocks},

(3.2)

where
\lambda_{j}^{\pm}=c_{j}^{\pm}-W
are the eigenvalues of the
matrix
-\partial^{2}_{u}Z(u^{\pm})
. Note that, generally
speaking,
1
1

1

if the operator
{\cal M}
is not scalar
the
characteristic numbers of singular points of equation (
2.1
))
do not coincide with
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
but are closely
related to it: a singular point is a node if
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}>0
and a saddle if
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<0
.

Conditions (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) imply that the type of the
critical points
u^{+}
and
u=u^{-}
of the function
Z
is as
follows: either
u
is a saddle and
u^{+}
is a local minimum, or

u
is a local maximum and
u^{+}
is a saddle. Since the type of a
singular point is determined by the sign of expression
(
2.4
), we obtain the evolutionary condition in the form

\Big((-u_{1}+W/2)^{2}-u_{2}^{2}\Big)\Big((-u_{1}^{+}+W/2)^{2}-{u_{2}^{+}}^{2}\Big)<0;\\

(3.3)

in this case, (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) give an additional
condition:

\displaystyle\mbox{either the matrix $\partial_{u}^{2}Z(u^{+})$ is positive definite (fast wave)},

\displaystyle\mbox{or the matrix $\partial_{u}^{2}Z(u)$ is
negative definite (slow wave)}.

Let us verify if the evolutionary conditions hold at the points of
the Hugoniot locus. Recall that the quantity
u_{2}^{+}
is assumed to
be nonnegative.

Proposition 3.1

The states
u^{-}
corresponding to slow
shocks lie in the interval
AB
, and the values of
u^{-}

corresponding to fast shocks lie on the straight line
CA
to the
right of the point
A
and on the line
AB
to the right of the
point
B
.

Proof
. On the component (
2.9
) of the Hugoniot locus,
inequality (
3.3
) takes the form

\Big((u_{1}-u_{1}^{+})^{2}/4-{u_{2}^{+}}^{2}\Big)^{2}<0.

It does not hold at any point of the line (
2.9
).

On the component (
2.10
), inequality (
3.3
) turns
out to be as follows:

-\Big({u_{2}^{+}}^{2}-(u_{1}-u_{1}^{+}+u_{2}^{+})^{2}\Big)^{2}<0.

It holds always (if
u_{1}-u_{1}^{+}+u_{2}^{+}\neq\pm u_{2}^{+}
). Since

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{u}Z(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}u_{2}^{+}&-u_{1}+u_{1}^{+}-u_{2}^{+}\\
-u_{1}+u_{1}^{+}-u_{2}^{+}&u_{2}^{+}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{u}Z(u^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-u_{1}^{+}+u_{1}+u_{2}^{+}&-u_{2}^{+}\\
-u_{2}^{+}&-u_{1}^{+}+u_{1}+u_{2}^{+}\end{array}\right),

we find that fast waves correspond to the interval

and that there are no slow waves.

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{u}Z(u^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}u_{1}-u_{1}^{+}-u_{2}^{+}&-u_{2}^{+}\\

we find that fast waves on the line (

4
Rarefaction waves

Rarefaction waves in equation (
1.1
) are solutions of the
form
u=u(\vartheta)
,
\vartheta=x/t
. Substitution into (
1.1
)
yields a system of ordinary differential equations

(\partial_{u}^{2}Q-\vartheta)\partial_{\vartheta}u=0.

(4.1)

The nontrivial solution
\partial_{\vartheta}u
of the linear
homogeneous system of equations (
4.1
) exists if
\vartheta
is
an eigenvalue of the linear operator
\partial_{u}^{2}Q(u(\vartheta))
,
in other words, if it is the characteristic velocity.

Assuming that
u_{2}>0
, at every point
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
we obtain the
eigenvalues
\vartheta_{s}<\vartheta_{f}
and eigenvectors
\beta_{s},\beta_{f}

corresponding to slow and fast rarefaction waves:

\vartheta_{s}=2u_{1}-2u_{2},\quad\beta_{s}=\bigg(\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-1\end{array}\!\!\bigg),\qquad\vartheta_{f}=2u_{1}+2u_{2},\quad\beta_{f}=\bigg(\!\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\end{array}\!\bigg).

Self-similar solutions corresponding to rarefaction waves have the
form

\displaystyle\!\!\!\vartheta_{s}=\vartheta,\quad 2u_{1}+2u_{2}=\mbox{const}.\quad\mbox{(slow rarefaction waves)},

\displaystyle\!\!\!\vartheta_{f}=\vartheta,\quad 2u_{1}-2u_{2}=\mbox{const}.\quad\mbox{(fast rarefaction waves)}.

Thus, rarefaction waves exist when

\displaystyle u^{+}-u^{-}=\lambda\Big(\!\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\end{array}\!\Big),\quad\mbox{(fast waves)},

\displaystyle u^{+}-u^{-}=\lambda\Big(\!\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-1\end{array}\!\Big)\quad\mbox{(slow waves)}

(
\lambda>0
is an arbitrary constant) and have the form

\displaystyle u=u_{s}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}u^{-}&\mbox{if }\vartheta<2u_{1}^{-}-2u_{2}^{-},\\
\displaystyle\frac{\vartheta}{4}\Big(\!\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-1\end{array}\!\Big)+\frac{u_{1}^{+}+u_{2}^{+}}{2}\Big(\!\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\end{array}\!\Big)&\mbox{if }2u_{1}^{-}-2u_{2}^{-}<\vartheta<2u_{1}^{+}-2u_{2}^{+},\\
u^{+}&\mbox{if }2u_{1}^{+}-2u_{2}^{+}<\vartheta,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle u=u_{f}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}u^{-}&\mbox{if }\vartheta<2u_{1}^{-}+2u_{2}^{-},\\
\displaystyle\frac{\vartheta}{4}\Big(\!\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\end{array}\!\Big)+\frac{u_{1}^{+}-u_{2}^{+}}{2}\Big(\!\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-1\end{array}\!\Big)&\mbox{if }2u_{1}^{-}+2u_{2}^{-}<\vartheta<2u_{1}^{+}+2u_{2}^{+},\\
u^{+}&\mbox{if }2u_{1}^{+}+2u_{2}^{+}<\vartheta.\end{array}\right.

Particles in the rarefaction wave move with different velocities.
This velocity monotonically changes from the left end (point

u^{-}
) to the right end (point
u^{+}
), which move with velocities

\displaystyle c_{s}(u^{-})=2u_{1}^{-}-2u_{2}^{-},\quad c_{s}(u^{+})=2u_{1}^{+}-2u_{2}^{+},

(4.12)

\displaystyle c_{f}(u^{-})=2u_{1}^{-}+2u_{2}^{-},\quad c_{f}(u^{+})=2u_{1}^{+}+2u_{2}^{+}.

(4.13)

In the case of
u_{2}^{+}<0
, the subscripts
s
and
f
in these
equalities should be interchanged.

Rarefaction waves are defined by continuous (moreover, piecewise
smooth) functions
u_{s}(\vartheta)
and
u_{f}(\vartheta)
that can be
obtained as the limits as
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\rightarrow 0
of the smooth
solutions
u(x,t,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
,

u(\pm\infty,t,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=u^{\pm}
of system (
1.8
).

5
Undercompressive shocks and saddle connections

For the system of equations (
1.1
), there may exist
undercompressive (nonclassical) shocks with structure. The
structure of an undercompressive shock is represented by an
integral curve connecting two saddle points of system (
2.1
).
The existence condition of a saddle connection determines the
velocity
W
of the undercompressive shock. This value of the
velocity of the undercompressive shock provides an additional
condition on the shock.

Undercompressive shocks exist when inequalities (
1.6
) are
satisfied. Equivalently,

The statement of the problem needs comments. Since we consider the
solutions of equation (
1.1

the solutions of the regularized equation (

construct a solution of the Riemann problem only from the waves
with structure. Recall that the limit as
{\cal M}\to 0

considered for a fixed ratio

absence of structure of a discontinuous solution essentially
depends on this fact. Therefore, the parameter
\mu_{1}/\mu_{2}

explicitly involved in the statement of the Riemann problem and in
its solution. From the viewpoint of the physical meaning of the
solution, it is also important to raise the question of stability
of the structures obtained. We do not discuss this question.
However, we note that in any numerical experiment we did not
observe dynamically unstable wave solutions in the system that
arise from the heteroclinics of equation (
2.1
).

For a regularized
