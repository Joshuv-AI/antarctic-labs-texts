Estimation and Inference for Causal Functions with Multiway Clustered Data
Nan Liu Yanbo Liu Yuya Sasaki

Abstract
This supplementary material consists of three appendix sections. Section C presents the auxiliary lemmas and results that support the main theorems. Section D complements the main paper by providing identification results for the causal functions discussed in the main draft. Finally, Section E outlines the estimation and testing procedures for causal functions under a general multiway clustering scheme.



1
Introduction

Multiway cluster dependence is ubiquitous in empirical data.
Units in such data exhibit strong dependence within geographical locations, industrial sectors, and other clusters.
For instance, data for airline and automobile industries used for demand estimation
(
Berry, 1992
;
Berry
et al., 1995
, e.g.,)
are indexed by markets and products, which represent two cluster sources of strong dependence through demand and supply shocks, respectively.
Datasets used to analyze the causal effects of trade and technology shocks on employment
(
Autor and
Dorn, 2013
;
Autor
et al., 2015
;
Acemoglu et al., 2016
, e.g.,)
exhibit two-way cluster dependence by commuting zones and job occupations.
Financial data used to analyze causal effects of technological innovation
(
Hsu
et al., 2014
, e.g.,)
exhibit two-way cluster dependence by countries and industries.

Accounting for this type of cross-sectional dependence is essential for conducting accurate statistical inference as such dependence may invalidate the conventional asymptotic theory developed for iid sampling and can produce substantial size distortions for causal inference
(
Cameron
et al., 2011
)
. The challenges that arise from multiway clustering necessitate special care to address complicated cross-sectional dependence structures.

The recent literature develops methods of inference about various estimands under multiway clustering, but they do not directly apply to inference for causal functions.
This paper addresses this gap.
Under multiway clustering, we propose novel methods for estimating and conducting uniform inference for a general class of non-parametric causal functions
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
, which can be indentified as a conditional expectation of the form

\displaystyle\tau_{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\psi(\eta_{0})|X=x],

(1.1)

where the signal
\psi(\eta_{0})
may depend on a high-dimensional nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}
. Examples of such functions
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
include the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) and continuous treatment effects (CTE), among others, as discussed in more detail later. Typical examples of nuisance parameters
\eta_{0}
include the propensity score, conditional density, etc.

There are extensive discussions in the literature concerning causal functions with high-dimensional nuisance parameters. However, nearly all the results are provided under the iid sampling scheme. For instance,
Semenova and
Chernozhukov (2021)
and
Fan
et al. (2022)
consider estimation and inference for the CATE function under iid sampling while
Kennedy
et al. (2017)
consider estimation and inference for the CTE function under the framework of iid sampling. To our knowledge, this literature is silent about sampling schemes with strong cross-sectional dependence, such as multiway clustering, which is relevant to many empirical data used in economics.
The current paper aims to address this gap in the literature.

We propose a two-step procedure to estimate the causal function
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
.
The first step involves estimating the high-dimensional nuisance parameters
\eta_{0}
by a machine learner (ML; e.g., lasso, neural network, random forest).
The second step involves a sieve estimation
(
Chen, 2007
)
of the causal function. We consider two approaches to implement this two-step procedure, namely, the full-sample and multiway cross-fitting approaches.
In the full-sample approach, the nuisance parameters
\eta_{0}
and causal function
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
are estimated based on all the observations.
On the other hand, the multiway cross-fitting approach splits the data into multiway folds according to the clustering scheme, so the estimation of causal functions relies on one multiway fold independent of the multiway folds in which the ML estimates nuisance parameters.
Our full-sample approach is similar to
Belloni et al. (2017)
under the iid case.
On the other hand, our cross-fitting estimator differs from the iid counterpart
(
Chernozhukov
et al., 2018
)
, and we apply a variant of the multiway cross-fitting method
(
Chiang
et al., 2022
)
.

Since our sieve estimator entails a non-Donsker issue
(
Andrews, 1994
)
, a functional central limit theorem (CLT) is not applicable.
Instead, we apply the high-dimensional CLT for the separately exchangeable arrays
(
Chiang
et al., 2021
)
and approximate the standardized process by an intermediate Gaussian process of an increasing dimension.
As the limiting null distribution of the sup-test statistic is non-Gaussian, we also develop a resampling method that consistently approximates its critical values.
Related bootstrap methods that work under multiway clustering include the pigeonhole bootstrap
(
McCullagh, 2000
)
, polyadic bootstrap
(
Davezies et al., 2021
)
, and wild bootstrap
(
MacKinnon
et al., 2021
;
Menzel, 2021
)
, but none of these methods applies to a high-dimensional score vector.
Therefore, we extend the sieve score bootstrap for iid data
(
Chen and
Christensen, 2018
)
to our novel setting of multiway clustering, introducing the
multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap
.
Finally, we prove the uniform probability coverage of the uniform confidence bands (UCBs) achieved by this novel bootstrap method.

In addition to the theoretical developments, we conduct simulations to examine the finite-sample performance of our proposed methods. The results reveal that both our full-sample and multiway cross-fitting UCBs perform well with reasonable size controls. Overall, the multiway cross-fitting UCBs achieve more accurate probability coverage in finite samples than the full-sample UCBs. Therefore, we suggest using the multiway cross-fitting UCBs over the full-sample ones in practice. Furthermore, with multiway clustered data, our proposed sieve score bootstrap method demonstrates superior probability coverage compared to the conventional method developed for the iid case, further corroborating the necessity of incorporating cross-sectional dependence into inference.
Finally, we illustrate an empirical application to the analysis of the causal relationship between the mistrust levels in Africa and the history of slave trade.
Our findings reveal heterogeneous treatment effects, with significant impacts at higher trade volumes.
These findings significantly enhance our understanding of this critical empirical question.

2
The Model Setup and Sampling Framework

We consider a class of causal functions
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
identified in the form of (
1.1
) with the Neyman-orthogonal signals,
\psi(\cdot)
, that satisfy the Neyman-orthogonality condition

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[\psi(\eta_{0}+r(\eta-\eta_{0}))|X=x]|_{r=0}=0

(2.1)

for all
x
and
\eta
. If the signal
\psi(\cdot)
satisfies the Neyman-orthogonality (
2.1
), then
\psi(\eta)
is immune to the first-order bias of
\eta
, helping deliver a more efficient estimation of the causal function
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
.
For the sake of generality,
\psi(\cdot)
and
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
are left unspecified for the moment, but we provide two concrete examples shortly in Sections
2.1
–
2.2
.

Suppose that an econometrician observes copies of
Z
including
X
as a subvector, where the copies may exhibit cross-sectional dependence in the form of multiway clustering.
Without loss of generality and for brevity, we consider the two-way clustering – see Appendix
E
in the supplementary material for a general multiway cluster sampling framework.
That is,
\{Z_{ij}\}_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}
is a double-indexed sample of sizes
N
and
M
satisfying the following condition.

Assumption 2.1
:

The following conditions hold for each
n
.

(i)

Separate Exchangeability:
For any permutations
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
on
\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\{Z_{ij}\}_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}\overset{d}{=}\{Z_{\pi_{1}(i),\pi_{2}(j)}\}_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}.

(ii)

Dissociation: For any disjoint subsets
A
,
B\subset\mathbb{N}
,

\{Z_{ij}\}_{(i,j)\in A^{2}}\text{ is independent of }\{Z_{ij}\}_{(i,j)\in B^{2}}.

Assumption
2.1
(
i
) imposes the condition of separate exchangeability
(
Davezies et al., 2021
)
, which is comparable with the identical distribution assumption of the iid case, that is, perturbing labels
i
and
j
separately does not affect the joint distribution of
\{Z_{ij}\}
. Assumption
2.1
(
ii
) requires that
\{Z_{ij}\}
is independent if they do not share a common index. However, the random vectors
\{Z_{ij}\}
are allowed to be dependent in any form if they share the same index,
i
or
j
.

Throughout this paper, we consider scenarios in which researchers use cross-sectionally dependent data
\{Z_{ij}\}_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
to estimate
\eta_{0}
satisfying (
2.1
), and thence
\tau_{0}
via (
1.1
).
We propose two estimation strategies – one based on the full sample and the other based on the multiway cross fitting – and develop their asymptotic theory.
We further propose a novel method of bootstrap resampling to construct uniform confidence bands of
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
.

The next two subsections overview two leading examples of our setup: one is the conditional average treatment effect (CATE); and the other is the continuous treatment effect (CTE).
Each subsection introduces a causal function
\tau_{0}
and an associated Neyman-orthogonal score
\psi
satisfying (
2.1
), that in turn identifies
\tau_{0}
through (
1.1
).

2.1
Example I: the CATE

For each
i\in[N]
and
j\in[M]
, let
D_{ij}
indicate whether the individual is treated,
Y_{ij}(1)
and
Y_{ij}(0)
denote the potential outcomes under treatment and no treatment, respectively, and
W_{ij}
denote a vector of covariates. The observed outcome is constructed by
Y_{ij}=D_{ij}Y_{ij}(1)+(1-D_{ij})Y_{ij}(0)
. The observed data
Z_{ij}
consist of
Z_{ij}=(D_{ij},W_{ij}^{\prime},Y_{ij})^{\prime}
for
i\in[N]
,
j\in[M]
. The quantity of our interest is the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) function
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
defined by

\displaystyle\tau_{0}(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y_{ij}(1)-Y_{ij}(0)|X=x],

(2.2)

where
X_{ij}
is a
d_{X}
-dimensional subvector of
W_{ij}
of a fixed dimension
d_{X}
.
We allow for high-dimensional data, where the dimension of the covariates
W_{ij}
grows with the sample size.

Assume the observational unconfoundedness
(
Rosenbaum and
Rubin, 1983
)
. That is, the treatment status
D_{ij}
is independent of the potential outcomes
(Y_{ij}(1)
,
Y_{ij}(0))
conditional on observed controls
W_{ij}
:

\displaystyle(Y_{ij}(1),Y_{ij}(0))\perp D_{ij}|W_{ij}.

Define
\mu_{0}(l,w)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{ij}|D_{ij}=l,W_{ij}=w]
for
l=0,1
. The law of iterated expectations and unconfoundedness yield
\mathbb{E}[Y_{ij}(l)|X_{ij}=x]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{0}(l,w)|X_{ij}=x]
. Thus, the CATE function
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
can be identified by
\tau_{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{0}(1,w)-\mu_{0}(0,w)|X_{ij}=x]
.
Moreover, we consider a more robust result based on the Neyman-orthogonal signal
(
Fan
et al., 2022
)
adapted to our multiway clustered setup:

\displaystyle\psi(Z_{ij};\eta):=\frac{D_{ij}(Y_{ij}-\mu(1,W_{ij}))}{\pi(W_{ij})}+\mu(1,W_{ij})-\frac{(1-D_{ij})(Y_{ij}-\mu(0,W_{ij}))}{1-\pi(W_{ij})}-\mu(0,W_{ij}),

(2.3)

where
\pi_{0}(w)=\mathbb{P}(D_{ij}=1|W_{ij}=w)
denotes the propensity score. Lemma
D.1
in the supplementary appendix shows that the signal (
2.3
) identifies (
2.2
) via (
1.1
) and is both locally and doubly robust with respect to the nuisance parameter

\eta_{0}(w):=(\pi_{0}(w),\mu_{0}(1,w),\mu_{0}(0,w))
.

Whenever the CATE is discussed throughout the rest of this paper, we shall assume its identification condition, formally stated in the supplementary appendix as Assumption
D.1
.

2.2
Example II: the CTE

For each
i\in[N]
and
j\in[M]
, let
X_{ij}
be a continuous treatment intensity that takes values in
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
\{Y_{ij}(x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
be the potential outcomes indexed by
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
W_{ij}
be a covariate vector. An econometrician observes the actual outcome
Y_{ij}=Y_{ij}(X_{ij})
instead of the potential outcome
Y_{ij}(x)

per se
.
Let the observed data
Z_{ij}=(X_{ij},W_{ij}^{\prime},Y_{ij})^{\prime}
be a multiway clustered sample supported on
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{R}^{d_{W}}\times\mathcal{R}
.
We consider the setup in which the dimension
d_{W}
of the covariates,
W_{ij}
, grows with sample size, while the continuous treatment
X_{ij}
of interest is a scalar random variable. The main target function is the continuous treatment response,
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
, defined by

\displaystyle\tau_{0}(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y_{ij}(x)].

In causal inference, we are often interested in its linear transformations that measure continuous treatment effects (CTE), such as the derivative function
d\tau_{0}(\cdot)/dx
or a difference function
\tau_{0}(\cdot)-\tau_{0}(\bar{x})
from a fixed benchmark value
\bar{x}
of
x
.

Assume the observational unconfoundedness. That is, the potential outcomes
\{Y_{ij}(x),x\in\mathbb{R}\}
are independent of the continuous treatment
X_{ij}
conditional on the observed controls
W_{ij}
:

\displaystyle\{Y_{ij}(x),x\in\mathbb{R}\}\perp X_{ij}|W_{ij}.

Similarly to the notations in Section
2.1
, we define
\mu_{0}(x,w)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{ij}|X_{ij}=x,W_{ij}=w]
. The law of iterated expectations and the unconfoundedness yield
\tau_{0}(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y_{ij}(x)]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{0}(x,W_{ij})]
for any given
x
. We consider to extend the doubly robust signal from
Kennedy
et al. (2017)
to the multiway clustering setting:

\displaystyle{\psi}(Z_{ij};\eta):=\frac{Y_{ij}-\mu(X_{ij},W_{ij})}{f(X_{ij}|W_{ij})}\omega(X_{ij})+\int_{\mathcal{W}}\mu(X_{ij},w)d{P}_{W}(w),

(2.4)

that relies on the marginal distribution
{P}_{W}(\cdot)
of
W_{ij}
, the generalized propensity score

\displaystyle f_{0}(x|w)=\left.\frac{d\mathbb{P}(X_{ij}\leq t|W_{ij}=w)}{dt}\right|_{t=x},

(2.5)

and the marginal treatment density

\displaystyle\omega_{0}(x)=\left.\frac{d\mathbb{P}(X_{ij}\leq t)}{dt}\right|_{t=x}=\mathbb{E}_{W}[f_{0}(x|W_{ij})].

(2.6)

In particular, Lemma
D.2
in the supplementary appendix shows that the signal (
D.4
) can identify the causal function via (
1.1
) in both locally robust and doubly robust manners with the nuisance parameter

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(x,w):=(f_{0}(x|w),\mu_{0}(x,w),\omega_{0}(x)).

The corresponding estimation and inference procedures for the CTE can be further established based on the doubly robust signal
{\psi}(Z_{ij};\eta)
defined in (
D.4
).

Whenever the CTE is discussed throughout the rest of this paper, we shall assume its identification conditions, formally stated in the supplementary appendix as Assumptions
D.2
–
D.3
.

3
Estimation

This section presents estimation approaches for the general causal functions
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
(encompassing the CATE and the CTE in particular) identified via (
1.1
) based on the Neyman-orthogonal signal
\psi(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfying (
2.1
).
We propose two versions of non-parametric estimators, depending on whether the first-step ML estimation and the second-step sieve regression use the same set of empirical data. These two approaches to estimation, referred to as the full-sample and multiway cross-fitting approaches, are separately introduced below.

1.

Full-Sample Estimator:
Define
\widehat{\eta}(\cdot)
as the first-step estimator for the high-dimensional nuisance parameter,
\eta_{0}(\cdot)
. For the case of the CATE, we define
\widehat{\eta}(\cdot)=(\widehat{\pi}(\cdot),\widehat{\mu}(0,\cdot),\widehat{\mu}(1,\cdot))
, where
\widehat{\pi}(\cdot)
,
\widehat{\mu}(0,\cdot)
and
\widehat{\mu}(1,\cdot)
are the full-sample estimators for
\pi_{0}(\cdot)
,
\mu_{0}(0,\cdot)
and
\mu_{0}(1,\cdot)
, respectively. For the case of the CTE, we define
\widehat{\eta}(\cdot)=(\widehat{f}(\cdot),\widehat{\mu}(\cdot,\cdot),\widehat{\omega}(\cdot))
, where
\widehat{f}(\cdot)
,
\widehat{\mu}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\widehat{\omega}(\cdot)
are the full-sample estimators of
f_{0}(\cdot)
,
\mu_{0}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\omega_{0}(\cdot)
, respectively.

The second-step full-sample estimator
\widehat{\tau}(\cdot)
of
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
is obtained from the sieve expansions that approximate the target function
\tau(\cdot)
by a linear form
x\mapsto p(x)^{\prime}\beta_{0}
:

\displaystyle\tau_{0}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}\beta_{0}+r_{\tau}(x),

(3.1)

where
p(x)
is a
p
-dimensional vector of basis functions of
x
,
r_{\tau}(x)
is the linear approximation error with the moment condition:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[p(X_{ij})r_{\tau}(X_{ij})\right]=\mathbf{0}_{p\times 1}.

(3.2)

We further write the sieve approximation to the Neyman-orthogonal signal as

\displaystyle\psi(Z_{ij};\eta_{0})=\tau_{0}(X_{ij})+u_{ij},

(3.3)

where
u_{ij}=\psi(Z_{ij};\eta_{0})-\tau_{0}(X_{ij})
represents the error term. Combining (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) yields

\displaystyle\psi(Z_{ij};\eta_{0})=p(X_{ij})^{\prime}\beta_{0}+r_{\tau}(X_{ij})+u_{ij}.

(3.4)

Given an estimate
\widehat{\eta}(\cdot)
of
\eta(\cdot)
, we obtain the generated dependent variable
\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta})
. Then, the pseudo-true parameter
\beta
can be estimated by

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=(\mathbb{E}_{n}[p_{ij}p_{ij}^{\prime}])^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n}[p_{ij}\psi(Z_{ij};\widehat{\eta})],

(3.5)

with
p_{ij}(:=p(X_{ij}))
. Finally, the full-sample estimator
\widehat{\tau}(\cdot)
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{\beta}.

(3.6)

2.

Multiway Cross-Fitting Estimator:
With a fixed integer value
K>1
, randomly partition
[N]
into
K
equal folds
\{I_{1},I_{2},...,I_{K}\}
and
[M]
into
K
equal folds
\{J_{1},J_{2},...,J_{K}\}
. For each
(k,\ell)\in[K]^{2}
, we obtain a first-step estimate
\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell}=\widehat{\eta}((Z_{ij})_{(i,j)\in I_{k}^{c}\times J_{\ell}^{c}})
of
\eta_{0}
based on the subsample
I_{k}^{c}\times J_{\ell}^{c}
. Given
\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell}
, we have the generated random variables
\psi(Z_{ij};\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})
corresponding to the subsample
(I_{k}\times J_{\ell})
. The parameter
\beta
can be estimated over the subsample as

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{k\ell}=(\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}p_{ij}^{\prime}])^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}\psi(Z_{ij};\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})].

(3.7)

We define the second-step non-parametric estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{k\ell}(\cdot)
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{kl}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{\beta}_{k\ell},

for each
(k,\ell)\in[K]^{2}
. Averaging the
K^{2}
second-step estimates yields a more efficient estimate:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{(k,\ell)\in[K]^{2}}\widehat{\tau}_{k\ell}(x).

Discussion 3.1
:

Instead of the local linear smoothing estimator studied in
Fan
et al. (2022)
, we follow the approach of
Semenova and
Chernozhukov (2021)
and apply the sieve method for estimating causal functions. Since the non-parametric methods are comparable, we can also incorporate the local smoothing or polynomial methods in our second-step estimation.

Discussion 3.2
:

Our estimators allow for high-dimensional covariates and apply ML methods to estimate
\eta_{0}
, such as lasso, random forests, neural networks, and conventional non-parametric methods. The only requirement we impose on the estimators
\widehat{\eta}
and
\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell}
is that it converges to the true nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}
at a fast enough rate so that the estimation error term is asymptotically negligible.

Discussion 3.3
:

Regarding the CATE function, our full-sample and multiway cross-fitting procedures that rely on the Neyman-orthogonal moment also apply to estimating the average treatment effect (ATE). The ATE estimation replaces the second-step non-parametric estimator with a parametric estimator, which is the sample average of the estimated Neyman-orthogonal signal. In other words, the full-sample and multiway cross-fitting estimators are defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{A}=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\psi\left(Z_{ij};\widehat{\eta}\right)\right],

(Full-sample estimator for ATE)

(3.8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}_{A}=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{(k,\ell)\in[K]^{2}}\widehat{\tau}_{A,k\ell},

(Multiway cross-fitting estimator for ATE)

(3.9)

where the subsample estimator follows
\widehat{\tau}_{A,k\ell}=\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[\psi(Z_{ij};\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})]
. Following a similar procedure to
Chiang
et al. (2022)
, we can verify that the ATE estimators of (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) are both consistent with a parametric rate
\sqrt{N\wedge M}
under different conditions, similar to the root-
n
rate of the iid case.

4
Uniform Limit Theory

This section provides a uniform limit theory for the sieve estimator of causal functions. To facilitate our discussions, we first introduce some additional notations.

The sup-norm of the sieve basis functions will be denoted by
\xi_{p}:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|p(x)\|
.
Let
\alpha(x):=p(x)/\|p(x)\|
denote the normalized basis
p(x)
, and let

\displaystyle\xi_{p}^{L}:=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},x\neq x^{\prime}}\frac{|\alpha(x)-\alpha(x^{\prime})|}{|x-x^{\prime}|}

denote its Lipschitz constant. With these notations, we now introduce some conditions for the second-step non-parametric estimation.

Assumption 4.1
(Conditions for the Second-Step Series Estimation):

(i)

(Identification) Let
Q:=\mathbb{E}[p_{ij}p_{ij}^{\prime}]
denote the population covariance matrix of
p_{ij}
. There exist constants
C_{\min}
and
C_{\max}
such that
0<C_{\min}<\lambda_{\min}(Q)<\lambda_{\max}(Q)<C_{\max}<\infty
where
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
and
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
denote the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
Q
.

(ii)

(Growth Condition)
\xi_{p}
and
\xi_{p}^{L}
grow sufficiently slowly to satisfy

\displaystyle\sqrt{\xi^{2}_{p}\log(p)/(N\wedge M)}\rightarrow 0,

\log(\xi_{p})\lesssim\log(p)
, and
\log(\xi^{L}_{p})\lesssim\log(p)
.

(iii)

(Misspecification Error) There exist sequences of deterministic values,
\ell_{p}
and
r_{p}
, such that the norms of the mis-specification error are controlled as follows:

\displaystyle\|r_{\tau}\|_{\mathbb{P},2}\lesssim r_{p}~\text{and}~\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|r_{\tau}(x)|\lesssim\ell_{p}r_{p}.

(iv)

(Bounded Moment)
The
m
-th moment of the stochastic error
\{u_{ij}\}
conditional on
X_{ij}
is bounded from above:
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}[u^{m}_{ij}|X_{ij}=x]\lesssim 1
with some
m>2
.

Assumption
4.1
collects several standard regularity conditions used in the non-parametric estimation literature. Assumption
4.1
(
i
), as an identification condition that eliminates collinearity in the population signal matrix, is standard in the literature of non-parametric estimation
(
Andrews, 1991
;
Newey, 1997
)
. Assumptions
4.1
(
ii
)–(
iii
) bound the non-parametric approximation error. Specifically, the sequence of constants
r_{p}
bound the
\ell^{2}
-norm while
\ell_{p}r_{p}
bounds the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm. Assumption
4.1
(
iv
) bounds the conditional variance of
\left\{u_{ij}\right\}
uniformly, facilitating the uniform limit theory. In particular, Assumption
4.1
(
iv
) implies that
2<m\leq q
.

Define the lower and upper bounds of the conditional second moment of
u_{ij}
given
X_{ij}
by

\displaystyle\underline{\sigma}^{2}:=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}[u_{ij}^{2}|X_{ij}=x]\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\sigma}^{2}:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}[u^{2}_{ij}|X_{ij}=x].

We impose the following condition on the upper bound.

Assumption 4.2
(Bounded Moments of the Error):

\overline{\sigma}^{2}\lesssim 1
.

In what follows, we state two conditions that bound the estimation errors of the Neyman-orthogonal moments for each of the two cases of full-sample and multiway cross-fitting estimators. First, we give high-level conditions for the full-sample estimator, lower-level sufficient conditions for which will be presented later in the contexts of the CATE and the CTE.

Assumption 4.3
(Full-Sample First Step):

The estimation error of the full-sample estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathbb{E}_{n}[\alpha(x)^{\prime}(\psi(Z_{ij};\widehat{\eta})-\psi(Z_{ij};\eta_{0}))p_{ij}]|=o_{p}(\sqrt{\log(p)/(N\wedge M)}).

(4.1)

Assumption
4.3
assumes the robustness condition for the unobserved orthogonal signal
\psi(Z_{ij};\eta_{0})
regarding the biased estimation of the nuisance parameter. The above condition is comparable to the upper bound for estimation errors in
Fan
et al. (2022, Equations (14) and (15))
and
Semenova and
Chernozhukov (2021, Assumption 3.8)
.

Next, we also bound the estimation errors of the Neyman-orthogonal moments in the cross-fitting case. For simplicity, we assume that all the observations are partitioned into blocks of equal size; that is,
|I_{k}|=|I|\asymp N
and
|J_{\ell}|=|J|\asymp M
for all
k
,
\ell\in[K]
.

Assumption 4.4
(Multiway Cross-Fitting First Step):

For all
k,\ell\in[K]
, the estimation error of the multiway cross-fitting estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[\alpha(x)^{\prime}(\psi(Z_{ij};\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij};\eta_{0}))p_{ij}]|=o_{p}(\sqrt{\log(p)/(|I|\wedge|J|)}).

(4.2)

We will show the plausibility of Assumptions
4.3
and
4.4
in the context of CATE and CTE with lower-level sufficient conditions in Sections
4.1
–
4.2
. Given these conditions, Theorem
4.1
below establishes the uniform linear representation and uniform convergence rate for the estimator of general causal functions.

Theorem 4.1
(Uniform Linearization and Uniform Convergence Rate):

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
4.1
,
E.1
,
4.3
and
4.4
hold.

(a)

(Full-Sample Estimator) The full-sample sieve estimator is linearly approximated uniformly over
\mathcal{X}
:

\displaystyle|\sqrt{N\wedge M}\cdot\alpha(x)^{\prime}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0})-\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{G}_{n}[p_{ij}u_{ij}]|\lesssim_{p}R_{1,n}(\alpha(x))+R_{2,n}(\alpha(x)),

where
R_{1,n}(\alpha(x))
and
R_{2,n}(\alpha(x))
bound the approximation errors as

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|R_{1,n}(\alpha(x))|\lesssim_{p}\Lambda_{n}+\xi_{p}\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{N\wedge M}}((NM)^{1/m}\sqrt{\log(p)}+\ell_{p}r_{p}\sqrt{p})=:\overline{R}_{1,n},

with
\Lambda_{n}=\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)/(N\wedge M)}
uniformly over
x\in\mathcal{X}
and

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|R_{2,n}(\alpha(x))|\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)}\ell_{p}r_{p}=:\overline{R}_{2,n}.

The full-sample estimator
p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{\beta}
is bounded uniformly as

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|p(x)^{\prime}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0})|\lesssim_{p}\frac{\xi_{p}}{\sqrt{N\wedge M}}(\sqrt{\log(p)}+\overline{R}_{1,n}+\overline{R}_{2,n}).

(4.3)

(b)

(Multiway Cross-Fitting Estimator) For any
(k,\ell)\in[K]^{2}
, the subsample sieve estimator that relies on the block
(I_{k}\times J_{\ell})
satisfies

\displaystyle|\sqrt{|I|\wedge|J|}\cdot\alpha(x)^{\prime}(\widehat{\beta}_{k\ell}-\beta_{0})-\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}u_{ij}]|\lesssim_{p}R_{1n,k\ell}(\alpha(x))+R_{2n,k\ell}(\alpha(x)),

where
R_{1n,k\ell}(\alpha(x))
satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|R_{1n,k\ell}(\alpha(x))|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\Lambda_{n,k\ell}+\xi_{p}\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{|I|\wedge|J|}}((|I||J|)^{1/m}\sqrt{\log(p)}+\ell_{p}r_{p}\sqrt{p})=:\overline{R}_{1n,k\ell},

with
\Lambda_{n,k\ell}=\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)/(|I|\wedge|J|})
uniformly over
x\in\mathcal{X}
and

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|R_{2n,k\ell}(\alpha(x))|\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)}\ell_{p}r_{p}=:\overline{R}_{2n,k\ell}.

The cross-fitting estimator
p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{\beta}_{k\ell}
is bounded uniformly as

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|p(x)^{\prime}(\widehat{\beta}_{k\ell}-\beta_{0})|\lesssim_{p}\frac{\xi_{p}}{\sqrt{|I|\wedge|J|}}(\sqrt{\log(p)}+\overline{R}_{1n,k\ell}+\overline{R}_{2n,k\ell}).

(4.4)

See Appendix
A
for a proof.

Theorem
4.1
provides the linear representations of the full-sample and cross-fitting estimators with uniform upper bounds of their remainder terms. These linear representation forms show the leading terms of the empirical process of interest and serve as foundations for strong Gaussian approximation and uniform inference procedures for the causal functions.

Before introducing the high-dimensional central limit theorem (CLT), we remark that two-way clustered data satisfying Assumption
2.1
are known to accommodate the so-called Aldous-Hoover-Kallenberg representation
(
Kallenberg, 1989
)
:

\displaystyle Z_{ij}=f(U_{(i,0)},U_{(0,j)},U_{(i,j)}),

(4.5)

where
U_{(i,0)}
,
U_{(0,j)}
and
U_{(i,j)}
follow the iid
U[0,1]
distribution and are mutually independent. The above factor representation implies that we may focus on the three iid leading components in orthogonal directions, (
U_{(i,0)},U_{(0,j)},U_{(i,j)}
).

Now, define
g_{i0}(U_{(i,0)})=\mathbb{E}[p_{ij}u_{ij}|U_{(i,0)}]
and
g_{0j}(U_{(0j)})=\mathbb{E}[p_{ij}u_{ij}|U_{(0,j)}]
, where
U_{(i,0)}
and
U_{(0,j)}
follow
\text{iid }U(0,1)
. For simplicity, we write
g_{i0}(U_{(i,0)})
and
g_{i0}(U_{(i,0)})
as
g_{i0}
and
g_{0j}
when there is little risk of ambiguity.
With these definitions and notations, we now introduce the following necessary conditions for the high-dimensional CLT.

Assumption 4.5
:

Letting
D_{n}
be a constant that can depend on the cluster sizes
N
and
M
, we impose the following moment conditions for the full-sample estimator:

(i)

\max_{1\leq s\leq p}\mathbb{E}[|g^{(s)}_{i0}|^{2+\kappa}]\leq D^{\kappa}_{n}
and
\max_{1\leq s\leq p}\mathbb{E}[|g^{(s)}_{0j}|^{2+\kappa}]\leq D^{\kappa}_{n}
,

(ii)

\mathbb{E}\|p_{ij}u_{ij}\|_{\infty}^{6}\leq D^{6}_{n}
, and

(iii)

\max_{1\leq s\leq p}\mathbb{E}[|g^{(s)}_{i0}|^{2}]\geq\underline{\sigma}^{2}
and
\max_{1\leq s\leq p}\mathbb{E}[|g^{(s)}_{0j}|^{2}]\geq\underline{\sigma}^{2}
,

where
g^{(s)}_{0j}
denotes the
s
-th entry of the vector
g_{0j}
. Similarly, the conditions for the cross-fitting estimator follow (
i
)–(
iii
) by replacing
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
with
\mathbb{E}_{k\ell}[\cdot]
and
D_{n}
with
D_{n,k\ell}
.

Assumption
4.5
imposes conditions that ensure the applicability of the high-dimensional CLT for the separately exchangeable array
(
Chiang
et al., 2021
)
. Assumption
4.5
(
i
) requires that each coordinate of
p_{ij}u_{ij}
is bounded. Assumption
4.5
(
ii
) requires the maximum of the third moment across coordinates to be increasing at a speed no faster than the polynomials of
D_{n}
. Assumption
4.5
(
iii
) further ensures that the Hájek projection of the multiway clustering empirical process of interest is nondegenerate.

Following the Hájek projection given in (
C.1
)–(
C.2
) in the supplementary appendix, we define the asymptotic variance:

\displaystyle\Sigma:=\overline{\mu}_{N}\Sigma_{N}+\overline{\mu}_{M}\Sigma_{M}=\overline{\mu}_{N}\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}\right]+\overline{\mu}_{M}\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}u_{ij}u_{i^{\prime}j}\right],

(4.6)

with
\overline{\mu}_{N}=\lim(N\wedge M)/N
and
\overline{\mu}_{M}=\lim(N\wedge M)/M
.

Define the approximating Gaussian variate,
\gamma_{\Sigma}\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{p\times 1},\Sigma)
. In what follows, Theorem
4.2
establishes a strong Gaussian approximation to the non-parametric sieve estimator by a sequence of zero-mean Gaussian processes,
\gamma_{\Sigma}
.

Theorem 4.2
(High-Dimensional CLT):

Assume that
1\lesssim\underline{\sigma}^{2}
. Suppose Assumptions
2.1
,
4.1
,
E.1
,
4.3
,
4.4
and
4.5
hold.

(a)

(Full-Sample Estimator) The full-sample estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}_{n}[p_{ij}\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta})]\in R)=\gamma_{\Sigma}(R)+\left(\frac{D^{2}_{n}\log^{7}(p\cdot(N\vee M))}{N\wedge M}\right)^{1/6}

for any arbitrary subset
R
of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

(b)

(Multiway Cross-Fitting Estimator) The multiway cross-fitting estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})]\in R)=\gamma_{\Sigma}(R)+\left(\frac{D^{2}_{n,k\ell}\log^{7}(p\cdot(|I|\vee|J|))}{|I|\wedge|J|}\right)^{1/6}

for any arbitrary subset
R
of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

In the proof of this theorem, provided in Appendix
A
, we apply the high-dimensional CLT for separately exchangeable arrays
(
Chiang
et al., 2021
, Theorem 1)
and check all its prerequisite conditions.

To further facilitate the uniform inference procedure for general causal functions, we need to estimate the covariance matrix
\gamma_{\Sigma}
by considering the iid components of the Hájek projection in Proposition
2
in the supplementary appendix. Specifically, when we have the full sample, the covariance matrix of the Gaussian approximating term,
\Sigma
, can be estimated by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{N\wedge M}{N^{2}M^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij^{\prime}}+\frac{N\wedge M}{N^{2}M^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in[N]}\sum_{j\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{i^{\prime}j}.

(4.7)

When we consider the multiway cross-fitting method, the covariance matrix of the Gaussian approximating term of the
(k,\ell)
-block can be estimated by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k\ell}=\frac{|I|\wedge|J|}{|I|^{2}|J|^{2}}\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in J_{\ell}}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij^{\prime}}+\frac{|I|\wedge|J|}{|I|^{2}|J|^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in I_{k}}\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{i^{\prime}j}.

(4.8)

Averaging across partitions, we have the covariance estimator given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{(k,\ell)\in[K]^{2}}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{k\ell}.

(4.9)

We also define the population and sample signal matrices by

\displaystyle Q=\mathbb{E}[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij}],~\widehat{Q}=\mathbb{E}_{n}[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij}]~\text{and}~\widetilde{Q}=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{(k,\ell)\in[K]^{2}}\widehat{Q}_{k\ell}

with
\widehat{Q}_{k\ell}:=\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij}]
. We then define the population variance
\sigma^{2}_{\tau}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\Sigma Q^{-1}p(x)
with the full-sample and multiway cross-fitting estimators:

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\tau}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{Q}^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\widehat{Q}^{-1}p(x)~\text{for the full-sample estimator};\text{ and }

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\tau}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}\widetilde{Q}^{-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}\widetilde{Q}^{-1}p(x)~\text{for the cross-fitting estimator}.

Theorem 4.3
:

Suppose that the assumptions invoked in Theorem
4.1
hold. Then, the full-sample non-parametric estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{Q}-Q\right\|\lesssim_{p}\xi_{p}{\frac{\sqrt{\log(p)}}{\sqrt{N\wedge M}}}\rightarrow 0\qquad\text{ and }

(4.10)

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|\lesssim_{p}\left((NM)^{2/m}\vee 1\right){\frac{\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)}}{\sqrt{N\wedge M}}}\rightarrow 0.

(4.11)

The variance estimator for the full-sample estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{\tau}(x)}{\sigma_{\tau}(x)}-1\right|\lesssim_{p}((NM)^{2/m}\vee 1){\frac{\xi_{p}^{2}\sqrt{\log(p)}}{\sqrt{N\wedge M}}}\rightarrow 0

(4.12)

uniformly over
x\in\mathcal{X}
. The multiway cross-fitting estimator
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\tau}(x)
also satisfies similar results to those above by replacing
N
and
M
with
|I|
and
|J|
, respectively.

See Appendix
A
for a proof.

With consistent variance estimates, we can stabilize the test statistic for various causal functions and further asymptotically approximate this test statistic by a Gaussian process whose distribution depends on data only by the variance function
\sigma_{\tau}(\cdot)
. Enhanced by this theory of the strong Gaussian approximation, we can conduct uniform inference accompanied by the bootstrapped critical values, which will be provided in Section
5
.

in the context of the CATE and the CTE in the following two subsections.

\mu_{0}(1,\cdot)
,

We are going to restrict the estimation errors of the first-step nuisance parameter estimations. The upper bounds of estimation errors are shown separately for the cases of full-sample and multiway cross-fitting estimators. We first impose the following conditions for the full-sample estimator
\widehat{\eta}
.

Assumption 4.6
(Full-Sample First Step):

Let
\delta_{1n}
,
\delta_{2n}
,
\delta_{3n}
,

and

\mathcal{G}^{(l)}_{n}

, let
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G}^{(l)}_{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|,\varepsilon)
be the covering number of
\mathcal{G}^{(l)}_{n}

satisfies the error bounds

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0,1}\|\widehat{\mu}(l,W_{ij})-\mu_{0}(l,W_{ij})\|_{\mathbb{P},2}\times\|\widehat{\pi}(W_{ij})-\pi_{0}(W_{ij})\|_{\mathbb{P},2}=O_{p}(\delta^{2}_{1n}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(l,\cdot)\in\mathcal{G}^{(l)}_{n},~l=0,1~\text{and}~~\widehat{\pi}(\cdot)\in\mathcal{G}^{(\pi)}_{n},

\mathbb{P}
.

.

) and

Assumption 4.7
(Multiway Cross-Fitting First Step):

The splitting-sample first-step estimators
\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell}
for all

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0,1}\|\widehat{\mu}_{k\ell}(l,W_{ij})-\mu_{0}(l,W_{ij})\|_{\mathbb{P}_{(I_{k}\times J_{\ell})},2}\times\|\widehat{\pi}_{k\ell}(W_{ij})-\pi_{0}(W_{ij})\|_{\mathbb{P}_{(I_{k}\times J_{\ell})},2}=O_{p}(\delta^{2}_{1n,k\ell}),

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0,1}\left(\|\widehat{\mu}_{k\ell}(l,W_{ij})-\mu_{0}(l,W_{ij})\|_{\mathbb{P}_{(I_{k}\times J_{\ell})},\infty}+\|\widehat{\pi}_{k\ell}(W_{ij})-\pi_{0}(W_{ij})\|_{\mathbb{P}_{(I_{k}\times J_{\ell})},\infty}\right)=O_{p}(\delta_{2n,k\ell}),

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X},l=0,1}\|(\widehat{\mu}_{k\ell}(l,W_{ij})-\mu_{0}(l,W_{ij}))\|p(x)\|^{1/2}\|_{\mathbb{P}_{(I_{k}\times J_{\ell})},2}\times\|(\widehat{\pi}_{k\ell}(W_{ij})-\pi_{0}(W_{ij}))\|p(x)\|^{1/2}\|_{\mathbb{P}_{(I_{k}\times J_{\ell})},2}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(\delta^{2}_{3n,k\ell}),

where

,

5
Uniform Inference Based on Sieve Score Bootstrap

This section provides a uniform inference method by extending the sieve score bootstrap approach
(
Chen and
Christensen, 2018
)
to the cases of multiway clustered data. We call this extended method the “multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap”. Our testing approach facilitated by this bootstrapping method uses the estimated nuisance parameters of the first step and randomizes the empirical process of the sieve estimator in the second step, avoiding the heavy computation burdens for high-dimensional covariance matrices. Our multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap method also differs from its iid version
(
Chen and
Christensen, 2018
)
in what types of empirical processes are involved in re-sampling. In the following discussion, we will detail the challenges the conventional sieve score bootstrap faces under multiway clustered data and introduce the uniform inference procedure that relies on our multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap method.

5.1
A Review of the Sieve Score Bootstrap for iid Data

Under random sampling,
Chen and
Christensen (2018)
propose to use the sieve score bootstrap method to calculate critical values
cv_{n}^{b}(1-\alpha)
so that the confidence bands can attain the uniform probability coverage. Adapted to our two-index notations, they define
\{\omega_{ij}\}_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}
as the iid standard Gaussian variates which are independent of data
\{Z_{ij}\}_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}
. Then,
Chen and
Christensen (2018)
define the sieve score bootstrapping
t
-statistic empirical process:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\ni x\mapsto t^{b,\ast}_{\tau}(x):=\frac{p(x)^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}_{n}[p_{ij}p_{ij}^{\prime}])^{-1}\{(\sqrt{NM}/(NM))\sum_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}[\omega_{ij}p_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij}]\}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\tau}(x)},

(5.1)

with the residuals
\widehat{u}_{ij}
from Section
3
. The test statistic process can capture the distribution of
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}t^{b}_{\tau}(x)
and calculate its critical values.

The above sieve score bootstrapping method cannot be applied to data with strong cross-sectional dependence. The iid perturbation process
\{\omega_{ij}\}
utilizes degrees of independence (or weak dependence) in
Z_{ij}
across both
i\in[N]
and
j\in[M]
, so the validity of resampling relies crucially on the condition of independence (or weak dependence). However, the presence of strong within-cluster dependence violates this prerequisite condition when we consider the multiway clustered data. Alternatively, we rely on the independence across clusters and further modify the bootstrapping method of
Chen and
Christensen (2018)
to ensure its applicability in the present context.

In the following subsection, we first revisit the key machinery, the Hájek projection, that facilitates the establishment of a novel sieve score bootstrap method that stays robust to multiway clustering. Further, we will apply this bootstrapping method and discuss how it helps construct the bootstrapping uniform confidence bands (UCBs) for causal functions.

5.2
Multiway Cluster-Robust Sieve Score Bootstrap

Though two-way clustered data introduces strong cross-sectional dependence in two dimensions, Proposition
2
in the supplementary appendix shows that this type of data can be projected onto two orthogonal directions expanded by iid random variables
U_{(i,0)}
and
U_{(0,j)}
, which follow uniform distributions, as shown by (
C.1
) in the supplementary appendix. (See the Aldous-Hoover-Kallenberg representation (
4.5
) for
U_{(i,0)}
and
U_{(0,j)}
.) This intuition motivates us to randomize the projected score vector rather than the original score as in (
5.1
).

Even though Proposition
2
in the supplementary appendix illustrates that the score vector of interest can be projected on two orthogonal spaces, it is infeasible to directly approximate the unknown projections
g_{i0}(U_{(i,0)})
and
g_{0j}(U_{(0,j)})
since
U_{(0,i)}
and
U_{(0,j)}
are latent. Using the idea of
Menzel (2021)
, we approximate
g_{i0}(U_{(i,0)})
and
g_{0j}(U_{(0,j)})
by the following averages

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{i0}(U_{(i,0)}):=(1/M)\sum_{j\in[M]}p_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij}\quad\text{ and }\quad\widehat{g}_{0j}(U_{(0,j)}):=(1/N)\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij},

respectively,
where
\widehat{u}_{ij}
is given in Section
3
. For simplicity, we write
\widehat{g}_{i0}(U_{(i,0)})
as
\widehat{g}_{i0}
and
\widehat{g}_{0j}(U_{(0,j)})
as
\widehat{g}_{0j}
.

The multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap method can be summarized as follows. Let
\{\omega_{1,i}\}_{i\in[N]}
and
\{\omega_{2,j}\}_{j\in[M]}
be independent
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables independent of the data. Then, we obtain the multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap empirical process:

\displaystyle t^{b}_{\tau}(x):=\frac{(\sqrt{N\wedge M})p(x)^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}_{n}[p_{ij}p_{ij}^{\prime}])^{-1}\{(1/N)\sum_{i\in[N]}\omega_{1,i}\widehat{g}_{i0}+(1/M)\sum_{j\in[M]}\omega_{2,j}\widehat{g}_{0j}\}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\tau}(x)}.

(5.2)

To compute the
1-\alpha
critical value,
cv_{n}^{b}(1-\alpha)
, one can calculates
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|t^{b}_{\tau}(x)|
based on two sequences of independent draws of
\{\omega_{1,i}\}
and
\{\omega_{2,j}\}
, which are independent between each other. Specifically,
cv_{n}^{b}(1-\alpha)
is given by the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|t^{b}_{\tau}(x)|
over the multiple random draws of
\{\omega_{1,i}\}
and
\{\omega_{2,j}\}
:

\displaystyle cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)=(1-\alpha)\text{ quantile of}~\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|t^{b}_{\tau}(x)|~\text{over the draws of }\{\omega_{1,i}\}_{i\in[N]}~\text{and}~\{\omega_{2,j}\}_{j\in[M]}.

(5.3)

Then, we compute the bootstrapping UCBs for the causal function
\tau_{0}(\cdot)
by

\displaystyle[\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{l}(x),\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{u}(x)]=[\widehat{\tau}(x)-cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)\cdot\widehat{\sigma}_{\tau}(x),~\widehat{\tau}(x)+cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)\cdot\widehat{\sigma}_{\tau}(x)],~~\text{for}~x\in\mathcal{X},

(5.4)

where
cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)
ensures that
\tau(x)\in[\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{l}(x),\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{u}(x)]
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
with the level of confidence
100(1-\alpha)\%
asymptotically, as formally shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
(Multiway Cluster-Robust Sieve Score Bootstrap):

Suppose that the assumptions invoked in Theorem
4.2
hold. In addition, assume that
cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)
is calculated as in (
E.2
). Then, the bootstrapping UCBs defined in (
E.3
) satisfy

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\tau(x)\in[\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{l}(x),\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{u}(x)]~\text{for all }x\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}=1-\alpha+o(1).

See Appendix
A
for a proof.
Theorem
5.1
extends the conventional sieve score bootstrap method
(
Chen and
Christensen, 2018
)
that suits iid data to the case of multiway clustering. In such a case, though the cross-sectional correlation that can result in additional technical challenges exists, we can still apply the high-dimensional central limit theorem of
Chiang
et al. (2021)
to recover the Gaussian approximation and prove the uniform probability coverage of the multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrapping UCBs. Also, the asymptotic validity of the UCBs holds regardless of whether the estimator relies on the full sample. Regardless of whether the full-sample or cross-fitting estimator is considered, the uniform size control brought by our bootstrapping method always holds.

8
Conclusion

This paper introduces novel methods for the estimation and uniform inference of causal functions under the conditions of multiway clustering. Our focus is on a general class of causal functions, such as the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE) and Continuous Treatment Effect (CTE), which are characterized as conditional expectations of a Neyman-orthogonal signal dependent on high-dimensional nuisance parameters. We propose a two-step procedure that leverages machine learning techniques to estimate nuisance parameters and then applies a sieve estimation method for the parameter of interest.

Our primary methodological contribution is the development of the multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap, an extension of the conventional sieve score bootstrap that is specifically tailored to account for multiway clustering in data. This resampling procedure is crucial for accurate inference in contexts where strong dependencies exist within clusters, which can otherwise result in significant size distortions if conventional asymptotic methods are applied. The proposed method achieves uniform confidence bands with desirable finite-sample properties, as evidenced by both our theoretical developments and extensive Monte Carlo simulations.

Overall, our findings contribute to the growing literature on causal inference under complex dependence structures. By developing tools that accommodate multiway clustering, we provide researchers with more reliable methods for drawing inferences from data characterized by such dependencies. Future research could extend these methods to other types of clustering structures or explore their applicability in broader empirical settings. The methodological innovations presented here open new avenues for robust causal analysis, particularly in fields where data are inherently clustered across multiple dimensions.

Our work underscores the importance of accounting for cross-sectional dependencies in empirical research. The multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap not only improves the accuracy of statistical inference but also prevents misleading conclusions that can arise from ignoring the complex dependence structures often present in real-world data. We encourage further exploration and application of these methods in diverse empirical contexts, aiming to enhance the robustness and reliability of causal inferences in social science research.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
:
We focus on the case of multiway cross-fitting estimator. The case of the full-sample estimator follows by similar lines of argument and is hence omitted. The least squares estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{k\ell}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{k\ell}-\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})]-\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]+\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0})-p^{\prime}_{ij}\beta_{0})]

\displaystyle=\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]+\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(u_{ij}+r_{ij})].

Hence, we can write

\displaystyle\sqrt{|I|\wedge|J|}\alpha(x)^{\prime}(\widehat{\beta}_{k\ell}-\beta_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(u_{ij}+r_{ij})]+\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(u_{ij}+r_{ij})]

\displaystyle+\sqrt{|I|\wedge|J|}\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]

\displaystyle+\sqrt{|I|\wedge|J|}\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{1,k\ell}(x)+S_{2,k\ell}(x)+\sqrt{|I|\wedge|J|}S_{3,k\ell}(x)+\sqrt{|I|\wedge|J|}S_{4,k\ell}(x),

where

\displaystyle S_{1,k\ell}(x)=\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(u_{ij}+r_{ij})],

\displaystyle S_{2,k\ell}(x)=\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(u_{ij}+r_{ij})],

\displaystyle S_{3,k\ell}(x)=\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))],

\displaystyle S_{4,k\ell}(x)=\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}\left(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0})\right)].

First, we are going to show a uniform upper bound for
S_{2,k\ell}(x)
. Define
\{\check{\omega}_{\{i,j\}}\}
as independent Rademacher variates (
\mathbb{P}(\check{\omega}_{\{i,j\}}=1)=\mathbb{P}(\check{\omega}_{\{i,j\}}=-1)=1/2
) that are independent of data. Let
\mathbb{E}_{\check{\omega}}[\cdot]
denote the expectation with respect to the distribution of
\{\check{\omega}_{\{i,j\}}\}
. By the maximal inequality in Lemma
C.3
in the supplementary appendix, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}S_{2,k\ell}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}u_{ij}]\right|\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\mathbb{E}_{\check{\omega}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}u_{ij}\check{\omega}_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}]\right|\right]\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\int^{\theta}_{0}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(T,\|\cdot\|_{n,k\ell,2},\varepsilon)}d\varepsilon,

where

\displaystyle\theta=2\sup_{t\in T}\left\|t\right\|_{n,k\ell,2}=2\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}\left[\alpha(x)^{\prime}(\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1})p_{ij}u_{ij}\right]^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{i\in I_{k},j\in J_{\ell}}\left|u_{ij}\right|\left\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}\right\|\left\|\widehat{Q}\right\|^{1/2},

T:=\{t=\{t_{ij}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|\times|J|}:t_{ij}=\alpha(x)^{\prime}(\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1})p_{ij}u_{ij},x\in\mathcal{X}\}
, and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{n,k\ell,2}
is defined as
\|t\|^{2}_{n,k\ell,2}=\frac{1}{(|I||J|)}\sum_{i\in I_{k},j\in J_{\ell}}t^{2}_{ij}
. Note that, for any
x
,
\widetilde{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[\alpha(x)^{\prime}(\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1})p_{ij}u_{ij}-\alpha(\widetilde{x})^{\prime}(\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1})p_{ij}u_{ij}]^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\xi^{L}_{p}\max_{i\in I_{k},j\in J_{\ell}}|u_{ij}|\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}\|\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\|^{1/2}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|.

The upper bound of the covering number is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(T,\|\cdot\|_{n,k\ell,2},\varepsilon)\lesssim_{p}\left(\frac{\xi^{L}_{p}\max_{i\in I_{k},j\in J_{\ell}}|u_{ij}|\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}\|\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\|^{1/2}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int^{\theta}_{0}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(T,\|\cdot\|_{n,k\ell,2},\varepsilon)}d\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\xi^{L}_{p}\max_{i\in I_{k},j\in J_{\ell}}|u_{ij}|\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}\|\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\|^{1/2}\int^{\theta}_{0}\sqrt{d\log(\xi^{L}_{p}/\varepsilon)}d\varepsilon

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}(|I||J|)^{1/m}\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)/(|I|\wedge|J|)}\sqrt{\log(p)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}(|I||J|)^{1/m}\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log^{2}(p)/(|I|\wedge|J|)},

where
\max_{i\in I_{k},j\in J_{\ell}}|u_{ij}|\lesssim_{p}(|I||J|)^{1/m}
,
\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}\|\lesssim_{p}1
and the upper bound of
\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}\|
is given by Lemma
C.5
in the supplementary appendix. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}u_{ij}]\right|\right]\lesssim(|I||J|)^{1/m}\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log^{2}(p)/(|I|\wedge|J|)}.

(A.1)

Then, the Markov’s inequality gives the desired result for
\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(u_{ij})]
. For the remaining component of
S_{2,k\ell}(x)
, we apply Hölder’s inequality as

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}r_{ij}]\right|\lesssim_{p}\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}\|\|\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}r_{ij}]\|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log^{2}(p)/(|I|\wedge|J|)}\cdot\ell_{p}c_{p}\sqrt{p}.

(A.2)

Combining (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we attain the upper bound for
S_{2,k\ell}(x)
.

Second, we are going to bound
S_{1,k\ell}(x)
. Consider the function class

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:=\{\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}p_{ij}r_{ij}:~x\in\mathcal{X}\}.

Observe that
|\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}p_{ij}r_{ij}|\lesssim_{p}\ell_{p}c_{p}\xi_{p}
,
\mathbb{E}[(\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}p_{ij}r_{ij})^{2}]\lesssim_{p}(\ell_{p}c_{p})^{2}
, and for any
x
,
\widetilde{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
|\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}p_{ij}r_{ij}-\alpha(\widetilde{x})^{\prime}Q^{-1}p_{ij}r_{ij}|\lesssim_{p}\ell_{p}c_{p}\xi^{L}_{p}\xi_{p}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sup_{P}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}),\varepsilon\ell_{p}c_{p}\xi^{L}_{p})\leq\left(\frac{\xi^{L}_{p}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}.

Then, the maximal inequality for the multiway clustering process in Lemma
C.4
in the supplementary appendix and Markov’s inequality yield

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}r_{ij}]|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\ell_{p}c_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)}.

Also, consider the following class of functions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\{(u,x)\mapsto u\cdot\alpha(v)^{\prime}Q^{-1}p(x):~v\in\mathcal{X}\}.

Observe that
|\alpha(v)^{\prime}Q^{-1}p(x)|\lesssim_{p}\xi_{p}
and
var(\alpha(v)^{\prime}Q^{-1}p(x))\lesssim_{p}1
. For any
v
,
\widetilde{v}\in\mathcal{X}
, it follows that
|u\cdot\alpha(v)^{\prime}p(x)-u\cdot\alpha(\widetilde{v})^{\prime}p(x)|\leq|u|\xi^{L}_{p}\xi_{p}\|v-\widetilde{v}\|
. Thus, taking
G(u,x):=|u|\xi_{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{P}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}),\varepsilon\|G\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})})\leq\left(\frac{\xi^{L}_{p}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}.

The maximal inequality for the multiway clustered data in Lemma
C.4
in the supplementary appendix together with the Markov’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\alpha\left(x\right)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{G}_{n,k\ell}\left[p_{ij}u_{ij}\right]\right|\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)}.

Next, we are going to bound
S_{3,k\ell}(x)=\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]
and
S_{4,k\ell}(x)=\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]
.
Note that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\alpha(x)^{\prime}[\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\text{tr}(\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1})\right|\cdot\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[\alpha(x)^{\prime}p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]|

(A.3)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{p}\|\widehat{Q}^{-1}_{k\ell}-Q^{-1}\|\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[\alpha(x)^{\prime}p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]|

(A.4)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{p}\xi_{p}\log(p)/(|I|\wedge|J|)\lesssim\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)/|I|\wedge|J|)},

where (
A.3
) follows the definition of matrix trace and (
A.4
) is based on the definitions of the induced
\ell_{2}
-norm and the largest eigenvalue of a matrix. Similarly, it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\alpha(x)^{\prime}Q^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathbb{E}_{n,k\ell}[\alpha(x)^{\prime}p_{ij}(\psi(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}_{k\ell})-\psi(Z_{ij},\eta_{0}))]|

(A.5)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\xi_{p}\cdot\sqrt{\log(p)/(|I|\wedge|J|)},

where (
A.5
) holds once
Q=I_{p\times p}
, similar to the setup of
Newey (1997)
and
Belloni et al. (2015)
. The proof for multiway cross-fitting estimator is then complete.

The proof for the full-sample estimator follows a similar procedure and is thus omitted.
\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
4.2
:
We focus on the full-sample case since the proof for the cross-fitting estimator follows the identical procedure. With Assumption
4.3
, the high-dimensional CLT
(
Chiang
et al., 2021
, Theorem 1)
for separately exchangeable arrays yields

\displaystyle\sup_{R\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[p_{ij}u_{ij}\right]\in R\right)-\gamma_{\Sigma}\left(R\right)\right|\lesssim{\left(\frac{D^{2}_{n}\log^{7}\left(p\cdot\left(N\vee M\right)\right)}{N\wedge M}\right)^{1/6}}.

(A.6)

For any
R=\prod^{p}_{s=1}\left[a_{s},b_{s}\right]
with
a=(a_{1},...,a_{p})
and
b=(b_{1},...,b_{p})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[p_{ij}\psi\left(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}\right)\right]\in R\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{-\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[p_{ij}\psi\left(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}\right)\right]\leq-a\right\}\cap\left\{\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[p_{ij}\psi\left(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}\right)\right]\leq b\right\}\cap\left\{\left\|\sqrt{N\wedge M}\cdot R_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\leq t\right\}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\sqrt{N\wedge M}\cdot R_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{\Sigma}\left(\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:-y\leq-a-t,y\leq b+t\right\}\right)+{\left(\frac{D^{2}_{n}\log^{7}\left(p\cdot\left(N\vee M\right)\right)}{N\wedge M}\right)^{1/6}+t^{-1}\left(\overline{R}_{1,n}+\overline{R}_{2,n}\right)}

(A.7)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{\Sigma}\left(R\right)+t\sqrt{\log(p)}+{\left(\frac{D^{2}_{n}\log^{7}\left(p\cdot\left(N\vee M\right)\right)}{N\wedge M}\right)^{1/6}}+t^{-1}\left(\overline{R}_{1,n}+\overline{R}_{2,n}\right),

(A.8)

where
\overline{R}_{1,n}
and
\overline{R}_{2,n}
collect all the remainder terms of score vector and

\displaystyle\Sigma=\mu_{N}\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p_{ij^{\prime}}^{\prime}\psi\left(Z_{ij},\psi_{0}\right)\psi\left(Z_{ij^{\prime}},\psi_{0}\right)\right]+\mu_{M}\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p_{i^{\prime}j}^{\prime}\psi\left(Z_{ij},\psi_{0}\right)\psi\left(Z_{i^{\prime}j},\psi_{0}\right)\right]

with
\mu_{N}=(N\wedge M)/N
,
\mu_{M}=(N\wedge M)/M
.
The line (
A.7
) follows from (
A.6
) and the line (
A.8
) holds by Nazarov’s inequality
(
Chiang
et al., 2021
, Lemma G.1)
, together with the fact that the smallest diagonal element of
\Sigma
is bounded from below by
\underline{\sigma}^{2}
. By choosing the tuning parameter
t=(\xi^{1/2}/(N\wedge M)^{1/4})\wedge(1/\ell_{p}r_{p})^{1/2}
, approximation errors are dominated and converge to zero when
(N,M)
diverge. Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[p_{ij}\psi\left(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}\right)\right]\in R\right)\leq\gamma_{\Sigma}\left(R\right)+\left(\frac{D^{2}_{n}\log^{7}\left(p\cdot\left(N\vee M\right)\right)}{N\wedge M}\right)^{1/6}.

(A.9)

A similar derivation yields the reverse inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[p_{ij}\psi\left(Z_{ij},\widehat{\eta}\right)\right]\in R\right)\geq\gamma_{\Sigma}\left(R\right)+\left(\frac{D^{2}_{n}\log^{7}\left(p\cdot\left(N\vee M\right)\right)}{N\wedge M}\right)^{1/6}.

(A.10)

With (
A.9
) and (
A.10
), we prove the desirable result.
\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
4.3
:
We focus on the full-sample case here too. The proof for the multiway cross-fitting estimator follows the identical procedure and is omitted.

The result for
\widehat{Q}
follows from Lemma
C.5
in the supplementary appendix.

For
\widehat{\Sigma}
, consider

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij^{\prime}}+\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in[N]}\sum_{j\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{i^{\prime}j}

\displaystyle-\overline{\mu}_{N}\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}\right]-\overline{\mu}_{M}\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}u_{ij}u_{i^{\prime}j}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\underbrace{\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\left(\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij^{\prime}}-u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}\right)}_{(\text{Part I})}+\underbrace{\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in[N]}\sum_{j\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}\left(\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{i^{\prime}j}-u_{ij}u_{i^{\prime}j}\right)}_{(\text{Part II})}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}\left(p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}-\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}\right]\right)}_{(\text{Part III})}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in[N]}\sum_{j\in[M]}\left(p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}u_{ij}u_{i^{\prime}j}-\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}u_{ij}u_{i^{\prime}j}\right]\right)}_{(\text{Part IV})}.

For Part I, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\left(\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij^{\prime}}-u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\max_{i\in[N],j\in{[M]}}\left|p^{\prime}_{ij}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0})\right|^{2}\left\|\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\right\|

\displaystyle+\max_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\left|u_{ij}\right|\max_{i\in[N],j\in{[M]}}\left|p^{\prime}_{ij}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0})\right|\left\|\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\xi^{2}_{p}\frac{(\sqrt{\log(p)}+\overline{R}_{1,n}+\overline{R}_{2,n})^{2}}{N\wedge M}+\left(NM\right)^{1/m}\xi_{p}\frac{\sqrt{\log(p)}+\overline{R}_{1,n}+\overline{R}_{2,n}}{\sqrt{N\wedge M}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\left(NM\right)^{1/m}\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)/(N\wedge M)},

which relies on the fact that
\|((N\wedge M)/(NM)^{2})\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\|\lesssim_{p}1
.
Similarly, for Part II, we have

\displaystyle\left\|((N\wedge M)/(NM)^{2})\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in[N]}\sum_{j\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}(\widehat{u}_{ij}\widehat{u}_{i^{\prime}j}-u_{ij}u_{i^{\prime}j})\right\|\lesssim(NM)^{1/m}\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)/(N\wedge M)}.

For Part III, we use the Rademacher variable
\{\check{\varepsilon}_{i}\}
with
\mathbb{P}\left(\check{\varepsilon}_{i}=-1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\check{\varepsilon}_{i}=1\right)=1/2
that is independent of the empirical data. Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{N\wedge M}{(NM)^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}\left(p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}-\mathbb{E}[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}]\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\check{\varepsilon}}\left[\left\|\frac{N\wedge M}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\check{\varepsilon}_{i}\left(\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}\right)\right\|\right]\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{N\wedge M}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left\|\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}u_{ij}u_{ij^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\xi_{p}\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\max_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\left|u_{ij}\right|\right)^{2}\left(\frac{N\wedge M}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left\|\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\xi_{p}\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{N}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\max_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\left|u_{ij}\right|\right)^{4}\right]\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{N\wedge M}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left\|\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\in[M]}p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(NM)^{2/m}\xi_{p}\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{N}},

where the first inequality holds by symmetrization inequality for iid data
(
van der Vaart and
Wellner, 1996
, Lemma 2.3.6)
and the second inequality holds by Khinchin’s inequality
(
Belloni et al., 2015
, Lemma 6.1)
. Similarly, for Part IV, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{N\wedge M}{\left(NM\right)^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in[N]}\sum_{j\in[M]}\left(p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}u_{ij}u_{i^{\prime}j}-\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}p^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}u_{ij}u_{i^{\prime}j}\right]\right)\right\|\lesssim(NM)^{2/m}\xi_{p}\sqrt{\log(p)/M}.

\sqrt{N\wedge M}(\widehat{\tau}(x)-\tau_{0}(x))/\widehat{\sigma}_{\tau}(x)
and
p(x)^{\prime}Z/\sigma_{\tau}(x)
are both bounded by
\check{\delta}_{n}
, we apply the procedure of Step 2 in
Li
et al. (2022, Lemma A.2)
and show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq l\leq L_{n}}\left|\frac{\sqrt{N\wedge M}(\widehat{\tau}(x_{l})-\tau_{0}(x_{l}))}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\tau}(x_{l})}\right|\leq r\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{1\leq l\leq L_{n}}\left|\frac{p(x_{l})^{\prime}Z}{\sigma_{\tau}(x_{l})}\right|\leq r^{5\check{\delta}_{n}}\right)-\frac{\psi_{n}(C)+S/C}{1-\psi_{n}(C)},

where
S
is a constant value. Then, we can define
\epsilon_{n}(C)=(\psi_{n}(C)+S/C)/(1-\psi_{n}(C))
and show the fact that
\sup_{n}\epsilon_{n}(C)\rightarrow 0
as
C\rightarrow\infty
. The proof is complete.
\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
5.1
: Following the procedures in
Chernozhuokov et al. (2022, Proposition 2.1)
and
Chiang
et al. (2021, Theorem 2)
, we bound the supreme norm of the difference between the estimated variance of the bootstrapping process and the true variance. With a mild abuse of notations, we define
n:=\min\{N,M\}
. We also define the empirical process

\displaystyle S^{B}:=(\sqrt{n}/N)\sum_{i\in[N]}\omega_{1,i}\widehat{g}_{i0}+(\sqrt{n}/M)\sum_{j\in[M]}\omega_{2,j}\widehat{g}_{0j},

and note the fact that, conditionally on data
\{Z_{ij}\}
,
S^{B}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{p\times 1},\widehat{\Sigma})
where

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{n}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\widehat{g}_{i0}\widehat{g}^{\prime}_{i0}+\frac{n}{M^{2}}\sum_{j\in[M]}\widehat{g}_{0j}\widehat{g}^{\prime}_{0j}.

Hence, by Lemma
C.6
of the supplementary appendix, to obtain a bound on
\sup_{R\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}|\mathbb{P}(S^{B}\in R|\{Z_{ij}\}_{i\in[N],j\in[M]})-\gamma_{\Sigma}(R)|
, it suffices to bound
\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\infty}
. Note that

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{\max_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in[p]}\left|\frac{n}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{1})}_{i0}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{2})}_{i0}-\frac{n}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}\right]\right|,\right.

\displaystyle\left.\max_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in[p]}\left|\frac{n}{M^{2}}\sum_{j\in[M]}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{1})}_{0j}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{2})}_{0j}-\frac{n}{M}\mathbb{E}\left[W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(0,j)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(0,j)}\right]\right|\right\}.

We denote the terms
\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1}:=\max_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in[p]}|(n/N^{2})\sum_{i\in[N]}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{1})}_{i0}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{2})}_{i0}-(n/N)\mathbb{E}[W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}]|
and
\widehat{\Delta}_{W,2}:=\max_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in[p]}|(n/M^{2})\sum_{j\in[M]}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{1})}_{0j}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{2})}_{0j}-(n/M)\mathbb{E}[W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(0,j)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(0,j)}]|
.

Without loss of generality, the proof shows the bound for
\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1}
as a similar upper bound stays valid for
\widehat{\Delta}_{W,2}
. Observe that we can write

\displaystyle\frac{n}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{1})}_{i0}\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{2})}_{i0}-\frac{n}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left(\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{1})}_{i0}-W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}\right)\left(\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{2})}_{i0}-W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}\right)+\frac{n}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left(\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{1})}_{i0}-W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}\right)W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}

\displaystyle+\frac{n}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}\left(\widehat{g}^{(\ell_{2})}_{i0}-W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}\right)+\frac{n}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left(W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}-\mathbb{E}\left[W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}\right]\right).

From the above decomposition, it follows that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1}\lesssim_{p}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\max_{\ell\in[p]}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left(\widehat{g}^{(\ell)}_{i0}-W^{(\ell)}_{(i,0)}\right)^{2}}_{=:\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,1}}+2\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,1}\sqrt{\max_{\ell\in[p]}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left|W^{(\ell)}_{(i,0)}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\max_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in[p]}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}-\mathbb{E}\left[W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}\right]\right|}_{=:\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,2}}.

(A.13)

For the second term in (
A.13
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left|W^{(\ell)}_{(i,0)}\right|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|W^{(\ell)}_{(i,0)}\right|^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left(W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}-\mathbb{E}\left[W^{(\ell_{1})}_{(i,0)}W^{(\ell_{2})}_{(i,0)}\right]\right)\leq\check{\sigma}^{2}+\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,2},

where
\check{\sigma}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[|W^{(\ell)}_{(i,0)}|^{2}]
. Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1}\lesssim_{p}\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,1}+\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,1}^{1/2}\check{\sigma}^{2}+\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,2}.

By
Chiang
et al. (2021, Theorem 2)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,2}\geq C\{n^{-1}D_{n}^{2}\log^{1/2}(pn)+n^{-1+4/q}D^{2}_{n}\log(p)\}\right)\leq Cn^{-1}.

(A.14)

Then, there exists some
\xi\in(2/q,1)
, such that
\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,2}(\log(p))^{2}\leq Cn^{-\xi/2}
with probability at least
1-Cn^{-1}
. We then check

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,1}=\max_{\ell\in[p]}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left(\widehat{g}^{(\ell)}_{i0}-W^{(\ell)}_{(i,0)}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left\|\widehat{g}_{i0}-W_{(i,0)}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Also, conditional on
\{U_{(i,0)}\}
,
Z_{ij}
is separately exchangeable with mean vector
W_{(i,0)}
generated by
\{U_{(0,j)}\}
. We then apply
Chiang
et al. (2021, Corollary)
and show

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{g}_{i0}-W_{(i,0)}\|_{\infty}^{q}|U_{(i,0)}]\lesssim_{p}\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{n}}\mathbb{E}[\|p_{ij}\widehat{u}_{ij}\|^{q}_{\infty}|U_{(i,0)}]\lesssim_{p}n^{-1/2}D^{2}_{n}\log^{2}(p).

This implies that
\mathbb{E}[(\check{\sigma}^{2}\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,1}\log^{4}(p))^{q/2}]\lesssim n^{-\xi q/2}
. The Markov’s inequality further yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\check{\sigma}^{2}\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1,1}\log^{4}(p)>n^{-\xi+1/(2q)}\right)\lesssim n^{-1}.

(A.15)

Combining (
A.14
) and (
A.15
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\Delta}_{W,1}>(n^{-\xi/2+1/(4q)}/\log^{2}(p))\vee(n^{-\xi/2}/\log^{2}(p))\right)\lesssim n^{-1}.

Similarly, we derive the same upper bound for
\widehat{\Delta}_{W,2}
. We apply Lemma
C.6
of the supplement and derive the upper bound for the Kolmogorov distance of the bootstrapping score vector by defining

\displaystyle\Delta:=(n^{-\xi/2+1/(4q)}/\log^{2}(p))\vee(n^{-\xi/2}/\log^{2}(p)).

Following Proposition
1
, we obtain the high-dimensional CLT for the sup-test that relies on the multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrapping process. The proof is complete.
\blacksquare

Proposition 2
:

[Hájek Projection;
Chiang and
Sasaki (2019, Lemma 1)
] Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds with non-degeneracy. Let
f
:
\text{supp}(Z)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{11})]^{2}<C
for a constant
C<\infty
that is independent of
n
. Then, there exist iid uniform random variables
U_{(i,0)}
and
U_{(0,j)}
such that the Hájek Projection
\mathbb{H}_{n}f
of
\mathbb{G}_{n}f
on

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}:=\left\{\sum_{i\in[N]}g_{i0}(U_{(i,0)})+\sum_{j\in[M]}g_{0j}(U_{(0,j)}):~g_{i0},g_{0j}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})\right\},

is equal to

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{n}f:=\sum_{i\in[N]}\frac{\sqrt{N\wedge M}}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.f(Z_{i1})-\mathbb{E}f(Z_{11})\right|U_{(i,0)}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in[M]}\frac{\sqrt{N\wedge M}}{M}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.f(Z_{1j})-\mathbb{E}f(Z_{11})\right|U_{(0,j)}\right],

(C.1)

for each
n
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\mathbb{G}_{n}f)

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(\mathbb{H}_{n}f)+O(1/(N\wedge M))

\displaystyle=\overline{\mu}_{M}\text{Cov}(f(Z_{11}),f(Z_{12}))+\overline{\mu}_{N}\text{Cov}(f(Z_{11}),f(Z_{21}))+O(1/(N\wedge M)),

(C.2)

holds almost surely with
\overline{\mu}_{N}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(N\wedge M)/N~\text{and}~\overline{\mu}_{M}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(N\wedge M)/M
.

Proposition
2
provides the orthogonal projection representation of the multiway clustering empirical process, facilitating its Gaussian approximation. Specifically, Equation (
C.1
) shows that leading terms of approximation include summations of independent components, which allow for the conventional high-dimensional central limit theorem
(
Chernozhuokov et al., 2022
)
. Meanwhile, the approximation error can also be bounded by diminishing upper bounds.

Lemma C.1
:

Let
\{\omega_{ij}\}
denote a sequence of iid Redemacher variables and
\{Z_{ij}\}
be a deterministic sequence. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\omega_{ij}Z_{ij}\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sum_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}Z_{ij}^{2}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
C.1
:
These results hold based on the Hoeffding inequality
(
Hoeffding, 1963
)
and the fact that
\omega_{ij}Z_{ij}\in[-|Z_{ij}|,|Z_{ij}|]
.
\blacksquare

Lemma C.2
(Symmetrization for Multiway Clustered Empirical Process):

Let
\{Z_{ij}\}
be a sample from
\mathcal{S}
-valued separately exchangeable random variables. Assume the pointwise measurable function
\mathcal{F}\ni f
:
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with an envelope function
F\in L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
,
q\geq 4
. Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{E}_{n}f(Z_{ij})-\mathbb{E}f(Z_{ij})|\right]\lesssim\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{E}_{n}[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})]|\right],

where
\omega_{\{i,j\}}
are iid Rademacher variables independent of
\{Z_{ij}\}
,
\mathbf{e}(:=(e_{1},e_{2})^{\prime})
is an element of
\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}
,
\mathcal{E}_{r}
denotes the set
\{\mathbf{e}\in\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}:\sum^{2}_{j=1}e_{j}=r\}
for
r=1
,
2
and
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product.

Proof of Lemma
C.2
:
The proof follows
Davezies et al. (2021, Lemma S2)
.
\blacksquare

Lemma C.3
(Entropy Integral Inequality for Multiway Clustered Empirical Process):

Let
\{Z_{ij}\}
be a sample from the
\mathcal{S}
-valued separately exchangeable random variables. Assume the pointwise measurable function
\mathcal{F}\ni f
:
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with envelope
F\in L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
,
q\geq 4
, that is,
F(\cdot)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f(\cdot)
. In addition, suppose
B:=\sup_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\left|F(Z_{ij})\right|
. Define
\sigma^{2}_{n}:=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[f^{2}(Z_{ij})]
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\mathbb{G}_{n}f\right|\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{\sigma_{n}}_{0}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},\varepsilon\right)}d\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}\int^{\sigma_{n}/\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}}_{0}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},\varepsilon\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}\right)}d\varepsilon\right],

where
\left\|f-g\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{n}[\left(f(Z_{ij})-g(Z_{ij})\right)^{2}]}
.

Proof of Lemma
C.3
:
By Lemma
C.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{G}_{n}f|\right]\lesssim\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{G}_{n}[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})]|\right].

Since the Rademacher variables are independent of data, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})\right]\right|\right|\{Z_{ij}\}_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})\right]\right|\right],

where
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\cdot]
means taking expectation with respect to
\{\omega_{\{i,j\}}\}
only and treating the observed data as deterministic. In addition, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{G}_{n}[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})]|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\left\|\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{G}_{n}[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})]|\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{\omega},1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\left\|\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{G}_{n}[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})]|\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{\omega},2}

\psi_{2}(\cdot)

\psi_{2}(x)(:=\exp(x^{2}-1)

, where
z_{ij}=Z_{ij}

(
van der Vaart and
Wellner, 1996

C.1

\mathbf{e}
and functions
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}\left(f(Z_{ij})-g(Z_{ij})\right)\right]\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{\left\|f-g\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}}\right),

where
\left\|f-g\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}:=\mathbb{E}_{n}[(f(Z_{ij})-g(Z_{ij}))^{2}]

van der Vaart and
Wellner (1996, Lemma 2.2.1)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}\left(f(Z_{ij})-g(Z_{ij})\right)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C\left\|f-g\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}.

(C.3)

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\left\|\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})\right]\right|\right\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\int^{2\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|f(Z_{ij})\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}}_{0}\sqrt{1+\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},\varepsilon\right)}d\varepsilon+\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\mathbb{G}_{n}\left[\omega_{\{i,j\}\odot\mathbf{e}}f(Z_{ij})\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}

(C.4)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\int^{2\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|f(Z_{ij})\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}}_{0}\sqrt{1+\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},\varepsilon\right)}d\varepsilon+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|f\left(Z_{ij}\right)\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}

(C.5)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\int^{\sigma_{n}}_{0}\sqrt{1+\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},\varepsilon\right)}d\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}\int^{\sigma_{n}/\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}}_{0}\sqrt{1+\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},2\varepsilon\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}\right)}d\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}\int^{\sigma_{n}/\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}}_{0}\sqrt{1+\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},\varepsilon\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}\right)}d\varepsilon,

(C.6)

where (

) follows from Lemma
C.2
and

Wellner (1996, Lemma 2.2.4)
, (
C.5
) is based on (

), and (

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},2\varepsilon\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}\right)\leq\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2},\varepsilon\left\|F\right\|_{\mathbb{P}_{n},2}\right).

The proof is now complete.

Lemma C.4
(Maximal Inequality for Multiway Clustered Empirical Processes):

Let
\{Z_{ij}\}
be a sample from
\mathcal{S}
-valued separately exchangeable random variables. Assume the pointwise measurable function

This section provides the identification results for the general causal functions by relying on the Neyman orthogonal moment in the presence of multiway clustered data. Following the main draft, we focus on the CATE and CTE functions.

Identification of the CATE

In the setting of multiway clustering, the CATE function can be identified from the Neyman orthogonal signal. Adapting the identification condition for iid data presented by
Fan
et al. (2022)
to our multiway clustered setup, we state the following assumption.

Assumption D.1
:

For the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
induced by
\left\{Z_{ij}\right\}

(i)

(Unconfoundedness)
(Y_{ij}(1),Y_{ij}(0))\perp D_{ij}|W_{ij}

(ii)

(Moments)

(iii)

(Overlap) there exists some constant

(No interference) potential outcomes

and
Y_{ij}(0)
for any unit

assigned to other units with

(

D.1
(

(

Lemma D.1
:

Let Assumptions

and
D.1
hold.

(i)

The following conditions hold as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\frac{D_{ij}\left(Y_{ij}-\mu_{0}\left(1,W_{ij}\right)\right)}{\pi_{0}\left(W_{ij}\right)}+\mu_{0}\left(1,W_{ij}\right)\right|X_{ij}=x\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{ij}\left(1\right)|X_{ij}=x\right],

(D.1)

(ii)

Appendix E
General Multiway Clustering

In the main text, for brevity of notations and ease of exposition, we focus on two-way clustering where each cluster intersection contains only one unit of observation.
However, we may allow for an arbitrary dimension of multiway clustering and an arbitrary number of observations in each cluster intersection.
Our theory naturally extends to such general settings.
In this appendix section, we extend the main results of our uniform inference for causal functions to the general multiway cluster sampling framework

We follow the notations of
Chiang
et al. (2022)
and introduce the following symbols. We consider the
\ell
-way clustered data for a fixed dimension
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. With
C_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
denoting the number of clusters in the
i
-th cluster dimension for each
i\in[\ell]
, each cell of the
\ell
-way clustered sample is indexed by the
\ell
-dimensional multiway cluster indices
{j}=(j_{1},...,j_{\ell})\in\times_{i=1}^{\ell}[C_{i}]
. Define
{C}=(C_{1},...,C_{\ell})
,
\prod_{C}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}C_{i}
,
\underline{C}=\min_{1\leq i\leq\ell}C_{i}
,
\overline{C}=\max_{1\leq i\leq\ell}C_{i}
, and
\mu_{i}=\underline{C}/C_{i}
for each
i\in[\ell]
. Assume
\mu_{i}\rightarrow\overline{\mu_{i}}
for some constant
\overline{\mu}_{i}
for each
i\in[\ell]
. The number of observations in the
j
-th cell is denoted by
N_{j}
.

Full-Sample Estimation

Given the first step estimator
\widehat{\eta}
that relies on the whole sample, we can generate the estimated dependent variable that is formed as the Neyman orthogonal moment
\psi(Z_{\ell},\widehat{\eta})
. We define the sample moment by

\displaystyle\widehat{J}:=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}p(X_{lj})p(X_{lj})^{\prime}\right],

where the empirical measure of the general clustering scheme follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}f(Z_{lj})\right]:=\frac{1}{\prod_{C}}\sum_{j\in\times_{i=1}^{\ell}[C_{i}]}\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}f(Z_{lj})

for each for each
k\in[K]^{\ell}
. Then, the second-step least squares estimator is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=\widehat{J}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}p(X_{l,j})\psi(Z_{lj};\widehat{\eta})\right].

The second-step nonparametric estimator for causal functions is given by
\widehat{\tau}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{\beta}
.

Let
I(j)
denote the multiway clustering block that contains the
j
-th multiway cluster, i.e.,
I(j)\subset\times_{i=1}^{\ell}\left[C_{i}\right]
satisfies
I=I(j)
for
j\in I(j)
. We give the variance estimate
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(x)
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{J}^{-1}\left[\frac{|\underline{C}|}{\prod_{C}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,j^{\prime}\in\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}C_{i},\\
I_{i}(j)=I_{i}(j^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[N_{j^{\prime}}]}p(X_{l,j})p(X_{l,j^{\prime}})^{\prime}\widehat{u}_{l,j}\widehat{u}_{l^{\prime},j}\right](\widehat{J}^{-1})^{\prime}p(x),

where
\widehat{u}_{l,j}
is the residual of the second-step nonparametric estimation.

Cross-Fitting Estimation

For a given integer
K
, we randomly split the data into
K
folds in each of the
\ell
cluster dimensions, and obtain
K^{\ell}
folds in all. In particular, we randomly separate
[C_{i}]
into
K
parts
\{I_{i}^{1},...,I_{i}^{K}\}
for each
i\in\{1,...,\ell\}
. We apply the
\ell
-dimensional indices
k:=(k_{1},...,k_{\ell})
to index the
\ell
-way fold
I_{k}(:=I_{k_{1}}\times...\times I_{k_{\ell}})
and the product
I_{k}^{c}:=I_{k_{1}}^{c}\times...\times I_{k_{\ell}}^{c}
for each
k\in[K]^{\ell}
. We define
\widehat{\eta}_{k}=\widehat{\eta}(\left(Z_{l,j}\right)_{l\in[N_{j}],j\in I_{k}^{c}})
to be a nonparametric estimate of
\eta
using the subsample
(W_{l,j})_{l\in[N_{j}],j\in I_{k}^{c}}
for each
k\in[K]^{\ell}
. We define the sample moment by

\displaystyle\widehat{J}:=\frac{1}{K^{\ell}}\sum_{k\in[K]^{\ell}}\mathbb{E}_{n,k}\left[\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}p(X_{l,j})p(X_{l,j})^{\prime}\right],

where the empirical measure of the general clustering scheme follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,k}\left[\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}f(Z_{lj})\right]:=\frac{1}{|I_{k}|}\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}f(Z_{l,j})

for each for each
k\in[K]^{\ell}
. The second-step least squares estimator is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=\widehat{J}^{-1}\frac{1}{K^{\ell}}\sum_{k\in[K]^{\ell}}\mathbb{E}_{n,k}\left[\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}p(X_{l,j})\psi(Z_{l,j};\widehat{\eta}_{k})\right].

The second-step nonparametric estimator for causal functions is given by
\widehat{\tau}(x)=p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{\beta}
.

To motivate the uniform testing, we define
|I_{k}|:=\lfloor\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}C_{i}}{K^{\ell}}\rfloor
and
|\underline{I_{k}}|=\min\{|I_{k_{1}}|,...,|I_{k_{\ell}}|\}
. Also, we let
I(j)
denote the multiway clustering fold that contains the
j
-th multiway cluster, i.e.,
I(j)\subset\times_{i=1}^{\ell}\left[C_{i}\right]
satisfies
I_{k}=I(j)
for some given
k\in[K]^{\ell}
and
j\in I(j)
. We give the variance estimate
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(x)
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(x)=

\displaystyle p(x)^{\prime}\widehat{J}^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{K^{\ell}}\sum_{k\in[K]^{\ell}}\frac{|\underline{I_{k}}|}{|I_{k}|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,j^{\prime}\in I_{k}\\
I_{i}(j)=I_{i}(j^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in[N_{j^{\prime}}]}p(X_{l,j})p(X_{l,j^{\prime}})^{\prime}\widehat{u}_{l,j}\widehat{u}_{l^{\prime},j}\right](\widehat{J}^{-1})^{\prime}p(x),

where
\widehat{u}_{l,j}
is the residual of the second-step nonparametric estimation.

Uniform Inference

In the current subsection, we focus on the case of the two-way clustering data for brevity as in the main draft, but we continue to allow for an arbitrary number of observations in each cluster intersection,
j=(j_{1},j_{2})
.

Similar to the discussions introduced in the main paper, we project the score vector of interest onto two orthogonal directions as

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{j_{1},0}(U_{(j_{1},0)}):=(1/C_{2})\sum_{j_{2}\in[C_{2}]}\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}p_{lj}\widehat{u}_{lj}\quad\text{ and }\quad\widehat{g}_{0,j_{2}}(U_{(0,j_{2})}):=(1/C_{1})\sum_{i\in[C_{1}]}\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}p_{lj}\widehat{u}_{lj},

where
\widehat{u}_{ij}
is the residuals reported in the second step. For simplicity, we write
\widehat{g}_{j_{1}0}(U_{(j_{1},0)})
as
\widehat{g}_{j_{1},0}
and
\widehat{g}_{0,j_{2}}(U_{(0,j_{2})})
as
\widehat{g}_{0,j_{2}}
.

To motivate the bootstrapping uniform testing method, we give the perturbation processes
\{\omega_{1,j_{1}}\}_{j_{1}\in[C_{1}]}
and
\{\omega_{2,j_{2}}\}_{j_{2}\in[C_{2}]}
be independent
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables independent of the observed data. Then, we obtain the multiway cluster-robust sieve score bootstrap empirical process:

\displaystyle t^{b}_{\tau}(x):=\frac{\sqrt{\underline{C}}\cdot p(x)^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}_{n}[\sum_{l\in[N_{j}]}p_{lj}p_{lj}^{\prime}])^{-1}\{(1/C_{1})\sum_{i\in[C_{1}]}\omega_{1,i}\widehat{g}_{i0}+(1/C_{2})\sum_{j\in[C_{2}]}\omega_{2,j}\widehat{g}_{0j}\}}{\widehat{\sigma}(x)}.

(E.1)

The critical value
cv_{n}^{b}(1-\alpha)
is given by the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|t^{b}_{\tau}(x)|
over the multiple random draws of
\{\omega_{1,j_{1}}\}
and
\{\omega_{2,j_{2}}\}
:

\displaystyle cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\text{ quantile of}~\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|t^{b}_{\tau}(x)|~\text{over the draws of }\{\omega_{1,j_{1}}\}_{i\in[C_{1}]}~\text{and}~\{\omega_{2,j_{2}}\}_{j_{2}\in[C_{2}]}.

(E.2)

Then, we compute the bootstrapping UCBs for the causal functions
\tau_{0}(x)
by

\displaystyle[\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{l}(x),\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{u}(x)]=[\widehat{\tau}(x)-cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)\cdot\widehat{\sigma}(x),~\widehat{\tau}(x)+cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)\cdot\widehat{\sigma}(x)],~~\text{for}~x\in\mathcal{X},

(E.3)

where
cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)
ensures that
\tau(x)\in[\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{l}(x),\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{u}(x)]
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
with the level of confidence
100(1-\alpha)\%
asymptotically, as formally shown in the following theorem.

To show the uniform size controls of our bootstrapping testing method, we impose the assumption below.

Assumption E.1
:

The following conditions hold for each
n
.

(i)

The array
(N_{j},(W_{l,j})_{1\leq l\leq\overline{N}})_{j\geq\boldsymbol{1}}
is an infinite sequence of separately exchangeable random vector. That is, for any
\ell
-tuple of permutations
\left(\pi_{1},...,\pi_{\ell}\right)
of
\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle(N_{j},(W_{l,j})_{1\leq l\leq\overline{N}})_{j\geq 1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left(N_{\pi_{1}(j_{1}),...,\pi_{\ell}(j_{\ell})},\left(W_{l,\pi_{1}(j_{1}),...,\pi_{\ell}(j_{\ell})}\right)_{1\leq l\leq\overline{N}}\right)_{j\geq 1};

(ii)

(N_{j},(W_{l,j})_{1\leq l\leq\overline{N}})_{j\geq\mathbf{1}}
is dissociated. That is, for any
\mathbf{c}\geq\mathbf{1},(N_{j},(W_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq\overline{N}})_{\mathbf{1}\leq j\leq\mathbf{c}}
is independent of
(N_{j^{\prime}},(W_{l^{\prime},j^{\prime}})_{1\leq l^{\prime}\leq\overline{N}})_{j^{\prime}\geq\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{1}}

(iii)

\mathbb{E}[N_{\mathbf{1}}]>0
and
N_{j}\leq\overline{N}
for each
\mathbf{1}\leq j\leq\boldsymbol{C}
, where
\overline{N}\in\mathbb{N}
does not depend on
n
.

The next theorem formally states the validity of the UCBs constructed above.

Theorem E.1
:

Suppose that the assumptions invoked in Theorem
4.2
and Assumption
E.1
hold. In addition, assume that
cv^{b}_{n}(1-\alpha)
is calculated as in (
E.2
). Then, the bootstrapping UCBs defined in (
E.3
) satisfy

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\tau(x)\in[\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{l}(x),\widehat{\tau}^{b}_{u}(x)],~\text{for all }x\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}=1-\alpha+o(1).

The proof follows the same lines of argument as that of Theorem
5.1
presented in the main paper and is thus omitted accordingly. Theorem
E.1
shows that the general multiway clustering scheme that incorporates multiple individuals in a cell can still use the multiway cluster-robust bootstrapping method to construct a uniform inference procedure.
