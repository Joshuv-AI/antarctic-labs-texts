The Haar State values of monomials and a method to pick orthonormal bases on 𝒪 ​ ( U q ​ ( 3 ) ) \mathcal{O}(U_{q}(3))
Ting Lu

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the evaluation problem of the Haar state on the quantum group 𝒪 ​ ( U q ​ ( n ) ) \mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n)) [ faddeev1988quantization , klimyk2012quantum ] ( n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 ) which is a q q -deformation of the Haar measure on the Lie group U ⁡ ( n ) U(n) . The relation between the Haar state values of monomials on 𝒪 ​ ( U q ​ ( n ) ) \mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n)) is studied. On 𝒪 ​ ( U q ​ ( 3 ) ) \mathcal{O}(U_{q}(3)) , the Haar state values of monomials are explicitly computed and these values are expressed as a finite summation of rational polynomials in q q . As an application, we compute the Gram matrices of the irreducible co-representations of 𝒪 ​ ( U q ​ ( 3 ) ) \mathcal{O}(U_{q}(3)) which is essential to the method of constructing orthonormal bases on 𝒪 ​ ( U q ​ ( 3 ) ) \mathcal{O}(U_{q}(3)) proposed by Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi [ noumi1993finite ] . New connections between the Haar state values of monomials and basic hypergeometric multi-summations are found during our computation.



1.
Introduction

The existence and uniqueness of the Haar measure, a translation invariant probability measure, is one of the fundamental properties of compact topological groups. Its applications ranges from pure math to various scientific fields including representation theory, random matrix theory, theoretical physics, mathematical physics, statistics and many other fields. In this paper, we present the first systematic investigation on the evaluation problem of the Haar state
h
on the quantum group
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))

[
faddeev1988quantization
,
klimyk2012quantum
]
(
n\geq 3
) which can be considered as a
q
-deformation of the Haar measure on
U(n)
. A brief review of the algebraic property of
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
and its Haar state
h
can be found in Section 2. All results in this paper is directly applicable to Woronowicz’s compact quantum group
SU_{q}(n)

[
woronowicz1988tannaka
,
woronowicz1987compact
]
.

The interest of evaluating Haar integrals stems from studies in theoretical physics. Let
U_{i,j}
’s (
1\leq i,j\leq n
) be coordinate functions on a matrix Lie group
G
and
\overline{U_{i,j}}
the conjugate of
U_{i,j}
. Haar integrals in the following form

(1.1)

\int_{U\in G}U_{i_{1},j_{1}}\cdots U_{i_{m},j_{m}}\overline{U_{l_{1},k_{1}}\cdots U_{l_{m},k_{m}}}\text{d}U

appear in lattice gauge theory and theoretical physicists become the first group of researchers to investigate the evaluation of (
1.1
) on compact matrix Lie groups. Related notable works include but not limit to Weingarten
[
weingarten1978asymptotic
]
, Creutz
[
creutz1978invariant
]
, and Brezin and Gross
[
brezin1980external
]
. The evaluation of (
1.1
) is also related to the method of moments in the study of random matrix theory. In the work of Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
diaconis1994eigenvalues
]
, the evaluation of a Haar integral similar to (
1.1
) is the essential step in understanding the distribution of eigenvalues of random matrices on compact matrix Lie groups. Following their work there is a surge of interest in searching for a systematic method to evaluate Haar integrals on compact groups. This quest came to an end when the Weingarten calculus is proposed in the work of Collins
[
collins2003moments
]
and Collins and Śniady
[
collins2006integration
]
.

The key point of the Weingarten calculus is that the evaluation of Haar integral in (
1.1
) is converted to computing certain matrices associated with the invariant tensor space under the action of
G
. Readers may find more details on the Weingarten calculus in the work of Collins, Matsumoto, and Novak
[
collins2022weingarten
]
. Since its emergence, the Weingarten calculus has been applied to numerous studies across multiple fields and proved to be a fruitful theory as summarized by Collins
[
collins2022moment
]
. Especially, the theory has been successfully extended to free compact quantum groups
[
wang1995free
,
banica2007integration
,
banica2007permutationintegration
,
banica2008integration
]
.

However, on
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
, the evaluation provided by the Weingarten calculus is not as efficient as the classical case since the Haar state is non-tracial (
h(xy)\neq h(yx)
). In particular, the consequence of
h
being non-tracial cannot be neglected where
q
approaches
0
. Our strategy to this issue is to break the integrand in (
1.1
) into smaller pieces and consider the evaluation problem of

(1.2)

h(x_{i_{1},j_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{m},j_{m}}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-a_{m}}).

We will call the integrand in (
1.2
) a monomial on
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
.

On
SU_{q}(2)

[
sl1987twisted
]
which is a special case of
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(2))
, the evaluation of (
1.2
) is studied by Woronowicz
[
sl1987twisted
]
and Masuda
et al.

[
masuda1991representations
]
independently. Both of their works rely on the fact that every monomial on
SU_{q}(2)
with a non-zero Haar state value can be written as a linear combination of a set of special monomials and those special monomials are mutually commutative. On
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
(
n\geq 3
), such a set of special monomials can be found but they are not mutually commutative. Hence, their approaches on
SU_{q}(2)
cannot be applied on higher rank
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
directly.

In Section 3, we discuss the evaluation of (
1.2
) on general
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
. A special set of monomials that serves like a basis of the support of the Haar state is chosen in Section 3.1. The method of finding linear relations between the Haar state values of our chosen monomials is given in Section 3.2. The techniques of Woronowicz and Masuda
et al.
are combined and we use these new techniques to construct the Source matrix in Section 3.4. Although a complete solution is still in searching, the Source matrix allows us to find
h\left((\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,n+1-i})^{m}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\right)
. The importance of
h\left((\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,n+1-i})^{m}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\right)
in the evaluation of (
1.2
) will be explained in Section 4.

In Section 4, we give a complete evaluation of (
1.2
) on
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(3))
(Theorem 4.1). Our approach rely on the invariant property of the Haar state under the action of
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{3})
on
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(3))
. This approach is inspired by the work of Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi
[
noumi1993finite
]
in which the evaluation of (
1.1
) on the homogeneous quantum space
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))\backslash\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n-1))
is given. In Corollary 4.2, the result in Theorem 4.1 is extended to general
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
.

Just like the classical case,
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
is a pre-Hilbert space with the inner product
\langle a,b\rangle_{L}=h(a^{*}b)
(or
\langle a,b\rangle_{R}=h(ab^{*})
) as proved by Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi
[
noumi1993finite
]
. They further proposed a method to construct orthonormal bases on
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
given that we can find orthonormal bases of irreducible corepresentations of
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
. In Section 5, we apply our result to compute the Gram matrices of irreducible corepresentations of
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(3))
(Theorem 5.8 and 5.9). With these Gram matrices, one can construct orthonormal bases on the irreducible corepresentations by the Gram-Schmidt process and then an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(3))
is obtained by the method of Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi
[
noumi1993finite
]
.

The evaluations of Haar integrals on compact (quantum) groups are closely related to combinatorial problems. This connection has been studied in the work of Brouwer and Beenakker
[
brouwer1996diagrammatic
]
, Collins, Gurau, and Lionni

collins2023tensor
]

magee2019matrix

[
kumar2015connections
]
show that the evaluation of a special case of (
1.2

is related to the Alon-Tarsi conjecture. In our opinion, it would be interesting to explore a combinatorial explanation for our result (
4.55
) and (
).

The study of

is also connected with basic hypergeometric series
[
masuda1991representations
,
koelink1989clebsch
,
vaksman1990algebra

bergeron2019suq
]

In our computations, multi-summations of basic hypergeometric terms appear as the inner products between basis vectors of irreducible corepresentions. By comparing our computations with the results of Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi

2.
Preliminaries on
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))

In this paper, we work in the framework of Faddeev, Reshetikhin, and Takhtajan
[
faddeev1988quantization
,
dijkhuizen1994cqg
]
.
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
is the ”compact” real form of
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
. The existence and uniqueness of the Haar state on
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
relies on the fact that
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
is a co-semisimple Hopf algebra
[
sweedler1969hopf
]
. In particular, the algebra of coordinate functions on
U(n)
is co-semisimple and the unique Haar state is the integral with respect to the normalized Haar measure. Readers can find a detailed study of the algebraic properties and representation theory on
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
from Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi
[
noumi1993finite
]
. Here we give a brief review of the Hopf algebra
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
, the relation between
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
and
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
, the Peter-Weyl decomposition of
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
, and the Haar state on
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
. Then, we introduce the method of picking orthonormal basis on
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
proposed by Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi
[
noumi1993finite
]
.

2.1.
Algebraic properties of
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))

Before we introduce
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
, we define the bi-algebra
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
. Denote the generators of
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
as
\{x_{i,j}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
. We will assume that these generators are put in a ”generator matrix” in the following way.

(2.0)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x_{1,1}&\cdots&x_{1,n}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
x_{n,1}&\cdots&x_{n,n}\end{bmatrix}

The bialgebra
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
is generated by the commutation relations (
i<l
,
j<k
):

(2.1.a)

\displaystyle x_{i,j}x_{l,j}=q\cdot x_{l,j}x_{i,j}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ x_{i,j}x_{i,k}=q\cdot x_{i,k}x_{i,j},

(2.1.b)

\displaystyle x_{ik}x_{lj}=x_{lj}x_{ik},

(2.1.c)

\displaystyle x_{i,j}x_{l,k}=x_{l,k}x_{i,j}+(q-q^{-1})x_{ik}x_{lj}.

The co-multiplication
\Delta:\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))\otimes\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
and co-unit
\epsilon:\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is defined by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{i,j})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{i,k}\otimes x_{k,j}\quad\text{ and }\quad\varepsilon(x_{i,j})=\delta_{i,j}.

Notice that
\Delta
and
\epsilon
are
\mathbb{C}
-algebra homomorphisms. If we set
q=1
, then we get the bi-algebra of coordinate functions on
n\times n
matrices.

Let
I=\{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{r}\}
(
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{r}
) and
J=\{j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{r}\}
(
j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{r}
) be two subsets of
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. The quantum
r-
minor determinant
\xi_{J}^{I}
can be defined in two equivalent forms

(2.3.a)

\displaystyle\xi_{J}^{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}(-q)^{l(\tau)}x_{i_{1},j_{\tau(1)}}\cdots x_{i_{r},j_{\tau(r)}}

(2.3.b)

\displaystyle\xi_{J}^{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}(-q)^{l(\tau)}x_{i_{\tau(1)},j_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{\tau(r)},j_{r}}

where
\mathfrak{S}_{r}
is the permutation group on
r
letters and
l(\tau)
is the number of inversions of
\tau
. Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi
[
noumi1993finite
]
showed that
\xi_{J}^{I}
’s are actually the matrix coefficients of the alternating tensor representation of
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
of degree
r
. The quantum determinant
D_{q}=\xi_{\{1,\cdots,n\}}^{\{1,\cdots,n\}}
is defined by

(2.4.a)

\displaystyle D_{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\sigma)}x_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots x_{\sigma(n),n}

(2.4.b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\tau)}x_{1,\tau(1)}\cdots x_{n,\tau(n)}.

We remark that since
\xi_{J}^{I}
’s are matrix coefficients they satisfy

(2.5.a)

\displaystyle\Delta(\xi_{J}^{I})=\sum_{|K|=r}\xi_{K}^{I}\otimes\xi_{J}^{K}.

Especially we have

(2.5.b)

\displaystyle\Delta(D_{q})=D_{q}\otimes D_{q}.

Let
\widehat{k}=\{1,\cdots,k-1,k+1,\cdots,n\}
. By Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi
[
noumi1993finite
]
, we have

(2.6.a)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-q)^{i-k}\xi_{\widehat{i}}^{\widehat{k}}x_{k,j}=\delta_{i,j}D_{q},

(2.6.b)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-q)^{k-j}x_{i,k}\xi^{\widehat{j}}_{\widehat{k}}=\delta_{i,j}D_{q},

(2.6.c)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-q)^{k-j}x_{k,i}\xi_{\widehat{j}}^{\widehat{k}}=\delta_{i,j}D_{q},

(2.6.d)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-q)^{i-k}\xi^{\widehat{i}}_{\widehat{k}}x_{j,k}=\delta_{i,j}D_{q}.

The Hopf algebra
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
is defined by adjoining an extra generator
\text{det}_{q}^{-1}
, the inverse of
D_{q}
, to
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
satisfying

(2.7.a)

\displaystyle D_{q}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}=\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\cdot D_{q}=1,

(2.7.b)

\displaystyle\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\cdot x_{i,j}=x_{i,j}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}.

If we identify
D_{q}
with
1
, we get the Hopf algebra
\mathcal{O}(SL_{q}(n))
. Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi
[
noumi1993finite
]
showed that the center of
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
is generated by
D_{q}
and
\text{det}_{q}^{-1}
when
q
is not a root of unity. The co-multiplication and co-unit of
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
can be extended to
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
by

(2.8)

\displaystyle\Delta(\text{det}_{q}^{-1})=\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\otimes\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad\varepsilon(\text{det}_{q}^{-1})=1.

The antipod
S:\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
is defined by

(2.9)

\displaystyle S(x_{i,j})=(-q)^{i-j}\xi_{\widehat{i}}^{\widehat{j}}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}.

When
q\in\mathbb{R}
, we can define a conjugate linear anti-homomorphism
*:\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
by

(2.10)

\displaystyle x_{i,j}^{*}=S(x_{j,i})=(-q)^{j-i}\xi_{\widehat{j}}^{\widehat{i}}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}.

With this
*
map,
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
becomes a Hopf
*
-algebra and
(\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n)),*)
is denoted as
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
, the ”compact” real form of
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
. If we consider Woronowicz’s compact quantum group
SU_{q}(n)
as a
*
-Hopf algebra, then it is
(\mathcal{O}(SL_{q}(n)),*)
.

Let
I^{c}
denote the complement of
I
. Define

\text{sgn}_{q}(I;J)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}0&\text{if}&I\cap J\neq\varnothing,\\
(-q)^{l(I;J)}&\text{if}&I\cap J=\varnothing,\end{array}\right.

where
l(I;J)
is the number of pair
(i,j),i\in I,j\in J
such that
i>j
. Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi showed that

(2.11.a)

\displaystyle\left(\xi_{J}^{I}\right)^{*}=S\left(\xi_{I}^{J}\right)=\frac{\text{sgn}_{q}(J;J^{c})}{\text{sgn}_{q}(I;I^{c})}\xi_{J^{c}}^{I^{c}}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}

and in particular

(2.11.b)

\displaystyle\left(D_{q}\right)^{*}=\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\text{ and }\left(\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\right)^{*}=D_{q}.

2.2.
The action of
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
on
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))

In this subsection, we review the relation between the quantum universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})

[
drinfeld1986quantum
,
jimbo1985aq
]
and the quantum group
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
.

Let
F_{n}
be the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
n
with basis
\{\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{n}\}
with the symmetric bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
such that
\langle\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i,j}
. Elements of
F_{n}
are called
integral weights
.

The generators of
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
are denoted as
e_{k},f_{k}(1\leq k<n)
and
q^{\lambda}(\lambda\in\frac{1}{2}F_{n})
. For a detailed description of the algebraic structure of
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
, we refer to
[
noumi1993finite
]
. The Hopf algebra structure on
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
is defined by

(2.12.a)

\displaystyle\Delta(q^{\lambda})=q^{\lambda}\otimes q^{\lambda},\quad\varepsilon(q^{\lambda})=1,\quad S(q^{\lambda})=q^{-\lambda},

(2.12.b)

\displaystyle\Delta(e_{k})=e_{k}\otimes q^{-(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{k+1})/2}+q^{(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{k+1})/2}\otimes e_{k},

\displaystyle\varepsilon(e_{k})=0,\quad S(e_{k})=-q^{-1}e_{k},

(2.12.c)

\displaystyle\Delta(f_{k})=f_{k}\otimes q^{-(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{k+1})/2}+q^{(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{k+1})/2}\otimes f_{k},

\displaystyle\varepsilon(f_{k})=0,\quad S(f_{k})=-qf_{k}.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two Hopf algebras. By a
pairing of Hopf algebras
we mean a bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying

(2.13.a)

\displaystyle(a,\varphi\psi)=(\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(a),\varphi\otimes\psi)\quad\text{ and }\quad(a,1)=\varepsilon_{\mathcal{A}}(a)

,

(2.13.b)

\displaystyle(ab,\varphi)=(a\otimes b,\Delta_{\mathcal{B}}(\varphi))\quad\text{ and }\quad(1,\varphi)=\varepsilon_{\mathcal{B}}(\varphi)

,

(2.13.c)

\displaystyle(S_{\mathcal{A}}(a),\varphi)=(a,S_{\mathcal{B}}(\varphi))

for all
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
and
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{B}
. Here, the pairings in (
2.13.a
) and (
2.13.b
) from
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
to
\mathbb{C}
is induced from the bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
and we keep the same notation for simplicity.

There is a unique pairing of Hopf algebras between
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
and
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
satisfying

(2.14.a)

\displaystyle(q^{\lambda},x_{i,j})=\delta_{i,j}q^{\langle\lambda,\epsilon_{i}\rangle},

(2.14.b)

\displaystyle(e_{k},x_{i,j})=\delta_{i,k}\delta_{j,k+1},\quad(f_{k},x_{i,j})=\delta_{i,k+1}\delta_{j,k},

(2.14.c)

\displaystyle(q^{\lambda},\text{det}_{q}^{m})=q^{m\langle\lambda,\epsilon_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{n}\rangle}\quad\quad(m\in\mathbb{Z}),

(2.14.d)

\displaystyle(e_{k},\text{det}_{q}^{m})=(f_{k},\text{det}_{q}^{m})=0\quad\quad(m\in\mathbb{Z})

where
\text{det}_{q}^{m}=D_{q}^{m}
when
m\geq 0
.

With this unique pairing of
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
and
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
, we may regard
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
as a bimodule over
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
. The left and right action of
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
on
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
can be defined in the following way. If
a\in\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
and
x\in\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
with
\Delta(x)=\sum x_{i}^{1}\otimes x_{i}^{2}
, then

(2.15.a)

\displaystyle a\cdot x=(id\otimes(a,\cdot))\circ\Delta(x)=\sum(a,x_{i}^{2})x_{i}^{1},

(2.15.b)

\displaystyle x\cdot a=((a,\cdot)\otimes id)\circ\Delta(x)=\sum(a,x_{i}^{1})x_{i}^{2}.

The actions of generators of
\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
on generators of
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
are given by

(2.16.a)

\displaystyle q^{\lambda}\cdot x_{i,j}=q^{\langle\lambda,\epsilon_{j}\rangle}x_{i,j}

\displaystyle x_{i,j}\cdot q^{\lambda}=q^{\langle\lambda,\epsilon_{i}\rangle}x_{i,j}

(2.16.b)

\displaystyle e_{k}\cdot x_{i,j}=\delta_{j,k+1}x_{i,j-1}

\displaystyle x_{i,j}\cdot e_{k}=\delta_{i,k}x_{i+1,j},

(2.16.c)

\displaystyle f_{k}\cdot x_{i,j}=\delta_{j,k}x_{i,j+1}

\displaystyle x_{i,j}\cdot f_{k}=\delta_{i,k+1}x_{i-1,j}.

If
a\in\mathcal{U}_{q}(gl_{n})
such that
\Delta(a)=\sum a_{i}^{1}\otimes a_{i}^{2}
, we have

(2.17)

\displaystyle a\cdot(\psi\varphi)=\sum_{i}(a_{i}^{1}\cdot\psi)(a_{i}^{2}\cdot\varphi)\quad\text{ and }\quad(\psi\varphi)\cdot a=\sum_{i}(\psi\cdot a_{i}^{1})(\varphi\cdot a_{i}^{2})

due to (
2.13.a
). With (
2.17
), we find that

(2.18.a)

\displaystyle e_{k}\cdot\xi_{J}^{I}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\xi_{J_{k+1\rightarrow k}}^{I}&\text{if }k\notin J\text{ and }k+1\in J\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(2.18.b)

\displaystyle f_{k}\cdot\xi_{J}^{I}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\xi_{J_{k\rightarrow k+1}}^{I}&\text{if }k+1\notin J\text{ and }k\in J\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

where
J_{k+1\rightarrow k}
(
J_{k\rightarrow k+1}
) is the set obtained by removing
k+1
(
k
) from
J
and adding
k
(
k+1
) to
J
.

2.3.
Irreducible co-representations and the Peter-Weyl decomposition

A quantum subgroup of a quantum group
G
is a quantum group
K
endowed with a surjective homomorphism
\pi_{G}:G\rightarrow K
of Hopf algebras.

Define the diagonal subgroup
\mathcal{O}(D_{n})
of
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))

2.4.
The Haar state and the method of Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi

The Haar state

is the unique linear functional satisfying the translation invariant property

(2.56)

\displaystyle(id\otimes h)\circ\Delta(x)=h(x)\cdot 1=(h\otimes id)\circ\Delta(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))

where

is the multiplicative unit of
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
. Noumi, Yamada, and Mimachi showed that

is actually the projection of
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
onto the trivial
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
co-module
W((0,\cdots,0))=\mathbb{C}
whose matrix coefficient is
1\in\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
. Hence, if we take the limit
q\rightarrow 1
of the Haar state
h
on
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
, we get the Haar state on
\mathcal{O}(U_{1}(n))
which is the integration with respect to the normalized Haar measure on the unitary group
U(n)
. Moreover, shown by Reshetikhin and Yakimov
[
reshetikhin2001quantum
]
, we can write the Haar state on the quantized algebra of coordinate functions on the compact real form of a simply connected Lie group as an integral. In their expression, the Haar state is an integral over the maximal torus of the compact real form and the integrand is the trace of an operator on an infinite dimensional space.

Recall the action of

on

2.15.a

2.15.b

\displaystyle h(a\cdot\varphi)

\displaystyle=(a,(h\otimes id)\circ\Delta(\varphi))=(a,h(\varphi)\cdot 1)=h(\varphi)\varepsilon_{\mathcal{U}_{q}}(a)

(2.37.b)

\displaystyle h(\varphi\cdot a)

\displaystyle=(a,(id\otimes h)\circ\Delta(\varphi))=(a,h(\varphi)\cdot 1)=h(\varphi)\varepsilon_{\mathcal{U}_{q}}(a)

On the compact real form
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))
(

(2.58)

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{L}=h(\varphi^{*}\psi)\text{ and }\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{R}=h(\varphi\psi^{*})

and showed matrix coefficients of different dominant integral weights are orthogonal with respect to these two Hermitian forms. They also gave an explicit description of the modular property of the between the two Hermitian forms. Define the
modular automorphism

as

(2.59)

\displaystyle\rho(x_{i,j})=q^{2n+2-2i-2j}\cdot x_{i,j}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(det_{q}^{-1})=det_{q}^{-1}.

Theorem 2.4

(NYM)
.

\displaystyle h(\varphi\psi)=h(\rho(\psi)\varphi)\text{ for all }\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n)).

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{L}=\langle\rho(\psi),\varphi\rangle_{R}\text{ for all }\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n)).

m

, we make the following remark on the co-multiplication of a monomial. If
x=\prod_{k\in K}x_{i_{k},j_{k}}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
is a monomial, then

(3.10)

\displaystyle\Delta(x\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\in K}\left(\sum_{l_{k}=1}^{n}x_{i_{k},l_{k}}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\otimes x_{l_{k},j_{k}}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}z_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\otimes y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}.

where the summation rums over all tensor products
z_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\otimes y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
satisfying the
Order Restriction
of co-multiplication:

i)

the
k
-th generator of the left component
z_{i}
is in the
i_{k}
-th row

ii)

the
k
-th generator of the right component
y_{i}
is in the
j_{k}
-th column

iii)

The column index of the
l
-th generator in
z_{i}
equals to the row index of the
l
-th generator in
y_{i}
.

As a direct consequence of the order restriction of co-multiplication, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
z_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\otimes y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
be a tensor product appearing in (
3.10
). Then, we have

(3.11)

\alpha(x)=\alpha(z_{i}),\beta(z_{i})=\alpha(y_{i}),\beta(y_{i})=\beta(x).

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
x\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
be a monomial of order
m
. We have

(3.12)

(id\otimes h)\Delta(x\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})=\sum_{i\in I}h(y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})z_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}

where
I
is a finite set consisting of those
i
’s such that
h(y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})\neq 0
. For every
i\in I
,
y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
and
z_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
are monomials of order
m
.

Remark
.

Similar result is true for the left translation
(h\otimes id)\Delta(x\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})
.

Proof.

Assume that
\Delta(x)\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
is in the same form as in (
3.10
). Then, we have

(3.13)

(id\otimes h)\Delta(x\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})=\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}h(y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})z_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}.

By Lemma
3.7
we must have
\beta(y_{i})=\beta(x)=m\cdot\vec{1}
and
\alpha(z_{i})=\alpha(x)=m\cdot\vec{1}
. If
h(y_{i})\neq 0
, then by Theorem
3.4

\alpha(y_{i})=\beta(y_{i})=m\cdot\vec{1}
. By Lemma
3.7
again,
\beta(z_{i})=\alpha(y_{i})=m\cdot\vec{1}
. Hence,
h(y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})\neq 0
implies that both
y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
and
z_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
are monomials of order
m
. Let
I
be the subset of
I^{\prime}
consisting of those indices
i
such that
h(y_{i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})\neq 0
, then we get (
3.12
) in the described form.
∎

Fix a set of psudo-basis of order
m
,
P_{m}
, and an
x_{M}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\in P_{m}
. Combining Proposition
3.5
with Proposition
3.8
, we get

(3.14)

\displaystyle(id\otimes h)\Delta(x_{M}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})=\sum_{L}\left(\sum_{K}c^{M}_{K,L}h(x_{K}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})\right)\cdot x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}

where
x_{K}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
and
x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
run over all psudo-basis of order
m
in
P_{m}
. On the other hand, notice that
D_{q}^{m}
is a summation of monomials of order
m
. Hence, we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle 1=D_{q}^{m}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}=\sum_{L}b_{L}\cdot x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}

where
x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
run over all psudo-basis of order
m
in
P_{m}
. Substituting (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) into the translation invariance property
(id\otimes h)\Delta(x_{M}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})=h(x_{M}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})\cdot 1
, we get

(3.16)

\begin{split}&\sum_{L}\left(\sum_{K}c^{M}_{K,L}h(x_{K}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})\right)\cdot x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}\\
&=\sum_{L}b_{L}h(x_{M}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})\cdot x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}.\end{split}

By comparing the coefficients of each
x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
, we get

(3.17)

\begin{split}&\sum_{K}c^{M}_{K,L}h(x_{K}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})=b_{L}h(x_{M}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})\\
\Longleftrightarrow&\sum_{K}c^{M}_{K,L}h(x_{K}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})-b_{L}h(x_{M}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m})=0.\end{split}

We will say (
3.17
) is a linear relation derived from
equation basis

x_{M}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
and
comparing basis

x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}
. Notice that the equation basis and the comparing basis in (
3.17
) could come from two different sets of psudo-basis of order
m
. The total number of linear relations in the form of (
3.17
) is
|B_{n}(m)|^{2}
.

Our aim is to construct a linear system consisting of the Haar states of psudo-basis of order
m
. This will be a linear system with
|B_{n}(m)|
unknowns. All linear relations in the form of (
3.17
) have zero right-hand-side. Hence, we need a linear relation with non-zero right-hand-side. Evaluating the Haar state on both sides of (

(3.18)

\displaystyle 1=h(1)=\sum_{L}b_{L}h(x_{L}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}).

Thus, we can pick linear relation (

3.3.
Linear system of order
1

\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))

In this subsection we will pick a set of psudo-basis of order
1
and construct a linear system of order
1
to find the Haar state values of our chosen psudo-basis vectors.

B_{n}(1)
is just the set of all
n\times n
permutation matrices and there is an one-to-one correspondence between
B_{n}(1)
and the permutation group on
n
letters
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Hence, we will use permutations
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
to denote those matrices in
B_{n}(1)
. The set of psudo-basis of order
1
we pick are in the form of

(3.19)

\displaystyle x_{\sigma}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma(i)}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}.

With this choice we have

(3.20.a)

\displaystyle 1=D_{q}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\tau)}\cdot x_{\tau}.

(3.20.b)

\displaystyle 1=h(D_{q}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1})=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\tau)}h(x_{\tau})

Taking the right translation operation of our psudo-basis, we get

(3.21)

\begin{split}(id\otimes h)\Delta(x_{\sigma})&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}h\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{\tau(i),\sigma(i)}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\tau(i)}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\\
&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}h\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{\tau(i),\sigma(i)}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\right)\cdot x_{\tau}\end{split}

due to Proposition
3.8
and the order restriction of the comultiplication.

Denote
\sigma_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as the permutation such that
\sigma_{0}(i)=n+1-i
. for those
\sigma\neq\sigma_{0}
, we denote
\sigma_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as the permutation such that
\sigma_{1}(i)=n+1-\sigma(i)
. To pick the
|B_{n}(1)|-1
many linear relations in the form of (
3.17
), consider the linear relation derived from equation basis
x_{\sigma}
and comparing basis
x_{\sigma_{1}}
for all
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\ni\sigma\neq\sigma_{0}
. We have

(3.22)

\displaystyle h\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{\sigma_{1}(i),\sigma(i)}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}\right)=(-q)^{l(\sigma_{1})}h(x_{\sigma}).

Notice that
x_{\sigma_{1}(i),\sigma(i)}=x_{n+1-\sigma(i),\sigma(i)}
(
1\leq i\leq n
) is located on the reverse diagonal of the generator matrix (
2.0
). Hence, they commute with each other and we have

(3.23)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{\sigma_{1}(i),\sigma(i)}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,n+1-i}\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-1}=x_{\sigma_{0}}.

Since
l(\sigma_{1})=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-l(\sigma)
and
l(\sigma_{0})=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
, (
3.22
) becomes

(3.24)

\displaystyle h(x_{\sigma_{0}})=(-q)^{l(\sigma_{0})-l(\sigma)}h(x_{\sigma}).

Substituting (
3.24
) into (
3.20.b
), we get

(3.25.a)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{2l(\sigma)-l(\sigma_{0}))}\right)h(x_{\sigma_{0}})=1

(3.25.b)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle h(x_{\sigma_{0}})=\frac{(-q)^{l(\sigma_{0})}}{\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{2l(\sigma)}}.

Substituting (
3.25.b
) into (
3.24
), we get

(3.26)

\displaystyle h(x_{\sigma})=\frac{(-q)^{l(\sigma)}}{\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{2l(\sigma)}}

for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. let
I_{n}(k)
be the number of permutations on
n
letters with
k
inversions. The denominator in (
3.26
) can be written as

(3.27)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{2l(\sigma)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}I_{n}(k)q^{2k}.

By Andrews
[
andrews1998theory
]
, the generating function of
I_{n}(k)
is

(3.28)

\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}I_{n}(k)x^{k}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1-x^{j}}{1-x}.

Denote
[n]_{q}=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}
. The denominator of (
3.26
) can be rewritten as

(3.29)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{2l(\sigma)}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{2j}}{1-q^{2}}=[n]_{q^{2}}!,

and we get

(3.30)

h(x_{\sigma})=\frac{(-q)^{l(\sigma)}}{[n]_{q^{2}}!}

for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

3.4.
Construction of the Source matrix on
\mathcal{O}(U_{q}(n))

For large

m
the enumeration of matrices in
B_{n}(m)
becomes a primary issue of constructing higher order linear systems. The number of matrices in

is known by Stein and Stein
[

[

. However, their results are obtained by the method of generating function and cannot be used to enumerate matrices in

\displaystyle R_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&m\\

\vdots&\vdots&\iddots&\vdots&\vdots\\

m&0&\cdots&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in B_{n}(m).

In this sense, the Haar state of psudo-basis vector

(
=h(x^{m}_{\sigma_{0}})

in Proposition

In the following we describe the construction of the

We start with introducing the unknowns of the Source matrix of order

corresponds to

, denoted as

(3.32)

\displaystyle x_{m}^{\sigma}=x_{\sigma}\cdot x_{\sigma_{0}}^{m-1}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x^{m-1}_{i,n+1-i}\right)\cdot\text{det}_{q}^{-m}.

The Haar states of these

’s will be the unknowns of the Source matrix of order
m

The linear relations between

3.17

2.59

x_{\sigma_{0}}^{m}

3.5.
Two value-preserving (anti)automorphisms on

In this subsection we introduce two (anti)automorphisms that preserve the Haar state values of elements in

Define the
diagonal flip automorphism

\gamma:\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
by

(3.54)

\displaystyle\gamma(x_{i,j})=x_{j,i}.

Lemma 3.10
.

The following statements are true.

1

\gamma(D_{q})=D_{q}
, and

2

\Delta\circ\gamma=\iota\circ(\gamma\otimes\gamma)\circ\Delta
where
\iota
is the map that flips the left and right component of a tensor product.

Proof.

1.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We have

(3.55)

\begin{split}\gamma(D_{q})&=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\sigma)}\gamma\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\sigma)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{\sigma(i),i}=D_{q}.\end{split}

2.

It suffices to verify the relation on generators of
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
. We have

(3.56)

\begin{split}\Delta\circ\gamma(x_{i,j})&=\Delta(x_{j,i})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{j,k}\otimes x_{k,i}=\tau\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k,i}\otimes x_{j,k}\right)\\
=&\tau\circ(\gamma\otimes\gamma)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{i,k}\otimes x_{k,j}\right)=\tau\circ(\gamma\otimes\gamma)\circ\Delta(x_{i,j}).\end{split}

∎

By Lemma (
3.10
.1), we can extend
\gamma
onto
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
by

(3.57)

\displaystyle\gamma(\text{det}_{q}^{-1})=\text{det}_{q}^{-1}.

Define the
double flip anti-automorphism

\omega:\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
by

(3.58)

\displaystyle\omega(x_{i,j})=x_{n+1-i,n+1-j}.

Lemma 3.11
.

The following statements are true.

1

\omega(D_{q})=D_{q}
, and

2

\Delta\circ\omega=(\omega\otimes\omega)\circ\Delta
.

Proof.

1

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We have

(3.59)

\begin{split}\omega\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)&=\prod_{i=n}^{1}x_{n+1-i,n+1-\sigma(i)}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j,n+1-\sigma(n+1-j)}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j,\tau(j)}.\end{split}

where
\tau=\sigma_{0}\sigma\sigma_{0}
. It is easy to check that
l(\tau)=l(\sigma)
. We have

(3.60)

\begin{split}\omega(D_{q})&=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\sigma)}\omega\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\sigma)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma_{0}\sigma\sigma_{0}(i)}\\
&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-q)^{l(\tau)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\tau(i)}=D_{q}\\
\end{split}

2

It suffices to verify the relation on generators of
\mathcal{O}(Mat_{q}(n))
. We have

(3.61)

\begin{split}\Delta\circ\omega(x_{i,j})&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{n+1-i,k}\otimes x_{k,n+1-j}\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{n+1-i,n+1-k}\otimes x_{n+1-k,n+1-j}\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega(x_{i,k})\otimes\omega(x_{k,j})=(\omega\otimes\omega)\circ\Delta(x_{i,j}).\end{split}

∎

By Lemma (
3.11
.1), we can extend

onto

by

(3.62)

\displaystyle\omega(\text{det}_{q}^{-1})=\text{det}_{q}^{-1}.

Theorem 3.12
.

On
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
, the following statements are true.

1

h\circ\gamma
is identical with the Haar state
h
.

2

h\circ\omega
is identical with the Haar state
h
.

Proof.

By the uniqueness of the Haar state, it suffices to show that
h\circ\gamma
and
h\circ\omega
are states on
\mathcal{O}(GL_{q}(n))
and possess the translation invariant property. In the proof, we will write
\Delta(x)=\sum x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}
. Notice that
\gamma^{2}=id
and
\gamma(1)=1
, and
\omega^{2}=id
and
\omega(1)=1
.

1

By the right translation invariant property of
h
, we have

(3.63)

\begin{split}h(\gamma(x))\cdot 1=&(id\otimes h)\circ\Delta\circ(\gamma(x))\\
=&(id\otimes h)\circ\iota\circ(\gamma\otimes\gamma)\circ\Delta(x)\\
=&(h\otimes id)\circ(\gamma\otimes\gamma)\circ\Delta(x)\\
=&\sum h(\gamma(x_{(1)}))\cdot\gamma(x_{(2)}).\end{split}

Applying
\gamma
on both sides of (
3.63
), we get

(3.64)

\displaystyle h(\gamma(x))\cdot\gamma(1)=\sum h(\gamma(x_{(1)}))\cdot\gamma^{2}(x_{(2)})=\sum h(\gamma(x_{(1)}))\cdot x_{(2)}.

Since
\gamma(1)=1
, (
3.64
) implies that

(3.65)

\displaystyle(h\circ\gamma(x))\cdot 1=((h\circ\gamma)\otimes id)\circ\Delta(x)

which means that
h\circ\gamma
possess the left translation invariant property. Similarly, the left translation invariant property of
h
implies the right translation invariant property of
h\circ\gamma
. We also have
h\circ\gamma(1)=h(1)=1
. Hence, the uniqueness of
h
implies that
h\circ\gamma=h
.

2

By the right translation invariant property of
h
, we have

(3.66)

\begin{split}h(\omega(x))\cdot 1=&(id\otimes h)\circ\Delta\circ(\omega(x))\\
=&(id\otimes h)\circ(\omega\otimes\omega)\circ\Delta(x)\\
=&\sum h(\omega(x_{(2)}))\cdot\omega(x_{(1)}).\end{split}

Applying
\omega
on both sides of (
3.66
), we conclude that

(3.67)

\begin{split}h\circ\omega(x)\cdot 1&=h(\omega(x))\cdot\omega(1)=\sum h(\omega(x_{(2)}))\cdot\omega^{2}(x_{(1)})\\
&=\sum h(\omega(x_{(2)}))\cdot x_{(1)}=(id\otimes h\circ\omega)\circ\Delta(x).\end{split}

Hence, the right translation invariant property of
h
implies that of
h\circ\omega
. The left translation invariant property of
h\circ\omega
is proved similarly. We also have
h\circ\omega(1)=h(1)=1
. Hence, the uniqueness of
h
implies that
h\circ\omega=h
.

∎
