A robust LightGBM model for concrete tensile strength forecast to aid in resilience-based structure strategies
Authors: Chukwuemeka Daniel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tensile strength (TS) of concrete is a factor in the design of civil engineering structures. Traditional methods for assessing concrete TS have notable limitations, leading to the emergence of non-destructive testing (NDT) as an innovative alternative. This study introduces a novel, optimized approach for accurate, non-destructive TS prediction by integrating Gradio, Bayesian optimization (BO), and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP). The input parameters for the model were ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) and electrical resistivity (ER). Using a comprehensive dataset of 3460 datapoints, a light gradient-boosting machine (LightGBM) model was developed. Through extensive hyperparameter tuning facilitated by BO, the model achieved a robust prediction accuracy of approximately 98 %. The novel application of SHAP in this study provided deep insights into model behavior, confirming UPV as the most critical variable for TS prediction. The deployment of the model using Gradio further enhances its accessibility and usability. This combined methodology not only advances the application of machine learning (ML) in civil engineering but also offers a powerful, globally applicable tool for accurately forecasting concrete TS. This supports resilience-based management strategies, essential for the sustainable development of infrastructure. The study's novel integration of BO for optimization and SHAP for interpretability marks a significant step forward in the precise prediction of TS, offering practical benefits for both researchers and industry professionals.Keywords: LightGBM, SHAP, Non-destructive tests, Tensile strength

Introduction

Concrete has been widely used in construction from ancient times to the present. The mechanical properties of concrete play a key role in the analysis and design of concrete structures [[1], [2], [3]]. These properties include flexural strength (FS), compressive strength (CS), static modulus of elasticity (MoE), and tensile strength (TS). Of these, tensile and compressive strength are particularly important for evaluating the structural integrity of concrete. CS is often a critical factor in the design specifications of concrete frameworks, while TS is essential for assessing a material's resistance to cracking [4,5]. Additionally, the performance of reinforced concrete (RC) structures is significantly influenced by the TS of the concrete. That is, the TS of concrete regulates the flexural reinforcement and deflection in RC constructions [6,7].

Given the importance of TS, researchers have increasingly turned to machine learning (ML) for estimating this property. ML has gained attention due to its potential for developing more cost-effective, efficient, and accurate methods [[8], [9], [10]]. Traditional methods for measuring TS are labor-intensive, time-consuming, destructive, and result in material waste. In contrast, ML-based TS predictions are faster, more sustainable, and less cumbersome than conventional approaches.

For example, Yan et al. [11] successfully employed ML to predict TS from CS, which spurred further interest in using ML for TS estimation. Subsequently, Pakzad et al. [12] used ML to predict the TS of steel fiber-reinforced concrete. ML has also been applied to predict the TS of concrete containing recycled aggregate [[13], [14], [15]], silica fume [16], basalt fiber [17], manufactured sand [18], and bacterial cells [19]. All these studies reported reliable accuracy in their ML models.

However, many current models depend heavily on the concrete's history (workability, curing conditions, age, cement content, water-cement ratio, sand and gravel composition, mechanical mixtures, chemical additives, reinforcement, etc.) and calibration. As a result, their application for in-situ testing is limited [20]. To reduce reliance on destructive testing methods, research is increasingly focused on developing accurate non-destructive testing (NDT) predictive models for concrete composites. NDTs are preferred because they avoid destructive sampling and can be conducted quickly and easily [21]. Furthermore, the increasing requirement to evaluate damaged concrete structures has led to the rise in the popularity of NDT. Additionally, the growing need to assess damaged concrete structures has driven the increased popularity of NDTs. However, few studies have reported on predicting TS based on non-destructive concrete test results.

ML models are vital for providing optimal solutions and revealing the complex interrelationships between variables. Despite the effectiveness and widespread use of the light gradient-boosting machine (LightGBM) in other sectors, it has rarely been applied to forecast the TS of concrete using NDT parameter [22,23]. Similarly, although Bayesian optimization (BO) has proven to be a successful optimization technique in related civil engineering fields, it has seldom been applied in TS prediction modeling. Moreover, while Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) have become valuable tools for interpreting ML models, they are not commonly used in NDT-based TS predictions. Lastly, the use of graphical user interfaces (GUIs) to facilitate the application and evaluation of models remains uncommon in this field.

To address these gaps, this work proposes a novel methodology for predicting the TS of concrete through NDTs, while also including BO and SHAP. The integration of SHAP enables a deeper understanding of the model's decision-making process. Additionally, the development of a globally accessible GUI ensures that the model can be widely used, thoroughly evaluated, and applied in construction projects. This research advances the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in civil engineering.

Information about the dataset

The dataset used in this research is the proprietary ConcreteXAI dataset, sourced from Ref. [24]. The concrete samples in the dataset were characterized by a variety of properties. First, the dataset included three types of cement commonly used in construction: CPC 30R, CPC 40R, and CPO 30R RS BRA. Second, the cement brands represented in the dataset were Apasco and CEMEX. Third, several additives were used in the concrete mixtures, including 10 % blast furnace slag, fluidizer, no additions, optuntia ficus indica, and starch-based fluidizer. Fourth, the concrete samples had target compressive strengths of 25 MPa, 28 MPa, and 35 MPa. Lastly, the curing periods varied, with samples tested at 3, 7, 14, 28, 40, 60, 90, and 120 days. These characteristics provide a diverse set of concrete samples.

This dataset comprises results from tensile strength (TS), electrical resistivity (ER), and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) tests. The objective of this research was to develop a TS prediction model that could be easily applied using only two non-destructive parameters. Therefore, UPV and ER were chosen as predictors for TS, resulting in 3460 datapoints.

TS refers to the capacity of concrete to withstand tensile forces. While concrete is strong in compression, its tensile strength is comparatively weak. Understanding TS is essential for structural design, especially in situations where concrete elements are subjected to tensile stress. TS can be measured using direct tension tests, split tensile tests, or flexural strength tests. Although lower than compressive strength, TS plays a role in ensuring the structural integrity and durability of concrete structures.

UPV is a non-destructive testing method used to evaluate the quality and integrity of concrete by measuring the velocity of an ultrasonic pulse passing through the material. In typical laboratory conditions, transducers are placed on opposite sides of the concrete specimen. The time it takes for the pulse to travel from the transmitting to the receiving transducer is recorded, and the UPV is calculated. This method enables continuous monitoring of concrete structures without causing damage, contributing to their safety and longevity.

ER measures a material's ability to resist the flow of electrical current and provides insights into the microstructural properties of concrete. ER is measured using a resistivity meter with electrodes placed on the concrete surface, and the potential difference between the electrodes is recorded to calculate the ER. The application of ER in monitoring concrete structures enhances resilience, sustainability, and cost-efficiency in urban infrastructure.

Light gradient-boosting machine

Light gradient-boosting machine (LightGBM) is a gradient-boosting framework that utilizes tree-based learning algorithms [25]. LightGBM grows trees leaf-wise instead of level-wise, prioritizing the most significant splits first, which leads to improved accuracy [26]. It efficiently processes large datasets by converting continuous values into discrete bins and supports categorical features natively, eliminating the need for one-hot encoding [23]. Additionally, LightGBM allows for parallel and distributed training, making it highly scalable for extensive datasets [25].

Given its ability to handle large datasets and detect complex patterns, LightGBM is particularly well-suited for accurately predicting TS. Its application in forecasting the TS of concrete represents a significant breakthrough in enhancing the resilience of cities and infrastructure. By facilitating early detection of potential issues LightGBM promotes the sustainability and durability of urban structures.

Bayesian Optimization

Bayesian Optimization (BO) is a highly efficient method for finding the minimum (or maximum) of an objective function that is costly to evaluate [27]. It is especially valuable for optimizing hyperparameters in ML models. BO builds a surrogate model, typically a Gaussian Process, to approximate the objective function [28]. This surrogate model, guided by an acquisition function, selects the most promising hyperparameters to evaluate in the next iteration.

BO can manage both continuous and discrete hyperparameters as well as their interactions, and it is robust to noise in the objective function evaluations. By effectively locating the global optimum of complex functions, BO significantly enhances model performance [23,29,30].

Hyperparameter optimization process

Selecting the right hyperparameters is critical for constructing a high-performing LightGBM model. To improve the model's accuracy and robustness, BO was employed to optimize key hyperparameters, including max_bin, learning_rate, num_trees, and num_leaves.

The BO process begins by evaluating a small initial set of observations and fitting a surrogate model to these data points. The acquisition function is then optimized to choose the next evaluation point, where the objective function is reassessed. This new observation is added to the surrogate model, and the process is repeated until convergence or a predefined number of iterations is completed. The optimal hyperparameter configuration is determined by finding the combination that yields the best performance metric.

Shapley additive exPlanations

Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) enable researchers to illustrate the relationships between input parameters and model output [17,32,33]. SHAP promotes transparent and accountable AI systems by ensuring that decisions are comprehensible and subject to scrutiny. It provides a theoretical explanation of ML model outputs by integrating classical Shapley values from game theory with related extensions [34,35]. SHAP was employed in this research to elucidate the contributions of various variables.

Graphical user interface

A graphical user interface (GUI) was developed to predict TS using ER and UPV. Created with Gradio and hosted on Hugging Face, the interface allows users to input ER and UPV values and receive immediate TS predictions. This enhances the accessibility and practical application of the predictive model, supporting resilience-based management.

Gradio is an open-source Python library that simplifies the creation of ML model interfaces. It provides an intuitive platform for deploying models as web applications, allowing users to interact with the model without extensive programming knowledge.

Hugging Face is a prominent platform for sharing ML applications and models. Hosting the GUI on Hugging Face makes it publicly accessible, enabling practitioners, engineers, and researchers to use the analytical tool easily. Users can input values, click “Submit,” and instantly receive TS predictions from the pre-trained model, offering rapid insights.

Conclusions and limitations of the study

Conclusions

To advance resilience-based management techniques, this study introduces a novel approach for accurately forecasting concrete tensile strength (TS) by integrating SHAP with Bayesian optimization (BO). These advanced methods were combined with LightGBM to develop a prediction model. The model utilized only two input variables: ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) and electrical resistivity (ER). The utilized dataset used in this research encompasses a wide range of concrete samples with varying cement types (CPC 30R, CPC 40R, and CPO 30R RS BRA), brands (Apasco and CEMEX), additives (including blast furnace slag, fluidizers, and optuntia ficus indica), target compressive strengths (25 MPa, 28 MPa, and 35 MPa), and curing periods (ranging from 3 to 120 days). Analysis of this diverse and substantial dataset comprising 3460 samples demonstrated that the BO-LightGBM model produced innovative and validated results. The BO-LightGBM model achieved an impressive 99 % accuracy in training and 98 % accuracy in testing. SHAP analysis highlighted the crucial role of UPV in predicting TS. Furthermore, the significant positive contributions of both UPV and ER underscore their importance in accurately forecasting concrete TS. To enhance accessibility, a user-friendly graphical user interface (GUI) was developed. The GUI-based analysis confirmed the effectiveness of the LightGBM model in predicting concrete TS.

Limitations and future research

This study enhances our understanding of TS predictors and sets a new benchmark for using LightGBM in concrete strength prediction. However, despite the dataset's size of 3460 datapoints, it only represents a fraction of the diverse concrete conditions and compositions. To advance our knowledge in concrete strength prediction, future research should focus on expanding the range of predictive variables. Moreover, while the LightGBM model demonstrated considerable strengths, the study did not explore the potential advantages of other machine learning models, which might offer additional insights or performance improvements. Therefore, future research should consider comparing different models to uncover these potential benefits.
