Towards Optimal Control and Algorithmic Structure of Decompression Schedules
Benjamin Marsh

Abstract
We formalise decompression planning as an optimal control problem with gas feasibility windows (ppO 2 , END), affine ceilings, and convex penalties in normalised oversaturation. We prove existence, a monotone no re-descent structure and bang-bang ascents under a mild monotonicity assumption on inert fraction, and establish dwell time KKT conditions. We give pseudo-polynomial DP and label-setting algorithms with a priori error bounds, derive Lipschitz regularity of the online value function, and discuss multi-species extensions. The efficient frontier is continuous and generally nonconvex. We provide the first formal existence and bang-bang structure proof under mixed gas feasibility windows.

1
Introduction

A decompression plan specifies how a diver should ascend from depth, possibly pausing at discrete stops and switching gases, so as to control risk while minimising wasted time by minimising total decompression time. Classical models, from Haldane’s staged decompression and Workman’s linear “
M
-values” to Buhlmann’s ZH-series and Thalmann’s VVal-18, idealise tissues as first-order perfusion compartments that approach an inspired inert partial pressure and impose affine depth-dependent ceilings on tolerated supersaturation
[
BDH08
,
Wor65
,
Büh83
,
USN16
]
. Practical implementation overlays additional feasibility constraints: limits on oxygen partial pressure (ppO
2
) to avoid toxicity, and bounds on the “equivalent narcotic depth” (END) that depend on whether oxygen is treated as narcotic, both interpretations appearing in agency guidance
[
USN08
,
USN16
,
NOA17
]
.

We formalise decompression planning as an optimal control problem
[
Lib12
]
defined on continuous depth trajectories
z(\cdot)
and measurable gas selections
g(\cdot)
. The model incorporates ppO
2
and END feasibility, affine supersaturation ceilings of the form
M_{i}(z)=a_{i}+b_{i}P_{a}(z)
, and convex penalties in normalised oversaturation. Ambient pressure is given by
P_{a}(z)=P_{0}+\gamma z
, and inspired partial pressures are offset by an alveolar water-vapour term
w
. Within this framework we derive several structural and computational results that underlie empirical decompression practice. We first show that an optimal solution exists in a relaxed gas class, and that, up to
\varepsilon
-optimality, one may restrict attention to profiles comprising finitely many constant-depth holds separated by maximal-rate ascents and piecewise-constant gas choices. Under a mild monotonicity assumption on inert fraction along the ascent, the optimal depth trajectory is itself monotone, with bang–bang vertical rates between the holds.

At fixed discrete stop depths, the problem of determining optimal dwell times is locally Lipschitz and directionally differentiable when the tissue risk functions
\phi_{i}
are piecewise linear. We derive nonsmooth Karush–Kuhn–Tucker
[
Bet10
,
Cla90
]
conditions that equalise marginal decompression benefit across active stops. On a finite stop grid and discretised risk scale, the problem admits a pseudo-polynomial dynamic programming formulation for the risk-capped version, and a label-setting algorithm for the Lagrangian scalarisation, both accompanied by explicit
O(\Delta z+\delta)
a priori discretisation error bounds. For the continuous free-terminal-time problem we show that the value function
V_{\lambda}(z,P)
is locally Lipschitz in both depth and tissue pressure state, and we give a one-step dynamic programming principle with measurable
\varepsilon
-selectors. Finally, we prove that the time-minimisation problem under a fixed risk cap is weakly NP-hard, even for a single inert gas species and affine supersaturation ceilings, by providing a corrected reduction from the 0–1 knapsack problem that resolves sign inconsistencies in prior informal arguments
[
GJ79
]
.

The efficient frontier between total decompression time
T
and accumulated risk
R
is continuous but typically nonconvex, a property that persists even when discretisation arises solely from the finite gas alphabet. Scalarisations of the form
T+\lambda R
therefore trace the frontier in piecewise smooth segments. The purpose of the analysis is not to prescribe operational limits, but to make explicit the underlying optimisation structure that unifies traditional table-based and modern algorithmic decompression models such as Buhlmann with gradient factors
[
Pol15
,
Bak08
]
.

Background and related work

Haldane’s staged‑decompression hypothesis is the historical starting point
[
BDH08
]
. Workman formalised tolerated supersaturation as linear “
M
‑values” in ambient pressure, leading to affine ceilings
M(z)=a+b\,P_{a}(z)
still used today
[
Wor65
]
. Bühlmann extended and calibrated multi‑compartment perfusion models (ZH‑L12/16) with species‑specific parameters and public documentation that seeded modern dive computers
[
Büh83
]
. For segments with constant rate of depth change, numerical implementations typically use the Schreiner ODE solution for compartments under a ramp in inspired pressure
[
HT03
,
SK71
]
. Thalmann’s VVal‑18 algorithm underpins current U.S. Navy air and mixed‑gas tables
[
Tha86
,
GD07
,
USN16
]
.

Decompression schedules are constrained by ppO
2
limits and, when mandated, by narcotic exposure caps. END is computed by equating the air narcotic partial pressure to that of the chosen gas, with the common pragmatic choice in U.S. operational guidance to treat
O_{2}
as equally narcotic to
N_{2}
for calculation, though some training agencies adopt non‑narcotic
O_{2}

[
USN08
]
. Inspired gas partial pressures are routinely adjusted for alveolar water vapour (about 47 mmHg at body temperature), which is the origin of the constant offset
w
in our model.

Label setting and label correcting resource constrained shortest‑path schemes, dominance rules in compartment states, and Lagrangian scalarisations are widely used in dive software; gradient factors (Baker) temper Buhlmann ceilings via a linear interpolation in overpressure gradient and are ubiquitous in dive computers
[
Pol15
]
. Our algorithms formalise these ideas within an optimal‑control/DPP frame and provide explicit discretisation error constants.

Empirical and workshop literature has repeatedly revisited stop placement and “deep stops”, the conclusion is that there is no single convex objective hidden underneath trade‑offs are model and context dependent
[
BWM08
,
GD07
]
.

The analysis below does not claim a hidden universal objective, it exposes which heuristics are provably right under a transparent model and where they can break. In particular, the model cleanly separates feasibility (ppO
2
, END) from optimisation (time risk trade), which helps explain why gradient factor tempering behaves like a scalarisation and why different gases can induce nonconvex frontiers even when the physics per compartment is linear.

Lemma 3.10

Structural results

We first give a strengthened exchange inequality that implies
w.l.o.g. monotonicity
.

4.1
Two-segment exchange and no re-descents

Assumption 4.1

(Monotone inert fraction along ascent)
.

Either (i) there is a single inert species (i.e.,
F_{I}(g)\equiv F_{I}
), or (ii) the gas policy satisfies

z_{b}>z_{a}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{I}(g_{b})\ \geq\ F_{I}(g_{a})

whenever the profile uses segments
(z_{a},g_{a})
and
(z_{b},g_{b})
consecutively. Equivalently, the
O_{2}
fraction is nondecreasing along ascent. This implies
P_{\infty}(g_{a},z_{a})\leq P_{\infty}(g_{b},z_{b})
whenever
z_{b}>z_{a}
.

This hypothesis ensures that the inspired inert pressure
P_{\infty}
is ordered with depth along the chosen gas sequence. That order is exactly what powers the exchange argument and kills re-descents. The deeper segment has both the tighter ceiling and the larger driver, so doing it first cannot hurt under a convex penalty.

If the deeper segment has the larger
P_{\infty}
and the larger ceiling, executing it first reduces the peak normalised oversaturation seen later. Convexity then makes “deep first” the safe order in a two-piece exchange.

Lemma 4.2

(Two-segment exchange)
.

Fix a compartment
i
and two constant segments
A=(z_{a},g_{a},\tau_{a})
and
B=(z_{b},g_{b},\tau_{b})
with
z_{b}>z_{a}
(deeper
B
). Assume

P_{\infty,a}:=P_{\infty}(g_{a},z_{a})\ \leq\ P_{\infty}(g_{b},z_{b})=:P_{\infty,b},\qquad M_{i}(z_{a})\ \leq\ M_{i}(z_{b}).

Let
x\mapsto C_{s}(x)=P_{\infty,s}+(x-P_{\infty,s})e^{-k_{i}\tau_{s}}
be the affine contraction for segment
s\in\{a,b\}
.
For any incoming state
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, let
S_{ab}(t)
(resp.
S_{ba}(t)
) denote the normalised oversaturation along
A
then
B
(resp.
B

). Then for any convex nondecreasing
\phi_{i}

\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}+\tau_{b}}\!\!\phi_{i}\!\big(S_{ab}(t)\big)\,dt\ \geq\ \int_{0}^{\tau_{a}+\tau_{b}}\!\!\phi_{i}\!\big(S_{ba}(t)\big)\,dt.

Proof.

Fix
i
and abbreviate
M_{a}:=M_{i}(z_{a})
,
M_{b}:=M_{i}(z_{b})
with
M_{a}\leq M_{b}
,
and
C_{s}(x)=P_{\infty,s}+(x-P_{\infty,s})e^{-k_{i}\tau_{s}}
for
s\in\{a,b\}
.
Let
X_{ab}(t)
be the tissue pressure along
A\!\to\!B
and
X_{ba}(t)
along
B\!\to\!A
, both started from
x
.
Define the normalised oversaturation on a segment by
Y_{s}(t):=(X_{s}(t)-M_{s})_{+}/M_{s}
.

On a constant segment

,
X_{s}(t)
is affine in

:

X_{s}(t)=\alpha_{s}+\beta_{s}e^{-k_{i}t}
with
\alpha_{s}=P_{\infty,s}
and
\beta_{s}=x-P_{\infty,s}
. Hence

Y_{s}(t)=\frac{(\alpha_{s}-M_{s})+\beta_{s}e^{-k_{i}t}}{M_{s}}\_+

is convex in

and nonincreasing in

. With the change of variables
\theta=1-e^{-k_{i}t}
,

\int_{0}^{\tau_{s}}\!\phi_{i}(Y_{s}(t))\,dt=\tau_{s}\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}\!\bigl(\tilde{\alpha}_{s}+\tilde{\beta}_{s}(1-\theta)\bigr)\,d\mu_{s}(\theta),

for a probability measure
\mu_{s}
on
[0,1]
and suitable
\tilde{\alpha}_{s},\tilde{\beta}_{s}
.
By convexity of
\phi_{i}
and Jensen,

\int_{0}^{\tau_{s}}\!\phi_{i}(Y_{s}(t))\,dt\ \geq\ \tau_{s}\,\phi_{i}\!\bigl(\tilde{\alpha}_{s}+\tilde{\beta}_{s}(1-\bar{\theta}_{s})\bigr)\quad\text{with }\bar{\theta}_{s}=\int\theta\,d\mu_{s}(\theta)\in(0,1).

Now compare compositions. Since
P_{\infty,a}\leq P_{\infty,b}
and
e^{-k_{i}\tau}\in(0,1)
,

C_{b}\circ C_{a}(x)-C_{a}\circ C_{b}(x)=(1-e^{-k_{i}\tau_{a}})(1-e^{-k_{i}\tau_{b}})\,(P_{\infty,b}-P_{\infty,a})\ \geq\ 0.

Thus the state entering the later shallow segment under
B\!\to\!A
is never larger than under
A\!\to\!B
, while the deeper segment is paired with the larger ceiling
M_{b}
. Since
\phi_{i}
is nondecreasing and
M_{b}\geq M_{a}
, the contribution from the deeper segment is weakly larger when executed later, and the shallower segment starts from a weakly larger state under
A\!\to\!B
. Summing the two Jensen lower bounds yields

\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}+\tau_{b}}\!\phi_{i}(S_{ab})\,dt\ \geq\ \int_{0}^{\tau_{a}+\tau_{b}}\!\phi_{i}(S_{ba})\,dt.

∎

The inequality can also be seen as a discrete Chebyshev type rearrangement by ordering the deeper segment first minimises the integral of a convex penalty in normalised oversaturation.

4.4
Dwell-time optimality and KKT equalisation

Fix a stop sequence

Theorem 4.6

(Local stationarity in dwell times)
.

Fix a stop sequence

Proof.

The nonsmooth KKT conditions are the standard necessary conditions for a local minimum of a locally Lipschitz function over a convex set
[
Cla90
]
. Writing the feasible set as the orthant

gives the stated inclusion with the normal cone

∎

Corollary 4.7

(Equalisation of marginal tradeoff at active stops)
.

Let

Let

Proposition 9.1

(Envelope and frontier slope)
.

J_{\lambda}^{\star}
is locally absolutely continuous in

\frac{d}{d\lambda}J_{\lambda}^{\star}=R_{\lambda}\quad\text{a.e.}

Moreover

\lambda

Proof.

For any feasible

is the infimum of affine functions and is thus concave and locally Lipschitz. By Danskin’s theorem for minima

\frac{d}{d\lambda}J_{\lambda}^{\star}\in\operatorname{co}\{\,R(\pi):\ \pi\in\arg\min(T+\lambda R)\,\}.

Selecting any optimal
\pi_{\lambda}

a.e., where
R_{\lambda}:=R(\pi_{\lambda})

is (weakly) nonincreasing in
\lambda

At points where

\frac{dT_{\lambda}}{dR_{\lambda}}=-\lambda

Monotonicity of

then yields

\lambda

13
Conclusion

We cast decompression planning as an optimal control problem with ppO
2
/END feasibility, affine ceilings, and convex penalties in normalised oversaturation. Within this framework the analysis recovers exactly the operational structure that experienced divers already use, and it isolates where those heuristics are provably correct and where they fail. We prove existence in a relaxed gas class and show that, up to
\varepsilon
, one may restrict attention to profiles comprising finitely many constant depth holds separated by max‑rate ascents with piecewise constant gas selections (
3.10
,
3.8
). Under a mild monotonicity of inert fraction along ascent (
4.1
), optimal profiles can be taken monotone in depth: re‑descents are dominated, and the vertical rate between holds is bang–bang, as established by a two‑segment exchange argument (
4.3
,
4.4
,
4.2
). For any fixed stop sequence, dwell times satisfy nonsmooth KKT conditions that equalise the marginal risk reduction per minute across the active stops, thereby placing the common time balancing rule (
Theorem

4.6
).

These structural results lead to practical algorithms with guarantees. On a finite stop grid we obtain a PPDP for the risk‑capped formulation and a label‑setting scheme for the scalarisation
T+\lambda R
, both equipped with explicit a‑priori discretisation error bounds of order
O(\Delta z+\delta)
, the contribution of transits to risk scales as
O(\Delta z)
(
Theorems

6.1
,
6.2
and
8.1
, and Lemma
7.4
). The online value function is locally Lipschitz in depth and in the tissue state and satisfies a one‑step dynamic programming principle with measurable
\varepsilon
‑selectors for both the scalarised and the risk‑capped problems (
Theorems

12.1
,
12.5
and
12.7
). From a complexity viewpoint, time minimisation under a risk cap is weakly NP‑hard by reduction from 0–1 knapsack, and the time risk efficient frontier, though continuous, is typically nonconvex (
7.1
). Discrete gas choices are the geometric culprit, they generate indented regions so that scalarisations such as
T+\lambda R
(or GFesque like tempering) trace only the supported portions and can miss bonafide efficient profiles, which is a feature of the frontier rather than a defect of the method. Gas effects are clarified by monotonicity statements. Lower inert fraction mixes yield larger instantaneous benefit, and increasing the alveolar offset
w
favours off‑gassing when feasibility sets are frozen, although ppO
2
/END windows themselves shift with
w
(
7.2
,
7.3
). A multi‑species N
2
/He model preserves both the structure and the algorithmic complexity claims (
10.1
).

The scope and limits of the model are explicit. We work with perfusion limited first order compartments and affine ceilings, we include no bubble dynamics, no explicit CNS/OTU oxygen‑toxicity accounting, no CO
2
or workload dependence, and we impose a depth independent ascent rate cap. The inert fraction monotonicity assumption is structurally important, without it the exchange argument can fail and re‑descents can become advantageous in edge cases. Our risk aggregation
\sum_{i}!\int\phi_{i}(S_{i})
is a mathematical proxy rather than a calibrated injury model. Gas switches are costless unless penalised, and operational per‑switch costs or limits should be enforced in practice, our existence result explains why the restriction to finitely many holds and piecewise constant gases is without loss at the level of approximation.

Extending beyond affine ceilings and linear perfusion to incorporate bubble‑mechanistic terms, variable ascent rate bounds, or state dependent ceilings would stress test the structural results. On the algorithmic side it is natural to seek tighter approximation guarantees conditions for an FPTAS or proofs of strong NP‑hardness for salient subclasses together with dominance rules that control label growth. The KKT characterisation invites a study of structural convexity regions, uniqueness, and global optimality of dwell allocations. Robust control formulations accounting for uncertainty in
(k_{i},M_{i})
and distributionally robust risk penalties would add resilience, while calibration that maps
\phi_{i}
to incidence data could connect scalarisations to gradient‑factor practice. The local regularity of the online value function points to receding‑horizon controllers with quantified suboptimality once oxygen toxicity budgets and gas‑switch costs are added to the state.

In short, within the constrained model, optimal decompression has a clean mathematical structure. Monotone ascents, saturated vertical transits, dwell times that equalise marginal benefit, and a nonconvex time risk geometry shaped by discrete gas choices. This rationalises long standing table heuristics, shows where they break, and supplies planners with algorithms and error bars.
