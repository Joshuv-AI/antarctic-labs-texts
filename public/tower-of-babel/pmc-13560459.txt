Normal cholestanol in a genetically confirmed cerebrotendious xanthomatosis case presenting as neonatal jaundice
Authors: Andrea E. DeBarber, Maya Fowler, P. Barton Duell, Eric Dybbro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) is a treatable genetic disorder associated with deficiency of the sterol 27‐hydroxylase enzyme (CYP27A1), important in bile acid synthesis. CTX may present in the newborn period as hepatic jaundice/cholestasis, that can resolve or can progress to fatal liver disease. Many cholestasis gene panel tests now include CYP27A1. For infants presenting with cholestasis that are identified to have CTX, follow‐up biochemical testing is recommended. Here, we describe a case of an infant with neonatal jaundice genetically diagnosed with CTX through the use of cholestasis gene panel testing. Follow‐up biochemical testing determined the infant had normal plasma cholestanol, inconsistent with a diagnosis of CTX according to expert guidance. We report that quantitative biochemical testing for bile acid precursors and bile alcohols provided definitive biochemical evidence of CTX and recommend that such testing is advised in infants with CYP27A1 gene analysis indicative of CTX.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX; OMIM213700) is a genetic disorder associated with deficiency of the sterol 27‐hydroxylase enzyme (CYP27A1), that is important in bile acid synthesis. The resulting lack of chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) in CTX leads to high activation of the cholesterol 7α‐hydroxylase enzyme (CYP7A1), that catalyzes the first step in neutral bile acid synthesis (Figure 1). This results in a high rate of bile acid precursor synthesis, and includes elevation of 7α‐hydroxy‐4‐cholesten‐3‐one (7αC4) and 7α,12α‐dihydroxy‐4‐cholesten‐3‐one (7α12αC4). 1 , 2 These precursors are metabolized to form cholestanol and bile alcohols, 2 the accumulation of which is thought to play a role in the progressive pathophysiology of disease. 3 CDCA has been the globally recognized standard of care for the treatment of CTX for approximately 40 years. 3 , 4 Prior to its Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval to treat adults with CTX in 2025, CDCA was considered a medical necessity for the treatment of CTX by the FDA and was used off‐label in CTX patients of all ages to repress CYP7A1 activation, decrease levels of cholestanol and bile alcohols 2 , 3 , 4 and improve clinical outcomes in patients with CTX.

Clinical signs and symptoms of untreated CTX are heterogeneous, but may include chronic diarrhea from birth, juvenile cataracts, seizures, epilepsy, developmental delay, and intellectual disability that can present in childhood and adolescence. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 As the disease progresses into adulthood, characteristic tendon xanthomas can develop and neurological dysfunction can become more prominent. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Patients can develop peripheral neuropathy, cerebellar ataxia, spastic paraparesis, and dementia. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Due to non‐specific early findings, diagnosis is often delayed until adulthood. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

In the newborn period, CTX can present with jaundice and hepatic cholestasis, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 that can resolve or can progress to fatal liver disease. In one study, CTX was determined to be the cause of severe cholestasis in eight infants, of which five died from, or were transplanted for, liver failure. 12

If the underlying cause of cholestasis can be identified as CTX shortly after birth, treatment can be initiated early in the course of disease, which has been shown to be critical for improving neurological outcomes in CTX and is recommended from diagnosis onward. 4 CTX is a candidate disorder for newborn screening that would identify CTX shortly after birth to allow early treatment. Available data on treatment of CTX affected infants with bile acids is provided in Table S1. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Initially the CDCA dose in children was advised at 15 mg/kg body weight per day (mg/kg/daily), 5 but good metabolic control has been achieved using lower doses between 5 and 10 mg/kg/daily. Note that ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), used to treat jaundice and cholestasis in infants, does not repress CYP7A1 activation, decrease levels of cholestanol or bile alcohols and is ineffective to treat CTX. 18

Next generation sequencing, capable of identifying pathogenic variants in multiple genes, can be helpful in diagnosing genetic liver disease in infants with cholestasis. Many commercially available cholestasis gene panel tests now include CYP27A1. For infants presenting with cholestasis who are genetically diagnosed with CTX, follow‐up biochemical testing is recommended. Although biallelic pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in CYP27A1 are confirmatory for CTX, genetic analyses may not always provide definitive diagnostic information (e.g., if variants of uncertain significance are found), and in these cases biochemical testing is critical for confirmation of CTX.

A recent expert consensus study suggested that genetic analyses and/or determination of serum/plasma cholestanol levels should be used for diagnostic confirmation of CTX. 4 Although expert panelists agreed that patients with CTX have elevated cholestanol at diagnosis, and that measuring serum cholestanol is the diagnostic biomarker of choice, 4 recent studies have demonstrated that some patients with clinically and genetically confirmed CTX do not have elevated plasma cholestanol. 2 Notably, CTX patients with atypically normal cholestanol levels have elevated levels of other diagnostically useful biomarkers of CTX that include bile acid precursors and bile alcohols (that are predominately present as glucuronides in the blood and urine). 2

Herein, we describe the case of an infant with neonatal jaundice who was genetically confirmed to have biallelic pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in CYP27A1, but the diagnosis of CTX was initially in question because the plasma cholestanol concentration was normal.

METHODS

Ethics statement

Routine standard of care clinical assessment, genetic/biochemical testing, and management were provided by the patient's physician during 2023–2025.

Case report

The patient was a full‐term female infant who presented at 3 months of age for evaluation of progressive neonatal jaundice for which UDCA treatment was initiated. A broad workup included a liver biopsy, which demonstrated cholestatic hepatopathy (see Figure S1).

Genetic testing using a cholestasis gene panel identified CTX as the cause of cholestasis at around 4.5 months of life with compound heterozygous variants in CYP27A1; c.1183 C > T, p.Arg395Cys, pathogenic, and c.1420 C > T, p.Arg474Trp, likely pathogenic.

Conflicting results were obtained for the follow‐up biochemical testing performed. At 3.5 months of age, qualitative urine fast atom bombardment‐mass spectrometry (FAB‐MS) showed elevation in bile alcohols, but the result report provided an interpretation of cholestasis (not a bile acid synthesis disorder) given the elevation in bile acids (see Table 1). An elevated serum bile acid result was also interpreted as consistent with cholestasis and without evidence of a bile acid synthesis defect.

After genetic confirmation of CTX was obtained, normal to mildly increased plasma cholestanol measurements were obtained at 4.5 and 5 months, that were not considered sufficiently elevated to provide biochemical confirmation of CTX (Table 1). Therefore, a second qualitative urine FAB‐MS profile analysis was performed at 5 months of age, which provided an interpretation of marked elevation in (polyhydroxylated) bile alcohol glucuronides that characterize sterol 27‐hydroxylase deficiency (CTX). Although qualitative FAB‐MS biochemical evidence for CTX was obtained, treatment for CTX was not initiated, in part due to the prior negative biochemical test results for CTX, and the perceived exclusion of a diagnosis of CTX due to the lack of elevated plasma cholestanol.

The patient's jaundice had resolved during treatment with UDCA, with serum bile acid measurements and bilirubin levels found to have normalized at 5 months (Table 1), after which the UDCA treatment was discontinued. At around 8.25 months of age, the patient experienced new onset of looser stools. Unfortunately, the stool frequency was not recorded, nor were any stool studies performed.

Given the genetic test result and abnormal qualitative FAB‐MS, quantitative liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) testing was performed measuring bile acid precursors and bile alcohols from 8.5 months of age onward, which provided additional biochemical confirmation of CTX (Table 1).

Treatment for CTX

Standard of care treatment of CTX with CDCA was initiated at 9 months of age, 3 , 4 initially at a reduced dose 4 mg/kg/daily (in three divided doses) to ensure patient tolerance and reduce concern for possible hepatotoxicity. 17 Weight based dosing was accomplished by dissolving 250 mg CDCA tablets in water and providing dose in appropriate volume. Her aminotransferases initially mildly increased (below 2x upper limit normal [ULN]) but slowly down trended over time. Bile acid precursors and bile alcohol concentrations decreased rapidly after 1 month of treatment at 4 mg/kg/daily (Table 1). The CDCA dose was increased to 8 mg/kg/daily at 12 months of age and further increased to 10 mg/kg/daily at 15 months of age. Aminotransferase results were stable during dose titration and, importantly, CTX biomarkers remained <10% of baseline values, reflecting good metabolic control with CDCA at doses ≤10 mg/kg/daily, averting the need for higher 15 mg/kg/daily dosing. 5 The decrease in biomarkers in response to treatment with CDCA was associated with a decrease in frequency of loose stools compared to pre‐treatment. At 22–26 months of age her neurological exam remains normal, she is meeting all developmental milestones and an ocular exam is normal.

DISCUSSION

We describe the case of an infant with neonatal jaundice that was genetically diagnosed with CTX through use of a cholestasis gene panel test. This case underscores the importance of gene panel testing for genetic causes of cholestasis, which in this patient provided the first evidence of CTX.

Initial biochemical testing, with a finding of normal/mildly increased plasma cholestanol, was believed to be inconsistent with a diagnosis of CTX. In recognition that the plasma cholestanol concentration may be normal in CTX, 2 analysis of bile acid precursors and bile alcohols was performed, providing definitive biochemical evidence of CTX. The case demonstrates that a normal/mildly increased cholestanol measurement does not exclude a diagnosis of CTX in infants. This finding is especially important in this patient population, who may have minimal symptoms, signs, or other clinical diagnostic clues suggestive of CTX. Accordingly, a clinical suspicion index for diagnosis of CTX that was developed to decrease diagnostic delay, 6 is less applicable in infants.

In addition to providing diagnostic confirmation of CTX, quantitative biochemical testing is recommended at baseline and after initiating treatment for CTX to facilitate monitoring of the efficacy of therapy to repress activation of CYP7A1, 4 and thereby decrease levels of bile acid precursors, cholestanol, and bile alcohols.

Standard of care treatment with CDCA was initiated in our patient after diagnosis. 4 The initial dose was 4 mg/kg/daily, with titration to 10 mg/kg/daily over 6 months. Acquisition of quantitative baseline values for plasma and urine biomarkers at 8.5 months of age allowed documentation of the approximately 80% or more decrease in biomarkers on treatment compared to baseline. The confirmation of adequate metabolic control avoided the need to increase the CDCA dose. Although higher doses of 15 mg/kg/daily have been recommended, 5 the 10 mg/kg/daily dose was effective to decrease elevated CTX biomarkers with no signs of hepatotoxicity from CDCA. Although initial aminotransferase levels were transiently elevated to < twice the ULN after initiation of treatment, the patient tolerated CDCA well during gradual dose titration up to 10 mg/kg/daily over 6 months.

CDCA is widely considered to be the preferred treatment for CTX, 3 , 4 but rarely may cause hepatotoxicity in infants at higher doses. 17 Although there is less data to support its use in CTX, treatment with cholic acid (a potentially less hepatotoxic therapy) may be a viable treatment option when CDCA is no longer tolerated or effective in infant patients with hepatic jaundice/cholestasis. 13 Cholic acid is FDA approved for treatment of bile acid synthesis disorders in adults and children, including CTX.

We propose, if an infant is identified with a CYP27A1 genotype suggestive of CTX and has a normal/mildly increased cholestanol concentration, that quantitative 7αC4, 7α12αC4 or bile alcohol biomarker testing is obtained prior to considering starting the patient on treatment to more definitively assess treatment response. Biomarkers can be quantified in plasma or urine (see Table 1). Analysis of plasma provides data on multiple biomarkers (bile acid precursors and bile alcohol glucuronide), while urine analysis provides data for bile alcohol. Plasma bile acid precursors (7αC4 and 7α12αC4) and plasma bile alcohol glucuronide (5β‐cholestane‐3α,7α,12α,25‐tetrol‐3‐O‐β‐D‐glucuronide) concentrations decreased to 1%–21% of pretreatment values after 1 month of treatment with CDCA 4 mg/kg/daily (Table 1). Urine bile 5β‐cholestane‐3α,7α,12α,23S,25‐pentol (23S‐pentol) concentration decreased to <200 ng/mL (<3% of pretreatment value) after 1 month of treatment. Since there were no clinical signs or symptoms of CTX to guide assessment response to therapy, other than resolution of loose stools, our results highlight the importance of using serial biomarker monitoring to assess adequacy of therapy.

Both CYP27A1 variants present in the infant patient we describe have been previously reported in older CTX patients with elevated cholestanol, 19 , 20 and have been demonstrated as detrimental to CYP27A1 enzyme activity through functional assays. 19 , 20 The variant c.1183C>T greatly reduced CYP27A1 enzymic activity 19 and the c.1420C>T variant was shown to result in <5% of wild‐type CYP27A1 activity in vitro. 20 It may be that in infant patients there has not been sufficient duration of time for cholestanol to accumulate to the high levels observed in older patients. There are few reports available that characterize the biochemistry of CTX in newborns and infants, but there is some evidence that cholestanol concentrations may not always be elevated at birth (Table S1). A higher proportion of infant CTX cases may have normal/mildly increased cholestanol levels than has been previously appreciated, although cholestanol measurements in CTX infants with cholestasis may be complicated by the elevation in cholestanol that can be observed in cholestasis/liver diseases unrelated to CTX.

CONCLUSION

The information in this report is important for clinical practice because it demonstrates the potential value of cholestasis gene panel testing to provide the first evidence for CTX as the cause of jaundice/cholestasis in infants. Moreover, this report emphasizes that a normal/mildly increased plasma cholestanol concentration does not exclude the diagnosis of CTX, and highlights the nuance in use and interpretation of follow‐up biochemical testing for diagnosis and monitoring of treatment efficacy. Although measurement of plasma cholestanol has been recommended as the diagnostic biomarker of choice for CTX, 4 we now understand that some patients with CTX may have normal levels of cholestanol, necessitating quantification of more sensitive biomarkers for CTX. 2 In conclusion, infants with cholestatic liver disease warrant screening, initially with cholestasis gene panel testing, and if the CYP27A1 gene analysis suggests CTX, follow‐up should include quantification of sensitive bile acid precursor and bile alcohol biomarkers for diagnostic confirmation and to monitor the response to treatment.
