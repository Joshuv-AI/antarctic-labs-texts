Stable Port-Hamiltonian Neural Networks
Fabian J. Roth, Dominik K. Klein, Maximilian Kannapinn, Jan Peters, Oliver Weeger

Abstract
In recent years, nonlinear dynamic system identification using artificial neural networks has garnered attention due to its broad potential applications across science and engineering. However, purely data-driven approaches often struggle with extrapolation and may yield physically implausible forecasts. Furthermore, the learned dynamics can exhibit instabilities, making it difficult to apply such models safely and robustly. This article introduces , a machine learning architecture that incorporates physical biases of energy conservation and dissipation while ensuring global Lyapunov stability of the learned dynamics. Through illustrative and real-world examples, we demonstrate that these strong inductive biases facilitate robust learning of stable dynamics from sparse data, while avoiding instability and surpassing purely data-driven approaches in accuracy and physically meaningful generalization. Furthermore, the model’s applicability and potential for data-driven surrogate modeling are showcased on multiphysics simulation data.

Background

2.1
Stability of dynamical systems

We consider autonomous dynamical systems as first-order ordinary differential equations, where the state
\bm{x}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
evolves according to a vector-valued function
\bm{f}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:

\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t)).

(1)

Suppose
\bm{f}
is sufficiently smooth to ensure a unique solution
\bm{x}(t)
for any initial condition
\bm{x}(t_{0})
. A solution
\hat{\bm{x}}(t)
is
stable
in the sense of Lyapunov if, for any small perturbation in the initial condition, the perturbed solution
\bar{\bm{x}}(t)
remains close for all times, that is, if for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that
\left\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0})-\bar{\bm{x}}(t_{0})\right\rVert<\delta
implies
\left\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}(t)\right\rVert<\epsilon
for all
t>t_{0}

[
35
,
52
]
.
A stronger version of stability, namely
asymptotic stability
, is obtained if additionally the perturbed solution
\bar{\bm{x}}(t)
converges to
\hat{\bm{x}}(t)
for
t\rightarrow\infty
for initial conditions
\left\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}(t_{0})-\bar{\bm{x}}(t_{0})\right\rVert<\Delta
with some
\Delta>0

[
52
]
.

Lyapunov stability is a
local
property, in the sense that for sufficiently large perturbations, the perturbed solution may stray arbitrarily far even from asymptotically stable solutions
[
49
]
.
Quantifying the permissible extent of perturbations on the initial condition such that the perturbed solution still converges to the unperturbed solution leads to the concept of a
basin of attraction

[
30
]
.
Asymptotically stable equilibria, for which the basin of attraction extends to the entire phase space, are called
globally asymptotically stable

[
30
]
. A system with a globally stable equilibrium cannot have any other equilibria, as their existence would imply that there are solutions (namely, the other equilibria) that do not converge to the globally stable equilibrium.

In the following, we consider the case of an equilibrium solution
\bm{x}(t)=\bm{0}
, i.e.,
\bm{f}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
. Placing the equilibrium at the origin is done for ease of notation and is without loss of generality, as arbitrary equilibria can be translated to the origin with a time-independent transformation.
The stability of the equilibrium can be shown through the use of Lyapunov’s stability theory by finding a suitable scalar Lyapunov function
V

[
52
,
24
]
. Let
V:D\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be continuously differentiable and positive definite, that is,
V(\bm{0})=0
and
V(\bm{x})>0
in
D\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, where
D\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a neighborhood of the origin. If the value of
V
is nonincreasing along trajectories of the system in
D
, then the equilibrium is stable in the sense of Lyapunov. Mathematically, this is the case if
\dot{V}(\bm{x})
is negative semi-definite:

\dot{V}(\bm{x})=\frac{\partial V}{\partial\bm{x}}^{\intercal}\dot{\bm{x}}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial\bm{x}}^{\intercal}\bm{f}(\bm{x})\leq 0\quad\forall\bm{x}\in D\setminus\{\bm{0}\}.

(2)

In the case of a negative definiteness (
\dot{V}(\bm{x})<0
in
D\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
),
asymptotic
stability can be concluded.
To obtain
global
asymptotic stability, these conditions must be satisfied
globally
, that is, for all
{\bm{x}\in D={\mathbb{R}}^{n}}
,
and
the Lyapunov function must be radially unbounded, i.e.,
V(\bm{x})\rightarrow\infty
for
\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert\rightarrow\infty

[
24
]
.
The latter condition avoids the existence of solutions that do not converge to the equilibrium by diverging to infinity without violating the decrease condition in
Equation

2
.

2.2
Port-Hamiltonian systems

Port-Hamiltonian systemstheory provides a framework for modeling physical systems that generalizes the underlying mathematical structure of Hamiltonian dynamics. It incorporates the modeling of energy-dissipating elements, which are typically absent in classical Hamiltonian systems
[
51
]
. Additionally, port-Hamiltonian systemstheory has roots in control theory, emphasizing the interactions of dynamic systems with their environment.
In this work, we consider port-Hamiltonian systemsof the form

\dot{\bm{x}}=\left[\bm{J}(\bm{x})-\bm{R}(\bm{x})\right]\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}(\bm{x})+\bm{G}(\bm{x})\bm{u}(t).

(3)

Here,
\bm{x}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
describes the system’s state,
\bm{u}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
its input, and
{\mathcal{H}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
denotes the Hamiltonian, which typically represents the total energy of the system
[
51
,
4
]
.
The skew-symmetric
structure matrix

{\bm{J}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}},\,{\bm{J}(\bm{x})=-\bm{J}^{\intercal}(\bm{x})}
describes the conservative energy flux within the system.
The symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
{\bm{R}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}},\,{\bm{R}(\bm{x})=\bm{R}^{\intercal}(\bm{x})\succeq 0}
is called
dissipation matrix
and describes the energy losses. Finally, the
input matrix

{\bm{G}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}}
describes how energy enters and exits the system via the inputs.
Port-Hamiltonian systemsextend the energy conservation of Hamiltonian systems to the energy dissipation inequality:

\begin{split}\dot{\mathcal{H}}&=\underbrace{\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}^{\intercal}\mkern-8.0mu\bm{J}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vphantom{\geq}=0\end{subarray}}-\underbrace{\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}^{\intercal}\mkern-8.0mu\bm{R}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\geq 0\end{subarray}}+\underbrace{\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}^{\intercal}\mkern-8.0mu\bm{G}\bm{u}(t)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s(\bm{x},\bm{u})\end{subarray}}\leq s(\bm{x},\bm{u}).\end{split}

(4)

In the language of system theory, the system is dissipative
with respect to the supply rate
s(\bm{x},\bm{u})
.
The formal definition of dissipativity requires
{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0
, which will be ensured later.
Since the supply rate
s(\bm{x},\bm{u})
is linear in the input
\bm{u}
, it vanishes for zero-input
s(\bm{x},\bm{0})=0
. The inequality thus ensures that the system cannot gain energy in the absence of an external excitation (
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
). Besides this physical motivation, the dissipativity of port-Hamiltonian systemsplays a crucial role in stability analysis.
For the unforced system with
{s=0}
, the Hamiltonian inherently fulfills the decrease condition
\dot{\mathcal{H}}\leq 0
.
Thus, by using
{\mathcal{H}}
as a Lyapunov function, it can be shown that any strict minimum in the Hamiltonian implies a stable equilibrium of the unforced dynamics
[
51
]
.
Furthermore, the equilibrium is asymptotically stable if the system continuously dissipates energy (
\bm{R}\succ 0
) for states near the minimum.

The use of the port-Hamiltonian formulation as a basis for an machine learningmodel has multiple favorable consequences.
When learning system dynamics from data, the structure of
Equation

3
provides both a physical bias and the opportunity for interpreting the resulting trained model, while enabling the modeling of a wide range of relevant systems.

Equation

4
highlights the distinctive roles played by the three matrix-valued functions. The structure matrix
\bm{J}(\bm{x})
results in conservative dynamics, the dissipation matrix
\bm{R}(\bm{x})
contributes only to the dissipative dynamics, and the input matrix
\bm{G}(\bm{x})
captures the energy flow over the system boundaries due to the inputs. This knowledge improves the interpretability of the trained model components. Furthermore, it allows new ways of incorporating prior knowledge into the system.
For example, the learned system can be constrained to be conservative by fixing
\bm{R}=\bm{0}
.
Furthermore, the connection of port-Hamiltonian systemto the decrease condition from Lyapunov’s theory provides the opportunity to introduce further desirable stability constraints.

To illustrate the above concepts with a simple example, consider the dynamics of a three-dimensional spinning rigid body described by Euler’s rotation equations.
Expressing the angular velocity vector
\bm{\omega}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in the principal-axis coordinate frame, the evolution equation is given by

\bm{I}\dot{\bm{\omega}}+\bm{\omega}\times(\bm{I}\bm{\omega})=-\mu\bm{\omega}\quad\forall t>0.

(5)

Here,
\bm{I}=\diag(I_{1},I_{2},I_{3})
denotes the inertia matrix and
\mu
is a damping coefficient.
By introducing

{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{\omega})=\frac{1}{2}I_{1}\omega_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}I_{2}\omega_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}I_{3}\omega_{3}^{2},

(6)

which describes the kinetic energy
E

\bm{J}

\bm{R}
, and vanishing input matrix
\bm{G}

\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\omega}_{1}\\
\dot{\omega}_{2}\\
\dot{\omega}_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\Bigg(\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&-\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}I_{2}}\omega_{3}&\frac{I_{2}}{I_{3}I_{1}}\omega_{2}\\
\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}I_{2}}\omega_{3}&0&-\frac{I_{1}}{I_{2}I_{3}}\omega_{1}\\
-\frac{I_{2}}{I_{3}I_{1}}\omega_{2}&\frac{I_{1}}{I_{2}I_{3}}\omega_{1}&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{J}(\bm{\omega})}-\underbrace{\mu\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{I_{1}^{2}}&0\vphantom{-\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}I_{2}}\omega_{3}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{I_{2}^{2}}&0\vphantom{-\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}I_{2}}\omega_{3}}\\
0\vphantom{-\frac{I_{3}}{I_{1}I_{2}}\omega_{3}}&0&\frac{1}{I_{3}^{2}}\\

(7)

Our goal is to learn the dynamics of physical systems in the form of
Equation

with desirable stability properties from observations.
To this end, we parameterize the components

,
\bm{J}
,
\bm{R}

of port-Hamiltonian systemwith neural networks.

3.1
Stability of port-Hamiltonian dynamics

To guarantee the existence of stable equilibria of port-Hamiltonian systems, it is sufficient to enforce the existence of strict local minima in the Hamiltonian. However, these stability implications are only
local
, meaning the size and shape of the corresponding basin of attraction are not constrained by
Equation

3
. Small perturbations might be enough to stray far from the stable equilibria.
Furthermore, the general port-Hamiltonian systemcan represent dynamics such as unbounded trajectories, which must be considered unstable from a practical standpoint.
The following describes the necessary requirements for achieving
global
stability.
Essentially, the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}
is required to be a suitable Lyapunov function, which includes positive definiteness and radial unboundedness.
These properties are achieved by constraining the Hamiltonian to be convex and ensuring the existence of a strict minimum. This approach is similar to the construction of a Lyapunov function by
[
27
]
, but differs in the way the minimum is enforced.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the port-Hamiltonian system
Equation

3
in the unforced case
\bm{u}(t)=\bm{0}
:

\dot{\bm{x}}=\left[\bm{J}(\bm{x})-\bm{R}(\bm{x})\right]\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}(\bm{x}),\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{J}=-\bm{J}^{\intercal},\,\bm{R}=\bm{R}^{\intercal}\succeq 0.

(8)

Suppose the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})
is convex, twice continuously differentiable, and fulfills:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{0})=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}\bigg\rvert_{\bm{x}=\bm{0}}=\bm{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{{\mathcal{H}}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}\partial{\bm{x}}}\bigg\rvert_{\bm{x}=\bm{0}}\succ 0.

(9)

Then,
{\mathcal{H}}
is a suitable Lyapunov function for showing stability of the equilibrium at
\bm{x}(t)=\bm{0}
, and all solutions are bounded.

Furthermore, the equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable if
\bm{R}(\bm{x})\succ 0
.

A proof of
Theorem

3.1
is provided in
Appendix

A
.
However, its implications also follow a clear physical intuition:
If a dynamical system continuously loses energy via internal dissipation (
\bm{R}\succ 0
), it will eventually settle into an energy minimum corresponding to an asymptotically stable equilibrium. The convexity requirement, together with
Equation

9
, guarantees the existence of a unique strict and global minimum at the origin. Additionally, it ensures that the energy for states at infinity is unbounded, i.e.,
{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})\to\infty
as
\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, no finite state has more energy than a state at infinity, and all trajectories must be bounded. As a result, all solutions eventually converge to the global energy minimum.
While convexity is sufficient to ensure global asymptotic stability, it is not a necessary condition. To enhance model expressiveness when needed, techniques such as input warping
[
27
]
can be employed, but were not required in the applications considered in this work.

For conservative systems with
\bm{R}=\bm{0}
, energy is not dissipated, and attractive equilibria cannot exist.
Therefore, a conservative system cannot possess asymptotically stable equilibria.
Nevertheless, the minimum in the energy still represents a stable equilibrium, and all trajectories stay bounded.
The mixed case, where
\bm{R}\succeq 0
, only allows the same conclusions as the conservative case.
However, unless the dissipation matrix vanishes in a large region in phase space, it is unlikely that a system with
\bm{R}\succeq 0
is not globally asymptotically stable.

To fulfill the constraints of

{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})
is represented by a fully input convex neural network

. fully input convex neural networkscan approximate any convex function
[

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}\left(\bm{W}_{0}\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{0}\right),

(10)

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}\left(\bm{U}_{i}\bm{z}_{i}+\bm{W}_{i}\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{i}\right),\;i=1,2,...,k-1,

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=z_{k},

with the pass-through weight matrices
\bm{W}_{i}
that map the input
\bm{x}
directly to the activation of the
k-1
hidden layers, bias vectors
\bm{b}_{i}
, and layer outputs
\bm{z}_{i}
.
If the weight matrices
\bm{U}_{i}
have non-negative components, the first activation function
\sigma_{0}
is convex, and all subsequent
\sigma_{i}
are convex and non-decreasing, then the output
f(\bm{x})
is convex in the input
\bm{x}
.
Since the Hamiltonian should be twice continuously differentiable, the same must hold for the activation functions. One suitable choice is the softplus activation.
The conditions for stability in
Equation

9
are then fulfilled by normalizing the fully input convex neural network
f
:

{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})\underbrace{-f(\bm{x}^{*})-\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{x}}\bigg|_{\bm{x}^{*}}^{\intercal}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})}_{f_{\text{norm}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})}+\underbrace{\epsilon\left\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\vphantom{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{x}}\bigg|_{\bm{x}^{*}}}}_{f_{\text{reg}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})}\,.

(11)

Consequently, the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}
and its gradient vanish at
\bm{x}^{*}
due to the normalization term
f_{\text{norm}}
. Moreover, the regularization term
f_{\text{reg}}
with an arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
ensures positive definiteness of the Hamiltonian’s Hessian.
However, in practice,
f_{\text{reg}}
can be omitted since the fully input convex neural networkprovides enough bias towards local strict convexity. After training, the stability guarantee can then be recovered by verifying the Hamiltonian’s positive definiteness at
\bm{x}^{*}
.
Choosing
\bm{x}^{*}=\bm{0}
fulfills all requirements of
Theorem

3.1
. However, the presented approach is more general. As the assumption that the equilibrium is located at the origin is only made for ease of notation, the normalization approach in
Equation

11
can position the stable equilibrium anywhere in phase space and is not restricted to the origin.

If the true equilibrium position is known beforehand, fixing
\bm{x}^{*}
introduces an additional inductive bias.
While this information can often be obtained from prior physical knowledge, it may not always be available.
In such cases, the equilibrium can be inferred directly from data by treating
\bm{x}^{*}
as a trainable parameter during optimization.

Structure, dissipation and input matrices
The matrix-valued functions
\bm{J}(\bm{x}),\,\bm{R}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{G}(\bm{x})
are parameterized by feedforward neural networks, where the outputs are suitably reshaped into matrices.
As
\bm{J}
is constrained to be skew-symmetric, the respective feedforward neural network’s output vector is mapped to the space of skew-symmetric matrices via a linear transformation.
Similarly, the positive definiteness (or semi-definiteness) of
\bm{R}
is ensured via the Cholesky factorization
\bm{R}=\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\intercal}
, where the reshaped feedforward neural networkoutput
\bm{L}
is a lower triangular matrix with positive (or non-negative) elements along the main diagonal.
For some applications,
\bm{J},\,\bm{R}
and
\bm{G}
are independent of
\bm{x}
, and thus, learning constant matrices is sufficient.
In particular, this is the case when the conservative dynamics of the system under consideration are Hamiltonian in the given coordinates.
Furthermore, by fixing
\bm{J}
to the symplectic matrix (and choosing
\bm{R}=\bm{G}=\bm{0}
), the stable port-Hamiltonian neural network can be reduced to a Hamiltonian neural network.

3.3
Training stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks

There are two fundamentally different approaches for training time-continuous dynamic models such as neural ordinary differential equations and stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks:
derivative
and
trajectory fitting
.
The former directly compares the predicted state derivatives
\dot{\bm{x}}
from
Equation

3
to the true ones.
While this approach is efficient, it requires the true time derivatives to be available as training data, which is typically not the case for real-world measurement data. Furthermore, derivative fitting is not applicable when augmented states are used
[
12
]
, i.e., when
\bm{x}
is padded with additional dimensions compared to the observable states to enrich the representable dynamics.
Further, a model proficient in predicting
\dot{\bm{x}}
does not necessarily produce accurate predictions for trajectories
\bm{x}(t)
, as small derivative errors can accumulate exponentially when integrating an ODE
[
17
]
.
On the other hand, trajectory fitting directly compares integrated model trajectories
\bm{x}(t)
with ground-truth trajectories, ensuring long-term accuracy.
Here, the optimization process necessitates either the propagation of the loss gradients through the ODE solver or the use of the adjoint sensitivity method
[
7
]
, which computes the gradients by solving a modified ODE. In both cases, trajectory fitting incurs significant computational costs due to gradient propagation and model integration, making trajectory fitting much slower than derivative fitting.

A distinct advantage of stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks is the favorable influence the stability constraint exerts on the training process when using trajectory fitting.
Neural ordinary differential equations and similar architectures can suffer from exploding gradients, where small parameter changes lead to large loss gradients.
This can slow the training process or prevent it from converging.
A potential cause is the crossing between the boundaries of local basins of attraction during training, leading to significant differences in long-term behavior
[
41
]
.
Since stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks have only one global basin of attraction, this source of training difficulties is eliminated.
In our numerical experiments, we observe good convergence of the training error of stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks where neural ordinary differential equations struggle without the use of regularization terms or gradient clipping.

4
Experiments

We evaluate the proposed model on multiple example problems, including measurement data from a real physical system and multiphysics simulation data.
The performance of stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks is compared to that of neural ordinary differential equations, PHNNs, and bounded PHNNs.
The latter two are port-Hamiltonian system-based neural networkmodels that do not enforce or relax the convexity constraint of stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks, and thus do not guarantee global stability.
In PHNNs, the Hamiltonian is modeled by an unconstrained feedforward neural network, whereas in bounded PHNNs, it is constructed to be positive and radially unbounded, which ensures bounded trajectories.
The latter architecture is intentionally designed to resemble OnsagerNet
[
55
]
, but with the key difference that it supports arbitrary time-dependent inputs, making it suitable for our experimental setting.
For examples that purely rely on derivative fitting, we further include stable NODEs
[
27
]
.
Using trajectory fitting, we could not achieve convergence of stable NODEs, excluding them from most experiments.
This is likely due to difficulties caused by discontinuous dynamics stemming from the projection-based stability enforcement, as also mentioned by
[
46
]
.
Finally, we include a variant of stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks with learnable minima (stable port-Hamiltonian neural network-LM).
In contrast to stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks, which have
\bm{x}^{*}

\bm{x}^{*}

Predictions are generated by rollout with a numerical integrator using the Runge-Kutta scheme Tsit5, with adaptive step size and a known input function

5
Conclusion

This work proposes stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks for accurate, robust, and reliable identification of stable nonlinear dynamics.
They leverage the port-Hamiltonian framework, which provides a basis for model interpretation and ensures adherence to the laws of thermodynamics in terms of energy conservation or dissipation balances with respect to a learned Hamiltonian energy function.
Additionally, the approach ensures global asymptotic stability of the identified dynamics, without requiring projection, by constraining the Hamiltonian to be a convex, positive definite Lyapunov function.
While this limits stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks to modeling systems that appear globally stable within the region of interest, it provides a strong inductive bias that enables robust learning and reasonable generalization from sparse data.
We demonstrate the approach’s viability using data from synthetic examples, real-world measurements, and complex multiphysics simulation models.
The resulting models, built with small neural network architectures, exhibit low variance across instances, accurate prediction, and robust generalization. Furthermore, the proposed method can successfully exploit augmented dimensions where unconstrained alternative models struggle with instabilities or data scarcity.
While the approach can identify the equilibrium point from data, we find that providing the equilibrium as a prior can further enhance prediction accuracy and reduce data requirements.
Future work will extend the approach to multistable systems by allowing Hamiltonians with multiple minima, while retaining favorable stability properties.

Appendix A
Proof of stability

This section provides a proof of
Theorem

3.1
, which describes the essential requirements for achieving stable dynamics with . Due to the relevance of convexity to the following discussion, the following Lemma first provides some widely known equivalent formulations of convexity for scalar-valued multivariate functions.

Lemma A.1
.

Consider a twice continuously differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},\,\bm{x}\mapsto f(\bm{x})
.
If
f
is convex, the following statements hold:

1.

\lambda f(\bm{x}_{1})+(1-\lambda)f(\bm{x}_{2})\geq f(\lambda\bm{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\bm{x}_{2})\quad\forall\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\,\lambda\in[0,1]
,

2.

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{x}}\big\rvert_{\bm{x}_{1}}^{\intercal}(\bm{x}_{2}-\bm{x}_{1})\leq f(\bm{x}_{2})-f(\bm{x}_{1})\quad\forall\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

3.

\frac{\partial^{2}{f}}{\partial{\bm{x}}\partial{\bm{x}}}(\bm{x})\succeq 0\quad\forall\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

For a proof, the reader is referred to
[
6
]
.
Note that for strict convexity, the inequalities in statements 1 and 2 must be strict for all
\bm{x}_{1}\neq\bm{x}_{2}
, and the Hessian in statement 3 must be positive definite.
With the notion of convexity established, attention can now be directed to the proof of
Theorem

3.1
. For convenience, the statement of the theorem is repeated verbatim below:

Theorem A.2
.

Consider the port-Hamiltonian system
Equation

3
in the unforced case
\bm{u}(t)=\bm{0}
:

\dot{\bm{x}}=\left[\bm{J}(\bm{x})-\bm{R}(\bm{x})\right]\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}(\bm{x}),\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{J}=-\bm{J}^{\intercal},\,\bm{R}=\bm{R}^{\intercal}\succeq 0.

(A.1)

Suppose the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})
is convex, twice continuously differentiable, and fulfills:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{0})=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}\bigg\rvert_{\bm{x}=\bm{0}}=\bm{0},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{{\mathcal{H}}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}\partial{\bm{x}}}\bigg\rvert_{\bm{x}=\bm{0}}\succ 0.

(A.2)

Then, the system in
Equation

A.1
has a stable equilibrium at
\bm{x}(t)=\bm{0}
, and all solutions are bounded. Furthermore, the equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable if
\bm{R}(\bm{x})\succ 0
.

Proof.

Since the gradient of the Hamiltonian vanishes and its Hessian is positive definite at
\bm{x}=\bm{0}
, it has a strict local minimum at the origin. This implies the existence of an
R>0
such that

{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})>{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{0})=0\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\{\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert\leq R,\,\bm{x}\neq\bm{0}\}.

(A.3)

Consider the function
g(\bm{x})=\frac{a}{r}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert
for some
r\in(0,R]
with
a=\inf_{\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert=r}{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})>0
. We claim that
g
is a lower bound for
{\mathcal{H}}
, i.e.
g(\bm{x})\leq{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})
for all
\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert>r
.

Suppose there existed an
\tilde{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that violated the claim, that is
\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{x}}\right\rVert>r
and
g(\tilde{\bm{x}})>{\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})
. Due to the convexity of
{\mathcal{H}}
, it holds

\lambda{\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})+(1-\lambda){\mathcal{H}}(\bm{0})\geq{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda\tilde{\bm{x}}+(1-\lambda)\cdot\bm{0})\quad\forall\lambda\in[0,1]

(A.4)

and thus

\lambda g(\tilde{\bm{x}})>\lambda{\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})\geq{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda\tilde{\bm{x}}).

Choosing
\lambda=\frac{r}{\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{x}}\right\rVert}
and applying the definition of
g
we obtain

\frac{r}{\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{x}}\right\rVert}g(\tilde{\bm{x}})=a>{\mathcal{H}}\bigg(\frac{r}{\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{x}}\right\rVert}\tilde{\bm{x}}\bigg)\geq a.

(A.5)

This is a contradiction, and therefore
g
is a lower bound.

With the bound established, showing that
{\mathcal{H}}
is radially unbounded is straightforward.
For any path
\bm{x}(t)
with
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\lVert\bm{x}(t)\right\rVert=\infty
, once
\left\lVert\bm{x}(t)\right\rVert>r
we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x}(t))\geq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}g(\bm{x}(t))=\infty.

(A.6)

We would like to remark that the same conclusion could alternatively be reached by showing that
{\mathcal{H}}
has at least one bounded sublevel set due to its strict minimum and then concluding radial unboundedness (i.e., coercivity) using Corollary 8.7.1 in
[
45
]
together with the standard equivalence for proper closed convex functions. However, the presented approach using a lower bound can directly be extended to show global positive definiteness of
{\mathcal{H}}
.
For this, note that for all
\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert>r
the lower bound implies

0<g(\bm{x})\leq{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x}).

Together with
Equation

A.3
, this implies that
{\mathcal{H}}
is indeed positive definite for all
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

From the port-Hamiltonian structure of
Equation

A.1
we immediately obtain the decrease condition
\dot{\mathcal{H}}\leq 0
(see
Equation

4
).
Using the
{\mathcal{H}}
as a Lyapunov function, we obtain all requirements to conclude
local
stability of the equilibrium.
Furthermore, the decrease condition implies that a solution starting at
\bm{x}^{*}
cannot leave the sublevel set
\Omega_{\mathcal{H}}=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}|{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x})\leq{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x}^{*})\}
.
Since
\Omega_{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\Omega_{g}=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}|g(\bm{x})\leq{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x}^{*})\}
and
\Omega_{g}
is clearly bounded, all solutions of
Equation

A.1
must be bounded as well.

Finally, to show that
global
asymptotic stability follows from
\bm{R}\succ 0
, it remains to be demonstrated that
\dot{\mathcal{H}}<0
for all
\bm{x}\neq\bm{0}
.
This is implied by
Equation

4
if
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}\neq\bm{0}
for all
\bm{x}\neq\bm{0}
. Proving that the gradient of
{\mathcal{H}}
vanishes only at the origin is done by contradiction. Assume that there exists an
\tilde{\bm{x}}\neq\bm{0}
such that
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}\big\rvert_{\tilde{\bm{x}}}=\bm{0}
. Furthermore, let
\bm{x}^{*}=\bm{0}
.
Since
{\mathcal{H}}
is convex it follows (see
Lemma

A.1
) that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x}^{*})-{\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})\geq\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}\bigg\rvert_{\tilde{\bm{x}}}^{\intercal}(\bm{x}^{*}-\tilde{\bm{x}})=0,

(A.7)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})-{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x}^{*})\geq\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\bm{x}}\bigg\rvert_{\bm{x}^{*}}^{\intercal}(\tilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}^{*})=0.

(A.8)

This implies
{\mathcal{H}}(\bm{x}^{*})={\mathcal{H}}(\tilde{\bm{x}})=0
. However, since
{\mathcal{H}}
is globally positive definite and thus only vanishes at the origin, we have
\bm{x}^{*}=\tilde{\bm{x}}
. This is a contradiction as
\tilde{\bm{x}}\neq\bm{0}
, which concludes the proof.

Appendix F
Thermal field data

F.1
Dimensionality reduction

The following describes the proper orthogonal decompositionmethod for dimensionality reduction of the field data applied in
Section

4.4
.
The process is first described for general field data given as a matrix
\bm{A}=[\bm{a}_{0},\dots,\bm{a}_{M}]^{\intercal}
of
M
snapshots
\bm{a}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, where
M
is the number of snapshots and
N
is the number of finite elementnodes.
To identify the dominant modes of the data, we perform a singular value decompositionof
\bm{A}
:

\bm{A}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\intercal}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times N},

(F.1)

where
\bm{U}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M}
and
\bm{V}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
are unitary matrices and
\bm{\Sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times N}
is a rectangular diagonal matrix containing the singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{M}
on its diagonal.
Dimensionality reduction is achieved by truncating the singular value decomposition
\bm{A}\approx\tilde{\bm{U}}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\tilde{\bm{V}}^{\intercal}
, where
\tilde{\bm{U}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times n}
and
\tilde{\bm{V}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times n}
contain the first
n
columns of
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}
, and
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}=\diag(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
is the truncated matrix of the dominant singular values.
The dominant modes in the data are then given by
\bm{M}=c\tilde{\bm{V}}
. Here, the scaling factor
c
is the standard deviation of the elements in
\tilde{\bm{U}}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
. This scaling ensures that the latent trajectories will have unit variance.
To map a latent state
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to the full-order field
\bm{X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
we compute:

\bm{X}=\bm{M}\bm{x}.

(F.2)

Conversely, to map from the full-order field to the latent space, we solve the least-squares regression problem:

\bm{x}=\argmin_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{M}\bm{x}^{\prime}\right\rVert=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\bm{M}^{\intercal}\bm{X}.

(F.3)

In
Section

4.4
, we consider two fields: The thermal field describing the state of the system and the excitation given by the heat source field. For each, a separate singular value decompositionis performed to compute
\bm{M}_{T}
and
\bm{M}_{q}
using the respective snapshots from the two training trajectories.
We thus obtain two pairs of encoders
\psi
and decoders
\phi
:

\displaystyle\psi_{T}(\bm{T})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{x}}\left\lVert\bm{T}-\bm{M}_{T}\bm{x}\right\rVert,

\displaystyle\phi_{T}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\bm{M}_{T}\bm{x},

(F.4)

\displaystyle\psi_{q}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{u}}\left\lVert\bm{q}-\bm{M}_{q}\bm{u}\right\rVert,

\displaystyle\phi_{q}(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\bm{M}_{q}\bm{u}.

(F.5)

To fully leverage the benefits of stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks, it is essential to ensure that the absence of a heat source is encoded as the zero-vector in the corresponding latent space. Since the mapping from the latent space
Equation

F.2
is linear, and
\bm{q}=\bm{0}
corresponds to the absence of a heat source in the full-order space, this requirement is automatically fulfilled.
However, for applications where a nonzero state
\tilde{\bm{x}}
describes the absence of an external energy source or for general nonlinear latent mappings (such as autoencoders), the condition can be met by applying a constant translation in the latent space, which shifts the encoded state
\tilde{\bm{x}}=\psi(\tilde{\bm{x}})
to the origin.
In
Section

4.4
, we use this method to ensure that the thermal equilibrium corresponds to the origin of the temperature latent space. While this is not strictly necessary, knowledge of the equilibrium location in the latent space enables its integration into the stable port-Hamiltonian neural network’s architecture by fixing the stable equilibrium position.
Consequently, we arrive at the representation of the latent state

\displaystyle\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\psi_{T}(\bm{T})-\psi_{T}(\bm{T}_{\text{eq}}),

\displaystyle\bm{T}

\displaystyle\approx\phi_{T}(\bm{x}+\psi_{T}(\bm{T}_{\text{eq}})).

(F.6)

where
\bm{T}_{\text{eq}}
is the full-order state vector describing thermal equilibrium.
The complete model architecture is represented in
Figure

F.1
. There, the function
\bm{f}
is used as a placeholder for an arbitrary evolution equation, to generalize to all model architectures used in
Section

4.4
.

Figure F.1
:
Model setup used in
Section

4.4
. The initial temperature field
\bm{T}(t_{0})
and input field
\bm{q}(t)
are mapped via encoders
\phi_{T},\phi_{q}
into the latent space, where the latent dynamics
\bm{f}
are integrated. Here,
\bm{f}
is used as a placeholder for a specific model, e.g., neural ordinary differential equation or stable port-Hamiltonian neural network. Finally, the predicted latent trajectory
\bm{x}(t)
is mapped to the full-order field via the decoder
\phi_{T}
.

Finally, we note that alternative dimensionality reduction techniques could be applied. In preliminary experiments, we explored the use of autoencoders for this purpose. Although autoencoders offer advantages over the linear singular value decompositionregarding achievable compression rates, they exhibited poor extrapolation performance when trained on sparse data. This issue could potentially be mitigated by training the autoencoders with additional synthetic data, which would not necessitate costly simulations; however, further research is needed to explore this approach.
