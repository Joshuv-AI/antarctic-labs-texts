Evaluation of parameters for early detection of pediatric keratoconus
Authors: Sharon H. Zhao, Carla Berkowitz, Hantamalala Ralay Ranaivo, Kelly Laurenti, Brenda L. Bohnsack, Surendra Basti, Jennifer L. Rossen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKeratoconus in children is often more severe and rapidly progressive compared to adults. While Scheimpflug imaging (Pentacam) improves screening, there are no established pediatric guidelines. Due to challenges in diagnosis, we aimed to correlate clinical factors with abnormal imaging results to aid in the development of screening protocols.MethodsA single-center retrospective cohort analysis of patients 3–18 years screened for keratoconus with ophthalmic examination and initial Scheimpflug imaging between 2021 to 2023. Scheimpflug indices including final D, ART max, and calculated CAIRO 8, which is a corneal metric combining pachymetry, ART max, and elevation, were compared to published pediatric ranges for normal and keratoconus corneas.ResultsSeventy-eight patients (11.8 ± 3.9 years) were screened with the majority referred due to high and/or progressive astigmatism (74 patients, 94%). Forty-nine patients (63%) were male and 48 (62%) self-reported Hispanic ethnicity. Average astigmatism was 4.9 ± 1.8 D and 86% had with-the-rule astigmatism. Fourteen patients (18%) had corneal findings on slit lamp. Average LogMAR CDVA was 0.243 ± 0.274 D. By Pentacam, 55 (70%) patients had ≥ 1 indeterminate or keratoconus value. Patients stratified as keratoconus by all parameters were older (15 vs. 11 years, p = 0.015–0.02), had worse visual acuity (p < 0.0001), and were more likely to have oblique astigmatism (0.0002—< 0.0001) and corneal abnormalities on examination (p < 0.00001) compared to eyes with normal or indeterminate indices.ConclusionsOlder age, lower visual acuity, oblique astigmatism, and corneal findings were more commonly found in patients with abnormal screening Pentacam. Most patients clinically suspicious for keratoconus had ≥ 1 indeterminate/abnormal indices. Future studies will follow these patients to identify clinical risk factors for progression.

Background

Keratoconus is a corneal ectasia characterized by progressive corneal thinning that can cause vision loss and impact social functioning, education, and mental health [1]. While presentation in children and adolescents correlates with more severe and progressive disease, diagnosis can be delayed due to difficulty in examining young patients [2, 3]. In addition, certain syndromes such as Trisomy 21 are commonly associated with keratoconus, and intellectual and developmental disabilities can make disease detection even more challenging.

Classically, keratoconus is characterized by high, progressive, irregular astigmatism, which may be asymmetric [4]. Risk factors for disease progression include eye rubbing and associations with allergies, particularly vernal keratoconjunctivitis. Clinical signs include a scissor reflex on retinoscopy and irregularity of mires on keratometry. Slit-lamp findings can include thinning and protrusion of the inferior cornea, Fleischer ring in the corneal epithelium at the base of the cone, Vogt’s striae in the corneal stroma, and corneal scarring [5]. Corneal tomography and topography are key for diagnosing and monitoring the disease, especially subclinical keratoconus which lacks slit lamp findings and visual symptoms [6]. The Pentacam imaging system (OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) utilizes a rotating Scheimpflug camera to generate a three-dimensional map of the cornea and anterior segment and provides additional measurements including pachymetry and curvature of the anterior and posterior cornea [7]. Normative values of Scheimpflug (Pentacam) indices have been reported in healthy pediatric eyes in one study [8]. In adults, there has been more research on stratifying risk of keratoconus based on these indices including final D, Ambrósio’s relational thickness maximum (ART max), and Corneal assessment index from the relational thickness and other OCULUS values analyzed for 8 mm zone (CAIRO 8) [3, 9–11].

Early diagnosis of keratoconus has clinical implications as a treatment is now available for progressive disease identified before end-stage disease (e.g. hydrops, dense scarring, and extreme corneal thinning). The treatment, corneal cross-linking, stabilizes keratoconus progression prior to end-stage disease and has been shown to improve visual acuity in pediatric keratoconus [12–14]. It is FDA approved for children and adults over the age of 14 years old. The aim of this study was to identify factors that increase the probability of finding keratoconus-associated abnormalities on initial screening Scheimpflug (Pentacam) images in pediatric patients. Three Scheimpflug indices—final D value, ART max, and combined index CAIRO 8—were utilized as the key indices for evaluation as there are pediatric normative values for ART max and CAIRO 8, and final D is the summation of many indices and frequently used for keratoconus monitoring [3, 8, 15].

Methods

This cohort consisted of a retrospective case series of patients (3–18 years old) who were referred for keratoconus screening with Scheimpflug imaging (Pentacam) at the Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children’s Hospital of Chicago between January 2020 to August 2023. Patients younger than 3 years old were excluded due to inability to cooperate with imaging. In addition, patients in whom Scheimpflug images had a poor-quality index (Quality Specification box shows “blinking” or is color-coded “red”) were excluded [15]. This study was covered by IRB protocol #2021–4146 by the Institutional Review Board of Lurie Children’s Hospital. The study abided by the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was conducted in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Data collection was deidentified.

One eye per patient was included in the analysis. Imaging indices for keratoconus (e.g., final D, pachymetry, D values, ART max) were reviewed to identify the number and severity of indices in abnormal (indeterminate and keratoconus) range for each eye. The more severely affected eye with more abnormal values was included unless one eye had a history of hydrops, in which case the contralateral eye was included. In cases where both eyes showed the same number of abnormal values, the right eye was chosen.

Collected data included age at presentation, self-reported gender, race, ethnicity, ophthalmic diagnoses, and prior surgeries. Examination findings from initial presentation were obtained including corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), which was obtained by optotype based on patient age and ability (Snellen Linear, HOTV, or LEA), refraction (cycloplegic retinoscopy and/or manifest refraction), slit lamp findings and Scheimpflug images. Visual acuity was converted into LogMAR values.

The Scheimpflug imaging displays of 4 Map Refractive Topography and Belin Ambrósio Enhanced Ectasia were used to extract pachymetric and topometric indices including pachymetry, astigmatism, final D, and ART max. The three main indices that were analyzed as indices of keratoconus were ART max, calculated CAIRO 8, and final D values. The CAIRO 8 index was calculated using one pachymetry index, ART max, and one elevation index, anterior elevation (AE) at 8 mm, in the equation CAIRO 8 Index = 5.0549 + 0.6392 (AE at 8 mm)—0.02251 (ART max) [11]. Each patient’s ART max and calculated CAIRO 8 index were graded based on published pediatric ranges for normal, and keratoconus corneas [3]. Values in between these two ranges were considered indeterminate as they are not within the published normal range but are not keratoconus range either. For final D values, normal values were < 1.6 SD from the average, indeterminate values were ≥ 1.6 SD and < 2.6 SD, and keratoconus values were ≥ 2.6 SD [15].

Choosing which Scheimpflug variables to analyze was decided based on a prior report indicating high sensitivity and specificity of ART max and CAIRO 8 indices in identifying keratoconus in a pediatric population in Egypt [3]. While CAIRO 8 is one the highest-sensitivity indices in differentiating keratoconus and normal eyes [11], it has not been shown to be consistently reliable when used as a single index [16, 17]. In our study, the final D value was also included due to its combination of several important indices for keratoconus (Df (front elevation), Db (back elevation), Dp (pachymetric progression), Dt (thinnest point), and Da (thinnest point displacement)), and it is a standard index to monitor in patients with keratoconus [9, 18].

Statistical analyses included ANOVA and Chi-square tests and were performed using Prism (Version 10.2.0). All tests were 2-sided and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Over 90% of patients (74 patients, 94%) were screened for keratoconus due to high and/or progressive astigmatism per the referring physician. In addition, the majority of patients identified as Hispanic (49 patients, 62%), and 47 of the 49 Hispanic patients self-reported their race as “Other”. On clinical examination, optotype CDVA was available for 75 patients; the LogMAR CDVA was 0.243 ± 0.274 (median 0.176, range 0–1.398) and the average astigmatism was 5.0 ± 1.1 D (median 5.0, range 0–9.5). Seventy-two patients (90%) had ≥ 3 D of astigmatism on cycloplegic refraction (Table 1). Sixty-seven patients (86%) had with-the-rule astigmatism (axis 70–110 degrees), 1 patient (1%) had against-the-rule astigmatism (axis 160–20 degrees), 7 patients had oblique astigmatism (axis 21–69 or 111–159 degrees), and 3 patients had no astigmatism. Fourteen (18%) patients had corneal findings on slit lamp examination including Vogt’s striae (7 patients), Munson’s sign (3 patients), Fleischer ring (4 patients), and stromal scarring (3 patients). Average K Max was 49.1 D, average central corneal thickness was 538.95 μm, and the thinnest point of the cornea was most commonly in the IT quadrant (67 patients, 86%).

For Final D readings by Scheimpflug imaging (Table 2), 41 patients (52.6%) had readings within the normal range, while 23 (29%) patients had indeterminate readings. Fourteen patients had values in the keratoconus range, which were more evenly distributed between Hispanic and Non-Hispanic patients (p = 0.062) compared to the higher percentage of Hispanic patients in the group with normal final D values. Of the 14 patients with final D readings consistent with keratoconus, 10 had keratoconus readings for ART max and CAIRO 8 while 4 had indeterminate readings for those two indices. Unlike final D readings, more patients were found to have indeterminate readings by ART max (36 patients, 46.2%) (Table 3) and CAIRO 8 (42 patients, 53.8%) (Table 4). For all 3 indices (final D, ART max, and CAIRO 8), the patients with readings consistent with keratoconus were older (p = 0.015, 0.02), had poorer presenting visual acuity (p < 0.0001), were more likely to have oblique astigmatism (p = 0.0002—< 0.0001), and had a higher percentage of corneal findings (p < 0.0001).

Discussion

Scheimpflug imaging is a very helpful diagnostic modality to distinguish between normal and keratoconus corneas with reliable indices that have been published in the literature [3, 7–10, 16, 19]. It is an excellent diagnostic test for detecting early keratoconus and is also useful in detecting disease progression [6]. Importantly, the diagnosis of keratoconus requires the following findings: abnormal posterior elevation, abnormal corneal thickness distribution, and clinical noninflammatory corneal thinning, as well as evidence of consistent change over time [6].

In its end-stages, keratoconus can lead to significant loss of vision. The FDA-approval of cross-linking for patients at least 14 years-old has monumentally improved clinical outcomes for those with early-detected disease in the US [13]. It is now more important than ever to identify patients with keratoconus before the disease is too advanced for cross-linking. Furthermore, delaying CXL can not only result in further keratoconus progression, but can also led to a greater economic burden, as demonstrated by Pagano et al. in their UK study [20]. However, we do not yet know which children with high astigmatism, which can be an early sign of keratoconus, will develop keratoconus. At this point, it is still unclear which pediatric patients with clinical suspicion for keratoconus should be screened with Scheimpflug imaging, when screening should start, and how frequently it should be done. The purpose of this study was to evaluate how many pediatric patients with clinical suspicion for keratoconus had abnormalities on their initial screening with Scheimpflug imaging and also to evaluate which of these clinical factors are most predictive of keratoconus.

Although 70% of patients had at least one abnormal or indeterminate value in their more severely affected eye, only 14% of screened patients showed keratoconus on all 3 indices. Therefore, clinical suspicion (mostly high astigmatism) had a low specificity for identifying pediatric patients with a high likelihood of keratoconus on initial screening.

Not surprisingly, the clinical factor that was most specific for keratoconus were well-established corneal findings such as Vogt’s striae, Munson’s sign, conical shape, corneal thinning, Fleischer ring, and stromal scarring. Patients with readily identifiable findings on clinical exam are labeled keratoconus even without topography. In these cases, Scheimpflug imaging is still valuable to monitor for progression, but this does not help with the question of which subclinical patients have keratoconus.

Notably, lower presenting CDVA was also associated with keratoconus values for all 3 indices. The average CDVA was 20/27—20/33 for normal and indeterminate values for each index and 20/67—20/85 for keratoconus values for each index.

Another factor associated with keratoconus on initial screening was older age, which is also expected as keratoconus often progresses in puberty [2]. In fact, the youngest patient in this study to have at least 1 of the 3 indices in the keratoconus category was 11 years old, even though children as young as 4 years old were screened. In a study by Rocha-de-Lossada and colleagues of children with diagnosed pediatric keratoconus, they found that children > 14 years old had significantly different KC index and Topographic Keratoconus Classification values on Pentacam compared to the ≤ 14 years age group [21]. Our study’s finding that an older age group had more significantly abnormal Pentacam indices is similar to the findings from the earlier study by Rocha-de-Lossada et al. It may therefore be reasonable to start screening with Scheimpflug imaging for children who have high astigmatism but no corneal abnormalities at the age of 8 to 9 years-old.

While the amount of astigmatism was not statistically significant between the groups, the type of astigmatism differed significantly. There was a higher percentage of with-the-rule astigmatism and a lower rate of oblique astigmatism in normal and indeterminate values compared to keratoconus for all 3 indices. This is consistent with findings from Choi and Kim, who identified higher prevalence of with-the-rule or against-the-rule astigmatism on initial exam in patients with mild keratoconus who then had subsequent change toward oblique astigmatism as axial changes and disease progression occurred [22]. Similarly, de Toledo et al. found higher rates of oblique astigmatism in patients who underwent penetrating keratoplasty for keratoconus and had recurrence compared to those without progression [23].

Furthermore, in all 3 Scheimpflug imaging indices, patients with normal values were composed of a higher percentage of Hispanic patients than indeterminate and keratoconus, although only the final D value was statistically significant. This suggests that although Hispanic patients may have a high rate of high astigmatism [24], this astigmatism is not necessarily directly associated with keratoconus [25]. Further studies and longitudinal follow-up are needed to determine the role of race in predisposition to progression to keratoconus.

The major limitation of this study is that keratoconus is a disease known to progress with time. This study only evaluated the initial presenting examination and screening Scheimpflug imaging of these patients. Future studies will follow these patients over time to determine which patients progress to keratoconus and which do not. This will be particularly helpful for eyes with clinical suspicion for disease but do not have classic slit lamp findings or Scheimpflug images diagnostic of keratoconus. In addition, our study utilized normative pediatric data published by Hashem et al. and not a control group within our institution [8]. It is important to note that there may be geographic, racial, and ethnic differences which may lead to variations in normative data. This study describes the presentation of pediatric patients undergoing screening for keratoconus at a single institution in the US to help improve future detection and diagnosis.

Conclusions

A high likelihood of keratoconus did not develop before 11 years-old, suggesting that screening with Pentacam in the earliest years of childhood may have low sensitivity in detecting keratoconus in those who have disease later on in adolescence or those with subclinical disease. Those with lower presenting CDVA (worse than 20/60), oblique or against-the-rule astigmatism, and characteristic corneal findings are also at higher risk for having abnormal values on screening Scheimpflug imaging suggestive of keratoconus.

The patients in this cohort with clinical suspicion for keratoconus had a high rate of at least one indeterminate value on Scheimpflug imaging among final D, ART max, CAIRO 8 indices. These readings that are above normal range but not yet within keratoconus range can be helpful markers in monitoring for subclinical disease over time. Future studies are in progress to determine which factors are associated with progressive pediatric keratoconus to allow for better screening guidelines to identify patients for cross-linking and minimize unnecessary screening in those at low risk.
