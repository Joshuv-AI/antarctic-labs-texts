The concept of Laparoscopic Intracorporeal Rectus Aponeuroplasty (LIRA) technique in complex abdominal hernias based on size and location
Authors: Salvador Morales-Conde, Veronica Tamburi, Antonio Curado Soriano, Juan Ramón Naranjo Fernández, Marta Domínguez-Muñoz, Ignacio Cañizares Jorva, David Castrodá Cópa, Julio Gómez-Menchero, Andrea Balla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of complex abdominal wall hernias remains a surgical challenge, particularly in selecting the most appropriate technique. This study reports our experience with the laparoscopic repair of complex hernias using the laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty (LIRA) technique concept and its evolution as LIRA-like, LIRA with transabdominal preperitoneal extension (LIRA-TAPE), and the LIRA-Sugarbaker technique in case of parastomal hernia.MethodsA retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data was conducted from 2019 to 2025. Hernias were classified according to the European Hernia Society classification. Techniques were selected based on hernia location: LIRA for M1-M4 W3 defects, LIRA-like for L1-L4 W1-W3, LIRA-TAPE for M5 W1-W3, and the LIRA-Sugarbaker for parastomal hernias. Postoperative seroma was classified according to the Morales-Conde classification.ResultsNine patients underwent LIRA. Median defect width was 11 cm (range 10–15 cm) and median operative time was 100 min (range 60–173 min). Seromas occurred in 3 patients (33.3%) (two type I and one type II-a), all managed conservatively. Recurrences did not occur. In the LIRA-like group (11 patients), median defect width was 8 cm (range 1.5–14 cm), and median operative time was 60 min (range 25–110 min). Seromas occurred in 6 patients (54.5%) (one type I, two type II-a and 3 type II-b). One recurrence (9.1%) occurred due to central mesh failure. Seven patients with median defect width measured 10 cm (range 7–18 cm) underwent LIRA-TAPE. Median operative time was 70 min (range 45–110 min). One patient (14.3%) developed ileus and one (14.3%) seroma (type III-a), treated conservatively. Recurrences did not occur. Three patients, with median defect width of 9 cm (range 7–11 cm) underwent LIRA-Sugarbaker technique. Median operative time was 95 min (range 85–110 min). Across all groups, no intraoperative complications or conversions occurred.ConclusionsIn this single-center retrospective study, the LIRA concept for the treatment of complex ventral hernias appears to be a safe and feasible approach, allowing tension-free defect closure with encouraging short-term outcomes.Keywords: Minimally invasive surgery, Laparoscopic ventral hernia repair (LVHR), Laparoscopic Intracorporeal Rectus Aponeuroplasty (LIRA), Complex hernia, Lateral hernia, Parastomal hernia, Postoperative outcomes

Introduction

Ventral hernia represents one of the most common conditions in the general population, and its surgical repair is among the most frequently performed abdominal wall procedures [1, 2]. Laparoscopic ventral hernia repair (LVHR) has progressively improved postoperative recovery and reduced wound-related complications, while maintaining acceptable recurrence rates in comparison to open surgery [3–5].

Over the years, the laparoscopic approach has progressively expanded, enabling retromuscular and preperitoneal mesh placement, the treatment of larger defects through component separation techniques, as well as its application in emergency settings and increasingly complex abdominal wall hernias; however, despite these advances, significant limitations persist, particularly in achieving restoration of abdominal wall function and tension-free closure in large or complex defects [6–12].

Complex abdominal wall hernias represent a heterogeneous group of defects, including large size, difficult anatomical locations, recurrences, or association with complicating factors such as stomas or loss of domain [10–12]. In these cases, no standardized surgical strategy has been clearly established, and the choice of technique often relies on surgeon experience rather than high-quality evidence. As a result, achieving adequate defect closure while minimizing complications and recurrence challenging [13].

Recently, we have proposed the laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty (LIRA) technique, based on intracorporeal reconstruction of the posterior rectus sheaths, allowing tension-free closure of the defect [14–19].

Building on this principle of intracorporeal tension free reconstruction of posterior sheaths, we extended the approach to the treatment of ventral hernias in complex locations by developing the LIRA-like for lateral defects, and LIRA with transabdominal partially extraperitoneal (LIRA-TAPE) repair, for midline defects near to bony structures, while for parastomal hernias we continue to employ the LIRA-Sugarbaker technique [14–19].

We hypothesized that the application of the LIRA concept across different types of complex abdominal wall hernias is feasible and may allow effective defect closure with acceptable short-term outcomes. Therefore, the aim of the present study is to report our experience with this approach in a series of patients with complex hernias.

Methods

This study is a retrospective case series based on prospectively collected data, conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Cohort Studies in Surgery (STROCSS) guidelines [20].

Institutional review board approval (1002-N-15) and signed informed consent from all participants were obtained (Clinical trial number: not applicable).

From September 2019 to March 2025, all consecutive patients who underwent laparoscopic complex hernia repair were enrolled.

Inclusion criteria

Patients over 18 years, with ventral hernia classified intraoperatively as M1-M4 W3 (defect width ≥ 10 cm), M5 W1-W3, L1-L4 W1-W3, according to the European Hernia Society (EHS) classification [12], or parastomal hernia, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class I, II or III, who underwent elective surgery, and after signing the informed consent were included in this study.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with ventral hernia M1-M4 W1-W2 according to the EHS classification [12], ASA IV, who underwent surgery in emergency setting, with contraindications for general anesthesia or laparoscopic approach were excluded.

Indication for surgery and surgical technique

Based on the type of hernia, patients underwent repair of the abdominal wall defects with one of the following techniques: LIRA, LIRA-like, LIRA-TAPE, or the modified LIRA-Sugarbaker. The choice of technique depended on hernia location: LIRA was performed in patients with hernias M1-M4 W3, LIRA-like was adopted in patients with hernias L1-L4 W1-W3, LIRA-TAPE for M5 W1-W3 hernias, and the modified LIRA-Sugarbaker technique for parastomal hernias. Surgery was performed by two expert minimally invasive surgeons specialized in abdominal wall reconstruction (S.M.C., and A.C.S.).

All patients underwent surgery under general anaesthesia. A urinary catheter was not routinely used [21]. Pneumoperitoneum was established using a Veress needle inserted in the left upper quadrant, with intra-abdominal pressure maintained at 14 mmHg.

The following surgical materials were employed according to the specific requirements of each case. For fascial closure, a double-loop long-lasting absorbable monofilament polyglyconate suture 1 (Maxon™, Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) was used. Different meshes were adopted depending on the availability: a polypropylene mesh with a hydrogel barrier on the visceral side (Ventralight™ Mesh, C.R. Bard, Murray Hill, New Jersey, USA); a hybrid composite mesh composed of a bioabsorbable 3D web scaffold combined with permanent dense polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) monofilament macro-porous knit (GORE® SYNECOR Intraperitoneal Biomaterial, W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc., Newark, Delaware, USA); a synthetic nonabsorbable three-dimensional polyester (PE) monofilament coated with a bioabsorbable collagen film (Symbotex™, Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA); or a composite non-absorbable polypropylene and expanded PTFE (ePTFE) mesh (Composix™, C.R. Bard, Murray Hill, New Jersey, USA). Mesh fixation was achieved with either titanium helical tacks (ProTack™ 5 mm Fixation Device, Covidien, Mansfield, Massachusetts, USA) or absorbable tacks (AbsorbaTack™ 5 mm Fixation Device, Covidien, Mansfield, Massachusetts, USA), and fibrin sealant (Tissucol Duo R, Baxter, Deerfield, Illinois, USA) was applied when indicated. The trocar site used for the optical device was closed with a fascial closure system (Weck EFx™ Endo Fascial Closure System, Teleflex Incorporated, Wayne, Pennsylvania, USA). Briefly the procedures are reported below [14–19, 22].

Laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty (LIRA) for large M1-4 W3 hernias

A 12-mm trocar for a 30° laparoscope and two 5-mm working trocars were introduced along the left midclavicular line. Following adhesiolysis, which was carried out using blunt dissection and cold scissors, the pneumoperitoneum pressure was lowered to 8–9 mmHg. The dimensions of the abdominal wall defect (width and length) were then assessed with a ruler. A bilateral flap of the posterior rectus sheath was fashioned by making an incision approximately 1 cm from its medial margin and extending it along the full length of the defect. The two flaps were subsequently approximated with continuous running sutures, using 1 to 3 double-loop absorbable monofilaments. An intraperitoneal mesh was then placed with at least 2 cm of overlap beyond the fascial margins and secured with some permanent fixation in the outer crown and absorbable fixation devices to complete the outer crown and for the inner crown following a double-crown fashion. Finally, fibrin sealant was applied to close the potential space between the mesh and the abdominal wall and to cover the metal part of the permanent fixation device exposed, to avoid contact with the bowel [14–16, 22] (Fig. 1).

Laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty (LIRA) technique. A Abdominal wall defect. B Bilateral flap. C Intracorporeal suturing. D Mesh placement

Laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty like (LIRA-like)

The patient was placed in a semi-lateral decubitus position at approximately 30°, on the side opposite to the defect. A 12-mm trocar for a 30° laparoscope and two 5-mm working trocars were introduced along the midclavicular line. The procedure involved the concept of opening the fibrous tissue around the edge of the defect in order to decrease the tension when closing it. This manoeuvre is performed after the peritoneum and fascia were dissected, as a lateral flap in order to identify the abdominal wall nerves, thereby reducing the risk of nerve entrapment and postoperative pain and to partially cover the mesh. This approach involves opening the peritoneum and transversalis fascia to create a lateral peritoneal flap, which covers part of the prosthesis while leaving a portion exposed to the bowel, thereby combining intraperitoneal and extraperitoneal components in a hybrid TAPE-like fashion. The defect was closed after performing those incisions using double-loop absorbable monofilament suture, allowing tension release. Fixation was achieved with absorbable or permanent fixation devices following previous system, and fibrin glue was applied to seal potential gaps between the mesh and the peritoneum and to cover permanent fixation devices [17] (Fig. 2).

Laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty (LIRA) like technique. A Creation of the preperitoneal space. B Incision and release of fibrotic tissue. C Intracorporeal suturing. D Mesh placement

Laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty with transabdominal preperitoneal extension (LIRA-TAPE)

A 12-mm trocar for a 30° laparoscope and two 5-mm working trocars were introduced along the midclavicular line. The procedure started with the opening of the peritoneal flap, as it is performed during transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair for inguinal hernia repair. This peritoneal flap is conducted bilaterally in order to expose properly the pubis, Cooper ligament and the vessels and nerves of the area. This flap is continued with the opening of the posterior fascia, as we perform during the LIRA technique, if the hernia defect is above the arcuate line. The defect was closed using double-loop absorbable monofilament running sutures applied through rectus muscle, below the arcuate line, and the posterior rectus sheath above it. A mesh was then placed intraperitoneally with at least 2 cm overlap beyond the lateral fascial edges and fixed to the pubis and Cooper ligaments using permanent fixation devices. Absorbable or permanent fixation was applied to the remaining margins, and the peritoneal flap is used to cover partially the mesh. Fibrin glue was added to seal potential gaps between the mesh and the peritoneum and to cover permanent fixation devices [18] (Fig. 3).

Laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty (LIRA) with transabdominal preperitoneal extension (TAPE) technique. A Opening of the posterior fascia. B Creation of the peritoneal flap. C Intracorporeal suturing. D Partially intraperitoneal mesh placement

Laparoscopic intracorporeal rectus aponeuroplasty (LIRA) Sugarbaker technique

The patient was placed in a supine position, with the surgeon and assistant standing on the side opposite to the stoma. One 12-mm trocar and two 5-mm trocars were introduced. Careful adhesiolysis was performed to free the stoma loop and release the peritoneum from the trephine opening, ensuring sufficient mobilization to allow at least 4 cm of overlap between the abdominal wall and the prosthesis. According to the LIRA concept of tension release, additional incisions of fibrotic tissue, peritoneum, or transversalis fascia, depending on defect location, were performed as needed to create a lateral flap. The defect was then covered with an intraperitoneal mesh. Care was taken to avoid narrowing of this tunnel or excessive angulation of the bowel at the entry site. The mesh was fixed to the abdominal wall using the double-crown technique using absorbable or permanent fixation devices [19] (Fig. 4).

Prehabilitation with botulinum toxin

Botulinum toxin prehabilitation was not routinely applied and was used only in selected cases. In patients with defect classified as W3 according to the EHS classification at preoperative computed tomography (CT) scan, preoperative prehabilitation with botulinum toxin A was performed to facilitate abdominal wall closure and reduce lateral muscle tension. Ultrasound-guided infiltration of the internal oblique muscle was carried out 3–4 weeks before surgery. Botulinum toxin type A was administered at a total dose of 500 IU, divided into 250 IU per side. Each side received injections at three points, with 2 cc administered per point [23].

The internal oblique muscle was selectively targeted, as previous evidence suggests that a single central injection within this muscle may allow diffusion of the toxin beyond its anatomical boundaries, producing a relaxation effect also on the external oblique and transversus abdominis muscles [23]. When the dose is distributed along multiple injection points, the biological activity may remain more confined to the target muscle, while still achieving the desired overall lateral wall relaxation [23].

Study design

Demographic variables (including gender, age, body mass index - BMI, comorbidities, American Society of Anaesthesiologist - ASA class, previous abdominal surgery), defect characteristics (including hernia type according to the EHS classification [12], length, width and area defect), intraoperative variables (including intraoperative complications and conversion to open surgery, suture type, mesh type and size, fixation type, operative time) and postoperative outcomes (including 30-day postoperative complications, seroma, pain, length of hospital stay - LOS, bulging and recurrence) were recorded and analysed.

Preoperatively, CT scan with Valsalva manoeuvre was performed in all patients to evaluate hernia content and identify any additional abdominal wall defects.

Postoperative complications were classified according to Clavien-Dindo classification [24].

Seromas were classified according to the Morales-Conde classification [25]. Type 0 seromas, including both 0-a and 0-b, were not considered complications, as they represent incidental findings without clinical relevance [25]. Since type 0-b is detected only by radiological examination and routine postoperative imaging was not performed, types 0-a and 0-b were grouped together. An additional imaging technique was performed only if suspected complications occurred during physical examination.

Postoperative pain was evaluated using the visual analogue scale (VAS) score (from 0 to 10) at postoperative day (POD) 1 and 7.

Bulging was defined as an abdominal wall area of weakness at physical examination without defects. Recurrence was defined when an abdominal wall defect with hernia sac occurred.

Data were stored in the Microsoft Excel program (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as median and range, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

In the LIRA-like group, eleven patients had a median defect width and length of 8 (range 1.5–14 cm) and 7 cm (range 1.2–18 cm), respectively (median defect area of 49 cm², range 1.8–252 cm²). According to the EHS classification, defects were distributed across categories, with L1W2 and L4W2 being the most common (2 cases each, 18.2%), followed by L1W3, L2W1, L2W2, L2W3, L3W2, L3W3, and L4W3 (1 case each, 9.1%) (Table 2). One L1W3 patient underwent prehabilitation with botulinum toxin 26 days before surgery.

The median operative time was 60 min (range 25–110 min). Postoperatively, one patient developed type I (9.1%) seroma, two developed type II-a (18.2%), and three developed type II-b (27.3%), none requiring further treatment. Pain was reported by ten patients on POD 1 (90.9%), with a median VAS score of 6 (range 1–8), which improved on POD 7 when four patients (36.4%) still reported pain with a median VAS score of 0 (range 0–7). The median LOS was 1 day (range 1–2 days). During a median follow-up of 25 months (range 13–55 months; individual follow up duration were 55, 54, 48, 46, 40, 25, 23, 22, 14, 14, and 13 months), one recurrence (9.1%) was observed (Table 3). In this patient recurrence was caused by a central mesh failure (nonabsorbable three-dimensional PE monofilament mesh coated with a bioabsorbable collagen film) which occurred 6 months after the first LIRA-like and underwent IPOM plus (intraperitoneal onlay mesh closing the defect) plus 33 months later.

In the LIRA-TAPE group, seven patients had a median defect width and length of 10 (range 7–18 cm) and 15 cm (range 8–22 cm), respectively (median defect area of 180 cm², range 64–220 cm²). According to the EHS classification, defects were most frequently identified as M5W2 (3, 42.9%) or M5W3 (4, 57.1%) (Table 2). The median operative time was 70 min (range 45–110 min). Postoperatively, one patient developed ileus (14.3%, Clavien-Dindo I). One patient presented seroma type III-a, not being necessary to drain it. Pain was reported by all patients on POD 1 (100%), with a median VAS score of 5 (range 4–9), while by POD 7 five patients (71.4%) still presented pain, with a median VAS score of 2 (range 0–8). The median LOS was 1 day (range 1–5 days). During a median follow-up of 22 months (range 14–58 months; individual follow up duration were 58, 40, 28, 22, 22, 19, and 14 months), no bulging or recurrence occurred (Table 3).

In the modified LIRA-Sugarbaker group, the median defect width and length was 7 (range 7–11 cm) and 17 cm (range 15–19 cm), respectively (median defect area of 153 cm², range 105–209 cm²). According to the EHS classification, all cases were classified as L2W3 (3, 100%) (Table 2). The median operative time was 95 min (range 85–110 min). Pain was reported by all patients on POD 1 (100%), with a median VAS score of 5 (range 4–6), which improved by POD 7, when only one patient (33.3%) reported pain with a VAS score of 0 (range 0–2). The median LOS was 3 days (range 2–4 days). During a median follow-up of 23 months (range 18–79 months; individual follow up duration were 79, 23, and 18 months), no bulging or recurrence occurred (Table 3).

Discussion

The findings of the present study suggest that the principles of LIRA can be extended beyond their original indication, allowing the minimally invasive treatment of midline, lateral, and parastomal hernias with encouraging short-term outcomes. Postoperative complications were acceptable, and recurrence remained low despite defect heterogeneity. A relatively high rate of type II-b seromas was observed in the LIRA-like group, possibly related to the extent of dissection and creation of potential spaces. However, these did not require treatment and had no clinical impact, suggesting limited clinical relevance. The single recurrence observed in the LIRA-like group was most likely related to a technical failure (due to a central mesh rupture) rather than to the reconstructive concept itself.

Intracorporeal aponeurotic reconstruction with mesh reinforcement appears to be a versatile minimally invasive strategy for complex abdominal wall hernias. The LIRA concept can be interpreted as a reconstructive approach aimed at restoring abdominal wall continuity and physiological tension lines. This study explored its applicability across different anatomical scenarios through a tailored surgical strategy.

Complex abdominal wall hernias represent a heterogeneous group of defects that often require individualized operative strategies according to their anatomical location and surrounding structures [10, 11]. Accordingly, the present study aimed to explore the applicability of a unified reconstructive principle across different types of complex abdominal wall hernias.

Different technical adaptations were selected according to hernia location [14–18]. Standard LIRA was used for midline defects, LIRA-like for lateral hernias, and LIRA-TAPE when preperitoneal mesh extension was required [14–18]. In parastomal hernias, the reconstructive principle was combined with a LIRA-Sugarbaker technique [19]. Although not a direct technical evolution, it shares the same objective of facilitating tension-free reconstruction [19]. These cases were therefore included in the analysis.

Given the relatively low incidence and anatomical heterogeneity of complex abdominal wall hernias, large prospective comparative studies are difficult to conduct. Therefore, a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data was considered an appropriate methodological approach to explore the feasibility and early outcomes of applying the LIRA concept across different anatomical settings.

Several technical aspects may explain the potential advantages of the LIRA concept [14–17]. The minimally invasive approach provides magnified visualization, enabling precise identification of fascial planes and controlled dissection even in challenging regions such as the costal margins, pelvis, or stomas. This is particularly relevant in complex hernias, where prior surgery or distorted anatomy may hinder open reconstruction.

A key element of the LIRA concept is the intracorporeal closure of the fascial defect prior to mesh reinforcement [14–19]. This restores fascial continuity and physiological tension lines, redistributing mechanical forces and reducing stress on the prosthetic material [14–19]. In this setting, the mesh acts as a structural support rather than a simple reinforcement, potentially reducing postoperative bulging and improving functional outcomes [14–19].

Conceptually, LIRA can be viewed as an evolution of traditional intraperitoneal techniques [14–19]. While IPOM relies primarily on mesh reinforcement, LIRA incorporates a reconstructive component that reproduces some biomechanical advantages of retromuscular repair while preserving the simplicity of minimally invasive intraperitoneal surgery [5, 14–19]. By avoiding the extensive dissection, it may offer a balance between anatomical reconstruction and procedural complexity [14–19].

Another advantage of the LIRA concept is the adaptability to different anatomical locations [14–19]. As shown in this series, the same reconstructive principle can be applied to midline, lateral, and parastomal hernias through specific adaptations, allowing a consistent approach tailored to each defect.

When focusing on midline defects, the results of the present study are in line with those reported in the literature, although direct comparison remains limited by the heterogeneity of patient populations and surgical strategies [26–31]. Most available series include mixed cohorts and are not restricted to midline defects, often requiring adjunctive techniques to facilitate fascial closure [26–31].

Open component separation remains a widely adopted strategy for large midline defects, allowing restoration of fascial continuity with reported recurrence rates ranging between approximately 8% and 22% at mid- to long-term follow-up, but is associated with significant wound morbidity and invasiveness [26]. Similarly, open intraperitoneal repair with anterior component separation achieves recurrence rates of around 12% but with high complication rates, with surgical site occurrences exceeding 30% in some series [27].

To improve the feasibility of fascial closure in large defects, preoperative conditioning strategies such as botulinum toxin A have been increasingly adopted [28]. By inducing temporary relaxation of the lateral abdominal wall muscles, it facilitates medialization of the rectus complex and primary closure even in large W3 hernias, although it remains an adjunct rather than a reconstructive technique [28].

More recently, minimally invasive reconstructive approaches have been developed to reproduce retromuscular principles [29]. Laparoscopic transversus abdominis release (TAR) is feasible with acceptable early morbidity but increased technical complexity and operative time [29]. Robotic approaches may optimize visualization and mesh positioning, with promising short-term outcomes but limited long-term evidence [30].

M5 defects remain particularly demanding because of the proximity to the pubic bone, bladder, iliac vessels, and the absence of a posterior rectus sheath below the arcuate line [31]. In this setting, Sikar et al. reported 19 laparoscopic repairs of large suprapubic hernias with a mean defect area of 86.5 cm², a mean operative time of 108.3 min, a complication rate of 21.1%, and no recurrence at a mean follow-up of 31.2 months, supporting the role of minimally invasive surgery in this difficult anatomical region [31]. These findings are in line with the results observed in our LIRA-TAPE subgroup, where fascial closure combined with preperitoneal extension was used to achieve adequate inferior overlap.

In this context, the LIRA concept may represent a minimally invasive alternative that enables intracorporeal fascial reconstruction without the need for extensive retromuscular dissection or routine preoperative conditioning. Although direct comparison is not possible, the low recurrence and acceptable morbidity observed appear consistent with these other complex reconstructive strategies, while potentially reducing invasiveness.

Lateral abdominal wall hernias represent a challenging subset, with comparison limited by their rarity and heterogeneity [32–38]. Their anatomical location between bony structures and neurovascular bundles limits mesh fixation and complicates tension-free repair [32–38]. Moreover, a standardized surgical approach is still lacking.

Data from the Spanish Registry of Abdominal Wall Surgery (EVEREG), including 1742 lateral incisional hernias, show that most repairs are still performed using an open approach (87.7%), with recurrence rates reaching 27% at 24 months [33]. These findings highlight both the complexity of these defects and the lack of consensus on optimal management [33]. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis including 10,771 patients reported a pooled recurrence rate of 5.4%, with laparoscopic repair associated with lower recurrence compared to open surgery, although with similar complication rates [34].

Different surgical strategies have been proposed, ranging from open retromuscular or sandwich repairs to minimally invasive approaches [32–38]. Hybrid open–robotic techniques have shown promising results but with longer operative times and increased complexity [35]. Robotic approaches are feasible and safe, but evidence remains limited to small retrospective series with short follow-up [32].

More recently, minimally invasive retromuscular approaches such as the extended Totally Extraperitoneal (eTEP) - TAR have been proposed for lateral defects, with encouraging results [36, 37, 39]. Small series have reported low recurrence and morbidity, and improved postoperative recovery compared with IPOM plus, although with longer operative times [36, 37, 39]. However, registry data highlight variability in practice, with a predominance of open approaches and relatively high recurrence rates [33]. A recent meta-analysis reported a pooled recurrence of 5.4%, suggesting lower recurrence after laparoscopic than open repair [34].

Overall, the results of the present series appear consistent with those reported in literature on lateral abdominal wall hernias.

Parastomal hernias represent a particularly challenging subset of abdominal wall defects, with incidence rates reaching 30–50% within the first two years after stoma creation [38, 40]. Despite multiple surgical options, no technique has been clearly established as superior due to heterogeneous evidence [38, 40].

Meta-analyses comparing mesh-based techniques suggest that newer configurations may reduce recurrence with acceptable complication rates, although results remain inconsistent [38, 40]. Minimally invasive approaches appear to reduce morbidity and hospital stay compared to open surgery, without significant differences in recurrence [38, 40]. Robotic-assisted repair has also been shown to be feasible, with reported recurrence rates around 5% at short-term follow-up, but remains limited by higher complication rates and scarce long-term data [40].

Overall, the literature highlights the lack of a clearly superior strategy, supporting an individualized approach. In this context, the results of the present series appear encouraging and consistent with the current literature.

Finally, a relevant concern related to intraperitoneal mesh placement is the potential development of intra-abdominal adhesions [41]. Although composite meshes are routinely used, adhesions may still occur, and their clinical significance remains debated [41]. In the present study, all meshes used were composite covered mesh. Moreover, in approaches such as LIRA-like and LIRA-TAPE, the mesh is partially covered, potentially reducing direct contact with the bowel. However, in the present series, as well as in our previously published experience with longer follow up, no mesh-related complications attributable to intraperitoneal placement were observed [5, 14–16].

The main limitations of this study include its retrospective design, which may introduce selection bias and limits causal inference despite prospective data collection. In addition, the choice of surgical technique was not randomized but based on hernia location and surgeon preference, which may have introduced further selection bias and influenced the observed outcomes. The small sample size and heterogeneity of defects reflect real-world complexity but limit subgroup analyses. Moreover, mesh materials and fixation techniques were not standardized across patients, potentially introducing additional variability in outcomes. The absence of a control group further limits the ability to compare outcomes with other established techniques. Follow-up is limited to the short- to mid-term and postoperative imaging was not routinely performed, which may have led to underestimation of asymptomatic recurrences; therefore, longer observation and standardized imaging protocols are needed to confirm durability. Finally, the single-center experience may limit generalizability.

Conclusion

The findings of the present study suggest that the LIRA concept represents a feasible and effective minimally invasive strategy for the management of complex abdominal wall hernias across different anatomical locations. By prioritizing intracorporeal fascial reconstruction and restoration of physiological tension lines, this approach enables a tailored yet conceptually consistent repair with encouraging short-term outcomes. Although further prospective and comparative studies are warranted, the LIRA concept may be considered a potential addition to current minimally invasive abdominal wall surgical strategies.
