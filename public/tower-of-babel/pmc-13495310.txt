The mediating and moderating role of COVID-19 coping attitudes in the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondria
Authors: Ferhat Bolukçu, Şebnem Yücel, Recep Yücel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study uses structural equation modeling (SEM) to examine the relationships between coronavirus anxiety, coping attitudes toward COVID-19, and cyberchondria. It also tests the mediating and moderating roles of coping attitudes in the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondria.MethodThe Coronavirus Anxiety Scale, the Cyberchondria Severity Short Version and the Pandemic Coping Scale was used in the study. The population of the study consisted of adults aged 20–65 residing in the eight central districts of Ankara, the capital of Turkey (Keçiören, Çankaya, Yenimahalle, Mamak, Etimesgut, Sincan, Altındağ, and Pursaklar). The study used a convenience sampling method, and an online survey has been administered to a total of 427 volunteer participants residing in Ankara’s central districts.FindingsCoronavirus anxiety and COVID-19 coping attitudes were significantly associated with cyberchondria, explaining 43% of the variance. More cyberchondrial behavior was observed in individuals experiencing higher coronavirus anxiety or exhibiting stronger coping attitudes towards COVID-19. Coronavirus anxiety showed a statistically significant positive association with cyberchondria (β = 0.426, p < 0.001). Similarly, COVID-19 coping attitudes were also positively associated with cyberchondria (β = 0.181, p = 0.007). However, no statistically significant association was found between coronavirus anxiety and COVID-19 coping attitudes (β = 0.00, p = 0.964, p > 0.05). Additionally, coping attitudes toward COVID-19 did not mediate the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondria (β = 0.000, p = 0.062, p > 0.05, CI = -0.000, 0.000). Furthermore, coping attitudes did not regulate the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondria (β = 0.048, p = 0.212, p > 0.05).ConclusionIn conclusion, this study shows that coping attitudes in the face of pandemic-related uncertainty do not function as a buffering or explanatory mechanism in this model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04957-w.Keywords: Coping Attitudes Towards COVID-19, Cyberchondria, Coronavirus Anxiety

Introduction

Since its emergence in December 2019, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) has not only caused high rates of morbidity and mortality, but has also increased stress, anxiety and other psychological disorders in individuals [1, 2]. The pandemic has paved the way for multifaceted problems, particularly in health, economic, social, cultural, and environmental spheres, and continues to impact the world. Fear of contracting COVID-19 has become widespread throughout society, and increases in anxiety levels have been observed, particularly among individuals with chronic diseases (such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, asthma and cancer) [3, 4].

Anxiety disorders, commonly observed during the pandemic, manifest with symptoms similar to fear and worry [5]. Individuals with anxiety disorders experience symptoms such as distraction, insomnia, somatic discomfort (headaches, stomach problems), and increased heart rat [6, 7]. In this period, with the development of information and communication technologies, individuals’ desire to access health information through digital platforms has increased. Research shows that access to reliable online health resources can reduce individuals’ anxiety levels [8]. However, when individuals lack health training misinterprets this information and self-diagnose, it can further exacerbate anxiety [9]. Consequently, individuals’ constant and excessive online searches for health information increase anxiety levels [10, 11]. Web-based searches, particularly for individuals with health anxiety, become a repetitive and compulsive cycle, further fueling anxiety [12]. In line with the literature, this study aims to examine the relationship between cyberchondria and coronavirus anxiety during the COVID-19 pandemic and to evaluate the potential mediating and moderating role of COVID-19 coping strategies in this relationship. In particular, the analysis of how individuals’ strategies for coping with the pandemic shape their online health information seeking behaviors highlights the importance of this study. In addition, Furthermore, the aim of this study is to make original contributions to the literature by explaining the relationship between these strategies and individuals’ health-seeking tendencies.

Literature review

Coronavirus anxiety

The uncertainty how long the pandemic will last, how long social distancing and mask-wearing restrictions will remain in place, and when an effective and permanent treatment method will be found has led to an increase in psychiatric disorders such as anxiety disorders, depression, acute stress disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder, as well as somatic complaints such as hopelessness, fatigue, pessimism [13, 14]. COVID-19 anxiety varies according to sociodemographic factors. For example, some studies have indicated that older individuals generally experience less loneliness and stress compared to younger individuals and perceive a lower risk of COVID-19 infection [15–17]. In fact, it has been observed that the psychological effects of COVID-19 vary across age groups. In this context, anxiety levels are more pronounced among the younger population, and a research by Brailovskaia et al. (2021) found that the proportion of university students experiencing suicidal thoughts in 2020 doubled compared to previous years [18].

Cyberchondrial behavior

The term “cyberchondria,” first used in the early 2000s, is defined as an anxiety disorder that develops as a result of individuals constantly searching for health information via the internet and/or digital platforms [11]. The term is derived from the words “cyber” (internet, digital) and “hypochondria” (hypochondria) [11, 19]. As a result of the manifestation of this behavior, anxiety and stress levels increase in individuals [20].

A study conducted by Diviani et al. (2019) on individuals living in five countries found that participants frequently searched for health information online but had difficulty assessing the reliability and accuracy of this information [21]. A study conducted in the USA revealed that 70% of adults search for health-related information on the Internet, while 35% use the Internet directly as a diagnostic tool [22]. Interviews conducted with people who tested positive for COVID-19 in Turkey, it was found that the vast majority of participants trusted health information on the Internet and acted without consulting a doctor. In addition, it has been reported that the psychological effects of the pandemic have been experienced intensively, which has strengthened the tendencies towards cyberchondria [23].

Coronavirus-related anxiety significantly increases cyberchondria behavior in individuals, studies show [24, 25]. As a matter of fact, in the study conducted by Uysal Toraman, Kalkım and Konal Korkmaz (2022), a positive correlation was found between COVID-19 anxiety and cyberchondria (r = 0.423, p < 0.001) [26]. Similarly, Alanka, Çimen, and Değirmenci (2022) found a strong positive relationship between pandemic-related anxiety and online health information searching and determined that this relationship was strongly associated with cyberchondria (γ = 0.72, t = 15.07) [27]. It is noted that individuals with high levels of anxiety tend to seek health information online more frequently, and that this reinforces symptoms of cyberchondria in a vicious cycle [28]. Likewise, in a study conducted by Wu et al. (2021), fear of COVID-19 (β = 0.39, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001, t = 11.16) and anxiety (β = 0.25, SE = 0.03, p < 0.001, t = 7.67) caused by COVID-19 were found to predict cyberchondria [24]. In line with this literature information, Hypothesis 1 was developed:

Hypothesis-1. Coronavirus anxiety is positively associated with cyberchondria behavior

COVID-19 coping attitudes

COVID-19 emerged in China in December 2019 and quickly spread to more than 180 countries, leading to a pandemic declaration by the WHO [29, 30]. The epidemic has deeply affected social and economic life, creating serious traumatic effects on the psychological and mental health of individuals [31, 32]. During this period, individuals developed various cognitive and behavioral strategies to cope with stress and ensure well-being [33, 34].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, individuals’ perceptions of the pandemic, psychological reactions and coping strategies they develop have gained great importance. In this context, it has become essential to adopt new habits such as exercising, getting regular sleep, eating a balanced diet, paying attention to personal hygiene and following social distance rules [35]. Research shows that coronavirus anxiety drives individuals to develop a variety of coping strategies, both at the cognitive and behavioral levels [36, 37]. It has been observed that individuals who experience high levels of anxiety tend to be more cautious about their health, seek information and take various protective measures; therefore, it can be argued that there is a positive relationship between coronavirus anxiety and COVID-19 coping attitudes [38, 39]. Therefore, the following hypothesis was developed based on the literature:

Hypothesis-2. Coronavirus anxiety is positively associated with attitudes toward coping with COVID-19

Given the disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, it is important to assess how proactively individuals can adapt to potential future outbreaks. Sam et al. examined the relationship between coping attitudes and cyberchondria and found that proactive coping was a weak but significant negative predictor of cyberchondria. Accordingly, as individuals developed more positive and proactive coping attitudes during the COVID-19 pandemic, cyberchondria, characterized by compulsive online health information searching, decreased [40].

Similarly, another study reported that proactive coping attitudes were negatively correlated with compulsive internet use (cyberchondria) [41]. In the study by Jeong, Lee, and Jin (2023), it was found that low levels of cyberchondria reduced the negative impact of COVID-19 fear on coping effectiveness [42]. The literature also shows that coping strategies significantly shape the relationship between excessive health-related information use and cyberchondria [43].

Common coping mechanisms during the COVID-19 period included information seeking, taking protective measures, and turning to online health resources. Individuals facing uncertainty used the internet to both obtain information and reduce anxiety; however, this behavior can get out of control over time and increase cyberchondria [36, 44]. Similarly, increased internet use related to health has been found to be associated with higher levels of cyberchondria behavior [36, 45]. Furthermore, although limiting exposure to news has been suggested as a stress management strategy [46], individuals with anxiety disorders may remain highly sensitive to information and exhibit anxious responses even when using such strategies. Therefore, this study develops this hypothesis based on the assumption that individuals’ use of the internet as a tool for information seeking and coping in the face of uncertainty regarding the coronavirus pandemic may, over time, become uncontrolled and transform into cyberchondria. One study found a positive correlation between avoidance-based coping and cyberchondria [40]. Another study also found a significant positive association between cyberchondria and creative coping strategies [47]. Indeed, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the increased perception of threat and fear related to a newly identified and insufficiently understood disease, difficulties in coping with pandemic-related uncertainty, the lack of reliable and authoritative health information sources, challenges in dealing with excessive, confusing, contradictory, and constantly changing information, reduced ability to filter unnecessary information, and the inability of excessive online health information seeking to provide reassurance or adequate information all contributed to increased fear and distress, heightened perceptions of threat and uncertainty, and ultimately more online health-related searches [48]. While the relationship between coping styles and cyberchondria is not fully understood in the literature, current findings support a limited [40] but growing number of empirical studies that highlight the impact of coping strategies on cyberchondria.

Hypothesis-3. There is a significant relationship between coping styles and cyberchondria

Constant exposure to information overload and the uncertainty surrounding the pandemic, combined with increasing coronavirus anxiety, can frequently lead individuals to search for health information online; this behavior can become compulsive over time, leading to the development of cyberchondria [11]. Individuals’ coping strategies for dealing with COVID-19 may play an intermediary role in this process. Studies have shown that individuals who use coping strategies are better able to manage anxiety and are less likely to engage in behaviors such as excessive online health information seeking [38, 39]. A study by Wu et al. (2021) found that intolerance of uncertainty and anxiety sensitivity mediated the relationship between coronavirus-induced fear and anxiety and cyberchondria [24]. Similarly, in the literature, health anxiety, cyberchondria, and virus anxiety have been found to be positively associated. The combination of health anxiety and cyberchondria is associated with high levels of virus anxiety. Being informed and practicing adaptive emotion regulation may have a beneficial effect [36]. Likewise, COVID-19 has fueled health-related anxiety and worsened behaviors associated with cyberchondria [24, 49–51]. The same way, in the study conducted by Rahme et al. (2021), stress, fear of COVID-19, and to a lesser extent obsessive-compulsive disorder were found to mediate the relationship between cyberchondria severity and physical quality of life, whereas anxiety, stress, and fear of COVID-19 mediated the relationship between cyberchondria severity and mental quality of life [52].

Hypothesis-4. Attitudes toward coping with COVID-19 mediate the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondria behavior

According to the results of a web-based online study that examined the moderating effect of coping strategies on the relationship between excessive healthcare use, cyberchondria, and anxiety, fueled by increased information due to the Covid-19 pandemic, emotion-focused and avoidance-focused coping strategies significantly influenced the relationships between excessive healthcare use and cyberchondria, and between excessive healthcare use and anxiety [24]. A study by Jungmann and Witthöft determined that positive coping strategies such as planning, positive reinterpretation, and acceptance played a moderating role in the relationship between coronavirus-induced anxiety and cyberchondria [36]. In another study, higher intolerance of uncertainty predicted lower problem-focused coping and higher pandemic-related anxiety. It was also associated with greater threat perception and more emotion-focused coping [53]. In another study, a meta-analysis examining the treatment outcomes and moderating factors of cognitive behavioral therapy for hypochondriasis/health anxiety found that higher pre-treatment severity of hypochondriasis/health anxiety was associated with larger effect sizes in follow-up assessments; while the severity of depression symptoms was associated with a significant decrease in post-treatment effect sizes [54]. Therefore, based on the literature, the following hypothesis was formulated.

Hypothesis-5. Attitudes toward coping with COVID-19 moderate the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondria behavior

Research methodology

Purpose of the research

Given that the mediating role of COVID-19 coping attitudes has been more frequently examined in the literature, this study primarily aimed to test the mediating mechanism in the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondriacal behavior. Additionally, considering theoretical perspectives suggesting that this relationship may change under different conditions, a moderation analysis was conducted to examine whether the effect of coronavirus anxiety on cyberchondriacal behavior differs according to the levels of COVID-19 coping attitudes.

Participants and procedure

The study population consisted of adults aged 20–65 residing in the central towns of Ankara province, namely Keçiören, Çankaya, Yenimahalle, Mamak, Etimesgut, Sincan, Altındağ, and Pursaklar. Based on the 2020 census by the Turkish Statistical Institute, total population of these towns are 4,926,916, and this number constitutes the population size of the study universe. A total of 487 participants were reached from the central districts of Ankara using convenience sampling method. Given that the Ministry of Health is situated in Turkey’s capital, Ankara, and that this region constitutes a more sensitive area for health practices, Ankara has been chosen as the sample. Despite its limited generalizability, convenience sampling has become the most widely used method due to its ease of reaching a sufficient sample size and is often preferred for enabling the practical execution of research [55–58]. Following the attainment of ethical committee approval, the study data have been gathered within two months, during April and May 2022. The data have collected through online surveys. In the course of data collection, the survey prepared through Google Forms has sent to participants through various social media platforms such as Twitter, Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp and LinkedIn. The study received an ethics committee certificate numbered 267 dated March 31, 2022 from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences of Selçuk University, Republic of Turkey.

Scales used in the research

Online surveys consists of four parts. The first part contains the demographic information form, and the second section contains the Pandemic Coping Scale (COVID-19 Coping Attitudes [CC]). The third part includes the Cyberchondria Severity Scale Short Form (CSS-12 [CB]), and the fourth section includes the Coronavirus Anxiety Scale Short Form (CAS [CA]).

Coronavirus anxiety scale

In this study, CAS (CA) developed by Lee (2020) has employed to assess participants’ anxiety levels related to COVID-19 [59]. Scale serves as a short-term mental health screening tool designed to detect signs of anxiety associated with COVID-19. The Turkish adaptation of this scale, which has a one-dimensional structure, was made by Biçer and his friends (2021) [60].

Scale consists of five items and is answered by using a five-point Likert-type scale. In the original version, the response options were as follows: 0 = never, 1 = less than one or two days, 2 = a few days, 3 = more than seven days, and 4 = almost every day in the last two weeks [59]. In this study, responses have recoded on a 1–5 scale to facilitate data analysis. In Lee’s (2020) original study, scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficient of 0.93 [59]. In the original study, data have collected through an online survey. Similarly, this study employed an online data collection method.

The short form of the cyberchondria severity scale

The 12-item CSS-12 (CB), developed by McElroy et al. (2019), consists of four dimensions. Items 1, 3, and 6 constitute the excessiveness dimension; items 4, 8, and 9 constitute the distress dimension; items 5, 11, and 12 constitute the assurance dimension; and items 2, 7, and 10 constitute the compulsion dimension [61]. Scale is rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = always), and score obtained from scale ranges from 0 to 60. While the Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficient has 0.90 in the original study [61], it has reported as 0.89 in the Turkish adaptation Tuğtekin and Barut Tuğtekin (2021) [62].

The Pandemic Coping Scale

In this study, Pandemic Coping Scale developed by Lotzin and colleagues (2022) has used to assess individuals’ coping with pandemic-related stressors and their attitudes toward pandemic [33]. Scale consists of four dimensions: the healthy lifestyle dimension (items 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7), joyful activities dimension (items 8, 9, 12, and 13), daily structure dimension (items 10 and 11), and adherence to prevention dimension (items 1 and 2). This scale consists of 13 items rated on a four-point Likert-type scale. The scale is rated on a four-point Likert-type scale (0 = “I never did this” and 3 = “I did this a lot”). Minimum score on scale is 0 and the maximum score is 39. For the purposes of this study, items have been recoded on a 1–4 scale to facilitate analysis. Original study reported the Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient as 0.83 [33].

Asessment of research data

Descriptive statistical methods such as frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation have used in the analysis of the data. Reliability analysis has performed to test the internal consistency of the scales and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) has applied to evaluate the model fit. Correlation analysis was used to examine relationships between variables, followed by application of “Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) incorporating path analysis” to test the mediating effects. Significance of the mediating effect has assessed using the Bootstrap technique.

Findings

36.1% of the participants are between the ages of 25–35; 28.6% are between the ages of 36–46; and 25.1% are between the ages of 14–24, with an average age of 32.85. Of the participants, 59% are male and 41% are female. In terms of marital status, 54.1% were married and 45.9% were single. In terms of educational level, 41.9% have a bachelor’s degree, 28.6% have an associate degree, 19.7% are elementary school graduates, and 9.8% have postgraduate education. In terms of health insurance coverage, 43.8% are covered by the Pension Fund, 51.6% by the Social Security Institution, 1.6% by private health insurance, while 3% have no health insurance coverage.

Participants have beenasked whether they immediately consulted a doctor when they became ill; 72.1% answered “no,” while 27.9% answered “yes.” In response to the question “Which health institution do you first visit when you become ill?”, 48.9% of the participants reported that they preferred public hospitals, 36.1% stated family health centers, 11.2% indicated private hospitals, and 3.7% reported university hospitals. Regarding daily internet use, the majority of participants (56.4%) reported using the internet for 2–4 h per day, 23.2% for 5–7 h, 11.5% for 8 h or more, and 8.9% for 0–1 h.

Additionally, bootstrap methods are commonly used in mediation analysis to evaluate indirect relationships. According to this approach, if the confidence interval for the indirect relationship does not include zero, the mediation relationship is considered statistically significant. Conversely, if the confidence interval includes zero, the mediation relationship is considered insignificant [78, 79].

The findings (see Fig. 4) showed that the indirect relationship between Coronavirus Anxiety (CA) and Cyberchondria (CB) was not statistically significant through COVID-19 Coping Attitudes (CC) (β = 0.000; p = 0.062; p > 0.05; CI = -0.000, 0.000). Therefore, Hypothesis 4 was not supported. In addition, a VAF value of 0 indicated the absence of a mediating relationship [79], suggesting that CC was not significantly associated with the relationship between CA and CB.

Moderating role of COVID-19 coping attitudes in the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondria

In this study, the moderating role of COVID-19 Coping Attitudes was investigated; however, no significant moderating relationship was found. As is widely accepted in the literature, a moderating variable does not need to be highly correlated with the independent or dependent variable, but some correlation may still exist. The basic function of a moderator is to influence the strength or direction of the relationship between variables [80]. Subsequently, structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to test the proposed hypotheses. The results of the structural model are presented below.

Discussion

This study examines the relationship between coronavirus anxiety and cyberchondria, and explores the potential mediating and regulatory roles of individuals’ COVID-19 coping attitudes in this relationship. Jokic et al. (2020) conducted a study on teachers during the pandemic and found that 46% of participants frequently searched online, their search behavior increased quantitatively, and the search rate reached 75% during the second wave of the pandemic [28]. In another study, as the negative effects of the coronavirus pandemic increased, there was a corresponding increase in information-seeking behavior and levels of cyberchondria [83]. It has been determined that there has been an increase in information seeking and cyberchondria severity due to the rising anxiety levels caused by the pandemic [36, 83, 84]. The literature has found a strong positive relationship between anxiety caused by the pandemic and online health information seeking, which is strongly linked to cyberchondria [26, 27]. Wang et al. (2020) conducted an online study involving 1210 participants and found that approximately half of the participants rated the psychological effects of the pandemic as moderate to severe, about one-third experienced moderate to severe depressive symptoms, and 16% reported moderate to severe anxiety [85]. Similarly, Wu, Nazari, and Griffiths (2021) reported in their study that cyberchondria significantly influenced both fear of COVID-19 (β = 0.45, p < 0.001) and anxiety about COVID-19 (β = 0.36, p < 0.001) [24]. Similarly, Gupta et al. (2025) demonstrated a positive association between health anxiety and cyberchondria [86]. In this context, the relationship between Coronavirus anxiety (CA) and Cyberchondria (CB) (H1) is supported in this study (β = 0.426; p < 0.001). In fact, our current study revealed that coronavirus anxiety and COVID-19 coping attitudes showed a significant association with cyberchondria, explaining 43% of its variance.

On the other hand, our study found no significant relationship between Coronavirus Anxiety and Coping Attitudes with Covid (H2 hypothesis). Consistent with the findings of the present study, in a study conducted with a UK sample, no significant relationship has been observed between pandemic anxiety and coping attitudes [37]. For instance in the Pandemic Coping Scale development study, no direct significant relationships have been found between COVID-19 anxiety and some adaptive coping attitudes [33]. Similarly, in other a study correlation analysis revealed a negative relationship between coronavirus anxiety and COVID-19 with coping attitudes, while regression analysis determined that the direct effect of COVID-19 anxiety on COVID-19 with coping was negative and significant (β = − 0.27, p < 0.01) [87]. On the other hand, according to the regression analysis results of the same study, it has been concluded that COVID-19 anxiety and coping attitudes with COVID-19 significantly contribute to the prediction of general health by explaining 32% of the total variance. The study by Kandeğer et al. (2021), which partially aligns with the results of our research, found that among hospitalized COVID-19 patients, not all coping strategies have significantly been associated with anxiety and depression levels; some coping responses showed weak or nonsignificant relationships [88].

On the other hand in a study examining the relationships between different coping styles and levels of depression, anxiety, and stress during the COVID-19 period, significant correlations have also been found between anxiety levels and coping styles [88, 89]. Similarly, in China, an online study conducted with 519 university students during the COVID-19 pandemic found a statistically significant direct relationship between students’ anxiety levels and coping strategies [90]. Likewise a study examining the relationship between social support, coping strategies against COVID-19, and anxiety among Chinese university students under home quarantine concluded that cognitive and emotional coping are effective in reducing anxiety [91]. As a result, it was concluded that various coping strategies have different effects in preventing or fostering anxiety symptoms.

Sam et al. (2023) examined the relationship between coping styles and cyberchondria and found that proactive coping was a weak but significant inverse predictor of cyberchondria (β = -0.12, p < 0.05). Their findings suggest that as individuals develop more positive and proactive coping attitudes during the COVID-19 pandemic, levels of cyberchondria, characterized by compulsive online health information searching, tend to decrease [40]. Correlation analyses in the same study also showed no significant relationship between preventive coping and cyberchondria, whereas avoidant coping was positively associated with cyberchondria. Similarly, Jungmann and Witthöft (2020) stated that adaptive coping strategies such as effective emotion regulation and feeling well-informed can buffer cyberchondria, highlighting the regulatory role of coping mechanisms [36]. In contrast to these findings, the present study revealed that coping attitudes toward COVID-19 were positively associated with cyberchondria (β = 0.181; p = 0.007) (H3). This suggests that coping strategies are not always protective. In contexts characterized by high uncertainty and health-related anxiety, such as pandemics, maladaptive coping responses particularly reassurance-seeking and avoidance can inadvertently increase the search for online health information, thus intensifying cyberchondria. The literature supports the idea that individuals frequently turn to the internet to cope with health-related uncertainty in the digital age [92]. However, this behavior can escalate into excessive and uncontrolled information seeking, particularly among individuals with high levels of anxiety, thus increasing the risk of cyberchondria. Previous research has shown that coping strategies play a significant role in both the development and maintenance of cyberchondria: avoidance-based coping has been positively associated with cyberchondria [40], and even creative coping styles have been linked to increased cyberchondrial tendencies [47]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, heightened threat perception, fear of an unknown disease, and difficulties in managing uncertainty likely intensified individuals’ tendency to seek online health information [48]. Furthermore, the abundance of unreliable, excessive, and contradictory information may have amplified distress and confusion, reinforcing rather than mitigating cyberchondria. Consequently, excessive online health information seeking often fails to provide reassurance and instead contributes to increased uncertainty and anxiety. Overall, while the relationship between coping styles and cyberchondria has not been sufficiently researched [40], current findings are consistent with new evidence suggesting that coping strategies play a significant and sometimes paradoxical role in shaping cyberchondria.

In our study, we did not observe any mediating role of COVID-19 Coping Attitudes (CC) in the relationship between Coronavirus Anxiety (CA) and Cyberchondria (CB). Consistent with our research findings, a study conducted outside Turkey also found that certain adaptive coping strategies did not significantly mediate the relationship between COVID-19 anxiety and psychological distress [89]. In response to this thesis Zolotareva (2022) found that fear of COVID-19 positively influenced cyberchondria and COVID-19 preventive behaviors [93]. In the mediation analysis, it was revealed that cyberchondria mediated the relationship between fear of COVID-19 and somatic load (effect = 0.08, BOOTSE = 0.01) [93]. Additionally, Jungmann and Witthöft (2020) emphasized that people who have elevated levels of health anxiety tend to excessively look for health -related medical information online, which may intensify COVID-19-related anxiety, but that coping strategies do not play a significant role in this process [36]. Similarly, Rahme et al. (2021) found that coping strategies had no significant effect on the relationship between COVID-19 anxiety and cyberchondria in a Lebanese sample [52]. In summary, although the findings of previous studies do not coincide with the results of the present study, they suggest that coping strategies may have a limited mediating role and that this role may vary across different cultural contexts and societies.

Finally, a web-based study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic reported that seeking health-related information increased the overuse of healthcare services, and that emotion-focused and avoidance-focused coping strategies significantly influenced the relationship between this behavior, cyberchondria, and anxiety [43]. Similarly, Jungmann and Witthöft’s study found that positive coping strategies such as planning, positive reinterpretation, and acceptance regulated the relationship between coronavirus-related anxiety and cyberchondria [36]. Another study revealed that individuals with uncertainty intolerance experienced a lower sense of control, used problem-focused coping strategies less frequently, and perceived the pandemic as more threatening. These individuals also tended to rely more on emotion-focused coping strategies and reported higher levels of anxiety [53]. Furthermore, findings from another moderator analysis showed that higher pre-treatment levels of hypochondria/health anxiety were associated with larger effect sizes during follow-up assessments, whereas higher levels of depressive symptoms significantly reduced treatment effects [54]. Previous literature has highlighted the regulatory and buffering roles of coping strategies, suggesting that coping may function more appropriately as a regulatory variable rather than a mediating one. Accordingly, our study also tested the moderating role of coping attitudes; however, no significant moderating relationship was observed. This findings implies that the relationship between coping styles and cyberchondria may be complex and context-dependent. Variations in sample characteristics, measures of coping styles, and the psychological effects of the post-pandemic period may help explain these inconsistent findings. Overall, the limited and inconsistent literature indicates that further research is needed on this relationship.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the literature by addressing a contemporary issue in digital mental health within the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. The results of this study indicate that Coronavirus Anxiety and Coping Attitudes toward COVID-19 together have a significant association with cyberchondria. Individuals experiencing greater degrees of anxiety related to COVID-19 tend to engage in more cyberchondria behaviors. Similarly, those who exhibit stronger coping attitudes toward COVID-19 also show an increase in cyberchondria behaviors. Oh the other hand, the study did not find a significant direct relationship between Coronavirus Anxiety and COVID-19 Coping Attitudes. In this context, COVID-19 Coping Attitudes did not play a mediating or moderating role in the relationship between Coronavirus Anxiety and Cyberchondria.
