Voiding dysfunction in pelvic organ prolapse mimicking obstruction: an unusual presentation of sacral chordoma – A case report

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPelvic organ prolapse (POP) is commonly associated with voiding dysfunction, often presumed to result from bladder outlet obstruction. However, neurological or structural lesions may mimic or coexist with prolapse-related dysfunction. Background

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is common among older women, with prevalence estimates ranging from 24 to 38% [1]. One of the most frequent lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) associated with POP is voiding dysfunction, which includes hesitancy, weak urinary stream, straining, incomplete emptying, and urinary retention [2]. Previous studies have demonstrated that LUTS and voiding dysfunction can occur across different stages of POP and that symptom severity does not consistently correlate with prolapse compartment or stage [2–4]. These symptoms are often attributed to bladder outlet obstruction from the descent of the anterior compartment. Improvement in voiding after prolapse correction supports this assumption, with studies showing up to 80% symptomatic relief following pessary use or surgery [3].

However, not all voiding dysfunction in POP is mechanical. Urodynamic studies reveal that detrusor underactivity (DU) or acontractile detrusor may contribute to retention in some patients [4]. Causes include diabetes, idiopathic neuropathy, or prior injury to pelvic nerves. Less commonly, spinal or sacral tumors can present with LUTS and mimic prolapse-related obstruction [5].

Chordoma is a rare, slow-growing malignant bone tumor that arises from remnants of the notochord. Its incidence is about 0.08 per 100,000 individuals annually, with a peak between 50 and 70 years [6]. Approximately one-third of chordomas occur in the sacrum, where their indolent growth allows them to reach large size before diagnosis. Symptoms are nonspecific and may include pelvic pain, constipation, or urinary retention due to sacral nerve compression [7].

Here, we describe an unusual case of sacral chordoma in a woman with POP who developed progressive voiding dysfunction. The case highlights the importance of comprehensive assessment, including urodynamics and imaging, when symptoms are disproportionate to prolapse severity.

Case Presentation

A 71-year-old Thai woman, gravida 2 para 2, presented with a two-year history of vaginal bulging and pelvic pressure. She reported mild lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) at baseline, including occasional stress urinary incontinence on coughing or sneezing, urgency once or twice per week, and mild voiding difficulty that did not initially impact her quality of life. She also had chronic constipation, which had been present for several years and improved with dietary modifications such as yogurt and fruit intake. She was widowed, sexually inactive, and had an educational attainment of primary school level.

Her past medical history included well-controlled hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia. She was taking atorvastatin, lercanidipine, and metformin. She denied any drug or food allergies, smoking, or alcohol use. Past surgical history included a total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy 30 years earlier for uterine myoma, with benign pathology. Family history was notable for a sister with breast cancer.

Two years prior, pelvic examination confirmed stage II pelvic organ prolapse, and a ring pessary with support was fitted. After 4–5 months, she discontinued its use due to discomfort. Given persistent prolapse-related symptoms and the patient’s preference for definitive management, surgical management with colpocleisis was subsequently considered. Five months before admission, she developed progressive voiding dysfunction with weak stream, straining, and incomplete emptying. At that time, the symptoms were perceived by the patient as mild and consistent with prolapse and therefore did not prompt further evaluation until subsequent progression occurred. Subsequently, she presented with acute urinary retention and required indwelling Foley catheterization. Four months prior to referral, she was hospitalized for febrile urinary tract infection and discharged with a Foley catheter in situ. There was no clear temporal worsening of bowel symptoms or new-onset fecal incontinence concurrent with the progression of her voiding dysfunction.

On physical examination, she appeared healthy, body mass index (BMI) 23.6 kg/m², with normal vital signs. General, cardiovascular, and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. The abdomen showed an old surgical scar and a palpable distended bladder. Extremities were without edema. Neurological examination demonstrated intact motor and sensory function in all extremities (power grade V), though straight leg raise test was positive on the right side, consistent with her past history of lumbar spondylosis. Pelvic examination demonstrated stage II pelvic organ prolapse based on the POP-Q system, with measurements of Aa + 1 cm, Ba + 1 cm, C − 1 cm, Ap − 2 cm, Bp − 2 cm, D − 4 cm, genital hiatus 4 cm, perineal body 3 cm, and total vaginal length 6 cm. These findings were comparable to those documented two years earlier, with no evidence of prolapse progression. Cough stress test was negative. Pelvic floor muscle strength, assessed by Brink score, was 6/12. Rectal examination identified an external hemorrhoid and a low transsphincteric fistula-in-ano, for which colorectal consultation was obtained.

Initial laboratory work-up showed normal urinalysis, serum creatinine of 0.7 mg/dL, and eGFR of 84 mL/min/1.73 m². Renal ultrasonography revealed no hydronephrosis, but the bladder demonstrated trabeculation and a 2.3-cm diverticulum on the left lateral wall. Colonoscopy was unremarkable.

Uroflowmetry showed negligible voided volume, with a post-void residual of 550 mL. Cystometry revealed normal bladder sensation (first desire 270 mL, strong desire 466 mL) and compliance. Voiding cystometry was performed after manual reduction of the prolapse to minimize the potential effect of anatomical obstruction, and demonstrated an acontractile detrusor with persistent high residuals (Fig. 1).

Pelvic MRI demonstrated a large heterogeneous presacral mass measuring 10.0 × 6.7 × 9.4 cm, centered at S1–S3, extending into the presacral space, compressing the thecal sac at L4/L5, and displacing both sciatic nerves and piriformis muscles (Fig. 2). Core needle biopsy confirmed conventional chordoma, with immunohistochemical positivity for AE1/AE3 and EMA, focal positivity for S100, and negativity for SOX10.

The planned colpocleisis for POP was cancelled, and she was referred to a multidisciplinary team including orthopedic surgery, colorectal surgery, and radiation oncology. Given the tumor’s extent, radical en bloc resection was not feasible. She commenced proton beam radiotherapy (30 fractions over 8 weeks). Bladder management consisted of indwelling Foley catheterization, as she was unable to perform self-catheterization. Constipation was managed with lactulose. At follow-up, the prolapse persisted but was no longer her primary concern, as her care priorities shifted to cancer treatment.

Discussion and Conclusion

This case represents an unexpected and rare presentation of sacral chordoma in a patient with POP, highlighting the diagnostic challenges when common pelvic floor disorders coexist with rare malignant disease. POP is frequently associated with voiding dysfunction, most often due to bladder outlet obstruction, with a prevalence of voiding difficulty reported between 45 and 57% of affected women [8]. While voiding dysfunction is commonly encountered in women with pelvic organ prolapse, its severity and underlying mechanisms are heterogeneous and not solely determined by prolapse stage. In clinical practice, it is tempting to attribute all LUTS to prolapse, particularly in elderly women. In this patient, the progression of voiding dysfunction despite pessary use is a clinically important observation. Pessary placement can serve not only as a therapeutic option but also as a functional test to assess whether voiding symptoms are attributable to prolapse-related outlet obstruction [3]. Persistence or worsening of symptoms following prolapse reduction should prompt re-evaluation and consideration of alternative etiologies [2]. In such cases, urodynamic studies play a key role in distinguishing mechanical obstruction from non-obstructive causes of voiding dysfunction, as demonstrated in this patient [6]. Notably, in this patient, the progression to complete urinary retention and the finding of an acontractile detrusor on urodynamics were disproportionate to her stage II prolapse, supporting a non-obstructive, neurogenic etiology.

Chordoma is a malignant bone tumor of notochordal origin, with an incidence of approximately 0.08 per 100,000 per year and a median age of onset of 58 years. The sacrum is the second most common site after the skull base, accounting for nearly one-third of cases [7]. Its clinical manifestations are nonspecific and depend on location and size. In the sacrum, tumors may present with localized pain, radiculopathy, constipation, and bladder dysfunction due to sacral nerve involvement [9]. Because of their indolent growth, sacral chordomas are often large at diagnosis, with mean reported sizes > 8 cm [10].

From a neurourology perspective, this case emphasizes several key lessons. First, not all LUTS in POP are obstructive; severe or progressive symptoms out of proportion to the degree of prolapse should raise suspicion for neurogenic or structural causes. Although bowel symptoms were not progressive in this patient, concurrent bowel dysfunction may serve as an additional clinical clue to neurogenic pathology in similar cases. Second, urodynamic studies are invaluable in such scenarios; here, demonstration of an acontractile detrusor provided the diagnostic clue that voiding dysfunction was neurogenic rather than obstructive [11]. Third, MRI is essential in atypical presentations, enabling identification of a large presacral mass compressing the sacral plexus [12]. Finally, management priorities shifted entirely once the diagnosis of chordoma was established; POP surgery was cancelled, and oncologic therapy with proton beam radiotherapy was initiated, avoiding unnecessary and potentially harmful intervention.

Given the finding of an acontractile detrusor, a neurological etiology must be carefully considered. Referral for neurological evaluation is warranted to investigate further potential underlying causes, including sacral nerve compression or rare entities such as sacral chordoma. In this case, despite the absence of overt neurological deficits or bowel dysfunction, which may argue against an advanced primary neurological syndrome, the presence of a large sacral mass on imaging confirmed a structural neurogenic cause. This highlights that significant neurological pathology may still be present even in the absence of classic neurological symptoms.

Importantly, evaluation of detrusor underactivity in this patient must also account for other contributing factors. Long-standing pelvic organ prolapse may lead to chronic bladder outlet obstruction and subsequent detrusor decompensation. Additionally, diabetes mellitus is a well-recognized cause of autonomic neuropathy and impaired bladder contractility. Therefore, while the sacral chordoma represents the primary pathological driver in this case, the patient’s clinical presentation is likely multifactorial. Recognizing this interplay is essential for accurate diagnosis and individualized management.

Although urinary retention has been described in sacral chordoma, the coexistence with POP is extremely rare and has not been well reported in the literature. This overlap is educational, as it demonstrates how common gynecologic conditions may mask or delay the recognition of rare malignancies. By documenting this case, we shed light on the pathophysiology of LUTS in the setting of sacral tumors, provide an example of how comprehensive evaluation can alter clinical management, and emphasize the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration.

Although urinary retention has been described in patients with sacral chordoma, previous case reports have typically presented in the context of neurological or musculoskeletal complaints, such as back pain, radiculopathy, or isolated urinary dysfunction [13]. To our knowledge, no prior report has described sacral chordoma masquerading as voiding dysfunction in the setting of concomitant pelvic organ prolapse. This coexistence created diagnostic ambiguity, as the presence of prolapse initially suggested a more common and benign explanation for the patient’s symptoms. Our case, therefore, expands the literature by highlighting a unique overlap between a prevalent benign condition and a rare malignant tumor, reinforcing the need for heightened diagnostic vigilance.

From a clinical perspective, this case emphasizes the role of urodynamic studies as a gatekeeper before prolapse surgery. In women with POP, surgical correction is often pursued for symptom relief, but when LUTS appear disproportionate, urodynamics can reveal underlying detrusor underactivity or acontractile bladder, prompting further investigation [14]. Comprehensive preoperative evaluation, including urodynamic studies, is essential in patients with pelvic organ prolapse and suspected detrusor underactivity to ensure accurate diagnosis and to avoid inappropriate surgical intervention, in line with current clinical guidelines. In our patient, urodynamics were critical in redirecting the diagnostic pathway toward neurogenic dysfunction rather than outlet obstruction. For neuro-urology practice, this case also underlines the importance of a multidisciplinary approach. Management of sacral chordoma often requires proton beam radiotherapy when radical resection is not feasible, while bladder dysfunction is best approached with individualized strategies including catheterization, UTI prevention, and, in selected cases, sacral neuromodulation or other advanced interventions [15]. The ultimate goal remains preservation of renal function, prevention of infection, and maintenance of quality of life.
