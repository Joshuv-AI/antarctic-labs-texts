Stage IIIC Bilateral Dysgerminoma in a 16‐Year‐Old Phenotypic Female With 46,XY Complete Gonadal Dysgenesis and Primary Amenorrhea: A Case Report
Authors: Mahshid Vasef, Noushin Afshar Moghaddam, Masoomeh Raoufi, Maryam Iranipour, Samar Moghadami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDysgerminoma, the most common malignant ovarian germ cell tumor, has a significantly increased incidence in individuals with 46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis (Swyer syndrome). However, primary amenorrhea is the hallmark presentation of Swyer syndrome in adolescence. This case highlights the critical intersection of these conditions and the oncologic risks of diagnostic delay. A 16‐year‐old phenotypic female presented with primary amenorrhea. Physical examination revealed normal secondary sexual characteristics and unremarkable pelvic findings. Laboratory evaluation revealed hypergonadotropic hypogonadism with markedly elevated FSH and LH levels. Karyotype analysis revealed a 46,XY chromosomal complement, diagnosing Swyer syndrome. Imaging identified multiple pelvic masses, including a 10.4 cm anterior uterine mass and a para‐aortic mass, highly suspicious for malignancy. Exploratory laparotomy with tumor resection and staging confirmed bilateral ovarian dysgerminoma with para‐aortic and pelvic lymph node metastasis, classified as FIGO Stage IIIC (pT1bN1Mx). The patient was referred for adjuvant chemotherapy. This case underscores that primary amenorrhea with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism mandates karyotype analysis to exclude 46,XY DSD and its associated high risk of gonadal malignancy. Symptomatic treatment without definitive etiological investigation can lead to dangerous delays, allowing for progression to advanced cancer. A prompt, systematic diagnostic approach is essential to prevent serious oncologic sequelae in these patients.

Introduction

Dysgerminoma is the most common malignant germ cell tumor of the ovary, predominantly occurring in children and young adults [1]. While these tumors are rare in the general population, their incidence is significantly increased in individuals with Disorders of Sex Development (DSD), particularly in those with 46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis (Swyer syndrome) [2, 3]. In this syndrome, due to a failure in gonadal differentiation during embryonic development, an individual with a male karyotype (46,XY) presents with a female phenotype, normal Müllerian structures, and immature, dysfunctional gonads. The high risk of malignant transformation primarily into gonadoblastoma and dysgerminoma in these dysgenetic gonads necessitates early diagnosis and prophylactic management [4].

However, the primary diagnostic challenge arises when the initial clinical presentation of this condition is not an incidental finding, but is accompanied by signs of an advanced malignancy. Primary amenorrhea is the classic and often sole complaint in patients with Swyer syndrome identified during adolescence. The concurrent diagnosis of primary amenorrhea due to gonadal dysgenesis and an advanced malignant germ cell tumor represents a relatively uncommon and clinically complex scenario [4, 5].

In this case report, we present the case of a 16‐year‐old adolescent with a complete female phenotype and a 46,XY karyotype who presented with the complaint of primary amenorrhea. Subsequent evaluations revealed the presence of bilateral dysgerminoma at FIGO Stage IIIC. The objectives of this report are: (1) to emphasize the importance of considering disorders of sex development and the high risk of malignancy in any adolescent patient presenting with primary amenorrhea and hypergonadotropic hypogonadism; (2) to review the management challenges when advanced disease coincides with the initial diagnosis of the underlying syndrome; and (3) to contribute to the limited body of literature reporting cases where the tumor is at an advanced stage (Stage III) at diagnosis.

Case History/Examination

A 16‐year‐old phenotypic female with fully developed secondary sexual characteristics (Tanner V) was referred to our tertiary gynecology‐endocrinology clinic for the evaluation of primary amenorrhea. Her history was notable for an episode of menstrual irregularity 3 years prior, which was investigated with pelvic ultrasound and revealed a hemorrhagic ovarian cyst. She was treated with combined oral contraceptives (LD pills) for approximately 14 months (age 13–14 years), resulting in withdrawal bleeding but no spontaneous cyclic menses after discontinuation. Breast development to Tanner V occurred during this exogenous estrogen exposure. At presentation to our clinic (8 months after stopping contraceptives), estradiol was severely suppressed (< 9 pg/mL), reflecting underlying hypogonadism. She reported no other significant medical or surgical history and was not on any chronic medications.

On presentation, physical examination confirmed a normal female phenotype with appropriate breast development and unremarkable external genitalia. Abdominal and bimanual pelvic examinations were normal, with no palpable masses, despite the subsequent imaging findings of a large pelvic mass.

Laboratory and Imaging Findings

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed a profound hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, with markedly elevated follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH 45.1 IU/L, later 58.8 IU/L) and luteinizing hormone (LH 27.7 IU/L) levels concurrent with a severely suppressed estradiol level (< 9 pg/mL). Anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH) was initially reported as 5.0 ng/mL; however, repeat testing confirmed an undetectable level (< 0.1 ng/mL), consistent with the absence of functional ovarian tissue in complete gonadal dysgenesis. Tumor marker assessment showed a significantly elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 695 U/L, while alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) was normal at 2.3 ng/mL and free beta‐human chorionic gonadotropin (β‐hCG) was borderline at 1.93 IU/L. Other markers, including CA‐125, were within normal limits (See Table 1).

Given the clinical picture of primary amenorrhea with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, a karyotype analysis was performed, which revealed a 46,XY chromosomal complement, consistent with a diagnosis of 46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis (Swyer syndrome).

Pelvic ultrasound and subsequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were critical in identifying the associated gonadal pathology. Imaging demonstrated a normal but immature uterus with a thin endometrium and strikingly small, prepubertal‐appearing ovaries. However, multiple solid‐cystic pelvic masses were identified, the largest being a 10.4 cm mass anterior to the uterus with necrotic components, alongside bilateral adnexal masses. Additionally, a para‐aortic mass was noted on imaging. The radiological differentials strongly favored malignant germ cell tumors arising from dysgenetic gonads (See Table 2).

Differential Diagnosis

The primary differential diagnosis based on the clinical presentation of primary amenorrhea with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism included: The differential diagnosis encompassed 46,XY Complete Gonadal Dysgenesis (Swyer Syndrome), confirmed by karyotype and explaining both the endocrine profile and high malignancy risk; Turner Syndrome, which shares hormonal features but not the tumor risk; and Premature Ovarian Insufficiency, uncommon in adolescence and unrelated to Y‐chromosome malignancy. Other considerations included pelvic malignancies such as non‐dysgerminoma germ cell tumors or epithelial tumors, though elevated LDH and imaging favored dysgerminoma, and benign ovarian masses like dermoid cysts, later excluded by imaging and histology.

The confirmation of a 46,XY karyotype, elevated LDH, and characteristic imaging findings narrowed the diagnosis to Swyer syndrome with a high suspicion for associated malignant germ cell tumor, later confirmed as bilateral dysgerminoma.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Due to the high suspicion of malignancy in the setting of XY gonadal dysgenesis, the patient underwent exploratory laparotomy. The surgical procedure included bilateral salpingo‐gonadectomy, resection of a para‐aortic mass, bilateral pelvic and common iliac lymphadenectomy, and omentectomy. Histopathological examination of the resected specimens confirmed bilateral ovarian dysgerminoma, with the largest tumor measuring 12 cm on the left side (See Figure 1). Furthermore, the para‐aortic mass was also identified as dysgerminoma, and metastatic involvement was confirmed in one pelvic lymph node. The postoperative course was uncomplicated, and the patient was referred to oncology for adjuvant chemotherapy. The patient's legal guardian provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Discussion

Our patient's clinical journey echoes and amplifies several key themes highlighted in the literature, most notably in the recent report by Sowińska‐Przepiera et al., which described a late diagnosis of Swyer syndrome following contraceptive treatment for primary amenorrhea [6]. Both cases underscore the alarming reality that a significant diagnostic delay can occur when primary amenorrhea in an adolescent with a female phenotype is managed symptomatically such as with hormonal contraceptives to induce withdrawal bleeding without pursuing its underlying etiology. In our patient, a three‐year history of irregular spotting post‐LD pills masked the need for a definitive work‐up, allowing for the progression of malignancy. This aligns with Sowińska‐Przepiera et al.'s finding that empirical treatment can dangerously postpone the identification of the high‐risk dysgenetic gonad [6].

This case also highlights the evolving role of genetic diagnostics in DSD. While the underlying genetic etiology was not identified in this patient, advanced molecular techniques can identify causal mutations in a subset of 46,XY DSD cases. However, in clinical practice, the absence of a specific molecular diagnosis does not delay the imperative for prophylactic gonadectomy once the 46,XY karyotype is confirmed.

The association between dysgerminoma and congenital reproductive tract anomalies is notably rare and underscores the diverse origins of this tumor. An illustrative contrast is provided by Kim et al., who reported a case of ovarian dysgerminoma in a 34‐year‐old woman with a concurrent Müllerian anomaly but a normal 46,XX karyotype [7]. This case highlights that dysgerminoma can occasionally arise in the setting of anatomical anomalies without an underlying disorder of sex development or Y‐chromosome material. Our case, however, presents the prototypical and high‐risk association between dysgerminoma and 46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis, where the presence of dysgenetic gonads harboring Y‐chromosome material confers a dramatically increased malignant potential. This stark dichotomy reinforces a critical clinical axiom: the discovery of a dysgerminoma, particularly in a young patient with amenorrhea or genital tract abnormalities, must prompt a thorough investigation for both anatomical anomalies and, more urgently, for DSD with karyotype analysis.

Conclusion

This case underscores that primary amenorrhea requires definitive diagnosis, not just symptomatic treatment. In adolescents with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, karyotyping is mandatory to rule out 46,XY DSD and its high risk of malignancy. Early detection and prophylactic gonadectomy can prevent advanced cancer, as seen in our patient's progression to stage IIIC dysgerminoma. This report reinforces the need for prompt, systematic evaluation to avert serious oncologic and reproductive sequelae.
