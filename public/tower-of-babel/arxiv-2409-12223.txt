Lie algebraic invariants in quantum linear optics
Pablo V. Parellada, Vicent Gimeno i Garcia, Julio José Moyano-Fernández, Juan Carlos Garcia-Escartin

Abstract
Quantum linear optics is a promising candidate for obtaining a quantum computational advantage. However, linear optics without post-selection is not powerful enough to produce any quantum state from a given input state. This limits its utility since some computations require entangled resources that are difficult to prepare. Thus, we need a deeper understanding of linear optical state preparation. In this work, we give a recipe to derive conserved quantities in the evolution of arbitrary states along any possible passive linear interferometer. We obtain the invariants by projecting the density operator of the state onto the Lie algebra of passive linear optical Hamiltonians. The conservation of the invariants gives necessary conditions for exact and approximate state preparation with passive linear optics: if input and output states have different invariants, it will be impossible to design a linear optical experiment that evolves one into the other or a sufficiently close one. Therefore, the invariants allow us to narrow the search when trying to prepare entangled states useful for quantum information, like Bell or NOON states, from easy-to-prepare states, like Fock states. We conclude that future exact and approximate state preparation methods will need to take into account the necessary conditions given by our invariants to weed out impossible linear optical evolutions.



Introduction.

Passive linear interferometers, like the Michelson interferometer, are simple devices that can be readily built in the lab and have ubiquitous applications in physics. They consist of a set of modes (distinct spatial paths, orthogonal polarizations…) in which light can propagate and some elements, beam splitters and phase shifters, that create an interaction between the light in the modes.

Even though linear interferometers arose as classical optical devices, they can be studied from the quantum formalism, where they exhibit surprising properties like the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect
Hong et al. 1987
. Quantum linear optics has brought lots of applications in quantum metrology
Barbieri 2022
, quantum communication
Duan et al. 2001
and quantum computation
Kok et al. 2007
.

However, linear optics alone is not powerful enough to perform universal quantum computation, since it cannot implement all unitary gates —except for an exponential number of modes and one photon
Cerf et al. 1998
;
Moyano-Fernández and Garcia-Escartin 2017
. To perform optical quantum computing we generally need some kind of non-linearity, like measurement
Knill et al. 2001
, or exotic resources, like GKP states
Konno et al. 2024
. This might seem a bit disappointing, but Aaronson and Arkhipov proved that linear optics alone can solve some problems —namely
boson sampling
— much faster than classical computers
Aaronson and Arkhipov 2013
, giving rise to the first “quantum advantage” that could be tested experimentally
Brod et al. 2019
. This result positioned quantum linear optical devices as an intermediate candidate between a classical computer and a fully-fledged quantum computer (see, for example, photonic integrated circuits
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Elshaari et al. 2020
;
Bogaerts et al. 2020
;
Taballione et al. 2021
;
Hoch et al. 2022
;
Taballione et al. 2023
).

As we have said, not all quantum evolutions can be implemented with passive linear optics. This raises the question: given two arbitrary photonic states, is there a linear optical evolution connecting them? This state preparation problem is especially interesting since many useful applications of linear optics begin with some entangled input state. While giving a sufficient condition is tough and hasn’t been answered yet in the general case (for two modes or two photons see
Migdał et al. 2014
), there are necessary conditions that must be fulfilled. The simplest one is the conservation of the mean number of photons: if the states have different mean values, the transition is impossible. Other invariants have been previously proposed for a fixed number of photons
Migdał et al. 2014
;
Parellada et al. 2023
.

In this Letter, we use the Lie algebras describing passive linear optics to define other quantities that must be conserved if there is a linear optical evolution connecting two states. That is, each state can be assigned different invariants and if any of them does not coincide there cannot exist a passive interferometer connecting them. An advantage over previous invariants in the literature is that they can be applied to arbitrary mixed states in Fock space. Additionally, we show that for many of those invariants, if we want to reach a state close in trace distance to a given target, the input and target states must also have close invariants.

Moreover, readers not particularly interested in linear optics can find useful mathematical tools in this article. For example, the adjoint action and the projections of quantum states onto subalgebras —which we use to derive the invariants— have been used recently to study barren plateaus in variational quantum circuits
Fontana et al. 2024
;
Ragone et al. 2024
.

Mathematical preliminaries.

Mathematically, passive linear interferometers are described by a unitary matrix
S
of size
m\times m
, being
m
the number of modes in the interferometer
Reck et al. 1994
. In classical optics, this matrix gives the evolution of the amplitudes of each mode. In quantum optics, it gives the evolution of the creation and annihilation operators:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
\end{array}\right)\rightarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}S_{11}&S_{12}\\
S_{21}&S_{22}\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
\end{array}\right)\>.

(1)

The unitarity of the scattering matrix
S
implies that passive linear optics conserves the total number of photons.

Can we find more invariants in passive linear optics? It turns out we can and to do it we are going to use mathematical tools like Lie algebras, homomorphisms and the adjoint action. We will first review the Lie algebras of linear optical systems. Then, we will link the quantum evolution of linear optical Hamiltonians (in the Heisenberg picture) with the adjoint action.

The evolution of a quantum state along a passive linear interferometer is given by a unitary operator

{\rm U}(m)
and

.

is a Hermitian

Our invariants will arise from expectation values of a basis of linear optical Hamiltonians. But not any basis is valid; as we shall see, we need one that originates from an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{u}(m)
. Let
\{b_{i}\}
be such orthonormal basis, we map it to a basis of linear optical Hamiltonians:
\{O_{i}=d\varphi(b_{i})\}
. An example of such a basis is given by:

\displaystyle O_{i}=n_{j}=a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j}

\displaystyle{\rm for}\quad j=1,\ldots,m,\!\qquad

\displaystyle O_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{k}+a^{\dagger}_{k}a_{j}\right)

\displaystyle{\rm for}\quad 1\leq j<k\leq m,

(2)

\displaystyle O_{i}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{k}-a^{\dagger}_{k}a_{j}\right)

\displaystyle{\rm for}\quad 1\leq j<k\leq m,

which is the image of an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{u}(m)
. This basis is especially interesting because we can measure the expectation values of these operators experimentally. First, we can count the number of photons in each mode (although experimentally it is not trivial). Then, operators
a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{k}+a^{\dagger}_{k}a_{j}
correspond to a 50:50 beam splitter in modes

and
k
, and measuring
n_{j}-n_{k}

after the beam splitter

Now, why do we need
\{b_{i}\}
to be orthonormal? This becomes clear when we study the evolution of operators
O_{i}
under a linear optical unitary
U
(Heisenberg picture). Using properties of Lie group and Lie algebra homomorphism, the adjoint action
{\rm Ad}_{U^{\dagger}}(O_{i})
turns out to be

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}O_{i}U=\varphi(S^{\dagger})\,d\varphi(b_{i})\,\varphi(S)=d\varphi\left(S^{\dagger}b_{i}S\right)=

(3)

\displaystyle d\varphi\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m^{2}}C_{ij}b_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{m^{2}}C_{ij}d\varphi(b_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{m^{2}}C_{ij}O_{j}.

To understand the previous equation, note that the adjoint action of a Lie group on its Lie algebra always stays inside the algebra:
{\rm Ad}_{S^{\dagger}}(b_{i})=S^{\dagger}b_{i}S=\sum_{j}C_{ij}b_{j}
. And the same applies to the adjoint action
{\rm Ad}_{U^{\dagger}}(O_{i})
.

If we choose an orthonormal
\{b_{i}\}
, the coefficient matrix
C
must be orthogonal:
CC^{T}=I
. This is straightforward from
{\rm tr}\left(b_{i}b_{j}\right)={\rm tr}\left({\rm Ad}_{S^{\dagger}}(b_{i}){\rm Ad}_{S^{\dagger}}(b_{j})\right)
. The fact that the quantum evolution of the operators
O_{i}
is given by an orthogonal matrix in equation (
3
) is the key to deriving our invariants:

Lemma 1
.

If
\{b_{i}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{u}(m)
and
\{O_{i}\}
is its image under
d\varphi
, then for any linear optical unitary
U
we have
{\rm Ad}_{U^{\dagger}}(O_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{m^{2}}C_{ij}O_{j}
, where
C
is an orthogonal matrix.

Now that we have all the mathematical ingredients ready, we can give some examples of invariants that we have found useful (we could define as many as we wanted using the same tricks). The invariants will be quantities associated with a density operator in Fock space that will be conserved along the evolution of that state through any passive linear interferometer.

Invariants.

Our first invariant is defined via the projections of a density operator,
\rho
, into the subalgebra of linear optical Hamiltonians generated by
\{O_{i}\}
:

I(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}{\rm tr}(O_{i}\rho)^{2}\>.

(4)

This invariant is similar to the
Lie algebraic purity
invariant defined in
Barnum et al. 2003
, with the difference that the
O_{i}
neither have to be orthonormal nor restricted to a finite-dimensional Hilbert space (they are, in fact, unbounded operators). As mentioned before, the expectation values
\langle O_{i}\rangle_{\rho}={\rm tr}(O_{i}\rho)
can all be measured, so the invariant can be determined experimentally as long as we have multiple copies of the state
\rho
.

To prove that it is indeed invariant, we evolve
\rho
under linear optical evolution:

\displaystyle I(U\rho U^{\dagger})=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}{\rm tr}(U^{\dagger}O_{i}U\rho)^{2}=\sum_{i}{\rm tr}(\sum_{j}C_{ij}O_{j}\rho)^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\,j^{\prime}}\delta_{jj^{\prime}}{\rm tr}(O_{j}\rho){\rm tr}(O_{j^{\prime}}\rho)=\sum_{j}{\rm tr}(O_{j}\rho)^{2}=I(\rho)\>,

(5)

where
\sum_{i}C_{ji}C_{ij^{\prime}}^{T}=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}
because
C
is orthogonal. This proves the following Theorem:

Theorem 1
.

I(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}{\rm tr}(O_{i}\rho)^{2}
is invariant under linear optical evolution.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, the conservation of
I(\rho)
gives a necessary condition for the existence of a linear optical
U
connecting
\rho
with another state in Fock space.

Figure 1:
For a given state
\rho
, there is a subset of states in Fock space that we can reach with linear optics (LO) and it is contained in the set of states sharing the same invariant.

Oftentimes, we are not interested in preparing an exact state, but a sufficiently similar one (Fig.
2
). How close are the invariants of similar states? Bounding this difference gives a necessary condition for preparing similar states with linear optics. We now give a bound for pure states, leaving the details and the more complicated case of mixed states for the Supplemental Information.

Figure 2:
Given an input state
\rho
and a target
\sigma
, can we find a passive interferometer
S
such that
\rho^{\prime}=U\rho U^{\dagger}
is close to
\sigma
?

Let
\rho
be an input pure state,
\rho^{\prime}
the output and
\sigma
a target pure state that we would like to prepare from
\rho
. From Theorem
1
, the invariants of the input and output are equal. The distance between the input and target invariants is bounded by the number of modes
m
, the mean total photon number
\langle n\rangle
and the trace distance between the output and the target states:

|I(\rho)-I(\sigma)|\leq m^{2}(\langle{n}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime}}+\langle{n}\rangle_{\sigma})^{2}\lVert\rho^{\prime}-\sigma\rVert_{1}\>.

(6)

This inequality gives a necessary condition for approximate state preparation with linear optics: if the invariants of the input and target are very distant, approximate preparation of
\sigma
with linear optics is impossible.

The invariant from Theorem
1
is quite simple, but it is one-dimensional and does not provide a lot of information. We can define more invariants following the same recipe (for other invariants see the Supplemental Information).

For example, let us define the projection of the density matrix onto the subalgebra of linear optical Hamiltonians:
\rho_{T}:=\sum_{i}{\rm tr}(O_{i}\rho)O_{i}
. This projection is very interesting because linear optical evolution keeps it inside the subalgebra:

\displaystyle(U\rho U^{\dagger})_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}{\rm tr}(O_{i}U\rho U^{\dagger})O_{i}=

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}C_{ij}{\rm tr}(O_{j}\rho)O_{i}=\sum_{j}{\rm tr}(O_{j}\rho)UO_{j}U^{\dagger}=U\rho_{T}U^{\dagger}\>.

This immediately implies that the spectrum of this projection is invariant:

Theorem 2
.

The spectrum of
\rho_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}{\rm tr}(O_{i}\rho)O_{i}
is invariant under linear optical evolution.

Once again, close states must have a close spectral invariant. Let
\rho
be an input,
\rho^{\prime}
an output and
\sigma
a target state. Using a stability theorem for the perturbation of Hermitian operators
Bhatia 1982
(see the Supplemental Information), for any
\delta>0
there exists an ordering of the eigenvalues of the projections
\rho_{T}
and
\sigma_{T}
(
\{\lambda^{\rho}_{i}\}
and
\{\lambda^{\sigma}_{i}\}
, respectively) such that

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\lambda^{\rho}_{i}-\lambda^{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\lVert\rho^{\prime}-\sigma\rVert_{1}+\delta\>.

(8)

Therefore, if the projected input and target states have very distant spectra, approximate state preparation is impossible.

Another interesting invariant from a physical point of view arises from the covariance matrix:

Theorem 3
.

The spectrum of the covariance matrix

M(\rho)_{ij}=\langle O_{i}\rangle_{\rho}\langle O_{j}\rangle_{\rho}-\left\langle\frac{O_{i}O_{j}+O_{j}O_{i}}{2}\right\rangle_{\rho}

(9)

is invariant under linear optical evolution.

To prove it, note that

{\rm tr}\left(O_{i}O_{j}U\rho U^{\dagger}\right)=\sum_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{m^{2}}C^{T}_{ii^{\prime}}{\rm tr}\left(O_{i^{\prime}}O_{j^{\prime}}\rho\right)C_{j^{\prime}j}

(10)

and similarly for the other terms. This implies that the spectrum of the covariance is, indeed, invariant:

{\rm spec}\left(M(U\rho U^{\dagger})\right)={\rm spec}\left(C^{T}M(\rho)C\right)={\rm spec}\left(M(\rho)\right)\>.

(11)

This covariance, in theory, can be measured experimentally
Campos et al. 1989
, but the implementation of successive measurements of
O_{i}
and
O_{j}
would require non-destructive measurements, which are challenging with current technology
Grangier et al. 1998
;
Nemoto and Munro 2005
.

Additionally, in the Supplemental Information we show that if two states are close in trace distance, their covariance invariants must be close too.

Examples.

An advantage of this method to define invariants is that we can apply them to any state in Fock space. For example, let
\left|\alpha\right\rangle\left|\beta\right\rangle
be two coherent states, one in each mode of the multiport interferometer. The invariant from Theorem
1
,
I(\left|\alpha\right\rangle\left|\beta\right\rangle)
, can be easily calculated from the expectation values:

\displaystyle\langle{n}_{1}\rangle^{2}+\langle{n}_{2}\rangle^{2}+\left\langle{\frac{a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{2}+a^{\dagger}_{2}a_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}}\right\rangle^{2}+\left\langle{i\frac{a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{2}-a^{\dagger}_{2}a_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=|\alpha|^{4}+|\beta|^{4}+2|\alpha|^{2}|\beta|^{2}=(|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2})^{2}\>.

(12)

Any passive linear evolution of two coherent beams must conserve this quantity. This is consistent with the fact that, as long as
\langle n_{1}\rangle+\langle n_{2}\rangle=|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}
is conserved, we can change the amplitudes of the coherent beams with a beam splitter.

Other applications of (the orthonormalized version of) this invariant can be found in
Parellada et al. 2023
, where it is used to prove the impossibility of preparing a Bell state from a Fock state and the impossibility of preparing a
\left|{\rm GHZ}\right\rangle
from a
\left|{\rm W}\right\rangle
state
Dür et al. 2000
, even if we add Fock ancillas in both cases.

For another example, we can use the projection invariant from Theorem
2
to give an alternative proof to
Migdał et al. 2014
and show it is impossible to evolve one Fock state,
\left|k_{1},\ldots k_{m}\right\rangle
, into another Fock state,
\left|k^{\prime}_{1},\ldots k^{\prime}_{m}\right\rangle
, unless they are a permutation of one another. The projection of
\left|k_{1},\ldots k_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle k_{1},\ldots k_{m}\right|
is given by the following matrix elements:

\left\langle\ell_{1},\ldots\ell_{m}\right|\sum_{i}\left\langle k_{1},\ldots k_{m}\right|n_{i}\left|k_{1},\ldots k_{m}\right\rangle n_{i}\left|\ell^{\prime}_{1},\ldots\ell^{\prime}_{m}\right\rangle\>.

(13)

The matrix is diagonal, with elements
\sum_{i}k_{i}\ell_{i}
(for each
\left|\ell_{1},\ldots\ell_{m}\right\rangle
in the Fock basis). If we choose the states
\left|0\ldots\ell\ldots 0\right\rangle
, with
\ell
in position

, we conclude that
k_{i}\ell
is in the spectrum and thus the number of photons in each mode,
k_{i}
, is conserved, except for a possible permutation of the modes.

The covariance invariant (
9

VanMeter et al. 2007
and show that it is impossible to go from a Fock state
\left|k,k^{\prime}\right\rangle
to a NOON state

M(\left|k,k^{\prime}\right\rangle)=-(2kk^{\prime}+k+k^{\prime})\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&0&0\\

(15)

Discussion and outlook.

As quantum information with linear optics develops, state preparation of useful quantum states continues to be a fundamental challenge. Our work shines a light on the mathematical structure of linear optics, giving a recipe to define invariant quantities that must be preserved by passive linear optical evolution. This is especially useful when trying to prepare exactly or approximately some state from another one (presumably, easy to generate) as it gives necessary conditions for this preparation to be possible. These conditions could be implemented in some of the numerous algorithms for computer-aided state preparation
Knott 2016
;
Gao et al. 2020
;
Krenn et al. 2016
to discard impossible preparations.

Our invariants have the advantage that the quantum state is not restricted to a fixed number of photons (i.e. a finite-dimensional space): we can work with the full Fock space. This improves other invariants in the literature that can only be applied to finite dimensional spaces
Somma 2005
;
Migdał et al. 2014
;
Parellada et al. 2023
. A second improvement is that they can be used with mixed states, not only pure ones, like the invariant in
Migdał et al. 2014
(although with some slight modifications it can also be applied to mixed states). Another good property of the invariants involving expectation values of the operators
O_{i}
is that they can, in principle, be measured experimentally
Campos et al. 1989
.

One could wonder: what about sufficient conditions for state preparation? While we haven’t addressed it in this article, in future works we plan to investigate whether we can provide a sufficient condition by combining several invariants, restricting more and more the allowed linear optical evolutions.

Another future line of research is to use the distance between the invariants to give necessary conditions for preparing states with post-selection. This could, in turn, be used to explore the limits of heralded and post-selected optical quantum gates
Knill 2002
;
Stanisic et al. 2017
.

Finally, one could ask if this approach can be applied to active linear optics. It turns out it cannot, since the map between the quasiunitary matrices describing active linear optics
Leonhardt and Neumaier 2003
and the unitary matrices describing their quantized version is not a group homomorphism. And we really need the map
\varphi
to be a homomorphism to derive equation (
3
). Nonetheless, in future research we will try to circumvent this issue by decomposing the active linear optical system into a passive one with squeezing in between.

{\rm tr}(O_{i}\rho)={\rm tr}\left(\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}O_{i}^{N}p_{N}\rho_{N}\right)\>,

(21)

where we define the projections over the subspace of
N
total photons:
\rho_{N}:=\mathcal{P}_{N}\rho\mathcal{P}_{N}
and
O_{i}^{N}:=\mathcal{P}_{N}O_{i}\mathcal{P}_{N}
. We also define the probabilities of measuring N total photons:
p_{N}:={\rm tr}(\mathcal{P}_{N}\rho)
and
q_{N}:={\rm tr}(\mathcal{P}_{N}\sigma)
. We use this decomposition to avoid getting infinite values for
\|O_{i}\|_{\infty}
. Instead, we can bound
\|O_{i}^{N}\|_{\infty}\leq N
in each subspace with a fixed number of photons because each annihilation operator will introduce a factor of at most
\sqrt{N}
and each creation operator a factor of at most
\sqrt{(N-1)+1}
. The following results are valid for optical states with a finite expected number of photons
\langle n\rangle
.

With this decomposition in mind, we can start bounding the difference of Theorem
1
invariants:

|I(\rho)-I(\sigma)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}|{\rm tr}(O_{i}(\rho+\sigma))|\cdot|{\rm tr}(O_{i}(\rho-\sigma))|\,.

(22)

(We will not distinguish between input and output states because
I(\rho)=I(U\rho U^{\dagger})
).

First, we bound the term
|{\rm tr}(O_{i}(\rho+\sigma))|
:

\displaystyle|{\rm tr}(O_{i}(\rho+\sigma))|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\text{see \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{Kittaneh_1985}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle\|O_{i}(\rho+\sigma))\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

(24)

\displaystyle\|O_{i}^{N}\sum_{N}(p_{N}\rho_{N}+q_{N}\sigma_{N})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

(25)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\text{Hölder,}\>\>\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{1}\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\infty}\right)

(26)

\displaystyle\sum_{N}p_{N}\lVert O_{i}^{N}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\rho_{N}\rVert_{1}+q_{N}\lVert O_{i}^{N}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\sigma_{N}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\lVert\rho_{N}\rVert_{1},\lVert\sigma_{N}\rVert_{1}=1\right)

(27)

\displaystyle\sum_{N}p_{N}\lVert O_{i}^{N}\rVert_{\infty}+q_{N}\lVert O_{i}^{N}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\lVert O_{i}^{N}\rVert_{\infty}\leq N\right)

(28)

\displaystyle\sum_{N}p_{N}N+q_{N}N=\langle n\rangle_{\rho}+\langle n\rangle_{\sigma}\>.

(29)

The strategy to bound the second term differs between pure and mixed states. For pure states:

\displaystyle|{\rm tr}(O_{i}(\rho-\sigma))|=

\displaystyle\left(\rho^{2}=\rho,\>\sigma^{2}=\sigma\right)

(30)

\displaystyle|{\rm tr}(O_{i}(\rho^{2}-\sigma^{2}))|=

(31)

\displaystyle\left|{\rm tr}\left(O_{i}\frac{(\rho-\sigma)(\rho+\sigma)+(\rho+\sigma)(\rho-\sigma)}{2}\right)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left(\text{see \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{Kittaneh_1985}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}}\right)

(32)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(O_{i}\frac{(\rho-\sigma)(\rho+\sigma)+(\rho+\sigma)(\rho-\sigma)}{2}\right)\right\|_{1}\leq

(33)

\displaystyle\left(\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{1}\right)

(36)

While for mixed states, the bound is more elaborated:

\displaystyle\left|{\rm tr}\left(O_{i}(\rho-\sigma)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\leq

(38)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\lVert O_{i}^{N}\rVert_{\infty}\leq N\right)

\displaystyle\leq

III
Distance between Theorem 2 invariants

To bound the difference between invariants in Theorem
2
, we use the Hoffman-Wielandt inequality in infinite dimensions, which tells us that for any
\delta>0
there exists an ordering of the eigenvalues of
\rho_{T}
and
\sigma_{T}
(
\{\lambda^{\rho}_{i}\}
and
\{\lambda^{\sigma}_{i}\}
, respectively), such that
Bhatia and Elsner 1994

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\lambda^{\rho}_{i}-\lambda^{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\lVert\rho_{T}-\sigma_{T}\rVert_{2}+\delta\>.

(49)

(We will not distinguish between input and output states because their invariants are equal).

Now, because the norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
arises from an inner product, the Pythagorean Theorem allows us to decompose
\rho-\sigma
into two orthogonal components:

\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert\rho_{T}-\sigma_{T}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert(\rho-\rho_{T})-(\sigma-\sigma_{T})\rVert_{2}^{2}\>.

(50)

This implies that

\lVert\rho_{T}-\sigma_{T}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{2}
. And, because of Schatten norm inequalities, this can be bounded again:
\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}
. Therefore, the distance between the eigenvalues of
\rho_{T}
and
\sigma_{T}
can be bounded by

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\lambda^{\rho}_{i}-\lambda^{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{2}+\delta\leq\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}+\delta\>.

(51)

IV
Distance between Theorem 3 invariants

The covariance invariant, (
9
), is the spectrum of the covariance matrix:

{\rm spec}\left(M(\rho)_{ij}\right)={\rm spec}\left(\langle O_{i}\rangle_{\rho}\langle O_{j}\rangle_{\rho}-\left\langle\frac{O_{i}O_{j}+O_{j}O_{i}}{2}\right\rangle_{\rho}\right)\>.

(52)

Suppose
\rho
and
\sigma
are two quantum states. To bound the distance between the eigenvalues of
M(\rho)
and
M(\sigma)
we use the
\ell^{2}
norm
Bhatia 1997
:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}|\lambda^{\rho}_{i}-\lambda^{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\|M(\rho)-M(\sigma)\|_{2}\leq m^{2}\|M(\rho)-M(\sigma)\|_{\rm max}=

(53)

\displaystyle m^{2}{\max}_{ij}\left|\text{tr}\left(O_{i}\rho\right)\text{tr}\left(O_{j}(\rho-\sigma)\right)+\text{tr}\left(O_{j}\sigma\right)\text{tr}\left(O_{i}(\rho-\sigma)\right)-\text{tr}\left(\frac{O_{i}O_{j}+O_{j}O_{i}}{2}(\rho-\sigma)\right)\right|\leq

(54)

\displaystyle m^{2}{\max}_{ij}\left\{|\text{tr}\left(O_{i}\rho\right)|\cdot|\text{tr}\left(O_{j}(\rho-\sigma)\right)|+|\text{tr}\left(O_{j}\sigma\right)|\cdot|\text{tr}\left(O_{i}(\rho-\sigma)\right)|+\left|\text{tr}\left(\frac{O_{i}O_{j}+O_{j}O_{i}}{2}(\rho-\sigma)\right)\right|\right\}\>.

(55)

We have already bounded all the terms but the last one. For pure states, we repeat the argument in equations (
23
) - (
29
):

\left|\text{tr}\left(\frac{O_{i}O_{j}+O_{j}O_{i}}{2}(\rho-\sigma)\right)\right|\leq\big(\langle{n}^{2}\rangle_{\rho}+\langle{n}^{2}\rangle_{\sigma}\big)\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}\>.

(56)

For mixed states it is slightly more complicated (follow steps (
37
) - (
46
)):

\left|\text{tr}\left(\frac{O_{i}O_{j}+O_{j}O_{i}}{2}(\rho-\sigma)\right)\right|\leq\frac{\langle n^{2}\rangle_{\rho}+\langle n^{2}\rangle_{\sigma}}{2}\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}+\sum_{N}N^{2}|p_{N}-q_{N}|

(57)

(recall that
p_{N}:={\rm tr}(\mathcal{P}_{N}\rho)
and
q_{N}:={\rm tr}(\mathcal{P}_{N}\sigma)
).
Thus, for pure states:

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}|\lambda^{\rho}_{i}-\lambda^{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq m^{2}\left(\big(\langle{n}\rangle_{\rho}+\langle n\rangle_{\sigma}\big)^{2}+\langle{n}^{2}\rangle_{\rho}+\langle n^{2}\rangle_{\sigma}\right)\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}\>.

(58)

While for mixed states:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}|\lambda^{\rho}_{i}-\lambda^{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq

(59)

\displaystyle m^{2}\left(\frac{\big(\langle{n}\rangle_{\rho}+\langle{n}\rangle_{\sigma}\big)^{2}}{2}\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}+\big(\langle{n}\rangle_{\rho}+\langle{n}\rangle_{\sigma}\big)\sum_{N}N|p_{N}-q_{N}|+\frac{\langle n^{2}\rangle_{\rho}+\langle n^{2}\rangle_{\sigma}}{2}\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}+\sum_{N}N^{2}|p_{N}-q_{N}|\right).

(60)

This implies that if
\rho
and
\sigma
are close, their invariants must be close too.
