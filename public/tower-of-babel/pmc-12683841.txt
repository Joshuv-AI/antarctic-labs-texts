Mesoporous multimetallic PdPtBi nanozymes target redox imbalance and glutamine deprivation for immuno-chemodynamic therapy
Authors: Guanhua Qiu, Jie Long, Feng Lin, Zeyan Yu, Renchuan Liang, Jiaxin Luo, Xuezhong Mo, Duo Wang, Lei Jiao, Yunxi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Therapeutic resistance remains a formidable challenge in oncology, mainly due to the chaotic tumor microenvironment (TME), which drive tumor progression and adaptive resistance via a self-sustaining metabolic–oxidative feedback loop. Herein, a multifunctional nanotherapeutic platform based on PdPtBi multimetallic nanozymes (PPB MMNs) is rationally engineered to address these challenges by simultaneously targeting reactive oxygen species (ROS) modulation and glutamine (Gln) metabolic disruption. Specifically, the PPB MMNs display cascade enzyme-mimicking activities resembling peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione oxidase (GOD-SH). These activities effectively promote the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and facilitate the decomposition of endogenous H₂O₂ into O₂, thereby alleviating hypoxia and inducing oxidative stress. Upon ultrasound (US) activation, their catalytic performance is further amplified, enabling deep-tissue therapeutic efficacy. Concurrently, PPB MMNs impair Gln metabolism, aggravating redox dyshomeostasis and undermining tumor metabolic flexibility. Both in vitro and in vivo studies validate the superior antitumor activity of PPB MMNs in suppressing primary and metastatic tumor growth by disrupting the redox-metabolic axis. Introduction

Despite significant advances in cancer treatment, including the rapid development of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and gene therapy, therapeutic resistance remains a major unresolved challenge [1–3]. A key contributor to treatment failure lies in chaotic state of the tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by dynamic heterogeneity, metabolic dysregulation, and genetic/epigenetic instability [4–7]. Among them, hypoxia, acidosis, and nutrient competition induce metabolic reprogramming (e.g., the Warburg effect), further amplifying stochasticity in signaling pathways [8, 9]. Specifically, glycolysis activation drastically increases tumor cell demand for glutamine (Gln), which is the most abundant circulating and intracellular free amino acid, to serves as a critical energy substrate in poorly vascularized and nutrient-deprived TME [10–12]. Therefore, Gln metabolism supplies essential carbon and nitrogen sources to sustain tumor growth, a dependency often termed “glutamine addiction”. In many cases, the chaotic pattern in TME, Gln metabolism, and oxidative stress form a tripartite vicious cycle [13–15]: Chaotic TME upregulates oncogenic signals (e.g., MYC, RAS) to reprogram Gln metabolism, while Gln-derived GSH and NADPH scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) to protect tumors. Conversely, ROS-induced DNA damage and epigenetic alterations exacerbate tumor heterogeneity and adaptive evolution [16, 17]. Thus, the development of methods for disrupting this self-reinforcing cycle is necessary to overcome tumor adaptive resistance.

While the inherent chaos of tumors presents significant therapeutic challenges, their stochastic behavior remains constrained within deterministic boundaries governed by the system’s microenvironmental redox dynamics [18]. Selective manipulation of tumor redox metabolism could induce targeted biochemical alterations capable of exceeding these deterministic thresholds, potentially enabling precise and transformative therapeutic interventions [19, 20]. Critical to this approach is the precise modulation of ROS levels to disrupt the self-defense mechanisms of chaotic tumors [21–24]. Recent advances in nanozyme [25–30], such as glucose oxidase (GOD)-integrated IrOx nanozymes, nitrogen-doped porous carbon nanospheres, and MnO2@PtCo nanoflowers, have demonstrate promising capabilities for selectively reprogramming redox metabolism in the TME. However, current systems predominantly focus on singular regulation of either ROS or glutathione (GSH) [31–36], thereby limits therapeutic efficiency via the internal evolutionary fitness of the tumor’s oxidative stress self-defense network. Therefore, it is urgent to explore a comprehensive strategy simultaneously regulating multiple redox nodes, as well as Gln metabolic intervention, to achieve robust therapeutic efficacy against evolving tumor ecosystems.

Inspired by this, we rationally designed multimetallic PdPtBi multimetallic nanomaterials (PPB MMNs) that simultaneously target both ROS accumulation and Gln metabolic pathways to address this challenge (Scheme 1). Briefly, PPB MMNs leverage the TME to generate ROS and mitigate the hypoxia of tumor cells, mimicking the functions of peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT) and glutathione oxidase (GOD-SH). Beyond that, the introduction of ultrasound (US) is expected to expand the catalytic effect, making it more suitable for the treatment of deep tumors. Meanwhile, PPB MMNs have the ability to interference Gln metabalism and exacerbate redox dyshomeostasis. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that, after uptake PPB MMNs by tumor cells, intracellular H2O2 can decompose into O2 to promote hypoxia relief and produce ROS, which can efficiently disturb oxidative stress in the cytosolic region and further breaking tripartite vicious cycle. This multimodal approach achieves significant suppression of both primary and metastatic tumors while overcoming the limitations of conventional ROS-based therapies. The design principles established here provide a framework for developing next-generation metabolic-modulatory nanotherapeutics against treatment-resistant malignancies.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of PPB MMNs nanozymes

The PPB nanospheres with a core-shell mesoporous structure were successfully synthesized using a simple reduction method via a surfactant direction (Fig. 2a). As shown in transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, the prepared PPB nanospheres exhibit a spherical porous structure and an average particle diameter of 163 ± 16 nm (Fig. 2b and Fig. S1). The polycrystallinity characteristics of PPB were confirmed by selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns (Fig. 2c, inset), in which the concentric diffraction rings with bright discrete diffraction spots are indexed to a face-centered cubic (fcc) structure. Figure 2c presented a high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image of PPB, clearly displaying the crystallinity and distinct lattice fringes with interplanar crystal spacings of 0.22 nm, corresponding to the (111) plane of the face-centered cubic (fcc) PPB. The elemental mapping (Fig. 2d) and the line scanning (Fig. 2e) revealed that PPB exhibit a core-shell structure with a Pd-rich core, and the elements of Pt and Bi are distributed throughout the nanospheres. Subsequently, the electronic structure of PPB nanozymes was revealed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. 2g and Fig. S2, the dominant metallic states and partial oxidation of Pt, Pd, and Bi atoms were observed in XPS results. Fig. 2g illustrated the high-resolution Pt 4f XPS spectra for PPB and PdPt (PP) nanozymes, with the deconvolution of two characteristic peaks corresponding to Pt0 and Pt2+ species, which indicated that metallic Pt constitutes the major component. Notably, the Pt 4f7/2 peak binding energy for PPB is lower than that of the pristine PP. This observation is indicative of an electron transfer from Bi to Pt atoms. The alteration in the electronic structure of Pt is anticipated to diminish electron adsorption onto intermediate species, accelerate electron transfer kinetics, and consequently, augment catalytic activity. Furthermore, Fig. S2 exhibits the high-resolution Bi 4f XPS spectrum of PPB, featuring two peaks assigned to Bi 4f5/2 and Bi 4f7/2, confirming the presence of Bi3+. The electronic structure at the active sites is significantly influenced by the robust interactions of Pt, Pd and Bi elements.

The enzyme-like activity of PPB nanozymes was further investigated in detail. The POD-like activity of PPB nanozymes was first investigated by using 3, 3’, 5, 5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as an electron donor. As shown in Fig. 2h, with the increment of incubation time, TMB is oxidized by H2O2 under the catalysis of PPB nanozymes, delivering an obvious color at 652 nm and demonstrating that PPB nanozymes can catalyze H2O2 to produce hydroxide radical (•OH). Besides, the POD-like activity of PPB nanozymes conform to the Michaelis-Menten equation with the maximum reaction velocity (Vmax) and Michaelis-Menten constants (Km) of 1.1 × 10−6 M/s and 20.98 mM, respectively (Table S1). In addition, the specific activity of PPB nanozymes toward POD-like activity reaches to 181.3 U/mg, which is much better than previously reported nanozymes (Fig. S3a) [37, 38]. In addition, PPB nanozymes also demonstrate good CAT-like activity, which can catalyze H2O2 to produce O2, alleviating hypoxic TME. According to the previous work [39], the CAT activity of PPB nanozymes was investigated by using dopamine as a probe. With the increment of incubation time, the generated O2 is gradually increased, confirming that PPB nanozymes have the CAT-like activity (Fig. 2i). Similarly, CAT-like activity of PPB nanozymes also conform to the Michaelis-Menten equation with the Vmax and Km of 7.59 × 10−7 M/s and 0.866 M, respectively (Table S1). Besides, the specific activity of PPB nanozymes toward CAT-like activity reaches to 448.2 U/mg, which is much better than previously reported nanozymes (Fig. S3b) [40, 41]. We also found that the PPB nanozymes have the glutathione oxidase (GSH-OD)-like activity, which can consume the GSH in tumor cells to broken redox balance and thus accelerate cell apoptosis (Fig. 2j). Finally, we found that the enzyme-like activity of PPB nanozymes including POD-like, CAT-like and GSH-OD-like activity can be increased under the ultrasound treatment, further improving the therapeutic efficiency (Fig. 2k).

Cellular uptake and cytotoxicity of PPB in vitro

Before exploring its therapeutic effect at the cellular level, we initially assessed the cellular uptake behavior of PPB in vitro. The endocytosis of Cy5.5-labeled PPB was evaluated by observing the red fluorescence (cy5.5) intensity at different time periods after co-incubation with Hepa1-6 mouse liver tumor cell line. As shown in Fig. S4, the red fluorescence intensity increased significantly in a time-dependent manner, indicating that PPB can be effectively endocytosed by tumor cells and reaches its peak in 8 h. Subsequently, we verified the in vitro antitumor effect of PPB. To intuitively observe the anti-tumor effect, calcein AM/PI staining was used to identify live and dead cells. As shown in Fig. 3a, b, compared with the control and individual US groups, the red fluorescence (indicating dead cells) of the PP and PPB groups slightly increased. Nevertheless, when both were exposed to US irradiation, the red fluorescence intensity of the PPB + US group was significantly enhanced compared to the PPB group. Subsequently, human umbilical vein cells (HUVECs) were selected as healthy somatic cells to verify the biocompatibility of PPB. After 24 h of incubation with PPB, cell viability was measured using the CCK8 assay. As shown in Fig. S5, even at high concentrations of PPB, the impact on normal cell viability was minimal, indicating that PPB has good biocompatibility. Whereas when PPB was incubated with Hepa 1–6 cells and simultaneously exposed to US irradiation, the cell viability decreased in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3c). The Annexin V-7-ADD apoptosis detection kit (Fig. 3d, e) and the one-step apoptosis detection Tunel kit (Fig. 3f, Fig. S6) were used to confirm that PPB + US have ability to induce tumor cell apoptosis, and both detection methods yielded the same results. We further assessed the anti-proliferation ability of PPB + US through clone formation and EdU cell proliferation experiments. Figure 3g showed that US irradiation alone had minimal inhibitory effects on colony formation, while PP and PPB slightly delay colony formation. However, when combined with US separately, the inhibitory proliferation ability of PP, PPB was significantly amplified. And the colony formation rate of the PPB + US treatment group was less than 35% compared to control group (Fig. 3h). Moreover, the number of EDU-positive cells was almost undetectable in the EDU cell proliferation assay (Fig. 3i, j). Simultaneously, we tested the ability of PPB + US to resist tumor invasion and migration. Notably, cell invasion and migration experiment further confirmed that the PPB + US group could markedly suppress the invasive and migratory capacities of Hepa 1–6 cells (Fig. 3L-k), suggesting that PPB possesses a distinct capacity to inhibit tumor metastasis. Notably, western blot (WB) analysis of Hepa1-6 cells following PPB + US treatment revealed that this combination significantly suppressed the expression of multiple cancer stemness-related proteins, including EpCAM, CD24, and CD133 (Fig. S7). It is well established that the maintenance of cancer stemness is closely associated with therapeutic resistance. These findings suggest that PPB-mediated sonodynamic therapy (SDT) not only directly induces tumor cell apoptosis but also reverses the drug-resistant phenotype by downregulating stemness markers, thereby resensitizing tumor cells to treatment and substantially enhancing the overall antitumor efficacy. Collectively, the PPB MMNs combined with US treatment could significantly induce apoptosis of tumor cells, suppress tumor proliferation, invasion and migration, indicating a robust synergistic antitumor effect.

Antitumor mechanism of PPB in vitro

Considering that the activities of PPB are similar to those of POD, CAT and GSH-OD enzymes, it is capable of exerting cytotoxic effects by generating ROS, thereby damaging mitochondria and reversing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (ITM). Encouraged by the satisfactory anti-tumor effects, we further explored the anti-tumor mechanism of PPB in vitro. We initially utilized the 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe to detect the level of ROS in tumor cells. The fluorescence images (Fig. 4a, Fig. S8b) and FCM data (Fig. 4b, Fig. S8a) indicated that the oxidative stress level in Hepa1-6 cells treated with PPB + US was the highest. An increase in ROS levels and the activity of GSH-OD leads to the consumption of GSH. Subsequently, we used ThiolTracker probes to further analyze the intracellular GSH levels. As shown in Fig. 4a, the Hepa1-6 cells in the PPB + US group showed the lowest GSH level (Fig. 4c). We also quantitatively analyzed intracellular GSH levels using a GSH detection kit and found that the intracellular GSH level in the PPB + US group was much lower than other groups (Fig. S9). As GSH depletion increases, Gln becomes its primary source, capable of synthesizing substantial amounts of GSH in the cytoplasm to counteract oxidative stress damage. As depicted in Fig. 4d, the Gln level significantly decreased in the PPB + US group, which subsequently led to insufficient cellular ATP levels, thereby affecting the energy supply (Fig. 4e). Additionally, when cells were exposed to the combination of PPB and US irradiation, they generated a substantial amount of ROS, leading to cell apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction. To evaluate the extent of mitochondrial dysfunction, we assessed changes in mitochondrial membrane potential through JC-1 staining. As shown in Fig. 4a and Fig. S10 Hepa 1–6 cells treated with PPB + US exhibited the strongest green fluorescence and the weakest red fluorescence, indicating that PPB + US could cause severe mitochondrial dysfunction. Meanwhile, the JC-1 FCM analysis also confirmed these results (Fig. S11a, b). Furthermore, changes in mitochondrial morphology under different treatments were observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Fig. 4f, compared to other groups, the PPB + US group exhibited the most pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by mitochondrial swelling and a decrease in mitochondrial cristae density. To further explore whether PPB can catalyze the production of oxygen to alleviate hypoxia, we used the [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 oxygen detection probe to uncover intracellular oxygenation levels. Under hypoxic conditions, strong red fluorescence can be observed in cells. It is worth noting that after treatment with PPB + US, the red fluorescence signal inside the cells was almost attenuated, indicating that PPB + US could significantly reverse the insufficient oxygen content during hypoxia (Fig. 4a, Fig. S12). Additionally, the polarization of M2 macrophages into M1 macrophages is a complex biological transformation process that is precisely regulated by diverse network of factors. Several studies [42–46] have revealed that enhancing the level of ROS through GSH depletion, and improving the hypoxic state in the TME can effectively triggering and promotion of the transformation of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from immunosuppressive M2 type to tumoricidal M1 type. To verify the ability of PPB to facilitate this transformation, we pretreated bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) with interleukin-4 (IL-4) to induce their transformation into M2 macrophages. Subsequently, we detected the expression of relevant surface biomarkers of BMDMs by using various experimental techniques. As shown in Fig. 4g, compared to the other groups, the PPB + US treatment group significantly promoted the increase of M1 phenotype TAMs, specifically manifested by the upregulation of markers such as CD86. At the same time, a decrease in M2-TAMs was observed in PPB + US group, as evidenced by the downregulation of M2 phenotype markers such as CD206. Furthermore, we evaluated the cultured BMDM polarization by FCM analysis. Consistent with western blot analysis, the proportion of M1 were obviously increased in the PPB + US group (Fig. 4h-k). Moreover, qPCR assay also confirmed that the expression of M1-related marker indicators CD86 were up-regulated and M2-related marker CD206 expression were down-regulated (Fig. S14a, b). Additionally, the obviously increased secretion of M1-related IL-12 and decreased M2-related IL-4 secretion were observed by ELISA anlysis of the cytokine content in the supernatant of tumor cells co-cultured with the BMDM (Fig. S15a, b). Meanwhile, to further investigate the relationship between oxidative stress and the immune microenvironment, we collected conditioned media from Hepa1-6 cells treated with various drug regimens and co-cultured them with IL-4-induced M2 macrophages. As illustrated in Fig. S16, the western blot analysis results demonstrated that drug-treated tumor cells induced significant ROS production and GSH depletion, which subsequently promoted the repolarization of M2 macrophages toward the M1 phenotype. This shift effectively remodeled the immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby contributing to synergistic antitumor effects. These finding conclusively demonstrate that PPB leveraged ROS-mediated tumor cell killing and mitochondrial dysfunction to regulate tumor cell energy metabolism and oxidative stress. Furthermore, PPB can mitigate immunosuppression by effectively reprogramming M2 macrophages into M1 macrophages.

Biodistribution and biosafety in vitro

Given the remarkable therapeutic effects demonstrated by PPB in vitro, our next task is to evaluate their biodistribution and biological safety before exploring their antitumor potential in vivo. Firstly, the biocompatibility of PPB was verified through hemolysis experiments (Fig. S17), revealing that even with a significant increase in PPB concentration, the hemolysis rate remained extremely low (below 5%), which demonstrated their excellent blood compatibility. Subsequently, we monitored the fluorescence intensity changes of PPB at predetermined time points after intravenous injection. Figure 5a illustrated the accumulation of PPB in the tumor region, and the maximum accumulation effect occurring approximately 24 h later. Notably, compared to the Free-cy5.5 treatment alone, the fluorescence intensity in the tumor sites of mice treated with PPB@cy5.5 were significantly enhanced, indicating that PPB can effectively target and accumulate in tumor tissues. Furthermore, we analyzed the distribution of PPB in excised organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) using fluorescence imaging technology (Fig. 5b, Fig. S18). The results indicated that PPB primarily accumulated in the liver, lung, and tumor site. Subsequently, to further elucidate the pharmacokinetic profile of PPB in vivo, we collected major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and tumor tissues at various time points post-injection and quantified the elemental content using ICP-MS analysis. As shown in Fig. S19, PPB exhibited sustained and significant accumulation in tumor tissue, reaching its peak around 24 h. Meanwhile, the retention levels in key organs such as the heart, spleen, and kidneys gradually decreased over time, indicating that PPB can be effectively cleared via metabolic pathways with minimal non-specific accumulation in vital organs. These data further confirmed the high tumor-targeting efficiency and favorable biosafety profile of PPB, providing critical pharmacokinetic support for its subsequent in vivo antitumor applications. To comprehensively assess the biosafety of PPB, we performed blood routine and biochemical tests on mice 48 h after tail vein injection of PPB and conducted H&E staining of major organs. The experimental results showed that the liver and kidney functions of the mice remained within normal ranges (Fig. S20), and no significant tissue structural damage was observed in the H&E-stained sections which robustly confirming the excellent biological safety of PP and PPB in vivo (Fig. S21). Antitumor effect of PPB in vivo

Motivated by the remarkable tumor-specific toxicity of PPB demonstrated in preliminary cellular experiments, we further explored its antitumor activity in vivo. The experimental process was shown in Fig. 5c and Fig. S22. a, b. We found that US therapy alone did not exhibit significant antitumor effects, and the tumor inhibitory effects of PP and PPB when used alone were also relatively limited. However, when PP or PPB was combined with US therapy, the antitumor effects were significantly enhanced, and the PPB combined with US therapy group showing particularly outstanding results (Fig. S23a) and the tumor weight were the lightest (Fig. S23b). Besides, the tumor volume of mice were significantly decreased (approximately 87 mm3) compared with control group (approximately 1013 mm3) (Fig. S23c, d), but no apparent change in body weight were observed among these mice (Fig. S23e). These results strongly demonstrate the enormous potential of the combined application of PPB + US in antitumor treatment.

Subsequently, to further explore the mechanism of tumor killing by PPB in vivo, we performed transcriptome sequencing of tumors. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed significant differences among different groups (Fig. S24). And there were significant differences in gene expression between the different groups (Fig. S25). The protein interaction network indicated that the differentially expressed genes between control and PPB + US had more interactions (Fig. S26). Besides, compared with the control group, the PPB + US group showed significantly higher expression of Gln-related genes, suggesting that PPB enhances Gln consumption in tumor cells (Fig. 5d). KEGG analysis revealed that the differential genes between the PPB + US and PBS groups were mainly concentrated in Gln metabolism (Fig. 5e). And Gene ontology (GO) analysis also demonstrated identical results (Fig. 5f). Considering the crosstalk among various components in the TME, and given that GSH serves as one of the weapons for cells to combat oxidative stress and achieve adaptive survival, we further quantified the levels of GSH and Gln in tumors. Similar to in vitro results, both Gln and GSH were significantly reduced in the PPB + US group (Fig. 5g, h). As a result, the ATP production in tumor cells significantly decreases, leading to starvation-induced cell death (Fig. 5i). Furthermore, we identified three genes (STAC2, P2RX1 and EDN1) that were highly expressed in the PPB + US group compared to the control group. Subsequently, we found that these three genes were associated with increased patient survival (Fig. S27a), suggesting that they may mediate the immunotherapeutic effect of PPB (Fig. S27b). We further examined the effects of these genes on immune cells and found that all these genes could induce immune system responses (Fig. S28).

Given the results of the influence of PPB on tumor immune microenvironment, we further employed FCM and ELISA to analyze the TME, aiming to make clear the anti-tumor mechanism of PPB in vivo. Macrophages can activate T cells and inhibit Treg through multiple pathways, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment, and enhance anti-tumor immunity. Consistent with the cell experiments, a significant increase in the M1/M2 ratio (2.5-fold) was observed in the tumors of the PPB + US group treatment (Fig. 5j, Fig. S29). It is evident that, as the primary effector cells responsible for tumor cell killing, the proportion of CTL (Fig. S30a, b) in the PPB + US treatment group increased significantly. Similarly, the proportions of IFN-γ+CD8+T cell (Fig. S30c, d) and GZMB+CD8+T cell (Fig. S30c, e) also increased significantly. Additionally, the proportions of immunosuppressive Treg cells were significantly reduced (Fig. S30f, g). Furthermore, ELISA analysis of cytokine levels in the blood revealed that, compared to other groups, M1 related cytokines IL-12 (Fig. S31a) increased, while M2 related cytokines IL-4 (Fig. S31b) decreased markedly in PPB + US group.

To deeply explore the mechanisms of tumor suppression, we discovered that among six experimental conditions, tumor cells treated with PPB combined with US irradiation exhibited pronounced cell death, manifested by a massive loss of cell nuclei, which is a direct indication of severe DNA damage. Given the typical characteristics of TME, including hypoxia, mildly acidic conditions, accumulation of endogenous H2O2, high levels of GSH, and overexpression of specific enzymes, these features not only accelerate tumor angiogenesis and metastatic potential but also construct a crucial basis for therapeutic resistance. Therefore, we focused on the remodeling effects of US-catalyzed mediated therapy on the TME. Our experimental results revealed that after PPB + US irradiation treatment, tumor cells were almost ceased to proliferate (Fig. 5k). Due to the GSH levels in the TME dropped sharply, while effectively alleviating hypoxia in the TME through increased O2 production, the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α was significantly downregulated, thereby inhibiting tumor angiogenesis. Furthermore, TUNEL staining (used to detect DNA fragmentation, a hallmark of apoptosis) showed the number of apoptotic cells labeled with green fluorescence was increased. Lastly, we observed a phenotypic shift of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from immunosuppressive M2 to proinflammatory M1, signifying the effective reversal of the originally immunosuppressive TME. In summary, these results indicated that PPB successfully remodified the TME through a synergistic therapeutic strategy combining metabolic reprogramming and ROS therapy with the consumption of overexpressed GSH within tumors and the improvement of hypoxic conditions, disrupting the environment necessary for tumor cell survival and activation of systemic antitumor immunity, which providing robust support for the achievement of more effective tumor treatment.

Combination of PPB and US irradiation resist distant tumor growth

By investigating the therapeutic potential of PPB combined with US irradiation on primary tumors and their underlying immune effects, we discovered that this combined therapy can trigger a systemic immune response, which not only effectively eliminates residual malignant cells within the primary tumor but also has the potential to significantly inhibit tumor growth at distant sites. Hence, to validate this hypothesis, we established a bilateral subcutaneous tumor model in C57BL/6J mice, wherein Hepa1-6 hepatocellular carcinoma cells were implanted on both sides of the mice’s limbs, with only the right-sided tumor (designated as the primary tumor) receiving the combined PPB + US treatment. The mechanism of distant tumor killing effect is shown in Fig. 6a and the experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 6b. By monitoring every two days, we observed that both the primary and distant tumors in the control group and the group receiving US irradiation alone significantly increased in size. In contrast, compared to the remaining three groups, the PPB + US combined therapy group not only exhibited potent anti-tumor effects on the directly treated primary tumor but also unexpectedly inhibited the growth of the distant, untreated tumor, strongly suggesting an immune-mediated abscopal effect (Fig. S32). The fluorescence imaging of the mice confirmed the same result (Fig. 6c). Subsequently, the results of monitoring the tumor growth volume and metastasis showed that the PPB + US group could significantly inhibit the volume growth of both the treated and metastatic sides (Fig. 6d, e, Fig. S33a, b). Tumor weights also confirmed the same result (Fig. 6f, g). Furthermore, the observation of the mice’s body weight changes revealed that all groups maintained stable weights throughout the 21-day treatment period, indicating that the PPB + US combined therapy did not cause significant systemic toxicity or adverse reactions in the mice (Fig. S34). To explore the immune-related mechanisms, we found that the PPB + US combined therapy significantly upregulated the expression of immunostimulatory cytokines such as IL-2 (Fig. 6h), TNF-α (Fig. 6i), and IL-10 (Fig. 6j), which are crucial for activating immune cells and enhancing anti-tumor immunity. Concurrently, the expression of immunosuppressive Treg cells were markedly decreased (2.63%) in blood (Fig. 6k, l). Additionally, the proportion of GZMB+CD8+T cell (42.9%) (Fig. 6m, n) and IFN-γ+CD8+T cell (69.8%) (Fig. 6o, p) were also increased significantly. These shifts favored the disruption of tumor immune evasion and promotes tumor immune clearance. Moreover, the PPB + US group exhibited the most severe nuclear membrane fragmentation, the highest number of induced apoptotic bodies, and the lowest number of Ki67-positive cells (Fig. S35). Immunofluorescence also showed a significant infiltration of CD8+T cells at the tumor site in the PPB + US group (Fig. S35). In summary, the PPB + US combined therapy not only acts directly on the primary tumor but also effectively inhibits the growth of distant tumors through complex immune mechanisms, offering novel insights and strategies for tumor treatment.

Combination of PPB and US irradiation resist orthotopic tumor growth

To more accurately reflect the effectiveness of PPB + US treatment in liver tumor and considering the immense potential of US in deep tumor treatment due to its advantages such as high tissue penetration and high spatio-temporal selectivity, we established an orthotopic Hepa1-6-luc tumor model. Grouping was performed as previously described, and the specific experimental protocol was illustrated in Fig. 7a. We employed bioluminescence imaging technology to dynamically monitor changes in the primary tumor lesions at different time points (Fig. 7b and Fig. S36). The results indicated that the bioluminescence intensity in the control group and the single US treatment group significantly increased, directly suggesting substantial tumor growth and potential severe metastasis in these mice. In contrast, while the PP group, PPB group, and PP + US group mice exhibited some inhibition of the orthotopic tumor, the effects were relatively modest. Encouragingly, mice in the PPB + US combined treatment group demonstrated a highly significant anti-tumor effect, with tumor growth nearly completely suppressed (Fig. 7c), and there has been a significant improvement in survival analysis (Fig. S37). Additionally, mice liver ultrasound images reveal the same trend . At the end of treatment, photographs of excised livers (Fig. 7d) and H&E staining analysis visually corroborated the same results (Fig. 7e). Thus, these results suggest that our material has significant potential in inhibiting deep tumor growth and holds promise for clinical transformation.

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully designed and synthesized PPB MMNs that mimic the enzymatic activities of POD, CAT, and GSH-OD, thereby disrupting the tripartite vicious cycle and achieving effective liver tumor treatment. Specifically, after being taken up by tumor cells, the MMNs can exploit the TME to generate ROS and mitigate the hypoxia of tumor cells. Meanwhile, sonication can utilize the vacuolar effect to bestow the MMNs with dynamic affinity sites, emulating the persistent activity of GSH-OD. The generation of ROS through these mechanisms can simultaneously interfere with mitochondrial function and further impair energy production. Meanwhile, MMNs can effectively interfere with Gln metabolism, significantly reducing the Gln levels in tumor cells and thereby inducing Gln deprivation. This synergistically enhances ROS-based anti-tumor effects, ultimately leading to the demise of numerous tumor cells, suppressing tumor proliferation and metastasis. As a result, the US-responsive MMNs adopt a multimodal biochemical combination mechanism to accurately intervene the complex crosstalk within the chaotic TME, oxidative stress and metabolic reprogramming, which are expected to provide insightful perspectives and strategies for precise tumor treatment.
