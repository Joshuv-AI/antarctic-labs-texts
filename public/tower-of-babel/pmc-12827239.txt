Edge digital twin for coordinating flexible resources in distribution networks with communication constraints
Authors: Jinfeng Guo, Fei Chen, Dong Liu, Jiaming Weng, Shiqian Ma, Tianhao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the widespread integration of Controllable Flexible Resources (CFRs), distribution networks are progressively evolving toward multi-source global coordinated control, a paradigm that relies heavily on secondary communication networks. However, when communication becomes unstable, certain CFRs may become isolated as “information islands,” rendering them unable to follow the control center’s strategies for a period of time and thus posing threats to the operational security of the distribution network. To address this issue, this paper proposes an Edge-deployed Deduction Framework (EDF) within the Cloud–Edge–Terminal collaborative architecture (C–E–T), which consists of an Edge Digital Twin (EDT) and a Deduction Model for CFRs (DM4CFRs). By enabling local deduction based on limited but structured information retained at the edge, the EDF allows CFRs within communication-isolated areas to approximate coordinated operation even under prolonged communication degradation. A case study on a modified IEEE 33-node distribution network with multiple energy storage units demonstrates that the proposed framework maintains stable CFR operation during communication outages while preserving network security and economic performance.

Introduction

With the continuous integration of energy storage systems, flexible loads, controllable distributed generation, and other CFRs, the operation paradigm of distribution networks is shifting from centralized power supply to multi-source coordinated supply1. As a core automation platform, the Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) achieves centralized coordination and control of CFRs through a closed-loop information flow2. However, the rapid growth in the number of CFRs imposes significant communication and computational burdens on the control center. To alleviate the workload of the control center, existing SCADA commonly introduces computationally capable fusion terminals between the control center and terminal devices, thereby constructing a C-E-T3,4. This architecture integrates wide-area perception, global decision-making, edge computing, and terminal execution to enable efficient coordination of CFRs and reliable operation of distribution networks5.

C–E–T collaborative architectures rely heavily on the communication infrastructure of secondary distribution networks, thereby forming a typical cyber–physical system (CPS)6. In such systems, the communication and physical layers are tightly coupled; any degradation in communication performance–such as delays, congestion, equipment malfunctions, or external interference–can directly affect the physical operational states of the network7,8. Given that communication quality critically determines the effectiveness of global coordination, the research community has explored various approaches to reduce communication complexity and enhance communication robustness9. For example, several studies introduce edge-layer intelligent decision-making mechanisms to reduce reliance on centralized communication and enhance adaptability under communication degradation10–12. These approaches typically utilize local measurements and limited exchanged information to maintain acceptable operational performance when communication quality deteriorates. Although such methods aim to improve robustness by simplifying or weakening communication links, reducing communication requirements does not eliminate communication dependencies13. Their decision-making still relies on the availability of essential system information. Once strict communication isolation occurs, the required information flow becomes inaccessible, making it impossible to maintain global consistency and preventing CFRs within isolated regions from achieving effective system-wide coordination14,15.

To address this issue, several studies have investigated different strategies. Some works enhance the robustness of centralized control by switching to local control during communication interruptions16; others formulate bi-level optimization models for CFR scheduling to mitigate voltage deviations and reduce load shedding risks17. Moreover, Reference18 proposes a fully communication-free adaptive mechanism that utilizes remaining controllable CFRs to compensate for uncontrollable resources. Although these approaches can maintain local operability and stability to some extent, their reliance on purely local measurements limits their ability to preserve consistency with system-level operational objectives under communication-isolated conditions. As a result, CFRs within isolated regions often fail to coordinate with the rest of the network, leading to insufficient reactive power margins, increased network losses, and potential line overloading19.

With the advancement of digital twin technology, the operational states of physical distribution networks can be virtually mapped and dynamically synchronized in real time, providing enhanced capabilities for system sensing, simulation, and optimal control20. In practical applications, digital twins have been widely employed for state estimation21, load forecasting22, and dispatch optimization23, significantly improving system observability, predictability, and controllability. Recent developments further extend digital twins from centralized simulation environments to edge-level deployment, enabling lightweight modeling and operational state deduction under limited communication conditions. This technological evolution provides a promising pathway to overcome the inherent limitations of traditional approaches that rely solely on local measurements.

Motivated by these insights, this paper proposes an EDF within the C–E–T architecture to enable coordinated control of CFRs in information islands. The EDF comprises two key components: (i) an EDT that synchronizes and stores recent global operational data when communication is available, and (ii) a DM4CFRs that integrates local real-time measurements with stored global information to infer control actions during communication degradation or outages. By leveraging the EDF, edge devices can sustain coordinated CFR operations even under unreliable communication, thereby enhancing the stability and resilience of distribution network control.

Toward robust C-E-T architecture via EDF

Operating mechanism and challenges of the conventional C-E-T architecture

The SCADA of distribution networks is evolving into the C-E-T architecture to meet increasingly complex dispatch requirements. The cloud-based control center aggregates wide-area measurement data for global state estimation and generates control strategies to be dispatched to edge devices. These edge devices, typically located at access points, are responsible for local data acquisition, real-time monitoring, and preliminary processing. Through communication with the cloud, edge devices receive control instructions and make decisions based on local data, thereby relieving the computational burden of the cloud. Terminal-side devices (e.g., switches, storage units, energy management systems) execute control instructions from the edge devices to adjust the state of local CFRs and upload real-time measurements. Architecture enhancement via EDF deployment

To address the aforementioned issues, this paper proposes deploying the EDF at edge devices to enhance the C–E–T architecture. The EDF consists of two synergistic modules: the EDT, which maintains an up-to-date contextual state of the distribution network under normal communication, and the DM4CFRs, which infers missing control strategies when communication becomes constrained. By integrating the EDT and DM4CFRs at the edge layer, the EDF enables each edge device to preserve essential operational context and autonomously generate coordinated control decisions even under communication-constrained conditions, thereby improving both resilience and autonomy of the C–E–T architecture.

As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), the EDT state, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {T}_l(t)$$\end{document}, serves as a structured memory that stores recently observed global operating conditions, external disturbances, local strategy records, and predicted disturbance sequences. These components collectively provide the contextual support required for strategy inference during communication constraints. Further details of the EDT construction are provided in Section 1.1.

Operational mechanism of the EDF framework

The EDF consists of two components: the EDT and the data-driven DM4CFRs model. This section provides a detailed exposition of the EDT construction method, its online update mechanism, and the offline deduction mechanism applying DM4CFRs.

Construction of the EDT

To ensure that edge devices retain sufficient information for local deduction during communication-constrained conditions, the EDT maintains a structured internal state that captures both the historical evolution of the distribution system and the predicted future trends. The first component of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {T}_l(t)$$\end{document} is the sequence of operational states around the target node. The third component,14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C_l{}_{(t-k+1:t)}=[c_l(t-k+1),\ldots ,c_l(t)], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}records the historical control strategies applied to the system. Since power generation from renewable sources and load evolution often exhibits strong trend-driven behavior, future disturbance predictions guide the EDT to anticipate upcoming conditions and maintain consistency with global objectives even under communication constraints.

By jointly storing historical trajectories and future trends, the EDT constructs a physically meaningful, temporally coherent, and prediction-aware representation of system evolution, enabling reliable deduction of control strategies at the edge.

Online update of the EDT

To keep the EDT aligned with the real-time system evolution, the control center issues a structured global factor at each time step,16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {G}_l(t)=\{r_l(t),\ d(t),\ c_l(t),\ \hat{D}_{(t+1:t+T)}\}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}containing the latest operational state, disturbance, control strategy, and the complete future disturbance prediction horizon. Since edge devices cannot store or receive long historical data, the EDT reconstructs its temporal structure locally based on the recursively received single-step information.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, upon receiving the new global factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {G}_l(t+1)$$\end{document} at the next time step, the EDT updates its internal state from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {T}_l(t)$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {T}_l(t+1)$$\end{document} by applying two different update rules to the historical sequences and the future disturbance prediction, respectively. The historical components adopt a FIFO sliding mechanism. The disturbance D and strategy sequences \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_l$$\end{document} follow the same sliding update. This mechanism ensures that each historical window strictly maintains a fixed length of k while advancing forward in time. In contrast, the future disturbance prediction sequence does not rely on FIFO sliding. Instead, it is refreshed through a full replacement mechanism:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{D}_{(t+2:t+T+1)}\ \xrightarrow [\ ]{\text {replace}}\ \hat{D}_{(t+1:t+T)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}meaning that the entire prediction received at time t is discarded and replaced at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t+1$$\end{document} by the newly issued prediction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{D}_{(t+2:t+T+1)}$$\end{document}. This replacement rule ensures that the EDT always utilizes the most up-to-date forecasting information provided by the control center.

Offline deduction mechanism applying DM4CFRs

As illustrated in Fig. 3, during training, samples are constructed by applying a systematic sliding window over historical operation records. Since the EDT state contains a length-k historical sequence and a length-T disturbance prediction, each training sample must satisfy temporal continuity and structural consistency. When the sliding window is positioned at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_0$$\end{document}, the EDT state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {T}_l(t_0)$$\end{document} is composed of historical operation states, environmental disturbances, locally executed strategies, and the predicted future disturbances available at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_0$$\end{document}.

With a sufficiently rich dataset, DM4CFRs learns the intrinsic mapping among operational states, disturbances, and strategy evolution, enabling reliable performance even under prediction uncertainty and communication disruptions.

Design of DM4CFRs

Sequence modeling architecture of the DM4CFRs framework

After establishing how DM4CFRs generates future multi-step control policies from the EDT state, this section provides a detailed description of its sequence modeling architecture, which enables the model to support long-horizon deduction under communication constraints and edge resource limitations. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, DM4CFRs adopts a Transformer-inspired encoder–decoder structure with a Seq2Seq input–output design24, allowing it to jointly process EDT states, external disturbances, historical control actions, and future predictive information. To further address the challenges associated with long-horizon temporal reasoning in deduction tasks25, the model integrates an interpretable heterogeneous feature embedder tailored for the deduction workflow, together with multi-head sparse attention, inter-layer distillation, and one-shot decoding. These architectural components collectively enhance the robustness and stability of the EDT-driven offline deduction process.

In the encoding stage, DM4CFRs is designed to capture long-range temporal dependencies with minimal computational overhead. To achieve this, the encoder adopts a lightweight architecture that incorporates sparse attention and hierarchical sequence compression to reduce computational complexity while preserving critical temporal structure. The sparse attention mechanism is formulated as23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {SparseAttention}(Q, K, V) = \text {Softmax}\!\left( \frac{Q'K^\textrm{T}}{\sqrt{d}} \right) V, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where only the top-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document} most relevant keys are retained for each query, significantly reducing computational cost without sacrificing representational capacity. In addition, a hierarchical distillation module is employed to compress long temporal sequences into compact multi-scale features. Through attention-guided convolution, ELU activation, and pooling operations, this process is described by24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F^{(l+1)} = \textrm{MaxPool}\!\left( \textrm{ELU}\!\big ( \textrm{Conv1D}(\textrm{AB}(F^{(l)})) \big ) \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}allowing efficient temporal abstraction suited for resource-constrained edge environments.

Because communication failures in distribution networks commonly persist for non-negligible durations, edge devices must be able to generate a complete sequence of future control actions promptly to maintain system stability. Traditional autoregressive decoding generates actions step-by-step, which not only induces error accumulation but also increases inference latency–making it inadequate for time-critical deduction tasks. To address this limitation, DM4CFRs employs a non-autoregressive one-shot decoder capable of producing the full T-step control sequence within a single forward pass. The decoder input is constructed as25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H_{\textrm{dec}} = \textrm{Concat}(H_{\textrm{token}}, H_0), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_0$$\end{document} provides prediction slots for the future T steps, with selected channels replaced by the disturbance prediction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{D}^{\textrm{seq}}(t)$$\end{document}. A lower-triangular mask is applied to enforce causal consistency:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {MaskedAttention}(Q, K, V) = \text {Softmax}\!\left( \frac{QK^\textrm{T}}{\sqrt{d}} + M\right) V. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This one-shot decoding scheme effectively suppresses error propagation and substantially reduces inference latency, ensuring adequate foresight and responsiveness when communication is interrupted.

Overall, the lightweight encoder ensures that DM4CFRs can operate efficiently on resource-limited edge devices, while the one-shot decoder guarantees rapid generation of long-horizon control sequences during communication outages. Together, these components form the foundation of the sequence modeling architecture that enables DM4CFRs to perform robust and stable EDT-based deduction.Fig. 4Network architecture of the DM4CFRs (a) and interpretable heterogeneous feature embedder (b).

Interpretable heterogeneous features embedder

In the offline deduction phase, the input state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {T}_l(t)$$\end{document} comprises three categories of heterogeneous data with distinct physical properties: network global states, environmental disturbance, and local strategies. These data types exhibit different mechanisms of influence on the system. If they are simply concatenated and uniformly aggregated, their structural characteristics and semantic differences may be obscured, leading to the loss of key information and diminished model performance and interpretability. To address this, this paper proposes an interpretable heterogeneous data aggregation approach that designs differentiated strategies based on the physical nature and interdependencies of each data type, enabling efficient modeling and semantically clear representation of multisource information.

This dual-layer GCN structure captures high-order spatial dependencies while ensuring computational efficiency, making it well-suited for the lightweight and real-time requirements of edge digital twins. The topology-aware fusion enables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_l(t)$$\end{document} to encode both local and neighborhood influences, thereby enhancing spatial awareness. In contrast, environmental disturbance D(t) and local strategies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_l(t)$$\end{document} originate from external meteorological conditions and local decision-making, respectively, and do not possess topological attributes. Therefore, they are not subjected to spatial aggregation to avoid introducing invalid structural assumptions.

To further capture the cyclic patterns of system behavior and disturbances at different time scales, we incorporate a time encoding mechanism at the encoder input. This includes absolute positional encoding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE}_{\text {local}}$$\end{document} and global periodic encoding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE}_{\text {global}}$$\end{document}, which model the stepwise order and long-term periodicity, respectively.

The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE}_{\text {local}}$$\end{document} is based on sinusoidal functions that provide each time step with a unique position encoding:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {PE}_{\text {local}}(pos, 2i) = \sin \left( \frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text {model}}}}\right) \quad \text {PE}_{\text {local}}(pos, 2i+1) = \cos \left( \frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text {model}}}}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where pos denotes the time step index, i is the feature dimension index, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\text {model}}$$\end{document} is the encoding dimension. Since distribution networks often exhibit strong periodic characteristics (e.g., daily load peaks and valleys or seasonal climatic variations), a global time encoding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE}_{\text {global}}$$\end{document} is further introduced to capture the long-term periodic patterns of DER behaviors and environmental changes:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {PE}_{\text {global}}^{\text {day}}(pos, 2i) = \sin \left( \frac{t_{\text {day}}}{96^{2i/d_{\text {model}}}}\right) \quad \text {PE}_{\text {global}}^{\text {year}}(pos, 2i) = \cos \left( \frac{t_{\text {year}}}{365^{2i/d_{\text {model}}}}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{\text {day}}$$\end{document} is the index of the current time step within a day (assuming 15-minute intervals, totaling 96 steps/day), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{\text {year}}$$\end{document} is the index within the year (ranging from 1 to 365). The final temporal embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE}(t)$$\end{document} is obtained by concatenating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE}_{\text {local}}(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE}_{\text {global}}(t)$$\end{document}.

Given the strong dependence of system evolution on recent history, the structured state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{r}_l(t)$$\end{document} is organized into a time sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{R}_l{}_{(t-k+1:t)}$$\end{document}. Alongside the local strategies sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_l{}_{(t-k+1:t)}$$\end{document}, environmental disturbance sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_l{}_{(t-k+1:t)}$$\end{document}, and time encoding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PE}_{(t-k+1:t)}$$\end{document}, these sequential inputs collectively form the temporal input to the deduction module. To address this, a one-dimensional convolution operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Conv1D}(\cdot )$$\end{document} is applied to each sequence. The resulting representation retains the physical sequence of system evolution and enhances the model’s capacity to capture dynamic correlations among operational states, disturbances, and control strategies, providing a unified, semantically meaningful, and physically interpretable input for downstream deduction.

Cyber–physical security assessment and multi-level resilience quantification

Beyond ensuring continuous controllability under unstable communication, the EDF framework also provides a foundation for further enhancing cyber–physical security, motivating the integration of the hierarchical resilience perspective introduced in26 to support a more comprehensive assessment of abnormal control behaviors and system-wide robustness.

Cyber–physical anomaly detection and command trustworthiness evaluation

Beyond maintaining control continuity under weak communication, the EDF also preserves the inherent operating patterns of CFRs learned from historical data, allowing it to function as an independent and reliable reference for identifying maliciously manipulated control commands.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_l(t)$$\end{document} denote the cloud-issued control command, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{c}_l(t)$$\end{document} denote the locally deduced control action generated by the EDT. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{c}_l(t)$$\end{document} is derived from device dynamics, physical constraints, and operational patterns, it serves as a physically consistent reference for validation. Under normal conditions, both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_l(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{c}_l(t)$$\end{document} originate from the same operational state and should differ only within an acceptable range. In contrast, when the communication channel is subjected to malicious setpoint manipulation or contaminated data injection, the deviation between them becomes abnormally large, providing a quantifiable indicator for detecting security threats.

Based on this property, a Data Trustworthiness Identification Module (DTIM) is introduced to evaluate the credibility of cloud-issued commands. DTIM first computes the deviation between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_l(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{c}_l(t)$$\end{document} as31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \delta _l(t) = \frac{\left\| c_l(t)-\hat{c}_l(t) \right\| }{\left\| \hat{c}_l(t) \right\| }\times 100\%. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}To eliminate static biases arising from heterogeneous modeling accuracy across nodes, historical deviations under normal communication are used for normalization. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}$$\end{document} denote the baseline period with stable communication and no malicious manipulation. The normalized deviation is defined as32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{\delta }_l(t)=\frac{\delta _l(t)}{\bar{\delta }_l},\quad \text {where}\quad \bar{\delta }_l=\frac{1}{|\mathscr {O}|}\sum _{t\in \mathscr {O}}\delta _l(t). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which reflects how much the current deviation is amplified relative to the node’s normal operating level. A trustworthiness threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} is subsequently applied. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\delta }_l(t)>\tau$$\end{document}, DTIM flags the incoming command as untrustworthy and replaces it with the EDT-generated action:33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} c_l^{\textrm{exec}}(t)= {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} c_l(t), & \tilde{\delta }_l(t)\le \tau , \\ \hat{c}_l(t), & \tilde{\delta }_l(t)>\tau . \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Through this mechanism, DTIM provides a unified protection layer: it maintains short-term controllability without relying on communication when the channel deteriorates, and it actively filters maliciously manipulated or abnormal commands even when communication appears normal, thereby enhancing the security and reliability of the system under adversarial conditions.

Multi-level resilience quantification framework

Following the identification of untrustworthy commands, this work adopts a multi-level resilience perspective to quantify both node-level and system-level robustness. At the node level, the robustness of each controllable node is quantified using the normalized deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\delta }_l(t)$$\end{document}. The controllable-node resilience is defined as34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{CNR}_l(t)=\exp \!\left( -\alpha \,\tilde{\delta }_l(t)\right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha>0$$\end{document} controls the decay speed. When the cloud-issued command matches the locally deduced action, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\delta }_l(t)$$\end{document} remains low and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{CNR}_l(t)\approx 1$$\end{document}. Under malicious setpoint manipulation, the deviation increases and node resilience declines accordingly.

To further capture the system-wide robustness of the distribution network, a weighted resilience metric is constructed that integrates both controllable-node resilience and node-specific structural vulnerability factors. In this formulation, each controllable node is assigned an influence weight that jointly reflects three key characteristics: its real-time photovoltaic power output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P^{\textrm{PV}}_l(t)$$\end{document}, which determines the instantaneous magnitude of renewable-induced variability; its electrical distance to the point of common coupling \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d^{\textrm{PCC}}_l$$\end{document}, i.e., the electrical interface at which the distribution system connects to the upstream grid and through which voltage disturbances are most prominently transferred; and its graph connectivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_l$$\end{document}, which captures the extent to which disturbances originating at that node may propagate across the network. By embedding these characteristics into the weighting process, the resulting metric incorporates both local deviation behaviors and structural vulnerability attributes.35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w_l = \beta _1\,\frac{P_l^{\textrm{PV}}(t)}{\max _j P_j^{\textrm{PV}}} + \beta _2\,\frac{d_l^{\textrm{PCC}}}{\max _j d_j^{\textrm{PCC}}} + \beta _3\,\frac{h_l}{\max _j h_j}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1 + \beta _2 + \beta _3 = 1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _i \ge 0$$\end{document}.

The system-level resilience is then defined as36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{DSLR}(t)= \frac{\sum _{l=1}^{N} w_l\,\textrm{CNR}_l(t)}{\sum _{l=1}^{N} w_l}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N denotes the number of controllable nodes equipped with EDF. This formulation jointly reflects controllable-node deviation responses, PV-induced intermittency, PCC-distance voltage sensitivity, and topology-driven disturbance propagation.

Results

Scenario description

Based on the modified IEEE 33-bus distribution network, this study validates the applicability and effectiveness of the Edge Digital Twin (EDT) in practical distribution network scenarios. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the nodal loads are categorized into three types: industrial, commercial, and residential, and are modeled using the one-year public load dataset from ELMAS. Meanwhile, photovoltaic (PV) units with an installed capacity of 5 MW are integrated into the network through inverters at buses 4, 13, 16, 17, 21, and 31.Fig. 5Modified IEEE 33-bus System.

Modified IEEE 33-bus System.

The PV output is derived from the 2022 solar irradiance data in Xinjiang, China, sampled at 15-minute intervals, from which a year-long PV generation profile is constructed. As shown in Fig. 6, three representative days in March, August, and November are further selected as typical cases. The results reveal pronounced seasonal variations in solar irradiance, with summer peak values reaching 32–34 MJ/m\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2}$$\end{document}. In the absence of an energy storage system, the midday high-irradiance periods are predominantly curtailed due to network capacity and voltage constraints, which constitute the primary cause of PV curtailment.Fig. 6Relationship between solar irradiance and PV curtailment rate.

Relationship between solar irradiance and PV curtailment rate.

To enhance the PV utilization rate, integrated PV–storage systems are deployed at nodes 15, 16, 21, and 31. Each node is equipped with a 1.5 MW/5 MWh storage unit that is tightly coupled with the local PV source. The information interface of the integrated PV–storage system is connected to the control center through fusion terminals, which act as edge devices. These terminals not only provide north–south communication capabilities but also support local edge computing, thereby enabling efficient interaction between the PV–storage units and the distribution network. Every 15 minutes, the fusion terminal receives a grid-connected power setpoint issued by the control center as the local operation strategy. Upon receiving this setpoint, the terminal dynamically decomposes it based on real-time PV output to allocate the charging and discharging power of the storage units. As shown in Fig. 7, this mechanism maximizes PV utilization and minimizes curtailment, with the results illustrated for Bus 16. When the charging power of the storage unit reaches its upper limit or the state of charge (SOC) becomes full while the PV output still exceeds the reference value, curtailment measures are enforced to ensure the safe and stable operation of the system.Fig. 7Operation of PV–storage integration across three typical days at Bus 16.

Operation of PV–storage integration across three typical days at Bus 16.

Although the integrated PV–storage system can significantly reduce PV curtailment and improve utilization efficiency, however, its effective operation still relies heavily on the real-time grid-connected power setpoints issued by the control center. In practical applications, communication links may experience delays or interruptions. If a fusion terminal fails to receive the setpoint in a timely manner, the regulation of the storage unit becomes ineffective, potentially worsening PV curtailment or even causing voltage violations.

To address this challenge, the EDF is implemented at the fusion terminal to locally deduce the required grid-connected reference values. In this manner, short-term control can be continuously sustained even under communication failures.

Experimental verification of DM4CFRs

After completing the training of DM4CFRs, a fitting evaluation was first conducted to assess its deduction capability. To further confirm its feasibility for edge deployment, we measured the inference overhead on a representative edge device (ARM Cortex-A72 quad-core 1.5 GHz, 8 GB RAM). Each deduction requires 27.4 ms on average, with a memory footprint of approximately 145 MB and an energy consumption of 0.21 J–well within the limits of typical edge hardware.

As illustrated in Fig. 8, four representative cases of DM4CFRs deducing local setpoints under communication fluctuations are presented. In Cases 1 and 2, the fluctuations occur during the storage charging stage, and the trajectories deduced by the EDT closely follow the grid-connected power setpoints of the nodes, demonstrating high accuracy with an average mean squared error (MSE) of 0.445. In contrast, Cases 3 and 4 correspond to the storage discharging stage. During this stage, the operational strategy is more sensitive to external factors such as electricity price and load fluctuations, leading to noticeably larger deviations between the EDT-deduced trajectories and the real setpoints, with an average MSE of 0.850. This discrepancy arises because the training logic of DM4CFRs is primarily designed around daytime PV–storage co-optimization aimed at minimizing PV curtailment, and most of the disturbance features incorporated in the environmental disturbance channel are strongly correlated with PV generation. Electricity price, by contrast, is not included in the model’s training process, resulting in limited learning of the nighttime discharging logic. Improving the model’s performance in such nighttime scenarios would require incorporating discharging strategies and their related features into the training process. However, this nighttime-strategy optimization lies outside the main scope of this study.Fig. 84 representative cases of DM4CFRs deducing grid-connected power setpoints.

4 representative cases of DM4CFRs deducing grid-connected power setpoints.

Conclusion

This paper proposes an EDF designed to ensure global coordination and stable operation of CFRs under communication fluctuations and potential data integrity attacks. When communication functions normally, EDF maintains a structured mapping of global operational states and coordination information through real-time synchronization with the control center. When communication deteriorates or suspicious data anomalies emerge, EDF activates local deduction to generate interpretable and trustworthy substitute control strategies, enabling CFRs in “information islands” to respond reliably to system-level objectives using only local information. Moreover, by comparing cloud-issued commands with locally deduced trajectories, EDF provides a reliable reference baseline for detecting malicious command tampering or disturbance injection, thereby offering inherent cyber–physical defensive capability.

Within the EDF, the interpretable deduction model DM4CFRs incorporates explicit temporal encoding, 1D convolutional aggregation, and decoupled modeling of environmental disturbance sequences, significantly enhancing deduction accuracy and stability. Experimental results show that EDF substantially outperforms traditional time-series models across different deduction lengths, with its advantage becoming increasingly pronounced over longer horizons. Module and feature ablation studies further verify the indispensable roles of key architectural components and disturbance information. Under noise perturbations and command-manipulation attacks, the deduced local trajectories of EDF serve as a trustworthy benchmark for assessing command integrity, demonstrating the framework’s practical value in enhancing operational resilience.

Overall, EDF delivers high accuracy, robustness, and transparency, providing verifiable, traceable, and deployable deduction-based support for distribution network operation under constrained or unreliable communication. Future research may proceed in several directions: (1) deploying EDF on real-world edge controllers to evaluate real-time performance under varying communication and computational conditions; (2) integrating EDF with reinforcement learning and model predictive control to form a unified hybrid framework for “deduction–control” joint optimization; and (3) enhancing EDF’s defensive capabilities against broader classes of data integrity attacks and high-dimensional disturbances through multi-source anomaly detection and trustworthy control mechanisms.
