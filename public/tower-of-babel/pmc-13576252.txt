HFpEF in perioperative medicine
Authors: Maria Giulia Bellicini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent perioperative literature has increasingly questioned the central role of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in cardiovascular risk stratification. While patients with preserved LVEF may experience perioperative complications, acute heart failure (AHF) in appears uncommon in the absence of identifiable major structural or hemodynamic dysfunction, such as severe valvular disease, precapillary pulmonary hypertension with cor pulmonale, restrictive cardiomyopathies, or terminal renal disease. This distinction has important implications for the interpretation of perioperative risk in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).In the absence of these conditions, the HFpEF label may often identify a heterogeneous syndrome dominated by exertional dyspnoea, frequently with substantial extracardiac and peripheral contributors, rather than a cardiac phenotype intrinsically prone to acute decompensation. Perioperative vulnerability in these patients may therefore reflect advanced age, extracardiac comorbidity burden, pulmonary or renal disease, and generalized multisystem frailty, while cardiac systolic performance itself remains largely preserved. A physiology-guided perioperative approach integrating structural abnormalities, pulmonary pressures, congestion, and right ventricular function may therefore provide greater clinical relevance than LVEF-based categorization alone.

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the recent narrative review by Rabis and Moldzio (2026) discussing the limitations of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in perioperative cardiovascular assessment. The authors appropriately emphasize that perioperative vulnerability cannot be reduced to a single systolic parameter and that congestion, right ventricular dysfunction, filling pressures, and volume status often provide more clinically relevant information than EF alone. Moreover, the review correctly highlights that patients with preserved EF may experience perioperative instability despite apparently normal systolic function.

However, I believe the discussion surrounding heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) still deserves further conceptual clarification. Yet this observation does not necessarily imply that preserved-EF physiology itself represents an intrinsically failing cardiac phenotype.

The distinction may be clinically relevant because unlike classical systolic heart failure (HFrEF), the structurally preserved LV frequently maintains adequate contractile performance even in elderly or multimorbid individuals. Instead, in almost all pcaseatients, congestion occurring in the presence of preserved EF may instead reflects alternative and well-recognizable structural or hemodynamic disorders (Bellicini, 2025). Consequently, many so-called HFpEF phenotypes may resemble a syndrome of exertional dyspnea, reduced physiological reserve, and multisystem frailty more than a primary decompensating cardiomyopathy (Landsteiner et al. 2025).

Our centre recent observations in a cohort of more than 770 consecutive patients admitted with diagnosis acute HF (AHF) support this interpretation. When comprehensive echocardiography was systematically performed before diuretic administration and HFpEF mimics were rigorously excluded, truly isolated preserved-EF decompensation became uncommon in patients with congestion. Most congested patients with preserved EF exhibited major structural abnormalities for their presentation, particularly severe valvular dysfunction (most frequently), precapillary pulmonary hypertension due to cor pulmonale, restrictive cardiomyopathies (Bellicini, 2025).

Pocus

From a practical bedside perspective, in the absence of major structural abnormalities, a heart with preserved EF does not typically progress to decompensated HF with systemic congestion. Perioperative point-of-care ultrasound should therefore primarily focus on identifying preserved LVEF and the major pathophysiological substrates capable of causing congestion despite preserved LVEF (HFpEF mimics).

In clinical practice, a LV with preserved parasternal end-diastolic diameter and inward wall thickening of all myocardial segments strongly supports normal LVEF more reproducibily than volumetric measurements.

Severe valvular lesions are the main structural abnormality that should be searched in preserved LVEF. Notably, hemodynamically significant valvular regurgitation or stenosis does not develop in the absence of an underlying structural substrate. Therefore, whenever severe valvular disease is suspected, echocardiographic assessment should also aim to identify the mechanism responsible for the lesion, for example in severe mitral regurgitation, one of the following mechanism should be almost always present: prolapsing valves in Barlow disease, flail, LV enlargement, inferior myocardial infarction with leaflet tethering, or marked annular dilatation in permanent atrial fibrillation, or high frequency atrial fibrillation. In severe aortic stenosis, parasternal imaging will typically show a heavily calcified valve with markedly restricted leaflet excursion and a visually very small systolic opening, while Doppler interrogation will demonstrate marked transvalvular flow acceleration. Similarly, severe tricuspid regurgitation should have an identifiable anatomical or haemodynamic substrate. It may occur secondary to significant left-sided heart disease able to cause decompensation or precapillary pulmonary hypertension; as atrial functional tricuspid regurgitation, characterized by marked right atrial and tricuspid annular dilatation, particularly in permanent atrial fibrillation; or as device-related regurgitation, when a transvenous pacing or defibrillator lead interferes with leaflet motion or coaptation. From a technical standpoint, severe valvular regurgitation should not be assessed rigidly from standard views alone, but by actively searching for the maximal vena contracta diameter across perpendicular planes, particularly apical four- and two-chamber views for mitral regurgitation and parasternal long-axis and apical five-chamber views for aortic regurgitation.

Less frequent mimics requiring bedside evaluation include precapillary pulmonary hypertension, (most often due to severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or pulmonary fibrosis), severe pericardial effusion, restrictive cardiomyopathies (transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis in elderly patients with marked hypertrophy), and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with significant outflow obstruction in younger individual.

Congestion assessment should also integrate pulmonary pressures. Sysitolic pulmonary artery pressure (SPAP) values up to approximately 40–45 mmHg may be normally observed in elderly patients in the absence of caval congestion. In pulmonary hypertension secondary to left-sided significant (see above) heart disease, the development of SPAP values around or above 50 mmHg typically coincides with the onset of systemic venous congestion and therefore is generally associated with a dilated and non-collapsible inferior vena cava (respiratory collapsibility even in presence of dilated vein means low venous pressure) (Bellicini 2025). For this reason, SPAP values ≥ 50 mmHg in the presence of caval congestion strongly support clinically relevant pulmonary and systemic congestion and, in presence of preserved LVEF, the presence of an HFpEF mimics, as also high LV filling pressure. Alternatively in the absence of isolated right-sided disease or precapillary pulmonary hypertension, LV filling patterns (normal or altered relaxation) alone may also already provide patient’s haemodynamic compensation status.

Similarly to congestion, RV is difficult to evaluate but its dysfunction rarely occurs in isolation and is normally due to progressive RV dilatation that presents only in case of longstanding above mentioned left-sided heart significant disease.

Regarding advanced echocardiographic parameters, strain-based measurements are frequently redundant: RV strain largely reflects abnormalities already appreciable through TAPSE and LV strain frequently parallels abnormalities of diastolic function. Three-dimensional echocardiography, remain strongly dependent on image quality and operator interpretation. In routine clinical practice, the quality of echocardiographic evaluation still primarily relies on understanding the clinical context, recognizing the underlying pathophysiological question, and interpreting measurements within an integrated physiological framework. Moreover, automated measurements should not replace clinical interpretation, as they may themselves be inaccurate or misleading when applied without adequate clinical reasoning or when relevant abnormalities are not fully visualized in standard imaging planes. Particular caution is warranted when rigid cut-offs imply a degree of precision beyond the actual sensitivity and reproducibility of the measurement, such as interpreting few percentage-point differences in LVEF or millimetre-level differences in linear dimensions as clinically meaningful.

Natriuretic peptides

Among biomarkers, natriuretic peptides remain the most established biomarkers of HF also in perioperative risk stratification. However, their interpretation should be cautious. In acute dyspoea, NT-proBNP values considered suggestive of AHF are substantially elevated and age-adjusted, whereas the widely used NT-proBNP threshold of 300 pg/mL in acute dyspnea was originally validated as a highly sensitive rule-out cut-off for AHF rather than rule-in (Januzzi et al. 2006). This limitation becomes even more relevant in elderly multimorbid patients and in atrial fibrillation, where baseline natriuretic peptide values are frequently substantially higher. Moreover, patients may exhibit markedly elevated natriuretic peptides despite preserved haemodynamic compensation, particularly during extracardiac acute illness or systemic stress. Similarly, lower chronic thresholds such as NT-proBNP ≥ 125 pg/mL are largely derived from healthy young population percentiles (Welsh et al. 2022). Therefore, in the perioperative setting, natriuretic peptides should primarily be interpreted as markers of increased risk and eventually exertional dyspnoea due to CV burden rather than as standalone evidence of HF. The propensity to develop AHF is instead more reliably suggested by the presence of above mentioned major structural abnormalities on echocardiography.

CPET and functional capacity tests

Although Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing (CPET) is increasingly used in research settings to characterize exercise intolerance, it is not routinely incorporated into standard diagnostic evaluation of HFpEF nor into perioperative assessment of patients with preserved LVEF. Importantly, conventional noninvasive CPET does not directly measure LV filling pressures and consequently, primarily describes exercise physiology and exertional limitation rather than directly identifying a cardiac substrate intrinsically prone to AHF.

Notably, invasive exercise frequently defines HFpEF by elevations in exercise pulmonary capillary wedge pressure compared with healthy controls, often using historical hemodynamic cut-offs derived from healthy populations. However, these studies rarely document overt pulmonary edema or clinically manifest pulmonary congestion during exercise. Interestingly overall cardiovascular performance under stress often remains relatively preserved. In the study by Reddy et al. patients labelled as HFpEF reached exercise SPAP values of 63 ± 14 mmHg (upper exercise stress cut-offs are around 60 mmHg) and still maintained peak oxygen consumption. Moreover, CPET alone showed poor discrimination in the large intermediate peak VO₂ range (14–20 ml/kg/min) (Reddy et al. 2018; Lancellotti et al 2017).

HFpEF findings may therefore be more consistent with an exertional dyspnea syndrome characterized by a borderline exercise-related increases in filling pressures accompanied by global muscular and respiratory limitations (Landsteiner et al. 2025) rather than with a condition intrinsically predisposed to AHF.

In routine perioperative practice, careful estimation of functional capacity frequently provides more clinically useful information regarding integrated physiological reserve than isolated resting echocardiographic findings. In particular, the ability to climb a flight of stairs without limiting symptoms, corresponding approximately to > 4 METs, generally indicates preserved functional capacity and should be interpreted as absence of exertional dyspnea (NYHA I). Finally, in very elderly frail individuals, perioperative vulnerability linked to mild dyspnoea frequently reflects global physiological fragility more than a specific cardiac functional parameter, reinforcing the importance of careful patient selection rather than increasingly sophisticated exercise testing alone.

Conclusion

Importantly, this perspective does not diminish the prognostic significance of dyspnea, elevated natriuretic peptides, or perioperative fragility. Rather, it suggests that contemporary HFpEF definitions may have progressively grouped together profoundly different conditions under an increasingly imprecise umbrella construct. Biomarkers, congestion indices, pulmonary pressures, and venous Doppler findings may therefore reflect the severity of systemic illness, extracardiac frailty, or alternative cardiac pathologies more than intrinsic failure of a structurally preserved LV.

In this regard, the transition from an EF-centered approach toward a physiology-guided perioperative assessment is highly welcome. Nevertheless, future studies should also reconsider whether current HFpEF definitions adequately distinguish true cardiac decompensation from heterogeneous syndromes of aging, congestion, pulmonary vascular disease, and multisystem frailty occurring in patients with preserved systolic function.
