The relationship between 24-hour QT interval variability and heart rate variability in patients with acute myocardial infarction and the occurrence of major cardiovascular adverse events after PCI surgery
Authors: Nan Wang, Rui Wang, Jingang Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality despite advancements in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). This study investigates whether 24-hour QT interval variability (QTV) and heart rate variability (HRV) can predict major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) post-PCI.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case-control study involving 238 AMI patients undergoing PCI between June 2021 and January 2023. Patients were divided into MACE (n = 96) and non-MACE (n = 142) groups. Variability in QT interval and HRV was assessed via continuous ECG monitoring pre- and post-PCI using a Holter recorder with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. QT variability index (QTVI) and other QT measures were evaluated for their predictive power regarding MACE occurrence. All QT interval measurements were corrected using the Fridericia formula (QTcF).ResultsAnalysis revealed that the corrected QT interval (QTcF) was significantly prolonged in the MACE group compared with the non-MACE group (425.34 ± 42.15 ms vs. 410.28 ± 33.47 ms, P = 0.004). Additionally, the standard deviation of QT intervals (QT-SD) was significantly greater in the MACE group (7.80 ± 1.07 ms vs. 7.54 ± 0.33 ms, P = 0.023).The QTVI was higher in the MACE group (-0.60 ± 0.11 vs. -0.65 ± 0.21, P = 0.007), as was the normalized QT variance (QTVN, calculated as QTv/QTm²)(0.38 ± 0.07 vs. 0.36 ± 0.04, P = 0.010). There was a significant correlation between hypertension and MACE occurrence (P = 0.006), and elevated pulmonary artery systolic pressure was more prominent in the MACE group (25.66 ± 3.14 mmHg vs. 23.77 ± 6.58 mmHg, P = 0.003). ROC analysis showed that combining QTVI with clinical variables achieved an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.72–0.84).ConclusionQTV is a significant predictor of MACE post-PCI in AMI patients, underscoring its potential utility in post-procedural monitoring and risk stratification.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, necessitating prompt and effective medical intervention to minimize adverse clinical outcomes [1]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a well-established therapeutic procedure that significantly improves the prognosis of patients experiencing AMI by promptly restoring coronary blood flow [2, 3]. Despite the advances in PCI technology and technique, the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), such as recurrent myocardial infarction, cardiac death, and the need for revascularization, continues to pose a significant challenge to long-term patient survival and quality of life [4, 5]. Identifying reliable predictors of MACE following PCI can optimize post-procedural management and improve patient outcomes.

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward assessing QT interval variability (QTV) and heart rate variability (HRV) as potential biomarkers for predicting adverse cardiac events [6]. The QT interval, representing the time from the onset of ventricular depolarization to complete repolarization, serves as a critical index of cardiac electrophysiological health [7]. Variability in the QT interval can indicate underlying instability in repolarization processes, potentially leading to life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias [8]. Simultaneously, HRV, reflecting autonomic nervous system modulation of heart rate, serves as an indicator of cardiac autonomic function [9]. Prior studies have demonstrated that both increased QT variability and decreased HRV are associated with heightened risk of cardiac mortality and morbidity across diverse patient populations, including those undergoing PCI [10].

Recent advances in Holter-based QTV assessment have further enhanced our understanding of repolarization dynamics. A 2025 study by Jarecka-Dobroń et al. demonstrated that patients with obstructive sleep apnea exhibited significantly elevated 24-hour Holter QTV, with severe OSA patients showing QTVI of −1.19 compared to −1.43 in controls, correlating with hypoxic burden [11]. Similarly, Solhjoo et al. in 2024 utilized a triple-design approach (cross-sectional, cohort, and experimental) to establish that sleep-disordered breathing destabilizes ventricular repolarization, with nocturnal QTVI elevation associated with increased all-cause mortality [12]. These findings underscore the clinical utility of continuous QTV monitoring in identifying high-risk patients.

Previous research has primarily focused on static measurements of the QT interval and HRV without fully exploring their dynamics over extended periods, such as the 24-hour recording period used in Holter monitoring [13]. Given the dynamic nature of myocardial repolarization and the influence of circadian rhythms on autonomic function, evaluating the variability of these indices over a 24-hour period provides a more comprehensive understanding of the electrophysiological milieu post-AMI. This study aims to explore the individual and combined utility of 24-hour QTV and HRV in predicting MACE in patients with AMI undergoing PCI.

Materials and methods

Case selection

This study was a retrospective case-control analysis involving 238 patients who were diagnosed with AMI and underwent PCI at our hospital between June 2021 and January 2023. The patients were categorized into two groups based on the occurrence of MACE within two years post-procedure: the MACE group (96 patients) and the Non-MACE group (142 patients). Data collected from the hospital’s medical record system included demographic information, general medical data, blood test results, echocardiography findings, and medication usage. The study focused on analyzing the variability in QT interval and heart rate to assess their predictive value for MACE following PCI.

This study received approval from our institution’s Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee. Informed consent was waived because the research exclusively involved de-identified patient data, ensuring no potential harm or impact on patient care. This waiver was granted following regulatory and ethical guidelines governing retrospective research studies.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of China-Japanese Friendship Hospital (2023-BJZ-5028).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Participants were eligible if they met the following conditions: (1) Diagnosed with acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction or non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction [14]; (2) Aged between 18 and 80 years; (3) Received PCI treatment within 24 h of illness onset; (4) Able to complete all necessary examinations and follow-ups cooperatively.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they had any of the following conditions: (1) Electrocardiographic abnormalities such as atrioventricular block, intraventricular conduction disturbances, paced rhythm, frequent ectopic beats, imperceptible T-wave, severe artifact interference, transient atrial fibrillation, or sick sinus syndrome; (2) Pregnant or lactating women; (3) Continued use of antiarrhythmic drugs before surgery; (4) Bleeding tendency or coagulation dysfunction; (5) Known allergy to drugs or materials used during PCI procedures; (6) Underwent other cardiovascular surgeries or interventional treatments within the previous 3 months.

Treatment approach and data acquisition

The patients’ electrocardiograms (ECGs) were continuously monitored using a cardiovascular function monitoring device (CVFD-II, Huiyironggong Technology Co., Ltd, Ji’nan, China) within 24 h before and after PCI surgery to record the QT interval and heart rate. The Holter recording system operated at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz, which meets the ≥ 500 Hz precision recommendation for ensuring QTVI reproducibility, as established by the 2017 ISHNE-HRS consensus statement [15].

Analysis of QTV and HRV: The RR and QT intervals were automatically and independently measured in each channel using an analyzer (SCM-6000, Fukuda Denshi Co., Tokyo, Japan). The automated QT measurement algorithm used in this study has been validated to have an average error of 3.95 ± 5.5 ms compared to expert manual annotations in 15,910 clinical ECGs, with 95% confidence intervals ≤ 5 ms [16]. All QT interval measurements were corrected for heart rate using the Fridericia formula (QTcF = QT/RR^1/3) to account for rate-related variations.

The RR interval was determined through peak detection, while the QT interval was defined as the time from the Q-wave to the point where the isoelectric line intersected a tangential line representing the maximal slope. Only recordings with stable RR and QT intervals, comprising less than 3% ectopic beats, were analyzed in a 512-beat series every 2 h. Ectopic beat-related large abrupt deflections in the resampled instantaneous RR and QT interval series were eliminated using a linear spline approach. Measurements were taken from the channel with the best quality recording. Linear trends in the heart rate and QT interval series over each 512-beat series were corrected by subtracting the best-fit line.

Statistical methods

QT variability index (QTVI) quantifies repolarization lability by normalizing the variance of QT intervals against heart rate variance over 256-second surface ECG segments, with the result expressed as a logarithm. Higher QTVI values have been demonstrated to correlate with increased risk of sudden cardiac death and ventricular arrhythmias [17, 18]. The QTVI was calculated using the equation: QTVI = log10 [(QTv/QTm²)/(RRv/RRm²)], where QTv and RRv represent the variances, and QTm and RRm represent the means of the respective time series. To assess repolarization lability without HRV adjustment, QT variance normalized for the mean QT (QTVN) was calculated as: QTVN = QTv/QTm².

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 29.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Sample size calculation using G*Power indicated that with α = 0.05 and effect size d = 0.4, our study of 238 patients achieved 90% statistical power. For multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied to maintain the family-wise error rate. Categorical data were represented as [n (%)]. The chi-square test was used when the sample size was ≥ 40 and the expected frequency (T) was ≥ 5. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses were performed incorporating intravenous diuretic use and RCA involvement as covariates in multivariate logistic regression models (see Supplementary Materials). ROC curves were constructed to evaluate the discriminatory power of QTVI combined with clinical variables (hypertension, ejection fraction, pulmonary artery systolic pressure) for predicting MACE, with comparisons to established risk scores.

Preoperative blood testing

Within 24 h post-surgery, a blood sample of approximately 5 milliliters was collected from the patient’s elbow vein to comprehensively evaluate cardiac injury markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein, and other significant biochemical indicators. The blood samples were delivered to the laboratory within 30 min and analyzed using an automated biochemical analyzer (Mindray, Shenzhen, China; model: BS-380).

Echocardiographic examination

An echocardiographic examination was conducted on patients 24 h after undergoing PCI. An experienced cardiac sonographer performed the examination using an echocardiography machine equipped with M-mode, two-dimensional, and Doppler modes (Philips Healthcare, Jiangsu Suzhou, model: EPIQ 7). Key measurements included the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and right ventricular function.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study population comprised 238 participants, divided into a MACE group (n = 96) and a non-MACE group (n = 142) (Table 1). There were no statistically significant differences between the groups regarding age (67.54 ± 3.98 vs. 68.06 ± 3.24, P = 0.287), gender distribution (71 male/25 female vs. 101 male/41 female, P = 0.632), body mass index (24.11 ± 2.35 vs. 24.06 ± 2.44, P = 0.883), systolic blood pressure (130.21 ± 20.56 mmHg vs. 129.77 ± 21.34 mmHg, P = 0.873), or diastolic blood pressure (79.03 ± 5.14 mmHg vs. 78.17 ± 4.36 mmHg, P = 0.167).

Additional key confounding variables were assessed: door-to-balloon time was similar between groups (82.4 ± 15.6 min vs. 84.1 ± 14.2 min, P = 0.392), peak troponin I levels showed no significant difference (18.5 ± 6.3 ng/mL vs. 17.8 ± 5.9 ng/mL, P = 0.384), and complete revascularization was achieved in comparable proportions (78.1% vs. 81.0%, P = 0.589). Notably, hypertension was significantly higher in the MACE group compared to the non-MACE group (53.12% vs. 35.21%, P = 0.006*), suggesting a potential link between hypertension and the occurrence of major cardiovascular adverse events.

Lesion characteristics

In the analysis of lesion characteristics between the MACE group and the non-MACE group, there were no statistically significant differences observed in the prevalence of acute total occlusion (33.33% vs. 28.87%, P = 0.464) or in infarct-related artery involvement of left anterior descending (LAD; 62.50% vs. 59.86%, P = 0.682) and left circumflex (LCx; 9.38% vs. 9.86%, P = 0.901) arteries (Table 2). However, a significant difference was noted in the involvement of the right coronary artery (RCA), with a higher percentage of RCA involvement in the non-MACE group compared to the MACE group (31.69% vs. 15.62%, P = 0.005*).

Blood testing

The mean levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were similar between the groups (0.13 ± 0.04 mg/dL vs. 0.13 ± 0.03 mg/dL, P = 0.085) (Table 3). TC levels showed negligible difference (182.56 ± 31.24 mg/dL vs. 181.04 ± 30.11 mg/dL, P = 0.706), as did low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels (110.24 ± 11.17 mg/dL vs. 110.56 ± 14.14 mg/dL, P = 0.847). High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels were closely comparable (44.52 ± 10.30 mg/dL vs. 43.99 ± 11.23 mg/dL, P = 0.713). Triglyceride levels (134.78 ± 15.56 mg/dL vs. 135.27 ± 9.84 mg/dL, P = 0.784), fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels (117.89 ± 14.94 mg/dL vs. 117.33 ± 15.40 mg/dL, P = 0.782), and HbA1c percentages (6.33 ± 0.97% vs. 6.34 ± 1.05%, P = 0.951) were also comparable between the two groups. These findings indicate no significant differences in these biochemical markers in relation to the occurrence of MACE following PCI surgery.

Echocardiographic findings

There were no statistically significant differences in left ventricular ejection fraction categories, with similar proportions exhibiting ejection fractions of ≥ 50% (69.79% vs. 67.61%, P = 0.722), 30%−49% (25.00% vs. 26.76%, P = 0.761), and < 30% (5.21% vs. 5.63%, P = 0.887) (Table 4). The mean LVEF was 48.2 ± 8.4% in the MACE group and 49.1 ± 7.9% in the non-MACE group (P = 0.402), with no statistically significant difference between groups. However, a significant difference was observed in the prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy, which was higher in the MACE group (46.88%) compared to the non-MACE group (33.80%, P = 0.043*). Furthermore, pulmonary artery systolic pressure was significantly elevated in the MACE group (25.66 ± 3.14 mmHg) relative to the non-MACE group (23.77 ± 6.58 mmHg, P = 0.003*).

QTV interval variability

The corrected QT interval (QTcF) was significantly prolonged in the MACE group (425.34 ± 42.15 ms) compared to the non-MACE group (410.28 ± 33.47 ms, P = 0.004*), suggesting a potential link between prolonged QT intervals and the occurrence of MACE (Fig. 1A). The standard deviation of the QT interval (QT-SD) was significantly greater in the MACE group compared to the non-MACE group (7.80 ± 1.07 ms vs. 7.54 ± 0.33 ms, P = 0.023*). The QTVI was notably higher in the MACE group (−0.60 ± 0.11) than in the non-MACE group (−0.65 ± 0.21, P = 0.007*), indicating a greater degree of QTV associated with adverse outcomes. Additionally, the QTVN was significantly elevated in the MACE group (0.38 ± 0.07) compared to the non-MACE group (0.36 ± 0.04, P = 0.010*).

HRV

The mean RR interval, reflecting the average time between R peaks on an electrocardiogram, was similar between the groups (917.82 ± 80.55 ms vs. 906.36 ± 71.79 ms, P = 0.251) (Fig. 2). The standard deviation of RR intervals (RR-SD), indicative of overall HRV, showed a nonsignificant difference (34.77 ± 6.96 ms vs. 35.92 ± 8.81 ms, P = 0.263). Low-frequency power of RR intervals (RR-LF, 0.04–0.15 Hz) and high-frequency power (RR-HF, 0.15–0.40 Hz) were similarly distributed between the MACE and non-MACE groups (222.34 ± 10.93 ms² vs. 223.16 ± 9.85 ms², P = 0.547 for RR-LF, and 109.52 ± 17.94 ms² vs. 114.24 ± 26.38 ms², P = 0.101 for RR-HF). The RR LF/HF ratio, representing the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic modulation, did not differ significantly between the groups (2.77 ± 0.45 vs. 2.67 ± 0.60, P = 0.125). These findings suggest that HRV differences were not associated with the occurrence of MACE in this cohort following PCI surgery.

Medication use

Medication use was comparable between groups (Table 5), with nearly universal DAPT (97.92% vs. 98.59%, P = 1.000), identical beta-blocker usage (88.54%, P = 0.332), and similar rates of ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNis (60.42% vs. 57.04%, P = 0.604), statins (98.96% vs. 99.30%, P = 1.000), and SGLT2 inhibitors (22.92% vs. 25.35%, P = 0.661). Intravenous diuretics during hospitalization were used more frequently in the non-MACE group (19.72% vs. 8.33%, P = 0.016).

Correlation analysis

The correlation analysis between 24-hour QTV, HRV, and the occurrence of MACE after PCI surgery in patients with AMI revealed several significant associations (Table 6). A positive correlation was observed between hypertension and MACE (rho = 0.178, P = 0.006), as well as between increased pulmonary artery systolic pressure and MACE (rho = 0.192, P = 0.003). Additionally, the presence of left ventricular hypertrophy showed a positive association with MACE (rho = 0.131, P = 0.043). The correlation analysis also indicated that QTV parameters, such as the QT interval (rho = 0.182, P = 0.005), QT-SD (rho = 0.139, P = 0.032), and QTVN (rho = 0.191, P = 0.003), were positively correlated with MACE occurrence. Conversely, significant negative correlations were found between RCA involvement and MACE (rho = −0.182, P = 0.005), as well as between intravenous diuretics during hospitalization and MACE (rho = −0.156, P = 0.016), suggesting that these may be protective factors. The QTVI also displayed a negative correlation with MACE (rho = −0.165, P = 0.011). The RR-HF parameter showed a nonsignificant trend towards a negative correlation with MACE (rho = −0.118, P = 0.070) indicating a potential area for further research. These results underscore the complex interplay of cardiovascular function and medication use in relation to the risk of adverse events post-PCI surgery.

Predictive model and ROC analysis

To evaluate the clinical utility of QTVI for MACE prediction, we constructed a multivariate predictive model incorporating QTVI with established clinical risk factors (Fig. 3). The combined model including QTVI, hypertension, ejection fraction, and pulmonary artery systolic pressure achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.72–0.84), which was significantly superior to the GRACE score alone (AUC = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.62–0.76, P = 0.018) and comparable to the TIMI risk score (AUC = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.68–0.80, P = 0.142). Sensitivity analysis incorporating intravenous diuretic use and RCA involvement as covariates yielded an adjusted odds ratio for QTVI of 2.31 (95% CI: 1.42–3.76, P < 0.001), confirming its independent predictive value.

Discussion

The present study investigates the association between 24-hour QTV, HRV, and the occurrence of MACE in patients with AMI undergoing PCI. Firstly, the study highlights the significance of prolonged QT intervals and increased QT variability as predictive markers for adverse cardiac events post-PCI. The QT interval, which reflects the time taken for ventricular depolarization and repolarization, is known to be influenced by various factors, including autonomic nervous system regulation and myocardial ischemia [19]. The prolongation of the corrected QT interval (QTcF) in the MACE group, as observed in this study, suggests a potential disruption in myocardial repolarization processes.

Recent studies have corroborated our findings regarding the prognostic value of QTV in cardiac patients. Reshef et al. (2024) demonstrated that even ultra-short HRV measurements obtained from 10-second ECG recordings in hospitalized NSTEMI patients could predict 30-day MACE, suggesting that brief electrophysiological assessments possess risk stratification value that could complement QTVI measurements [20]. This aligns with our 24-hour continuous monitoring approach, which captures both transient and sustained repolarization abnormalities.

The correlation between increased QT variability and MACE emphasizes the clinical relevance of dynamic electrophysiological monitoring in post-infarction patients. The QTVI and normalized QTVN serve as useful metrics for assessing repolarization instability [21]. The elevated QTVI and QTVN values in the MACE group suggest that patients at risk exhibit significant fluctuations in repolarization, which may not be adequately captured by static QT interval measurements alone.

Interestingly, whereas QT variability showed a strong association with MACE, HRV metrics did not demonstrate significant differences between the MACE and non-MACE groups. This finding warrants careful interpretation.A recent meta-analysis by Fang et al. (2020) reported that low HRV increases MACE risk (HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.16–1.72), though subgroup analysis revealed smaller differences within AMI cohorts specifically [22]. The high prevalence of beta-blocker use in our cohort (88.54% in both groups) may have masked underlying HRV differences. Airaksinen et al. previously demonstrated that beta-blockade significantly increases 24-hour HRV indices by 60–70%, potentially normalizing autonomic parameters that would otherwise differ between groups [23].

Furthermore, HRV primarily reflects autonomic nervous system modulation, while QTV represents temporal instability in ventricular repolarization—two distinct electrophysiological phenomena that may become “desynchronized” in the post-AMI setting. La Rovere et al. (2025) showed that QTVI measured one month post-discharge was a stronger predictor of three-year all-cause mortality (HR 2.3 for QTVI > −1.0) compared to HRV parameters measured at the same timepoint [24]. This suggests that QTV may capture repolarization heterogeneity more directly relevant to arrhythmogenesis in the recovering myocardium.

The study also identified key clinical variables correlating with MACE occurrence. The inverse relationship between RCA involvement and MACE (rho = −0.182, P = 0.005) warrants further discussion.RCA territory typically supplies less myocardial mass compared to LAD territory, and RCA infarctions more readily develop collateral circulation, potentially explaining their association with lower MACE risk [25]. Additionally, RCA occlusions may prompt more aggressive acute management given their association with bradyarrhythmias and hemodynamic instability, paradoxically leading to better long-term outcomes.

Clinical implementation feasibility

The translation of QTV monitoring into routine clinical practice appears feasible based on current technology and workflow considerations. According to the 2017 ISHNE-HRS consensus statement, standard 24-hour 3-lead Holter monitoring with commercial analysis software is sufficient for QTVI/HRV risk stratification [15]. Trained ECG technicians can complete the full analysis within 30 min per patient [26]. From a cost-effectiveness perspective, Holter ECG represents a low-cost risk stratification tool. Economic modeling suggests a single Holter examination costs approximately USD 35 (≈¥250), with a total incremental cost of USD 39.8 per patient and an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of USD 8,644/QALY, well below accepted thresholds [27]. Implementation would require: (1) 24-hour 3-lead Holter equipment with ≥ 500 Hz sampling rate; (2) Commercial software with QTVI calculation capability; (3) Brief training for ECG technicians (estimated 2–4 h); and (4) Integration into existing post-PCI monitoring protocols.

Study limitations

While the study provides valuable insights into the relationship between 24-hour QTV, HRV, and MACE following PCI in AMI patients, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the single-center retrospective design limits the generalizability of our findings to broader populations with different demographics, treatment protocols, or healthcare systems. Second, the observational design precludes establishment of causal relationships. Although we assessed key confounding variables including door-to-balloon time, peak troponin levels, and revascularization completeness, unmeasured confounders may still influence outcomes. Third, the relatively short two-year follow-up period may not capture very late cardiovascular events (beyond 2 years), potentially underestimating the long-term prognostic value of QTV parameters. Future multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up durations are needed to validate these findings and establish their applicability across diverse clinical settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms the prognostic value of QTV in predicting adverse cardiovascular events after PCI in the AMI population. The combination of QTVI with clinical variables provides incremental predictive value beyond traditional risk scores, with practical implementation appearing feasible within existing clinical workflows. Future research should focus on prospective validation in multicenter cohorts, development of QTVI-based risk algorithms, and evaluation of targeted interventions for high-QTVI patients.
