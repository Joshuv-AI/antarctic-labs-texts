Capsule Dynamics, Implantation, and Explantation of the Smaller Incision, New-Generation Implantable Miniature Telescope: a Miyake–Apple Study
Authors: Maximilian Hammer, Sonja Schickhardt, Lu Zhang, Donald Munro, Timur M. Yildirim, Gerd U. Auffarth
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

The SING IMT, an acronym for smaller-incision new-generation intraocular miniature telescope, is based on the principle of a fixed-focus Galilean telescope1,2 and may alleviate the symptom burden in patients with central vision loss.3,4 It improves on many of its predecessor's surgical disadvantages. It is implanted through a 6.5- to 7.5-mm incision; a 5.5-mm capsulorhexis is sufficient, less sutures are needed to close the incisional wound, and the duration of the implantation procedure is greatly reduced.5 Nevertheless, implanting the device still poses a challenge to surgeons because of its axial length of 4.4 mm, haptic diameter of 10.8 mm, its greater weight of 63 mg in the aqueous humor, the unusual unfolding action of the silicone haptics, and the delivery where the lens optic has to be aligned orthogonally to the direction of implantation (Figure 1).

The SING IMT and its injector system. A. The Miyake–Apple posterior view allows the visualization of the capsular bag, zonules, and ciliary body. The globe is dissected at the equator and glued to a glass plate. B. The SING IMT is an implantable telescope. With a haptic diameter of 10.8 mm, an axial length of 4.4 mm, and a weight of 63 mg in aqueous humor the implant is substantially bigger and heavier than conventional IOLs. The implant is placed into the capsular bag considerably stretching the average-sized capsular bag. C. The Tsert SI is the injector system of the SING IMT. It allows the injection of the implant into the capsular bag through a 6.5- to 7.5-mm injection. The optic is aligned orthogonally to the direction of injection. All haptics are released at the same time once the optic exits the nozzle tip.

The Miyake–Apple posterior view permits visualization of the capsular and zonular dynamics during lens implantations not detectable by the surgeon implanting a device. Consequently, the technique is a valuable tool for evaluating aspects of the efficacy and safety of new surgical techniques. Here, we used human donor eyes to visualize the implantation and explantation process of the SING IMT. We evaluated zonular stress and attempted different rescue techniques in case of an inadvertent sulcus implantation or in an explantation of the device. Finally, we assessed the impact of the SING IMT with its unique weight and haptic design on capsular stretching, posterior capsule striae, and the arc of contact between the haptics and the capsular bag.

Methods

Human Cadaver Eyes

Two human cadaver eyes from two donors were obtained from an eye bank 4 days postmortem and prepared in accordance with the Miyake–Apple technique previously described.6 The globe is dissected at the equator and fixed to a glass plate to visualize the capsular bag, zonules, and the ciliary body during surgery. Videos of the implantation and complication management were recorded from the anterior and the posterior Miyake–Apple View (Figure 1A).

Surgical Preparation and SING IMT Implantation

The SING-IMT (Samsara Vision Inc, Far Hills, NJ) was implanted (Figure 1, B and C). All surgical procedures were performed by one experienced ophthalmic surgeon (GUA) using a standard cataract surgery technique. To prepare for the implantation of the SING IMT, the Tsert SI injector (Samsara Vision Inc, Far Hills, NJ), which was specifically designed for this application, was primed. The injector nozzle was aimed deeply into the capsular bag. Slowly, the SING IMT was pushed through the injector nozzle opening and placed directly into the capsular bag.

Data Collection and Analysis

The collected videos of the posterior view were analyzed in ImageJ 2.7 Zonular stress was evaluated during implantation, explantation, and during rescue approaches for failed implantations by evaluating the zonular stretching in comparison to the unstretched capsular bag (Figure 2). After successful implantation, capsular stretching, posterior capsule striae, and the arc of contact were evaluated. Capsular deformation was evaluated as an area in mm2 as depicted in Figure 2. Finally, we evaluated the arc of contact defined as the length of haptics touching the equator of the capsular bag (Figure 2).

Results

Implantation

The process of implantation is depicted in Figure 3 and the online video (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/IAE/B973, real speed and slow motion). Optimal implantation is ensured when the tips of all haptics are placed posterior to the level of the capsulorhexis to immediately unfold into the capsule once the optic exits the nozzle of the injector. Zonular stress is low during successful implantation (0.69 mm2 of zonular stretching).

Successful and rescue implantation of the SING IMT (A) because of the unique design, to successfully implant the SING IMT, all three haptic tips must be placed below the capsulorhexis before the optic reaches the tip of the injector. Once the haptics are released, the implant centers itself in the capsular bag with tolerable zonular stress. B. the upper left haptic is implanted into the sulcus. Using two spatulas to generate counter pressure, the haptic can be folded and then pushed through the capsulorhexis with medium zonular stress.

Haptic Flexion as a Rescue Tool

Two different failed implantation scenarios were evaluated. First, in the case of one haptic implanted into the sulcus, the stability provided by the two haptics correctly placed in the bag allows the surgeon to fold the haptic that is in the sulcus. Two spatulas can be used in a bimanual technique to build up counter pressure to fold the haptic. Significant, but clinically tolerable zonular stress was observed (13,10 mm2 of zonular stretching). If all haptics are implanted into the sulcus, a similar technique with two spatulas can be applied to maneuver the haptics into the capsular bag (Figure 3 and Online Video 1, http://links.lww.com/IAE/B973).

Explantation of the SING IMT

Again, using haptic-flexion, we could use two spatulas and a counter-pressure on the haptic so that each haptic can be folded one by one. During this explantation process, there is visible zonular stress, because the whole SING IMT must be pushed contralaterally. The greatest amount of zonular stress occurs during the explantation of the first haptic (Figure 4) reaching a similar zonular stretching compared with the rescue maneuvers after sulcus implantation (12.89 mm2 of zonular stretching). For the second and third haptic, the device can be moved superiorly and the haptic can move along the sulcus; less zonular stress is necessary. Also, after the first haptic is removed, the surgeon may use a Doden forceps by pulling on the freed haptic in the sulcus (Figure 4).

Explantation of the SING IMT while preserving an intact capsular bag. Counter pressure is applied using two spatulas and the whole implant is moved toward the opposing part of the capsular bag to lift the haptic through the capsulorhexis. With a modified technique, the first explanted haptic can be grabbed with a Doden forceps in the sulcus (G, left). By pulling on this haptic, the SING IMT can be easily removed without further zonular stress.

Capsular Analysis

The capsular deformation was determined as presented in Figure 2. In both eyes examined, the capsular area increased on average 5.4±2.2 mm2. The arc of contact of the three haptics with the capsular bag was on average 11.3 mm, the capsular bag diameter of both eyes was comparable (9.3 and 9.2 mm). Two types of posterior capsular striae occurred: one was between two haptics (showcased in Figure 2), whereas the other occurs in line (parallel) with the haptic from its outer edge inward toward the optic (Figure 2).

Discussion

The Miyake–Apple view allows the comprehensive analysis of the capsular bag and zonules in a way that is usually invisible to surgeons during an operation.6,8–11 We evaluated the implantation, capsular dynamics, and explantation of the novel SING IMT. We proposed and tested two rescue techniques that can be directly implemented in clinical practice.

Implantation

During regular cataract surgery, the haptic can be used to guide the optic of the IOL as it slowly unfolds. The three haptics of the SING IMT unfold in a circular motion orthogonally to the direction of injection. As depicted in the online Video 1, the unfolding process of the SING IMT is rapid and takes place with sufficient force to not allow the surgeon to correct the direction or the placement of the injector nozzle. Here, we especially examined the complication of sulcus implantation, because we expect it to be common in clinical practice because of the injection mechanism. To date, there is only one single-surgeon retrospective study that reports adverse events in 30% of eyes with the SING IMT implanted. However, sulcus implantation was not mentioned.5 We found that the zonular stress observed during the studied rescue maneuvers was tolerable and no further, more challenging complications, such as posterior capsule rupture, arose from sulcus implantation.

Explantation

Adverse ocular events or a patient's request may necessitate the removal of the SING IMT.2 Boyer et al3 reported that 5.8% of patients with the IMT required explantation within 24 months. The feasibility of safe removal techniques is therefore mandatory to establish implantable telescopes as a treatment option for central vision loss. We saw a comparable capsular and zonular stress when explanting the SING IMT as is observed with standard IOLs. The device's foldable haptic allowed the safe removal of one haptic at a time from the bag. This is in line with data by Boyer et al3: In all cases in which explantation was necessary, the IMT could be replaced with a conventional in-the-bag IOL without posterior capsule ruptures or zonular damage.

Capsular Dynamics after Successful Implantation

In both of the examined eyes, the capsular bag area increased, indicating stretching and deformation of the capsular bag. Stretching stabilizes the implant and allows the centering of the optical zone. Because the SING IMT with its 63 mg in aqueous water is notably heavier compared with conventional IOLs, capsular stretching is necessary to ensure stable positioning. We observed posterior capsule striae in both eyes examined as a sign of stretching. If persistent, striae may lead to posterior capsule opacification and therefore visual impairment.12,13 Because of the unique design of the SING IMT, the noninvasive treatment option for posterior capsule opacification, YAG-capsulotomy, has not been evaluated and proven feasible. The arc of haptic contact is a measure of stability. In the present study, both examined eyes had a capsular diameter of 9.2 to 9.3 mm. We showed that the SING IMT has a greater arc of contact to support its telescope design, averaging at an arc of contact of 11.3 mm compared with 5 to 9 mm of conventional IOLs in capsular bags of this size,8 better supporting its size and weight.

Conclusion

In this study, we visualized surgical techniques around the use of the SING IMT. The implantation and explantation, are safe, effective, and possible with tolerable zonular stress using a two-spatula approach. Sulcus implantation may be a common complication that can be resolved using two spatulas and counter pressure. Capsular stretching was apparent in all eyes examined.
