Non-coding RNAs in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma: diagnostic biomarkers and emerging exosome-based therapeutic strategies
Authors: Mehmet Nuri Elgormus, Burcu Biltekin, Mustafa Ibas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Laryngeal Squamous Cell Carcinoma (LSCC) presents significant clinical challenges, including late-stage diagnosis and high rates of recurrence, underscoring an urgent need for novel molecular biomarkers. This review synthesizes current research on non-coding RNAs as pivotal players in the pathogenesis of LSCC. We focus on microRNAs (miRNAs) as powerful diagnostic and prognostic tools, exploring both tissue-specific and circulating exosomal miRNAs (e.g., miR-155, miR-141, miR-21) that reflect tumor biology and correlate with clinical outcomes. Additionally, we examine the therapeutic potential of small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated gene silencing, a strategy that enables the targeted knockdown of key oncogenes and pathways associated with LSCC progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance (e.g., targeting EpCAM, MGST1, and Notch1). We discuss the critical challenge of in vivo delivery and the potential of exosome-based systems to bridge the gap between preclinical success and clinical application. By integrating the diagnostic insights from miRNA profiling with the therapeutic precision of siRNA technology, this review outlines a novel integrated Diagnostic and therapeutic framework for laryngeal cancer. We propose that exosomes can serve as a unified platform, first as a source for non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers and subsequently as a natural, biocompatible vehicle for targeted siRNA delivery, bridging the gap between personalized diagnosis and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04106-0.

Introduction

Clinical burden and prognostic challenges of laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC)

Laryngeal cancer (LCA), of which laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) is the most prevalent histological subtype, represents one of the most common and aggressive malignancies of the head and neck [1–3]. Accounting for approximately 95% of malignant tumors of the larynx and hypopharynx, LSCC is characterized by high rates of morbidity, mortality, recurrence, and a generally poor overall survival (OS) [2, 4]. Global epidemiological data, such as the GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, report approximately 189,000 new cases of laryngeal cancer and 103,000 deaths worldwide annually [5]. A major contributing factor to these outcomes is the silent nature of the disease in its early stages, which often leads to a late diagnosis at an advanced stage (III or IV) in the majority of patients [6, 7]. At this point, the disease is frequently accompanied by locoregional infiltration and lymph node metastasis, which are the most significant factors contributing to treatment failure and mortality [8, 9].

Despite the implementation of various treatment strategies, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the 5-year OS rate for patients with advanced LSCC remains around 60% and has not seen substantial improvement in recent decades [3, 10, 11]. Current clinical practice relies heavily on well-established but limited prognostic parameters, primarily the anatomical tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system, primary tumor site, and histological characteristics [12, 13]. While these factors are crucial for initial treatment planning, they fail to fully capture the profound molecular heterogeneity inherent in LSCC [14, 15]. Tumors with identical clinicopathological features can exhibit different biological behaviors, leading to varied responses to therapy and divergent clinical outcomes. This heterogeneity is driven not only by the cancer cells themselves but also by their complex interplay with the surrounding tumor microenvironment (TME), which includes immune cells, fibroblasts, and vasculature that can either suppress or promote tumor progression. These limitations highlight an urgent clinical need for more precise, molecularly informed biomarkers that can enhance risk stratification, inform therapeutic decisions, and facilitate early detection.

The current gold standard for diagnosis relies on invasive procedures such as endoscopy and pathological examination of tissue biopsies [4]. These methods are not only invasive and challenging for serial monitoring but are also inadequate for the early detection of primary tumors or the identification of minimal residual disease (MRD) post-treatment, which is a key predictor of recurrence [16, 17]. The anatomical location of the larynx can limit the visibility of early-stage tumors, further complicating timely diagnosis [18]. Consequently, the field is actively seeking novel biomarkers that can be detected through minimally invasive means, providing a more dynamic and accurate reflection of tumor biology.

While several excellent reviews have discussed non-coding RNAs in LSCC, they often focus on either the diagnostic/prognostic role of miRNAs or the therapeutic potential of RNA interference (RNAi) as separate topics. The distinct novelty of this review lies in its synthesis of these two fields into a single, integrated “theranostic” framework. We specifically focus on the central, dual role of exosomes as a unified platform—first, as a source for non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers, and second, as a biocompatible vehicle for targeted siRNA therapeutic delivery. This paper outlines a cohesive clinical workflow from molecular diagnosis to personalized treatment.

Limitations of conventional biomarkers and the rationale for a molecular approach

Moving beyond traditional clinicopathological staging toward a molecularly based understanding of cancer signals a significant transformation in oncology. The molecular heterogeneity of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), including LSCC, is a hallmark of the disease, driven by a complex landscape of genetic and epigenetic alterations [14, 15]. Large-scale genomic sequencing efforts, such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), have provided unprecedented insight into the molecular pathogenesis of HNSCC, confirming frequent somatic alterations in key signaling pathways [12]. Genes such as TP53, CDKN2A, PIK3CA, FAT1, and NOTCH1 are commonly mutated, playing critical roles in tumorigenesis and progression [12, 19–21].

The tumor suppressor gene TP53, for instance, is the most frequently mutated in HNSCC, and its mutational status has profound clinical implications [12, 22]. Specifically, studies have demonstrated that not all TP53 mutations are equal; mutations that result in a truncated protein are significantly associated with worse overall and progression-free survival, serving as an independent and powerful prognostic factor [22]. This finding demonstrates how a deeper molecular understanding can refine patient stratification beyond what is possible with traditional staging alone. The evolution of research from identifying single high-impact gene mutations to developing more complex, multi-gene prognostic models based on comprehensive genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data signifies a maturation of the field [9, 23–26]. This shows that LSCC, as a complex disease, cannot be fully understood through one data point, leading to the development of risk models that integrate different molecular signals.

A critical innovation driven by this molecular focus is the development of “liquid biopsy,” a minimally invasive technique that analyzes tumor-derived material, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), in bodily fluids [16, 27]. Studies have successfully demonstrated that tumor-specific somatic mutations and human papillomavirus (HPV) DNA can be detected in the saliva and plasma of HNSCC patients, including those with laryngeal tumors [27, 28]. This approach is crucial for early cancer detection, monitoring treatment response, and identifying recurrence before it becomes clinically apparent, thereby directly addressing the limitations of invasive tissue biopsies [16, 17, 27].

An overview of non-coding RNAs as key regulators in carcinogenesis

Within the expanding universe of molecular biomarkers, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) have emerged as particularly attractive candidates due to their central roles in regulating gene expression and their remarkable stability in circulation [29, 30]. NcRNAs are broadly classified by size and function, with two classes being of principal interest in cancer biology: microRNAs (miRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) [31–33]. MiRNAs are small, single-stranded RNA molecules, typically ~ 22 nucleotides in length, that function as potent post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression [33, 34]. LncRNAs are a more heterogeneous class of transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that are involved in a wide array of cellular processes, including epigenetic regulation and signaling pathway modulation [31, 32].

The typical mechanism of miRNA action involves a multi-step biogenesis pathway that results in the incorporation of a mature, single-stranded miRNA into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) [33, 34]. Guided by the miRNA, the RISC complex binds to complementary sequences, typically located in the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs) [34]. This binding, which often relies on imperfect complementarity centered on a 2–8 nucleotide “seed region,” leads to either the degradation of the target mRNA or the repression of its translation [34]. Because a single miRNA can have hundreds of mRNA targets, miRNAs function as master regulators, capable of modulating entire cellular pathways involved in proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis [33, 35].

In the context of cancer, miRNAs can have a dual function. They can act as oncogenes (termed “OncomiRs”) when their overexpression leads to the downregulation of tumor suppressor genes, or they can function as tumor suppressors (TS-miRs) when their loss or downregulation results in the increased expression of oncogenes [33]. This duality makes their expression profiles in tumor tissues and circulation highly informative. Furthermore, the regulatory landscape is made more complex by the interplay between different classes of ncRNAs. For instance, some lncRNAs can function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) or “sponges,” binding to and sequestering specific miRNAs, thereby preventing them from acting on their primary mRNA targets [36, 37]. This intricate network of ncRNA interactions adds another layer of gene regulation that is frequently dysregulated in cancer, offering a rich source of potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies for this narrative review, with searches finalized in August 2025. We queried the PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases. The search strategy employed a combination of MeSH terms and keywords, including “laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma” OR “LSCC” AND “microRNA,” “miRNA,” “siRNA,” “RNA interference,” “non-coding RNA,” “exosomes,” “liquid biopsy,” “biomarkers,” and “targeted therapy.”

Inclusion criteria for articles were: (1) original research articles, meta-analyses, and comprehensive reviews; (2) studies published in the English language; and (3) articles with a primary focus on the molecular pathogenesis of LSCC, the role of ncRNAs as biomarkers, or the development of ncRNA-based therapeutics.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) articles not available in full text; (2) conference abstracts or editorials with insufficient data; and (3) studies not specifically pertaining to laryngeal cancer (e.g., general HNSCC studies with no specific data on LSCC).

The initial screening of titles and abstracts was performed by all of the authors to identify potentially eligible articles, with full-text reviews conducted to determine final inclusion. Priority was given to recent publications to ensure a current synthesis of the field.

microRNAs (miRNAs) as diagnostic and prognostic tools in LSCC

The biology of miRNA-mediated gene regulation

To fully understand the role of miRNAs as biomarkers in LSCC, a detailed understanding of their biogenesis and function is essential. The journey of a mature miRNA begins in the nucleus, where it is transcribed, typically by RNA polymerase II, as part of a long primary transcript known as a pri-miRNA, which can be thousands of nucleotides in length [33, 34]. This pri-miRNA contains a characteristic stem-loop structure that is recognized and cleaved by the microprocessor complex, a protein assembly consisting of the RNase III enzyme Drosha and its cofactor, DiGeorge Syndrome Critical Region 8 (DGCR8) [33]. This initial processing step separates a smaller, ~ 70-nucleotide hairpin-shaped precursor miRNA, or pre-miRNA.

The pre-miRNA is then actively transported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm by the nuclear transport receptor Exportin-5 [33]. Once in the cytoplasm, the pre-miRNA undergoes a final maturation step mediated by another RNase III enzyme, Dicer. Dicer removes the terminal loop of the pre-miRNA, yielding a short, ~ 22-nucleotide double-stranded RNA duplex [34]. This duplex is subsequently unwound, and one strand, the mature miRNA, is preferentially loaded into an Argonaute (AGO) protein to form the core of the RISC [34]. The other strand, known as the passenger strand, is typically degraded.

The functional specificity of the miRNA is determined by its “seed sequence,” a highly conserved region of 2–8 nucleotides at its 5’ end. This sequence guides the RISC to target mRNAs through base-pairing interactions, most commonly within the 3’ UTR. In animal cells, this pairing is often imperfect, which has a profound biological result: a single miRNA species can recognize and regulate the expression of hundreds of different mRNA targets [34]. This capacity to organize broad post-transcriptional regulatory networks allows miRNAs to exert powerful control over virtually all cellular processes, including those that are altered during carcinogenesis, such as cell cycle progression, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and metastasis [35].

Aberrant MiRNA expression profiles in laryngeal cancer

The central role of miRNAs in cellular homeostasis means that their dysregulation is a common feature of human malignancies, including LSCC. Extensive research has identified specific miRNAs with altered expression levels in laryngeal cancer, linking them mechanistically to tumor progression and establishing their potential as clinically relevant biomarkers. These can be broadly categorized into oncogenic miRNAs that promote tumorigenesis and tumor suppressor miRNAs that inhibit it.

Oncogenic miRNAs promoting tumor progression

Several miRNAs have been consistently identified as being upregulated in LSCC, where they function to drive key aspects of the malignant phenotype.

miR-155: This miRNA stands out as a potent oncomiR in LSCC. Studies have demonstrated that miR-155 levels are significantly upregulated in both LSCC tissue and the plasma of patients when compared to non-tumor tissues and healthy controls, respectively [38]. This matched expression in tissue and circulation enhances its utility as a non-invasive biomarker. At the molecular level, the oncogenic effects of miR-155 are mediated, at least in part, through its direct targeting of the Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling 1 (SOCS1) gene [39]. Downregulation of SOCS1 by miR-155 leads to the subsequent activation and elevated expression of Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3), a well-known oncogenic transcription factor [39].

Its expression is significantly higher in the serum exosomes of LSCC patients compared to individuals with benign vocal cord polyps [8]. Crucially, elevated exosomal miR-21 levels show a strong positive correlation with adverse clinical parameters, including advanced T classification (T3/T4), higher clinical stages (III/IV), and the presence of lymph node metastasis [8]. This strong association with tumor progression and metastasis makes circulating exosomal miR-21 a highly promising prognostic biomarker.

Tumor suppressor miRNAs and their role in inhibiting metastasis

Conversely, the downregulation or loss of specific miRNAs can remove critical brakes on tumor progression, allowing for enhanced malignancy.

miR-141: This miRNA functions as a key tumor suppressor in laryngeal cancer, primarily by inhibiting the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [41]. EMT is a fundamental biological process in which epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal characteristics, including increased motility and invasiveness, and is a critical prerequisite for metastasis [42]. Research has shown that miR-141 is downregulated in laryngeal cancer tissues [41]. Its tumor-suppressive function is executed by directly targeting the mRNA of Homeobox C6 (HOXC6), a transcription factor. By downregulating HOXC6, miR-141 effectively inhibits the Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway, a major driver of EMT [41] Experimental upregulation of miR-141 in laryngeal cancer cells has been shown to repress EMT, reduce cell viability, and inhibit migration and invasion both in vitro and in vivo [41]. These findings position miR-141 not only as a biomarker but also as a potential therapeutic agent, where restoring its function via “miRNA mimics” could be a strategy to prevent or reverse metastasis.miR-195: A comprehensive meta-analysis has established miR-195 as a tumor suppressor in HNSCC [43, 44]. The analysis revealed that higher expression of miR-195 is significantly associated with improved overall survival, indicating a favorable prognostic role [44]. MiR-195 is known to exert its tumor-suppressive effects by regulating the cell cycle, particularly the G1/S and G2/M transitions, and by promoting apoptosis through the inhibition of anti-apoptotic proteins like Bcl-2 [43]. Its downregulation in carcinomatous tissues, therefore, contributes to uncontrolled proliferation and survival of cancer cells.

Liquid biopsy: circulating and exosomal MiRNAs as non-invasive biomarkers

The clinical translation of molecular biomarkers is greatly enhanced when they can be reliably measured through minimally invasive methods. Liquid biopsy, which involves the analysis of biomarkers in bodily fluids like blood, has emerged as a transformative approach in oncology [16, 27]. MiRNAs are particularly well-suited for this application due to their exceptional stability in the extracellular environment. Unlike mRNAs, which are rapidly degraded, circulating miRNAs are protected from endogenous RNase activity by being encapsulated within extracellular vesicles (EVs), such as exosomes, or by forming complexes with proteins like Argonaute2 [18]. This inherent stability allows for their reliable and reproducible quantification in serum and plasma, making them ideal candidates for clinical diagnostics.

Exosomes, in particular, have attracted significant attention as vehicles for miRNA-based diagnostics [17]. These are small, lipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles, typically 40–150 nm in diameter, that are actively secreted by virtually all cell types, including cancer cells, into the extracellular space and surrounding biofluids [8, 17, 18]. A crucial feature of exosomes is that their molecular cargo—which includes proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids—is not randomly packaged but rather reflects the specific physiological or pathological state of the cell of origin [18]. Tumor cells have been shown to secrete a higher number of exosomes compared to normal cells, leading to an increased presence of these vesicles in the blood of cancer patients [18]. This phenomenon makes the analysis of exosomal contents as a powerful tool for obtaining a “liquid biopsy” of the tumor, providing a real-time molecular snapshot of the disease without the need for an invasive tissue sample.

The analysis of exosomal cargo represents more than just a passive measurement of tumor byproducts; it is similar to intercepting a functional intercellular communication system. Tumors utilize exosomes to transmit signals to distant sites, conditioning the pre-metastatic niche, promoting angiogenesis, and modulating the immune response. Therefore, the specific miRNAs found within circulating exosomes are not merely biomarkers; they are functional effectors of tumorigenesis. Measuring their levels provides a dynamic view of the tumor’s aggressive signaling and its attempts to systemically modify its environment. This understanding elevates the role of exosomal miRNAs from simple indicators to reporters of active, ongoing pathological processes, which has profound implications for early diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring disease progression.

The diagnostic power of this approach is significantly amplified when multiple biomarkers are analyzed in concert.

A landmark study in LSCC demonstrated that the combined detection of serum exosomal miR-21 and the lncRNA HOTAIR yielded a significantly higher diagnostic accuracy than either molecule alone [8]. The area under the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve for the combined panel was 87.6%, which was superior to that of miR-21 (80.1%) or HOTAIR (72.7%) individually. This two-marker panel could differentiate LSCC patients from those with benign laryngeal disease with a high sensitivity of 94.2% and a specificity of 73.5%, highlighting the synergistic value of multi-marker panels.Further supporting this concept, another study identified a novel serum-based three-miRNA signature for the early detection of LSCC [26]. This signature, comprising miR-93, miR-223, and miR-532, was shown to be a circulating marker endowed with high selectivity and specificity. Subsequent bioinformatic analysis revealed that the predicted gene targets of this signature are involved in seven critical biological processes, including inflammation, proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and angiogenesis, providing a strong biological rationale for its diagnostic utility. These studies indicate that the future of non-invasive cancer diagnostics likely lies in the development and validation of such multi-analyte miRNA signatures.

miRNA co-expression networks and regulation of oncogenic pathways

The regulatory impact of miRNAs in cancer is not merely the sum of individual miRNA-target interactions but arises from the complex, coordinated actions of multiple miRNAs functioning within intricate networks. A single oncogenic pathway can be targeted at multiple nodes by several different miRNAs, and conversely, a single miRNA can influence multiple pathways. Understanding these co-expression networks is crucial for deciphering the complex molecular logic of carcinogenesis and identifying the most critical regulatory hubs for therapeutic intervention.

A study focusing on advanced laryngeal carcinoma provided significant insights into these networks by investigating global miRNA profiling and evaluating correlations among the most deregulated miRNAs [11]. This work identified a significant co-expression relationship among miR-93-5p, miR-145-5p, and miR-210-3p. These three miRNAs were found to collectively regulate a host of major pathways central to cancer progression, including the cell cycle, EMT, PI3K/Akt signaling, hypoxia, metabolism, apoptosis, and angiogenesis.

The power of this network-based approach lies in the identification of shared targets and convergent pathways. The analysis revealed that:

miR-93-5p and miR-145-5p share several crucial, experimentally validated targets, including CDKN1A (a cell cycle inhibitor), TGFBR2 (a key receptor in the TGF-β pathway), VEGFA (a master regulator of angiogenesis), and ITGB8 (involved in cell adhesion and migration).miR-145-5p and miR-210-3p share common targets such as E2F3 (a transcription factor that promotes cell cycle progression) and BNIP3 (involved in apoptosis and mitochondrial function).

This systems-level view provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the collective impact of the miRNAome on LSCC and underscores the potential of targeting these regulatory networks for therapeutic benefit.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) as a therapeutic strategy for laryngeal cancer

The mechanism and therapeutic potential of RNA interference

While miRNAs offer a window into the molecular state of a tumor, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) provide a tool to actively intervene. RNAi is a natural biological process of sequence-specific gene silencing that can be used for therapeutic purposes by synthetic siRNAs [31, 45]. Unlike miRNAs, which are endogenously produced and often have hundreds of imperfectly matched targets, therapeutic siRNAs are exogenously administered, synthetic, double-stranded RNA molecules, typically ~ 22 nucleotides in length, that are engineered to be perfectly complementary to the mRNA sequence of a single, specific gene of interest.

The mechanism of action for synthetic siRNAs is direct and highly efficient. Upon entering the cytoplasm, the double-stranded siRNA bypasses the initial Drosha and Dicer processing steps required for endogenous miRNAs [45]. It is directly loaded into the RISC, where the passenger strand is discarded, and the guide strand is retained by the Argonaute 2 (Ago2) protein. This siRNA-loaded RISC then acts as a guided missile, scanning the cytoplasm for mRNAs that contain a sequence perfectly complementary to the siRNA guide strand. Upon finding its target, the Ago2 protein, which possesses endonuclease activity, precisely cleaves the mRNA molecule [45]. This cleavage event signals the mRNA for rapid degradation by the cell’s machinery, leading to a potent and highly specific knockdown of the target gene’s expression and, consequently, a reduction in the synthesis of the disease-causing protein.

The therapeutic potential of this technology is immense. One of the most compelling features of RNAi is its catalytic nature; a single siRNA-loaded RISC can cleave multiple mRNA targets, meaning that only a few hundred siRNA molecules are required per cell to achieve efficient and sustained gene silencing that can last for weeks [45]. This high potency makes siRNA a powerful therapeutic modality. Since siRNAs can be designed to target virtually any gene with a known sequence, RNAi technology opens up the possibility of drugging the “undruggable” proteome [35, 46]. This includes targeting proteins that lack enzymatic active sites or are otherwise difficult to inhibit with traditional small-molecule drugs, such as transcription factors and non-coding RNAs like lncRNAs [46].

Preclinical validation of SiRNA targets in LSCC models

The utility of siRNA as both a research tool and a potential therapeutic has been extensively demonstrated in preclinical models of LSCC. By selectively silencing key genes, researchers have been able to validate their functional roles in driving the disease and demonstrate the potential efficacy of targeting them. These studies show a sophisticated, problem-oriented approach to therapy development, focusing on the most lethal aspects of LSCC, such as metastasis, chemoresistance, and uncontrolled proliferation.

Targeting metastasis and invasion (EpCAM and MGST1): Lymph node metastasis is a critical determinant of poor prognosis in LSCC [23]. A comprehensive cytomembrane proteomics study identified two proteins, Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (EpCAM) and Microsomal Glutathione S-Transferase 1 (MGST1), as being abundantly expressed in LSCC, particularly in cases with lymph node metastasis [23]. To confirm their functional role, siRNA-mediated knockdown of EpCAM and MGST1 was performed in LSCC cell lines. The results were definitive: silencing these genes significantly inhibited in vitro proliferation, invasion, and migration. Further mechanistic investigation revealed that their knockdown hindered the activity of the Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K signaling pathways, two critical oncogenic cascades. This work not only validated EpCAM and MGST1 as drivers of metastasis but also established them as actionable therapeutic targets for siRNA-based interventions. Reversing chemoresistance (Notch1): Multidrug resistance (MDR) remains a formidable obstacle in the chemotherapy of laryngeal cancer [47]. One of the key drivers of MDR is tumor hypoxia, which can activate the Notch1 signaling pathway. A pivotal study demonstrated that culturing laryngeal cancer cells under hypoxic conditions upregulated Notch1 expression and activity, which in turn increased the expression of the MDR1 and survivin genes, conferring resistance to chemotherapy [47]. The therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway was tested using siRNA to suppress Notch1 expression. This intervention effectively downregulated Notch1 signaling and the expression of its downstream resistance genes. As a result, the hypoxic cancer cells were re-sensitized to a variety of chemotherapeutic drugs, exhibiting significantly increased drug-induced apoptosis. This study provides a clear proof-of-concept for using siRNA to overcome a major clinical challenge in LSCC treatment. Inhibiting cell proliferation and migration (STC2): Through integrative bioinformatic analysis, Stanniocalcin 2 (STC2), a secreted glycoprotein, was identified as a gene that is highly expressed in LSCC and is associated with poor prognosis and EMT [2, 48]. To validate its function, RNAi was used to silence STC2 expression in the TU686 laryngeal cancer cell line. The silencing of STC2 led to a substantial reduction in cell proliferation and migration [2]. Furthermore, it decreased the expression of key mesenchymal markers like N-cadherin and vimentin, indicating that STC2 plays a role in promoting the EMT phenotype. This confirms STC2 as a valid oncogenic driver and a promising target for siRNA therapy. Investigating therapeutic tolerance (CDK1): Not all promising targets prove to be effective, and understanding the mechanisms of therapeutic failure is as important as identifying successes. Cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) is a crucial protein for cell cycle control and is known to be upregulated in LSCC, making it an attractive therapeutic target [49]. However, a study designed to test this hypothesis yielded a surprising and important result. Using siRNA to knock down CDK1 expression by more than 50% in two different LSCC cell lines, researchers observed no effect on either cell proliferation or viability [49]. This demonstration of “therapeutic tolerance” is a critical finding, suggesting that LSCC cells may possess redundant pathways or compensatory mechanisms that allow them to bypass the inhibition of a single, albeit important, cell cycle node. This work adds a crucial layer of nuance, highlighting the complexities of target validation and the need to anticipate potential resistance mechanisms when designing targeted therapies.

Overcoming the delivery challenge: from nanoparticles to exosomes

Despite the immense therapeutic promise and preclinical success of siRNA, its translation into a mainstream clinical modality has been limited by one obstacle: effective in vivo delivery [31, 45, 50]. Naked siRNA molecules are inherently unstable in the bloodstream, susceptible to rapid degradation by nucleases, and are inefficiently taken up by target cells due to their size and negative charge [31]. Therefore, the development of safe and efficient delivery vehicles is the most critical challenge facing the field.

To address these issues, extensive research has focused on developing nanocarriers to protect the siRNA cargo and facilitate its delivery to tumor cells. These systems, which can be engineered from lipids, polymers, or other materials, encapsulate the siRNA, shielding it from degradation and improving its pharmacokinetic profile [31, 46]. Some advanced nanocarriers can be functionalized with targeting ligands, such as antibodies or aptamers, that bind to specific receptors overexpressed on cancer cells, thereby enhancing tumor-specific uptake and reducing off-target effects [45].

While synthetic nanoparticles have shown promise, an even more exciting improvement in siRNA delivery involves using the body’s own natural nanoparticle: the exosome [35]. As previously discussed, exosomes are endogenous extracellular vesicles that function as natural carriers for intercellular communication. This makes them almost ideal vehicles for drug delivery. Compared to synthetic nanoparticles, exosomes offer several distinct advantages, including superior biocompatibility, reduced toxicity and immunogenicity, and an extended half-life in circulation [35]. Their natural origin allows them to evade immune clearance more effectively.

Although synthetic systems like Lipid Nanoparticles (LNPs) have achieved landmark success with several FDA-approved siRNA drugs, they can sometimes be associated with immunogenicity and off-target accumulation in organs like the liver [51]. Exosomes, by contrast, are viewed as potentially safer due to their endogenous nature and inherent biocompatibility, though their manufacturing is currently more complex and less standardized than that of LNPs. This positions exosomes as a highly promising ‘next-generation’ delivery platform that may overcome some limitations of existing synthetic carriers [52]. However, it is important to note that the clinical translation of exosome-based therapeutics faces its own significant hurdles, including challenges in scalable manufacturing, purification, and cargo loading, as well as navigating a complex regulatory landscape, all of which will be discussed in further detail in Sect. "Roadmap to Clinical Implementation: The Need for Large Scale Validation and Standardized Assays".

Extensive research has demonstrated that exosomes can be loaded with therapeutic siRNAs and used as effective nanosystems for targeted gene knockdown [35, 53]. Exosome-mediated delivery of siRNAs has been shown to result in superior anticancer therapeutic effects in a variety of preclinical models, with some formulations already progressing into clinical trials [35]. This convergence of diagnostics and therapeutics around a single biological platform represents a significant conceptual advance. The potential to use the body’s own communication system to both diagnose a disease and deliver a precision therapy tailored to that diagnosis is the ultimate goal of personalized nanomedicine.

Synthesis and future perspectives

Integrating MiRNA diagnostics with SiRNA therapeutics for personalized oncology

The research synthesized in this review points toward a powerful and cohesive future vision for the management of laryngeal cancer, centered on the integration of miRNA-based diagnostics and siRNA-based therapeutics. This approach sees a clear path from diagnosis to personalized treatment, based on understanding the molecular details of a patient’s tumor. The evidence presented suggests that exosomes could be the central enabling technology for this vision, serving as a unified platform for both biomarker discovery and targeted drug delivery.

A potential future clinical workflow could proceed as follows: A patient with suspected or diagnosed LSCC would provide a blood sample for liquid biopsy. Exosomes would be isolated from the serum, and their miRNA cargo would be profiled to generate a comprehensive molecular signature of the tumor. This signature, incorporating markers like miR-155, miR-21, miR-93, and miR-141, would not only confirm the diagnosis non-invasively but also provide critical prognostic information and identify the specific oncogenic pathways (e.g., STAT3, PI3K/Akt, TGF-β) that are aberrantly active in that patient’s disease [8, 26, 38, 39, 41].

Based on this personalized molecular profile, a tailored siRNA therapeutic could be designed. For a patient whose tumor signature indicates high metastatic potential driven by the TGF-β pathway, a therapeutic exosome could be engineered to carry siRNA against key pathway components like HOXC6 or TGFBR2 [11, 41]. For a patient exhibiting signs of chemoresistance linked to Notch1 signaling, an exosome carrying anti-Notch1 siRNA could be administered in combination with conventional chemotherapy to re-sensitize the tumor [47]. This approach uses biomarkers to move past uniform treatments, assisting doctors to predict results and choose the best targeted therapy (as shown in Fig. 2). The use of natural, biocompatible exosomes as the delivery vehicle would mitigate many of the toxicity and immunogenicity concerns associated with synthetic nanoparticles, potentially making this a safer and more effective therapeutic strategy [35].

The interplay of non-coding RNAs with cancer stem cells and the tumor microenvironment

The impact of ncRNAs extends beyond the cancer cell itself to influence the broader ecosystem of the tumor, including the cancer stem cell (CSC) niche and the TME. A complete understanding and effective treatment of LSCC will require targeting these critical components, and ncRNAs are emerging as key regulators in these domains.

Recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies have provided unprecedented resolution of the cellular landscape in LSCC, identifying distinct populations of CSCs that are believed to drive tumor initiation, progression, and recurrence [54]. These LSCC-CSCs are characterized by the high expression of stemness markers like PROM1 (CD133) and ALDH1A1 and show enriched activity in oncogenic pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin and Notch signaling [54]. Notably, these are the same pathways that are regulated by prognostic miRNAs and targeted by therapeutic siRNAs in LSCC models [11, 23, 47]. These findings suggest that ncRNAs play a key role in cancer stemness. This means miRNA and siRNA therapies might do more than shrink tumors—they could also attack cancer stem cells, tackling the root cause of the disease and helping to prevent relapse.

Similarly, the TME, a complex environment of immune cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), blood vessels, and extracellular matrix, plays a crucial role in supporting tumor growth and facilitating immune evasion [7, 55]. Key signaling pathways like PI3K/Akt are not only active within cancer cells but are also pivotal in modulating the TME, for example, by promoting the immunosuppressive activity of CAFs and influencing the behavior of innate immune cells [23, 55]. Since these pathways are regulated by ncRNAs, it follows that ncRNA-based therapies could have a powerful dual effect. An siRNA targeting a gene in the PI3K/Akt pathway could simultaneously inhibit cancer cell proliferation and remodel the TME to be less immunosuppressive, potentially rendering the tumor more susceptible to immunotherapy. This indicates the potential for combining ncRNA-based therapies with immune checkpoint inhibitors to achieve synergistic anti-tumor effects.

Roadmap to clinical implementation: the need for large-scale validation and standardized assays

While the preclinical evidence is compelling and the future vision is transformative, the path to routine clinical implementation of ncRNA-based diagnostics and therapeutics is loaded with challenges that must be systematically addressed. The transition from promising laboratory findings to robust clinical tools requires rigorous validation, standardization, and a clear understanding of regulatory pathways.

First and foremost is the need for large-scale validation. Many of the studies identifying promising miRNA biomarkers in LSCC have been conducted on relatively small patient cohorts [38]. To establish the clinical utility of these markers, their diagnostic and prognostic accuracy must be validated in large, prospective, multi-center clinical trials. These trials are essential to confirm their sensitivity, specificity, and predictive power across diverse patient populations and to define clear cutoff values for clinical decision-making.

Second, the field must overcome the challenge of standardization. Currently, there is no universally accepted “gold standard” for the isolation of exosomes or the quantification of circulating miRNAs [18]. Different methodologies can lead to variability in results, making it difficult to compare findings across studies and hindering the development of reliable clinical assays. An effort is needed from the research community to establish and adopt standardized protocols for sample collection, processing, and data analysis to ensure that results are reproducible and clinically meaningful.

Beyond validation, the translation of exosome-based siRNA therapeutics faces significant manufacturing and regulatory hurdles. A key challenge lies in ‘Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls’ (CMC), which includes developing scalable methods for isolating exosomes from cell culture with high purity and yield. Ensuring batch-to-batch consistency in terms of size, concentration, and molecular cargo is paramount for a reliable therapeutic product. Furthermore, optimizing the loading of siRNA into exosomes without compromising the vesicle’s integrity or the siRNA’s function remains a complex task. From a regulatory perspective, exosome-based products represent a new class of biologic therapy, and navigating the approval pathways will require robust data on their biodistribution, potential immunogenicity, and long-term safety, establishing clear guidelines that are still under development.

Finally, for siRNA therapeutics, the challenge of delivery remains paramount, despite the promise of exosomes [31, 50, 53]. Optimizing drug loading, ensuring stability, minimizing off-target effects, and scaling up the manufacturing of therapeutic exosomes are significant technical and logistical obstacles that must be overcome. Navigating the regulatory landscape for approval of these novel, complex therapies will require clear guidelines and robust data on safety and efficacy.

Conclusions

Non-coding RNAs occupy a central role in the biology of laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC), offering both diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities. Circulating and exosomal miRNAs enable minimally invasive diagnosis, risk stratification, and longitudinal disease monitoring, while siRNAs provide pathway-specific gene silencing with therapeutic precision. A unified exosome-based platform that integrates these dual functions has the potential to establish a true theranostic paradigm. To translate this promise into clinical reality, the field must move beyond proof-of-concept studies and focus on assay standardization, validation of molecular signatures in large prospective cohorts, and the development of scalable, regulatory-compliant manufacturing strategies for vesicle-mediated delivery. While significant challenges remain, the convergence of diagnostic insights from the miRNAome with the therapeutic potential of siRNA technology, reinforced by rigorous clinical validation and technological innovation, holds the promise of ushering in a new era of personalized, ncRNA-guided oncology for patients with LSCC.
