Esketamine vs. sufentanil for quality of recovery after outpatient gynecological surgery: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Xu-Lin Wang, Yan-Na Li, Qiao-Ling Ma, Ming-Cui Qu, Jian-Wen Zhang, Ming-Zhu Jing, Jia-Qi Chen, Tao Xu, Na Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative administration of esketamine has been reported to improve early quality of recovery (QoR). However, data on its effects in outpatient surgery are limited. This study aimed to assess the impact of esketamine on QoR in patients undergoing outpatient gynecological procedures.MethodsIn this investigator-initiated, double-blind, randomized clinical trial, patients aged 18–65 years scheduled for outpatient gynecological surgery under sedation were allocated to receive esketamine (0.2 mg/kg) or sufentanil (0.1 μg/kg) combined with propofol (1.5–3 mg/kg). The primary outcome was quality of recovery on postoperative day (POD) 1. Secondary outcomes included quality of recovery on POD2, sedation success rate, length of post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) stay, injection pain, postoperative pain, nausea and vomiting, fatigue, patient and clinician satisfaction, sleep quality, and anxiety and depression.ResultsA total of 126 patients were randomized, with 63 assigned to the esketamine group and 63 to the sufentanil group. Of these, 125 patients were included in the final analysis (62 in the esketamine group and 63 in the sufentanil group), as one patient lacked follow-up data. The mean (SD) QoR-15 score on POD1 was 137.9 (14.5) in the esketamine group and 137.8 (10.7) in the sufentanil group, with no significant difference between groups (absolute difference, 0.2; 95% CI, −4.2 to 4.6; p = 0.93). For secondary outcomes, the esketamine group had a longer PACU stay (median, 28.0 vs. 23.0 min; p < 0.001), a lower incidence of severe injection pain (22.6% vs. 50.8%; p = 0.002), a higher proportion of patients with pain scores ≥ 4 at 30 min postoperatively (30.6% vs. 6.3%; p = 0.001), and higher fatigue scores (median, 3.0 vs. 2.0; p = 0.01). Other secondary outcomes did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionAmong patients undergoing sedation for outpatient gynecological procedures, esketamine did not significantly improve quality of recovery on POD1 compared to sufentanil.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2500098466

Introduction

Over the past two decades, numerous surgical procedures have shifted from traditional operating rooms to ambulatory settings, which offer advantages such as lower costs, less disruption to daily life, faster recovery, and a reduced risk of infection and respiratory complications [1]. As of 2014, millions of surgeries were performed annually in outpatient facilities [2], and gynecological procedures are among the most commonly performed in ambulatory surgical centers. In 2014, approximately 500,000 outpatient gynecological surgeries were conducted in the United States [3]. With ongoing advances in minimally invasive surgical techniques and anesthesia management, an increasing number of gynecological surgeries can now be performed safely and effectively in ambulatory settings.

Propofol combined with opioids is routinely used for sedation or anesthesia in outpatient gynecological procedures [4–8]. In recent years, opioid-free anesthesia has gained attention for its potential to provide effective analgesia while minimizing opioid-related adverse effects and promoting postoperative recovery [9]. Among various opioid alternatives, esketamine—a novel N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist—has emerged as a promising option. In addition to its anesthetic and sedative properties, esketamine appears to enhance postoperative recovery, as it has been shown to alleviate pain, improve sleep quality, stabilize mood, and preserve cognitive function [10–13]. Meta-analyses further suggest that perioperative administration of ketamine or esketamine can significantly improve patients’ early subjective quality of recovery [14]. However, most existing studies have focused on inpatient populations, and evidence regarding the effects of esketamine on quality of recovery in outpatient gynecological procedures, in which rapid and high-quality recovery is particularly important, remains limited.

Therefore, we conducted this trial to test the hypothesis that esketamine combined with propofol for sedation in outpatient gynecological procedures would improve patients’ postoperative quality of recovery.

Methods

Study design

This investigator-initiated, double-blind, randomized clinical trial was conducted from March to July 2025 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, a tertiary academic medical center in China. The study was approved by the Institutional Scientific Research and Clinical Trial Ethics Committee (2025-KY-0206-001) and was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500098466) on March 10, 2025, before enrollment of the first participant later that day. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study protocol and reporting adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Study population

Patients aged 18 to 65 years scheduled for outpatient gynecological surgery under sedation were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included: refusal to participate; known contraindications to ketamine, esketamine, opioids, or propofol; alcohol dependence or substance abuse; planned pregnancy or breastfeeding; history of schizophrenia, epilepsy, Parkinson’s disease, or myasthenia gravis; severe dementia or language impairment; moderate to severe depression requiring pharmacologic treatment; use of ketamine or esketamine within the previous week; history of transient ischemic attack, stroke, angina, myocardial infarction, or heart failure within the past three months; participation in another clinical trial; and severe renal impairment requiring renal replacement therapy or severe hepatic impairment (Child–Pugh class C).

Randomization and masking

Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the esketamine group or the sufentanil group. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician not involved in other aspects of the trial. Randomization was performed using permuted blocks of random sizes (4, 6, or 8), without stratification. Opaque, sealed envelopes, sequentially numbered according to the randomization sequence, were opened immediately prior to sedation by an independent research assistant (not involved in clinical care or outcome assessment) to prepare the study medication based on the group allocation. Both esketamine and sufentanil were prepared identically and administered in equal volumes. Anesthesiologists, clinicians, patients, and outcome assessors remained blinded throughout the trial.

Study intervention

The sedation target was a Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score < 2, assessed every minute throughout the procedure. Patients in the esketamine group received an initial intravenous dose of esketamine (0.2 mg/kg), while those in the sufentanil group received sufentanil (0.1 μg/kg), followed by propofol (1.5–3 mg/kg) in both groups for induction of sedation. Additional boluses of propofol (0.5 mg/kg) were intermittently administered as needed to maintain the target depth of sedation and facilitate procedural management. If analgesia was insufficient, an additional dose of esketamine (0.1 mg/kg) could be administered in the esketamine group, or sufentanil (0.1 μg/kg) in the sufentanil group. A rescue dose of propofol (dose determined by the anesthesiologist) was administered if the sedation target was not achieved or surgical conditions remained inadequate after two consecutive top-up doses of propofol within 2 min. All patients received supplemental oxygen via face mask (3 L/min) and maintained spontaneous respiration throughout the procedure. In cases of upper airway obstruction or hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90%), appropriate airway interventions were performed as needed, including jaw thrust, positive-pressure ventilation via face mask, or endotracheal intubation. All patients also received adjunctive medications, including dexamethasone (5 mg) and palonosetron (0.25 mg) for the prevention of postoperative nausea and vomiting, and propacetamol (2 g) for analgesia.

After surgery, patients were transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for continued monitoring once their MOAA/S score was ≥ 4 on two consecutive assessments. Discharge from the PACU was considered when patients had an Aldrete score of ≥ 9 and experienced no significant discomfort, such as dizziness or nausea.

Patient baseline characteristics were recorded preoperatively, and intraoperative data were documented by the attending anesthesiologist. Early postoperative outcomes were assessed either 30 min after surgery or at the time patients met criteria for PACU discharge, whichever occurred first. Postoperative outcomes on days 1, 2, and 7 were self-reported by patients using structured questionnaires delivered via WeChat between 8:00 and 9:00 am each day.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was quality of recovery on postoperative day (POD) 1, measured using the validated quality of recovery (QoR)-15 questionnaire. This patient-reported outcome measure evaluates postoperative recovery across five dimensions: pain, physical comfort, physical independence, psychological support, and emotional state. Each of the 15 items is rated on an 11-point scale, with negatively worded items reverse-scored. Total scores range from 0 to 150, with higher scores indicating better recovery.

Secondary outcomes were the quality of recovery on POD2; sedation success rate (successful sedation was defined as completing the procedure without rescue medication and requiring no more than five top-up doses in any 15-minute interval) [15,16]; length of PACU stay; incidence of severe injection pain (range, 0–3; score ≥ 2 indicated severe pain) [17,18]; pain scores at 30 min and on POD1 (Numeric Rating Scale [NRS]; 0 = no pain, 10 = worst possible pain); fatigue score at 30 min postoperatively (NRS; 0 = no fatigue, 10 = extreme fatigue); patient and clinician satisfaction (range, 1–5; 1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = highly satisfied); incidence of vomiting at 30 min and on POD1; nausea scores at 30 min and on POD1 (NRS; 0 = no nausea, 10 = worst possible nausea); sleep quality scores on POD1, POD2, and POD7 (NRS; 0 = best possible sleep, 10 = worst possible sleep); and anxiety and depression scores on POD1 and POD7, assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS; total score range 0–42, with scores ≥ 15 indicating severe psychological distress) [19].

Statistical analysis

Previous studies have reported that the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the postoperative QoR-15 score is 8 [20]. In our preliminary study, the mean (SD) QoR-15 score on POD1 was 123 (14). We hypothesized that esketamine would improve the postoperative QoR-15 score to 131. Assuming an SD of 14, a sample size of 50 participants per group was required to achieve 80% power at a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Allowing for a 20% dropout rate, we planned to enroll 63 patients in each group.

All analyses followed the modified intention-to-treat principle. The primary outcome analysis included all randomized patients who received the assigned intervention and had at least one postoperative QoR-15 score. A linear mixed-effects model was used to assess the effect of the treatment group on the postoperative QoR-15 score, with group, time, and the group-by-time interaction included as fixed effects, and participant as a random effect. The between-group difference in the QoR-15 score on POD1, which was the primary outcome, along with its 95% CI, was derived from this model. The same model was also used to estimate the between-group difference and 95% CI for the QoR-15 score on POD2. Continuous secondary outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, t tests, or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical secondary outcomes were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Adverse events were compared between groups using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test.

Prespecified subgroup analyses of the primary outcome were conducted according to marital status (married vs. unmarried), type of surgery (dilation and curettage vs. other), education level (college or above vs. high school or below), combined HADS score (≥ 15 vs. < 15), anxiety HADS score (≥ 7 vs. < 7), depression HADS score (≥ 7 vs. < 7), and nulliparous (yes vs. no). Prespecified sensitivity analyses included multiple imputation of missing data and a complete-case analysis restricted to patients with full follow-up data.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3), with a two-sided p value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Without adjustment for multiplicity, findings for secondary endpoints should be interpreted as exploratory.

Patient and public involvement

Formal patient and public involvement was not incorporated into the design, conduct, outcome selection, interpretation, or reporting of this trial. The primary outcome was assessed using the QoR-15, a validated patient-reported outcome measure of postoperative quality of recovery.

Results

A total of 144 patients were screened for eligibility, of whom 126 were randomized. One patient in the esketamine group was excluded from the primary analysis because of missing outcome data at all three follow-up time points (POD1, POD2, and POD7). Thus, 125 patients (62 in the esketamine group and 63 in the sufentanil group) were included in the primary analysis (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups (Table 1; Table S1 in Supplement 1). The overall mean (SD) age was 32.7 (6.7) years. The median (IQR) preoperative QoR-15 score was 138.0 (128.0–144.0). Dilation and curettage was the most common procedure, performed in 75.2% of cases.

There was no statistically significant difference between groups in the mean (SD) QoR-15 score on POD2 (141.9 [11.5] for the esketamine group vs. 139.8 [12.1] for the sufentanil group; absolute difference, 1.8; 95% CI, −2.7 to 6.2; p = 0.43). The sedation success rate was not significantly different between the groups (91.9% in the esketamine group vs. 98.4% in the sufentanil group; absolute difference, −6.5%; 95% CI, −15.5 to 2.6; p = 0.20). The length of PACU stay was significantly longer in the esketamine group (median, 28.0 vs. 23.0 min for the sufentanil group; absolute difference, 5.0 min; 95% CI, 1.0 to 9.0; p < 0.001). The incidence of severe injection pain was significantly lower in the esketamine group (22.6% vs. 50.8% for the sufentanil group; difference, −28.2%; 95% CI, −46.0 to −10.5; p = 0.002). At 30 min postoperatively, the prevalence of a pain score ≥ 4 was higher in the esketamine group (30.6%) than in the sufentanil group (6.3%), whereas the two groups were similar on POD1 (4.9% vs. 6.3%). The median (IQR) fatigue score was significantly higher in the esketamine group (3.0 [1.2–5.0]) than in the sufentanil group (2.0 [0.0–3.8]; absolute difference, 1.0; 95% CI, 0.5 to 3.0; p = 0.01). The median (IQR) nausea score at 30 min postoperatively was significantly higher in the esketamine group (0.0 [0.0–0.8]) than in the sufentanil group (0.0 [0.0–0.0]; p = 0.02). However, the incidence of moderate-to-severe nausea (score ≥ 4) at 30 min postoperatively did not differ significantly between groups (6.5% vs. 0.0%; difference, 6.5%; 95% CI, −1.3 to 14.2; p = 0.12). There were no statistically significant differences between the groups for the other secondary outcomes, including patient satisfaction, clinician satisfaction, vomiting, sleep quality scores, and anxiety and depression scores (Table 3; Table S3 in Supplement 1).

The incidence of dizziness or headache did not differ significantly between the groups: 22.6% in the esketamine group and 12.7% in the sufentanil group (absolute difference, 9.9%; 95% CI, −5.0 to 24.7; p = 0.22). No patients experienced hallucinations or nightmares during the study, while emergence agitation occurred in one patient in the esketamine group. Hypoxia or airway intervention occurred in 27.4% of patients in the esketamine group and 31.7% in the sufentanil group (p = 0.74). The incidence of body movement was similar between the two groups (27.4% vs. 22.2%; p = 0.64), and the incidence of cough was low and not significantly different between groups (8.1% vs. 3.2%; p = 0.42). Hemodynamic adverse events, including hypertension, hypotension, bradycardia, and tachycardia, did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 4).

Discussion

In this randomized clinical trial of sedation for outpatient gynecological procedures, esketamine combined with propofol did not improve the QoR-15 score on POD1 compared to sufentanil combined with propofol.

Our findings differ from those of several previous randomized controlled trials, which reported that intraoperative esketamine administration was associated with improved postoperative quality of recovery. For example, in patients undergoing elective laparoscopic partial or total nephrectomy, a bolus dose of esketamine (0.25 mg/kg) at anesthesia induction followed by a continuous infusion at 0.125 mg·kg−1·h−1 was associated with improved quality of recovery at 24 h postoperatively [21]. Similarly, in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lung resection, a single intraoperative dose of esketamine (0.3 mg/kg) improved recovery outcomes at 24 h postoperatively [22]. In gynecological laparoscopic surgery, pre-incisional administration of a single low-dose esketamine bolus (0.25 or 0.5 mg/kg) also enhanced quality of recovery within the first 24 postoperative hours [23]. Conversely, one study supports our findings, showing that a single intraoperative low-dose bolus of esketamine (0.25 mg/kg) did not improve postoperative quality of recovery at 24 h in patients undergoing total laparoscopic hysterectomy [24]. The discrepancies between our study and previous reports may be explained by differences in esketamine dosing regimens—including bolus dose, infusion rate, and duration of administration—as well as differences in the outpatient population studied, who typically have better baseline health status than inpatient populations. Differences in the magnitude of surgical stress associated with various procedures may also contribute to the inconsistent findings.

Intravenous bolus doses of esketamine for procedural sedation commonly range from approximately 0.2 to 0.5 mg/kg [25]. However, a recent meta-analysis of gastrointestinal endoscopy sedation reported that esketamine doses ≥ 0.3 mg/kg were associated with an increased risk of postoperative dizziness [26]. Because the procedures in our study were typically brief and associated with limited surgical stress, we selected a single bolus dose of 0.2 mg/kg to balance analgesic-sedative effects against dose-related adverse events and to facilitate rapid recovery. This dose therefore reflects a compromise between analgesic efficacy and tolerability rather than an attempt to maximize the analgesic effect of esketamine. Even at this low dose, patients in the esketamine group had a prolonged PACU stay and higher early fatigue scores. It remains possible that higher doses or continuous infusion regimens could provide greater analgesic efficacy and yield different effects on quality of recovery on POD1, although they might also increase dose-related adverse events. These findings therefore apply primarily to the low-dose bolus regimen evaluated in this trial and may not extend to other esketamine dosing strategies.

In the present study, esketamine administration was associated with a prolonged PACU stay, consistent with previous reports [27,28]. This delay may be partly attributable to the sedative properties of esketamine. The between-group difference was statistically significant. However, because no adjustment for multiplicity was performed for the secondary outcome analyses, this finding should be considered exploratory, and its clinical relevance is likely to depend on the magnitude of the difference and the clinical setting. Although the median PACU stay increased by only 5 min, we acknowledge that in high-volume ambulatory surgical centers, even modest prolongations in PACU stay could cumulatively affect patient flow, resource utilization, and operational efficiency, particularly during peak hours. In contrast, such effects are less likely to be clinically meaningful in lower-volume settings, where PACU capacity is less constrained.

This study demonstrated that esketamine effectively prevented injection pain, consistent with previous randomized controlled trials [29]. Besides its central analgesic effects, ketamine also provides analgesia via peripheral mechanisms [30]. Notably, esketamine exhibits a higher affinity for the NMDA receptor compared to ketamine. Therefore, the observed reduction in injection pain with esketamine may be primarily attributed to its enhanced activity on peripheral NMDA receptors [29].

Several studies have reported that intravenous esketamine reduces postoperative anxiety and depression [12,31–33]. However, this effect was not observed in the present trial, consistent with previous findings [11]. Luo et al. demonstrated that an intraoperative dose of 0.5 mg/kg alleviated postoperative anxiety and depression, whereas a dose of 0.2 mg/kg produced no significant effect [12]. These discrepancies may be partially explained by differences in esketamine dosing regimens.

In the present study, the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain (score ≥ 4) at 30 min after surgery was higher in the esketamine group. A likely explanation is relative under-analgesia within our opioid-sparing protocol: the subanesthetic single bolus of esketamine (0.2 mg/kg) may not fully replicate the potent, sustained analgesia provided by sufentanil, despite esketamine’s known analgesic properties. A review of 39 randomized controlled trials (2,482 patients) similarly found that perioperative low-dose intravenous ketamine reduced opioid consumption by approximately 40% rather than fully replacing potent opioids [34]. Comparable findings have been reported in gynecological laparoscopic surgery, where esketamine-based opioid-free anesthesia produced pain control similar to sufentanil-based anesthesia, with numerically higher pain scores in the esketamine group [35]; another trial in the same surgical setting likewise found no improvement in postoperative pain or morphine consumption with esketamine-based opioid-free anesthesia [36].

The higher fatigue scores observed in the esketamine group at 30 min after surgery may reflect residual sedative effects of esketamine during early emergence in the PACU, such as dizziness, which can be difficult for patients to distinguish from fatigue on a subjective rating scale. This is consistent with esketamine’s known central nervous system side-effect profile, even at subanesthetic doses. Although fatigue scores were higher in the esketamine group, the median scores in both groups corresponded to mild fatigue [37], suggesting that the clinical relevance of this difference to overall patient experience is likely limited.

Sedation profiles differed between groups. Although sedation depth was titrated according to the prespecified MOAA/S target, patients in the esketamine group spent a greater proportion of time at deeper levels of sedation and had a longer overall duration of sedation than those in the sufentanil group. These differences may reflect the distinct pharmacodynamic profiles of the two agents: esketamine, through NMDA receptor antagonism, produces dissociative sedative and analgesic effects, whereas sufentanil, at the low dose used in this trial, was intended primarily to provide μ-opioid receptor-mediated analgesia. Differences in sedation depth and duration may have contributed to the prolonged PACU stay and higher fatigue scores observed in the esketamine group.

Regarding postoperative nausea, nausea scores at 30 min postoperatively were statistically significantly higher in the esketamine group, despite a median score of 0 in both groups. This finding likely reflects the skewed and zero-inflated distribution of nausea scores, in which small differences in mild nausea can result in a statistically significant between-group difference. Importantly, the incidence of moderate-to-severe nausea (score ≥ 4) was low in both groups and did not differ significantly between groups. Therefore, although esketamine was associated with slightly higher nausea scores during early recovery, the clinical significance of this difference may be limited.

These findings suggest that esketamine should not be considered superior to sufentanil for improving quality of recovery after outpatient gynecological procedures. Although esketamine reduced injection pain and may be useful in opioid-sparing strategies, this potential advantage should be weighed against less favorable early recovery outcomes, including higher pain and fatigue scores, and a prolonged PACU stay.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, and the findings require confirmation in multicenter settings. Second, the sympathomimetic effects of esketamine—characterized by increased blood pressure and heart rate—may have enabled intraoperative anesthesiologists to infer treatment allocation despite identical infusion volumes. Such potential partial unblinding could have resulted in bias in intraoperative management and clinician-related secondary outcomes. However, its potential influence on the primary outcome assessment was likely limited, as the primary outcome (QoR-15 scores) was a patient-reported outcome, and patients remained blinded to treatment allocation throughout the study. Furthermore, while intraoperative anesthesiologists collected intraoperative data, they were not involved in the assessment of postoperative outcomes, which were evaluated by independent, blinded assessors. Third, our study population was relatively homogeneous and low-risk, consisting predominantly of young women undergoing brief, minor outpatient gynecological procedures. Our findings may therefore not be generalizable to longer or more complex outpatient gynecological surgeries, or to higher-risk populations, in which surgical stress, postoperative pain, and recovery may differ substantially. Fourth, the study was performed under deep sedation (MOAA/S < 2), and the results may not be generalizable to settings involving lighter levels of sedation. Fifth, two patients in the esketamine group had missing primary outcome data, which may have reduced statistical power and introduced bias if the missingness was not completely at random; nonetheless, sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the findings. Sixth, this trial was designed and powered to test superiority. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant between-group difference in QoR-15 scores should be interpreted as a failure to demonstrate superiority of esketamine over sufentanil, rather than as evidence of equivalence or non-inferiority between the two regimens. Because no equivalence or non-inferiority margin was pre-specified, the confidence interval around the treatment effect should not be used to infer clinical equivalence or non-inferiority. Seventh, the sample size calculation was based on anticipated postoperative QoR-15 scores that were lower than those ultimately observed. In addition, baseline QoR-15 scores were already high in both groups, and postoperative scores appeared to return rapidly toward baseline levels. Under these circumstances, the capacity of the QoR-15 to capture further clinically meaningful improvement may have been limited. This potential ceiling effect may have reduced the sensitivity of the trial to detect between-group differences in quality of recovery. Eighth, postoperative rescue analgesic use was not recorded in this trial; therefore, we could not determine whether the higher early postoperative pain scores in the esketamine group were accompanied by increased analgesic requirements. Finally, we did not evaluate outcomes beyond POD7, limiting insights into the longer-term effects of esketamine.

Conclusions

Among patients undergoing sedation for outpatient gynecological procedures, esketamine did not demonstrate superiority over sufentanil in improving quality of recovery on POD1. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence or non-inferiority between the two regimens.
