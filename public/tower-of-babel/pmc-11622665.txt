The effect of computer-based Stress Inoculation Training (SIT) approach on the pelvic pain, depression, and anxiety in students with primary dysmenorrhea: a clinical trial study
Authors: Leila Dailer, Hajar Adib-Rad, Fatemeh Bakouei, Mahbobeh Faramarzi, Soraya Khafri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary dysmenorrhea (PD) is one of the most common pains experienced by women. Stress Inoculation Training (SIT) is a cognitive-behavioral technique that immunizes them against future periods of stress. The purpose of this research is to investigate the effectiveness of a computer-based stress inoculation approach on pelvic pain, depression, and anxiety symptoms in students with PD.MethodsThe present study is a clinical trial conducted on 100 students with PD. The SIT intervention consisted of eight consecutive sessions. The content of the sessions was created as multimedia on a computer platform of a site. In each session, several interactive questions were asked about the topics of that session, and participants answered them, with responses recorded in the system. Then, each individual session, lasting about 50 min, was sent as a link (offline) to a participant. Data collection tools included the demographics Checklist, McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ), Moos Menstrual Distress Questionnaire (MMDQ), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ), and Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-14), which were completed on the second menstrual day of the cycle before the study and for three consecutive cycles after the intervention by each student. The data were analyzed using chi-square, generalized linear mixed models (GLMM), and multiple linear regression tests. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.ResultsThe results of the GLMM test showed that the SIT intervention decreased depression (p = 0.002), anxiety (p = 0.001), menstrual distress (p = 0.001), pain intensity (p = 0.003), and perceived stress (p = 0.002), while it increased pain self-efficacy (p = 0.001). Based on multiple linear regression analysis, the main predictors of depression were the student’s age and residence in a dormitory (β=-0.255, p = 0.047 and β=-0.376, p = 0.005, respectively). The factors influencing pain self-efficacy were birth rank and the type of university admission (β=-0.351, p = 0.027 and β=-0.249, p = 0.030, respectively). In the presence of confounding variables, increasing age and living in a dormitory were risk factors that increased depression, while studying with tuition fees and a higher birth rank were associated with decreased self-efficacy.ConclusionThe computer-based SIT was effective in reducing pelvic pain, and psychological factors, and in increasing pain self-efficacy in students with primary dysmenorrhea. Therefore, it can be used as a useful solution to manage complications associated with primary dysmenorrhea.Trial registrationIRCT20230130057274N5Date of registration: 2024-04-29Retrospectively registeredKeywords: Stress inoculation training, Primary dysmenorrhea, Pelvic pain, Depression, Anxiety

Introduction

Dysmenorrhea is a very common complaint, affecting about three-quarters of women during adolescence and early adulthood in their reproductive years. Clinically, dysmenorrhea is often divided into primary and secondary types. Primary dysmenorrhea (PD) refers to painful menstruation in the absence of any demonstrable pelvic pathology, usually starting a few hours before or after the onset of menstruation and lasting for 48 to 72 h [1, 2]. This pain, similar to labor pain, is accompanied by uterine contractions and lower back pain, is concentrated in the lower abdomen, and radiates to the back of the thighs. Symptoms may include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, fatigue, shivering, and rarely, fainting. Dysmenorrhea leads to poor sleep quality and negatively affects individuals’ moods, causing anxiety and depression [3, 4]. PD occurs predominantly in women aged 20 or younger and rarely persists beyond the age of 40 [5]. Various factors such as demographic, reproductive, lifestyle, psychological, and social factors can influence the occurrence and severity of PD [6].

The prevalence of primary dysmenorrhea has been reported between 50 and 90% in different societies [7, 8]. In severe cases, dysmenorrhea can lead to impairment in performance, education, or employment. In total, 140 million hours of absenteeism occur annually due to dysmenorrhea, which can disrupt daily activities [9]. Due to the increased need for medical care, associated medical costs, and reduced productivity in everyday tasks, dysmenorrhea is often considered an economic and social issue [10]. Some psychological factors, such as excessive emotional disturbance and mental symptoms, are often associated with more severe dysmenorrhea. The severity of dysmenorrhea increases with the severity of depression, anxiety, and physical complaints. Therefore, the experience of dysmenorrhea has both physical and psychological aspects that affect psychological well-being [11].

Most women initially manage their symptoms using pain relievers. Dissatisfaction with medical interventions, limited pain relief from drug-based methods, and fear of drug side effects lead women to prefer complementary and non-drug treatments [12]. Self-care techniques or lifestyle changes, both physical and psychological, such as exercise, heat, massage, or acupressure, have been effective [13]. Therefore, the need to provide a non-drug treatment method for patients who do not respond to medication, suffer from its side effects, or are unwilling to use drugs is evident [14]. Non-drug interventions, including stress control skills, relaxation skills, lifestyle changes, dietary adjustments, physical activity, and anger control skills, have shown success in reducing the severity of dysmenorrhea [15]. Stress Inoculation Training [SIT] is a cognitive-behavioral therapy developed by Meichenbaum. The SIT technique enhances an individual’s self-confidence to respond effectively when faced with a stressful situation. It also helps individuals prepare for difficult experiences that may have occurred in the past or may occur in the future. This therapeutic approach improves emotional distress and prevents maladaptive psychological responses to stress. The SIT technique includes a wide range of activities such as conceptualization, relaxation, problem-solving, exercises, and cognitive-behavioral methods. This type of psychotherapy can be conducted individually or in group therapy sessions [16]. It is used to prepare individuals for anticipated pressures, anxieties, nervous stress, and a wide range of adverse events or conditions that may incapacitate them [17].

Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach in reducing pain, anxiety, and depression, as well as improving professional performance. In a study, results showed that SIT intervention was effective in reducing PMS and stress [18]. The results of Szabo and colleagues study showed that SIT is an effective way to deal with stress in adolescents in the short term and three months after the intervention [17]. Another study reported that semi-in-person SIT was more effective than virtual SIT in reducing psychological distress of pregnant women and recommended semi-in-person SIT for pregnant women [19]. In one study, SIT intervention reduced anxiety in college students [20]. Also, the findings of a study showed that stress management in a short period creates a mindset that increases stress management and can improve stress [21]. The multidimensional stress immunization program addresses the thoughts, emotions, and behaviors of individuals undergoing intervention. Through components such as cognitive restructuring, meaning-making, coping exercises, and positive thinking, it can have a significant impact on the use of coping strategies, emotional regulation, and the enhancement of individuals’ mental health [22].

Computer-based education has become more popular today and refers to any computer application in educational settings, including practice, experience, simulation, academic management, problem-solving, planning, information development, and writing and reading through word processing [23]. With advances in computer technology, computer programs offer an innovative way to improve access to psychological treatments in research clinical settings [24]. This approach has advantages for users, such as privacy, ease of access, and convenience, while eliminating issues such as the stigma of seeing a therapist and providing anonymity [25]. Computerized psychotherapies help individuals better understand their illness, improve social functioning, and lead to greater patient knowledge and awareness. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported that this treatment is as effective as face-to-face therapy [26, 27]. Internet-based treatment, especially in populations with high symptoms of depression and anxiety, has shown increasing effectiveness in clinical groups [28].

In computer-based therapies, the lack of face-to-face contact or minimal contact with a therapist may initially be considered a disadvantage of Internet-based interventions. However, for certain individuals who prefer not to discuss their problems with others, it may be an advantage compared to conventional treatments. Additionally, participants may be more focused and responsible for treatment goals and exercises. Given the negative effects of stress, anxiety, and depression in individuals with dysmenorrhea, and considering that appropriate intervention and counseling can yield beneficial results in reducing these problems, the present study aims to investigate the effect of a computer-based SIT approach on depressive symptoms and anxiety in students with PD.

Materials and methods

Design and data collection

This study was a before and after clinical trial conducted on 100 unmarried female students aged 18–30 with PD from December 2021 to May 2023, after obtaining approval from the Ethics Committee of Babol University of Medical Sciences with ethics code IR.MUBABOL.REC.1400.184, and registered in the IRCT (IRCT20230130057274N5). Our study was not a randomized controlled trial, as we could not select a control group due to the interaction of students with each other at the university; therefore, a control group, randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding were unnecessary.

To determine the sample size, we used the study by Wong et al. [29] and GPower 3.1.9.2 software at a significance level of 95% and a power of 80%. Using the Pain Visual Analogue Scale (PVAS) with PVAS1 = 5.79, PVAS2 = 4.62, S1 = 1.62, and S2 = 4.00 for average pain before and after the intervention, the required sample size was 72 people. Using the McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ) with MPQ1 = 9.42, MPQ2 = 5.26, S1 = 5.62, and S2 = 3.87 for MPQ changes before and after the intervention, the required sample size was 14 people. Using the Moos Menstrual Distress Questionnaire (MMDQ) with d1 = 31.58, d2 = 25.84, S1 = 7.52, and S2 = 5.66 for MMDQ changes before and after the intervention, the required sample size was 15 people. Considering the maximum required sample size and a possible dropout rate of 10%, 80 participants were selected. The participants’ flowchart is illustrated in Fig. 1. Based on this figure, to select at least 80 participants with a Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) score higher than eight, 160 students from Babol University of Medical Sciences completed the HADS questionnaire. Of these, 100 students had a HADS score higher than 8, so the study was conducted with all 100 students.

Flowchart of participants

The inclusion criteria for the study included being Iranian, providing informed consent to participate, experiencing any degree (mild, moderate, severe) of PD, having regular menstrual cycles with intervals of 21 to 35 days and a duration of 3 to 10 days for at least the past six months, and obtaining a score of 8 or higher on the HADS. The exclusion criteria for the study included having history of reproductive system diseases, or surgeries, known chronic illnesses, using contraceptive pills, suffering from hormonal disorders or irregular menstruation, having pelvic infections, experiencing burning, itching, or abnormal vaginal discharge, engaging in professional sports activities, complaining of any other pain in the pelvic area as reported by the student, and having a current or past history of severe psychiatric disorders such as severe depression, bipolar disorder, suicidal thoughts, or substance abuse (including alcohol and drugs). Additionally, individuals experiencing symptoms of depression or anxiety, using antidepressant or antianxiety medications during the study, participate in other psychological interventions such as psychotherapy, meditation, or yoga, and have experienced stressful events (such as the death of close relatives, loss of close relatives, severe family disputes, parental separation, economic problems in the family, accidents, and other traumatic incidents) for at least six months prior to the start of the study, and drug addiction were also excluded. The primary outcomes of the study included changes in the mean scores of pelvic pain, depression, and anxiety symptoms, while the secondary outcomes encompassed changes in the mean scores of menstrual distress, pain self-efficacy, and perceived stress.

Measurements

The Demographic-Fertility Characteristics Checklist used in this study covers various variables, including age, body mass index (BMI), father’s job, mother’s job, father’s education, mother’s education, residence, satisfaction with income, menarche age, duration of bleeding, interval of bleeding, duration of dysmenorrhea, and family history of dysmenorrhea.

The student’s weight was measured while wearing light clothes and without shoes (using a Seca Sensa 804, Hamburg, Germany), and height was measured (using a Seca 206, Hamburg, Germany). BMI (kg/m2) was calculated for all participant by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of their height.

The Multidimensional Verbal Rating Scale (MVRS) is a tool for evaluating the grade of dysmenorrhea and systemic symptoms associated with dysmenorrhea, which has five components for each grade of dysmenorrhea and a total of 20 components. It includes four grades:

Degree Zero: Indicates the absence of painful menstruation and does not affect daily activities.

Degree Zero: Indicates the absence of painful menstruation and does not affect daily activities.

Degree One: Menstruation with mild pain that rarely interferes with daily activities, with mild systemic symptoms, requiring very little analgesic.

Degree One: Menstruation with mild pain that rarely interferes with daily activities, with mild systemic symptoms, requiring very little analgesic.

Degree Two: Moderate pain intensity, causing disruption in daily activities; however, there is no need for absence from school or work.

Degree Two: Moderate pain intensity, causing disruption in daily activities; however, there is no need for absence from school or work.

Degree Three: Severe pain to the extent that the individual is unable to perform daily activities, with severe systemic symptoms [30]. The results of a study in samples of Iranian PD showed a reliability of 0.87 for the questionnaire [31].

Degree Three: Severe pain to the extent that the individual is unable to perform daily activities, with severe systemic symptoms [30]. The results of a study in samples of Iranian PD showed a reliability of 0.87 for the questionnaire [31].

The Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ) consists of 12 words to assess the quality of pain in two dimensions: sensory and affective. Each word’s response is categorized as ‘none, mild, moderate, and severe,’ and the responses are scored from zero to three. The total score ranges from zero, indicating the minimum, to 36, indicating the maximum score. Additionally, this questionnaire includes a verbal scale (for pain intensity assessment) comprising 6 items (ranging from ‘no pain’ to ‘excruciating’), scored from 0 to 5 [32]. In an Iranian study, the content validity index (CVI) was higher than 0.71 and its reliability calculated by Cronbach’s alpha was 0.76 [33].

The Short-Form Moos Menstrual Distress Questionnaire (SF-MMDQ) aims to assess the impact of menstrual distress on daily activities. This questionnaire reflects patient complaints about various symptoms during menstruation. Individuals are asked to report the symptoms they experience during their menstrual cycle through the Likert scale classification system. The number 1 represents a state where no signs are experienced, and 5 indicates the most disabling condition [34, 35]. The shortened form of this questionnaire includes 16 items [29, 34]. In an Iranian study, CVI was higher than 0.79 and the reliability of the questionnaire was calculated at 0.93 using Cronbach’s alpha [36].

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was introduced by Zigmond and Snaith as a tool for screening psychiatric disorders. This tool concurrently measures anxiety and depression in ambulatory patients. HADS is a 14-item, 4-option questionnaire, with each item scored on a scale from 0 to 3 (0–3). Thus, the subscale scores for anxiety and depression in the HADS questionnaire range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating higher levels of anxiety and depression. For both subscales, Zigmond and Snaith consider scores in the range of 0 to 7 as normal or typical, 8 to 10 as mild, 11 to 14 as moderate, and 15 to 21 as severe [37]. In an Iranian study, the validity of the depression subscale was 0.77, and the validity of the anxiety subscale was 0.76. Also, its reliability for the depression subscale was 0.71, and for the anxiety subscale, it was 0.75 [38].

The Pain Self-efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ) was created based on the concept proposed by Bandura regarding self-efficacy [39]. This questionnaire on pain self-efficacy comprises 10 statements. Each statement assesses the patient’s evaluation of their ability to perform a group of activities despite the presence of pain, based on a 7-point Likert scale (0–6). Higher scores indicate higher self-efficacy [40]. The results of a study in samples of Iranian chronic pain patients showed that the reliability and validity were found to be strong, with an internal consistency coefficient of 0.92 and a reliability of 0.83 for the questionnaire [41].

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-14) is a 14-item scale designed by Cohen and colleagues (1983) [42]. This questionnaire is used to measure the perceived general stress over the past month. Scoring is done based on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4). The score range is from 0 to 56, with higher scores indicating higher levels of stress. In an Iranian study, the validity of this questionnaire was 0.63, and its reliability was 0.73 [43].

Interventions

All students signed a written informed consent form before participating in the study. The SIT intervention consisted of eight consecutive sessions (one session per week), each lasting 50 min. The content of these sessions was provided to the students in a computerized format. After preparing the digital content, a link was individually sent to each student via text message or social media platforms accessible to them. The content of the sessions was created as multimedia (text, images, sound, video, etc.) on the computer platform of a website (http://www.peacefulmindme.com). In each session, several interactive questions were asked about the meeting’s topics, and the participants answered them, with their responses recorded in the site system. Each individual session lasted about 50 min and was sent as a link (offline) to the participant. When each participant entered the offline meeting using the sent link, they viewed the session and answered the questions; all their activities (entering, exiting, and ending the meeting) were recorded in the site’s support system. Throughout the week, the researcher communicated with the students for 15 to 20 min via social media or phone to address any questions about the session and how to perform the weekly exercises. A one-time-use link, prepared for the number of participants, was made available to them via WhatsApp. After accessing the link and listening to the content, participants answered a few questions. Access to answers and evaluation of the participants’ responses were checked through the website, allowing responses to be submitted via the one-time-use links (each person had their specific link, which became inactive after use).

Counseling sessions consisted of two parts. The first part of each session, lasting 15–17 min, focused on teaching the steps of SIT. The counseling sessions, based on the SIT therapeutic approach, included problem conceptualization, identification of negative thoughts, problem-solving skills, concentration and distraction techniques [16]. The second part of the intervention involved teaching about menstrual concepts for 15–20 min and relaxation techniques for 15–17 min. For the relaxation portion, the text of the relaxation book [44] along with calming music, was sent to participants via WhatsApp in the form of 15 to 17-minute audio sessions over the eight sessions. To ensure sufficient time for the relaxation exercises (since they needed to be performed continuously throughout the week), the materials were sent on the final days of each week. We covered all dependent variables, including dysmenorrhea, pelvic pain, anxiety and depression, stress, distress, and self-efficacy, in the counseling sessions while simultaneously using the SIT model.

All students completed the questionnaires on the second day of their first menstrual cycle before the intervention. They were followed up in three consecutive cycles after the last intervention (first, second, and third month), and completed the questionnaires by responding through the link sent to each participant. Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0. Descriptive statistics indicators (mean, standard deviation, frequency distribution table, and relative frequency), were calculated. Additionally, Chi Square, GLMM, and multiple linear regression were used for data analysis. The significance level of the tests was set at P < 0.05.

Results

The results of this study indicated that the average age of the students was 23.12 ± 2.78 years. Of the participants, 53% resided in dormitories, 45% of their fathers were employed, and 73% of their mothers were homemakers. The educational level of fathers was university for 47% of them, while 40% of the mothers had a diploma. The average age of menarche among the students was 12.99 ± 1.21 years. Additionally, the average duration of menstruation and menstrual cycle lengths were 6.42 ± 1.27 and 28.83 ± 2.47 days, respectively, with the majority (86%) reporting regular menstruation. Changes in the perceived stress score before the intervention and three cycles after the intervention

The average self-efficacy score before the intervention was 30.60, increasing to 40.36 in the first month post-intervention and further rising to 44.83 in the second month. These changes in the average self-efficacy score were statistically significant (p < 0.001). The GLMM test indicated that, prior to the intervention, the average self-efficacy score for pain was low, but significant increases were noted in the first and second months post-intervention. In the third month, the increase plateaued, with a slight decrease compared to the previous month. Discussion

The objective of this research was to investigate the effectiveness of the computer-based SIT approach on pelvic pain, depression, and anxiety symptoms in students with primary dysmenorrhea. The results indicated that the SIT approach significantly reduced the average scores of pelvic pain, depression, and anxiety among female students experiencing PD. Before and after interventional studies are a type of clinical trial that despite their limitations, can provide services and contribute to the production of science [45, 46]. Similar studies have been conducted in the field of dysmenorrhea, as a before and after interventional and single-group study [47–49].

The results of a systematic review indicated that a number of studies showed the effect of behavioral interventions on dysmenorrhea [50]. In Zolfaghary and colleagues study, the effect of SIT on PMS was investigated and the results showed that SIT intervention was effective in reducing PMS and stress [18]. In a study by Selçuk et al., 90 nursing students aged 18 to 25 with regular menstrual cycles and a visual analog scale pain intensity of 5 or higher over the past six months were examined. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: dark chocolate, music, or control. Results indicated that both the dark chocolate and music groups experienced a significant decrease in the average severity of menstrual pain and anxiety levels post-intervention, while no significant changes were observed in the control group [51]. Although the pain intensity and anxiety scales used differed from those in our study, their findings align with ours, demonstrating that non-pharmacological interventions can effectively reduce dysmenorrhea pain and anxiety.

Another study explored the effectiveness of self-compassion and positive thinking training on improving sleep quality and reducing pain intensity in girls with painful PD. This educational intervention consisted of eight weekly sessions lasting one and a half hours each. Results indicated a significant positive effect of the positive thinking intervention on reducing pain intensity in these girls [52]. While the type of non-pharmacological intervention differed from our study, both demonstrated that such approaches can alleviate dysmenorrhea pain. Also, the sleep quality of girls increased with this non-pharmacological intervention. Supporting our findings, Abdi et al. showed that eight sessions of stress resilience training effectively reduced pre-competition anxiety in athletes by managing physiological arousal responses during competitions [53]. Similarly, Fatemi et al. compared the effectiveness of virtual and semi-presential SIT training techniques on improving symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in pregnant women with mental distress. The study, involving 96 pregnant women, found that SIT training significantly reduced anxiety, depression, mental distress, and pregnancy-related stress in both groups [19].

The SIT approach, which includes breathing exercises and relaxation techniques, positively influences physiological factors affecting anxiety, such as heart rate and muscle tension, thereby alleviating anxiety-inducing factors [54]. Overall, the results of these studies are consistent with our findings. Non-pharmacological treatment methods enhance patients’ ability to cope with pain, reducing their perception of pain, fear, and anxiety, thus making dysmenorrhea more manageable [18, 52]. Additionally, such interventions can augment the efficacy of analgesics. Since dysmenorrhea is associated with increased psychological disorders and depression in young women, controlling dysmenorrhea symptoms can decrease the incidence of depression, contributing to a better quality of life [51]. Counseling in managing and reducing pain intensity, anxiety, and depression in individuals with PD can be a useful solution for their mental well-being, and have a significant impact on the family and community’s economic process [53].

Our study demonstrated that the SIT approach led to reductions in dysmenorrheal distress scores, perceived stress, and increased pain self-efficacy across three time points post-intervention. Similarly, Rupita et al. assessed the effectiveness of group counseling using CBT techniques alongside SIT, finding it effective in improving stress and life satisfaction in students [55]. In line with our study, Various studies confirm that the SIT approach positively influences numerous outcomes across different populations. For example, one study showed that SIT intervention significantly improved perceived stress and treatment adherence in asthma patients [56], while another found significant improvements in cognitive capital among incarcerated women following SIT intervention [57]. Additionally, the results of a study demonstrated that stress resilience training could improve alexithymia and adherence to treatment in coronary artery disease patients [58]. The SIT program is multidimensional; its components, such as cognitive restructuring, relaxation exercises, and positive thinking, significantly enhance coping strategies, emotion regulation, and mental well-being [59]. In a study, the effectiveness of teaching coping strategies on awareness, pain self-efficacy, and indicators related to migraine headaches was investigated. Research indicates that teaching coping strategies can improve awareness, pain self-efficacy, and reduce migraine headache indicators [60]. SIT coping skills enable individuals to creatively address stressful situations and reassess their beliefs, thereby correcting misconceptions that contribute to depression and anxiety. This technique also teaches individuals how to seek social support, further aiding in the reduction of depressive symptoms.

Our multiple linear regression model revealed that among the relevant variables, only the student’s age and residence in a dormitory were predictors of depression. Additionally, birth rank and type of university admission were influential factors in pain self-efficacy. Adib-Rad et al. similarly noted that psychological distress, including depression, correlates with maternal education and family history of dysmenorrhea [4]. The findings suggest that living in a student dormitory and experiencing reduced familial support, combined with young girls’ inexperience in managing menstrual issues, may lead to increased depression. Thus, depression appears more common among younger students residing in dormitories. In summary, our study and existing literature support the assertion that SIT interventions positively affect pain, distress, perceived stress, and self-efficacy. We recommend the SIT approach for mitigating physical and psychological symptoms associated with dysmenorrhea and enhancing self-efficacy.

In interpreting our research findings, it is important to highlight that the SIT approach is a cognitive-behavioral therapeutic method. It posits that while therapists cannot change individuals’ life circumstances, they can modify their attitudes toward life and the associated stressors. This approach encourages individuals to reassess their criteria for coping with challenges, promoting self-criticism reduction and the exploration of new solutions to problems. Furthermore, through learning stress control, effective coping, and confrontation techniques, individuals can enhance their self-esteem and diminish feelings of helplessness, ultimately improving mood and reducing anxiety and depression.

We aim to elucidate how the SIT model effectively reduces pain and depression while enhancing self-efficacy. While the precise mechanisms remain uncertain, several hypotheses can explain these effects. The SIT model emphasizes cognitive restructuring, which diminishes negative thoughts and fosters positive thinking. Additionally, relaxation techniques incorporated within the model likely contribute to reductions in depression and pain, as supported by various studies [18, 19, 61]. The enhancement of self-efficacy may stem from skill acquisition and the learning of assertiveness, with reduced depression potentially leading to increased self-efficacy. These components are detailed in the intervention framework and supported by previous research [62, 63].

Limitations and strengths

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. Firstly, the psychological characteristics and personality traits of individuals may have influenced their engagement with the provided counseling and responses to the questionnaire. These factors were not fully controllable, potentially impacting the validity of the findings. Although the results indicated that participants were enthusiastic about engaging in studies on non-pharmacological approaches, the intervention may not be generalizable to other populations.

Another limitation was the absence of a control group, which hinders our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the effectiveness of the intervention. Additionally, prior to the intervention, 53% of participants reported using analgesics, and while we permitted the use of analgesics post-intervention, we did not monitor their use after the intervention. Upon data analysis, the research team recognized that tracking analgesic usage could have added valuable insights to our findings. Despite these limitations, the present study boasts several strengths. Notably, it is the first randomized controlled trial employing the SIT approach for managing dysmenorrhea and its associated psychological issues. Furthermore, the study effectively utilized virtual platforms for intervention and employed electronic questionnaires to collect necessary research data. This innovative approach not only facilitated participant engagement but also streamlined data collection, enhancing the overall research process.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that computer-based counseling utilizing the SIT approach effectively reduced pelvic pain, symptoms of depression and anxiety, menstrual distress, and perceived stress, while also increasing pain self-efficacy among students with primary dysmenorrhea. Given these positive outcomes, it is recommended that this non-pharmacological method be considered as a viable intervention for individuals experiencing PD.
