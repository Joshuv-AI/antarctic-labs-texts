Abstract
. We investigate fluctuation phenomena for the graph distance and the number of cut points associated with random media arising from the range of a random walk. Our results demonstrate a sequence of dimension-dependent phase transitions in the scaling behavior of these fluctuations, leading to qualitatively different regimes across dimensions lower than six, equal to six, and higher than six. In particular, in six dimensions, we show that the convergence to the normal distribution occurs under a scaling different from that of the standard central limit theorem. We also give the related results for the effective resistance of the random walk range.

As our motivation, random walks in random media have attracted considerable interest as mathematical models for transport phenomena in disordered environments. Typical examples include random conductance models, random walks in random environments, and random walks on percolation clusters. One of the central themes in this area is to understand how the geometry and randomness of the underlying medium influence the long-time behavior of the walk (e.g., see
[
8
,
9
]
). For example, for supercritical percolation clusters on

{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
, the walk behaves diffusively, in close analogy with the simple random walk on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
(e.g., see
[
3
]
). The situation is markedly different at criticality. In highly irregular media, such as large critical percolation clusters, it is widely believed that the random walk exhibits subdiffusive behavior (e.g.,
[
19
]
).
In addition,
[
8
,
10
]
deal with time changes of processes on resistance spaces, which cover the case when the resistance has a non-trivial scaling limit. We refer the reader to
[
14
]
for further references on these topics.

An important tool for analyzing random walks in random media is the effective resistance or graph distance associated with the underlying random media. In particular, the behavior of the effective resistance is closely connected to the spectral dimension of the random subset, which characterizes the long-time decay of return probabilities and provides a key indicator of whether the associated random walk exhibits diffusive or anomalous scaling.

Independent of the study of random walks on random media, understanding the graph distance in random media is of intrinsic interest. The graph distance captures the geometry induced by the random environment itself and provides a natural notion of distance that reflects how connectivity and paths are shaped by randomness.

The behavior of graph distance plays a central role in a variety of problems, including volume growth, metric scaling, and universality phenomena in random geometry. In particular, in light of recent developments, it is conjectured that graph distances in several models of random media exhibit non-trivial scaling behavior, potentially related to general universality phenomena. These considerations provide strong motivation for a detailed analysis of graph distances in random media, which is a focus of the present work.

Motivated by these considerations, we aim to understand the fluctuation behavior of the effective resistance and the graph distance in random media. In this paper, we take the range of a random walk as a concrete example of a random medium. According to the results of Croydon
[
7
]
, in
d\geq 5
the leading-order behavior is linear regardless of the dimension and does not possess a phase transition, which naturally raises the question of how fluctuations behave around this leading term.

Our analysis reveals that the fluctuation behavior depends sensitively on the dimension. In particular, we find distinct regimes in dimensions four, five, six, and seven and higher. A major phase transition occurs at dimension six: for
d\geq 6
, the fluctuations, when normalized by the square root of the variance, converge in distribution to a Gaussian random variable, whereas in dimensions four and five, the same normalization leads to convergence to zero. We also note that the variance of the normal random variable in
d=6
that appears in the central limit theorem is non-standard in the sense that it is actually not equal to the variance of the original random variable. Moreover, our proofs suggest that in dimensions four and five, if the fluctuations are rescaled by the appropriate order, they are expected to converge to non-Gaussian random variables.

Remark 1.6
.

We believe that the stronger results of Propositions
1.1
and
1.2
are true for the effective resistance as well, but we avoid writing the extra technical details for simplicity. Instead, we present a weaker version that holds under weaker assumptions, such as without any correlation assumptions on the cross terms; we believe that the analysis performed in this section is of independent interest and can be readily applicable to any random variable on the random walk range that is governed by heavy tailed behavior.

As a key ingredient of our approach to the analysis of fluctuations, we employ a decomposition of the random walk path. More precisely, we split the trajectory
S[0,n]
into two subpaths
S[0,n/2]
and
S[n/2,n]
, and compare the corresponding effective resistances or graph distances. The quantity

X[0,n/2]+X[n/2,n]-X[0,n]

naturally arises as error terms.

By iterating this decomposition procedure, we obtain a representation consisting of a sum of finely decomposed random variables, which behave approximately as independent and identically distributed terms, together with accumulated error terms. A key observation is that a phase transition occurs depending on the dimension: while in dimensions
d\geq 6
the sum of the i.i.d.-type terms dominates the fluctuations, in lower dimensions the error terms become dominant and significantly affect the scaling behavior. Consequently, it is expected that for
d\geq 6
the fluctuations converge in distribution to a Gaussian random variable, whereas such Gaussian behavior does not persist in lower dimensions.

We outline the main technical difficulties in the proof of our results.
For the graph distance and the number of cut points, establishing the validity or failure of the central limit theorem, which constitutes our main theorem, requires a precise and delicate analysis of the variance. In particular, this analysis necessitates a careful control of the correlations among the error terms arising from the decomposition employed in the proof.
As a first step, it is necessary to improve the result of
[
4
, Lemma 1.1]
by identifying the optimal strategy for events in the large deviation regime that give rise to large error terms. We show that such events correspond to situations in which the random walk forms a large loop.
The second step consists in estimating the correlations of these error terms. More precisely, one must show that these correlations are sufficiently small when the time intervals associated with the decomposition that generates the error terms are well separated.

A technical difficulty to show the validity or failure of the
central limit theorem is the fact that the large deviation probabilities governing these error terms exhibit heavy-tailed behavior.
In fact, a related phenomenon for the decomposition was already observed in earlier work of the authors
[
1
,
2
,
12
]
concerning the capacity of the range of a random walk. In that setting, when analyzing fluctuations via a similar decomposition scheme, a phase transition occurred between dimensions four and five, depending on which term became dominant in the decomposition.

While this qualitative behavior is closely related to the phenomena studied in the present paper, the analysis in our current setting requires substantially different techniques. More specifically, in the previous work the error terms arising from the decomposition became negligible in dimensions
d\geq 5
, whereas in the present case the error terms remain of polynomial order even for
d\geq 6
, necessitating a much more delicate analysis. Moreover, although in dimension four the error terms are leading in both settings, their control in the present work requires global information on the entire path, leading to more intricate estimates.

We also obtained partial results for the effective resistance; however, the resulting bounds are weaker than those for the other quantities. This is due to technical difficulties arising in the estimation of the correlations of the error terms.
Nevertheless, even without a detailed analysis of these correlations, once an estimate of the error terms corresponding to an improvement of
[
4
, Lemma 1.1]
is available, we are able to obtain an analogy that yields a phase transition for the central limit theorem of the effective resistance under weaker assumptions on the error terms. We believe that this analogy is useful for a broad class of random variables for which a decomposition of the type considered in this work can be carried out.

X^{2}_{(-\infty,\infty)}[a,b]
will be a lower bound for
X[a,b]
for any times
a
and
b
and that
X^{2}_{(-\infty,\infty)}[a,b]
is monotonically increasing as we increase the interval
[a,b]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{1})\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T^{1}\leq n+f(n),X^{2}_{(-\infty,\infty)}[T^{1},2n]\geq c_{d}n/2)-\mathbb{P}(T^{1}\geq n+f(n))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X^{2}_{(-\infty,\infty)}(0,n-f(n))\geq c_{d}n/2|0\text{ is cut point})-\mathbb{P}(T^{1}\geq n+f(n))=1-o(1)

if
f(n)=o(n)
and
f(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
with the aid of the estimate in the proof of (
2.1
).
Moreover, we have
\mathbb{P}(D_{2})=1-o(1)
(see
[
13
]
) and for any
\epsilon>0
, there is
C_{1}<\infty
such that
\mathbb{P}(D_{i})\geq 1-\epsilon
for
i=3,4
by the local central limit theorem and the reflection principle for random walks (see
[
15
, Theorem 1.2.1]
).
Hence, if we pick sufficiently large
C_{1}<\infty
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\cap_{i=1}^{4}D_{i})\geq 1/2
.
Let us define the set
A:=S[2n-sn,2n]
and
z:=S_{sn}
.

On the event
D_{1}\cap D_{2}
, we see that we have
\max_{x\in A}\|x-z\|\leq 2C_{1}n^{1/2}
. We now reverse the random walk
S_{0},\ldots,S_{sn}
, to create a new random walk
\tilde{S}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{S}_{sn}
, defined as,

\tilde{S}_{i}:=S_{sn-i}-S_{sn}.

\tilde{S}
is distributed as a random walk starting at the point
z
and, conditional on the value of
z
, it is independent of
A
.

We now seek to compute the probability that the random walk
\tilde{S}
intersects the set
A
. To do this, we derive a last hitting time decomposition of the random walk
\tilde{S}
with the set
A
before time
sn
, by considering the last time of intersection between
\tilde{S}
and the set
A
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{z}(T_{A}<sn)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in A}\sum_{i=1}^{sn}\mathbb{P}^{z}(\tilde{S}_{i}=x)\mathbb{P}^{x}(T_{A}>sn-i)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{x\in A}\sum_{i=1}^{sn}\mathbb{P}^{z}(\tilde{S}_{i}=x)\mathbb{P}^{x}(T_{A}=\infty)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{a\in A}\sum_{i=1}^{sn}\mathbb{P}^{z}(\tilde{S}_{i}=a)\sum_{x\in A}\mathbb{P}^{x}(T_{A}=\infty)

\displaystyle=\min_{a\in A}\sum_{i=1}^{sn}\mathbb{P}^{z}(\tilde{S}_{i}=a){\rm Cap}(A)\geq c(s)n^{-d/2+1}{\rm Cap}(A).

In the last line, we used the local central limit theorem. It is important that
\|x-z\|<Cn^{1/2}
, so for times
\frac{sn}{2}\leq i\leq sn
, we know that
\min_{a\in A}\mathbb{P}^{z}(\tilde{S}_{i}=a)\geq\frac{c}{n^{d/2}}
, for some constant
c
(cf.
[
15
, Theorem 1.2.1]
).

On the event
D_{2}
, we assumed that the capacity of
A
is greater than
sn
. Thus, conditionally on the intersection of the events
D_{2}
,
D_{3}
, and
D_{4}
, we can derive a lower bound on the probability of intersection between
\tilde{S}
(which is also
S[1,sn]
) and A (which is
S[2n-sn,2n]
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S[1,sn]\cap S[2n-sn,2n]\neq\emptyset|\cap_{i=1}^{4}D_{i})\geq c(s)n^{-d/2+2}.

We remark now that on this intersection event, we can derive the following lower bound on the cross term
E_{n}
. First, notice that,

\displaystyle X[n,2n]\geq c_{d}n,

\displaystyle X_{n}\geq 0,

\displaystyle X_{2n}\leq 2sn.

The first line comes from the event
D_{1}
and the second line is trivial.
To see the last line (for the graph distance), notice that
if we let
S_{t_{1}}
and
S_{t_{2}}
be the time indices of the intersection
\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{sn}\}
with
\{S_{2n-sn},\ldots,S_{2n}\}
with
t_{1}\in[0,sn]
and
t_{2}\in[2n-sn,2n]
, the shortest geodesic must be smaller than
t_{1}+(2n-t_{2})
. Namely, shortest geodesic must be shorter than the path
S_{0},\ldots,S_{t_{1}},S_{t_{2}+1},\ldots,S_{2n}
. Now,
t_{1}
and
2n-t_{2}
are both less than
sn
. The same argument applies for the number of cut points. To see how the proof holds for the effective resistance, notice that since the effective resistance decreases upon adding edges, we must have that
X_{2n}
is less than the effective resistance of the graph consisting only of the points
\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{t_{1}}\}\cup\{S_{t_{2}},\ldots,S_{2n}\}
. The effective resistance of this graph is less than
t_{1}+(2n-t_{2})
, the number of points along this graph.

Thus, we have that
E_{n}=X_{n}+X[n+1,2n]-X_{2n}\geq(c_{d}-2s)n
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{n}\geq 2(c_{d}-s)n)\geq\mathbb{P}(\{S[1,sn]\cap S[2n-sn,2n]\neq\emptyset\}\cap(\cap_{i=1}^{4}A_{i}))\geq c(s)n^{-d/2+2}.

∎

Remark 2.3
.

By (
2.1
), we obtain
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]/n\to c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}<\infty
with the aid of Hammersley’s lemma for
d\geq 5
.
Since
\mathbb{P}(S[1,\infty)\cap\tilde{S}[0,\infty)\neq\emptyset)<1
for independent two random walks in
d\geq 5
(e.g., see
[
15
, Theorem 1.2.1]
),
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}]=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mathbb{P}(S[1,i-1]\cap S[i,n]=\emptyset)\gtrsim n.

Hence, we have
c_{0}>0
.

As a consequence, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4

(
d=6
)
.

We have the following estimates for the error terms
E_{n}
.

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]\lesssim(\log n),\quad\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\asymp n.

As a consequence of the first and last inequality above, we have that
\mathrm{Var}(E_{n})\gtrsim n
.

Let
\Omega_{n}
be the event that
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]-E_{n}\geq 0
, then, we have that

(2.5)

\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}])^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{n}]]\lesssim(\log n)^{2}\ll n.

The previous equation implies that the largest contribution to the variance of
E_{n}
occurs when
E_{n}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]
is positive.

Proof.

Most of the previous estimates are a consequence of the layer cake representation of the expectation. For example, we have

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(E_{n}\geq l)\lesssim\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{1}{l}\lesssim(\log n).

In addition, we have

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]=\sum_{l=1}^{n}l\mathbb{P}(E_{n}\geq l)\lesssim\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{1}{l}l\lesssim\sum_{l=1}^{n}1=n.

Finally, we have,

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]=\sum_{l=1}^{n}l\mathbb{P}(E_{n}\geq l)\gtrsim\sum_{l=an}^{bn}l\frac{1}{l}\gtrsim n.

Since
E_{n}\geq 0
, we have that
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]-E_{n}\lesssim\log n
. This gives us the final estimate we desire.
∎

3.
Proof of CLT for
d\geq 7

In this section, we will show the central limit theorem for
d\geq 7
.

3.1.
Proof of variance

In this subsection, we will give the estimate of the variance.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
d\geq 7
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X^{(i)}_{n})\sim\alpha_{d}^{i}n

with
\alpha_{d}^{i}>0
for
i=1,2,3
.

To show it, we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
d\geq 7
, the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X_{n})}{n}

exists.

For simplicity, we write
\overline{X}:=X-\mathbb{E}[X]
.

Proof.

We refer to
[
18
, Lemma 6.2]
.

Define

a_{k}=\sup\left\{\|\overline{X}_{n}\|_{2}:2^{k}\leq n<2^{k+1}\right\},

where
\|X\|_{m}:=\mathbb{E}[|X|^{m}]^{1/m}
for
m\geq 1
.
For
k\geq 2
, let
n
be such that
2^{k}\leq n<2^{k+1}
.
Taking expectations and using the triangle inequality, we obtain

\|\overline{X}_{2n}\|_{2}\leq\|\overline{X}_{n}+\overline{X}[n,2n]\|_{2}+2\max\left(\|E_{n}\|_{2},\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]\right).

Since
X_{n}
and
X[n+1,2n]
are independent, we have

\|\overline{X}_{n}+\overline{X}[n+1,2n]\|_{2}=\left(\|\overline{X}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\overline{X}[n,2n]\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Using (
2.1
) and that
\max\left(\|E_{n}\|_{2},\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]\right)\leq C(\log n)^{M}
for
d\geq 8
and
\max\left(\|E_{n}\|_{2},\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]\right)\leq n^{1/4}
for
d=7
,
we find

\|\overline{X}_{2n}\|_{2}\leq\left(\|\overline{X}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\overline{X}[n,2n]\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}+c_{1}n^{1/4}.

From the definition of
a_{k}
, it follows that

a_{k}\leq\sqrt{2}a_{k-1}+c_{2}2^{k/4},

for some constant
c_{2}
. Setting
b_{k}=\frac{a_{k}}{2^{k/2}}
, we get a recursive inequality
b_{k}\leq b_{k-1}+c_{1}2^{-k/4}
. This implies
a_{k}\lesssim 2^{k/2}
, and hence

\operatorname{Var}(X_{n})\lesssim n.

Letting
\gamma_{n}=\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}])^{2}]
, we have

\gamma_{n+m}\leq\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{m}+\delta_{n+m},\quad\gamma_{n+m}\geq\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{m}-\delta_{n+m}^{\prime},

where
\delta_{n+m},\delta_{n+m}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}((n+m)^{3/4})
.
Indeed, if
n\leq m
and
E_{n,m}:=X_{n}+X[n,n+m]-X_{n+m}
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{n+m}=

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{m}+2\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}])(E_{n,m}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n,m}])]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X[n,n+m]-\mathbb{E}[X[n,n+m]])(E_{n,m}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n,m}])]+\mathbb{E}[(E_{n,m}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n,m}])^{2}].

Then, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality would give,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}])(E_{n,m}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n,m}])]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}])^{2}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[(E_{n,m}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n,m}])^{2}]^{1/2}\lesssim n^{1/2}m^{1/4},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X[n,n+m]-\mathbb{E}[X[n,n+m]])(E_{n,m}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n,m}])]\lesssim m^{1/2}m^{1/4},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(E_{n,m}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n,m}])^{2}]\lesssim m^{1/2}

and hence

\displaystyle\max(\delta_{n+m},\delta_{n+m}^{\prime})\lesssim(m^{1/2}m^{1/4}+m^{1/2}).

Therefore, by Hammersley’s lemma, the existence of the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\gamma_{n}}{n}

follows.
∎

To show that the above limit is positive, we will give a toy model to understand the proof.

Lemma 3.3

(Toy model)
.

We consider i.i.d.
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
such that
\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}=1)=p
for
0<p<1
.
Set
T_{i}=\{s>T_{i-1}:Y_{s}\neq 1\}

and
\hat{G}_{m}=\{l\leq m:T_{l+1}-T_{l}=2\text{ or }3\}
,

G_{n}=\{l:T_{l+1}\leq n,T_{l+1}-T_{l}=2\text{ or }3\}
,

\mathcal{\hat{F}}_{m}:=\sigma((Y_{T_{l}})_{l=1}^{m}\cup\cup_{l\notin\hat{G}_{m}}(Y_{i})_{i=T_{l}+1}^{T_{l+1}-1})
,

\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma((Y_{T_{l}\wedge n})_{l=1}^{\infty}\cup\cup_{l\notin G_{n}}(Y_{i})_{i=T_{l}+1}^{T_{l+1}-1})
.
Set
\hat{D}_{m}:=\{i\in[1,T_{m}]:Y_{i}=1\}
,
D_{n}:=\{i\in[1,n]:Y_{i}=1\}
.
Let
\mathbb{P}_{m}
and
\mathbb{E}_{m}
be the probability and expectation for
(Y_{l})_{l=1}^{T_{m}}
and

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{1}

\displaystyle:=|\{i\in[1,T_{m}]:i-1,i+1\not\in\hat{D}_{m},i\in\hat{D}_{m}\}|,

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{2}

\displaystyle:=|\{i\in[1,T_{m}]:i-1,i+2\not\in\hat{D}_{m},i,i+1\in\hat{D}_{m}\}|.

Moreover, let

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle:=|\{i\in[1,n]:i-1,i+1\not\in D_{n},i\in D_{n}\}|,

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle:=|\{i\in[1,n]:i-1,i+2\not\in D_{n},i,i+1\in D_{n}\}|.

Namely,
N_{1}
denotes the number of events in which
Y_{i}=1
occurs exactly once and
N_{2}
the number of events in which
Y_{i}=1
appears exactly twice in a row.

\mathbb{P}^{n}
denotes the probability for
(Y_{T_{l}\wedge n})_{l=1}^{\infty}
.
For large
M
, there exists
c>0
such that

\displaystyle A_{n}:=\{|N_{1}-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|\leq M\sqrt{n},\quad|N_{2}-\mathbb{E}[N_{2}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}\},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{n})>c>0.

In addition, for
|a-\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{1}]|\leq\sqrt{m}
,
|b-\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{2}]|\leq\sqrt{m}
and
c\leq\sqrt{m}
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{m}(\hat{N}_{1}=a,\hat{N}_{2}=b+c|\mathcal{\hat{F}}_{m})\leq C\mathbb{P}_{m}(\hat{N}_{1}=a+c,\hat{N}_{2}=b|\mathcal{\hat{F}}_{m}).

Eventually, for
|a-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}
,
|b-\mathbb{E}[N_{2}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}(N_{1}=a,N_{2}=b+\sqrt{n})\leq C\mathbb{P}^{n-\sqrt{n}}(N_{1}=a+\sqrt{n},N_{2}=b).

Proof.

It is easy to see that
\mathbb{P}(||G_{n}|-\mathbb{E}[|G_{n}|]|\leq\sqrt{n})>c
.
Since
(T_{l+1}-T_{l})_{l=1}^{m}
is i.i.d. and
\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{1}]=p(1-p)m
,
\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{2}]=p^{2}(1-p)m
, by the central limit theorem for the sum of Bernoulli random variables, there exists
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{m}:=\{|\hat{N}_{1}-\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{1}]|\leq\sqrt{m},\quad|\hat{N}_{2}-\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{2}]|\leq\sqrt{m}\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{A}_{m})>c>0.

We now define
m
to be the largest value of
l
such that
T_{l}\leq n
. We consider values of
m
that satisfy
|m-\mathbb{E}[|G_{n}|]|\leq\sqrt{n}
.
Under these conditions, note that if
|\hat{N}_{1}-\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{1}]|\leq\sqrt{m}
, then

|\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]-\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{1}]|+\sqrt{m}=O(\sqrt{n})

and hence
|N_{1}-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|=|\hat{N}_{1}-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}
for large
M<\infty
.
In addition, if
|\hat{N}_{2}-\mathbb{E}_{m}[\hat{N}_{2}]|\leq\sqrt{m}
, then
|N_{2}-\mathbb{E}[N_{2}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}
for large
M<\infty
.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(||G_{n}|-\mathbb{E}[|G_{n}|]|\leq\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|N_{1}-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|\leq M\sqrt{n},|N_{2}-\mathbb{E}[N_{2}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}\bigg|||G_{n}|-\mathbb{E}[|G_{n}|]|\leq\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\min_{|m-\mathbb{E}[|G_{n}|]|\leq\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(|N_{1}-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|\leq M\sqrt{n},|N_{2}-\mathbb{E}[N_{2}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}\bigg||G_{n}|=m\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\min_{|m-\mathbb{E}[|G_{n}|]|\leq\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{P}_{m}(\hat{A}_{m})\geq c

and hence we obtain (
3.1
).

Noting that
\mathbb{P}_{m}(\cdot|\mathcal{\hat{F}}_{m})
is the conditional probability that specifies the value of the variable
Y_{i}
except at those locations where
Y_{i}
occurs exactly once or twice in a row. Under this conditional probability, we are potentially allowed to change these locations where
Y_{i}
occurs exactly once to those where
Y_{i}
occurs twice in a row or vice versa.
Then, comparing the combinations, we notice that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{m}(\hat{N}_{1}=a,\hat{N}_{2}=b+c|\mathcal{\hat{F}}_{m})}{\mathbb{P}_{m}(\hat{N}_{1}=a+c,\hat{N}_{2}=b|\mathcal{\hat{F}}_{m})}=\frac{(a+b+c)!}{a!(b+c)!}p^{a}p^{2(b+c)}\times\bigg(\frac{(a+b+c)!}{(a+c)!b!}p^{a+c}p^{2b}\bigg)^{-1}.

Hence, Stirling’s formula yields (
3.2
).
Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}^{n}(N_{1}=a,N_{2}=b+\sqrt{n})}{\mathbb{P}^{n-\sqrt{n}}(N_{1}=a+\sqrt{n},N_{2}=b)}=\frac{(a+b+\sqrt{n})!}{a!(b+\sqrt{n})!}p^{a}p^{2(b+\sqrt{n})}\times\bigg(\frac{(a+b+\sqrt{n})!}{(a+\sqrt{n})!b!}p^{a+\sqrt{n}}p^{2b}\bigg)^{-1}

and Stirling’s formula yields (
3.3
).
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

It holds that

.

Proof.

Set
D:=\{i\in[0,n/2]:S_{2i}=S_{2i+2}=S_{2i+1}+e\}

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle:=|\{i\in[1,n/2]:i-1,i+1\not\in D,i\in D\}|,

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle:=|\{i\in[1,n/2]:i-1,i+2\not\in D,i,i+1\in D\}|,

\mathbb{P}^{n}
denotes the probability for
(Y_{T_{l}\wedge(n/2)})_{l=1}^{\infty}
.
By (

), there exists
c_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle A_{n}:=\{|N_{1}-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|\leq M\sqrt{n},\quad|N_{2}-\mathbb{E}[N_{2}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}\},

In addition, by (

), for any

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}(X_{n}=d,A_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|\leq M\sqrt{n},\\
|b-\mathbb{E}[N_{2}]|\leq M\sqrt{n}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}^{n}(X_{n}=d,N_{1}=a,N_{2}=b+\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle c_{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a-\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]|\leq M\sqrt{n},\\

Now, assume for contradiction that for any

, as

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\overline{X}_{n}|\leq\epsilon\sqrt{n})\to 1.

Since

, we have
\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\mathbb{E}X_{n}-\mathbb{E}X_{n-\sqrt{n}})/\sqrt{n}\geq c_{3}
for some

.

,
then

for large

and hence
a_{m}\leq c_{0}m^{2}/2
for large

if we pick small
c_{3}
.
We now pick a subsequence

such that

and choose

.
Then,

\displaystyle c_{1}/2\leq

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{2}\mathbb{P}(X_{n-\sqrt{n}}\geq\mathbb{E}X_{n}-\epsilon\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{2}\mathbb{P}(X_{n-\sqrt{n}}\geq\mathbb{E}X_{n-\sqrt{n}}+c_{3}\sqrt{n}/2).

).

.

3.2.
Proof of CLT

Lemma 3.5
.

For
d\geq 7
and for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[(\overline{X}_{n})^{2+\epsilon}]\lesssim n^{1+\epsilon/2}.

Proof.

Define

a_{k}=\sup\left\{\|\overline{X}_{n}\|_{2+\epsilon}:2^{k}\leq n<2^{k+1}\right\}.

Fix
k\geq 2
, and let
n
satisfy

. Then, by the triangle inequality,

\|\overline{X}_{2n}\|_{2+\epsilon}\leq\|\overline{X}_{n}+\overline{X}[n,2n]\|_{2+\epsilon}+\|E_{n}\|_{2+\epsilon},

Using the inequality

\displaystyle\|\overline{X}_{n}+\overline{X}[n,2n]\|_{2+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{n}|^{2+\epsilon}]+\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}[n,2n]|^{2+\epsilon}]+C\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{n}|^{1+\epsilon}]\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}[n,2n]|]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+C\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{n}|]\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}[n,2n]|^{1+\epsilon}]\big)^{1/(2+\epsilon)},

and (
2.1
) and Lemma
3.2
, which gives
\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{n}|^{1+\epsilon}]\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}[n,2n]|]\lesssim n^{1+\epsilon/2}
, we obtain

\|\overline{X}_{2n}\|_{2+\epsilon}\leq\left(\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}_{n}^{2+\epsilon}]+\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}[n,2n]^{2+\epsilon}]\right)^{1/(2+\epsilon)}+Cn^{1/2}.

From the definition of

a_{k}\leq 2^{1/(2+\epsilon)}a_{k-1}+c_{2}\cdot(2^{k})^{1/2},

for some constant

b_{k}\leq b_{k-1}+c_{2}2^{-k/(2+\epsilon)}(2^{k})^{1/2},

which implies that
(b_{k})\lesssim 2^{-k/(2+\epsilon)}(2^{k})^{1/2}

a_{k}\lesssim 2^{k/2}

\left(\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}_{n}^{2+\epsilon}]\right)^{1/(2+\epsilon)}\lesssim\sqrt{n}.

Proof of Theorem 1.1.

We decompose
X_{n}
as follows:

X_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}\tilde{X}_{(L,i)}+\mathcal{E},

where
\tilde{X}_{(L,i)}:=X[(i-1)n2^{-L},in2^{-L}]
and
\mathcal{E}:=-\sum_{k=0}^{L}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}E_{n}^{(k,l)}
.
Set
L=\lfloor\frac{1}{4}\log_{2}n\rfloor
so that
2^{L}=n^{1/4}
. Then,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{E}|]}{\sqrt{n}}\lesssim\frac{n^{1/4}\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Hence, it suffices to prove that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}\left(\tilde{X}_{(L,i)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(L,i)}]\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha_{d}).

We now apply the Lindeberg–Feller central limit theorem.
We write
Z_{L,i}:=\tilde{X}_{(L,i)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(L,i)}]
.

We verify the Lindeberg–Feller conditions:

(i) Variance Condition.
By Lemma 3.3, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_{(L,i)})\sim 2^{L}\cdot\alpha_{d}\cdot\frac{n}{2^{L}}=\alpha_{d}n,

hence

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{X}_{(L,i)})\to\alpha_{d}>0.

(ii) Lindeberg Condition.
For any
\epsilon>0
, we must show that as
n\to\infty
,

\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{L,i}|^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z_{L,i}|>\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\right]\to 0.

Using Hölder and Chebyshev’s inequality:

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{L,i}|^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z_{L,i}|>\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[|Z_{L,i}|^{2+\delta}]^{2/(2+\delta)}\mathbb{P}(|Z_{L,i}|\geq\epsilon\sqrt{n})^{\delta/(2+\delta)}.

By Lemma
3.5
,

\mathbb{E}[|Z_{L,i}|^{2+\delta}]^{2/(2+\delta)}\lesssim\frac{n}{2^{L}}.

Furthermore, Chebyshev’s inequality should give,

\mathbb{P}(|Z_{L,i}|>\epsilon\sqrt{n})\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}_{L,i})}{\epsilon^{2}n}\lesssim\frac{n}{\epsilon^{2}n(2^{L})}=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}(2^{L})}.

Thus, we have,

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{L,i}|^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z_{L,i}|>\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\right]\leq\frac{n}{(\epsilon^{2})^{\delta/(2+\delta)}(2^{L})^{1+\delta/(2+\delta)}}.

Finally, we can sum up all of the appropriate terms and see that,

\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{L,i}|^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z_{L,i}|>\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\right]\lesssim 2^{L}\frac{1}{n}\frac{n}{(\epsilon^{2})^{\delta/(2+\delta)}(2^{L})^{1+\frac{\delta}{(2+\delta)}}}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
and
2^{L}\to\infty
.

Therefore, both Lindeberg–Feller conditions are satisfied, and we conclude

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}(\tilde{X}_{(L,i)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(L,i)}])}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha_{d})

as
n\to\infty
and
2^{L}\to\infty
and hence combining this with (
3.5
), we see that

\displaystyle\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}X_{n}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Now, we produce an upper bound to the variance of the graph distance.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have the following upper bound on the variance of
X_{n}
:

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\lesssim n(\log n).

Proof.

We now perform the full binary decomposition as,

X_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}n}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}-E_{n}^{(k,l)}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}X[k,k+1].

We define the error term

\mathcal{E}_{k}:=\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}-E_{n}^{(k,l)}.

By independence of the components
E_{n}^{(k,l)}
, we have that,

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E}_{k})=\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathrm{Var}(E_{n}^{(k,l)})\lesssim\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\frac{n}{2^{k}}=n.

Thus we have, by expanding the variance and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, that

(4.1)

\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}n}\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}n}\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E}_{k})+2\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\log_{2}n}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{\log_{2}n}\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E}_{k_{1}})^{1/2}\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E}_{k_{2}})^{1/2}\lesssim n(\log n)^{2}+n.

In addition, since each
X[k,k+1]
is deterministically 1, we have that
\mathrm{Var}(X[k,k+1])=0
.
Then, we have

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\lesssim n(\log n)^{2}.

By our binary decomposition, we find that, for
a_{n}=n/(\log n)^{3}

X_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{a_{n}}X[(l-1)\frac{n}{a_{n}},l\frac{n}{a_{n}}]+\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}(a_{n})}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}(-E_{n}^{(k,l)}).

Now, we claim that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]\lesssim(\log n)^{2}.

Let
\tilde{S}
be the reversal of the first part, that is
\tilde{S}_{i}^{(k,l)}=S_{l\frac{n}{2^{k}}-i}-S_{l\frac{n}{2^{k}}}
.
\hat{S}
will be the second part, that is
\hat{S}_{i}^{(k,l)}=S_{l\frac{n}{2^{k}}+i}-S_{l\frac{n}{2^{k}}}
.
Indeed, if
E_{n}^{(k,l)}\geq a
,

\Omega(a,k,l)
holds, where

\displaystyle\Omega(a,k,l):=

\displaystyle\bigcup\{\tilde{S}^{(k,l)}[a/2,\frac{n}{2^{k}}]\cap\hat{S}^{(k,l)}[0,\frac{n}{2^{k}}]\neq\emptyset\}(=:A_{2}^{(a,k,l)}).

Then,

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]=\sum_{a,b}\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}\geq a]\mathbbm{1}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}\geq b]]\leq\sum_{a,b}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}^{(a,k_{1},l_{1})}\cap A_{j}^{(b,k_{2},l_{2})}).

In addition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{1}^{(a,k_{1},l_{1})}\cap A_{1}^{(b,k_{2},l_{2})})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\{\hat{S}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}[\frac{a}{2},\frac{n}{2^{k_{1}}}]\cap\tilde{S}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}[0,\frac{n}{2^{k_{1}}}]\neq\emptyset\}\cap\{\hat{S}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}[\frac{b}{2},\frac{n}{2^{k_{2}}}]\cap\tilde{S}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}[0,\frac{n}{2^{k_{2}}}]\neq\emptyset\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{u_{1}=\frac{a}{2}+l_{1}\frac{n}{2^{k_{1}}}}^{(l_{1}+1)\frac{n}{2^{k_{1}}}}\sum_{u_{2}=(l_{1}-1)\frac{n}{2^{k_{1}}}}^{l_{1}\frac{n}{2^{k_{1}}}}\sum_{u_{3}=\frac{b}{2}+l_{2}\frac{n}{2^{k_{2}}}}^{(l_{2}+1)\frac{n}{2^{k_{2}}}}\sum_{u_{4}=(l_{2}-1)\frac{n}{2^{k_{2}}}}^{l_{2}\frac{n}{2^{k_{2}}}}\sum_{x}\sum_{y}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{u_{1}}=S_{u_{2}}=x,S_{u_{3}}=S_{u_{4}}=y).

By

letting

for

we have that if

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b}\mathbb{P}(A_{1}^{(a,k_{1},l_{1})}\cap A_{1}^{(b,k_{2},l_{2})})

\displaystyle\lesssim

where

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\log_{2}(a_{n})}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{2^{k_{1}}}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+1}^{\log_{2}(a_{n})}\sum_{l_{2}=0}^{2^{k_{2}-k_{1}}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\log_{2}(a_{n})}2^{k_{1}}[a_{n}/2^{k_{1}}](\log n)^{2}\leq a_{n}(\log n)^{3}\ll n(\log n).

\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{a_{n}}\mathcal{E}_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}n}\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E}_{k})+2\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\log_{2}(a_{n})}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{2^{k_{1}}}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+1}^{\log_{2}(a_{n})}\sum_{l_{2}=0}^{2^{k_{2}-k_{1}}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]\lesssim n(\log n)

\mathrm{Var}(MT)\leq Ca_{n}[n/a_{n}(\log(n/a_{n}))^{2}]\leq Cn(\log\log n)^{2}.

Finally, we can apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to say that,

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\leq\mathrm{Var}(MT)+\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E})+2\mathrm{Var}(MT)^{1/2}\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E})^{1/2}\lesssim n(\log n).

This completes the proof of the assertion.

We start with the following lower bound.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists
0<c<\infty
such that

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\geq cn\log n.

Proof.

Fix
\delta>0
very close to 1. We will see where this condition plays a role close to the end of the proof.

By our binary decomposition, we find that,

(4.2)

X_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{n^{\delta}}X[(l-1)n^{1-\delta},ln^{1-\delta}]+\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}(-E_{n}^{(k,l)}).

We will write

MT:=\sum_{l=1}^{n^{\delta}}X[(l-1)n^{1-\delta},ln^{1-\delta}],\quad\mathcal{E}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}(-E_{n}^{(k,l)}).

We have that

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})=\mathrm{Var}(MT)+\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E})+2\text{Cov}(MT,\mathcal{E})

and

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E})=\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathrm{Var}(-E_{n}^{(k,l)})+\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{2^{k_{1}}}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{2^{k_{2}}}\text{Cov}(-E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})},-E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}).

In addition, we obtain that, for sufficiently large
n
,

\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathrm{Var}(-E_{n}^{(k,l)})\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}c\frac{n}{2^{k}}\geq c\delta n\log n.

By Lemma
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(-E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})},-E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})})=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]+\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]\geq-(\log n)^{2}.

Furthermore, we remark that if the intervals
[n/2^{k_{1}+1}(2l_{1}-2),n/2^{k_{1}+1}(2l_{1})]
(this is the interval corresponding to the cross term
E^{(k_{1},l_{1})}
) and
[n/2^{k_{2}+1}(2l_{2}-2),n/2^{k_{2}+1}(2l_{2})]
(this is the interval corresponding to
E^{(k_{2},l_{2})}
) are completely disjoint from each other, then
E^{(k_{1},l_{1})}
and
E^{(k_{2},l_{2})}
are independent of each other and the covariance is
0
. Using these considerations, we see that a lower bound for the covariances is the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{2^{k_{1}}}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+1}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}\sum_{l_{2}=0}^{2^{k_{2}-k_{1}}}-(\log n)^{2}\geq-\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{\log_{2}(n^{\delta})}2^{k_{1}}[n^{\delta}/2^{k_{1}}](\log n)^{2}

\displaystyle=-n^{\delta}(\log n)^{3}\gg-n(\log n),\text{ if }\delta<1.

Furthermore, notice that by Lemma
4.1
, we have the upper bound
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\leq Cn\log n
. Thus, we can show that

\mathrm{Var}(MT)\leq Cn^{\delta}[n^{1-\delta}\log(n^{1-\delta})]\leq C(1-\delta)n\log n,

by setting
\delta
sufficiently close to
1
, this can be made very small.

Finally, we can apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to say that,

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\geq\mathrm{Var}(MT)+\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E})-2\mathrm{Var}(MT)^{1/2}\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E})^{1/2}.

Then for
\delta
close to but not equal to
1

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E})\geq cn\log n
and
\mathrm{Var}(MT)\leq C(1-\delta)n\log n
. Thus,
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\gtrsim n\log n
, as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Part 1: A CLT contribution

Define the value

Now,

\mathcal{G}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\delta}}X[(i-1)n/n_{\delta},in/n_{\delta}]-\mathbb{E}[X[(i-1)n/{n_{\delta}},in/n_{\delta}]]

Z_{n_{\delta},i}:=\frac{X[(i-1)n/n_{\delta},in/n_{\delta}]-\mathbb{E}[X[(i-1)n/n_{\delta},in/n_{\delta}]]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n/n_{\delta}})}}.

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\delta}}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{n_{\delta},i}^{2}\right]=1.

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\delta}}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n_{\delta},i}^{2}\mathbbm{1}[|Z_{n_{\delta},i}|\geq\epsilon]]=0,

Part 2: Controlling the error term at mesoscopic scales

Define
n_{\delta,-}:=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\log n-(\sqrt{\log n})^{1-\delta}\right]

\mathcal{M}:=\sum_{k=\log_{2}n_{\delta,-}}^{\log_{2}n_{\delta}}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}(-E_{n}^{(k,l)}+\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]).

Since at a given level

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}(-E_{n}^{(k,l)}+\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}])\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}[(-E_{n}^{(k,l)}+\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}])^{2}]\lesssim\frac{n}{2^{k}}2^{k}=n.

Thus, we see that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}^{2}]\leq 4n(\sqrt{\log n})^{2-2\delta}
.
Hence, by Markov’s inequality, we have that

(4.7)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}\geq\sqrt{n(\log n)^{1-\delta/2}})\to 0

as
n\to\infty
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Part 3: Controlling the error terms at the smallest scales

We let
\Omega_{k,l}
be the event that,

\Omega_{k,l}:=\left\{|E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]|\ \leq\left(\frac{n}{2^{k}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}\right\}.

k

\Omega_{k,l}

Define
\Omega_{k}:=\bigcap_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\Omega_{k,l}

Thus, we see that when
2^{k}\leq n_{\delta,-}\leq n^{1/2}
, by (
2.5
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{k}]\right]=\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}])^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{k,l}]]

\displaystyle+\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k,l_{1})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l_{1})}])\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{k,l_{1}}]]\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}])\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{k,l_{2}}]]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\left(\frac{n}{2^{k}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}+(2^{k})^{2}(\log n)^{2}=n^{1-\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}(2^{k})^{\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}+n_{\delta,-}^{2}(\log n)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n^{1-\frac{1}{2(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}+\exp[\log n-\sqrt{\log n}^{1-\delta}+2\log\log n].

In addition, we also have that when
2^{k}\leq n_{\delta,-}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{k}^{c})\leq\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{k,l}^{c})\leq\frac{2^{k}}{\left(\frac{n}{2^{k}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(2^{k})^{2-\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}}{n^{1-\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}}\leq\frac{\exp\left[\left(\frac{\log n}{2}-\sqrt{\log n}^{1-\delta}\right)\left(2-\frac{1}{(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}\right)\right]}{\exp[\log n-(\sqrt{\log n})^{1-2\delta}]}\leq\exp[-(\sqrt{\log n})^{1-\delta}],

for
n
sufficiently large.

If we now define
\Omega=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\log_{2}n_{\delta,-}}\Omega_{k}
, then we see that,

\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{c})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\log_{2}n_{\delta,-}}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{k}^{c})\leq\log n\exp[-(\sqrt{\log n})^{1-\delta}]\ll 1.

Furthermore, if we define

\mathcal{L}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}n_{\delta,-}}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}(E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]),

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\log_{2}n_{\delta,-}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k_{1}}}E_{n}^{(k_{1},l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l)}]\right)\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k_{2}}}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l)}]\right)\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{\log_{2}n_{\delta,-}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k_{1}}}E_{n}^{(k_{1},l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l)}]\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{k_{1}}]\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k_{2}}}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l)}]\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{k_{2}}]\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq(\log n)^{2}n^{1-\frac{1}{2(\sqrt{\log n})^{1+2\delta}}}+(\log n)^{2}\exp[\log n-\sqrt{\log n}^{1-\delta}+4\log\log n]\ll n.

By using Markov’s inequality, we see that,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}n_{\delta,-}}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}(E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]>\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{c})+n^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\log_{2}n_{\delta,-}}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}(E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]\right]\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
.

Recall that
\mathcal{G}
from Part 1 was a Gaussian random variable and the order of the variance of
\mathcal{G}
was
n\log n
, which is much larger than the error terms of Parts 2 and 3. Then, combining Part 1, 2, 3, we can obtain the desired result using the Lindeberg–Feller central limit theorem.

4.2.2.
Effective resistance

Next, we will give the proof of Theorem
1.4
for
d=6
. We present only the necessary adjustments from the proof of Theorem
1.2
. This involves changing Parts 1 and 4, while keeping Parts 2 and 3.
We also remark that the results of this section only depend on the tail behavior of the cross terms
E_{n}
behaving like
\mathbb{P}(E_{n}\geq l)\asymp\frac{1}{l}
. In particular, the results are general enough to not require any estimate on the correlation between the cross terms. Thus, the methods of this section are of general interest and can potentially be applied to other heavy tailed statistics of the random walk range.

We now define the function
f(n)
as,

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})=n(\log n)^{f(n)}.

Part 1: A CLT contribution

Define the value
n_{\delta}:=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\log n+(\sqrt{\log n})^{1-\delta}\right]
.
Now,

\mathcal{G}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\delta}}X[(i-1)n/n_{\delta},in/n_{\delta}]-\mathbb{E}[X[(i-1)n/{n_{\delta}},in/n_{\delta}]]

will be a normal random variable of variance
n(\log(n/n_{\delta}))^{f(n/n_{\delta})}
, provided
f(n/n_{\delta})\geq 0.

Define

Z_{n_{\delta},i}:=\frac{X[(i-1)n/n_{\delta},in/n_{\delta}]-\mathbb{E}[X[(i-1)n/n_{\delta},in/n_{\delta}]]}{\sqrt{n(\log n/n_{\delta})^{f(n/n_{\delta})}}}.

The variables
Z_{n_{\delta},i}
are all independent of each other and one

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\delta}}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{n_{\delta},i}^{2}\right]=1.

Furthermore, we have that,

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\delta}}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n_{\delta},i}^{2}\mathbbm{1}[|Z_{n_{\delta},i}|\geq\epsilon]]=0,

since deterministically, we have that
X[(i-1)n/n_{n_{\delta}},in/n_{\delta}]\leq n/n_{\delta}\ll\sqrt{n(\log n/n_{\delta})^{f(n/n_{\delta})}}
, provided
f(n^{1/2-\delta})\geq 0
.

Part 4: Nontriviality of the Limit

We will consider those values of
n
such that
f(n/n_{\delta})\geq 1-\delta/4
(by Lemma
4.2
, this should happen infinitely often.) We will divide the analysis into two cases.

Case 1:
f(n/n_{\delta})\geq 1-\delta/4
and
f(n)\leq f(n/n_{\delta})+\frac{1}{\log\log n}
happen infinitely often together.

Then, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})=n(\log n)^{f(n/n_{\delta})}(\log n)^{f(n)-f(n/n_{\delta})}\leq n(\log(n/n_{\delta})^{3})^{f(n/n_{\delta})}\log n^{\frac{1}{\log\log n}}

\displaystyle\leq 27en(\log(n/n_{\delta}))^{f(n/n_{\delta})}.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is Gaussian with variance
n(\log(n/n_{\delta}))^{f(n/n_{\delta})}
, with constant probability, it will be greater than
\sqrt{n(\log(n/n_{\delta}))^{f(n/n_{\delta})}}
. Equations (
4.8
) and (
4.7
) show that the remaining error terms will not be larger than
\epsilon\sqrt{n(\log n)^{1-\delta/2}}
with high probability. Thus, on a set of non-vanishing probability, we must have that as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{\mathcal{M}+\mathcal{L}}{\sqrt{n(\log(n/n_{\delta}))^{f(n/n_{\delta})}}}\geq\epsilon\bigg)\to 0

for any
\epsilon>0
.
Thus,
\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}}
converges to
\mathcal{N}(0,a)
.

Case 2:
f(n/n_{\delta})\geq 1-\delta/4
and
f(n)\leq f(n/n_{\delta})+\frac{1}{\log\log n}
do not happen infinitely often together.

Then there exists a sequence
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k},\ldots
such that
\frac{m_{k+1}}{(m_{k+1})_{\delta}}=m_{k},\forall k
and
f(m_{k+1})-f(m_{k})\geq\frac{1}{\log\log m_{k+1}}
. (Notice that by Lemma
4.2
, we will always be able to find elements such that
f(n/n_{\delta})\geq 1-\delta/4
, so we do not need to worry about the possibility that
f(n/n_{\delta})\leq 1-\delta/4
for all large
n
.)

The relationship
m_{k}=\frac{m_{k+1}}{(m_{k+1})_{\delta}}
will imply that
m_{k+1}\leq(m_{k})^{3}
. Thus,
m_{k+1}\leq(m_{1})^{3^{k}}
.
Therefore, we have that

f(m_{k+1})-f(m_{k})\geq\frac{1}{\log\log m_{k+1}}\geq\frac{1}{(k+1)\log 4},

for all
k
large enough.
This will imply that
\lim_{k\to\infty}f(m_{k})=\infty
. This contradicts the upper bound on
f
from Lemma
4.1
.

Thus, we see that this proof establishes that there is a subsequence where

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}},

does not converge to
0
. Moreover, along this subsequence
X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]
will be distributed (after appropriate normalization) as a normal random variable whose variance is given by
n(\log(n/n_{\delta}))^{f(n/n_{\delta})}
.
∎

Lemma 5.1
.

Proof.

Fix some small

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}:=\{((k_{1},l_{1}),(k_{2},l_{2})):[2^{-k_{1}}(l_{1}-1)n,2^{-k_{1}}l_{1}n]\cap[2^{-k_{2}}l_{2}n,2^{-k_{2}}(l_{2}+1)n]\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\sum_{((k_{1},l_{1}),(k_{2},l_{2}))\in\Lambda_{2}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]=\sum_{((k_{1},l_{1}),(k_{2},l_{2}))\in\Lambda_{2}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}],

we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E})\geq\sum_{k=0}^{(1-\alpha)\log_{2}n}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k,l)})^{2}]-\sum_{((k_{1},l_{1}),(k_{2},l_{2}))\in\Lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}].

By (

\sum_{k=0}^{(1-\alpha)\log_{2}n}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k,l)})^{2}]\geq cn^{3/2}
.

\displaystyle\sum_{((k_{1},l_{1}),(k_{2},l_{2}))\in\Lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]\leq n^{3/2-\alpha/2+o(1)}.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum_{((k_{1},l_{1}),(k_{2},l_{2}))\in\Lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(\log n)^{2}\sup_{\alpha\leq\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}\leq 1}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}:((1-\beta_{1})\log_{2}n,l_{1})((1-\beta_{2})\log_{2}n,l_{2})\in\Lambda_{1}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{((1-\beta_{1})\log_{2}n,l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{{(1-\beta_{2})}\log_{2}n,l_{2})}]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(\log n)^{2}\sup_{\alpha\leq\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}\leq 1}n^{\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}}n^{1-\beta_{2}}n^{\beta_{2}/2}n^{\beta_{1}/2}\leq n^{3/2-\alpha/2+o(1)}.

.
Now by the same argument as that of Lemma

, we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{(1-\alpha)\log_{2}n}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{2^{k_{1}}}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+1}^{(1-\alpha)\log_{2}n}\sum_{l_{2}=0}^{2^{k_{2}-k_{1}}}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]\leq n^{3/2-\alpha/2+o(1)}.

.

If we let

1.2
and

6.1.
Lower bounds for the Cross Term

We will first start by obtaining a lower bound of
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}].
Our heuristic argument is the following: if there is an intersection between
S[3n/2,2n]
and
S[0,n]
, then this will form a shortcut in the graph distance between the points
S_{2n}
and
S_{0}
. The amount that the graph distance would reduce via this shortcut will be related to the graph distance between
S_{n}
and
S_{2n}

O\left(\frac{n}{(\log n)^{1/2}}\right)
. Furthermore, the probability of the intersection of
S[3n/2,2n]
and
S[0,n]
is approximately
O((\log n)^{-1})

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]=\frac{n}{(\log n)^{3/2\pm o(1)}}
.

However, the exact value of the reduction of the graph distance is a complicated function of the loop intersection structure of the two walks. Thus, knowing that there is a long-range intersection may not be enough the deduce the reduction of the graph distance. Furthermore, to get the exact order
\frac{n}{(\log n)^{3/2}}
, that we desire, then we would need to argue that there is exact independence between the event that there is an intersection between
S[3n/2,2n]
and
S[0,n]
and the event that the graph distance between
S_{n}
and
S_{2n}
is
O\left(\frac{n}{(\log n)^{1/2}}\right)
. The following lemma will address these issues by finding appropriate events that will allow us to constrain the geometry of the random walk range.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
E_{n}
denote the cross term that appears when considering the number of cut points, the effective resistance or the graph distance.

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]\geq\frac{n}{(\log n)^{3/2+o(1)}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\hat{S}_{n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}}(\tau<n/(\log n)^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}^{\hat{S}_{n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}}(\hat{S}_{i+n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}=a)\mathbb{P}^{a}(\tau>n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}-i)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}^{\hat{S}_{n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}}(\hat{S}_{i+n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}=a)\mathbb{P}^{a}(\tau=\infty)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\min_{\hat{a}\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}\mathbb{P}^{\hat{S}_{n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}}(\hat{S}_{i+n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}=\hat{a})\right)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}^{a}(\tau=\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\min_{\hat{a}\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}\mathbb{P}^{\hat{S}_{n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}}(\hat{S}_{i+n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}=\hat{a})\right){\rm Cap}(\mathcal{S}).

On the union
A_{5}\cap B_{2}
, we can apply the local central limit theorem to deduce that
\left(\min_{\hat{a}\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}\mathbb{P}^{\hat{S}_{n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}}(\hat{S}_{i+n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}=\hat{a})\right)\geq cn^{-1}(\log n)^{-\epsilon}
, for some constant
c>0
on
\mathcal{Q}
. Furthermore, the event
A_{5}
ensures that
{\rm Cap}(\mathcal{S})\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\frac{n}{(\log n)^{1+\epsilon}}
on
\mathcal{Q}
. Multiplying these together, we see that there is some
c>0
such that
\mathbb{P}^{\hat{S}_{n-n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}}}(\tau<n/(\log n)^{\epsilon})>c/(\log n)^{1+2\epsilon}
on
\mathcal{Q}
. This means that
\mathbb{P}(B_{3}|B_{2}\cap\mathcal{Q})\geq c/(\log n)^{1+2\epsilon}
for some
c>0
. Returning to equation (
6.1
), we see that we see that we must have
\mathbb{P}(B_{1}\cap B_{2}\cap B_{3}\cap\mathcal{Q})\geq c/(\log n)^{1+2\epsilon}
for some
c>0
.

Now, on the event
B_{1}\cap B_{2}\cap B_{3}\cap\mathcal{Q}
, we claim we have
E_{n}>n/(\log n)^{1/2+\epsilon/2}
.
Due to the presence of the cut points from the event
A_{1}
and the lower bound on the graph distance
\tilde{X}_{n/2}
from
A_{2}
, we have that
\tilde{X}_{n}\geq\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\mathbb{E}[X_{n/2}]-\frac{n}{(\log n)^{7/12}}
.
By events
A_{3}
,
A_{1}
, and
B_{1}
, the graph distance between the points
\hat{S}_{n}
and
\tilde{S}_{n}
, is at most
2n/(\log n)^{1/2+\epsilon/2}+2\frac{n}{(\log n)^{7/12}}
. Computing the difference, we now see that
E_{n}>n/(\log n)^{1/2+\epsilon/2}
on
B_{1}\cap B_{2}\cap B_{3}\cap\mathcal{Q}
as desired and this event happens with probability
c/(\log n)^{1+2\epsilon}
. By multiplying these two terms together, we see that
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]>n/(\log n)^{3/2+\epsilon/2+2\epsilon}
.
This proof also works even for the effective resistance or the number of cut points.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

There exists a set
\Omega
with probability
\text{O}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
such that we have

E_{n}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega^{c}]\ll\mathbb{E}[E_{n}].

Proof.

Recall the notation
\hat{S}
and
\tilde{S}
from the proof of Lemma
6.1
. Consider the event

\displaystyle\Omega:=

\displaystyle\{\hat{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\tilde{S}[0,n]\neq\emptyset\}\bigcup\{\tilde{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\hat{S}[0,n]\neq\emptyset\}

\displaystyle\bigcup\left\{\sum_{i\in\left[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2}}\right]}1\{\tilde{S}[0,i-1]\cap\tilde{S}[i,n]=\emptyset\}=0\right\}

\displaystyle\bigcup\left\{\sum_{i\in\left[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2}}\right]}1\{\hat{S}[0,i-1]\cap\hat{S}[i,n]=\emptyset\}=0\right\}.

The first two events that compose
\Omega
ensure that there exists some long-range intersection between
\tilde{S}
and
\hat{S}
. The third and fourth events force there to be no cut points in
\tilde{S}
between
n/(\log n)^{4}
and
n/(\log n)^{2}
.

We claim both that
\Omega
holds with probability
O\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
and that on the event
\Omega^{c}
, we must have that the cross term
E_{n}
for number of cut points, the effective resistance, and the graph distance must by less than
\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2}}\ll\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]
by Lemma
6.1
.

By
[
15
, Theorem 4.3.6 part(ii)]
, we must have that
\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\tilde{S}[0,n]\neq\emptyset)=\text{O}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
. Similarly, by
[
11
, equation (7)]
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in\left[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2}}\right]}1\{\tilde{S}[0,i-1]\cap\tilde{S}[i,n]=\emptyset\}\right)=\text{O}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right).

Combining these bounds gives us that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega)\leq\text{O}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
, as desired.

Now, we will check that on
\Omega^{c}
, our cross term can be no more than
\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2}}
.
Notice that on
\Omega^{c}
, there must be some cut point in the interval
\left[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2}}\right]
for both
\tilde{S}
and
\hat{S}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{\tilde{S}}
denote the time instance of the cut point of
\tilde{S}
(cut time) in this interval and let
\mathcal{C}_{\hat{S}}
denote the time instance of the cut point of
\hat{S}
in this interval. Furthermore, since there is no intersection between
\hat{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]
and
\tilde{S}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\hat{S}}
must still be a cut point in the full graph
\hat{S}\cup\tilde{S}.

Notice that we have
\hat{X}_{n}=\hat{X}[0,\mathcal{C}_{\hat{S}}]+\hat{X}[\mathcal{C}_{\hat{S}},n]\leq\hat{X}[\mathcal{C}_{\hat{S}},n]+n/(\log n)^{2}.

Similarly, we have
\tilde{X}_{n}\leq\tilde{X}[\mathcal{C}_{\tilde{S}},n]+n/(\log n)^{2}.
Lastly, we see that
X_{2n}\geq\hat{X}[\mathcal{C}_{\hat{S}},n]+\tilde{X}[\mathcal{C}_{\tilde{S}},n]
. Altogether, we see that on
\Omega^{c}
, we have
E_{n}=\tilde{X}_{n}+\hat{X}_{n}-X_{2n}\leq\frac{2n}{(\log n)^{2}}\ll\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

If one is interested in the graph distance or the number of cut points, then we can proceed to more simply define
\Omega
by considering only its first line from its definition in Lemma
6.3
. We will use this fact later in the proof of Lemma
6.8
.

By applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain the following lower bound on
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]
as a corollary.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
E_{n}
denote the cross term that appears when computing the number of cut points, the effective resistance, or the graph distance. Then, we have,

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\geq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2+o(1)}}.

Proof.

Let
\Omega
be the set from Lemma
6.3
.
Note that by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have that,

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\mathbb{P}(\Omega)\geq\mathbb{E}[E_{n}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]]^{2}\geq(1-o(1))\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]^{2}.

Since
\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=O\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
, we can take ratios and deduce that
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\geq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2+o(1)}}
, as desired.
∎

6.2.
Upper bounds for the Cross terms

Our proof of the upper bound for
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]
requires more steps. Indeed, unlike our proof of the lower bound, we cannot establish our upper bound directly. However, we will first show that if we have the inequality
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\leq\frac{Cn^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}+(\log n)^{-\epsilon}\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})
, this will be enough to show that
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2-o(1)}}
and ultimately that
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2-o(1)}}
.

6.2.1.
Our first reduction

The goal of this section is to establish our reduction that showing
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\leq\frac{Cn^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}+(\log n)^{-\epsilon}\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})
is enough to establish
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}],\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2+o(1)}}
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
X_{n}
denote the number of cut points or the graph distance.
Assume that there is some
\epsilon>0
such that for all
n
, we have

(6.2)

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\leq\frac{Cn^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}+(\log n)^{-\epsilon}\mathrm{Var}(X_{n}).

Then, we must have that,

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2-o(1)}},\quad\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2-o(1)}}.

Proof.

Fix any
\kappa>0
, we will first show that for
n
large enough, we must have that,

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2-\kappa}}.

Let us write

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})=\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}.

We will use the dyadic decomposition to find an equation for
f(n)
. We see that,

\mathrm{Var}(X_{2n})=\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})+\mathrm{Var}(X_{[n,2n]})+\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{E}_{n})+\text{Cov}(\mathcal{E}_{n},X_{n}+X_{[n,2n]}).

We then have that using the previous expansion on the variance and equation (
6.2
) that,

\displaystyle\frac{(2n)^{2}}{(\log(2n))^{f(2n)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}+\frac{Cn^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}+(\log n)^{-\epsilon}\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{(\log n)^{-\epsilon}n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}+\frac{Cn^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{2n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}}.

We can bound the right-hand side as,

\displaystyle\frac{2n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}+\frac{Cn^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}+\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)+\epsilon}}+C\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)+\epsilon/2}}+C\frac{n^{2}\sqrt{\log\log n}}{(\log n)^{f(n)/2+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2+\delta)n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}+C\frac{n^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}},

where
\delta>0
can be set to be any small constant and
C
is some universal constant (that could depend on
\delta
) and could change from line to line.

We consider two cases:

Case 1:

f(n)\geq 2-\kappa

In this case, note that for
n
large enough, we must have that
\frac{n^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}\leq\delta\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2-\kappa}}
.
Thus, we obtain the inequality,

(6.3)

\displaystyle\frac{4n^{2}}{(\log 2n)^{f(2n)}}\leq\frac{(2+2\delta)n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2-\kappa}},

\displaystyle\log\frac{4}{2+2\delta}+(2-\kappa)\log\log n\leq f(2n)\log\log 2n,

\displaystyle f(2n)\geq(2-\kappa)\frac{\log\log n}{\log\log n+\log\left[1+\frac{\log 2}{\log n}\right]}+\frac{1}{\log\log 2n}\log\frac{4}{2+2\delta}

\displaystyle\geq(2-\kappa)-\frac{2-\kappa}{2\log n\log\log n}+\frac{1}{\log\log 2n}\log\frac{4}{2+2\delta}\geq(2-\kappa),

when
n
is large enough.

Case 2:
f(n)\leq 2-\kappa

In this case, when
n
is large enough, we can still obtain the inequality for any small
\delta>0
,
\frac{n^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}\leq\delta\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}
. Thus, we obtain the inequality,

(6.4)

\displaystyle\frac{4n^{2}}{(\log 2n)^{f(2n)}}\leq\frac{(2+2\delta)n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}},

\displaystyle(\log 2n)^{f(2n)}\geq\frac{4}{2+2\delta}(\log n)^{f(n)},

\displaystyle f(2n)\log\log 2n\geq\log\frac{4}{2+2\delta}+f(n)\log\log n,

\displaystyle f(2n)\geq f(n)\frac{\log\log n}{\log\log 2n}+\frac{1}{\log\log 2n}\log\frac{4}{2+2\delta}

\displaystyle\geq f(n)-\frac{f(n)}{2\log n\log\log n}+\frac{1}{\log\log(2n)}\log\frac{4}{2+2\delta}\geq f(n)+\frac{c}{\log\log n},

where
c>0
will be some universal constant, and the least line will hold provided
n
is sufficiently large.

Now, let
n_{0}
be large enough integer such that both equations (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) hold. Furthermore, consider
k
large enough so that

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{c}{\log\log(2^{j+1}n_{0})}\geq 2-\kappa.

We claim that for any
n\geq 2^{k}n_{0}
, we must have that
f(n)\geq 2-\kappa
. First, consider the sequence
f(n),f(n/2),f(n/4),\ldots,f(n/2^{L})
, where
L
is the largest integer such that
n/2^{L}\geq n_{0}
. If there existed some
0\leq i\leq L
such that
f(n/2^{i})\geq 2-\kappa
, then by repeatedly applying equation (
6.3
), we must have that
f(n)\geq 2-\kappa
.

Otherwise, (
6.4
) must hold for all
0\leq i\leq L
, and we see that
f(n)\geq f(n/2^{L})+\sum_{j=0}^{L}\frac{c}{\log\log(n/2^{j})}\geq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{c}{\log\log 2^{j+1}n_{0}}\geq 2-\kappa
. Here, we remark that we used the fact that
f(n)\geq 0
is a trivial lower bound for all
n
.

By substituting our bound on
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})
into equation (
6.2
), we derive an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]
.
∎

Assuming the consequence of the above result, we also have the following corollary.

Corollary 6.7
.

Assume that we know
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2+o(1)}}
, then we have
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]\leq\frac{n}{(\log n)^{3/2+o(1)}}
.

Proof.

Recall the set
\Omega
from Lemma
6.3
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the definition of
\Omega
, we have

\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\mathbb{P}(\Omega)\geq\mathbb{E}[E_{n}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]]^{2}\geq(1-o(1))\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]^{2}.

Using the fact that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=O(\log\log n/\log n)
gives the result.
∎

6.2.2.

The goal of this section is to establish equation (

Before we start, let us recall the notation that we used in the proof of Lemma

In this subsection, we will give the estimate only for cut points and the graph distance.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
X_{n}
denote the number of cut points, or the graph distance.
Assume that there exists some
\epsilon>0

(1)

\tilde{X}_{n}\geq\frac{l}{2}

\hat{X}_{n}\geq\frac{l}{2}

\Omega

\{E_{n}>l\}\subset(\{\tilde{X}_{n}\geq l/2\}\cap\Omega)\cup(\{\hat{X}_{n}\geq l/2\}\cap\Omega).

(2)

Furthermore, (again still considering that
E_{n}>l

\tilde{X}_{n}\geq\frac{l}{2}

\tilde{S}{[n/(\log n)^{4},n]}
and
\hat{S}
by the proof of Lemma
6.3

(3)

Vice versa, in the case
\hat{X}_{n}\geq\frac{l}{2}

\tilde{S}

Thus, we have that,

\mathbb{P}(E_{n}>l)\leq 2\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{n}\geq l/2,\tilde{S}{[n/(\log n)^{4},n]}\cap\hat{S}\neq\emptyset).

Furthermore, observe that,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{2}]\leq 2\sum_{l=1}^{n}l\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{n}\geq l/2,\tilde{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\hat{S}\neq\emptyset)\leq 2\sum_{l=1}^{4\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]}4\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]\mathbb{P}(\tilde{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\hat{S}\neq\emptyset)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{l>4\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]}l\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{n}\geq l/2,\tilde{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\hat{S}\neq\emptyset)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Cn^{2}\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}+2\sum_{l>4\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]}l\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{n}\geq l/2,\tilde{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\hat{S}\neq\emptyset).

We used
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]\leq\frac{n}{(\log n)^{1/2+o(1)}}

[

[

]

Now, assume that equation (

) holds with probability significantly less than
(\log n)^{-2}
. Let
\Omega_{B}
be the set of walks
\tilde{S}
such that equation (
6.5
) holds. We have that,

\displaystyle\sum_{l>4\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]}l\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{n}\geq l/2,\tilde{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\hat{S}\neq\emptyset,\tilde{S}\not\in\Omega_{B})

\displaystyle+\sum_{l>4\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]}l\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{n}\geq l/2,\tilde{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\cap\hat{S}\neq\emptyset,\tilde{S}\in\Omega_{B})

\displaystyle\leq n\sum_{l>4\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{S}\not\in\Omega_{B})

\displaystyle+\sum_{l>4\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{n}]}l\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}\cap\tilde{S}[n/(\log n)^{4},n]\neq\emptyset|\tilde{S}\in\Omega_{B},\tilde{X}_{n}>l/2)\mathbb{P}(\tilde{S}\in\Omega_{B},\tilde{X}_{n}\geq l/2)

6.3.
Establishing equation (
6.5
)

Lemma 6.9
.

With probability greater than
1-\frac{1}{(\log n)^{2}}
on walks
\tilde{S}
, there exists some
c>0
such that we have,

\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}\cap\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\neq\emptyset|\tilde{S})\leq(\log n)^{-c}.

We start with the following observation.
We define a sequence of scales
0=K_{0}<K_{1}<K_{2}<K_{3}<\ldots<K_{f}
and consider the following bound. Also, let
A(R_{o},R_{i})
denote the annulus around the origin with outer radius
R_{o}
and inner radius
R_{i}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{i,n}:=A[\sqrt{n/K_{i}},\sqrt{n/K_{i+1}}]
.

By considering the last time of intersection between
\hat{S}
and the set
\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]
, we can derive the following bound on the probability of intersection.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}\cap\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\neq\emptyset|\tilde{S})

(6.7)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{10}\text{Cap}(\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\cap\mathcal{A}_{i,n})\max_{z\in\mathcal{A}_{i,n}}G(z)

(6.8)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=11}^{f-1}\text{Cap}(\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\cap\mathcal{A}_{i,n})\max_{z\in\mathcal{A}_{i,n}}G(z).

Our heuristics for (
6.8
) are the following:

(1)

The capacity of
\text{Cap}(\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\cap\mathcal{A}_{i,n})
can be bounded by
|\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\cap\mathcal{A}_{i,n}|

(2)

The volume can be bounded very closely by
\frac{n}{K_{i}^{1+\delta}}

\tilde{S}
in the annulus
\mathcal{A}_{i,n}
.

(3)

For (
6.7

Eventually, we will choose

, and after this we choose
K_{i}=2K_{i-1}
for
i\geq 12

Furthermore, we can choose
K_{f}=n^{1/10}
. Note that by the local central limit theorem, the probability a point
\tilde{S}_{k}

that lies in the ball of radius

around the origin is at most
\frac{nn^{1/5}(\log n)^{8}}{n^{2}}\ll(\log n)^{-2}
.

6.3.1.
The large scales

We will first begin our analysis of equation (

) by considering the largest scales after
K_{11}
.

We need the following lemma.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
S[0,n]
be a random walk starting at the origin in
d=4
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{r}
be a ball of radius
r
centered at the origin. Let
L(\mathcal{B}_{r})

L(\mathcal{B}_{r}):=\sum_{t=\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}}}^{n}\mathbbm{1}[S_{t}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}].

Namely, it is the amount of times that
S_{t}
lies in the ball
\mathcal{B}_{r}
.

Then, we have that

(6.9)

\mathbb{E}[L(\mathcal{B}_{r})]\leq C\frac{r^{4}(\log n)^{4}}{n}.

Furthermore, we would also have,

(6.10)

\mathbb{E}[L(\mathcal{B}_{r})^{k}]\leq C^{k}\frac{r^{4}(r^{2})^{k-1}(\log n)^{4}}{n}.

Proof.

By using the local central limit theorem, one observes that the probability of a point
S_{t}
being in the ball
\mathcal{B}_{r}
is of order
\frac{r^{4}}{t^{2}}
. If we sum up this probability over all times
t
from
n/(\log n)^{4}
and
n
, we see that the expectation of the local time within this ball is
\frac{r^{4}(\log n)^{4}}{n}
.

Now, if we want to consider the expectation of the
k
th power of the local time. We need to consider the probability that there are k distinct times
t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{k}
such that
S_{t_{i}}
are all in the ball with radius
r
. First, we remark that given that
S_{t_{1}}
is in
\mathcal{B}_{r}
, the probability that
S_{t_{2}}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
is at most
\text{O}\left(\frac{r^{4}}{(t_{2}-t_{1})^{2}}\right)
if
t_{2}-t_{1}\geq r^{2}
or
1
if
t_{2}-t_{1}\leq r^{2}
. Thus, we see a bound on
\mathbb{E}[L(\mathcal{B}_{r})^{k}]
would be the following.

\sum_{t_{1}=n/(\log n)^{4}}^{n}\sum_{t_{2}-t_{1}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{t_{k}-t_{k-1}=1}^{n}\frac{Cr^{4}(\log n)^{4}}{n}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{Cr^{4}}{\max(r^{4},(t_{i+1}-t_{i})^{2})}\leq C^{k}\frac{r^{4}(r^{2})^{k-1}(\log n)^{4}}{n}.

Here, we used the fact that
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{Cr^{4}}{\max(r^{4},t^{2})}\leq Cr^{2}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma

: Estimate for (

Define the event

On this event, we will analyze (

Notice that we must have
|\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\cap\mathcal{A}_{i,n})|\leq|L(\mathcal{B}_{\sqrt{n/K_{i}}})|\leq\frac{n}{(K_{i})^{1+\delta}}\ll 1
. By using subadditivity of the capacity and the fact that the capacity must be less than the volume, we must have that

\displaystyle\text{Cap}(\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\cap\mathcal{A}_{i,n})\leq|\tilde{S}[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{4}},n]\cap\mathcal{A}_{i,n}|

Notice that by the moment estimate for the capacity of the random walk in four dimensions, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let

be a random walk starting at the origin. For any integer
k>0

(6.12)

\mathbb{P}\left({\rm Cap}(S[0,n])\geq\frac{n}{(\log n)^{1-\chi}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{(\log n)^{k}},

Proof.

In

In order to discuss the properties of the parts of the random walk inside each annulus, we need to consider the following stopping times.

Define

\displaystyle T^{i}_{k}:=\inf\{t\geq\tau^{i}_{k-1}:S_{t}\leq\sqrt{n/K_{i}}\},

\displaystyle\tau^{i}_{k}:=\inf\{t\geq T^{i}_{k}:S_{t}\geq 2\sqrt{n/K_{i}}\}.

These are a sequence of times indicating when the random walk hits that ball of radius
\sqrt{n/K_{i}}
and later details when it escapes said ball.

The following lemma will ensure that with high probability that there are not too many times in which the random walk will make an excursion into the ball of radius
\sqrt{n/K_{i}}
. Furthermore, each excursion into this ball will lie very close to its expected time.

Lemma 6.12
.

Let
M^{i}=\sup\{k:\tau^{i}_{k}\leq n\}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{i}
be the event that

\mathcal{H}^{i}:=\{M^{i}\leq(\log n)^{C}\}\bigcap_{k=1}^{(\log n)^{C}}\{|\tau^{i}_{k}-T^{i}_{k}|\leq\frac{n}{K_{i}^{1-\kappa}}\}\bigcap_{k=1}^{(\log n)^{C}}\{|\tau^{i}_{k}-T^{i}_{k}|\geq\frac{n}{K_{i}^{1+\kappa}}\}.

We claim that there is some

such that
\mathbb{P}((\mathcal{H}^{i})^{c})\ll\frac{1}{(\log n)^{2}}
.

Proof.

Notice that
M^{i}
can be bounded from above by the number of spheres of radius

that are necessary in order to cover the full random walk
S[1,n]
. Considering a sufficiently large

with high probability is the estimate of
[

, Lemma 3.4]
.

Note that the probability that
|\tau^{i}_{k}-T^{i}_{k}|\geq\frac{n}{K_{i}^{1-\kappa}}
is bounded by the probability that a Brownian motion stays within a ball of radius

for time
\frac{n}{K_{i}^{1-\kappa}}
. But, this happens with probability less than
\exp[-cK_{i}^{\kappa}]
. By taking a union bound, this is less than
(\log n)^{C}\exp[-cK_{i}^{\kappa}]\ll(\log n)^{-2}
.

Furthermore, the probability that
|\tau^{i}_{k}-T^{i}_{k}|\leq\frac{n}{K_{i}^{1+\kappa}}
can be bounded by
\exp[-cK_{i}^{\kappa}]
by the reflection principle. Again, by taking a union bound, this is less than
(\log n)^{C}\exp[-cK_{i}^{\kappa}]\ll(\log n)^{-2}
.
∎

6.4.
An analysis of
X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]

In this section, we will prove that
\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}}\to 0
in distribution. We will first start by proving upper and lower bounds on
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})
. Note that an upper bound on
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})
was already derived in Lemma
6.6
. We now derive the corresponding lower bound for
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})
.
Is this subsection, we will give the estimate only for cut points and graph distance.

Lemma 6.13
.

Let
X_{n}
denote the number of cut points, or the graph distance. We have that

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\geq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2+o(1)}}.

Proof.

We perform a dyadic decomposition of
X_{n}
up to
(\log n)^{\epsilon}
levels for some
\epsilon>0
.
We thus write

X_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\epsilon\log_{2}(\log n)}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}-E_{n}^{(k,l)}+\sum_{j=0}^{(\log n)^{\epsilon}-1}X[jn/(\log n)^{\epsilon},(j+1)n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}].

Notice, now that since
\sum_{j=0}^{(\log n)^{\epsilon}-1}X[jn/(\log n)^{\epsilon},(j+1)n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}]
is a sum of i.i.d. terms where each individual term has variance at most
\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2-o(1)}}
, we can compute its variance as

(\log n)^{\epsilon}\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2\epsilon}(\log n)^{2-o(1)}}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2+\epsilon-o(1)}}.

This will not be the major contributor to the variance.

We will now consider the contribution to the variance from
\sum_{k=0}^{\epsilon\log_{2}(\log n)}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}-E_{n}^{(k,l)}
.
Notice, we have,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\epsilon\log_{2}(\log n)}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}-E_{n}^{(k,l)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\epsilon\log_{2}(\log n)}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathrm{Var}(-E_{n}^{(k,l)})+\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},l_{1},l_{2}:(k_{1},l_{1})\neq(k_{2},l_{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Var}(E_{n}^{(0,1)})-\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},l_{1},l_{2}:(k_{1},l_{1})\neq(k_{2},l_{2})}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2+o(1)}}-(\log n)^{2\epsilon}\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{3+o(1)}}\gg\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2+o(1)}}.

We used the fact that
E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}
are all positive terms, so the only possible negative contribution to the covariance comes from the product of expectations. However, we know from Corollary
6.7
that the product of expectations of these expectations is bounded from above by
\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{3+o(1)}}
.
This completes the proof of our assertion.
∎

With a small modification to the proof above, we can derive the following main result.

Theorem 6.14
.

Let
X_{n}
denote the number of cut points, or the graph distance. We have that as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}}\to 0,

in distribution.

Proof.

We proceed with the same dyadic decomposition as we have done in the previous proof.

X_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\epsilon\log_{2}(\log n)}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}-E_{n}^{(k,l)}+\sum_{j=0}^{(\log n)^{\epsilon}-1}X[jn/(\log n)^{\epsilon},(j+1)n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}].

Let
\Omega^{(k,l)}
be the set from Lemma
6.3
(constructed for the appropriate cross term
E_{n}^{(k,l)}
) and consider
\Omega_{A}:=\bigcap_{(k,l)}\Omega^{(k,l)}
.
By Lemma
6.3
, we can ensure that
(\Omega^{(k,l)})^{c}
holds with probability no larger than
\text{O}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)
. By taking a union bound, we see that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{A}^{c})=\text{O}\left(\frac{\log\log n}{(\log n)^{1-\epsilon}}\right)\ll 1.

On the event,
\Omega^{(k,l)}
, we see that

|-E_{n}^{(k,l)}+\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]|\leq\frac{n}{(\log n)^{3/2+o(1)}}.

Thus, on
\Omega_{A}
, we must have

\left|\sum_{k=0}^{\epsilon\log_{2}(\log n)}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}-E_{n}^{(k,l)}+\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]\right|\leq(\log n)^{\epsilon}\frac{n}{(\log n)^{3/2-o(1)}}.

Furthermore, notice that by Lemma
6.6
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{(\log n)^{\epsilon}-1}X[jn/(\log n)^{\epsilon},(j+1)n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}]\right)\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{2+\epsilon+o(1)}}.

Let
\Omega_{B}
be the event that

\sum_{j=0}^{(\log n)^{\epsilon}-1}X[jn/(\log n)^{\epsilon},(j+1)n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}]-\mathbb{E}[X[jn/(\log n)^{\epsilon},(j+1)n/(\log n)^{\epsilon}]]
is smaller than
\frac{n}{(\log n)^{1+\epsilon/4}}
. By Markov’s inequality
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{B}^{c})\leq(\log n)^{-\epsilon/2}
.

Now, on
\Omega_{A}\cap\Omega_{B}
, we have

X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]\leq\frac{n}{(\log n)^{1+\epsilon/4}}+(\log n)^{\epsilon}\frac{n}{(\log n)^{3/2-o(1)}}\ll\frac{n}{(\log n)^{1+o(1)}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}.

Furthermore,
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{A}\cap\Omega_{B})=1-o(1)
by a union bound. This completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle N_{(k_{1},l_{1}),(k_{2},l_{2})}:=-\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}])\mathbbm{1}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}]>0]

\displaystyle\times(E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}])\mathbbm{1}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]<0]]

E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}
must be positive. Thus, under the condition that
E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]
is negative, the expectation of
\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}])^{2}\mathbbm{1}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]<0]]^{1/2}\leq\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},l_{2})}]
. The second to last inequality used the definition of
g
along with equation (
6.16
) for
n
large enough. Finally, the last inequality used the fact that
\frac{n}{(\log n)^{k}}
is increasing for
n
large enough as well as the definition of
g(n,K)
.

This should contrast with the positive contribution coming from,

\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}])^{2}]=\frac{(n2^{-k_{1}})^{2}}{(\log n2^{-k_{1}})^{g(n2^{-k_{1}})}}.

There will be at least one term
(k_{1},l_{1})
where
g(n2^{-k_{1}})
attains its maximum value, and for this specific value
k^{\max}
, we have that,

\frac{(n2^{-k^{\max}})^{2}}{(\log n2^{-k^{\max}})^{g(n2^{-k^{\max}})}}=\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+o(1)}}.

The factor of
o(1)
is to compensate for possible changes of the constant factor in front. We remark that due to these computations, there is a gap of of a factor of
(\log n)^{1/2}
between the positive term above and the negative contribution from equation (
6.17
). This will ensure a sufficiently good lower bound for the expectation of
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
.
Indeed, we have that,

(6.18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}^{2}]\geq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}])^{2}]+\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{K}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{2^{k_{1}}}\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{K}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{2^{k_{2}}}N_{(k_{1},l_{1}),(k_{2},l_{2})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+o(1)}}-K^{2}4^{K}\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+o(1)}}.

Furthermore, if we define

\mathcal{I}:=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{K}}(\tilde{X}_{(K,j)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(K,j)}]),

we notice that we have,

(6.19)

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{I}^{2}]\leq 2^{K}\frac{(n2^{-K})^{2}}{(\log n2^{-K})^{f(n2^{-K})}}\leq 2^{K}\frac{(n2^{-K})^{2}}{(\log n2^{-K})^{g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}}}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}}}.

In the first inequality, we used the fact that
\mathcal{I}
is a sum of independent random variables. Since
g(n,K)
lies between
b_{2}+o(1)
and
b_{1}+o(1)
, the final inequality must be true for large enough
n
.

Notice now, that we have, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(6.20)

\mathbb{E}[(\overline{\mathcal{E}}+\mathcal{I})^{2}]\geq(\mathbb{E}[\overline{\mathcal{E}}^{2}]^{1/2}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{I}^{2}]^{1/2})^{2}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+o(1)}}.

∎

Remark 6.19
.

By (
6.18
), (
6.19
) and (
6.20
), we have
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}{n^{2}/(\log n)^{b_{2}+\epsilon}}>0,\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}{n^{2}/(\log n)^{b_{1}-\epsilon}}<\infty

noting
(\mathbb{E}[\overline{\mathcal{E}}^{2}]^{1/2}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{I}^{2}]^{1/2})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[(\overline{\mathcal{E}}+\mathcal{I})^{2}]\leq(\mathbb{E}[\overline{\mathcal{E}}^{2}]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{I}^{2}]^{1/2})^{2}
.

We now present the heuristic argument we will use to analyze the behavior of
\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}}
.

•

We start by taking the high probability event
\Omega
to be the event that all
E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]\leq\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]
.

•

If there exists an infinite sequence of
m
such that we have
f(n2^{-K})
is greater than
g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}
, then we have
f(n)\leq g(n,K)-o(1)
. For such
n
, we will be able to show that
\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}}
will go to
0
.

•

In the case that the above does not happen, it must be the case that eventually all
f(n2^{-K})\leq g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}<g(n,K)+\frac{1}{2}
. (The latter condition will ensure that the contribution to the variance of the sum of the graph distance terms will be greater than the contribution of the cross terms.) Then, if we find an infinite sequence of
n
such that
f(n)-f(n2^{-K})\leq\frac{1}{\log\log n}
infinitely often, then we will be able to show for these
n
that
\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}}
will go to
0
.

•

Finally, we will show that there must be an infinite sequence of
n
such that
f(n)-f(n2^{-K})\leq\frac{1}{\log\log n}
infinitely often by contradiction.

We can now prove our main result

Theorem 6.20
.

Let
X_{n}
be a series of random variables that satisfies the assumptions of
6.17
.
We have that there exists a subsequence such that as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}0.

Proof.

Initial Estimates :

Recall that the function
f(n)
is defined as
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})=\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}
. Now, we start by performing the dyadic decomposition to
K
levels.
Thus, we have,

X_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{2^{K}}\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}E_{n}^{(k,l)}.

Here,
\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}=X[(l-1)n2^{-K},ln2^{-K}]
is a collection of independent copies with the same distribution as
X_{n2^{-K}}
and for each given
K
,
E_{n}^{(k,l)}
is a collection of independent random variables with the same distribution as
E_{n2^{-k}}
.

We now define the event,

\displaystyle\Omega_{(k,l)}:=\left\{|E_{n}^{(k,l)}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]|\leq\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]\right\},\quad\Omega:=\bigcap_{k,l}\Omega_{(k,l)}.

By the third Assumption
6.17
, we see that
\Omega
occurs with probability
1-o(1)
.
We define
\mathcal{E}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{2^{k}}E_{n}^{(k,l)}
and observe that,

(6.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\mathcal{E}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}])^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2})}\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k_{1},l_{1})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{1},j_{1})}])^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{(k_{1},l_{1})}]]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k_{2},j_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k_{2},j_{2})}])^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega_{(k_{2},j_{2})}]]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{2K+2}4\max_{(k,l)}(\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]^{2})\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+1-o(1)}}.

In the last equation, we used equation (
6.16
) to bound
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}^{(k,l)}]^{2}
relative to
\mathbb{E}[(E_{n}^{(k,l)})^{2}]
and we placed irrelevant constant factors into an
o(1)
exponent of
\log n
in the denominator.

In addition, we have that,

(6.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{K}}(\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}])\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{K}}(\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}])\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{l=1}^{2^{K}}\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}])^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{K}\frac{(n2^{-K})^{2}}{(\log n2^{-K})^{f(n2^{-K})}}=\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}2^{-K}\frac{(\log n)^{f(n)}}{(\log n2^{-K})^{f(n2^{-K}})}.

Analysis of properties of
f

Now, we seek to analyze various cases.

Case 1: There exists an infinite sequence of
n
such that
f(n2^{-K})\geq g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}

In this case, we observe that Lemma
6.18
would imply that
f(n)\leq g(n,K)+o(1)
along this infinite sequence of
n
.
Furthermore, we would have that,

\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}2^{-K}\frac{(\log n)^{f(n)}}{(\log n2^{-K})^{f(n2^{-K}})}\leq C\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}\frac{1}{(\log n)^{1/4+o(1)}}\ll\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}},

when
n
is sufficiently large.
Indeed, we used that for
n
large and fixed
K
, we can bound
\frac{1}{(\log n2^{-K})^{f(n2^{-K})}}\leq\frac{1}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}+o(1)}}
where the
o(1)
term in the exponent is introduced to from
\log n2^{-K}\to\log n
in the denominator. Recall, we assume
f(n2^{-K})\geq g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}.
In the numerator, we bounded
(\log n)^{f(n)}
by
(\log n)^{g(n,K)+o(1)}
.

Combining this estimate of equation (
6.22
), we see that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{K}}(\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}])\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]\right]\ll\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}.

Thus, there will be some set
\Omega^{\prime}
, which will still hold with probability
1-o(1)
such that,

\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2^{K}}(\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}])\right)<\epsilon\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{m})}.

Furthermore, recall from equation (
6.21
) that the sums of the cross terms
\mathcal{E}
satisfies,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\mathcal{E}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}])^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]]\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+1-o(1)}}\ll\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}.

Again, there will be a further subset
\Omega^{\prime\prime}\subset\Omega^{\prime}
that will still hold with probability
1-o(1)
on which we have additionally that
|\mathcal{E}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}]|<\epsilon\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}
. Thus, on
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
, we have
|X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]|<2\epsilon\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}
.

By our assumption, we can do this for an infinite sequence of
n
.

Case 2: Eventually, all
f(n2^{-K})\leq g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}

This can be divided into two more subcases.

Case 2a: There does not exists an infinite sequence of
n
such that
f(n)-f(n2^{-K})\leq\frac{1}{\log\log n}

In this case, we must be able to find a sequence
f(a2^{mK})
such that

f(a2^{mK})-f(a2^{(m-1)K})\geq\frac{1}{\log\log(a2^{mK})}.

Now, since
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\log\log(a2^{mK})}
diverges,
this must mean that
f(a2^{mK})
is unbounded from above. This contradicts our assumption that
f(n)\leq g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}
for
x
large enough. Thus, we do not have to worry about this case.

The only remaining case is the following.

Case 2b: There exists an infinite sequence of
n
such that
f(n)-f(n2^{-K})\leq\frac{1}{\log\log n}

In what follows, we will perform computations on values
n
that satisfy
f(n)-f(n2^{-K})\leq\frac{1}{\log\log n}
.
In this case, we notice that,

\displaystyle\frac{(\log n)^{f(n)}}{(\log n2^{-K})^{f(n2^{-K}})}\leq(\log n)^{f(n)-f(n2^{-K})}\left(\frac{\log n}{\log n-K\log 2}\right)^{f(n2^{-K})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\log n)^{\frac{1}{\log\log n}}\left(\frac{\log n}{\log n-K\log 2}\right)^{2+\frac{1}{4}}\leq 2e,

where the final inequality will hold for large enough
m
. Notice that we also used that
f(n2^{-K})\leq g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}\leq\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+o(1)
, by our assumption that eventually all
f(n)\leq g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}
and our upper bound on
g(n,K)
.

Returning to equation (
6.22
), we can bound the expectation of the square of
\sum_{l=1}^{2^{K}}(\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}])
by
2e2^{-K}\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})
for large enough
n
. Thus, by Markov’s inequality, we have that,

|\sum_{l=1}^{2^{K}}(\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{(K,l)}])|\geq\epsilon\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})},

with probability at most
2e2^{-K}\epsilon^{-2}
.

Furthermore, recall again from equation (
6.21
) that we have,

\mathbb{E}[(\mathcal{E}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}])^{2}\mathbbm{1}[\Omega]]\leq\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{g(n,K)+1-o(1)}}\ll\frac{n^{2}}{(\log n)^{f(n)}}.

Again, here we used that first we are considering an
m
such that first we have
f(n)\leq f(n2^{-K})+\frac{1}{\log\log n}
. Secondly, under the general assumption of case 2, we have
f(n2^{-K})+\frac{1}{\log\log n}\leq g(n,K)+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{\log\log n}\leq g(n,K)+1+o(1)
for
n
large enough. Thus, we have
f(n)\leq g(n,K)+1+o(1)
for some infinite sequence of
n
.
Thus (again by Markov’s inequality on the set
\Omega
), with probability
o(1)
, we have that
|\mathcal{E}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}]|\geq\epsilon\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}
.

In total, we see that with probability at most
o(1)+2e2^{-K}\epsilon^{-2}
, we have that

|X_{n}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]|\geq 2\epsilon\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})}.

Finishing the Argument

Notice that in all cases, for a fixed
K
, we can find a subsequence
X_{n_{1}},X_{n_{2}},\ldots
such that with probability at least
1-4e2^{K(1-q)}\epsilon^{-2}
, we must have that

|X_{n_{i}}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n_{i}}]|\leq\epsilon\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n_{i}})}

as
K\to\infty
, we see that
1-4e2^{-K}\epsilon^{-2}\to 0
. By a diagonalization argument, we can find a subsequence
\tilde{n}_{i}
such that
|X_{\tilde{n}_{i}}-\mathbb{E}[X_{\tilde{n}_{i}}]|\leq\epsilon\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{\tilde{n}_{i}})}
with probability going to 1 for each
\epsilon
. This subsequence converges in distribution to
0
.
∎
