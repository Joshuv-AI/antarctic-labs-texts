Challenges in delineating B-cell lymphoma: A case of plasmablastic lymphoma mimicking Burkitt lymphoma
Authors: Kundendu Arya Bishen, Sakshi Arora, Shweta Rehani, Madhvika Patidar, Jaya Joshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An immunocompromised body state following the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection increases the risk of certain cancers like Kaposi sarcoma and lymphoma. B-cell lymphomas are the common neoplasms that occur, and Oral Plasmablastic lymphoma is one of its types. It is categorized as a large B-cell lymphoma and an aggressive type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. These are rare tumours that histologically resemble other lymphomas, like Burkitt lymphoma, causing a diagnostic dilemma for pathologists. Here we report a rare case of Oral Plasmablastic lymphoma in a 32-year-old HIV-positive male with tuberculosis infection. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to confirm the diagnosis, and the markers used for differentiating various lymphomas have been discussed.

INTRODUCTION

Malignant lymphomas are a group of neoplasms with differing severity levels that originate from the fundamental cells of lymphoid tissue, such as lymphocytes and histiocytes, at any stage of their development. They are further divided into Hodgkin’s disease and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL). Lymphomas are the third most frequent type of cancer in the head and neck region, with non-Hodgkin lymphoma accounting for about 5% of all head and neck cancers.[1]

NHL is a heterogeneous group of lymphoproliferative malignancies that mostly occur in the B-type lymphocyte lineage. A rare but aggressive type of large B-cell lymphoma is Plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL), which is mostly associated with HIV infection. Its extranidal presentation is usually seen and was first described by Delecluse et al.[2] in the oral cavity of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-infected individuals in 1997.

In histopathology, delineating PBL morphology from other large B-cell lymphomas such as Plasmablastic plasma cell lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) positive anaplastic Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), multicentric Castleman large B-cell lymphoma, and HHV-8 positive DLBCL is a diagnostic dilemma. However, differentiation can be achieved through factors such as clinical history, site of disease, immunophenotype of the tumour cells and the detection of Epstein-Barr encoding region (EBER).[3]

CASE REPORT

A 32-year-old male presented to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathology with a complaint of toothache in the left lower jaw for the past 2 months. The pain in his jaw had been increasing over several weeks and he started noticing a swelling of the left lower buccal vestibule. His medical history was significant for tuberculosis infection from the past 1 month and HIV infection with 200 CD4+ cell count per µL 2 months before reporting. He also had a history of tobacco chewing for the past 10 years.

On extraoral examination, marked facial asymmetry was noted. A diffuse swelling was present on the left cheek of size 5 × 5 cm that was firm in consistency and tender on palpation. Intraorally, a soft tissue growth was present in the left lower buccal vestibule region extending anteroposterior from the left lower 1st premolar to the 2nd molar. It was pale in colour, soft in consistency and had a shiny pebble surface [Figure 1]. Radiographic examination revealed no specific abnormality or bony perforation. An incisional biopsy was recommended.

Solitary soft tissue mass on the left lower buccal vestibule

Histopathology revealed broad sheets of uniformly distributed small monomorphic tumour cells resembling lymphocytes. Tumour cells showed oval to round hyperchromatic nuclei with minimal basophilic cytoplasm, and atypical mitotic figures were also seen. Light-stained macrophages in the background of hyperchromatic neoplastic lymphoid cells appeared as starry sky-like patterns [Figure 2]. Based on this morphological pattern of cells, it seemed as Burkitt lymphoma. So, for confirmation, an immunohistochemistry test was performed to establish lymphoid cell lineage. CD45 marker came positive, confirming lymphoid cell lineage. To delineate B-cell type, CD20, CD19, and CD22 markers are used. However, all these came negative in present case. Hence, no further IHC markers were used for subtyping of B-cell lymphomas. Then, T-cell marker CD3 was used, which was also negative. Following this, Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining with Plasma cell markers like CD138 and MUM1 was performed that was found positive in tumour cells [Figure 3]. So, the final diagnosis was given as Plasmablastic lymphoma. Also, tumour activity was very high as the Ki-67 marker had 70%–80% reactivity, suggesting the tumour’s aggressive and poorly differentiated nature [Figure 3]. The patient was advised chemotherapy but was lost for follow-up as he did not turn up for further treatment.

DISCUSSION

Patients with HIV are at high risk of developing oral malignancy due to immunodeficiency. Many predisposing factors increase the probability of malignancy like immunosuppression and persistent viral-induced chronic inflammation. Patients with HIV who engage more in activities such as excessive alcohol consumption and tobacco use have elevated cancer risk. They are also more susceptible to co-infections with viruses like human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8), Epstein-Barr virus and bacteria like Mycobacterium tubercular causing malignancy and tuberculosis, respectively. Additionally, a more aggressive progression of cancer is seen in HIV-positive individuals and lower survival rates when compared to uninfected individuals.[4]

Malignancies that are seen more frequently in AIDS than in the general population include Kaposi sarcoma and non-Hodgkin lymphoma. HIV/AIDS-related lymphomas are high-grade tumours usually of B-cell phenotype. These include DLBCL, Plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL) and Burkitt’s lymphoma. The incidence of anaplastic large-cell lymphoma and several Hodgkin’s lymphoma subtypes have increased similarly. Due to a lack of tumorous endothelial cell proliferation, Kaposi sarcoma was excluded. Due to the absence of Reed-Sternberg cells, Hodgkin lymphoma was excluded. Histopathological resemblance to starry sky appearance was suggestive of Burkitt lymphoma, but age, ethnicity and other clinical features corroborated the diagnosis. So, IHC profiling of the tumour was done using a panel of markers of differentiation and the diagnosis of Plasmablastic lymphoma was confirmed. Plasmablastic lymphoma is a type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, mainly B-cell type, typically involving the oral cavity. More than two-thirds of cases of PBL are seen in HIV-positive patients as described in case series published by Delecluse[2] and other workers.[10]

PBL shows cells called plasmablasts that exhibit blastoid morphology and an immunophenotype like that of plasma cells. These cells have an antigenic profile typical of plasma cells but retain the blastoid characteristics of immunoblots. Because of this similarity between the antigenicity of plasma cells and reactive plasmablasts, Delecluse et al.[2] proposed the name plasmablastic lymphoma.

Histopathological, PBL typically presents as sheets of large cells having prominent nucleoli in an oval nucleus surrounded by moderate cytoplasm, giving them an immunoblastic appearance. They may also display a ‘starry sky pattern’ due to the presence of macrophages between the immunoblastic cells. Extranidal PBL often resembles myeloma as its tumour cells are like plasma cells, having an eccentric nucleus, a perinuclear halo and basophilic cytoplasm. Thus, histopathologic features of PBL overlap with myeloma and other high-grade lymphomas, creating a Catch-22 situation for pathologists if a thorough workup is not conducted using IHC.[9]

The prognosis is generally poor in PBL patients, with a poor overall survival (OS) rate of 2 years. Improved survival is reported when treatment of HIV, along with appropriate chemotherapy, is used.[5]

CONCLUSION

Plasmablastic lymphoma is an aggressive type of neoplasm that histopathological resembles Burkitt lymphoma. So, delineating them is a tricky task for pathologists. Great care should be taken when diagnosing them using IHC panels to differentiate the type of tumour cell.
