Association of a ‘three meals and sleep routine’ lifestyle intervention with weight and metabolic health in children with obesity: a prospective, single-arm pilot study
Authors: Qi Long, Ming Ma, Dan Wang, Fei Chen, Xuelian Zhou, Wei Wu, Guanping Dong, Junfen Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntensive health behavior and lifestyle treatment is the foundation of pediatric obesity care, but sustained adherence and weight-loss maintenance remain difficult. Regular meal and sleep timing may complement standard care, although pediatric interventional evidence is limited. This prospective, single-arm pilot study aimed to evaluate longitudinal changes in weight, body composition, and metabolic markers during a ‘three meals and sleep routine’ (TMSR) intervention.MethodsThis prospective, single-center, single-arm pilot study consecutively included 119 children with obesity aged 5–18 years [body mass index (BMI)-for-age Z-score (BAZ) >2] from January 2022 to December 2023. Families received nutritionist-led written guidance for three structured meals per day, no caloric intake between meals, age-based energy targets of 1,500–2,300 kcal/day, a protocol-defined minimum of 7.5 hours of nighttime sleep with lights off by 22:30, and maintenance of habitual physical activity. Follow-up was planned at approximately 2-month intervals but occurred according to clinical availability. Anthropometry, bioelectrical-impedance body composition, and fasting metabolic markers were assessed. Adherence, dietary intake, sleep, and physical activity were not objectively monitored. Generalized linear mixed models used available repeated measurements to estimate average within-cohort change over time.ResultsThe cohort had a mean age of 10.24±2.33 years; 82 participants (68.9%) were male and 37 (31.1%) were female. The fitted BMI trajectory decreased from approximately 26.0 kg/m2 at baseline to 24.5 kg/m2 at month 4, then increased to 25.4 kg/m2 by month 12. BAZ followed the same pattern, decreasing from 2.9 to 2.3 before increasing to 2.4. Visceral fat area and percentage body fat also declined through month 4 and subsequently rebounded. Fasting insulin and glucose showed early declines followed by partial rebound, whereas triglycerides and serum uric acid declined across follow-up. In linear-time models, average monthly coefficients were negative for BMI, BAZ, visceral fat area, percentage body fat, fasting insulin, glucose, triglycerides, and serum uric acid. These coefficients summarize within-cohort associations and do not capture the observed nonlinear trajectories.ConclusionsIn this uncontrolled cohort, several anthropometric and metabolic measures improved during the first 4–6 months, but BMI, BAZ, adiposity measures, insulin, and glucose later showed partial rebound. The findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating; the absence of a comparator and objective adherence data precludes causal attribution to TMSR and limits assessment of durability and safety. Controlled, multicenter studies with age-appropriate sleep targets and objective behavioral monitoring are needed.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In 119 children with obesity, several within-cohort adiposity and metabolic measures improved during the first 4–6 months of a structured meal-and-sleep routine, but body mass index (BMI), BMI-for-age Z-score, percentage body fat, visceral fat area, fasting insulin, and glucose subsequently showed partial rebound. Without a comparator or objective adherence monitoring, these patterns cannot establish intervention efficacy or safety.

What is known and what is new?

• Intensive, family-centered behavioral treatment remains first-line care for pediatric obesity. Meal timing and sleep regularity are biologically plausible adjuncts, but pediatric intervention data remain limited.

• This single-arm study provides preliminary longitudinal observations and identifies a clinically important loss of early benefit over time.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• ‘Three meals and sleep routine’ should be tested as an adjunct—not a replacement—for comprehensive pediatric obesity care. Practice-changing claims are premature; future trials require a standard-care comparator, age-specific sleep targets, predefined outcomes, objective dietary/sleep/activity monitoring, systematic participant-flow and adverse-event reporting, and strategies to sustain adherence beyond month 4.

Introduction

Childhood obesity has emerged as one of the most urgent global public health challenges (1). Over the past few decades, its prevalence has risen sharply across both developed and developing countries (2). This trend carries significant long-term implications for metabolic and cardiovascular health (3). The increase in childhood obesity has been particularly pronounced in developing countries. For example, national survey data from China revealed a dramatic increase in overweight prevalence among school-aged children—from 1.1% in 1985 to 20.4% by 2014 (4). Rapid economic growth in these regions has been accompanied by significant shifts in dietary patterns and lifestyle habits, including increased consumption of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods and reduced levels of physical activity (5). These changes have contributed to a faster acceleration of childhood obesity than that observed in high-income countries, positioning obesity as a significant threat to child health in many developing regions (6-8). Childhood body size may also have long-term implications for cardiovascular disease risk later in life (9).

Current pediatric obesity care extends beyond simple advice to ‘eat less and exercise more’. Its foundation is structured, intensive, family-centered health behavior and lifestyle treatment that integrates nutrition, physical activity, behavior-change techniques, and longitudinal support (10). Access and sustained engagement remain difficult, and partial weight regain after an early response is common. Reductions in physical activity among children and adolescents were also widely reported during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (11-13). A routine focused on meal and sleep timing could therefore be evaluated as a potentially low-burden adjunct to comprehensive care, but it should not be presented as a substitute for established multicomponent treatment.

The ‘three meals and sleep routine’ (TMSR) intervention combines three structured, nutritionally complete meals per day, avoidance of caloric intake between meals, and a regular nighttime routine (Figure 1). The approach emphasizes behavioral regularity rather than pharmacotherapy or an added exercise prescription.

The rationale is that meal timing and the light-sleep cycle act as time cues for central and peripheral circadian systems (14,15). Irregular eating, late caloric intake, and insufficient or irregular sleep may contribute to circadian misalignment, altered glucose regulation, appetite signaling, and lipid metabolism (16,17). Accordingly, regularizing these behaviors could plausibly influence the anthropometric and metabolic outcomes measured here. However, the mechanisms were not measured directly, and sustained benefit depends on sustained adherence. Real-world disruptions to routine care and lifestyle may also attenuate pediatric obesity treatment outcomes (18). We therefore aimed to describe feasibility and longitudinal within-cohort changes during TMSR, while treating the findings as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0402/rc).

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a prospective, single-center, single-arm, pilot study embedded in routine care at the Department of Clinical Nutrition, Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Children with obesity aged 5–18 years were consecutively included between January 2022 and December 2023 and received the TMSR behavioral regimen. The present analysis was subsequently performed using prospectively collected routine clinical data. There was no usual-care or untreated comparator. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2024-IRB-0011-P-01). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants. The study was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500097737).

Participants and eligibility criteria

Participants were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: aged 5–18 years; obesity is defined as body mass index (BMI)-for-age Z-score (BAZ) >2; child and guardian willing to receive the TMSR counseling protocol.

Participants were excluded for any of the following: presence of cardiovascular, pulmonary, renal, hepatic, or gastrointestinal diseases; presence of chronic infectious diseases; a psychiatric disorder or endocrine condition known to affect weight regulation, including diabetes mellitus or hyperthyroidism; current use of medications that affect body composition or metabolism; participation in other weight-loss programs within the past 3 months; concurrent participation in a structured exercise or weight-management program that would materially alter habitual activity or dietary exposure.

Baseline assessment and measurement procedures

At baseline and follow-up visits, trained research coordinators and clinical nutritionists used standardized procedures to measure height and weight. Body composition, including percentage body fat (PBF), visceral fat area (VFA), and skeletal muscle index (SMI), was assessed by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Fasting blood testing included glucose, insulin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, alanine aminotransferase, serum uric acid, and glycated hemoglobin. Staff received centralized training in measurement procedures and instrument calibration. Dietary intake, sleep duration, and physical activity were not measured with objective devices or validated repeated instruments.

Intervention procedures

Dietary and meal-routine counseling

At the initial outpatient consultation, a clinical nutritionist reviewed the protocol with the child and guardian and provided the written guidance shown in Figure 1. Families were instructed to provide three structured meals daily and to avoid caloric foods and drinks between meals. Daily energy guidance ranged from 1,500 to 2,300 kcal according to age, with tailored portions for major food groups. Sugar-sweetened beverages and highly processed snack foods were discouraged; staple packaged foods such as bread, steamed buns, and dumplings could be incorporated within meals. Exact clock times for the three meals were not mandated. Counseling was revisited during routine clinical follow-up, but the number and spacing of contacts varied with attendance.

Sleep and physical-activity components

The original protocol specified at least 7.5 hours of nighttime sleep and lights off no later than 22:30. This was a uniform protocol-defined minimum, not an age-specific recommendation for adequate sleep. It is lower than consensus recommendations for much of the 5–18-year age range and is addressed as a limitation.

Follow-up, adherence assessment, and participant disposition

Follow-up was planned at approximately 2-month intervals for 12 months; in practice, visits occurred according to each family’s clinical availability. At attended visits, anthropometry, BIA-derived body composition, and available fasting metabolic measurements were collected. No standardized dietary diary, sleep log, phone-based reminder record, wearable device, or formal adherence score was used. Consequently, adherence and implementation fidelity could not be quantified or entered into the models. A contemporaneous screening log and standardized visit-disposition record were not available, preventing reliable retrospective reconstruction of numbers screened, excluded before enrollment, completing each planned time point, or lost to follow-up.

Outcomes

The primary anthropometric outcomes analyzed in this report were longitudinal changes in BMI and BAZ. Secondary exploratory outcomes were weight, height, PBF, VFA, SMI, fasting glucose, insulin, triglycerides, total cholesterol, alanine aminotransferase, serum uric acid. Because the pilot protocol did not designate a single confirmatory endpoint, this categorization is used for reporting clarity and the secondary analyses should not be interpreted as confirmatory.

Sample size

No formal a priori power calculation was performed. The sample comprised all 119 consecutive eligible participants enrolled during the predefined recruitment window. The study was exploratory and was not powered to establish clinical efficacy or to estimate uncommon adverse events.

Safety and adverse events

The source records did not include a prospectively defined, standardized adverse-event surveillance dataset. Height and SMI were evaluated as growth and body-composition outcomes, but their trajectories were not treated as substitutes for systematic safety assessment. Counts of adverse events, side effects, and unintended consequences therefore could not be reported reliably.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation when approximately symmetric and as median (interquartile range) when visibly skewed; categorical variables are summarized as n (%). No formal normality test was prespecified. Longitudinal associations between study time and anthropometric, metabolic, and body-composition outcomes were assessed with generalized linear mixed models using all available repeated measurements; missing observations were not imputed. Study visit time was entered as the sole fixed predictor. Thus, the reported coefficient is an average monthly within-cohort association and does not model the nonlinear rebound apparent in several visit-specific trajectories. For graphical presentation, longitudinal trajectories were visualized using GAM smoothing, with the dashed lines representing 95% confidence intervals (CIs). These curves were used for descriptive visualization and were separate from the linear-time mixed-effects models. No separate paired t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, or month-to-month pairwise comparisons were performed. All tests were two-tailed with α=0.05. No adjustment was made for multiple secondary outcomes; these P values are exploratory. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.0 and EmpowerStats (X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

Participant disposition and data completeness

The analytic cohort comprised 119 enrolled children. Because screening and visit-disposition logs were not available in a form that permitted reliable reconstruction, the numbers screened, excluded before enrollment, completing each nominal follow-up time point, and lost to follow-up could not be reported. We therefore did not create a flow diagram with inferred counts. Longitudinal models used the measurements available at attended visits without imputation.

Standardized adverse-event data were also unavailable; absence of a recorded adverse-event count should not be interpreted as evidence that no adverse events occurred.

Anthropometric changes

The fitted weight trajectory showed a U-shaped trajectory: it decreased from approximately 58 kg at baseline to 56 kg during the first 4 months and then increased to approximately 60 kg by month 12 (Figure 2). Because children were growing and there was no comparator, the increase in absolute weight cannot be separated from expected growth or other time-varying influences.

The fitted height trajectory increased from approximately 147 cm at baseline to 153 cm at month 12 (Figure 5). Discussion

This exploratory single-arm study identified early within-cohort decreases in BMI, BAZ, adiposity measures, and several metabolic markers during TMSR. The central finding is not a sustained monotonic response: BMI, BAZ, PBF, VFA, fasting insulin, glucose, and total cholesterol showed partial rebound after their early nadirs. In the absence of a comparator, the observed trajectories may reflect the intervention, natural growth and pubertal change, regression to the mean, seasonal variation, concurrent behaviors, selection and follow-up processes, or combinations of these factors. The data therefore support hypothesis generation rather than claims of efficacy.

The rebound after month 4 is clinically important and suggests limited durability of the initial changes. Plausible explanations include declining adherence, behavioral fatigue, reduced family support over time, compensatory appetite or eating, metabolic adaptation after early weight loss, and changes in school schedules or seasonal activity. Because food intake, sleep, physical activity, and adherence were not objectively recorded, these explanations cannot be distinguished in the present dataset. The pattern indicates that any future TMSR program should include maintenance contacts, objective feedback, and predefined strategies for relapse prevention.

Early decreases in fasting insulin, glucose, triglycerides, and alanine aminotransferase were consistent with improved metabolic status during the first months, but within-person change in an uncontrolled cohort cannot be attributed specifically to meal or sleep timing. The partial rebound in insulin and glucose further argues against describing the metabolic response as uniformly sustained. Lifestyle interventions have demonstrated benefits in the management of childhood overweight and obesity in China (19). Treatment response may vary with age, while broader disruptions in daily routines can also influence obesity-related behaviors and treatment outcomes in children and adolescents (20,21).

Meal timing, sleep, diet quality, and physical activity can each influence energy balance and metabolic regulation. Diet quality and sleep are also closely interrelated in adolescents with obesity (22). The early PBF and VFA patterns are broadly consistent with literature on time-restricted eating and chrononutrition (23-26), but TMSR combined meal structure, energy guidance, avoidance of between-meal calories, and a sleep routine. Without component-specific adherence measures, the contribution of each element cannot be isolated. Longitudinal evidence also suggests an interplay between sleep, eating behaviors, and anthropometric outcomes in adolescents (27).

Height increased and SMI did not show a significant average linear decline during follow-up. These observations are reassuring descriptive findings, but they do not establish safety: normal linear growth is expected in this age range, the study had no comparator, and adverse events were not systematically ascertained. Future studies should prespecify pediatric growth, nutritional adequacy, eating-disorder symptoms, compensatory behaviors, and other adverse outcomes.

TMSR may be reasonable to evaluate as an adjunct to comprehensive pediatric obesity treatment because its behavioral rules are simple. However, feasibility was not quantified, effectiveness was not established, and scalability cannot be inferred from a single-center cohort. Any clinical implementation should remain within age-appropriate, family-centered obesity care. It should not replace established nutrition, activity, behavioral, pharmacologic, or surgical options when indicated.

Meal timing has also been associated with sleep quality (28). Several mechanisms are biologically plausible but were not measured in this study:

Circadian synchronization: regular meal timing could act as a time cue for peripheral clocks involved in glucose and lipid metabolism. Experimental and observational evidence links circadian disruption with impaired insulin sensitivity, but the present study did not measure circadian phase, melatonin, clock-gene expression, or chronotype (29,30).

Overnight fasting and reduced snacking: avoiding calories between three meals may extend the overnight fasting interval and reduce opportunities for unplanned intake. Sleep and appetite regulation: adequate, regular sleep may influence leptin, ghrelin, appetite, and insulin sensitivity. Contemporary guidance identifies intensive health behavior and lifestyle treatment as foundational pediatric obesity care (34). Consensus sleep guidance recommends 10–13 hours per 24 hours for 5-year-olds, 9–12 hours for children aged 6–12 years, and 8–10 hours for adolescents aged 13–18 years (35). Recent pediatric data also associate later meal timing or a longer eating window with less favorable diet quality and metabolic markers (36), while a recent controlled pediatric time-restricted-eating protocol illustrates the value of a comparator, prespecified outcomes, regular follow-up, food diaries, adherence measures, and adverse-event surveillance (37). These developments support further testing of circadian-informed routines but also underscore the design features required before causal or practice-changing claims can be made.

Limitations

First, the absence of a standard-care comparator is the central limitation. Natural growth, puberty, regression to the mean, seasonal variation, maturation, and secular or concurrent behavioral changes could account for part or all of the observed trajectories. The study therefore cannot estimate a treatment effect or support causal claims about TMSR.

Second, implementation fidelity and key time-varying confounders were not measured adequately. Dietary intake, meal times, sleep duration and quality, physical activity, and adherence were not captured with diaries, validated questionnaires, reminders, wearables, or other objective tools. Self-report was not available in a standardized longitudinal form. Consequently, we could not verify exposure, adjust for behavior changes, perform dose-response analyses, or determine whether declining adherence explained the rebound.

Third, the uniform 7.5-hour sleep minimum was below consensus recommendations for most of the age range and may have introduced exposure heterogeneity. Fourth, contemporaneous screening, visit-completion, dropout, and standardized adverse-event records were insufficient to reconstruct a reliable participant flow diagram or characterize attrition and safety. Height gain and a stable average SMI coefficient do not substitute for systematic adverse-event assessment.

Fifth, the single-center convenience cohort limits generalizability. No formal sample size calculation or single confirmatory endpoint was specified, multiple exploratory outcomes were tested without multiplicity adjustment, and linear-time models summarized average slopes despite visibly nonlinear trajectories. Missing data were not imputed, and the assumptions underlying available-case mixed modeling could not be verified. Mechanistic outcomes, pubertal stage, socioeconomic factors, and seasonal exposures were not assessed.

Future research directions

Future research should use prospective multicenter controlled designs, preferably randomized when feasible, with a standard-care comparator; prespecified primary outcomes and sample-size calculations; age-specific sleep targets; fixed follow-up windows; complete screening and retention logs; objective or validated measurement of food intake, meal timing, sleep, and physical activity; repeated adherence assessment; and systematic adverse-event surveillance. Maintenance support should be intensified before and after month 4, and analyses should model nonlinear trajectories and examine whether adherence mediates early response and later rebound.

Conclusions

In this prospective, single-center, single-arm pilot study, several anthropometric and metabolic measures improved during the first 4–6 months of TMSR, followed by partial rebound in BMI, BAZ, adiposity, insulin, and glucose. These preliminary within-cohort associations do not establish efficacy, durability, scalability, or safety because the study lacked a comparator and objective monitoring of diet, sleep, activity, adherence, attrition, and adverse events. Larger controlled multicenter trials with age-appropriate sleep targets and robust implementation and safety measurement are required.
