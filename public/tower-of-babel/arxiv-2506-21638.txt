IRanker: Towards Ranking Foundation Model
Tao Feng, Zhigang Hua, Zijie Lei, Yan Xie, Shuang Yang, Bo Long, Jiaxuan You, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

Abstract
Ranking tasks are ubiquitous, encompassing applications such as recommendation systems, LLM routing, and item re-ranking. We propose to unify these tasks using a single ranking foundation model (FM), as it eliminates the need for designing different models for each specific ranking task. However, unlike general supervision tasks in LLMs, ranking tasks do not have clear labels for supervision, posing great challenges to developing a ranking FM. To overcome these challenges, we propose IRanker, a ranking FM framework with reinforcement learning (RL) and iterative decoding. Our insight is to decompose the complex ranking task into an iterative decoding process that eliminates the worst candidate from the candidate pool step by step, which significantly reduces the output combinatorial space and better utilizes the limited context length during RL training. We meticulously train and comprehensively evaluate an IRanker-3B model on nine datasets across three scenarios: recommendation, routing, and passage ranking. The results show that a single IRanker-3B achieves state-of-the-art results on several datasets compared to models of similar size, and even surpasses the performance of larger models on certain datasets. We further demonstrate the effectiveness of our RL design and the robustness of the iterative mechanism across different LLM sizes. Moreover, we conducted both in-domain and out-of-domain zero-shot generalization experiments, which showed that IRanker-3B achieved good generalization on in-domain ranking tasks compared to the base LLM by at least 5% improvement. Surprisingly, on out-of-domain generic LLM tasks, IRanker-3B outperformed the base model by at least 9% on GSM8K, IFEval, and MathQA. In addition, the thoughts generated by IRanker-3B during training could further enhance zero-shot LLM performance. Our code for IRanker is released at https://github.com/ulab-uiuc/IRanker .

Introduction

Ranking tasks are ubiquitous across various domains, playing a crucial role in applications such as recommendation systems
[
1
,

, large language model (LLM) routing
[

, and passage re-ranking in retrieval-based systems
[

,

. The goal of a ranking task
[
7
,
8
]
is to generate an ordered list of candidates that best satisfy a user’s query. Unifying all ranking tasks using a single Ranking Foundation Model (FM) is crucial, as it eliminates the need to design separate methods for each specific ranking task and generalizes better for unseen ranking tasks. However, due to the semantic differences among various ranking tasks
[

,

and the diversity of supervision signals
[

, traditional embedding-based approaches struggle to effectively address this challenge
[

. LLMs have emerged as one of the most promising approaches for unifying multiple tasks and building foundation models, as they can represent diverse tasks using textual descriptions to align them within a shared semantic space
[
14
,
15
]
. Therefore, our paper aims to raise attention to this pressing research question:
How can we build a ranking FM to unify multiple ranking tasks with LLMs?

Existing studies primarily leverage LLMs to address ranking tasks in two main aspects: LLM-as-embedding methods and LLM-as-ranker methods. The insight of LLM-as-embedding approaches is that instruction-tuned LLMs can generate semantically rich and generalizable representations that enhance performance in various ranking tasks
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
,
e.g.
, LLMEmb uses contrastively fine-tuned LLMs to generate item embeddings for sequential recommendation
[
16
]
.
However, as embedding-based methods, they still struggle to unify task-specific ranking semantics, thus limiting their ability to generalize across diverse real-world ranking tasks
[
5
,
6
,
9
]
. LLM-as-ranker approaches
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
leverage the reasoning and instruction-following capabilities of large language models to directly generate candidate rankings in a unified text-based format,
e.g.
, RankGPT
[
20
]
explores the use of generative LLMs for relevance ranking in information retrieval tasks, and LLM4Ranking
[
23
]
provides a framework that enables users to adopt various ranking methods using open-source or API-based LLMs for document reranking tasks. Despite their promising results, LLMs-as-ranker methods often suffer from limited ability to incorporate downstream feedback for iterative improvement and the lack of explicit supervision signals
[
24
,
25
]
. These challenges significantly hinder the development of a unified and ranking FM with LLMs.

To overcome these challenges, we propose IRanker, a ranking FM framework with reinforcement learning (RL) and iterative decoding. Two major insights contribute to the success of IRanker – RL and iterative decoding. We propose to train a ranking FM with RL, treating the performance feedback of ranking tasks as rewards and incorporating the long-thought ability of LLMs to enhance RL optimization. Here, we considered a simpler approach that directly asks the LLM to generate the candidate rankings and optimizes the rankings according to the rewards with RL, named DRanker. However, since the DRanker needs to compare and rank multiple candidates, it faces challenges such as an excessively large output space and limited context length for thinking.
Instead, our proposed IRanker decomposes the complex ranking task into an iterative decoding process that eliminates the worst candidate from the candidate pool step by step, which significantly reduces the output combinatorial space and better utilizes the limited context length of LLMs during LLM training, leading to a more efficient and effective ranking procedure. To optimize IRanker, we further design a step-wise reward to guide IRanker to first exclude lower-ranked candidates; candidates excluded later receive higher ranks, and the final ranking is constructed by reversing the exclusion order.

We meticulously train and evaluate a unified 3B ranking foundation model, IRanker-3B, on nine representative datasets spanning three scenarios: recommendation, routing, and passage ranking. Our results show that a single IRanker-3B matches or surpasses the domain-specific methods for each task, achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance compared to general ranker baselines, and even surpasses larger 7B LLM models on some tasks and achieves a 15.7% relative improvement on average. We also perform extensive ablation studies, confirming the effectiveness of our RL design and the robustness of the iterative mechanism across different LLM sizes. Additionally, both in-domain and out-of-domain zero-shot generalization experiments demonstrate strong performance: IRanker-3B improves over the base model by at least 5% on in-domain ranking tasks, and by over 9% on out-of-domain tasks such as GSM8K, IFEval, and MathQA. Notably, we show that the intermediate thoughts generated during training further enhance the zero-shot ranking capabilities of other LLMs. For example, based on the 3B backbone model, intermediate thoughts can lead to a 22.87% relative improvement in zero-shot performance for the base model.

Preliminaries

Ranking tasks
.
Given a query
q
, a ranking task
[
7
,
26
,
27
]
is to learn a ranker
f
that ranks a set of candidates
D=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\}
with
n
elements. The candidate set is typically divided into a positive candidate set
D_{p}
and a negative candidate set
D_{n}
. The positive candidate set refers to the items actually selected by the user, for example, the items a user really purchased in a recommendation system. To evaluate how well the ranker recovers these positive candidates, the performance of the ranking task is measured by an evaluator
E
, which is usually a ranking metric such as Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG)
[
28
]
or Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)
[
29
,
1
]
. The goal of the ranker
\pi
is to learn a function that maps a query
q
and its candidate set
D
to a ranking order
O=\{c_{1}^{r_{1}},c_{2}^{r_{2}},\ldots,c_{n}^{r_{n}}\}\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
with
r_{i}
as the rank of candidate
c_{i}
, such that the evaluation metric
E
is maximized:

\pi:(q,D)\rightarrow O,\quad O\in\mathbb{S}_{n},

(1)

where
\mathbb{S}_{n}
denotes the set of all possible permutations over
n=|D|
elements.

Formally, the optimal ranker
\pi^{*}
is learned by solving:

\pi^{*}=\arg\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{(q,D)\sim\mathcal{Z}}\left[E(\pi(q,D))\right],

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}
is the function class and
\mathcal{Z}
is the data distribution over queries and candidate sets.

.

, we have listed some representative instantiations that can be unified into a ranking foundation model from three aspects.
(a) Recommendation
, shown in Figure

(a), the goal of the recommendation
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
is to capture user preferences by analyzing their histories, rank the current set of candidate items, and predict those that the user is most likely to favor. Here, the user history is modeled as query
q
, each element of the candidates is an item, and positive candidate set
D_{p}
contains the real items that the user would choose.
(b) Routing
, routing task
[
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
aims to recommend appropriate LLMs for handling diverse user queries by ranking them based on both effectiveness and response cost, shown in Figure
1
(b). The final LLM recommendation list is generated through a ranking process that balances performance with efficiency. Specifically, a user query is regarded as query
q
, each element of the candidates is a LLM name or LLM description
[
34
]
, and the positive candidate set
D_{p}
means the ground truth LLMs for the query.
(c) Passage ranking
, passage ranking
[
37
,
38
,
9
]
aims to identify and reorder the most relevant passages from a set of candidates given a user query, often arises in retrieval-augmented generation, shown in Figure
1
(c). This is achieved by modeling the relevance between the query and each passage to produce a final ranking list. For this task, the user query is regarded as query
q
, each element of the candidates is a passage, and the positive candidate set
D_{p}
contains the ground truth passages.

3
DRanker: A Basic Ranking FM with Reinforcement Learning

Figure 2:
Framework of our proposed ranking FMs.
Both DRanker and IRanker are RL-enhanced LLM frameworks. They take as input the candidate information in text form, along with user information (such as user history or a query), and utilize the LLM’s reasoning capabilities to produce a final candidate ranking. This ranking is then evaluated by an evaluator to generate a corresponding reward function, which is used to optimize the decision-making of both rankers. The key distinctions are: 1) DRanker generates the full ranking in a single step, whereas IRanker iteratively excludes the least likely item from the candidates to arrive at the final ranking. 2) The reward in DRanker is a ranking reward based on the final candidate ranking, while the reward in IRanker is an exclusion reward given for each individual exclusion decision. 3) DRanker always receives the full set of candidates as input with a fixed size, whereas IRanker’s input candidates are dynamically updated based on the excluded items.

3.1
Unify ranking tasks with LLM

Due to the significant semantic differences among various ranking tasks and the varying supervised signals, traditional embedding-based methods struggle to unify all ranking tasks
[
9
,
5
,
6
]
. To address this issue, we propose DRanker as shown in the upper part of Figure
2
, which maps diverse ranking tasks into a unified text space. DRanker leverages LLMs to prompt both the task query and its candidates, and directly outputs the ranking of the candidates. Specifically, we use a prompt
P
to convert the task query
q
and its candidates
D
into a textual description
P(q,D)
. Motivated by the strong reasoning capabilities of LLMs in enhancing generation quality
[
39
,
40
,
15
,
41
]
, we follow
[
41
]
and augment
P(q,D)
with a system prompt
P_{t}
to encourage reasoning. The final prompt is then fed into the DRanker
\pi_{\theta}
to produce the ranked output:
O_{d}=\pi_{\theta}(P_{t}(q,D))
. The detailed prompts for different ranking tasks can be seen in Appendix
A
.

3.2
Training and inference via RL

Since ranking tasks do not have explicit ground truth, it is difficult to perform supervised learning directly. However, as we explained in Section
2
, the performance of the ranking task is measured by an evaluator
E
. Considering that the evaluator
E
serves as a non-differentiable feedback signal for the DRanker
\pi_{\theta}
, we introduce reinforcement learning to address this problem.

Ranking reward
. We first model the evaluator
E
as a ranking reward
r_{a}=E(O_{d})
, with the objective of maximizing
E
. Specifically, since the number of candidates varies across different ranking tasks, we choose MRR as the evaluator
E
. However, due to the limited instruction-following ability of LLMs, the output candidate rankings often miss or add candidates, which violates the standard requirements of a valid ranking. To regularize the output of the DRanker, we introduce a format reward as a penalty term
r_{g}=\Omega(O_{d},D)-1
, where
\Omega
calculates the F1 score measuring the overlap between
O_{d}
and
D
. Thus, the final reward is defined as
r_{d}=r_{a}+r_{g}
.

Training and inference via PPO
.
To optimize the DRanker
\pi_{\theta}
for maximizing the final reward
r_{d}
, we adopt the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm
[
42
]
, a widely used actor-critic RL method for LLMs. PPO trains the policy by maximizing the following objective:

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(q,D)\sim\mathcal{Q},\,y\sim\pi^{\text{old}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid P_{t}(q,D))}\bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\min\Bigg(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid P_{t}(q,D),y_{<t})}{\pi^{\text{old}}_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid P_{t}(q,D),y_{<t})}A_{t},\\
\text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid P_{t}(q,D),y_{<t})}{\pi^{\text{old}}_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid P_{t}(q,D),y_{<t})},1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\right)A_{t}\Bigg)\bigg]\end{split}

(3)

where
\pi_{\theta}
and
\pi^{\text{old}}_{\theta}
denote the current and previous policies, respectively.
The pair
(q,D)\sim\mathcal{Q}
is sampled from the training distribution, where
q
denotes the input query and
D
the associated candidate set.

y=\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{|y|}\}
is the token-level sequence generated by the policy model.
This sequence is parsed into a ranking candidate list
O_{d}=\texttt{Parse}(y,D)
using a matching function based on string similarity or identifier alignment with
D
. The advantage estimate
A_{t}
is computed using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)
[
43
]
, based on future rewards
\{r_{\geq t}\}
and a learned value function
V_{\phi}
.
The clipping parameter
\epsilon
is used to ensure stable policy updates. After obtaining the optimal
\pi_{\theta}^{*}
, we can obtain the final ranking candidate list
O_{d}=\texttt{Parse}(\pi_{\theta}^{*}(P_{t}(q,D)),D)
.

4
IRanker: Advanced Ranking FM with RL and Iterative Decoding

4.1
Decompose candidates ranking into single candidate selections

Although DRanker can rank candidates in a simple and direct manner, it is limited by the large action space and the constrained context length for reasoning. To address this problem, we propose IRanker, as shown in the lower part of Figure
2
. The insight of IRanker is to decompose the complex ranking task into an iterative process that excludes a candidate from the candidate pool step by step, making the ranking procedure more efficient and effective. Similar to the method introduced in section
3.1
, we design a prompt
P_{d}
(details in Appendix
A
) that encodes the query
q
and the current candidate set
D^{(k)}
, and instructs the IRanker
\pi_{\omega}
to exclude one candidate
c_{k}\in D^{(k)}
at each iteration
k
. The process is defined as:

c_{k}=\pi_{\omega}(P_{d}(q,D^{(k)})),\hskip 14.22636ptD^{(k-1)}=D^{(k)}\setminus\{c_{k}\},\hskip 14.22636ptr_{k}=|D|-k+1

(4)

After
|D|
iterations, we obtain the complete ranking list:

O_{d}=[c_{1}^{r_{1}},c_{2}^{r_{2}},\dots,c_{|D|}^{r_{|D|}}]

(5)

Here, candidates excluded later receive higher ranks, and the final ranking
O_{d}
is constructed by reversing the exclusion order.

Training and inference with via iterative

Exclusion reward
. To train this iterative process, we first define the exclusion reward
r_{e}^{k}
for each exclusion step

:

r_{e}^{k}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }c_{k}\in D_{n}\\

(6)

where

is the negative candidate set illustrated in section

\pi_{\omega}

. Similar to section

, we utilize PPO to optimize the IRanker

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\omega)=\mathbb{E}_{(q,D^{(k)})\sim\mathcal{B},\,y\sim\pi^{\text{old}}_{\omega}(\cdot\mid P_{d}(q,D^{(k)}))}\bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\min\Bigg(\frac{\pi_{\omega}(y_{t}\mid P_{d}(q,D^{(k)}),y_{<t})}{\pi^{\text{old}}_{\omega}(y_{t}\mid P_{d}(q,D^{(k)}),y_{<t})}A_{t},\\

(7)

where
(q,D^{(k)})\sim\mathcal{B}
is sampled from the training distribution;
P_{d}(q,D^{(k)})
is the prompt encoding the query
q
and current candidate pool
D^{(k)}
;
\pi_{\omega}
and
\pi^{\text{old}}_{\omega}
denote the current and previous policies, respectively;
A_{t}
is the estimated advantage at decoding step
t
;
\epsilon
is the PPO clipping threshold;
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{T})
is the response sequence generated by the policy;
c_{k}=\texttt{Parse}(y,D^{(k)})
is the excluded candidate parsed from
y
. Once the optimal
\pi_{\omega}^{*}
is obtained, we can get the final ranking candidate list
O_{d}
with equation (
5
) by repeating the equation (
4
) with
\pi_{\omega}^{*}
.

Experiments

We conduct comprehensive training and evaluation of the proposed ranking FMs, DRanker and IRanker. Notably,
the same ranking FM is being evaluated across diverse 9 interdisciplinary tasks
, which is compared against general ranking methods and domain-specific methods. First, we introduce the tasks within the ranking FM framework.

Task description
. The details of the tasks are summarized across three aspects in Table
1
.

(1) Recommendation (Rec)
: For the recommendation ranking task, we utilize three widely-used sequential recommendation datasets: MovieLens ml-1m
[
44
]
, Amazon’s CD and Vinyl dataset
[
45
,
46
]
, and Amazon’s Video Game dataset
[
45
,
46
]
. For each user across all datasets, following the settings of
[
44
]
, we extracted 20 consecutive interactions as the historical sequence and designated the 21st interaction as the ground truth item. To create a balanced candidate set, we randomly sampled 19 items from the complete item catalog (excluding items in the user’s history and the ground truth item) and combined them with the ground truth item to form a candidate list of 20 items. This approach creates a realistic recommendation scenario with a 5% chance of randomly selecting the relevant item, while maintaining a manageable evaluation space.

(2) Routing (Router)
: For the LLM routing task, following the setting of
[
34
]
, we selected four datasets from
[
34
]
and ten large language models (LLMs). Based on different weights of LLM response effectiveness and cost, we followed three settings in
[
34
]
: Performance First (Performance), Balance, and Cost First (Cost), corresponding to scenarios where users prioritize high performance, value both high performance and low cost equally, or prioritize low cost, respectively.
For each query, we computed the reward based on the weighted sum of effectiveness and cost, and selected the LLM with the highest reward as the ground-truth LLM. The remaining nine LLMs were treated as negative LLMs.

(3) Passage Ranking (Passage)
: For the passage ranking task, we employ the MS MARCO passage dataset
[
47
]
, a large-scale information retrieval benchmark derived from Bing search logs. To evaluate models under different retrieval complexity scenarios, we created three distinct settings with varying candidate passage sizes: 5, 7, and 9 passages per query. For each query, these candidate sets include one relevant passage (as judged by human annotators in the dataset) and 4, 6, or 8 irrelevant passages respectively. This configuration allows us to assess how model performance scales with increasing candidate pool sizes and how effectively models can identify the single relevant passage among varying numbers of distractors.

: A SOTA graph-based router that can trade off between performance and cost. Finally, for the passage ranking task, we implemented three specialized baselines that were pre-trained on the MS MARCO passage dataset: 1)
RankBERT

[
5
]
: A BERT-based passage reranker that fine-tunes the BERT model on MS MARCO relevance judgments, treating ranking as a binary classification problem. It is pretrained on 503k query-passage pairs. 2)
MonoT5

[
52
]
: A T5-based reranker that formulates passage ranking as a sequence-to-sequence task, generating relevant or irrelevant tokens for each query-passage pair. It is pretrained on 503k examples. 3)
RankLLama-8B

[
51
]
: A ranking-specialized version of Llama-2-8B that has been fine-tuned specifically for passage ranking tasks using pairwise and listwise ranking objectives. The model is pretrained on 503k ranking examples.

Implementation details
. We train and evaluate a single ranking FM across all tasks, comparing its performance against both general-purpose ranking baselines and domain-specific methods tailored for each task. For reinforcement learning, we adopt Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
42
]
, following the implementation details provided in VeRL
[
56
]
. Both the DRanker and IRanker are initialized from Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct, and optimized using KL-regularized policy gradients. To manage policy divergence, we incorporate a low-variance KL loss with a regularization coefficient of 1e-4. Rollouts are conducted using vLLM with a maximum GPU memory utilization cap of 40%, temperature set to 0.9, and a maximum response length of 1024 tokens. Training is performed over 5 epochs, using a learning rate of 1e-6 for the actor and 2e-6 for the critic. The optimization uses a global mini-batch size of 36 and a micro-batch size of 8. To ensure memory efficiency, we enable gradient checkpointing and apply Fully Sharded Data Parallelism (FSDP) with both parameter and gradient offloading. All experiments are conducted on a setup of 4 NVIDIA A6000 GPUs.

5.3
IRanker boosts zero-shot performance

To further investigate the generalization ability of IRanker as a foundation model, we conduct analyses from the following three aspects.

IRanker achieved good generalization on in-domain ranking tasks compared to the base model
. We first investigate the in-domain generalization capability of IRanker on ranking tasks, by training it on two different ranking tasks and then conducting zero-shot testing on the target ranking task, reported in Table

. It can be observed that IRanker-3B (zero-shot) outperforms Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct-iter on nearly all tasks and even approaches the performance of IRanker on the Performance and 7 Passages tasks. This demonstrates the strong in-domain generalization ability of IRanker.

Thoughts emerged from IRanker could enhance zero-shot performance of the base model
. To investigate whether the emergent thoughts generated during IRanker training can enhance the zero-shot performance of the base model, we propose IRanker-COT-3B. This model adopts an iterative framework that, for each test query, retrieves semantically similar queries along with their corresponding thoughts produced during IRanker training. These retrieved thoughts are used as templates (details in Appendix

Additional Related Work

Generative LLMs for ranking tasks
. Generative large language models (LLMs) have emerged as powerful tools for ranking tasks beyond traditional recommendation systems, demonstrating strong performance in domains such as information retrieval, document ranking, and sequential recommendation
[
16
,
20
,
21
,
57
,
22
]
. Their ability to understand and generate natural language makes them particularly well-suited for diverse ranking paradigms. Prompting-based methods have explored ways to use LLMs for document relevance ranking
[
58
]
and user-item recommendation
[
44
]
, leveraging their generalization and semantic capabilities to produce effective rankings with minimal architectural modification or fine-tuning. Beyond prompting, instruction tuning and alignment have been adopted to further specialize LLMs for ranking tasks. Approaches such as GPT4Rec
[
59
]
and RankRAG
[
60
]
fine-tune backbone LLMs—either standalone or with external modules—allowing them to better capture ranking-specific signals in domain-adapted scenarios.

Reinforcement learning for LLM-Based ranking
. While LLMs perform well in zero-shot and instruction-tuned ranking settings, RL has been increasingly explored as a mechanism to refine their ranking behavior through reward-driven optimization. Methods such as Rank-R1
[
61
]
employ reinforcement learning to enhance document reranking by encouraging better reasoning over complex input texts. In sequential recommendation, models like comPelling
[
62
]
adopt an RLHF approach inspired by InstructGPT
[
63
]
, aiming to generate recommendations that are both relevant and explainable. Similarly, Rec-R1
[
50
]
applies PPO to iteratively improve user and item representations, enabling closed-loop optimization tailored to personalized recommendation tasks. These efforts highlight the potential of RL to align LLM outputs with downstream ranking goals in both general and user-centric domains.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we address the challenge of unifying diverse ranking tasks by introducing IRanker, an iterative ranking FM optimized via reinforcement learning. By decomposing ranking into a step-wise exclusion process and leveraging the reasoning capabilities of large language models, IRanker overcomes limitations of traditional embedding-based and direct-ranking methods. Our proposed IRanker-3B achieves competitive or state-of-the-art performance across nine datasets from recommendation, routing, and passage ranking scenarios. Extensive experiments demonstrate its strong generalization abilities, achieving over 5% improvement in in-domain zero-shot settings and over 9% gains in out-of-domain LLM tasks. These results highlight IRanker’s effectiveness as a unified and scalable ranking FM framework, setting a foundation for future advances in LLM-based ranking systems with wide applications in recommendation, retrieval, and decision making.

For recommendation tasks, the reasoning process should center on aligning candidate items with the user’s demonstrated preferences across categories such as genre, style, or theme. Effective evaluation involves identifying dominant patterns in the user’s historical choices and comparing them to the attributes of each candidate item. Items are ranked higher if they closely match frequently occurring features in the user’s history, while items that diverge significantly in tone, genre, or thematic elements are deprioritized. Appendix D
Analysis on Computational Efficiency of DRanker and IRanker

We conduct inference time comparisons between DRanker and IRanker using vLLM version 0.6.3 on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU. The evaluation involves batch inference over the entire test set for each respective dataset, employing default vLLM settings.

DRanker (Direct Ranker) performs ranking in a single, comprehensive iteration. This approach means that for each query, it processes the full candidate list at once, which can result in longer output sequences being generated and processed in that single step. Consequently, its average time per query (which is also its time per iteration, as it only has one) can be notable, particularly as the length of the candidate list to be considered in that single pass increases. As shown in
32
, DRanker’s ’Avg / Iter (s)’ reflects this single-pass processing time.

In contrast, IRanker (Iterative Ranker) employs an iterative refinement strategy. This method inherently introduces the potential for additional latency due to multiple processing rounds. However, to mitigate this, IRanker incorporates a crucial early stopping mechanism: if the target positive passage is definitively excluded (or identified, depending on the exact exclusion logic) during an intermediate ranking round, the process for that query is terminated prematurely. This optimization is key, as it avoids unnecessary subsequent iterations.

The impact of these differing strategies is detailed in
32
(and further in
33
and
34
). While IRanker’s ’Total (s)’ inference time is observed to be longer than DRanker’s for the datasets tested, this is a consequence of its multi-iteration nature (’Avg. Iter / Case’ > 1). Crucially, IRanker demonstrates a significantly lower ’Avg / Iter (s)’, meaning each of its individual iterations is faster than DRanker’s single, more comprehensive pass. Furthermore, the early stopping mechanism ensures that the increase in total inference time for IRanker does not scale linearly with the candidate size or the maximum potential number of iterations. This intelligent stopping makes IRanker’s iterative approach more scalable for scenarios with very large candidate pools where exhaustive single-pass ranking might become prohibitively slow, even if its total time is higher in these specific benchmarks. The ’Avg. Iter / Case’ metric reflects the effectiveness of this early stopping.

Appendix F
Broader Impacts

Our work on IRanker, a unified ranking foundation model, has several potential positive societal implications. By creating a single model capable of handling multiple ranking tasks across recommendation systems, LLM routing, and passage retrieval, we significantly increase efficiency and reduce computational resources needed across various applications. This could lead to more sustainable AI deployment and democratize access to high-quality ranking technologies for smaller organizations with limited resources.
The improved zero-shot capabilities demonstrated by IRanker could enhance information retrieval in low-resource domains or for underrepresented languages where task-specific training data is scarce. This has the potential to bridge information access gaps across different communities. Furthermore, IRanker’s ability to perform well on out-of-domain tasks suggests that the techniques developed in this work may have beneficial spillover effects to other AI domains beyond ranking.
Our iterative decoding approach, which decomposes complex ranking tasks into simpler decisions, represents a more interpretable way of understanding how AI systems make ranking decisions. This improved transparency could foster greater trust in recommendation and information retrieval systems. Additionally, the efficient use of context length in our approach enables more effective reasoning with limited computational resources, potentially reducing the environmental footprint of deploying such systems at scale while maintaining high performance.
