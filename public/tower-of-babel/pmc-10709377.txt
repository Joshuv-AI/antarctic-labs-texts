Diagnostic efficacy of intraoperative histopathological examination of lesions with unknown diagnosis suspicious for malignancy
Authors: Tevfik İlker Akçam, Ezgi Köse, Seda Kahraman Aydın, Ahmet Kayahan Tekneci, Dilara Özyiğit Büyüktalancı, Ayşe Gül Ergönül, Ali Özdil, Deniz Nart, Kutsal Turhan, Alpaslan Çakan, Ufuk Çağırıcı
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn this study, the efficiency of intraoperative histopathological examination (frozen section examination; FS) in patients operated per suspected lung malignancy was evaluated.MethodsThe data of 136 patients who underwent surgery in our clinic due to suspected lung malignancy between January 2020 and June 2021 was evaluated prospectively.ResultsThe FS was inconclusive in 7.3 % of the 136 patients. In contrast, the accuracy of differentiating between benign and malignant lesions was 99.2 %, while the rate of false negative was 0.8 % in 126 patients with a prediagnosis. FS examination led to an accurate diagnosis in 91.9 % of the 98 patients without a history of extrapulmonary malignancy (EPM), with a false negativity rate of 1 %, whereas a paraffin-embedded examination was recommended in 7.1 %. The accuracy of the FS was 98.9 % in 91 patients prediagnosed based on an FS, with a false negativity rate of 1.1 %. In the same group of patients, the FS examination was successful in establishing the subtype in 32.9 % of the patients with primary lung cancer (PLC), whereas the efficacy of the FS examination in determining the subtype was better in benign diseases (63.6 % vs 32.9 %, p = 0.009). The FS examination was unable to differentiate between benign and malignant lesions in 92.1 % of patients with EPM but differentiated between primary and metastatic lesions in 48.3 % of patients who had malignancy. Furthermore, FS examination successfully guided surgery in 89 patients with no history of EPM (90.8 %) and 20 patients (52.6 %) with a history of EPM.ConclusionAlthough FS is insufficient in subtyping lung cancers and distinguishing PLC and metastasis, it is an important and effective diagnostic approach with its overall ability to distinguish benign and malignant lesions and guiding surgical procedures.

Introduction

According to World Health Organization (WHO) GLOBOCAN 2020 data, lung cancer is the second most common form of cancer around the world after breast cancer and it is the leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. For this reason, every lung lesion detected on radiological imaging must be investigated for malignancy. Although non-invasive investigations such as spiral computed tomography (CT) and positron emission tomography (PET-CT) scans can determine the malignancy potential of a lesion, histopathological examination still remains the most accurate approach to diagnosis [2,3]. In this case, one of the important points is to identify patients with a high clinical and radiological risk of malignancy. Especially patients with lung lesions with a positive family history, advanced age, smoking, increased fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake on PET-CT, and radiological evidence (increased nodule diameter, presence of solid component, presence of spiculations, etc.) showing disease progression during follow-up should be examined histopathologically. For histopathological diagnosis of nodules in the preoperative diagnosis, relatively minimally invasive procedures such as sputum cytology, bronchoalveolar lavage, bronchoscopy (Transbronchial needle aspiration, Endobronchial ultrasound-transbronchial needle aspiration) and transthoracic needle aspiration biopsy or tru-cut needle biopsy are used [4,5]. That said, the diagnosis of some pulmonary nodules preoperatively using invasive procedures can be difficult due to their small size, sampling problems and localization [6,7]. In this case, some patients with lung lesions that cannot be diagnosed histopathologically are subjected to surgery for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.

Lung resections in which malignancy is not confirmed histologically or cytologically may impose an unfair burden of morbidity on the patient. Furthermore, treatment schemes for small cell lung cancer (SCLC), non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and subtypes of NSCLC differ from each other. Therefore, it is very important to differentiate benign and malignant lesions by FS examination and to determine the subtypes of primary lung carcinomas. Thus, the treatment of patients can be completed in the same anesthesia session. Identifying lung lesions as the primary lung carcinoma or secondary lung cancer (i.e. metastatic lung disease, MLD) in patients with a history of extrapulmonary malignancy (EPM) can also influence the surgical procedure. Parenchyma-preserving surgical procedures are often preferred in cases with suspected lung lesions where an FS examination suggests metastasis, whereas an anatomical resection is performed in eligible patients identified with primary lung malignancy. From all this perspectives, intraoperative histopathological and frozen section examinations can provide the surgeon with vital information that supports the management of diagnosis, treatment and operation decisions. It is thus apparent that diagnoses based on FS examination can steer the surgical treatment approach. This raises a need to determine the diagnostic accuracy and efficacy of FS examinations in terms of their ability to differentiate between benign and malignant lung lesions, and between primary and secondary malignancies, as well as to determine the subtypes of the primary lung cancer.

The present study evaluates the ability of the FS examinations approach to differentiate between benign and malignant lesions, to determine the subtype, and to differentiate between primary and secondary malignancies in patients with primary or metastatic cancer undergoing surgery for lung lesions with an unknown diagnosis in which malignancy is suspected.

Materials and method

The data of 136 patients who underwent surgery and intraoperative FS examination due to lung lesions of unknown diagnosis but suspected malignancy, between January 2020 and June 2021 were evaluated prospectively. Included in the study were 98 patients with such clinical risk factors as a positive family history, advanced age, smoking, increased fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake on PET-CT, radiological evidence (increased nodule diameter, presence of solid component, presence of spiculations, etc.) of disease progression during follow-up and high risk of PLC in whom a histopathological diagnosis could not be established. Also included in the study were 38 patients with a history of EPM in whom lung lesions could not be differentiated as PLC or MLD, but who were suspicious for PLC, and in whom a histopathological examination did not suggest a diagnosis. The patients whose frozen section examination could not be performed due to technical reasons, whose data could not be accessed, and who were diagnosed histopathologically in the preoperative period were excluded from the study. Additionally, EPM patients who had multiple nodules supporting metastatic lung disease, had a history of EPM of less than 5 years, or whose primary disease could not be controlled were excluded from the study. The study was thus conducted with 136 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Wedge resection or enucleation method was applied to patients whose lesions were peripherally located and could be removed completely, while punch biopsy method was applied to patients whose lesions were centrally located and could not be completely removed (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). As a result of, samples were obtained from the lung lesions of patients undergoing surgery under general anesthesia using techniques such as enucleation, wedge resection and punch biopsy, and submitted for FS examination. The course of surgery was determined based on the results of the FS examination. Among the patients diagnosed with PLC in whom the subtype could be determined, those with SCLC underwent surgical resection if appropriate for the disease stage, while those with NSCLC underwent surgical procedures in line with oncological principles to ensure the excision of a sufficient amount of material. Patients diagnosed with MLD underwent metastasectomy in accordance with oncological principles, parenchyma-preserving surgery preferred in patients with malignancies when no PLC-MLD differentiation or subtyping could be made. The operation was terminated in patients in whom the FS failed to support a diagnosis, or in those identified as benign, while surgery proceeded with parenchyma-preserving procedures in line with oncological surgical principles in the remaining participants. The definitive pathology results of patients in whom no diagnosis could be made after completing the surgical treatment and follow-up period were examined. The demographic data of the patients, including age, sex, comorbidities, history of malignancy, surgical procedures employed, results of FS examination and definitive histopathological diagnoses, were recorded. The results of the definitive FS examination pathology reports were reviewed by two experienced pathologists (D.N. and D.Ö.B.) to determine the accuracy of the FS examination data.

The patients were divided into two homogenous groups to allow the accurate analysis of the efficacy and consistency of the FS examination approach, with 98 patients with no history of extrapulmonary malignancy who underwent surgery due to suspected PLC assigned to one group, and 38 patients with a history of extrapulmonary malignancy who underwent surgery due to suspected malignancy assigned to the other group.

In the first stage of the study, the performance and effectiveness of the FS examination approach in differentiating between benign and malignant lesions were analyzed in patients with no history of extrapulmonary malignancy who underwent surgery due to suspected PLC. This group of patients was then divided into malignant and benign groups, based on the result of a definitive post-surgery histopathological examination. The ability of the FS examination approach to recognize and differentiate between the two groups was then investigated.

In the second stage, the ability of the FS examination approach to determine the lesion subtype was investigated through an evaluation of the results of the FS examination together with the results of an examination of paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. The patients were also divided into malignant and benign groups based on the results of a histopathological examination, and the two groups were compared to determine the ability of the FS examination approach to distinguish between subtypes. In addition, the efficacy of the FS examination to distinguish between pathological subtypes was investigated.

In the third stage of the study, the performance of the FS examination in differentiating between benign and malignant lung lesions was investigated in patients with a history of extrapulmonary malignancy. The patients with known malignant lesions were divided into PLC and MLD groups based on their histopathological diagnoses. The ability of the FS examination approach to identify lung lesions as PLC or MLD was then analyzed in these patients, as well as its ability to predict and differentiate between the two groups.

In the fourth and final stage of the study, the ability of the FS examination approach to identify the pathological subtype of the lesion, which would aid in the intraoperative management of surgical procedures and direct the surgical methods used in patients, was examined, along with the effect on the results of the methods used for the collection of specimens for FS examination. Accordingly, the patients were divided into a punch biopsy group and a wedge resection group, and their ability of the FS examination approach to determine the lesion subtypes was compared.

Written informed consent was obtained from each patient or their relatives, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The retrospective study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (No: 22-5.1 T/25).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and as median range (minimum-maximum). Categorical data, in turn, were expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%). A Chi-square test was used for between-group comparisons and a post hoc analysis was applied. All data were evaluated at a 95 % confidence interval, and a statistical significance level of p < 0.05 was determined.

Of the 136 patients included in the study, 92 (67.6 %) were male, and the mean age of the entire sample was 62.5 ± 10.1 years (30–86 years). The specimens collected from the lung lesions underwent FS examination during surgery, and the results were evaluated cumulatively with the final diagnoses. The FS examinations identified, malignant lesions in 100 patients (73.5 %) and benign lesions in 26 patients (19.1 %), while the results were inconclusive in 10 patients (7.3 %). The results of examinations of permanent histopathological sections revealed the lung lesion to be malignant in 105 patients (77.2 %) and benign in 31 patients (22.8 %). The diagnosis was confirmed as benign from the permanent sections of 25 of the 26 patients (96.1 %) in whom the lesion was reported as benign based on an FS examination, with the lesion identified as a minimally invasive adenocarcinoma in the remaining patient (3.9 %). FS examination thus led to an accurate diagnosis in 91.9 % of the patients, with a false negativity rate of 0.7 %, while a paraffin-embedded tissue block examination was recommended in 7.3 %. The accuracy of the FS examination was 99.2 % in 126 patients for whom the FS examination permitted a prediagnosis, while the rate of false negativity was 0.8 % (Table-1).

Included in the study were 38 patients with a history of extrapulmonary malignancy (EPM) and 98 patients with no history of malignancy, who were evaluated separately. The specimens collected from the lung lesions underwent FS examination during surgery and were reported as malignant in 71 (72.5 %) of the 98 patients and benign in 20 (20.4 %), whereas the examination was inconclusive in seven patients (7.1 %) (Table-2). The examination of the permanent histopathological sections revealed the lung lesion to be malignant in 76 patients (77.6 %) and benign in 22 patients (22.8 %) (Table-2). Of these patients, only one patient was to have a benign lesion in the FS examination, with a final diagnosis of minimally invasive adenocarcinoma. Aside from this patient, the results of the examinations of the permanent sections were no different from those identified by the FS examination as either malignant or benign. In the results of the examination of the permanent sections of the seven patients in whom the FS examination was inconclusive, the lesion was reported as benign in three and malignant in four. Discussion

Definitive diagnoses of lung cancer are based on pathological examinations of tissue preparations sourced from primary or metastatic tumors or are based on a cytological examination of malignant effusions. The purpose of pathological examinations is to differentiate tumors from such tumor-like conditions as infections, inflammatory masses, immunological disorders, developmental abnormalities and pneumoconiosis, and to establish a histopathological tumor diagnosis [8]. Most patients undergo surgery for lung lesions after a preliminary histopathological diagnosis has been made, while others undergo surgery for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes, even in the absence of a histopathological diagnosis from preoperative investigations. That said, very few surgeons would consider carrying out a lung resection without histological or cytological confirmation of malignancy. Intraoperative histopathological examinations provide surgeons with peerless information to support the diagnosis, treatment and management of surgical decisions. Intraoperative FS examinations can identify radiologically unspecified lesions as malignant or benign during surgery, can confirm the adequacy of a specimen that indicates a need for further specific molecular or chromosomal studies, can determine the grade and stage of lung cancer, and can evaluate the conformance of the surgical margin with the principles of oncologic surgery [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]].

To attain the best results from an FS examination, the pathologist must have full knowledge of the patient's clinical history, radiological findings, surgical procedure employed, reason for requesting a pathological consultation, and the possible effects of an intraoperative diagnosis on the surgical treatment. On the other hand, poor communication, inadequate sampling, technical artifacts and interpretation errors are factors that can limit the accuracy of FS examinations [8]. Furthermore, unfavorable factors such as impaired cytoarchitecture, the formation of ice crystals and the complete collapse of alveolar spaces during cryosection in pulmonary nodules can complicate the diagnostic accuracy of the FS examination approach [6,15]. Especially in the evaluation of gastrointestinal and thoracic pathologies, the error rate of FS is higher than other patient groups [16]. This can lead to a delay in the diagnosis of some patients, meaning that second surgical procedures may be required under general anesthesia after a final diagnosis has been established based on an examination of permanent sections cut from paraffin-embedded blocks [15]. The need for FS examinations in pulmonary nodules differs from other cancer types, being the most useful pathological examination for determining the scope of the surgical procedure. The results of an FS examination during surgery may lead to the termination of the surgical procedure in some patients, or may prompt lung resection in others. The accuracy of FS examinations has been verified in many cancer types in previous studies, and its efficacy has been well established.

A false negative rate of 2%–13.1 % and false positive rates of 0%–0.2 %, compared to definitive pathology results, are among the most important problems of FS [17,18]. In a study of 1000 patients involving all tissue types, Oneson et al. [19] reported an overall accuracy rate of 91 %, a false negativity rate of 2 % and a false positivity rate of 0.2 % for FS examination, while an FS examination did not yield a prediagnosis in 6 % of the patients. The reported false positive and false negative rates were approximately 1 % among thoracic specimens, which account for 10 % of the tissues submitted for examination [19]. In a multicenter study of 1952 patients conducted by Howanitz et al. the FS examination approach was nondiagnostic in 3.9 % of the patients, while the accuracy was 96.5 % in patients in which the FS examination suggested a diagnosis [20]. In another multicenter study involving approximately 80,000 patients, the final diagnosis was pending in 4.2 % of the patients until the results from paraffin-embedded tissue blocks became available, while the overall concordance of the FS diagnoses with the final diagnoses was 98.3 % in the patients for whom the FS examination suggested a diagnosis [11]. Imai et al. in their series of 169 patients with undiagnosed lung tumors, reported the overall definitive diagnosis rate of FS, the rate of identified tumor origin, and the rate of identified histological type as 76.92 %, 85.80 %, and 90.53 %, respectively [17]. In a study by Gephardt et al. involving a series of 122 patients with lung cancer, the sensitivity, specificity and false negativity rates reported for intraoperative FS examinations of the lymph nodes were 95 %, 100 % and 1.6 %, respectively [21]. Sugiyama et al. reported in their series of 206 patients, the diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy were 98.2 %, 100 %, 100 %, 92.1 % and 98.5 %, respectively, for FS [22]. In recent years, cytological examinations have been used alongside intraoperative FS examinations and sometimes yield results as effective as FS examinations [23]. In a study of patients undergoing thoracic surgery who underwent a cytological examination as part of an intraoperative pathological examination, Biancosino et al. reported a diagnostic sensitivity of 82 %, a specificity of 99 %, an NPV of 86 % and a PPV of 99 % for cytological examination [24]. Mohamed et al. in their study comparing the effectiveness of FS examination with cytological examination of intraoperative fine needle aspiration materials in thoracic malignancies, reported that the sensitivity of FS was 97 % and the specificity was 99 % [25]. In their study, Sirmali et al. compared FS diagnoses with final diagnoses derived from permanent sections in a series of 721 patients undergoing thoracic surgery, and reported a false negative rate of 1.9 % and a false positive rate of 0.2 % [26]. In a study by Clark et al. no false positivity was observed in a series of 36 patients undergoing FS examination for lung cancer staging, while the sensitivity and specificity of the FS examinations were 94.1 % and 100 %, respectively [27]. Concurring with literature, the accuracy was 91.9 % and the false negative rate was 0.7 % in the present study, while the results of the pathological examination of paraffin-embedded tissue blocks was delayed in 7.4 %. The accuracy of the FS examination approach in 126 patients in whom the FS examination suggested a diagnosis was 99.2 %, with a false negative rate of 0.8 %.

Although the FS examination approach can, in general, be considered a successful diagnostic instrument, its performance in diagnosing benign pulmonary lesions differs from its performance with malignant lesions. For this reason, the efficacy of FS examination in diagnosing benign and malignant lesions must be evaluated as a separate study area, as there have been few studies to date addressing this issue. In a series of patients undergoing surgery due to suspected lung malignancy, Nashef et al. reported a sensitivity of 94 % for FS examination in the identification of malignant lesions and 90 % for benign lesions [28]. In a series of 522 patients undergoing cytological examination, Lee et al. reported a diagnostic accuracy of 92.9 % in benign tumors and 97.2 % in malignant tumors [29]. In the present study, there was little difference in the sensitivity of the FS examination approach in patients with a history of extrapulmonary malignancy for benign and malignant lung lesions (93.4 % vs. 86.4 %, p = 0.287).

The lungs are the most common location for metastatic diseases associated with extrapulmonary tumors. The differentiation of PLC from pulmonary metastasis in patients with a history of EPM can be difficult due to histological resemblance of the two conditions [15]. Determining whether a lesion reported as malignant during surgery is PLC or metastatic lung disease, however, can affect the surgical procedures to be followed as PLC may require additional resections, while a parenchyma-preserving limited resection may suffice in metastatic lung disease [30]. The treatment algorithms following surgery also differ from patient to patient, and so it is, therefore, important for an FS examination to differentiate between PLC and metastatic disease. A history of EPM, the presence of multiple nodules and the peripheral localization of nodules may suggest metastatic disease, although some features may similarly point to PLC, such as the use of TTF-1 during an FS examination [31]. None of these characteristics, however, are diagnostic, and do not rule out other diagnoses [32].

There have been few studies to date investigating the efficacy of the FS examination approach in patients with a history of EPM and pulmonary nodules. In a study of patients with pulmonary nodules and a positive history of malignancy, Konno et al. reported a success rate of 83.3 % of the FS examination approach to the differentiation of PLC from metastasis, while the approach was nondiagnostic in 14.6 % and yielded a false diagnosis in 2.1 % of the patients, and was inconclusive in three of the 38 patients (7.9 %) with a history of EPM, yielding an overall diagnostic accuracy of 92.1 % [33]. In these patients, the FS examination approach was better able to detect malignant lesions than benign lesions (100 % vs. 66.7 %, p = 0.001). In addition, the FS examination was able to differentiate between primary and metastatic lesions in 48.3 % of the patients, while its performance was the same in differentiating between PLC and metastatic lesions. It was thus concluded that the FS examination approach was able to clearly differentiate between malignant and benign lesions in patients with a history of EPM, while its efficacy was low in differentiating between PLC and metastasis.

It is well known that patients exhibit different prognostic courses depending on the histopathological characteristics of the tumor, and surgical and systemic treatment algorithms can vary greatly. Accordingly, several different investigations and diagnostic approaches have been described, such as those analyzing histopathological sub-characteristics, immunohistochemical staining methods and mutation analyses [8]. While is important to garner information not only for the differentiation of SCLC and NSCLC, but also for the specific subtypes of NSCLC, many pathologists prefer to defer making a definitive diagnosis until they have the results of an examination of paraffin-embedded blocks due not only to the technical difficulties associated with FE examinations, but also medicolegal concerns, and report the result of FS examination as benign or malignant without commenting on the subtype [28,[34], [35], [36]]. There have been a limited number of studies to date investigating the intraoperative histopathological subtyping of lesions reported as malignant following an FS examination (SCLC, NSCLC, SCC or AC, etc.), and the majority of those that are available are based on the differentiation of adenomatous hyperplasia, adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) in patients known to have adenocarcinoma. Even in this patient group, however, the accuracy of the FS examination approach in differentiating between AIS and MIA ranges between 46 % and 74 %, and this finding is one of the leading indications of the low efficacy of the FS examination approach for subtyping [36,37].

He et al. In their series of 136 cases consisting of minimally invasive adenocarcinoma and adenocarcinoma in stu patients, the overall accuracy rate of FS was reported as 63.24 %, while the rate of diagnosing adenocarcinoma was reported as 32.35 % [38]. When the FS examination results were evaluated together with those from the examination of permanent sections, the overall performance of the FS examination in subtyping was 39.8 %, while its performance was 36.2 % for adenocarcinomas, 29.4 % for SCC, 100 % for SCLC and 63.6 % for benign lung lesions, allowing a definitive intraoperative diagnosis in these patients. In addition, the efficacy of FS examination in subtyping was higher for benign lesions than for malignant lesions (63.6 % vs. 32.9 %, p = 0.009).

Qian et al. In their study for the intraoperative diagnosis and management of solitary pulmonary nodules, they reported that the overall accuracy of FS was 61.3 % and that as a result of the combination of clinical features and FS, adequate surgical treatment was applied in 81.2 % of the cases [39]. Konno et al. While the overall accuracy rate of FS was reported as 83.3 % and the rate of incorrect evaluation as 2 %, a definitive diagnosis could not be reached with FS in 14.6 % of the cases. However, as a result of the decisions taken with the help of FS, the surgical treatment of 91 % of the cases was adequate [33]. In our study, FS successfully guided the surgical treatment in 89 of 98 cases (90.8 %) without a history of EPM and in 20 of 38 cases (52.6 %) with a history of EPM, thus helping to organize the surgical treatment of a total of 109 cases (80.1 %).

The present study, which is the first prospective study to date evaluating the efficacy of the FS examination approach in all aspects, revealed it to have high diagnostic accuracy and sensitivity in differentiating between benign and malignant lesions, but to be particularly successful in determining malignant lesions than benign lesions in all patients and those with a history of EPM. It was shown, however, to have only limited efficacy in differentiating between PLC and metastasis in patients with a history of EPM. The ability of the FS examination approach to determine subtypes was greater for benign diseases than malignant lesions, although its ability to determine subtypes was not high in either group due to the technical difficulties associated with FS examinations and medicolegal concerns. Furthermore, no significant relationship was found between the surgical procedure employed for intraoperative sampling and the efficacy of the FS examination approach. The most remarkable data finding related to the FS examination approach is the low rate of false negatives and the almost complete absence of false positive results. It can thus be concluded that the FS examination approach prevents patients from undergoing unnecessarily extensive lung resections, while indicating the necessary extent of surgical procedures in the majority of patients.

Conclusion

Intraoperative pathological examinations of lung lesions can aid in the determination of an intraoperative diagnosis, steering the treatment and management of pulmonary diseases. It must be kept in mind, however, that the intraoperative diagnosis of some lesions may be challenging, that the pathologist may not be able to make a definitive classification of a lesion during surgery, and that the examination may yield false results in a small number of patients. The FS examination approach can thus be considered an important and effective diagnostic tool, considering its overall accuracy rate and its ability to differentiate between benign and malignant lesions, and thus guide surgical procedures. The most remarkable finding related to the efficacy of FS examination is the low false negativity rate and the almost complete absence of false positive results, which can prevent patients from undergoing unnecessarily extensive lung resections, while indicating the necessary extent of surgical procedures in the majority of patients. It must be kept in mind, however, that an FS examination cannot sufficiently differentiate between PLC and metastasis, nor can it aid in the determination of the subtypes of lung cancer. The advances in histopathological diagnostic methods and techniques, as well as the clinical experience of the pathologist, can have a profound effect on the results of FS examinations. For this reason, the efficacy of FS examinations must be further evaluated through multicenter studies involving larger series of patients.

Limitations

The present study has some limitations. Firstly, the results of the present study cannot be generalized due to the heterogenous nature of the study group, which includes patients with various malignancies. Furthermore, the accuracy of intraoperative frozen section examinations in pulmonary nodules is related directly to the previous cancer type and the likelihood of malignancy before the examination. Secondly, the verification method used to differentiate between a second PLC and pulmonary metastasis is quite confusing, and is largely dependent on the experience and diagnostic skills of the pathologist. Thirdly, this study features only a short period of observation, and so data related to long-term outcomes is lacking, which would have allowed data on the appearance of a second lesion or multiple lesions to be taken into account. Despite these limitations, the present study contributes to literature as the first prospective study to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of FS examination in pulmonary lesions.
