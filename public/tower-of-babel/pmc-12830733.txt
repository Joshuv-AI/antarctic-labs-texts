Integrated anaerobic membrane bioreactor-yeast biorefinery for co-production of hydrogen, volatile fatty acids, and microbial oil from food waste
Authors: Prawat Sukphun, Panitan Jutaporn, Alissara Reungsang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With growing food waste challenges globally, acidogenic fermentation using an anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) was developed for the co-production of hydrogen, volatile fatty acids (VFAs), and microbial oil from food waste. Batch experiments identified optimal conditions of pH 6.0 and substrate/inoculum ratio of 3:1, which were applied in a semi-continuous AnMBR with in situ VFA recovery. At an organic loading rate of 3.3 g-VS L-1·d-1, the system achieved hydrogen production of 223.9 ± 3.4 mL L-1·d-1 and VFA productivity of 1.68 ± 0.15 g-CODVFA L-1·d-1. A critical total suspended solids threshold of 15.7 g L-1 was identified for stable membrane performance. The VFA-rich permeate was dominated by acetic and butyric acids. Cultivation of Candida tropicalis on the VFA-rich permeate resulted in biomass and lipid yields of 0.47 and 0.15 g g-VFAconsumed-1, respectively, with 94.2% VFA consumption. These findings indicate that the integrated AnMBR-yeast system offers promising potential as a biorefinery platform for converting food waste into multiple value-added bioproducts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32939-2.

Introduction

With economic development, a number of environmental problems have gradually been exposed. Nearly one-third of global food is lost or wasted along the food supply chain, contributing to 6% of annual global greenhouse gas emissions, equivalent to 3.3 gigatons (Gt) of carbon dioxide1. This insight has triggered awareness and efforts to recover maximum resources from food waste in environmentally friendly ways for sustainable waste management. Although food waste could be upcycled as feed for food-producing animals, this practice is prohibited in the United States and European Union due to the risk of pathogen contamination2. On the other hand, the natural decomposition of untreated food waste in landfills leads to resource dissipation and causes significant negative environmental impacts, primarily through the release of methane, a potent greenhouse gas. Therefore, food waste management has become a serious problem worldwide that urgently needs to be addressed3.

Anaerobic digestion technology has been effectively employed in full-scale applications for waste treatment and energy recovery. This process consists of four main stages involving a series of biochemical reactions converting biodegradable materials into biogas (a mixture of methane and carbon dioxide) in the absence of oxygen. Recent studies have exploited the initial metabolic pathways of anaerobic digestion, known as acidogenic fermentation, in which organic matter is degraded while producing hydrogen and volatile fatty acids (VFAs). These products are considered to offer greater economic benefits compared to biogas. One of the key advantages of hydrogen is its high energy density (122 MJ/kg) and the fact that it releases only water upon combustion, making it a zero-emission fuel. Similarly, VFAs are recognized as crucial precursors for biorefineries due to their versatile applications. Currently, the commercial production of hydrogen and VFAs depends on non-renewable petrochemical pathways, which contribute to environmental impacts and the gradual depletion of fossil fuels4. Accordingly, producing VFAs from renewable resources has recently attracted global attention as a strategy to promote circular resource utilization and substantially reduce environmental burdens at multi-levels.

Previous studies have demonstrated that pH exerts a strong influence on both hydrogen production and the VFA profile in the acidogenic fermentation of food waste. The optimal pH for biohydrogen production from food waste has been reported to range between 5.0 and 6.0, where hydrogenase activity is maximized and methanogenic activity is suppressed5. Slightly acidic conditions (pH 6.0–6.5) favor the butyrate-fermentation pathway; in fact, the key enzyme associated with butyrate formation has been shown to have the highest activity at pH 6.36. Additionally, this pH range consistently yields higher hydrogen7,8. In contrast, the alkaline or near-neutral conditions tend to shift fermentation toward propionate formation, which is less favorable for hydrogen production9. Thus, understanding pH-dependent fermentation pathways is essential for maximizing hydrogen and VFA yields and enhancing the efficiency of downstream VFA recovery.

Over the past decades, acidogenic fermentation has been extensively studied for various applications aimed at maximizing VFA production and applications, such as bioplastics, chemicals, and biological treatment of wastewater10. However, a major bottleneck hindering the practical application of VFAs produced by acidogenic fermentation lies in challenging recovery due to typically low concentrations and complex nature11. Researchers have investigated several technologies for VFA recovery from complex mixtures, including electrodialysis12, liquid–liquid extraction13, and vapor permeation membrane contactor14 Nonetheless, their practical application remains limited due to high operational costs and relatively low effectiveness. In this context, anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) has been increasingly applied in acidogenic fermentation to enhance VFA recovery and stabilize the co-production of hydrogen and VFAs, linking the gap between fermentation performance and downstream separation.

Pressure-driven membrane processes are often preferred because they require minimal pretreatment and simple operation10. Additionally, pressure-driven recovery enables flexible process design for subsequent VFA utilization. For example, Parchami et al.15 and Jomnonkhaow et al.16 employed submerged flat-sheet microfiltration membranes during long-term, high-solids fermentation. Additionally, several studies have integrated membrane filtration processes to separate VFA from fermented media for other potential use, such as polyhydroxyalkanoates17 and edible fungi18.

VFAs are highly promising feedstocks for microbial lipid production owing to higher theoretical yield and rapid conversion rate compared to sugar-based substrate. This is because VFAs can be utilized by oleaginous yeast through the direct conversion into acetyl-CoA, a building block of fatty acids used for lipid accumulation and ATP synthesis for cell proliferation in the TCA cycle19. Furthermore, microbial lipids are potential platform chemicals that can be converted to biodiesel, linear alpha-olefin, bio-naphtha, and jet fuel by further catalytic reactions20. The key advantages of oleaginous yeast cultivation include fewer requirements for land, labor, and resources compared to the production of vegetable oils at similar productivity21. Additionally, the current production of commercial oleochemicals relies on vegetable oils and animal fats, raising concerns about food competition22. Although microbial lipids have an estimated market price of about 0.9–1.3 USD per kg, comparable to vegetable oils, their commercialization is still limited mainly because of the high cost of substrates21. Therefore, utilizing waste-derived VFAs as a carbon source for microbial lipid production could significantly enhance overall process cost-effectiveness and further support the biorefinery concept.

The objective of this study is to develop acidogenic fermentation using an AnMBR system, that allows for the recovery of VFA-rich permeate concurrent with hydrogen production. Subsequently, VFA-rich permeate would be fed as a cost-effective substrate for oleaginous yeast cultivation. The crucial parameters on acidogenic fermentation of food waste were optimized in batch experiments. Thereafter, the co-production of hydrogen and VFAs, as well as the filtration performance in terms of filtration flux were evaluated as a function of operational organic loading rates (OLRs). The feasibility of utilizing VFA-rich permeate was analyzed from different aspects including microbial specific growth rate, product yields and productivities, and VFA consumption rates. Consequently, the present study could establish a comprehensive scientific framework for food waste upcycling using coupled bioprocesses.

Materials and methods

Substrate and inoculum

Fresh food waste was taken from the central cafeteria in Khon Kaen University, Thailand. The inert materials, such as animal bones, chopsticks, and plastic bags, were sorted out manually. Food waste was then made into a slurry using a food homogenizer, and then the blended food waste was aliquoted into plastic bottles and stored in a freezer at a temperature of −20 °C to prevent natural decomposition.

Anaerobic sludge granules were obtained from a UASB at a brewery wastewater treatment plant (Khon Kaen Brewery, Thailand) and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C. The granules were washed with tap water to eliminate inorganic contaminants and placed in an oven at 105 °C for 3 h to ensure inactivation of methanogens23. The dried granules were enriched in 10 g L−1 glucose solution supplemented with 1 mL L−1 of nutrient medium and incubated in an orbital shaker for 2 days before use as inoculum. The feedstock and inoculum were physicochemically characterized in Table S1 as the supplementary material.

Batch experiment on acidogenic fermentation

In this study, different conditions of acidogenic fermentation were conducted in 120-mL serum bottles with a working volume of 70 mL. To determine optimal conditions for VFA production, two key parameters were systematically investigated based on the one-factor-at-a-time approach at a total concentration of organic matter of 10 g-VS L−1. The initial pH varied, ranging from 5.0 to 7.0 with 0.5 increments, while the substrate-to-inoculum (SIR) ratio was constant at 1:1. Then, the SIR was adjusted to ratios of 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, and 5:1 (on a VS basis), respectively, at the optimal pH condition. These two parameters were selected based on the conditions with the highest VFA yield in literature. Prior to incubation, bottles were tightly capped with rubber stoppers and aluminum caps and subsequently purged with nitrogen gas for 10 min to remove the residual oxygen from the headspace. The experiments were carried out in triplicate in an orbital shaker at a temperature of 35 ± 3 °C and 150 rpm for 7 days.

Anaerobic membrane bioreactor design and operation

The semi-continuous AnMBR was operated in a 3.5-L glass vessel with a 2-L working volume. The reactor was equipped with a submerged hollow-fiber membrane module and reactor controller system (Figure S1). The pH was maintained by an automatic dosing pump feeding 1 M NaOH solution, ensuring the optimal level of 5.8–6.2 for acidogenic fermentation. The fermentation was run under a temperature of 35 ± 2 °C and an agitation rate of 150 rpm. The top plate was connected to a water displacement gas counter, which was used to record volumetric biogas production. Food waste was fed into the reactor once every day with the constant hydraulic retention times (HRTs) of 4 days, and the OLRs were stepwise increased when the pseudo-steady state was attained, indicated by less than 10% of the variation of produced gas. The operational conditions of AnMBR were shown in Table S2. The hollow fiber membrane module was made up of eight polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) filaments (STERAPORE™ 5000 series), purchased from Mitsubishi Chemical Corp., Ltd., Japan (Table S3), with a nominal pore size of 0.4 μm. Membrane filaments were potted into a rod-shaped polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), providing an effective filtration area of 112 cm2. Each filament was enclosed in a PTFE housing, with epoxy resin applied to seal the lower dead-end, while the upper end remained open. The upper open-end was directly connected to a peristaltic pump, which was used to withdraw the permeate in an “outside-in” flow regime. The membrane module was submerged in the reactor and was operated once daily through batch filtration by performing the filtration cycle before daily feeding. The filtration cycle consisted of 4 min forward filtration, 30 s backwashing, and a 30 s rest phase. The pressure gauge was fixed between the suction pump and membrane module to monitor the transmembrane pressure (TMP). The filtration process was maintained at a constant pressure of 40 kPa. During the filtration cycle, high-purity nitrogen gas was intermittently purged into the liquid phase to avoid negative pressure and to prevent membrane fouling. The chemical cleaning was performed offline once the cake layer formed on the membrane surface and at the end of each experimental phase. The membrane module was carefully flushed using distilled water and cleaned using the following steps: Soaking in 1% w/v NaClO solution followed by 1% w/v NaOH solution with an air sparging for 3 h. After chemical cleaning, the membrane was rinsed using DI water and tested for membrane permeability in a clean-water tank. The permeate flow rate was measured as the volumetric flow collected in a cylinder over a certain time by manual recording. The permeate rich in VFAs was collected in a freezer at −20°C for later use as a carbon source for yeast cultivation. It should be mentioned that the same membrane was used throughout the experiment without change. The membrane permeability was determined according to Trad et al.24. Filtration of clean water was performed at several TMP levels in the range of 10–40 kPa and corresponding fluxes were measured at each point. The relationship between flux and TMP is linear. Oleaginous yeast preparation and cultivation on VFA-rich effluent

The isolated strain ARY3, identified as Candida tropicalis, was maintained in yeast extract-peptone-dextrose (YPD) agar plates at 4 °C. The compositions of YPD medium were 20 g L−1 glucose, 20 g L−1 peptone, and 10 g L−1 yeast extract. Subculturing was performed every three months to maintain the culture in active form. Before inoculation, pre-culturing was conducted by transferring one full loop of the strain from the agar plate into a 250-mL flask with 50 mL of YPD liquid medium and incubating at 30 ± 2 °C, 120 rpm for 24 h. After reaching the late exponential growth phase, 2 mL of seed culture was collected and centrifuged at 6700 × g for 5 min and resuspended twice with 0.7% w/v NaCl solution. Subsequently, the cell suspension was used to determine the optical density at 600 nm (OD600). The remaining culture was used as an inoculum for lipid production.

The ARY3 strain cultivations were performed in 250-mL Erlenmeyer flasks filled with 100 mL of autoclaved VFA-rich permeate. The medium pH was controlled at 6.0 ± 0.2 and supplemented with a sterile synthetic mineral medium containing, in g L−1: KH2PO4, 7.0; Na2HPO4, 2.5; MgSO4·7H2O, 1.5; CaCl2, 0.15; FeCl3·7H2O, 0.15; ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.02; (NH4)2SO4, 0.5; yeast extract, 0.5; without an external carbon source26. A 1 mL pre-culture was initially inoculated into the flask to obtain the initial OD600 of approximately 0.3 and was incubated in an orbital shaker at 25 ± 3 °C and 150 rpm for 6 days.

Analytical methods

The total solids (TS) and volatile solids (VS) were analyzed according to the standard methods27. Liquid samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm polypropylene syringe filter before quantifying VFAs and lactic acid (HLa) concentrations using a high-performance liquid chromatograph (LC-10AD, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a refractive index detector (RID) and Aminex HPX-87H column (Biorad Inc., USA)28. The biogas volume was measured using the gas-tight syringe method29. The hydrogen fraction in biogas was analyzed by a gas chromatograph (GC-2014, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a column packed with Shin carbon (50/80 mesh)30. During the flask cultivation, cell growth was measured by spectrophotometry as optical density at 600 nm. To determine cell dry weight, 2 mL of culture were collected in pre-weighted micro centrifuge tubes. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6700 × g for 5 min and washed twice with 0.7% w/v NaCl solution and subsequently dried in a hot-air oven at 60 °C to a constant weight. The cell dry weight in grams per liter of sample was determined by the gravimetrical method. To quantify the lipid content, the cells were collected in the same manner mentioned previously. Commercial canola oil was dissolved in chloroform (10 mg in 10 mL for a final concentration of 1 mg L−1) to prepare standard lipid samples. Standard lipid samples were prepared at different concentrations ranging between 5–100 μg L−1 in clean glass vials. The vials were then evaporated at 60 °C before quantifying the lipid content. Oven-dried cells and standard lipid samples were simultaneously analyzed for lipid content using the colorimetric method based on the sulfo-phospho-vanillin (SPV) reaction as proposed by Byreddy et al.31.

Results and discussion

This study assessed biohydrogen and VFA co-production through batch optimization and then applied these conditions in a semi-continuous AnMBR for in situ VFA recovery, after which the VFA-rich permeate served as the substrate for microbial lipid production.

Batch experiments of acidogenic fermentation

Figure 1 shows the influence of pH and the SIR on hydrogen production from food waste. As shown in Fig. 1a, the highest hydrogen yield (HY) was achieved at a pH of 6.0. However, the HYs obtained at pH values between 5.0 and 6.5 were not significantly different, ranging from 50.4 to 55.5 mL g-VS−1. In contrast, the lowest HY of 39.1 ± 2.2 mL g-VS−1 was observed at a pH of 7.0. Figure 1b demonstrates that all treatments reached peak hydrogen production at 24 h, which then declined and ceased within 72 h of fermentation. These results evidence that the slightly acidic conditions are favorable for hydrogen production. Considering the metabolic pathway, hydrogen production is accompanied by the formation of VFAs. High hydrogen production correlates with acetate/butyrate formation, whereas low hydrogen production is associated with propionate formation. This is because propionate has a lower oxidation state and requires electron uptake, thereby consuming reducing equivalents that would otherwise be directed toward hydrogen production32. Neutral pH has been reported to accelerate the hydrolysis rate, leading to rapid VFA accumulation, followed by a sharp drop and subsequent inhibition of hydrogen-producing bacteria (HPB)33. In contrast, at lower pH levels, hydrogen producers adapted to the acidic conditions, generating hydrogen at moderate levels without substantial acid accumulation. As a result, the pH remained more stable, allowing hydrogen producers to remain active for a longer period. Since the operating pH determined the relative proportions of key soluble metabolites in the fermentation system, the present findings are consistent with previous study indicating that slightly acidic conditions (up to pH 6.0) promote butyrate formation38. These results are consistent with the strong production of HAc and HBu under acidic conditions reported by Jankowska et al.39. Nonetheless, different outcomes have been documented, where HAc and HBu were identified as the dominant metabolites under neutral or alkaline conditions. Lu et al40. observed that the acetate/butyrate pathway was favored under alkaline and neutral pH due to enhanced hydrolysis efficiency, resulting in greater fermentation stability and overall process performance compared to acidic conditions.

A pH of 6.0 was then carried out to investigate the effects of SIR on acidogenic fermentation. Figure 1c,d show that the highest HY of 84.6 ± 6.3 mL g-VS−1 was achieved at 3:1 SIR. HY increased with increasing SIR. However, at SIRs of 4:1 and 5:1, the HYs were only 69.7 ± 7.1 and 62.8 ± 3.2 mL g-VS−1 each, respectively, which were 18% and 26% lower than that of an SIR of 3:1. Figure 1d shows that SIRs of 3:1, 4:1, and 5:1 followed a similar trend of rapid increase in hydrogen production during the first 12 h, reaching their maximum HYs within 48 h. In contrast, the peak hydrogen production for lower SIRs (1:1 and 2:1) occurred at 24 h. These results suggest that higher SIR promotes hydrogen production efficiency in terms of both hydrogen production rate (HPR) and HY. Carrillo-Verástegui et al.23 evaluated the effect of SIR on the dark fermentation of biomass hydrolysate and revealed that an SIR of 5:1 yielded the highest production of both VFAs and hydrogen. Similarly, in a study conducted by Nathao et al.41, the use of a high SIR of 7.5 resulted in the highest HY of 55 mL g-VS−1 when using food waste as the feedstock. It is apparent that the optimal SIR exhibits variability across studies, which can be attributed to differences in the substrate composition and the physicochemical properties of the inoculum42,43.

It is noteworthy that the performance may decline if the SIR is too high. Existing literature suggests that, due to the presence of indigenous microbes, the substrate can decompose and generate hydrogen even without the addition of external inoculum44. However, the impact of self-fermentation on hydrogen production depends strongly on the composition of these indigenous communities and the prevailing environmental conditions, which may shift metabolism toward pathways that produce little hydrogen (e.g., lactate pathway) or consume it (e.g., homoacetogenesis, propionate pathway)32,35. In our study, the HLa accumulation observed in Fig. 2b likely reflects the metabolic burden on VFA-producing microbes to convert HLa formed during the early stages at high SIR. This accumulation could cause pH fluctuations that further inhibit VFA production, particularly in batch tests conducted without automatic pH regulation.

Effect of OLR on VFA production in anaerobic membrane bioreactor

Acidogenic fermentation of food waste was carried out in a semi-continuous AnMBR to evaluate hydrogen and VFA production, as well as in situ VFA recovery, under different OLRs. Four different OLRs were applied (2.0, 2.5, 3.3, and 5.0 g-VS L−1‧d−1) and designed as phases I–IV, followed by phase V as a confirmation phase. The daily VFA and hydrogen production are shown in Fig. 3. The results showed a positive effect of increased OLR on the performance of VFAs and hydrogen co-production since both hydrogen production rate (HPR) and TVFA concentration increased significantly with higher OLRs. The maximum HPR (223.9 ± 3.4 mL L−1·d−1), hydrogen content (36.3 ± 6%), and HY (67.8 mL g-VS−1) were obtained at the pseudo-steady state of phase III (OLR of 3.3 g-VS L−1·d−1). However, a further stepwise increase to an OLR of 5.0 g-VS L−1·d−1 (phase IV) resulted in a marked decline in HPR (103.4 ± 4.5 mL L−1·d−1), hydrogen content (24.5 ± 0.6%), and HY (20.7 ± 0.9 mL g-VS−1). This suggests that an OLR of 5.0 g-VS L−1·d−1 exceeds the optimal load for the system and substantially reduces overall performance. As illustrated in Fig. 3b, the composition of individual VFAs indicated that HAc and Hbu were the dominant products, which together accounted for 80–97% of TVFA across the different OLRs. Additionally, the most remarkable difference occurred in the proportion of HAc. The percentage of HAc increased with rising OLR, whereas the other three acids (HPr, HBu, and HLa) showed a minor fluctuation within variations of 6–14% variation. The results indicate that VFA production was less susceptible to changes in OLR compared to hydrogen production. Fluctuations were observed mainly in HAc, yet it was still the major component. This conclusion implies the robustness of the acetate/butyrate pathway and the adaptability of the microbial consortium for the co-production of hydrogen and VFAs under the tested conditions.

The transition from phase III to phase IV involved an increase in organic loading, which caused notable changes in the VFA profile. This led to a 12.6% decrease in the HAc proportion, accompanied by increases of 5.6% and 5.2% in HPr and HLa, respectively. These findings were in good agreement with previous literature33,45. Higher OLRs have been reported to promote the acrylate pathway, where propionate is generated through the reduction of acrylyl-CoA to propionyl-CoA, particularly when carbohydrate-rich substrates are utilized. Carbohydrate-rich substrates contribute more to the formation of short-chain VFAs, whereas protein-rich substrates are associated with enhanced valerate formation15. However, the product spectrum is also affected by the environmental conditions, the fermentative microbial, and their metabolic pathways. The average VFA concentration on a COD basis shows that the desirable fermentation performance was obtained at an OLR of 3.3 with the highest VFA concentration (6.70 ± 0.19 g-CODVFA L−1) and productivity (1.68 ± 0.15 g-CODVFA L−1·d−1). However, the highest VFA yield of 0.58 ± 0.03 g-CODVFA g-VS−1 was obtained at an OLR of 2.5 g-VS L−1·d−1. These findings could be explained by the fact that with increased OLR, more biodegradable substrates are available for microbial degradation, stimulating microbial proliferation and enhancing VFA production. Higher OLR may lead to accumulating inhibitory compounds and unutilized solid food waste in the reactor, in particular due to the solid retention ability of AnMBR, which might negatively affect mass transfer efficiency in the medium, resulting in decreased yield. These results are in line with VFA yields range of 0.33–0.62 g-CODVFA g-VS−1 obtained by Tayou et al.46, where continuous VFA production was achieved under uncontrolled-pH conditions (4.5–5.0). Compared to the study by Gottardo et al.47, the VFA yields obtained in this study were slightly lower than the 0.53–0.87 g-CODVFA g-VS−1 reported for acidogenic fermentation of prehydrolyzed food waste.

Performance of the filtration system

The filtration system demonstrated effective performance at different OLRs from phase I to phase III. Following multiple cleaning cycles, the filtration flux remained stable within a range of 14–22 L m−2·h−1 throughout the operational period. Under optimal conditions (phase III), the filtration flux reached 18 L m−2·h−1, corresponding to a permeate flow rate of 0.2 L h−1 (Fig. 4). This is lower than the 0.4 L h−1 (corresponding flux of 19.5 L m−2·h−1) reported by Parchami et al.15, yet remained comparable to the permeate flow rate of 0.2–0.52 L h−1 (corresponding flux 20–51.7 L m−2·h−1) reported by Xiao et al.48 for similar kitchen waste slurry treatment. While inadequate filtration flux is frequently identified as a constraint on downstream recovery efficiency49, this constraint was not observed in the pilot-scale AnMBR system50. Notably, the membrane surface area-to-working volume ratio of the pilot scale system (0.02 m2 L−1) exceeded the ratio obtained herein (0.0056 m2 L−1) by a factor of 3.6, yet their filtration flux was approximately half of that achieved in the present investigation under comparable operating conditions. Moreover, physical cleaning alone was sufficient to maintain a stable permeate flow rate without encountering significant membrane fouling issues. This contrasts with several bench-scale AnMBR studies that necessitated frequent physical and chemical membrane cleaning protocols for efficient fouling control. Additionally, Parchami et al.50 emphasized that the effective membrane fouling control requires careful regulation of OLR to ensure sustained membrane performance and minimize operational costs over extended operational periods.Fig. 4Changes in TSS and filtration flux during the daily filtration process. As illustrated in the previous section, AnMBR demonstrated impressive co-production performance for both hydrogen and VFAs in terms of rate and yield during phases I and II (days 0–60). Furthermore, switching to phase III resulted in the highest production rates for hydrogen and VFAs, as shown in Fig. 3. However, total suspended solid (TSS) concentration began accumulating in the reactor from day 68 onwards (Fig. 4). TSS concentrations above 15.7 g L−1 adversely affected membrane performance, as indicated by a rapid decline in permeate flux. Comparable results were first reported in a previous study25, which employed similar substrate and operating conditions but utilized a different membrane module configuration. Their investigation observed a 16.4% overall reduction in permeate flux after 40 days of operation. In comparison, the present study recorded a 20.3% reduction in permeate flux from phase I to phase III. The variation in membrane module design, feed concentrations, and membrane pore size likely contributed to the variation of permeate flux reduction patterns.

At the beginning of phase IV, TSS concentration continued increasing up to 35.6 g L−1 on day 87, which may explain the significant decline in hydrogen and VFA co-production observed in Fig. 4, potentially attributable to reduced mixing efficiency and compromised mass transfer characteristics16. As TSS concentration increases, larger amounts of soluble microbial products (SMP), biomass, and undigested organic matter accumulate on the membrane surface, leading to the formation of a thicker cake layer49. Under constant flux operational mode, this enhanced resistance necessitates elevated TMP to maintain flux performance. Conversely, in constant-pressure mode, the TMP remains fixed while the permeate flux decreases due to increased membrane fouling from cake layer formation and pore clogging mechanisms51. Experiments by Mineo et al.51 and Buakaew and Ratanatamskul52 revealed that the main fouling mechanism was the irreversible cake deposition (higher than 90% of the total resistance) when treating organic substrates during extended operational periods. Consequently, sludge drainage and chemical cleaning procedures were implemented on day 88 to control reactor TSS levels. These findings agree with Jomnonkhaow et al.16, who demonstrated that stepwise increases in OLR resulted in colloidal material and extracellular polymeric substance accumulation, which progressively deposits on the membrane surface, and the cake layer subsequently densifies due to permeation drag effects. Additionally, the hydrophobic nature of these compounds contributed to microbial adhesion to abiotic surfaces, thereby influencing filtration performance in AnMBR systems25.

Yeast growth on VFA-rich effluent and lipid accumulation

The effect of using VFA-rich permeate as a carbon source on microbial lipid production was investigated and shown in Fig. 5. It is noteworthy that some studies have reported significant inhibition of microbial growth and lipid accumulation when VFA is used as a substrate at concentrations exceeding 5 g L−153. This is consistent with the findings of Fei et al.54, who observed that lipid production by Cryptococcus albidus decreased as the concentration of VFA increased to 8 g L−1. Similarly, Cryptococcus curvatus demonstrated reduced growth rates on acetate, as reported by Christophe et al.55. It has been reported that VFAs inhibit oleaginous yeast by interfering with the transport of phosphate across the cell membrane. Such interference may increase ATP consumption, thereby limiting lipid accumulation under high initial VFA concentrations in batch cultures56. Conversely, recent studies by Llamas et al.19 and Morales-Palomo et al.57 revealed that high concentrations of VFA did not negatively affect microbial growth but diminished the lipid content. These inhibition effects were likely attributed to different individual VFA profiles and mixture ratios. Thus, the undiluted permeate containing a VFA concentration of 6.95 ± 0.27 g L−1 was used as the carbon source in this experiment, regardless of possible inhibition effects. This approach was chosen to explore the potential of utilizing successfully recovered VFA from AnMBR, thereby enhancing the integration of biotechnology and membrane technology.Fig. 5C. tropicalis growth on VFA-rich permeate in batch cultivation: (a) TVFA consumption, biomass production, lipid accumulation and (b) consumption of individual VFAs.

Similarly, Kolouchová et al. reported the maximum growth rate of C. tropicalis on pure HAc and HPr in the range of 0.02–0.03 h−1 with lipid contents of 15.7–24.2% w/w58. In comparison, the present study achieved a specific growth rate of 0.034 h−1 with no lag phase and a lipid content of 31.2% w/w. Notably, biomass productivity was slightly higher than values reported in the literature, whereas moderate lipid productivity was achieved. A possible reason for higher biomass productivity could be that biomass yield increased with increasing VFA chain length19, as HBu is one of the most dominant VFA in this study. Additionally, the presence of low concentrations of HLa in the medium appears to have promoted yeast cell proliferation without enhancing lipid accumulation60.

Interestingly, the biomass and lipid production obtained in this study were close to those obtained in recognized yeast strains, indicating the potential of C. tropicalis to utilize VFA-rich permeate as an alternative carbon source. The results regarding VFA utilization align with literature findings that demonstrate preferential utilization of short-chain VFAs over long-chain VFAs by oleaginous yeasts. HAc was completely consumed at 72 h, while HBu was nearly exhausted with only 14.2% remaining after 96 h. Although HPr and HLa were presented in negligible concentrations, it was completely consumed within 72 h. Regarding the VFA consumption rates, HAc showed the highest rate of 0.039 g L−1·h−1, followed by HBu of 0.025 g L−1·h−1. In contrast, HPr and HLa showed negligible consumption rates (0.0016–0.0023 g L−1·h−1), which was expected given their very low initial concentrations in the medium. These consumption rates were considerably faster compared to other studies19,61, which utilized VFA mixtures containing high proportions of longer-chain VFAs, such as valeric acid (5 C-atoms), and caproic acid (6 C-atoms). These data suggest that individual VFA profiles present in the medium have crucial effects on TVFA consumption rates, and that focus should be placed on the specific composition of VFA rather than relying solely on TVFA concentration. It should be noted that only a few studies have reported the effect of VFA chain length on oleaginous yeast cultivation, pointing to the requirement for further in-depth studies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the successful integration of AnMBR technology with oleaginous yeast cultivation for sustainable food waste valorization. Optimal conditions of pH 6.0, S/I ratio of 3:1, and OLR of 3.3 g-VS L−1·d−1 achieved maximum hydrogen yield (67.8 ± 1.0 mL g-VS−1) and VFA productivity (1.68 ± 0.15 g-CODVFA L−1·d−1). A critical TSS threshold of 15.7 g L−1 was identified for maintaining stable membrane performance, providing valuable operational guidelines for industrial scale-up. Cultivation of C. tropicalis on VFA-rich permeate achieved biomass and lipid yields of 0.47 and 0.15 g g-VFAconsumed−1, respectively, with 94.2% VFA utilization efficiency. The integrated approach addresses the critical bottleneck of VFA purification costs by utilizing low-purity solutions directly for microbial lipid production, eliminating expensive separation steps while maintaining high conversion efficiency. The multi-product biorefinery concept demonstrates promising potential for economically viable and environmentally sustainable food waste management. By simultaneously producing hydrogen, VFAs, and microbial oil, this system offers superior value creation compared to single-product approaches and provides a practical pathway for implementing circular economy principles in waste valorization.
