Neonatal rotavirus encephalopathy with white matter injury: A case report
Authors: Siddharth Madabhushi, Shiji Chalipath, Anusha Rao, Umesh Vaidya, Manali Bhuyan, Komal Bijarniya, Vedika Mule, Rameshwar Gurao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRotavirus is a well-recognised cause of gastroenteritis in infants but has increasingly been linked to neurological complications in neonates. We report a 35-week preterm male neonate, the second twin of an in vitro fertilisation pregnancy, who was readmitted on day 9 of life with lethargy and hypotonia in the absence of fever, diarrhoea, or perinatal asphyxia. Brain MRI on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) demonstrated bilateral periventricular and deep white matter diffusion restriction resembling periventricular white matter injury seen in preterm infants with hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy. A comprehensive septic, metabolic, and congenital screen was negative. Electroencephalography (EEG) was normal. Stool polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for rotavirus returned positive on day 15, establishing the diagnosis of rotavirus-associated leukoencephalopathy. The infant was managed supportively with empirical antibiotics (subsequently discontinued) and antiseizure medications (ASMs) for clinically suspected seizure-like events. He showed gradual clinical improvement and was discharged on day 17 in a stable condition with outpatient neurodevelopmental follow-up arranged. This case highlights the importance of including rotavirus stool testing in the evaluation of neonates with unexplained encephalopathy and a characteristic DWI white matter injury pattern, even in the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms. Early identification prevents diagnostic delay, avoids unnecessary interventions, and enables appropriate neurodevelopmental surveillance.Keywords: Rotavirus, Neonatal encephalopathy, White matter injury, Diffusion-weighted imaging, Preterm neonate, Leukoencephalopathy

Introduction

Rotavirus is classically recognised as a leading cause of gastroenteritis in infants and young children worldwide, but it has also been linked to a range of neurological complications including seizures and encephalopathy [1]. In neonates, rotavirus infection often remains asymptomatic from a gastrointestinal standpoint, which historically led to under-recognition of its role in neonatal seizures the so-called "fifth-day fits" [2]. Recent case series have demonstrated that rotavirus infection in the first weeks of life can induce a distinctive diffuse cerebral white matter injury visible on MRI diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), even without direct viral invasion of the central nervous system [3,4]. Adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes have been reported in a significant proportion of affected neonates, with the extent of white matter injury on initial DWI correlating with long-term neurocognitive outcome [5,6].

We report a preterm neonate with rotavirus-associated leukoencephalopathy, highlighting the clinico-radiological features and the diagnostic value of DWI MRI and stool rotavirus PCR testing.

Case presentation

A 9-day-old late preterm male neonate, born at 35 weeks gestation, was readmitted for poor feeding and lethargy. He was the second-born of dichorionic diamniotic twins from an in vitro fertilisation pregnancy, delivered by emergency caesarean section for transverse lie. Birth weight was 2.05 kg (appropriate for gestational age). Apgar scores were 7 and 9 at 1 and 5 min respectively; no resuscitation was required. He had received continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) support for two days for transient tachypnoea of the newborn and was discharged home on oral feeds by day 3 of life.

At home, the neonate remained well until day 8, when he developed reduced activity and feeding refusal. There was no fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, or rash at onset. By day 9, progressive lethargy and hypotonia prompted readmission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Vital signs on admission were: temperature 36.7°C, heart rate 150/min, respiratory rate 50/min, and blood pressure 60/40 mmHg. Perfusion and oxygenation were maintained; there were no signs of shock or meningismus. Physical examination revealed a mildly hypotonic but arousable neonate. Head circumference was 32 cm, the anterior fontanelle was soft, and no dysmorphic features were noted. Intermittent bradycardia (down to 80/min), sometimes with oxygen desaturation, raised clinical concern for subclinical seizures. No overt tonic-clonic movements were witnessed. An initial working impression of neonatal encephalopathy possible late-onset sepsis — was made.

Investigations

Complete blood count and C-reactive protein (1.5 mg/L) were normal. Blood culture was sterile. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed 4 lymphocytes, protein 89 mg/dL, and glucose 32 mg/dL with a negative Gram stain. A viral meningoencephalitis CSF panel (herpes simplex virus, enterovirus, parechovirus), CSF PCR for chikungunya and dengue, and serum TORCH screen (toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex, and HIV) were all negative. CSF rotavirus PCR was not sent as it is not included in the standard viral panel at our institution. EEG performed during a bradycardic episode showed age-appropriate continuity with no epileptiform discharges. Metabolic screen (ammonia, lactate, tandem mass spectrometry of acylcarnitines, and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry of urine organic acids) revealed no inborn error of metabolism.

Brain MRI (performed on day 12 of life) showed multiple foci of restricted diffusion in the periventricular and deep white matter of both cerebral hemispheres on DWI (high DWI signal with corresponding low apparent diffusion coefficient [ADC]), including the peritrigonal regions and fronto-parietal deep white matter tracts (Fig. 1A, B). Conventional T2-weighted imaging at the same level showed relatively unremarkable white matter signal (Fig. 1C). Conventional T1-weighted images showed faint hyperintensities; T2/FLAIR sequences were largely unremarkable. No cortical involvement, haemorrhage on susceptibility-weighted imaging, or gross oedema was identified. The radiological differential included periventricular white matter injury in a preterm brain; however, the delayed clinical onset and absence of perinatal asphyxia raised suspicion for a rotavirus-associated aetiology.

Brain MRI (day 12 of life). (A) Axial diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) demonstrating bilateral foci of restricted diffusion in the periventricular and peritrigonal white matter (yellow arrows). (B) Corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map at the same level showing bilateral dark signal (yellow arrows), confirming true restricted diffusion. (C) T2-weighted axial sequence at the same level showing relatively unremarkable white matter signal, illustrating the superior sensitivity of DWI over conventional sequences in detecting this pattern of white matter injury.

Stool PCR for rotavirus returned positive on day 15 of life; stool PCR for enterovirus was negative. Ophthalmological screening for retinopathy of prematurity was normal.

Management

Empirical intravenous antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam, subsequently escalated to meropenem) were commenced for possible late-onset sepsis and discontinued once cultures and inflammatory markers remained negative. Antiseizure medications (ASMs) were initiated empirically for clinically suspected seizure-like episodes specifically the recurrent apnoea-bradycardia spells despite a normal interictal EEG. In the preterm neonate, EEG correlation during every episode was not feasible, and subclinical seizures could not be excluded on clinical grounds alone. Phenobarbitone was given as a loading dose, followed by maintenance levetiracetam. Empirical L-carnitine supplementation was stopped after the metabolic screen returned normal.

Neurological monitoring showed progressive improvement from the end of the second week. Alertness and spontaneous activity improved, and demand feeding was established by day 16. No further bradycardic or apnoeic episodes occurred after day 12. No immunomodulatory therapy was deemed necessary given the self-limiting course and normal EEG. The infant was discharged on day 17 of life (postmenstrual age approximately 37 weeks) on low-dose levetiracetam. Repeat MRI and outpatient neurodevelopmental follow-up were arranged. Parents were counselled regarding seizure warning signs, and enhanced hand hygiene and isolation measures were maintained throughout admission to prevent nosocomial spread.

On outpatient follow-up, the infant demonstrated a normal neurological examination. Levetiracetam was subsequently discontinued after neurological normalisation was confirmed, consistent with current recommendations against routine continuation of ASMs following discharge in neonates with normal EEG [7].

Discussion

This case illustrates neonatal rotavirus-induced encephalopathy presenting with diffuse white matter injury on MRI a pattern described predominantly in case series from East Asia and India [3,4,8]. Our patient, a 35-week preterm neonate, developed lethargy and clinically suspected seizures in the second week of life, with bilateral periventricular white matter diffusion restriction in the absence of perinatal asphyxia or systemic illness, and a positive stool rotavirus PCR.

Yeom et al. reported 18 term neonates with seizures or apnoea around day 5 of life showing symmetric restricted diffusion in periventricular white matter, with rotavirus identified in 94% of stool samples [3]. Notably, only 2 of those infants had diarrhoea, underscoring that gastrointestinal symptoms are frequently absent in neonatal rotavirus infection. Our case mirrors this — there was no diarrhoea throughout, and the diagnosis would have been missed without targeted stool testing. Rotavirus should therefore be included in the diagnostic evaluation of any neonate with unexplained encephalopathy and this DWI pattern, irrespective of gastrointestinal symptoms [3,4].

The MRI pattern resembles periventricular white matter injury in preterm infants with hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE). In our patient, the radiologist initially considered perinatal hypoxic injury given the preterm brain distribution. However, several features favoured rotavirus aetiology over classical hypoxic-ischaemic injury: the infant had no significant birth asphyxia, clinical deterioration was delayed to day 9, and the lesion distribution was bilateral but patchy, extending into subcortical white matter atypical for pure periventricular leukomalacia (PVL). Published rotavirus encephalopathy cases show white matter involvement beyond the periventricular zones, including deep white matter, corpus callosum, internal capsules, and posterior thalami [3,8]. Follow-up MRI in reported cases has demonstrated evolution of extensive confluent diffusion restriction into cystic change and volume loss [8]. Thus, rotavirus may be an under-recognised infectious cause of PVL-like white matter damage in neonates without traditional perinatal risk factors.

The pathophysiology appears to involve indirect rather than direct viral CNS invasion. In most reported cases, neither rotavirus antigen nor nucleic acid was detectable in CSF, consistent with the hypothesis that brain injury is mediated by indirect systemic effects rather than direct viral neuroinvasion [3,8]. CSF rotavirus PCR was not performed in our case as it is not included in the standard viral panel at our institution; the existing literature supports that stool PCR is sufficient for diagnosis, as rotavirus is consistently absent in CSF even in confirmed encephalopathy cases. Animal models and in vitro studies suggest that rotaviral RNA may activate microglia via toll-like receptors (TLR-7/8), triggering cytokine release and oligodendroglial injury. Additionally, the rotavirus non-structural protein 4 (NSP4) enterotoxin has been implicated in blood-brain barrier disruption and cellular homeostasis alteration [4]. Circulating cytokines and viral byproducts may preferentially damage maturing oligodendrocytes and white matter tracts without direct CNS invasion an inflammatory cascade analogous to that seen in neonatal parechovirus leukoencephalopathy [9].

Management remains supportive, as no specific antiviral therapy exists for rotavirus. Immunomodulatory therapies (steroids, intravenous immunoglobulin) have been discussed in the context of severe viral encephalopathies, but there is no established evidence for their use in rotaviral leukoencephalopathy, and most cases resolve spontaneously [4]. Early identification is important to (i) avoid mislabelling the injury as hypoxic or genetic, (ii) prevent unnecessary invasive investigations, (iii) institute NICU infection control measures given rotavirus infectivity, and (iv) facilitate appropriate neurodevelopmental follow-up.

Prognosis correlates with lesion extent. Milder cases show complete MRI resolution and normal neurodevelopment, while extensive white matter damage can result in cerebral palsy, epilepsy, or cognitive impairment [5,6]. Our patient showed early neurological recovery at discharge with subsequent normalisation of neurological examination on outpatient follow-up. No repeat MRI was performed given the favourable clinical course; repeat neuroimaging is planned if any neurodevelopmental concerns arise on serial assessment.

A limitation of this case is the absence of a repeat MRI at follow-up, which would have enabled assessment of evolving cystic change or white matter volume loss as described in the literature. Additionally, while CSF rotavirus PCR was not sent, this is consistent with published practice given its consistently low yield in confirmed cases [3,8].

Conclusion

Rotavirus infection in neonates, even without diarrhoea can cause acute leukoencephalopathy that radiologically resembles periventricular white matter injury in the preterm brain. DWI MRI is key to early recognition, and stool rotavirus PCR testing should be considered when this imaging pattern is encountered in any neonate with unexplained encephalopathy, irrespective of gastrointestinal symptoms. Timely diagnosis prevents misattribution of injury, avoids unnecessary interventions, guides supportive management, enables NICU infection control, and informs neurodevelopmental prognosis.
