Chern-Simons Induced Thermal Friction on Axion Domain Walls
Saquib Hassan, Gaurang Ramakant Kane, John March-Russell, and Georges Obied

Abstract
We study the dynamics and interactions of the solitonic domain walls that occur in realistic axion electrodynamics models including the Chern-Simons interaction, a ​ ϵ μ ​ ν ​ λ ​ σ ​ F μ ​ ν ​ F λ ​ σ a\epsilon_{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}F^{\mu\nu}F^{\lambda\sigma} , between an axion a ⁡ ( x ) a(x) of mass m a m_{a} , and a massless U(1) gauge field, e.g. EM, interacting with strength α = e 2 / 4 ​ π \alpha=e^{2}/4\pi with charged matter, e.g. electron-positron pairs. In particular, in the presence of a U(1) gauge-and-matter relativistic thermal plasma we study the friction experienced by the walls due to the Chern-Simons term. Utilizing the linear response method we include the collective effects of the plasma, as opposed to purely particle scattering across the wall (as is done in previous treatments) which is valid only in the thin wall regime that is rarely applicable in realistic cases. We show that the friction depends on the Lorentz- γ \gamma -factor-dependent inverse thickness of the wall in the plasma frame, ℓ − 1 ∼ γ ​ m a \ell^{-1}\sim\gamma m_{a} , compared to the three different plasma scales, the temperature T T , the Debye mass m D ∼ α ​ T m_{D}\sim\sqrt{\alpha}T , and the damping rate Γ ∼ α 2 ​ T \Gamma\sim\alpha^{2}T , and elucidate the underlying physical intuition for this behavior. (For friction in the thin-wall-limit we correct previous expressions in the literature.) We further consider the effects of long-range coherent magnetic fields that are possibly present in the early universe and compare their effect with that of thermal magnetic fields. We comment on the changes to our results that likely apply in the thermal deconfined phase of a non-Abelian gauge theory. Finally, we briefly discuss the possible early universe consequences of our results for domain wall motion and network decay, stochastic gravitational wave production from domain wall networks, and possible primordial black hole production from domain wall collapse, though a more complete discussion of these topics is reserved for a companion paper.

1.3
Overview of friction on thin axion domain walls

Phase transitions in the early universe are expected to produce strings and domain walls through the Kibble-Zurek mechanism
[
65
,
66
]
. These have complicated dynamics and can interact with the thermal bath in the early universe. These interactions exchange energy and momentum between the wall and the plasma, inducing friction as the wall evolves over time. This friction can affect the phenomenologically important signatures of these domain walls. For instance, oscillating domain walls produce a strong gravitational wave signal that is a target for many detectors but a large frictional force can remove energy from the coupled gravitational wave-wall system and dump it into the plasma. Even if this does not happen, the friction may delay the onset of an ultra-relativistic regime where gravitational wave emission is dominant. Collapsing domain walls also provide a production mechanism for primordial black holes
[
27
]
but if the wall collapse is slowed down or if it loses enough energy to its surroundings, this process may be inhibited. Finally, the decay of domain walls into Beyond the Standard Model (BSM) relics is also sensitive to the total energy of the wall and is thereby affected by the friction. This can have a major effect on the bounds derived by assuming the existence of these relics
[
54
]
. Understanding the effect of friction on these phenomena is essential for axion searches.

Frictional forces on ALP domain walls can be classified according to their physical origin including particle scattering, changing masses across the wall, or collective dissipative plasma effects. The former two cases have been studied extensively in the literature (such as
[
67
,
61
,
56
]
and references within), and they apply in the thin-wall limit, where the particle mean-free-path is longer than the wall thickness. The latter case has been discussed in a general context in
[
58
]
, without an application to ALP domain walls, which is the subject of this work. We will discuss this in detail in the following sections where we study the friction experienced by (thin and thick) ALP domain walls due to the Chern-Simons interaction term. In the thin wall limit, this interaction can also be treated along the lines described above, i.e. by studying particle scattering off the domain wall.

In the thin wall limit, the friction calculation is done in two steps: (a) calculating the reflection coefficient of the particle interaction with the wall using the semi-classical WKB approximation and (b) calculating the pressure difference on the two sides of the wall in the wall frame. In the first step, one only considers the particle-wall interaction and ignores any interactions between the particle and the plasma. In the domain wall frame, the problem reduces to finding the reflection probability of the plasma particle in an effective potential induced by the wall, which can be treated using the WKB approximation. The force from one particle reflection is then thermally averaged to give the total friction (more details are given below around eq. (
11
)). Heuristically, one can think of this as integrating across the wall first followed by thermal averaging. This procedure can be carried out for any domain wall and any particle that interacts with it.

[

m_{a}^{2}T^{2}~~~~~~~~~~T\gg m_{a}~~.\end{cases}

(10)

As we saw in the last section, the thermal average can be expressed in terms of the ALP field
\varphi
and some other functions yet to be calculated. Hence, we have everything in terms of
\varphi
and one can solve the entire equation exactly. On the other hand, we can treat this term as a force acting on the free wall. The motivation behind this is that the term is
(\alpha/\pi)^{2}
suppressed and hence will not affect the overall structure and behavior of the wall at the leading order. Consider a general equation of the form,

\partial_{t}^{2}\varphi=\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi-\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial\varphi}+F(t,x)

(35)

where
F(t,x)
comes from the
\langle F\wedge F\rangle
term. We are interested in extracting terms that have an explicit dependence on velocity “
v
” (i.e. not just through the
\gamma
factor). Such terms can be interpreted as friction in a conventional manner. To find these terms, we start by writing the energy in the ALP field
``a"
(which is nothing by
f_{a}\varphi
):

E=f_{a}^{2}A\int dx\Big(\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\Big)^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\Big)^{2}+V(\varphi)\Big)~~,

(36)

where
A
is the area in the transverse direction of the wall considering the fact that
\varphi
is dependent on a single coordinate. This approximation should be valid as long as the thickness is small compared to the radius of the wall. The change in the energy with respect to time, i.e., the power exchange is,

\dfrac{dE}{dt}=f_{a}^{2}A\int dx\Big(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial t^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial t\partial x}+\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial\varphi}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\Big)~~.

(37)

We can then substitute the equation of motion of the field to get a function in terms of
F(t,x)
. This gives us,

\dfrac{dE}{dt}=f_{a}^{2}A\int dx\Big(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}F(t,x)+\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial t\partial x}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x^{2}}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\Big)~~.

(38)

The last two terms together are derivatives. Hence rearranging the equation gives,

\dfrac{dE}{dt}=f_{a}^{2}A\int dx\Big(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}F(t,x)+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\Big)\Big)~~.

The final term is a pure boundary term. This term can be thought of as the power loss through the boundary of the system. As long as the boundary is far compared to where
\varphi
is localized, this power loss is zero. Hence, what we have is,

\dfrac{dE}{dt}=f_{a}^{2}A\int dx~\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}F(t,x)~~.

(39)

If the wall is moving with velocity
v
, then, for the zero mode (or the collective coordinate of the wall), we can write
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}=-v\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}
. Then,

\dfrac{dE}{dt}=-vf_{a}^{2}A\int dx~\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}F(t,x)~~.

(40)

Hence, the force acting on the wall is,

F_{avg}=-f_{a}^{2}A\int dx~\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}F(t,x)

(41)

giving the pressure

P=\dfrac{F_{avg}}{A}=-f_{a}^{2}\int dx~\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}F(t,x)~~.

(42)

We know that
\partial_{x}\varphi
is a locally peaked function. This function appropriately acts as a weight function to be multiplied by the force to calculate the average force.
14
14

14

One can note that this expression exactly matches the thermally averaged Lorentz force mentioned in Sec.(
1.4
).
A limiting case to see is that if the wall is extremely thin, one can approximate
\partial_{x}\varphi\approx\delta(x-x_{0})
at some time
t
. In this case, the force is nothing but
F(t,x_{0})
, as expected. An important feature of the friction pressure above is that it is proportional to the zero modes
(\partial\varphi/\partial x)
of the wall. This feature is one of the common behaviors we have with the expression in
[
58
]
. A similar expression is also found in literature which tries to compute friction on a phase transition bubble such as
[
80
,
81
]
. With Eqn.(
42
) in mind, we return to the thermal averages to find the final expression for friction pressure.

3.2
Case 2: Thermal magnetic fields

Now that we have simplified the expression for the contribution from a background magnetic field, we move on to the thermal magnetic field contribution. In this section, we calculate the integral corresponding to the thermal part of the magnetic field given by:

\mathcal{I}_{2}=i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\langle[\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{th}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{th}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle_{0}\varphi(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})~.

(57)

To simplify this, let us define the Retarded Green’s function

\mathcal{G}^{R}_{th}(t-t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{th}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{th}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle_{0}~~.

(58)

Then in this notation, the integral of interest takes the form

\mathcal{I}_{2}=\int dt^{\prime}\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\mathcal{G}^{R}_{th}~~\varphi(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})~~.

We can express the retarded Green’s function in terms of its Fourier transform. This can be written as

\mathcal{I}_{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{R}_{th}(\omega,\mathbf{k})\varphi(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})~~.

(59)

As before, we expand the Fourier transform of the retarded Green’s function around
\omega=0
. The integral becomes

\mathcal{I}_{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\Big(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{R}_{th}(0,\mathbf{k})+\omega\dfrac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{R}_{th}}{\partial\omega}(0,\mathbf{k})\Big)\varphi(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})~~.

(60)

Taking the inverse Fourier transform with respect to
\omega
and then integrating with respect to
t^{\prime}
, we get

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{2}=\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\Big\{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{R}_{th}(0,\mathbf{k})\varphi(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+i\dfrac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{R}_{th}}{\partial\omega}(0,\mathbf{k})\partial_{t}\varphi(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\Big\}~~.\end{split}

(61)

As we saw in the earlier calculation, the friction comes from that part of the integral that has a time derivative acting on the wall field
\varphi

3

\mathcal{I}_{2,2}=-\dfrac{1}{2T}\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\rho_{0}(0,\mathbf{k})\partial_{t}\varphi(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})~~,

(62)

where

\rho_{0}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\mathsf{FT}\Big(\langle\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{th}(t,\mathbf{x})~\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{th}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle_{0}\Big)~~.

(63)

Refer to Appendix (
C.2
) for the derivation. Here,
\rho_{0}
is the Fourier transform of the correlation between two thermal fluctuations. One can get a physical intuition of the equation above by rewriting it as :

\mathcal{I}_{2,2}=-\dfrac{1}{2T}\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\partial_{t}\varphi(t,\mathbf{x^{\prime}})\int dt^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{th}(t^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})~\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{th}(0,0)\rangle_{0}~~.

A thermal fluctuation, including particles, collective excitations, and quasi-particles has a lifetime. Hence, a fluctuation of
\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}
that happens at

will be strongly correlated for some small time to the other local fluctuations. Further, the correlation of the two thermal fluctuations depends on their spatial separation. As the electric fields are screened at the Debye scale, one can see that the fluctuations at longer distances will be weakly correlated. On the other hand, fluctuations at scales smaller than the Debye length will tend to have a stronger correlation. Thermal fluctuations also decay which can be because of collisions or other damping thermal processes in plasma. Qualitatively speaking, the second term in the integral will depend on these thermal quantities.

To simplify

(64)

4
Comparison with Hubble friction

In the previous section, we have estimated the frictional pressure on domain walls due to the Chern-Simons interaction term using a simple toy model. There are two types of domain walls that are of interest in cosmology: (i) superhorizon walls which typically form cosmic networks and (ii) sub-horizon walls which can be either topologically closed surfaces or have an axion string boundary. Chern-Simons friction can be important for the evolution of both classes of domain walls. In the latter case, it has been demonstrated that collapsing walls attain a terminal velocity even in the absence of friction. We do not expect friction to change this qualitative conclusion but it may affect quantitative details such as the energy loss, terminal velocity, etc. These questions have to be studied with numerical simulations and we will not pursue this direction here. Instead, we will focus on analysing superhorizon walls.

Superhorizon walls already experience friction due to the expansion of the universe. This Hubble friction can be seen from the axion equation of motion in an FLRW metric which contains a term of the form

5
Summary and comments

In this work, we discussed the method of using linear response to calculate plasma friction on the axion domain walls. In particular, we were interested in the effect of the Chern-Simons interaction term. We started by describing some standard ALP domain wall preliminaries and also summarized some crucial aspects of calculating friction that have been discussed in the literature. Following this, we proceeded to work out the friction from the linear response perspective, which captures the collective processes in plasma happening faster and at smaller scales compared to the wall motion and size. In doing this, we found that the friction can be written in terms of the spectral functions of the thermal operators. Furthermore, this method allowed us to calculate the friction in the case of background magnetic fields. To extract analytical expressions, we considered simple toy models for the spectral function that capture important properties of this function. Finally, we compared this friction to the Hubble friction and qualitatively commented on how including the full time-dependent plasma friction can lead to rich wall dynamics. In the calculation above, we considered the case where
N=1
in the axion wall potential Eqn.(
2
). For
N>1
, one can always do field redefinitions to rewrite the axion field, so that in this case, the final effect is that there is an overall

factor that multiplies the friction.

The main result of this work is summarized in Fig.
3
where we show that it is essential to include collective thermal processes to get the correct functional dependence for the frictional force on a wall thicker than the Debye length
(eT)^{-1}
. For thinner walls, we correct previous expressions in the literature. This thermal contribution can dominate Hubble friction for domain walls of heavy axion (i.e. with masses larger than about a GeV) as we discussed in Section
4
though, as mentioned in the caption to Fig.
7
, a broader range of axion masses allow for large friction effects if we relax the assumptions, including, e.g, long range coherent magnetic fields. We will return to this and related issues in a companion paper.

Notwithstanding these generalised early cosmology scenarios, we note that heavy axions have anyway been argued to provide natural candidate solutions to the strong CP problem in grand unification scenarios

and also in more general settings like

The idea behind this mechanism is to introduce a mixing between the QCD axion and the heavy axion which leads to the formation of QCD axion domain walls that overlap with the heavy axion domain walls. The collapse of the heavy axion domain wall also causes the QCD axion domain wall to collapse, producing a relic density of QCD axion dark matter as well as gravitational wave signals. This can happen even if the QCD axion has pre-inflationary initial conditions. More generally, domain walls of heavy axions can form in the early universe and for masses and couplings in the allowed region of Fig.
7
we get gravitational wave signals that peak at frequencies anywhere between
10^{-14}
to
10^{-2}
Hz corresponding to the annihilation of these walls and these gravitational waves constitute potential targets for detectors (see for example
[

]
for a survey of these detectors and signals).

There are various simple extensions of the scenario we described above, of which we mention two. First, one can consider a monodromic axion coupling
[

]
whereby the axion couples to the U(1) gauge fields via a more general function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\supset\frac{h(a)}{16\pi^{2}}F\tilde{F}

) by the Fourier transform of

.

Another simple extension of the above result involves considering a non-Abelian deconfined thermal plasma coupled to the wall (instead of the Abelian case we studied in this paper). This system is similar to
[
57
]
. The thermal effects in a non-Abelian plasma are quite different compared to the Abelian case. In the non-Abelian case, the damping of collective modes is anomalously large

(116)

This then gives the frictional pressure on the ALP domain wall to be,

P\sim\alpha_{s}^{4}(\gamma m_{a})T^{3}v~~,

(117)

The above contribution accounts for thermal gluons only and including the fermions charged under these gluons further modifies the result. In this case, the friction becomes proportional to the chirality flipping rate

which, in turn is related to
\Gamma_{sph}
. More details on this can be found in

and references within.

In Section

we studied the effect of thermal friction on super-horizon walls and neglected subhorizon closed walls which are also affected by this friction. This system is more complicated. As shown in
[

,

. A simple case to study initially is the effect of long-range magnetic fields on such closed walls in the absence of a plasma. This can be followed by a full thermal treatment. This system can be realized if the axion is coupled to a hidden sector including heavy charged matter that is annihilated to leave only large-scale coherent electromagnetic fields. This is a phenomenologically rich system which we return to in our companion paper.

One further aspect to study is how axion particles produce friction on ALP domain walls. For sine-Gordon models this vanishes as shown in
[
56
,
100
]
. However, the presence of non-renormalizable terms (e.g. Planck-suppressed operators that arise in a UV quantum gravity to forbid global symmetries) causes a tilt in the potential of the walls. As argued in
[

These arguments are quite general and are applicable to an ALP as well. If such terms are included, then, ALPs in the environment will change mass across the domain wall and give rise to a different kind of friction, similar to the one discussed recently in
[

Our results were derived for an axion domain wall neglecting mixing with QCD mesons (such as

Lastly, we stress that degrees of freedom on the domain wall can also play a crucial role and provide new scattering channels that impact the friction these walls experience. These world-volume field theories can be interesting to study in their own right. For instance, they can certainly include theories as complicated as the FQHE, and there can also be inhomogeneous or even tachyonic (e.g.

) field theories that require new techniques to analyse.

We hope that our work and the discussion above shows that domain walls have very rich phenomenological aspects to be studied in relation to early universe physics. These depend on various factors including temperature, wall motion, type of interaction (e.g. Abelian vs. non-Abelian) and many more. In conclusion, there are likely many phenomenological insights hidden within the intricate details of domain wall dynamics that warrant further exploration.

Appendix A
Thin wall friction - leading approximation

In this section, we derive the thin wall particle scattering friction for photons. Initially, consider a particle with mass
m
following the Bose-Einstein statistics and scattering off the wall. Consider that the reflection coefficient corresponding to this scattering is given by
\mathcal{R}(p)
. Along with this, consider that the wall is like a flat sheet in the
x-y
plane and moving in the positive
z
direction. Then, in the wall frame, the pressure exerted by particles on the two sides of the wall is,

\begin{split}P_{R}=2\int\dfrac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\dfrac{dp_{z}}{2\pi}\mathcal{R}(p_{z})\dfrac{p_{z}^{2}}{E}\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(E+vp_{z})}-1}\\
P_{L}=2\int\dfrac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int^{\infty}_{0}\dfrac{dp_{z}}{2\pi}\mathcal{R}(p_{z})\dfrac{p_{z}^{2}}{E}\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(E+vp_{z})}-1}\end{split}

(118)

Now, we take the difference between these two. Note that
\mathcal{R}(p_{z})=\mathcal{R}(-p_{z})
. This will give

P=P_{R}-P_{L}=2\int\dfrac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{dp_{z}}{2\pi}\dfrac{p_{z}^{2}}{E}\mathcal{R}(p_{z})\Big(\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(E-vp_{z})}-1}-\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(E+vp_{z})}-1}\Big)~~.

(119)

To evaluate this integral, we can first integrate the
p_{x},p_{y}
momenta. In the equation above,

E=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+p_{z}^{2}+m^{2}},~~\rho=\sqrt{p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}}.

(120)

By appropriately changing the variables, the above integral can be written as,

\begin{split}P=\dfrac{2}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp_{z}~p_{z}^{2}\mathcal{R}(p_{z})\int_{0}^{\infty}d\rho\Big\{\dfrac{\rho}{\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+\rho^{2}+m^{2}}}\\
\times\Big(\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+\rho^{2}+m^{2}}-vp_{z})}-1}-\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+\rho^{2}+m^{2}}+vp_{z})}-1}\Big)\Big\}~~.\end{split}

(121)

Substituting
\sqrt{\rho^{2}+p_{z}^{2}+m^{2}}=x
simplifies the integral above to,

P=\dfrac{2}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp_{z}~p_{z}^{2}\mathcal{R}(p_{z})\int_{\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+m^{2}}}^{\infty}dx\Big(\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(x-vp_{z})}-1}-\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(x+vp_{z})}-1}\Big)~~.

(122)

We can do the integrals individually. Let us first look at the first term in the integral:

\begin{split}\int_{\sqrt{p_{z}+m^{2}}}^{\infty}dx\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta(x-vp_{z})}-1}=\int_{\sqrt{p_{z}+m^{2}}-vp_{z}}^{\infty}dx\dfrac{1}{e^{\gamma\beta y}-1}=\dfrac{1}{\beta\gamma}\int_{\gamma\beta(\sqrt{p^{2}_{z}+m^{2}}-vp_{z})}^{\infty}dx\dfrac{1}{e^{y}-1}\\
=\dfrac{1}{\beta\gamma}\Big(\beta\gamma(\sqrt{p^{2}_{z}+m^{2}}-vp_{z})-\ln(e^{\beta\gamma(\sqrt{p^{2}_{z}+m^{2}}-vp_{z})}-1)\Big)\end{split}

(123)

Similarly doing the other integral and taking the difference gives,

P=\dfrac{2}{(2\pi)^{2}\beta\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp_{z}~p_{z}^{2}\mathcal{R}(p_{z})\left(\ln\left(\dfrac{e^{\gamma\beta(E_{z}+vp_{z})}-1}{e^{\gamma\beta(E_{z}-vp_{z})}-1}\right)-2\gamma\beta vp_{z}\right),~~E_{z}=\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+m^{2}}

(124)

Let us now look at the reflection coefficient. The WKB approximation can be done by writing the equations of motion of the vector potential in the presence of a wall and treating the vector potential as a wave function. We direct the readers to
[

(125)

\dfrac{d\varphi}{dz}=\dfrac{2m_{a}}{\cosh(m_{a}z)}~~.

Solving this equation analytically in closed form is not possible. We can estimate the order of friction by modeling the wall by replacing this potential by,

V(z)=\begin{cases}2\pi m_{a}~~~~0<z<m_{a}^{-1}\\
0~~~~~~~~~|z|>m_{a}^{-1}~~.\end{cases}

(126)

This potential gives introduces a

Appendix C
Useful thermal field theory results

In this Appendix we re-derive the linear response relations and the general expressions for retarded Green’s functions in terms of the Spectral Density Function for the sake of completeness. Similar derivations can be found in canonical texts and references dealing with thermal field theory such as
[
75
,
77
,
78
]
.

C.1
Average in the linear response limit

Consider a general Hamiltonian of the form,

H=H_{0}+H_{int}~~.

(137)

Here,
H_{0}
is the Hamiltonian without any perturbation, and
H_{int}
is the perturbation part. Consider an operator
\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})
. Then the average of this operator is given by,

\langle\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})\rangle=\text{Tr}(\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})\rho)=\text{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{I}(t,\mathbf{x})\rho_{I})~~,

(138)

where
\rho
is the density matrix of the full system. The second equality comes from the fact that the transformation from the Heisenberg picture to the interaction picture is a unitary transformation that leaves the trace invariant. In the interaction picture, the density matrix satisfies,

\dfrac{d\rho_{I}}{dt}=-i[(H_{int})_{I},\rho_{I}]~~.

(139)

Hence, in the linear limit, this gives us,

\rho_{I}(t)=\rho_{I}(t_{0})-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}[(H_{int})_{I}(t^{\prime}),\rho_{I}(t_{0})]+O(2)~~.

(140)

In the above equation
t_{0}
is the time when the interaction Hamiltonian is turned on. One can take the limit
t_{0}\rightarrow-\infty
. Also,
\rho_{I}(t_{0})=\rho_{0}(t_{0})
, where
\rho_{0}
is the density matrix calculated with respect to
H_{0}
at time
t_{0}
. This gives us,

\rho_{I}(t)=\rho_{0}-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})[(H_{int})_{I}(t^{\prime}),\rho_{0}]~~.

(141)

Using this, we get,

\langle\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})\rangle=\langle\mathcal{O}_{I}(t,\mathbf{x})\rangle_{0}-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[\mathcal{O}_{I}(t,\mathbf{x}),(H_{int})_{I}(t^{\prime})]\rangle_{0}~~,

(142)

where, the average is taken with respect to the
\rho_{0}
matrix.

C.2
Retarded Green’s function

Consider an operator
\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})
. The retarded Green’s function of this operator is given by,

\mathcal{G}^{R}(t-t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle~~.

(143)

We define the Fourier transform of the left-hand side expression as,

\mathcal{G}^{R}(t-t,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega,\mathbf{k})~~.

(144)

On the other hand, we can derive the same by expressing the right-hand side of Eqn.(
143
). We can express the thermal average as follows,

\langle\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle=\sum_{n,m}\langle n|\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})|m\rangle\langle m|\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})|n\rangle e^{-\beta E_{n}}~~.

(145)

We can use the spatial translation operator
\mathbf{P}
and the Hamiltonian as follows,

\mathcal{O}(t,x)=e^{iHt-i\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{O}(0,0)e^{-iHt+i\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{x}}~~.

This when substituted in the equation above gives us,

\langle\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle=\sum_{n,m}e^{-i(E_{m}-E_{n})(t-t^{\prime})+i(\mathbf{k}_{m}-\mathbf{k}_{n})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}e^{-\beta E_{n}}|\langle n|\mathcal{O}(0,0)|m\rangle|^{2}

(146)

Similarly, we can exchange
(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leftrightarrow(t,\mathbf{x})

\langle\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle=\sum_{n,m}e^{-i(E_{m}-E_{n})(t-t^{\prime})+i(\mathbf{k}_{m}-\mathbf{k}_{n})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}e^{-\beta E_{m}}|\langle m|\mathcal{O}(0,0)|n\rangle|^{2}

(147)

Taking the difference then gives us,

\begin{split}\langle[\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle=\sum_{n,m}e^{-i(E_{m}-E_{n})(t-t^{\prime})+i(\mathbf{k}_{m}-\mathbf{k}_{n})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}e^{-\beta E_{n}}\\
\times(1-e^{-\beta(E_{m}-E_{n})})|\langle m|\mathcal{O}(0,0)|n\rangle|^{2}\end{split}

(148)

We define
E_{m}-E_{n}=\omega
. This gives us,

\begin{split}\langle[\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle=\sum_{n,m}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})+i(\mathbf{k}_{m}-\mathbf{k}_{n})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}e^{-\beta E_{n}}\\
\times(1-e^{-\beta\omega})|\langle m|\mathcal{O}(0,0)|n\rangle|^{2}\end{split}

(149)

Hence, this tells us that the Fourier transform of the commutator is

\mathsf{FT}\Big(\langle[\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle\Big)=(1-e^{-\beta\omega})\mathsf{FT}\Big(\langle\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle\Big)~~.

(150)

Let us denote,

\mathsf{FT}\Big(\langle\mathcal{O}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle\Big)=\rho_{0}(\omega,\mathbf{k})~~.

(151)

Furthermore, we can write the Heaviside function
\theta(t-t^{\prime})
as,

\theta(t-t^{\prime})=\dfrac{i}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega}{\omega+i\varepsilon}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}~~.

(152)

Using this, we can write the RHS of Eqn.(
143
) as,

-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega}{2\pi}\dfrac{e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}}{\omega+i\varepsilon}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})}\int e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}(1-e^{-\beta\omega^{\prime}})\rho_{0}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k})~~.

(153)

Rearranging this gives us,

-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\int e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\dfrac{1}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}+i\varepsilon}(1-e^{-\beta\omega^{\prime}})\rho_{0}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k})~~.

(154)

Comparing Eqn.(
144
) and Eqn.(
154
) gives us,

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\dfrac{1}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}+i\varepsilon}(1-e^{-\beta\omega^{\prime}})\rho_{0}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k})~~.

(155)

using the identity,

\dfrac{1}{\Delta\pm i\varepsilon}=\mathbb{P}\Big(\dfrac{1}{\Delta}\Big)\mp i\pi\delta(\Delta)~~,

gives us,

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\dfrac{i}{2}(1-e^{-\beta\omega})\rho_{0}(\omega,\mathbf{k})-\mathbb{P}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\dfrac{1}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}(1-e^{-\beta\omega^{\prime}})\rho_{0}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k})

(156)

From this, we can write,

\mathcal{\widetilde{G}^{R}}(0,\mathbf{k})=\mathbb{P}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\dfrac{1}{\omega^{\prime}}(1-e^{-\beta\omega^{\prime}})\rho_{0}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k}),~~.

(157)

and

\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{\widetilde{G}^{R}}}{\partial\omega}(0,\mathbf{k})=\dfrac{i}{2T}\rho_{0}(0,\mathbf{k})~~.

(158)

In the above equation, we have used the fact that the spectral density function
\rho(\omega,\mathbf{k})=(1-e^{-\beta\omega})\rho_{0}(\omega,\mathbf{k})
is an odd function of
\omega

-\partial_{i}\partial^{\prime}_{0}(\langle[A_{0}(t,\mathbf{x}),A_{j}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle\theta(t-t^{\prime}))\\
-\partial_{0}\partial^{\prime}_{j}(\langle[A_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}),A_{0}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle\theta(t-t^{\prime}))\\
+\partial_{0}\partial^{\prime}_{0}(\langle[A_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}),A_{j}(t^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle\theta(t-t^{\prime}))\\
-i\delta_{ij}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\end{split}

(160)

Let us include the
\delta
function at the end. In the standard notation of
[
77
]
where,

D^{R}_{ij}=i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[A_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}),A_{j}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]\rangle_{0}~~,

(161)

we can express the quantity of interest as,

\mathcal{G}_{ij}(t-t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\partial_{i}\partial^{\prime}_{j}D^{R}_{00}-\partial_{i}\partial^{\prime}_{0}D^{R}_{0j}-\partial_{0}\partial^{\prime}{j}D^{R}_{i0}+\partial_{0}\partial^{\prime}_{0}D^{R}_{ij}

(162)

In the Temporal Gauge, the only non-vanishing propagator is
D_{ij}
, hence, this reduces our expression to,

\mathcal{G}_{ij}(t-t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\partial_{0}\partial^{\prime}_{0}D^{R}_{ij}

(163)

In the Fourier modes, this looks like,

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ij}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\omega^{2}\widetilde{D}^{R}_{ij}(\omega,\mathbf{k})~~.

(164)

We have a standard expression for
\widetilde{D}^{R}_{ij}
given by
[
77
]

\widetilde{D}^{R}_{ij}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=-\Big(\dfrac{1}{\omega^{2}-k^{2}-F}P_{ij}^{L}+\dfrac{1}{\omega^{2}-k^{2}-G}P_{ij}^{T}\Big)

(165)

where,
|\mathbf{k}|=k
, and

\begin{split}P_{ij}^{L}=\dfrac{\omega^{2}-k^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\dfrac{k_{i}k_{j}}{k^{2}}\\
P^{T}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\dfrac{k_{i}k_{j}}{k^{2}}\\
F=-2\dfrac{m^{2}(\omega^{2}-k^{2})}{k^{2}}\Big(1-\dfrac{\omega}{k}Q_{0}\Big(\dfrac{\omega}{k}\Big)\Big)\\
G=m^{2}\Big(\dfrac{\omega}{k}\Big)\Big[\dfrac{\omega}{k}+\Big(1-\Big(\dfrac{\omega}{k}\Big)^{2}\Big)Q_{0}\Big(\dfrac{\omega}{k}\Big)\Big]\\
Q_{0}\Big(\dfrac{\omega}{k}\Big)=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\Big(\dfrac{\omega+k}{\omega-k}\Big)-\dfrac{i\pi}{2}\theta(k^{2}-\omega^{2})\end{split}

(166)

The propagator can be expressed in other gauges but as shown in
[
77
]
, will leave us with the same result. Here, the quantity
m
is related to the plasma temperature as
m=eT/\sqrt{6}
.

As we are interested in the regime where we expand near
\omega=0
, in this limit, the terms that are relevant to us are:

\mathsf{Re}(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ij})(0,k)=-\dfrac{k^{2}}{2m^{2}+k^{2}}

(167)

and,

\mathsf{Im}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ij}}{\partial\omega}(0,k)\Big)=\dfrac{m^{2}\pi k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}

(168)

The imaginary part of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ij}(\omega,\mathbf{k})
and the real part of
\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ij}(\omega,\mathbf{k})
vanishes in the limit
\omega\rightarrow 0
. If these parts were non-zero, they would correspond to microscopic
oscillations
of the axion profile. However, as we look at the coherent state, these microscopic oscillations are of less importance when looking at macroscopic motion. The integral then looks like,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})+i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\Big(-\dfrac{k^{2}}{2m^{2}+k^{2}}\\
+i\omega\dfrac{m^{2}\pi k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\Big)P^{L}_{ij}\xi_{ij}\varphi(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})~~.\end{split}

(169)

D.2
Simplifying the two parts

Let us now first concentrate on the second integral, i.e.,

\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\dfrac{m^{2}\pi k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}P^{L}_{ij}\xi_{ij}\partial_{t}(\varphi(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))~~.

(170)

We consider that the wall’s radius of curvature is large compared to its thickness. This is a valid assumption to make as usually, the walls are of the horizon size or a few orders smaller, which is still much larger than the thickness. This allows us to treat the wall flat locally. We consider that
\varphi
is a function of
x_{\perp}
. In this case,

\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-m^{2}\pi\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\xi_{ij}(0,|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|)P^{L}_{ij}\partial_{t}(\varphi(t,x^{\prime}_{\perp}))~~.

(171)

Let us Fourier transform both
\xi_{ij}
and
\partial_{t}\varphi
. This is given by,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-m^{2}\pi\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\eta(p)\Big(\delta_{ij}-\dfrac{p_{i}p_{j}}{p^{2}}\Big)\dfrac{k_{i}k_{j}}{k^{2}}\\
\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{dp_{2}}{2\pi}e^{ip_{2}(x^{\prime}_{\perp}-vt)}g(p_{2})\end{split}

(172)

Here we have considered that the wall depends on a single coordinate given by
x^{\prime}_{\perp}
. As mentioned before, the tensor structure of
\xi_{ij}
comes from the
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}_{bg}=0
condition.
The tensor structure is,

\Big(\delta_{ij}-\dfrac{p_{i}p_{j}}{p^{2}}\Big)\dfrac{k_{i}k_{j}}{k^{2}}=1-\dfrac{(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{k})^{2}}{p^{2}k^{2}}=1-\dfrac{(p_{\perp}k_{\perp}+p_{\parallel}\cdot k_{\parallel})^{2}}{(p_{\perp}^{2}+p_{\parallel}^{2})(k_{\perp}^{2}+k_{\parallel}^{2})}

(173)

The coordinates parallel to the wall are denoted by
x^{\prime}_{\parallel}
. We can integrate with respect to them. This gives,
(2\pi)^{2}\delta^{(2)}(k_{\parallel}+p_{\parallel})
.
This can then be integrated with respect to
d^{2}p_{\parallel}
. This then leaves us with,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-m^{2}\pi\int dx^{\prime}_{\perp}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{dp_{\perp}}{2\pi}e^{i(k_{\perp}+p_{\perp})\cdot(x_{\perp}-x^{\prime}_{\perp})}\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\eta(\sqrt{p_{\perp}^{2}+k_{\parallel}^{2}})\\
\times\Big(1-\dfrac{(p_{\perp}k_{\perp}-k_{\parallel}^{2})^{2}}{(k_{\parallel}^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})(k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2})}\Big)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{dp_{2}}{2\pi}e^{ip_{2}(x^{\prime}_{\perp}-vt)}g(p_{2})\end{split}

(174)

Now, one can integrate with respect to
x_{\perp}^{\prime}
. This will gives
2\pi\delta(k_{\perp}+p_{\perp}-p_{2})
. This object can then be integrated with respect to
p_{2}
variable. This gives,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-m^{2}\pi\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{dp_{\perp}}{2\pi}e^{i(k_{\perp}+p_{\perp})\cdot(x_{\perp}-vt)}\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\eta(\sqrt{p_{\perp}^{2}+k_{\parallel}^{2}})\\
\times\Big(1-\dfrac{(p_{\perp}k_{\perp}-k_{\parallel}^{2})^{2}}{(k_{\parallel}^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})(k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2})}\Big)g(k_{\perp}+p_{\perp})\end{split}

(175)

We can simplify the bracket inside the integral as:

1-\dfrac{(p_{\perp}k_{\perp}-k_{\parallel}^{2})^{2}}{(k_{\parallel}^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})(k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2})}=\dfrac{k_{\parallel}^{2}(p_{\perp}+k_{\perp})^{2}}{(k_{\parallel}^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})(k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2})}

(176)

Hence, the integral looks like,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-m^{2}\pi\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{dp_{\perp}}{2\pi}e^{i(k_{\perp}+p_{\perp})\cdot(x_{\perp}-vt)}\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\eta(\sqrt{p_{\perp}^{2}+k_{\parallel}^{2}})\\
\times\Big(\dfrac{k_{\parallel}^{2}(p_{\perp}+k_{\perp})^{2}}{(k_{\parallel}^{2}+p_{\perp}^{2})(k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2})}\Big)g(k_{\perp}+p_{\perp})\end{split}

(177)

One can substitute
p_{\perp}+k_{\perp}=q
. This gives,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-m^{2}\pi\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{dq}{2\pi}e^{iq(x_{\perp}-vt)}\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\eta(\sqrt{(q-k_{\perp}^{2}+k_{\parallel}^{2}})\\
\times\Big(\dfrac{k_{\parallel}^{2}q^{2}}{(k_{\parallel}^{2}+(q-k_{\perp})^{2})(k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2})}\Big)g(q)\end{split}

(178)

Rearranging this gives us,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-m^{2}\pi\int\dfrac{dq}{2\pi}e^{iq(x_{\perp}-vt)}g(q)q^{2}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\eta(\sqrt{(q-k_{\perp})^{2}+k_{\parallel}^{2}})\\
\times\Big(\dfrac{k_{\parallel}^{2}}{(k_{\parallel}^{2}+(q-k_{\perp})^{2})(k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2})}\Big)\end{split}

(179)

Now, we can shift the vector
\mathbf{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{k}+q\hat{k}_{\perp}
. This will leave the measure invariant. This will give us,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-m^{2}\pi\int\dfrac{dq}{2\pi}e^{iq(x_{\perp}-vt)}g(q)q^{2}\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\dfrac{1}{(2m^{2}+k_{\parallel}^{2}+(k_{\perp}+q)^{2})^{2}}\eta(k)\\
\times\Big(\dfrac{k_{\parallel}^{2}}{k^{2}\sqrt{(k_{\parallel}^{2}+(k_{\perp}+q)^{2})}}\Big)\end{split}

(180)

Here,
k_{\parallel}=k\sin\theta,~k_{\perp}=k\cos\theta
. Substituting this gives us,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi^{2}}m^{2}\pi\int\dfrac{dq}{2\pi}e^{iq(x_{\perp}-vt)}g(q)q^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\dfrac{\sin\theta}{(2m^{2}+k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta+(k\cos\theta+q)^{2})^{2}}\\
\times\eta(k)\Big(\dfrac{k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\sqrt{(k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta+(k\cos\theta+q)^{2})}}\Big)\end{split}

(181)

Further, if write
\cos\theta=y
, then,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi^{2}}m^{2}\pi\int\dfrac{dq}{2\pi}e^{iq(x_{\perp}-vt)}g(q)q^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkk^{2}\eta(k)\\
\times\int_{-1}^{1}dx\dfrac{1-x^{2}}{(2m^{2}+k^{2}+q^{2}+2kqx)^{2}}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{k^{2}+q^{2}+2kqx}}\end{split}

(182)

The integral with respect to
x
is done by Mathematica,

\begin{split}\int_{-1}^{1}dx\dfrac{1-x^{2}}{(2m^{2}+k^{2}+q^{2}+2kqx)^{2}}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{k^{2}+q^{2}+2kqx}}=\\
\dfrac{1}{8(\sqrt{2}m)^{3}k^{3}q^{3}}\Big(\sqrt{2}m((\sqrt{(k-q)^{2}}-\sqrt{(k+q)^{2}})(3(\sqrt{2}m)^{2}+k^{2}+q^{2})\\
+2kq(\sqrt{(k-q)^{2}}+\sqrt{(k+q)^{2}}))+3(\sqrt{2}m)^{4}-(k^{2}-q^{2})^{2}\\
+2(\sqrt{2}m)^{2}(k^{2}+q^{2})\Big(\text{arccot}\Big(\dfrac{\sqrt{2}m}{\sqrt{(k+q)^{2}}}\Big)-\text{arccot}\Big(\dfrac{\sqrt{2}m}{\sqrt{(k-q)^{2}}}\Big)\Big)\Big)\end{split}

(183)

Note that the function
g(q)
, i.e., the Fourier transform of
\partial_{t}\varphi
peaks around
q=0
. It has a characteristic width of
\gamma m_{a}
. The Linear response with initial simplification of expanding the electric field correlator around
\omega=0
is correct when
\gamma m_{a}<eT
. Otherwise, for a relativistic wall, the self-crossing time is approximately around
(\gamma m_{a})^{-1}\sim(eT)^{-1}
where the
\omega=0
approximation is not a good one. In the limit where
\gamma m_{a}<eT
, the leading order contribution of the integral in
q
can be calculated by setting (
183
) near
q=0
. This is given by,

\dfrac{4}{3}\dfrac{1}{k(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}

(184)

Hence, what we have is,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=-\dfrac{1}{3\pi^{2}}m^{2}\pi\int\dfrac{dq}{2\pi}e^{iq(x_{\perp}-vt)}g(q)q^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\eta(k)\end{split}

(185)

The
q
integral is nothing by,

\int\dfrac{dq}{2\pi}e^{iq(x_{\perp}-vt)}g(q)q^{2}=-\partial_{x_{\perp}}^{2}\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x_{\perp})

(186)

Hence, we get,

\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=\dfrac{m^{2}}{3\pi}\partial_{t}\partial_{x_{\perp}}^{2}\varphi(t,x_{\perp})\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\dfrac{k}{(2m^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}\eta(k)~~.

(187)

The same procedure can be repeated for the first term in Eqn.(
46
). This simplifies the equation to,

\mathcal{I}_{1,1}=\dfrac{1}{3\pi^{3}}\partial_{x_{\perp}}^{2}\varphi(t,x_{\perp})\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{k^{2}}{2m^{2}+k^{2}}\eta(k)~~.

(188)

Appendix E
Calculating the spectral density

Let us start with the equation at hand,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{3}=\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\langle\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}\partial_{\sigma}A_{\tau}\partial^{\prime}_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\partial^{\prime}_{\gamma}A_{\delta}\rangle=\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\int\prod\dfrac{d^{4}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})t}e^{i(\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2})\cdot\mathbf{x})}\\
\times e^{-i(\omega_{3}+\omega_{4})t^{\prime}}e^{i(\mathbf{p}_{3}+\mathbf{p}_{4})\cdot\mathbf{x})}(p_{1})_{\mu}(p_{2})_{\sigma}(p_{3})_{\alpha}(p_{4})_{\gamma}\langle\widetilde{A}_{\nu}(p_{1})\widetilde{A}_{\tau}(p_{2})\widetilde{A}_{\beta}(p_{3})\widetilde{A}_{\delta}(p_{4})\rangle\end{split}

(189)

We look at the
tree level diagram
without considering any fermion loops between the four-photon operators. We can contract the fields in two ways:
\{(\nu,\beta),(\tau,\delta)\},~\{(\nu,\delta),(\tau,\beta)\}
. The remaining contraction vanishes due to the
\epsilon
-tensor sitting outside the integral. We write:

\langle\widetilde{A}_{\nu}(p_{1})\widetilde{A}_{\beta}(p_{3})\rangle=(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}(p_{1}+p_{3})(P^{T}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{T}(p_{1})+P^{L}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{L}(p_{1}))

(190)

Here,
P^{T/L}
are the transverse and longitudinal projection operators respectively and
\Delta^{T/L}
are the longitudinal and transverse propagators. Note that these are not time-ordered thermal averages. Hence, in the standard thermal field theory notation, these are
D_{21}
type propagators
[
75
]
. We can substitute this in the equation above. This will give us,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{3}=\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\int\prod\dfrac{d^{4}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})t}e^{i(\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2})\cdot\mathbf{x})}e^{-i(\omega_{3}+\omega_{4})t^{\prime}}e^{i(\mathbf{p}_{3}+\mathbf{p}_{4})\cdot\mathbf{x})}(p_{1})_{\mu}(p_{2})_{\sigma}(p_{3})_{\alpha}(p_{4})_{\gamma}\\
\times(2\pi)^{8}(P^{T}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{T}(p_{1})+P^{L}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{L}(p_{1}))(P^{T}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{T}(p_{2})+P^{L}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{L}(p_{2}))\\
(\delta^{(4)}(p_{1}+p_{3})\delta^{(4)}(p_{2}+p_{4})-\delta^{(4)}(p_{1}+p_{4})\delta^{(4)}(p_{2}+p_{3}))\end{split}

(191)

The negative sign in the last bracket comes from exchanging
\beta,\delta
in the
\epsilon
tensor. This then gives us,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{3}=\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\int\prod\dfrac{d^{4}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i(\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\\
\times(P^{T}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{T}(p_{1})+P^{L}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{L}(p_{1}))(P^{T}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{T}(p_{2})+P^{L}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{L}(p_{2}))\\
((p_{1})_{\mu}(p_{2})_{\sigma}(p_{1})_{\alpha}(p_{2})_{\gamma}-(p_{1})_{\mu}(p_{2})_{\sigma}(p_{2})_{\alpha}(p_{1})_{\gamma})\end{split}

(192)

The term in the bracket can be rearranged using the property of
\epsilon
tensor to get,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{3}=2\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\int\prod\dfrac{d^{4}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i(\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\\
\times(P^{T}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{T}(p_{1})+P^{L}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{L}(p_{1}))(P^{T}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{T}(p_{2})+P^{L}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{L}(p_{2}))\\
((p_{1})_{\mu}(p_{2})_{\sigma}(p_{1})_{\alpha}(p_{2})_{\gamma})\end{split}

(193)

We can now define
p_{1}+p_{2}=k
. This gives us,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{3}=2\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\int\dfrac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\int\dfrac{d^{4}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\\
\times(P^{T}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{T}(p_{1})+P^{L}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{L}(p_{1}))(P^{T}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{T}(k-p_{1})+P^{L}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{L}(k-p_{1}))\\
((p_{1})_{\mu}(k-p_{1})_{\sigma}(p_{1})_{\alpha}(k-p_{1})_{\gamma})\end{split}

(194)

One can think of this as the following diagram, where one operator injects momentum
k
and the other one collects it.

\mathcal{O}
\mathcal{O}

k-p_{1}

Figure 9
:
The correlator can be thought of as an exchange at one point where the operator takes momentum
k
from the corresponding mode of the wall and the other operator collects it. Note that as mentioned in the text, these are the
D_{12}
correlators.

This tells us that the quantity we want is,

\begin{split}\rho_{0}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\dfrac{1}{32}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\int\dfrac{d^{4}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\times(P^{T}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{T}(p_{1})+P^{L}_{\nu\beta}\Delta^{L}(p_{1}))\\
(P^{T}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{T}(k-p_{1})+P^{L}_{\tau\delta}\Delta^{L}(k-p_{1}))((p_{1})_{\mu}(k)_{\sigma}(p_{1})_{\alpha}(k)_{\gamma})\end{split}

(195)

Now, we can choose a gauge to work with. The choice of gauge only affects the projectors and not the
\Delta
propagators. Let us work in the Coulomb Gauge. In this gauge, the projection tensors take the form,

P^{T}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\dfrac{p_{i}p_{j}}{p^{2}},~~P^{L}_{\mu\nu}=\dfrac{p_{0}^{2}-p^{2}}{p^{2}}u_{\mu}u_{\nu},~~p=|\mathbf{p}|

(196)

where
u_{\mu}
is frame four velocity. Here, we are considering everything in the plasma frame which is at rest hence
u=(1,0,0,0)
. From the structure of these, we can directly see that
\Delta^{L}(p)^{2}
terms won’t contribute. For the transverse part to be non-zero,
\nu,\beta,\tau,\delta\in\{1,2\}
. This means
\mu=\alpha=0
. Hence, what we get is,

\epsilon^{\nu\tau 03}\epsilon^{\beta\delta 03}\Big(\delta_{\nu\beta}-\dfrac{p_{\nu}p_{\beta}}{p^{2}}\Big)\Big(\delta_{\tau\delta}-\dfrac{p_{\tau}p_{\delta}}{p^{2}}\Big)p_{0}^{2}\Delta^{T}(p)\Delta^{T}(k-p)

(197)

This is nothing but,

2\Big(1-\dfrac{p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}}{p^{2}}\Big)p_{0}^{2}\Delta^{T}(p)\Delta^{T}(k-p)=2\cos^{2}\theta p_{0}^{2}\Delta^{T}(p)\Delta^{T}(k-p)

(198)

Similarly, the
\Delta^{T}\Delta^{L}
also has a non-zero coefficient. It is given by,

\epsilon^{\mu\nu 03}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta 03}\Big(\delta_{\nu\beta}-\dfrac{p_{\nu}p_{\beta}}{p^{2}}\Big)p_{\mu}p_{\alpha}=(p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2})=p^{2}\sin^{2}\theta

(199)

So, the integral looks like:

\begin{split}\rho_{0}(0,k)=\dfrac{1}{32}k^{2}\int\dfrac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\Big(2\cos^{2}\theta p_{0}^{2}\Delta^{T}(p)^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta(p_{0}^{2}-p^{2})(\Delta^{T}(p)\Delta^{L}(k-p)\\
+\Delta^{T}(k-p)\Delta^{L}(p))\Big)\end{split}

(200)

We can integrate with respect to the azimuthal angle to get an additional factor of
2\pi
