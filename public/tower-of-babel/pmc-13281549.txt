Abortion care pathways in Addis Ababa healthcare facilities: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Negash Wakgari, Gizachew A. Tessema, Stuart J. Watson, Delayehu Bekele, Zoe Bradfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding how women navigate induced abortion care pathways is critical to ensuring person-centred, quality reproductive health services. Evidence indicates that persistent abortion stigma, the lack of choices of abortion methods and respectful care during abortion remain a global challenge to reproductive healthcare. Yet there is minimal evidence regarding abortion care pathways. This study explored induced abortion care pathways in Addis Ababa healthcare facilities.MethodsWe used a descriptive qualitative approach, adopting purposive sampling techniques to recruit women who sought induced abortion care from seven facilities. The data were collected from May to July 2024. In-depth semi-structured interviews with sixteen women were digitally recorded and transcribed into the local language before being translated into English. Data were coded, organised, and analysed using inductive thematic analysis.ResultsFive main themes and their corresponding subthemes were developed through data analysis. Themes were: (i) social and emotional support, (ii) moral and social meanings shaping abortion care, (iii) accessibility and service delivery, (iv) perceived competency of abortion providers, and (v) physical and emotional effects of abortion. Many women attended the clinic alone, without their families, and received no support. Women often sought care at clinics away from their community due to concerns related to fear of stigma and social pressure. This study found long waiting times to receive abortion care, a lack of medicine and ultrasound at some facilities and limited availability of second-trimester abortions. Women reported that many providers were welcoming and competent, while others reported poor communication, the use of medical jargon, and stigmatising behaviours. Participant reported pressure to accept methods they did not want during contraceptive counselling and fear of breaches in privacy and confidentiality. Participants also described physical symptoms such as bleeding and pain, and felt ashamed and upset after the abortion, which could be associated with negative experiences.ConclusionsInadequate social support, abortion stigma, and barriers to accessing abortion services, such as long waiting times and insufficient resources, were identified as significant gaps. These findings emphasised the need to strengthen person-centred abortion care and address systemic and socio-cultural barriers that undermine the quality of care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02333-w.

Background

Abortion is recognised as a global public health issue, with 4.7%-13.2% of global maternal deaths annually attributed to a lack of quality and safe abortion services [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes quality abortion care as accessible, equitable, and acceptable for women receiving services [2, 3]. Induced abortion is defined as the termination of pregnancy using an approved medical or surgical method [4]. Medical abortion uses medications such as mifepristone and misoprostol, or misoprostol alone, to terminate pregnancy. It is a non-invasive method commonly used in early pregnancy, which can be provided in clinical settings or at home with appropriate guidance [5]. Surgical abortion is a medical procedure performed by a trained provider involving the removal of the contents of the uterus using manual vacuum aspiration, dilation and evacuation [2]. A comprehensive induced abortion care includes pre-abortion, abortion, and post-abortion services [6, 7]. Safe and quality abortion services emphasise the establishment of good communication, supportive care, and emotional support, which improve outcomes for women throughout the abortion care pathways [8, 9].

Abortion care pathways refer to the sequence of steps, interactions and experiences that women navigate from the decision to have an abortion through accessing information, receiving an abortion, post-abortion care, and navigating the broader social, health-system, and personal circumstances that shape these processes [2, 10, 11]. Abortion care pathways encompass information-seeking regarding options and service availability, overcoming logistical and social barriers, influences of interpersonal and community factors, such as stigma, partner dynamics and provider attitudes, which shape the trajectory of the abortion care pathways [12, 13]. The concept of abortion care pathways helps to identify barriers to quality care, understand service disparities, and enhance integration of services so that abortion care is accessible, respectful, and person-centred [2, 10].

Studies revealed legal and access barriers to abortion care pathways, highlighting delays to receive care, information barriers and travel burdens [7, 14]. A review from 42 countries reported on various experiences women come across while seeking abortion, from supportive care to abusive and undignified care [14]. Physical and emotional challenges after abortion, mixed experiences with social support and abortion providers were revealed [15]. Moreover, research has identified challenges to abortion care pathways, such as availability, acceptability, affordability, and appropriateness of the care, which are threats to the quality of abortion services globally [16–18]. Achieving quality abortion care pathways is crucial in realising patient-centred care and providing quality health services. Evidence indicates that the lack of person-centred and respectful abortion services remains a global challenge, possibly due to a recognised linkage between social stigma and lack of service accessibility and availability [19, 20].

Despite work to improve the quality of abortion services in the last two decades, lack of availability, poor quality services, and stigma remain challenges to women’s reproductive health rights and abortion care pathways in Ethiopia [21, 22]. Evidence indicates that basic safe abortion care capacity increased from 66.7 to 82.8%, and the number of safe abortions increased by 9.7% from 2018 to 2020 [23]. However, abortion-related morbidity and negative abortion care experiences, such as long waiting times, disrespect from providers, and stigma, remain challenges to the abortion care pathways and the quality of service provision in Ethiopia [24–26] .

Although studies have been published on the quality of care and abortion care experiences from client perspectives [18, 27–29], we could not identify studies that have explored the abortion care pathways at all levels of healthcare facilities where abortion services are being offered: hospitals, health centres, and private clinics in Ethiopia. Ethiopia is one of the countries with semi-liberalised abortion policies in the Horn of Africa. Abortion is legally permitted in Ethiopia, either to save a woman’s life, to maintain physical and mental health, for pregnancy due to rape or incest and for foetal impairments [21]. Despite the supportive legal framework, women often face multiple barriers, including stigma, informational, social, and structural barriers, delaying or deterring access to safe abortion care [30, 31]. Evidence shows a lack of adequate awareness about abortion’s legal status in Ethiopia, which influences how women navigate abortion care pathways [32, 33]. From a human rights perspective, exploring women’s experiences of the abortion care pathways is crucial and can determine treatment outcomes and reduce abortion-related morbidity and mortality [14, 29, 34]. Understanding how women navigate abortion care pathways, experience abortion stigma, and undesirable interactions between women and abortion providers is crucial to improve the safety and quality of care. Therefore, this study aimed to explore women’s abortion care pathways in Addis Ababa healthcare facilities.

Methods

Design

A descriptive qualitative methodology was selected for this study [35, 36]. Descriptive qualitative approaches enable researchers to stay ‘data-near’ and gain an understanding of individuals’ experiences by focusing on participant descriptives in a low-inference manner [37].

Research setting and study population

This study was conducted among women who sought abortion care in Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. Addis Ababa is the most densely populated city in Ethiopia, with a population exceeding 5 million [38]. The city has 14 public hospitals, 98 public health centres, 40 privately owned hospitals and about 759 private clinics [39]. The Ethiopian healthcare system is a three-tier system with primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care. At the grassroots level, primary care includes primary hospitals, health centres, and health posts. Primary healthcare focuses on preventive care, provides maternal and child health services, and treats common illnesses. Health Post, the lowest primary healthcare level, is not authorised to provide abortion care. Secondary and tertiary levels provide inpatient and ambulatory services and a more extensive range of specialised medical and surgical services, including abortion services [40]. Seven healthcare facilities across the region: three public hospitals, two privately owned clinics (one sexual and reproductive health clinic, one maternal and child health centre), and two health centres, formed the setting for this study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women over 18 years of age who sought induced abortion care, presenting to seven healthcare facilities across the region during the study period, were eligible for inclusion in the study. In addition, women who attended the selected healthcare facilities for the follow-up after a previous induced abortion were also eligible. However, women who received postabortion care following a spontaneous abortion or miscarriage, and women who had an abortion with clinical complications or were clinically unstable for interviews, were excluded from the study.

Recruitment

Purposive sampling techniques were used to recruit study participants. Maximum variation sampling methods were used to include women seeking induced abortion care from different demographic backgrounds, enhancing the variation within the study population [41, 42]. Women’s different experiences of abortion care pathways were accessed through maximum variation sampling. Maximum variation sampling was ensured by selecting women from diverse socio-demographic backgrounds in terms of place of residence (urban versus rural), age, marital status, educational level, and occupation and service-related factors such as type of healthcare facility (public hospital, health centre, and private clinic) and level of facility (primary, secondary, tertiary).

Participants were enrolled from healthcare facilities providing induced abortion, including medical abortion, surgical abortion, and follow-up care after induced abortion. Women accessing abortion care at different gestational weeks within the service scope of the participating healthcare facilities were invited to participate in the study. Participants were recruited from the three public hospitals, two privately owned clinics, and two health centres.

The data collector contacted the abortion provider at the clinic to access the women’s medical registration book/record. A medical registration book/record containing a list of women who sought abortion was provided to the data collector by the abortion provider. The registration book provided information on the types of abortion women received (medical, surgical, follow-up after induced abortion or spontaneous/miscarriage). The medical registration book/record was accessed solely to verify participant eligibility for the study; no data were collected from participants’ medical records. A trained data collector reviewed the registration records within the healthcare facility. Eligible participants were approached by a data collector after they had received an abortion and before leaving the healthcare facility.

Women were approached privately and provided with detailed information about the study. Written informed consent was obtained only after women had been fully informed about the study and reassured that their participation was voluntary and would not affect the care they received. The women who wished to participate in the study provided written informed consent. All information collected from the registration book to link to the woman, such as names and card numbers, was destroyed once the woman was identified and approached for consent. This information was kept secure and confidential until researchers destroyed it. A total of 19 participants were approached. Of which, 16 consented, and 3 indicated they were not interested in participating in the study. As participants declined at the point of invitation, no further data were collected from them. Reasons for declining were also not recorded.

The principle of data saturation, where no new descriptions of the phenomenon under study were found, was used to determine the final sample size [43]. Participants’ recruitment and interviews were conducted simultaneously with preliminary data analysis to monitor the emergence of new themes. After fourteen interviews, no new codes were identified during the ongoing analysis, indicating that thematic saturation was being approached. To ensure that no new themes were present, two further interviews were conducted. Data saturation was deemed achieved as these interviews did not yield additional themes, and recruitment concluded with 16 participants.

Data collection tools and procedures

Semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted from May to July 2024. The interview guide was focused on how participants decided to seek safe abortion, support received from partners, family and friends. It also asks about abortion procedure received, abortion provider approach and communication, privacy, stigma, judgment, and feelings about abortion. Once women provided written informed consent to proceed with the study, a face-to-face interview was conducted. Two master’s degree-qualified and trained midwife interviewers, experienced in data collection, conducted the interview, which supported this process [44]. One trained data collector conducted each interview. The interviews lasted between 35 and 60 min. The interviews were conducted in Amharic. A member of the research team provided one-day training on the study’s aim and data collection procedures to the data collectors and coordinated and supervised the data collection.

Given the sensitive nature of abortion services, special attention was given to creating a supportive and respectful interview environment. Interviews were conducted in a private, secured, and comfortable room within the healthcare facilities to ensure participants’ privacy and confidentiality and minimise interruptions. At the beginning of each interview, the interviewer emphasised voluntary participation and reassured participants that their responses would remain confidential and would not affect the care they received. Before commencing formal interview questions, time was taken to build rapport through informal conversation and by checking whether participants felt comfortable to proceed. Interviewers used empathetic listening and remained mindful of maintaining a non-judgmental approach to encourage open discussion. Participants were also reminded that they could decline to answer any question or stop the interview at any time if they felt uncomfortable, a point emphasised during the interview.

To further protect participants’ confidentiality, no names or personal identifiers were recorded during the interviews. A unique study identification number was assigned to each participant and used throughout data collection and analysis. During transcription, any potentially identifying information mentioned during interviews was removed to ensure anonymity. All audio recordings and transcripts were stored in the Curtin University R-drive and in password-protected electronic files accessible only to the research team. These procedures complied with the ethical approvals obtained from the Curtin University Human Research Ethics Office (HRE2024-0299).

A voice recorder was utilised to capture participants’ descriptions, with their permission obtained beforehand. Audio files were transcribed into written Amharic and subsequently translated into English. A member of the research team with experience in transcribing Amharic data and English translation for publication transcribed and translated the data. While the member of the research team was experienced in transcription and translation, there were also several strategies in place to ensure the validity and rigour of translation: Firstly, the first author received assistance from two fellow Doctor of Philosophy Candidates who had experience in transcription and translation of Amharic data to each undertake three transcriptions and translations to ensure validity and conceptual consistency. Secondly, two members of the research team, who are native Amharic speakers and have experience translating Amharic data into English, assessed the translated data from randomly selected interviews and rechecked it before proceeding to the analysis to mitigate data loss. In this way, transcription and translation of interviews were verified and validated by two qualified, independent research team members.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis [45, 46]. Six steps to employ inductive thematic analysis were adopted: (i) familiarisation with the data, (ii) creating codes, (iii) searching for themes, (iv) reviewing themes, (v) defining and naming themes, and (vi) producing the report [47]. Unlike deductive analysis, which begins with predefined themes based on a theoretical framework or prior research, inductive thematic analysis starts with the data, identifying emergent themes within the text [48].

First, all audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim, and the first author repeatedly read the transcripts to become familiar with the data and gain an overall understanding of participants’ experiences. Transcripts were cross-checked by the research team before coding. Second, initial coding was conducted by carefully reviewing the transcripts line by line to identify meaningful segments of data relevant to the study objective. Codes were produced inductively from the data. NVivo 15 software was used to support the organisation and management of data during the coding process. The research team held meetings regularly during the coding process to review code content, confirm coding consistency, and organise the main themes. Third, related/connected codes were grouped into preliminary categories. These categories were then assessed to identify patterns and relationships across participants’ data. The broader themes with shared meanings within the data were developed during this stage. Fourth, the initial themes were reviewed and refined to ensure they accurately represented participants’ narratives. This was done by comparing initial themes with the coded extracts and the full dataset. During this stage, themes were merged, modified, or separated as necessary. Fifth, the research team met regularly to discuss the development of the thematic structure. Thematic development was discussed through a consensus approach, with reference to the verbatim text to resolve any discrepancies. The research team resolved differences in data interpretation by referring to the original transcripts and discussing them. Finally, themes were clearly defined and named, and verbatim quotations were selected to support the findings and demonstrate how themes were derived from participants’ experiences. Verbatim quotes appear italicised in text using participant numbers to indicate the range of responses. For brevity, removed text is indicated via … and any text inserted to support meaning appears in square brackets.

Rigour and trustworthiness of the data

Rigour and trustworthiness were enhanced through purposive maximum variation sampling, which allowed the inclusion of participants from diverse socio-demographic backgrounds across seven healthcare facilities in Addis Ababa, and through adequate time spent on sensitive data collection and data immersion. In addition, we employed member checking and peer debriefing to enhance the qualitative rigour of our research and considered the “Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research” [49], which is attached as Additional File 1. An audit trail documenting methodological decisions, coding development, and theme refinement was maintained throughout the study.

Furthermore, researcher reflexivity was used to avoid prejudgments and any personal biases during the interview and data analysis. Researchers and participants had no prior relationship and did not know each other before the interview, which mitigated bias and enhanced credibility and trustworthiness. The research team included midwives, a psychologist, reproductive health experts, and qualitative researchers. The research team have extensive experience in abortion research in Ethiopia and global health settings. Researchers’ professional backgrounds provided contextual understanding of abortion services and the broader healthcare system environment in which the study was conducted. Additionally, the research team were attentive to how their disciplinary perspectives, professional experiences, and prior knowledge of abortion care could influence the research process, including data collection, interpretation, and theme development.

Reflexive discussions were held throughout the research process. The research team recorded their thoughts and reactions to the data, allowing them to identify potential biases and amend their approach accordingly. We used open-ended questioning and neutral prompts during the interview to minimise leading participants’ responses. To ensure interpretations were grounded in participants’ data, transcripts were independently reviewed. The research team discussed codes and themes supported by verbatim text to avoid the researchers’ assumptions. These practices enhanced the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings.

Themes

The findings show how participants navigated abortion care through interconnected stages that together formed an abortion care pathway, resulting in the five key themes. Theme one describes that once pregnancy is recognised, making decisions about abortion is often influenced by participants’ close social networks, Social and emotional support. Participants then moved through stages of information seeking, disclosure, and negotiation of moral and social meanings surrounding abortion, which shaped participants’ willingness and ability to access care, Moral and social meanings shaping abortion care. The third theme describes service unavailability, waiting times, and costs, which further influence participants’ access to care, Accessibility, and service delivery. In the fourth theme, interactions with abortion providers, respectful communication and counselling skills shaped participants’ experiences and perspectives of care, Perceived competency of abortion providers. The pathway extended beyond the procedure itself, encompassing women’s physical and emotional responses following abortion, Physical and emotional effects of abortion. The themes displayed below in Fig. 1 reflect these interconnected stages and illustrate how social, structural, and healthcare factors collectively shape women’s abortion care pathways. In addition to those provided in text, further supporting quotations for each sub-theme are summarised in a Table and attached as an additional file 2.

Themes and sub-themes

Theme 1: social and emotional support

Social and emotional support played a critical role in determining how women navigated abortion care pathways. Support from partners, family members, and friends influenced care pathways. The presence or absence of social support affected decision-making processes, women’s well-being, and overall experiences. In some contexts, participants sought reassurance from partners and family members, reflecting the importance of social and emotional support in the abortion process. Participants’ reasons for abortion shaped the context in which the decision-making process happened. Further, when positive reactions were received to abortion disclosure, emotional support was enhanced, demonstrating the importance of social relationships and emotional support during abortion care. This first theme contains four sub-themes: support from family and friends, reasons for abortion, decision-making, and disclosing abortion.

Support from family and friends

Abortion stigma and fear of judgment limited women’s willingness to seek information and support. Supportive relationships from close social networks shaped how women navigate their abortion journey. Friends, partners and family members provided emotional reassurance, accompanied women to the clinic, and assisted with finances. Participants who obtained practical support from close social networks often reported feeling more confident during and after the procedure. On the contrary, lack of support intensified stress and feelings of loneliness. This sub-theme highlighted variation in experiences of support from family and friends in seeking care, receiving financial assistance, and receiving encouragement.

Women attended the healthcare facility on their own, without an accompanying family member or friend: I came alone. No one was with me. I did not receive any support…(P#6). Some women reported not receiving assistance because they did not disclose their upcoming abortion procedure to their relatives, fearing pressure to continue pregnancy and isolation. I have decided to take care of myself. I do not want my family to know; they want him [husband] to have a baby…(P#2). Women who received support from family and friends described feeling happy and less worried about abortion: I have a supportive mother who is always there for me. She covered the medical expenses and supported me throughout…(P#5).

Reasons for abortion

Reasons for seeking abortion were linked to participants’ social and personal circumstances. Social support often influenced how women interpret and manage these circumstances. A lack of support or understanding from partners or family members led women to experience internal conflict (pursuing an abortion or continuing a pregnancy), delaying seeking services.

Participants mentioned various reasons for abortion, such as relationship breakups, lack of support from partners, financial dependency, unwanted or unplanned pregnancy, family planning failure, pregnancy from rape, and medical reasons. Summarised by the participant: I had a boyfriend. Currently, we have broken up. He told me that he did not want me after this [pregnancy] happened. I decided to have an abortion…because he refused [to support me]…(P#6). P#7 added, I think it is my income and a lack of support person that forced me to have an abortion…without any financial support, it is not easy to continue the pregnancy and give birth.

The woman also described sexual violence: Three people raped me, and I don’t want to have a child like that. I don’t want to remember the situation; I want to forget.(P#4). The participant also mentioned family planning failure: How did I get pregnant after taking the medication [contraception]? I said that the provider gave me an expired medication. I took three months of birth control contraceptives…(P#3). Medical reasons were also reported: Of course, unexpected things sometimes happen…I was convinced not to worry about having an abnormal child [congenital malformation] (P#1).

Decision-making

Whilst abortion is a private process, its decision-making often involves consultation with close social networks. Support from partners and friends enhanced informed decision-making by letting participants discuss their concerns, explore options, seek information, and obtain reassurance. The level of involvement in abortion decision-making varied depending on cultural expectations and autonomy. When supportive dialogue was presented, participants often reported greater confidence and reduced emotional distress in their decision-making.

Boyfriends, husbands, mothers, sisters, and friends were involved in women’s abortion decision-making, and women felt happy in their involvement. Partner involvement was frequently mentioned. This is explained as follows: My husband was involved in the decision-making process. After asking the doctors about everything [the available services], he brought me here, saying this clinic is better…I felt reassured, realising that he cares about me … and I’m happy about his involvement in decision-making because my life was saved (P#12).

In contrast to this, some women decided to have an abortion by themselves: I was alone when I made the decision. I was about to explode with worry. I was talking to myself while sleeping [Nightmare]… I felt distressed; it was not easy to share with my relatives (P#6).

Disclosing abortion

Abortion disclosure refers to whether, how, and to whom women reveal their abortion. Stigma, cultural norms, and the anticipated reactions of others influenced decisions about disclosure. Abortion disclosure served as a mechanism through which participants accessed reassurance and financial support and influenced their progression to the next stages of care. Disclosure acted as a pathway to support but also represented a potential risk in contexts where abortion is stigmatised.

Participants disclosed or, in some instances, planned to disclose their abortion to partners, friends, and people they knew who had had or were planning to have an abortion, either to get support or to share their experiences. Women who disclosed their abortions received both positive and negative feedback, summarised by a participant: After I took the [abortion inducing] medication, I talked to the close people around me. When we sat quietly in a group, I said, “Uff… she [the provider] saved me.” I told them I did not want to have an abortion. However, I did that because of my life condition. Then, half of them say that you “did well”, and the other half say that you “should not have an abortion”. People’s views are varied. I have been living alone…Therefore, I shared my secret with someone I met today, like they were my best friend (P#9). One participant added that I shared my experience with her [close friend]. Other than that, I do not share this with anyone; it is my secret. I do not want anyone else to know about it…(P#14).

Some women did not disclose their abortion due to fear of stigma, isolation, and potential pressure to continue the pregnancy. I have not told anyone about this. I may be ashamed if I say that I did such a thing [abortion]. Maybe if I disclose to my family, they will advise me to give birth. They may be angry if they hear about my abortion (P#3). Similarly, some women reported not receiving assistance because they did not disclose their upcoming abortion procedure to their relatives, fearing pressure to continue pregnancy and isolation. I have decided to take care of myself. I do not want my family to know; they want him [husband] to have a baby…(P#6).

Theme 2: moral and social meanings shaping abortion care

Women’s moral conflicts, as well as community views, are important for the abortion care pathways as they shape women’s abortion care experiences. Moral conflict, stigma, and anticipated social judgment shaped participants’ care-seeking behaviours, including delaying service use or travelling to healthcare facilities outside their communities. This finding demonstrated how social norms and stigma influence movement through the abortion pathway, particularly in the stages of information seeking and service access. These are explained by two sub-themes: moral conflict and emotional struggle around abortion, and community stigma and social judgment.

Moral conflict and emotional struggle around abortion

This subtheme reveals the moral and emotional complexity surrounding participants’ decisions, depicting abortion not experienced as a simple medical event, but as a deeply personal and morally negotiated procedure. Some participants described the perception of abortion as a ‘sin’ and ‘killing’. I am terminating the human soul. However, other people pray for a child. So, it is hard for us to do that (P#10). Another woman also supported this: But abortion is very difficult…I lost the life of my child.It shouldn’t be. Prevent it [abortion] from happening in the first place. Once conceived, it is better to raise… (P#3).

Some women have had mixed emotions describing challenging experiences after their abortion: I am relieved because I decided I cannot raise it, but for me, abortion is a sin. At that time, it seemed I was taking medication simply and having an abortion immediately…I thought it was easy. However, it is not easy (P#7). On the other hand, some women perceived that the decision for abortion was right for them: I do not want to dwell on these thoughts and add more stress to myself. But overall, I believe what I did [abortion] was the right thing (P#14). Another woman was also confident in her decision: This decision is personal…Whether you choose to continue the pregnancy or have an abortion is your decision, and you must protect and support yourself (P#8).

Community stigma and social judgment

This subtheme highlighted that social stigma and fear of social exclusion surrounding abortion within communities shape the abortion care pathway. Participants reported that abortion is socially constructed as wrongdoing within the community, not framed as a personal decision, and stigma extends beyond the individual. They described that the community viewed abortion as sinful and did not recognise their right to have an abortion. This is reflected in the description by (P#16). In our community, a woman who has had an abortion is often labelled as sinful. Others may also be criticised for associating with her. Another participant also described her experience in her community: The community do not accept abortion happily…They say, “She was pregnant and had an abortion.” They gossip about me. I had an abortion because I could not afford to raise a child…(P#7). Participant highlighted the intense community stigma: Wow! It is like facing death [seeking safe abortion care]! Seeking abortion service is considered the ultimate shame (P#2).

In addition, women often sought care at clinics away from their community due to concerns related to fear of stigma and social pressure: Abortion is impossible in our society; that’s why I ran away from the area where I live and came here… Years ago, my aunt encountered an unwanted pregnancy, and she aborted it. The family talked to her, saying that it was wrong. And I will never forget how they spoke to her (P#4).

Theme 3: accessibility and service delivery

Structural factors such as waiting time, unavailability of abortion methods, referral pathways, and cost of services facilitated or delayed timely abortion care. These factors shaped participants’ progression through the service access stage of the care pathways. Accessibility and availability of abortion methods are summarised in this theme with three subthemes: waiting time to obtain abortion care, availability of abortion methods, and abortion costs.

Waiting time to obtain abortion care

Some women described long waiting times to receive abortion care, while most participants mentioned that they experienced short waiting times to access abortion. For example, a participant described her experience of a relatively short waiting time. My husband was able to receive medications a short time after they were prescribed. The services were easily accessible (P#12).

In contrast, participants described prolonged waiting time due to high patient volume, indicating that abortion services were delivered within a busy clinical environment. There was a long waiting time. They told me that drugs would be prescribed for me, and I would come back after a day. Because there was a long wait for the patient’s order [due to high patient volume], they did not explain it to me as thoroughly as I would have liked… (P#7).

Availability of abortion methods

Many participants visited healthcare facilities to receive abortion services for the first time, predominantly through self-referral, and were able to access abortion procedures with ease. The participant summarised this: This is my first time…I came here because I am familiar with the facility. There were no supply issues; everything was readily available…(P#13).

On the other hand, some women reported that providers from another facility referred them due to a lack of medicine and an ultrasound. Women explained this: They ordered an ultrasound, but since they didn’t have it available, and it was nighttime, I went to a private facility (P#16). P#10 adds I went to another clinic, [Y facility]. They said we had finished the medicine. They directed me to another facility [X health centre]. The participant discussed the availability of methods in line with gestational ages: I have been checked at another place…The place where I was living never aborted a pregnancy older than three months. I have been told that [X Hospital] treats me well, and that is why I came here (P#2).

Abortion costs

Women who accessed abortion care at public facilities were not worried about the costs: paying 300–700 birr ($2.5–5.5) and, in some facilities, they received the service free of charge. Participants reported cost variation in accessing abortion. Even when the procedure itself is free, the overall economic burden could still be substantial. For financially dependent individuals, relatively small fees could contribute to stress and prolong the care-seeking process. This is explained: No cost… It may cost more than 10,000 birr if done at a private institution. When I was worried about what I was going to do… someone told me that the current institution [Hospital X] gives abortion carefree of charge (P#9). Another description also supported this: The cost is cheap… 700 birr is nothing. I do not think it would be a challenge, as I use it for myself (P#6).

However, there were also reports from women who could not afford the fees at public facilities for medication and laboratories, in addition to food and transportation costs. I didn’t have to pay anything; the providers supported me and even gave me money for a taxi (P#2). Though abortion services were often free of charge at public facilities, women who received abortion care at private facilities explained the cost challenge. In a public facility, it may not cost too much; you may need to pay for medication unavailable in the facility, but here in private, it costs too much…(P#14).

Theme 4: perceived competency of abortion providers

Interactions with abortion providers, counselling quality, communication, and privacy during the procedure shaped participants’ experiences during the clinical care stage. Respectful communication and confidentiality influenced participants’ comfort, trust, and engagement with abortion providers during the procedure. The perceived competency of abortion providers from women’s perspectives is described in three sub-themes: welcoming and unwelcoming environments, communication and counselling skills, and privacy and confidentiality.

Welcoming and unwelcoming environments

This subtheme presents the dual experiences within abortion care pathways, where competent care coexists with administrative inefficiencies, disrespectful care, and provider stigma. The findings highlighted how the quality of abortion care is shaped by interpersonal and structural factors on top of technical competence. Participants noted that many healthcare staff were very welcoming and competent in their profession. The staff were well-prepared and attentive in providing care, demonstrating great competence. They were ready to understand and assist their clients satisfactorily (P#5). Additionally, a participant noted that the staff were very welcoming…Everyone is cheerful, and I feel comfortable. They treat me respectfully and kindly (P#15).

In contrast, some women described challenges in the patient registry (identification card departments) and unwelcoming environments at health centres. Participants encountered the bureaucratic barriers within health centres, particularly in the early stages of abortion care access. On top of clinical competence inside the procedure room, addressing administrative inefficiencies and person-centred care, enhance abortion care pathways. Inefficiency in administrative processes and non-clinical systems within health centres, such as registration, accessing medical records, and triage, can significantly shape overall abortion services.

The lack of responsiveness undermined participants’ dignity, leaving them feeling unwelcome. They are good at hospitality, except in the card department [patient registry departments]. Patient registry departments are tiresome. They will make you angry. However, there is good care in this room and in the room upstairs. I got my folder on the fifth repeated visit to the card room.I hate the card room (P#3). The sense of unwelcome was reinforced in descriptions. When I think of a public facility, I think of a health centre, where disappointment is often present…some providers ignore you (P#7).

Although stigma-free care was reported in some cases, others reported feeling stigmatised by the providers because of having an abortion. A woman said, abortion providers should leave their phones and give care to clients… There was shouting. The providers said, “Crazy woman, shut up”. I know they disgraced me because of my behaviour…(P#6). A participant also spoke about the abortion provider’s stigmatising attitude: I consulted [X provider], who works in the first upstairs room. She was shocked and asked, “How could you decide that?” I told her, “This is my life…”(P#3).

Communication and counselling skills

This subtheme described inconsistencies in counselling quality, gaps in informed consent, limited client-provider communication and missed opportunities for contraceptive use during abortion. Women thought that the care was explained to them very clearly and was easily understandable. I was told that there could be complications during the process. The information seems sufficient. Because of the medication I used for the abortion, I was warned that bleeding could start within three days…She has ensured everything is properly clarified (P#15).

On the other hand, some described the key information as not being addressed during the counselling and that the counselling was insufficient. Summarised by P#5: They told me nothing about the possible pain during the procedure. I would have liked to know because there’s always a fear of the unknown. We often asked other women who had been through it because we assumed there could be pain, and we wanted to be prepared (P#2). Others agreed, saying they had told me it wasn’t too painful, so I shouldn’t worry. They told me that it is painful when you get worried. I don’t think it’s enough; it didn’t alleviate my anxiety…(P#7).

Similarly, confusion and difficulty in understanding what was said during communication with providers were also explained: They said it’s labour. The information was not enough. Because when you say “labour”, I don’t know what you mean by “labour”. It would have been better if they had explained, saying, “It’s very painful, it has cramps…” (P#14). This was supported: The description was difficult to understand; I thought it was because of too many people. They say, “Don’t worry, it’s easy; you will be in labour for three hours,” but I was in labour for six hours. When they said you would have cramps, I thought it would be like menstrual cramps (P#6).

Some women also reported a lack of clear communication during the procedures, as providers used medical terminology. A participant mentioned the following: When wiping with the machine [Manual Vacuum Aspiration], I heard them say “empty” in English. When I asked them if they were ‘finished, they said, “Who communicated with you?” They encourage you to ask questions. But their answer is only short: “Yes, No, Ok, Wait.” (P#12). Unclear and unhelpful communication left women feeling uncomfortable and anxious. There was poor communication between us, and they did not clarify what was happening. I felt extremely uncomfortable and tried to explain my situation to them…The issue is the lack of clarification…(P#11).

Women were counselled regarding future pregnancy and contraceptive options. This pregnancy was unplanned. If the previous providers were still here, they would likely be disappointed in me, as they had properly informed me about family planning and provided a follow-up schedule (P#13). On the other hand, women reported being coerced into accepting contraceptive methods after the procedure, with choices made by the provider without considering their willingness. Then, the provider told me that something was to be inserted under my arm [contraceptive implant]. I said my preference is injectable [Depo Provera] … She got angry with me, saying, “No, you cannot deny after you receive the service” [abortion]. She said, “No, I will provide you with the one to be inserted under the arm…” Then, she inserts it without my permission [P#10].

Participant reported a missed opportunity for contraceptive provision, shaped by limited person-centred care, and expressed regret and a sense of a preventable outcome. The request for physical proof of menstrual bleeding before providing contraception led to an unintended pregnancy. Participant described this as follows: I was not exposed to this [abortion] at all from the beginning. It is 3 days past the end of the period. I told the provider to give me contraceptives. She told me to wipe the menstrual blood and bring it. She said, “If I don’t see it, I won’t give you contraception.” There was no menstrual bleeding on that day…But if she had given me family planning, the pregnancy would not have occurred (P#6).

Privacy and confidentiality

This subtheme presents the importance of privacy and confidentiality in shaping abortion care pathways, making participants feel comfortable, trust, and openness during abortion care. Keeping structural privacy, including closed doors, curtains, and using isolated rooms and relational privacy by creating an atmosphere where clients can speak openly, is crucial to improve overall experiences and willingness to engage openly with abortion providers.

Many participants described feeling confident that their privacy and confidentiality were protected. P#7 said: At first, I was worried that someone else might see or hear me, but later, they put me in a safe room… isolated me, and the door was closed. I was talking freely… we were just the two of us. A curtain is applied. Participants trusted that providers would keep the health information confidential: I believe the confidentiality of the information I provided will be maintained. The reason is that they are professionals; they are bound to maintain confidentiality (P#16). However, some women had concerns about their privacy, dignity, and confidentiality: The door was open when I was served. Someone else could hear what she was talking about…She closed the door after I swallowed the medication…(P#6).

Theme 5: physical and emotional effects of abortion

Earlier stages of the abortion care pathway, including social support, counselling, interaction with the abortion provider and participants’ experience during the procedure, influenced women’s post-procedure physical and emotional outcomes. The experiences women had during the procedure, physical symptoms after the procedure, and a range of emotions women experienced during abortion care are described in this theme. These are described in three subthemes: experience during the procedure, physical symptoms after the procedure, and emotional responses: anxiety, sadness, shame, self-blame, relief, and gratefulness.

Experience during the procedure

Participants appreciated their positive experiences and interactions with providers. The participant summarised positive experiences with the person who performed the ultrasound and everyone who interacted with them; they were all supportive and caring, and everything was excellent (P#14). However, some women were disappointed with the care because providers were abusive during the procedure, saying: After I went to bed, they didn’t talk much because I thought they were overloaded with many patients. He [the provider] told me to keep silent when I shouted at him when he did a pelvic exam… he was angry at me (P#7). Another woman also said I often had to remind them about my medication… They tend to forget and don’t always ask you proactively. There’s some fatigue on their part and a gap in “treating” patients…. (P#5).

Physical symptoms after the procedure

Participants reported various physical symptoms, including backache, headache, bleeding, passing clots, cramping, fatigue, and pain. P#6 said that when I returned from the clinic, I felt pain in my right leg and a headache…After I lay prone, I started gasping. Then, I failed [fainted]. Another woman added, ‘I thought I was born and lost.’ I was shocked when I saw something sticky dripping. After that, I was bleeding…(P#9). Descriptions of physical symptoms were provided with accompanying anxiety. I was bleeding, passing clots, and had abdominal pain for a few days after the abortion; I was worried that I was going to die (P#1).

Emotional responses: anxiety, sadness, shame, self-blame, relief and gratefulness

Women felt shameful, anxious, upset, lost trust and worried that their faith in God had changed after abortion. I was very sad when I saw it, especially after it was expelled…I felt a lot of shame and self-blame. My faith in God has changed… I sinned (P#9). P#7 added that I was deeply hurt. I thought I cried blood, not tears…I took my mind to a bad situation. I don’t talk to anyone. Some women also thought they had made mistakes and felt guilty: ‘I feel guilty…I feel Satan has misled me…First, I had to abstain. I had to care for myself while working at someone’s home. I feel like it’s my fault (P#8).

Despite anxiety and feelings of guilt, some women felt relieved after the abortion and satisfied with the care they received. Summarised by P#4: [Because of my pregnancy] I might have committed suicide. I thought I was going to die. Now, thanks to God…I am relieved (P#8). Another woman supported this: Before, I was depressed and anxious; now, after the abortion, I am relieved, and I feel happy (P#15).

Discussion

This study explored the abortion care pathways of women in Addis Ababa healthcare facilities and identified multiple social and structural factors shaping care pathways. Social and emotional support, abortion stigma, moral perceptions of abortion, service accessibility, and provider interactions collectively shape participants’ ability to access person-centred and respectful abortion care.

The findings of this study highlighted the pervasive influence of stigma on participants’ abortion care experiences. Women who attended healthcare facilities, especially when alone, reported feeling stigmatised because they decided to have an abortion. This may be due to a lack of emotional and informational support, which made navigating the healthcare process more difficult, with potentially increased vulnerability to negative experiences. The absence of social support during abortion may heighten women’s perceptions of judgment or discrimination from abortion providers or the broader healthcare facility. This finding is consistent with other studies [50–52], which have described social stigma [53, 54], leading women to feel discouraged from disclosing and seeking support from relatives [55]. Fostering non-judgmental and supportive healthcare environments for all women is necessary, with intentional support recommended for those who are seeking abortion care alone.

Family and friends were involved in abortion decision-making, while some women made decisions alone, which was stressful. In line with this finding, complex and stressful abortion decision-making was also reported in previous studies [56, 57]. Family and friends’ involvement in pregnancy termination decision-making that accommodates the autonomy of women’s decision-making, and their families’ engagement, may help to improve the quality of abortion care and outcomes [58, 59]. Evidence indicates that women benefit from sharing their emotional experiences and from having support during abortion [52, 60]. Some studies report that the level of support that a woman experiences may influence her ability to cope with social stigma and abortion-related burdens [61–63] and may help them feel relieved after an abortion [64, 65], as evidenced in this study. This finding suggests that support from close social networks provided reassurance to participants, highlighting the importance of social support in improving experiences across care pathways.

The findings reveal that abortion is often interpreted within moral and social frameworks. Some participants described and perceived abortion as a sin and as the murder of an unborn child. These perceptions reflect broader sociocultural, abortion legal context, and religious norms that shape attitudes toward abortion in many settings [53, 66–69], which may affect the accessibility of quality services. Abortion-related moral conflict is influenced by social pressure, cultural expectations, and legal frameworks that frame abortion as morally contentious [26, 70, 71]. Such moral and social interpretations can influence decisions about where and how to seek care. The studies revealed that moral and social interpretations of abortion could result in abortion secrecy, delay in seeking care, or discourage seeking care from a formal healthcare facility, which may lead to unsafe abortion [54, 72–74]. Policymakers and healthcare systems leadership should strengthen values clarification programs at the community level. In addition, community engagement initiatives such as community forums, mass media and social communication campaigns involving community leaders should be used to mitigate abortion stigma and support person-centred abortion care.

The findings highlight that stigma and fear of social judgment influenced care-seeking behaviour, including participants’ decisions to avoid nearby healthcare facilities. This is due to fear of being seen at the clinic and judged, which may pressure women to travel elsewhere to access abortion services, as evidenced in this study. In contrast to our finding, some studies found that travel to obtain abortion care may compel women to disclose their abortion to others, endangering their privacy and confidentiality [62, 75]. This might be due to differences in participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, the country’s abortion policy, and reasons for travel.

Moreover, evidence also indicated that though women could travel due to various reasons, such as women’s preference to travel, service unavailability, and policy restrictions, women who travel for abortion care suffer from emotional burdens combined with financial costs [62, 75–77]. These findings suggest that stigma-driven mobility may represent an important barrier to timely and equitable abortion care. Community-level awareness efforts that normalise safe abortion services, promote supportive environments for reproductive health services, and reduce stigma, thereby decreasing the need for secrecy-driven travel, are recommended. Training abortion providers on non-judgmental and stigma-sensitive care is necessary, so women feel safe accessing services closer to home.

Structural and health system barriers also shaped abortion care pathways. Participants in our study noted long waiting times, a lack of medicine and ultrasound at some facilities, and limited availability of second-trimester abortions. Although abortion services are free at public facilities, women who sought care at private facilities reported cost barriers. In line with this, evidence from other studies also indicates that delays and long waiting times to obtain care [78–81], availability, accessibility and affordability of abortion care hinder the quality of services [16, 17, 82, 83]. Such barriers show the importance of strengthening the healthcare system to ensure equitable access to safe abortion care, consistent with WHO recommendations for person-centred abortion care [2].

Our findings highlight the importance of person-centred care during abortion. Participants reported inadequate counselling, limited explanations during procedures, and the use of medical terminology that was difficult to understand. These findings are consistent with other studies worldwide [14, 34, 54, 84–87] that conclude lack of respectful care undermines informed decision-making and women’s confidence in the care they receive. Failing to explain the procedures clearly to the women may cause confusion, anxiety and reduced trust in the healthcare facility and affect care outcomes [88]. Effective communication and counselling during abortion are essential to ensure person-centred care, as they enable women to understand the procedure, anticipate potential symptoms, and actively participate in decisions about their care [9, 28, 54]. These findings underscore the need for strengthened abortion provider training in effective counselling and communication skills to provide person-centred abortion care.

Coercive post-abortion contraceptive practices, where providers selected contraceptive methods without fully considering women’s preferences, were reported by study participants. Such practices raise concerns regarding women’s reproductive autonomy and informed consent. Contraception should be offered voluntarily, with women receiving balanced information about available methods and being supported to choose based on their own preferences [2]. This finding is consistent with other studies [9, 89]. Quality abortion care should be responsive to women’s needs, containing the preferences, culture and values of women seeking services to prevent discouraging women from seeking timely reproductive healthcare in the future [2, 9]. Improving the quality of post-abortion contraceptive counselling is critical for strengthening engagement with reproductive health services.

Privacy and confidentiality were also important aspects within the abortion care pathways. Our study highlighted that participants were confident that their privacy and confidentiality were protected, trusting that providers would keep their health information confidential. This contributed to feelings of safety and openness during the consultation and abortion procedure. These findings are consistent with other studies [9, 66, 85, 90], which reported that guaranteeing privacy and confidentiality during care increased clients’ trust in providers, kept them safe, and improved the perceived quality of care.

A range of physical and emotional outcomes characterised abortion care pathways. Our study found that women faced various physical symptoms, a finding corroborated in other studies [20, 64, 81, 91], which have reported various forms of pain and discomfort during the procedure. Similarly, women felt shameful, anxious, upset, lost trust and worried that their faith in God had changed after abortion [9, 68, 69, 80, 83, 86, 92]. In contrast to this, some women felt relieved after the abortion, satisfied with the care they received and appreciated the providers’ competence. These findings highlight that abortion care experiences and outcomes are not uniformly negative but reflect a complex combination of emotional responses. This is consistent with the other studies [80, 93, 94]. As abortion is an emotional and morally charged procedure framed in social, spiritual, and legal contexts, the perception of pregnancy termination is diverse, resulting in the access to safe abortion varying across the world [71, 76, 77]. Abortion services that are sensitive to women’s emotional and social contexts are crucial to improve abortion care experiences and outcomes [78]. The current lack of reliable, consumer-co-developed standardised measurements, such as Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) and Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs), to regularly assess abortion care experiences and outcomes should be addressed. PREMs and PROMs for abortion care would improve the quality of abortion services and enhance care outcomes [95–97].

Strengths and limitations

As a descriptive qualitative study, the findings provide in-depth insights into abortion care pathways in the study setting. Since study participants were included only from healthcare facilities, the study did not capture the experiences of women who sought abortion services through informal pathways. Because of the challenge of recruitment, this study did not include women who obtained medical abortion services from pharmacies; findings are limited to the health facility context. Whilst maximum variant sampling techniques were used to enrol participants with different backgrounds, including both those who came from urban and rural settings, this study may not have recruited women struggling to obtain abortion care in remote areas, as it was conducted in the capital city of Ethiopia. Comprehensive demographic data enable the reader to consider the transferability of findings to individual settings. In addition, given the sensitive nature of safe abortion, social desirability bias may have influenced how women responded during interviews, even though privacy and confidentiality were maintained. Despite several validation steps previously described, translating interview data from Amharic to English may have introduced conceptual discrepancies, potentially affecting the interpretation of the study’s findings.

Conclusions

Though positive experiences and supportive care were noted, this study identified abortion stigma and a range of challenges experienced by women accessing abortion care in facilities. Addressing these problems requires inter-sectoral collaboration to create strategies to improve community-based awareness to decrease abortion stigma and promote and provide safe, quality abortion care. The findings provide recommendations to improve the safety and quality of abortion care, with implications for research, policy, clinical practice, and professional education.

Recommendations and policy implications

This study has important implications for policymakers, healthcare managers, providers, community stakeholders, and researchers.

For policymakers and health system leaders

Strengthening the implementation of Ethiopia’s abortion policy is essential to ensure access to safe abortion care. Ensuring the availability of necessary stocks for second-trimester abortion across healthcare facilities and implementing monitoring mechanisms to support voluntary and rights-based post-abortion contraception to prevent coercive practices.

For facility managers and abortion providers

Person-centred and stigma-free abortion care initiatives should be applied at the healthcare facility level to ensure privacy and confidentiality during the procedure, improve appointment systems to avoid long waiting times to receive abortion care, and create non-judgemental, welcoming environments for women seeking services.

For community stakeholders and civil society organisations

Community-based awareness is necessary to reduce abortion-related stigma, including beliefs framing abortion as ‘sin’. Engaging religious leaders and women’s groups in culturally sensitive dialogue may help normalise access to safe abortion services.

For researchers

Gaps identified in this study provide an important signal to areas where further evidence would support the continued goal of access to safe, quality abortion. A key area for further research is the development of abortion specific, reliable measurements, such as PREMs and PROMs, to assess experiences and outcomes of abortion care routinely. Ongoing research should also include women accessing abortion care outside formal healthcare facilities to better understand informal care pathways.
