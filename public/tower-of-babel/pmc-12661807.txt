Congenital descending aorta to pulmonary vein fistula: an unusual cause of left ventricular dilation in a 21-year-old female
Authors: Xiang Pan, Chan Yu, Zhouhui Chen, Bowen Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital descending aorta to pulmonary vein fistula (cDAPVF) is a rare congenital vascular malformation diagnosed in infancy and childhood. Adult cDAPVF is often asymptomatic and usually detected incidentally by imaging. At present, cases of adults with an enlarged left heart due to cDAPVF have rarely been reported.Case presentationWe reported a case of 21-year-old female who presented with an unexplained continuousmurmur and left ventricular enlargement followed up for 6 years. The patient developed chest tightness five days ago. The patient was diagnosed with cDAPVF, as revealed by echocardiography combined with computed tomography angiography (CTA). The patient underwent arteriovenous embolization, with satisfactory improvement in symptoms and left ventricular size during follow-up examinations. ConclusionscDAPVF should be considered in patients with unexplainable left ventricular continuous murmur. CTA serves as a valuable adjunct for diagnosing abnormal vascular connections that cannot be identified by Doppler echocardiography. Transcatheter embolization may be an effective treatment for cDAPVF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03691-5.Keywords: Congenital descending aorta to pulmonary vein fistula, Diagnosis, Treatment echocardiography, Computed tomography angiography

Introduction

Congenital descending aorta to pulmonary vein fistula (cDAPVF) is a rare congenital extracardiac vascular malformation. Fewer than 30 cases of cDAPVF have been reported in a review of existing English-language literature, and only 14 cases [1–5] have been reported in the past 30 years. cDAPVF, with unclear embryonic origin, may be related to an abnormal degeneration of the posterior branch of the embryonic aorta supplying the pulmonary buds. The exact incidence of cDAPVF remains unknown, and patients may present with nonspecific symptoms. In the case of neonatal cDAPVF, cardiac failure is identified as the primary symptom [6], whereas in adulthood, sufferers may experience asymptomatic condition or present with hemoptysis. It poses a great challenge for accurate diagnosis, which depends mainly on multimodal imaging. This case report describes a 21-year-old adult female who was found to have a left ventricular enlargement on physical examination and was ultimately diagnosed with cDAPVF. This study was conducted to underscore the importance of considering cDAPVF in the differential diagnosis of unexplained left ventricular enlargement, to emphasize the critical role of computed tomography angiography (CTA) in the diagnosis of cDAPVF, and to demonstrate the efficacy of endovascular embolization in managing large arteriovenous fistulas.

Case presentation

A 21-year-old female was admitted to our hospital with acute chest tightness, showing cardiac murmur and left ventricular dilatation during physical examination six years ago (age 15). Subsequent echocardiographic examinations at five hospitals consistently confirmed left ventricular enlargement, yet without the recommendation of further intervention. The patient remained asymptomatic until five days before admission (Table 1).

The patient denied any history of hypertension, renal illness, cancer, trauma or allergies and had no relevant family history. No abnormalities were observed in laboratory results or initial vital signs. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm. Physical examination revealed a continuous murmur, categorized as grade 4/6, on the left sternum.

Echocardiography showed left ventricular dilatation, with a left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDd) of 61.3 mm1, accompanied by the identification of an abnormal vessel close to the left atrium, somewhere between the descending aorta and the left pulmonary vein (Fig. 1A and B). Meanwhile, a positive blood flow spectrum from the descending aorta was observed during both systole and diastole (Fig. 1C), which was displayed in more detail by an additional movie file (Additional file 1). In addition, there was no abnormality in the left ventricular function, with an ejection fraction of 67%.

CTA revealed an abnormal branch vessel on the left side of the descending aorta. This vessel was abnormally connected to a branch of the left inferior pulmonary vein with significant thickening (Fig. 2A and B). A markedly tortuous and dilated vascular mass was observed in the left lower lung. Besides, radiological assessment revealed a descending aorta to the left inferior pulmonary venous fistula, with a suspected intralobar pulmonary sequestration.

Furthermore, patient underwent pulmonary artery CTA to rule out the possibility of pulmonary sequestration. Consequently, branches of the pulmonary artery normally supplied the relevant lung area, without signs of embolization or stenosis (Fig. 2C). Bronchoscopy confirmed a proper connection between the left lower lung segment and the bronchial tree (Fig. 3).

Following the confirmation of cDAPVF through multi-disciplinary consultation, the patient underwent a digital subtraction angiography (DSA)-guided embolization on the seventh day after admission. Intraoperative aortic arch imaging revealed a thick, tortuous branch of the descending aorta, showing an abnormal structure that extended into the left lower lung, and was connected to the pulmonary vein (Fig. 4A). The fistula was approximately 11.5 mm, and a 16-mm Amplatzer II vascular plug was released within the fistula. Post-embolization angiography showed minimal residual shunting in the proximal segment (Fig. 4B).

On the same day after operation, the patient was examined with blocker-like echoes at the abnormal branch vessel via echocardiogram, without blood flow (Fig. 5A). The LVEDd was 53 mm (Fig. 5B), representing a notable reduction from preoperative values; and the systemic ventricular ejection fraction was 60%.

(A) Postoperative echocardiography demonstrating the vascular plug with hyperechogenity (arrow). (B) Postoperative echocardiography denoting a reduction in the size of the left ventricle (LVEDd: 53 mm)

Three months after treatment, the patient underwent echocardiography with further reduction in the LVEDd to 52 mm.

One year after the operation, follow-up CTA showed a fixed location of the vascular plug without fistula recurrence or vasodilatation (Fig. 6A and B). The patient remained in good health without any complications.

Discussion

Left ventricular dilatation and heart murmurs may suggest valve disease, coronary artery disease, or congenital heart malformations (patent ductus arteriosus, aortopulmonary window etc.), which are usually well depicted on echocardiography. However, these symptoms can sometimes indicate underlying extracardiac abnormalities, and require further investigation.

cDAPVF is a rare congenital disorder associated with abnormal extracardiac vascular connections. Clinical manifestations may depend on the fistula location, dimensions, and involved vessels. In general, individuals may exhibit no evident symptoms in the early stages. However, larger fistulas can result in serious clinical symptoms, such as hemoptysis or congestive heart failure, suggesting the necessity for early diagnosis and treatment.

Hemodynamic changes

The clinical signs of cDAPVF exhibit intimate association with hemodynamic changes (Fig. 7). One possible outcome is an incorrect connection, resulting in a left-to-left shunt, which may increase the preload on the left ventricle and induce heart failure eventually [8]. It may, to some extent, explain the presence of enlarged left heart and cardiac murmur in our case. Additionally, retrograde perfusion through the fistula may trigger elevated pulmonary venous pressure, leading to pulmonary venous dilation or rupture [9]. It may be an unusual cause of hemoptysis in adults. There were only four case reports of cDAPVF with recurrent episodes of hemoptysis in adult so far [1, 2, 8, 9]. The majority of cases are asymptomatic and incidentally discovered during unrelated evaluations, posing significant challenge to the early diagnosis of this condition.

Diagnosis

Imaging remains the major approach for diagnosis considering nonspecific symptoms of patients with cDAPVF.

Doppler echocardiography, a noninvasive, non-radioactive modality, is generally used as first-line cardiac imaging modality and providing valuable information for evaluating aortopulmonary venous fistulas [10]. Echocardiogram may show abnormal dilatation of the veins, continuous arterial flow, and diastolic runoff Doppler patterns, which are markers of abnormal systemic arterial supply [11]. However, in this case, the diagnosis of cDAPVF was missed during previous echocardiography over six-years. It may be attributed to limited acoustic windows, operator dependence, and its lower sensitivity (78.7%) for extracardiac anomalies [12]. Consequently, possible extracardiac manifestations should be discussed clinically in cases where an echocardiogram is unable to establish the origin of heart chamber dilation, particularly when there are noticeable murmurs.

CTA is highly sensitive in identifying extracardiac abnormalities associated with congenital heart disease, with a detection rate of 93.3% [12]. Unlike echocardiography, CTA, despite not available to indicate hemodynamic patterns or abnormalities, can enable a more comprehensive visualization of the lung parenchyma and airways, as well as its relation to the adjacent vascular structure [13]. In this case, initial echocardiography, even though inconclusive, revealed abnormal extracardiac flow. Subsequent CTA and 3D reconstructive imaging definitively identified a fistula between the descending aorta and left inferior pulmonary vein, with the exclusion of pulmonary abnormalities simultaneously. It is pivotal for confirming the diagnosis of cDAPVF and can provide valuable guidance for subsequent surgery.

Moreover, bronchoscopy also offer a solution for diagnosing tracheobronchial connections and endobronchial inflammation. In this case, it served as an adjunct to CTA, supplying diagnostic assessment of the tracheobronchial anatomy and mucosal inflammation.

Differential diagnosis

cDAPVF requires differentiation from other vascular anomalies (Supplemental Table 1; Additional File 2). Specifically, pulmonary sequestration involves non-functional lung tissue supplied solely by systemic arteries, contrasting with the typical dual systemic/pulmonary supply of cDAPVF [5, 14]. Anomalous systemic arterial supply to a normal basal segment mimics the aortic origin of cDAPVF, but lacks direct venous fistulization, with the preservation of normal pulmonary venous drainage [8]. Different from pulmonary arteriovenous malformation involving a pulmonary artery-to-pulmonary vein shunt without capillary intervention, cDAPVF features a direct systemic arterial shunt (from the aorta) to the pulmonary vein [5]. Scimitar syndrome can be excluded based on its diagnostic triad of right lung hypoplasia, anomalous pulmonary venous drainage (typically to the inferior vena cava), and systemic arterialization of the right lower lobe, none of which are characteristics of cDAPVF [15]. Similarly, different from left-to-right shunts observed in coronary-pulmonary artery fistulas, aortopulmonary window and patent ductus arteriosus, cDAPVF produces a left-to-left shunt through a direct connection of the aortic branch to the pulmonary vein. In addition, the possibility of cDAPVF can also be excluded by considering the elevated partial pressure of oxygen in the pulmonary artery, a characteristic of left-to-right shunts.

Treatment and prognosis

cDAPVF is a rare condition without widely accepted treatment so far. Limited evidence suggests that positive prognoses in patients undergoing surgical ligation or interventional embolization [16]. Embolization, with minimal invasiveness, immediate therapeutic outcome, and low rate of complications, is a preferred therapeutic option for arteriovenous fistulas since the 1980s. However, surgical treatment remains a solution for patients with contraindication for or failure of embolization [17]. In terms of the modalities of embolization currently, patients may usually undergo coil embolization for small fistulas, and Amplatzer embolization for medium to medium-to-large fistulas, with the latter achieving a 100% technical success in vascular occlusion [4, 18].

Through literature review, the primary therapeutic choice for cDAPVF remains surgical ligation, with embolization employed in only a small number of cases, including three cases of coil embolization [1, 19, 20], one case of Amplatzer-coil surgery [21], and one case of Amplatzer embolization [22], all of which achieved good therapeutic outcomes. In our case, an 11.5-mm fistula was successfully occluded using the new Amplatzer II plug. To our knowledge, it was the first case in which embolization via Amplatzer II was applied to treat a large cDAPVF in an adult. This device offers advantages such as higher rate of rapid closure and lower risk of complications, with well-established efficacy in treating large peripheral arteriovenous fistulas [23–25]. In addition, our case was followed up one month postoperatively, showing complete closure of the arteriovenous fistula without complications, validating the clinical value of this device. Anyway, further follow-up and data collection are required to assess the long-term effectiveness of this method, even with encouraging results yielded in this case report.

Conclusion

cDAPVF is a rare cause of left ventricular dilatation in adults. cDAPVF should be considered in adults showing left ventricular dilatation with continuous murmur but no obvious cardiac cause. Given the challenges associated with the diagnosis of cDAPVF, CTA is recommended to be applied early for diagnosis after the initial echocardiographic evaluation. Amplatzer II vascular plug embolization can be accepted as a viable therapeutic option for cases with large arteriovenous fistulas. Ongoing follow-up of such cases in the future will offer valuable information to confirm the long-term effects of cDAPVF treatment, which in turn will optimize treatment strategies and improve patient prognosis.
