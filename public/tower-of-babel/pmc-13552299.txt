Proteomic profiling of cephalic vein reveals potential biomarkers for arteriovenous fistula neointimal hyperplasia in ESRD patients
Authors: Liming Liang, Liangzhen Gu, Lina Shi, Qun Luo, Xianglei Kong, Fuyi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArteriovenous fistula (AVF) is the preferred vascular access for patients with end-stage renal disease; however, its failure is primarily due to neointimal hyperplasia. Five patients who underwent initial AVF surgery served as the control group, and another five patients with failed AVF surgery served as the experimental group. Herein, we employed mass spectrometry (MS)-based quantitative proteomics coupled with tandem mass tag labeling to screen differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the anastomotic cephalic vein, followed by bioinformatics analyses and verification experiments. A total of 121 DEPs were identified in the failed AVF group. GO analysis was primarily enriched in protein binding, nucleic acid binding, enzyme binding, mRNA binding, cadherin binding, catalytic activity, and cell adhesion molecule binding. KEGG pathways were mainly enriched in cell aggregation and adhesion, actin cytoskeleton, extracellular matrix-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, complement and coagulation cascades, and cholesterol metabolism. Protein-protein interaction network consisted of 86 (71.07%) DEPs, including complement VII (C7), factor IX (F9), SERPINC1, microfibril-associated glycoprotein 4 (MFAP4), complement C1s subcomponent, complement C1q subcomponent subunit A, complement C1q subcomponent subunit B, tissue factor, and von Willebrand factor, which interacting with numerous other proteins. In the expanded validation for different patients, C7, F9, SERPINC1, and MFAP4, were verified by immunohistochemical staining and Western blotting, which were consistent with the proteomics results. Collectively, this study identifies a series of potential diagnostic biomarkers, and explores the underlying mechanisms associated with AVF dysfunction.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant threat to global public health. According to data from 2017, approximately 698 million individuals suffer from CKD worldwide, accounting for 9.1% of the global population [1]. Recent data from JAMA Internal Medicine indicate that the prevalence of adult CKD in China is 8.2% [2]. CKD often progresses insidiously, with many patients presenting with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), necessitating renal replacement therapy to sustain life [3]. Hemodialysis remains the primary treatment modality for CKD. The age‑standardized prevalence of dialysis patients in China rose from 311.29 to 419.12 per million between 2015 and 2016, reflecting a continued increase in uremic patients [4]. Consequently, establishing and maintaining patent dialysis access for a growing number of patients with renal failure is a critical issue. Arteriovenous fistula (AVF) is considered the gold standard for vascular access in hemodialysis, often referred to as the ‘lifeline’ of hemodialysis patients. Recent data indicate that the primary patency rate of AVF at one year is 64%, and the secondary patency rate is 79%, while the failure rate within one year after surgery is as high as 36% [3]. Preliminary data from our center showed that the failure rates of AVF at one and two years after surgery were 14.4% and 21.3%, respectively, with a failure rate of 26.8% at five years [5]. The primary cause of AVF failure is venous outflow stenosis [6]. The stenosis rate of the reflux vein in failed AVFs ranges from 65% to 100%, with more than half occurring behind the anastomosis and often involving the anastomosis itself, where neointimal hyperplasia (NIH) is the main pathological basis [7,8]. Continuous thickening of the vascular intima leads to venous outflow stenosis, insufficient blood flow, and even vascular occlusion. Therefore, effective inhibition of AVF failure is crucial for improving the quality of hemodialysis and represents a significant challenge that urgently needs to be addressed.

Currently, there are no effective treatments for preventing AVF failure, which is closely related to an incomplete understanding of its pathogenesis. Studies conducted at the transcriptional level have inherent limitations, as they can only partially reflect changes in gene expression products. The process from genetic information to functional proteins involves multiple steps, including transcription, post-transcriptional processing, translation, and post-translational modifications. Whether gene expression can be effectively linked to the onset of diseases depends on whether any issues arise in these steps, any of which may lead to divergent outcomes and introduce considerable unpredictability. Therefore, investigating protein expression in humans represents the most direct and authentic approach to elucidating various biological phenomena. Proteins play a pivotal role in cellular functions and offer direct insights into the pathophysiology of diseases [9,10]. Previous studies have demonstrated that proteomics can identify important protein markers for predicting the risk of adverse clinical outcomes in many diseases [11,12].

The cephalic vein of the anastomosis site is an ideal biological material that directly reflects the pathological changes in the lesion site of AVF failure, and can directly reflect changes in the protein spectrum. Tandem Mass Tag (TMT) labeling-based quantitative proteomics is a technique developed by Thermo Scientific for the in vitro labeling of peptides, which employs isotopic tags – including 2-plex, 6-plex, 10-plex, 11-plex, 16-plex, and up to 18-plex variants – to specifically label amino acid groups on peptides. Following analysis by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), it enables the simultaneous comparison of protein abundance across 2 to 18 different sample groups. In vitro labeling techniques coupled with LC-MS/MS have been extensively developed for protein identification and relative quantification [13–15], and TMT has been established as a highly sensitive quantitative proteomics method for high-throughput protein identification and quantification, proving particularly suitable for studies involving precisely matched samples of limited quantity. In addition to analyzing the concentrations of thousands of proteins in tissues, the greatest advantage of isotope labeling technology in mass spectrometry is its ability to simultaneously measure peptides in multiple samples, which greatly limits the impact of variable background signals on detection and quantification interference [16,17].

To date, no studies have utilized TMT-based proteomics to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying AVF failure. This study analyzed proteomic alterations in the cephalic veins at the anastomotic sites of patients with AVF failure compared to the control group, aiming to identify potential protein signatures in the vascular tissues of AVF failure patients and further explore the underlying mechanisms associated with AVF dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The study design and implementation were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (No. 2020-S075). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample and data collection

From August 2021 to December 2022, 34 ESRD patients hospitalized at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University were enrolled in this study. Based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria, seventeen patients who underwent initial AVF surgery served as the control group, and another seventeen patients with failed AVF surgery served as the experimental group. Among them, 5 controls and 5 AVF failure patients were allocated for proteomics analysis [18,19], with the remaining twenty-four utilized for expanded validation. The inclusion criteria for the control group: 1) eGFR ≤ 15 mL/min/1.73 m2; 2) radial artery and cephalic vein diameter > 2 mm; 3) no significant NIH. The inclusion criteria for the AVF failure group: 1) AVF establishment time exceeding three months, continuous pump blood flow < 200 mL/min during dialysis, ultrasound examination showing anastomotic outflow tract stenosis > 50%, inability to complete hemodialysis treatment three times weekly; 2) planned standard forearm radial artery cephalic vein fistula; 3) confirmed by Doppler ultrasound (DU) as being associated with chronic NIH-related stenosis. Exclusion criteria were: 1) age < 18 years; 2) concurrent malignant tumors or active systemic immune diseases; 3) presence of acute/chronic infectious diseases; 4) severe malnutrition; 5) heart failure; 6) acute thrombotic events; 7) use of anticoagulant drugs, ESA or iron therapy; and 8) patients not agreeing to participate in the study. All the cases were evaluated under DU before intervention by an independent ultrasound specialist [20]. During surgery, a 0.5 cm cephalic vein near the fistula was excised. Clinical data collection included age, sex, primary diseases, blood pressure, history of cardiovascular disease, use of medications, the time from AVF creation to failure, DU assessment data, routine blood examination, renal function tests, and routine coagulation examination. All biochemical indicators were tested at the central laboratory of hospital.

Proteomics Analysis [21]

Protein extraction and digestion

Vascular tissues from 5 controls and 5 AVF failure patients were individually minced and 50 mg of the homogenate tissue was lysed using 8 M urea-containing TEAB lysis buffer for protein extaction. The total protein concentration of each protein extract was determined using a BCA protein assay kit, with the total protein content ranging between 65 and 82 μg/μL. Aliquot (100 μg) of each tissue was transferred to a new tube and adjusted to 100 μL. The samples were reduced using TCEP at 55 °C for 1 h and alkylated with iodoacetamide at room temperature for 30 min. The samples were precipitated with six volumes of acetone at −20 °C for 6 h. After centrifugation, the precipitate was dried for 3 min. The protein pellet was resuspended in TEAB buffer and trypsin digestion was performed overnight at 37 °C. The digested protein sample was centrifuged at 10000 g in a 10 KDa ultrafiltration tube for 40 min, followed by washing twice with 20 μL 50 mM TEAB buffer and 30% acetonitrile. The obtained tryptic digest was dried by vacuum centrifuge, and then redissolved and dilute to 100 μg/mL with 50 mM HEPES (pH 8.5) for subsequent TMT labeling.

Tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling

Aliquot (41 μL) of 100% acetonitrile was added to 0.8 mg of a TMT channel reagent (TMT 10 plex™ Isobaric Label Reagent Set), to each tryptic peptide sample described above, the mixture was shaked for reaction at 25 °C for 1 h and then quenched by adding 8 μL of 5% hydroxylamine. The labeled peptides derived from control and AVF failure group were then equivalently mixed, desalted and dried by vacuum dryer.

The TMT labeled peptide mixture was re-dissolved in 100 μL 2% (vol/vol) ACN/H2O containing 4.5 mM ammonium formate (pH 10) for basic reverse-phase chromatography pre-fractionation by HPLC (Agilent Technologies 1260 infinity, US) with an Agilent ZORBAX 300 Extend-C18 column. The gradient started with 0% phase B (90% (vol/vol) acetonitrile containing 4.5 mM ammonium formate, pH = 10), until 7 min and continuously increased to 16% B at 13 min, 40% B at 73 min, 44% B at 77 min, and 60% B at 82 min and kept until 96 min, then increased to 90% B at 100 min. The flow rate was 1 mL/min. The fractions were collected chronologically into twelve tubes from 3 to 96 min on the basis of crossfading fractionation [22]. The pooled fractions were spun to dried and re-dissolved in H2O containing 0.1% formic acid (FA) to a 500 μg/μL concentration of peptides for mass spectrometry analysis.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometer/mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Aliquot (3 µL) of each pooled fraction described above was analyzed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer coupled with an EASY-nLC 1200 nanoUPLC system equipped with an Acclaim™ PepMap™ 100 pre-column (20 mm × 75 µm, 3 µm) and an Acclaim™ PepMap™ RSLC C18 analytical column (150 mm × 75 µm, 2 µm). The mobile phases were (A) water containing 0.1% formic acid (FA) and (B) acetonitrile containing 0.1% FA. A reversed-phase column (Acclaim™ PepMap™ RSLC analytical column 150 mm × 75 µm, 2 µm) was used to separate peptide mixture with a flow rate of 300 nL/min and a gradient of 5% B-70% within 90 min. ESI voltage was 2300 V, and the collision mode was HCD with 35% energy. The mass resolution for precursors and fragments was 120,000 and 30,000, respectively, at m/z 200. The scan range for precursors was 350–2000 m/z, for fragment ions 110–1700 m/z.

Protein identification

The protein database search was performed using Proteome Discoverer 2.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with the search engine Sequest HT for peptide spectrum matching (PSM). Oxidation of methionine and phosphorylation of serine, threonine and tyrosine were selected as dynamic modifications. The carbamidomethylation at cysteine, TMT labeling at N-terminus of any n-peptide were selected as static modifications. The quantitative results were normalized based on the total peptide amount in each sample. Only proteins identified with false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.01, p-value ≤ 0.05 and abundance ratio (AVF failure group vs. control) ≤ 0.67 or ≥ 1.50 were included for bioinformatics analysis. To ensure the absolute reliability of intergroup comparisons and avoid biases introduced by the imputation of missing values, stringent filtering criteria were applied. Only proteins successfully quantified across all patients were retained for subsequent differential analysis. Although this may result in the loss of information pertaining to some low-abundance proteins, it guarantees the integrity and high comparability of all differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) data that underpin the core conclusions, rendering the results highly conservative and credible. DEPs were identified by Bayesian testing of all the quantified proteins using the Limma package.

Bioinformatics analysis

The proteins identified with AR > 1.5 or < 0.67 with FDR ≤ 0.01, p-value ≤ 0.05 were input to the data pool of STRING (https://string-db.org/) and the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) program (QIAGEN Digital Insights) for bioinformatics analysis. The bioinformatic results included GO functional enrichment, KEGG pathway enrichment, and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction of the DEPs. The R language and ggplot 2 were used to depict the bioinformatics analysis results.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized with a xylene-alcohol gradient, repaired with Tris-EDTA antigen repair solution at high pressure for 5 min, and blocked with 3% H2O2 for 30 min. After washing with PBS, the sections were blocked with normal serum for 30 min. The corresponding primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following antibodies were used: complement VII (C7) rabbit polyclonal antibody (1: 50; Proteintech), factor IX (F9) rabbit polyclonal antibody (1: 200; Proteintech), serine protease C1/SERPINC1 mouse monoclonal antibody (1: 100; Proteintech), and microfibril-associated glycoprotein 4 (MFAP4) rabbit polyclonal antibody (1: 100; Proteintech). The following day, the sections were warmed to 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, the reaction enhancer was added and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG polymer was then added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. DAB staining was performed, followed by hematoxylin staining for 2 min. The sections were dehydrated and sealed with neutral gum. Microscopic examination was performed the following day.

Western blotting

Fresh vascular tissues were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF, followed by shaking lysis at 4 °C for 2 h. Protein loading buffer was added and the samples were denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Polyacrylamide gels (10%) were prepared using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes via electroblotting. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h and incubated with the corresponding primary antibody at 4 °C for 12–16 h. The following antibodies were used: C7 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1: 500; Proteintech), F9 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1: 1000; Proteintech), serine protease C1/SERPINC1 mouse monoclonal antibody (1: 2000; Proteintech), MFAP4 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1: 1000; Proteintech), and β-actin mouse monoclonal antibody (1: 40,000; Proteintech). The next day, HRP-labeled secondary antibody were added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h, followed by detection using a gel imaging analysis system. Gray value analysis was performed using the Image J software, and statistical analysis and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (29.0, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism software were used for data analysis, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Count data that followed a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and inter-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test. Count data that did not follow a normal distribution were presented as medians and interquartile ranges, and inter-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Measurement data were described using frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. No correction for multiple hypothesis testing was performed in the statistical analysis of all data.

GO functional enrichment analysis

To further explore the molecular mechanisms associated with AVF failure, we performed GO functional enrichment analysis of DEPs. From the overview of the number of genes in the second-level classification of GO, it can be seen that the number of downregulated genes in the AVF failure group was greater than the number of upregulated genes in biological processes (BPs), cellular components (CCs), and molecular functions (MFs) (Figure 3(a)). In the analysis of DEPs, BPs mainly involved mRNA metabolism, RNA processing, peptide metabolism, peptide breakdown, peptide biosynthesis, ribonucleoprotein complex biosynthesis, cellular nicotinamide metabolism, amide biosynthesis, cellular nitrogen compound metabolism, intracellular transport, etc. (Figure 3(b)); CCs were mainly located in the cytoplasm, intracellular organelles, membrane-bound organelles, ribonucleoprotein complexes, extracellular vesicles, extracellular organelles, extracellular vesicles, etc. (Figure 3(c)); and MFs mainly involved protein binding, nucleic acid binding, enzyme binding, mRNA binding, cadherin binding, catalytic activity, and cell adhesion molecule binding (Figure 3(d)).

KEGG signaling pathway enrichment analysis

To further identify the potential metabolic and signaling pathways, we performed KEGG pathway annotation analysis of the DEPs. Compared with the control group, the DEPs in the AVF failure group were mainly enriched in cell aggregation and adhesion, complement and coagulation cascades, regulation of the actin cytoskeleton, cholesterol metabolism, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, protein digestion and absorption, endocytosis, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, platelet activation, and tryptophan metabolism (Figure 4(a)). The z-score bubble chart listed the top 20 significantly enriched pathways and performed certain functional classifications, mainly including six aspects: cellular processes, organic systems, metabolism, environmental information processing, human diseases, and genetic information processing. Based on the differences in the three most significant aspects, at the cellular process level, the main differential pathways were cell aggregation, adhesion, and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton, while at the organic system level, the main differential pathways were extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. At the metabolic level, the main differential pathways included the complement and coagulation cascades and cholesterol metabolism (Figure 4(b)). The enrichment circle diagram provided more detailed information, including the DEPs in each pathway, their P values, the number of upregulated and downregulated genes, and the enrichment factor values (Figure 4(c)). The B-level classification diagram highlighted a strong association between AVF failure and the immune system. Additionally, in the human disease classification, the AVF failure group showed a relatively high correlation with infectious diseases, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, and metabolic disorders (Figure 4(d)).

Discussion

According to data from the 2019 Global Kidney Health Atlas (GKHA), the global average number of newly diagnosed patients with ESRD is 144 per million people [23]. In most countries, hemodialysis remains the most commonly used dialysis method for renal replacement therapy, and vascular access is a critical procedure. Vascular access primarily includes AVF, central venous catheter (CVC), and arteriovenous graft (AVG). CVCs are mainly used for short-term vascular access but are associated with higher rates of complications, such as infection, thrombosis, and venous stenosis. Compared with AVF, AVGs have a shorter lifespan and are prone to infection and thrombosis. AVF had the highest patency rate and relatively fewer complications [24–26].

An AVF is created by surgically anastomosing a proximal artery with a superficial vein, thereby ‘arterializing’ the outflow vein, allowing fistula cannulation and use for hemodialysis. The National Kidney Foundation Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative defines a mature AVF as having a diameter greater than 6 mm, subcutaneous depth less than 6 mm, and blood flow of at least 600 mL/min [26]. Reports from the National Institutes of Health indicate that 60% of AVF are not mature enough for dialysis treatment after surgery [27]. The Hemodialysis Fistula Maturation study showed that the incidence of AVF dysfunction at one year was 24% [28]. Meta-analysis data showed that the primary patency rate of AVF at one year was 64%, the primary assisted patency rate was 73%, and the secondary patency rate was 79% [29]. AVF failure is characterized by venous angiographic stenosis, which often occurs near the venous-arterial anastomosis site [27]. AVF dysfunction is a multifactorial process involving upstream events, such as uremic toxins, hemodynamic changes, and cellular damage caused by surgical trauma, as well as downstream events, such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell migration and proliferation. Currently, there are no effective clinical strategies to prevent AVF failure. For example, the use of heparin increases the risk of bleeding but does not benefit AVF patency [30]. The use of antiplatelet drugs did not reduce the incidence of AVF failure [31]. A Cochrane systematic review reported that there is no reliable evidence to support the use of aspirin, clopidogrel, dipyridamole, dipyridamole plus aspirin, warfarin, sulfinpyrazone, nitroglycerin (patch), or fish oil for preventing AVF thrombosis [32]. Therefore, it is urgent to identify new diagnostic biomarkers, reveal dysregulated signaling pathways, and identify potential drug targets, which are crucial for the prevention and treatment of AVF failure.

In terms of clinical characteristics, patients in the AVF failure group exhibited obvious NIH and vascular lumen stenosis near the anastomosis site of the cephalic vein. Hemoglobin levels were significantly higher in the AVF failure group than in the control group. In the case of AVF failure, the proliferation of fibrous tissue, such as elastic fibers in the vascular wall, leads to vascular lumen stenosis, local tissue ischemia and hypoxia. Hypoxia can promote hemoglobin synthesis to increase the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood [33,34]. Thus, elevated hemoglobin might be associated with decreased expression of hypoxia-adaptive proteins. The calcium level in the AVF failure group was higher than in the control group. A high-calcium microenvironment drives the transdifferentiation of VSMCs from a ‘contractile’ to an ‘osteoblast-like’ phenotype, leading to the deposition of calcium salts within the vessel wall [35,36]. Given the established link between vascular calcification (VC) and AVF failure [37], this molecular-level shift may be reflected proteomically as an aberrant overexpression of ossification-related proteins and a concomitant, widespread activation of inflammatory/oxidative stress pathways. In lipid-related tests, triglyceride levels in the AVF failure group were significantly higher than those in the control group. Triglycerides are an independent risk factor for AVF failure [38], and hyperlipidemia can activate the VEGF-MMP9 signaling pathway, causing intimal hyperplasia and atherosclerosis [39]. The elevated triglyceride can trigger a cascade of protein expression changes linked to inflammation (including complement activation), ECM remodeling, and cellular stress. Routine coagulation examination revealed that fibrinogen in the AVF failure group were significantly lower than those in the control group. Primarily, reduced fibrinogen places the vascular tissue repair environment in a state characterized by a diminished acute-phase response, insufficient repair substrates, and concurrent malnutrition [40,41]. This altered state may be reflected as the underexpression of coagulation/platelet-related proteins, a shift in inflammatory profile (toward a non-thrombotic phenotype), and a global suppression of anabolism-associated proteins.

Although genetic analysis has made some progress in AVF failure [42], owing to the limitations of gene coding, the genome mainly functions at the protein level. Proteomics-based analyses offer a new perspective for identifying new potential disease-related biomarkers and candidate molecular targets. This study employed MS-based quantitative proteomics coupled with TMT labeling to analyze the proteome of the cephalic vein of the anastomosis site of patients with AVF failure and identified 1125 proteins. Compared with the control group, there were 121 DEPs in the AVF failure group, including 53 upregulated and 68 downregulated proteins. This indicates that changes in the proteome of vascular tissue may reflect AVF failure.

To explore the molecular mechanisms associated with AVF failure, we performed GO functional clustering analysis of DEPs. Compared to the control group, the number of downregulated genes in the AVF failure group was greater than the number of upregulated genes in BPs, CCs, and MFs. GO enrichment analysis was primarily enriched in protein binding, nucleic acid binding, enzyme binding, mRNA binding, cadherin binding, catalytic activity, and cell adhesion molecule binding. To further identify the important metabolic and signaling pathways, we performed KEGG pathway annotation analysis of the DEPs. Compared to the control group, the DEPs in the AVF failure group were mainly enriched in cell aggregation and adhesion, complement and coagulation cascades, regulation of the actin cytoskeleton, cholesterol metabolism, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, protein digestion and absorption, endocytosis, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, platelet activation, and tryptophan metabolism. At the cellular process level, the main differential pathways were the aggregation and adhesion of cells and the regulation of the actin cytoskeleton. NIH is composed of disordered endothelial cells, VSMCs, myofibroblast-like cells, inflammatory cells, and the extracellular matrix, with VSMCs being the main cellular component [7]. Under physiological conditions, VSMCs are located in the vascular media and are highly differentiated cells with extremely low proliferative and synthetic capabilities that express a series of contractile proteins (such as α-SMA). When the vascular environment or blood flow conditions change, VSMCs can transform from a contractile to a synthetic type, with reduced expression of contractile proteins. Dedifferentiated VSMCs can migrate to the vascular intima, showing uncontrolled proliferation, exacerbating the NIH process, leading to vascular lumen stenosis and even AVF failure [43]. At the organic system level, the main differential pathways were ECM-receptor interactions and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. Following AVF surgery, activated VSMCs and fibroblasts undergo a phenotypic switch from a contractile to a secretory state, leading to excessive production of ECM components such as collagen types I and III, and fibronectin. This promotes intimal hyperplasia and increases vessel wall stiffness [44,45]. Meanwhile, the imbalance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their tissue inhibitors is a key step in degrading the normal basement membrane and elastic lamina, thereby enabling VSMCs migration from the media to the intima [45]. This dysregulated ECM homeostasis not only provides a physical pathway for cell migration, but also generates biologically active degradation products that can further modulate cellular behavior. Activation of the PI3K-Akt pathway serves as a critical hub that connects upstream inflammatory/growth factor signals with downstream cellular proliferation and survival. Under the high-shear-stress environment of an AVF, vascular cells perceive both mechanical and biochemical stimuli, leading to PI3K-Akt pathway activation [46]. Activated Akt exerts multiple pro-NIH effects through phosphorylation of downstream targets, such as mTOR and FOXO, including: 1) promoting the proliferation and hypertrophy of VSMCs and fibroblasts; 2) enhancing cell survival by inhibiting pro-apoptotic proteins; 3) upregulating hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), thereby facilitating the expression of genes that adapt to hypoxic/inflammatory conditions; and 4) engaging in crosstalk with inflammatory pathways, such as complement signaling. For example, Akt can positively regulate the transcriptional activity of NF-κB, forming a self-reinforcing inflammation-proliferation network [47]. At the metabolic level, the main differential pathways are complement and coagulation cascades and cholesterol metabolism. The complement system is an important part of the innate immune system, which forms C3 convertase, C5 convertase, a membrane attack complex (MAC, also known as C5b-9), and related products through complex cascade reactions, achieving effects such as cell lysis, opsonization, phagocytosis, and participating in inflammation [48–51]. The acute vascular wall injury and dramatic hemodynamic changes caused by arteriovenous anastomosis serve as potent triggers for complement cascade activation [45]. Activated complement products, such as C5a, are powerful chemotactic factors that recruit and activate neutrophils and monocytes. These cells infiltrate the vascular wall, where monocytes differentiate into macrophages and release a large number of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby initiating and amplifying the local inflammatory response [52]. In animal models of AVF, complement inhibition has been shown to attenuate intimal hyperplasia [53], which supports our findings and suggests that the complement system is a potential therapeutic target for AVF-associated NIH. The complement system interacts inseparably with the coagulation system, activating, regulating, and modulating each other to jointly respond to host defense and the repair of injury. Excessive activation of the complement system not only damages vascular endothelial cells, but also promotes microthrombosis through interaction with platelets, coagulation factors, and inflammatory factors. This study observed a significant increase in upstream complement components (e.g., C1s, C1qa, C1qb, and C7) in patients with AVF failure, but no significant difference in the terminal complex C5b-9. Possible explanations for this finding include: (1) The short in vivo half-life of C5b-9; if sample collection occurred after the peak of activation, degradation may have already occurred [54]. (2) Incomplete activation or regulatory mechanisms: complement system activation may have been suppressed by local regulatory mechanisms, limiting the formation of C5 convertase and preventing full activation of the terminal pathway [55,56]. (3) Limitations of the detection method. If C5b-9 levels were low or clearance was rapid, detection may have been challenging [57]. C1q could bind to the C1q receptor on platelets, inducing the release of P-selectin and promoting platelet aggregation and adhesion to endothelial cells or white blood cells [58]. Coagulation factors and thrombin activate the complement system and play a role in complement convertase [59]. Studies have shown that there are common histological features between NIH in failed AVFs and atherosclerosis [60]. Dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism in macrophages is the pathological basis for atherosclerosis development. In addition to playing an important role in the structure and function of the plasma membrane, cholesterol metabolites can also act as solvents in other lipid signaling processes to play biological roles [61]. Liver X receptor (LXR)-623 could reduce intracellular cholesterol content by activating the LXR downstream signaling pathway to kill glioma cells and prolong mouse survival [62]. In breast cancer, the cholesterol derivative, 6-oxo-cholestan-3β,5α-diol (OCDO), could bind to the glucocorticoid receptor to promote tumor cell proliferation [63]. In triple-negative breast cancer, retinoic acid orphan nuclear receptor γ activates cholesterol synthesis to promote malignant progression [64]. These studies show that dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism may be an important pathological mechanism for NIH in AVF failure. Simultaneously, the B-level classification data showed a high correlation between AVF failure and the immune system, which is consistent with the above results. In addition, in the human disease classification, it could be seen that AVF failure has a relatively high correlation with infectious diseases, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, and metabolic diseases, which might due to many similarities in their pathogenesis.

In addition, a PPI network was constructed to further explore hub proteins related to AVF failure. Our findings have demonstrated that C7, F9, SERPINC1, MFAP4, C1S, C1QA, C1QB, TF, vWF, and others interact with numerous other proteins. Finally, we verified four potential targets-C7, F9, SERPINC1, and MFAP4 by immunohistochemical staining and western blotting. The results of immunohistochemical staining showed that compared with the control group, the vascular tissue in the AVF failure group exhibited obvious NIH, and the expression of C7, F9, SERPINC1, and MFAP4 were increased, particularly in VSMCs, which are the main components of NIH. Western blotting confirmed that the expression of C7, F9, SERPINC1, and MFAP4 was significantly upregulated. These results further demonstrate the universality of proteomics data.

The screening of tissue-specific biomarkers based on TMT-based proteomics demonstrates relatively high reliability. The current study still existed some study limitations. First, the sample size for proteomic analysis is relatively small. This may reduce the statistical power and increase the risk of type II errors, meaning that some true biological differences might remain undetected due to insufficient sample size. To minimize these risks, we implemented the following rigorous measures in the analysis: ①Stringent quality control criteria: Strict quality control thresholds were applied to protein identification and quantification data to ensure that only high-confidence data were included. ②Independent validation methods: For key candidate proteins, we performed validation using Western blot and immunohistochemistry in an independent validation cohort, which provided further support for our results. Despite these measures, the findings of this study should still be considered exploratory and preliminary. The conclusions need to be further verified in larger, prospective cohort studies. Second, some baseline clinical parameters differ between the groups. These factors may act as confounders influencing the proteomic profile of vascular tissue. We acknowledge the difficulty in perfectly matching all clinical variables in a larger cohort. The potential impact of these baseline differences cannot be fully excluded and therefore necessitates validation in future prospective studies with more stringent matching criteria. Third, since this is a cross-sectional observational study, the temporal sequence cannot be determined, and causality cannot be inferred. Lastly, this is a single-center study and may have been influenced by the medical conditions of the center and technical level of the surgeons. Therefore, our findings provide only preliminary associative evidence and require further validation through prospective cohort studies and functional experiments.

In summary, this comprehensive proteomic analysis provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms associated with AVF failure, broadening our understanding of the molecular characteristics related to this disease. This study identified a series of potential candidate biomarkers and offers research directions for potential therapeutic strategies that contribute to the advancement of knowledge in this field.
