Looking Beyond the Annulus
Authors: Ana Paula Tagliari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: aortic root anatomy, computed tomography, sinotubular junction, structural heart disease, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR)

Since its introduction, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has evolved from a procedure guided primarily by anatomical feasibility to one driven by anatomical precision. Advances in multidetector computed tomography (CT) have transformed preprocedural planning from simple annular sizing into a comprehensive assessment of the aortic root and surrounding structures, enabling operators not only to select the most appropriate transcatheter heart valve (THV), but also to anticipate procedural complexity, device–anatomy interaction, and potential procedural pitfalls.

This concept is elegantly illustrated by Sharma et al1 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, in which the authors successfully performed a TAVR in a patient with a severely calcified and markedly constricted sinotubular junction (STJ). Although this anatomy is relatively uncommon, the report highlights a principle that extends far beyond an isolated technical challenge. Rather than serving merely as an anatomical landmark, the STJ is a key determinant of valve expansion, leaflet motion, and procedural strategy. More importantly, it reminds us that predicting THV performance requires understanding the entire aortic root—not simply measuring the annulus.

The STJ forms the anatomical transition between the aortic root and the ascending aorta and represents the outflow boundary of the aortic root. Although it is not the primary landing zone for THVs, it plays an important role in device performance. Its diameter, compliance, and calcium burden directly influence stent expansion, leaflet motion, and outflow hemodynamics.2

When heavily calcified and significantly smaller than the annulus, the STJ behaves as a rigid supra-annular constraint that may prevent complete valve expansion despite appropriate annular sizing. The consequences extend well beyond simple underexpansion, leading to asymmetric radial force distribution, distorted frame geometry, impaired leaflet excursion, elevated transvalvular gradients, compromised coronary access, paravalvular regurgitation, and, in extreme cases, catastrophic complications such as aortic root or ascending aortic injury.2,3

Despite its clinical relevance, standardized CT assessment of the STJ is still evolving. Recent studies have proposed objective definitions of a “narrow and calcified STJ,” defining a narrow STJ as a minimum diameter smaller than that corresponding to a 10% oversized annulus area and a calcified STJ as circumferential calcification involving an angle >90°. Applying these criteria, Mitsis et al4 identified this anatomical phenotype in approximately 13% of patients undergoing TAVR, suggesting that hostile STJ anatomy may be considerably more common than previously appreciated.

Returning to the case presented by Sharma et al,1 perhaps one of its most compelling aspects is that it moves beyond anatomical description to explore the underlying biomechanical mechanism. Using a simplified bench model reproducing the patient's anatomy, the authors demonstrate how severe STJ constraint results in asymmetric frame expansion and leaflet crowding, producing the characteristic “cogwheeling” phenomenon.1 Although simplified, this model provides a convincing mechanistic explanation linking anatomy to prosthesis behavior and shifts the discussion from describing anatomy to predicting valve function within a patient-specific anatomical environment.

This concept is further supported by computational fluid–structure interaction studies. By simulating supra-annular valve implantation across different STJ anatomies, Oks et al5 demonstrated that smaller STJ diameters impair frame expansion, reduce geometric orifice area, increase transvalvular pressure gradients, and generate disturbed flow patterns associated with greater platelet stress accumulation and thrombogenic potential. Structural analyses further showed that severe STJ narrowing may reduce the prosthetic frame cross-sectional area by more than 50% at the crown region, directly affecting leaflet motion and overall valve performance.5 Collectively, these observations suggest that STJ morphology is not merely another anatomical measurement but a fundamental determinant of THV biomechanics.

Recognition of severe STJ constraint also may influence prosthesis selection. Balloon-expandable valves appear particularly susceptible to rigid STJ calcification because balloon expansion transmits substantial radial force to this calcified region, increasing the risk of incomplete expansion, balloon rupture, or aortic injury. Conversely, self-expanding valves may better accommodate selected supra-annular anatomical constraints through their gradual expansion characteristics, although severe STJ narrowing also can limit complete frame expansion.4 Procedural strategy should likewise be tailored to this challenging anatomy. For balloon-expandable valves, a recent 2-stage balloon inflation technique has been proposed to reduce radial stress at the level of the calcified STJ while preserving accurate valve positioning. According to this technique, the first inflation is intentionally performed with the deployment balloon filled with 2 mL less than the nominal volume, providing secure annular anchoring while minimizing excessive force against the rigid STJ. After stable valve fixation is achieved, the balloon is advanced 1 to 2 mm toward the inflow under fluoroscopic guidance and inflated to the full nominal volume, completing valve deployment.6 In contrast, Sharma et al1 successfully adapted the implantation strategy for a self-expanding prosthesis through balloon predilatation, careful deployment, valve repositioning, and optimization of implantation depth.1 Together, these experiences illustrate that understanding STJ anatomy influences not only device selection but also procedural execution.

Ultimately, this case reflects the broader evolution of structural heart interventions and the expanding role of preprocedural TAVR planning (Figure 1). Contemporary CT is no longer used solely to determine anatomical suitability or select the appropriate prosthesis size. Instead, it is increasingly expected to anticipate device behavior, predict biomechanical interactions, identify procedural hazards, and guide individualized procedural strategies. As TAVR expands to younger patients and increasingly complex anatomies, the role of standardized CT assessment of the STJ—including its diameter, height, eccentricity, calcium burden, and relationship to the intended prosthesis size—deserves further investigation and may ultimately become an integral component of preprocedural planning.

The Sinotubular Junction: An Overlooked Determinant of Transcatheter Aortic Valve Performance

Preprocedural cardiac computed tomography (CT) should extend beyond annular sizing to include systematic evaluation of the sinotubular junction (STJ). A narrowed and heavily calcified STJ may act as a rigid supra-annular constraint, limiting transcatheter heart valve expansion despite appropriate annular sizing. This constraint may result in asymmetric frame expansion, leaflet crowding (“cogwheeling”), impaired leaflet motion, increased transvalvular gradients, and potentially reduced coronary access and valve durability. Integrating comprehensive CT assessment of the STJ with an understanding of valve biomechanics enables patient-specific procedural planning, optimizes prosthesis selection and implantation strategy, and may improve procedural safety and long-term transcatheter heart valve performance.
