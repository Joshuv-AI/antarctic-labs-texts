Gold Nanostars as a Novel and Sensitive Indicator of Iodometry for Colorimetric Sensing
Authors: Ting Yu, Chao Qi, Ying Fu, Yuxiaoting Li, Si Shen, Zhi Ruan, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iodometry (iodometric method) is one of the simplest, fastest, and most accurate oxidation–reduction titrations for colorimetric detection. Starch is a key indicator for determining the reaction end point. However, the low solubility and instability of starch limit the sensitivity and application of the iodometry. Plasmonic nanomaterials have emerged as innovative tools to inject modern vitality into traditional methods. In this study, we report gold nanostars as a novel and sensitive indicator of iodometry for colorimetric sensing. Branched gold nanostars possess localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) properties, and their protruding tips can be specifically reshaped by iodide to tune the optical readout. As a color indicator, gold nanostars can be gradually etched with increasing iodide concentration, displaying a variety of color changes from blue to purple and finally to red. This colorimetric sensing strategy enables highly sensitive detection of iodine standards with a linear range from 0.125 μM to 20 μM and a limit of detection of 0.0375 ± 0.0013 μM. Proof-concept experiments demonstrate the colorimetric performance of gold nanostar indicators in direct iodometry, remnant iodometry, and displacement iodometry by detecting chlorine, sulfide, and copper ions in hospital wastewater. Gold nanostars have advantages such as good stability, easy handling, low cost, and high sensitivity, which provide great prospects for the widespread application of iodometry.

Introduction

Iodometry (iodometric method) is one of the simplest, fastest, and most accurate oxidation–reduction titrations for colorimetric detection, which is widely used in the fields of environmental monitoring, food safety, medicine, and chemical industry. , Through valence state transformation of iodine­(I), iodometry enables quantitative analysis of both reducing and oxidizing substances, such as residual chlorine, heavy metals, and dissolved oxygen in water, hydrogen sulfide in gases, and ascorbic acid and glucose in food. − Iodometry includes direct iodometry and indirect iodometry (remnant iodometry and displacement iodometry) (Scheme ). In direct iodometry, iodine (I2) as an oxidizing agent is directly reduced by reductive analytes into iodide (I–) (I2 + 2e– → 2I–). Indirect iodine measurement quantifies iodine consumption and generation by a thiosulfate standard (I2 + 2S2O3 2– → 2I– + S4O6 2–). Starch serves as an indicator and forms a dark blue starch-iodide complex starchiodide) with iodine (I2 + 6n­(C6H10O5) → 2n­(C18H30O5I)), which is key in determining the end point of the titration. , However, starch suffers from multiple disadvantages: (1) poor solubility in cold water, (2) instability of the water dispersions, as the stock solution soon deposits a flocculent precipitate of retrograded starch, (3) the formation of a water-insoluble complex between starch and iodine, which prevents the use of an indicator early in the titration, and (4) large analytical errors caused by the ″drift″ of the end point, all of which make iodometry troublesome in application. ,−

To improve stability and sensitivity, researchers have introduced partially saponified poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) as an alternative to starch in iodometry. ,− The PVA and iodine form a PVA/iodine complex with colors spanning from wine red to deep blue-black, exhibiting a higher molecular absorption coefficient twice that of starch. , However, the instability issue still persists since the stability of both PVA-iodine and starch-iodine complexes is temperature-dependent. , Burgeoning nanotechnology provides a novel platform for developing colorimetric sensing technologies to overcome these challenges. − Gold nanomaterials are outstanding candidates for developing color indicators due to their remarkable localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) properties. − The corresponding absorption spectra featuring characteristic LSPR bands can be precisely modulated by tuning the morphology of gold nanomaterials. − Our previous work has shown that branched gold nanostars feature two absorption bands, including a weaker band originating from the core and an intense band from the branches. − Following iodide-mediated surface etching, the intense band showed a blue shift and an increase in the absorbance of the weaker band, accompanied by the solution color changing from blue to purple and finally to red. − Such a color change can be easily visualized by the naked eye, providing a colorimetric readout for the qualitative detection.

In this work, we for the first time develop gold nanostars as a novel and sensitive indicator of iodometry for colorimetric sensing (Scheme ). Iodine (I2) is a mild oxidizing agent that is reduced to iodide (I–) by reductive analytes in oxidation–reduction titration. The so-produced iodide further mediates the surface etching of gold nanostars and induces a color change in the solution. , Based on this sensing principle, gold nanostars can achieve colorimetric readout of iodine standards in the linear range from 0.125 μM to 20 μM and a detection limit of 0.0375 μM, which is about 2.27 × 104 times lower than that of traditional starch indicators. Owing to the good stability and anti-interference properties, gold nanostars can determine various oxidants and reductants such as chlorine, sulfides, and copper ions in hospital wastewater through direct iodometric titration, residual iodometry, and displacement iodometry, providing broad prospects for the extensive application of iodometry.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Reagents

Chloroauric acid (HAuCl4), 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid sodium salt (HEPES), trisodium citrate, hydroxylamine hydrochloride, sodium dodecyl sulfate, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium iodide (NaI), and copper sulfate (CuSO4) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). A water sulfide standard sample was purchased from Aladdin Reagent Company (Shanghai, China). Ethanol was purchased from Hushi Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). A sodium hypochlorite standard solution was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). An iodine standard solution was purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The sulfide determination kit was purchased from Lohand Environment Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). All of the reagents required for the experiments were of analytical grade and used as received.

Instrumentation

The features of gold nanoparticles (Au seeds, gold nanostars, and gold nanospheres) were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), the absorption spectrum, the particle size, the surface potential, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The morphology of gold nanoparticles was characterized by TEM (JEM-1200EX, Japan) operated at a 120 kV accelerating voltage. A Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Company, England) was used to characterize the particle size and zeta potential. The absorption spectrum was measured by an Infinite 200Pro (TECAN, Switzerland) to get the absorbance from 400 to 1000 nm with a wavelength step of 1 nm. The maximum absorption wavelength was obtained from the absorption spectrum. The XPS analysis was conducted using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA) with an excitation source of Al–Kα rays (hv = 1486.6 eV). The FTIR spectra were recorded on a Nicolet iS50FT-IR apparatus (Thermo Nicolet, USA) in a KBr matrix.

Synthesis of Au Seeds

Au seeds were synthesized by the citrate-mediated reduction of HAuCl4. First, 100 mL of HAuCl4 (1 mM) was added to a 250 mL round-bottom flask and heated to 120 °C with continuous stirring at 1200 rpm. After the boiling and reflux of the solution, 10 mL of trisodium citrate (38.8 mM) was added rapidly and heated continuously to ensure the complete reduction of HAuCl4. After reacting for 15 min, the solution changed from colorless to deep red implying the complete formation of Au seeds. The solution was allowed to reflux for 15 min with slowly stirring at 600 rpm, followed by cooling at room temperature. Finally, nitrocellulose membrane (0.22 μm) was applied to remove the large impurities to prepare the Au seeds needed for experiment.

Synthesis of Gold Nanostars

Gold nanostars were prepared using a seed-mediated growth method. First, 750 μL of the Au seed solution was added to 750 μL of hydroxylamine hydrochloride (40 mM), 18.75 mL of HEPES buffer (100 mM, pH = 8.0), and 38.5 mL of water. The solution was allowed to mix under rigorous stirring at 1450 rpm. Then, 22.5 mL of HAuCl4 (1 mM) was added to the above solution dropwise at room temperature. After that, the solution was allowed to further react for 15 min under moderate stirring at 700 rpm, followed by centrifugation (5000 rpm, 15 min) three times. Finally, the solution was redispersed in HEPES, filtered by a nitrocellulose membrane, and kept at 4 °C for long-term storage.

Surface Etching of Gold Nanostars by Iodide

In a typical etching experiment, 100 μL of the prepared gold nanostars was directly incubated with 100 μL of different concentrations of a sodium iodide solution or iodine standard solution to reach a final volume of 200 μL in a 96-well plate. The mixture of the solution reacted at room temperature for 15 min to etch the gold nanostars. The absorption spectra of gold nanostars were measured by the plate reader to get the absorbance from 400 to 1000 nm with a wavelength step of 4 nm.

Colorimetric Sensing of Sulfide, Chlorine, and Copper Ions by Iodometry

For colorimetric assays using gold nanostars as an indicator, 50 μL of an iodine standard solution (2 μM) was mixed with 50 μL of the test substances (sulfide, chlorine, or copper ions) dissolved in hospital wastewater at different concentrations. The mixed solution was allowed to stand at room temperature for 5 min to complete the reaction. Then, 100 μL of gold nanostars was added to reach a final volume of 200 μL. After incubating for 15 min for the surface etching, absorption spectra of gold nanostars were measured by the plate reader to get the absorbance from 400 to 1000 nm with a wavelength step of 4 nm. For starch used as an indicator, the concentration of the iodine standard solution was 2 mM, and the range of the microplate reader was 300–1000 nm with a wavelength step of 4 nm.

Statistical Analysis

Data for all charts were collected from three independent replicates and were plotted via mean ± standard deviation of the mean (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software), Origin Pro 2025 (OriginLab), and SPSS 26.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences).

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Gold Nanostars

We synthesized gold nanostars using a facile seed-mediated growth method in 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-piperazine-1-ethane-sulfonic acid sodium salt (HEPES) buffer (pH = 8.0) (Figure a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images showed good dispersion and spherical shape of Au seeds synthesized by the citrate-mediated reduction of HAuCl4. The average particle diameter of Au seeds was 13.1 ± 0.9 nm, which is in agreement with the hydrodynamic diameter of 13.0 ± 0.4 nm obtained by dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Figures b, c, and S1). The polymer dispersity index (PDI) of Au seeds was 0.150 ± 0.002, indicating a monodisperse system with uniform particle morphology (Figure S2). Au seeds had a red solution color and characteristic absorption spectra with the maximum absorption band at 519 nm (Figure d). HEPES acted as the capping ligand to stabilize gold nanostars under alkaline conditions. , After seed-mediated growth, the PDI of the gold nanostars increased to 0.625 ± 0.002 due to the branched structure (Figure S2). The average diameters of gold nanostars were 122.9 ± 6.0 nm with multiple high eccentricity branches characterized by TEM (Figures e and S3). The DLS curve showed two size peaks, with the 8.9 ± 0.1 nm peak corresponding to the branches of the gold nanostars, and the 117.7 ± 0.7 nm peak corresponding to the core with branches (Figures f and S4). Gold nanostars displayed a blue solution color and two absorption bands, including a weaker band at 540 nm originating from the core and an intense band at 776 nm from the branches (Figure g). Gold nanostars remained spectrally stable after long-term storage at room temperature for half a year (Figure S5).

Following 15 min of treatment with 1 mM iodide, gold nanostars underwent a morphological transformation from a branched structure to a spherical shape with PDI reduced to 0.329 ± 0.003, indicating sensitive and rapid surface etching (Figures h and S2). Hydrodynamic dimensions agreed well with the TEM images, presenting a single DLS band of 82.8 ± 0.6 nm (Figure i). The absorption spectrum of etched gold nanostars exhibited a pronounced blue-shift from 776 ± 1 nm to 540 ± 1 nm, accompanied by a solution color change from blue to pink (Figure j). We measured the zeta potentials to monitor the surface charge of Au seeds, gold nanostars, and gold nanospheres (Figure k). The zeta potential of Au seeds synthesized via citrate-mediated reduction was −27.7 ± 1.0 mV. After growth with HEPES as a capping agent, the negative surface potential of the prepared gold nanostars increased to −38.2 ± 1.8 mV. Following branch etching, the specific surface area and active sites of the gold nanospheres decreased, resulting in a reduction of the negative potential to −30.2 ± 1.5 mV. We employed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) for the compositional characterization of gold nanoparticles. XPS spectra revealed the presence of C­(1s), N­(1s), O­(1s), and Au­(4f) electrons on Au seeds, gold nanostars, and gold nanospheres, indicating the successful preparation of gold nanoparticles with ligands on the surface (Figure l). Notably, the relative sulfur content on gold nanostars and gold nanospheres increased significantly, demonstrating that sulfur-containing HEPES participated in the anisotropic growth and stabilization of gold nanostars (Figure S6). In addition, the presence of I­(3d) on the etched gold nanospheres further confirmed the interaction between iodide and the gold nanostars. FTIR spectrum of Au seeds showed the characteristic absorption of sodium citrate, with peaks at 1079 cm–1 and 1633 cm–1 corresponding to C–C stretching vibration and CO bonds (Figure m). After the seed-mediated growth, new peaks in gold nanostars and gold nanospheres at 1048 cm–1 and 1199 cm–1 correspond to the SO stretching in HEPES and the C–N stretching in hydroxylamine, respectively. After iodide-induced surface etching, the generated hydroxide ions (OH–) caused the deprotonation of carboxyl groups (−COOH) in HEPES to form carboxylate anions (−COO–). Meanwhile, the decrease in the specific surface area of the gold nanospheres reduced the adsorption of HEPES, thereby enhancing the relative intensity of the C–C and C–O stretching vibrations at 1046 cm–1 and 1189 cm–1.

Analytical Performance of the Gold Nanostars Assay

We first investigated the feasibility of gold nanostars as a novel indicator of iodometry for colorimetric sensing. We employed a commercial iodine standard solution (0.0998 M) to investigate the analytical performance of indicators, which contains both iodine (I2) and iodide (KI) (Figure a). After the introduction of the iodine standard, gold nanostars can be etched by I– in it, resulting in a significant morphological change of gold nanostars. Correspondingly, the absorption bands of the gold nanostar underwent a distinct blue-shift from 776 ± 1 nm to 541 ± 1 nm, with the solution color changing from blue to pink (Figure b). We utilized the absorbance ratio at 776 to 540 nm (A776/A540) to indicate the extent of surface etching. The A776/A540 value of gold nanostars decreased remarkably following exposure to the iodide standard (Figure c). The A776/A540 value showed a linear relationship with the iodide concentration in the range of 0.125 μM to 20 μM, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.0375 ± 0.0013 μM (3δblank), demonstrating a sensitive response toward iodide.

To assess the potential of gold nanostars as a color indicator in iodometry, we compared the optical signal output capability and colorimetric detection range of gold nanostars with those of traditional starch. In the presence of an iodine standard, starch reacted with iodine (I2) to form a dark blue starch iodine complex (C18H30O5I), resulting in a significant Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorbance at about 592 nm (Figure d). We used A592 to evaluate the color change of the solution at 592 nm, which increased with the iodine standard. The iodine standard concentration ranged from 2 mM to 6 mM with an LOD of 0.85 mM (Figure e). In contrast, gold nanostars demonstrated superior sensitivity and LOD, improving by about 2.27 × 104 times. Additionally, gold nanostars broadened the linear detection range by about 2 orders of magnitude compared to starch. These results indicated that gold nanostars could be used as a novel indicator of iodometry for colorimetric sensing.

Analytical Performance of Gold Nanostars for Sulfide Detection by Direct Iodometry

To assess the practicality of gold nanostars in direct iodometry, we performed quantitative analysis of sulfide in actual hospital wastewater (Figure a). Sulfide, a poisonous pollutant that endangers the environment, can react directly with iodine (I2). Meanwhile, iodometry is one of the water sulfide determination methods specified by the PRC National Environmental Protection Standards (HJ/T60–2000). Before the experiment, we investigated the colorimetric ability of this approach in the presence of potential interferents in hospital wastewater. The main pollutants such as anions (Cl–, SO4 2–, Br–, I–), cations (Na+, K+, Fe3+, Cu2+), organic matter (ethanol, sodium dodecyl sulfate, bovine serum albumin), bacteria (Escherichia coli), and suspended solids (soil) at a concentration of 5 μg/mL or 104 CFU/mL were used to study the specificity. None of the pollutants other than iodide affected the stability and surface etching of gold nanostars, indicating good stability and selectivity of this colorimetric sensing strategy (Figures S7 and S8).

In this plasmonic assay, as the sulfide concentration increased from 0 to 430 mg/mL, I2 in the iodine standard gradually reduced, resulting in a gradual color change from yellow to colorless (Figure b). We used A350 to represent the UV–vis absorbance of iodine at 350 nm, which decreased with increasing sulfide concentration, with a linear range of 0–430 mg/mL (Figure c). For the starch indicator, the solution gradually turned from blue to colorless with an increase in sulfide concentration from 0 to 430 mg/mL (Figure d). The A592 value showed a linear correlation with sulfide concentration in the range of 43–258 mg/mL with an LOD of 6.81 ± 0.64 mg/mL (Figure e). For the gold nanostars indicator, as the sulfide concentration increases, the iodide (I–) produced increased the degree of surface etching, resulting in a band shift and color change of the solution (Figure f). Sulfide alone at 0–20.5 μg/mL was unable to affect the stability and surface etching of gold nanostars (Figures S9 and S10). The sulfide concentration and A776/A540 value were negatively correlated, which was consistent with the direct iodometric results of iodine and starch as indicators. The linear range of sulfide detection was 0.86 μg/mL to 10.75 μg/mL with an LOD of 0.165 ± 0.009 μg/mL (Figure g). These results demonstrated that gold nanostars as an indicator of direct iodometry improved the LOD and expanded the linear range (Figure h). Furthermore, we evaluated the anti-interference ability through the recovery experiment in actual hospital wastewater. The average recoveries of sulfide by three indicators were 89.3–108.2%, 89.6–109.7%, and 97.2–108.8%, respectively (Figure i). These results demonstrated the prominent reliability and accuracy of gold nanostars for sulfide detection by direct iodometry.

We then verified the quantitative accuracy by comparing our strategy with the standard methylene blue spectrophotometry (Water quality-determination of sulfide-methylene blue spectrophotometric method: GB/T 16489–1996) for sulfide determination in real hospital wastewater samples (Table S1). In acidic conditions, sulfide reacted with N,N-dimethyl-p-phenylenediamine and ferric iron to form stable methylene blue at about 670 nm, whose absorbance increased with the rise in sulfide concentration (Figure a). The linear range of sulfide detection by methylene blue spectrophotometry was 0.08 μg/mL to 20 μg/mL with an LOD of 0.029 ± 0.009 μg/mL (Figure b). We evaluated the sulfide in actual hospital wastewater at different spiked concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 5.0 mg/mL by the two methods. The good correlation between the standard method and this strategy indicated its excellent accuracy in testing a real sample (R2 = 0.993) (Figure S11). In accordance with the PRC Integrated Wastewater Discharge Standard (GB 8978–1996) and the PRC Discharge Standard of Pollutants for Municipal Wastewater Treatment Plants (GB 18918–2002), the maximum permissible concentration of sulfide in wastewater is limited to 1.0 mg/L. The false positive rate of the methylene blue spectrophotometry was 13.3% (Figure c). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis presented an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.9967, suggesting favorable classification performance (Figure d). By comparison, our strategy showed a false positive rate of 20% and an AUC of 0.9783 (Figures e and f). Both methods achieved high AUC values above 0.97, demonstrating outstanding reliability and practical applicability for sulfide overlimit detection in complex wastewater.

Analytical Performance of Gold Nanostars for Chlorine and Copper Ions Detection by Indirect Iodometry

We further investigated the capability of gold nanostars as an indicator in indirect iodometry, including remnant iodometry and displacement iodometry. Chlorine is extensively utilized for water sterilization and disinfection, but the excessive residual chlorine in water presents a substantial hazard to human health. , According to the PRC National Standard (Discharge standard of water pollutants for medical organization: GB 18466–2005), the total residual chlorine in wastewater directly discharged into surface waters and marine areas should be less than 0.5 mg/L. Therefore, we employed this colorimetric sensing strategy to detect residual chlorine by remnant iodometry (Figure a). Residual chlorine first reacted with excess iodine, and the remaining iodine or the produced iodide was then quantified using a colorimetric indicator. For the iodine indicator, the chlorine concentration and the A350 value exhibited a linear relationship in the range of 18.8–375 mg/mL, and the LOD was 3.56 ± 0.36 mg/mL (Figures b and c). For the starch indicator, the linear detection range was from 37.5 mg/mL to 3000 mg/mL with an LOD of 12.92 ± 0.94 mg/mL (Figures d and e). These results indicated that it was impossible to determine the residual chlorine concentration of 0.5 mg/L using iodine and starch as indicators. In contrast, for the gold nanostars indicator, the linear range of chlorine detection was 93.8 μg/mL to 750 μg/mL with an LOD of 16.32 ± 1.54 μg/mL (Figures f, g, S12, and S13), enabling the iodometry to detect residual chlorine in hospital wastewater.

Copper, a type of heavy metal, can cause harm to both the human body and the water environment. , Owing to the significant roles of copper ions in industrial and biological processes, copper ions remain a key item on the daily monitoring list of civil water. The PRC National Standard (Emission standard of pollutants for copper, nickel, cobalt industry: GB 25467–2010) stipulates that the direct discharge standard for copper in water should not exceed 0.5 mg/L. We then performed quantitative analysis of copper ions using displacement iodometry (Figure h). The copper ion solution exhibited a blue color and a characteristic absorption band at 810 nm. The copper concentration and the A810 value exhibited a linear relationship in the range of 1.27 g/L-31.8 g/L with an LOD of 0.137 ± 0.021 g/L (Figure S14). In the iodimetric assay, copper oxide ions quantitatively oxidized iodide to iodine. For the iodine indicator, the introduction of copper ions caused the iodine solution to change color from yellow to green, with enhanced characteristic absorption at 810 nm and decreased absorption at 350 nm (Figure S15). The A810/A350 value significantly increased with the copper concentration from 1.27 g/L to 25.4 g/L with an LOD of 0.21 ± 0.04 g/L (Figures i and j). For the starch indicator, a linear relationship between the copper concentration and the A592 value fell in the range of 127 mg/L-2542 mg/L, and the LOD was 32.8 ± 2.5 mg/L (Figures k and l). For the gold nanostars indicator, the linear range of copper detection was 16 μg/L to 1271 μg/L with an LOD of 0.58 ± 0.07 μg/L (Figures m, n, S16, and S17). These results demonstrated that gold nanostars could improve the analytical sensitivity and expand the application scenarios of the iodometry.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we develop gold nanostars as a novel and sensitive indicator of iodometry for colorimetric sensing. Iodide-mediated surface etching can modulate the plasmonic properties of gold nanostars that allow for a colorimetric readout for the qualitative detection. The advantages of gold nanostars as an indicator of iodometry include: (1) the good stability of gold nanostars allows for long-term storage; (2) the surface etching enables the detection of iodide and iodide-related substances with high selectivity and specificity; (3) the high sensitivity in colorimetric detection with a limit of detection of 0.0375 ± 0.0013 μM; (4) the rapid response time enables detection to be completed in 15 min; and (5) the anti-interference ability makes them suitable for iodide quantification in complex samples. Therefore, gold nanostars, as a novel indicator, provide great prospects for the widespread application of iodometric methods.
