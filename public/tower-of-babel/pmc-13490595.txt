Entomopathogenic activity of Purpureocillium takamizusanense against diverse agricultural pests
Authors: Pei-Hsin Lo, Khaled Abdrabo El-Sayid Abdrabo, Yu-Shin Nai, Hsiao-ling Lu, Yin-Tse Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable control of agricultural pests requires alternatives to synthetic pesticides and to the Beauveria/Metarhizium products that dominate fungal biocontrol. Purpureocillium takamizusanense, first described from cicadas in Japan and recently from lychee stink bug in Taiwan, is an emerging entomopathogenic fungus. We assessed the virulence of strain TCTeb01, isolated from naturally infected Tessaratoma papillosa, against three pests from three insect orders. Inoculation significantly reduced cumulative survival in all three hosts (Kaplan–Meier with log-rank, all P < 10⁻3): Hypothenemus hampei reached 100% mortality by day 6, Thrips palmi 77.5% by day 7, and T. papillosa nymphs 67.5% by day 42. For adult T. papillosa, virulence was concentration-dependent, with 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL producing 94% cumulative mortality by day 42, significantly higher than at 1 × 105 and 1 × 10⁶. Median lethal times were 3.7 days for H. hampei and T. palmi at 1 × 10⁸ conidia/mL, 19.7 days for T. papillosa nymphs at 1 × 10⁷, and 14.5 days for T. papillosa adults at 1 × 10⁷. Morphological characterization of seven Taiwanese strains extended conidiophore length (up to 169 μm) and documented an Acremonium-like synanamorph; a four-locus phylogeny confirmed species identity. Introduction

The genus Purpureocillium (Ascomycota: Hypocreales) was established in 2011 to accommodate Paecilomyces lilacinus (syn. P. lilacinum) (Thom) Samson and related species, characterized by their purple to lilac-colored conidia and distinct phylogenetic position within Ophiocordycipitaceae1. Currently, the genus includes 12 described species, including fungi exhibiting diverse ecological roles, being commonly isolated from soil, decaying vegetation, insects, nematodes, salt crystals, and even as opportunistic pathogens in clinical settings1–6.

Members of the genus Purpureocillium have garnered significant attention as biological control agents due to their broad host range and demonstrated effectiveness against agricultural pests7,8. Purpureocillium lilacinum, the most extensively studied species, has been successfully commercialized for controlling plant-parasitic nematodes, particularly Meloidogyne species7,9, which are responsible for substantial annual crop losses worldwide. In addition, P. lilacinum has shown efficacy against a variety of pests, including whiteflies10, aphids8, spider mites11, and leaf-cutting ants12. Similarly, P. lavendulum has exhibited effectiveness primarily against nematodes13. The infection mechanisms of these fungi typically involve direct penetration of host cuticles, secretion of extracellular enzymes such as proteases and chitinases, and the production of bioactive metabolites, including leucinostatins, which possess both nematocidal and insecticidal activities14.

Purpureocillium takamizusanense, originally described from cicada adults in Japan15,16, represents an emerging biological control agent with promising potential17. Although initial studies focused on its occurrence in cicadas, recent work has documented its natural infection of the lychee stink bug (LSB), Tessaratoma papillosa (Drury) (Hemiptera: Tessaratomidae)17,18, a serious pest of lychee and longan production throughout Southeast Asia that causes significant fruit drop and quality reduction. This finding suggests P. takamizusanense may have applications against other economically important insects.

Given the urgent need for sustainable control options against major agricultural pests, P. takamizusanense warrants investigation for its efficacy against insects of global economic significance. The coffee berry borer (CBB), Hypothenemus hampei (Ferrari) (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), represents coffee’s most destructive pest globally, causing estimated annual losses exceeding $500 million19. Its cryptic lifecycle inside coffee berries makes conventional control challenging, while its developing resistance to synthetic insecticides further complicates management20,21. Likewise, southern yellow thrip (SYT), Thrips palmi (Karny) (Thysanoptera: Thripidae), causes substantial economic damage to vegetable and ornamental crops through both direct feeding damage and transmission of tospoviruses, with global annual losses exceeding $1 billion22,23. Despite the economic significance of these pests and the potential of P. takamizusanense as a control agent, comprehensive studies on host specificity, infection mechanisms, and efficacy remain limited.

In this study, we provide a detailed characterization of P. takamizusanense strains isolated from T. papillosa, including expanded morphological descriptions, molecular phylogenetic analyses, and virulence assays against three diverse agricultural pests. Our findings broaden the known host range of P. takamizusanense and evaluate its potential as a broad-spectrum biological control agent, contributing to the growing body of knowledge on Purpureocillium species as important tools in sustainable agriculture.

Results

Virulence test

Virulence against agricultural pests

Purpureocillium takamizusanense strain TCTeb01 was virulent against all three agricultural pests tested (Fig. 1). Hypothenemus hampei showed high susceptibility, with 100 ± 0.00% cumulative mortality by observation point 6 (6 DPI). Thrips palmi showed intermediate susceptibility, reaching 77.5 ± 4.79% cumulative mortality by observation point 7 (7 DPI). Tessaratoma papillosa nymphs were the slowest to die, with 67.5 ± 5.59% cumulative mortality at observation point 6 (42 DPI). Kaplan–Meier survival curves of inoculated and control groups differed significantly for all three pests (log-rank test: H. hampei χ2 = 29.3, df = 1, P = 6.1 × 10⁻⁸; T. palmi χ2 = 22.6, df = 1, P = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶; T. papillosa nymph χ2 = 14.0, df = 1, P = 1.8 × 10⁻4).

Virulence against adult T. papillosa at different concentrations

Three conidial concentrations (1 × 105, 1 × 10⁶, and 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL) produced a concentration-dependent reduction in cumulative survival of adult T. papillosa (Fig. 2). No mortality was observed in any treatment during the first 7 days post-inoculation. By day 42, cumulative mortality reached 94.4 ± 5.55% at 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL, compared to 41.7 ± 5.55% at 1 × 10⁶ and 63.9 ± 6.55% at 1 × 105 conidia/mL. A Cox proportional-hazards model with concentration as a categorical fixed effect (1 × 105 as reference) detected a strong concentration effect (likelihood-ratio test χ2 = 48.8, df = 2, P = 3 × 10⁻11): the hazard at 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL was approximately five-fold higher than at 1 × 105 (HR = 4.97, 95% CI 2.87–8.62, P = 1.1 × 10⁻⁸), whereas 1 × 10⁶ did not differ significantly from 1 × 105 (HR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.33–1.21, P = 0.16). Bonferroni-corrected pairwise log-rank tests corroborated that 1 × 10⁷ produced significantly faster mortality than both 1 × 105 (P = 1.7 × 10⁻⁹) and 1 × 10⁶ (P = 2.3 × 10⁻⁹), whereas 1 × 105 and 1 × 10⁶ were not statistically distinguishable (P = 0.66; Table 1). Together, these results indicate that 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL is the threshold concentration above which virulence against adult T. papillosa is achieved, while 1 × 105 and 1 × 10⁶ conidia/mL induced indistinguishable, sub-threshold mortality.

Lethal time analysis across pest species

Median lethal times (LT₅₀) differed substantially among pest species and conidial concentrations, as indicated by non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals. Hypothenemus hampei and T. palmi exhibited high susceptibility, with LT₅₀ values of 3.75 days (95% CI: 2.77–4.73) and 3.67 days (95% CI: 3.10–4.23), respectively, when treated with 1 × 10⁸ conidia/mL. Tessaratoma papillosa nymphs were less susceptible, with an LT₅₀ of 19.7 days (95% CI: 7.96–31.5) at 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL. For adult T. papillosa, LT₅₀ shortened from 29.9 days (95% CI: 20.6–39.3) at 1 × 105 conidia/mL and 25.8 days (95% CI: 21.4–30.3) at 1 × 10⁶ conidia/mL to 14.5 days (95% CI: 11.7–17.3) at 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL. Cox proportional-hazards modelling and Bonferroni-corrected pairwise log-rank tests confirmed that 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL produced significantly faster mortality than both 1 × 105 and 1 × 10⁶ conidia/mL (both P < 10⁻⁸), whereas 1 × 105 and 1 × 10⁶ conidia/mL were not statistically distinguishable (Bonferroni P = 0.66), with overlapping LT₅₀ confidence intervals.

Fungal morphological and molecular identification

Morphological characterization

The morphological characteristics of strains TCTeb01 was examined on various culture media (Fig. 3A-C). Colonies appeared floccose to powdery in texture, pale vinaceous to lilac coloration at the center and whitish toward the margins. The reverse was distinctly yellowish to light brown, showing a diffusible pigment.

Conidiophores dimorphic: Long erect conidiophores 94–169 μm × 2.5–3 μm, smooth to rough-walled. Short irregularly branched conidiophores 5.7–9.9 × 3.1–3.2 μm. Phialides elongated clavate or cylindrical with tapered apex, 9.2–11 × 2–2.1 μm. Conidia in basipetal chains, smooth-walled, ellipsoidal, 2–2.9 × 1.5–1.9 μm. Acremonium-like synanamorph consistently present in all examined strains with: Conidiophores 5.3–7.1 μm long; Two types of conidia: (1) Cylindrical slightly curved conidia 6.3–8.7 × 1.8–1.9 μm, and (2) Ellipsoidal conidia in slimy masses 2.3–3.2 × 1.6–2 μm (Fig. 3).

The Taiwanese strains show similar overall morphology to the type specimen from Japan15,16, which was described as having smaller conidiophores (65–110 μm long) and slightly larger conidia (2.5–4 × 1.4–1.8 μm). Our observations extend the known morphological variation of this species, particularly in conidiophore length (up to 169 μm) and document the consistent presence of an Acremonium-like synanamorph with two distinct conidial types. This synanamorph feature was not explicitly described in the original Japanese specimens.

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

To determine the taxonomic placement of the seven strains, phylogenetic analyses were performed using a concatenated dataset comprising four genetic loci: ITS, LSU, TEF, and RPB1, resulting in a total alignment length of 4126 bp. The best-fit substitution models, selected by ModelTest-NG, were TrN for ITS, TIM2 + G4 for LSU, K80 + G4 for RPB1, and K80 + I + G4 for TEF.

The maximum likelihood phylogeny (Fig. 4) clearly demonstrated that our seven Taiwanese isolates (TCP05, TCTeb01, TP21-2, TP17, TP18, TP19, and TP20) formed a strongly supported monophyletic group within the species P. takamizusanense. These isolates clustered closely together and subsequently formed a robustly supported larger subclade with the syntype strains of P. takamizusanense, NBRC 108,982 and NBRC 110,232, representing partspore and conidial isolates from the same specimen. This subclade was sister to another P. takamizusanense group, including NBRC 100,742 and strain F1724, which was supported with moderate confidence.

Our multilocus phylogeny distinguished the P. takamizusanense clade from other Purpureocillium species, with most species-level relationships receiving high bootstrap values (> 90%) and high posterior probabilities (> 0.95). Bayesian inference yielded a congruent topology that strongly supported the same evolutionary relationships observed in the maximum likelihood analysis.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive characterization of P. takamizusanense as a broad-spectrum entomopathogenic fungus with significant activity against multiple agricultural pests. Our results demonstrate that P. takamizusanense strain TCTeb01 exhibits notable virulence against coffee berry borer (H. hampei), southern yellow thrip (T. palmi), and lychee stink bug (T. papillosa), expanding its known host range beyond the originally described cicada hosts.

The efficacy of P. takamizusanense against H. hampei (LT₅₀ = 3.6 days at 1 × 10⁸ conidia/mL) is comparable to commercial B. bassiana strains (LT₅₀ = 2.52–2.9 days at 1 × 105–1 × 10⁹ conidia/mL)24 and superior to other reported agents like B. bassiana CG11 and M. anisopliae CG46 tested at 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL25. Similarly, its performance against T. palmi matches established biocontrol agents including B. bassiana JEF-350 (LT₅₀ = 3.73 days at 1 × 10⁸ conidia/mL) and approaches that of Metarhizium lepidiotae NCHU-9 (LT₅₀ = 2.89 days at 1 × 10⁸ conidia/mL)26,27. Beyond comparable mortality, P. takamizusanense offers practical advantages as a complement to existing B. bassiana- and M. anisopliae-based programmes, i.e. it is taxonomically distinct, hedging against cross-resistance from the saturated commercial use of Beauveria/Metarhizium products;the sister species, Purpureocillium lilacinum is already commercialized for plant-parasitic nematode control9,28, providing an established regulatory and production pathway; and strain TCTeb01 is endemic to Taiwanese lychee/longan orchards, supporting conservation biocontrol with a locally adapted strain.

Against T. papillosa, P. takamizusanense showed slower action than B. bassiana strains29–31 , likely due to this pest’s larger size and robust cuticle. The prolonged infection-to-mortality interval is a limitation, since infected insects may continue to feed and cause damage during this period. The adult concentration assay lacked an untreated control, so absolute mortality at each concentration may include a background component and the lowest dose (1 × 105 conidia/mL) is a low-dose treatment rather than a healthy baseline; concentration-specific values are therefore best read as relative. Nevertheless, the strain achieved similar mortality on T. papillosa as in other fungal pathogens such as P. lilacinum (LT₅₀ = 2.46 days at 1 × 10⁸ conidia/mL) and B. bassiana (LT₅₀ = 4.89 days at 1 × 10⁸ conidia/mL)29. Overall, while P. takamizusanense TCTeb01 does not act as rapidly as some highly virulent fungal strains, it exhibits a broad host range and ultimately achieves high mortality rates across pests from different insect orders, an attribute that may be useful within integrated pest management (IPM). Within IPM, the slow action of TCTeb01 against T. papillosa is best deployed as a long-term population suppressor in lychee/longan orchards. In addition, because TCTeb01 was isolated from naturally infected T. papillosa in Taiwanese orchards, conserving its endemic presence in the field is itself a worthwhile management objective. When rapid suppression is needed, this can be combined with faster-acting tactics (e.g., B. bassiana, mechanical control, or selective insecticides), with sublethal effects of fungal infection — such as reduced feeding or oviposition [e.g. Castillo et al.8] — could further reinforce the concurrent measures even before terminal mortality is reached, although such effects have not yet been quantified for P. takamizusanense. The effectiveness of P. takamizusanense against pests from three distinct insect orders—Coleoptera (H. hampei), Thysanoptera (T. palmi), and Hemiptera (T. papillosa)—demonstrates a broader spectrum activity than previously known for this species, which is particularly valuable for integrated pest management across diverse crops within regional production systems. The variable LT₅₀ values observed across pest species are consistent with host-specific factors known to modulate entomopathogenic-fungal infection, including cuticle structure and host immune responses32–35; identifying which of these contribute to the differences seen here will require mechanistic follow-up work.

While P. lilacinum has been extensively studied for its efficacy against plant-parasitic nematodes, whiteflies, aphids, and spider mites with several commercial formulations available8–11, this study represents the first comprehensive evaluation of P. takamizusanense as a biological control agent beyond its natural association with cicadas and lychee stink bugs. The demonstrated broad-spectrum efficacy across multiple insect orders, combined with preliminary biosafety assessments indicating low virulence to non-target organisms18, suggests substantial potential for IPM programs as a sustainable alternative to chemical pesticides.

The extended conidiophore dimensions and previously undocumented Acremonium-like synanamorph in our Taiwanese strains, compared to Japanese specimens15,16, suggest morphological plasticity potentially influenced by host adaptation or geographic variation. The distinct yellowish to light brown diffusible pigment on culture media further distinguishes these strains and may have taxonomic significance.

This study demonstrates that P. takamizusanense possesses significant potential as a broad-spectrumbiological control agent against diverse agricultural pests. Its ability to infect and kill insects from different orders (Coleoptera, Thysanoptera, and Hemiptera) represents a valuable attribute for IPM strategies. The expanded morphological description and molecular characterization provided here contribute to the taxonomic understanding of this species and facilitate its accurate identification in future studies. Whether the morphological differences observed between the Taiwanese isolates and the Japanese type specimens translate into virulence differences across geographic populations remains untested; comparative bioassays of geographically distinct strains under identical conditions will be needed to address this question. We also did not perform biochemical assays on strain TCTeb01 to confirm leucinostatin production or quantify extracellular protease and chitinase activity; whether the genus-level infection mechanisms reported for Purpureocillium are operative in TCTeb01 under entomopathogenic conditions remains to be tested directly14. Further research should focus on optimizing application methods, assessing field efficacy, infection mechanisms, and investigating potential synergistic effects with other control measures to fully exploit the biocontrol potential of P. takamizusanense in sustainable agriculture.

Methods

Fungal isolation

Seven Purpureocillium takamizusanense strains were isolated from naturally infected T. papillosa collected from longan orchards in Taiwan between 2018 and 2019. Infected insects were examined under a stereomicroscope (Leica DM2000LED) to identify mycosed tissues. Fungal samples were aseptically collected from infected tissues using flame-sterilized scalpels and inoculated onto Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA; HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., India) plates. The plates were incubated at 25 °C and examined for fungal growth at 7-day intervals. Characteristic colonies of P. takamizusanense were subcultured onto fresh PDA plates for purification. Pure cultures were maintained on PDA slants at 4 °C for experimental use. Reference cultures of all fungal strains were deposited in the PHL’s lab at Taichung District Agricultural Research and Extension Station Bioresource Collection, a reference strain TCTeb01 was deposited at Research Center (BCRC) in Taiwan under accession numbers BCR930208.

Insect source

We assessed the virulence of P. takamizusanense against three agricultural pests: H. hampei, T. palmi, and T. papillosa. Hypothenemus hampei adults were collected from infested Coffea arabica L. berries grown in sun plots in Nantou, Taiwan (23°94′40.9"N 120°95′76.8"E). Adults were obtained using a Mini Insect Breeder (BD7001, MegaView Science Co., Ltd., Taichang, Taiwan) covered with aluminum foil and a strip of kitchen paper in the middle36. Coffee berry borers were reared for more than 14 consecutive generations on an artificial diet37 in glass vials maintained in a growth chamber at 25 °C and 85% relative humidity under dark conditions. Female adults of T. palmi were sourced from laboratory colonies maintained at the World Vegetable Center. The thrips were reared at 25 °C, relative humidity 65–70%, and a photoperiod of 12:12 h (L:D) in growth chambers. Tessaratoma papillosa adults and nymphs were collected from longan orchards in Taiwan. To ensure experimental stability, insects were acclimated on longan branches under controlled conditions (25 °C, relative humidity 70%) for three days before experiments.

Inocula preparation

For all virulence bioassays, P. takamizusanense strain TCTeb01 was cultured on PDA at 25 °C for 14 days prior to experimentation. Strain TCTeb01 was selected as the representative isolate for all virulence assays because it has been adopted in our parallel pesticide-formulation development work. Conidial suspensions were prepared using 0.01% Tween 80 solution and adjusted to specific concentrations following previously inoculation protocols for each pest: 1 × 10⁸ conidia/mL for H. hampei38 and T. palmi26; 1 × 105, 1 × 10⁶, and 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL for adult T. papillosa concentration assays, and 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL for 3rd–4th instar T. papillosa nymph assays29. Concentration was verified using a hemocytometer. Germination rates for all fungal preparations were confirmed to exceed 90% before use in bioassays by observing spore germination on PDA. A single screening concentration was used for H. hampei, T. palmi, and T. papillosa nymphs to establish virulence, whereas three concentrations were tested on adult T. papillosa — the damaging life stage and our primary target— to characterize concentration dependence.

Virulence test

For bioassay of H. hampei, eight adults per replicate and three replicates were used with each experimental unit. Adults were sprayed with 300 μL of conidial suspension (1 × 108 conidia/mL) using micro spray bottles, allowed to air-dry for 15 min in a laminar flow hood, then transferred onto a Water agar (WA) medium and incubated in a growth chamber at 25 °C in the dark. Control insects were treated with 300 μL 0.01% Tween 80 solution. All boxes were maintained at 25 °C in the dark38. Dead insects were removed daily, surface-sterilized with 2% sodium hypochlorite, rinsed twice with sterile water, and placed in moist chambers to confirm mycosis. Survival rate was recorded daily for seven days.

For bioassay of T. palmi, four replicates were used with each experimental unit containing ten female adults (sexed under a stereomicroscope). The experiment was conducted in plastic cups (55 mm diameter) containing 25 mL of 3% water agar with a circular sterilized bean leaf disc (55 mm diameter) on the surface. Each replicate was sprayed with 1 mL of conidial suspension (1 × 108 conidia/mL) using micro spray bottles before introduction of thrips. Control groups were treated with 0.01% Tween 80 solution. All cups were covered with 250-mesh netting and lids, then maintained at 25 °C with daily monitoring. Dead insects were collected daily, surface-sterilized, and placed in moist chambers to confirm mycosis. Survival rate was recorded daily for seven days.

For the T. papillosa nymph bioassay, twelve 3rd–4th instar nymphs per replicate were used, with two control replicates (0.01% Tween 80) and eight inoculated replicates (1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL) using micro spray bottles; insects were sprayed in situ on potted longan branches (3 mL per replicate). For the T. papillosa adult concentration bioassay, three conidial concentrations (1 × 105, 1 × 10⁶, and 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL) were tested with twelve adults per replicate and three replicates per concentration; this experiment did not include an untreated control. The three concentrations span a tenfold logarithmic series bracketing the working range of Purpureocillium-based formulations (10⁷–10⁸ conidia/mL for the closest commercialized congener P. lilacinum) and extend one log below to map the lower tail of the time–mortality response.

All insects were maintained at 25 °C and 70% relative humidity. Dead insects were collected periodically, surface-sterilized, and placed in moist chambers to confirm mycosis. Survival was assessed at days 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42 post-inoculation.

Fungal morphological and molecular identification

Culture characters were examined on Czapek Dox Agar (CDA; HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., India), Malt Extract Agar (MEA; malt extract 20 g, dextrose 20 g, peptone 1 g, agar 20 g, distilled water 1 L), and PDA. The center-inoculated plates were incubated at 25℃ for 7 days. Morphological characters were examined and measured using a Nexcope NE620 compound microscope, and features were documented with a WXCAM microscopic digital camera (Wenxin Instument Co., Ltd., Taiwan). Spores, conidiophores, and conidiogenous apparatus were measured from specimens mounted in 10% potassium hydroxide mount, with at least 50 structures measured for each feature.

For molecular identification, total genomic DNA of strains TCP05, TCTeb01, and TP21-2 was extracted using Qiagen PowerPlant kit, and the whole genomes were sequenced and de novo assembled. Four loci targeted for phylogenetic analysis — internal transcribed spacer (ITS), large subunit ribosomal RNA (LSU), RNA polymerase II largest subunit (RPB1), and translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF) — were retrieved in silico from the assemblies by local BLAST against reference sequences, with hits parsed by a custom Python script; no PCR amplification of these loci was performed for these three strains. Additionally, the ITS, LSU, and TEF regions were amplified from genomic DNA of four other strains (TP17–TP20) using primer sets specific to each targeted region: 983F (GCYCCYGGHCAYCGTGAYTTYAT) and 2218R (ATGACACCRACRGCRACRGTYTG) for TEF (Rehner and Buckley, 2005), V9G (TACGTCCCTGCCCTTTGTA) and LR1 (GGTTGGTTTCTTTCCT) for ITS39,40, and LR5 (TCCTGAGGGAAACTTCG) and LROR (ACCCGCTGAACTTAAGC) for LSU40,41.

For phylogenetic analysis, sequences from related species were retrieved from GenBank (Supplementary Table 1). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT v7.490 with default parameters42. The aligned datasets were concatenated into a single dataset after trimming with ClipKIT v2.3.0 using smart-gaps mode to retain phylogenetically informative sites43. ModelTest-NG v.0.1.7 was used to select the best-fit model of DNA evolution using default parameters44. Maximum likelihood (ML) analyses were conducted using IQ-TREE v2.0.7 with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates45. Bayesian inference was performed using MrBayes v.3.2.7a46, with two runs of four chains each, sampling every 100 generations for 1 million generations. Simplicillium lamellicola (F.E.V. Sm.) Zare & W. Gams and Simplicillium lanosoniveum (J.F.H. Beyma) Zare & W. Gams were used as outgroups. Trees were visualized and edited using iTOL47.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed in R 4.148. For each replicate, the number of dead and surviving insects at each observation point was recovered from the cumulative mortality percentage and the replicate’s cohort size (eight H. hampei adults, ten T. palmi female adults, twelve T. papillosa nymphs, and twelve T. papillosa adults). Each individual was assigned a time of death equal to the observation point at which it was first scored dead, or right-censored at the final observation point if it was still alive; data manipulation and reshaping were performed with the tidyverse package49. For the three virulence bioassays (H. hampei, T. palmi, and T. papillosa nymph), cumulative survival of control and inoculated groups was compared using Kaplan–Meier estimators with survfit() and the log-rank test with survdiff() from the survival package50; curves were plotted using ggsurvplot() from the survminer package51. For the concentration-dependent bioassay on T. papillosa adults, a Cox proportional-hazards model was fitted with coxph(), using conidial concentration (1 × 105, 1 × 10⁶, 1 × 10⁷ conidia/mL) as a categorical fixed effect (1 × 105 as reference); hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals are reported, and pairwise differences between concentrations were assessed by log-rank tests using survdiff() with Bonferroni correction for the three pairs.

Median lethal time (LT₅₀) and 95% confidence intervals were estimated by time–mortality analysis with a three-parameter log-logistic model fitted using drm() with LL.3() from the drc package52, with one model fitted per cohort or per concentration so that observation points were not treated as independent dose levels.
