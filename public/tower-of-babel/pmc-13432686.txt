Growth Hormone Treatment Response and Machine Learning‐Based Prediction in Idiopathic GHD and ISS: Analysis of the Korean LG Growth Study
Authors: Jisun Park, Eun Young Joo, Su Jin Kim, Myung Ji Yoo, Ji Eun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveIndividual responses to recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) therapy vary widely among children with idiopathic growth hormone deficiency (iGHD) and idiopathic short stature (ISS), making accurate prediction of treatment outcomes clinically important. This study aimed to develop and compare machine learning (ML)‐based and conventional statistical models to predict short‐term growth response and mid‐parental height (MPH) attainment following rhGH therapy in iGHD and ISS patients.DesignRetrospective observational cohort study using a nationwide, real‐world registry.PatientsA total of 2215 children (1877 with iGHD and 338 with ISS) treated with rhGH were identified from the Korean LG Growth Study database. All included patients had at least 1 year of follow‐up with available clinical data.MeasurementsPrimary outcomes were 1‐ and 2‐year changes in height SDS (ΔHSDS) and achievement of MPH SDS. Predictive models included multiple linear regression, logistic regression, Random Forest, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, and Elastic Net. Model performance was evaluated using R², error metrics and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP values.ResultsIn the iGHD group, ensemble ML models modestly outperformed linear regression for predicting 1‐year ΔHSDS (R² ≈ 0.19 vs. 0.16), but predictive performance declined at 2 years across all models. Prediction of MPH attainment showed high specificity but very low sensitivity at 1 year, with no clear advantage of ML over logistic regression. In ISS patients, all models demonstrated poor predictive performance for both ΔHSDS and MPH attainment, reflecting substantial clinical heterogeneity.ConclusionsML approaches provided limited but clinically meaningful improvements in predicting short‐term growth response in iGHD, while offering no clear benefit in ISS. These findings highlight both the potential and limitations of ML models based solely on routine clinical variables and underscore the need for integrating multimodal data to improve growth prediction, particularly in heterogeneous ISS populations.

Introduction

Short stature is a significant concern in paediatric endocrinology, affecting children's physical and psychological well‐being. Among its various etiologies, growth hormone deficiency (GHD) and idiopathic short stature (ISS) are common conditions that require careful diagnosis and management [1]. Recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) therapy has been well‐established as an effective treatment for these conditions, thereby promoting linear growth and improving adult height outcomes [2]. However, individual variability in response to rhGH treatment is considerable, making accurate treatment efficacy prediction crucial for personalised clinical decision‐making and optimising therapeutic strategies [2, 3, 4]. Importantly, ISS, by definition, lacks an identifiable cause and represents a heterogeneous condition, making its treatment response more challenging to predict than that of iGHD [5, 6]. Various factors affect growth in patients with ISS, leading to a generally smaller growth response to rhGH therapy compared with their GHD counterparts [5, 7]. Considering these complexities and the significant variability in treatment outcomes, a more meticulous and multidimensional prediction of rhGH treatment response, encompassing growth velocity and likelihood of achieving target height, holds immense clinical value for both patients with GHD and ISS. Such advanced predictive capabilities would enable clinicians to better tailor treatment plans, provide more accurate patient counselling, and improve overall therapeutic efficacy.

In recent years, the application of advanced computational methods, particularly machine learning (ML) appeared as a promising approach to develop more robust and precise prediction models in various medical fields, including endocrinology [8]. ML models integrate diverse clinical and biochemical parameters to identify complex patterns and predictors of rhGH therapy response that might be overlooked by conventional statistical methods [3]. Developing such models have demonstrated potential in predicting future growth in paediatric patients receiving rhGH [2, 9, 10].

Accordingly, this study aims to apply ML techniques, which allow integrated, multidimensional analyses, to develop, validate and compare advanced prediction models for short‐term rhGH treatment response in both iGHD and ISS using the large and real‐world Korean cohort, the LG Growth Study (LGS) database. This study aims to move beyond the limitations of conventional statistical approaches and contribute to the development of a more clinically useful predictive tool through the application of ML analyses to improve the prediction of rhGH treatment response in both iGHD and ISS. Ultimately, such advancements may support clinicians in making more precise, individualised treatment decisions and help minimise the administration of unnecessary or suboptimal long‐term rhGH therapy.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective observational analysis utilised data from the LGS, a multicenter, long‐term observational cohort study in South Korea. The LGS was designed to assess the long‐term effectiveness and safety of rhGH therapy (specifically LG Chem rhGH products) in children diagnosed with various growth disorders, including GHD and ISS [11, 12]. Patients with iGHD or ISS who had initiated rhGH and completed at least 1 year of follow‐up with available height standard deviation scores (SDS) data from the 4229 patients diagnosed with GHD or ISS were included in this study. To ensure a homogeneous population suitable for predictive modelling, patients with organic GHD, chromosomal or genetic abnormalities, chronic systemic illnesses affecting growth, or insufficient baseline information (age, sex, mid‐parental height [MPH] SDS, bone age [BA], insulin‐like growth factor‐1 [IGF‐1] SDS, and IGF‐binding protein‐3 [IGFBP‐3] SDS, GH peak value, rhGH dose or injection frequency) were excluded from this study. The Lambda‐Mu‐Sigma (LMS) method based on the L, M and S parameters from the 2017 Korean National Growth Charts for children and adolescents, were used to calculate auxological measurement SDS [13]. After applying these criteria, the final analysis included 1877 patients with iGHD and 338 with ISS (Figure 1). The study protocol was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of Inha University Hospital and determined to qualify for exemption from ethical review (IRB No. 2025‐03‐010), as the study used previously collected, de‐identified registry data.

Flow diagram of patient selection from the Korean LG Growth Study Database. Among 4229 children diagnosed with growth hormone deficiency (GHD) or idiopathic short stature (ISS), patients with insufficient follow‐up (< 1 year), organic GHD, genetic abnormalities, systemic illness or incomplete baseline data were excluded from the study. The final analysis set included 2215 patients (1877 with idiopathic GHD and 338 with ISS).

Clinical Assessments and Baseline Variables

Baseline assessments were conducted at the screening visit and included age, sex, BA, height, weight, height SDS, weight SDS and MPH SDS. Parental heights based on Korean reference standards were used to calculate MPH SDS [13]. The Greulich–Pyle method was employed to assess BA. Endocrine evaluation included GH peak levels from at least two stimulation tests, IGF‐1 SDS and IGFBP‐3 SDS. All biochemical analyses were conducted at individual institutions using local standard procedures, consistent with the observational nature of the LGS registry. Treatment‐related variables were the initial rhGH dose (mg/kg/week) and mean rhGH dose during each annual interval (Table 1).

Outcome Measures

To compare multiple strategies for growth prediction, a range of predictive models was developed for each cohort. Multiple linear regression (MLR), random forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Elastic Net regression models were applied for the continuous outcome of change in height SDS (ΔHSDS). Logistic regression, RF, XGBoost and Elastic Net classification models were implemented for the binary outcome evaluating achievement of MPH SDS at each year of treatment, defined as a 1‐ or 2‐year height SDS greater than or equal to the patient's MPH SDS. Model interpretability for nonlinear ML models was ensured by calculating SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values, which quantify the contribution of each predictor to the model output and provide transparent insight into variable importance. Feature selection was performed using Lasso regularization to reduce multicollinearity and identify stable and clinically meaningful predictors in linear models.

Statistical Analysis

The t‐test or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test according to the normality, was used to evaluate baseline differences between the iGHD and ISS groups. The paired t‐test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test according to the normality, was used to analyse the within‐group changes in height SDS. The coefficient of determination (R²), root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) for regression analyses were conducted to evaluate model performance. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and overall accuracy were used to assess classification models. Python (scikit‐learn) and R (version 4.5.2, fastshap, shapviz) were used for all analyses.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Overall Growth Response

The analysis set included 2215 patients from the LGS, comprising 1877 patients with iGHD and 338 with ISS. Figure 1 summarise the participant flow. Among them, 1919 patients (iGHD: n = 1610; ISS: n = 309) had 1‐year follow‐up data, and 1323 (iGHD: n = 1164; ISS: n = 159) had 2‐year follow‐up data. Table 1 summarises the baseline characteristics of the two cohorts. At screening, mean height SDS did not significantly differ between the iGHD and ISS groups (median, −2.36 vs. −2.38, respectively; p = 0.4871). Conversely, the ISS group demonstrated a substantially higher GH peak level than the iGHD group, consistent with the diagnostic definitions (14.91 vs. 6.57 ng/mL; p < 0.0001). Both groups demonstrated significant catch‐up growth after initiation of GH therapy. In the iGHD group, mean ΔHSDS was 0.76 at 1 year and 1.18 at 2 years in the iGHD group and 0.74 and 1.19 in the ISS group, respectively (all p < 0.0001 vs. baseline by Wilcoxon signed‐rank test). The 1st‐year ΔHSDS was slightly greater in the iGHD group than in the ISS group (p = 0.0250), whereas the cumulative ΔHSDS was virtually identical between groups by 2 years (p = 0.7105) (Table 2).

Model‐Based Prediction of Change in HSDS and MPH Achievement

We first assessed the ability of baseline clinical variables to predict short‐term growth response as ΔHSDS using MLR and three ML models (RF, XGBoost and Elastic Net), which were analysed separately for iGHD and ISS. RF and XGBoost demonstrated the highest R² values (≈0.19) in the iGHD group, exceeding those of traditional linear models (R² ≈ 0.16), with comparable error metrics across methods (RMSE ≈ 0.29; MAE ≈ 0.23) in the prediction of 1‐year ΔHSDS. By contrast, prediction of 2‐year ΔHSDS showed universally low performance (R² ≤ 0.17) (Table 3). All models exhibited notably poorer performance in the ISS than in iGHD group. Explanatory power was minimal across approaches for 1‐year ΔHSDS (R² ≈ 0.00–0.01). Even at 2 years, R² values remained low (≤ 0.08) (Table 3). The analysis of MPH SDS attainment at each year of treatment, revealed that logistic regression demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.65, and the other ML models exhibited comparable or slightly lower performance (RF 0.61, XGBoost 0.64), except for Elastic Net, which yielded a marginally higher AUC of 0.68) (File S1). Despite small differences in AUC, all models demonstrated extremely low sensitivity (0.04–0.05) with near‐perfect specificity, indicating that none of the approaches could accurately identify patients who eventually achieved the MPH SDS when applying the standard probability threshold. Logistic regression again provided the highest AUC (0.70) by 2 years, with RF (0.67), Elastic Net (0.69) and XGBoost (0.64) demonstrating similar but not superior discriminative performance. Sensitivity and specificity became more balanced across all models at 2 years, in contrast to the 1‐year results. Overall, ML approaches did not demonstrate a clear improvement over logistic regression in predicting MPH SDS attainment in the iGHD group (File S1). Logistic regression demonstrated an AUC of 0.64 at 1‐year in the ISS group, which was slightly higher than RF (0.55) and XGBoost (0.56) and, comparable to Elastic Net (0.63). Sensitivity remained very low across all models (0.04–0.23), whereas specificity was consistently high, indicating limited clinical utility at this time point. At 2 years, the AUC of logistic regression (0.64) was nearly identical to that of RF (0.64) and XGBoost (0.65), with Elastic Net demonstrating only a marginally higher value (0.66). Further, sensitivity and specificity became more evenly distributed at 2 years (File S1); however, no ML model significantly surpassed logistic regression. These findings indicate that, for ISS, ML techniques did not provide additional predictive value beyond the conventional logistic model.

Key Predictors and Model Interpretation

Model interpretation was performed across all four predictive models (MLR, RF, XGBoost and Elastic Net). SHAP analyses were conducted using the models to investigate the determinants of height SDS changes at 1 and 2 years. For the 1‐year response, baseline height, age at GH initiation, GH dose and weight SDS emerged as the most influential predictors in the RF model, which demonstrated the best predictive performance (Figure 2A). The RF analysis revealed that higher baseline height, younger treatment initiation age, higher GH dose and greater weight SDS were associated with more favourable increases in height SDS during the first year, although this reflects the model's internal decision process rather than underlying biological effects. The predictive performance of RF declined for the 2‐year response; however, SHAP analysis similarly identified baseline height, age and weight SDS as key contributors. ML models did not outperform MLR in the ISS group, whereas SHAP plots indicated that baseline height and baseline weight SDS modestly contributed to model predictions; however, these effects were small and unstable, consistent with the very low predictive performance in ISS (Figure 2B).

Discussion

This is the first study to systematically compare ML methods for predicting rhGH treatment response in patients with iGHD and ISS patients using a large real‐world cohort. Most studies evaluating rhGH therapy response in patients with iGHD and ISS have relied primarily on traditional statistical approaches, including linear regression and logistic regression; thus, this study aimed to investigate the multidimensional, complex and nonlinear relationships among predictive variables employing ML methods [11, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Considering that most previous research has primarily relied on conventional statistical models, such as linear and logistic regression [17], this study aimed to explore the multidimensional, complex and potentially nonlinear relationships among predictive variables using advanced ML techniques.

Ensemble‐based ML models in the iGHD group, namely RF and XGBoost, demonstrated modest but meaningful improvements in predicting 1‐year ΔHSDS compared with MLR (R² = 0.19 vs. 0.16). However, even the improved R² values remained modest, and model performance declined at 2 years across all methods. This pattern has been well described in previous studies and reflects the natural waning of rhGH treatment effects over time [18, 19]. Pubertal changes and other time‐dependent factors likely contributed to reduced predictability, which are factors not fully captured by baseline variables alone [19]. These findings indicate that ML models provide some benefit over linear regression in iGHD, whereas growth prediction remains inherently complex and cannot be fully explained by standard clinical variables. Meanwhile, when predictive performance for MPH SDS attainment was evaluated, sensitivity at 1 year was very low, whereas specificity was nearly perfect. Clinically, this indicates that the models performed well in identifying children unlikely to reach MPH SDS but were less capable of identifying future responders, thereby limiting their utility for positive prediction. This indicates that the models are effective screening tools to rule out unlikely responders, potentially guiding clinicians toward earlier intervention for those with predicted insufficient response, similar to how high specificity is valued in diagnostic tests to minimise false positives [20]. In this context, SHAP analysis improved interpretability by highlighting baseline height SDS, age at treatment initiation, rhGH dose and weight SDS as primary contributors to model predictions in iGHD. Specifically, based on the SHAP analysis of the ensemble models, we can identify distinct clinical phenotypes associated with rhGH treatment responsiveness. For instance, a ‘high‐responder profile’ in iGHD is characterised by patients who initiate therapy at a younger age, have a lower baseline height SDS (greater growth deficit), and a higher baseline weight SDS. Conversely, a ‘low‐responder profile’ typically includes older children with relatively preserved baseline height and lower weight SDS. The negative association between baseline height and ΔHSDS suggests a ‘catch‐up’ phenomenon where those with the most significant height deficit gain the most benefit. The positive contribution of weight SDS may reflect better nutritional status or metabolic readiness for growth. These insights allow clinicians to move beyond ‘black‐box’ predictions and categorise patients into response‐likelihood groups during initial counseling. While the incremental gain in predictive performance might appear modest, its clinical utility in daily practice should not be underestimated. Traditional linear models often fail to capture the complex, non‐linear interactions between auxological parameters and biochemical markers that characterise the growth response. In the context of shared decision‐making, even a small improvement in prediction accuracy can help clinicians set more realistic treatment expectations with patients and families. By offering a more accurate prediction of the first‐year growth response, clinicians can manage parental expectations more effectively, which is crucial for treatment adherence and long‐term success [15]. Furthermore, our ML‐based approach demonstrated near‐perfect specificity in identifying poor responders. In clinical settings, this capability acts as a high‐precision screening tool to ‘rule out’ patients who are unlikely to benefit significantly from expensive rhGH therapy, thereby optimising resource allocation and preventing the psychological burden of failed treatment [21, 22]. This shift from a ‘trial‐and‐error’ approach to a model‐informed strategy represents a significant step toward personalised medicine in paediatric endocrinology.

Conversely, in the ISS group, ML models failed to demonstrate superior predictive performance over traditional statistical approaches for 1‐ and 2‐year treatment responses (R² = 0.00 vs. 0.01 at 1‐ and 2‐year treatment responses). Importantly, the clear difference in predictability between the iGHD and ISS groups indicates that ISS represents a more heterogeneous clinical category. This is consistent with recent studies revealing that ISS comprises a broad spectrum of underlying conditions, including genetic growth disorders, partial GH insensitivity, and constitutional short stature [7, 15]. The heterogeneity of ISS is a major challenge to reliable growth prediction, as multiple genetic, epigenetic, endocrine and environmental factors interact in complex, frequently nonlinear ways to affect growth trajectories [14, 15, 23, 24]. For instance, advances in genetic studies reveal that variants in genes including NPR2 (2%–15% of ISS), SHOX (2%–15% of ISS) and ACAN (2%–15% of ISS) cause diverse growth phenotypes within ISS, thereby reflecting variable GH sensitivity and signalling abnormalities [6, 25, 26]. These variants influence bone plate physiology and GH sensitivity in ways that are not reflected in baseline IGF‐1 levels or auxological measurements [27]. Unfortunately, The current clinical parameters used in our models‐primarily auxological measures and basic biochemical markers‐cannot capture this molecular heterogeneity. To advance precision medicine for ISS, future studies must integrate multi‐omics data, including genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic and metabolomic profiles, along with advanced imaging biomarkers and environmental factors. Therefore, the ‘failure’ of our clinical‐based ML models in ISS highlights the urgent necessity for precision medicine approaches that integrate novel biomarkers and multi‐omics data. Future research should incorporate genomic, transcriptomic and proteomic profiles to subtype ISS patients more accurately. Integrating these molecular layers with ML models will likely be essential to uncover the complex, non‐linear biological drivers of growth in this population and to identify those truly eligible for rhGH therapy [24, 28].

Especially, the inherent characteristics of the LGS data registry, a large multicenter, real‐world observational cohort, need to be acknowledged as a potentially significant factor affecting our models’ predictive capabilities. Data collected across multiple institutions, rather than from a centralised laboratory, can introduce variability in measurements of key biochemical markers including IGF‐1 and IGFBP‐3 [29]. Such a lack of standardization or quantification in real‐world data inherently presents challenges for ML models, as their performance is highly sensitive to data quality, completeness and consistency [30]. Furthermore, efforts were made to handle missing data, whereas the presence of intermittent data points, which is common in real‐world registries [31], requires imputation strategies that, despite their sophistication, can sometimes yield biased results or reduce statistical power, thereby affecting the accuracy and reliability of predictive models [32, 33]. This variability and potential for noise within the dataset may have limited the ability of even advanced ML algorithms to fully capture the underlying patterns, thereby contributing to the modest R² values observed in iGHD and the particularly low predictive power in the heterogeneous ISS group [30, 34]. Had the dataset been subjected to more rigorous standardization and completeness, it is plausible that the ML models could have achieved higher R² values and demonstrated a more pronounced improvement in predictive performance.

In conclusion, this study provides the first systematic comparison of ML models for predicting rhGH treatment response in a large real‐world cohort of patients with iGHD and ISS, demonstrating modest but clinically meaningful improvements in iGHD and clear predictive limitations in the highly heterogeneous ISS group. These results highlight both the promise and the boundaries of ML approaches when they depend solely on routine clinical variables. The interpretability gained through SHAP analysis further highlights the potential of ML to inform individualised treatment decisions in iGHD, even as overall R² values represent the inherent complexity of growth biology. Importantly, our results indicate the need to incorporate genomic, imaging‐based and metabolic biomarkers to advance prediction in ISS, where current models remain insufficient. We hope that our study will provide a foundation for integrating multimodal data and conducting broad external validation, thereby advancing the development of more precise and personalised rhGH treatment strategies in paediatric endocrinology.
