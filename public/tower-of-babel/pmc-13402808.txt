Development of an energy router for DC nanogrid-based building applications
Authors: Geonhee Lee, Seungho Choi, Sangyoung Park, Junhee Hong, Jehyuk Won
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent energy router (ER) research has primarily focused on high-voltage DC systems and regional microgrid clusters, enabling energy exchange among large-scale photovoltaics (PV), energy storage systems (ESS), and grid infrastructures; however, compact low-voltage (LV) ERs for in-building DC distribution remain underexplored despite the need for fine-grained power control and integration of localized resources. This paper proposes a modular multi-port ER tailored for building-level DC nanogrids, where distributed PV, ESS, and diverse DC loads are coordinated under real-time control within installation-space constraints. The proposed ER employs a USB-C power delivery (PD) interface, enabling simultaneous transmission of over 200 W power and 10 Gbps data through a single cable, while achieving over 98% conversion efficiency and supporting autonomous bidirectional power flow with integrated protection and battery management. A 50 kW nanogrid living lab comprising 255 nodes, including 51 hardware ER units and 204 software-defined nodes, was developed for validation. Experimental results under realistic conditions—including renewable intermittency, islanded operation, dynamic load variation, and plug-and-play events—demonstrate stable operation and effective energy coordination, highlighting the feasibility of LVDC ER-based nanogrids as a scalable solution for future building energy systems.

Introduction

Modern power systems face the critical challenge of simultaneously achieving sustainable energy transitions and stable electricity supply. The conventional centralized power grid, which delivers electricity from large-scale power plants to consumers through long-distance transmission lines, inherently suffers from issues such as transmission losses, vulnerability to large-scale blackouts due to natural disasters or aging infrastructure, and increased carbon emissions from fossil fuel-based generation. As an alternative, distributed generation, which produces and consumes electricity near the point of use, has gained global attention. In particular, small-scale autonomous power networks utilizing renewable energy sources like photovoltaics (PV) and wind power, along with energy storage systems (ESS), are being actively explored1–3.

In such DC-based distributed energy systems, the energy router (ER) is emerging as a key component. The ER enables real-time control of power flows among various energy sources (PV, ESS, AC grid) and loads, functioning similarly to a data router in information networks by intelligently routing energy to destinations4–7. Existing ERs fall into two broad classes: (i) high-voltage, high-power solid-state-transformer (SST)–based ERs and (ii) low-voltage (LV) multi-port-converter (MPC)–based ERs suited to building-scale DC environments8. SST-based ERs target substation-level applications, such as medium-/high-voltage interfaces at 6–35 kV distribution feeders or ≥ 69 kV transmission nodes, but they are generally costly and difficult to scale for multi-port use. In contrast, MPC-based ERs employ partial-power processing at small voltage differentials to achieve high efficiency and structural simplicity, and they enable flexible power routing among multiple ports9.

This concept of ER was initially introduced in the future renewable electric energy delivery and management system (FREEDM)10 and has evolved into the energy internet concept, incorporating plug-and-play (PnP) distributed renewable energy sources, distributed ESSs, intelligent energy management, and distributed grid intelligence nodes11. Chen et al. further explored a reconfigurable multi-input multi-output (MIMO) ER architecture, which allows dynamic port reconfiguration through a cell-switch network, enabling compact and flexible implementations in high-density applications12. More recently, with the rise of DC microgrid clusters and energy local area networks (E-LAN), there is increasing demand for peer-to-peer (P2P) energy exchange and energy path conflict management across communities. Jiang et al. introduced an ER system designed for P2P energy trading with built-in path conflict resolution algorithms and distributed decision-making, transforming ERs into cyber-physical systems with communication, control, and optimization capabilities13. These advancements suggest that ERs must support advanced control capabilities, including user demand response and real-time energy path optimization, requiring ERs to include not only power electronics but also network communication, data-driven optimization, and cybersecurity features.

Despite this progress, most current research focuses on inter-community microgrids or regional clusters. There is limited investigation into building-centric ERs capable of fine-grained control of internal power flow. In reality, building-scale systems face distinct technical requirements such as space constraints, load diversity, building energy management systems (BEMS) integration, and real-time responsiveness. In this context, demand-side management strategies have been explored in building-scale DC microgrids to improve the utilization of locally generated renewable energy. By coordinating deferrable loads with generation profiles, such approaches can reduce battery cycling and enhance overall system efficiency. For example, while Chen et al.’s approach12 is highly flexible, it is not specifically designed for in-building deployment and still requires further exploration in terms of modularization, ease of installation, and integration with indoor loads.

Recent energy router (ER) research has predominantly focused on medium- to high-voltage DC links in the 400–800 V range, emphasizing grid-tied microgrid integration, including coordination of photovoltaic generation, energy storage systems, and high-voltage DC power routing14. Building upon this direction, many studies have addressed hierarchical and grid-forming control as well as stability of grid-connected DC ERs at the microgrid level15. While effective for regional energy management, such approaches leave a gap in compact solutions for low-voltage DC (LVDC) distribution within buildings. In practical building environments, most DC loads operate at 24 V and 48 V levels16. This motivates building-edge, low-voltage multi-port energy routers (LV-MERs), which represent a generalized concept for distributed energy exchange among multiple low-voltage ports.

However, existing LV-MER concepts remain largely at a structural level and do not sufficiently address decentralized operation without a supervisory controller or practical implementation constraints such as compactness, plug-and-play capability, and integration with real building loads.

To address these limitations, this work extends the LV-MER concept into a practically realizable system, referred to as a low-voltage DC energy router (LVDCER). The proposed LVDCER integrates port-level power conversion with decentralized neighbor-state-based control and is specifically designed for building-scale LVDC nanogrid applications. This study focuses on enabling stable bidirectional power exchange under fully decentralized operation and identifying hardware design requirements for compact and deployable implementations. It is hypothesized that a modular LVDCER architecture can support scalable nanogrid operation while maintaining stable energy sharing under practical conditions. This hypothesis is validated through both converter-level evaluation and system-level experimental implementation.

Accordingly, the design objectives include: (i) development of a compact LVDCER for 24 V and 48 V endpoints, (ii) decentralized bidirectional multi-port power transfer, (iii) high conversion efficiency to reduce cumulative losses, and (iv) practical deployment features such as hot-swap capability and integrated protection. The proposed architecture is experimentally validated in a building-scale DC nanogrid environment.

Through this approach, nanogrids evolve beyond small-scale power systems into intelligent energy management platforms, offering substantial potential to enhance grid efficiency and sustainability. A 50 kW living-lab nanogrid comprising 255 nodes was constructed to assess feasibility and commercialization. Of these, 51 nodes were implemented as ER hardware units in the living lab, and 204 nodes were software-defined on a web server; all nodes were interconnected over Wi-Fi. System efficiency and stability were validated across diverse operating scenarios. The proposed system is applicable to smart buildings, microgrids, and renewable energy integration scenarios, with significant potential for improving energy efficiency in large-scale electricity consumption facilities.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II presents the operating methodology and development of the proposed ER, including a brief architectural review, key features, hardware design, and meshed implementation. Section III outlines the DC nanogrid living lab configuration. Section IV reports experimental results—module efficiency, hot-swap performance with bidirectional transfer in a two-ER setup, and building-scale validation. Section V concludes and highlights future work.

Before presenting the architecture of the proposed ER, it is essential to review representative structures introduced in recent literature. As shown in Fig. 1, various ER topologies have been developed for different application scales—from high-voltage DC grid clusters to modular configurations for P2P energy trading. These architectures highlight the functional diversity and evolution of ERs in supporting distributed energy systems, encompassing roles such as bidirectional power routing, real-time control, communication integration, and system-level scalability. Figure 1(a)–(e) illustrates typical ER configurations that have been explored in previous works, each addressing specific voltage levels, power interfaces, and control frameworks. This comparative overview provides the technical context and limitations that motivate the development of a building-level ER for LVDC nanogrids, which remains underexplored despite its practical relevance in modern energy infrastructures.

The architecture shown in Fig. 1(a) introduces a LV-MER architecture that interconnects multiple LVDC communities within a DC cluster. The LV-MER orchestrates power exchange across DC buses and energy storage through coordinated port converters and a supervisory communication layer, enabling imports and exports between communities and operation in both islanded and grid-tied modes [8]. As shown in Fig. 1(b), a reconfigurable MIMO ER architecture adopts modular cells that can dynamically reassign input and output ports through a cell switch network. This configuration enhances flexibility and scalability in dense energy systems by supporting various voltage sources and loads [12]. Figure 1(c) introduces a cyber-physical ER designed for P2P trading within an E-LAN. Each node autonomously manages energy routing and conflict resolution, using embedded algorithms to ensure stable power quality and real-time path optimization [13]. A virtual mesh topology of ERs is depicted in Fig. 1(d). Here, SSTs and three-port power hubs are arranged in parallel to support fault-tolerant and distributed energy management. Each node operates independently, enabling resilient energy packet routing across the mesh network [17]. Finally, in Fig. 1(e), a three-port configuration enables bidirectional energy transfer among PV, AC grid, and battery storage. The system employs a dual active bridge (DAB) for each energy path and integrates maximum power point tracking (MPPT) and state-of-charge (SoC)-based controllers to optimize power flow in real time, ensuring high efficiency and responsiveness [18].

While existing ER architectures primarily focus on structural design and power routing capabilities, building-scale DC nanogrids require coordinated operation between distributed resources and loads. In this regard, demand-side management strategies have been explored to improve renewable energy utilization and reduce unnecessary battery cycling [19].

Benchmarking of energy router performance aspects

To clarify the positioning of the proposed LVDC energy router, representative studies were examined from complementary perspectives related to system reliability, dynamic operation, and architectural extensibility, as summarized in Table 1.

A reliability-focused distribution network structure employing electric energy routers has been explored in [20], where multi-port routing and redundant power modules are utilized to enhance supply continuity in hybrid AC/DC systems. While this approach provides valuable insights into fault-resilient network configuration and reliability requirements at the distribution level, its primary focus remains on structural reliability analysis rather than coordinated control strategies or building-scale LVDC deployment scenarios.

From an operational perspective, coordinated control of LV-MERs has been demonstrated in [21], including seamless transitions between grid-connected and islanded modes as well as battery-integrated energy management through both simulation and experimental validation. This work offers a meaningful reference for dynamic operation in ER-based systems; however, its emphasis is mainly on operational coordination and mode transition, without explicitly addressing structural scalability in highly distributed building environments.

In parallel, architectural enhancements for LV energy routers have been investigated in [22], where a series–parallel topology and flexible power flow control strategy are introduced to expand the operating range of active and reactive power and enable multiple operating modes. Although this approach improves operational flexibility and extends the feasible operating region of the router, it primarily focuses on topology-level improvements and power flow capability, rather than practical validation of large-scale distributed deployment.

These observations indicate that existing studies tend to address reliability, dynamic operation, and structural extensibility in a fragmented manner, with each aspect considered independently depending on the research focus. In contrast, the proposed LVDC energy router is developed from a building-oriented perspective to integrate these key aspects within a unified framework, simultaneously considering robust operation under practical disturbances, coordinated dynamic response, and scalable system architecture. Based on this integrated design approach, the following section presents the overall system configuration and operating methodology for building-level DC nanogrid applications.

Features of the proposed ER

Fig. 2Proposed ER architecture for DC nanogrid integration in buildings.

Proposed ER architecture for DC nanogrid integration in buildings.

Figure 2 illustrates the proposed ER architecture for DC nanogrid-based building applications. This architecture aims to overcome the structural limitations of traditional power systems and enable efficient integration of distributed energy resources (DERs). The system introduces the concept of a microgrid SST, replacing traditional medium-voltage AC (MVAC) distribution transformers, to deliver a 400 V (or 800 V, or bipolar) DC bus to several buildings. This 400 V DC bus runs vertically through all floors of the building, and at each floor, a nanogrid SST converts it to a lower voltage DC (LVDC) bus—typically 24–48 V—for intra-floor energy distribution.

At the core of this framework is the low-voltage DC energy router (LVDCER), which regulates and supplies power to various DC loads at the room or office level. The LVDCER integrates high-efficiency DC–DC converters to regulate voltage levels from diverse energy sources and loads into a unified network. Designed as a MPC, each LVDCER typically features two input and two output ports, with the number of ports scalable depending on the network topology. As shown on the third floor in Fig. 2, multiple LVDCER units are interconnected in a mesh configuration, enhancing the system’s flexibility and scalability. To ensure sufficient energy availability, the mesh is integrated with distributed ESSs positioned at intermediate nodes, enabling autonomous balancing of supply and demand across the network. Additionally, rooftop PV panels can supplement the 400 V DC bus or directly support the 24 V bus via dedicated DC–DC converters, ensuring reliable utilization of renewable energy. This forms the foundation for distributed energy optimization within the building.

While the proposed architecture focuses on building-level LVDC energy routing, it is also aligned with the broader characteristics of DC microgrid systems, which have been identified as particularly suitable for applications such as smart homes, commercial buildings, and data centers. In such systems, the use of a regulated DC bus and the direct integration of DC-native loads and distributed energy resources can reduce unnecessary conversion stages and facilitate more efficient and coordinated energy utilization [23].

As a result, the LVDCER plays a critical role in overcoming conventional power grid limitations—such as unidirectional energy flow, integration difficulties of DERs, and limited real-time control capabilities—by enabling direct connection of diverse DERs (e.g., PV, batteries) and building loads (e.g., residential, commercial, EV chargers). Real-time bidirectional energy flow monitoring and control significantly improve network-wide efficiency and resilience. This architecture aligns with the vision of the energy internet, where energy is routed and managed much like data in communication networks [24]. Analogous to how data routers determine optimal paths for data packets, ERs dynamically detect and control energy flows in real time, coordinating multiple sources to ensure optimal energy distribution. As such, the LVDCER is expected to become a key enabler of future intelligent power infrastructures, effectively realizing this router-like coordination at the building edge such as 24 V and 48 V LVDC distribution [25].

Conventional ERs typically support PnP functionality and real-time power flow control, allowing seamless integration or removal of DERs or loads without disrupting ongoing operations. These ERs include a microcontroller unit (MCU); precision current and voltage sensors; and both wired and wireless communication modules, enabling port-level monitoring and autonomous P2P energy trading among nodes. The decentralized and autonomous control architecture mitigates single points of failure and enhances the system’s scalability and fault tolerance. Hardware-wise, ERs comprise high-efficiency DC–DC converters, bidirectional battery chargers, wireless communication module, and MCU. Software-wise, control schemes such as droop control are employed for optimal energy path selection and dynamic reconfiguration. However, such general ER functions may fall short in addressing the specific requirements of building-scale nanogrids. Buildings present unique physical and operational constraints, such as space limitations, dense module deployment, various loads, high PnP frequency, and dense network topologies. Therefore, specialized LVDCER designs are necessary for DC-based building application. This study defines and implements the following design objectives to fulfill these constraints:

Design of the proposed ER

In this study, a non-isolated bidirectional four-switch buck–boost converter is adopted as the core power conversion unit of the proposed LVDCER optimized for DC nanogrid applications in building environments. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the converter comprises two cascaded bidirectional stages—boost and buck—sharing a central inductor and utilizing four power switches (S₁–S₄) in total. The topology consists of two half-bridge modules: the input-side half-bridge (S₁ and S₂) and the output-side half-bridge (S₃ and S₄), interconnected through an inductor L. This configuration enables seamless bidirectional energy transfer, supporting both step-up and step-down operations within a single hardware platform, making it well-suited for dynamic voltage conditions in DC nanogrid systems. Compared to isolated topologies, the non-isolated configuration offers several practical advantages in LV environments. First, by eliminating high-frequency isolation transformers, the design reduces overall system size, cost, and power loss. Such ERs offer improved power density and modular integration, which are critical under space and wiring constraints typically observed in building interiors. Second, under 48 V operation, building-scale DC systems may comply with safety regulations without mandatory galvanic isolation. Third, this bidirectional topology enables each port to seamlessly switch between acting as a source and a sink, allowing two-way power transfer over a single line. It eliminates the need for separate power lines or relays, thereby simplifying wiring complexity and improving scalability in dense nanogrid environments. In summary, the proposed ER circuit topology is carefully tailored to meet the unique requirements of building-integrated DC nanogrids. It enables efficient bidirectional power control, compact integration, and high-performance operation under practical load scenarios.

Fig. 3Topology of proposed ER: Four-switch buck-boost.

Topology of proposed ER: Four-switch buck-boost.

Using a four-switch buck-boost topology as shown in Fig. 3, the proposed ER for DC nanogrid applications was designed as illustrated in Fig. 4. The switching devices employed in this design are BSC0802LS MOSFETs, chosen for their low RDS(on) fast switching capability, and solid thermal performance; regulation and synchronous gate driving are provided by a Texas Instruments LM34936 controller. These devices support synchronous rectification, thereby minimizing conduction losses during bidirectional operation. Overall efficiency and thermal balance were key considerations in component selection and layout.

To overcome the limitations of conventional standards, a custom-designed USB-C power delivery (PD) protocol was implemented for the prototype. The USB-C connector was selected as the primary interface due to its compact form factor, ability to support high power transfer, and compatibility with high-speed data communication—features well aligned with the goals of this research. The implemented USB-C PD interface enables precise power control up to 240 W under 48 V and 5 A operating conditions, exceeding the conventional USB-C PD specification of 100 W; 20 V and 5 A. To realize this, the custom PD communication protocol was defined and embedded into the firmware. For data communication, the system adheres to the USB 3.1 Gen 2 standard, supporting up to 10 Gbps. Through battery charge–discharge testing, the system demonstrated stable bidirectional power transmission over a single USB-C cable.

To ensure protection against external surges and electrostatic discharge during PnP operations, each ER port was equipped with a protection circuit combining a radio frequency interference line filter and a transient voltage suppressor diode. Additionally, a hot-swap controller IC was integrated to safeguard the system from surge and inrush currents. This controller regulates the voltage and current slew rate and promptly disconnects the circuit in the event of overcurrent conditions. To align with the distributed and decentralized control architecture of the ER, each ER unit was designed to operate autonomously without a central controller. Each unit is equipped with a high-performance 32-bit MCU STM32F407ZGT6 (ARM Cortex-M4 core) from STMicroelectronics, which independently manages power conversion control, communication protocol handling, and protection functionalities. Furthermore, a soft-start algorithm was implemented via the MCU to limit inrush current during node connection.

This design supports a wide input voltage range, 12–52 V and provides a rated output of 25.2 V with up to 10 A of current, enabling stable power transfer exceeding 200 W per line. Each unit is also equipped with a Wi-Fi module, namely an ESP8266, allowing real-time monitoring of node parameters—such as voltage, current, power, temperature, and SoC—and transmission to a central server.

To maximize PnP capability and system scalability, the ER system adopts a modular slot-based architecture. The core idea is to use an MCU-equipped mainboard that allows ER units to be flexibly added or removed in a plug-in fashion. The mainboard MCU oversees the overall power flow during PnP operations and mediates communication between nodes. Each node can support up to n-ER units, which are directly connected to adjacent nodes in a 1:1 configuration using standard USB-C cables. This design provides users with the flexibility to expand or reconfigure the system dynamically during operation.

Implementation of the meshed ER

In keeping with the objective of a fully decentralized, controller-less architecture, each node makes power-transfer decisions using only the states of its immediate neighbors. Specifically, a node compares the neighboring SoC values and requests or supplies power accordingly; no global coordinator or long-range communication is assumed. As shown in Fig. 5, Node 1 is at 100% SoC, while its adjacent nodes—Node 2 and Node 4—are at 40% and 75%, respectively. Both neighbors therefore draw power from Node 1; Node 2, being the more depleted, receives 6 A, and Node 4 receives 2.5 A. A similar behavior appears at Node 7 (SoC 40%): it is supplied simultaneously from Node 4 and Node 8 with 3.5 A and 2 A. Because Node 7 feeds DC Load #4 demanding 4.5 A, it forwards 4.5 A to the load and uses the remaining 1.0 A to charge its local battery. By contrast, at Node 3 the local DC Load #2 must be served, while the node’s SoC is not sufficient to export power. Hence, Node 3 does not supply any neighbors and instead discharges its local battery to provide the required load current. On the other hand, when all adjacent nodes have lower SoC—e.g., around Node 8—the node serves nearby loads using its own battery rather than importing energy. Consequently, under light loading the mesh naturally equalizes SoC by exchanging power among neighbors, whereas during transient or heavy loading the network draws on the previously stored energy and propagates it through multiple paths to satisfy local demands.

Figure 5 illustrates the meshed LVDCER structure, where each node autonomously exchanges power based on the SoC information of its neighboring nodes. Such decentralized power-sharing behavior can be interpreted as a distributed energy redistribution process driven solely by local interactions, without relying on a centralized supervisory controller. This operating principle is consistent with cyber-physical energy sharing frameworks in building-scale energy systems, where local energy storage units coordinate energy exchange through distributed control mechanisms [26]. In addition, the proposed operation is closely related to distributed SoC balancing strategies, in which energy storage modules exchange local SoC information with neighboring modules to achieve balanced operation without centralized coordination [27].

From (8)–(10), it follows that the current exchange is naturally formed in a direction that reduces SoC mismatches among neighboring nodes. In summary, the proposed meshed LVDCER architecture preserves the fundamental properties of consensus-based coordination while extending them to a physically realizable port-level current control framework. This enables decentralized power sharing and load support in practical LVDC nanogrid environments, effectively bridging multi-agent consensus theory and power electronic energy routing systems [28].

System configuration of a DC nanogrid living lab

Fig. 6Block diagram of the DC nanogrid living-lab architecture.

Block diagram of the DC nanogrid living-lab architecture.

Based on the architecture proposed in Fig. 2, a living lab environment was established, as illustrated in Fig. 6, to validate the complex operations of the ER under practical DC-based building scenarios. This living lab represents a DC nanogrid system centered on DC distribution, combining a 400 V DC bus that run through the entire building and a LVDC bus utilized at the end-user level. The LVDC bus operates at 24 V and serves as the primary voltage level for connecting various DC loads and ERs.

While the microgrid SST associated with MVAC, as defined in Fig. 2, was not physically implemented in the hardware, the 400 V DC bus in the living lab is supplied by an external DC power source to emulate its function. Examining the left portion of Fig. 6 in detail, a rooftop PV system is installed, and one ESS and one nanogrid SST are deployed to emulate per-floor configurations of a typical building. These elements are included to represent the essential components that would normally be distributed across multiple floors, thereby enabling a realistic validation of the proposed architecture within a living lab setup.

In the LVDC domain, a DC–DC converter precisely regulates the 24 V bus, which supplies power to various DC loads such as LED lighting, refrigerators, and electronic loads, thereby emulating realistic energy usage scenarios.

Nanogrid SST (2 kW): To satisfy both galvanic isolation and bidirectional power conversion requirements, the DAB converter topology was adopted. The converter integrates a high-frequency transformer with a 6:1 turn ratio, which connects the 400 V DC bus to a LV secondary. Voltage regulation and power transfer are achieved through a phase-shift control strategy between the primary and secondary bridges. On the primary side, a high-voltage, high-speed silicon carbide power switch (AIMW120R045M1XKSA1, Infineon) rated for 1.2 kV and 52 A was employed. On the secondary side, LV, high-current switch (FDP083N15A-F102, Onsemi) rated for 150 V and 105 A were used in a three-parallel configuration to ensure sufficient current handling capability. The entire system is controlled by a high-performance real-time MCU (TMS320F280049, Texas Instruments). DC–DC converter for renewable energy integration (1.2 kW): To interface PV and ESS units with the 400 V DC bus, a non-isolated synchronous half-bridge DC–DC converter was designed. The converter integrates the TDHBG1200DC100 power board from Renesas Electronics as the main power stage, and utilizes the LMG3410-HB-EVM gate driver board incorporating 650 V Gallium Nitride FETs for high-efficiency switching operation. A custom interface board including current and voltage sensors was implemented to facilitate precise feedback control. The same MCU (TMS320F280049, Texas Instruments) was utilized for converter operation. The converter supports both buck and boost modes, making it suitable for various PV and ESS voltage levels. Furthermore, bidirectional operation is essential for enabling both charging and discharging of the ESS, as the battery voltage differs from the 400 V DC bus. The converter facilitates efficient power transfer between the battery system and the DC bus under varying operating conditions. One additional LVDCER was connected per two groups, resulting in a living lab with 51 LVDCERs. To demonstrate the feasibility of diverse network patterns, in each of the six groups the eight LVDCERs were interconnected in different structures. Three electronic loads connected at the terminals emulate independently varying load conditions. All LVDCERs are connected to other LVDCERs and to the electronic loads via USB-C cables. PV system emulation (400 W): To emulate realistic PV characteristics under diverse environmental conditions, a PV simulator (SAS1000, Keysight) was used. The simulator reproduces dynamic I-V curves corresponding to conditions such as clear skies, partial shading, and variable irradiance, enabling precise evaluation of the MPPT algorithm. The PV output voltage is approximately 48 V, which is boosted to 400 V through the DC–DC converter. The MPPT algorithm is implemented to ensure continuous tracking of the optimal operating point for maximum power transfer; in addition, enhanced MPPT approaches are also applied in practice to handle diverse partial-shading conditions and to accelerate convergence [29]. ESS emulation (1 kW): A programmable battery simulator (BSS2000, Keysight) was employed to emulate a bidirectional energy storage system. The simulator supports smooth transitions between charging and discharging modes and allows real-time control and logging of key battery parameters such as voltage, current, power, SoC, and capacity. The ESS was configured to model a 100 Ah, 48 V battery cell. A bidirectional DC–DC converter connects the ESS to the 400 V DC bus and regulates power flow according to the system’s energy balance. Power allocation is performed in a priority-based manner to ensure uninterrupted supply to critical loads under all operating conditions. When PV generation exceeds load demand, surplus energy is stored in the ESS, while the loads continue to be supplied through the DC bus. When demand surpasses generation, the ESS discharges to support the bus voltage. A protection mechanism is implemented to suspend ESS discharging when the SoC falls below 20% to prevent over-depletion, while load demand continues to be supported by other available sources.

Experimental results

Efficiency evaluation of the ER module

High-efficiency power conversion is critical in nanogrid ER applications. Accordingly, the main power-conversion stage—explicitly designated as the four-switch buck–boost converter in Fig. 3—was characterized separately for efficiency measurement. A precision programmable DC source and an electronic load established the test bench. High-accuracy power analyzers were connected at the ER module input and output to measure input power and output power to compute efficiency. The load current was swept from 0.5 A to 8.5 A in 0.5 A increments, and efficiency was recorded at each operating point.

Results are shown in Fig. 7. At light load—specifically, for currents up to approximately 1 A—the efficiency is comparatively lower, primarily due to switching losses and the fixed consumption of the control circuitry. As the load current increases into the main operating region, from about 3 A and above, the efficiency surpasses 98% and remains stable. These measurements provide experimental evidence that the proposed ER module is well suited to multi-node nanogrid deployments, where minimizing cumulative conversion losses is essential.

Hot-swap performance and bidirectional transfer in a Two-ER configuration

Fig. 8ER hot-swap surge-protection comparison: (a) without the protection circuit. (b) with the protection circuit.

ER hot-swap surge-protection comparison: (a) without the protection circuit. (b) with the protection circuit.

In the preceding section, the efficiency of a single energy router (ER) was characterized. Building on that result, this section evaluates two ERs interconnected in a mesh-oriented topology, verifying the two core functions of the LVDC ER—hot-swap operation and bidirectional power transfer—under realistic inter-ER connection scenarios. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the absence of the hot-swap surge-protection circuit causes large bus-voltage disturbances during node insertion and removal. By contrast, Fig. 8(b) shows that enabling the hot-swap surge-protection circuit provides a smooth voltage ramp and maintains a stable bus voltage throughout the hot-swap events.

Bidirectional power flow and reliable battery charging and discharging are essential functions of an ER. To validate these capabilities, two ER units were directly interconnected via a USB-C cable, and a battery pack was attached to each unit to enable a bidirectional charge–discharge test. One unit (ER-A) was configured in discharge mode to supply power, while the other (ER-B) operated in charge mode to receive power, as illustrated in Fig. 9. Figure 10 records ER-A’s output voltage and output current over time. After approximately 34 min of discharge, when the battery SoC reached the preset lower threshold of 20%, the ER-A controller entered an end-of-discharge protection mode and commanded a controlled current ramp-down, progressively reducing the output current until the transfer was safely terminated. Throughout this sequence, the output voltage remained stable, and the mode transition proceeded smoothly without noticeable overshoot or abrupt excursions. These results demonstrate that the developed LVDCER supports stable bidirectional power transfer while autonomously managing and protecting the embedded battery.

Conclusion

A LVDCER—a key device for efficient and stable operation of building DC nanogrids—was designed and its performance was validated in a living lab environment that closely emulates real deployment. To overcome limitations of conventional power systems and to better integrate DERs, the ER was engineered for high-efficiency bidirectional conversion, intelligent decentralized control, and PnP scalability. Main contributions are as follows. (1) A proof-of-concept ER prototype was built that exceeds standard USB-C PD limits, enabling simultaneous transfer of more than 200 W per line and 10 Gbps-class data over a single cable. (2) Efficiency measurements of a single ER converter module confirmed greater than 98% efficiency in the rated operating range, indicating low cumulative losses in multi-node networks. (3) Bidirectional charge–discharge tests, including hot-swap events at the ER ports, demonstrated stable power transfer and autonomous SoC management. (4) A large-scale living lab comprising 255 nodes with a capacity of 50 kW was constructed; under realistic composite scenarios—PV variability, ESS charging or discharging, rapid load changes, and PnP events—the proposed nanogrid exhibited robust stability and favorable dynamic response.

These results indicate that LVDCER-based DC nanogrids are not merely conceptual but constitute a practical solution for real building applications. The approach is expected to play a central role in maximizing energy efficiency and improving power autonomy across diverse domains, including smart and net-zero buildings, high-reliability power for data centers, EV charging infrastructure, and renewable-integrated microgrids. The present study was conducted in a simulator-assisted living lab; hence, long-term effects of real weather–driven renewable uncertainty and heterogeneous building load profiles were not fully captured. Future efforts will deploy the system in an operational building to collect long-duration field data and assess reliability and economics. Building on these data, advanced energy-management algorithms will be developed by combining large-scale datasets with AI for more accurate demand and generation forecasting and optimized market-aware operation—thereby improving technical maturity and accelerating commercialization of DC nanogrid technology.
