Macrocyclization of native peptides through (thio)urea crosslinking of two amines
Authors: Jinyao Liu, Peiru Chen, Meilin Tang, Qiuyu Chen, Yixin Liao, Yu Liu, Shafi Ullah, Jinwu Zhao, Yubo Long, Gong Chen, Wenfang Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyclopeptides have emerged as promising candidates for drug development. However, owing to the scarcity of efficient cyclization strategies for natural peptides, the development of diverse cyclopeptides remains a formidable challenge. In this study, a variety of macrocyclic peptides featuring (thio)urea bridges are developed through the site-selective lysine‑lysine crosslinking of native peptides, employing N, N’-carbonyldiimidazole or N, N’-thiocarbonyldiimidazole as bridging reagents. In particular, dual thiourea-bridged macrocyclic peptides are further synthesized by using a one-pot two-step multicomponent macrocyclization. These strategies enable the chemoselective macrocyclization of peptides bearing diverse nucleophilic residues to afford (thio)urea-bridged macrocyclic analogs, as exemplified by the successful transformation of the targeted peptide RGD (Arg-Gly-Asp) and the antimicrobial peptide Anoplin analogue. Biological evaluation demonstrates that the cyclopeptides, particularly the thiourea- and bis-thiourea-bridged analogs, exhibit enhanced target binding affinity, potent antitumor activity, as well as improved membrane permeability and stability. Molecular docking elucidates the structural origin of the enhanced bioactivity, suggesting that the reinforced molecular interactions might be a potential underlying cause.Subject terms: Chemical modification, Peptides

Introduction

Cyclopeptides have garnered significant research attention in recent years for their potential in the development of bioactive molecules and drugs due to the enhanced stability, cell permeability, and affinity toward protein surfaces involved in protein-protein interactions (PPIs)1–4. With the continuous expansion of the therapeutic application landscape, ranging from antibacterial and antitumor treatments to neurodegenerative diseases and immunomodulation, the development of diverse and versatile cyclopeptides is increasingly critical5–8. Nevertheless, the presence of diverse reactive functional groups, such as thiols (e.g., Cys), guanidines (e.g., Arg), carboxyl groups (e.g., Asp and Glu), hydroxyl groups (e.g., Ser), amines (e.g., Lys), together with the requirements for rapid kinetics and mild reaction conditions, poses significant challenges for cyclizing native peptides. Over the past decades, numerous cyclization strategies have been developed to address these challenges by pre-installing reactive functional handles, such as azides, alkynes, or alkenes, into the peptide backbone9–16. However, such approaches often reduce cyclization efficiency and introduce the synthetic limitations associated with unnatural amino acids. Recently, enzymatic strategies have emerged as promising tools for the cyclization of native peptides17. For instance, Bandarian group demonstrated that PapB could efficiently couple an internal Cys thiol to the C-terminal carboxylate of peptides bearing D- or β-amino acids, yielding α- or β-thioether macrocycles18. This method provides an efficient route to structurally diverse cyclic peptides.

Crosslinking of two nucleophilic side chains within natural peptides provides another robust and versatile strategy for their direct cyclization19–24. This method obviates the need for unnatural residues and has therefore attracted considerable attention. Nevertheless, the limited number of nucleophilic residues, coupled with the need for high selectivity, poses a substantial challenge for peptide side-chain coupling. Recent advances have established diverse strategies for inter-residue cross-linking through the reactions of nucleophilic sites with coupling reagents, including carbonyl-mediated condensation reactions25–31, SNAr reactions8,32–34, esterification35, and so on36–40. (Fig. 1A) Despite significant progress, it remains desirable and critically challenging to achieve diverse and selective cyclization of natural peptides using their limited nucleophilic residues, which is key to constructing structurally diverse cyclopeptides.

Urea and thiourea motifs are privileged scaffolds in medicinal chemistry. These motifs can form multiple stable hydrogen bonds with proteins and receptor targets, which is essential for their molecular recognition and bioactivity. The central carbonyl oxygen or sulfur serves as a hydrogen‑bond acceptor capable of engaging two hydrogen bonds, whereas the two nitrogen‑bound protons act as hydrogen‑bond donors, contributing two to four hydrogen bonds depending on the substitution pattern. Due to the delocalization of nonbonded electrons from the nitrogen atoms into the adjacent carbonyl or thiocarbonyl group, these motifs usually exhibit conformational restriction. Moreover, these motifs, as bioisosteres of the amide bond, exhibit enhanced stability41–44. Thus, these core scaffolds are widely present in diverse bioactive compounds and therapeutic agents possessing a broad range of pharmacological activities, such as anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, anti-viral and antimicrobial activities45–50. One important area that we have recently become interested in is the cyclization of natural peptides. Inspired by the properties and broad bioactivities of the (thio)urea group, we propose incorporating the (thio)urea moiety into cyclopeptides to improve the physicochemical properties and physiological activities of natural peptides. It is important to note that, despite the established role of urea and thiourea as privileged motifs in small molecules, their strategic incorporation into macrocyclic peptide architectures has been scarce, with few examples reported in the past decade.

In this work, we report a straightforward cyclization of natural peptides via N, N’-carbonyldiimidazole (CDI) and N, N’-thiocarbonyldiimidazole (TCDI) mediated crosslinking between two amino groups, enabling efficient access to macrocyclic peptides featuring (thio)urea linkages (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the direct multicomponent macrocyclization of natural peptides was achieved by using a one-pot, two-step protocol, generating macrocyclic peptides featuring dual thiourea linkages. Notably, these strategies exhibit excellent chemoselectivity for the macrocyclization and broad compatibility with various native bioactive peptides containing multifunctional nucleophilic residues, such as Arg, Tyr, Asp, Thr, Met, His, and others. Biological evaluation confirmed that the (thio)urea-bridged macrocycles display enhanced cytotoxicity against multiple cancer cell lines, with concomitant improvements in membrane permeability and proteolytic stability.

Results and discussion

Optimization and scope of (thio)urea-bridged cyclopeptides

In the initial optimization studies, peptide P1 (with sequence Ac-Lys-Leu-Val-Lys-OMe) was chosen as a model substrate for reaction conditions optimization, employing CDI (2) as carbonylation reagent. Pleasingly, in the presence of NEt₃, the reaction afforded the desired cyclopeptide in 84% yield as determined by analytical HPLC with the external standard method based on absorbance at 220 nm (Supplementary Table S1, entry 1). Notably, the major byproducts were peptide derivatives generated by mono- or bis-isocyanation of the side-chain amines, and no linear dimeric peptides were observed under the standard reaction conditions. To facilitate the purification of the macrocyclic product, 1,3-diaminopropane was added upon completion of the reaction to quench any uncyclized isocyanate byproducts. Screening of bases, including DIPEA, DBU, DABCO, TBD, and t-BuOK, identified NEt₃ as the most effective base, giving a higher isolated yield (Supplementary Table S1, entries 2–6). Notably, the absence of NEt3 also yielded the desired product, albeit with lower yield (Supplementary Table S1, entry 7). Subsequently, the solvent was found to critically influence the reaction. Replacing DMSO with DMF slowed the cyclization, yet it remained efficient, giving a 48% yield (Supplementary Table S1, entry 8). The use of MeCN led to low reactivity, yielding only 4% of 1a (Supplementary Table S1, entry 9). Furthermore, no desired macrocyclic product was observed in either MeOH or PBS buffer, with recovery of the starting peptide instead (Supplementary Table S1, entries 10-12). Finally, higher reaction concentrations favored the isocyanation pathway, compromising both reaction efficiency and the target product yield (Supplementary Table S1, entries 13-14).

Next, we performed this (thio)urea crosslinking on a range of peptides with varying amino acid compositions under the optimized conditions, using both CDI (2) and TCDI (3) as the carbonylating and thiocarbonylating reagents, respectively. As expected, this approach enabled the efficient construction of both urea-based and thiourea-based cyclopeptides, demonstrating the potential for generating structural diversity (Fig. 2). Intriguingly, it was also applied to noncanonical amino acids, enabling efficient crosslinking between β-Ala and Dab to afford the conformationally constrained 13-membered macrocyclics 4a and 4b. Subsequently, peptides with two Lys at varied spacings were examined for the (thio)urea crosslinking. Linear peptide P5 bearing adjacent Lys residues did not yield any significant loss of efficiency and gave the desired products (5b) with 44% yield. Moreover, a series of structurally diverse linear peptides featuring Lys residues with varying spacing, such as (i, i + 2) (6a and 6b), (i, i + 3) (7b), (i, i + 4) (8a and 8b) and (i, i + 5) (9a, 9b, 10a and 10b), underwent efficient cyclization under the standard conditions, providing the corresponding cyclic products in reasonable yields. Additionally, this (thio)urea crosslinking was also effective for the more distal (i, i + 8) pair (11a and 11b), demonstrating the robustness of this approach for accessing larger ring systems.

To further demonstrate the broad applicability of the protocol, a wide range of peptide substrates containing unprotected reactive residues were tested under standard conditions. As shown in Fig. 3, most desired cyclopeptides featuring thiourea moieties were obtained in moderate to excellent yields. For example, linear peptide P12, which incorporates both unprotected tyrosine residue and free carboxylic acids, successfully underwent lysine-lysine cyclization to form the well-defined macrocyclic structure in high yield. It is worth noting that cysteine residue must be protected for compatibility with this reaction system (13b). Furthermore, a peptide containing a nucleophilic arginine residue was also found to be applicable to this cyclization strategy, as evidenced by the cyclization of peptide (P14). In addition, an amide group (P15) as well as His (P18) and Tyr (P18) residues were all well tolerated under the reaction conditions, affording the corresponding cyclopeptides in good yields. Overall, except for Cys, all nucleophilic amino acid residues in unprotected form are compatible with this reaction system, as exemplified by Glu (12b, 16b, 18b), Tyr (12b, 14b, 15b, 16b, 18b), Arg (13b, 14b, 15b, 16b), Thr (18b), Gln (18b), Ser (18b), Met (17b, 18b), His (18b), Pro (14b, 15b, 16b, 17b) and Trp (18b). Excellent reactivity was maintained with substrates featuring either clusters of identical nucleophilic residues (14b, 15b, 16b) or combinations of different nucleophiles (Lys, Arg, His, Gln). Such broad compatibility renders this approach a powerful method for the late-stage modification of natural active peptides without compromising their bioactivity.

In addition, the (thio)urea crosslinking between the N-terminal amine and side-chain amine was also examined to expand the scope of the methodology (Fig. 4). Unfortunately, the cyclization reaction failed to proceed when the N-terminal amine was reacted with either an adjacent (P19) or a distal (P20) lysine residue, and instead generated isocyanate derivatives. We speculated that although the N-terminal α-amine possesses a lower pKa and thus higher theoretical nucleophilicity than the lysine ε-amine at slightly basic pH51,52, the failure to undergo cyclization suggests that steric constraints or restricted accessibility imposed by the peptide backbone dominate over intrinsic reactivity under the conditions19,53. Interestingly, when peptide P21, bearing an isopropylidene group at the N-terminal amine, was used as substrate, cyclization with side-chain residues successfully afforded the desired product (21b). Moreover, despite the failure of cyclization between the N-terminal amine and a side-chain amine (19b, 20b), a peptide bearing an N-terminal Lys residue enabled chemoselective cyclization between two side chain amines, thereby affording the cyclic product while preserving the native N‑terminal bioactivity and bypassing conventional deprotection steps.

Given the critical role of the linkage group in modulating biological activity, we set out to develop bis-urea-bridged cyclic peptide analogs. Following optimization, the target bis-thiourea-bridged macrocyclic peptide was successfully obtained via a one-pot, two-step protocol, albeit in lower yield (Fig. 5). This result demonstrates the potential of our method for constructing macrocyclic scaffolds that are difficult to access by conventional means. Notably, CDI proved unreactive under these conditions, and the macrocyclic peptide with a bis-thiourea linkage was obtained exclusively when TCDI was used.

Macrocyclization for constructing macrocyclic peptides featuring bis-thiourea linkage.

1) Activation of lysine ε-amino groups on linear peptide P1 using CDI or TCDI, NEt3 as base, DMF, r.t. 2) Addition of 1,3-propanediamine triggering intramolecular cyclization to afford the bis-(thio)urea macrocycle.

Next, the strategies were extended to the macrocyclization of various bioactive natural peptides (Fig. 6). Anoplin (GLLKRIKTLL-NH2) is one of the shortest natural liner AMPs derived from the venom sac of the solitary wasp Anoplius samariensis, which exhibits broad-spectrum activity, as well as anti-fungal, anti-tumor, and anti-malarial activities54,55. Therefore, unprotected Anoplin analog was explored as a starting substrate. However, the corresponding cyclopeptide product could only be observed by LCMS analysis. In contrast, when the N-terminal Fmoc-protected Anoplin analog was used as the substrate, cyclic peptides 23a and 23b, incorporating urea and thiourea moieties, were successfully obtained. Notably, the cyclization occurred concomitantly with spontaneous N-terminal deprotection. To unambiguously determine the structure, the product 23a was definitively characterized by Edman degradation (Supplementary Fig. S34). Further, the desired bis-thiourea-bridged macrocyclic peptide 23c was also successfully accessed via the multicomponent stapling approach with reasonable yield. Moreover, the cyclization of the naturally occurring cationic antimicrobial peptide HHC-36 (KRWWKWWRR-OH) was also performed, generating the macrocyclic urea 24a and thiourea 24b derivatives. This Trp/Arg-rich bioactive peptide is known for self-aggregation propensity and selective interaction with anionic membranes, properties that are relevant to anticancer therapeutic development56,57.

Activity evaluation

To assess the efficacy of the cyclization strategies, we evaluated the stability of the macrocyclic peptides. Firstly, the chemical stability was evaluated in H2O2 (80 mM) conditions, and P24, 24a and 24b were chosen as representative peptides (Supplementary Figs. S39, S40, S45A). The results showed that P24 degraded rapidly within 4 hours, generating a mixture of uncharacterized degradation products. Comparing with P24, no significant degradation was observed for either 24a or 24b under the same conditions, indicating their enhanced resistance to oxidative degradation. Analogously, the urea-modified cyclopeptides exhibited comparable stability under alkaline conditions (pH 10.0) (Supplementary Figs. S41, S42, S45B). Further, the stability of 24a and 24b in the presence of trypsin were evaluated. In contrast to the P24, 24a and 24b underwent no significant digestion within 4 hours, exhibiting enhanced trypsin resistance (Supplementary Figs. S43, S44, S45C).

Furthermore, to investigate the cell membrane permeability of the cyclopeptides, we designed FITC-labeled cyclic derivatives 25a-25c (Fig. 7A) and evaluated their cellular uptake efficiency in A549-Luc cells (Fig. 7B, C). Following 6 hours of co-incubation with A549-Luc cells, the cellular uptake of FITC-labeled peptides (P25, 25a, 25b, and 25c) was assessed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) imaging. In contrast to P25, which showed no intracellular fluorescence, all cyclic analogues including those with urea, thiourea, and bis-thiourea linkages, demonstrated signal enhancement, indicating superior cell-penetrating efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S52). Among these, 25b, incorporating a single thiourea linkage, demonstrated the most pronounced fluorescence intensity enhancement. Flow cytometry quantification further confirmed the superior cellular uptake of all cyclic derivatives over the linear peptide. Among them, 25b exhibited the most pronounced enhancement. In contrast, the urea-bridged analog 25a and the bis-thiourea analog 25c showed moderate improvements. The restrained uptake of 25a may result from its inherent polarity, while the structure of 25c might confer excessive conformational restraint that slightly compromises its cellular uptake.

Next, we investigated the biological activities of the (thio)urea-bridged cyclopeptides. First, the classic RGD-containing linear peptide P26 targeting the tumor-associated integrin αvβ6 58,59 was selected to investigate the effect of (thio)urea‑based cyclization on the target interaction. Fortunately, its thiourea-bridged cyclic derivative 26b was successfully prepared in a yield of 71% under standard conditions. Subsequently, 26b was employed as a representative of integrin inhibitors to evaluate its binding affinity toward αvβ6 integrin by competitive ELISA (Fig. 8). The results demonstrated that 26b (IC50 = 0.051 µM) exhibited enhanced binding affinity compared to its linear precursor P26 (IC50 = 0.425 µM), approaching that of the positive control (c(RGDfK), IC50 = 0.034 µM).

Inspired by this promising result, we then sought to investigate whether this (thio)urea-based macrocyclic scaffold could be broadly applied to enhance the antitumor potency of other bioactive peptides. Accordingly, the (thio)urea-bridged cyclic derivatives 23a-23c, derived from Anoplin analogue with broad pharmacological activities, and 24a-24b, derived from the Trp/Arg-rich bioactive peptide, were selected for antitumor evaluation. Their cytotoxicity was then assessed against several human cancer cell lines, including A549-Luc, Caco-2, and A498, using the CCK-8 assay (Table 1). Overall, most of the selected cyclopeptides demonstrated improved anticancer activity relative to the linear analogues. Most notably, the bis-thiourea cyclized analog 23c showed greater potency compared to the parent compound P23 across all three cell lines. This enhancement was most pronounced in the A549-Luc cell line, where its IC50 value was 10.81 μM. A substantial increase in potency was also observed in the Caco-2 cells (IC50 = 18.04 μM). Even in the A498 cell line, where all compounds showed reduced potency, 23c exhibited better activity compared to 23a and 23b. In addition, compared to compound P24, analog 24a showed slight improvement in the A549-Luc and A498 cell lines. Notably, 24b displayed lower IC50 values across all cell lines compared to both P24 and 24a. Thus, these results suggest that thiourea-cyclized peptides may exhibit somewhat higher antitumor activity compared with their urea-cyclized analogs. Among these, the bis-thiourea derivative 23c was observed to have relatively low IC50 values across all evaluations. Subsequently, the cytotoxicity of the cyclic peptides 23c and 24b, which showed potent antitumor activity, was evaluated against the normal MCF-10A cell line, along with their linear precursors P23 and P24. The results demonstrated that 23c and 24b displayed low toxicity against normal cells, with cell viabilities exceeding 80% at their antitumor IC50 concentrations, indicating a favorable safety profile.

Subsequently, the apoptotic impact of 23c was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry. A549-Luc and Caco-2 cell lines were co-incubated with 23c and then stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI). As detailed in Supplementary Fig. S55, the untreated control group displayed a minimal percentage of apoptotic cells. Compared with the control group, after a 48 h treatment with concentrations of 20, 50, 70 μM for A549-Luc or 20, 35, 60 μM for Caco-2, the respective percentages of apoptotic cells in the A549-Luc cell line population were 25.88%, 77.79%, and 86.80%, while the respective percentages of apoptotic cells in the Caco-2 population were 30.94%, 45.00%, and 74.40%. These results demonstrated 23c showed dose-dependent induction of apoptosis.

Having established potent bioactivity profiles for the (thio)urea-bridged derivatives, we attempted to investigate the underlying reasons for this enhanced activity. First, the circular dichroism (CD) spectra of 23a, 23b, and 23c were measured in PBS solution to explore the relationship between their secondary structure and antitumor activity. The results showed that the α-helicity of the cyclized peptides 23a (α = 6%), 23b (α = 6%), and 23c (α = 9%) decreased slightly compared to the linear precursor P23 (α = 12%). The results indicate that helicity may not be the primary factor influencing their biological activity54. From a physical organic chemistry perspective, thioamides show longer C = S bonds of 1.65 Å compared to 1.23 Å for C = O bonds, and sulfur has a larger van der Waals radius (1.80 Å) than oxygen (1.52 Å)60,61. These properties can alter the local geometry and hydrogen-bonding capacity of the backbone. Accordingly, the thiourea linkage may favor a bioactive conformation,potentially reducing the entropic penalty upon binding and enhance affinity. From a reactivity standpoint, thioamide-containing cyclic peptides exhibit high chemical stability60. Nevertheless, it remains possible that intracellular environments could promote H₂S release. To assess whether their biological activity involves H₂S release, we used lead acetate (Pb(OAc)₂) test paper to detect endogenous H₂S formation following treatment with 23c62. The absence of detectable H₂S after 23c treatment suggests that the enhanced activity is unlikely to arise from H₂S release. These findings suggest that the enhanced antitumor activity of thioamide-containing cyclic peptides, particularly 23c, is associated with conformational rigidification induced by the thiourea structure, potentially optimizing pharmacophore orientation for target engagement and enhancing binding affinity.

Next, we sought to gain mechanistic insights into their potent efficacy at the molecular level. To this end, we performed molecular docking to visualize its potential binding mode with the target protein. Firstly, we performed molecular docking between integrin αvβ6 and 26b. The crystal structure of the integrin αvβ6 protein (PDB ID: 4UM9) was used as the receptor model, and the lowest energy conformation was selected for analysis63. The result showed that 26b exhibited superior binding affinity to αvβ6, which is consistent with the observed enhancement in antitumor activity (Supplementary Table S2). Within a cutoff distance of 4.50 Å for potential interactions, 26b was found to interact with αvβ6 residues including Ile-216; Asp-218; Asp-219; Arg-248; Glu-316; Lys-338 (Fig. 9A). Furthermore, the hydrogen bond distances formed between 26b and receptor (2.27 Å; 2.38 Å; 2.18 Å; 2.33 Å; 1.94 Å; 2.04 Å; 2.87 Å; 2.57 Å) were shorter than those formed by P26 (2.20 Å; 1.99 Å; 2.61 Å; 1.98 Å; 2.25 Å). Subsequently, we conducted molecular docking between the target and 23c. In order to obtain the possible targets, we first employed network pharmacology to systematically analyze potential targets for compound P23 (Supplementary Figs. S58, S59)64,65. Due to the well-established roles in promoting tumor cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis, EGFR was selected from the PPI network as a candidate to evaluate compound binding affinity. Subsequently, we performed molecular docking between EGFR and the peptides P23 and 23c. The crystal structure of the EGFR (PDB ID: 9QXN) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank and served as the receptor model for molecular docking66. Consistent with its superior antitumor efficacy, compound 23c also demonstrated a stronger computational binding affinity for EGFR than the parent compound P23, with docking scores of − 14.66 and − 13.77, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). Analysis of the binding modes indicated that both P23 and 23c are well-bound by the active site pocket (Fig. 9B). Within a cutoff distance of 4.50 Å for potential interactions, 23c was found to interact with EGFR residues, including Leu-718; Phe-795; Cys-797; Thr-854. The interaction is stabilized by hydrogen bonds with distances of 2.05 Å; 2.09 Å 2.28 Å 2.74 Å. Notably, 23c achieved a more optimal fit within the binding pocket than compound P23, which exhibited a relatively loose binding mode. This superior complementarity correlates with the more favorable docking energy computed for 23c. These results provide a reasonable computational explanation for the observed superior bioactivity of thiourea-bridged cyclopeptides, suggesting that their enhanced bioactivity efficacy is likely mediated through stronger and more stable interactions with the target.

In conclusion, we have developed a straightforward macrocyclization of diverse natural peptides through CDI/TCDI-mediated lysine-lysine crosslinking, generating an array of macrocycles incorporating (thio)urea scaffolds. More importantly, a multicomponent macrocyclization of natural peptides through a one - pot two - step protocol was further realized for the synthesis of dual thiourea bridged macrocyclic peptides. In addition, the macrocyclization strategies feature broad substrate compatibility, amino acid residue compatibility and excellent chemoselectivity. Biological evaluation showed that these macrocyclic peptides exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity across a panel of cancer cell lines, concomitant with improved membrane permeability and increased proteolytic stability. Molecular docking provided structural insights for these observations, suggesting that their enhanced bioactivity may be mediated through stronger interactions with the target.

Methods

All chemical reagents, linear peptides, and biological reagents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used as received without further purification unless otherwise noted. The cell lines, including A549-Luc (human non-small-cell lung carcinoma), Caco-2 (human colorectal adenocarcinoma), A498 (human renal carcinoma), and MCF-10A (human normal breast epithelial cells), were obtained from commercial sources and used without further authentication unless otherwise noted. All details regarding reagents, instruments, reaction optimization, experimental procedures, characterization of (thio)urea-containing cyclic peptides, and molecular docking are provided in the Supplementary Information.

General procedure for the synthesis of (thio)urea-containing cyclic peptides

To a solution of lysine-containing peptides in DMSO (0.01 mmol, 0.001 M, 10 mL), a solution of N, N’-carbonyldiimidazole or N, N'-thiocarbonyldiimidazole (CDI or TCDI, 1.5 equiv., 0.015 M in DMSO, 10 mL) and NEt3 (7 equiv., 0.0035 M) was added dropwise successively via a constant-pressure dropping funnel under stirring. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 6 hours. After the reaction was completed, the crude mixture was quenched with excess 1,3-propanediamine and lyophilized to remove DMSO. The resulting residue was dissolved in a small volume of MeCN/H2O, filtered, and then analyzed by HPLC and LCMS. Subsequent purification was performed by semipreparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on a reversed-phase C18 column to afford the desired product. The purified peptides were then lyophilized to yield white freeze-dried powders.

General procedure for the synthesis of dithiourea-containing macrocyclic peptides

To a solution of lysine-containing peptides in DMF (0.01 mmol, 0.002 M, 5 mL), the N, N’-thiocyanyldiimidazole (TCDI, 4 equiv., 0.008 M) and NEt3 (1 equiv., 0.002 M) was then added under stirring. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 hours. Reaction progress was monitored by withdrawing a small aliquot for mass spectrometry (LCMS/MS) analysis to confirm intermediate formation. Subsequently, a solution of 1,3-propanediamine (2 equiv., 0.001 M, 20 mL) in the same solvent was prepared and then slowly added dropwise to the reaction mixture using a constant pressure dropping funnel under continued stirring at room temperature overnight. After the reaction was completed, the crude mixture was quenched with excess 1,3-propanediamine and lyophilized to remove DMF. The resulting residue was dissolved in a small volume of MeCN/H2O, filtered, and then analyzed by HPLC and LCMS. Subsequent purification was performed by semipreparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on a reversed-phase C18 column to afford the desired product. The purified peptides were then lyophilized to yield white freeze-dried powders.

Stability studies

Oxidative stability

A 2 mL EP tube was charged with 160 µL H2O2 (80 mM), 20 µL of 1.0 M Tris Buffer (pH 8.0), 20 µL of peptide (10 mM stock solution). The resulting reaction mixture was capped and incubated in 37 °C water bath for 4 h. At each time point from 0 to 4 h, 5 μL of the crude reaction mixture was taken, quenched with 195 μL of a 50:50 (v/v) water/acetonitrile solution, and analyzed by HPLC.

Stability in pH 10.0 buffer conditions

A 2 mL EP tube was charged with 160 µL of deionized H2O, 20 µL of 0.5 M CAPS Buffer (pH 10.0), 20 µL of peptide (10 mM stock solution). The resulting reaction mixture was capped and incubated in 37 °C water bath for 4 h. At each time point from 0 to 4 h, 5 μL of the crude reaction mixture was taken, quenched with 195 μL of a 50:50 (v/v) water/acetonitrile solution, and analyzed by HPLC.

Proteolytic stability

A 2 mL EP tube was charged with 196 µL of phosphate buffer pH 7.4, 2 µL of trypsin (0.05 mg/mL stock solution in phosphate buffer pH 7.4), 2 µL of peptide (10 mM stock solution). The resulting reaction mixture was capped and incubated at room temperature for 4 h. At each time point from 0 to 4 h, 5 μL of the crude reaction mixture was taken, quenched with 195 μL of a 50:50 (v/v) water/acetonitrile solution, and analyzed by HPLC.

Integrin binding assay

Flat-bottom 96-well ELISA plate were coated overnight at 4 °C with 100 μL/well of 0.4 μg/mL LAP(TGF-β1) in carbonate buffer (15 mM Na2CO3, 35 mM NaHCO3, pH 9.6). Each well was then washed with PBS-T buffer (PBS, 0.01% Tween 20, pH 7.4, 3 × 200 μL) and blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 150 μL/well of TSB-buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM MnCl2, pH 7.5, 1% BSA). After being washed three times with PBS-T, equal volumes of test compounds were mixed with 1.0 μg/mL human integrin αvβ6, giving a final dilution in TSB buffer of 0.0001-200 µM for the inhibitors and 0.5 μg/mL for integrin αvβ6. These solutions (100 μL/well) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The plate was washed three times with PBS-T buffer, and 100 μL/well of 1:400 diluted mouse antihuman αv was added to the plate. After incubation for 1 h at room temperature, the plate was washed three times with PBS-T, and 100 μL/well of 4.0 μg/mL of secondary peroxidase-labeled antibody (anti-mouse IgG-POD) was added to the plate and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After the plate was washed three times with PBS-T, it got developed by adding 100 μL/well of TMB and incubated for 1–5 min at room temperature. The reaction was stopped with 100 μL/well of 3 M H2SO4, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a plate reader.

Cytotoxicity

A549-Luc and Caco-2 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI-1640) medium. A498 cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM). The medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For the assay, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5000 − 8000 cells per well and cultured under the same conditions for 24 h. The cells were then treated with serial dilutions of the peptide at the indicated final concentrations and incubated for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for a further 1 − 4 h at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader.

Cellular uptake experiment

A549-Luc cells were seeded into confocal-specific glass-bottom dishes at a density of 100000 cells/well and cultured to 70-90% confluency. FITC-labeled peptides P25, 25a, 25b, 25c were added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ for 6 h. After incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10-15 minutes and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, Hoechst 33258 (1–5 μg/mL) was added to stain nuclei, followed by a 20 min incubation and two additional PBS washes. Finally, imaging was performed using a laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 63 × oil-immersion objective.

Flow cytometry

A549-Luc cells were seeded in triplicate in 6-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well and cultured to 90% confluence in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Peptides P25, 25a, 25b, 25c were administered at a final concentration of 50 μM (1 mL/well). After 6 h of incubation, the supernatant was aspirated, and cells were trypsinized with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA for 2 min at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Detached cells were collected, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min at room temperature, washed three times with pre-warmed PBS, resuspended in 1 mL PBS, and filtered through a 40 μm nylon mesh. Flow cytometry analysis was performed on a BD LSRII system (excitation wavelength: 488 nm, detection channel: FITC). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1.

Cell apoptosis analysis

A549-Luc and Caco-2 cells, cultured during the logarithmic growth phase, were plated into six-well plates at a seeding density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Thereafter, 23c was introduced to achieve drug concentrations of 0, 20, 50, and 70 μM for A549-Luc and 0, 20, 35, 60 μM for Caco-2 cells. Following a 48 h treatment period, the cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min and washed three times with cold PBS buffer. The cell apoptosis analysis was carried out using an Annexin V-FITC cell apoptosis kit. According to the manufacturer’s procedure, resuspend cells in 500 μL of 1 × Binding Buffer and adjust the cell concentration to 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Prepare flow cytometry tubes labeled as blank control, single-positive controls (for compensation), and experimental samples. Add 500 μL of the filtered cell suspension (passed through a 200-mesh strainer) to each tube. For single-positive controls, add either 5 μL of Annexin V-Alexa Fluor™488 or 10 μL of propidium iodide (PI) to their respective tubes and mix gently. For sample tubes, first add 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC followed by 10 μL of PI, mixing gently after each addition. Incubate all tubes at room temperature in the dark for 10–20 min, then add 500 μL of 1 × Binding Buffer and place on ice before flow cytometry analysis. The instrument settings should be configured as follows: Alexa Fluor™488 detection at 488 nm excitation with 530/30 nm emission collection, and PI detection at 561 nm excitation with 610/20 nm emission collection.
