Challenges and coping experiences in caring for children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) in central region, Ghana
Authors: Cynthia Essel, Isaac Nyarko Kwakye, Daniel Adom-Fynn, Adwoa Oforiwa Antwi, Dorothy Baffour Awuah, Rhoda Kwakyewah Agyei, Nuworza Kugbey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaregiving for children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a demanding role, particularly in low-resource settings. This study explored the challenges and coping experiences of caregivers of children with ASD in the Central Region of Ghana.MethodsAn Exploratory-Descriptive Qualitative (EDQ) approach was adopted, guided by a constructivist epistemological stance. Seventeen participants, including family (parent and guardians) and institutional caregivers (staff) were selected. Participants were selected from a Special School, which serves children with developmental and learning disabilities, using a purposive sampling technique. A semi-structured interview guide was used to collect data and analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework, employing an inductive approach to generate themes from the data.FindingsCaregivers experienced multiple constraints, including poor public assistance, inadequate rest, inability to meet the financial demands of caregiving, and inadequate knowledge of care. Despite these challenges, caregivers exhibited proactive self-care behaviours, such as prompt attention to ailments and self-medication practices. Participants also employed a variety of coping strategies to manage the physical, emotional, and financial burden of caregiving. These strategies included seeking emotional support from family and peers, engaging in diversionary therapy, and applying relaxation techniques to sustain their caregiving roles.ConclusionsASD caregiving is a public health and social welfare concern. It highlights the need for culturally sensitive support systems, increased awareness, caregiver education, and stronger policy interventions to improve caregiver wellbeing and child development outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04821-x.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterised by persistent deficits in social communication and interaction, and restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately one in every 100 children is diagnosed with ASD globally [1]. In the United States, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported a prevalence of 1 in 36 children aged eight years in 2020, up from 1 in 44 in 2018, underscoring a rising global burden [2, 3]. The male-to-female diagnostic ratio remains approximately 3:1, although emerging evidence suggests that ASD is systematically underdiagnosed in females [3].

In sub-Saharan Africa, epidemiological data on ASD remain scarce, creating significant gaps in service planning [4]. Nigeria has reported the highest diagnosed caseload in the region [5]. Studies from East Africa, particularly Kenya, document especially challenging caregiving contexts characterised by insufficient services and constrained social support [6]. Older paternal age has been identified as a risk factor for ASD across multiple large-scale population studies [7–10], though the mechanisms linking parental age to ASD risk remain incompletely understood. In Ghana, research on ASD caregiving remains nascent and methodologically sparse. The limited evidence available indicates that cultural misconceptions, including attributions of ASD to spiritual or supernatural causes, delay diagnosis, reinforce stigma, and impede access to evidence-based intervention [11, 12]. Institutional support for children with ASD is inadequate, and families disproportionately absorb the burden of care with minimal formal assistance [13]. The psychosocial toll of caregiving for a child with ASD is well documented. At the time of diagnosis, caregivers commonly experience grief-like responses including disbelief, anxiety, and emotional distress [14, 15]. Over time, unrelieved caregiving demands compromise physical health, mental health, marital relationships, and employment [14–16]. Mothers are disproportionately affected due to gendered caregiving norms [15].

This study contributes to the literature by examining the intersection of weak disability policy implementation, widespread acceptance of supernatural explanations, and the near absence of formal respite and psychosocial services for caregivers. Although studies in high-income and some low- and middle-income countries have explored caregiver burden and coping, the Ghanaian context, especially the roles of faith-based coping, traditional medicine use, and structural financial exclusion, remains insufficiently studied. Addressing this gap is critical for informing context-specific, evidence-based policy and practice. The present study aimed to explore the lived experiences of both family and institutional caregivers of children with ASD in the Central Region of Ghana, specifically examining the challenges they face and the coping strategies they employ, with a view to informing evidence-based policy and practice. Specifically, the study sought to:

Identify and characterise the challenges experienced by family and institutional caregivers of children with ASD in the Central Region, Ghana.Examine the self-care behaviours adopted by caregivers in response to caregiving demands.Explore the coping strategies employed by caregivers to manage the physical, emotional, and financial burden of ASD caregiving.

Methods

Research design

An Exploratory-Descriptive Qualitative (EDQ) design was adopted for this study. Epistemologically, the study was grounded in constructivism, which holds that knowledge and meaning are co-constructed through individuals’ lived experiences and social interactions [17]. This stance is congruent with the study’s aim of understanding caregiving as a subjective, contextually situated phenomenon rather than a fixed, objectively measurable state. The EDQ design was selected over alternatives on deliberate methodological grounds. A phenomenological design, while potentially suited to exploring lived experience, would have restricted the analytical focus to the essence of shared experiential structures. A grounded theory approach would have prioritised theory generation as the primary output, which was not the study’s aim. The EDQ design, as described by Hunter et al. [17], was chosen because it is specifically appropriate for complex social and health phenomena that have received limited research attention, as is the case with ASD caregiving in Ghana, and because it enables systematic description and exploration of participants’ emotions, meanings, and lived experiences without the prescriptive constraints of more theory-driven designs. This flexibility allowed the research team to remain attentive to unexpected findings and contextually specific patterns emerging from participants’ narratives. The study was further guided by Lazarus and Folkman’s [18] Stress and Coping Theory, which conceptualises coping as a dynamic, transactional process in which individuals appraise stressors and mobilise both problem-focused and emotion-focused strategies in response. This framework was particularly appropriate given that the study sought to examine not only the stressors caregivers faced but also the active, intentional, and sometimes adaptive strategies they deployed, as well as the emotional and relational resources they drew upon in managing those demands.

Population and sample

The study was conducted in the Central Region of Ghana, which has two special schools for children with developmental and learning disabilities, including ASD. The region was selected due to its diversity of educational and caregiving settings and the availability of both institutional and family caregivers directly involved in supporting children with autism. The target population consisted of family (parents and guardians) and institutional caregivers (staff) of children living with ASD. A total of 17 participants were purposively selected for the study. Purposive sampling identified individuals with in-depth knowledge and direct experience of caring for children with ASD, thereby ensuring rich data. Of the 17 participants, nine were family caregivers and eight were institutional caregivers. The eight institutional caregivers were interviewed at a special school, while the nine family caregivers were interviewed in their private homes.

Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached after the fourteenth interview. Three additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation, given the heterogeneity of the sample. Beyond the invocation of saturation, the sample size of 17 is justified on multiple grounds. First, purposive qualitative samples of 15–20 participants are well established as sufficient in EDQ and descriptive qualitative designs when the research aim is thick, contextually grounded description rather than statistical representativeness [19, 20]. Second, the sample encompassed two distinct caregiver types (family and institutional), varied educational backgrounds, ages ranging from 18 to 78 years, and caregiving durations from 1.5 to 13 years, providing substantial heterogeneity within a focused, purposive frame. Third, the iterative process of concurrent data collection and analysis confirmed that new codes and categories ceased to emerge after the fourteenth participant, satisfying the criterion of informational redundancy. It is acknowledged that the single-setting focus – specifically, participants recruited through or connected to a single Special School in the Central Region – constitutes a potential source of sampling bias. Caregivers connected to formal educational institutions may differ systematically from those with no institutional engagement; they are likely to have higher levels of awareness, greater access to peer information, and potentially different socioeconomic profiles compared to caregivers of children with ASD who are entirely outside the formal educational system. Findings must therefore be interpreted in light of this potential selection effect, and generalisability beyond similarly situated institutional settings should be exercised with caution.

The inclusion criteria for the study comprised caregivers, both family and institutional, who were responsible for children under the age of 15 diagnosed with ASD. This was to focus on early-to-middle childhood, a critical period for intervention in developmental and behavioural needs. At this age, children depend on primary caregivers, allowing exploration of caregiver responsibilities, challenges, and coping strategies. The caregivers of children with ASD must be a parent, guardian, or institutional staff member, and be willing to provide informed consent and openly share their caregiving experiences. Conversely, caregivers of children with multiple disabilities or other developmental disorders apart from ASD were excluded from the study. Individuals who were unwilling or unable to participate in an in-depth interview were also not considered eligible for inclusion.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide developed specifically for this study and reviewed by the research team for contextual relevance and content validity. The guide was organised around four principal thematic domains: (i) caregivers’ beliefs about the aetiology of ASD; (ii) the day-to-day demands and challenges of caregiving; (iii) caregivers’ self-care practices and health-seeking behaviours; and (iv) coping strategies employed to manage caregiving demands. Representative questions included: ‘What do you think could have been the cause of your child’s condition?’; ‘Tell me about what you do for the child from day to day’; ‘How do you take care of your own health while managing caregiving responsibilities?’; and ‘How do you react if you get overwhelmed?’ These open-ended questions encouraged detailed narrative responses while allowing the interviewer to probe emerging issues with flexibility. The instrument was pilot-tested with four caregivers who were not part of the main study. Minor revisions were made to improve question clarity, reduce ambiguity, and enhance contextual appropriateness. The English-language version of the guide is available as a supplementary file.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected through face-to-face in-depth interviews conducted by two members of the research team (CE and INK). Prior to data collection, rapport was established with each participant through introductory visits to explain the study’s purpose. Interviews were scheduled at venues and times convenient for participants and conducted in private, comfortable settings, either at participants’ homes or in designated spaces within the special school, to promote openness and psychological safety. With participants’ permission, all interviews were audio-recorded and lasted approximately one and a half hours. Interviews were conducted in English and Fante languages. Audio recordings were stored on a password-encrypted device. Field notes were taken during and after each interview to capture non-verbal cues, contextual observations, and initial analytical reflections.

Both interviewers (CE and INK) were university-level researchers with postgraduate qualifications in nursing and health promotion, respectively. Neither had prior personal relationships with any participant. The interviewers were aware that their professional identities, as health-trained, university-affiliated researchers, could potentially influence participant responses, particularly by encouraging socially desirable answers or discouraging the expression of unorthodox (for example, traditional or spiritual) beliefs. To mitigate this, the team adopted a deliberately non-judgmental, empathic interview stance, explicitly affirmed the value of all perspectives at the outset of each interview, and used neutral, open-ended probes rather than leading questions. Reflective journaling and team debriefing were conducted after each interview session to monitor potential interviewer effects and maintain analytical reflexivity throughout.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, measures were taken to enhance credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Credibility was achieved through prolonged engagement with participants, member checking, and peer debriefing. Member checking was conducted with eight of the 17 participants, selected purposively to include both family and institutional caregivers and to ensure representation across educational backgrounds and caregiving durations. Participants were provided with written summaries of the themes and illustrative quotes attributed to them and asked to comment on the accuracy, completeness, and fairness of the representation. Two participants suggested minor rewordings to better capture the emotional tone of their experiences; these revisions were incorporated into the final themes. No participant disputed the core findings or indicated that their views had been misrepresented. Three participants were unreachable by the time of member checking and were therefore excluded from this phase. Transferability was strengthened by providing rich and detailed descriptions of the study context, the participants, and the data collection procedures.

Dependability was ensured by maintaining a transparent audit trail that documented all methodological decisions, data management steps, and analytical processes, allowing the study to be replicated or reviewed for consistency. Confirmability extends beyond reflexivity during data collection to encompass the active management of analytical bias throughout the interpretive process. In addition to the reflective journaling and team debriefing noted above, the research team employed two specific strategies to manage bias during analysis: (i) independent coding by three analysts prior to cross-analyst discussion, reducing the risk that any individual’s assumptions would dominate the initial coding; and (ii) regular ‘devil’s advocate’ discussions in which team members deliberately challenged emerging interpretations by returning to the raw transcripts to assess whether alternative readings were plausible. This process of disciplined reflexivity was applied consistently from the initial coding phase through to the final naming and report production stages.

Researchers’ reflexivity

The research team comprised male and female university lecturers and a senior nursing officer with postgraduate qualifications and expertise in nursing, psychology, and public health. No prior personal relationships existed between researchers and participants. Before data collection, the team made introductory visits to explain the study’s purpose and establish rapport. Semi-structured interview guides, reflective journaling, and team debriefing were employed to minimise potential bias. Interviews were conducted in an open, non-judgmental, and culturally respectful manner. The team also acknowledged the potential influence of their health-professional identities on participants’ willingness to disclose traditional health-seeking behaviours and spiritual beliefs, and took active steps to communicate acceptance of all perspectives.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Institutional Review Board (Ref. No. GHS-ERC: 041/02/23). Additional institutional permission was secured from the school administration. Participation was entirely voluntary; written informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were fully briefed on the study’s purpose, procedures, audio recording, and their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence. Confidentiality, anonymity, and secure data storage were guaranteed throughout. The study was conducted in full compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data processing and analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim immediately after each session. Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s [21] six-phase reflexive framework using an inductive approach: themes were derived from the data without reference to a pre-specified coding frame, thereby capturing the full range and texture of participants’ experiences.

The six analytical phases were operationalised as follows. In Phase 1 (Familiarisation), CE, DAF, and AOA transcribed and immersed themselves in the data through repeated reading. In Phase 2 (Initial Code Generation), all three analysts independently generated preliminary codes by identifying recurring words, phrases, and patterns. Codes were organised hierarchically into a coding tree: first-level codes captured concrete phenomena (example, ‘lack of government support’, ‘night wakefulness’, ‘traditional remedy use’); second-level codes captured broader conceptual patterns linking first-level codes like ‘systemic neglect’, ‘sleep disruption and fatigue’, ‘accessible self-care alternatives’). In Phase 3 (Theme Construction), CE and DAF grouped second-level codes sharing coherent conceptual meaning into candidate themes. In Phase 4 (Theme Review), all three analysts and INK assessed internal coherence and fit with the coded data. In Phase 5 (Defining and Naming Themes), all authors engaged in collaborative naming. In Phase 6 (Report Production), CE, INK, NK, and DAF integrated illustrative participant quotes and synthesised findings into a coherent narrative.

Disagreements regarding theme boundaries and code assignments arose at multiple points, most notably whether ‘self-medication’ constituted a distinct self-care subtheme or should be subsumed under coping strategies, and whether ‘cultural knowledge deficit’ was primarily a challenge or a determinant of coping. These were resolved through structured discussion sessions in which each analyst presented transcript evidence supporting their interpretation; decisions were made on the weight of evidence, conceptual clarity, and theoretical coherence. This process is documented in the analytical audit trail. The finalised coding structure generated three overarching themes and nine sub-themes, summarised in Table 2.

Caregivers’ constraints and challenges

Caring for children living with ASD presents significant and multidimensional challenges. Caregivers navigated daily struggles encompassing limited external support, profound fatigue, financial strain, and gaps in knowledge, all of which compromised their ability to sustain consistent, high-quality care.

Poor public assistance

Many caregivers expressed deep frustration with the absence of governmental and community support for families managing ASD care. Participants consistently reported receiving little to no formal assistance, leaving them feeling isolated and abandoned by systems intended to support them.

“There are limited government services, financial support, or community programmes to assist us. All medical care, schooling, and daily needs of the child are handled entirely by the institution.” (P5, staff).

“There are limited government services, financial support, or community programmes to assist us. All medical care, schooling, and daily needs of the child are handled entirely by the institution.” (P5, staff).

“We receive no support from public health or social services, so the family manages all aspects of the child’s care.” (P17, mother).

“We receive no support from public health or social services, so the family manages all aspects of the child’s care.” (P17, mother).

These accounts reflect more than individual dissatisfaction; they signal a systemic structural failure. Ghana’s Persons with Disability Act (Act 715) establishes legal commitments to disability support, yet participants’ narratives indicate a profound gap between legislative intent and lived reality. The near-total dependence on institutional and family self-reliance is both a policy failure and a public health risk: without formal support structures, caregiver burnout is not merely a personal possibility but a structural inevitability. The absence of state support also functions as a form of institutionalised stigma, a signal to caregivers that their children’s needs are not a public priority.

Sleep disruption and caregiver fatigue

Sleep disruption constituted a structured and recurring pattern with direct implications for caregiver health. Children’s sleep difficulties, characterised by shortened sleep duration, delayed sleep onset, and nocturnal wakefulness, created chronic sleep deprivation for caregivers who lacked alternative care support.“I am always told I need rest, every time I go to the hospital.” (P6, staff).“Most of the time he will be awake the whole night – how do I leave him to go and rest? Tell me how, madam? It is just worrisome.” (P16, grandmother).

“I am always told I need rest, every time I go to the hospital.” (P6, staff).

“Most of the time he will be awake the whole night – how do I leave him to go and rest? Tell me how, madam? It is just worrisome.” (P16, grandmother).

Nocturnal wakefulness in children with ASD generates a chronic physiological stressor for caregivers, with cascading consequences for immune function, emotional regulation, and cognitive performance. What is analytically distinctive in this context is the structural dimension: in the absence of respite services or shared caregiving arrangements, no external mechanism exists through which caregivers can access consolidated rest. The sleep problem is therefore simultaneously clinical in its effects and structural in its origins, a distinction with direct implications for intervention design, pointing toward system-level rather than individual-level solutions.

Financial hardship

Financial strain was among the most pervasive and destabilising challenges. Costs associated with ASD care – encompassing therapy, specialised schooling, medication, transportation, and dietary requirements – were described as prohibitive, particularly for caregivers without stable income or formal support.“We wish she could go to school, but the schools are expensive, and they even pay monthly, where is the money to do that? We don’t have any support from anywhere… Even money to eat is a problem, all because of her.” (P12, mother).“Taking care of my child costs a lot, school fees, therapy, and even the special diet. Sometimes I have to borrow money just to keep things going.” (P11, mother).

“We wish she could go to school, but the schools are expensive, and they even pay monthly, where is the money to do that? We don’t have any support from anywhere… Even money to eat is a problem, all because of her.” (P12, mother).

“Taking care of my child costs a lot, school fees, therapy, and even the special diet. Sometimes I have to borrow money just to keep things going.” (P11, mother).

Financial hardship functions as an amplifier of all other caregiving challenges, creating a self-reinforcing cycle: unaffordable therapy and specialised schooling simultaneously deny caregivers the tools for managing their child’s behaviour and force them, predominantly mothers, to reduce or abandon employment, which deepens poverty and heightens burden. This structural economic entrapment also deepens gender inequality by curtailing women’s economic participation and independence. The cumulative effect extends beyond immediate financial difficulty into constrained child development access and long-term caregiver wellbeing.

Inadequate knowledge of ASD and its management

A significant proportion of caregivers reported insufficient understanding of ASD. At the time of diagnosis, many had attributed their child’s behaviours to spiritual or supernatural causes, a reflection of prevalent Ghanaian cultural beliefs, delaying formal diagnosis and appropriate intervention.“At first, I didn’t understand what was wrong with my child. People told me it was a curse or something spiritual. It took a long time before I learned what autism really means.” (P12, mother).“I don’t know what to do. Whatever I do for him, he will still cry. I have read, but I still need more training to be able to handle him.” (P1, staff).

“At first, I didn’t understand what was wrong with my child. People told me it was a curse or something spiritual. It took a long time before I learned what autism really means.” (P12, mother).

“I don’t know what to do. Whatever I do for him, he will still cry. I have read, but I still need more training to be able to handle him.” (P1, staff).

The knowledge deficit documented here is not simply informational; it is epistemological. Caregivers’ initial explanatory frameworks were culturally embedded and functionally coherent within their social contexts, even where they diverged from biomedical understanding. This finding has critical implications for educational programme designed: interventions that simply provide biomedical information without engaging with, and respectfully reframing, existing cultural explanatory models are unlikely to be effective. Culturally adapted, narrative-based education that starts from where caregivers are, not from where educators assume they should be, is more likely to bridge the gap between awareness and actionable knowledge.

Caregivers’ self-care behaviours

Despite the challenges of caregiving, participants described intentional self-care practices shaped by their awareness of the need to remain healthy for their children’s sake. Two key subthemes emerged: prompt health-seeking behaviour and self-medication.

Prompt health-seeking behaviour

Most caregivers described a heightened attentiveness to their own health, driven by what might be conceptualised as ‘protective motivation’, a recognition that their children’s survival and wellbeing depended on their own physical fitness.“When I start feeling weak or dizzy, I don’t wait for it to get worse. I go straight to the hospital because I know my child depends on me. If I fall sick, there will be no one to look after him.” (P14, mother).“I have learnt to pay attention to my body. Once I notice any sign that I’m not feeling well, I seek help immediately because I cannot afford to be down for long.” (P11, mother).“What else do I have to do than go to the hospital? Even the last time I went, they said my BP is high, so I try not to stop taking my BP medicines… I can’t afford to be very sick knowing the kind of responsibility I have towards my ASD child.” (P3, staff).

“When I start feeling weak or dizzy, I don’t wait for it to get worse. I go straight to the hospital because I know my child depends on me. If I fall sick, there will be no one to look after him.” (P14, mother).

The pattern of proactive health-seeking evident here is analytically notable because it contrasts with the ‘caregiver self-neglect’ pattern widely documented in the ASD caregiving literature, particularly in high-income contexts. This divergence may reflect contextual differences: in settings where no respite or substitute care is available, complete caregiver incapacitation is not merely inconvenient but existentially threatening for the child. Health-seeking thus becomes not an act of personal self-care in the conventional sense, but an act of continued caregiving, a form of ‘instrumental self-care’ motivated primarily by duty rather than personal wellbeing. This conceptual reframing has significant implications for how LMIC-specific caregiver support models should be theorised.

Self-medication (orthodox and traditional)

While some caregivers prioritised formal health-seeking, others resorted to self-medication, using both over-the-counter orthodox medications and traditional herbal remedies, as a more accessible, affordable, and time-efficient alternative. Self-medication was driven by structural barriers, including long hospital wait times, financial constraints, and the logistical difficulty of attending crowded health facilities with a child with ASD.“I prefer going to the drug store to buy medicines rather than going to the hospital. That way I won’t have to queue, it’s easier and affordable… Imagine being sick and chasing your child around a whole hospital, and everyone will be looking at you. He can’t even talk, so seeing the crowd, he will be crying until we leave the place.” (P11, staff).“I don’t like the hospital drugs, so when I pass behind the building, I go into the bush and get myself some herbs to treat my ailments.” (P15, mother).

Self-medication in this context is not primarily a health literacy failure; it is a rational adaptive response to structural barriers. The logistical impossibility of managing a child with ASD in a busy public health facility, combined with financial constraints and time pressure, makes informal self-medication the only practically accessible option for many caregivers. However, the dual use of traditional herbal remedies and orthodox medications raises clinically significant risks of herb-drug interactions and delayed formal diagnosis, underscoring the need for healthcare system adaptations, including disability-friendly facility designs, dedicated appointment tracks for ASD caregivers, and community health worker outreach, to make formal care more accessible.

Coping strategies

Participants employed a range of adaptive strategies to manage the psychological and physical demands of ASD caregiving. Three sub-themes were identified: seeking emotional and social support, engaging in diversionary activities, and applying relaxation techniques. These strategies were not mutually exclusive; caregivers often employed multiple strategies fluidly, depending on the nature and intensity of the stressor. Collectively, they reflect a repertoire of emotion-focused coping that is structurally constrained yet resourceful.

Seeking emotional and social support

The most consistently reported coping strategy was seeking emotional support from family members, friends, religious leaders, and social networks. Religious and spiritual coping was particularly salient, providing emotional relief and a sense of transcendent hope and meaning.

“I talk to my sisters; they encourage me, and at the end, they make us watch funny videos. I find some videos that make me forget about what is even happening to me, I laugh my heart out. They have been of great help to me.” (P15, mother).

Theoretically, these findings align with Lazarus and Folkman’s [18] emotion-focused coping: caregivers regulate the emotional distress of caregiving rather than directly altering the stressor, a strategy particularly adaptive when structural barriers render problem-focused solutions inaccessible. Social and religious support in this context serves multiple intersecting functions: it provides emotional validation, reduces the subjective sense of isolation, and offers cognitive reframing through spiritual meaning-making. The use of social media and digital humour (for example, funny videos) as an emerging coping modality is analytically significant: it represents a low-cost, temporally flexible, and individually controllable coping resource that does not require physical mobility or financial expenditure, making it particularly accessible to caregivers whose time and resources are severely constrained. This finding has implications for digital health interventions targeting caregiver wellbeing in low-resource settings.

Most caregivers reported engaging in diversionary activities, consciously or opportunistically, to achieve temporary relief from caregiving demands and restore emotional equilibrium.

These activities were undertaken opportunistically rather than as structured self-care routines, reflecting caregivers’ severely constrained temporal and financial circumstances. Nature-based activities (farm walks, vegetation observation) align with attention restoration theory and evidence linking nature exposure to stress reduction and cognitive restoration. Physical exercise is consistent with the established evidence base on exercise and caregiver mental health. The fact that exercise was conducted in the narrow pre-waking window underscores how thoroughly caregiving demands structurally eliminate personal time, a reality that limits the viability of conventional self-care programme models in this context.

Applying relaxation techniques

Participants described the deliberate use of brief rest and relaxation as a strategy to manage fatigue and sustain caregiving capacity. Given chronic sleep disruption, structured rest – even in short, fragmented episodes, was understood by caregivers as essential.“I leave everything and lie intermittently, even though he might be awake. These micro-rest episodes, taken in proximity to the child without full disengagement from caregiving vigilance, represents a form of adaptive resilience within structurally constrained conditions. However, they also expose the inadequacy of informal coping: fragmented rest is not a substitute for consolidated, restorative sleep, and the reliance on improved palliation in the absence of formal respite provision means caregivers are managing a chronic physiological deficit rather than addressing it. This reinforces the imperative for formal, accessible respite care services that would enable caregivers to achieve genuine recovery.

Discussion

This study explored the challenges and coping experiences of family and institutional caregivers of children with ASD in the Central Region of Ghana, providing contextually grounded evidence from a setting that remains underrepresented in the global caregiving literature. The findings reveal a consistent pattern of multidimensional burden, characterised by poor public assistance, sleep disruption, financial hardship, knowledge deficits, and limited self-care capacity, that mirrors evidence from comparable settings across sub-Saharan Africa and the broader LMIC literature [14, 22, 23].

The near-total absence of formal public support reported by participants corroborates Appah et al. [13], who documented social exclusion and minimal welfare access among Ghanaian ASD caregivers despite the existence of enabling legislation. Kisangala et al.’s [23] systematic review similarly noted sub-Saharan African caregivers’ heavy dependence on informal networks due to weak state structures and poor policy implementation. What the present study adds is a more granular account of how this structural deficit plays out in practice: caregivers do not merely ‘lack support’ in the abstract; they must personally absorb every cost and responsibility – financial, physical, informational, and emotional – that formal systems would otherwise provide. The policy implication is not merely increased resourcing but a fundamental reimaging of how disability support is organised, delivered, and accountable in Ghana.

The sleep disruption findings warrant particular analytical attention. Caregivers’ sleep deprivation was mechanistically linked to the characteristic nocturnal profile of their children with ASD, shortened total sleep time, delayed onset, and phase-shift problems, consistent with Wang et al.’s [24] documentation of significantly greater sleep disturbances in children with ASD compared to typically developing peers. The clinical implications are substantial: chronic sleep deprivation undermines immune function, emotional regulation, and cognitive performance, elevating long-term risks of burnout and depression [24]. In the Ghanaian context, where respite services are virtually non-existent, the sleep problem is simultaneously clinical in its manifestation and structural in its causation, a distinction that points towards system-level rather than individual-level solutions.

Financial stress emerged as one of the most pervasive burdens, consistent with Appah et al. [13] and Catalano et al. [25], who documented the prohibitive costs of therapy, medication, transportation, and specialised schooling in low-income ASD caregiving contexts. In Ghana, out-of-pocket expenditure constitutes the primary financing mechanism for disability care [26], with severe equity implications [27]. Critically, the economic toll was amplified by caregivers’ reduction or cessation of employment, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of poverty and heightened burden with direct consequences for child development access and caregiver wellbeing.

The cultural knowledge deficit, wherein ASD was attributed to spiritual or supernatural causes, mirrors findings by Oti-Boadi [11] and Oti-Boadi et al. [12] on supernatural explanatory models among Ghanaian caregivers. Beyond its role in delaying diagnosis, this finding invites a deeper interpretive engagement: supernatural aetiological explanations are not simply informational errors to be corrected; they are culturally embedded meaning-making frameworks that serve important psychological and social functions, including the management of causal uncertainty and the preservation of family honour. Shamsaei et al. [28] have demonstrated that culturally adapted training initiatives can significantly improve caregivers’ competence and reduce stigma-related barriers; the implication is that effective educational interventions must start from respectful engagement with existing explanatory frameworks, not from their wholesale dismissal.

Caregivers’ prompt health-seeking behaviour, conceptualised as ‘instrumental self-care’, represents a theoretically significant departure from the self-neglect pattern documented in high-income ASD caregiving contexts [29]. When no formal substitute care exists, caregiver health is not a personal luxury but a prerequisite for the child’s survival. This insight challenges the direct application of high-income caregiving frameworks to LMIC settings and highlights the need for contextually grounded, LMIC-specific conceptual models of caregiver behaviour. The high rate of self-medication, encompassing both orthodox and traditional remedies, is consistent with Ghanaian and broader sub-Saharan African patterns [30–32] and constitutes a rational adaptive response to structural barriers rather than a health literacy deficit. Gyasi et al. [32] note that traditional remedies are regarded as culturally meaningful and accessible, particularly when formal healthcare experiences are stigmatizing. The concurrent use of herbal and orthodox remedies carries clinically significant herb-drug risks [33] that healthcare systems must proactively address through targeted provider education and community outreach.

With respect to coping strategies, caregivers’ reliance on emotional and social support, through family networks, religious communities, and pastoral counselling, is consistent with established ASD caregiving literature [34–36]. Social support buffers distress through empathy, reassurance, and practical assistance, while religious coping provides meaning-making and transcendent hope within Ghanaian cultural frameworks [35]. The emerging use of digital platforms for humour and virtual community represents a low-cost coping modality with implications for digital health intervention design in LMIC contexts. Diversionary activities and relaxation techniques align with evidence on physical activity, nature exposure, and rest for caregiver wellbeing [37–39], however, their improvised, marginal character, undertaken in the narrow margins of caregiving rather than as deliberate scheduled routines, distinguishes them from the structured self-care programmes documented in high-income settings [40, 41], emphasising how structural constraints shape both the severity of caregiving challenges and the repertoire of adaptive responses available.

Theoretically, this study extends Lazarus and Folkman’s [18] transactional stress-coping model by demonstrating that in contexts of acute structural deprivation, the boundary between problem-focused and emotion-focused coping dissolves: caregivers’ health-seeking behaviour is simultaneously an emotional response to vulnerability and a problem-focused act of ensuring the child’s survival. This blurring of coping modalities challenges the applicability of high-income coping typologies to LMIC settings and points towards the need for context-sensitive theoretical frameworks. This study contributes to the ASD caregiving literature in three analytically distinct ways. First, it provides empirically grounded documentation of the specific, compounding challenges faced by both family and institutional caregivers in a West African LMIC context, extending an evidence base predominantly generated in high-income and East African settings. Second, it demonstrates that caregiver self-care behaviours in this context are not primarily expressions of personal health valuation but rather structurally shaped, instrumentally motivated adaptations to the absence of formal support, a conceptual reframing with significant theoretical and policy implications. Third, it reveals the layered, intersecting character of coping: strategies employed by caregivers simultaneously address emotional, physical, and relational dimensions of burden and are constrained or enabled by the same structural factors (financial resources, available time, social networks) that determine the severity of caregiving challenges in the first place.

Recommendations

It is recommended that the Ministry of Health and the Department of Social Welfare develop targeted support programmes for families of children with ASD, including financial aid, therapy services, and respite care to ease economic and emotional strain. Regular training for health professionals, teachers, and caregivers on autism management and early intervention should be prioritised to improve confidence and caregiving skills. Public education campaigns are also needed to dispel myths, reduce stigma, and encourage social inclusion of children with ASD. Healthcare facilities should implement disability-friendly designs, dedicated appointment pathways, and community health worker outreach to make formal care structurally accessible for ASD caregivers. Additionally, collaboration among community leaders, faith-based organisations, and NGOs should be strengthened to establish support networks that offer counselling and shared learning opportunities for caregivers. Finally, further research is essential to examine the long-term psychosocial impacts of caregiving and assess the effectiveness of support interventions, particularly in rural and underserved areas of Ghana.

Conclusion

This study documents three analytically interconnected dimensions of ASD caregiving in Ghana: the structural challenges that define and constrain caregivers’ daily lives; the instrumentally motivated self-care behaviours that enable them to sustain their role; and the adaptive coping strategies – primarily social, spiritual, and physical – that provide emotional restoration within severely resource-constrained conditions. Viewed through Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional framework, the findings reveal that structural deprivation reshapes the very nature of coping, collapsing the distinction between problem-focused and emotion-focused strategies into a unified imperative of survival-oriented self-maintenance. A key conceptual finding is that caregivers’ apparently proactive health behaviours are not merely expressions of personal health valuation, but structurally necessitated responses to the absence of formal support: when no substitute care exists, a caregiver’s physical capacity is their child’s lifeline. The study also establishes that the central challenge of ASD caregiving in Ghana is not primarily attitudinal or behavioural at the individual level, but structural at the systems level: the near-complete absence of formal public support, accessible respite services, and affordable specialist care creates conditions under which caregiver burnout is not an individual risk but an organisational certainty. Addressing this requires fundamental policy reform, not only awareness campaigns. Culturally adapted caregiver education, multi-sectoral collaboration, and meaningful implementation of existing disability legislation are among the most urgent priorities identified by this study.

Limitations

This study was limited by its small sample size and focus on caregivers from a single region, which may affect the generalisability of the findings to other parts of Ghana. The qualitative design also means the findings provide depth of understanding rather than statistical representation. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into the lived experiences and coping strategies of caregivers of children with ASD.
