Implicit rationing of nursing care in Turkish public nursing homes and rehabilitation centres: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Şirin Özkan, Zeynep Acar, Güven Özkaya, Franziska Zúñiga, Lisa Geyskens, Mieke Deschodt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImplicit rationing of nursing care refers to situations in which healthcare workers must delay, postpone, or omit required nursing activities due to limited resources. İmplicit rationing of nursing care is particularly important in nursing homes, where it is associated with poorer resident outcomes, increased adverse events, preventable hospitalisations, and negative consequences for care providers such as emotional exhaustion and job dissatisfaction. This study aimed to describe the prevalence and types of implicit rationing of nursing care in public nursing homes and rehabilitation centers (NHRC) in Türkiye, and to analyse its associations with nurses' and geriatric care workers' professional experience, work experience in the institution, and geriatric care training level.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in six NHRCs across different provinces in Türkiye. The Basel Extent of Rationing of Nursing Care for Nursing Homes (BERNCA-NH) instrument was used to assess the frequency of implicit rationing of nursing care. Categorical variables were summarised with frequencies and percentages, continuous variables with means and standard deviations, and responses to the open-ended question were analysed using qualitative content analysis.ResultsOf the 489 eligible nurses and geriatric care workers, 280 (57%) participated in the study. Rationing of nursing care rates ranged from 9.3% to 51.9% across different activities. Assisting with eating or drinking was the least rationed activity, while social care activities, such as "cultural activity for residents with contact outside of the nursing home", were rationed the most. Significant associations were found between implicit rationing of activities and characteristics such as total professional experience, working experience at the same institution, and level of geriatric care training. The main reasons for implicit rationing included tasks out of professional scope, heavy workloads, insufficient staffing, and low motivation.ConclusionsImplicit rationing of nursing care remains a challenge in NHRCs in Türkiye. To reduce the implicit rationing of nursing care, the roles and responsibilities of care workers with different training levels may need clearer definition in nursing homes, and their competencies can be enhanced through in-service training programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07576-7.

Introduction

The higher demand for long-term care and the increasingly complex care needs of an ageing population is a challenge for many healthcare systems [39]. In contrast to the higher care needs, there is an increasing shortage of healthcare workers across all settings, and finding sufficient care workers specialised in geriatric care is even more difficult [5, 6, 14]. With higher care needs and fewer staff, many of the active healthcare workers experience a higher workload. As a result, they are more likely to have to delay or are unable to fulfil some of their care responsibilities [42].

In recent years, rationing of nursing care has been studied under different names. Although the underlying phenomenon is the same, there is no international consensus on the term [4]. Commonly used terms include unfinished care [35], prioritisation [15], missed care [19], implicit rationing care [34], unmet nursing needs [23] and omitted nursing care [11, 13]. Across all definitions, the shared element is the delay, postponement, or omission of required nursing care due to insufficient resources [24, 25]. In this study, we will use the term "implicit rationing of nursing care" to refer to individual healthcare workers making in-the-moment decisions to withhold or postpone certain nursing activities due to limited resources, without predefined criteria or transparency to patients [44].

Implicit rationing of nursing care is of paramount importance to clinical practice, as evidence has shown that it negatively affects patient outcomes and threatens quality of care and patient safety [16, 31]. Implicit rationing of nursing care has shown to negatively affect the quality of (resident-centered) nursing home care, residents’ mortality and adverse events, such as the occurrence of geriatric syndromes (e.g. cognitive or functional decline, depression, falls, incontinence, malnutrition, pressure ulcers), medical comorbidities (e.g. cardiovascular events, respiratory and urinary tract infections, dehydration), loneliness, and preventable hospitalisations [28, 31]. For care providers, rationing of nursing care is associated with emotional exhaustion [9, 38], depersonalisation, job dissatisfaction, and engagement in multiple jobs [8, 37].

Implicit rationing of nursing care was initially studied in hospitals and later extended to nursing homes. A study in Canadian nursing homes reported that 75% of healthcare aides rationed at least one care task in their last shift [21]. In US nursing homes, White et al. [38] found that 75% of nurses missed one or more care tasks in their last shift due to a lack of time or resources, and Nelson & Flynn [26] reported that this occurred in 48.2% of nurses. The study of Hackman et al. [12] in Finnish nursing homes reported that 92% of care workers (i.e., nurses and nurse aids) sometimes or often rationed at least one nursing activity in the last seven working days. The most frequently rationed activities in nursing homes were social care activities, such as talking to residents [38] and planned group activities [5, 12]. The least neglected activities were related to activities of daily living [9, 41].

The high dependency of nursing home residents on care workers makes them more vulnerable. Therefore, health managers, health professionals, and policymakers need more information about rationed nursing care to improve the quality of life for nursing home residents [12]. Measuring the implicit rationing of nursing care for nursing home residents at specific time intervals can serve as an evaluation criterion to improve patient safety and quality of care [30]. However, observing the field to identify implicit rationing of nursing care in daily care requires significant resources and is not feasible, as some processes that cannot be directly observed may be implicitly rationed [30]. Therefore, it may be effective to use measurement tools that are already available to assess implicit rationing care activities among nurses in a nursing home setting, at least based on employee self-reports. If we can measure the nursing care rationing, we can identify the types and frequency of care rationing and develop targeted interventions to improve residents' quality of life [44].

Türkiye is experiencing rapid population ageing, with the proportion of adults aged 65 + rising from 8.5% in 2017 to 9.9% in 2022, increasing demand for residential geriatric care (TUIK, 2022) [29]. Accordingly, since the quality of care is a crucial factor affecting the health of older people, there is a need to evaluate the quality of nursing care provided in nursing homes and rehabilitation centers, which offer healthcare services exclusively to older people, in all aspects [1]. Although rationing has been studied in hospitals (Kalisch et al., 2014) [40], no research has examined implicit rationing in Türkiye’s residential care facilities.

Therefore, this study provides the first evidence on implicit rationing of nursing care in Turkish nursing homes using a validated measurement tool. It also contributes internationally by examining how experience, tenure, and geriatric training relate to rationing patterns—an understudied combination in nursing home research. The study aimed to describe the prevalence and type of implicit rationing of nursing care reported by nurses and geriatric care workers in public nursing homes and rehabilitation centers (NHRC) in Türkiye. Secondly, we explored whether educational background and working experience were associated with implicit rationing of nursing care activities.

Methodology

Study design and setting

Using a convenience sampling approach, this cross-sectional study was conducted in six NHRCs located in six different provinces in Türkiye. Because nursing homes in Türkiye are scarce, with often only one nursing home per province, we conducted the study in the provinces with the highest number of nurses and geriatric care workers, as reported by the NHRC. In Türkiye, public “nursing homes” primarily accommodate older adults who can perform basic activities of daily living independently. In contrast, NHRCs serve residents experiencing physical or functional decline and therefore require support, protection or rehabilitative services. As residents in NHRCs typically have care needs and require support, only NHRCs were included in the scope of this study.

Sample

The study encompassed nurses and geriatric care workers responsible for providing care to the residents. In Türkiye, the title of ‘nurse’ is recognised for those who have completed at least four years of undergraduate nursing education, corresponding to registered nurses. To work as a geriatric care worker in a nursing home, individuals must meet one of the following qualifications; 1) Graduation from a 2-year Geriatric Care Services program at a university and hold a university diploma; or 2) If only a high school diploma or a university degree outside the health field is obtained, at least 560 h of geriatric care training with certificate in geriatric care must be obtained. In accordance with the legislation, nursing homes are required to assign at least one nurse for every 50 older residents and one geriatric care worker for every 15 residents [32]. A later regulation [33] stipulates that institutions should employ an adequate number of personnel, including managers, social workers, psychologists, physicians, nurses, geriatric care workers, and support staff, based on the size of the facility. This regulation briefly outlines the job descriptions for personnel. In our sample, an NHRC with around 100 older residents employs an average of 6 nurses and 20 geriatric care workers. All nurses and geriatric care workers who worked in NHRC and who could be reached through an online connection were invited to participate in the study.

Data collection

The data were collected between June and September 2023 through an online survey. The questionnaire and consent form were transferred to Google Forms. A survey link was sent to a contact person, and the managers at each included NHRC, who shared it with all eligible nurses and geriatric care workers via e-mail and/or instant messaging platforms (WhatsApp). Before starting the online survey, participants read the study information form and were asked to provide informed consent for their voluntary involvement. Additionally, printed versions of the questionnaires and consent forms were sent to facilities for nurses and geriatric care workers who preferred to complete the questionnaire on paper. Assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a finite population of N = 489 nurses and geriatric care workers, the required sample size was n = 216. This corresponds to a minimum response rate of 216/489 = 44.2%.

Measures

Demographic variables

The following demographic variables were collected: age, gender, total professional experience, working experience in the same institution, weekly working hours, shift pattern (daytime, always nighttime, shift work), geriatric care training level, and average income (monthly).

The Basel Extent of Rationing of Nursing Care for Nursing Homes instrument (BERNCA-NH)

The BERNCA-NH instrument developed by Zúñiga et al. [44] was used to determine the frequency of implicit rationing of nursing care in nursing homes due to time constraints. The instrument contains 21 nursing care activities that are structured in four sub-dimensions, including (i) activities of daily living (items 1–5); (ii) care, rehabilitation, and monitoring (items 6–15); (iii) documentation (items 16–18); and (iv) social care (items 19–21). In the instrument, nurses and geriatric care workers are asked how often they were unable to perform care activities due to a lack of time in the last seven working days. The answer options are ‘never’, ‘seldom’, ‘sometimes’, ‘often’, ‘activity was not necessary’, and ‘not within my field of responsibility’. Cronbach's alpha coefficient for all subscales ranged between 0.77 and 0.89 [44]. (See Supplementary Materials).

We also asked two open-ended questions: Are there any activities you do that are different from those included in BERNCA-NH activities? and 2) Do you have anything to add about implicit rationing of nursing care?”.

Translation and cultural adaptation process

The BERNCA-NH was translated and culturally adapted into Turkish using a standard procedure for the cross-cultural adaptation of self-report scales [7, 17]. After obtaining permission for the translation into Turkish from the original authors of BERNCA-NH, we followed six additional steps.I.Initial translation: Two native Turkish translators translated the BERNCA-NH from English to Turkish. The interpreters, both experts in health sciences, were blinded from each other’s translations.II.Synthesis of translations: The two translations of the instrument were compared by the researchers (ŞÖ, ZA) and a Turkish language expert. Two researchers with a nursing background (ŞÖ, ZA) combined the two Turkish translations into a single form. The semantic, conceptual, linguistic, and contextual differences were evaluated, and a common decision was made for the translation of each item, resulting in the first draft of the Turkish instrument.III.Back-translation: Two nursing experts, who were blind to the original version and not involved in the initial process, independently conducted back-translation of the first draft from Turkish to English. The two translated texts were compared by the back translators and the researchers (ŞÖ, ZA), and an English draft of the instrument was agreed upon. The original instrument and the Turkish and English versions obtained after translation were then evaluated in terms of conceptual and cultural equivalence.IV.Expert panel: To test the content validity of the original instrument, whose linguistic validity had been examined, the draft instrument was sent to 20 nursing academics. They compared the Turkish version of each item with the original text in terms of content and evaluated it according to the Davis technique as "1 = not appropriate, 2 = the item needs to be adapted, 3 = appropriate, but minor changes are required, 4 = very appropriate" (Davis, 1992). In line with the recommendations of the experts, the items in the instrument were finalised, and content validity ratios and content validity index were calculated.V.Pilot test and cognitive analysis: This Turkish BERNCA-NH draft was administered face-to-face to 30 healthcare workers in NHRC to test the instrument's comprehensibility across different occupational groups. They were asked whether there were any incomprehensible expressions, and if so, they were asked for their suggestions.VI.Final documentation: The results from the pilot test were analyzed by the first and second translation groups, and subsequently, the Turkish version of the BERNCA-NH was finalized [Additional file 1: BERNCA-NH in Turkish].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS ver.28.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2021. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics were reported as numbers with percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables.

For calculating implicit rationing of nursing care, the answer options "not within my field of responsibility” and “activity was not necessary” were merged and defined as “not applicable”, following the analysis approach used in previous studies [3, 12, 27, 44]. The responses categorised as “sometimes” and “often” were then calculated as percentages, representing the frequency with which these activities were rationed.

The normality of the continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), along with frequencies and percentages. A comparison of care activities was performed based on total professional experience, working experience in the same institution, and geriatric care training level using Pearson chi-square and Fisher-Freeman-Halton tests. The Bonferroni test was used for pairwise comparison. No multivariable adjustment was performed; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory rather than independent or causal relationships.Total professional experience may be associated with greater familiarity with patient care standards, improve efficiency, and strengthen problem-solving skills, which could contribute to the rationalisation of nursing care activities. Likewise, longer tenure within the same organisation may be linked to increased knowledge of institutional policies, workflow processes, and team collaboration. In addition, the level of geriatric care education may be related to competencies in performing complex care tasks and applying evidence-based practices. However, given the bivariate nature of the analyses, these interpretations should be considered cautiously and not as evidence of independent effects.

Qualitative content analysis was used for the data obtained from the open-ended question. The qualitative analysis was conducted by two independent researchers who first coded the responses separately. They then compared codes, discussed any discrepancies, and reached consensus to ensure consistency and reliability across all stages of the analysis. An inductive approach was used to identify similarities and differences. The original statements were simplified and sub-categorised according to their content. Then, subcategories with similar content were grouped into main categories [22].

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University (Approval No: 230003/36, Date: 02.03.2023). Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. All participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, assured of confidentiality and anonymity, and notified of their right to withdraw at any time without any consequences.

Experience within the same institution was significantly and negatively associated with activities of daily living, including "oral/dental hygiene," "assist to drink or eat," and "providing patients with food at times other than regular meals." Additionally, it was significantly negative associated with activities under the Care, Rehabilitation, and Monitoring subheading, including "monitoring residents as needed" and "supervising confused or cognitively impaired residents, as well as the use of restraints/sedatives." Furthermore, it was negatively associated with tasks under the documentation subheading, such as "reviewing care plans at the beginning of shifts" and "creating or updating residents’ care plans."

The level of geriatric care training demonstrated significant associations with activities of daily living such as "sponge bath, partial sponge bath, and/or skin care," "oral/dental hygiene," and "providing patients with food outside of regular meal times.", Respondents with certificate-level geriatric care training reported lower percentages of "sometimes & often" compared to those with training at the associate degree level. In the Activities of Daily Living subheading, except for the "mobilise/change the position" item, the percentage of geriatric care training graduates who answered "sometimes & often" was higher compared to certificate-level geriatric care training graduates, while registered nurses generally showed similarities with both groups. Similarly, in the Care, Rehabilitation, and Monitoring subheading, for items with significant differences, the proportion of geriatric care training graduates was again higher than that of certificate-level geriatric care training graduates, whereas bachelor's degree registered nurses generally exhibited similarities with both groups. In the Documentation subheading, the proportion of associate degree graduates was higher compared to certificate programme graduates. However, no significant differences were found in terms of the Social Care subheading.

In summary, the frequency of responses indicating "sometimes & often" decreased in correlation with greater total professional experience and longer tenure within the same institution. However, the proportion of the "sometimes & often" responses also declined as the duration of education shortened.

Discussion

This study aimed to describe the prevalence and type of implicit rationing of nursing care reported by nurses and geriatric care workers in NHRC. To our knowledge, this was the first study investigating the phenomenon of nursing care rationing within NHRC in Türkiye. In this study, the nursing care activity with the highest frequency of implicit rationing was ‘Cultural activities that nursing home residents can contact outside the nursing home,’ reported to occur frequently, with a rate of 51.9%, which is slightly higher compared to other studies Studies from various countries using the BERNCA-NH instrument have reported that implicit rationing of nursing care activities occurs in nursing homes with varying frequency. Care activities were reported as rationed either often or at least once during the previous seven working days, with prevalence rates ranging from 22.8% to 92%. [5, 9, 12, 41].

Our study revealed that social care activities are the most common implicit rationing activities under time constraints for health care workers in Türkiye. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that implicit rationing of social care activities is most prevalent in nursing home settings across various countries. In Sweden, "scheduled group activity" and "single activity with care recipients" are often rationed [3]. Similarly, in Finland, social care is frequently rationed, which may affect residents' quality of life [12]. In western Canadian nursing homes, interactions with residents are often rushed or overlooked [21]. In Shanghai, China, "cultural activities outside the nursing home" are commonly rationed [41], and documentation and social care are frequently rationed in Switzerland [43]. Comparing our findings with those of international studies suggests that similar patterns emerge across different contexts. In particular, social and psychosocial care activities appear to be disproportionately vulnerable to omission when staffing levels are insufficient or workloads are high.

In this study, the fact that the least rationed activities fell within the scope of ‘activities of daily living’ and ‘care, rehabilitation, and monitoring’ may reflect that nurses and geriatric care workers prioritised residents' basic living activities in the presence of heavy workloads and staff shortages. However, rationing social activities can heighten feelings of isolation and loneliness, which in turn may reduce psychosocial well-being and decrease the overall quality of life for nursing home residents [12, 28]. This indicates that clinical practice requires the integration of psychosocial care as an essential part of daily routines, rather than treating it as optional or secondary.

It was found that respondents with longer overall professional experience and experience within the same institution reported a lower incidence of implicit rationing in activities of daily living. This may be due to professionals with longer experience within the same institution may have developed more effective strategies for managing care and organisational demands. As a result, they may be better able to balance their workload and report lower levels of implicit care rationing. Similarly, other studies have found that less experience plays a decisive role in care workers ' rationing of nursing care [21, 41]. It is recommended that nurse leaders consider individual characteristics and limited professional experience during performance appraisals, as this may help identify strategies to minimise implicit care rationing [12]. Respondents with a 6-month geriatric care certificate reported a lower frequency of implicit care rationing compared to those with a 2-year associate degree in geriatric care services. Those with an associate's degree may be more likely had a better understanding of residents' care needs, making them more sensitive to identifying and reporting instances where care could not be completed. This finding may also reflect differences in reporting bias rather than actual differences in care rationing. Care workers with shorter training may underrecognise or underreport such situations due to differences in professional role perception or awareness of care standards. Studies on rationing of care have similarly suggested that awareness of care rationing increases with education and professional socialisation [20, 41]. Higher education may increase awareness with professoinal care standards and the potential consequences of rasioning care, thereby enhancing the ability to recognise situations in which necessary care is not delivered. Nurses with higher education reported more missed nursing care, suggesting greater awareness of the consequences of rationing nursing care [10]. Therefore, lower reported levels of rationing may reflect differences in awareness, role expectations, or reporting practices among care workers with different educational backgrounds. Nursing home managers may clarify the professional roles of geriatric care workers according to their educational background and increase the knowledge and skills of these workers through in-service training programs.

Care workers responding to the open-ended question indicated that their inability to perform the activities was primarily due to challenging working conditions. Even in the absence of direct inquiries, nurses and geriatric care workers may feel compelled to spontaneously explain the reasons for activities they cannot perform, driven by conscientious motives [36]. The most frequently cited reasons for rationing nursing care in this study included tasks outside the scope of practice, heavy workload, and staffing shortages. Similarly, findings from another study indicated that workload and work-related stress among nursing home staff in Türkiye during the COVID-19 pandemic were correlated with work organisation, emotional demands, and staff relationships [2]. From a structural perspective, “nursing homes and rehabilitation centers” typically serve residents with higher levels of physical dependency and more complex care needs than standard nursing homes. These structural characteristics may increase staff workload and the demand for time-intensive care activities. Under such conditions, staff may be required to prioritise certain tasks over others, which may contribute to the occurrence of implicit rationing of care. Nursing home managers can ease this burden by delegating tasks outside the scope of practice to non-care staff instead of demanding them from trained care personnel. Additionally, regular monitoring and awareness-raising can help reduce implicit rationing of nursing care, thereby improving the working environment and quality of care in nursing homes. Also, there is a need for further research into the working environment, staffing, outcomes for care workers, and the causes of implicit rationing of nursing care.

The BERNCA-NH instrument was translated into Turkish and culturally adapted. However, a relatively large proportion of “intervention was not necessary” and “not within my field of responsibility” responses prevented the factor analysis from being performed, as these options—introduced in the latest versions of the instrument to accommodate heterogeneous staff groups—are treated as “not applicable” and must be excluded entirely from psychometric analyses [3, 27]. In our dataset, the high frequency of such responses resulted in insufficient analyzable data per item, making factor analysis statistically inappropriate and unreliable. For this reason, we deliberately refrained from computing composite or overall scores and restricted our analyses to item-level descriptive and comparative evaluations. The proportion of not applicable responses might have been high for two reasons. First, some activities are included in the legally job description of nurses and geriatric care workers, but they do not perform these activities in practice. For example, in practice, only certain health personnel are responsible for carrying out specific activities, such as social care activities, which social workers and psychologists primarily perform. Also, the two most common activities described as “not within my field of responsibility” tasks in the BERNCA-NH questionnaire, such as "changing/applying necessary wound dressings" and "administering prescribed medication at the recommended time", are legally permitted only for registered nurses in Türkiye. In addition, with the number of nursing homes across the country (OECD 2021), the number of registered nurses working in these institutions is inadequate. Moreover, geriatric nursing is a relatively new specialty, and there are not enough geriatric nurses nationwide. The few available geriatric nurses are mostly employed in hospitals. The limited presence of registered nurses in NHRC may have contributed to the high rate of not applicable responses. The second reason may stem from the certificate level of geriatric care training. The 6-month training programme for care workers may have focused more on daily care activities, rehabilitation, and monitoring. It may not have adequately addressed the importance of social care activities, which were most often considered not applicable.

Implications for clinical practice and policy

Our findings have important implications for clinical practice and political decision-makers. From a clinical perspective, they emphasise the importance of balancing physical and psychosocial care, as well as strengthening in-service training and task redistribution. They also show that we need to encourage multidisciplinary collaboration and provide regular geriatric training that is tailored to staff's levels of experience. Identifying rationed care can also support the early detection of residents at risk of loneliness, functional decline or preventable hospitalisation, enabling more proactive care planning.

Politically, our findings emphasise the need to address staffing shortages, clarify professional roles and invest in geriatric nursing as a speciality. Policymakers should consider developing national workforce strategies to increase the number of registered nurses in NHRCs, define minimum staffing and skill mix standards, and expand certified geriatric care programmes.

Limitations

Several limitations must be considered. Firstly, due to the cross-sectional design of our study, the findings are not suitable for inferring causality. Secondly, since our data were collected exclusively from large public nursing home settings, the generalizability of these findings to private and small nursing home settings may be limited. Thirdly, there is an inherent risk of under-reporting. Data were collected through online self-administered questionnaires, and participant recruitment was voluntary, which may have introduced response bias. Individuals with more favourable attitudes may have been more inclined to participate.

Also, the analytical strategy was limited to bivariate analyses, and no multivariable adjustment was performed. Therefore, potential confounding variables such as age, shift work patterns, weekly working hours, and professional role were not controlled for. As a result, the observed associations cannot be interpreted as independent effects. The findings should be understood as descriptive and exploratory rather than causal. The cross-sectional design of the study prevents any inference about the directionality of the relationships observed.

Furthermore, given the sensitive nature of the topic, participants may find it challenging to respond to questions about tasks they cannot perform, even if they are expected to, whether intentionally or unintentionally. Consequently, they may be hesitant to accurately report the extent of rationing, which could lead to underreporting. Care workers are often aware that care is being implicitly rationed and may be hesitant to openly discuss implicit rationing of nursing care due to feelings of guilt, powerlessness, and fear, which can lead to it remaining a "secret" [18]. In addition, differences in educational background may influence awareness and reporting of omitted care. Care workers with higher levels of training may be more likely to recognise and report instances of implicit rationing, whereas those with shorter training may underrecognise such situations. Therefore, lower reported levels of rationing among some groups should be interpreted cautiously.

In addition, despite the study's coverage of six provinces, achieving a broader geographical distribution was impeded by the limited number of nursing homes at the national level. Another limitation of this study is that the scale used to assess the constructs of interest has not yet undergone a full psychometric validation in the Turkish context. In line with this, we did not compute any overall scale scores and restricted our analyses to item-level assessments. Future research with larger samples of nurses and geriatric care workers is needed to perform a comprehensive cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of this instrument. The relatively high proportion of responses categorized as “not within my field of responsibility” suggests potential challenges regarding the contextual applicability of the BERNCA-NH instrument in the Turkish nursing home setting. The present study included care workers with different educational backgrounds and professional roles. In settings characterised by a highly unbalanced skill mix, certain care activities may not fall within the responsibilities of all staff categories, which may influence the content validity of some items and the interpretation of responses. Finally, it should be noted that the BERNCA-NH measures health care workers' perceived experiences of implicit rationing, rather than providing an objective assessment of rationing care.

Conclusion

This study presents the initial empirical evidence regarding the frequency of implicit care rationing in public nursing home settings in Türkiye. The findings indicate that social care activities are among the most commonly implicit rationing tasks. In addition, participants with more overall professional experience and longer tenure at the same institution reported a lower frequency of implicit rationing in activities. Also, participants with a shorter duration of geriatric care training reported a lower frequency of rationalisation of care. To reduce the implicit rationing of nursing care, the roles and responsibilities of care workers with different training levels may need clearer definition in nursing homes, and their competencies can be enhanced through in-service training programs. Future research endeavours could prioritise the exploration of (1) the underlying reasons for implicit care rationing within the national context (2) the impact of implicit care rationing on healthcare personnel, residents, quality of care, and health outcomes, and (3) identifying specific working conditions in nursing homes that require improvement to mitigate implicit care rationing.
