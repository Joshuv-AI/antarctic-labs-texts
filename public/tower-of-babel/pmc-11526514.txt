Factors influencing self-regulated learning ability among Medical undergraduates in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jinmeng Huang, Chunxia Huang, Jian Qin, Kaiyong Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-regulated learning (SRL) is the core purpose of lifelong learning and the precondition for medical staff to continuously adapt to and excel in their current and future positions. Good SRL abilities positively impact the academic performance and clinical skills of medical students. Despite its importance, the factors influencing SRL among medical students in China remain under-explored. This study investigates these factors among Chinese medical undergraduates.MethodsThis cross-sectional survey was conducted among a convenience sample of undergraduate students at all four medical universities in Guangxi, using a questionnaire developed and tailored by Zude Zhu. Responses from 2,179 Chinese medical undergraduate students were included in the analyses. The data were collected using the College Student SRL Questionnaire which consisted of 69 items rated on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very inconsistent) to 6 (very consistent), and scored by calculating the mean and standard deviation. The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences, version 22.0 (IBM SPSS Corp).ResultsUnivariate analysis showed that registered residence, specialty, educational system, parental educational background, parenting style, annual household income, parental occupation, grade point average (GPA), engagement in SRL activities, and participation in SRL training were significantly associated with SRL scores (p < 0.05 for all). The results of the multiple linear regression analysis revealed that educational system (“5 + 3”-year) (p < 0.001; 95% Confidence Interval (CI), 9.842 to 15.686), mother’s occupation (staff of government agency/institution) (p < 0.001; 95% CI, − 3.798 to − 2.081), GPA (p < 0.001; 95% CI, − 10.009 to − 5.712), engagement in SRL activities (p = 0.001; 95% CI, − 11.903 to − 3.162), and participation in SRL training (p < 0.001; 95% CI, − 15.966 to − 9.972) were positively related to SRL abilities in medical students.ConclusionsFurther studies are necessary to identify additional external (e.g., academic environment, parents’ expectations for their children) and internal factors (e.g., student cadres or not, interest in medicine or not, whether there is a clear career planning) affecting SRL abilities. Initiating relevant training early in the college career could enhance SRL capabilities in medical students.

Background

With the rapid advancement of science and technology, continuously updating one’s knowledge base and adapting thought processes to align with societal developments have become crucial. This ongoing evolution necessitates that individuals, particularly those in the medical profession, cultivate the ability to learn independently and integrate this learning throughout their entire careers. Such lifelong learning is essential for medical staff to stay current and effective in their roles as healthcare evolves.

Self-direction and self-regulation are integral components of lifelong learning. Over the past few decades, global medical education has undergone a significant transformation, shifting from a teacher-centered approach to a student-centered model [1]. This change reflects a broader trend where individuals increasingly seek learning opportunities outside traditional classroom settings [2]. Consequently, the ability to manage one’s own learning has become critically important. For medical students, possessing strong self-regulation skills is essential. Self-regulation, defined as the ability to control one’s thoughts, feelings, and actions to achieve academic goals [3], is considered a fundamental skill for medical students [4]. This skill set enables them to effectively navigate their educational journey, adapting to both structured and self-directed learning environments.

Self-regulated learning (SRL) is an active and independent process in which students manage their cognition, motivation, and behavior to achieve academic goals [5, 6]. According to Zimmerman’s model of SRL, this process is divided into three phases: the forethought phase before an activity, the performance phase during the activity, and the self-reflection phase after the activity [5]. Currently in China, given that it advocates for students to learn how to learn and emphasizes the cultivation of their learning abilities, SRL is becoming an important topic in education and psychology research: it has become a hot topic of concern for researchers and educational practitioners alike. In cognitive self-regulation, students employ strategies such as rehearsal, organization, elaboration, and critical thinking. Motivational self-regulation involves managing beliefs such as self-efficacy, goal orientation, and the perceived value of tasks. Behavioral self-regulation entails the active control or utilization of available resources [7]. Specifically, self-regulated learners set and pursue goals, plan and execute strategies, and manage resources during the forethought phase. During the performance phase, they aim to control their emotions, cognition, and behavior to achieve these goals. Finally, in the self-reflection phase, learners assess their progress, adjusting their goals and strategies as needed for future learning [6–8].

SRL is fundamental to lifelong learning and is essential for medical staff to continually adapt and excel in their roles. Research underscores the significance of SRL, linking it to superior learning outcomes in medical education [2]. Students proficient in SRL approach learning as a personal responsibility, demonstrating proactivity and diligence. This enhances their ability to set clear learning objectives, monitor their progress, access necessary resources, and effectively analyze information [1, 9]. Such students typically employ deep learning strategies to meet their pre-defined goals. Many studies exploring the factors influencing SRL and the relationship between SRL and achievement levels have been carried out in Western countries. However, research on the SRL of medical students in China is limited and confined to a specific population, being pharmacy students, nursing students, and traditional Chinese medicine students. Conducting large-scale research on what previous studies have missed will obtain more comprehensive and accurate results.

Notably, research among Chinese nursing students shows that those with greater hardiness (defined as self-perception of commitment, control, and willingness to face challenges) [10] exhibit higher self-efficacy (the individual’s belief in their ability to perform the actions required to achieve specific outcomes) [11] and more positive, stable academic emotions (emotions directly linked to learning, classroom instruction, and achievement) [12], which contribute to stronger SRL abilities [9]. SRL significantly predicts medical students’ success in various areas, including written exams, clinical skills, and innovative capabilities [6, 13–15]. Moreover, robust SRL ability is not only linked to academic and clinical performance but also supports mental health and overall well-being. This evidence highlights the pervasive influence of SRL across multiple dimensions of medical training and professional development [16, 17].

Over the years, China’s higher medical education has evolved to feature distinctive educational systems tailored to its unique needs. Currently, these systems include various program durations: 3-year programs leading to an associate degree, 4-year programs for nursing and medical-related undergraduate degrees, 5-year medical undergraduate programs, combined “5 + 3”-year tracks for medical master’s degrees, and comprehensive 8-year programs that encompass bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral studies. This diversity in educational offerings reflects the growing societal emphasis on medical education in China, driven by the critical roles of education and health in public concerns. Given the unique demands of the medical profession, medical students are required to develop not only solid theoretical knowledge and proficient practical skills but also strong moral character. These requirements necessitate ongoing enhancement of SRL and lifelong learning capabilities, ensuring that medical professionals can meet the challenges of their roles effectively [18].

In recent years, various studies have investigated the factors influencing SRL abilities in medical students. Most existing research has focused on the relationships between self-efficacy, academic emotions, and their impacts on SRL abilities. A qualitative study in Iran identified five primary factors—family, peers, instructors, educational environment, and individual student characteristics—that may affect SRL abilities [19]. To investigate how achievement emotions and SRL predict burnout among medical students in online education, a study conducted in Hong Kong and southern mainland China revealed that medical students’ self-efficacy positively predicted their enjoyment and online SRL [20]. In the past decade, with the reform of medical education in China, Chinese scholars have begun to conduct research on SRL among medical students in college. A cross-sectional online survey among third-year clinical medicine students in a medical college in Wuhan showed that having a clear career plan and a professional idol, providing full-time clinical teachers, and seeking the help of classmates, teachers, or senior students were significant predictors of SRL [6]. Additionally, another cross-sectional survey conducted at a traditional Chinese medical university showed that factors such as gender, monthly family income, the father’s educational background, educational system, specialty, score ranking, scholarship, students’ affinity for their specialty, and whether they were tutored in middle school could predict 32% of the variance in SRL abilities [18]. However, comprehensive investigations into the current status of SRL abilities among specific groups of medical students in China have been limited and conducted on a small scale. Consequently, the factors affecting SRL abilities in Chinese medical undergraduates remain poorly understood.

To address this gap, the purpose of this multi-center cross-sectional study with a large sample size is to explore the factors influencing SRL ability among medical undergraduate students through expanding on the findings from these previous studies, aiming to provide a clearer understanding of how these factors contribute to effective SRL within this demographic. Methods

Participants and procedures

The study was conducted at all four medical universities that enroll undergraduate students in Guangxi Province, China: Guangxi Medical University, Guangxi University of Chinese Medicine, Guilin Medical University, and Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities. The major settings and talent cultivation models of these four medical universities in Guangxi province are similar to those in other provinces in China. A cross-sectional survey was administered from March 2024 to June 2024 at these universities. A convenience sample of 2,340 undergraduate students was recruited and 2,246 were collected. After excluding 67 unqualified questionnaires, 2,179 effective questionnaires were ultimately retained, comprising 584 males and 1595 females. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) full-time undergraduate students, and (2) understanding of the study purpose and willingness to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were part-time undergraduate students and foreign students. The foreign students at the four medical universities in Guangxi mainly come from Asian and African countries, and their education systems and teaching models differ greatly from those in China. Therefore, to reduce sample selection bias, foreign students were excluded.

Data collection was conducted using a self-administered online questionnaire through “Wen Juan Xing,” a professional online survey platform in China. In order to minimize the limitations (non-random selection bias) of convenient sampling, questionnaires were distributed on a class by class basis. The link and Quick Response code for the questionnaire were distributed to class monitors by the university’s teaching management department, who then forwarded them to students willing to participate. To improve the participation rate and quality of the questionnaire, the monitor explained to the classmates before distributing the questionnaire the importance of this study and the instructions for filling out the questionnaire, encouraging each student to participate in the survey. Prior to the survey, all participants were informed that their participation was voluntary, anonymous, and confidential. Participants completed the questionnaires independently using their personal mobile phones. The questionnaire was designed to be submitted only when fully completed. The survey consisted of demographic details and the College Student SRL questionnaire [4, 21]. Initially, a small-scale preliminary survey involving 45 undergraduates was conducted at Guangxi Medical University using “Wen Juan Xing.” Based on the outcomes of this preliminary survey, a portion of the questionnaire was revised by three experts in epidemiology and statistics to refine its content. Finally, parental education methods and whether the respondent had participated in SRL training were added in the questionnaire, while college entrance examination score was deleted because many students did not remember exact scores.

Demographic information questionnaire

We collected a range of sociodemographic information including sex, age, ethnicity (Han, Zhuang, and others), registered residence (rural area, township, and urban area), whether participants were an only child, university attended, specialty (clinical medicine, preventive medicine, stomatology, pharmacy, nursing, and others), educational system (4-year, 5-year, and “5 + 3”-year), grade (1st–5th grade), parental educational background (primary school, middle school, high school, and university and above), parenting style (authoritarian, tolerant, and neglectful types), annual household income, and parental occupation (staff of government agency/institution, company/enterprise, factory worker, farmer, service worker, self-employed, and others). Additionally, we gathered data on GPA (categorized as ≥ 3.0, 2.5–2.9, and < 2.5), whether to carry out SRL activities (e.g., online learning courses, self-learning platform, electronic database), and whether to participate in SRL training (e.g., theme class meeting on SRL ability, SRL ability training course, independent research projects, active learning strategies, and scenario simulation or role-playing in class).

Some of the questionnaire content can be explained as follows. (1) Authoritarian parenting style: Parents show dictatorial and strict behavior toward their children and demand complete obedience from them; they expect their children to adhere to their stringent disciplinary guidelines at all costs [22, 23]. (2) Tolerant parenting style: Parents show warmth and non-strict behavior; they rarely demand something from their child and do not have strict disciplinary rules [22, 23]. (3) Neglectful parenting style: The parents show low demand, low response, and neither warmth nor strict behavior; they usually provide only material support such as food, lodging, and clothing, without providing spiritual support [22, 23].

College Student SRL Questionnaire

The College Student SRL Questionnaire, developed by Zude Zhu and tailored to the specific learning circumstances of college students in China [4, 21], assesses learning motivation and strategy across 69 items. Responses for each item are recorded on a 6-point Likert scale: 1 = very inconsistent, 2 = inconsistent, 3 = somewhat inconsistent, 4 = somewhat consistent, 5 = consistent, and 6 = very consistent. The total possible score for SRL ability ranges from 69 to 414, with higher scores indicating greater SRL ability. In this study, the questionnaire demonstrated excellent reliability with a Cronbach’s α of 0.970.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. Categorical variables are summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The sociodemographic characteristics and educational characteristics differences in attitudes toward SRL ability were assessed using t-test and ANOVA test. A p-value of < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. To mitigate multicollinearity, 17 separate tests explored the relationships between sociodemographic characteristics and perception/practice. To control for type I errors from multiple testing, a Bonferroni correction was applied, setting the threshold for significance at p-values of < 0.003 (0.05/17). For further analysis of the factors influencing SRL, variables with p-values of < 0.05 were included in a multiple linear regression model and a forward selection procedure was used to select the significant independent variables, controlling for gender, ethnic and grade. The outcome variable was the SRL ability score. The independent variables were registered residence, specialty, educational system, father’s educational background, mother’s educational background, parenting style, annual household income, father’s occupation, mother’s occupation, GPA, whether to engage in SRL activities, whether to participate in SRL training.

Results

Sample characteristics

In total, 2,340 questionnaires were distributed and 2,246 were collected, yielding a recovery rate of 95.98%. After excluding 67 questionnaires that were deemed ineffective due to discrepancies such as significant deviations in the average answer time and illogical responses to interference items, 2,179 effective questionnaires were retained, resulting in an effective rate of 93.12%. The sociodemographic characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1. Among the respondents, 1,595 (73.2%) were female, 1,374 (63.1%) were from the Han ethnic group, and 1,475 (67.7%) came from rural areas. The majority of students (84.2%) were not the only child in their family, and approximately 37% were from Guangxi Medical University.

In terms of specialty, 607 (27.9%) students were studying clinical medicine, 345 (15.8%) were in nursing, and 268 (12.3%) were in preventive medicine. Regarding the educational system, 1,184 (54.3%) students were enrolled in 5-year programs, and 889 (40.8%) were enrolled in 4-year programs. Nearly 60% of the respondents were first- and second-year students. Concerning parental educational background, approximately 60% of students had parents whose highest level of education was primary or middle school. More than 34% of the parents were farmers, and more than 21% were self-employed. Of the 2,179 students, 1,073 (49.2%) lived in low-income families with an annual household income of less than CNY 40,000. The majority of students (86.2%) engaged in SRL during their college studies, although only 975 (44.7%) participated in SRL training. Regarding GPA, 874 (40.1%) students had a GPA of ≥ 3.0 and 897 (41.2%) had a GPA of 2.5 to 2.9 (Table 1).

Univariate analysis of factors influencing SRL ability

As shown in Table 2, several factors were significantly associated with SRL scores (p < 0.05 for all), including registered residence, specialty, educational system, parental educational background (both father’s and mother’s), parenting style, annual household income, parental occupation (both father’s and mother’s), GPA, engagement in SRL activities, and participation in SRL training. Students from urban areas demonstrated better SRL abilities than those from rural areas. Among the different specialties, clinical medicine students had the highest SRL scores, outperforming students in preventive medicine, pharmacy, and nursing. Students enrolled in the “5 + 3”-year educational system and those with a GPA of ≥ 3.0 also had the highest SRL scores.

Higher parental education levels and greater annual household income were associated with higher SRL scores among students. Additionally, students who reported an authoritarian parenting style had the highest SRL scores. Those whose parents worked in government agencies, institutions, or companies/enterprises had better SRL scores than students whose parents had other occupations. Finally, students who engaged in SRL during their college studies and those who participated in SRL training also had higher SRL scores. However, no differences existed in other personal characteristics, such as gender, ethnic, grade, came from which university, and the only child or not (Table 2). Undeniably, the convenience sampling method or the reliance on self-reported data through an online questionnaire might introduce certain biases. Therefore, it is important to conduct a larger nationwide multi-center study through more scientific design to obtain more accurate results.

Discussion

Self-regulation is considered one of the most fundamental skills for medical students. SRL is the core purpose of lifelong learning and a prerequisite for medical staff to continuously adapt to and excel in their current and future positions. Therefore, identifying the influencing factors of SRL ability and implementing timely, targeted interventions to improve students’ SRL ability may achieve better educational outcomes at a lower cost. We conducted a multi-center cross-sectional study with a large sample size to explore the factors influencing SRL ability among medical undergraduate students in China.

The present study revealed that the educational system and GPA were two important factors influencing SRL ability among medical undergraduate students. Students in the “5 + 3”-year educational system had the highest SRL scores (337.93 ± 31.96), and these scores were significantly higher than those of the students in other groups. This may be indirectly related to college entrance examination (commonly known as “Gaokao”) scores and SRL ability [18]. In China, the 5-year clinical medicine program is the basis of clinical medicine education. Graduates need to complete three years of standardized residency training to become doctors. The “5 + 3”-year clinical medicine model combines the 5-year medical undergraduate education, 3-year standardized residency training and postgraduate education [24]. The training objective of “5 + 3”-year clinical medicine is guided by the competency of clinical doctors; it aims to cultivate high-level clinical residents with medical scientific and humanistic spirit, strong practical ability, SRL ability, and innovation ability. The “5 + 3”-year clinical medicine system places more emphasis on independent thinking, SRL, and critical thinking skills compared to the 4- and 5-year systems. The “5 + 3”-year medical undergraduate students usually perform exceptionally well in high school and gain admission to medical universities with very high Gaokao scores. Similarly, 5-year medical undergraduate students typically have higher Gaokao scores than 4-year students (nursing and related medical undergraduates). Previous studies have shown that students with higher average Gaokao scores tend to have higher average GPAs in colleges [25]. These students might have already developed strong SRL abilities in high school, leading to better performance in college and higher GPAs. In our study, higher GPAs were associated with higher SRL scores, which is consistent with previous findings [4, 26–28]. Generally speaking, students with high GPAs are more likely to receive scholarships. Similar research has revealed that students who receive scholarships tend to have strong SRL abilities, and obtaining scholarships could be positive reinforcement for independent learning ability [18].

Although the univariate analysis in our study showed that students whose parents were employed by government agencies, institutions, or companies had better SRL scores than others, the multivariate analysis showed that only the mother’s occupation was a significant influencing factor; the father’s occupation was not. The education system in China is led by central and local government and coordinated by multiple departments (such as the Ministry of Education, Ministry of Science and Technology, and Ministry of Finance, the Health Commission), with social participation. Parents employed by government departments, institutions engaged in teaching research and education training, and cultural publishing companies might have more opportunities to acquire advanced educational concepts, more teaching resources, and better teaching methods than parents in other professions, thereby guiding their children toward better SRL. However, farmers, service workers, and self-employed persons might have much fewer opportunities to be exposed to advanced educational concepts and teaching methods due to the nature of their work and working environment. In addition, it is common in China that mothers generally provide more education and companionship for their children than do fathers. So, it is not at all surprising that the father’s occupation is not a significant factor influencing SRL.

Our univariate analysis results indicated that the higher the education level of students’ parents, the higher the students’ SRL ability. However, the multiple linear regression analysis did not show a significant effect of the highest level of parental education on SRL, which aligns with the findings reported by Berger [29]. Conversely, related studies have shown that the higher the educational background of parents, the higher the students’ SRL ability [18]. However, another study in China did not reveal a correlation between parental education level and students’ SRL [6]. These discrepancies may be due to differences in national or local policies, teaching strategies, ethnic culture, economic status, and individual differences among students and parents. Generally speaking, highly educated parents might pay more attention to their children’s education and management, and provide more help in the learning process. However, this study was conducted in Guangxi, a province with weak educational resources, lower economic levels, and lower educational levels compared to residents in China generally, which led to significant differences in educational concepts and parenting styles between the parents in Guangxi and other developed provinces.

Although the multiple linear regression analysis did not indicate that annual household income was an important factor influencing SRL ability, the univariate analysis showed that higher annual household income was associated with higher SRL scores, similar to findings reported by Li [18]. High household income may provide more resources for purchasing better learning tools, such as paid online courses or training, which could enhance students’ SRL ability. Similarly, although registered residence was not an important factor influencing SRL ability, the univariate analysis also showed that students from urban areas demonstrated better SRL abilities than those from rural areas, consistent with the results reported by Yang [4]. That may be because urban areas have better educational concepts, learning resources, and access to SRL training than rural areas. Additionally, the income of urban residents is generally higher than that of rural residents.

In the past decade, the number of female students has been much higher than that of male students in medical universities in China. Especially for nursing students, the majority are female. Therefore, similar to other studies in China [18], the majority of participants in this study were female. Although neither univariate nor multivariate analysis in the study indicated gender as an important factor influencing SRL ability, we cannot draw such a hasty conclusion, especially in light of the gender imbalance in the sample. On the contrary, a similar study in China showed that male students achieved higher SRL scores than female students [18]. Therefore, in the future, a multi-center study should be conducted and the sample size of male students should be appropriately expanded to obtain more reliable results.

Furthermore, the present study showed that “whether to carry out SRL activities” and “participation in SRL training” significantly affected medical undergraduate students’ SRL ability. Those who engaged in SRL activities and participated in SRL training had higher SRL abilities. Self-assessment is a key component of SRL. A study by Alfakhry [30] showed that self-assessment practice and training improved SRL and that using self-assessment training to cultivate SRL ability had potential advantages. Scholars have promoted SRL and lifelong clinical learning through medical training, which effectively enhanced students’ SRL [31]. After exploring the factors influencing SRL, Dong [32] recommended providing training for students and teachers when necessary to promote SRL. Additionally, implementing personal learning plans is an important part of SRL that can foster reflection and the development of SRL skills [33]. Participating in online learning courses is a common SRL activity. It was reported that online learners who were more active reported higher self-efficacy, higher intrinsic motivation, and more frequent use of positive SRL strategies than those who were less active [34]. Therefore, actively participating in online course learning could effectively promote the development and acquisition of medical students’ SRL abilities [35].

SRL training includes theme class meeting on SRL ability, an SRL ability training course, independent research projects, active learning strategies, and scenario simulation or role-playing in class. Unquestionably, theme class meeting on SRL ability and the SRL ability training course, as important components of SRL training programs, can effectively guide undergraduate students on how to engage in SRL, enabling them to master the methods and skills of SRL and thereby effectively improving their SRL abilities. Similarly, independent research projects play an important role in improving SRL ability. Findings from an Australian university showed that medical students’ involvement in independent research projects could enable students to develop valuable self-directed learning skills and enhance their SRL abilities [36]. Active learning strategies include problem-based strategies, such as case studies and concept-based mapping; collaborative and cooperative learning strategies, such as group work and discussions; and experiential approaches, such as the use of simulation or standardized patients and reflective practice [37]. A focus group study in the Middle East indicated that the use of active learning strategies in medical education significantly improves critical thinking abilities, clinical performance, knowledge competence, the translation of knowledge to clinical skills, and SRL abilities [37]. In summary, both engaging in SRL activities and participating in SRL training have a significant positive effect on students’ SRL abilities.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study that should be acknowledged. Firstly, this was a cross-sectional study conducted in four medical universities in Guangxi province, which may not represent all medical undergraduate students in China. Guangxi is a province in southwest China, where social, educational, and economic levels are lower than those in eastern and central China. Therefore, it is important to conduct a larger nationwide multi-center study to obtain more scientific and accurate results. Secondly, no standardized scale exists for measuring students’ SRL ability, which may lead to variations in results between related studies. It is necessary to develop a standardized scale for future research. Thirdly, all data were collected via self-reported online questionnaires, with no opportunity for participants to consult or seek clarification on any questions. Consequently, to obtain more authentic and accurate data, we can adopt a mixed-method approach in future studies, which includes a quantitative survey and qualitative interviews. Meanwhile, this limitation could be addressed by piloting the questionnaire more extensively or adding opportunities for follow-up clarification during data collection.

Conclusions

The present study explored the factors influencing SRL ability among medical undergraduate students in China. The findings indicated that the educational system, mother’s occupation, GPA, whether to carry out SRL activities, and participation in SRL training were important determinants of SRL ability among medical undergraduate students. These findings provide initial insights into the factors influencing SRL ability. In further, a nationwide multi-center study with longitudinal designs research is needed to explore the effects of other external and internal factors on students’ SRL ability, and assess the effectiveness of SRL training. Therefore, relevant training should be implemented early in the college career to enhance medical students’ SRL abilities.
