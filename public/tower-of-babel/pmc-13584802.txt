One-Step Synthesis of Dihydropyrroloquinazolinone Alkaloid Natural Products: S‑(−)- and R‑(+)-Vasicinone and Their Novel Analogues
Authors: Tanvir Khaliq, Veerpal Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A single-step synthesis of the biologically active dihydropyrroloquinazolinone alkaloid natural products, S-(−)- and R-(+)-vasicinone (5 and 6) has been developed for the first time. This reaction employs a POCl3-mediated coupling of methyl 2-aminobenzoate with either (S)- or (R)-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-2-one resulting in the synthesis of the two enantiomers in excellent yields. The enantiomeric excess (ee) was found to be >99% for both enantiomers and the specific rotations are consistent with the literature values. The reaction can be successfully extended to obtain diverse quinazolinone scaffolds such as substituted benzenoids, naphthalenes, indoles, thiophenes, phenylthiophenes, piperidines, oxazepines, and isoindolinones. We applied this reaction to the synthesis of our orally active antileishmanial lead peganine, and reduction of S-(−)-vasicinone led to a conformationally more flexible analogue, 3a,4-dihydropeganine (28).

Introduction

Tetrahydropyrroloquinazoline alkaloids, vasicine 1 and 2 and deoxyvasicine 3, and dihydropyrroloquinazolinone alkaloids, deoxyvasicinone 4 and vasicinone 5 and 6 (Figure ), have been isolated from the medicinal plants,Adhatoda vasicaand Peganum harmala Peganine is the epimer of vasicine and has only been isolated fromP. harmalaand is not found inA. vasica Many structurally related alkaloids have also been isolated from natural sources or synthesized. , These alkaloids exhibit a diverse range of biological activities, such as antitumor, antileishmanial, antiasthmatic (bronchodilating), antihypertensive, anthelmintic, antianaphylactic, antitrypanosomal, anti-inflammatory, and antidepressant activities. −

Naturally occurring quinazoline and quinazolinone alkaloids isolated from Adhatoda vasica and Peganum harmala.

Several approaches have been reported for the synthesis of these biologically active natural products and their synthetic derivatives. − Some examples include asymmetric oxidation, Aza-Wittig or Aza-Nazarov reactions, coupling of anthranilic acid with succinic anhydride, photoredox cyclization, reductive cyclization of different anthranilic acid derivatives, an enzymatic azidoreductive cyclization strategy employing TMSCl–NaI and baker’ yeast, selective copper­(II) acetate- and potassium iodide-catalyzed oxidations of aminals, and redox condensations of o-nitrobenzaldehydes with amines. Eguchi et al. (Scheme A, (i) and Mhaske et al. (Scheme A, ii) have reported the multistep synthesis of S-(−)-vasicinone using O-protected γ-lactam and O-acetylated succinic anhydride starting materials, respectively. Richers et al. synthesized dihydroquinazoline and quinazolinone alkaloids via aminal synthesis in which they reacted 2-aminobenzaldehyde with various cyclic amines, followed by oxidation with copper­(II) acetate/acetic acid/O2 and potassium iodide/tert-butylhydroperoxide systems. , More recently, Afanasyev et al. (Scheme A, iii) reported the multistep synthesis of R-(+)-vasicinone using nitrobenzaldehyde and a hydroxyproline substrate, in very low yield (16%). The C9 substituted tetrahydropyrroloquinazolines and related polyannular scaffolds were recently synthesized using a Tf2O-mediated tandem synthesis from amino amides and aldehydes. (+)-Vasicinone has also been obtained via a lipase-mediated chemoenzymatic synthesis. However, these syntheses require either multiple steps, protection of sensitive functional groups (OH, NH), or the use of unstable and hazardous reagents such as diazomethane, benzaldehydes, azides, anhydrides, etc. Therefore, a simple, direct, and efficient approach is prudent for the synthesis of the biologically active quinazolinone alkaloids S-(−)- and R-(+)-vasicinone and their novel analogues for biological investigation.

We report herein (Scheme B, iv) a one-step, efficient synthesis of S-(−)-vasicinone 5 and R-(+)-vasicinone 6 using methyl 2-aminobenzoate 5a and (S)- or (R)-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-2-one 5b in the presence of POCl3. POCl3 has been used for the synthesis of deoxyvasicinone (4) and related molecules; − however, a one-step synthesis of vasicinone has not been reported in the literature. We successfully applied the reaction to synthesize diverse quinazolinone scaffolds such as substituted benzenoids, naphthalenes, indoles, thiophenes, phenylthiophenes, piperidines, oxazepines, and isoindolinones. Our synthesis is advantageous over the reported reactions as it uses a one-step procedure to prepare S-(−)-vasicinone 5 and R-(+)-vasicinone 6 in excellent yields with >99% ee, as opposed to multiple methods reported, and has functional group tolerance (Table ). Specifically, the reaction can be accomplished without protecting the reactive 3-hydroxy group of pyrrolidinone, in contrast to tert-butyldimethylsilyl chloride (TBDMSCl) protection employed by Eguchi et al., for the synthesis of l-vasicinone. The yield of R-(+)-vasicinone 6 is also superior (80%, >99% ee) to the reported yield of 16% by Afanasyev et al. Finally, we applied our reaction for the synthesis of the orally active antileishmanial lead peganine and obtained a conformationally more flexible analogue, 3a,4-dihydropeganine (28).

Results and Discussion

We are interested in developing tetrahydropyrroloquinazoline alkaloids and their novel analogues for potential development as treatments for the protozoan parasitic diseases leishmaniasis and human African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness). We are designing and synthesizing novel analogues of the orally active antileishmanial lead natural product peganine 1 , for biological evaluation − against these neglected tropical parasitic diseases. While a direct synthesis of racemic (±)-vasicine is available, synthesis of either of the enantiomers has not been reported. Moreover, S-(−)- and R-(+)-vasicine can be efficiently obtained from their respective precursors, S-(−)- and R-(+)-vasicinone by reduction with either borane-tetrahydrofuran complex (BH3-THF), Zn-Hg/AcOH or via thioamide reduction. Therefore, a rapid and efficient synthesis of peganine and its novel analogues is crucial for their biological investigation.

We undertook the synthesis of S-(−)-vasicinone 5 from methyl 2-aminobenzoate 5a and (S)-3-O-tert-butyldimethylsilyl-protected pyrrolidin-2-one 5c using POCl3 in refluxing dichloromethane. Vasicinone 5 was obtained in low yield (21%) due to the formation of 3-chlorodeoxyvasicinone 7 as a side product (Scheme , i). It appears that POCl3 mediates C-3 dehydroxylation of vasicinone followed by chlorination, as confirmed by mass and NMR spectroscopic analysis (Figures S10–S12). We carried out reaction optimization by modifying reaction conditions (Scheme , ii–iv; Table S1). A parallel reaction was carried out by coupling methyl 2-aminobenzoate 5a directly with (S)-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-2-one 5b, but the yield (41%) of the product did not improve significantly. Addition of triethylamine or 4-(dimethylamino)­pyridine (DMAP) as a base to enhance the nucleophilicity of the amine also did not improve the yield (33%).

(i) and (ii) POCl3 (2 equiv), Anhydrous Dichloromethane, Reflux, 12 h; (iii) POCl3 (1.8 equiv), Anhydrous Dichloromethane, Reflux, 12 h; (iv) POCl3 (1.6 equiv), 0 °C, 15 min, Anhydrous Dichloromethane, Reflux, 8 h

However, decreasing the molar equivalents of POCl3 from 2 to 1.8 led to an improved yield of S-(−)-vasicinone 5 from 41% to 63% (Scheme , reactions ii and iii). Further optimization of reaction conditions by decreasing the molar amount of POCl3 to 1.6 equiv, and slowly adding POCl3 at 0 °C over 15 min, followed by refluxing in anhydrous dichloromethane for 8 h, led to the synthesis of S-(−)-vasicinone 5 (Scheme , iv) in excellent yield (93%). Its enantiomer, R-(+)-vasicinone 6 was also obtained in very high yield (80%) using the optimized reaction conditions (Scheme B, iv). The optical purity was determined by chiral HPLC, and the enantiomeric purity (% ee) was found to be >99% for both enantiomers (Figures S79–S82 for HPLC chromatograms). The specific rotations of S-(−)-vasicinone (5) and R-(+)-vasicinone (6) were consistent with the reported values for natural and synthetic vasicinone.

Synthesis of Benzenoid Analogues

With the optimized reaction conditions in hand, we investigated the substrate scope of our reaction (Scheme and Table ). First, various substituted methyl 2-aminobenzoates were evaluated to probe the benzenoid ring for steric and electronic effects. Benzenoid rings substituted with electron-donating groups such as 5-OMe, 3-CH3, 3-OH, and 5-OH yielded the corresponding products 8–11 in moderate to excellent yields (87%, 73%, 36%, and 37%), respectively. The moderate yields of 5- and 7-hydroxyvasicinones 10 and 11 appear to be due to a competing nucleophilic substitution at the chloroiminium carbon by either the anilino amine of the anthranilate or the hydroxyl group. However, the presence of electron-withdrawing groups such as 6-F, 3-Cl, 5-Br, and 5-CF3 also led to excellent yields of desired products 12–15 (73%, 81%, 79%, 83%), except in 7-cyanovasicinone 16, which was obtained in moderate yield (37%). The higher yield of benzenoid analogues with electron-withdrawing groups appears to be due to an increase in the electrophilicity of the carbonyl of the acceptor anthranilate, which may undergo nucleophilic addition by the donor amidine nitrogen of the activated pyrrolidinone moiety. Simultaneously, the nucleophilicity of the anilino amine may be enhanced by the presence of electron-donating groups on the benzenoid ring, thus improving the yields of the corresponding analogues.

Scaffold Hopping of the Core Benzenoid Nucleus

Scaffold hopping (Scheme ) of the core benzenoid nucleus was carried out by replacing the hydrophobic phenyl with a more hydrophobic naphthalene nucleus to afford the corresponding (S)-3-hydroxy-2,3-dihydrobenzo­[g]­pyrrolo­[2,1-b]­quinazolin-11­(1H)-one 17 in excellent yield (70%). Replacement of the core with a more hydrophilic indole scaffold also resulted in the formation of the corresponding (S)-3-hydroxy-1,2,3,5-tetrahydro-10H-pyrrolo­[1“,2”:1,2]­pyrimido­[4,5-b]­indol-10-one 18, albeit in moderate yield (42%). Additional modification of the central core with thiophene and phenylthiophene scaffolds resulted in the desired compounds 19 and 20 in moderate yields (33–59%). This clearly demonstrates that our one-step coupling of methyl 2-aminobenzoate with the unprotected (S)-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-2-one can be successfully extended to diverse scaffolds such as hydrophobic naphthalene, hydrophilic indole, thiophene, and phenylthiophene.

Scaffold Hopping of the Pyrrole Nucleus

Next, to further broaden the scope of our synthetic methodology, we investigated scaffold hopping on the pyrrole moiety of vasicinone 5 (Scheme ). Ring expansion of pyrrole led to conformationally more flexible analogues: (S)-8-hydroxy-6,7,8,9-tetrahydro-11H-pyrido­[2,1-b]­quinazolin-11-one 21; 1,2,4,5-tetrahydro-11H-[1,4]­oxazepino­[5,4-b]­quinazolin-11-one 23; and 7,8,9,10-tetrahydroazepino­[2,1-b]­quinazolin-12­(6H)-one 24, which were obtained in near-quantitative yields (96%, 90%, and 96%), with the exception of racemic-6-hydroxy-6,7,8,9-tetrahydro-11H-pyrido­[2,1-b]­quinazolin-11-one 22, obtained in low yield (14%). This demonstrates that our reaction is amenable to ring expansion to facilitate further diversification of the quinazolinone core structure.

The above reactions clearly demonstrate that our one-step synthesis of S-(−)- and R-(+)-enantiomers of vasicinone can be successfully extended to a broad range of substrates, such as substituted benzenoids, naphthalenes, indoles, thiophenes, phenylthiophenes, piperidines, oxazepines, and isoindolinones. The presence of electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups on the benzenoid ring has mixed effects on reactivity. While electron-donating 5-hydroxy-, 7-hydroxy-, 5-methyl-, and 7-methoxypeganone analogues 8–11 showed moderate to very high yields, electron-withdrawing 8-fluoro-, 5-chloro-, 7-bromo-, and 7-trifluoromethylpeganone analogues 12–15 showed excellent yields, except for 7-cyanopeganone 16. Electron-donating groups appear to enhance the nucleophilicity of the amine, whereas electron-withdrawing groups increase the electrophilicity of the ester carbonyl to facilitate the reaction. Therefore, it is not surprising that both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups contribute to the formation of the desired products.

Extension of the benzenoid ring to carbocyclic naphthalene 17, heterocyclic indole 18, thiophene 19, or phenylthiophene 20 scaffolds also proceeded smoothly, albeit with moderate yields for indole 18 and thiophene 19 analogues. Ring expansion of the pyrrole ring was also successfully accomplished to obtain conformationally more flexible analogues: 8-hydroxytetrahydropyridoquinazolin 11-one 21, 6-hydroxytetrahydropyridoquinazolin-11-one 22, tetrahydrooxazepinoquinazolin-11-one 23, and tetrahydroazepinoquinazolin-12-one 24 in moderate to excellent yields. Appending an isoindolinone ring in place of the pyrrole ring to the quinazolinone nucleus was also successful, as the resultant isoindoloquinazolinone analogues 25 and 26 were obtained in near-quantitative yields.

From the foregoing discussion, it can be inferred that POCl3-mediated condensation of methyl 2-aminobenzoate with various lactams proceeds smoothly, giving rise to novel quinazolinones with structural diversity. This reaction exhibits functional-group tolerance, as the hydroxyl groups of pyrrole and piperidine moieties were inert under the reaction conditions. Most products were obtained in high to near-quantitative yields, with broad substrate specificity. Taken together, synthesis of the natural product vasicinone can be successfully accomplished in a single step by coupling methyl 2-aminobenzoate with R- or S-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-2-one in the presence of POCl3. The reaction is rapid and can be conveniently extended to a broad range of substrates, such as substituted benzenoids, naphthalenes, indoles, thiophenes, phenylthiophenes, piperidines, oxazepines, and isoindolinones.

Proposed Mechanism for the Synthesis of Vasicinone

A proposed mechanism of POCl3-mediated condensation of 2-aminobenzoate with (S)-3-hydroxypyrrolidin-2-one to form vasicinone is shown below (Scheme ). The reaction appears to proceed via a known Vilsmeier–Haack-type reaction. The first step involves lactam activation in which the nucleophilic carbonyl oxygen of the pyrrolidinone attacks the highly electrophilic phosphorus atom of POCl3, generating a phosphodichloridate intermediate (A) that is displaced by the outgoing chloride ion via nucleophilic attack, forming a highly reactive chloroiminium intermediate (B). The second step involves a nucleophilic attack by the anilino group of 2-aminobenzoate (C) on the electrophilic chloroiminium carbon of intermediate B, forming a highly unstable intermediate that rapidly collapses to form the amidine intermediate (D). The last step is the intramolecular condensation involving the nucleophilic attack by the amidine nitrogen on the carbonyl group of the ester moiety of substrate C, forming a tetrahedral intermediate (E), which upon displacement of the alkoxy group at C-9 rearranges to form the quinazolinone scaffold of vasicinone (F).

Proposed Mechanism of POCl3-Mediated Formation of Vasicinone

The 3-chloro derivative 7 is presumably obtained through the initial formation of an activated C-3 phosphate ester intermediate, resulting from a nucleophilic attack by C-3 hydroxyl group on POCl3. This is followed with a second nucleophilic attack by the displaced chloride ion from POCl3 on the activated carbon at C-3 to yield the target 3-chloro derivative 7. Additional control experiments, such as running the reaction without POCl3 or using isotope labeling are needed in future studies to confirm the reaction mechanism which are beyond the scope of the present study.

Application in the Synthesis of Antileishmanial Lead Peganine

We applied our reaction to the synthesis of the orally active antileishmanial lead, the tetrahydropyrroloquinazoline alkaloid natural product peganine. S-(−)-vasicinone 5 was subjected to chemoselective reduction of the amide carbonyl at C-9 using a borane-THF complex. However, we observed complete reduction in 5 with both the C-9 carbonyl and the 3a-4 imine bond reduced under these conditions, to form the 3a,4-dihydropeganine analogue 28 (Scheme ). This novel compound is conformationally more flexible than the natural product and will be evaluated for antileishmanial activity in future studies to improve the potency of the lead molecule.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed an efficient one-step synthesis for the biologically active dihydropyrroloquinazolinone alkaloid natural products, S-(−)-vasicinone and R-(+)-vasicinone with ee >99%. The reaction can be successfully applied to a diverse range of substrates such as substituted benzenoids, naphthalenes, indoles, thiophenes, piperidines, isoindolinones, and oxazepines to obtain novel quinazolinone derivatives in moderate to excellent yields and has a very broad substrate scope. Reduction of vasicinone to a conformationally more flexible analogue was also accomplished. These novel analogues and their derivatives will be evaluated against Leishmania donovani intracellular amastigotes for their activity against leishmaniasis in our future studies.

Experimental Methods

Melting points were determined using a MEL-TEMP instrument. Chiral HPLC was carried out using a Waters e2695 separation module equipped with a PDA detector. A Daicel IC column (4.6 mm × 250 mm; 5 μm) with conditions of 80% hexanes and 20% isopropanol at 1 mL/min was used to elute the enantiomers. Specific rotation was determined using a Jasco P-2000 polarimeter with a 589 nm sodium lamp (NuMega Resonance Laboratories, San Diego, CA). NMR spectra were recorded at ambient temperature on a 600 MHz Bruker NMR spectrometer in the solvents indicated. Mass spectra were recorded using LC/MS (single quadrupole Agilent mass spectrometer) in the electrospray ionization (ESI) mode, in the positive and negative ion modes. Analytical thin-layer chromatography was performed on Supelco precoated aluminum TLC silica gel 60 F254 plates with 200 μm thickness (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO). Flash column chromatography was performed on a Teledyne ISCO CombiFlash NextGen 300+ chromatography system (Lincoln, NE) using RediSep Gold and Silver columns with a mesh size of 60–120 μm.
