Accelerating autism prevalence in California with changepoint in the mid-2010s and divergence by county and socioeconomic indicators
Authors: Cynthia Nevison, Walter Zahorodny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/ObjectivesThe Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring (ADDM) network has observed an ongoing increase in autism spectrum disorder (ASD) among U.S. children, with highest prevalence in California. Recent county-level trends in California were analyzed to provide insight into this increase.MethodsASD prevalence was estimated from the California Department of Developmental Services (DDS) ASD caseload in 2025, stratified by county, race/ethnicity, and age across birth years 1993-2020. Denominators were estimated from the mean of public school and U.S. census data.ResultsDiagnosed ASD prevalence increased among all race/ethnicity groups across this period, with a strong uptick in the mid-2010s, most commonly in birth year 2016, in many low and middle income counties. The estimated values varied widely by county and by race/ethnicity, ranging from 1.2% ± 0.2% in San Mateo to 9.5 ± 1.3% in Los Angeles among white and black children, respectively, born in 2019. The rate of change in diagnosed prevalence from birth year 2015-2019 was significantly correlated to county Medicaid (Medi-Cal) fraction for Hispanics and all races, although this ecological association could not distinguish individual-level risk. Comparison of diagnosed ASD prevalence in San Diego County between DDS and ADDM (for which California is represented by San Diego) suggested that DDS tends to underestimate total ASD, likely because it represents the more severely affected subset. Within DDS, diagnosed prevalence trends in San Diego captured statewide California trends relatively well but prevalence was about 20% higher in San Diego by birth year 2019.ConclusionsDiagnosed ASD prevalence has risen unequally across California counties and race/ethnicity groups since the birth years of the mid-2010s, with the steepest increases in low and middle income counties. Understanding these patterns and disentangling true increases in ASD from diagnostic changes are urgent priorities for future research.Keywords: autism spectrum disorder, autism, prevalence, time trends, California, county, medicaid, socioeconomic, race/ethnicity

1. Introduction

Autism is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by impairments in social interaction, communication and restricted or stereotyped behaviors. 1 The diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) encompasses a wide range of presentations, ranging from mildly to profoundly affected, 2 often with co-occurring medical and developmental disorders. 3 ASD is increasingly understood to result from a complex interaction of genetic factors and early environmental insults. These interactions may lead to neuroinflammation, immune dysregulation, and oxidative stress, culminating in brain injury in young children during critical stages of neurodevelopment.4–7 However, the ongoing rise in prevalence among U.S. children may reflect multiple factors, including true incidence changes but also diagnostic substitution, broadened criteria, and increased awareness. 8

The most recent report of the Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring (ADDM) network found a mean ASD prevalence of 1 in 31 (i.e., 3.2 %) of 8-year-olds born in 2014 at 16 U.S. sites, including Puerto Rico. 8 The 2014 result reflects an increase by nearly a factor of 5 from the first ADDM report in birth year 1992. 9 Since California (represented by “part of one county in metropolitan San Diego”) joined the ADDM network in surveillance year 2018, it has stood out as having the highest ASD prevalence among all U.S. sites in the last 3 biannual ADDM reports. ADDM ASD prevalence in California reached 5.31% among children aged 8 born in 2014 and 6.06%, among children aged 4 years born in 2018.

County-level ASD prevalence was investigated previously in California using a 2019 age-resolved snapshot from the California Department of Developmental Services (DDS) for birth years 1993-2013. 10 Strikingly, that study found that while ASD prevalence had increased among all children across birth years 1993-2000, prevalence after 2000 had plateaued among white and to a lesser extent Asian children from wealthy counties. In contrast, ASD prevalence increased continuously across 1993-2013 among whites from lower income counties and Hispanics from all counties. A subsequent study corroborated the divergent trends in California by race/ethnicity and socioeconomic status (SES). 11 Both studies focused on describing the time trends, rather than explaining their etiology.

This paper examines a 2025 age-resolved snapshot of the ASD caseload from California DDS that is stratified by county and race/ethnicity. The dataset permits the estimation of race/ethnicity-specific time trends in ASD prevalence among Asian, Hispanic, black, and white children across 36 California counties, encompassing wealthy coastal, large metropolitan, and rural/agricultural communities. The motivations for revisiting this topic are: First, to examine whether ASD prevalence in San Diego, the county featured in ADDM, is broadly representative of the state of California. Second, to describe how county- and race/ethnicity-specific birth-year trends in DDS-diagnosed ASD prevalence have evolved through 2020, with particular attention to potential changepoints in the mid-2010s; Third, to explore ecological associations between county-level socioeconomic indicators and changes in ASD prevalence. While the analysis is descriptive and cannot attribute causation, it can provide information relevant for understanding why diagnosed ASD prevalence continues to increase and offer insight into the trends observed by ADDM at the national level.

2. Materials and methods

2.1 ASD counts from DDS

This study used California Department of Developmental Services (DDS) autism counts, stratified by county, by birth year from 1993-2020, and by race/ethnicity for each of the following groups: Asian, Hispanic, black, white, and all races, where the latter encompassed the entire population, including other specific categories (e.g., American Indian/Alaskan Native and two or more races) as well as those of unknown race. The ASD counts were obtained on January 13, 2026, through a public records request to DDS. All California DDS participants are reassessed on a regular basis. Once their condition is established, DDS normally serves people from cradle to grave. 12 Thus, the counts represent an “age-resolved” snapshot of the DDS Code 1 caseload as of late 2025, representing eligible individuals living in California who met the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria 1 for ASD. Only the 36 most populous California counties were considered for the county-level analysis, as data cells with low counts were not made available as per DDS policy. However, statewide ASD counts, encompassing all 58 California counties and stratified by the same birth years and race/ethnicity groups, were also obtained. Since the datasets were aggregated by age at the county or state level, with low counts excluded, and did not include personally identifying information, this study did not require institutional review board approval or written informed consent from study subjects.

2.2 Denominators for the prevalence calculation

Total population denominators were obtained from two alternative sources: 1) U.S. Census data 13 and 2) public school populations from the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES). 14 Both datasets were stratified by California county, age, and the same 5 race/ethnicity groups used for the ASD counts described above. Statewide data for California, stratified by race/ethnicity and age, also were obtained from both the U.S. Census and NCES. The difference between these two alternative denominators provided the main basis for quantifying the uncertainty in ASD prevalence at both the county and state level, as described in Section 2.3 below.

2.2.1 Method D1: US census data

Official 2025 Census totals were obtained by California County and each of the 5 race/ethnicity groups. The data were available as totals over 5-year age groups, including, 5-9, 10-14, 15-19, 20-24, 25-29, and 30-34 years. 13 In the absence of more detailed information, these totals were distributed equally among all individual years in each age group. Age was subtracted from 2025 to convert to birth year, from 1993-2020.

2.2.2 Method D2: NCES public school populations

Public school populations were obtained from the NCES website as annual reports, compiled at the beginning of the Fall semester, for school years extending from 2008-09 to 2024-25. 14 The reports were stratified by California county, race/ethnicity and grade from K-12. Grades K-12 were assumed to correspond to ages 5-17, respectively. These ages in turn were subtracted from the Fall report year to convert to birth year. The most recent 2024-25 NCES report, which lagged the 2025 ASD data described in Section 2.2.1 by 1 year, was used to estimate the populations of public school children aged 6-15 (birth years 2009-2018). The kindergarten populations from the 2024-25 report were not used because NCES kindergarten populations in California include Transitional Kindergarten (i.e., pre-kindergarten) and thus tend to overestimate the 5-year-old population in many counties. Instead, the populations for birth years 2019 and 2020 (corresponding to children aged 6 and 5 years in 2025, respectively) were assumed to equal the 6-year-old population from the 2024-25 NCES report. For older cohorts born in 1993 to 2008, the 10th grade (15-year-old) populations from NCES reports 2008-09 to 2023-24, respectively, were used. Age 15 was chosen to avoid losses due to dropouts for older teenagers. Several additional details and exceptions from the standard D2 method are described in the Supplementary Materials in Section S1.

2.3 Prevalence calculation

The denominators from each of Methods D1 and D2 were used to estimate alternative ASD prevalence values statewide and as a function of county, each stratified by race/ethnicity. The mean ASD prevalence averaged over Methods D1 and D2 was used as the default in all results presented below, unless otherwise noted, while the difference between the two methods provided a basis for defining the uncertainty of the estimated ASD prevalence. The error bars presented in the figures reflect plus or minus half this difference. Notably, the error bars capture only the denominator component of uncertainty, since there was not an obvious way to define the numerator uncertainty based on the ASD counts from DDS. Mean prevalence and estimates of uncertainty are provided in Supplementary Table S1, while the D1 and D2 denominators are provided in Supplementary Tables S2_D1 and S2_D2, respectively.

Compared to the U.S. Census and NCES denominators, a larger fraction of the DDS ASD counts did not fall under the 4 available individual race/ethnicity categories of Asian, Hispanic, black, and white (which could be determined by calculating the difference between the all-races totals and the sum of the 4 individual categories). This suggested some discrepancies in the race/ethnicity fractions that were unknown or assigned to another category. Section 4.6 discusses the implications of these numerator vs. denominator discrepancies for the race/ethnicity-stratified prevalence calculation.

2.4 Estimation of the changepoint in prevalence

In many counties, visual inspection of the ASD prevalence data suggested a discernible changepoint in the rate of increase in recent years. A hockey-stick model approach 15 was used to formally quantify the birth year of the changepoint, stratifying by county and race/ethnicity. This was a descriptive approach aimed at characterizing changes in the slope of observed prevalence over time rather than inferring causal changepoints in incidence. The hockey-stick model involved fitting piecewise linear regressions to two periods of data spanning birth years 2006-2019 and varying the midpoint between 2012-2017. The combined residual sum of squares (RSS) for the two regression lines was calculated and the minimum RSS was used to identify the changepoint. The end of period 1 was set as the beginning of period 2 and the overlapping year was identified as Yc-1. The actual changepoint Yc was assigned to the following year, to allow for growth in ASD prevalence between birth years Yc-1 and Yc in the calculation of the period 2 linear regression slope. Continuity at the join of the two fit lines was not enforced. Following, 15 clear statistical evidence that the post-changepoint slope exceeded the pre-changepoint slope was required, i.e., the changepoint was determined to be significant only if the maximum slope of period 1 was less than the minimum slope of period 2 within the respective 95% confidence intervals. In addition, the difference between the two slopes was evaluated using a t-test.

Weighted linear regressions were performed for the default case in which the mean of the D1 and D2 denominators was used to calculate ASD prevalence. Here, the inverse square of the uncertainty (i.e., half the difference between prevalence calculated with D1 vs. D2) was assigned as the weight. In counties with 1 or more datapoints for which the D1-D2 difference was very small (<0.01%), a base uncertainty of 0.05% was added to avoid inordinately large or near-infinite weights. This base value was chosen because it was the typical prevalence uncertainty in the dataset prior to the mid 2010s.

The start of period 1 was set at birth year 2006, because the Census-based denominators had anomalously large populations in the 20-24 year age group in some counties (e.g., Butte, San Luis Obispo, Santa Barbara, Santa Cruz), corresponding to birth years 2001-2005 (Supplementary File S2_D1). These same bumps in population were not seen in the NCES-based denominators (Supplementary File S2_D2) and may have reflected a transient college population. However, the sensitivity of the changepoint to an earlier start year of 2000 was tested. The end of period 2 was set at birth year 2019, because ASD prevalence in many counties decreased slightly from 2019 to 2020. This was likely due to underascertainment at age 5 compared to age 6, which can yield misleading trends if not taken into account.16,17 However, the sensitivity of the changepoint to end year 2020 was tested.

Several additional sensitivity tests were performed. The first two used D1 or D2 alone as the denominator in the ASD prevalence calculation. For those cases, the fit lines were calculated using unweighted linear regressions, since there was not a systematic way to define the uncertainty of the individual prevalence data points. A sensitivity test using unweighted linear regressions for the default denominator case (mean of D1 and D2) was also conducted. The results of all sensitivity tests are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

2.5 Socioeconomic indices

Since previous studies have indicated that mean ASD prevalence and rate of change in prevalence may be correlated to both income and private vs. public insurance rates,10,18 two alternative metrics for evaluating socioeconomic status were obtained at the county level. The first was median household income. 19 The second was the percentage of the county population served by Medicaid (which in California is called Medi-Cal). 20 An ecological correlation analysis was performed to test the hypothesis that these indices were significantly correlated at the county level to the rate of increase in ASD prevalence in the pre- or post-changepoint period. Since 5 races and 2 time periods were considered, a Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for false positives in multiple simultaneous hypotheses.

3. Results

3.1 Autism prevalence trends by race/ethnicity and county in the 2025 DDS dataset

A changepoint was evident in the ASD prevalence vs. birth year trend for at least one race/ethnicity in the majority of the most populous California counties. For example, among Hispanic children, a changepoint was detected by the RSS method in 24 out of 36 counties (Table 1). The time trends in selected counties and race/ethnicities are shown in Figures 1 and 2, with all data shown in Supplemental Figure S1. The data generally could be divided into 2 periods, before and after the changepoint. The linear fit to the data in the first period in most cases had a nearly flat or moderately increasing slope, while the linear fit to the data in the second period had a more steeply increasing slope. The RSS method suggested that the changepoint typically occurred around birth year 2016, although it occurred as early as 2013 in some counties and as late as 2018 in others (Figures 1 and 2, Table 1). The increase in the slope from period 1 to 2 was highly significant in many counties, as illustrated in Table 1 for Hispanics.

ASD prevalence has evolved in different ways among wealthy vs. lower-income California counties and among Hispanics vs. whites. The latter are the 2 largest race/ethnicity groups among California children, representing 56% and 20%, respectively, of the public-school population. In the wealthiest counties (Figure 1, top row), ASD prevalence among whites remained flat or increased only relatively slowly since birth year 2000, with the absolute prevalence staying at or below 1-2%. In most of these wealthiest counties, white prevalence did not have an obvious changepoint based on visual inspection. However, the RSS method yielded a significant changepoint in some of the counties largely because the period 1 slope was flat or even slightly negative and transitioned to a positive slope in period 2. ASD prevalence among Hispanics in these counties increased more quickly, with a changepoint occurring between birth year 2013-2016 (Figure 1). A crossover in which Hispanic ASD prevalence overtook white prevalence occurred shortly after the changepoint, by birth year 2016. In Santa Clara, the crossover occurred earlier, around birth year 2010.

In middle income counties (Figure 1, middle row), including the large metropolitan areas of San Diego and Los Angeles, ASD prevalence was generally higher than in the wealthy counties and increased continuously among whites across birth year 2000-2019, with a changepoint around birth year 2015-2016 in Los Angeles and Solano Counties. ASD rates among Hispanics in these middle-income counties generally increased at a rate similar to that among whites, with a changepoint in birth year 2016 in Los Angeles and Solano counties and in birth year 2013 in San Diego and Sacramento Counties. In San Diego and Los Angeles, the 2 most populous counties in California, Hispanic prevalence overtook white prevalence in birth year 2013 and 2018, respectively. Absolute prevalence reached as high as 5.7% ± 0.4% among Hispanics and 4.5% ± 1.2% among whites in Los Angeles by birth year 2019.

In lower income counties, ASD prevalence increased steadily between birth years 2000-2014 and at an accelerating rate after birth year 2015 among both whites and Hispanics, with a changepoint in birth year 2015 or 2016 (Figure 1, bottom row). In contrast to the wealthy and middle-income counties, absolute ASD prevalence in most cases continued to increase after 2019, with prevalence among 5-year-olds born in 2020 as high as 8.7% ± 0.7% among whites in Butte County and 4.9% ± 0.6% among Hispanics in both Fresno and Tulare Counties (Supplementary Table S1 and Figure S1).

The absolute value and trends in ASD prevalence among Asians, who comprise 13% of California’s public-school population, were similar to those among whites (Supplemental Figure S2). The main difference was that Asians showed a steeper increase in prevalence, with a significant changepoint in birth year 2015-2017 in Los Angeles, San Diego, and Sacramento Counties.

Blacks comprise less than 5% of the public-school population in California. As a result, their ASD counts were small for many counties and not available due to DDS data release policies. However, in the 9 selected counties with the most complete data, ASD prevalence among blacks was invariably higher than among whites, Asians, and Hispanics (Figure 2). A significant changepoint, most commonly around birth year 2015-2016, occurred in each of the 9, including wealthy, middle- and lower-income counties. ASD prevalence among blacks reached 9.5% ± 1.3% in Los Angeles in birth year 2019, around 8% in Sacramento, Fresno, and Kern counties, and 6-7% in Orange and San Diego Counties. In Los Angeles, prevalence continued to increase after 2019 to a high of 9.9% ± 1.3% among 5-year-olds born in 2020.

3.2 Sensitivity of changepoint

The changepoint year, as calculated using the RSS minimization method, ranged from 2013 to 2018 with 2016 as the most common year (Figure 3). In some counties, the changepoint varied depending on the denominator used in the ASD prevalence calculation (i.e., the mean of D1 and D2 (the base case) vs. D1 or D2 alone) (Figure 3; Supplementary Figure 3a). However, 16 out of 24 changepoints identified for Hispanics agreed for all denominators, and the base case changepoint agreed with at least one of D1 or D2 for 21 out 24 cases. The period 2 slopes, i.e., the rate of change in ASD prevalence from Yc-1 to 2019, were generally in good agreement for those 21 cases for all denominators, with steeper slopes tending to occur in counties with a changepoint in 2016. In most counties, the changepoint was not sensitive to pushing back the start year of the calculation from 2006 to 2000, or to extending the end year from 2019 to 2020, or to the use of weighted vs. unweighted linear regressions (Supplementary Figure S3b; Supplementary Table S3).

3.3 County-level correlations to socioeconomic indicators

The rate of change in county-level ASD prevalence over birth years 2015-2019 was significantly correlated to the percentage of the county population served by Medicaid for all races (R= 0.70, p < 0.001) (Figure 4). Correlations were also found for blacks (R= 0.68, p = 0.03) and Hispanics (R=0.62, p<0.001) over this period. The county-level correlations for Hispanics and all races remained statistically significant after applying a Bonferroni correction of 10 to account for multiple simultaneous hypotheses, while the correlation for blacks did not. For the earlier period 2006-2015, the rate of change in county-level ASD prevalence at the county level was weakly correlated for all races to the Medicaid fraction (R= 0.46, p = 0.009) but the correlation was not significant after applying the Bonferroni correction.

Similar county-level correlations, but negative in sign, were found between median household income and rate of change in ASD prevalence for all races between birth years 2006-2015 as well as 2015-2019. Some correlations for individual races were found, including for whites over the pre-changepoint period (R= -0.48, p = 0.02) and whites and Hispanics over the post-changepoint period (R = -0.48, p = 0.02 and R= -0.47, p= 0.01, respectively) but none of these was significant after applying the Boneferroni correction. Since median household income and percentage served by Medicaid are themselves significantly inversely correlated (R= -0.74, p<<0.001), only the results using the Medicaid indices are shown in Figure 4.

3.4 Statewide California ASD prevalence trends and comparison to San Diego and ADDM

Summing the DDS ASD counts across all 58 California counties tended to blur the sharp changepoint around birth year 2015-2016 that was evident in much of the county level data. The statewide prevalence trend appeared as a smooth curve that could be fit better to a continuous quadratic function rather than to 2 piecewise linear fits (Figure 5). Statewide prevalence among 5-year-olds born in 2020 was slightly lower than among 6-year-olds born in 2019 for all races.

Within DDS, the time trend in statewide ASD prevalence agreed well with San Diego County prevalence for all race/ethnicities over most of the dataset, with Pearson correlation coefficients ≥ 0.9 (Table 2). However, these correlations were weaker for blacks and whites after 2015 (Table 2). ASD prevalence in San Diego in relative terms was approximately 33% and 20% higher for Asians and Hispanics, respectively, and 10% lower among whites and blacks compared to the statewide values over the period from 2015-2020 (Table 2). Similar to the DDS data, ADDM prevalence in San Diego diverged strongly by race/ethnicity, especially among 4-year-olds, with Hispanic, black, and Asian children more likely to be diagnosed than white children.

4. Discussion

4.1 Accelerated increase in ASD growth rate with changepoint circa birth year 2016

The new county-level time trends in DDS-diagnosed ASD prevalence reported here, which extend through birth year 2020, differ substantially from the study of, 10 which extended through birth year 2013. The earlier study found flat or even declining trends among whites in wealthy counties, contrasted with increasing trends among blacks and Hispanics, and with ongoing increases among all races in low and middle income counties. The new data show similar divergence in trends along lines of race/ethnicity and county income, but are characterized by a new, statistically significant changepoint in the mid 2010s. This occurs typically around birth year 2016 and is most pronounced among Hispanics and blacks in many counties, as well as whites and Asians in many middle- and lower-income counties. The changepoint potentially can provide valuable information for formulating hypotheses about causation. 15 However, this paper focuses on identifying the year of the changepoint, rather than on specific mechanistic explanations. Similarly, this paper does not attempt to distinguish whether the recent increases seen in ASD prevalence are due to a true increase in incidence as opposed to changes in diagnostic criteria and access to services. A true growth in ASD prevalence is consistent with first-hand reports from school officials, who have testified to the California State Legislature about the financial burden that the rapidly increasing ASD caseload is creating for their districts. 21 However, these anecdotal reports are consistent with rising DDS caseloads but are not formal prevalence data. In addition, the last statewide hearing on this topic took place in 2018, leaving important unanswered questions about current trends from the perspective of schools.

4.2 Divergence in ASD trends by race/ethnicity

An important caveat on all the race/ethnicity-specific prevalences for Asians, Hispanics, blacks, and whites is that the values presented here may tend to underestimate true prevalence. The underestimate may occur because a larger portion of the DDS ASD cases are categorized as “other” compared to either the D1 or D2 denominators, with unknown relative effects on each of the 4 individual race/ethnicity groups depending on how the denominator-minus-numerator discrepancy in “other” cases is redistributed (see Section 4.6 for further discussion).

With that caveat, the California DDS data indicate a divergence and demographic reversal in ASD prevalence over time along lines of race/ethnicity. White prevalence in DDS historically exceeded Hispanic prevalence, by a factor of 2 as recently as birth year 2002, but the gap has closed since then. A Hispanic/white crossover in ASD prevalence occurred in birth year 2018 at the statewide level in DDS (Figures 5 and 6) and had occurred sometime between birth year 2010 and 2016 in several of the wealthier California counties (Figure 1). This demographic reversal has been noted by other researchers 11 and is all the more remarkable in the context of the likely underestimate of Hispanic prevalence by DDS due to hesitancy among recent immigrants to apply for state-level administrative aid. 22

ASD prevalence in the recent DDS data is higher in black children than other race/ethnicities in California and was more than 50% higher than the statewide average for all races combined by birth year 2019. The black/white crossover in statewide DDS data occurred around birth year 2004, considerably earlier than the Hispanic/white crossover (Figures 5 and 6). In comparison, studies based on ADDM data have noted a black/white catch-up and crossover in ASD prevalence that occurred later, around birth year 2008-2010.23,24 Similarly, 25 noted a black/white crossover around birth year 2010 in U.S. Department of Education Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) data, with a Hispanic/white crossover that was projected to occur around birth year 2013. The black/white crossover may have occurred earlier in DDS than in ADDM or IDEA because the fraction of ASD with co-occurring intellectual disability is substantially higher, by as much as a factor of 2 in some ADDM data, among blacks compared to whites. 26 Thus, black children may be disproportionately represented in DDS, which serves more severe ASD cases.

The race/ethnicity disparities in DDS data appear to be widening in the youngest birth cohorts, consistent with recent trends in ADDM data from California. By birth year 2020, DDS prevalence had climbed as high as 9.9% ± 1.3% in Los Angeles County among black children, more than twice the level for whites in that county (Figures 1 and 2). Similarly, California ADDM ASD prevalence among 4-year-old black children born in 2018 was 8.1%, a factor of 2.3 higher than the 3.6% prevalence in white children (Figure 5). 8

While sex-stratified DDS data were not obtained for the current study, the high ASD prevalence in California has particular implications for boys, especially in counties and among race/ethnicity groups where DDS prevalence for boys and girls combined exceeds 5% and, in some cases, e.g., black children in Los Angeles County, approaches as high as 10%. Under the assumption of a 3:1 male:female ratio, 8 ASD prevalence among black boys in the Los Angeles DDS caseload would be about 15%. Under the further assumption of substantial underascertainment by DDS (see discussion below), the upper bound of ASD prevalence among black boys in Los Angeles could be substantially higher than 15%. These estimates, if accurate, have profound implications for society and highlight the urgent need to understand why increasing numbers of children are being diagnosed with ASD.

4.3 Divergence in ASD trends by county

The divergence in ASD prevalence at the county level in the DDS data was equally if not even more striking than the divergence along lines of race/ethnicity. Prevalence among whites was as low as 1.2 ± 0.3% in birth year 2019 in wealthy counties like San Mateo, and as high as 5.3% ± 1% and 7.3% ± 0.65% in lower income counties like Tulare and Shasta, respectively (Figure 1, Supplemental Figure S1 and Table S1). Similarly, prevalence among Hispanics ranged from as low as 1.7% to 2.2% in San Mateo and Santa Clara Counties, respectively to as high as 4.8% ± 0.06 in Tulare and 8.6% ± 0.6 in Imperial County. Similar large disparities were found by 10 by California county, particularly among whites, both in terms of mean ASD prevalence and mean rate of change in prevalence, both of which were negatively correlated at the county level to median household income for the birth year period 2000-2013.

This study found similar county-level correlations between the 2015-2019 mean rate of change in ASD prevalence and county Medicaid fraction, which were statistically significant for Hispanics and all races. One hypothesis is that differences in ASD prevalence by socioeconomic status reflect differences in risk factors, such as prenatal exposures or lifestyle factors,6,27 although this hypothesis cannot be tested with ecological data presented here. Furthermore, the use of aggregated county-level data limits causal inference. The observed correlations between county-level socioeconomic indicators and ASD prevalence do not establish that individual-level socioeconomic status causes differences in ASD risk.

Diagnostic practices and unequal patterns of service access or participation in DDS also may play a role in the observed county-level differences and associations with Medicaid fraction. Counties with higher Medicaid fractions may have more aggressive outreach or lower thresholds for service eligibility. Conversely, wealthier counties may have more private services outside DDS, leading parents to out of DDS in favor of private care. In addition, parents and/or school districts from middle and low-income areas may be incentivized to seek an ASD diagnosis for children to secure support services, funding opportunities, and other benefits attendant to the diagnosis. Future work using individual-level data is needed to disentangle these possible confounders to determine if lower-income populations are truly experiencing larger increases in ASD prevalence.

4.4 San Diego vs. statewide ASD prevalence

The California DDS data analyzed in this study indicate that ASD prevalence in San Diego County is on average about 20% higher from birth year 2015-2020 than statewide but otherwise (e.g., in terms of upward time trend) largely consistent with ASD prevalence statewide in California. This is true for the time trends across most race/ethnicity groups, as well as for the absolute value of ASD prevalence (Figures 5 and 6). The relative consistency between the statewide and San Diego DDS data suggests that the strikingly high values and increasing trends found by ADDM in San Diego are generally representative of California as a whole. In the most recent ADDM report for surveillance year 2022, California’s ASD prevalence exceeded the estimated U.S. nationwide mean by 65% for children aged 8 years and 110% for children aged 4 years. 8

4.5 California DDS vs. ADDM

A caveat in comparing ASD prevalence for DDS vs. ADDM in San Diego, especially for Hispanics, is that recent immigrants to California may be hesitant to apply to state-funded agencies like DDS for aid. In contrast, they may be more comfortable using services from their local school districts.22,28 Since ADDM reviews school-based evaluations as well as health care records to determine cases, Hispanic children with ASD may be more likely to be identified by ADDM than by DDS. The implementation of California SB75, which took effect in summer 2016 and made children under 19 years old eligible for full-scope Medi-Cal benefits regardless of immigration status, may be relevant to this issue. 29 This law would have brought many Hispanic children into the health care system in California, although it is unclear how it would have affected their likelihood of being identified with an ASD diagnosis under ADDM vs. DDS criteria.

Another caveat is that DDS, historically, has served the more severe end of the autism spectrum,30,31 while ADDM considers the full range of the autism spectrum. Although the ASD subtypes enumerated in DSM-IV (autistic disorder (AD), PDD-NOS and Asperger’s) are no longer distinguished following the adoption of DSM-5, 1 historically over half of the ADDM caseload was diagnosed with PDD-NOS or Asperger’s Disorder, which are milder presentations than AD. 32 In the most recent ADDM report, under the assumption that co-occurring intellectual disability (ID) provides a measure of ASD severity, 40% of ASD cases nationwide had co-occurring ID (IQ ≤ 70), while the majority had either normal IQ > 85 (36%), or IQ in the borderline range 71-85 (24%). 8

Since DDS requires a demonstration of functional disability in several key aspects of self-care and daily life, 33 the DDS values presented here could be assumed to capture only a subset of total ASD cases. This supposition is supported by the comparison of San Diego DDS and ADDM data for 8-year-olds, which suggests that ADDM prevalence is about 2 times higher than DDS prevalence for a given birth cohort (Figures 5 and 6). However, lacking ADDM data from other counties, it is unclear if this factor-of-2 multiplier is generalizable to the entire state.

4.6 Limitations and uncertainties

About 20% of the DDS caseload falls into the race/ethnicity category “other,” which was not included as a separate category in the dataset obtained for this study. 34 The fraction assigned to “other” has grown sharply in recent years in some counties, most notably Humboldt, Riverside, and San Bernardino, such that the sum of Asian, black, Hispanic, and white ASD counts has deviated strongly from the all-races count (Supplementary Figure S4). These strong deviations suggest that the ASD prevalence estimates from Humboldt, Riverside, and San Bernardino Counties in the DDS dataset are not reliable. These counties were therefore excluded from the changepoint and correlation analyses.

A more general issue, affecting most counties as well as the statewide data, is that a larger proportion of the ASD counts than total population denominators fall in the “other” category. Here “other” is inferred as the difference between all races and the sum of the 4 available individual race/ethnicities (Asian, black, Hispanic, and white). The “other”ASD counts underestimate the all-races ASD count by 10% to 40% in most counties from 2015-2019. In contrast, the “other” denominator populations underestimate the all-races population by only ∼9% on average at the county and state level (Supplementary Figure S5). This difference can lead to an artificially inflated absolute ASD prevalence of > 10% in the “other” category, as illustrated in Supplementary Table S2 using statewide data as an example. Conversely, it suggests that the Asian, Hispanic, black, and white ASD prevalence values in this study may underestimate the true race/ethnicity-specific values (by an absolute 0.7% in the example in Supplementary Table S2) if the “other” data reflecting mixed race/ethnicity populations are redistributed among the 4 available individual groups.

In addition to the race/ethnicity uncertainties described above, the ASD caseload recorded in DDS may not reflect the true number of cases in the population because DDS tends to serve the more severe end of the autism spectrum and may exclude approximately half of all cases. It is also possible that some parents may choose private care for their children with autism rather than public DDS services. Conversely, the rising diagnosed caseload in DDS could result from increased awareness, changes in diagnostic criteria or practices, improved service access, or diagnostic substitution, in addition to or instead of a true increase in ASD incidence. The current data cannot distinguish between these mechanisms.

Another source of uncertainty lies in the denominators used to estimate race/ethnicity and county specific ASD prevalence. The use of alternative U.S. Census-based and NCES-based data helps quantify that uncertainty but may not provide a full or comprehensive assessment of the true uncertainty in the denominators. The NCES denominators include only public-school populations and thus may underestimate the total population in counties with large private school populations. The Census-based denominators in contrast include both public and private school populations but are based on 5-year age group totals. This study assumed an even distribution of those totals across each of the 5 years, but that assumption may not accurately capture the true annual variation.

Finally, the RSS minimization algorithm used to determine the changepoint in ASD prevalence is sensitive in some counties to the choice of denominator as well as to the weights assigned to each data point (Figure 3; Supplementary Figure S3). Since this study based those weights on the estimated uncertainty in prevalence, the numerator and denominator uncertainties discussed above can influence the changepoint calculation. The RSS algorithm may also be sensitive to individual anomalous ASD values, especially when they occur around the time of the changepoint.

5. Conclusions

California DDS-diagnosed autism prevalence shows a changepoint around birth year 2016, which is most striking when the data are stratified by county and race/ethnicity. Aggregating statewide and across all races tends to smooth the sharp uptick observed at the county level. The high diagnosed ASD prevalence can be considered in the context that DDS has historically served the more severe end of the ASD spectrum, such that the values presented here likely underestimate the full prevalence of ASD in California. That assumption is supported by comparison to California ADDM data, which are collected in San Diego and yield ASD values that are twice as high as DDS for the same birth cohort. Within the DDS dataset, San Diego County ASD prevalence and trends are largely consistent with statewide values. This suggests that the data collected in San Diego are ∼20% higher overall but otherwise generally representative of California as a whole in terms of their upward trend. Strong variations in DDS-diagnosed ASD prevalence were observed both among counties and by race/ethnicity. Wealthier California counties had substantially lower diagnosed ASD prevalence, particularly for whites, and were less likely to display a strong changepoint in their ASD growth rate over birth years 2006-2019. Understanding the factors driving diagnosed ASD prevalence up to the high levels reported in this study is a pressing public health concern.
