Carbon dot based topical application for therapy of phosphorus oxide-induced cutaneous burns
Authors: Halyna Kuznietsova, Iryna Byelinska, Alexander Zaderko, Olha Pylypova, Nataliia Rybalchenko, Vladimir Lysenko, Nataliia Dziubenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
White phosphorus and phosphorus oxide (PO) burns combine thermal and chemical injury, causing deep, progressive necrosis and systemic inflammation. This study evaluated whether carbon dots (CDs) synthesized from citric acid and urea can improve healing of PO-induced skin burns and reduce systemic damage. CDs showed broad antimicrobial activity, most pronounced against Staphylococcus aureus. Application of CD-containing dressings stabilized burn progression, preventing early aggravation and excessive edema, while promoting transient epithelial proliferation during the first week post-burn. Systemically, CDs normalized elevated white blood cell and neutrophil counts, restored lymphocyte balance, and suppressed IL-6 elevation, confirming anti-inflammatory action. They also mitigated erythrocyte loss and anemia development without affecting liver or kidney function. Overall, CD topical application demonstrated antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective effects, moderating PO burn progression and supporting balanced wound repair. These findings identify carbon dots as a promising multifunctional platform for the treatment of complex chemical–thermal skin injuries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48649-2.

Introduction

Throughout the history of warfare, both past and present, a wide spectrum of injury patterns has been encountered and treated among soldiers and civilians alike. Among these, burns and traumatic wounds remain among the most frequent in modern conventional conflicts.

In contemporary warfare, many anti-personnel munitions incorporate white phosphorus (WP). Although Protocol III of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) restricts WP use near civilian populations, its repeated deployment in Fallujah, Gaza, Lebanon, and more recently in Ukraine highlights persistent legal loopholes and humanitarian concerns1.

WP is chemically unstable, with a low melting point of 44 °C and an auto-ignition temperature of only 30 °C. Above this threshold, particles spontaneously ignite in contact with air, oxidizing to phosphorus pentoxide. In wounds, WP fragments continue to combust until they are surgically removed, neutralized, or fully consumed. Because of its low melting point, irrigation with warm water accelerates liquefaction and raises the risk of re-ignition at body temperature, while the liquid form is more difficult to detect or remove2.

WP injuries are associated with profound physiological consequences. Life-threatening hypocalcemia and/or hyperphosphatemia may occur within an hour of exposure. WP produces combined chemical and thermal burns. During combustion of WP, multiple phosphorus oxides (PO) are formed, including P4O10 (P2O5), P4O6 (P2O3), and intermediate oxides such as P4O7–P4O9. Upon contact with moisture in air or tissues, these oxides rapidly hydrolyze into a mixture of phosphoric acids (e.g., H3PO4, H3PO3, H4P2O7), polyphosphates, and occasionally phosphine (PH3). Each of these products exerts its own toxicological effect. Notably, P4O10 acts as a highly reactive dehydrating agent, generating concentrated (poly)phosphoric acids through rapid exothermic reactions with tissue water, leading to severe local chemical injury. Thus, burn damage associated with white phosphorus is not caused by a single compound but results from the combined thermal, corrosive, and chemical toxicity of phosphorus, its oxides, and their hydrolysis products acting simultaneously3,4. As a result, WP burns often heal more slowly than other thermal injuries5.

Burn wounds are also highly susceptible to opportunistic infections due to extensive tissue necrosis, impaired barrier function, and local immunosuppression. Early infections are typically caused by Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, while later stages are often dominated by Gram-negative pathogens including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Fungal infections (e.g., Candida spp., Aspergillus spp.) may also arise in severe or prolonged cases and can progress to systemic infection if not controlled6,7.

A key clinical challenge in complex burn injuries is antimicrobial resistance. Burn wounds frequently harbor multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms due to prolonged hospitalization, extensive antibiotic exposure, and biofilm formation within the wound environment. This significantly limits therapeutic options and often necessitates the use of combination regimens or last-line antibiotics, which may be associated with increased toxicity and suboptimal efficacy8.

Although survival rates have improved with advances in burn care, phosphorus burns remain complex injuries requiring prolonged management9. Current emergency protocols stress three immediate priorities: (1) exclusion of oxygen by immersion or wet dressings to halt combustion; (2) surgical debridement rather than copper sulfate application, given the latter’s nephrotoxic risks; and (3) continuous cardiac monitoring to prevent arrhythmias. Novel strategies such as chelating hydrogels and extracorporeal phosphorus adsorption show encouraging preclinical data but lack validation in battlefield conditions3.

Effective management of WP burns requires a deep understanding of their mechanisms. Since phosphorus continues to burn in the presence of oxygen, and phosphorus oxides, these injuries are typically deep, extremely painful, and may extend to bone. Immediate local treatment is more urgent than in conventional burns, as WP injuries cause both local and systemic disturbances. Yet despite decades of clinical experience, consensus is lacking on the most effective acute and long-term interventions10.

In recent years, nanotechnology has introduced novel opportunities for wound care. Carbon-based nanomaterials, in particular, have attracted considerable attention due to their diverse biological activities. Carbon nanocomposites, enriched with heterogeneous atoms and functional groups, exhibit anti-inflammatory11, and antibacterial effects12, and can either promote or inhibit cellular proliferation13. These properties make them attractive candidates for wound-healing applications, especially in the context of burn injuries. Our recent study demonstrated the therapeutic potential of carbon dots derived from citric acid and urea in managing alkali-induced cutaneous burns14.

Against this backdrop, the present study was designed to investigate whether carbon dots synthesized from citric acid and urea can enhance the healing of PO-induced skin burns and mitigate the systemic damage caused by this unique and devastating type of chemical injury.

The colloidal solutions of CDs have a yellowish hue but remain transparent when exposed to sunlight (see inset in Fig. 1-b) and their Z-potential was found to be about − 20 mV.

Burn healing

As one can see, in the positive control (Burn) group, the relative burn area initially increased, reaching a peak at the mid-time points, and then gradually declined, forming a bell-shaped curve over time. The Hydrosorb gel group showed a similar overall trend but with a markedly attenuated increase in burn area and a faster transition toward resolution. In contrast, when evaluating the burn healing dynamics following CD-containing dressing application, a marked reduction in the relative burn area was observed during Days 1–9 post-treatment (Fig. 5a, b). Although the burn area did not significantly differ from initial one, the healing curve appeared smoother, without the transient aggravation typically seen during the first post-burn week.

According to histological picture, healthy mouse skin is well organized, with a thin stratified epidermis, a collagen-rich dermis containing normal hair follicles and sebaceous glands, and an underlying adipose subcutis. Examination of skin samples collected from the burn margins revealed pronounced edema and inflammatory infiltration in the Burn group on Day 7, which subsided by Day 14, accompanied by a notable accumulation of connective tissue (Fig. 6). The epithelial layer was thicker than in healthy skin on both Days 7 and 14, although the increase at Day 14 was not statistically significant due to high inter-individual variability, likely reflecting differences in healing progression among animals (Table 2).

Treatment with Hydrosorb gel did not alter histological parameters compared with untreated burns on either Day 7 or 14. In contrast, the CD-containing dressing prevented the development of pronounced edema at Day 7 and excessive connective tissue deposition (based on a semi-quantitative assessment) at Day 14 (values comparable to healthy controls). Interestingly, a significant increase in epithelial thickness was detected on Day 7 relative to untreated burns, which subsided by Day 14. This finding suggests that CD dressing may stimulate epithelial proliferation during the early phase of burn wound repair, however, this effect needs to be proved.

Inflammation

Hematological analysis in case of PO-induced burn investigation is essential to monitor systemic toxicity and inflammatory responses, as well as for assessing treatment safety and detecting potential complications such as infection or impaired oxygen-carrying capacity. Complete blood count analysis revealed a sustained increase in white blood cells (WBC) levels in rats with PO-induced burns, indicating an active systemic inflammatory response. WBC counts increased by 23% on Day 7 and remained 12% above baseline on Day 14 post-injury. This was accompanied by a marked rise in neutrophil populations — band forms increased 4.3-fold and segmented neutrophils 2.7-fold on Day 7 — with elevated levels persisting through Day 14. Concurrently, lymphocyte counts decreased by 38%, reflecting immune cell redistribution during inflammation (Fig. 7). These hematological changes appeared to be in line with the observed histopathological alterations in burned skin. Treatment with CD dressing effectively mitigated inflammation, lowering total WBC counts down to baseline on both Day 7 and 14. Lymphocyte counts tended to normalize toward control values, suggesting partial restoration of immune homeostasis (Fig. 7).

PO burns also induced hematological complications associated with erythrocyte alterations, leading to normocytic anemia. Hemoglobin concentration, erythrocyte count, and hematocrit were significantly reduced on Day 7, with partial recovery by Day 14, consistent with erythrocyte loss and transient impairment of tissue oxygenation (Fig. 8). CD dressing application demonstrated greater efficacy than vehicle in preventing erythroid disruption and reducing anemia risk, supporting its complex regenerative potential. A mild delay in corpuscular hemoglobinization was observed but without clinically relevant consequences (Fig. 8).

Assessment of IL-6 and IL-10 levels provides insight into the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses following injury. IL-6 reflects the magnitude of the acute inflammatory reaction, while IL-10 indicates the activation of regulatory pathways that limit excessive inflammation and support tissue recovery. As can be seen, IL-6 levels were substantially increased in Burn and Hydrosorb gel groups on Day 7 post-burn, with decreasing to the level of healthy animals on Day 14 (Fig. 9), which reflected the dynamics of inflammation development and was in line with hematological and histopathological findings. Notably, that CD prevented IL-6 increase on Day 7, suggesting its anti-inflammatory effect. IL-10 levels were substantially increased in all burn-experienced groups on both Day 7 and 14, suggesting the intense resolution processes.

Discussion

PO-induced burns are distinct in their pathogenesis, as two independent factors – thermal and chemical – simultaneously contribute to tissue injury. Upon exposure to air, WP rapidly oxidizes, releasing intense heat that causes coagulative necrosis at the contact site. Owing to its lipid solubility, molecular phosphorus (P₄) penetrates deeply into subcutaneous tissues, producing progressive liquefactive necrosis that continues even after the surface flame is extinguished. During oxidation, WP forms phosphorus oxides, which subsequently hydrolyze to phosphoric and phosphorous acids (and related oxyacids), leading to additional chemical injury at the contact site. Consequently, WP/PO burns represent a combined thermal–chemical injury, where the corrosive action of phosphoric acid, the heat generated by phosphorus pentoxide, and its hygroscopic nature collectively result in extensive and progressive tissue destruction3,21. Residual WP/PO particles can remain embedded in tissue, where they may smoulder or re-ignite, continuously producing heat and corrosive byproducts until they are completely removed. This ongoing necrosis can persist for hours to days post-exposure, and such progressive tissue loss is a hallmark feature of WP/PO injury22,23.

In our study, we observed that the dynamics of PO burn development did not follow a simple decline but instead exhibited a bell-shaped progression, consistent with literature describing early burn evolution. The acute phase is marked by a combination of thermal and chemical damage, producing necrosis that extends beyond the visible margins of the wound. This phase typically lasts several hours to days, after which the wound transitions to granulation and epithelialization10. When CD-containing dressings were applied, the burn area did not markedly decrease compared to vehicle-treated controls; however, the injury progression curve flattened, with no aggravation during the first post-burn week. This stabilization suggests an early anti-inflammatory effect of CDs, preventing time-dependent injury exacerbation. Supporting this hypothesis, we observed reduced IL-6 levels on day 7 post-burn, no significant changes in IL-10, and normalization tendencies in WBC subpopulation distribution in CD-treated groups. Together, these findings indicate that CD’s benefit may lie primarily in anti-inflammatory modulation rather than direct stimulation of tissue repair.

From a mechanistic standpoint, PO burns most closely resemble alkali-induced chemical burns. Although PO oxidation produces acid derivatives (phosphoric and phosphorous acids), its behavior functionally parallels that of alkalis. Embedded phosphorus particles continue to oxidize and penetrate tissues, leading to progressive liquefactive necrosis and lipid saponification – pathophysiological features characteristic of alkali burns24. Consequently, the healing process in PO burns is notably slower compared to both acid and alkali injuries25. Indeed, when comparing burn recovery kinetics in our laboratory models, we observed the most rapid closure in acid-induced burns (78–80% closure by day 12 post-burn), followed by alkali-induced burns (67–70%)14,26 and the slowest healing in PO burns (50–60% closure by day 12, current study and unpublished data). Macroscopic appearance, histopathology, and hematological profiles in PO-burned animals also resembled those of alkali burns rather than acid burns. Similar to alkali injuries, the therapeutic effect of CD dressings in PO burns was most pronounced during the first week post-injury, after which wound closure dynamics did not differ significantly from vehicle controls14.

A frequent complication that exacerbates WP/PO-induced injury is secondary infection10. Accordingly, topical antimicrobial agents are routinely used and have shown efficacy as adjuncts in managing various burn types, including white/yellow phosphorus burns3,10,27. CD-based topical treatments represent a promising alternative approach. These nanomaterials exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, including efficacy against MDR strains, often via mechanisms such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and disruption of microbial membranes – mechanisms that reduce the likelihood of resistance development28. Therefore, the antimicrobial activity of CDs may also contribute to their therapeutic effect during the early stages of PO burn development.

Thus, CD-based dressings demonstrated protective efficacy during the early phase of PO-induced burn progression. Although CD treatment did not markedly accelerate wound closure, it effectively stabilized injury dynamics, preventing time-dependent aggravation. This effect was accompanied by decreased IL-6 levels, normalization of leukocyte distribution, and absence of protracted inflammation, suggesting that the primary mechanism of CD action is anti-inflammatory modulation rather than direct stimulation of tissue repair. The antimicrobial properties of CDs may also contribute to their early therapeutic benefit by preventing secondary infection. Overall, CD dressings exerted a protective and modulatory effect in the acute stage of PO injury, likely through combined anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Carbon dots (CDs) synthesis, characterization and dressing preparation

CDs powder was prepared via the solvothermal carbonization of a mixture of urea and anhydrous citric acid, combined in a weight ratio of 2:1. The total mass of the precursor mixture was 10 g. After mixing, the components were transferred into an open 100 mL Pyrex glass reactor. The reactor was placed in a shaft furnace, heated to 135 °C over a period of 15 min, and maintained at this temperature for one hour. During this stage, the mixture melted, and the resulting melt acquired a yellow color. Subsequently, the temperature was increased to 165 °C and maintained for one hour; significant evolution of water and ammonia was observed during this step. Upon completion of the carbonization process, gas and vapor evolution virtually ceased, and the melt transformed into a brown-black, lustrous, brittle mass. This carbonized mass was dissolved in 80 mL of 15% isopropanol under mild heating and subsequently acidified with 10 mL of glacial acetic acid, resulting in the coagulation and precipitation of the CDs. The resulting precipitate was filtered and air-dried at 120 °C.

Size distribution measurements of diluted colloidal solutions of CDs were performed using Zetasizer Nano ZS from Malvern Instruments (He–Ne laser 633 nm, 5mW, with 173° Non-Invasive Back Scatter (NIBS) detector and narrow band filter).

FTIR (Fourier-transform infrared) spectra of the lyophilized samples were acquired on IRAffinity-1, Shimadzu® in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode, using PIKE MIRacle™ ATR platform (diamond crystal plate). Dried amorphous powders of the lyophilized samples were allocated on a crystal plate, in 600–4000 cm − 1 range with a resolution of 0.5 cm− 1 were recorded and analyzed with Shimadzu IR solution v1.50®.

Fluorescence measurements were performed with a Cary Eclipse Fluorescence Spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a 75 kW xenon flash lamp (pulse width 2 µs, 100 Hz repetition rate). UV–visible absorbance spectra were collected in the 200–800 nm wavelength range at room temperature (20 °C) using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies).

For preparation of the CD dressing, an aqueous saline solution of CDs (1 mg/mL) was blended in a 3:1 ratio with Hydrosorb Gel (Paul Hartmann AG, Germany) – a commercially available hydrogel composed of Ringer’s solution, carboxyethyl cellulose, and carboxymethyl cellulose, widely applied in wound and burn care14. This gel was selected both as the delivery matrix for CDs and as a reference material in this study. The final concentration of CD in the formulation was 0.25 mg/mL, and the mixture was freshly prepared prior to each application.

CD antimicrobial activity assay

The antimicrobial activity of the samples was evaluated using reference test strains obtained from the Ukrainian Collection of Microorganisms (Institute of Microbiology and Virology, NAS of Ukraine): Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923 (Gram-positive), Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 (Gram-negative), and Candida albicans ATCC 885 − 653 (yeast-like fungus).

Antimicrobial activity was assessed using the classical agar well diffusion method29. Bacteria were cultured on Luria–Bertani agar and Candida albicans on wort agar. Microbial suspensions were standardized to 0.5 McFarland (≈ 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). Plates with Mueller–Hinton agar were inoculated with 0.1 mL of suspension, and wells were filled with 100 µL of the test samples. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C, inhibition zones were measured. Activity was classified as: ≥20 mm – strong; 15–19 mm – moderate; 9–14 mm – weak; <8 mm – inactive.

The MIC and MBC/MFC were determined by the broth microdilution method30,31. Serial twofold dilutions of samples in Mueller–Hinton broth (0.25–20 g/L) were inoculated with standardized suspensions (1.5 × 105 CFU/mL for bacteria; 1 × 104 CFU/mL for Candida). After incubation (18–24 h, 37 °C), MIC was defined as the lowest concentration showing no visible growth. For MBC/MFC, aliquots from clear tubes were plated on Mueller–Hinton agar and incubated for 24 h; the lowest concentration with no visible colonies was recorded as MBC/MFC. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Animals

The experiment was carried out on young male Wistar rats with an initial body weight ranging from 180.1 to 220.8 g (CV = 9.7%). The animals were bred on-site and maintained in the animal facility of Taras Shevchenko National University of Kyiv under natural light conditions at 20–23 °C, with unrestricted access to standard rodent chow and tap water. All procedures adhered to bioethical standards, relevant legislative requirements, and the principles of the European Convention for the Protection of Vertebrate Animals used for Experimental and Other Scientific Purposes, as well as the ARRIVE guidelines and the General Ethical Principles for Experiments on Animals adopted at the First National Bioethics Congress (Kyiv, 2001). The in vivo protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Taras Shevchenko National University of Kyiv (Protocol No. 3, dated May 2, 2019).

Burn induction and group division

Animals were randomized to the groups based on their body weight the day prior to the burn induction (day 0). In this study, P₂O₅ was used to model the chemical component of WP-induced burns, as it is the primary oxidation product responsible for tissue dehydration and acid-mediated damage. This approach allows for safer and more controlled experimental conditions compared to handling pyrophoric WP. We acknowledge that this model does not fully reproduce the thermal effects associated with burning WP particles; however, it provides a relevant approximation of the chemical phase of WP-induced injury. For the simulation of PO-induced burns, rats were anesthetized using telazol (5 mg/kg, administered intraperitoneally) and xylazine hydrochloride (8 mg/kg, given intramuscularly), and the fur on their upper back was shaved. P2O5 powder (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 298220) (about 100 mg) was quickly applied on wet skin (moisturized with 100 uL of water) and evenly distributed on the area about 1 cm2. Two hours after burn induction, a dressing containing either the vehicle or carbon dots (CD) was applied to the burn area, with treatments repeated daily.

There were 4 groups of male Wistar rats, n = 10 in each group: (1) group 1 (Control) – healthy rats with no burns; (2) group 2 (PO burn) – rats subjected to PO burn induction and received no treatment; (3) group 3 (PO burn+Vehicle) - rats subjected to PO burn induction, Hydrosorb gel (served as a vehicle) was applied on the burn in 2 h after induction and thereafter daily for 14 days; (4) group 4 (PO + CD) - rats subjected to PO burn induction, CD-contained dressing was applied on the burn in 2 h after induction and thereafter daily for 14 days. Necropsies with blood and skin samples collection and fixation were performed at the terminal sacrifice on the 7th, and 14th days, where 5 rats from each group were assigned randomly. Euthanasia was performed under CO2 anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation.

Burn area measurements

To monitor burn healing, daily photographs were taken prior to each dressing application. Burn areas were quantified from these images using ImageJ 1.8.0_172 software (NIH, USA; https://imagej.net/software/fiji/. For each animal, the burn area change was expressed as a percentage relative to Day 1 (24 h after burn induction and the first dressing application). The burn area on Day 0 was not assessed, as the complete lesion boundaries became evident only after 24 h. The initial variation in burn size may therefore reflect early differences in tissue response between treated and untreated sites.

Blood assays

For biochemical analysis, blood was collected from the femoral vein immediately after anesthesia, and serum was obtained after centrifugation. Serum biochemical parameters—alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), urea, creatinine, and total protein—were measured using commercial kits (DiagnosticumZrt, Hungary) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Hematological parameters, including red and white blood cell counts, hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), were determined using standard methods32. Hemoglobin was quantified with a colorimeter (KFK-3, Ukraine) using a Hemoglobin Assay Kit (Felicit-Diagnostics, Ukraine). Red and white blood cells, as well as platelets, were counted manually using a hemocytometer and a light microscope (MICROmed XS-3320, Ningbo Shenghens Optics & Electronics). Hematocrit values were determined after centrifuging blood in microcapillary tubes, and MCV, MCH, and MCHC were calculated from standard formulas. Differential leukocyte counts were performed on Pappenheim-stained blood smears. One hundred leukocytes, including neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes, were counted per sample. The analysis was conducted by an experienced pathologist blinded to the treatment groups.

IL-6 and IL-10 were determined in blood serum using commercial kits (RAB0312, and RAB0246, respectively, Merck, USA).

Skin histopathology

For histopathological evaluation, skin samples from the burn sites were collected immediately after sacrifice and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. The tissues were processed and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard procedures33, and examined under a light microscope. The assessment included characterization of inflammatory cell infiltration (and evaluation of tissue alterations such as edema, apoptosis, necrosis, hemorrhage, and connective tissue formation. All analyses were performed by an experienced pathologist blinded to the treatment groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism v.10.0.0 (https://www.graphpad.com/). Two-way ANOVA (or mixed-effects analysis) with appropriate multiple comparison tests was applied to assess parametric data. Histopathological scores were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data. Parametric results are presented as Mean ± SEM, whereas non-parametric data are shown as median [1st; 3rd quartiles]. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
