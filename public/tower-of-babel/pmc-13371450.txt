A mixed methods examination of organizational approaches to fentanyl test strip distribution and training across multiple states
Authors: Kitty H Gelberg, Megan E Hall, Janet E Childerhose, Mary R Russo, Fernando Montero, Kaleigh Niles, Jill Oliveri, Haley Ramsey Harden, Rachel A Vickers-Smith, Arnie Aldridge, Sarah G Stenger, Katherine R Marks, Louisa Gilbert, Sharon L Walsh, Nabila El-Bassel, Carter Roeber, Redonna Chandler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFentanyl test strips (FTS) have become a critical harm reduction tool, allowing people who use drugs (PWUD) to detect fentanyl in their drug supply and make informed decisions about their drug use. While FTS distribution is increasingly common, there is limited understanding of distribution strategies used by community organizations, and the education and training provided around FTS use. Given the proliferation of fentanyl in the drug supply, and the diverse motivations of PWUD, research is needed to better understand FTS distribution and training practices. This study examines FTS distribution strategies, training approaches, and the facilitators and barriers faced by harm reduction organizations in Kentucky, Ohio, and New York.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study as part of the Stay Safe Study, using a quantitative survey and qualitative interviews with organizations distributing FTS. Organizations included syringe service programs, mobile outreach units, recovery centers, and public health departments. Survey data were used to describe organizational characteristics and distribution practices, while interview data were coded and analyzed for themes related to distribution practices, training, and facilitators and barriers of FTS distribution.ResultsTwenty-eight organizations completed the organizational survey, and staff from 21 organizations participated in interviews. FTS distribution settings varied widely. Most organizations distributed between 100 and 500 FTS monthly, primarily on an “as needed” basis. Legalization of FTS was identified as a key facilitator, and innovative distribution methods included vending machines and mail distribution. Partnerships within the community expanded the reach and accessibility of FTS. Training approaches for both staff and PWUD varied substantially, and organizations faced barriers related to securing buy-in and the time required for training.ConclusionsThis study highlights organizational practices related to FTS distribution and training. Participants viewed FTS as a gateway to providing broader harm reduction services, fostering trust and engagement with PWUD. To maximize the impact of FTS as an overdose prevention tool, it is important to address persistent barriers through tailored distribution and training strategies, sustainable funding, and scalable implementation models.Keywords: Fentanyl test strips, Drug checking, Community organizations, PWUD

Background

The proliferation of fentanyl and other synthetic drugs in the United States drug supply has led to increased overdose fatalities in the United States [1]. Harm reduction strategies, such as drug checking services, offer people who use drugs (PWUD) critical information about the contents of their drug supply [2, 3]. Among these strategies, fentanyl test strips (FTS) can rapidly detect low concentrations of fentanyl and many of its analogues, when used correctly [4, 5]. FTS are single-use, paper immunoassays that can qualitatively and rapidly detect low fentanyl concentrations [6–8]. FTS use among PWUD is associated with overdose risk reduction behaviors including reducing the amount of drug used [9, 10], and with a decrease in morbidity and mortality among PWUD [11].

FTS distribution has been widely adopted by harm reduction organizations, public health departments, and healthcare facilities, including emergency departments [12–14]. Beyond reducing overdose risk, FTS distribution is viewed as a strategy to decrease stigma [15, 16], build rapport with PWUD and their social networks, and connect them to additional overdose prevention services [13]. Studies have found implementing FTS distribution in harm reduction and treatment facilities has been relatively easy in regions where FTS are legally permitted [6, 17].

Despite the growing use of FTS, there is limited research on the organizational-level factors that influence FTS distribution and training practices. Specifically, there is a gap in understanding how community organizations navigate legal, structural, and operational barriers, and how they tailor training for both staff and PWUD. This ancillary study of the HEALing Communities Study (HCS) [9, 18, 19] addresses these gaps by examining organizational practices for FTS distribution and training in three of the four states participating in the HCS—Kentucky (KY), New York (NY) and Ohio (OH). FTS have been legal in NY since 2014 [20]; statutes criminalizing their distribution and/or possession as drug paraphernalia were overturned in April 2023 in OH [21] and in June 2023 in KY [22]. Once FTS were decriminalized, these states started providing the strips to some organizations through state-sponsored programs such as the KY Overdose Response Effort (KORE) [23], MATTERS [24] in NY, and Project DAWN [25] in OH. Specifically, this study explores how multi-level contextual factors in these three different states influence FTS distribution practices, and the types of education and training provided to people who use FTS.

Methods

Study design and setting

The Stay Safe Study is a prospective multi-state study exploring the primary hypothesis that FTS use is associated with overdose risk reduction behaviors among PWUD [9, 19]. This mixed-methods study explored a secondary outcome of the Stay Safe Study, involving FTS distribution practices, training approaches and organizational facilitators and barriers across organizations in KY, NY and OH. Data for this paper was drawn from a survey completed by multiple individuals in theorganizations distributing FTS regarding organizational characteristics, and semi-structured interviews of key administrators and providers from these organizations examining FTS distribution. The Stay Safe Study protocol (Pro00068304) was approved by Advarra Inc., the single Institutional Review Board for the study.

Study sample

Each state identified organizations to participate in the survey and in one-on-one interviews. Organizations were often chosen based upon previous relationships with the researchers through the HCS study. Eligibility for participation included whether they had, currently or planned to distribute FTS within the next year, or were trying to distribute but encountered barriers. Organizations were selected with the aim of achieving heterogeneity in the type of services provided and FTS distribution methods. All identified organizations were invited (n = 36), 28 organizations participated in the study, and all were currently distributing FTS.

Data collection

A web-based survey developed for this study, completed between June and October 2023, collected data on organizational characteristics (e.g., non-profit or state agency, affiliations with larger consortiums, services provided, primary sources of funding); staff time and effort spent on acquisition, readying for distribution, and training on the use of FTS; and populations served. The web-based survey could be completed by multiple respondents, but only one survey was submitted from each organization.

Interviews were conducted from August to November 2023 with management and front-line workers who interact with PWUD and were involved in distributing FTS. Multiple individuals from one organization could participate. These semi-structured interviews aimed to explore survey responses in more depth and lasted between 45 and 60 minutes. They examined organizational characteristics, the initiation and evolution of FTS distribution programs, staff and client training approaches, the reach of FTS distribution, and the alignment of FTS distribution with overdose prevention and the organizations’ missions.

Each state provided incentives to the organizations separately as part of the larger Stay Safe Study. These included incentives for recruitment of PWUD for the primary hypothesis in NY ($5000 plus $25 for each person screened) and OH ($2000), and/or payments for participating in the survey and interviews ($50 for those not included in the primary hypothesis in NY, and $30 for the survey with an additional $30 for the interview in KY). The survey, interviews and procedures were reviewed by persons with lived and living experience of drug use, and revisions were made based on their input.

Data analysis

Participating organizations completing at least one survey question were included in the summaries of data collected on the organizational survey. Organization characteristics and FTS distribution practices were summarized across all organizations for each state as well as within each of the three states using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means, standard deviations, medians, and minimum and maximum values for continuous variables. Analyses were conducted using SAS Version 9.4.

Following completion of the interviews, a cross-state team, consisting of research staff from each state and leads from the data coordinating center involved in the larger HCS project [19], convened to begin qualitative analysis. Among this group, coders included those working with HCS, were involved in other research processes in the Stay Safe Study, or were experienced in qualitative methodologies. The qualitative research team followed a framework analysis technique [26], developing key themes both deductively in dialogue with existing literature and inductively through close reading of all interview transcripts. The cross-state team met weekly for three weeks to develop the preliminary codebook via consensus. The lead qualitative researcher in each state divided a small number of transcripts among coders, who then coded the files using NVivo 12 [27] to achieve intercoder reliability. The cross-state team met weekly to refine the codebook and discuss emerging themes. Each time the codebook was modified, the lead at each state would recode the already coded transcripts. New sets of transcripts (20% of the interviews) were coded by each state team until each coding team achieved an intercoder reliability score above 80% agreement [28].

For this paper, a new cross-state qualitative analysis team convened to further explore the emerging themes: FTS legal status, FTS distribution practices, cost, and staff and client training. Two members of the team chose a specific topic and reviewed the coded transcripts to ensure relevance, summarized associated codes and identified relevant and informative quotes to illustrate and support their findings. Analyses were conducted independently by each team member, and results were presented, discussed and agreed upon at a cross-state meeting.

Results

Organizational characteristics

Twenty-eight organizations completed the organizational survey and 21 (75%) participated in the interviews. A total of 27 interviews were conducted as four organizations completed more than one interview.

Eighty-six percent of the participating organizations checked they provided harm reduction services, 64% provided HIV/AIDS prevention/treatment, almost half (46%) provided substance use treatment, and a quarter (25%) provided primary healthcare. Table 2 summarizes survey data on the participating organizations’ FTS distribution strategies and procurement methods. Organizations in OH reported distributing FTS for an average of three years, KY slightly more than two years, and NY for over three years. Seventy-five percent of the organizations distributed between 101 and 500 FTS each month, primarily ordering them when they were needed (64%). Partnerships emerged as a key strategy for expanding the reach and accessibility of FTS with 64% of the FTS distributed being provided to other organizations for distribution. All organizations reported offering FTS upon request, and 11% distributed FTS via mail order. Organizational benefits of FTS distribution

In order to understand the organizations’ overall views regarding FTS, the interviewers asked whether FTS distribution aligned with overdose prevention and their mission. All organizations stated FTS distribution fit into their mission, although the benefits of distributing FTS went beyond detecting fentanyl in the drug supply. One interviewee emphasized “distributing a strip itself is not harm reduction. It’s a tool to wrap up harm reduction” (OH 3875-2), while another stated “[It’s a] relationship building tool also to connect them to other types of care and resources.” (OH 4289). Although in NY, where the strips have been in use for a longer period (Table 2), there was discussion that clients were not as interested in FTS “because they already know that there's fentanyl in their drugs, … I say, “Do you want fentanyl testing strips?” and they’re like, “We already know there's fentanyl in it so we're not even going to bother testing it.”” (NY 6619-2). There was agreement among interviewees that organizations believed FTS were useful for reaching those using non-opioids and for conducting low-barrier outreach among relatives of PWUD.

Reach of FTS distribution

Interviewees were asked about which populations they were successfully reaching with FTS distribution. Overwhelmingly, organizations across all states noted they were distributing FTS to individuals experiencing homelessness and shelter clients —“I've seen us…have FTS conversations with folks who are coming on the van who are inadequately housed, who are living on the streets…we've been successful there” (NY 6619-3). Considering sociodemographic factors, organizations felt they connected with males and females equally. Both NY and OH interviewees shared that rural residents in their catchment areas were able to easily access FTS. Interviewees did find they were reaching new populations with the strips, such as non-opiate users (cocaine, crack), potential new initiates such as college and high school students, and middle-aged clients who were more likely to be “weekend warriors”; however, some stated their regular clients were not as interested in using FTS because they already knew fentanyl was in their drug supply. Reaching college students was a priority for several organizations, especially those located on or near college campuses —“if you're going to go to college, you're going to run into someone who's using, so you always want to have this available, you keep it in your room, here's how you use it, here's what it's for…. we try to just sort of do it like it's, I just want to normalize the behavior, everyone needs to test” (OH 5530).

Interviewees frequently reported they had not adequately reached marginalized and underserved populations. Some interviewees noted they could improve outreach efforts to connect with Black and Brown communities, non-English-speaking individuals, LGBTQIA + individuals, and those with disabilities (e.g., blindness). People who do not inject drugs, such as many stimulant users, were also highlighted as a group that could benefit from improved outreach efforts due, in part, to the fact they perceive themselves to be less susceptible to fentanyl exposure but are also most interested in avoiding fentanyl as it counteracts the desired effects of their drugs of choice.

Training

Staff training

Organizations took varied approaches to training staff in FTS use, ranging from structured onboarding to informal peer-to-peer learning. Many interviewees characterized much of the staff training as ad hoc which occurred either through instruction by other staff during onboarding or self-training via videos “So the staff basically kind of learned by different videos…But during like onboarding training for our harm reduction staff, our supervisors will… train them on that.” (NY 6619–1) or FTS box inserts —“We followed the information on the actual strips themselves to figure out how to do it” (OH 3875-1). Some organizations sought out educational resources independently —"I think that we had to really educate ourselves because none of us really were understanding exactly what it did, so lots of that research" (OH 5530). The perceived simplicity of FTS led some organizations to minimize formal training. Several interviewees indicated staff either already possessed the necessary knowledge or found the strips intuitive enough to use without extensive instruction. One participant noted "Our staff are geniuses, they just by osmosis know" (NY 6619-3), while another stated "It's like super simple so I don't think there was like much need for instruction, it was like pretty easy to use" (NY 5534-2).

Client training

Training provided to clients (e.g., PWUD, family members, friends) varied, if any training was provided. While all organizations had some type of pamphlet or tri-fold card describing how to use the FTS, there was the belief that “you can’t give that to people and think that they walk away and they’re going to read it” (KY 7571). Therefore, some organizations changed from “written instructions to picture … picture instructions were much easier” (OH 3875-2).

Organizations employed multiple training methods with varying levels of engagement. Some interviewees stated they used demonstrations with fake “drugs” (such as Tylenol or sugar) but admitted while they prefer this method, time constraints for both staff and clients often limited this approach. Many organizations relied upon training materials provided with the FTS such as the manufacturers’ information, or QR codes linking to websites with videos, while others created their own videos for social media —“We made a little card that just has QR codes on it …, We thought it'd be easier for people to just take a picture, watch a video, and then use it, instead of follow the instructions, although instructions, at least how to read them, are on the individual packets.” (NY 8428-1). Interviewees from all three states incorporated FTS training for clients in resource fairs and outreach events. A challenge identified when educating clients about FTS was “people [not] being completely sober when they hear and being able to actually take in the information and retain it” (NY 8533-2).

Most interviewees indicated they did not discuss inaccuracies in the tests, such as false positive and false negative results; however, they did leverage dialogue around FTS use to address overdose risk reduction behaviors such as not using drugs alone and having naloxone nearby.

Facilitators for FTS distribution

State funding

Having state resources allowed the organizations to distribute a higher volume of FTS. In KY and NY, state-sponsored programs provided funding for organizations to purchase FTS or receive them for free. This funding was critical in facilitating organizations to distribute FTS without bearing the full costs themselves —“… the state of course made KORE funds available to us to be able to purchase fentanyl test strips so they weren't coming out of a local …” (KY 7571), while a NY participant noted, "we get all ours through MATTERS NY so we have no problem getting the fentanyl test strips and handing them out" (NY 5534-1). In OH, the connection between legalization and state funding was explicit —"So since it was like passed to be legal we are able to use Ohio Department of Health funds to purchase fentanyl test strips. Previously we were not able to purchase those with ODH state funds, where once the legality of it passed we were able to purchase them from there, so by doing that it just allows us to be able to purchase higher quantities of it and have it be able to ship more promptly to us as well" (OH 6370). However, financial constraints still played a role in limiting FTS distribution in some locations, with one OH participant explaining that despite the legal shift, "we still have to… provide it at a reduced cost" (OH 5530) to other agencies requesting FTS.

Legislative impact on uptake

According to interviews, decriminalization of FTS distribution/possession significantly impacted FTS uptake by PWUD in KY and OH. Some locations had concerns about the legality of FTS, with one participant noting, "no one wants to knowingly break the law even if they know it's… the right way to be" (KY 1316). In KY, while distribution methods remained consistent, interviewees reported the legalization of FTS led to increased distribution. Additionally, the state implemented a mail distribution program which expanded access to FTS, allowing people to receive them “from the comfort and safety of their own home confidentially” (KY 5690). In OH, the legal change also led to increased funding which allowed for more distribution. Furthermore, the legal shift in OH was believed to have resulted in more public tolerance, with one respondent explaining, “since then it’s… more widely accepted” (OH 6370). OH legalization of FTS distribution and possession enabled the use of vending machines for FTS distribution, with over 800 test strips distributed by one organization in September 2023 alone through this mechanism (OH 7720).

Using partners to enhance FTS distribution

Reaching beyond traditional harm reduction organizations can increase FTS distribution [7]. Partnerships within the community enabled access to new strategic sites such as methadone clinics, and non-traditional harm reduction outlets who previously hesitated due to the legal ambiguity around FTS (KY 2585). Occasionally, unexpected partnerships, including local law enforcement and other emergency responders, businesses such as bars and coffee shops, and homeless shelters occurred because many in the community were willing to help try to prevent overdoses (KY 7571, NY 8533-1). One KY participant described how "multiple businesses or other organizations… reached out and said how can I get fentanyl test strip kits from your organization" once they no longer faced concerns about legal repercussions.” (KY 5690). Many interviewees in NY noted that partnering with local county outreach programs facilitated FTS distribution, with one participant sharing their county government provided FTS for their organization’s street outreach and mobile van programs (NY 6619-1). Similarly, the establishment of vending machines to distribute FTS represented successful community partnerships that allowed for greater distribution.

Barriers to FTS distribution

Cost

Even though FTS distribution and possession was legal in each of the three states, state-sponsored FTS or funding was not consistently provided to the organizations. Therefore, the high cost of FTS remained a substantial barrier when these funds were not available. One participant explained “I think that the … biggest issue we had … for a while there, we were just having a hard time getting them to distribute them. It was … cost prohibitive for us to get them, we weren't getting them for free so it was about trying to find funding for them, trying to get grants, trying to use unrestricted funds, trying to get donations.” (NY 6619-3).

Stigma

Stigma around drug use continues to be an ongoing challenge when distributing FTS [15, 16]. While community partnerships helped expand the reach for FTS distribution, some partners were not comfortable with the provision of other harm reduction materials (KY 2585). Some organizations needed to respond to concerns that distributing FTS could encourage drug use. Stigma was reported as a problem when trying to reach populations such as African Americans and individuals who do not use syringe service programs, underscoring the complexity of expanding harm reduction efforts beyond traditional networks. Furthermore, some interviewees noted that reaching Black communities was difficult due to community member distrust resulting from prior negative experiences.

Staff buy-in

One of the main barriers organizations who were not focused on harm reduction faced when starting to distribute FTS was lack of awareness of harm reduction modalities among their staff. Sometimes staff reported resistance or apprehension from within the organization, or concern with legal issues the organization may have faced in the past related to drug paraphernalia laws. Some staff had limited awareness of or willingness to distribute FTS particularly when harm reduction was a newer approach in the organization, such as treatment or abstinence focused organizations —“Particularly myself and several others that came from an abstinence based only treatment program, you know, we had to really open our, our knowledge base and our understanding, you know, to get over that hurdle of, oh my gosh, this is not what we were taught” (KY 2585). In other cases, staff buy-in was described as low because of a lack of buy-in from clients and participants.

Client attitudes

The organizations interviewed serve a variety of clients with diverse needs and motivations. Client understanding about the reasons to use FTS was a frequently mentioned challenge for FTS distribution as one participant explained —“We really need to, to get out there that substances can be contaminated with fentanyl and it is important to test your substances, all substances, because there's also, strangely enough, like a lot of stigma within the drug using community depending on the substance. You know, you can get a person who, in front of you who only snorts pills and they could be very judgmental against a population that injects and they will say, you know, I don't do that, I don't, so I don't need to, you know, test anything because I'm not doing that” (OH 5238). Frequently, organizations reported their clients were not trying to avoid fentanyl, were aware that it was in their supply, and as a result did not see the purpose of FTS—“…we have a lot of them because people don't even really want them anymore because they already know that there's fentanyl in their drugs, … I say, “Do you want fentanyl testing strips?” and they’re like, “We already know there's fentanyl in it so we're not even going to bother testing it.”” (NY 6619-2). People who work in harm reduction services know the importance of “meeting clients where they are,” but training materials may not reflect this approach if they assume clients want to avoid fentanyl in their supply. In other cases, clients who used stimulants, but not opioids, did not think fentanyl was an issue in their supply. Several interviewees mentioned that a near-death overdose experience or presence at an overdose event were reasons why some clients started using FTS. Some interviewees discussed realizing people were still going to use their drugs; therefore, requiring changing the messages from “don’t use” to “use safely”.

Discussion

A range of organization types participated in this study. FTS distribution occurred in multiple venues which facilitated access to individuals who may not frequent traditional harm reduction settings like syringe service programs. FTS decriminalization in KY and OH spurred greater openness among businesses and organizations to partner for distribution, allowing organizations to raise awareness and expand the reach of this overdose reduction tool [7].

While many interviewees were aware of clients’ varied attitudes toward FTS use [6, 7, 12], it is still important for staff to use different distribution strategies to meet the clients’ needs. Client training and education efforts should recognize that views on fentanyl have shifted significantly since it first appeared in the drug supply. Current client training focuses primarily on people using illicit opioids; expanding this to include people using illicit stimulants or other drugs may improve harm reduction outcomes. Several interviewees mentioned using FTS training to start conversations about drug supply risks focusing staff and client training on framing FTS as a tool for increasing knowledge of possible risks from their drugs, rather than solely as a method to avoid fentanyl. Interviewees stated illicit stimulant users are more likely to try to avoid fentanyl and may be unaware of the presence of fentanyl in their supply; therefore, focusing future training efforts on them could prove useful. With the addition of xylazine and other new drugs and adulterants into the drug supply [29], this is an important opportunity to refresh training efforts as many PWUD, even those who are seeking fentanyl, may want to avoid the negative effects of whatever new drug appears on the market.

Most interviewees expressed either limited knowledge about using FTS or skepticism about the instructions provided with the strips. This may reflect a broader perception that FTS alone are an insufficient overdose prevention tool [13]. A consistent training curriculum for FTS and other drug-checking methods, delivered with minimal time commitment from staff, as has been conducted for many overdose education and naloxone distribution programs [30] is important. Relying on person-to-person training has limitations, particularly in fast-paced direct services environments.

It is not enough to just distribute FTS, staff and clients need standardized detailed training [6] which includes education on proper use including differences in testing techniques based on the strip manufacturer, and drug or drug type (e.g., pills versus powder); and on FTS limitations, such as their inability to detect all fentanyl analogues, and measure concentration or potency [6, 31–33]. While QR codes provide convenient access to training materials, not all PWUD have smartphones, and mail distribution may not reach individuals lacking stable housing. Tailored training materials should address the diverse needs and backgrounds of PWUD and staff. Training materials should include easy-to-understand graphics, discuss overdose risk reduction behaviors, highlight the limitations of FTS, and be translated into languages used in local communities.

Barriers to distributing FTS were shaped by legal status, organizational culture, funding, stigma [7, 12, 14, 15, 16, 34] and community trust. Policies and laws criminalizing drug paraphernalia remain a substantial barrier to implementing evidence-based harm reduction strategies [35–37]. Many interviewees were uncertain about the legal status of FTS, highlighting the need for clear communication regarding their legality. The recent legalization of FTS distribution and possession in KY and OH facilitated greater reach and higher volumes of FTS distribution. However, similar to other studies, challenges persisted in reaching populations at increased risk of overdose including PWUD who do not inject, people of color, and individuals experiencing homelessness [6]. In two of the three participating states, FTS were legalized just a few months prior to the start of the organizational survey data collection.

The lack of consistent funding for FTS was identified as a significant barrier. The high cost of FTS created supply inconsistencies, forcing organizations to seek alternate funding sources, underscoring the critical role of financial resources in determining the reach and effectiveness of harm reduction programs.

Recommendations for research

We make four recommendations for research that can provide an evidence base to optimize FTS distribution and training of harm reduction organization staff and PWUD who use their services. (1) Because multiple distribution approaches exist but do not always reach PWUD at highest risk of overdose, it is important to assess the reach of different distribution strategies and evaluate the effectiveness of the educational opportunities they present. (2) Evaluation of educational programming for harm reduction staff regarding changing attitudes towards FTS use over time among both staff and clients, why PWUD do or do not use FTS, FTS as an educational tool to increase PWUD knowledge of risks associated with drug use, and tailored to substance class and consumption routes is also important. (3) Information needs of PWUD and staff at harm reduction organizations may vary depending on how they view fentanyl, and the drug classes and consumption routes they use. There is value in conducting a needs assessment of various populations for their preferences in FTS training media and delivery approaches (e.g., in-person demonstrations, instructions posted to website, printed documents, peer instruction), along with examining the impact of length of employment at the agency. This assessment can be paired with an evaluation of implementation outcomes, user knowledge of FTS limitations, accurate interpretation of results, and length of instruction retention. (4) Finally, comparing the effectiveness of standardized FTS educational content and delivery media could provide meaningful information regarding how to best train staff and PWUD about the value of using FTS and how to correctly test a sample.

Limitations

These findings should be considered in light of several limitations. First, the organizations that participated in this study were not randomly selected; participation in the organizational survey and interviews was based on interest and availability. Additionally, all organizations included in this study were currently distributing FTS, limiting our ability to examine barriers faced by organizations not distributing FTS, nor the reasons why some organizations may choose not to distribute them. This study was based on a relatively small sample, with data collected at one point in time through self-reports. Finally, these results are context-dependent and influenced by policies specific to the participating states.

Conclusions

This study provides valuable insights into how FTS are distributed and integrated into harm reduction efforts across diverse organizational and state contexts. Organizations are using FTS as more than just a tool for overdose prevention—they serve as gateways for building trust, fostering engagement, and connecting PWUD to broader harm reduction resources. Legalization and funding mechanisms enabled organizations to implement innovative distribution methods, such as vending machines and mail programs, which expanded access and improved public acceptance of FTS.

Despite these advances, significant barriers to distributing FTS persist. Stigma, financial constraints, and uneven reach continue to limit the effectiveness of FTS distribution efforts. Organizations also reported variability in training approaches, with many relying on ad hoc or inconsistent methods that fail to address the diverse needs and unique motivations of PWUD. These findings underscore the need for tailored training materials and scalable distribution strategies that meet PWUD where they are—both physically and in terms of readiness to engage with harm reduction services.

Future research should evaluate the impact of these strategies on improving reach, equity, and overdose prevention outcomes. By addressing these challenges, harm reduction organizations can maximize the role of FTS in mitigating the risks of an evolving drug supply while advancing their broader mission of reducing harm in communities disproportionately impacted by the overdose crisis.
