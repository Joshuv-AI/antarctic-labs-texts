Surgical repair of extremely late presentation of supracardiac total anomalous pulmonary vein connection: a case series
Authors: Dicky Fakhri, Salomo Purba, Indriwanto Sakdijan, Ardiansyah, Aditya Agam Nugraha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA late-presenting total anomalous pulmonary venous connection is extremely rare, and surgical repair remains a significant challenge.Cases presentationWe present two cases of surgical repair for late-presenting supracardiac total anomalous pulmonary vein connection: a 14-year-old female and a 32-year-old female. Both were successfully operated on and were discharged on postoperative days 9 and 29. The second patient had a prolonged hospital stay due to complications of acute kidney injury after surgery and the need for hemodialysis. Both patients had a small left ventricle and pulmonary hypertension preoperatively. All pulmonary veins drained to a common pulmonary vein, which drained to a left-sided vertical vein, which drained into the innominate vein. Postoperative evaluation of both patients found no pulmonary vein obstruction and good biventricular contraction.ConclusionLate diagnosis may occur in TAPVC patients, but a surgical repair may still be possible.Keywords: Total anomalous pulmonary vein connection, Supracardiac total anomalous pulmonary vein connection, Pulmonary hypertension, Congenital heart disease, Cardiac surgery

Introduction

Total anomalous pulmonary venous connection (TAPVC) is a rare but severe congenital heart defect (CHD), with a prevalence of around 0.5–2% of all CHD cases. It is characterized by all pulmonary veins (PV) failing to connect to the left atrium (LA), usually draining into the right atrium (RA) directly or via one of its tributaries. Supracardiac TAPVC is the most common type of TAPVC, accounting for about 43% of all cases [1]. Clinical symptoms are usually related to the presence of PV outflow obstruction, ranging from mild pulmonary hypertension (PH) to severe pulmonary congestion. If there is an obstruction to PV flow, emergency surgery is needed immediately for repair [1, 2]. Only about 20% survive the first year of disease without intervention [3]. TAPVC persisting into adulthood is very rare and is usually associated with the presence of a large ostium secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) and a large draining vertical vein without stenosis [4–7].

Surgical correction of TAPVC remains a challenge. The most serious complication of TAPVC surgery, which can lead to reoperation, is the presence of pulmonary vein obstruction (PVO). PVO increases the risk of mortality post-TAPVC repair surgery [8]. In this study, we describe two cases of late-presenting TAPVC who underwent successful surgical repair.

Case

Case 1

A 14-year-old female patient weighing 35 kg presented with mild dyspnea on exertion, New York Heart Association (NYHA) grade II for the past few months. She had a history of isolated atrial septal defect (ASD) diagnosis at 1 year old, but did not continue the treatment and had no further follow-up until presentation and was referred to the National Cardiovascular Center Harapan Kita (NCCHK). On physical examination, mild central cyanosis and clubbed fingers were found. The chest X-ray suggested cardiomegaly and a widening of the superior mediastinum producing a “snowman” appearance [Figure 1A]. Laboratory tests were normal. Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed enlarged right atrium (RA) and right ventricle (RV), a large bidirectional ASD shunt with dominant right-to-left shunt, small-sized left ventricle (LV), and moderate tricuspid regurgitation (Table 1). All the pulmonary veins (PV) were drained to the common PV and then drained to the vertical vein (VV) and joined with the innominate vein on the left side, and then entered the RA. Cardiac catheterization confirmed the TTE findings; the oxygen saturation in the common PV was 97.1%, and the oxygen saturation in the RA was 91.9%. Pulmonary vascular resistance index (PVRi) was 2.3 wu.m2 with a flow ratio of 1.8 and was concluded as high-flow, low resistance pulmonary hypertension (PH). A surgical conference was held between the paediatric cardiac surgeon and paediatric cardiologist, and decided to perform surgical repair of TAPVC, ASD closure, and management of the small-sized LV.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient. Elective surgical repair was performed via a median sternotomy approach. During the operation, the pericardium was preserved, and the aorto-bicaval cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) conduct was performed, and the patient was cooled to 34 °C. The aorta was cross-clamped, and cardiac arrest was induced with antegrade cold cardioplegia. The heart was retracted to the cephalad and right, so that the left posterior pericardial area was exposed. The common PV and vertical vein were found behind the pericardium, adjacent to the posterior of the left atrium (LA) [Figure 2]. Rerouting of the pulmonary vein drainage was performed with side-to-side anastomosis between the posterior wall of the LA and the anterior wall of the common PV with a continuous suture technique [Figure 3]. The interatrial defect was closed with a pericardial patch. After weaning from CPB and once the hemodynamics were stable, the VV was ligated. PH and small LV management were performed on the first postoperative day. The patient received milrinone (0.375–0.75 mcg/kg/min) and nitroglycerin (0.5 mcg/kg/min), weaned by postoperative day (POD) 2 The postoperative course was uneventful, and no sign of low cardiac output syndrome (LCOS). The patient was discharged 9 days after surgery.

Case 2

A 32-year-old woman weighing 40 kg with the chief complaint of fatigue and a history of repeated fainting and controlled epilepsy. Complaints began to appear at the age of 17, but did not carry out routine treatment. Preoperative laboratory results were within normal limit. On chest X-ray examination, superior mediastinum widening was seen [Figure 1B]. On TTE examination, it was found that all PVs flow into the vertical vein, which then flows into the v. innominate and the SVC. There was a 31 mm bidirectional shunt secundum ASD, good ventricular contraction, but there was a small LV during diastole with a D-shape configuration during systole. Then cardiac catheterization was performed, confirming the TTE findings, saturation in RA was 90%, and in SVC was 94%, with a PVRi of 1.09 WU/m2. Cardiac MRI was performed to confirm LV dimensions [Figure 4], and found that the LV was D-shaped with borderline LV systolic function (EDVi 45.52 ml/m2, ESVi 22.38 ml/m2, LVEF 50.82%). The patient received regular treatment for PH using sildenafil. The cardiac conference decided to perform surgical TAPVC repair, small-sized LV management, and PH.

The surgery was performed electively with a median sternotomy approach. Conduct CPB was performed with aorta-bicaval cannulation, the pericardium was preserved, and the temperature was lowered to 34 °C. Cross-clamped aorta and antegrade cardioplegia were performed. The heart was retracted cephalad and right to expose the left posterior part of the heart. Rerouting of common PV flow to LA was performed with side-to-side anastomosis between the anterior part of the common PV and the posterior wall of the LA. The ASD was partially closed using a pericardial patch. After weaning from CPB, hemodynamics were stable, and ligation of the vertical vein was performed. Postoperative management included milrinone (0.5–0.75 mcg/kg/min, POD 0–4), dobutamine (5 mcg/kg/min, POD 0–18), adrenaline (0.05–0.08 mcg/kg/min, POD 0–16), and vasopressin (0.025–0.05 mcg/kg/min, POD 1–3). The antiepileptic medication was continued postoperatively without interruption. No dosage adjustment was needed given stable preoperative control and absence of perioperative seizure.

There was a period of LCOS with symptoms of renal insufficiency in postoperative care. There was a decrease in kidney function and a trend of increasing ureum and creatinine levels, with a peak of ureum of 255 and creatinine of 2.52. Extra hemodialysis treatment was given twice and kidney function returned to normal. There was prolonged mechanical ventilation for up to 15 days and prolonged ICU stay up to 16 days. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 29 after surgery.

Discussion

TAPVC is a critical congenital heart disease (CHD) with a prevalence of < 1% among all CHD, characterized by the absence of a direct connection between the PV and LA [3, 9]. TAPVC has four subtypes depending on where the anomalous vein flows: supracardiac (45%-66%), infracardiac (13%-25%), intracardiac (20%-30%), and mixed (10%) [1, 2, 10].

In patients with non-obstructive TAPVC, pulmonary arterial pressure may be normal, but the presence of high blood flow into the pulmonary circulation may result in medial hypertrophy of the pulmonary artery and will become symptomatic early in life [7, 11]. If corrective surgery is not performed, the patient will develop moderate and then severe pulmonary hypertension, which results in severe tissue hypoxia, heart failure, and death, with mortality rate up to 80% if left untreated [11, 12]. One of the factors responsible for the survival of unoperated patients in infancy and childhood is the presence of a large ASD, thus prevents pulmonary overcirculation, thus preventing pulmonary hypertension, and also improves saturation with good mixing at the atrial level [6, 7, 12]. Bae et al.. also stated that in TAPVC patients who underwent corrective surgery at the age of 14–48 years, the presence of unobstructive pulmonary venous drainage, large inter-atrial communication, maintenance of vascular resistance, and pulmonary pressure closer to the normal limit was an important factor for survival in unoperated patients with TAPVC [5]. This was consistent with the findings in our patients, both of whom had large ASD as mixing sites and there was no obstruction to PV flow. A diagnosis of preoperative PVO was made based on echocardiography data indicating a non-phasic flow velocity greater than 1.5 m/s. LA maximum diameter was measured at the end systole on the parasternal long- axis view. The ASD or PFO diameter was measured on the subcostal long-axis view [1].

Surgical repair is the only treatment for TAPVC, although the risk of surgery in adults may be increased due to large and friable pulmonary and systemic veins. The goal of surgical repair is to facilitate non-obstructive flow of pulmonary venous drainage to the LA which involves creating an anastomosis between the LA and the venous flow, closure of the ASD, and removal of the VV [7, 11]. In patients without pulmonary vascular disease, complete repair can be performed, but in patients with TAPVC who have developed severe pulmonary hypertension, right heart decompression is necessary using the ASD left during surgery [5]. There are at least 2 approaches for the surgical repair of supracardiac TAPVC: the posterior and the biatrial (lateral) approach. In both of our cases, posterior approach was used. The key to the success of the operation is determined by the anastomosis between the common PV and LA being large enough to ensure the return of pulmonary vein blood flow [11]. The posterior approach with cephalad and rightward heart retraction provides optimal exposure of the posterior confluence adjacent to the left atrial wall, allowing a direct side-to-side anastomosis to create a wide, tension-free ostium exceeding mitral valve annulus size. From Michielon et al., (2002), side-to-side anastomosis technique achieves 85–90% freedom from reintervention at 10–18 years with only 3% arrhythmia rate versus 17% for double patch methods [13]. The incision of the posterior wall of the LA itself can cause damage to the autonomic ganglia in the fat pad in the posterior atrial septum and damage to the atrioventricular sulcus, which can affect the occurrence of local autonomic dysfunction postoperatively [11].

The decision to ligate the VV is still controversial. Some authors chose to leave the VV patent for left heart decompression, reducing suprasystemic pulmonary artery pressures especially in obstructed TAPVC or non-compliant left heart, with reports of spontaneous VV closure due to high-resistance blood flow relative to the low-pressure LA pathway [7]. We performed VV ligation immediately after stable cardiopulmonary bypass weaning in both cases, with confirmation of hemodynamic stability on transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) with no LV or RV dysfunction escalation. Adjustable vertical vein ligation is indeed a valuable strategy, and the Chowdhury et al. (2020) series of 99 supracardiac TAPVC patients demonstrates that leaving the vertical vein temporarily patent with an adjustable ligature can reduce risk of PH crisis and improve survival in high-risk obstructive cases with small, non-compliant left-sided chambers [14]. In our center, however, we apply this technique selectively rather than routinely. Because of resource limitations, we reserve adjustable vertical vein ligation for situations where intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography does not demonstrate a clear, laminar, unrestricted flow from the pulmonary venous confluence to the left atrium or when we suspect anastomotic stenosis.

Postoperative management focuses on small LV adaptation and PH crisis prevention, as acute ventricular volume loading frequently precipitates RV failure and/or LCOS. Literature reports incidence rates of PH crisis of 4–35% across populations, with early mortality directly attributable to PH crisis reaching up to 16.7%. The occurrence of postoperative PH crisis is seen to be triggered by hypoxemia, metabolic acidosis, inadequate pain control, insufficient sleep, or endotracheal suctioning, which all precipitate pulmonary vascular constriction [15]. Preoperative cardiac catheterization in our patient demonstrated high-flow, low-resistance pulmonary hypertension, therefore, targeted PH-directed therapy was initiated immediately after surgery. The goals for anesthetic management are to reduce PA pressures and maintain adequate cardiac output, which in our center include: (1) intentional ASD fenestration for controlled RV decompression; (2) deep sedation for at least 24 h to minimize agitation and pain triggers; (3) delayed extubation until TTE confirms recovery of right and left ventricular function and stable hemodynamics; (4) minimal vasoconstrictor dosing to preserve coronary perfusion; and (5) temporary pacemaker on standby for episodes of reduced cardiac output, prioritizing heart rate optimization over vasopressors. Inodilators such as milrinone (0.25–0.75 µg/kg/min), dobutamine (5–10 µg/kg/min), and sildenafil (0.4–1.6 mg/kg/day) will help in managing PA pressures and providing necessary inotropy for managing RV and LV dysfunction. The use of pulmonary vasodilators such as milrinone, sildenafil, and bosentan, with inhaled nitric oxide (NO), along with hyperventilation to maintain PaCO2 around 25–30 mmHg with a FiO2 of 1.0, will help in decreasing PA pressures and tiding over the PAH crisis [16]. Milrinone administration starting from 0.5 mcg/kg/min is recommended as postoperative PH therapy [15], but milrinone administration can also have the effect of decreasing systemic vascular resistance and causing hypotension, so we performed close hemodynamic monitoring during the patient’s postoperative ICU care through full TTE study.

In the second case, there was a period of LCOS marked by a decrease in kidney function and the need for dialysis, one possible cause of which was the adaptation of the small LV after correction. Based on a study in the surgical closure of ASD with small-sized LV, EDVi < 53.3 ml/m2 can increase the incidence of LCOS after surgical repair [17]. In our patient, cardiac MRI was performed with EDVi results of 45.52 ml/m2. However, in the first case, cardiac MRI was not performed, although based on TTE, EDVi was obtained as 20 ml/m2. Preoperative laboratory evaluations in both cases showed preserved renal function, indicating that postoperative renal impairment was more likely secondary to LCOS related to ventricular adaptation to the new postoperative physiology.

In congenital heart defect surgery, especially in TAPVC repair surgery, the problem of a small left ventricle can cause low cardiac output syndrome (LCOS) after surgery [15]. Some centers state that small LV is relative; this is because in TAPVC, there is no specific pathology that causes a reduction in LV diameter, and small LV is due to underfilling. After all, not all PV flows to the LV, which then develops a dilated RV and presses on the LV [15, 18]. Small-sized LV produces restrictive physiology, which has the effect of reducing exercise capacity after surgery [14]. After TAPVC repair, PV flow completely enters the LV, filling in the repaired LV, and the RV no longer pressurizes the LV [15]. Rapid normalization of LV volume occurs after optimization of ventricular volume and pressure load after TAPVC repair is performed [18]. In both cases, LV function and dimensions adapted well after corrective surgery, with resolution of the D-shaped LV, indicating relief of the compression. No PVO was detected in both patient on TTE study, which demonstrated unobstructed flow from the PV to the LA and absence of pulmonary congestion. Finally, late diagnosis may occur in adult TAPVC patients, but a surgical repair remains feasible with favorable outcomes.
