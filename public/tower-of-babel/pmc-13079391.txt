Energetics of biomolecular shells in core–shell nanocomplexes
Authors: Kristina Lilova, Tamilarasan Subramani, Isabella Montini, Anne Harrison, Manuel Scharrer, Hongwu Xu, Alexandra Navrotsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe drug delivery efficiency of engineered nanocarriers depends critically on their stability in biological media. Here, we introduce the stabilization enthalpy concept to describe experimentally determined thermodynamic stability relations of three patchy core–shell nanocomplexes and separate the contributions of the hydration enthalpy of the complex and the biomolecular adsorption energetics. We select three model biomolecules: a polysaccharide, a protein, and a fatty acid coated on magnetite nanoparticles, one of the most commonly used nanocarriers. The same concept can in principle be used to assess the stability, behavior, and fate of other complex inorganic–organic nanosystems, including biogenic nanominerals and functional materials.

The energetics of the primary hydration of core–shell nanoparticles strongly influence their interactions in organic and biological media and provide a fundamental scientific basis for engineered nanocarriers with tailored colloidal stability, immune interactions, and drug delivery behavior (1). Here, hydration enthalpy refers to the enthalpy of water adsorption on the surface of a dry (dehydrated) core–shell nanocomplex. However, hydration is only one of the factors governing the thermodynamic stability of such nanocomplexes, and a complete assessment requires quantifying the excess surface enthalpy of the complexes, which reflects the role of the coating in the total stabilization.

Except for sintered nanomaterials with very small total surface area, adsorbed molecules are always present on the surface of nanosized compounds. The adsorbed species stabilize the nanostructure, thus preventing the particle from coarsening. Their type, organic and biomolecules, water, carbonate depend on the synthesis method, surface functionalization, and environmental medium This surface layer is a dynamic structure and changes with the environmental conditions, particularly for the loosely bound molecules, which have direct (though weak) interactions with the surface.

The surface energy of a nanomaterial is defined as its excess energy over that of the bulk analog, normalized by the surface area of the nanomaterial. This excess energy is a result of the fewer bonds on the surface of the materials in comparison with the interior resulting in incomplete bonding of those atoms or molecules, which decreases their thermodynamic stability and increases the chemical reactivity. In such a thermodynamic analysis, the reference state is the bulk phase with close to zero surface area. A reference state serves as a specific baseline for measuring thermodynamic properties and allows for a consistent comparison of the measured thermodynamic properties. We have employed a combination of high-temperature oxide melt solution calorimetry and gas adsorption calorimetry to determine the surface energies of different nanophase oxides and chalcogenides with a hydration layer consisting of both chemisorbed (tightly bound) and physiosorbed (weakly bound) water (2, 3). The physi- and chemisorbed water molecules have different energetics contribution to the surface energetics due to difference in the bonds’ strength and mechanism. Using this methodology, we measured both surface energy of the hydrated and the anhydrous (bare) surface. The surface energy of the hydrated surface includes the stabilization effect of the water molecules adsorbed on the surface treated as bulk water (2, 3).

This concept can be applied to a sorbed molecular layer, which can be easily removed by degassing in vacuum at 373 K (3), allowing one to obtain the surface area of the nanomaterial using the Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) technique. Removing the adsorbed species from the surface of nanoparticles followed by gas adsorption (usually N2) provides the accessible surface based on the amount of gas required to form a monolayer. In many biologically relevant core–shell nanoparticles, the surface is not uniformly covered by a continuous layer, which results in heterogeneous regions, including exposed inorganic core and adsorbed biomolecules with different conformation and arrangements. We refer to those systems as “patchy core–shell nanocomplexes” emphasizing that the different regions interact differently with the surrounding media. The real geometric surface area of those patchy core–shell nanocomplexes cannot be determined reliably by the standard techniques—BET with N2, XRD crystallite size of the core, or BET water adsorption isotherms (1). As demonstrated previously, the BET N2 area significantly underestimates the surface area of both free biomolecules and the nanocomplexes (1), while the hydrophilic surface area (BET water adsorption) reflects the accessibility and energetics of the interfacial regions rather than the intrinsic geometric area of the core–shell structure.

Given the above-described complex behavior, improved concepts and methodology are needed to define and determine experimentally the excess enthalpy and the stability of complex core–shell nanosystems. As a start to this process, we quantify the thermodynamic stability of three nanocomplexes by adopting the concept of stabilization enthalpy and separate the contributions of the hydration enthalpy of the complex and the biomolecular adsorption energetics. The stabilization enthalpy reflects how the adsorbed species lower the excess surface enthalpy and stabilize the nanostructure without involving the surface area of the materials. The present work expands an earlier study (1) focused on establishing a quantitative basis to determine the patchy core–shell nanoparticle behavior and fate in biological media, and particularly their role as drug delivery nanocarriers.

Stabilization Enthalpy of Nanocomplexes

The three nanocomplexes studied here consist of a magnetite (Fe3O4) core and organic shells of bovine serum albumin (BSA), soluble potato starch, and lauric acid (LA). A hydrated (uncoated) nanophase magnetite sample was used to test and verify the concept. The synthesis by direct precipitation and characterization of the core–shell nanocomplexes and the uncoated magnetite were reported in the first part of the study (1). The same samples were used for both water adsorption measurements done previously (1) and the high-temperature oxidative drop solution calorimetry reported in this work.

High temperature drop solution calorimetry has been extensively used to determine various thermodynamic properties of materials, including enthalpies of formation, mixing, phase transitions as well as, in combination with other techniques such as water adsorption—the surface energetics of nanomaterials (4). The calorimeter measures heat flow between a sample and a heat sink maintained at an essentially constant temperature; thus, it is of isoperibol (constant surroundings) type. A standard experiment consists of dropping a sample from room temperature into an appropriate solvent at a temperature at which it dissolves rapidly and completely. While primarily developed for inorganic compounds, the technique has also been applied to hybrid inorganic–organic materials such as metal organic frameworks (MOFs) and polymer-derived ceramics (PDCs) (5). The high temperature oxidative drop solution calorimetry involves dropping free biomolecules or core–shell nanocomplexes from room temperature to 1,073 K into a molten sodium molybdate (3Na2O.4MoO3) solvent under pure oxygen atmosphere. The resulting heat effect called drop solution enthalpy includes several contributions which cannot be separated energetically. These include the heat content of the sample from room temperature to 1,073 K, a complete oxidation of the biomolecules and the divalent iron from the inorganic core, and the dissolution of the core and the shell. The oxidation occurs inside the solvent and results in the formation of H2O, CO2, and N2 (depending on the composition), which leave the melt and the calorimeter. The sulfur from the bovine serum albumin dissolves in the melt, forming sulfate anion similarly to inorganic sulfides (6, 7). The measured drop solution enthalpy of the biomolecules (SI Appendix, Tables S1) thus differs from the enthalpy of combustion measured by bomb calorimetry (8), though they have very similar magnitudes since the oxidation is the dominant effect.

The stabilization enthalpy can be defined as the enthalpy of formation of core–shell complex from uncoated core, water, and biomolecules. The reference states chosen in this work are hydrated uncoated core, hydrated starch and BSA, and anhydrous lauric acid. All reference states correspond to stable solid phases. Here, the term “hydrated” refers to dry powders stabilized by chemisorbed water and not aqueous solutions. The stabilization enthalpy includes contributions from the enthalpy of adsorption of the biomolecules on the surface of the nanomagnetite and the primary hydration of the newly formed core–shell complex. Additional effects from any conformational effects, agglomeration, formation of clusters or disordered layers, and interactions among the adsorbed species can also affect the overall energetics. The primary hydration enthalpies of the same core–shell complexes were measured previously using a highly sensitive coupled calorimeter—gas adsorption system (SI Appendix, Table S2) (1).

The drop solution enthalpies of the uncoated core were calculated using literature experimental data performed by us in a previous study on the thermodynamics and surface energetics of bulk and nano magnetite–maghemite solid solutions (9, 10). More details on the calculations are included in the SI Appendix.

The stabilization enthalpy concept was verified using the data for the hydrated (uncoated) magnetite sample (SI Appendix, Table S4). The experimental drop solution enthalpy of the hydrated sample corrected for water is 0.11 ± 0.004 kJ/g and the calculated drop solution enthalpy for a hydrated magnetite–maghemite solid solution with the same Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio and crystallite size is 0.11 ± 0.005 kJ/g (SI Appendix, Table S4) The surface energy of the hydrated magnetite, calculated using the drop solution of the bulk magnetite with the same Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio (9, 10) and the BET N2 surface area (1), is 0.78 ± 0.03 J/m2, which is consistent with 0.80 J/m2 measured previously for all magnetite–maghemite solid solutions (2, 10). The BET N2 and the XRD surface areas of the hydrated magnetite are essentially the same within the error (1), which indicates no agglomeration of the particles. The XRD surface area is calculated from the crystallite size of the sample determined from the X-ray powder diffraction peak broadening using the Scherrer equation assuming idealized morphology (11).

The stabilization enthalpies of all complexes are exothermic within the experimental errors, which corresponds to a decrease in the surface energy in comparison with the uncoated sample. Both BSA and LA core–shell complexes have uncoated patches with physiochemical properties different from those of the hydrated (uncoated) magnetite due to the effect of the adjacent organic molecules (1)

Although the three complexes contain different amounts of biomolecular coatings and their stabilization enthalpies cannot be compared directly, our results show a common trend. The core–shell nanoparticles are predominantly stabilized by the formation of the primary hydration layer since it is the largest exothermic contribution (Table 1). The biological relevance of the hydration contribution has been described in more detail in a previous work on the same nanocomplexes (1). In that work, the primary hydration energetics of biomolecule-coated nanomaterials was shown to determine hydrophilicity, the accessible hydrophilic surface, and the interaction potential of the nanocomplex surface with biological media. The hydration enthalpy was established as a quantitative basis to determine and interpret nanoparticle interactions with proteins, membranes, and biological fluids, and provide a thermodynamic foundation for designing nanocarriers with predictable biological reactivity and immune interactions.

The hydration enthalpy of the BSA-coated magnetite is more exothermic or similar to the stabilization enthalpy within the error. The starch-coated magnetite is completely encapsulated by the biomolecules (1), with hydration enthalpy is still the dominating contribution to the enthalpic stabilization. The hydrophobic lauric acid molecules rearrange into a partial hydrophilic bilayer when adsorbed on the core, which effectively lowers the surface energy by increasing the surface interaction potential toward water (1).

These results suggest the stabilization of a complex system (inorganic core, biomolecular shell, and hydration layer) with a given ratio between the magnetite and the coating (1). Increasing the amount of BSA and/or LA will shift the relative hydration and biomolecular adsorption contributions, and decrease or increase the stabilization enthalpy due to a complex interplay of several competing effects: coating density, structure, local rearrangements, and the interactions among the adsorbed molecules.

As a first approximation, the stabilization enthalpy and the hydration enthalpy of the core–shell nanocomplexes can be used to estimate the enthalpic contribution of the biomolecule adsorption on the magnetite surface. It is a simplification since the stabilization enthalpy also includes enthalpic contributions from dehydration, reorganization of surface atoms, secondary interactions between the adsorbed species, and any structural rearrangements. The biomolecule adsorption on nano magnetite core is a spontaneous process and can be enthalpy or entropy driven, depending on the surface chemistry and the molecular structure (12–14).

Stabilization of Bovine Serum Albumin–Coated Magnetite

The difference between the stabilization enthalpy and the primary hydration enthalpy of the BSA nano-coated magnetite is 0.03 kJ/g (Table 1), which indicates a near zero but slightly endothermic BSA contribution. According to Rahdar et al. (12), BSA adsorption on magnetite surface in aqueous solution is a spontaneous process (negative Gibbs energy) with moderate positive enthalpy and positive entropy. The partial unfolding of the proteins when being adsorbed on the metal oxide surface, i.e. breaking the hydrogen bonds and reorganizing the secondary structure, is enthalpically unfavorable but increases the entropy. However, the adsorption measurements by Rahdar et al. (12) were conducted in an aqueous solution, where the protein exists in a fully hydrated state. That does not allow a direct comparison of the enthalpic values due to different reference states. The hydration of bovine serum albumin in aqueous solutions is a complex phenomenon, beginning with a tightly bound hydration shell and continuing with outer shells of increasingly loosely bound water gradually approaching bulk like behavior with distance (15). Therefore, the adsorption of that protein onto the nano magnetite involves rearrangement and restructuring of the hydration shells. In contrast, the BSA used in this work was a dry powder containing only 0.3 mol% total residual water (SI Appendix, Table S1).

The small endothermic BSA adsorption contribution is consistent with the formation of a “soft” dynamic protein corona, which would allow a reversible exchange with other serum proteins in a biological environment (16). Traditional strategies in nanomedicine have tried to avoid corona formation via “stealth coatings” (PEG, Poly(2-oxazoline)s, zwitterionic polymers, cell membranes), thus avoiding the premature clearance of the nanocarriers by the immune system or promoting the release of proinflammatory cytokine (17). Since corona formation appears to be unavoidable in situ, there has been growing interest in exploiting its advantages instead of eliminating it. Prefunctionalizing the nanocarriers with desirable serum proteins like albumin can enhance targeted drug delivery and biocompatibility by providing initial stealth behavior, i.e., avoiding rapid recognition and clearance by the immune system. (17)

Stabilization of Starch-Coated Magnetite

The difference between the stabilization enthalpy and the primary hydration enthalpy of the starch nano coated magnetite is −0.07 kJ/g or −15.4 kJ/mol per glucose unit (Table 1) [73.69 wt% starch (1)], which indicates a small exothermic or near zero starch adsorption contribution. This implies the occurrence of hydrogen bonding and surface complexation between surface iron and hydroxyl groups (18, 19) rather than strong covalent chemisorption.

There are, to our knowledge, no prior experimental measurements of the adsorption enthalpy of starch on magnetite, though Yang et al. (20) report adsorption energies between −1 and −2 kJ/mol glucose for several starch models on hematite (Fe2O3) from molecular-simulations. Similar to the BSA-coated magnetite, a direct numerical comparison between those values and the enthalpy of adsorption of starch obtained in this work is not straightforward because of the different reference states, physical definitions, and the iron oxide (hematite vs. magnetite). Khosla et al. (21) measured the enthalpy of adsorption of starch on hematite in an aqueous solution using a microcalorimeter. The initial strongest heat of interaction at low surface coverage was around −50 kJ/mol glucose but decreased in magnitude at higher coverages.

The starch coating improves the colloidal stability by reducing aggregation and provides high biocompatibility (22) which is critical for targeted drug delivery applications. It also results in improved protein corona resistance, thus increasing the circulation time and reducing opsonization (23). The exothermic enthalpy of polysaccharide adsorption suggests that the coating will remain attached to the magnetite surface with a more dynamic hydrated outer region which can bind hydrophilic drugs (24). While the protein corona can be generally beneficial, the therapeutic function of some drug delivery nanocarriers with polysaccharide coatings relies on specific surface functional groups and engineered ligands for targeting and controlled release of the drugs (25).

Stabilization of Lauric Acid–Coated Magnetite

The reference state of the lauric acid in the core–shell nanocomplex is a solid crystalline molecular phase. Hence, the energetic contributions include dissociation of the lauric acid, adsorption of both dissociated and nondissociated species on the magnetite surface, and formation of bilayer clusters. Subtracting the hydration enthalpy (Table 1) of the tightly bound water of the core–shell nanocomplex from the stabilization enthalpy results in −0.10 kJ/g or around −200 ± 40 kJ/mol lauric acid [9.84 wt% LA (1)] contribution which indicates very strong chemisorption. The previous experimental measurements of lauric acid adsorption on metal oxide surfaces are either in aqueous solution (e.g., sodium laurate) or in organic solvents. All experimental values are exothermic (26–28), but they are based on a different reference state and thus cannot be directly compared to the lauric acid adsorption contribution obtained in this work. Gattinoni et al. (13) employed density functional theory (DFT) to calculate the adsorption energy and dissociation behavior of both intact and deprotonated hexanoic acid (6-carbon chain) on α-Fe2O3 (hematite) at low and high coverage and obtained the adsorption energy values between −207 and −230 kJ/mol. Those theoretical values would be expected to increase for lauric acid due to its additional six CH2 groups and the partial bilayer formation, indicating that the solid phase lauric acid adsorption enthalpy obtained here remains of comparable magnitude.

The strong stabilization effect of the lauric acid coating suggests prevention of core exposure under physiological conditions, thus making the nanocomplex a reliable drug delivery carrier. The bilayer of the fatty acid shell also provides a hydrophobic interface, which can be used as a platform for loading hydrophobic therapeutics and for magnetic hyperthermia or imaging-guided delivery (29). In addition, the hydrophilic surface can provide functional chemical groups for drug conjugation or ligand attachments (29).

In summary, we have adopted the concept of stabilization enthalpy instead of surface energy to assess the thermodynamic stability of complex core–shell nanosystems when the real geometric surface area cannot be measured reliably. The two dominant contributions to the stabilization enthalpy are the water adsorption on the complex, i.e., the primary hydration enthalpy, and the enthalpy of adsorption of the biomolecules on the core. Other factors including coating density, structure, rearrangements, and the interactions among the adsorbed species are also included in the total stabilization effect, but cannot be energetically separated. The stabilization enthalpies of the three nanocomplexes are exothermic, indicating a decrease in the enthalpy of the surface in comparison with the uncoated core. The hydration enthalpy of the core–shell nanocomplex is the major stabilization contribution for all three nanocomplexes thus connecting the thermodynamic stability in biological media to the surface hydrophilicity and interaction potential. The enthalpy of adsorption of bovine serum albumin on the magnetite surface is slightly endothermic or zero. It indicates soft dynamic corona formation which could be beneficial for drug delivery applications by providing initial “stealth” behavior and limiting irreversible opsonin adsorption and phagocytosis. Starch adsorption results in a small exothermic contribution to the stabilization enthalpy of the complex, which suggests retaining the coating in biological media. The strong chemisorption of lauric acid on the magnetite core, together with the reorganization of the fatty acid molecules in a partial bilayer, provides a platform for attaching hydrophobic drugs. At the same time a hydrophilic surface for further hydrophilic conjugation creates an amphiphilic option for dual mode loading strategies.

In addition to drug delivery nanocarriers, the stabilization enthalpy concept can in principle be extended to determine the stability of other complex core–shell nanosystems, including biogenic nanominerals and functional materials. Hence, this concept has potentially broad applications in describing the thermodynamic stability relations of different components in complex nanomaterials.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis.

FeSO4*7 H2O (Alfa Aesar) and FeCl36H2O (Merck) were used to synthesize the magnetite core. Dry soluble potato starch (Baker), bovine serum albumin (Millipore Sigma), and lauric acid (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.) were used for the biomolecular coating. All samples were synthesized in aqueous solution and inert atmosphere (1).

Characterization.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to confirm the presence of organics while X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to verify the phase purity, crystal structure, and the crystallite size of the magnetite core. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed the size, shape, and aggregation of the nanocomplexes and related energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy provided the elemental compositions of the core. The Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio, the amount of magnetite, water, and biomolecular coating were determined using thermogravimetry and CHNS/O analysis, and from the lattice parameter of the magnetite (obtained by XRD). The synthesis and characterization have been described in detail before (1).

High Temperature Oxidative Drop Solution Calorimetry.

High temperature oxidative solution calorimetry was performed at 1,073 K in molten sodium molybdate (3Na2O 4MoO3) solvent. Oxygen gas was flushed over the solvent at 90 mL/min and bubbled through it at 5 mL/min. The calorimeter was calibrated against the heat of combustion of 5 mg pellets of benzoic acid (C7H6O2, Parr Instruments) (4).
