Analysis of prognostic risk factors in critically ill elderly patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury
Authors: Jiangwei Zeng, Yu Zhang, Mengxin Zhang, Kai Feng, Weiyan Guo, Yanlei Ge, Weibin Chen, Jinyu Li, Aibin Cheng, Jing Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the risk factors influencing the prognosis of critically ill elderly patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI).MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) Ⅳ database collected between 2008 and 2019. A total of 3,338 elderly patients with SA-AKI were included and categorized into a survival group (n = 2,448) and a death group (n = 898) based on 28-day mortality outcomes. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was utilized to identify potential predictors, and multivariate Cox regression was employed to analyze independent risk factors associated with 28-day mortality in elderly patients with SA-AKI.ResultsThe 28-day mortality rate in elderly patients with SA-AKI was 26.9%. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that age ≥ 76 years (Hazard Ratio[HR] = 1.392, 95% Confidence Interval[CI] 1.212–1.598, P < 0.001), creatinine ≥ 1.5 mg/dL (HR = 1.216, 95%CI 1.037–1.427, P = 0.016), urea-to-creatinine ratio (UCR) ≥ 20 (HR = 1.668, 95%CI 1.441–1.931, P < 0.001), liver disease (HR = 1.292, 95%CI 1.059–1.575, P = 0.011), renal replacement therapy (HR = 1.476, 95%CI 1.160–1.879, P = 0.002), use of norepinephrine (HR = 1.624, 95%CI 1.390–1.896, P < 0.001), dobutamine (HR = 1.644, 95%CI 1.225–2.206, P < 0.001), dopamine (HR = 1.260, 95%CI 1.008–1.576, P = 0.042), and vasopressin (HR = 1.708, 95%CI 1.430–2.041, P < 0.001) were significant risk factors for 28-day mortality in elderly patients with SA-AKI. In contrast, epinephrine use was associated with lower mortality (HR = 0.553, 95%CI 0.417–0.734, P < 0.001).ConclusionsElderly SA-AKI patients have a high short-term mortality. Advanced age, renal impairment, liver disease, renal replacement therapy, and the use of several vasoactive drugs were associated with poor outcomes, while the potential benefit of epinephrine requires further validation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Sepsis and septic shock have long been recognized as major contributors to the development of Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) in critically ill patients [1, 2]. The presence of comorbid AKI further increases the risk of sepsis and septic shock [3], highlighting the interdependent relationship between these conditions. Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is a heterogeneous clinical syndrome resulting from direct mechanisms related to infection, the host immune response, or indirect mechanisms arising from the adverse effects of sepsis treatment [4]. SA-AKI demonstrates significant epidemiological variation and marked heterogeneity in case fatality rates, and its global health burden should not be underestimated. Accumulating evidence indicates that the incidence of AKI is increasing among patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) [5], particularly among the elderly [6, 7]. Renal aging is characterized by structural changes such as glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, arteriosclerosis, and interstitial fibrosis [8], and the coexistence of senescent kidneys and chronic comorbidities is a major factor contributing to the poor prognosis of patients with SA-AKI. Although considerable progress has been made in standardized disease management, antimicrobial therapy, and organ support strategies in recent years, the prognosis for elderly patients with SA-AKI remains poor, largely due to the synergistic impact of global population aging and the limited availability of effective therapeutic interventions. Therefore, identifying prognostic risk factors in this population is essential to reduce mortality. The present study aims to investigate the prognostic risk factors of SA-AKI in elderly patients. By elucidating key determinants of prognosis, we seek to provide evidence that may facilitate timely recognition, early risk stratification, and prognostic assessment in clinical practice.

Information and methodology

Data sources and authorisation

The data were obtained from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care(MIMIC), a publicly available database containing clinical information on patients admitted to the ICU at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of both Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Patient information in the database is de-identified, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The authors obtained permission to use the data, as documented by certificate number 48,763,976.

Research population

Elderly patients with SA-AKI were included in the study. It is important to note that there is currently no universally accepted definition of SA-AKI. In this study, elderly individuals were defined as those aged 65 years and older. Based on the SA-AKI: Consensus Report of the 28th Acute Disease Quality Initiative Workgroup [4], AKI occurring within seven days of sepsis was classified as SA-AKI. Specifically, early SA-AKI was defined as AKI occurring within 48 h, while late SA-AKI referred to AKI developing between 48 h and seven days.

Criteria for inclusion

(1) Patients with sepsis meeting the Sepsis3.0 criteria. (2) Patients who fulfilled the 2012 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria for the diagnosis and staging of AKI. (3) For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU admission record was included.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Patients admitted to the ICU for less than 48 h. (2) Patients aged less than 65 years. (3) Patients with a history of chronic kidney disease(CKD). (4) Patients with incomplete clinical data. (5) Patients with sepsis who did not develop AKI within seven days. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Data were extracted using PostgreSQL. The following variables were collected from elderly patients with SA-AKI: general demographic information, comorbidities, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, laboratory values (including white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, potassium, creatinine, and urea-to-creatinine ratio[UCR]), and treatment measures (such as invasive mechanical ventilation[IMV], renal replacement therapy[RRT], and vasoactive medications). Based on the cut-off values, patients were divided into two age groups: ≥76 years and < 76 years. Laboratory indicators were categorized according to standard physiological reference ranges: Platelet count was divided into two groups: <100 × 10⁹/L and ≥ 100 × 10⁹/L; Creatinine levels were classified as ≥ 1.5 mg/dL and < 1.5 mg/dL; UCR was grouped as < 20 and ≥ 20; Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was classified as ≤ 65mmHg and > 65mmHg (1mmHg = 0.133 kPa). Comorbidities were dichotomized as either “yes” or “no”. All laboratory data were based on the average values recorded during the first 24 h of ICU admission.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.3.1. Data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and independent-samples t-tests were used for comparisons between groups. For non-normally distributed data, values were reported as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons of multiple independent proportions were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was used to identify independent predictors. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to assess risk factors for 28-day mortality in elderly patients with SA-AKI. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of clinical characteristics in elderly SA-AKI patients with different prognoses

This study found that the 28-day mortality rate among elderly patients with SA-AKI was 26.9%. Compared with the survival group, the death group had a higher proportion of male patients, individuals aged ≥ 76 years, and those with platelet counts < 100 × 10⁹/L, creatinine ≥ 1.5 mg/dL, UCR ≥ 20, and comorbidities such as cerebrovascular disease, congestive heart failure, and liver disease. Additionally, a significantly greater proportion of patients in the death group received RRT, IMVand vasoactive agents, including norepinephrine, dobutamine, and vasopressin (All P < 0.05). The death group also had higher SOFA scores, white blood cell counts, and serum potassium levels compared to the survival group (P < 0.05), as shown in Table 1.

A total of ten predictor variables were selected: age, serum creatinine, UCR, liver disease, RRT, dopamine, dobutamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, and vasopressin. These variables were then incorporated into a Cox proportional hazards regression model, and the results were visualized as a forest plot Fig. 3.

Discussion

SA-AKI is an independent predictor of prolonged hospitalization and increased mortality risk [9]. Moreover, the incidence of progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), as well as morbidity and mortality rates, are significantly higher in SA-AKI patients compared to those with non-sepsis-related AKI [6, 7, 10]. Although biomarkers that reflect kidney function and AKI progression have received considerable attention, their clinical utility remains limited due to factors such as high cost, technical difficulties in detection, and insufficient sensitivity and specificity [11–13]. In addition, incomplete coverage and diagnostic delays are major limitations of existing scoring systems. Previous studies have investigated SA-AKI across various patient populations; however, the scope and comparability of these studies vary due to differences in baseline characteristics and definitions of nadir values. Despite this heterogeneity, commonly identified risk factors include demographic variables, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, and treatment-related variables. Elderly ICU patients with SA-AKI typically present with compromised baseline health, complex comorbidity profiles, and a poor prognosis. Therefore, identifying prognostic risk factors in this population is crucial for guiding timely interventions and improving clinical outcomes.

In this study, the 28-day mortality rate for elderly patients with SA-AKI was 26.9%, which is comparable to the 30-day mortality rate of 29.3% reported by Xin et al. [14]. However, mortality rates can be influenced by a range of variables, including the characteristics of the study population, the underlying etiology of sepsis, and disease severity. Some studies have demonstrated that, compared with early SA-AKI, the onset and progression of late SA-AKI are more strongly associated with a poorer prognosis [15, 16]. The results of this study indicated no significant difference in short-term mortality between early- and late-onset SA-AKI in elderly patients. The study population consisted exclusively of older adults, whose clinical complexity, frailty, treatment variations across studies, and disease severity may all influence prognosis. Therefore, further validation of the prognostic differences between early- and late-onset SA-AKI in larger prospective cohorts of elderly patients is warranted.

With advancing age, the likelihood of developing AKI increases [17]. A meta-analysis by Søvik et al. [18] confirmed that advanced age is closely associated with the development of AKI in ICU patients. Another study found that the presence of multiple chronic comorbidities in septic patients was associated with a higher prevalence of AKI, particularly in the elderly [2], which is consistent with the findings of this study. Age-related renal structural changes, chronic illness burden, polypharmacy, and complex treatment histories may all contribute to the increased risk of SA-AKI in older patients. Notably, all patients included in this study were aged ≥ 65 years, and our results suggest that the adverse prognostic impact of age on SA-AKI is mainly observed in patients ≥ 76 years. A multicenter study on the outcomes of patients with severe pneumonia complicated by AKI reported higher case fatality rates in patients aged ≥ 50 years [19]. Consistent with our findings, a cohort study conducted by Herget-Rosenthal et al. [20] evaluated the incidence and short-term outcomes of AKI in elderly patients. The results showed that very old patients (≥ 80 years) had a significantly higher incidence of AKI, as well as increased 30-day mortality and major adverse renal events. However, previous studies have not consistently specified the age range that influences SA-AKI prognosis, and the cut-off values vary widely, with substantial heterogeneity in study populations. In future studies, we plan to perform prospective validation and age-stratified analyses in larger multicenter cohorts to more precisely evaluate differential prognosis among elderly SA-AKI patients across different age groups.

The diagnosis and staging of AKI still rely predominantly on serum creatinine levels and urine output, despite the inherent limitations of these indicators when used in isolation.It is well established that creatinine is a sensitive marker of glomerular filtration rate. Liu et al. [21] demonstrated that infection-induced inflammation and renal hypoperfusion can lead to elevated creatinine levels. However, factors such as medication effects, volume status, and nutritional deficiencies may obscure the accuracy of serum creatinine concentrations [22], particularly in elderly patients. In this study, elderly patients with SA-AKI and serum creatinine levels ≥ 1.5 mg/dL had a poor prognosis. Therefore, it is crucial to closely monitor serum creatinine levels in elderly septic patients to assess AKI risk. Timely clinical intervention may help reduce the risk of mortality.

UCR ≥ 20 was found to be associated with poor prognosis in elderly patients with SA-AKI, consistent with previous studies [23, 24]. Sepsis often leads to hemodynamic instability. Under hypovolemic conditions, aberrant activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) increases renal tubular sodium and water reabsorption, reduces urea excretion, and results in elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels due to partial reabsorption of BUN in the renal tubules [22]. Both creatinine and BUN levels are susceptible to various confounding factors. The UCR may offer greater stability and diagnostic accuracy by minimizing the influence of these variables [25]. UCR of 10 is considered normal and clinically relevant for identifying prerenal causes of kidney injury, thereby aiding in early AKI detection and improving patient outcomes.

The choice of vasoactive drugs and the timing of their initiation can significantly impact patient outcomes. Previous studies have demonstrated that vasoactive agents play a pivotal role in predicting prognosis in elderly patients with SA-AKI, which is consistent with the findings of the present study [26, 27]. A retrospective study by Bai et al. [28] demonstrated that administering norepinephrine within the first two hours of septic shock onset offers several benefits: it increases MAP, shortens the duration of hypotension and hypoperfusion, ensures perfusion of vital organs, and improves patient survival. Conversely, each hour of delay within the first six hours is associated with a 5.3% increase in mortality. However, it has also been shown that renal hypoperfusion and hypoxia typically begin in the renal medulla [29]. Although norepinephrine may temporarily improve renal function, it can simultaneously worsen renal medullary ischemia and hypoxia, potentially contributing to the development of AKI. Several studies have highlighted both the advantages and limitations of vasopressin, dopamine, and dobutamine in maintaining hemodynamic stability [30, 31]; however, none have been sufficient to conclusively determine the impact of these vasoactive agents on AKI outcomes. Previous studies have also indicated that the use of vasoactive drugs often serves as a marker of critical illness [32, 33]. In the present study, the use of noradrenaline, dopamine, dobutamine, or vasopressin was associated with increased mortality. This association is more likely to reflect underlying disease severity and the need for advanced hemodynamic support, rather than direct harmful effects of the drugs themselves.

According to previous studies [31, 34], the use of epinephrine in septic shock models has been associated with reduced renal blood flow and increased risk of hyperlactatemia and acidosis, thereby posing a potential threat to patients with SA-AKI. In this study, the use of epinephrine was associated with lower mortality; however, this association should not be interpreted as causal. The apparent “protective effect” may be confounded by factors such as disease severity, concomitant therapies, and the timing or dosage of administration. High-quality prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are warranted to further validate these findings. Overall, for elderly patients with SA-AKI, timely hemodynamic monitoring, the rational selection of vasoactive agents, and the reduction of renal hypoperfusion time are key strategies for improving renal function. Future studies are warranted to further optimize treatment regimens and to conduct high-quality investigations to clarify the specific effects of different vasoactive drugs on renal function.

Liver failure impairs the liver’s detoxification capacity, leading to the accumulation of endotoxins and substantially increasing the risk of sepsis if not properly managed [35]. Severe liver disease can induce renal injury and significantly elevate mortality rates, particularly in patients with end-stage liver dysfunction. Hepatorenal syndrome, a serious complication, often develops in the late stages of liver disease and is characterized by a marked reduction in renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate, ultimately resulting in prerenal-type acute kidney injury. Studies have reported a high prevalence of AKI in patients with end-stage liver disease, which is closely associated with increased morbidity and mortality [36, 37] consistent with the findings of the present study. Therefore, early prevention and timely management of SA-AKI in patients with liver disease are essential for improving patient outcomes.

RRT is commonly considered for the management of conditions such as AKI, volume overload, severe acid-base imbalances, and excessive systemic inflammatory response. A meta-analysis by Zou et al. [38] examined the impact of RRT on mortality in AKI patients and revealed conflicting findings, with some studies supporting a survival benefit and others indicating no significant improvement in outcomes. Barbar et al. [39] found that early initiation of RRT did not significantly reduce either 28-day or 90-day mortality in patients with severe sepsis and AKI. Similarly, Bagshaw et al. 40 reported that RRT did not improve overall survival, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Elderly patients with sepsis are particularly vulnerable to severe infections, persistent hypotension, acid-base disturbances, and underlying refractory comorbidities, all of which complicate clinical management and negatively impact outcomes. While the timing of RRT initiation may influence patient prognosis, its optimal timing remains unclear due to the complexity and heterogeneity of ICU patients. Many critically ill patients may die before RRT can be effectively implemented. Therefore, the decision to initiate RRT should be based on a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and overall disease severity, rather than relying solely on serum creatinine levels or urine output.

The results of this study showed that age ≥ 76 years, creatinine ≥ 1.5 mg/dL, UCR ≥ 20, comorbid liver disease, RRT, norepinephrine, dopamine, dobutamine, and vasopressin were independent predictors of 28-day mortality in elderly patients with SA-AKI. Among these predictors, vasopressin and UCR had the strongest associations with increased mortality risk. Specifically, patients who received vasopressin had a 1.708-fold higher risk of death within 28 days, while UCR was associated with a 1.668-fold increase. Age and comorbidities, such as liver disease, are important demographic and clinical factors that contribute to patient outcomes. Creatinine and UCR are closely linked to the severity of renal dysfunction. Vasoactive agents are routinely administered to maintain mean arterial pressure following adequate fluid resuscitation in patients with septic shock. The selection, timing, and dosage of these drugs can have a significant impact on clinical outcomes. The risk factors identified in this study are readily obtainable from electronic medical records and are not only statistically significant but also clinically actionable, providing a practical framework for the early prognostic assessment of elderly patients with SA-AKI. Recognition of high-risk features—including age ≥ 76 years, elevated serum creatinine, and concomitant organ dysfunction—enables clinicians to implement timely risk stratification upon ICU admission, thereby facilitating more precise and individualized management strategies. For patients classified as high-risk, intensified hemodynamic monitoring, optimized fluid therapy, judicious use of vasoactive agents, and early evaluation for renal replacement therapy may be warranted. Such proactive interventions have the potential to mitigate AKI progression, reduce complications, and ultimately improve both short-term and long-term outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center, retrospective analysis, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, this study employed complete-case analysis to ensure transparent data handling and robust results. However, if the missing data mechanism is not completely at random, this approach may slightly alter the sample structure and introduce selection bias. Future research will incorporate multiple imputation and sensitivity analyses to further assess the robustness of the findings. Third, subgroup analyses, including age stratification (65–75 vs. >75 years) and temporal onset of SA-AKI (early vs. late), were constrained by limited sample sizes, potentially limiting the ability to detect meaningful differences between subgroups. Finally, the study focused on identifying prognostic risk factors in elderly patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury, analyzing only associations between clinical variables and outcomes without constructing predictive models or performing individualized risk estimation. Future research should develop predictive models based on these findings and conduct rigorous internal and external validation to evaluate model performance, calibration, and generalizability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the pathophysiological mechanisms of SA-AKI are complex and remain incompletely understood. In the absence of targeted therapies, early recognition of high-risk patients and proactive management are essential. Using the MIMIC database, this study identified factors associated with 28-day mortality in elderly patients with SA-AKI, providing preliminary evidence that may help improve risk awareness and guide clinical decision-making. These associations should be interpreted with caution, and further large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate and refine risk stratification strategies in this population.
