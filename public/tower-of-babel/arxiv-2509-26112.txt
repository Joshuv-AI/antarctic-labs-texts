DNA shotgun sequencing evidence: sample-specific and unknown genotyping error probabilities Journal: arXiv
Mikkel Meyer Andersen

Abstract
DNA shotgun sequencing evidence is starting to gain a lot of attraction in forensic genetics. Methods to correctly interpret such evidence, including properly accounting for sequencing errors, are needed. This paper extends the wgsLR model by Andersen et. al. (2025) from only allowing for the same, known genotyping error probability for the two samples (trace sample from unknown donor and reference sample from person of interest), to allowing for different genotyping error probabilities (e.g., from trace hair sample and buccal swab reference sample). The model was also extended to be able to integrate out unknown genotyping error probabilities if only a prior probability is known. The sensitivity of the model against overdispersion was also investigated and it was found that it is very robust against overdispersion in estimating the genotyping error probability. It was also found that integrating out unknown genotyping error probability of the trace sample gave concordant weight of evidence under both the hypotheses (first hypothesis that the same individual was the donor of both trace and reference sample as well as the second hypothesis that two different individuals were the donors for the trace and reference sample). It was found that it is more consevative to use prior distributions with a too small mean rather than a too high mean. The extension of the model is implemented in the R package wgsLR .

Introduction

DNA shotgun sequencing evidence is starting to become of high interest
in forensic genetics, as this can technique can extract evidence from
traces not possible using short tandem repeat (STR) markers as for
example hairs and heavily degraded samples. Such samples can still be
analysed by DNA shotgun sequencing, but due to the relatively higher
error probability with DNA sequencing (partly because the amount of data
is much higher)
[
1
]
, methods to properly analyse and
interpret such data in a forensic context is of high importance.

Handling DNA shotgun sequencing evidence accounting for errors requires
a statistical model. Different models have been proposed, both some that
models the raw sequencing data

[
2
,
3
,
4
]
as well as the data with
called genotypes
[
1
]
.

In this paper the model by
[
1
]
to evaluate the
evidential value of single-source traces is extended. To explain in what
ways, first note that a central parameter of the model by

[
1
]
was the genotyping error probability that was
denoted by
w
. The original model
[
1
]
was used both
to estimate
w
using duplicated samples from the same individual and
to calculate the weight of evidence in terms of a likelihood ratio
(
LR
) assuming the same genotyping error probability,
w
, for both
the trace sample and reference sample.

This paper takes a closer look at the statistical model and extends it.
This paper contains three main results.

Firstly, it is investigated if the model is robust against
overdispersion. That is, how well can
w
be estimated as long as the
average genotyping error probability across the genome is
w
even
though some genomic ranges have higher genotyping error probability and
other genomic ranges lower genotyping error probability - as long as the
genotyping error probability on average is
w
. This is important for
when do estimate the genotyping error probability parameter.

Secondly, the model is extended to handle sample-specific genotyping
error probabilities. This is important as the
d
onor sample,

X^{D}
, can be of poor quality, and thereby have a higher value of

w
than a good quality sample,
X^{S}
, from a person of interest
(PoI), e.g., a
s
uspect. The
w
for the donor sample is
referred to as
w_{D}
and the
w
for the reference sample as
w_{S}

(
S
for suspect). This extension enables
LR
computations for
sample-specific genotyping error probabilities

Thirdly, different approaches to calculate an
LR
even when no good
estimates of
w
are available are investigated. This is done by using
the prior predictive distribution.

2.1
Overdispersion

If each base position in the genome has a genotyping error probability
of
w
, then
w
can be estimated
[
1
]
. Here, the
sensitivity of overdispersion for the estimator in
[
1

is investigated. That is, if some genomic regions are more likely to
have a genotyping error than other genomic regions (as long as the
average genotyping error probability is
w
). This was investigate by,
for each position, a genotyping error probability was drawn randomly
from a beta distribution (on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
) with mean value
w

for different choices of variances.

The different beta distributions (on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
) used are
displayed in Figure
1

Note, that the important thing in relation to overdispersion is the
coefficient of variation, i.e. the ratio of the standard deviation
relative to the mean value. Lower mean value of the beta distribution
means a larger sample size is necessary to obtain the same estimation
precision as larger mean values.

2.3
Unknown genotyping error
probabilities

Calculating the
LR
s given in Table
1
assumes that

w_{D}
and
w_{S}
are known. In practice this assumption is adhered to
for the reference sample as that can be sufficiently determined in the
lab (e.g. for buccal swabs), and thus
w_{S}
can be assumed known.

On the other hand, because the conditions and quality of the trace
sample is unknown, it can be problematic to determine
w_{D}
. Thus,
instead a Bayesian standpoint can be taken and view
w_{D}
as a random
variable. Recall that

\displaystyle LR=\frac{P(E\mid H_{1})}{P(E\mid H_{2})},

(1)

where
H_{1}
is that the same individual donated both the
trace sample and the reference sample and
H_{2}
is that two different
individuals donated the trace sample and reference sample. Then for each

H_{i}
this can be obtained by marginalising out
w_{D}
, i.e.

\displaystyle P(E\mid H_{1})=\int P(E\mid H_{i},w_{D})P(w_{D})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{D}

(2)

for the prior distribution
P(w_{D})
of
w_{D}
. This is
sometimes called the prior predictive distribution of the evidence
E

under the hypothesis
H_{i}
. It is also the expected value of the
likelihood function
P(E\mid H_{i},w_{D})
, where the expectation is
taken with respect to the prior distribution
P(w_{D})
of
w_{D}
.
Intuitively, it is a weighted average of the likelihood, so the
likelihood is calculated for each
w_{D}
and is weighted by how likely

w_{D}
is
a priori
.

In the software
wgsLR
, it is assumed
a priori
that

w_{D}
follows a beta distribution on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
with shape
parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
. Thus

\displaystyle LR

\displaystyle=\frac{P(E\mid H_{1})}{P(E\mid H_{2})}=\frac{\int P(E\mid H_{1},w_{D})P_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}(w_{D})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{D}}{\int P(E\mid H_{2},w_{D})P_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}}(w_{D})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{D}}.

(3)

Similar can be done if also
w_{S}
is unknown. This was
intentionally left out as this would not normally be the case, and it
may raise problems if both genotyping error probabilities (from donor
sample,
w_{D}
, and suspect sample,
w_{S}
) are unknown.

Note that there may be different prior distributions of
w_{D}
under

H_{1}
(e.g. beta distribution on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
with shape
parameters
\alpha_{1}
and
\beta_{1}
) and under
H_{2}
(e.g. beta
distribution on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
with shape parameters
\alpha_{2}

and
\beta_{2}
).

To analyse the sensitivity of wrongly specified prior distribution of

w_{D}
, for example mean value much lower or much higher than the
correct value, simulation experiments were conducted using

wgsLR
.

Three different ways of integrating was used: exact formulas (found via
Maple
[
7
]
) using the
R
library
Rmpfr

[
8
]
to increase the precision of the floating-point
computations, numeric integration (using
R
s

integrate()
), and Monte Carlo integration.

In all computation it was assumed that the prior distribution of
w_{D}

was the same under
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, i.e.
\alpha_{p}=\alpha_{d}
and

\beta_{p}=\beta_{d}
.

2.4
Simulations

The simulations were performed assuming Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium with
allele frequencies
q=0.75
(resulting in genotype frequencies

(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2})=\left(q^{2},2q(1-q),(1-q)^{2}\right)=(0.5625,0.375,0.0625)

for 0, 1, and 2 reference alleles, respectively) and
q=0.9

(resulting in
(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2})=(0.81,0.18,0.01)
).

For the study over overdispersion,
w
was simulated from the beta
distributions (on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
) displayed in
Figure
1
. They all have a mean value of

0.01
. Tables of sizes 1,000, 10,000, and 100,000 were simulated.
Each combination were replicated 1,000 times. Thus, a total of 1,000

\times
2 (
q
)
\times
3 (
w
variance)
\times
3 (table
sizes)
=
18,000 tables were simulated.

For the study on integrating out
w_{D}
, the same
q
s were used. A
fixed
w_{S}=10^{-4}
was used for all simulations. Different values
of
w_{D}
were used; namely

,

, and

(two

H_{1}
(same individual) was
simulated. Thus, a total of 1,000
\times
2 (
q
)
\times
3
(true
w_{D}
values)
\times
3 (number of markers)
=
18,000
cases under
H_{2}
were simulated and the same for
H_{1}
. For each

w_{D}
were used in
the integration, including a uniform prior on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
, some

) are shown in Table

4
Discussion

The sensitivity of the model against overdispersion was also
investigated and it was found that it is very robust against
overdispersion in estimating
w
(Figure
3
).

With this paper, the original model
[
1
]
for
single-source stains on called genotype data can now handle the
following situations: A) Two equal and known genotyping error
probabilities (originally described in
[
1
]
); B) Two
different and known genotyping error probabilities (e.g., hair trace
sample and buccal swab reference sample); and C) One known and one
unknown known genotyping error probability (e.g., known for buccal swab
reference sample and unknown for trace sample). The method described in
this paper can easily be extended to allow for both genotyping error
probabilities being unknown.

The results of this study demonstrate that when the genotyping error
probability for the trace sample,
w_{D}
, is lower than that of the
reference sample,
w_{S}
, then integrating out
w_{D}
gives concordant
results to if
w_{D}
was actually known
(Figure
7
and
Figure
8
) as long as the prior
distribution is chosen carefully. For example, if the mean is chosen to
be too high, then different genotypes may be explained by errors instead
of originating from different individuals
(Table
3
). If the prior is chosen to be equal to
or lower than the true value of
w_{D}
and there is a sufficient number
of markers (more than 40), then no simulated
H_{2}
cases (two
different individuals donated the trace sample and reference sample)
gave an integration WoE above 0.

Another important numerical experience obtained in this study, is that
if the prior distribution is too peaked, the numerical integration can
fail. Thus, it is important to verify that the results obtained from
different integration methods (exact, numerical, and Monte Carlo) give
concordant results.
