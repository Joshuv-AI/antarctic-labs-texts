Cell therapy comparison of dental pulp stem cells, hepatocytes, and their exosomes for liver fibrosis treatment in rats
Authors: Sahar Rahimi, Seyedeh Mahdieh Khoshnazar, Ali Derakhshani, Nader Shahrokhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExcessive extracellular matrix accumulation, primarily as a result of hepatic stellate cell activation, is a hallmark of hepatic fibrosis, a progressive outcome of chronic liver injuries. Recent research studies suggest that stem cells, hepatocytes, and extracellular vesicles may provide therapeutic advantages due to their anti‐inflammatory, antioxidative, and regenerative activities. This study aimed to comparatively evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of these agents in a rat model of carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)–induced hepatic fibrosis.MethodsLiver fibrosis was induced in male Wistar rats via intraperitoneal CCl4 injections for 8 weeks. Then the animals were intravenously administrated stem cells, hepatocytes, hepatocyte‐derived exosomes, or stem cell–derived exosomes. Also, a fibrosis, a sham, a intact, and a PBS‐treated group were consider the controls. After treatment, protein expression (alpha‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA), desmin), oxidative stress markers (superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, malondialdehyde), serum biochemical parameters (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, glucose, uric acid, cholesterol, triglycerides), and fibrosis‐related gene expression (matrix metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2), platelete‐derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRB), transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β), thymosin beta‐10 (TMSB10) and transmembrane protein 176B (TMEM176B)) were assessed.ResultsSignificant liver damage, changed metabolic parameters, increased oxidative stress, and upregulated fibrosis markers were all observed in the fibrosis group. On the contrary, all treatments caused considerable improvements, though exosomes derived from stem cells demonstrated the most significant effects. Along with improved histopathological features, this group exhibited significant decreases in oxidative damage, liver enzymes, and profibrotic marker expression.ConclusionLiver fibrosis was considerably reduced by stem cells, hepatocytes, and particularly their exosomes. Exosomes made from stem cells demonstrated the strongest therapeutic effect, confirming their potential as a viable noncellular hepatic fibrosis treatment approach.Keywords: CCl4 , dental pulp stem cells, exosomes, hepatocytes, liver fibrosis, stem cells

INTRODUCTION

As a major metabolic organ, the liver performs vital functions necessary for maintaining overall health. It has complex relationships with other organs and is essential for many different biochemical processes. Liver diseases are a growing public health concern. Their incidence in the Middle East and North Africa increased by 114.9% between 1990 and 2021. 1 Liver fibrosis can be caused by persistent metabolic stress and inflammation associated with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and advanced fibrosis greatly increases the risk of liver cancer. 2 Liver fibrosis can also result from chronic liver diseases such as viral hepatitis, NAFLD, and long‐term alcohol abuse. 3 Irregular remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and excessive collagen deposition are hallmarks of a process driven by chronic liver injury that disrupts liver structure and function, ultimately contributing to the progression of fibrosis and organ dysfunction. Collagen remodeling and deposition also influence fibroblast activity and the inflammatory response, two important factors in the progression of liver fibrosis. 4 Hepatic stellate cells (HSC), which store vitamin A and other retinoids, are in a quiescent state under normal, healthy conditions. However, after liver injury, HSCs differentiate into myofibroblast‐like cells and induce a fibrotic response characterized by changes in ECM remodeling and deposition. 5 Fibrosis requires a delicate balance between ECM production and degradation, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases are important players in ECM remodeling. 6 The liver's function and ability to regenerate are compromised by this fibrotic process that alters the liver's normal architecture. Therefore, inhibiting HSC activation, promoting ECM degradation, and regulating the inflammatory response are the three major goals of antifibrotic tactics. Notable strategies include MMP enhancers, transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) inhibitors, and peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor agonists. 7 , 8

Regarding therapeutic agents, hepatocytes play a crucial role in maintaining liver health not only by performing their own metabolic functions but also by interacting with other liver cells, particularly HSCs. Hepatocytes release a variety of cytokines and growth factors that alter the surrounding environment, lower inflammation, and prevent HSCs, the liver's main fibrogenic cells, from activating. 9 Additionally, hepatocytes have the ability to alter the immune system, which reduces the persistent inflammation that accelerates the development of fibrosis. 10

One promising method for treating liver fibrosis is stem cell therapy. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSC), induced pluripotent stem cells, and embryonic stem cells (pluripotent cells that can produce hepatocytes and other liver cells) are among the stem cell types that have demonstrated promise in the treatment of liver fibrosis. MSCs have been the subject of much research due to their ability to differentiate into hepatocyte‐like cells. 11 Through a variety of methods, stem cell therapy treats liver fibrosis with a broad range of positive effects. These cells have the ability to develop into functional hepatocytes and release a wide range of cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles (EV), such as exosomes, that suppress HSC activation and regulate the inflammatory response. 12 Notwithstanding these encouraging outcomes, there are still issues with the clinical use of stem cell therapy, such as guaranteeing safe and efficient stem cell differentiation, avoiding immunological rejection, and reducing the chance of tumor development. 13

Exosomes are tiny EVs, typically 30–150 nm in diameter, secreted from cells and play a key role in intercellular communication. They carry a variety of bioactive molecules, such as proteins, lipids, DNA, and RNA, that can alter the behavior of recipient cells. Due to this unique ability, exosomes have emerged as promising tools for therapeutic applications, particularly in targeted drug delivery and regenerative medicine. 14 Exosomes can deliver repair factors and genetic material to damaged tissues and regulate inflammation, angiogenesis, and cell proliferation. Their ability to remodel and transport bioactive molecules makes exosomes a promising approach to reduce liver fibrosis by enhancing tissue repair and fine‐tuning inflammation. 15 , 16 It is still unclear exactly how exosomes alter stem cell behavior, how they interact with each other, and whether exosome‐based therapies are reliable for long‐term safety and efficacy in the clinical setting. 17 , 18 , 19

Currently, there are a few and often ineffective treatment options for liver fibrosis. Although their efficacy varies greatly, recent advances in stem cell therapy demonstrate the potential of hepatocytes and dental pulp stem cells (DPSC) to support liver regeneration. Stem cell–derived exosomes hold promise, but to date, there is no comparative study evaluating their efficacy against hepatocyte‐based therapies and conventional cell therapy approaches. This project aims to fill this critical gap by systematically comparing the therapeutic effects of DPSCs, hepatocytes, and their respective exosomes in a CCl4‐induced rat model of liver fibrosis.

Research methodology.

Dental pulp stem cells

DPSCs were obtained from the stem cell bank of Kerman University of Medical Sciences, following institutional guidelines and ethical approval. 20

Hepatocyte isolation

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from healthy animals using a combined enzymatic perfusion and differential centrifugation protocol. Animals were anesthetized with ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (15 mg/kg). Excised liver tissue was thoroughly cleaned to remove nonhepatic components and immediately transferred to an ice‐cold hepatocyte washing medium. To facilitate hepatocyte release, the liver tissue was perfused and digested with collagenase type I (Gibco, cat. no. 17100‐017) to degrade the ECM, followed by gentle mechanical dissociation through removal of the Glisson's capsule and mild pipetting. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70‐μm nylon mesh to remove debris, connective tissue, and nonparenchymal cells.

Hepatocytes were enriched by allowing the suspension to settle and by repeated low‐speed differential centrifugation and washing steps at 4°C to preserve cell viability. Dead cells were removed by decanting the supernatant. The final cell pellet was resuspended in a hepatocyte culture medium and kept on ice prior to counting. Cell number and viability were assessed using trypan blue exclusion. Viable hepatocytes were seeded in defined density culture plates and incubated under standard culture conditions. After an initial attachment period, nonadherent and dead cells were removed, and fresh culture medium was added for subsequent experimental applications.

Exosome isolation and quantification

Exosomes were isolated from DPSCs and hepatocyte cell culture supernatant using a sequential filtration process as previously described in detail. 21 Briefly, the procedure began with dead‐end filtration through a 100‐nm membrane filter, which eliminated floating cells and large debris. Although large, rigid particles were effectively removed, some flexible particles exceeding 100 nm were able to pass through. After this, tangential flow filtration utilizing hollow fibers with a molecular weight cutoff of 500 kDa was performed to concentrate the sample and minimize contaminants. The resulting retentate underwent diafiltration and was then filtered again using a 100‐nm H track filter. To ensure optimal recovery of exosomes, the filters were rinsed after each step, and membrane pressure was carefully monitored during the second and third filtration stages. This method facilitated the isolation of exosomes with high purity achieved through low manipulation forces and preserved their functional properties, making them suitable for downstream applications in research.

The total protein concentration of exosome preparations was quantified using the Bradford protein assay. Briefly, 20 μL of each exosome sample was mixed with 200 μL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue G‐250 reagent in a 96‐well microplate and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader. Protein concentrations were determined by interpolation from a bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard curve constructed under identical conditions.

Exosome characterization

Dynamic light scattering test

The size distribution of exosomes, which normally falls within the nanometer range, was determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis. Characterizing fine particles in suspension or emulsion at both the nanometer and micrometer scales is a common application for this technique. The Horiba SZ‐100V2 DLS system was used to analyze the materials prepared as emulsions or suspensions. 22

Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to study the microstructure of materials, observe nanoparticles, and examine their internal structures. This test was conducted to confirm the presence of exosomes. 23

Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed to detect the exosomal marker CD63 in stem cell–derived and hepatocyte‐derived exosomes. Tissue samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer and centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay (DNAbioTech, DB0017) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg, 1:1) were mixed with 2 × Laemmli sample buffer, boiled for 5 min, and separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE). Proteins were transferred onto 0.2‐μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio‐Rad, cat. no. 162‐017777) and blocked for 1 h in 5% BSA containing 0.1% Tween‐20. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against CD63 (≈ 55 kDa) and β‐actin (loading control) for 1 h at room temperature, washed with Tris‐buffered saline with Tween‐20, and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (1:10 000, Abcam, ab6721) for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence with exposure times of 1–2 min. Band intensities were quantified using densitometric analysis using NIH ImageJ Gel Analyzer software (version 2010a). CD63 expression levels were normalized to β‐actin and compared across experimental groups. 24 , 25

Investigations of liver fibrosis (Masson's trichrome staining)

A portion of the right lobe of the animal's liver was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Four pathological sections, each 5 μm thick, were prepared from this tissue. Masson's trichrome staining was utilized to evaluate tissue changes, including the increase in collagen indicative of fibrotic changes such as liver fibrosis, as well as alterations in tissue thickness. In this procedure, Weigert's iron hematoxylin dye was initially applied to stain the nuclei. Subsequently, the cytoplasm was stained with a precise mixture of Acid Fuchsin and Ponceau 2R, both of which are acid dyes. Finally, collagen was selectively stained using aniline blue. These stained collagen fibers and other tissue components were then examined using a light microscope. After this, a pathologist assessed the histological changes in the liver tissue to analyze the extent of fibrosis. 26

Serum and liver tissue sample preparation

After the treatment procedure, the rats were euthanized using carbon dioxide. Blood samples were collected from all groups for serum biochemical analyses. A portion of the liver tissue was stored at −80°C for subsequent gene expression analysis. Another segment was homogenized for oxidant and antioxidant assessments, whereas an additional portion was fixed in 10% formalin for Masson's trichrome staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Measurements of biochemical factors

Biochemical factors, including hepatic enzymes such as aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), 27 plasma glucose (PG), triglycerides (TG), 28 uric acid, and cholesterol, were measured using the Selectra E autoanalyzer that measures with high diagnostic reliability.

Oxidant and antioxidant indices

Measurement of antioxidant indices (superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase)

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using the RANSOD diagnostic kit from RANDOX (Randox Laboratories Ltd., UK), with results reported in units of SOD per 10 mg of protein. Glutathione peroxidase (GPX) activity was assessed using the RANSEL diagnostic kit from RANDOX (Randox Laboratories Ltd), with results expressed in units of GPx per milligram of protein. 29

Determination of malondialdehyde levels

Malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration was quantified following the method established by Hiroshi Ohkawa, which involves its reaction with thiobarbituric acid to produce a colored complex. The optical density of the resultant chromophore was measured at 532 nm, utilizing a tetraethoxypropane‐derived standard curve for calibration. 30

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is a technique employed to detect and quantify specific cellular proteins. This method assesses protein expression by utilizing antibodies that are specially labeled to bind to the target proteins. In this experiment slides were placed in 1 × Tris‐buffered saline solution (T5912‐Sigma) in a microwave oven. After the boiling point was reached, the microwave was turned off, and the samples remained in the solution for 20 min. The samples were then washed with PBS (Sigma‐P4417) thrice, with a 5‐min interval between washes. Subsequently, 0.3% Triton was added for 30 min to permeabilize the cell membrane. The samples were washed again with PBS, and 10% goat serum was added for 45 min to block the secondary antibody reaction. The primary antibody, diluted (1:100) with PBS, was then applied to the samples. The samples were stored in a refrigerator between 2 and 8°C for 24 h to keep the tissue from drying out. After this incubation period, the tissue containers were taken out of the refrigerator and washed four times with PBS, each time for 5 min. After the diluted (1:150) secondary antibody was added, the samples were left in a dark incubator at 37°C for 1 h 30 min. After the samples were washed thrice in PBS, they were allowed to attain room temperature before being treated with DAPI (Sigma‐D9542). Twenty minutes later, the samples were washed once more with PBS. The samples were then treated with a glycerol and PBS solution, and coverslips were positioned for fluorescence imaging to validate the markers.

Gene expression analysis (real‐time polymerase chain reaction)

Gene expression analysis was performed using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) to quantify transcript levels with high precision. Total RNA was isolated from the samples of each treatment group following the manufacturer's protocol, using TRIzol reagent (Sigma, MI, USA), ensuring optimal yield and purity. RNA concentrations were assessed using NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was carried out using the Takara cDNA Kit, setting the stage for amplification. qPCR was conducted using SYBR Green fluorescence detection on the Corbett Rotorgene 3000 cycler system, enabling reliable quantification. GAPDH served as the reference housekeeping gene to normalize expression levels. Details of the genes and primers applied in this study are presented in Table 1. The expression of target genes was assessed using the 2−ΔCT method, with ΔCT calculated as ΔCT = CT (target) – reference gene (GAPDH). Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 8.0.2 (San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences among multiple experimental groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons post hoc test when a significant overall effect was detected. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Liver fibrosis assessment

Figure 3 shows the degree of fibrosis in different groups. In the olive oil group, liver architecture appeared normal with no evidence of fibrosis. In contrast, the fibrosis group (received CCl4) exhibited prominent fibrotic changes, particularly in the portal tracts, accompanied by significant inflammatory cell infiltration. The PBS group also exhibited significant fibrosis in the portal areas, with incomplete fibrous septa forming between hepatocytes.

Masson's trichrome staining.

Among the treatment groups, liver sections from the hepatocyte‐treated rats showed mild fibrosis, especially in the sinusoids involving the pericentral lobular regions. The stem cell group demonstrated a similar but slightly reduced degree of fibrosis, primarily localized to the pericentral lobules. Particularly, animals treated with hepatocyte‐derived exosomes exhibited only very thin septal fibrosis within the parenchyma, with no observable fibrosis in the portal tracts. Similarly, the DPSC‐derived exosome group exhibited minimal fibrotic changes, limited to thin septal strands within the parenchyma and absent in the portal regions. These findings suggest that exosome‐based therapies, particularly those derived from stem cells, exert superior antifibrotic effects compared to cellular treatments.

Characterization

Dynamic light scattering

DLS analysis (Figure 4A) revealed that the exosome sample showed a PDI (Polydispersity Index) of 0.3, indicating that the majority of exosomes fall within this size range.

Transmission electron microscopy

TEM analysis (Figure 4B) revealed the structural and morphological characteristics of exosomes, confirming their nanoscale vesicular nature. The observed exosomes exhibit a size range consistent with EVs (typically ~ 30–150 nm), which aligns with their role in intercellular communication and biomolecular transport. The uniform appearance of these vesicles indicates an optimal surface area for biomolecular interactions, which is critical for their therapeutic applications. The presence of homogeneous populations within the transmission electron microscopic image suggests minimal aggregation, ensuring their suitability for regenerative medicine.

Western blot

Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of the exosomal marker CD63 at ~ 55 kDa in both stem cell–derived and hepatocyte‐derived exosomes. Densitometric analysis revealed a significant increase in CD63 expression in the treated group compared with controls (normalized intensity = 1.85 ± 0.12 vs. 1.00 ± 0.08, p < 0.01). Overall, CD63 levels increased by about 85%, indicating effective experimental manipulation (Figure 4C).

DISCUSSION

Histological evaluation confirmed significant fibrosis in the portal tracts and the presence of incomplete fibrous septa in the fibrosis group compared with the olive oil group, aligning with previous findings that illustrate the hepatotoxic effects of CCl4. 31 , 32 CCl4 is a hepatotoxic compound that induces the death of hepatocytes, particularly in the centrilobular regions. It also initiates inflammatory responses, promotes the release of profibrotic mediators, and disrupts liver metabolism. Prolonged exposure to CCl4 can ultimately lead to liver fibrosis and progression to cirrhosis. 33

Treatment with hepatocytes attenuated liver fibrosis, and only mild sinusoidal fibrosis was observed, which could be attributed to the regenerative and antifibrotic properties of the hepatocytes. Stem cells also exhibited beneficial effects, reducing pericentral fibrosis, though to a lesser extent than hepatocytes, which is consistent with other studies on the effects of stem cells. 34 , 35 Particularly, the most pronounced therapeutic effects were observed in the exosome groups, corroborating the growing evidence on the efficacy of exosomes in liver regeneration. 36 , 37 Exosomes play an important role in the transmission of intercellular material and information. 36 As demonstrated by Dusabineza et al., 38 co‐transplantation of hepatocytes with HSCs into a healthy liver does not induce fibrosis but rather significantly enhances hepatocyte engraftment, likely by improving cell homing.

Our results demonstrated that the expressions levels of α‐SMA and desmin were significantly elevated in the CCl4‐treated group compared with the olive oil group, and these findings were further validated by IHC analyses. These observations are consistent with previous studies, which reported that CCl4 administration leads to HSC activation and fibrosis, as evidenced by increased α‐SMA and desmin expression. 39 , 40 Moreover, elevated α‐SMA levels have been proposed as a reliable and noninvasive biomarker for the early detection of liver fibrosis. 41 α‐SMA expression is widely recognized as a hallmark of activated HSCs and a key indicator of fibrogenic activity in liver tissue. 42 After liver injury, HSCs become activated and differentiate into myofibroblast‐like cells that proliferate and contribute to collagen accumulation in the ECM. 42 Desmin and α‐SMA are proteins that help support the structure of smooth muscle cells and some other types of cells. 43 These activated HSCs play a role in making more type I collagen, which occurs during liver scarring (fibrogenesis), and studies have shown that desmin plays a role in liver scarring, likely due to its early upregulation during the scarring process. 44 The results showed that treatment with hepatocytes and stem cells significantly decreased the expression of α‐SMA and desmin, indicating the therapeutic effects of both cell types in attenuating liver fibrosis. Hepatocytes contribute to liver regeneration by restoring hepatic function, whereas stem cells exert antifibrotic effects by modulating inflammation and inhibiting HSC activation.

Among the tested therapies, the exosome groups exhibited the most pronounced effects. Exosomes mediate their therapeutic benefits by delivering bioactive molecules, including microRNAs and proteins, which inhibit HSC activation and reduce ECM deposition. Additionally, they modulate immune responses and promote tissue repair and regeneration. 45 , 46 In a related study, exosomes significantly decreased the expression of key fibrogenic genes, including TGF‐β, α‐SMA, and collagen Iα, and prevented HSC activation through inhibition of Smad3 phosphorylation. 47 Exosomes transferred miR‐122 to HSCs, regulating miR‐122 target genes (IGF1R, CCNG1, and P4HA1) that are involved in HSC proliferation and collagen maturation. miR‐122 modification also enhanced therapeutic efficacy in CCl4‐induced liver fibrosis by inhibiting HSC activation and reducing collagen deposition. 48 EVs released from normal hepatocytes have been shown to reduce HSC activation, lower α‐SMA and CCN2 (Cellular Communication Network factor2) expression, and regulate the expression of more than 200 fibrosis‐related genes, collectively indicating that vesicle cargo can directly shape fibrogenic signaling pathways. 49 Human liver stem cell–derived EVs deliver miR‐146a 5p, which suppresses profibrotic transcripts in LX 2 cells, demonstrating a miRNA‐mediated antifibrotic mechanism. 7

The administration of CCl4 significantly increased the serum levels of AST and ALT, which is consistent with findings from previous studies. 50 These enzymes are well‐established biochemical markers for liver damage, with the AST‐to‐ALT ratio serving as a useful tool in diagnosing and evaluating various liver disorders, including hepatitis and cirrhosis. 27 The elevation of these enzymes may be attributed to CCl4‐induced hepatocellular degeneration, fatty changes, apoptosis, and necrosis. 51 However, treatment with hepatocytes and stem cells significantly reduced serum concentration of AST and ALT. Hepatocytes play a key role in liver regeneration by restoring essential metabolic and synthetic functions. 52 Together, these therapies can enhance liver repair, resulting in decreased serum levels of AST and ALT. Based on our results, among these therapies, stem cell–derived exosomes were the most effective in reducing enzyme levels. These results agree with previous studies demonstrating that both MSCs and MSC–derived exosomes alleviated AST and ALT levels in CCl4‐induced liver fibrosis. 53 , 54 Stem cell–derived exosomes exert their therapeutic effects by delivering microRNAs and proteins that suppress inflammation, inhibit HSC activation, and promote liver regeneration. Consequently, they help reduce hepatocyte damage, leading to lower serum levels of ALT and AST. Also, it has been reported that bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cell exosomes greatly reduced liver injury by lowering ALT and AST levels, and decreasing inflammation and cell death. This liver protection activity can be attributed to controlling NLRP3 (NOD‐like Receptor Family, Pyrin Domain Containing3) and caspase‐1 through miR‐223. 55

We found that the levels of glucose, uric acid, cholesterol, and TGs increased in the fibrosis group. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting the effects of CCl4 on blood metabolite profiles. 56 , 57 Liver diseases, such as liver fibrosis, are associated with disturbances in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism. 58 On the contrary, the results showed that treatment with stem cells and hepatocytes could significantly decrease the concentration of metabolites. The best performance was observed in exosomes. In line with these findings, previous studies have reported that MSC therapy ameliorates NAFLD progression in obese type 2 diabetic mice by reducing cholesterol and TG levels. 59 Similarly, Yang et al. 60 showed that MSC‐derived exosomes reduced cholesterol and TG levels. Also, exosomes can serve as potential therapeutic targets for liver fibrosis and show promise as drug carriers for its treatment. 61 They may play important roles in the crosstalk between hepatocytes and HSCs in the progression from simple steatosis to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis. 62

Serum cholesterol generally decreases in response to exosome‐mediated activation of the PPARγ/LXRα/ABCA1 pathway, especially in disease models characterized by lipid accumulation. 63 The PPARγ/LXRα/ABCA1 pathway coordinates lipid metabolism and fibrogenic responses by promoting cholesterol efflux and reverse cholesterol transport, thereby reducing intracellular lipid accumulation, suppressing HSC activation, and attenuating liver fibrosis.

The treatment groups effectively increased GPX activity and decreased MDA levels compared to the liver fibrosis group, confirming their antioxidant activity. Particularly, stem cells and exosomes increased SOD levels. MSCs and their derived exosomes have been shown to mitigate oxidative stress and enhance antioxidant defense mechanisms. 53 , 60 Exosomes deliver a cargo of antioxidant proteins (e.g., catalase, peroxiredoxin) and miRNAs (e.g., miR‐223, miR‐146a) that suppress NADPH‐oxidase expression and activate the Nrf2‐ARE pathway, thereby upregulating endogenous antioxidant genes such as GPX4, SOD1, and HO‐1. 64 This exosomal bioactive cargo also attenuates lipid‐peroxidation cascades, limiting ferroptotic cell death of HSCs and impeding their trans‐differentiation into myofibroblasts. 65 These actions promote liver tissue repair and the restoration of cellular homeostasis. In addition, antioxidants decrease liver damage, which in turn can lead to reductions in blood glucose and lipid levels.

Moreover, the results showed that the treatment with hepatocytes, stem cells, and especially exosomes significantly downregulated the expression of fibrosis and inflammation‐related genes, including TMSB10, TMEM176B, TGF‐β, PDGFRB, and MMP2. TGF‐β is a central profibrotic cytokine that activates HSCs and promotes ECM deposition. 66 MMP2 is involved in ECM remodeling and fibrotic tissue turnover. 67 TMSB10 regulates actin dynamics and may influence HSC activation. TMEM176B is associated with immune modulation and may contribute to chronic inflammation during fibrosis progression. The reduction observed in the expression of these gene reflects a suppression of HSC activation and modulation of inflammatory signaling pathways triggered by CCl4. Consequently, the downregulation of these fibrosis‐related genes indicates decreased fibrotic activity, attenuated ECM remodeling, and reduced hepatic inflammation.

Stem cell–derived exosomes are enriched in miRNAs (e.g., miR‐122, miR‐19b), cytokines, and lipid mediators that are transferred to recipient hepatic cells, where they reprogram transcriptional networks regulating fibrogenesis and inflammation. After uptake by HSCs, exosomal miRNAs inhibit SMAD‐dependent TGF‐β signaling by targeting SMAD2/3, thereby blocking the canonical profibrotic cascade responsible for ECM deposition. 68 Upon TGF‐β binding to its type II receptor, downstream phosphorylation of Smad2/3 occurs, followed by complex formation with Smad4 and nuclear translocation, where these Smad complexes promote transcription of desmin and α‐SMA genes. 69 Also TGF‐β can activate noncanonical MAPK (Mitogen‐Activated Protein Kinase) pathways (ERK (Extracellular Signal‐Regulated Kinase), JNK (c‐Jun N‐terminal Kinase), and p38), which further interact with or act independently of Smad signaling to enhance myofibroblast differentiation and ECM deposition. 70 In parallel exosomal cargo is associated with reduced PDGFRB expression, which may contribute to attenuation of PDGF‐mediated HSC proliferation and migration. 71 Matrix‐remodeling enzymes are also regulated through miR‐29‐enriched exosomes, which suppress MMP2 transcription and limit pathological ECM turnover while maintaining normal remodeling processes. 72 TMSB10 that regulates actin filament dynamics is silenced by exosomal miR‐223, thereby limiting cytoskeletal reorganization necessary for HSC activation. 68 The net effect of these molecular interventions is a pronounced suppression of HSC activation markers, lower collagen‐I synthesis, and diminished secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. MSC‐derived EVs have been shown to inhibit the PI3K/AKT and TGF‐β/Smad axes in HSCs, thereby reducing collagen deposition and promoting hepatocyte proliferation. 73

STUDY LIMITATIONS

This study has some limitations. The absence of multiple sequential observation time points limits the temporal resolution of therapeutic effects. Additionally, although different treatment groups were employed for comparison, future studies should incorporate detailed molecular pathway analyses specific to each exosome source. This includes the investigation of TGF‐β/Smad inhibition and miR‐34c/miR‐151‐3p/miR‐21 effects in MSC‐derived exosomes and autophagy activation and PI3K/AKT modulation in hepatocyte‐derived exosomes. A comprehensive evaluation of molecular mechanisms would strengthen future investigations in this field.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated that cell‐based therapies using hepatocytes, DPSCs, and especially their derived exosomes significantly improved liver structure and function in a rat model of CCl4‐induced liver fibrosis. Exosomes exhibited the most potent therapeutic effects, evidenced by significant reductions in fibrosis‐related markers (α‐SMA, desmin, TGF‐β, PDGFRB, MMP2, TMSB10, TMEM176b), serum liver enzymes (ALT, AST), and metabolic disturbances (glucose, cholesterol, TGs, and uric acid) and modulating oxidative stress markers (MDA, SOD, GPX). These effects are likely due to the ability of exosomes to deliver bioactive molecules that suppress inflammation, inhibit HSC activation, and promote tissue repair and antioxidant defense. In conclusion, these findings suggest that exosomes, as a noncellular therapeutic modality, may confer regenerative, anti‐inflammatory, and antioxidant effects, indicating potential preventive and protective properties.
