Patients with primary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis with detectable urinary CD80 are more similar to patients with minimal change disease in clinicopathological features
Authors: Xiaojie Gong, Jing Huang, Yimiao Zhang, Fang Wang, Xin Wang, Liqiang Meng, Xuyang Cheng, Gang Liu, Zhao Cui, Minghui Zhao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFocal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is an important cause of refractory nephrotic syndrome (NS) in children and adults. Urinary CD80 is elevated in some patients with primary FSGS, however, its clinical value is not fully clarified. This study aims to evaluate the clinical and pathological significance of urinary CD80 in patients with primary FSGS.MethodsSixty-one adult patients with biopsy-proven primary FSGS, with standard treatment and long-term follow up, were enrolled retrospectively. Urinary CD80, on the day of kidney biopsy, was measured using commercial ELISA kits and adjusted by urinary creatinine excretion. Their associations with clinical and pathological parameters were investigated.ResultsUrinary CD80 was detectable in 30/61 (49.2%) patients, who presented with a higher level of proteinuria (10.7 vs. 5.8 g/24h; p = 0.01), a lower level of serum albumin (19.3 ± 3.9 vs. 24.2 ± 8.2 g/L; p = 0.005), a higher prevalence of hematuria (70.0 vs. 38.7%; p = 0.01), and showed a lower percentage of segmental glomerulosclerosis lesion [4.8 (3.7–14.0) vs. 9.1 (5.6–21.1) %; p = 0.06]. The cumulative relapse rate was remarkably high in these patients (log-rank, p = 0.001). Multivariate analysis identified that the elevated urinary CD80 was an independent risk factor for steroid-dependent NS (OR 8.81, 95% CI 1.41–54.89; p = 0.02) and relapse (HR, 2.87; 95% CI 1.29–6.38; p = 0.01).ConclusionsThe elevated urinary CD80 is associated with mild pathological change and steroid-dependent cases of primary FSGS adults, which indicates these patients are more similar to minimal change disease (MCD) in clinicopathological features.Keywords: Urinary CD80, primary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, severe clinical manifestations, mild pathological change, steroid-dependent

Introduction

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is an important cause of refractory nephrotic syndrome in children and adults, and often progresses to the end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [1]. Approximately 80% of FSGS cases cannot be identified as a definite etiology and are defined as primary FSGS [2]. Corticosteroid is the first-line treatment to induce remission of proteinuria, but in adults about 52% of the partial remission patients and 36% of the complete remission patients have a relapse during follow-up [3]. About 40–60% of the primary FSGS patients present as steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome [4]. The mechanisms of these refractory cases remain unknown and a good indicator is needed to identify them.

Podocyte damage is the hallmark of FSGS. CD80 (also known as B7-1) was identified as a novel biomarker of podocyte damage [5]. It is a transmembrane protein expressed by antigen-presenting cells. After the first signal by major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-peptide-T cell receptor (TCR), CD80 binds to its receptors on T cell to provide the second signal to constitute a co-stimulatory–co-inhibitory system involved in regulating the immune response [6]. The binding of CD80 with CD28 leads to T cell activation, expansion, differentiation and survival, whereas CD80 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 (CTLA4) interaction inhibits T cell activation [6]. CD80 has a higher affinity for CTLA-4 than for CD28, whose interaction can be blocked by CTLA-Ig, like abatacept, and belatacept. Yu et al. [7] found that podocyte CD80 upregulation could block the activation of podocyte α3β1 integrin by interfering with its physiological binding to talin, resulting in morphological and functional changes in the podocyte. This phenotype was reversed by abatacept treatment in vitro. Five patients with primary or recurrent FSGS received abatacept and got partial remission, in addition to other immunosuppressive treatments.

However, numerous studies have reported negative staining for CD80 expression on podocytes, as well as the therapeutic potential of CD80 blockade remains controversial [8–13]. Research has found that the level of urinary CD80 is higher in patients with minimal change disease (MCD) and is associated with the severity of proteinuria and its recurrence [14–17]. Ling C, et al. reported that the higher level of urinary CD80 was a good indicator for the steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome in pediatric patients [16].

In this study, we assessed the urinary excretion of CD80 in patients with primary FSGS, and analyzed its associations with the clinical and pathologic features, therapeutic response and kidney outcomes, with the aim to investigate the clinical value of urinary CD80 in adult FSGS patients.

Materials and methods

Patients

We performed a retrospective single-centre study in primary FSGS patients. We included all eligible primary FSGS patients from January 2012 to November 2020 (n = 61), the age- and gender-matched MCD patients as disease controls (n = 24), and healthy donors (n = 22).

Inclusion criteria: kidney biopsy-proven primary FSGS according to the definition of primary FSGS in the Columbia classification; the patients were regularly followed up in our hospital. MCD was defined according to the Heptinstall’s Pathology of the Kidney [18].

Exclusion criteria: follow-up time <6 months; coexisting with malignant tumors; pregnancy or lactation; familial abnormality or secondary causes, such as obesity, virus infections, and drug-related FSGS; coexisting with other kidney diseases, such as IgA nephropathy, diabetic nephropathy, mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis, and so on.

Clinical data were collected at the time of diagnosis as well as during follow-up. A study flow diagram was drawn to summarize the study procedure (Figure 1).

Work flowchart.

The research followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant for sample collection.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated with the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [19].

Renal histopathology

Kidney biopsy was performed at the time of diagnosis. Renal specimens were evaluated with direct immunofluorescence, light microscopy, and electron microscopy, as described previously [20,21].

For direct immunofluorescence, IgG, IgM, IgA, C3c, C1q, fibrinogen, and albumin were detected by fluorescein isothiocyanate–conjugated antibodies (Dako, Copenhagen, Denmark) on frozen tissues. The fluorescence intensity of IgG, IgM, IgA, C3, and C1q was determined using a semi-quantitative scale from 0 to 4+: 0, negative; 1+, weak staining; 2+, moderate staining; 3+, strong staining; 4+, glaring staining.

For light microscopy, paraffin sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, periodic acid–Schiff, periodic acid-silver methenamine, and Masson’s trichrome. The kidney pathologic variants of FSGS were defined according to the Columbia classification [20].

For electron microscopy, the tissue was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide, then dehydrated in graded acetone, and embedded in Epon 812 (Emicron, Shanghai, China). Ultrathin sections were cut at a thickness of 80 nm, placed on nickel grids, stained with uranyl acetate and examined by a transmission electron microscope JEM-1230 (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Sample collection

Spot urine samples of patients were collected on the morning of kidney biopsy. The urine samples from 22 age- and gender-matched healthy donors were collected as normal controls. The urine was centrifuged immediately after collection, at 2500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and was stored in aliquots at −80 °C until use. Repeated freeze/thaw cycles were avoided. Urine samples from the patients with follow up were also collected and stored until use. At the time of testing, urine samples were thawed at 4 °C. If having precipitates, the samples would be solubilized by incubation at 37 °C for 10 min, with a slight vortex. Finally, the samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm at room temperature for 10 min and the supernatant was used for testing.

Urinary CD80 measurement

We measured the concentration of urinary CD80 using commercial ELISA kits (Bender MedSystems, eBioscience, Vienna, Austria). The principles of the test included four steps: (1) 96-well microassay plates were precoated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-human sCD80 monoclonal coating antibody; (2) undiluted urine samples were added to each well and incubated for 3 h at room temperature; (3) after incubation and washing, tetramethyl-benzidine substrate solution was added to each well and incubated for 10 min at room temperature while protecting from light; and (4) stop solution was added and then the absorbance was recorded using an ELISA reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Philadelphia, PA, USA) at 450/630 nm. The CD80 level of each sample was calculated using Curve Expert 1.3 (Curve Expert Software, Chattanooga, TN).

Treatment and response

The patients received treatments according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines for GN [22].

For evaluation of therapeutic responses, complete remission was defined as urinary protein excretion <0.3 g/24 h with stable serum creatinine. Partial remission was defined as urinary protein excretion <3.5 g/24 h, but >0.3 g/24 h and a decrease >50% from peak values with stable serum creatinine (≤25% increase from baseline). Treatment failure was defined as not reaching the criteria of complete or partial remission. Relapse was defined as urinary protein excretion >3.5 g/24h after remission. Steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome (SDNS) was defined as the relapses within two weeks after steroid dosage reduction or withdrawal. Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) was defined as the persistence of proteinuria >3.5 g/day with a < 50% reduction from baseline despite steroid treatments for 16 weeks.

For evaluation of kidney outcomes, the primary endpoint was ESKD, which was defined as the dependence on kidney replacement therapy; the secondary endpoint was kidney dysfunction, which was defined as the eGFR decreasing >50% from the baseline (at the time of kidney biopsy). Kidney survival time was the time point that the patients reached the end point or the last visit if the patients did not reach the endpoint during the entire follow-up period.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using statistical software SPSS 23.0 (IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL). Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as means ± SD, while variables with non-normal distribution were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and proportions. Differences between continuous variables were assessed using t-tests for data that were normally distributed and nonparametric tests for data that were not normally distributed. Differences in semi-quantitative data were tested using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparison between qualitative data were analyzed using χ2 or Fisher exact test. The logistic regression model was used to find the risk factors for SDNS in the patients with primary FSGS. The results were expressed as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Kaplan-Meier curves were used to analyze the cumulative relapse rate. Statistically significant parameters were enrolled into the Multivariate Cox regression models. Results were expressed as hazard ratio (HR) along with 95% CI. All p values were two-tailed and a p value <0.05 was considered as significant.

Results

Demographic and clinicopathological data

In total, 61 consecutive patients with biopsy-proven primary FSGS were enrolled (Table 1.). The median follow-up time was 42.3 (16.0–62.0) months. The average age was 45 ± 20 years old. Thirty-eight patients were male and 23 were female. They presented with proteinuria of 7.4 (4.3–15.0) g/24 h, including 52 (85.2%) patients with nephrotic syndrome and 33 (54.1%) patients with hematuria. The serum albumin was 21.8 ± 6.9 g/L and the serum creatinine was 96.2 (73.6–140.2) μmol/L at the time of kidney biopsy.

The urinary CD80 levels in primary FSGS and controls

We detected urinary CD80 levels by ELISA kits in the 61 patients with primary FSGS, 24 patients with MCD and 22 healthy donors. The CD80 values were normalized to urine creatinine concentration (ng/g).

The urinary CD80 levels could be detected (> 0 ng/g creatinine) in 49.2% (30/61) of the patients with primary FSGS, 79.2% (19/24) of MCD and 18.2% (4/22) of healthy controls (p < 0.001). The urinary CD80 levels of primary FSGS were significantly higher than those of healthy controls [141.2 (74.7–342.6) vs. 18.0 (10.5-64.4) ng/g; p = 0.02]. There was no difference in the urinary CD80 levels between the groups of primary FSGS and MCD [141.2 (74.7–342.6) vs. 120.2 (79.4–201.2) ng/g; p = 0.63] (Figure 2).

The concentration of urinary CD80 in the patients with primary FSGS, MCD and healthy controls. The urinary CD80 levels of primary FSGS were significantly higher than those of healthy controls (p = 0.02). There was no difference on urinary CD80 levels between primary FSGS and MCD (p = 0.63).

The association between urinary CD80 levels and clinical parameters

In FSGS patients, the clinical and pathological data were compared between the patients with detectable CD80 and those without (Table 1). The patients with detectable urinary CD80 presented with a higher percentage of nephrotic syndrome (96.7 vs. 74.2%; p = 0.03) and hematuria (70.0 vs. 38.7%; p = 0.01), a higher level of urinary protein [10.7 (5.9–18.5) vs. 5.8 (2.8–14.2) g/24 h; p = 0.01], a higher level of cholesterol (11.2 ± 4.2 vs. 8.3 ± 3.7 mmol/L; p = 0.01), a higher level of LDL [6.7 (5.1–8.5) vs. 4.6 (2.7–6.9) mmol/L; p = 0.03] and a lower level of serum albumin (19.3 ± 3.9 vs. 24.2 ± 8.2 g/L; p = 0.005).

The concentration of urinary CD80 was positively correlated with the urinary protein in the 30 patients with detectable urinary CD80 (r = 0.59; p = 0.001) (Figure 3(A)).

The association between urinary CD80 levels and pathologic parameters

The pathological variants of the 30 patients with detectable urinary CD80 included 15 tip variants, 13 NOS variants, and 2 cellular variants. In the patients without detectable urinary CD80, the pathological variates included 9 tip variants, 18 NOS variants, 2 cellular variants, 1 perihilar variant and 1 collapsing variant. There was no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.32).

The patients with detectable urinary CD80 showed a lower percentage of glomeruli with sclerosis lesion [11.3 (6.0–21.6) vs. 21.4 (6.7–42.3) %; p = 0.09] and segmental glomerulosclerosis lesion [4.8 (3.7–14.0) vs. 9.1 (5.6–21.1) %; p = 0.06], although not statistically significant (Table 1).

The association between urinary CD80 levels and treatment responses

The patients with or without detectable urinary CD80 received comparable treatments (p = 0.24). In the patients with detectable urinary CD80, there were 18/30 (60.0%) patients achieved remission, including 10 (33.3%) patients with complete remission and 8 (26.7%) patients with partial remission. This remission rate was lower than that in the patients without detectable urinary CD80 (60.0 vs. 83.9%; p = 0.04) (Table 1.).

In the 18 remission patients, 12 (66.7%) patients underwent relapse during follow-up, which was significantly higher than that of the patients without detectable urinary CD80 (7/26, 26.9%; p = 0.01) (Table 1.). Kaplan-Meier curve revealed that the cumulative recurrence was significantly higher in the patients with detectable urinary CD80 (p = 0.001) (Figure 4.). The multivariate Cox regression analysis identified the urinary CD80 as an independent risk factor for relapse after remission (HR, 2.87; 95% CI, 1.29–6.38; p = 0.01) (Table 2).

The association between urinary CD80 levels and kidney outcomes

During follow-up, four of the 30 (13.3%) patients with detectable urinary CD80 progressed to ESKD and six (20.0%) patients experienced kidney dysfunction with an over 50% decline of eGFR from the baseline, which were comparable to those of the patients without detectable urinary CD80 (Table 1).

Discussion

In the current study, we found that the urinary CD80 could be detected in 49.2% of adult patients with primary FSGS. These patients presented with a higher level of urinary protein and a lower level of serum albumin. Meanwhile, the relapse rate was higher in the remission patients. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified the elevated urinary CD80 as an independent risk factor for SDNS. This is the first study, to our best knowledge, that investigated the clinical value of urinary CD80 in adult patients with primary FSGS with long-term follow-up of treatment responses and kidney outcomes. These results indicate that primary FSGS patients with detectable urinary CD80 are more similar to patients with MCD in clinicopathological features, and the urinary CD80 may be a useful indicator for SDNS and a potential therapeutic target.

We found that the elevated level of urinary CD80 was associated with disease severity of NS. This is consistent with the previous studies on MCD and FSGS, mostly in pediatrics [14–17,23–25]. Garin et al. [15] found that the urinary concentrations of soluble CD80 in patients with relapsed MCD were significantly higher compared with those in remission. In contrast, serum CD80 did not distinguish patients with MCD in relapse and remission. Recently, Gonzalez Guerrico et al. [17] found that urinary CD80/creatinine values were higher in paired relapse versus remission cases of MCD and FSGS in a large cohort with many adult patients. Here in our adult patients with primary FSGS, which is thought to be similar to MCD on the podocyte pathogenesis, we validated the association to disease activity of urinary CD80 concentrations.

There are about one third of the primary FSGS patients as refractory cases of SDNS, but we lack a good indicator to identify them earlier, for example at the time of kidney biopsy. Here with a 3.5-years follow-up of therapeutic responses, we found that urinary CD80 was an independent risk factor for SDNS, in adult patients with primary FSGS. Some studies [15–17] have reported, in pediatric NS, the patients with higher levels of urinary CD80 were more sensitive to the initial steroid treatment than those with lower levels, which was consistent with the current findings, but they did not observe the treatment responses in a long-term follow-up [15,17]. In a Chinese pediatric cohort with primary NS, there were 32 patients with MCD and 3 with FSGS in the high-CD80 group, while there were 5 patients with MCD and 24 with FSGS in the low-CD80 group [16]. The disease distribution bias prohibits the application of this result on adult FSGS patients. In the current cohort, all the 61 patients with biopsy-proven primary FSGS were visited regularly and treated in our hospital according to KDIGO guideline. This could reduce, but not eliminate, the bias on statistical analysis. Under such circumstances, we found that the high level of urinary CD80 at biopsy was a risk factor for SDNS cases. This finding may facilitate to identify the high-risk patients at an early stage, while it needs validation in other large cohorts of primary FSGS with long-term follow-up.

The mechanisms underline the relationship of urinary CD80 to the clinical features of MCD and FSGS is unclear and controversial [6]. In the immune system, by binding to different ligands (CD28 or CTLA-4), CD80 could affect the balance of Th1 and Th2 cell subsets in vivo. This imbalance may be associated with steroid response in NS [26–28]. The CD80-CD28 co-stimulation pathway promotes the production of antibodies by T cell-dependent B cell activation. There are studies reporting that soluble CD80 is elevated in plasma of SLE patients, with high expression of CD80 in CD4+ T cells [29]. In clinical trials, a subgroup of abatacept treated lupus nephritis patients experienced a 20–30% greater reduction in uPCR compared with placebo [30]. In experimental rats of anti-GBM disease, the treatment with CTLA-4 Ig showed the reduction of autoantibody and the amelioration of clinicopathological manifestations [31]. In the kidney, CD80 may be expressed on podocytes in situ following glomerular injury caused by LPS [32]. CD80 blocks the integrin activation by disrupting the binding of talin to β1 integrin, reduces the podocyte migration and causes proteinuria [7]. There is a prospective, randomized clinical trial evaluating abatacept in patients with treatment-resistant nephrotic syndrome caused by FSGS or MCD, but the final results have not been reported yet [33]. However, other studies failed to demonstrate the benefit from it in FSGS [24,34]. Maybe the elevated urinary CD80 could be a potential biomarker to select a subgroup of FSGS patients for further trial designs.

We did not observe the difference in urinary CD80 levels between the patients with MCD and those with primary FSGS. This result is inconsistent with those from children in whom the level of urinary CD80 is much higher in MCD [15,16]. The current concentration of CD80/creatinine in MCD (120 ng/g) is comparable with that of European American MCD patients with proteinuria ≥ 2 g/g (139 ng/g) [17], while CD80 is much higher in our primary FSGS patients (141 ng/g) than in all FSGS patients of primary, genetic, and secondary reasons (66 ng/g) [17]. Gonzalez Guerrico et al. [17] have found that urinary CD80 is much higher in primary FSGS patients than that in secondary FSGS. The restriction of primary FSGS in our study may contribute to their high levels of urinary CD80, making them comparable to those in MCD. In addition, the few cases of FSGS limited assessment of values with the MCD cases.

There are several limitations in our study. First, we excluded secondary FSGS and without detection of genetic variants information for the SRNS patients, which may influence the data on CD80 in this subgroup, although Gonzalez Guerrico et al. [17] have found that the urinary CD80 concentration is comparable between the primary and genetic deficient FSGS. Second, we detected urinary CD80 in only two patients with sequential samples and could not determine the change of CD80 according to disease course. Third, this was a retrospective study. And the patients in our study were all from Chinese adult patients, thus the results may not be generalized to other ethnic cohorts or the FSGS in children.

In conclusion, the elevated level of urinary CD80 at kidney biopsy is associated with mild pathological change and steroid-dependent cases of adult patients with primary FSGS. This indicator indicates these patients are more similar to MCD in clinicopathological features. It might serve as a biomarker to alert these patients earlier and improve treatment monitoring and decision.
