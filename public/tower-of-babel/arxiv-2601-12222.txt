Song Aesthetics Evaluation with Multi-Stem Attention and Hierarchical Uncertainty Modeling
Yishan Lv, Jing Luo, Boyuan Ju, Yang Zhang, Xinda Wu, Bo Yuan, Xinyu Yang

Abstract
Music generative artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly expanding music content, necessitating automated song aesthetics evaluation. However, existing studies largely focus on speech, audio or singing quality, leaving song aesthetics underexplored. Moreover, conventional approaches often predict a precise Mean Opinion Score (MOS) value directly, which struggles to capture the nuances of human perception in song aesthetics evaluation. This paper proposes a song-oriented aesthetics evaluation framework, featuring two novel modules: 1) Multi-Stem Attention Fusion (MSAF) builds bidirectional cross-attention between mixture-vocal and mixture-accompaniment pairs, fusing them to capture complex musical features; 2) Hierarchical Granularity-Aware Interval Aggregation (HiGIA) learns multi-granularity score probability distributions, aggregates them into a score interval, and applies a regression within the interval to produce the final score. We evaluated on two datasets of full-length songs: SongEval dataset (AI-generated) and an internal aesthetics dataset (human-created), and compared with two state-of-the-art (SOTA) models. Results show that the proposed method achieves stronger performance for multi-dimensional song aesthetics evaluation.

Introduction

The advent of music generative AI is rapidly increasing the volume of music releases, making manual quality screening and manual aesthetics evaluation impractical. This highlights the urgency for automated song aesthetics evaluation. Such systems could provide valuable feedback to guide and enhance the music quality.

In speech quality assessment, NISQA
[
1
]
introduces self-attention into a CNN-based architecture for multi-dimensional assessment;
LDNet
[
2
]
models listener dependence to stabilize predictions;
and UTMOS
[
3
]
leverages self-supervised learning with model ensembling to enhance stability.
In singing quality assessment,
PS-SQA
[
4
]
leverages pitch and spectrum-aware predictors to enhance performance;
and Ju et al.
[
5
]
use cross-attention with data augmentation for singing skill evaluation.
In music-related evaluation,
MusicEval
[
6
]
provides a benchmark for the evaluation of short, accompaniment-only music clips and serves as the dataset for Track 1 of the AudioMOS Challenge 2025
[
7
]
;
MMMOS
[
8
]
evaluates clip-level text-to-speech, audio, and music;
Audiobox
[
9
]
benchmarks multi-domain audio aesthetics;
and SongEval
[
10
]
provides a benchmark dataset with baselines for song aesthetics.

However, these studies mostly do not target full-length song aesthetics evaluation. Simply transferring speech or singing frameworks to the task of song aesthetics evaluation yields limited effectiveness for two main reasons:
1) Compared to speech, songs present a more complex structure, consisting of interwoven vocal and accompaniment stems. Compared to singing, a song’s aesthetic evaluation also depends on arrangement, melodic structure and production, instead of just the vocal performance
[
11
]
;
2) Traditional approaches predict a precise MOS directly. In contrast, human experts follow a coarse-to-fine process: they first estimate an approximate score interval under subjective uncertainty and only then decide a precise score. This mismatch contradicts the human strategy, compromising stability and accuracy.

To overcome these two challenges of musical complexity and evaluation uncertainty, this paper proposes a song-oriented aesthetics evaluation framework, featuring two novel modules:
1) Multi-Stem Attention Fusion (MSAF) utilizes bidirectional cross-attention between the mixture and the vocal and accompaniment stems, and then fuses the three representations to model the complex interplay across stems and to capture richer musical features.
2) Hierarchical Granularity-Aware Interval Aggregation (HiGIA) trains three hierarchical classifiers to obtain multi-granularity score distributions, aggregates high-confidence bins into a consensus interval, and then performs regression within the interval to produce the final score. This coarse-to-fine process achieves more stable and accurate predictions.
To evaluate the proposed model, we conducted experiments on two datasets of full-length songs: 1) the SongEval dataset (mostly AI-generated) and 2) an internal aesthetics dataset (mostly human-created). Our framework outperforms two strong baselines across all four standard metrics. We also conducted ablation studies to analyze the effectiveness of MSAF and HiGIA.

Method

2.1
Model Architecture

We first apply music source separation to the input full-length song (a mixture of vocals and accompaniment) to obtain the vocal and accompaniment stems and then feed them into the pretrained MuQ
[
12
]
encoder to extract features. We employ a CBAM-based
[
13
]
attention-weighted sum over the multi-layer hidden outputs of MuQ to obtain a single embedding per stem. The resulting features are then passed through Transformer encoders to produce stem representations, which are fused by the Multi-Stem Attention Fusion. Finally, the Hierarchical Granularity-Aware Interval Aggregation processes this representation to yield the multi-dimensional aesthetic score predictions. Figure
1
illustrates the model architecture.

2.2
Multi-Stem Attention Fusion

The feature representations for the mixture, vocal stem, and accompaniment stem, output by Transformer encoders, are denoted as
X_{mix},X_{voc},X_{acc}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
, where
T
is the number of frames, and
d
is the feature dimension.

Based on the scaled dot-product attention
[
14
]
, we adopt bidirectional cross-attention
[
15
]
to efficiently compute a shared similarity matrix between the mixture and each of the vocal and accompaniment stems.

S_{\text{mv}}=\frac{Q_{\text{mix}}K_{\text{voc}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}=S_{\text{vm}}^{\top},\quad S_{\text{ma}}=\frac{Q_{\text{mix}}K_{\text{acc}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}=S_{\text{am}}^{\top}

(1)

Where
Q_{\text{mix}},K_{\text{voc}},K_{\text{acc}}
are obtained by applying separate learnable linear projections to
X_{mix},X_{voc},X_{acc}
, respectively.

From this shared similarity matrix, we obtain attention scores
{Attn}_{\text{mix}}^{\text{s}}
and
{Attn}_{\text{s}}^{\text{mix}}
, where the stem
s\in\{\text{voc},\text{acc}\}
. For example, the computation for the mixture-vocal pair is given by:

\begin{split}\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{mix}}^{\text{voc}}&=\operatorname{softmax}(S_{\text{mv}})V_{\text{voc}},\\
\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{voc}}^{\text{mix}}&=\operatorname{softmax}(S_{\text{vm}})V_{\text{mix}}\end{split}

(2)

Where
V_{\text{voc}},V_{\text{mix}}
are obtained by applying separate learnable linear projections to
X_{voc},X_{mix}
, respectively.

Finally, residual connections yield the outputs for the MSAF.
Figure
2
illustrates the process.

\begin{gathered}\text{Out}_{\text{mix}}=\text{X}_{\text{mix}}+\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{mix}}^{\text{voc}}+\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{mix}}^{\text{acc}},\\
\begin{aligned} \text{Out}_{\text{voc}}=\text{X}_{\text{voc}}+\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{voc}}^{\text{mix}},\quad\text{Out}_{\text{acc}}=\text{X}_{\text{acc}}+\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{acc}}^{\text{mix}}.\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

(3)

Multi-Stem Attention Fusion

2.3
Hierarchical Granularity-Aware Interval Aggregation

For each aesthetic dimension of a song, we set up three classifiers at different granularities,
\{C_{g_{1}},C_{g_{2}},C_{g_{3}}\}
, corresponding to coarse(
g_{1}
), medium(
g_{2}
), and fine(
g_{3}
) levels. At a given granularity
g
, the classifier
C_{g}
discretizes the continuous score into
K_{g}
score bins, denoted as
c_{g,1},...,c_{g,K_{g}}
. Each score bin
c_{g,k}
maps to a specific score interval
r(c_{g,k})=[l(c_{g,k}),u(c_{g,k})]
. Finer granularities utilize more bins, i.e.,
K_{g_{1}}<K_{g_{2}}<K_{g_{3}}
, thereby producing tighter intervals.
For instance, on a 100-point scale we can set
K_{g_{1}}=3
with 33-point bins
[0,33),[33,66),[66,100)
;
K_{g_{2}}=5
with 20-point bins;
K_{g_{3}}=10
with 10-point bins.

We apply softmax to the output logits
z^{(g)}
of each classifier
C_{g}
to obtain the posterior probability distribution, from which we then select a set
S
of candidate score bins.

\displaystyle p^{(g)}=\operatorname{softmax}(z^{(g)}),\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K_{g}}p_{k}^{(g)}=1

(4)

\displaystyle S=\{c_{g,k}\mid p_{k}^{(g)}>1/K_{g}\}

(5)

We select the score bins that have overlapping intervals to form an overlap set
O
. The complement of this set is then taken to form the isolated set
I=S\setminus O
.

O=\{c\in S\mid\exists c^{\prime}\in S,c^{\prime}\neq c,r(c)\cap r(c^{\prime})\neq\varnothing\}

(6)

When the overlap set
O
is larger than the isolated set
I
(i.e.,
|O|>|I|
), we obtain the score interval
[L,U]
by aggregating the lower/upper bounds over
O

[
16
]
.

L=\frac{1}{|O|}\sum_{c\in O}l(c),\quad U=\frac{1}{|O|}\sum_{c\in O}u(c)

(7)

Otherwise, (i.e.,
|O|\leq|I|
), indicating more disagreement among the classifiers, we adopt a more conservative aggregation of
O
and
I
using weights
w_{O}={|O|}/{|S|}
and
w_{I}=1-w_{O}
.

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=w_{O}(\frac{1}{|O|}\sum_{c\in O}l(c))+w_{I}\min_{c\in I}l(c)

(8)

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=w_{O}(\frac{1}{|O|}\sum_{c\in O}u(c))+w_{I}\max_{c\in I}u(c)

(9)

Given
[L,U]
, an MLP regressor is employed to predict an interpolation coefficient
\alpha\in[0,1]
, which determines the final aesthetic score. The entire coarse-to-fine process is illustrated in Figure
3
.

\hat{y}=(1-\alpha)\cdot L+\alpha\cdot U

(10)

EXPERIMENT

3.1
Dataset

We conduct experiments on two datasets: 1)
SongEval open-source aesthetics evaluation dataset

[
10
]
, containing 2,399 full-length songs in English and Chinese, mostly AI-generated, each annotated on five dimensions:
Musicality
,
Coherence
,
Memorability
,
Clarity
and
Naturalness
, on a 5-point scale. 2)
Internal aesthetics evaluation dataset
, containing 1,569 full-length Chinese songs, mostly human-created. The annotations were completed by four music experts across five aesthetic dimensions, each scored on a 100-point scale:
Overall
: overall musical quality;
Singing
: timbre and vocal technique in conveying emotion;
Melody
: integrity, flow and memorability of the melodic line;
Arrangement
: instrumental coordination and textural layering;
Audio Quality
: clarity, vocal-accompaniment balance, and stereo imaging of the song.

Baseline Models

To validate the advantages of the proposed framework, we select two models that demonstrated the strongest performance on SongEval benchmark for comparison:

SSL-based
: An SSL-based model following the design of
[
17
]
, adapted for song aesthetics by replacing the input features with those extracted from MuQ.
UTMOS-based
: Based on the UTMOS framework
[
3
]
, an ensemble learning system that won the VoiceMOS 2022 Challenge
[
18
]
.

3.3
Implementation Details

We split each song into overlapping 10-second segments with a 5-second hop and aggregate segment-level predictions to obtain the song-level aesthetic score.

At inference time, after the HiGIA produces a score interval
[L_{i},U_{i}]
and an interpolation coefficient
\alpha_{i}
for each segment
i
, we define a confidence weight as
w_{i}=1/(U_{i}-L_{i})
. The final aesthetic score is then obtained as weighted averages over segments.

\hat{y}_{\text{song}}=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}[(1-\alpha_{i})L_{i}+\alpha_{i}U_{i}]}{\sum_{i}w_{i}}

(11)

The data is split into train, validation, and test sets with an 8:1:1 ratio. The target scores for both datasets are linearly scaled to the [0, 1] range for training and inference. All audio is processed at 24 kHz. We use MDX-Net
[
19
]
as the source separation model and 3-layer Transformer encoders. For HiGIA, the number of score bins is set to
(K_{g_{1}},K_{g_{2}},K_{g_{3}})=(2,4,8)
for SongEval dataset and
(3,5,9)
for internal dataset. We train with a batch size of 32 using Adam (learning rate
5\times 10^{-4}
,
\epsilon=10^{-8},\beta_{1}=0.9,\beta_{2}=0.999
).

3.4
Evaluation Metrics

To quantify model performance, we use four widely used metrics in prior work
[
20
,
10
,
6
]
to measure the prediction accuracy and rank consistency between the model’s predictions and the human-annotated ground truth scores:
Mean Squared Error (MSE)
: Measures the average squared difference between scores. Lower values indicate better accuracy.
Linear Correlation Coefficient (LCC)
: Measures the degree of linear correlation. Higher values indicate better trend alignment.
Spearman Rank Correlation
Coefficient (SRCC)
: Evaluates the consistency between rankings. Higher values indicate better ranking ability.
Kendall’s Tau Rank Correlation (KTAU)
: Also measures rank correlation, but is often more effective in handling tied ranks. Higher values indicate a stronger ranking ability.

CONCLUSION

We propose a song aesthetics evaluation framework that overcomes limitations of prior work. It learns complex musical features by fusing information across the mixture, vocal stem, and accompaniment stem via a Multi-Stem Attention Fusion module, and then utilizes Hierarchical Granularity-Aware Interval Aggregation that mirrors experts’ coarse-to-fine decisions to achieve more stable and accurate predictions. Experiments on two diverse datasets covering AI-generated and human-created full-length songs show superior performance across multiple aesthetic dimensions compared with strong SOTA baselines.
