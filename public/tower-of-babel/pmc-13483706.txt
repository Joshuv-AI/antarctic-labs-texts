Esculentoside A suppresses the malignant progression of hepatocellular carcinoma by promoting RORB
Authors: Qing Ye, Dousheng Bai, Aoqing Wang, Xi Zhang, Ruiqi Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a highly aggressive disease associated with a poor prognosis. The present study attempts to discover the potential of esculentoside A (EsA) in inhibiting HCC progression. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 assays. At the same time, stemness and malignant behaviors were evaluated through sphere formation, Western blot (WB), flow cytometry, colony formation assays, and TUNEL. EsA treatment suppressed HCC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and stemness in vitro and tumor growth in vivo. Bioinformatics identified retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor beta (RORB) as a potential target of EsA, and EsA induced RORB expression in HCC cells. EsA also inhibited Wnt signaling activity, as shown by TOP/FOP Flash assays and WB. Knockdown of RORB through lentivirus infection reversed the effects of EsA. At the same time, Wnt pathway inhibitor rescued the promotive role of RORB knockdown in HCC malignant behaviors, suggesting that EsA inhibited HCC progression via the RORB/Wnt axis. RORB expression was reduced and associated with higher Wnt signaling in tumor tissues from patients with HCC, and low RORB expression showed significant correlation with higher TNM staging in HCC patients. This study offers novel insights into the molecular mechanism of EsA in HCC.Keywords: Esculentoside A, Retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor beta, Wnt, Cancer cell stemness, Hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common and lethal malignancies in the world, representing the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. One contributing factor to the dismal prognosis is the intrinsic stemness properties of HCC cells, which support tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance [2]. Moreover, HCC progression is driven by multiple converging signaling hubs, including Wnt/β‑catenin [3], NF‑κB [4], MAPK [5], and PI3K/AKT pathways [6], which integrate inflammatory cues and stemness programs to promote tumor growth and survival. Consequently, targeting liver cancer stem cells with these signaling has emerged as a promising approach for HCC therapy, highlighting the need for effective molecular targets [7].

Phytolacca acinosa (P. acinosa), a perennial herbaceous plant of the Phytolaccaceae family, has been extensively utilized in traditional medicine for the treatment of various disorders [8]. Recent research has demonstrated that extracts from P. acinosa possess significant antitumor activity, notably inhibiting the proliferation of HCC cells [9]. Among its bioactive constituents, esculentosides and related phytolaccosides have been reported to exhibit potent anti-inflammatory, antifungal, and anticancer properties [10]. For example, esculentoside A (EsA), a natural triterpenoid saponin extracted from the roots of Phytolacca esculenta, has demonstrated anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective properties, which have been shown to attenuate stemness features in certain cancers [11]. Nevertheless, how EsA functions in regulating cancer stemness in HCC remains unexplored.

Retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor beta (RORB), also known as RORβ, emerged from our bioinformatics analysis as a candidate target of EsA with significant expression changes in HCC and reported involvement in Wnt signaling in tumor initiation and self-renewal of colorectal cancer stem-like cells [12]. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a significant role in the development of HCC and is essential for the regulation of numerous physiological and pathological hepatic activities [13]. As such, inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling has gained attention as a potential therapeutic strategy [14].

Despite the absence of prior studies that have proposed a direct biochemical interaction between EsA and RORB, molecular docking was performed to explore this possibility. The docking results suggest a potential binding interface between EsA and RORB, providing preliminary structural support for their functional interplay. This study addresses a significant gap in the existing literature on the subject and provides a robust rationale for further investigation into the role of EsA-mediated regulation of RORB signaling in HCC. By identifying RORB as a functional mediator of EsA activity, this study hopes to lay a foundation for future drug development and cancer stem cell-targeted therapeutic strategies in HCC.

Results

EsA represses the malignant phenotype of HCC cells

To investigate the effect of EsA on HCC, we treated two HCC cell lines (HCCLM3 and MHCC97L) with different concentrations of EsA (100, 150, 200, 250, 300 µg/mL). The results of the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 1A) revealed that with increasing concentrations of EsA, the viability of HCC cells decreased significantly. In subsequent experiments, we selected 300 µg/mL EsA for the treatment of HCC cell lines. The sphere formation assay (Fig. 1B) showed that EsA treatment suppressed the sphere-forming ability of HCC cells. The clonogenic assay (Fig. 1C) indicated that EsA reduced the colony formation ability of HCC cells. WB (Fig. 1D) demonstrated that EsA significantly suppressed the expression of stemness markers Nanog and Oct4 in HCC cells. Since CD133 is a surface marker of HCC cancer stem cells, flow cytometry (Fig. 1E) revealed that EsA downregulated the proportion of CD133+ cells in HCC cell lines. TUNEL assay (Fig. 1F) showed that EsA treatment promoted apoptosis in HCC cell lines. Additionally, the results of the EdU assay (Fig. 1G), wound healing assay (Fig. 1H), and Transwell assay (Fig. 1I) indicated that EsA significantly reduced the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of HCC cells. Additionally, to validate the universal inhibitory effect of EsA on HCC malignant progression, we treated Huh-7 cells with 300 µg/mL EsA in parallel. We observed that EsA treatment similarly reduced malignant behaviors such as stemness in Huh-7 cells, manifested by decreased spheroid formation capacity (Fig. S1A), reduced expression of stemness-related proteins (Fig. S1B), and a decrease in the CD133-positive population (Fig. S1C), impaired proliferation (Fig. S1D, E), significant apoptosis (Fig. S1F), and reduced metastatic-like behavior (Fig. S1G, H).

EsA inhibits the tumor growth of HCC cells

Given the more aggressive behavior of HCCLM3 cells, we performed subcutaneous xenograft experiments by injecting HCCLM3 cells into nude mice, followed by EsA or saline treatment. EsA significantly inhibited tumor growth in nude mice, and tumor weights were markedly reduced (Fig. 2A, B). IHC (Fig. 2C) revealed that EsA treatment downregulated the expression of Ki67 and CD133 in the xenograft tumors. TUNEL assay (Fig. 2D) showed that EsA treatment promoted cell apoptosis in xenograft tumor tissues.

In the HCC orthotopic model, EsA reduced liver weight and decreased the maximum tumor size (Fig. 2E, F). Reduced expression of Ki67 and CD133 in orthotopic tumor tissues confirmed the suppression of malignant behavior and stemness by EsA (Fig. 2G). We analyzed tumor metastasis at this stage and found that the number of metastatic nodules in lung tissue was significantly reduced following EsA treatment (Fig. 2H). This demonstrates that EsA can significantly suppress the in vivo growth and stemness of HCC and impede its metastatic potential. Additionally, we evaluated body weight changes in normal mice treated with the same dose of EsA. EsA exhibited good biosafety and did not significantly affect mouse body weight (Fig. 2I). Additionally, HE staining of major mouse organs, including liver, kidney, lung, and heart tissues, revealed no evidence of significant damage (Fig. 2J). ALT and AST levels, as well as creatinine levels, were normal in mice (Fig. 2K).

EsA promotes the expression of RORB in HCC cells

To explore the mechanism by which EsA inhibits HCC progression, we obtained the chemical structure and SMILES notation of EsA (CC1(CCC2(CCC3(C(= CCC4C3(CCC5C4(CC(C(C5(C)CO)OC6COC(C(C6O)O)OC7C(C(C(C(O7)CO)O)O)O)O)C)C)C2C1)C)C(= O)O)C(= O)OC) from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) (Fig. 3A). Using the SMILES structure, we predicted potential targets of EsA through the Super-PRED database (https://prediction.charite.de/). Next, we analyzed the mRNA high-throughput RNA sequencing expression profile of fresh frozen HCC samples and paired adjacent normal liver tissues from patients who underwent liver resection or transplantation in the GSE184733 database. A threshold of p. adj < 0.01 and |Log2FoldChange| > 2 was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 3B). We performed a Venn analysis on the two datasets using the Jvenn website (https://jvenn.toulouse.inrae.fr/app/example.html) (Fig. 3C) and conducted a protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis of the intersecting genes in the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) (Fig. 3D). We observed an interaction between RXFP1 and RORB, and between GPR55 and CNR2. The roles of GPR55 [15] and CNR2 [16] in HCC have been reported. In the GSE184733 dataset, RXFP1 (Log2FoldChange = − 2.183) and RORB (Log2FoldChange = − 2.653) were significantly downregulated, with RORB showing a more prominent difference in expression. Thus, we chose RORB for further study.

RT-qPCR (Fig. 3E) and WB (Fig. 3F) demonstrated that RORB was expressed at lower levels in HCC cell lines compared to HHL-5 cells. We analyzed the changes in RORB protein expression induced by gradient doses of EsA through WB (Fig. 3G). Interestingly, RORB protein expression in HCC cell lines progressively increased with rising EsA concentrations, peaking at 300 µg/mL. This suggested that the dose-dependent inhibition of HCC tumor cell viability by EsA may be associated with elevated RORB protein expression. Additionally, RT-qPCR analysis showed no effect of EsA (300 µg/mL) on RORB mRNA expression (Fig. 3H). Subsequently, we performed docking using Autodock vina within the DeepMice tool (https://www.deepmice.com/) and identified a significant molecular interaction between EsA and RORB, with a best_vina_score of − 8.771 kcal/mol (Fig. 3I). We observed enhanced stability of RORB protein following EsA treatment after CHX-mediated inhibition of cellular protein translation (Fig. 3J), indicating that EsA primarily influenced the protein degradation pathway. We analyzed alterations in ubiquitination on RORB via Co-IP and observed that EsA treatment reduced ubiquitination levels on RORB (Fig. 3K).

Knockdown of RORB reverses the inhibitory effect of EsA on stemness in HCC cells

We performed gene knockdown of RORB in HCC cell lines using three shRNAs, and RT-qPCR (Fig. 4A) and WB (Fig. 4B) were used to examine the knockdown efficacy. Two lentiviral constructs with the best knockdown efficiency (RORB-KD #2 and RORB-KD #3) were selected for further experiments. The sphere formation assay (Fig. 4C) revealed that knockdown of RORB promoted sphere formation in HCC cells. The clonogenic assay (Fig. 4D) indicated that RORB knockdown enhanced colony formation in HCC cells. WB (Fig. 4E) showed that knockdown of RORB increased the expression of stemness markers Nanog and Oct4 in HCC cells. Flow cytometry (Fig. 4F) revealed that RORB knockdown increased the proportion of CD133+ cells in HCC cell lines. Furthermore, the TUNEL assay (Fig. 4G) demonstrated that the knockdown of RORB suppressed the pro-apoptotic effect of EsA on HCC cells. In Huh-7 cells, we similarly performed RORB knockdown. The decrease in RORB (Fig. S2A, B) significantly attenuated the inhibitory effects of EsA on the sphere-forming ability (Fig. S2C), stemness (Fig. S2D, E), and malignant behavior (Fig. S2F, G).

EsA suppresses HCC cell stemness via RORB-mediated inhibition of Wnt signaling

Additionally, analysis using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=background) revealed that high RORB expression was associated with favorable overall survival in patients with liver cancer (Fig. 5A). These findings suggest that EsA may suppress cancer stemness and malignant progression by upregulating RORB and thereby inhibiting Wnt signaling activation.

WB analysis (Fig. 5B) demonstrated elevated protein levels of total β-catenin in untreated HCC cells, which were significantly reduced following EsA treatment. However, knockdown of RORB reversed the suppressive effect of EsA on β-catenin protein expression. Additionally, the TOP/FOP flash luciferase reporter assay (Fig. 5C) confirmed that EsA attenuated Wnt pathway activity in HCC cells, while RORB knockdown restored Wnt pathway activation.

While the initial evidence of Wnt pathway suppression was provided by β‑catenin abundance and TOP/FOP ratios, the specificity of the pathway was validated through a functional rescue experiment with the Wnt pathway inhibitor XAV-939 or control DMSO in the presence of RORB knockdown. WB (Fig. 5D) results confirmed that XAV-939 reduced total β-catenin protein expression. TOP/FOP Flash assay (Fig. 5E) showed decreased Wnt signaling activity upon XAV-939 treatment. Moreover, treatment with XAV-939 inhibited the cancer stem-like properties of HCC cells, as evidenced by decreased sphere-forming ability (Fig. 5F), reduced colony formation (Fig. 5G), and downregulation of the stemness markers Nanog and Oct4 (Fig. 5H). Flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 5I) further revealed a decreased proportion of CD133⁺ cells following XAV-939 treatment. TUNEL assay (Fig. 5J) demonstrated a significant augmentation in the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells in HCC cells upon Wnt pathway inhibition. In Huh-7 cells, we similarly observed that inhibition of Wnt using XAV-939 treatment (Fig. S3A, B) suppressed sphere-forming ability (Fig. S3C), cellular stemness (Fig. S3D, E), and malignant behavior (Fig. S3F, G). The capacity of XAV-939 to reverse RORB knockdown-induced phenotypic alterations suggested that the observed effects were not merely attributable to general growth inhibition but rather involved direct modulation of Wnt signaling. This rescue-based strategy is analogous to the functional decoupling approaches that have been utilized in recent ion-channel and signaling studies [17]. These studies have served to reinforce the mechanistic specificity of the findings obtained.

EsA suppresses in vivo tumor growth of HCC through the RORB/Wnt axis.

An in vivo xenograft tumor model was established in nude mice by injecting HCCLM3 cells with NC-KD or RORB-KD (RORB-KD #2 for optimal efficacy), followed by treatment with EsA. The results showed that RORB-KD attenuated the therapeutic efficacy of EsA, as evidenced by increased tumor volume and weight (Fig. 6A, B). WB analysis of xenograft tumor tissues (Fig. 6C) revealed that EsA upregulated RORB expression and downregulated total β-catenin protein levels. However, knockdown of RORB abolished these effects. IHC staining (Fig. 6D) showed that RORB knockdown enhanced the stemness marker CD133 expression in xenograft tumors. TUNEL assay (Fig. 6E) further demonstrated that RORB knockdown reduced the apoptotic rate in xenograft tumor tissues. Ultimately, in the orthotopic tumor model, we similarly observed that RORB knockdown exacerbated tumor growth in the liver, resulting in increased liver weight and a larger maximum tumor diameter on the liver surface (Fig. 6F, G). The number of CD133-positive cells was higher in liver tissue (Fig. 6H). In lung tissue, we also found that RORB knockdown weakened the effect of EsA, leading to an increase in the number of metastatic nodules (Fig. 6I).

Low RORB expression in HCC tissues is associated with Wnt signaling activation

Finally, in the 21 clinical HCC tissue samples and their corresponding adjacent non-cancerous tissues included in our analysis, RT-qPCR and IHC confirmed the low expression of RORB in HCC tumor tissues (Fig. 7A, B). Patients were stratified into high-expression (≥ median value) and low-expression groups (< median value) based on the median positive rate of RORB in HCC. β-catenin activation in tumor tissues was assessed using IHC. We found that β-catenin expression was higher in tumor tissues from patients with low RORB expression (Fig. 7C). Correlation analysis revealed that RORB protein expression in HCC tissues exhibited a significant negative correlation with β-catenin expression (Fig. 7D). Meanwhile, higher RORB expression in HCC patients was negatively correlated with tumor TNM staging, while showing no association with factors such as age or gender (Table 1).

Discussion

EsA has been shown to consistently suppress multiple malignant phenotypes, including proliferation, migration, invasion, and stemness, while concomitantly promoting apoptosis. Although these outcomes align with the anticipated anticancer activity, their consistency across diverse assays underscores the robust nature of EsA’s effects.

Notably, EsA treatment markedly decreased the expression of Oct4 and Nanog, two key transcription factors essential for stem cell self-renewal and differentiation [18]. Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis showed a significant downregulation in the proportion of CD133-positive cells following EsA exposure. CD133 is a widely accepted surface marker used to identify cancer cells with stem-like traits [19]. These findings collectively indicate that EsA effectively attenuates stemness in HCC cells, thereby impeding tumor progression. Moreover, in vivo experiments confirmed the anti-tumor efficacy of EsA. EsA treatment led to a significant reduction in tumor growth in a xenograft mouse model and an HCC orthotopic tumor model. IHC analysis further revealed decreased expression of Ki-67, a proliferation marker that is typically elevated in aggressively growing HCC tumors [20], suggesting that EsA effectively impairs tumor proliferation in vivo.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism through which EsA exerts its effects in HCC, we investigated its downstream targets and identified RORB as a key candidate. Our findings demonstrated that EsA significantly upregulated RORB protein expression in HCC cells, while its mRNA levels remained largely unchanged. Low RORB expression was significantly associated with tumor size, clinical stage, and survival time in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [21]. RORB, a member of the orphan nuclear receptor family, has recently gained attention as a potential therapeutic target in the treatment of various cancers, such as colorectal cancer [22], which indicates its pharmacological relevance and plausibility as an EsA-responsive factor. Building on this premise, we examined the expression profile of RORB in HCC and found that its expression was significantly downregulated. This observation aligns with reports of similar nuclear receptor superfamily members, such as RORA, which has been shown to suppress tumor invasiveness by shaping an anti-tumor microenvironment, and its low expression correlates with poor prognosis in HCC patients [23], highlighting the broader relevance of orphan nuclear receptors in cancer biology. RORB attenuated the growth, invasiveness, and metastatic spread of neuroblastoma cells has been recently identified [24]. Despite these findings, the role of RORB in modulating stemness in HCC remains largely unexplored.

To address this gap, we further investigated the downstream signaling pathways modulated by RORB and found that increased RORB expression led to suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. This is in line with previous reports where RORB downregulates Wnt/β-catenin activity in gastric cancer stem cells [25] and glioblastoma, thereby reducing stemness characteristics [26]. Given that aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is known to enhance proliferation, invasion, and migration in HCC cells [27] and is crucial for the maintenance of tumor stemness and growth [28], our data support the notion that RORB functions as a negative regulator of this oncogenic pathway. To validate the involvement of the Wnt/β-catenin axis, we analyzed β-catenin protein expression, which serves as a key marker of canonical Wnt signaling activity [29], and we observed elevated β-catenin levels in HCC cells. Tankyrase 1 inhibition using XAV-939 decreased the viability, induced apoptosis, and led to the accumulation of neuroblastoma cells in the S and G2/M phase of the cell cycle [30]. More importantly, XAV-939 treatment resulted in a marked reduction in HCC cell proliferation and stemness characteristics, echoing earlier reports on its anti-cancer effects [31].

However, this study has some limitations. Initially, the dosage of 300 µg/mL was selected based on the findings of preliminary experiments. However, future studies will investigate its pharmacological relevance. Mechanistically, bioinformatics analysis identified several candidate interactors, among which RORB was prioritized for its reported role in Wnt signaling. It is imperative to consider RORB within the broader signaling landscape of HCC, where hubs such as NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and G protein pathways (e.g., RGS14) [32] also contribute to tumor progression. This highlights RORB as one node within a complex network rather than a lone driver. Finally, the present study provides evidence for the functional regulation of the Wnt pathway by RORB; however, the assessment of direct molecular interactions was not included in this study. Given that co-IP or ChIP assays were not performed, the possibility of indirect regulation cannot be excluded. Subsequent research endeavors will be necessary to delineate the precise molecular mechanism.

In conclusion, our study reveals a novel anti-cancer mechanism of EsA in HCC through the upregulation of RORB protein, which in turn suppresses the Wnt/β-catenin signaling and reduces cancer stem cell-like properties (Fig. 8). These findings not only shed light on the functional significance of RORB in HCC but also propose EsA as a promising candidate for targeting cancer stemness in HCC.

Materials and methods

Patients and specimens

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Subei People’s Hospital (approval no. 2024ky), and written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients. From February 2024 to January 2025, a total of 21 HCC patients were enrolled in Subei People’s Hospital for this study, comprising HCC samples and their corresponding adjacent non-cancerous tissues. All cases were pathologically confirmed as HCC through biopsy.

Cell line sources and treatment

Human hepatocyte line 5 (HHL-5, YaJi Biological, Shanghai, China) was cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium (R5158, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. HCC cells HCCLM3, MHCC97L, and Huh-7 (all from YaJi Biological) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (11966025, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. All cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

HCC cells were infected with lentivirus carrying a construct for RORB knockdown (RORB-KD) or a corresponding control (NC-KD), obtained from VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, China), at a titer of > 108 TU/mL for 24 h. After renewing the medium with fresh culture media, cells were further cultured for 48 h, followed by selection with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 7 days. For treatment, 300 µg/mL EsA (purity: 98%, SM6112-10mM, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was applied to HCC cell lines for 48 h, followed by 24-hour treatment with 10 µmol/L XAV-939 (purity: 99.80%, HY-15147, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) [33]. The control group was treated with an equivalent volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (HY-K0301, MedChemExpress). HCC cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/well in a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h. Afterward, different concentrations (100, 150, 200, 250, 300 µg/mL) of EsA were added [11], with the control group receiving an equivalent volume of DMSO. After 48 h of incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional hour. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA from HCC cells and tissues was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) kit (R223-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR amplification of cDNA was performed with ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Q411-02, Vazyme) on the SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler (A24811, ThermoFisher). Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH as an internal reference. Primer sequences are listed in Table 2.

Sphere formation assay

HCC cells, treated as described, were seeded at 1.2 × 104 cells/well in 6-well plates. Following that, cells were incubated at 1× B27 serum-free Ham’s F-12 medium (N6658, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with commercial hormone mix B27 (0080085SA, Thermo Fisher), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (HY-P72982, MedChemExpress), and 10 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (P5453-100 µg, Beyotime) for 7 days at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Spheres with a diameter of ≥ 50 μm were counted.

Clonogenic assay

Treated HCC cells were seeded at 500 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated for 14 days at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet. Stained colonies and colonies with more than 50 cells were counted.

Flow cytometry

Treated HCC cells were resuspended in cell staining buffer and incubated with phycoerythrin (PE) anti-CD133 antibody (1:1000, ab252128, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 30 min in the dark on ice. Cell sorting was performed using a BD FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

EdU assay

The Cell-Light EdU Apollo488 In Vitro Kit (100T) (C10310-3, RiboBio, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) was used to evaluate cell proliferation. HCC cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells/well in a 96-well plate and cultured in EdU medium for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 200 µL Apollo staining solution in the dark for 30 min. After adding 1 mg/L DAPI solution, the cells were incubated for 20 min and observed under a fluorescence microscope. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated.

Wound healing assay

When the treated HCC cells formed a confluent monolayer in 6-well plates, scratches were made using a 200 µL pipette tip. After removing the culture medium, fresh serum-free medium was added, and images were captured at 0 and 24 h to monitor wound closure. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

HCC cells (5 × 104 cells) were seeded in the apical chamber of a 24-well Transwell plate pre-coated with Matrigel. The basolateral chamber contained medium with 10% FBS. After 48 h, the cells in the lower chamber were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The stained cells were observed and counted under a light microscope.

TOP/FOP flash assay

Treated HCC cells were seeded in 24-well plates and serum-starved overnight. Cells were transfected with 0.2 µg TOP Flash (D2501-1 µg, Beyotime) or FOP Flash plasmids (D2503-1 µg, Beyotime) and 0.1 µg pRL-TK (D2760-100 µg, Beyotime) using X-tremeGENE HP DNA transfection reagent (XTGHP-RO, Sigma-Aldrich). After 48 h, a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (RG027, Beyotime) was used for measurement. The Wnt pathway activity was evaluated by calculating the TOP/FOP ratio, normalizing firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity.

Protein stability assay

HCC cells were treated with 100 µg/mL Cycloheximide (CHX, S7418, Selleck, Shanghai, China) and collected at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h post-treatment. RORB expression in cells was examined via Western blot (WB) analysis to assess changes in protein stability or half-life.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

In brief, HCC cells were treated with 10 µM MG132 (S2619, Selleck) for 6 h to inhibit proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation. Cells were then lysed using an IP lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The extracted protein was incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-RORB (1:200, orb630762, Biorbyt Ltd, Cambridge, UK) in a rotating incubator for immunoprecipitation. Following Protein A/G magnetic bead-mediated protein precipitation, the beads were eluted. The resulting protein complexes were analyzed via SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Subei People’s Hospital (approval no. 2024ky221). Male BALB/c nude mice, aged 4 weeks and weighing approximately 12–16 g, were purchased from CSTQSW (Changsha, Hunan, China). The nude mice were divided into four groups, with 10 mice in each group. Each group was further subdivided into two subgroups: one receiving subcutaneous tumor transplantation (n = 5) and the other receiving orthotopic tumor transplantation (n = 5). The grouping scheme was as follows: Saline, EsA, EsA + negative control (NC)-knockdown (KD), and EsA + RORB-KD groups.

HCCLM3 cells, which had been successfully infected with RORB-KD or NC-KD lentivirus, as well as untreated HCCLM3 cells, were resuspended in 100 µL PBS containing 10% Matrigel. For subcutaneously transplanted tumors, the cells were subcutaneously injected into the right axillary region of the nude mice with a cell count of 3.5 × 106 cells. Tumor volume was measured using a caliper, and the tumor volume was calculated using the previously established formula [34]. When the tumor volume reached about 100 mm3, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with 40 mg/kg EsA [11] or an equivalent volume of saline every three days. Tumor measurements were taken every three days starting from the administration. On day 24, the mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium, and tumor tissues were collected and weighed.

For orthotopic tumors, mice in each group were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane. A midline abdominal incision was made to expose the left hepatic lobe. We injected HCCLM3 cells (2 × 10⁶ cells) mixed with Matrigel into the left hepatic lobe of mice [35]. After suturing the wound, we administered 40 mg/kg of EsA or an equal volume of saline solution via intraperitoneal injection every three days. Four weeks later, the mice were euthanized to collect lung and liver tissues, weigh the liver tissue, and measure the maximum tumor diameter on the liver surface. Meanwhile, the number of metastatic nodules in lung tissue was determined via hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining.

To assess the biosafety of EsA, we administered 40 mg/kg of EsA or an equal volume of saline via intraperitoneal injection to BALB/c male nude mice without tumor cell injection. Injections were given every three days for four consecutive weeks. Mice were weighed every 7 days. After 4 weeks, mice were euthanized, and liver, kidney, lung, and heart tissues were collected for histopathological assessment via HE staining. Mouse serum was collected to analyze liver or kidney damage.

WB analysis

Tumor tissues and HCC cells were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) containing a protease inhibitor (20104ES03, YEASEN, Shanghai, China). Total protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit. Proteins were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk powder, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting RORB (1:1000, ab314650, Abcam), Nanog (1:1000, A25887, ABclonal, Wuhan, Hubei, China), octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (Oct4; 1:1000, ab181557, Abcam), Ubiquitin (1:1000, A23483, Abclonal), β-catenin (1:100, A11512, ABclonal), and GAPDH (1:50000, A19056, ABclonal). After incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, NB7160, Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA) for 1 h at room temperature (RT), protein bands were visualized using the Super ECL Detection Reagent (36208ES76, YEASEN).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Subcutaneous tumors and orthotopic liver tumor tissues from mice were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 μm. The mouse and clinical sample sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval with citrate buffer. The sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min and with 10% goat serum for 60 min (both at RT). The primary antibodies: anti-RORB (1:100, ab314650, Abcam), anti-β-catenin (1:100, A11512, ABclonal), anti-Ki67 (1:20, ab15580, Abcam), and anti-CD133 (1:500, ab284389, Abcam) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200, NB7160, Novus Biologicals) for 1 h at RT. The sections were stained with DAB and hematoxylin, followed by observation under a microscope to analyze the percentage of positive cells.

TUNEL assay

The CoraLite 594 TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (Red Fluorescence) (PF00009, Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA) was used to detect apoptosis in HCC cell lines and xenograft tumor tissues. For HCC cells, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min at RT. For xenograft tumor tissues, 100 µL Proteinase K was added to deparaffinized tissue sections and incubated at RT for 20 min. The TUNEL reaction mixture (50 µL) was added to the samples and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark. Afterward, 2 µg/mL DAPI solution was added, and the samples were incubated for 10 min in the dark. The samples were mounted with an antifade mounting solution and observed under a fluorescence microscope. The apoptosis rate was calculated as the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells among the total cell population.

HE staining

Lung tissue from mice with orthotopic tumors and liver, kidney, lung, and heart tissues from mice not inoculated with tumor cells were fixed in formalin overnight, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. After dewaxing and rehydration, sections were stained using a HE staining kit (E607318, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The number of metastatic tumor nodules in the lung tissue of mice with orthotopic tumors was counted. Pathological damage in the liver, kidney, lung, and heart tissues of mice not inoculated with tumor cells was observed.

Biochemical analysis

Mouse serum samples were analyzed for ALT, AST, and creatinine levels using the following assay kits: Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT/GPT) Activity Fluorometric Assay Kit (E-BC-F038, Elabscience, Wuhan, Hubei, China), Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST/GOT) Activity Fluorometric Assay Kit (E-BC-F043, Elabscience), and creatinine Assay Kit (sarcosine oxidase) (C011-2-1, JianCheng, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Prism 10.3.1 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Chi-square tests were used to assess the association between categorical variables of clinical parameters [36]. Comparison between two groups was performed using a paired or unpaired t-test, and for multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. For multi-factor comparisons, two-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Sidak’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post-hoc analysis. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used for the correlation analysis between two factors. For animal experiments, each group consisted of 5 nude mice, and for cell experiments, each group was independently repeated three times. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
