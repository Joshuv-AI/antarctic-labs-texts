Histone variant H2A.X represses deposition of active H3K4me3 marks and restricts H2A.W incorporation
Authors: Aravind Madhu, Vivek Hari-Sundar Gandhivel, Steffi Raju, Riju Dey, Padubidri V Shivaprasad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHistone variant H2A.X is a well-conserved histone that plays crucial roles in mediating DNA damage response across eukaryotes. Although H2A.X expresses and decorates gene bodies of actively expressed genes even without stress, it is not known if H2A.X has functions beyond DNA damage repair. Using genetic, high-throughput genomics, and molecular approaches, we identified a previously unappreciated role of H2A.X in regulating development-associated genes. Using custom-made antibodies specific to the rice H2A.X variant, we show that it repressed the deposition of active H3K4me3 marks over gene bodies and transposable elements (TEs), specifically regulating root development, photosynthesis, and pigmentation-related genes, as seen by the impairment of these processes in rice h2a.x knockout (ko) plants. H2A.X also repressed global deposition of repressive mark H3K9me2 by restricting the activity of H2A variant H2A.W. In agreement with this, there was a genome-wide relocalization of H2A.W to TEs and a few genes in ko plants. H2A.X-overexpressing plants exhibited stress phenotypes, including increased anthocyanin levels, mimicking the transcriptome of DNA-damaged wild-type (WT) plants. The transcriptome of knockdown (kd) lines of facilitates chromatin transcription (FACT) complex, a known chaperone of H2A.X, was largely similar to that of h2a.x ko plants, suggesting that the development-associated functions of FACT are at least partially due to H2A.X. These results suggest a specific role of H2A.X in regulating competing histone marks, a function that might also be conserved across plants.

Introduction

Histone octamers are made of a (H3-H4)2 tetramer and two dimers consisting of one each of H2A and H2B proteins [1, 2]. Regulatory activity of histones largely comes from their ability to have often conserved one or more post-translational modifications (PTMs), predominantly in their N-terminal tails, or rarely elsewhere. Although histone sequences are well-conserved, especially where they undergo these PTMs, there are also histone paralogs called histone variants where PTM sites are often replaced with other amino acids [3]. Similar to histones, these histone variants are also key players in regulating the chromatin structure and controlling diverse biological processes [4–7].

Plant genomes underwent several duplication and hybridization events during their evolutionary timeline that contributed to the presence of transposable elements (TEs) and repeats. Unlike the model plant Arabidopsis, such repeats and TEs are closely associated with genic regions in plants with larger genomes such as rice [8, 9]. Histone variants play specific roles in the transcriptional control of genomic regions, including repeats and TEs, along with genic regions. Histone variants mediate development of specific organs/tissues as well as stress responses [6]. These variants are very diverse in their function; however, conserved variants play similar roles across eukaryotes. The functions of these histone variants depends on their expression pattern, their unique PTMs that are different from the canonical forms, structural properties, chaperone specificities, and genomic sites where they get incorporated [7, 10–17].

Plants possess several conserved histone variants as well as specialized family/clade-specific variants exhibiting either tissue-specific expression or stress-induced expression. In plants, well-conserved histone variants of H3 includes CENH3 and H3.3, while H3.15, H3.14, and H3.10 are clade/genera-specific variants. CENH3 is an essential protein for cell division, and mutations in this variant led to haploid induction in multiple plant species [18, 19]. On the other hand, H3.3 variant regulates gene expression by positively influencing active histone marks. Mutants of H3.3 display early flowering phenotype, largely attributed to its influence on the FLC locus [20]. h3.3 knockout (ko) plants also show severe developmental defects, including smaller rosette leaves and siliques [21]. The sperm cell-specific H3.10, along with H2B.8, are important for compacting sperm nuclei, while H3.15 regulates callus induction [22–24]. Similarly, low-expressed but salt-stress-inducible H3.14 is key for mediating salt stress response in the roots of Arabidopsis [22–25]. The vital role of Oryza-specific H4 variant in mediating salt stress responses by acting as a gatekeeper to H3K5Ac marks is another example of the importance of genera-specific histone variants in plants [17]. The mutants of all these variants often have compromised growth and development and, in a few cases, show abnormal phenotypes under specific stress conditions. Mutants of histone variants such as H3.3 in Arabidopsis are lethal [26, 27].

H2A.Z and H2A.X variants of H2A are conserved across eukaryotes. Unlike H3 variants, the C-terminal PTM modifications and sites of their incorporation in the genome, as determined by their specific chaperone proteins, dictate the functional outcome of these variants [28]. H2A.Z variant is mostly associated with the promoters and transcription start sites (TSSs) of several stress-responsive genes [29]. Multiple key findings have systematically characterized functions of H2A.Z among plants. The minimal incorporation of H2A.Z in gene bodies prevents or interferes with DNA methylation [30]. There is a significant reduction in the levels of H2A.Z in the gene bodies of actively expressed genes having high gene-body DNA methylation [31, 32]. Dynamics of H2A.Z are mostly regulated by environmental stresses and often connected to the chromatin remodelling complexes that act as its chaperones [33–38]. Evidence from Arabidopsis suggests that gene-body occupancy of H2A.Z is repressive, while +1 nucleosome distribution of H2A.Z activates gene expression [39, 40]. The repressive role of H2A.Z might be due to its ability to create less-accessible chromatin environment, coinciding with reduced H3K4me3 levels and high levels of H3K27me3 marks [41]. In addition to this, specific PTMs in the H2A.Z, mostly monoubiquitination, contributes to repressive function of the variant [42]. H2A.Z seems to be playing roles in development too; for example, it acts as an activator of floral repressor FLC [30, 43].

Among the other H2A variants, H2A.W is a plant-specific variant having a C-terminal SPKK (or KSPK) motif that helps in compacting the chromatin and repressing TEs and repeats in heterochromatin regions [44, 45]. H2A.W is incorporated by decrease in DNA methylation1, and it is associated with repressive H3K9me2 marks [46]. A modified H2A.W variant having an SQEF motif in addition to SPKK motif is known to regulate heterochromatin-specific DNA damage repair (DDR) in Arabidopsis [28]. H2A.W mutant in Arabidopsis did not show any developmental defects; however, its mutant had increased occupancy of H2A and H2A.X across TEs. In addition, the loss of H2A.W from centromeres led to higher levels of H1, further reducing accessibility for DNA methyltransferases in these regions [45, 47]. Similar to H2A.W, the linker histone H1 is also important for regulating the accessibility of heterochromatin regions [45, 48, 49].

Unlike the plant-specific H2A.W, H2A.X is a well-conserved H2A variant that is also a major regulator of DNA damage response among eukaryotes [50, 51]. H2A.X is distributed across the genome in both euchromatic regions, including gene bodies, and heterochromatic regions [44]. It is known that Facilitates chromatin transcription (FACT) complex is the chaperone for H2A.X and, as expected, mutants of FACT complex reduced the levels of H2A.X incorporation [6, 52–54]. FACT complex consists of suppressor of TY (SPT) 16 and structure specific recognition protein 1 (SSRP1) proteins, and mutants of both these proteins exhibited severe developmental and reproductive defects in Arabidopsis [55–59]. Upon DNA damage, the phosphorylation of the conserved serine residue in the SQEF motif of DNA damage-induced H2A.X by ataxia telangiectasia mutated and ataxia telangiectasia and Rad-3-related kinases activate its function [60]. Phospho-H2A.X signal is a very important mark for the active DNA damage response across organisms. Mediator of DNA damage checkpoint 1 (MDC1) is a key DDR-associated protein that interacts with phosphorylated tail of H2A.X to recruit DDR proteins to the DNA damage sites in animals [61, 62]. Recent studies in Arabidopsis indicates that BRACT domain protein 4 is the homologue of MDC1 in plants and it interacts with phosphorylated H2A.X to recruit DDR proteins [63]. Similarly, another BRCT domain-containing protein, γ-H2A.X-interacting protein, is also involved in regulating H2A.X-dependent DDR in plants [64]. Absence of H2A.X or the machinery to phosphorylate H2A.X disrupts DDR process in plants [65]. In addition, the length of Arabidopsis h2a.x ko plant roots are reduced upon DNA damage, indicating the influence of compromised DDR on development [54, 65].

Mutants of H2A.X in Arabidopsis show negligible root and silique development phenotypes [28, 54, 66]. Since the mutants of H2A.X in Arabidopsis lack profound developmental defects, it has been generally assumed that function of H2A.X might be more specific to DNA damage response. Recent studies suggest that γ-H2A.X is involved in regulating gene expression of DNA damage-associated genes in mammalian cell lines, where it has been identified that high mobility AT-hook 2 generated nicks in the DNA, and these are the sites where γ-H2A.X is incorporated by the action of the FACT to induce transcription [67]. In plants, γ-H2A.X interacts with the promoters of abscisic acid insensitive genes to de-repress their expression [68]. Probably connected to this, the Arabidopsis h2a.x ko plants show increased root growth and reduced cotyledon greening phenotype under ABA treatment [66, 68].

Here, we show that rice H2A.X contributes to rice development and growth, especially in specific stages and tissues, and this role of H2A.X is largely independent of its functions in DNA damage response. Using genetic, biochemical, and whole-genome approaches, we show that H2A.X acts a repressor of active H3K4me3 deposition across several development-associated genes. H2A.X also restricted deposition of repressive H3K9me2 marks across genes and TEs by competing with another H2A variant, H2A.W. Unlike Arabidopsis, rice H2A.W deposition was not limited to pericentromeric heterochromatin regions alone, largely because dispersed TEs and repeats in rice genome are often present near genic regions. Immunostaining using H2A.X- and H2A.W-specific antibodies and genomic analysis demonstrated competition between these two variants at specific genomic regions. Together, these results indicate that H2A.X plays crucial development-associated roles by maintaining specific chromatin states across hundreds of genes. It is likely that H2A.X plays similar roles across plants.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction and cloning

To generate CRISPR-based double-knockout plants of H2A.X (ko), gRNAs were cloned in to pYLCRISPR/Cas9Pubi-H-based construct [69] using BsaI sites. The gRNA1 (5′ cguccaggacgaaggguucu 3′) to target OsHTA704 was cloned under OsU6a promoter, whereas the gRNA2 (5′ tcccgagguucagccacagc 3′) to target HTA711 was cloned under OsU3 promoter. To generate H2A.X overexpression (OE) plants, rice H2A.Xa (OsHTA704) CDS was amplified (the primers are listed in Supplementary Table S3) from PB-1 seedling RNA with Superscript III reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). The amplified CDS was inserted with a N-terminal FLAG tag in to a derivative of pCAMBIA1300 plasmid under maize Ubiquitin promoter. For double artificial microRNA mediated knockdown (amiR) lines of OsH2A.X (kd) and OsSPT16 (fact kd), OsamiR528 coding region was swapped using specific amiR sequence in pNW55 vector with the help of WMD3 tool. For generating h2a.x kd lines, amiR1 (3′ aacgacagagccagcagguuu 5′) was cloned to maize Ubiquitin promoter, while amiR2 (3′ agcgacaccgccagcagguuu 5′) was cloned under 35S promoter. For fact kd plant generation amiR1 (3′ ugcacguauuaagacuagauu 5′) was cloned to 35S promoter, while amiR2 (3′ agcagaguucuagaagcaauu 5′) was cloned under maize Ubiquitin promoter. All these binary vectors were mobilized to Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 with additional virulence plasmid pSB1.

Plant transformation

Pusa basmati-1 (PB-1), an indica rice variety (Oryza sativa indica) was used for the study. The plants were maintained in the greenhouse in 22 °C. Rice transformation for generating transgenic rice plants were performed by following already standardized protocol [70–72]. PB-1 rice seeds were dehusked and sterilized using 70% ethanol, 4% bleach, and 0.1% mercuric chloride. Seeds were placed in callus induction media for 21 days in dark to induce callus. These calli were used for Agrobacterium-mediated plant transformation and further selected using hygromycin. The regenerated plants were transformed to ½ MS media for rooting and further changed to soil.

RT-qPCR

RNA was isolated from different tissues using Trizol reagent, and qPCR analysis was performed using complementary DNA made from the total RNA using Thermo RevertAid RT kit following manufacturer’s protocol. SYBR green master mix (Solis Biodyne- 5x HOT Firepol EvaGreen qPCR master mix) was used for qPCR. OsActin (LOC_Os03g50885) or OsGAPDH (LOC_Os04g40950) was used as internal control. All the primers (Supplementary Table S1) and gene IDs of the genes (Supplementary Table S2) are mentioned.

Phylogeny

The phylogenetic tree was made using MEGA11 tool using maximum likelihood method with bootstrap values (1000 replicates) [73].

Southern hybridization

Southern hybridization to confirm the presence of T-DNA was performed as described previously [74, 75]. The hygromycin probe was amplified and labelled using Rediprime random primer labelling kit (Cytiva).

small (s)RNA northern hybridization

Northern blot to detect the sRNAs was performed as mentioned previously [76, 77]. For blotting, around 8 µg of total RNA was loaded on denaturing gel and electrophoresed. These sRNAs were blotted to a Hybond-N+ membrane (Cytiva) and cross-linked using UV light. The membrane was hybridized with specific radiolabelled DNA [T4 PNK, M0201, New England Biolabs (NEB)] probes, which was amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Overnight hybridization of the membrane and the probe was performed in ULTRAhyb buffer at 35 °C. The membrane was exposed to a phosphor screen and then scanned using Typhoon scanner (Cytiva).

Chlorophyll and anthocyanin estimation

Chlorophyll estimation was performed using 200 mg tissue of 14-day-old seedling leaves using previously mentioned protocol [78]. Anthocyanin estimation was performed from 200 mg stem tissue of 14-day-old rice seedlings as described previously [79, 80].

Immunofluorescence and microscopy

Immunofluorescence and microscopy was performed as described previously [44, 81]. Fourteen-day-old seedlings were cross-linked using 4% PFA, which was used to isolate nuclei, and these nuclei were fixed to a positively charged slide. Anti-H2A.X (1:100) and anti-H2A.W (1:100) antibodies were used as the primary antibodies, and these were detected using fluorescently tagged secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, goat-raised anti-mouse 488 and anti-rabbit 555). The nuclei were also counterstained using DAPI to detect the DNA, and the slides were imaged using confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000).

Antibody generation

Polyclonal antibodies were made for OsH2A.X and OsH2A.W with specific peptides. Peptide C-GGGKGDIGSASQEF-GGKDKADIGSASQEF was used for OsH2A.X (both the isoforms), while C-KEGKTPKSPKKATTKSPKKA was used to make OsH2A.W (OsH2A4) antibody. Both the antibodies were raised in rabbit and antigen-affinity purified by LifeTein LLC.

Transcriptome analysis

Transcriptomic analysis was performed as mentioned previously [82]. Fourteen-day-old whole seedlings were used for RNA sequencing. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, followed by poly(A) enrichment before sequencing. The libraries were sequenced using NovaSeq6000; a 2 × 100 bp format. For all the samples, >30 million reads were obtained. The adapter content from the obtained reads was removed using Trimmomatic [83] and aligned to the IRGSP-1 genome using HISAT2 [84]. Cufflinks were used to analyse the difference in gene expression and transcript abundance. Log2 (fold change) cut off >1.5-fold with P-value cutoff < .05 compared to wild-type (WT) sample was selected as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Supplementary Data S1, S2, S3, and S11). Specific plots were made using R, and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using ShinyGO v0.81 platform [85]. The IRGSP gene IDs were used, and GO categories were found using general biological processes with FDR cut-off of P-value: .05. For enrichment studies, BEDtools multicov was used, and the values were normalized to RPKM [86]. Multistress dataset analysis was performed using TENOR datasets (https://tenor.dna.affrc.go.jp/) as described previously [17, 87]. Photosynthesis, pigmentation, and root growth genes are selected from AgriGO https://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/ and Funrice https://venyao.xyz/funRiceGenes/ [88]. For DNA damage response genes ensemble plants GO category genes under DNA damage response was used.

Targeted bisulphite sequencing

The targeted bisulphite sequencing was performed using protocol mentioned previously [89]. For targeted bisulphite sequencing around 200–400 ng of DNA was isolated from 14-day-old seeding and treated using EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research). PCR for specific promoter regions was performed using this converted DNA as template using JumpStart™ Taq DNA Polymerase (Sigma). The obtained PCR products from transgenic lines were sequenced using paired-end mode (100 bp) in Hiseq2500 platform. The obtained reads were trimmed using Cutadapt and aligned to target regions generated using Bismark aligner tool [90–92]. Obtained results were analysed using methylation package under ViewBS, and plots were modified using R [92].

Bleomycin treatment and phenotyping

For bleomycin treatment, WT and transgenic seeds were sterilized and germinated in ½ strength MS (Murashige and Skoog) medium with 0.3% Phytagel (Sigma–Aldrich). Three days after germination, plants were changed to ½ MS media supplemented with 5 mg/l bleomycin sulphate (European Pharmacopoeia). These plants were grown for another 14 days and used for phenotyping and RNA sequencing.

Western blotting and MNase IP

For western blotting analysis, total nuclear protein was isolated from 500 mg of seedling tissue, otherwise mentioned specifically, using protocol described previously [28]. These histones were electrophoresed in 14% sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to Protran-supported nitrocellular membrane [93]. These membranes were hybridized with specific antibodies anti-H2A.X (custom generated, 1:1000), anti-H2A.W (custom generated, 1:1000), anti-H3K9me2 (Merck 07-473), anti-H3K4me3 (Abcam ab1220), and anti-H3 (Merck 07-10 254, 1:20 000) antibodies in a 5% milk in 1× Tris-buffered saline and Tween 20 (TBST) buffer. Bacterial protein expression, bacterial induced cells were grown upto OD 2.0 and lysed by heating with 2× Laemmli buffer. MNase IP was performed from seedling tissues by following previously mentioned protocol [28].

ChIP sequencing and analysis

For all ChIP sequencing experiments 14-day old seedlings were used. Around 1.5 g of 1% formaldehyde cross-linked tissue was used for nuclei isolation and chromatin shearing. Sheared chromatin was incubated with 5 µg of anti-H2A.X, anti-H2A.W, anti-H3K9me2, and anti-H3K4me3 antibodies overnight at 4°C. These chromatin fractions were incubated with Protein G Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 4 h. Purified DNA from the IP was used to prepare ChIP-seq library, using NEBNext Ultra II DNA library prep kit (NEB E7103), using the manufacturer’s protocol. The sequencing of ChIP-seq libraries were performed in NovaSeq 6000 platform (Supplementary Table S1). Adapter content from the sequencing reads were removed using Cutadapt, and mapped the reads to IRGSP-1 genome using Bowtie [90, 94] using parameters v, 1; k, 1; y; a; best; strata. PCR duplicates from the aligned reads were removed, and then converted the reads to coverage tracks by subtracting the reads to the sheared input DNA using deepTools [95]. Enrichment was calculated using computeMatrix under deepTools, and plotProfile tool was used to make plots. These plots were modified using custom R script using ggplot2 package [96]. ChIP peaks were obtained from each experiment using MACS2; broad peaks were selected for histone variants (H2A.X and H2A.W), while narrow peaks were called for histone marks (H3K9me2 and H3K4me3). All these peaks had a significant enrichment of two-fold compared to input sheared DNA. These peaks were merged, and ChIPseekeR was used to find the chromosomal regions of enrichment. Publicly available datasets were also analysed the same way (Supplementary Table S2). shinyCircos-V2.0 (https://venyao.xyz/shinycircos/) and shinyChromsome (https://venyao.xyz/shinyChromosome/) tools were used for generated circos and chromosome-wide plots, respectively.

Results

Tissue specifically expressed OsH2A.X is uniformly distributed in the nucleus

In order to understand the conservation of H2A.X sequences, we aligned canonical H2A and its variants to identify amino acid variations (Fig. 1A). In Arabidopsis, H2A variants are encoded by 13 genes that include four copies of canonical H2A, two copies of H2A.X, four copies of H2A.Z, and 3 copies of H2A.W, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Among the 14 copies of H2A members in rice, 4 code for canonical H2A, 2 for H2A.X, 3 for H2A.Z, and 5 coded for H2A.W. Majority of the variations in H2A variants are either at the N-terminal tail or at the C-terminal, suggesting that these variants can alter the functions through amino acid variations alone. Both copies of rice H2A.X have several amino acid variations from canonical as well as other H2A variants at both N- and C-terminal ends, while the conserved C-terminal SQEF motif, which is important for DNA damage signalling, is conserved.

Surprisingly, the well-conserved SPKK motif of H2A.W, important for chromatin compaction in Arabidopsis, has duplicated in three of the five copies of rice H2A.W variants (Fig. 1A). The conserved lysine residues in the N-terminal end of canonical H2A and H2A.Z, which are important for transcription control, are absent in rice H2A.X variants [97, 98]. H2A.X is phylogenetically closer to canonical H2A compared to other H2A variants such as H2A.W. (Fig. 1B). H2A variants in rice show distinct tissue-specific expression similar to Arabidopsis (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S1A). Among the two copies of H2A.X (named a and b), H2A.Xa expresses highly in endosperm, whereas H2A.Xb expresses highly in embryo, young endosperm, and seedling tissues (Fig. 1C and D). Such a tissue-specific expression was also observed among H2A.Z and H2A.W variants in rice (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1B). We generated specific antibodies for rice H2A.X that effectively detected both H2A.X variants in rice and also did not cross-react with Escherichia coli-expressed rice H2A or other H2A variants (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Further, we performed western blotting with this antibody and found high H2A.X protein levels in seedling and reproductive tissues, matching the RNA-expression data (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Immunostaining of nuclei derived from seedlings indicated an almost uniform distribution of H2A.X throughout the nuclei, excluding nucleoli, indicating a genome-wide distribution of H2A.X in the absence of DNA damage (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S1E). This distribution is markedly different from the nuclear distribution pattern of Arabidopsis γ-H2A.X, in DNA damaged tissues, where multiple studies report clear nuclear speckles correlating with the sites of DNA damage [63, 64]. All these indicate that unmodified plant H2A.X might have functions beyond DNA damage response as proposed previously [5]. Among animals and plants, such a possibility is not fully explored, even though some tissue-specific or condition/treatment-specific phenotypes were documented in h2a.x mutants [54, 66, 68].

Mis-expression of H2A.X led to development-associated phenotypes

In order to understand the function of H2A.X beyond DNA damage response, we generated transgenic lines mis-expressing H2A.X in indica rice, O. sativa indica Pusa basmati-1 (henceforth PB-1) lines. The complete ko of H2A.X was achieved by targeting both members of H2A.X (H2A.Xa and H2A.Xb) using two different gRNAs in a single gRNA cassette (Supplementary Fig. S2A, Methods). Nine plants were obtained after Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Supplementary Fig. S2B–G). The transgene integration was verified using junction-fragment Southern analysis, which indicated single-copy insertions of T-DNA in two independent ko lines, named ko 1 and ko 2 (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Further, T-DNA free, edited, homozygous lines were selected by segregation and verified by PCR (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Among the two ko lines selected, ko 1 had a 1-nt insertion in exon2 of H2A.Xa, while H2A.Xb targeting resulted in a 1-nt deletion in exon1, both mutants leading to missense mutations. The ko 2 plant also induced a 1-nt insertion at H2A.Xa and had a 1-nt deletion in exon1 of H2A.Xb, again leading to missense mutation (Supplementary Fig. S2E). The ko of H2A.X at the protein levels was verified through western blot analysis using H2A.X-specific antibody (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S2F). In agreement with the absence of H2A.X, ko 1 did not show a positive signal for H2A.X upon nuclear immunostaining (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S2E). Since H2A.X gets upregulated under DNA damage, we generated constitutively expressing (OE) H2A.Xa lines with 3×FLAG tag as a control that somewhat mimics DNA damage response (Supplementary Fig. S3A and G). Among the five transgenic lines obtained, OE 1 and OE 2 plants having T-DNA insertions, as assayed through junction-fragment Southern analysis, were selected (Supplementary Fig. S3B and C). Both transgenic lines expressed H2A.X at very high levels, as observed though RT-qPCR as well as the western analysis (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S3D–F).

The h2a.x ko and H2A.X OE lines had several developmental abnormalities. The ko line showed longer roots, while the overall seedling length was not significantly altered (Fig. 2A–D). On the other hand, the OE lines displayed shorter primary axile roots but had longer lateral roots (Fig. 2A–D and Supplementary Fig. S4C). The OE plants also had high levels of anthocyanin, particularly in the stems and awns of seeds (Fig. 2E–G). Apart from this, the ko seedlings had increased chlorophyll content (Fig. 2H), reduced height, increased tiller and panicle numbers, and a delayed heading date (Fig. 2J–L and Supplementary Fig. S4D). Both ko and OE plants had significant reproductive defects with partially sterile panicles with fewer seeds in each panicle (Fig. 2I and M). The seeds were smaller, and the weight of the seeds was also reduced in ko lines (Fig. 2M and Supplementary Fig. S4E). Both in ko and OE lines, pollen viability was reduced (Fig. 2N and O). The OE plants exhibited partial male sterility while ko pollens were deformed (Fig. 2Q and Supplementary Fig. S4L). The ko plants showed dehydrated stigmas, while anthocyanin accumulation was observed in the stigmas of OE plants (Fig. 2P). All these phenotypes conclusively indicate that both ko and constitutive expression of H2A.X led to severe phenotypic abnormalities.

Since h2a.x ko plants set very few viable seeds, we also generated H2A.X knockdown plants (kd) using artificial microRNAs (amiR) designed to target both isoforms in a single construct (Supplementary Fig. S3H). Optimal amiRs were selected based on the previously identified position specificity [99, 100]. We obtained eight kd lines having high expression of amiR, as seen in the northern analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3I–K). Among these, we selected kd 1, which showed around 50% reduction in the accumulation of both isoforms of H2A.X (Supplementary Fig. S3J–L). Similar to ko lines, kd lines also had abnormal growth and development-associated phenotypes, such as increased seedling root length and delayed flowering (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B, and F). Similar to ko, the kd lines also showed increased panicle number, reduced panicle filling rate, reduced seed number, and seed weight (Supplementary Fig. S4G–K). As expected, the kd phenotypes were less severe when compared to ko lines. Together, these results suggest a development-associated role of rice H2A.X beyond DDR.

H2A.X mutants display altered expression of development- and stress-related genes

In order to understand the molecular basis for the altered phenotypes in H2A.X mis-expression lines, we performed RNA sequencing of 14-day-old seedlings and obtained a mapping efficiency of 95% to the rice genome (IRGSP 1.0) (Supplementary Table S1). We obtained 367 upregulated and 130 downregulated genes in ko, as well as 612 upregulated and 353 downregulated genes in OE lines with a stringent log2 1.5-fold cutoff (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. S5A, and Supplementary Data S1 and S2). In agreement with root growth and chlorophyll-related phenotypes observed in the transgenic lines, several key genes in these pathways were altered in ko plants (Fig. 3A). GO analysis of DEGs in ko indicated an enrichment of photosynthetic and pigment-related genes (Fig. 3B). On the other hand, GO analysis of OE upregulated genes indicated enrichment in phenylpropanoid pathway genes, while downregulated genes in OE were associated with photosynthesis and related processes (Supplementary Fig. S5B and C). The upregulated genes common in ko and kd showed enrichment for important plant metabolic processes and stress responses (Supplementary Figs S5D and S6A; Supplementary Data S3). A principal component analysis (PCA) with ko DEGs indicated that the set of genes altered in h2a.x ko are very different from any other stress, including DNA damage stress (Fig. 3C) [87]. In agreement with the root-associated phenotypes, expression of genes involved in root development were altered in H2A.X mis-expression lines. The genes positively regulating root growth were highly expressed in ko, while those involved in negative regulation showed high expression in OE lines (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. S5F, and Supplementary Data S4). Increased chlorophyll content in ko plants was associated with increased expression of photosynthesis associated genes (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. S5F). Similarly, the genes associated with pigmentation were highly expressed in OE plants (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S5F).

Previous studies [67, 101] indicated that h2a.x ko in Arabidopsis and kd in human cell lines led to DNA hypomethylation. H2A.X was also found to be essential for regulating DNA methylation specifically in endosperm tissues [101]. In order to understand whether the gene expression changes observed in the rice h2a.x ko mutants were due to altered DNA methylation, we performed targeted gene-specific bisulfite PCRs in the promoters of several mis-expressed genes including SMALL AUXIN-UP RNA 8 (OsSAUR8) and GDSL ESTERASE/LIPASE PROTEIN 4 (OsGDSL/OsGELP4) (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S6C–G). There was clearly an absence of correlation with promoter DNA methylation levels and gene expression in transgenic lines (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S6C–G). Since promoter sRNA levels and sRNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) are involved in regulating DNA methylation, we performed genome-wide sRNA sequencing in 14-day old seedlings derived from WT, h2a.x kd and OE lines. This analysis clearly indicated that levels of sRNAs and DNA methylation did not correlate with gene expression changes in H2A.X mis-expression lines (Fig. 3H, Supplementary Figs S5G and S6B, and Supplementary Data S5). These results conclusively indicate that sRNA-mediated functions did not directly contribute to gene expression changes and associated plant phenotypes among H2A.X mis-expression lines, albeit in normal unstressed conditions.

H2A.X represses deposition of active H3K4me3 marks over thousands of gene promoters

Since histone variants alter deposition and maintenance of histone marks over the genomic regions that they decorate, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) for active and inactive histone marks (H3K4me3 and H3K9me2) in 14-day-old seedlings derived from WT and ko lines. As observed previously in rice [102], H3K4me3 mark was predominantly associated with promoters (Supplementary Fig. S7B). There were around 3700 additional peaks of H3K4me3 in ko plants, ~15% increase over 24 109 peaks in WT plants, based on a stringent two-fold enrichment cutoff (Supplementary Fig. S7C and Supplementary Data S6). Apart from the increase in number of peaks there was an overall increase in H3K4me3 enrichment in ko over the H3K4me3-marked regions shared with WT (Fig. 4A).

In order to explore if the additional as well as enriched peaks for H3K4me3 correlated with the H2A.X binding regions, we performed an H2A.X ChIP-seq experiment in WT plants using native antibody raised against rice H2A.X. There were around 23 158 H2A.X peaks in seedling tissues with a stringent cut-off of two-fold enrichment (Supplementary Data S7). These regions largely overlapped with promoters (60.9%) and distal intergenic regions (28.54%) (Supplementary Table S4). The overall distribution of H2A.X in rice matched the genome wide distribution in Arabidopsis except an unusually high binding to 3′-UTR in rice (3.48% compared to 0.28% in Arabidopsis) [7] (Supplementary Table S4). The peak width of H2A.X was broader with a median peak width of around 900 bp, while that of H3K4me3 was 704 bp in WT and 780 in ko (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Surprisingly, the peak boundaries of H2A.X peaks exhibited enrichment of H3K4me3 marks and this mark showed even more enrichment in ko (Fig. 4B). The increased H3K4me3 marks spread over to a total of 1.4 MB genomic region when compared to WT levels, while its loss was only over 0.075 MB genomic region in ko (Fig. 4C). There was an increase of H3K4me3 marks over several TEs (1363), while the increase was substantial over 2216 protein-coding genes (Fig. 4D and E; Supplementary Fig. S7D and E).

The distribution of H2A.X peaks in genes was mostly in the gene body while exhibiting a sharp reduction in the TSS sites. On the other hand, H3K4me3 marks were highly enriched in the TSS with a sharp reduction in gene bodies (Fig. 4H). This opposite pattern of enrichment was even more specific in H2A.X-bound genes. Most of the H3K4me3 was more strictly in the TSS and 3′UTR in H2A.X bound genes when compared to all genes where there is a more dispersed distribution of H3K4me3 over the TSS and gene bodies. Surprisingly, in ko, there was an overall increase in H3K4me3 levels in TSS spreading to the gene bodies (Supplementary Fig. S7G). Strikingly, the genes that gained H3K4me3 in ko had a distribution of H3K4me3 over their gene bodies without any strict bias towards the TSS regions (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. S8A). These results suggest that H2A.X restricts H3K4me3 over both TSS and gene bodies. An increased enrichment of active H3K4me3 marks coincided with increased expression of genes in ko and such genes included those associated with development (Fig. 4G and Supplementary Fig. S7F and H). Similarly, 115 genes that lost H3K4me3 showed a clear reduction in expression in ko (Fig. 4E and F). These results indicate that H2A.X acts as a repressor of H3K4me3 marks over protein coding genes.

In order to study if the loss of H2A.X and H3K4me3 neo-deposition led to gene expression changes, we checked H3K4me3 enrichment over DEGs obtained from h2a.x ko plants and found increased H3K4me3 in the upregulated genes (Fig. 4I and Supplementary Fig. S7I). On the other hand, the downregulated genes in ko did not show any substantial increase of H3K4me3 marks (Supplementary Fig. S7J). Since H2A.X displayed co-operative binding near the H3K4me3 peaks, we overlapped peaks of H2A.X and H3K4me3 marks and found noticeable overlap of these features across 10 000 sites when the H2A.X peaks overlapping 2 kb regions were considered (Fig. 4J). From this analysis, we obtained 645 genes that also gained H3K4me3 and significant H2A.X binding. As expected, these genes showed pronounced high expression in ko plants (Fig. 4K). Among the genes with increased levels of H3K4me3 marks, CYCLASE-LIKE 4 (OsCYL4), PHOSPHATE-INDUCED PROTEIN 1 (OsPhi-1)-related gene, ROOT GROWTH ASSOCIATED 1 CYS-PEROXIREDOXIN B (Os1-CysPrxB), and ZINC FINGER PROTEIN 350 (OsZFP350), as well as the photosynthesis-related HYDROXYPYRUVATE REDUCTASE 1 (OsHPR1) were associated with development (Supplementary Fig. S8B and E). Apart from this, there were also stretches of genic regions that gained H3K4me3 and most of the genes in these regions displayed increased expression in ko (Supplementary Fig. S8C). All these results conclusively indicate that upregulated genes in ko were due to increased H3K4me3 levels, and H2A.X represses the deposition of H3K4me3 marks over development-associated genes.

Although a genome-wide increase in H3K4me3 across many TEs and repeat regions was not observed (Fig. 4D), there were ~1363 TEs that gained H3K4me3 marks in ko (Supplementary Fig. S7E). This suggests that, similar to genic regions, there was increased levels of H3K4me3 marks in the TEs also (Supplementary Fig. S9A). This increase in active H3K4me3 levels was sufficient to activate RNA expression of the TEs, predominantly from LTR elements and a few repeats (Fig. 4L and Supplementary Fig. S9B and D). Transcription of TEs and expression from genomic regions in kd or OE lines did not lead to their RNA silencing, the hallmark of which is the production of 24-nt sRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S9C). The fate and activities of these TE-encoded RNAs in ko lines is an important avenue for future research.

The increase in the active mark H3K4me3 might have resulted from increased expression of writers of this mark or due to the reduced expression of its erasers. In order to understand this further, we checked the expression of histone modifiers in a candidate-gene specific manner. Among the writers that included several histone methyltransferases under Trithorax family, there was a negligible change in the expression in ko, while erasers of H3K4me3 including several Jumonji and LSD1-LIKE ZINC FINGER PROTEIN (Lsd1) showed marked reduction in the expression (Supplementary Fig. S8C). It is possible that the specific deposition pattern of H2A.X and H3K4me3 marks over genes and the reduced expression of several histone demethylases might have led to increased H3K4me3 and altered gene expression in ko plants.

Loss of H2A.X led to spreading of H3K9me2 across both genic and TE regions

In order to understand if H2A.X can influence other H2A variants and associated histone marks, we performed ChIP-seq using H3K9me2 mark-specific antibody in 14-day-old seedlings of WT and ko plants. Using a stringent cut-off of 1.5-fold, we identified around 8585 peaks in WT conditions overlapping as expected with TEs and repeats (Supplementary Fig. S10A and Supplementary Data S6). The peak width of H3K9me2 were around 350 bp. In ko, the number of H3K9me2 peaks increased to 11 593, indicating about a staggering 25% increase (Supplementary Fig. S10A–D). When we considered the length of DNA bound by the H3K9me2 mark, there was negligible decrease in ko (0.65 Mb region, 1775 peaks), whereas gained genomic region was 57% more than WT (4783 peaks, 1.9 Mb) (Fig. 5A). Most of the H3K9me2-gained regions were enriched with TEs, while very little increase was observed over genes (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S10E and F). Among the 4752 TEs that overlapped with increased H3K9me2 marks in ko, there were categories from both type I and type II TE members, such as Gypsy, Copia, and En-spm (Supplementary Fig. S10G). Surprisingly, these TEs showed negligible expression in WT, and the change in the expression of these TEs or sRNAs from these TEs was not observed in ko, indicating that the increased H3K9me2 did not alter either TE expression or sRNA production from these sites (Supplementary Fig. S10H–K). These results indicate that H2A.X restricts the distribution of H3K9me2 marks over TEs. It is likely that these TEs were part of the constitutive chromatin that did not require RdDM or other regulation to keep this region suppressed.

Interestingly, H3K9me2 marks also spread to genic regions encompassing ~200 genes when genes and their 1 kb promoter was considered. Among the 51 selected genes having expression in the tissues checked and having H3K9me2 peaks over gene body or 1 kb promoter regions, 27 genes showed reduced expression in ko plants as expected (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S11A). The other 24 genes that had increased H3K9me2, also had increased levels of active H3K4me3 marks in the regulatory regions, indicating that repressive mark was not sufficient to compete with the active marks (Supplementary Fig. S11B). Among the 27 genes that showed reduced expression in ko, there were development-associated genes including Os07g0476900 (CDSP32, a plant thioredoxin involved in photosynthesis) and an E3 ubiquitin ligase gene, Os07g0258700 (OsC2-BTB1). We performed RT-qPCR to further confirm the repressive role of H3K9me2 in these genes and found that the genes showed reduced expression in ko, while they were slightly upregulated in OE (Fig. 5D and E). These results further confirm the regulatory role of H2A.X in a set of protein coding genes by altering repressive H3K9me2 marks. Apart from this, there were several genes that lost H3K9me2 in the ko and they showed increased expression (Supplementary Fig. S11C). Together, these results indicate that altered histone marks, both active and repressive, contributed to the gene expression changes in ko plants, indicating that H2A.X dictates these important changes on a genome-scale.

H2A.X inhibits incorporation of histone variant H2A.W to restrict H3K9me2 spreading

We observed overlapping distribution of H3K9me2 with H2A.X binding sites among at least 2073 sites when 2-kb extended H2A.X peaks were considered (Supplementary Fig. S10L). However, there were also 724 unique regions with newer peaks of H3K9me2 in ko (Fig. 5F). We hypothesized that this increased H3K9me2 spreading might be due to increased activity of writers of this mark or altered incorporation of other H2A variants. It is well known that H2A variants, especially H2A.W, has the potential to influence H3K9me2 distribution [44]. In order to further study if the gain of H3K9me2 is associated with H2A.W, we performed ChIP-seq for histone variant H2A.W using the custom generated antibody that did not cross-react with other variants (Supplementary Fig. S1C). We also analysed the publicly available H2A.Z ChIP-seq datasets from rice seedlings (Supplementary Fig. S12A). We obtained around 37 853 peaks of H2A.Z and most of them were associated with gene promoters (Supplementary Data S7). On the other hand, H2A.X peaks were mostly associated with promoters and distal intergenic regions (Supplementary Fig. S12A). We also obtained around 30 197 peaks of H2A.W in WT and as shown in Arabidopsis, most of these peaks were associated with distal intergenic regions (61.77%). In addition to high levels of H2A.W over heterochromatin regions, H2A.W peaks were also enriched over promoters in rice (35.48%). Similar to the enrichment of these marks over specific chromatin regions, they displayed unique enrichment over either TEs or genes (Fig. 5G). Around 14 020 peaks of H2A.X overlapped with 11 623 genes, while 11 622 peaks overlapped with 15 013 TEs. H2A.Z peaks were mostly seen associated with genes, with around 21 247 peaks overlapping with 23 429 genes, while 16 606 peaks overlapping with 26 140 TEs. As expected, most of the H2A.W peaks, i.e. around 26 185 peaks, overlapped with 47 883 TEs, while fewer peaks of around 3999 overlapped with 3494 genes (Fig. 5G, Supplementary Fig. S13D, and Supplementary Data S8).

Unlike Arabidopsis where H2A.W preferentially binds to centromeric and peri-centromeric regions having TEs, rice H2A.W binding was spread out without a strict preference for peri-centromeric regions (Supplementary Figs S12B and S13A, B). This surprising observation might be due to the difference in the nature of heterochromatin distribution between these two model plants. H3K9me2 and H2A.W were strictly restricted to heterochromatic regions in Arabidopsis, but in rice, heterochromatic marks were spread out to regions including genic regions in most of the chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. S12B) [103–105]. H2A.W also showed enriched binding to repeats and a few promoters of protein-coding genes (Supplementary Figs S12A and S13A, B).

We analysed the expression levels of genes bound by these variants and observed that H2A.X bound genes were well-expressed without having enrichment of either H2A.W or H2A.Z (Fig. 5H and I) and this pattern matched previous observations [106]. On the other hand, H2A.W bound genes were generally low-expressed, while H2A.Z bound genes exhibited variable gene expression mostly in the range between the levels observed in H2A.X and H2A.W bound genes (Fig. 5H). In order to explore if the histone variants have shared genes where they bind to influence histone marks, the peaks of H2A.X overlapping with genes and TEs were overlapped with the peaks of histone variants and histone marks. Both H2A.W and H2A.Z peaks were seen overlapping with H2A.X peaks across genes, while H2A.X peaks overlapped with TEs and H2A.W bound regions. Surprisingly, levels of both H3K4me3 and H3K9me2 marks over TEs were increased in ko (Fig. 5J and K).

Further, we found that H2A.X and H2A.W bound peaks overlapped in around 5822 regions, while H2A.W overlapped with H2A.Z in 2223 genomic regions (Supplementary Fig. S13E). The common regions bound by H2A.X and H2A.W had more enrichment of H2A.W than H2A.X (Supplementary Fig. S13F). Since H3K9me2 marks that generally overlap with H2A.W were enriched in ko, we analysed the levels of H3K9me2, and found that this mark enriched much more in ko than WT, indicating that H2A.X influences distribution of H3K9me2 marks (Fig. 5L). These 5822 genomic regions contained largely TEs (12 140) and genes (934). The increased level of H3K9me2 was more pronounced over TEs than genes (Supplementary Fig. S13G and H). However, these genes exhibited reduced expression in ko when compared to WT or OE (Fig. 5M). On the other hand, several genes having H2A.X binding exhibited increased expression in ko (Supplementary Fig. S13I).

Since H2A.X peaks overlapped mostly with H2A.W, and these variants in Arabidopsis appear to form homotypic nucleosomes [7], we explored if H2A.X and H2A.W can co-occupy in a single nucleosome in rice, using micrococcal nuclease treatment followed by IP (MNase IP). In this assay, we could clearly see that both the variants form homotypic nucleosomes in vivo (Fig. 6A), indicating that they cannot occupy one nucleosome together. We performed ChIP seq for H2A.W in ko to check if there is any competition between these variants for specific nucleosomes. In fact, there was enriched levels of H3K9me2 over H2A.W peaks obtained in ko (Fig. 6B). Similar to the gain of H3K9me2 over TEs in ko, there was increased levels of H2A.W over TEs in ko plants, while the overall global distribution of H2A.W was not altered in ko (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S14A and C). The peak width of H2A.W was also similar between WT and ko (Supplementary Fig. S14B). Surprisingly, H2A.X showed reduced enrichment in TSS that is otherwise enriched with active H3K4me3 marks, whereas H2A.W was enriched specifically over TEs in proximal regions (Fig. 6C). We also observed a global redistribution of H2A.W in ko where it distributed to the neighbouring non-coding regions sometimes upto a few kb away from TSS (Supplementary Fig. S14D). Such a redistribution was confirmed using immunostaining of nuclei (Fig. 6E). Rice H2A.W was distributed in the periphery of the nuclei unlike Arabidopsis where it forms specific puncta corresponding to its distribution over centromeres [44]. Interestingly, H2A.W showed a more uniform distribution in ko and more signal towards the centre of the nuclei where H2A.X enrichment was observed in WT, indicating that H2A.W can replace this variant (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Fig. S14E–G).

H2A.W and H3K9me2 co-occupy similar genomic regions, while their increased enrichment was seen over TEs in ko

In order to understand the genome-wide redistribution of H2A.W in ko, we intersected the peaks overlapping with genes and TEs in WT and ko. The enrichment of H2A.W over genes was reduced in ko, while it increased over TEs (Fig. 6D and F; Supplementary Fig. S14I), without distinguishing between the types and families of TEs (Supplementary Fig. S14H and I). Increased occupancy of H2A.W over several TEs did not alter the expression levels of the TEs or sRNA levels from these regions in ko, probably because of its low expression (Supplementary Fig. S14J and K). There was also a redistribution of H2A.W peaks over several genes in ko. There were around 1336 genes that lost H2A.W, while 777 genes gained H2A.W in ko lines. Surprisingly, the genes that lost H2A.W in ko showed increased expression, indicating the repressive role of H2A.W over these genes in WT (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Fig. S15A–C). As expected, most of the H3K9me2 marks (5858) overlapped with H2A.W in WT, while there were 697 regions gaining both H2A.W and H3K9me2 in ko (Fig. 6H). These new H2A.W and H3K9me2 acquired peaks mostly overlapped with TEs, specifically of Gypsy family (Fig. 6I and J). Surprisingly, we could observe increased level of H2A.W in ko over H3K9me2 peaks obtained in ko, suggesting there is a global level increase of H2A.W and H3K9me2 in ko (Supplementary Fig. S15D). In addition to this, we observed higher levels of H2A.W over H3K9me2 gained regions (4783), as well as H2A.X and H2A.W bound common TEs (12 140) (Fig. 6K and Supplementary Fig. S15E). The increase of H2A.W over H3K9me2 gained regions was also mostly in TEs, including all DNA elements and LTRs (Supplementary Fig. S15F). Among the 697 regions that overlapped with 21 genes where H2A.W and H3K9me2 gained in ko, most of the genes were very less expressed in WT (Supplementary Fig. S15H). RT-qPCR analysis of two selected genes that gained H3K9me2 and H2A.W in promoters of Os07g0430501 (Thionin-like peptide-an antimicrobial peptide) and Os04g0269600 (OsDA02, important for oxidative deamination), showed clear downregulation of gene expression, indicating that H2A.X positively regulated their expression in WT by inhibiting both these repressive marks (Supplementary Fig. S15I). Several of the genes in this category did not show clear downregulation in ko, probably because of their very low expression, or involvement of other regulations (Supplementary Fig. S15G and H). Surprisingly, several genes that gained H3K9me2 marks and reduced expression in ko had increased levels of H2A.W, suggesting the association of these repressive marks (Supplementary Fig. S16). Apart from the collective gain of H2A.W and H3K9me2 in specific gene promoters, there were large chromosomal regions which gained both these marks in ko (Fig. 6L). All these results indicate that, although H2A.X suppresses gene expression, its competition with H2A.W and suppression of H3K9me2, ensures expression of a few specific genes.

Development-associated regulatory role of H2A.X is largely independent of its role in DNA damage responses

Since H2A.X is a well-known player in DNA damage response across eukaryotes, we explored if development-associated genes under its control are also contributing to DNA damage response. Since DNA damage response across plants requires H2A.X phosphorylation at C-terminal serine position, we expected the OE lines to mimic DNA damage [50]. We probed DNA damage responsive genes in rice and found that H2A.X binds to these genes (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Data S9). We analysed the expression levels of DNA damage responsive genes in H2A.X mis-expression lines in unstressed condition, and found that several of these genes were upregulated in OE, indicating that H2A.X is indeed a regulator of these genes (Supplementary Fig. S17A). Most importantly, the levels of DNA damage responsive genes were not altered in ko, indicating that the genes that are directly regulated by H2A.X for development are not among the DNA damage response genes. Further, we treated WT plants with DNA-damaging agent bleomycin (B5, a slow DNA-damaging agent that induces damage over time) (5 mg/l) as well as with UV treatment (3 KJ, a quick DNA-damaging agent (Supplementary Fig. S17B). Surprisingly, the OE plants showed similar expression pattern to that of WT upon treatment with bleomycin but not with UV treatment when all DNA damage responsive genes were considered (Supplementary Fig. S17C and D). In addition to this, the expression of DEGs in OE plants was similar between OE and WT plants treated with bleomycin (Fig. 7B). Further we selected the bleomycin responsive genes and found that they were also bound predominantly by H2A.X (Supplementary Fig. S17E–G and Supplementary Data S9). These bleomycin responsive genes consist of several well-known genes that come under both nucleotide excision repair and DNA double strand break repair (DSB) pathways, such as OsMORC and OsRAD family proteins (Supplementary Fig. S17H and I). The upregulated expression of these genes in OE indicated activation of enhanced stress response without DNA damage (Fig. 7C and D, Supplementary Fig. S18A, and Supplementary Data S10). In addition to this, OE plants that exhibited DNA damage sensitive phenotypes were resistant to external DNA damage given by bleomycin treatment unlike ko plants (Supplementary Fig. S18B–D). As shown in Arabidopsis previously, the root growth of ko plants were much more reduced under DNA damage, while OE plants were largely unaffected, mimicking enhanced DNA damage response (Supplementary Fig. S18B-G).

Analysis of loss-of-function mutants of histone chaperone of H2A.X correlated with gene regulatory role of H2A.X

In order to confirm the regulatory role of H2A.X in gene expression, we generated double kd lines of SPT16 isoforms, the partner of SSRP1 in forming the FACT complex (Supplementary Fig. S19A). Since the protein complex is critical for several biological processes, as shown in different systems [56, 58, 107], we failed to generate viable ko lines. However, the transgenic lines with abundant amiR expression and reduced target mRNAs were viable. The fact knock-down (kd) lines had reduced expression of both the isoforms of SPT16 (Supplementary Fig. S19B–D). These plants showed strong developmental and reproductive defects, including reduced height and abnormal panicles (Fig. 7E and F). Although ko plants showed similar phenotypes, it is important to note that only a part of phenotypes observed in fact kd lines might be due to H2A.X. In order to understand whether the gene expression changes observed in ko plants are also controlled by FACT complex, we performed RNA seq of 14-day old fact kd lines. We obtained 807 upregulated and 558 downregulated genes in fact kd lines and these genes had similar enrichment of GO category as among ko DEGs (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. S19E and F, and Supplementary Data S11). Surprisingly, the DEGs in fact kd lines were similar in expression to that of ko, suggesting that the RNA levels in fact kd lines are also reflected in ko lines (Fig. 7G–J and Supplementary Fig. S19G and H). Several developmental, reproductive, and stress-associated genes were misexpressed similarly in ko and FACT kd lines [108–113]. The shared upregulated genes among these lines had increased levels of H3K4me3, while shared downregulated genes did not show enrichment for H3K9me2 (Supplementary Fig. S20A and B). These results indicate that the developmental role of H2A.X is partly attributed to the abnormal phenotypes in the mutants of its chaperone complex.

Discussion

Histone variants are conserved across different organisms and they provide specific properties important for development and in mediating stress responses, often in a tissue-specific manner [6, 7, 17, 22–25, 114]. Functions of H2A variants are well-studied in plants, partly because they exhibit clear amino acid variations in C-terminal tails [106, 115]. Variations in the C-terminal tails such as the presence of multiple H2A.W isoforms with duplicated SPKK motif are observed in rice, but their importance is unknown [28, 44]. H2A.X has high expression in seedling and reproductive tissues and in agreement with this, ko plants displayed defects in both seedling and reproductive development. The increased roots observed in rice ko and kd was also observed in Arabidopsis mutant under ABA treatment [68]. Several genes including OsHAC1, Os1-CysPrxB, OsRcc3, EXO70FX15 and OsZFP350, involved in positively regulating the root growth were upregulated in ko [116–119]. The chlorophyll content in both ko and kd lines were high compared to WT, and the photosynthesis associated genes that exhibited higher enrichment of H2A.X including OsHPR1 and CDSP32 were upregulated in ko [120, 121]. These results document previously unappreciated role of H2A.X in mediating specific development-associated processes.

The distribution of H2A.X over gene bodies and TEs suggests its potential in regulating both genes and TEs. The enrichment of H2A.X over gene body and its absence over TSS is opposite to that of the H3K4me3 enrichment over TSS and its reduced levels on the gene bodies. The enrichment of H2A.X over gene bodies might be ensuring the active H3K4me3 marks are enriched over TSS but not in the gene bodies. Such a role for H2A.X can be proposed based on the observations that the levels of H3K4me3 over TSS was much more in H2A.X bound genes when compared to all other genes. Also, H2A.X bound genes displayed higher expression compared to genes bound by other H2A variants. More importantly, the genes that gained H3K4me3 in ko had increased enrichment of H3K4me3 over gene bodies but not in TSS, further suggesting the regulatory role of H2A.X in influencing H3K4me3 marks. Molecular basis for such a repressive function of H2A.X is unclear and these are several possibilities. The increased H3K4me3 might be an indirect readout of increased transcription or due to the increased occupancy of other histone variants such as H2A.Z or due to the incorporation of canonical H2A over these regions in ko. The increased H3K4me3 over TEs also led to their expression changes, since most of the TEs that gained H3K4me3 in ko plants exhibited de-repression (Fig. 4l). Recent studies indicated that H3K4me3 mark can induce DNA de-methylation by attracting DNA de-methylases in plants, and it will be interesting to explore this among H2A.X regulated H3K4me3 gained genes and TEs [122]. Signatures of repression of gene expression activity of H2A.X differs from other H2A variants such as H2A.Z. H2A.Z bound genes enrich with repressive H3K27me3 marks and the inducibility of H2A.Z occupied genes under stress is very different from highly expressed active genes having both H3K4me3 and H2A.X [34, 41, 123–125]. The polycomb group proteins (PcG) consisting of polycomb repressive complex 1 and 2 (PRC 1 and 2) regulate H2A and H2A.Z ubiquitination to induce H3K27me3 marks at several development-associated genes [42, 123, 126]. The C-terminal lysine residue required for this function is conserved in H2A.X [127], hence, there is a possibility that PRC-mediated control intervenes H2A.X functions.

The ALFIN-LIKE (AL) proteins contain a C-terminal PHD finger that binds to H3K4me3 marks to facilitate H2AUb in H2A and H2A.Z variants [128]. AL proteins interact with the SWR1 remodelling complex which is a chaperone of H2A.Z [129]. For AL proteins to act on the genome, H3K4me3 mark is essential and therefore it will be interesting to see if the incorporation of either H2A or H2A.Z in place of H2A.X in ko lead to increased levels of H3K4me3 through H2AUb. Such a regulation in plants is unlikely with H2A.Z, since this variant inhibits active H3K4me3 from gene promoters to promote repressive H3K27me3 marks [41].

ChIP-seq of FACT complex in Arabidopsis, a known chaperone of H2A.X [53, 54], revealed its binding to the euchromatin regions containing active genes, whereas it was less enriched over heterochromatic regions [57]. The distribution of FACT complex with actively expressed genes is very similar to the enrichment of H2A.X over active genes in rice and Arabidopsis. Similar to altered gene expression that we observed here, there are several studies in Arabidopsis that mention the gene regulatory function of FACT complex [58, 130]. Similarity between the transcriptome of fact kd and ko plants suggests that at least a part of gene expression change in fact kd is associated with the defects in H2A.X incorporation. The involvement of FACT complex in regulating anthocyanin accumulation under stress conditions correlates with the increased levels of anthocyanin observed in OE plants [131]. The shared developmental and reproductive defects observed in ko and fact kd along with shared transcriptomic changes are interesting correlations, however, it is also important to note that FACT complex has a bigger role in regulating several key processes including transcription and replication and hence, secondary effects cannot be ruled out [132]. FACT complex is crucial for suppressing the transcription from intergenic TSS by regulating H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 marks in Arabidopsis [133]. The repressive role of H2A.X in H3K4me3 deposition is similar to the suppressive role of FACT complex in preventing deposition of H3K4me3 over intergenic TSS. It will be interesting to see what proportion of the genome-wide H3K4me3 levels are dependent on H2A.X and FACT complex.

It is striking to note that the gene regulatory function of H2A.X under DNA damage is de-coupled from its role in development. The influence of H2A.X in regulating global DDR is linked to its phosphorylation at the conserved SQEF motif. Interestingly, several genes that were upregulated under bleomycin were already upregulated in OE. Moreover, the OE plants showed severe stressed phenotypes, some of which can be correlated with DNA damage response, even without any DNA damage. The OE plants might have accumulated high levels of γ-H2A.X protein mimicking DNA damage. It is known across several model systems that phosphorylated H2A.X helps in upregulating gene expression either by binding to the nicked DNA to increase the transcription from these sites, or by partnering with different proteins to bind to the promoter of specific genes [66–68]. It will be interesting to check if that kind of gene expression trigger based on H2A.X phosphorylation is seen in OE plants.

The nature of specific H2A variants binding to specific chromatin types and regions might not be similar across flowering plants. It has been well established that occupancy of heterochromatic regions in the nuclei among diverse plants showed unique properties [17, 134]. Any variation in the distribution of chromatin territories has the potential to alter functions of histone variants having specific regulatory regions to decorate. The distribution of TEs is strictly towards the heterochromatic regions including centromeric repeats and telomere containing chromatin arms in Arabidopsis [135]. However, in rice, the TEs are distributed across the genome interspaced with genic regions [8, 9]. Enrichment of rice H2A.W in the TEs proximal to protein-coding genes was clearly observed, as was the H2A.X enrichment in the gene bodies adjacent to them. This might have led to the enrichment of H2A.W in ko plants contributing to the altered expression of genes coming under the control of H2A.X and its competitor H2A.W. This diversity of functions of variants between two species is extremely interesting and a valuable starting point to explore its evolutionary significance. The genes bound by H2A.W were very diverse and low expressed when compared to genes bound by other H2A variants. The increased enrichment of H2A.W over TE regions in ko plants suggests that H2A.X also has a regulatory role in H2A.W enrichment of non-coding regions. Unlike in Arabidopsis, among monocots including rice, the nuclear puncta representing the centromere is not formed when H3K9me2 mark is assayed [134]. Similarly, H3K9me2 marks in rice and wheat show distribution in the periphery of the nuclei without forming the puncta. Similar distribution of H2A.W in rice and strong overlap of H3K9me2 and H2A.W peaks suggests that these two features are important in regulating heterochromatin in rice. It is also known that H2A.W restricts H1 to provide access for DNA methyltransferases to the centromeric repeats [136]. Even though the DNA methylation in specific regions was not altered in rice h2a.x kd, it will be interesting to check if global DNA methylation pattern gets altered. Most of the regions with H2A.X and H2A.W peaks were on TEs, often creating mosaics in the rice genome. These TEs exhibited increased levels of both H2A.W and H3K9me2 levels in ko, further suggesting the competition of H2A.X and H2A.W over TE regions. Surprisingly, the increased enrichment of H2A.W was enough to enrich H3K9me2 in these regions as well as globally increased levels of H2A.W was observed in the peak regions of H3K9me2 in ko plants. The evolutionary significance of the pattern of distribution of H2A.W and H3K9me2, and reasons why specific genes and specific TEs are enriched in such regions will be worth exploring.
