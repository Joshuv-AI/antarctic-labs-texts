Yu-Shiba-Rusinov-assisted Andreev transport in a molecular junction between superconductors
Authors: Cristina Mier, Alex Fétida, Roberto Robles, Parmenio Boronat, Divya Jyoti, Nicolás Lorente, Laurent Limot, Deung-Jang Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnetic impurities in superconductors host Yu-Shiba-Rusinov (YSR) bound states, whereas weak superconducting junctions sustain subharmonic charge transport through multiple Andreev reflections (MAR). We explore these phenomena using a molecular-scale junction created by placing a nickelocene (Nc) molecule atop an Fe adatom on Pb(111) and approaching it with a superconducting scanning-tunneling-microscope tip. Gradually increasing the junction transparency drives the system from conventional YSR spectroscopy in the tunneling regime to resonant MAR transport and, ultimately, to a Josephson-coupled state. The resulting spectra exhibit shifted MAR thresholds, asymmetric line shapes, and odd-even features that identify YSR-assisted MAR. A combined theoretical treatment of junction transparency and exchange coupling accounts for these observations, while density-functional calculations show that the Nc-Fe complex forms an antiferromagnetic ground state with a residual Fe spin-12 generating the YSR resonances. Our results demonstrate that the parameters governing YSR and MAR processes can be continuously tuned within a single superconducting nanojunction.Subject terms: Molecular self-assembly; Superconducting properties and materials; Surfaces, interfaces and thin films; Electronic properties and materials; Magnetic properties and materials

Introduction

Atomic- and molecular-scale junctions offer reproducibility, chemical tunability, and built-in functionality1. When such junctions are embedded between superconducting electrodes, qualitatively different transport regimes emerge2–5. For spin-bearing molecules, the superconducting gap suppresses quasiparticle decay channels and thereby enhances spin lifetimes, a desirable ingredient for quantum control6. At the same time, molecular magnetic anisotropy and surface hybridization determine how the spin couples to the electrodes and can drive quantum phase transitions7,8. In superconducting scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), the large BCS coherence peaks amplify inelastic features, enabling precise identification of spin excitations and selection rules9–11.

A key manifestation of magnetic exchange at the superconductor interface is the formation of Yu-Shiba-Rusinov (YSR) bound states inside the gap12–14, directly visible in differential conductance spectra3,9,15–18. In parallel, when two superconductors are weakly linked and biased at V, a subgap current is carried by multiple Andreev reflections (MAR), producing the characteristic subharmonic gap structure (SGS) with onsets

for unequal gaps Δt,s19–24. At very low bias, charge transport between two superconductors is dominated by the Josephson effect, in which Cooper pairs tunnel coherently between the electrodes. In STM junctions, however, the large junction impedance leads to strong thermal phase fluctuations, so that the Josephson contribution appears as a broadened finite-voltage peak rather than a dissipationless supercurrent21. Experiments on superconducting STM junctions further showed that such fluctuation-dominated Josephson tunneling coexists with SGS arising from MAR processes22. Subsequent work clarified the relation between Josephson transport, phase dynamics and MAR in superconducting vacuum junctions23,24. More generally, transport through a superconducting weak link evolves continuously from Josephson pair transfer near zero bias to MAR currents at finite bias, indicating that these processes form a unified transport regime rather than independent contributions25,26.

Although YSR spectroscopy (local electronic structure) and MAR (nonlinear transport) are often discussed separately, a magnetic weak link actually intertwines both: a discrete, spin-active in-gap resonance provides an energy- and spin-dependent transmission channel that can be part of the so-called “MAR ladder”. Theory anticipates that this interplay reorganizes the SGS, shifts apparent MAR thresholds, and yields interference line shapes, with pronounced odd-even effects when MAR sequences terminate on a YSR level27,28.

Here we show experimentally and theoretically that a single, chemically-defined molecular junction realizes this unified physics in a controlled manner. We use a superconducting STM tip to contact a nickelocene (Nc) molecule positioned above an interfacial Fe atom on Pb(111), thereby forming a spin-active superconducting weak link whose transparency can be tuned continuously by reducing the tunneling barrier. In the tunneling limit, the spectra are dominated by YSR resonances associated with the Nc–Fe complex. As the junction transparency increases, we observe (i) the emergence and growth of MAR features at the subharmonic voltages of Eq. (1), (ii) their systematic hybridization with the YSR resonance (YSR-assisted MAR), producing shifted and asymmetric subgap peaks with clear odd-even trends, and (iii) the eventual appearance of a Josephson contribution at zero bias. This continuity, from substrate-localized YSR spectroscopy to a phase-carrying weak link, demonstrates that MAR and YSR physics are two limits of the same coherent subgap scattering problem in a spin-active junction.

To interpret the data, we develop a non-equilibrium Green’s-function description of two BCS electrodes connected via a molecular bridge with electron transparency α, and a local exchange term for the interfacial Fe spin (classical-spin limit), following the theory of Refs. 27,28. In the present model, the impurity is treated as a classical spin generating a spin-dependent scattering potential. Classical-spin models capture the essential features of YSR states, particularly their energy, while quantum-spin treatments mainly introduce additional many-body effects such as Kondo screening or spin excitations29. The model, solved in Nambu-Keldysh space with a Floquet expansion for finite bias, captures YSR-assisted MAR without adjustable spectral templates and reproduces the observed evolution of peak positions and intensities as both junction transparency and exchange coupling increase27,30,31. The theory explains the non-monotonic evolution of features derived from the Δt + Δs coherence peaks and gives a unified picture of the parameters at play.

Experimental results

The system consists of self-assembled Nc layers on Pb(111)10,32 into which Fe atoms are codeposited. During growth, most Fe atoms diffuse beneath the molecular film and locally distort its registry, as observed previously for Ni–Nc co-adsorption33. Despite the known tendencies of transition-metal adatoms to migrate into Pb34,35 and of Nc to dissociate on metals36, our STM data confirm intact Nc layers hosting subsurface Fe atoms. Figure 1a shows a constant-current topograph where isolated bright protrusions mark Fe atoms beneath individual Nc molecules, producing an increased apparent height relative to their surroundings. The schematic in Fig. 1b depicts this configuration: a single Nc molecule adsorbed above an Fe atom at the interface. Depending on the adsorption configuration, Fe atoms may remain on top of the molecular layer or diffuse beneath an Nc molecule toward the Pb substrate. Only the latter configuration produces YSR in-gap states, reflecting the magnetic coupling of the Fe atom to the superconducting Pb surface. The spectroscopic features analyzed in this work correspond to this Nc–Fe configuration.

In order to characterize the SGS, a superconducting Pb-coated STM tip is employed throughout, which enhances the spectroscopic energy resolution beyond the thermal limit15,35,37. Differential conductance spectra acquired at different locations are shown in Fig. 1c. On the pristine Pb(111) surface (black curve), the spectrum displays the superconducting gap of Pb with coherence peaks at ±(Δt + Δs) ≈ ± 2.7 meV, reflecting the convolution of the substrate and Pb-coated tip densities of states. On a regular Nc molecule within the layer (red curve), we observe prominent inelastic steps at ±(D + Δt + Δs)/e = ± (6.5 ± 0.1) mV, where D = 3.9 meV is the Nc longitudinal anisotropy on Pb (111)10, accompanied by replicas of the superconducting coherence peaks9,10. The inelastic steps are characteristic of spin-flip excitations of the S = 1 ground state of the Nc molecule10,32. In contrast, spectra acquired on an Nc molecule sitting above an Fe atom (blue curve) reveal subgap resonances close to the superconducting coherence peaks shown by the bias-polarity asymmetry and the appearance of a Fano-like profile where the previous convoluted quasiparticle peaks of the clean surface were (black curve). These in-gap states correspond to YSR states induced by the exchange coupling between the Fe spin and the superconducting host15,34. The suppression of the inelastic steps in this configuration indicates that the S = 1 spin of the Nc is quenched by hybridization with the Fe atom, resulting in a composite Nc–Fe entity with distinct magnetic character. This also explains why the spectrum of the Nc–Fe complex resembles that of clean Pb(111) more than that of a pristine Nc molecule. In the latter case, the dominant fingerprint is the inelastic spin-excitation, which is absent in the Nc–Fe complex. The remaining difference with respect to clean Pb is the appearance of the bias-asymmetric YSR states near the coherence peaks. The transport model developed below is aimed at describing this spin-active YSR regime, not the inelastic S = 1 spectroscopy of pristine Nc.

Figure 1 demonstrates that embedding a single Fe atom beneath a single Nc of the molecular layer creates a well-defined superconducting impurity, visible as YSR states in tunneling spectroscopy. By approaching the Pb-coated tip to this site, we form a molecular-scale superconducting junction comprising two superconducting electrodes bridged by a spin-active Nc–Fe complex. As we show below, this configuration provides access to the interplay between YSR resonances and multiple Andreev reflection processes under controlled tuning of the junction transparency.

To explore how the junction evolves from tunneling spectroscopy into the transport regime, we gradually reduce the tip-sample distance while monitoring the conductance of the Nc–Fe device, as sketched in Fig. 2a. The conductance versus displacement trace in Fig. 2b shows an exponential increase at large tip-sample separations, consistent with tunneling transport, and a crossover towards higher transparency as the electrodes approach. Forward and backward sweeps reveal a small hysteresis, which we attribute to minute structural relaxations of the molecular junction upon mechanical loading. The spectra of Fig. 2c are recorded on the same Nc–Fe complex shown in Fig. 1c (blue curve). Starting from the tunneling spectrum where the YSR resonances are already visible, the junction conductance is progressively increased by approaching the STM tip, allowing us to follow the continuous evolution toward the MAR-dominated transport regime. In the low-conductance regime (upper panel), the spectra retain the YSR resonances already identified in Fig. 1c, which shift progressively from the vicinity of the 2Δ coherence peaks toward lower bias as the tip approaches (green arrows). At the same time, as the conduction from tip to sample and sample to tip tends to equalize when going into contact conditions, we observe a drastic reduction in the electron-hole asymmetry. At intermediate set-point conductances (G ≳ 0.1 G0), additional peaks appear at eV ≈ (Δt + Δs)/2, consistent with second-order MAR. Upon further increasing the barrier transparency, a sharp zero-bias feature develops, corresponding to the onset of the Josephson current through the molecular junction. In principle, the Josephson current is expected to appear at lower junction transparency than higher-order MAR processes because it corresponds to a first-order coherent tunneling process, whereas MAR involves multiple sequential Andreev reflections. Indeed, STM experiments with very high energy resolution have reported the onset of the Josephson current before MAR features become visible38. In our measurements, however, the early Josephson contribution is difficult to resolve because it appears as a very narrow zero-bias peak that is strongly broadened by thermal effects and phase fluctuations in the STM junction. Under these conditions, the finite-bias MAR structures are more easily identified in the differential conductance spectra.

The spectra also exhibit satellite structures near zero bias whose energies do not coincide with conventional n = 3 MAR. The thermal broadening in our experiments and the fact that MAR features reduce their inter-peak distance by 1/n do not permit us to assign this feature to uniquely YSR or MAR character, but rather being indicative of the mixing of both channels. We note that, with a superconducting tip at finite temperature, thermal tunneling via a YSR state can, in principle, generate conductance features at ∣eV∣ = Δtip − εYSR. In our experiment, however, no distinct peak is resolved at this energy in the low-conductance tunneling regime, where such a thermal contribution would be most naturally identified. Instead, the satellite structures develop only as the junction transparency increases, concomitantly with the emergence of MAR features and the evolution of the YSR-assisted channels. This behavior, together with the calculations of Fig. 4 showing mixed YSR-MAR contributions in the low-bias region, supports the interpretation that these satellites predominantly reflect the hybridization of YSR-assisted transport with higher-order Andreev processes rather than a purely thermal excitation effect.

Meanwhile, the n = 1 quasiparticle peak at Δt + Δs regains spectral weight, and the zero-bias Josephson contribution grows in amplitude. Together, these observations highlight a continuous crossover from tunneling spectroscopy of YSR states to coherent MAR transport, where the in-gap resonance actively reshapes the subharmonic gap structure and modifies the Josephson response of the junction. This evolution is consistent with the crossover reported by Ruby et al.15 for tunneling into Shiba states with a superconducting tip, where increasing conductance broadens the Shiba-related peaks and enhances Andreev contributions. In our case, the evolution proceeds further into a regime where multiple Andreev reflections and YSR-assisted MAR become prominent. As a result, the coherence-peak-related structures become less distinct, and the apparent YSR peak intensity is reduced at intermediate conductance because spectral weight is redistributed into overlapping mixed transport channels. Unlike in Ref. 15, the YSR-derived feature in our junction also shifts in energy as the tip approaches, which further enhances the overlap with MAR structures.

Theoretical results

To model the experimental junction, we first perform DFT calculations of the Nc–Fe complex on the Pb (111) surface (See the supplementary information.). Figure 3a shows the computed densities of states projected on all Nc states (green) and Fe states (red) exhibit opposite spin polarizations, indicating antiferromagnetic alignment. The spin density map (panel (b)) confirms that the Fe moment (1.05 μB) is compensated by the Nc moment (−1.20 μB), yielding a nearly quenched total spin. Despite this, the Fe retains a localized S ≃ 1/2 coupled to the substrate, which gives rise to a single pair of YSR resonances. Compressing Nc toward the surface broadens Fe states but preserves antiferromagnetic order. Thus, DFT shows the following model for the Nc–Fe system. While Nc quenches most of the spin, Fe provides a spin-active center.

The ladder of MAR in a superconductor-superconductor junction can be viewed as a sequence of electron exchanges between the two electrodes, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. It is useful to distinguish conventional MAR processes from YSR-assisted MAR processes. In the tunneling limit, the YSR resonance appears as the n = 1 limit of a YSR-assisted superconducting transport channel, whereas higher-order YSR-assisted MAR arises when the in-gap state participates in the MAR ladder. Pure MAR produces conductance peaks at the bias voltages of Eq. (1). When a YSR bound state participates in the MAR cycle, the bias thresholds shift from these values, giving YSR-assisted MAR28,34. Independent YSR resonances can be tuned directly by conventional dI/dV spectroscopy; such tunneling events correspond to the n=1 MAR process and therefore belong to the YSR-assisted category. Consequently, our calculations and the experimental dI/dV spectra of Fig. 2c reveal only two distinct features: pure MAR (black arrows in Fig. 4a) and YSR-assisted MAR (red arrows in Fig. 4a).

Our modeling of these results (see27,28 and (See the supplementary information.)) uses a transparency α to account for quasiparticle tunneling through the superconducting junction and a local exchange coupling J representing the spin-active Fe center. This effective description is supported by the DFT calculations, which show a strongly asymmetric junction: the Fe–electrode interface induces a finite magnetic response in Pb, whereas the Nc–electrode interface remains weakly hybridized and essentially non-magnetic. Thus, J describes the Fe–substrate exchange responsible for the YSR resonance, while α describes the transparency controlled by the tip approach. The purpose of this model is not to provide a complete microscopic Anderson description of the full Nc–Fe junction. Instead, it is a minimal superconducting transport model designed to isolate the two ingredients that are required to interpret the measured subgap spectra: spin-dependent exchange scattering, which produces the YSR-derived resonance, and electronic transmission through the weak link, which produces MAR. The parameters J and λ should therefore be understood as effective low-energy parameters. They are used to distinguish how the spectra respond to changes in the YSR energy and to changes in junction transparency, rather than as independently determined microscopic hybridization matrix elements.

When the initial or final state is the YSR resonance, there is an odd-even effect, revealed by the presence of Fano-like profiles for odd orders, n, in Fig. 4b. These profiles are well-known in the tunneling spectroscopy of YSR states when using a superconducting tip due to the sharpness of the tip’s density of states15. A useful way to understand the odd-even behavior is to consider where the MAR sequence terminates. Each Andreev reflection transfers the quasiparticle from one electrode to the other while shifting its energy by eV. For an odd number of reflections (e.g., n = 1,3), the quasiparticle ends its trajectory in the electrode hosting the magnetic impurity. The final tunneling event, therefore, probes the YSR resonance, producing a shifted threshold and the characteristic Fano-like line shape associated with tunneling into a discrete in-gap state. In contrast, for an even number of reflections (e.g., n = 2,4), the quasiparticle returns to the opposite electrode where no impurity state is present, and the process terminates in the continuum of the superconducting density of states. As a result, the threshold energies of these even-order processes remain essentially pinned to the conventional MAR values, while odd-order processes are strongly modified by the YSR level. This difference in the termination of the MAR ladder gives rise to the odd-even asymmetry visible in Fig. 4a, b.

The full SGS structure evolves in a complex way as the STM tip approaches the surface. To reproduce the effect of the reduced tunneling gap, we increase both J due to the pressure on the molecular layer exerted by the tip, and we increase α to reduce the tunneling barrier of the tip-sample junction. Indeed, Fig. 4b shows that simultaneous increases of J and α (tip approach) reproduce the experimental spectra, where the YSR-assisted MAR features shift to lower energies and pronounced odd-even asymmetries emerge. This behavior suggests that approaching the STM tip not only increases the junction transparency but also slightly enhances the exchange coupling between the impurity and the substrate, likely due to a small compression of the molecular junction.

To disentangle the effect of J and α on the SGS as well as a way to ascribe a physical meaning to the different features of the SGS, we freeze one of the parameters. Figure 4c shows that varying J at fixed α primarily shifts the YSR resonance without altering the basic MAR ladder. This is to be expected because J directly enters the binding energy of the YSR state, EYSR. As we increase J, we see the quasiparticle peak at Δt + Δs or n = 1 MAR channel, which splits into two. One peak pinned at Δt + Δs that clearly corresponds to the pure n = 1 MAR, and a second n = 1 peak that corresponds to the YSR-assisted MAR process with threshold, Eq. (5), as its strong Fano-like profile shows. In the tunneling limit (very low conductances), this last peak corresponds to the dI/dV feature of the YSR state.

The n = 2 order is also very revealing in Fig. 4c. Since J does not alter the pure MAR processes, we see that the peak is largely unperturbed even at the largest J. Only when J is large enough, the split-off peak due to the YSR-assisted MAR becomes noticeable, leading to an asymmetry in the n = 2 MAR peak, also seen in the experiment, Fig. 2c. The n = 3 is again a combination of pure and YSR-assisted MAR and splits into two peaks with increasing J. Indeed, we can see that the dashed line marking the n = 3 threshold presents an antiresonance (Fano profile) that is displaced with increasing J, due to the shifting of the YSR-assisted MAR. These behaviors reflect the odd-even effect that we mentioned above.

As we reduce the bias, we approach higher orders, where the same qualitative behavior is repeated and becomes increasingly difficult to disentangle. Near zero, the onset of the Josephson current is not reachable for the finite-order of our Floquet expansion. This is different from the experiment where phase fluctuations are important, changing the structure of the zero-bias peak and presenting negative-differential resistance (NDR) features21–23.

When J is kept fixed and the transparency, α is varied, the thresholds do not change, but the MAR become stronger. Figure 4d shows that pure MAR peaks evolve faster than the YSR-assisted ones. Overall, the thresholds do not change except perhaps because of the evolution of the Fano-like profiles, rendering it more difficult to identify thresholds. Notably, we see that the n = 2 peak is actually a composed peak, giving the peak an asymmetry at large transparencies. This asymmetry is clearly discernible in the experimental equivalent in Fig. 2c. The n = 3 Fano profile is very clear in Fig. 4d, because the thresholds do not shift, contrary to the case of Fig. 4c. Although the experimental resolution does not permit us to show the presence of the n = 3 Fano profile, the absence of a clear n = 3 MAR peak in the experiment is compatible with the data of Fig. 4b, showing that lower conductance correlates with lower J and junction transparency in these experiments.

The calculations clarify the evolution of the peaks in the experimental spectra of Fig. 2c. The feature marked by green arrows corresponds to the n = 1 YSR-assisted MAR peak: as the junction transparency increases, its transmission grows while its energy shifts to lower bias, reflecting the stronger impurity-substrate coupling J that binds the YSR state. To reproduce the experimental spectra, both parameters have to be changed. Thus, the differential conductance computed for fixed J, Fig. 4d, fails to reproduce the experimental behavior of the n = 1. At low transparencies, the calculation at fixed J yields a large NDR absent in the experiment, and the peak continuously grows with larger transparency, while the experimental feature shows saturations. Both effects are captured in Fig. 4b.

At higher transparencies, the red arrow peak, corresponding to the n = 2 YSR-assisted and pure MAR, becomes increasingly important. The peaks display pronounced asymmetry at intermediate transparencies but regain symmetry at larger transparencies owing to the increased spectral broadening. The overall subgap structure appears to drift away from the pure MAR thresholds because the YSR-assisted MAR feature moves to lower energy with increasing J.

Finally, Fig. 2c shows no evidence of YSR states in the superconducting tip, consistent with the weak-link geometry in which the spin-active Fe adatom couples only to the Pb(111) substrate.

The comparison between the fixed-J, fixed-α, and combined calculations gives a direct physical meaning to the two effective parameters of the model. The exchange coupling J controls primarily the energy of the spin-active in-gap resonance, while the transparency α controls the strength of superconducting transport through the junction. These parameters should not be viewed as independently tunable microscopic quantities, since both ultimately originate from the hybridization of the molecular junction with the electrodes. Rather, they provide a low-energy decomposition of the two main consequences of the tip approach: modification of the Fe-substrate exchange environment and increase of the junction transparency. This decomposition shows what is learned from the experiment: the complex evolution of the spectra in Fig. 2 does not require invoking unrelated transport channels, but can be rationalized as the redistribution of transport weight between conventional MAR and YSR-assisted MAR processes within one mechanically tuned molecular junction.

This separation gives the model its interpretive value: varying J at fixed transparency identifies spectral changes associated with the motion of the spin-active in-gap resonance, whereas varying α at fixed J identifies changes associated with superconducting transport through the junction. The experimental evolution requires both effects, showing that the observed spectra arise from YSR-assisted MAR processes in a single mechanically tuned molecular junction rather than from a simple superposition of independent YSR and MAR contributions. Our model provides a phenomenological decomposition of the conductance evolution rather than a unique microscopic extraction of independently tunable parameters.

The combined role of exchange coupling J and junction transparency α permits us to obtain Fig. 4b, and thus show the nature of each peak and its evolution in an evolving tunneling junction. The good agreement with the experiments explains the non-monotonic evolution of the spectra in Fig. 2 and rationalizes the odd-even effect observed in the MAR ladder. Furthermore, this study shows that the SGS cannot be cleanly associated with separated MAR processes and YSR states. We assumed an S = 1/2 impurity with thus a single possible YSR state, the above results show YSR-assisted MAR features across the full gap, showing the strong transport character of the measurements and the presence of interference between MAR and YSR states.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that subgap transport in atomic-scale superconducting junctions with magnetic impurities cannot be understood solely in terms of either YSR spectroscopy or MAR. Instead, both processes are intertwined: a localized spin provides a discrete resonance that modifies the MAR ladder, leading to shifted thresholds, asymmetric line shapes, and odd-even effects. By tuning transparency, we follow this crossover from YSR spectroscopy to resonant MAR transport and, ultimately, Josephson coupling. Our model indicates that the experimentally observed mechanical evolution can be rationalized only when both ingredients are included: the evolution of a YSR-derived resonance and the simultaneous modification of MAR spectral weight through changes in both the spin-electrode interaction and the junction transparency.

The combination of transport modeling and DFT clarifies the microscopic origin. The essential information gained from the model is that the mechanically induced spectral evolution can be separated into two effects: a shift of the YSR-derived resonance controlled mainly by J, and an enhancement and redistribution of MAR spectral weight controlled mainly by the junction transparency α. DFT shows that the Nc–Fe complex is antiferromagnetic with nearly compensated total spin. The presence of a S ≃ 1/2 on Fe shows that the presence of a single-pair of YSR states is due to the overlap of the magnetic orbitals of Fe with the substrate, despite an overall zero-spin in the molecule-adatom complex. The Fe spin couples to the substrate and generates a single pair of YSR states. In transport, the YSR state actively participates in the MAR channels at different biases and different degrees in a non-trivial way, strongly modifying the junction’s differential conductance.

Beyond unifying YSR and MAR, our findings suggest broader implications. Spin-active weak links may host non-trivial current-phase relations, including Josephson diodes4 and π-junction behavior38, when inversion symmetry or spin-orbit coupling is present. Control of transparency opens the way to test photon-assisted variants under microwave drives, relevant for quantum-coherent manipulation in electron spin resonance-STM. More generally, correlating microscopic spin structure with transport signatures offers a route to design molecular-scale superconducting devices where magnetism, superconductivity, and quantum transport are intimately coupled.

In summary, we have realized a spin-active superconducting weak link by positioning a nickelocene molecule above a subsurface Fe atom on Pb(111) and probing it with a superconducting STM tip. By tuning the junction transparency, we observed a continuous evolution from YSR spectroscopy to YSR-assisted MAR and finally to Josephson transport. A Kondo-Andreev model reproduces the spectra and identifies an odd-even effect in the MAR ladder, while DFT reveals an antiferromagnetic ground state with a residual Fe spin-1/2 responsible for the YSR states. These results unify two cornerstone phenomena of mesoscopic superconductivity, namely bound states from magnetic impurities and coherent MAR transport, and establish molecular weak links as versatile platforms to engineer spin-dependent superconducting devices.

Methods

Experiment

To conduct our measurements, we used an STM at a base temperature of 2.4 K under ultra-high-vacuum conditions. We evaporated the Nc molecules onto the Pb (111) surface, resulting in self-assembled molecular layers (see section S1 in the Supplementary Information). We used a Pb-coated STM tip, leading to a gap of Δt + Δs = 2.7 meV in the differential conductance as a function of bias. The procedure was completed with the in-situ Fe evaporation, reaching a coverage of less than 1% of individual Fe atoms on the Pb surface. For more details, please refer to the Supporting Information, S1. The junction conductance is defined from the stabilization parameters of the STM feedback loop as G = Iset/Vbias, using a bias voltage outside the superconducting gap. See S2.

Theory

The transport calculations, see S3, were performed using the same gap value for tip and sample Δt = Δs = 1.35 meV, a Dynes broadening Γ = 50 μeV, 150 points in the internal frequency integrals, a temperature of 3 K and the maximum Floquet index equal to 10. Increasing the maximum Floquet index to 150 allowed us to reasonably reproduce a Josephson peak, but without thermal phase fluctuations, which are dominating these junctions at this temperature21,22.

Density functional theory calculations, please refer to S4, were performed using the VASP code41 with the projected augmented-wave (PAW) method42 to represent the electron-atom interactions. The wave functions have been expanded using a plane wave basis set with an energy cut-off of 400 eV. The exchange and correlation functional is the PBE-GGA one43. This functional was completed with van der Waals interactions introduced through the D3-BJ scheme44,45.
