Net carbon losses in Central African forests revealed by high-resolution biomass change maps
Authors: Liang Wan, Philippe Ciais, Aurélien de Truchis, Yidi Xu, Martin Brandt, Jérôme Chave, Clément Bourgoin, Jean-Pierre Wigneron, Jean-François Bastin, Wei Li, Youngryel Ryu, Shidong Liu, David Purnell, Ibrahim Fayad, Le Bienfaiteur Sagang, Arthur Vander Linden, Timothée Besisa, Pierre Ploton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dense humid forests in Central Africa hold vast biomass carbon stocks but are increasingly affected by small-scale disturbances that are difficult to detect with current satellite observations. Here, we generate 10 m canopy height maps for 2019–2022 using deep learning with spaceborne lidar and Sentinel-1/−2 imagery, and derive aboveground biomass changes at 30 m resolution. The maps reveal widespread small disturbance patches, 87% smaller than 1 ha. These account for 48% of biomass carbon gains in regrowing forests and 37% of carbon losses from deforestation. We estimate a net forest carbon loss of −58 ± 14 Tg C yr−1, with gross losses of −126 ± 21 Tg C yr−1 partially offset by gains of 68 ± 10 Tg C yr−1. The Democratic Republic of the Congo emerges as a net carbon source (−46 ± 11 Tg C yr−1), representing 79% of the regional loss, primarily due to degradation, despite having the largest carbon gains in young secondary forests. Our remote-sensing estimates of national carbon budgets are broadly consistent with country-level inventories and bookkeeping model estimates, paving the way for spatially explicit and transparent carbon monitoring.

Introduction

Central African forests play a vital role in the global carbon cycle, storing approximately 10% of the world’s forest biomass carbon1–3. Although intact forests have historically acted as a major carbon sink4, recent studies suggest that this sink is weakening, tending toward carbon neutrality or even becoming a net source5–7. Disturbances from deforestation, degradation (canopy disturbances that reduce biomass without converting forest to non-forest8), and fragmentation are increasingly affecting these forests9,10. At the same time, carbon gains arise from sustainable forestry, restoration, plantations, and forest regrowth following clearing events from shifting cultivation and changes of environmental factors like rainfall, yet these gains remain difficult to detect and quantify using satellite observations. Legal protections have been put in place through the designation of protected areas and certified forest concessions, but the effectiveness of these measures remains uncertain11,12. While previous studies have explored the types and drivers of forest disturbances in Central Africa13–15, the associated carbon losses and gains remain poorly quantified16, limiting our understanding of the region’s carbon balance17.

The main approaches to estimate the carbon balance of African forests in previous carbon budget assessments relied on National greenhouse gas inventories (NGHGIs), bookkeeping models, and dynamic global vegetation models (DGVMs)18–20. NGHGIs provide nationally consistent estimates defining the UNFCCC reporting, while bookkeeping models track carbon fluxes associated only with land-use transitions19. Both approaches are generally not spatially explicit, limiting their verification on the ground. DGVMs simulate large-scale vegetation dynamics and climate–carbon interactions, but they have unconstrained parameters in Africa and oversimplify disturbances, for example, neglecting forest degradation. Building on these approaches, recent synthesis efforts such as the RECCAP221 analysis by Ernst et al. 22 provide improved estimates, although important uncertainties remain, particularly in resolving disturbance processes.

Advances in satellite remote sensing have improved the monitoring of forest carbon dynamics. Harris et al.23 developed a spatially explicit framework to estimate forest carbon fluxes using fixed forest growth rates and Landsat-derived tree cover loss. Gibbs et al.24 subsequently updated this framework with improved model inputs, emission and removal factors, uncertainty analysis, and extended temporal coverage, showing that Central African forests remained a net carbon sink (~210 MtCO2e year−1). But they ignored biomass loss and regrowth from degradation events, which are crucial in Central African forests8,25. Direct mapping of biomass carbon stock change from satellite observations can complement inventories and models based on growth rates by providing direct spatially explicit observations of forest carbon changes8,26,27. However, these approaches are also subject to uncertainties from sensor noise, errors in height-biomass allometric relationships, limited sensitivity to understory changes, and potential biases when the same algorithms are applied across structurally diverse forest types28,29. Despite these limitations, maps of stock changes offer a valuable opportunity to resolve the spatial distribution, size, and type of disturbance events30,31, which are often not captured by existing approaches. Existing biomass change maps relying on coarse-resolution satellites (500 m to 25 km) have shown inconsistent estimates of carbon sources and sinks across tropical regions3,32,33. A recent study has explored higher-resolution approaches (for example, at ~100 m resolution)6, but did not resolve sub-hectare disturbance processes. As a result, previous studies had limited ability to detect small-scale disturbances, especially those smaller than 1 ha, and to robustly quantify their contribution to regional carbon losses and gains34–38. This truncated representation of disturbance size, type, and spatial distribution limits our ability to quantify forest carbon dynamics34,39. This knowledge gap is particularly critical in Central African forests, where disturbances are small, spatially complex, and shaped by a combination of natural dynamics and human activities27,40,41.

To address these challenges, we generated annual maps of canopy height (CH) at 10 m resolution across Central African forests29. To do so, a deep learning model was trained to predict maximum CH measured by the GEDI spaceborne LiDAR42,43 from Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery, enabling spatially continuous estimation of CH (“Methods”, Supplementary Fig. 7). These 10-m CH estimates were then aggregated to 30 m and converted to aboveground carbon (AGC) using field inventory data and allometric relationships from ref. 29. The 30 m resolution matches the spatial scale of available field plots, knowing that large tropical tree crowns often extend over 20 m44. First, we validated the CH and AGC change estimates for the period 2019–2022 using different reference datasets (Supplementary Notes 5–7). We then analyzed carbon losses and gains across different sizes of disturbances to address the following questions: Do the small-scale disturbances revealed by our high-resolution maps represent real disturbance events that were overlooked by existing Landsat/Sentinel-1-based datasets8,26,27?; How do these small disturbances contribute to carbon losses and gains associated with degradation, deforestation, and regrowth? Finally, we extended the approach to non-dense forest systems such as dry deciduous woodlands (often referred to as Miombo in Africa) to obtain national budgets. This extension is based on the key methodological assumption that our height–biomass relationships calibrated for dense forests hold for more sparsely distributed woodlands, which may introduce additional uncertainty in woodlands north and south of the Congo Basin. To address this, as we lack field data in woodlands, we assessed the robustness of our biomass estimates against independent satellite-based biomass products (Supplementary Note 12). We also incorporated belowground carbon estimates to assess the carbon balance of protected areas, forest concessions, and countries, and discuss the implications for national carbon accounting, in comparison with inventories and bookkeeping land use change carbon models19,20.

Results

Evaluation of canopy height changes

To quantify CH changes from 2019 to 2022 from the raw height maps29 provided by our deep learning model trained on GEDI and Sentinel satellite data, we applied a multi-step filtering and validation procedure to minimize noise and eliminate implausible structural changes, due to uncertainties caused by instrumental, atmospheric, and processing artifacts27,28 (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Note 4).

The predicted height changes were evaluated against independent airborne LiDAR (ALS) campaigns at the only site with repeat acquisitions. Validation was primarily conducted at 30 m resolution due to scale compatibility with ALS data and consistency with biomass change data used in subsequent analyses, R2 = 0.53 and MAE = 3.19 m (Supplementary Fig. 12). Agreement at 10 m was lower (R2 = 0.22, MAE = 3.70 m) than at 30 m due to increased fine-scale heterogeneity and unresolved sub-pixel canopy gaps relative to ALS data, although MAE remained comparable across resolutions. Aggregation to 30 m improves the agreement through spatial smoothing, whereas the 10 m product preserves fine-scale canopy disturbances. Comparisons with changes from GEDI discrete footprints samples (RH100) showed weak agreement at 30 m but improved when aggregated to 10 km (R2 = 0.23, MAE = 4.35 m) (Supplementary Fig. 15). This reflects several factors, including scale mismatch, sparse GEDI footprint sampling with no revisit in time, and geolocation uncertainty45, as well as the difficulty of detecting small height changes at fine spatial scales. Using the RH95 or RH98 height metrics slightly improves agreement but does not change the overall pattern (Supplementary Fig. 16), indicating that discrepancies are largely independent of the choice of height metric. These results highlight that validation at very small spatial scales remains limited, and the maps are primarily interpreted in terms of aggregated spatial patterns rather than individual pixels. Nevertheless, our continuous maps of height change showed a spatial distribution of gains and losses that was consistent with the discrete individual GEDI footprints (Supplementary Fig. 17).

The sign of our height change also matches the Tropical Moist Forests (TMF) disturbance data8 with a negative sign indicating height loss for deforestation and degradation events and a positive sign for regrowth events (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19), and further distinguishes protection status and types (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). Finally, we conducted an independent validation using high-resolution (5 m) PlanetScope imagery with a stratified sampling design46 across patch sizes and change types (Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Note 6). This analysis indicated that the user’s accuracy (UA) was 84% for loss and 77% for gain across the 0–100 ha range (area-weighted), and 79% and 69%, respectively, for small disturbances (<1 ha) (Supplementary Table 1). Omission and commission errors were ~6% and ~5%, respectively, when evaluated relative to the full landscape, corresponding to undetected changes within no-change areas and false detections in stable forests. Further comparison showed that only 26–78% of the independently validated small CH loss patches overlapped with TMF disturbances, depending on the minimum patch-overlap criterion applied (10–50% of the CH loss patch area; Supplementary Table 2).

High-resolution maps of height change reveal more small disturbances

The CH map is shown in Fig. 1a, and the fraction of pixels with height change in Fig. 1b. Large height losses are observed across the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and southern Cameroon, whereas height gains are found in Gabon and Equatorial Guinea (Supplementary Fig. 19). Zooming over three small regions in Fig. 1c–e shows that the patterns of our height change have a good visual agreement with the TMF disturbances. Quantitative analysis confirms that 80% of the areas where we detect large height losses (>20 m) match TMF events at 30 m resolution (Supplementary Fig. 18), indicating consistency for large disturbances. The main difference arises for small CH losses (<10 m), where our CH loss estimates identify 65% more affected area than is captured by the TMF deforestation and degradation products within the same disturbed regions. Independent visual validation using high-resolution PlanetScope imagery confirmed that ~72% of these small height losses represent real canopy disturbances (Supplementary Table 3). In our maps, ~87% of patches exhibiting CH decrease from 2019 to 2022 fall within the smallest height-loss size class (<1 ha; Fig. 2b). Among the ~10 million 0.09 ha (single-pixel) CH loss patches identified in our maps, 56% coincide with disturbances recorded in the TMF dataset. The remaining differences may reflect either additional small disturbances not captured by TMF37 or uncertainties in our detection of small changes. Those small loss patches not captured in previous datasets are prevalent in persistently cloudy regions such as the northern Congo Basin (Supplementary Fig. 19), where disturbances are associated with logging37 and clearings from shifting cultivation14. Notably, the native 10 m CH-change maps reveal substantially more small disturbance patches than the aggregated 30 m maps (Supplementary Fig. 23).

Size-dependence patterns of biomass gains and losses

To better understand how disturbance size influences biomass carbon change, we classified each canopy height change event into six distinct gain and loss categories. Since our map captures more disturbance events than TMF (Fig. 2), we developed a framework (see “Methods”) to extend the TMF classification to all the events detected in our study. For each contiguous disturbance patch, we then calculated its size51. All patches classified as deforestation events show a strong decrease of biomass density between 2019 and 2022, as expected (Fig. 3a). Degradation events show a smaller decrease in biomass density than deforestation events (Fig. 3c), with substantial variability across patches, particularly at small sizes, likely reflecting a mixture of partial canopy loss, recovery, and heterogeneous disturbance processes, and resulting in a wide dispersion. The size distribution of deforestation and degradation events is dominated by small patches (mean = 0.9 ha; median = 0.18 ha), indicating a strongly right-skewed distribution, with very rare large-scale clearings (maximum size 10,000 ha) and many small clearings. Relative to the initial biomass density in 2019, the fraction of biomass lost after deforestation increases with patch size. Biomass loss is even higher when excluding edge pixels, reaching up to ~89%, suggesting that patch edge effects dilute the biomass loss signal because edge pixels contain cut and intact trees (Supplementary Note 8 and Supplementary Fig. 29). In contrast, degradation events result in a smaller fraction of initial biomass density being lost (1–34%). For each size category, we calculated the total biomass loss by multiplying the area of each event by its corresponding biomass loss density. The key finding is that degradation patches of approximately 1.5–10 ha and deforestation patches of approximately 1.5 ha contribute the largest share of total biomass loss in the study region, although uncertainty remains due to limitations in disturbance detection and classification (Supplementary Table 5). Altogether, small deforestation clearings of less than 1 ha contribute 37% of the total basin-scale biomass loss from deforestation (−8.6 Tg C year−1, Fig. 3f) while the largest clearings above 100 ha contribute only 2%. Then, we analyzed biomass changes in patches of regrowing forests that had previously been deforested but showed recovery several years later. The size distribution of these regrowth patches peaks at 0.1–1 ha, with the largest patches exceeding 1000 ha (Supplementary Fig. 30). Within those regrowth patches, we found that biomass density increases over time and shows a clear dependence on patch size, with a higher carbon gain rates in patches of less than 10 ha and a more gradual increase in larger patches (Fig. 3e). The biomass gains observed in regrowth patches of less than 1 ha are linked to post-disturbance recovery processes such as post-fire regeneration, small-scale afforestation, regrowth following fallow cycles in slash-and-burn systems (Supplementary Fig. 32), and recovery from road-related disturbances (Supplementary Fig. 33). In contrast, the biomass gains in larger regrowth patches reflect organized afforestation or plantations. Overall, small regrowth patches (<1 ha) contribute 48% to the basin-scale biomass regrowth gain (~7.0 Tg C year−1) while large patches (>100 ha) only contribute 3%.

Because gains or losses of AGC observed during 2019–2022 often reflect the legacy dynamics of past disturbance events, we analyzed AGC gain and loss rates associated with former disturbance events detected by TMF prior to our observation period (Supplementary Fig. 34). For regrowing forests, high AGC growth rates are sustained for two years after regrowth is detected, followed by declining growth rates thereafter (Fig. 3b). This temporal dynamics of growth rates is however sensitive to TMF legacy data, as early-period TMF estimates may be biased toward easier to detect larger and long duration (>1 year) disturbances due to limited Landsat data availability in the early 2000s. Nevertheless, Fig. 3f shows that small patches (<1 ha) of regrowth in young secondary forests exhibit substantial AGC gains and play a dominant role in basin-scale carbon gains. In contrast, forests that experienced degradation in the past show a large initial loss of AGC density that attenuates over time. Based on this space-for-time substitution approach that assumes no secular trends in degradation severity, recovery conditions, or climate over the past two decades, we found no clear evidence of a return to a carbon gain regime in the first ~20 years after degradation (Fig. 3d). This result is consistent with recent GEDI-based analyses showing saturation in young regrowth and persistent losses in degraded forests10.

Canopy height and biomass changes at the biome scale and per forest management type

Scaling up to the entire Central African forest biome (Fig. 4a), our data show a small net AGC loss of −58.3 ± 13.5 Tg C year−1 (see uncertainty estimates in “Methods”, Supplementary Note 10). This net loss is the sum of gross gains of 67.8 ± 10.0 Tg C year−1 and gross losses of −126.1 ± 20.6 Tg C year−1 (Fig. 4b). With a total AGC stock of 24.3 Pg C (8.65% relative uncertainty) in 2019, the turnover rate associated with disturbance-driven losses was 0.52 ± 0.10% year−1. The reported uncertainty was obtained by first-order propagation of the uncertainties in AGC loss (16.34% relative uncertainty) and the 2019 AGC stock, resulting in an overall relative uncertainty of 18.49% for the turnover-rate estimate (Supplementary Note 11). In terms of forest area affected, a fraction of 5.5% of the forest area shows a net AGC gain, and 9% a net loss. Two-thirds of the gross losses are attributed to degradation (−80.4 ± 11.5 Tg C year−1) while the rest is due to deforestation (−23.3 ± 6.6 Tg C year−1). The regrowth of secondary forests after deforestation accounts for 21.4% of the gross AGC gain (14.5 ± 2.6 Tg C year−1). The AGC budget of so-called ‘other forests’, classified as apparently undisturbed in TMF but not exempt from AGC gains and losses, is close to neutral and not distinguishable from zero within uncertainty (Supplementary Table 6), although these forests exhibit substantial localized gross gains and losses. Protected forest areas and forestry concessions together represent 34% (73.5 Mha) of the total dense forest area. In protected areas, we assess a net biomass carbon loss of −3.8 ± 1.4 Tg C year−1 (mean rate = −0.09 ± 2.40 Mg C ha−1 year−1), substantially lower than that observed in non-protected areas (−0.31 ± 3.55 Mg C ha−1 year−1). In concessions, we found a net loss of −5.4 ± 1.6 Tg C year−1 (mean rate = −0.15 ± 2.88 Mg C ha−1 year−1) (Fig. 4c–f and Supplementary Table 7). Overall, 59% of the protected areas and concessions experienced AGC losses during our study period. Importantly, the difference in AGC loss between different forest concessions (n = 268) is smaller than between protected areas (n = 180) (Fig. 4e). Concessions tend to experience more gross AGC losses from degradation and deforestation, whereas protected areas have more regrowth gains from secondary forests. Notably, 57% and 50% of the gross AGC losses from all the protected areas and concessions in our study area, respectively, occurred in the DRC, the country with the largest forest area in the region.

The mean loss rate of biomass per unit forest area is ~67% larger in concessions than in protected areas (Supplementary Table 7). Concessions and protected areas differ in environmental and socio-economic contexts, including accessibility, forest types, surrounding land use, and disturbance history (Supplementary Note 9). Therefore, this comparison does not imply a causal attribution to protection status or effectiveness alone. We found that many protected areas exhibit biomass losses52 near their boundaries where deforestation pressures occur (Supplementary Fig. 37). National parks and wildlife sanctuaries tend to have a higher AGC and greater carbon gains (Supplementary Fig. 38), while some long-established protected areas have larger carbon losses, suggesting a loosened effectiveness of conservation measures53. Interestingly, protected areas have a smaller mean AGC density (127 ± 47 Mg C ha−1) than concessions (144 ± 44 Mg C ha−1) (Supplementary Fig. 39). However, this difference is modest relative to the uncertainty in AGC estimates and is accompanied by substantial overlap between distributions, indicating that the two land-use types are only weakly separable in terms of obvious influence on mean biomass density. This pattern may partly reflect non-random spatial placement of protected areas, which may include lower-biomass ecosystems due to non-carbon-centric choices for deciding a protection status (cultural value, animal and plant diversity). It may also be influenced by differences in management regimes, for example, where logging in concessions is relatively low-intensity or in more fertile forests, and allows trees to retain or recover biomass12. In contrast, some protected areas were already degraded prior to their designation, which could contribute to their lower current AGC levels.

National carbon budgets

We finally estimate country-level carbon budgets after extending our method to non-dense forests, and including belowground biomass carbon (BGC)54 changes (Supplementary Note 12 and Supplementary Figs. 40–42). Our national budgets derived from satellite data are split into gains (post-degradation, secondary regrowth, and other forests) and losses (degradation, deforestation, and other forests), and compared with national inventories and bookkeeping model results in Fig. 5. Our definition of loss and gain is different from national inventories which primarily separate ‘forest remaining forest’ and ‘land converted to another land use type’ from deforestation and afforestation. National inventories report carbon stock changes only within managed lands, but all African countries shown in Fig. 5 classify their entire forest area as managed, hence the comparison with our study covering all forests is valid. In the context of LULUCF averaged from three bookkeeping models (BKs; BLUE, OSCAR, and HN), deforestation generally represents a carbon source, whereas forest land usually acts as a carbon sink, although a few cases for forest land may indicate the opposite. Net biomass carbon changes at the country scale (Fig. 5a) show broadly consistent magnitudes with national inventories20 harmonized by Grassi et al.19 in Gabon and Equatorial Guinea, which have relatively small and stable forest sectors, with differences of 1 and 0.2 Tg C year−1, and of 5 Tg C year−1 in Cameroon, where there is more deforestation. Differences between our estimates and Grassi et al. are larger in large countries such as DRC that dominate the regional carbon budget. In the DRC, our estimate indicates a smaller net carbon loss than the inventory (Supplementary Table 8). This discrepancy may reflect differences between the harmonized inventory framework19 and our satellite-based biomass change estimates, particularly in the partitioning of gross carbon losses and gains, as well as uncertainties in our estimates, including possible underestimation of biomass losses in low-canopy non-dense forests. In the DRC, our net biomass carbon change is the sum of a gain of 78.1 ± 13.0 and a loss of −168.3 ± 29.3 Tg C year−1 (Fig. 5b, c), which is comparable to 74.1 ± 14.0 and −197.1 ± 35.1 Tg C year−1 from the bookkeeping models18 (Supplementary Table 8). Similar levels of agreement were observed in Equatorial Guinea. In contrast, our estimates of gross gains and losses in Gabon and Congo are larger than bookkeeping models55. This may be due to the high spatial resolution of our data, which allows us to better detect gains and losses at the scale of individual patches, whereas bookkeeping models use an arbitrary national fraction of ‘shifting cultivation’ areas as a proxy of degradation and calculate harvest removal as a bulk without spatial details. Since our dataset includes natural disturbances which are not captured in bookkeeping models, the fair agreement between our approach and bookkeeping models suggests that most disturbances in the region are from anthropogenic land use activities in Fig. 5. Further comparison with DGVM models and the statistical division of the Food and Agricultural Organization of the United Nations (FAOSTAT)20,56 is given in the Supplementary Note 13 but they are not discussed in detail since DGVMs do not simulate fine-scale land use change and degradation and FAOSTAT assumed a growth rate of zero for forest remaining forest in some countries57.

Despite a fair agreement with our net biomass carbon changes, national inventories19 tend to report larger gross carbon gains (Fig. 5b). This overestimation could be from the use of Tier 1 IPCC methods58 in inventories, which are based on default values of intact forest growth rates applied uniformly across all forest types. To evaluate this assumption, we calculated the average forest carbon growth rate from our maps and found that our growth rates over intact (undisturbed) forests were close to zero or even negative (Supplementary Table 9), lower than the refined IPCC default values59. This result suggests that the gross carbon gains reported in national inventories may be overestimated by more than 50% in Gabon, Cameroon, and the DRC (Fig. 5b). At face value, this difference may also reflect methodological limitations in our satellite-based height change estimates, including potential regression-to-the-mean effects in deep-learning models and saturation of satellite data in very high biomass forests that may attenuate height gains in old-growth forests.

National inventories also report lower gross carbon losses than our estimates (Fig. 5c). This difference partly reflects methodological differences between the two approaches. National inventories primarily report emissions associated with deforestation and do not explicitly report human-induced degradation losses, whereas biomass losses in our study include both deforestation and forest degradation, as well as natural disturbance processes. By explicitly separating carbon gains and losses, our approach provides a complementary perspective on the dynamics of all disturbances and associated biomass carbon changes.

Discussion

Our high-resolution maps of forest carbon change provide detailed insights into fine-scale carbon dynamics in Central African forests and can help inform or complement national inventories by linking carbon changes to local processes such as small-scale clearing and regrowth. Our estimates of gross carbon losses (2019–2022) from degradation and deforestation are broadly comparable to previous coarse-resolution estimates based on L-band vegetation optical depth data for 2010–201960 (Supplementary Table 11), although the comparison should be interpreted with caution due to differences in the analyzed time periods and the coarse spatial resolution (25 km) of the L-band data, which does not allow detection of individual disturbances. Interestingly, our patch-scale AGC recovery rates from regrowing forests are broadly consistent with recent remote-sensing estimates of Amazonian forest growth61 and with global modeled estimates of tropical forest recovery, including those reported for African forests62, but exceed the refined IPCC guidelines 2006 default growth rates59. We also find high relative recovery rates in the first three years of regrowth after degradation or temporary deforestation, followed by regrowth. This highlights the important contribution of young regrowth forests in Central Africa (1–8 years old; Fig. 3), which exhibit faster biomass accumulation during early succession than older secondary forests62. Some of these areas may include a small proportion of agroforestry systems (e.g., shaded cacao plantations) that are difficult to distinguish from natural regrowth. Based on the Spatial Database of Planted Trees63, these systems account for ~3% of our total AGC gain within regrowth forests and do not substantially affect regional biomass recovery estimates.

We did not explicitly attribute the drivers of biomass change to fire, windthrow, plantation expansion, or afforestation64, due to the short time window covered by our maps and the lack of detailed and spatially explicit ancillary data. This constrains the analysis of long-term disturbance signals. Comparisons with long-term datasets like TMF can provide context, but temporal inconsistencies, especially in areas of small degradation or regrowth events48, limit their reliability for interpreting our maps of carbon changes. Our approach integrates annual height composites, strict detection thresholds, and external forest loss datasets to improve the robustness of change detection. Visual validation indicates that most detected loss patches correspond to real canopy disturbances, although ~16% do not represent true loss signals based on expert interpretation (Supplementary Table 1). A key next step will be to distinguish natural disturbances (e.g., storms, droughts, fires) from anthropogenic drivers (e.g., logging, land-use conversion)65, which is essential for carbon reporting, mitigation tracking, and evaluation of bookkeeping models19. Ongoing efforts to extend the dataset through 2024 and before, along with improved reference data, will support trend detection and the development of fully classified, high-resolution maps of forest carbon dynamics.

Reducing regional biases on biomass estimation related to forest type, disturbance severity, and recovery dynamics requires more extensive field inventory data42. Furthermore, our height mapping primarily reflects top-of-canopy structure and does not probe understory trees, which can be preferentially cut by fuelwood collection, selective logging of small trees, agro-forestry practices, low-intensity fires, or die from drought66. Because such disturbances often occur without substantially altering top canopy height, their contribution to biomass loss may be underestimated in our analysis. In particular, small-scale and sub-canopy disturbances that do not produce detectable height reductions may not be captured, contributing to uncertainty in biomass loss estimates. More broadly, uncertainties in canopy height change estimation itself, arising from detection limits, temporal sampling, and signal variability, further complicate the interpretation of both loss and gain.

Future work should expand field inventories and leverage higher-resolution satellite data and deep learning–based models able to map full vertical waveforms from GEDI67. Recent missions such as BIOMASS, whose P-band radar is sensitive to large stems and will provide forest vertical tomography, together with NISAR, help reduce uncertainties associated with height-to-biomass conversion and improve monitoring of biomass dynamics. Our results suggest that Central African forests acted as a net carbon source during 2019–2022, broadly consistent with previous studies reporting a weakening or reversal of the forest carbon sink5,6. However, substantial spatial variability remains, and some regions, particularly Gabon and Equatorial Guinea, may still function as carbon sinks (Fig. 5). Our findings highlight the urgent need for fine-scale monitoring and investment strategies that jointly prioritize carbon and biodiversity, especially in under-monitored regions17.

Methods

Annual canopy height and biomass mapping

To derive CH change in Central African forests, we produced annual 10-m CH maps (FORMS-Africa V1)29 using a deep learning U-Net model trained with Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar, Sentinel-2 L2A optical imagery, and GEDI L2A RH100 measurements68. Sentinel-1 GRD data (VH polarization) were preprocessed using radiometric terrain calibration69, orthorectification, and border noise removal. For each tile and year, a temporal stack of Sentinel-1 images acquired during the dry season (June–October) was generated, from which the ten images with the lowest mean backscatter were selected and averaged to reduce radar noise and moisture effects. Sentinel-2 predictors included bands B2, B3, B4, B8, and B11 (resampled to 10 m). Images were filtered using the ESA cloud mask, and the three least cloudy observations during the dry season were selected to build annual composites. All Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 inputs were normalized to the range [0, 1]. The training dataset was constructed from 600 tiles (20 × 20 km) distributed across African dense forests. Tiles were randomly divided into training (80%), validation (10%), and testing (10%) subsets. The U-Net model was trained to perform pixel-wise regression of CH from Sentinel-1/2 imagery, using a weighted mean absolute error (MAE) loss70 to balance the contribution of different CH classes. Annual CH inference was then performed across the entire region, producing yearly CH maps from 2019 to 2023. The maps show overall good agreement with GEDI RH100 observations and ALS-derived CH maps, respectively. Further details of the model architecture, training procedure, and preprocessing are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Following our recently published study29, the annual CH maps were aggregated to 30 m resolution and then combined with 1 km wood density (WD)71,72 maps to derive AGB (Mg ha−1) at 30 m grids using the empirical relationship in that study:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{AGB}}}=6.5981\times {({{\mathrm{CH}}}\times {{\mathrm{WD}}})}^{1.2670}$$\end{document}AGB=6.5981×(CH×WD)1.2670

Although biomass–height scaling relationships are theoretically expected to be non-linear, we evaluated several alternative formulations, including machine-learning approaches29. While some models achieved higher predictive accuracy of biomass on validation samples, they produced spatial artifacts and unstable temporal patterns when applied to wall-to-wall mapping at 30 m resolution. To ensure spatial coherence and temporal consistency across annual maps, we therefore adopted this simpler height-based formulation. The WD map was resampled from 1 km to 30 m for AGB estimation. Although this resampling does not introduce fine-scale variation in WD, including WD helps mitigate biomass saturation effects when converting CH to AGB. In practice, most spatial and temporal variability in AGB is driven by CH, while WD acts primarily as a regional modifier. Because the WD dataset did not fully cover all forest pixels in either 2019 or 2022, missing values were filled using the nearest available WD value. These filled pixels occur primarily in disturbed areas and low-density forest regions and account for only 1.5% of all pixels. The AGB model was calibrated using more than 400 field observations of AGB from African dense forests and showed moderate agreement with available field inventory data, with an R2 of 0.40 and a relative error of 32.6%. This validation statistic reflects scale mismatch and representativeness of the available field inventory plot validation dataset and should not be interpreted as the variance explained across the entire mapped domain. Field plots are sparse across Central Africa and are disproportionately located in high-biomass forests, which affects the validation statistics. Then, we converted AGB to carbon, assuming that AGC (in Mg C ha−1) is 45.6% of AGB, which lies within the commonly reported range of carbon fractions for tropical woody biomass73,74.

Following the definition used in the GLAD tree height product75, we defined forest pixels as those with CH ≥ 5 m. This is similar to the FAO FRA forest definition57, but does not consider the tree cover or patch size criteria. This height threshold may include some tree-based agricultural systems (e.g., shaded cacao agroforestry), although their influence on regional biomass estimates is expected to be limited in the dense forest regions analyzed here. The AGB model selected in this study was specifically calibrated for African dense forests. To enable country-level carbon accounting, we applied the same CH–AGB relationships to non-dense forest systems. This represents an important assumption, as structural differences in canopy architecture, species composition, and disturbance regimes may affect the transferability of these relationships (Supplementary Note 1). However, our previous ALS-based evaluation29 included a wide range of forest structures, including relatively short forests in wet regions that are not classified as dense forests, and showed generally consistent performance across these conditions. Due to the limited availability of field inventory data for non-dense forests, a direct recalibration of the model was not feasible. Instead, we evaluated the robustness of the resulting AGB estimates through comparison with the ESA-CCI AGB product as an independent satellite-based dataset. The two datasets showed broadly consistent spatial patterns (Supplementary Note 12 and Supplementary Figs. 40–42), supporting the large-scale applicability of the approach while acknowledging potential uncertainties at local scales.

Reference data for CH change validation

To evaluate the predicted maps of CH change, we used different reference datasets, including ALS CH, GEDI RH100, and published disturbance datasets (Supplementary Notes 2 and 3).

We used the available repeated ALS scans from 2015 and 202329 over the Yangambi Biosphere Reserve, DRC (Lat: 24.26583°–24.55838°; Lon: 0.77435°–0.85176°), obtained from the network of canopy observatories (www.CanObs.net). We compared the estimated CH changes against ALS-derived changes at spatial resolutions of 10 m, 20 m, and 30 m. The original ALS CH maps, generated at 2 m resolution, were aggregated to coarser grids by extracting the maximum value within each cell, representing the upper canopy envelope. Although this approach does not fully reproduce the GEDI waveform-derived RH100 metric, it provides a practical approximation of top-of-canopy height in the absence of waveform information. To enhance the quality of the ALS CH maps, we excluded edge pixels that were adjacent to invalid values within a 3 × 3 pixel window. Because ALS observations were only available for 2015 and 2023, we estimated CH changes comparable to the 2019–2022 observation period using temporal interpolation. Pixels with height loss occurring outside the comparison window were excluded to reduce temporal mismatch. CH gains and losses were then identified using a 0.5 m threshold61 to match the sensitivity of the satellite-derived CH maps. These steps improve the consistency of the comparison by reducing temporal mismatches between ALS and satellite observations (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). We also acknowledge that this comparison is subject to limitations, including temporal mismatches, the need for interpolation and sample filtering, and limited spatial representativeness. To assess the potential effects of temporal interpolation and sample filtering, we conducted sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Note 5).

We also compared our CH change maps to changes directly obtained from GEDI footprint data. We filtered GEDI footprints to retain high-quality, high-sensitivity L2A RH100 observations (V2), following quality control criteria established in previous studies76,77. We compared CH change over grid cells of various sizes (from 2019 to 2022) containing multiple GEDI footprints by averaging the data of all GEDI footprints in each grid cell. It is important to note that GEDI geolocation uncertainty and sensor- and atmosphere-related effects78 in Sentinel-2 images introduce uncertainty in matching GEDI and CH changes at the 30 m pixel scale. To improve the robustness of the comparison, we therefore aggregated CH change maps to coarser spatial resolutions (from 100 m up to 100 km) (Supplementary Note 3). Because the CH model is trained on GEDI RH100 observations, this comparison is not fully independent. It is therefore intended primarily as a consistency check of large-scale RH100 change patterns rather than as an independent validation.

Furthermore, we compared CH change with several disturbance datasets, which are independent from GEDI data, including TMF8, GFC26, RADD27 (Supplementary Note 2). These datasets provide independent satellite-based references for disturbance patterns, although they are not used here as formal validation because each product is based on different methods and has its own detection uncertainties. The GFC dataset, derived from time-series Landsat imagery, was used to identify forest loss pixels within Central African forests based on the loss-year layer. The RADD alerts, generated from Sentinel-1 data, were used to extract high-confidence disturbance pixels (value = 3) over the same period at 10 m resolution. The TMF dataset (v2023) provides annual 30 m disturbance maps, from which we derived deforestation, degradation, and regrowth (secondary forests growing after deforestation) from the annual change collection. Note that the TMF product uses a dedicated methodology tailored to Tropical Moist Forest dynamics, combining spectral decision rules with single-date image classification across the full Landsat archive8, which enables the detection of short-duration disturbances and consequently identifies more disturbance events than GFC and RADD.

To assess the reliability of mapped CH changes, we conducted an independent validation using high-resolution monthly PlanetScope imagery (5 m) (Supplementary Note 6). We adopted a stratified random sampling design following commonly used good practices for land-change accuracy assessment46, based on patch-size classes (0–1, 1–5, 5–10, and 10–100 ha) and change type (gain and loss). Samples were randomly selected across the study region, and only locations with sufficiently cloud-free PlanetScope imagery in both 2019 and 2022 were retained for interpretation. We visually interpreted whether sampled patches exhibited canopy structural decrease or increase consistent with the mapped CH change direction. Interpretation was based on comparisons between monthly PlanetScope imagery from 2019 and 2022, together with the mapped CH change layer, including changes in canopy cover, shadowing, canopy openings, and vegetation recovery signals. We included a total of 422 validated loss patches and 498 validated gain patches. Small disturbance patches, which are particularly difficult to identify because of subtle canopy structural changes, were independently interpreted by both an experienced forest remote sensing expert and a trained analyst following the same interpretation protocol. Interpretation consistency across the same validation samples reached 94%. Larger disturbance patches (1–100 ha) were interpreted by the trained analyst using the same protocol. Finally, we derived user’s accuracy for each size class as well as area-weighted accuracy46 to account for the disproportionate contribution of large patches to total mapped area change (Supplementary Table 1). In addition, omission errors were assessed using randomly sampled 2-ha reference patches selected from undisturbed forest areas. These reference areas were visually confirmed as stable forest using PlanetScope imagery together with independent disturbance datasets. To further evaluate the reliability of CH loss detections across different loss magnitudes, we conducted an additional visual assessment using randomly sampled 1-ha and 5-ha image chips extracted from the mapped CH change layer (Supplementary Table 3). These image-chip sizes refer to the area of the sampled image subsets rather than the size of individual CH loss patches, because CH changes cannot be reliably interpreted at the individual-pixel level using optical imagery alone. Image chips were stratified according to their mean estimated CH-loss magnitude (−10 ≤ ΔCH < 0 m, −20 ≤ ΔCH <−10 m and ΔCH <−20 m) (Supplementary Fig. 43). Monthly PlanetScope imagery acquired in 2019 and 2022 was visually interpreted to determine whether canopy loss was present within each sampled image chip. This assessment was designed to quantify the reliability of CH loss detection (true and false detections) across different loss-magnitude classes, rather than to validate the quantitative accuracy of the estimated CH loss values, which cannot be independently assessed by visual interpretation alone. Furthermore, we validated and characterized small CH changes against multiple reference datasets, such as canopy cover, road map, and local disturbance data47 (Supplementary Note 7).

CH change classification

To attribute CH changes to disturbance types, we developed a classification framework that categorizes CH changes into six classes, including other loss, deforestation, degradation loss, regrowth, degradation gain, and other gain. This classification is based on height changes derived from satellite observations rather than land-use definitions used in national inventories. We classified forest degradation as small CH reductions and deforestation as large losses, based on CH change estimates. Regrowth classification relies on TMF due to the short time span of our dataset. This specific classification extends the TMF classification to our dataset since TMF detects less disturbances than our CH change map. Our approach classifies disturbance types based on structural changes and may therefore not fully align with TMF labels, particularly for shifting cultivation and disturbances occurring within the last 3–5 years of the time series. Limited historical depth can make it difficult to distinguish permanent from non-permanent disturbances, while TMF relies on the duration and recurrence of spectral change sequences. These methodological differences may lead to reclassification between degradation and deforestation.Other forests: pixels identified as undisturbed forests in the TMF dataset were classified as “other forests” in our map if |ΔCH | ≤ 5 m86, representing 30.1% of all change pixels. TMF-undisturbed pixels with large ΔCH values in our maps were reclassified into other disturbance types using the criteria below.Deforestation and degradation (ΔCH < 0): although deforestation implies the removal of forest canopy, it is difficult to observe CH values close to 0 m using satellite data at 10–30 m resolution due to overestimation of short vegetation and mixed pixels. Following definitions used in ALS-based canopy gap studies86, we defined deforestation as pixels with ΔCH ≤ –5 m and a final CH below 10 m in 2022, while remaining loss pixels were classified as degraded forests. Although these thresholds were initially informed from other tropical regions, our analysis combining TMF classifications and CH change shows that the separation between degradation and deforestation is more clear using these thresholds (Supplementary Fig. 44). Our ΔCH threshold is consistent with previous studies showing that severe canopy loss is associated with mean height reductions of 5 m or more87,88. This CH-based definition does not fully overlap with TMF classifications. TMF deforestation represents persistent forest-cover disruption detected from long-term Landsat observations (>900 days)8, which serves as a proxy for deforestation processes, whereas our CH-based framework emphasizes the structural change. Consequently, some TMF-deforestation pixels retained relatively high post-disturbance CH or experienced rapid recovery, making them structurally more consistent with partial canopy disturbance than complete canopy removal in our framework. These pixels were therefore classified as degraded forests in the CH-based analysis (Supplementary Table 16).Regrowth and degradation (ΔCH > 0): to classify CH gains, we used the TMF dataset as an auxiliary reference. TMF labels pixels as regrowth, degraded, or “uncertain” (12.7%), the latter representing pixels classified as undisturbed or outside the TMF disturbance categories. For these uncertain pixels, we trained an XGBoost89 classifier using TMF-labeled degraded and regrowth pixels as training samples. The input features included CH values from 2019 to 2023, ΔCH2023−2019, ΔCH2023−2022, ΔCH2022−2019, and ΔCC2022−2019. The model was then used to assign uncertain pixels to either degraded or regrowth classes. Because the model is trained on TMF-derived labels, it does not provide an independent classification but is used to assign TMF “uncertain” pixels to regrowth or degradation categories based on canopy height features. This step extends the TMF disturbance classification to pixels lacking a definitive label. The model shows good agreement with TMF-defined classes (overall accuracy = 0.87), reflecting consistency with the TMF labeling scheme rather than independent classification accuracy. Class-specific F1-scores are 0.86 for regrowth and 0.88 for degradation, and are used to characterize classification uncertainty in subsequent analyses.

Deforestation and degradation (ΔCH < 0): although deforestation implies the removal of forest canopy, it is difficult to observe CH values close to 0 m using satellite data at 10–30 m resolution due to overestimation of short vegetation and mixed pixels. Following definitions used in ALS-based canopy gap studies86, we defined deforestation as pixels with ΔCH ≤ –5 m and a final CH below 10 m in 2022, while remaining loss pixels were classified as degraded forests. Although these thresholds were initially informed from other tropical regions, our analysis combining TMF classifications and CH change shows that the separation between degradation and deforestation is more clear using these thresholds (Supplementary Fig. 44). Our ΔCH threshold is consistent with previous studies showing that severe canopy loss is associated with mean height reductions of 5 m or more87,88. This CH-based definition does not fully overlap with TMF classifications. TMF deforestation represents persistent forest-cover disruption detected from long-term Landsat observations (>900 days)8, which serves as a proxy for deforestation processes, whereas our CH-based framework emphasizes the structural change. Consequently, some TMF-deforestation pixels retained relatively high post-disturbance CH or experienced rapid recovery, making them structurally more consistent with partial canopy disturbance than complete canopy removal in our framework. These pixels were therefore classified as degraded forests in the CH-based analysis (Supplementary Table 16).

Characterization of disturbance patterns

To characterize disturbance patterns, we identified contiguous clusters of disturbed pixels using an 8-connected component labeling algorithm applied to CH change maps. Annual CH maps were first generated at 10 m resolution and subsequently aggregated to 30 m by averaging, from which AGC maps were derived. To ensure spatial consistency with AGC estimates, patch delineation was primarily based on CH change maps derived from annual 30 m CH data. Disturbed pixels were defined as valid CH changes following the multi-step filtering procedure described above. Each connected cluster was labeled as a patch, and patch size was calculated based on the number of connected pixels (0.09 ha per pixel at 30 m resolution). To assess sensitivity to spatial resolution, we compared this approach with patch delineation based on native 10 m resolution CH change maps (Supplementary Fig. 23). In addition, we evaluated an alternative approach in which 10 m CH change maps were first aggregated to 30 m prior to patch identification. This approach produced CH change distributions consistent with those from our primary 30 m-based method, but missed some small disturbance patches that are not represented in the 30 m AGC change maps. In contrast, using native 10 m CH change maps results in a substantially larger number of small disturbance patches due to increased sensitivity to fine-scale variability, with the number of small patches differing by orders of magnitude compared to the 30 m-based analysis. Finally, we quantified both patch counts and their contributions to total AGC change across different bins of CH loss and gain, as well as across disturbance types defined above. Uncertainty associated with disturbance classification was explicitly propagated into the AGC change estimates for each class.

To explore potential drivers of disturbance patterns, we assessed the distance from patch pixels to the nearest mapped road. Road mapping (Supplementary Fig. 33) was derived from 5-m Planet imagery (S.L. et al., manuscript in preparation), and road fraction was calculated as the ratio of road pixels to total pixels within 30-m grids. Because optical imagery may miss small logging trails or canopy-covered roads, this dataset primarily captures visible roads and should be interpreted as a proxy for accessibility rather than a complete representation of all forest access routes. We note that our map of roads based on 5 m Planet data is the best available to date for the whole region, and it is consistent across all the tropics.

To quantify how patch size influences subsequent AGC change following past disturbances, we collocated disturbance information from the TMF dataset with AGC change observed during our study period (2019–2022), and jointly evaluated AGC change as a function of patch size and disturbance age, enabling comparisons of patch-size effects under comparable recovery stages. Because biomass recovery depends both on disturbance size and time since disturbance, the TMF disturbance year was used to evaluate disturbance age when interpreting these relationships. Our results focus on disturbances occurring during the 20 years preceding the observation period (2000–2019), as reliable Landsat-based disturbance monitoring in Central Africa generally begins around 20008. Disturbances prior to this period may still influence current biomass dynamics, but cannot be consistently detected using available satellite records. For completeness, results including the full TMF disturbance record (1990–2022) are provided in Supplementary Fig. 45. We conducted an additional sensitivity analysis excluding pixels classified as afforestation in the TMF Transition Map (v2023), which captures the sequential dynamics of forest change from the beginning of the monitoring period through 2023. The resulting AGC change estimates are presented in Supplementary Fig. 46 and show highly consistent patterns, indicating that afforestation has a minimal impact on our results.

AGC change in protected and concession areas

To evaluate the effectiveness of conservation and land-use policies in Central Africa, we quantified total AGC change (carbon budget) and AGC density change within protected areas and forest concessions. Protected area boundaries were obtained from the World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA)90, and forest concession boundaries were sourced from Global Forest Watch (https://data.globalforestwatch.org/). For the analysis, we only considered officially designated protected areas and allocated forest concessions. We examined whether significant differences exist in AGC density and its change across protected, concession, and other land types. Since these areas were established in different years, we also analyzed AGC change as a function of time since designation (Supplementary Fig. 38). Additionally, we compared AGC changes across different types of protected areas, such as National Parks, Biosphere reserve, Ramsar site, and Wildlife reserve.

Comparisons of AGC budget with inventories and bookkeeping models, country reports

In this section, we estimated forest biomass carbon budgets in Gabon, Equatorial Guinea, Cameroon, Congo, and DRC by combining AGC in dense and non-dense forests with BGC derived from country-level BGB/AGB ratios54 (Supplementary Note 12). Country-level carbon budgets were compared to national inventory reports harmonized by refs. 19,20, the average of three bookkeeping models used in the global carbon budget of the year 2022 (GCB2022)18, and additionally to DGVMs from TRENDY V8, V1191 of GCB202218, and FAOSTAT20,56. These datasets provide annual net carbon change data for 2019–2021. Bookkeeping models BLUE92, OSCAR93, and Houghton and Nassikas94 are driven by expanding areas of agricultural land from FAOSTAT downscaled into annual maps by the LUH2 model95 for BLUE and OSCAR, and by reconstructed forest and agricultural areas at the national scale for Houghton and Nassikas and OSCAR. We used the average of the simulations with both forcings from OSCAR. The bookkeeping models report carbon gains from afforestation, post-harvest regrowth, and shifting cultivation regrowth, and carbon losses from all land use transitions (dominated by deforestation), harvest removals, and shifting cultivation clearings. Harvest and shifting cultivation activities are not spatially explicit. National inventory data are from the harmonized database of Grassi et al. corrected19 to account for differences in the definition of managed land and for clerical errors, and uses additional REDD+ submissions and nationally determined contributions in addition to the original UNFCCC national reports19,20. The FAOSTAT emissions database estimates forest-sector carbon changes separately for ‘forest remaining forest’ and ‘forest converted to other land uses’. It combines activity data from national FRA reports (and FAO-analyzed geospatial datasets) with IPCC 2006 emission factors, resorting to country-specific factors where available.
