Development and Validation of a Nomogram for Predicting Recurrence in Patients Previously Treated Conservatively for Cervical Spondylosis
Authors: Hanze Mao, Guangqi Lu, Shuaiqi Zhou, Minghui Zhuang, Xin Xiu, Jing Li, Xinyue Sun, Yakun Liu, Mingming Ma, Jiaming Hu, Jie Yu, Liguo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical spondylosis frequently recurs after conservative treatment, but its predictors remain unclear. This study aimed to identify factors associated with recurrence and develop a predictive nomogram.MethodsThis retrospective analysis used data from a multicenter cross-sectional survey (six Chinese cities, March–November 2025). We identified 668 patients who reported remission after systematic conservative treatment and retrospectively collected their post-treatment recurrence status via self-report. Potential predictors included demographics, lifestyle, clinical history, and radiographic findings. The sam ple was randomly split into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors, and a nomogram was constructed. Model performance was evaluated using AUC, calibration, and decision curve analysis. Supplementary analyses examined episode frequency and time to recurrence.ResultsSix independent predictors were identified: exercise duration per session (OR=0.992), neck length (OR=0.850), disc space narrowing (OR=0.478), disease duration (overall P<0.001), pre-treatment attack frequency (overall P<0.001), and PCS score (OR=0.943). Compared with disease duration <3 months, 3–11 months increased risk (OR=2.691); pre-treatment attacks ≥6/month raised risk (OR=4.608). The nomogram showed good discrimination (training AUC=0.760) and net benefit. Longer exercise was associated with delayed recurrence, while longer disease duration and frequent prior episodes increased recurrence risk; disc narrowing paradoxically predicted lower recurrence.ConclusionPost-treatment recurrence is associated with multifactorial factors, including modifiable elements like exercise and physical health, as well as disease chronicity. Imaging changes may prompt protective behaviors. These findings support individualized risk stratification, but causal inference requires prospective validation.

Introduction

Global Burden of Disease studies have shown that musculoskeletal disorders have become the leading cause of disability in many countries.1 Cervical spondylosis, as a common musculoskeletal disorder, is a group of clinical syndromes based on degenerative changes of the cervical spine and can involve the vertebral bodies, intervertebral joints, ligaments, and surrounding neurovascular structures.2 With population aging, changes in work patterns, and increased use of electronic devices, the burden of cervical spondylosis continues to rise.3 Patients often present with neck and shoulder pain, stiffness, and limited mobility, and severe cases may be accompanied by upper limb numbness, dizziness, and neurological dysfunction, which can further affect quality of life, work efficiency, and social functioning.2,4

Currently, treatment for cervical spondylosis mainly includes health education, postural correction, pharmacotherapy, physical therapy, exercise-based rehabilitation, and, when necessary, surgical intervention.2,4 Although symptoms are relieved in most patients following standard treatment, recurrence remains a substantial challenge. Previous reports have shown that recurrence after conservative treatment is common, with over 70% of patients experiencing a subsequent episode of neck pain.5 This not only increases medical and economic burden but may also reduce treatment adherence and contribute to disease chronicity and complexity.6,7 Therefore, investigating the risk factors for cervical spondylosis recurrence is of significant clinical importance for optimizing long-term management and developing individualized prevention and treatment strategies.

Previous studies have suggested that the onset of cervical spondylosis is associated with age, occupation, prolonged poor posture, static loading, psychological factors, lack of physical activity, and obesity, among others.8–10 However, current evidence remains heavily focused on pathogenesis, diagnostic classification, and short‑term treatment outcomes. Crucially, to our knowledge, dedicated prognostic models or nomograms specifically designed to predict post‑treatment recurrence have not yet been developed and validated.

Based on this gap, the present study aimed to comprehensively investigate factors associated with recurrence in patients with cervical spondylosis, including demographic characteristics, occupational and lifestyle factors, disease history, and imaging findings, and to develop a simple, clinically applicable predictive nomogram, with the goal of providing theoretical guidance and a practical reference for stratified management, recurrence warning, and long-term follow-up interventions.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Wangjing Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Approval No.: WJEC-KT-2024-069-P002). All participants provided informed consent.

This is a retrospective analysis of cross-sectional survey data derived from a large multicenter survey conducted from March to November 2025 across six Chinese cities (Beijing, Shenzhen, Changchun, Xi’an, Changsha, and Suzhou), in which recurrence status was self-reported and retrospectively recalled by patients after their remission. The original survey was designed to characterize cervical spine health among Chinese adults. Community-dwelling adults aged 18 years or older were recruited through a multistage random sampling method. The survey initially planned to enroll 7000 participants, with 2000 from Beijing and 1000 from each of the other five cities. Trained investigators collected data via face-to-face interviews using a standardized questionnaire.

Study Participants

A total of 7250 individuals were initially surveyed. After excluding 281 participants due to incomplete data, 6969 provided valid responses and were included in the present analysis.

The inclusion criteria for cervical spondylosis in this study were: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) a previous diagnosis of cervical spondylosis based on both radiographic evidence of structural changes (such as disc space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and cervical curvature abnormalities) and characteristic clinical symptoms corresponding to each subtype (including axial neck pain, cervical radiculopathy, and cervical myelopathy).

The exclusion criteria were: (1) severe cervical myelopathy requiring surgical intervention; (2) a history of cervical spine surgery; (3) cervical trauma or fracture; (4) inflammatory or infectious spinal diseases; (5) spinal tumors; (6) other spinal disorders such as ankylosing spondylitis or ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament; and (7) pregnancy or lactation (due to contraindications for radiographic examination).

Based on these criteria, 1033 patients were identified as having been previously diagnosed with cervical spondylosis. Among these, 766 had received systematic hospital-based conservative treatment, and 668 achieved clinical remission following treatment. Data Collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a standardized electronic questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed based on a comprehensive review of relevant literature and underwent multiple rounds of expert consultation to reach a consensus, ensuring its scientific rigor and relevance to the study objectives. Prior to the formal survey, a pilot test was conducted with a small sample (n=50) to evaluate the clarity, comprehensibility, and reliability of the items; the questionnaire was subsequently refined based on the feedback received. These development and validation steps were implemented to minimize potential subjectivity and inconsistency in self-reported data.

During data collection, trained investigators administered the questionnaire. To enhance data accuracy, participants were provided with clear instructions and recall periods for key variables were deliberately kept short to reduce memory-related biases. Furthermore, upon completion of each interview, another researcher independently reviewed the questionnaire for completeness and logical consistency, with any missing or ambiguous entries clarified by follow-up contact with the participant. All predictor variables—including exercise duration, PCS score, lifestyle factors, and radiographic findings—were measured at the time of the survey, while recurrence status was retrospectively recalled by patients. The complete raw dataset used for this analysis is available in Supplementary File 1.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome of this study was disease recurrence following systematic hospital-based conservative treatment for cervical spondylosis. We defined “systematic hospital-based conservative treatment” as patient-reported receipt of at least one course of conservative treatment in a hospital setting, which may include physical therapy, manual therapy, medications, or other non-surgical interventions. Remission was defined as patient-reported symptom disappearance or substantial relief, with no need for further treatment following the course of conservative therapy. Recurrence was strictly defined as the reappearance of symptoms that necessitated repeated medical consultations or additional treatment, or that were objectively confirmed by a physician through clinical examination, following initial remission. For further supplementary analyses, recurrence frequency, categorized as no recurrence, occasional recurrence, or frequent recurrence, and time to recurrence, measured in days from symptom remission to the first recurrence, were also assessed to analyze the association between predictors and recurrence timing.

Selection of Predictors

Potential factors associated with cervical spine disorder recurrence were systematically assessed through a combination of questionnaire-based and radiographic measurements.

To ensure measurement reliability, all radiographs were independently read by two trained radiologists following standardized protocols. In cases of disagreement, final decisions were reached through discussion or consultation with a senior radiologist to guarantee the consistency and accuracy of the recorded findings.

Statistical Analysis

A retrospective analysis was conducted among patients who had completed treatment for cervical spondylosis. Recurrence status was determined based on patient-reported symptom reappearance after initial remission. The primary outcome was disease recurrence, defined as a binary variable. For baseline characteristics, continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and group comparisons were performed using the t-test. Continuous variables with a non-normal distribution were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) [n (%)], and group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test.

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 7:3. Univariate analysis was conducted on the training set to screen for variables potentially associated with recurrence. Variables with a P-value < 0.1 were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Variables with a P-value ≥ 0.05 were then sequentially removed, and the remaining independent predictors were retained. A nomogram was subsequently constructed based on these predictors to enable individualized prediction of recurrence risk. The selected variables and the resulting model were applied to the validation set. Model performance was evaluated in both the training and validation sets using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).

To examine the robustness of the findings, two supplementary analyses were performed. First, the outcome was redefined as frequency of episodes (no recurrence, occasional recurrence, frequent recurrence). An ordinal logistic regression model was used to assess the associations between the independent variables and the ordinal outcome. The proportional odds assumption was evaluated using the Brant test. Because the overall test rejected the parallel regression assumption, this indicated that the effects of the independent variables on the outcome were inconsistent across different cut-points of the ordinal outcome (Supplementary Table S1).

Therefore, a generalized ordinal logistic regression model was used to relax the parallel regression assumption, allowing the effects of covariates to vary across different outcome categories. Second, in the subgroup of patients who experienced recurrence, linear regression was used to explore the associations between each predictor and time to recurrence (in days), with time to recurrence as the dependent variable. Both supplementary analyses included the independent predictors that were ultimately identified in the main analysis. These supplementary analyses aimed to improve the reliability and generalizability of the findings. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1), and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Evaluation and Validation of the Predictive Model

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted separately for the two datasets, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the discriminative ability of the predictive model. The AUC of the training set was 0.760, exceeding the clinically acceptable threshold of 0.7, confirming that the model has good discriminative performance (Figure 4). Calibration curves indicated that, in the training set, the model closely followed the ideal line in both the low-to-moderate risk range (predicted probability 0.3–0.7) and the high-risk range (>0.8), suggesting high confidence and low bias in predicting low- and high-probability events. Slight overestimation was observed in the intermediate range (0.7–0.8), but the overall impact on calibration was minimal. The validation set showed some systematic deviations: slight overestimation in the low-to-moderate risk range and slight underestimation in the moderate-to-high risk range. Nevertheless, the calibration curve remained approximately parallel to the ideal line, without severe deviations (Figure 5). Decision curve analysis further demonstrated that, within a recurrence risk threshold range of approximately 0.1–0.9, using the model for risk-stratified management yielded a significantly higher net benefit than either the “treat all aggressively” or “treat all conventionally” strategies (Figure 6).Figure 4ROC curves of the training set (a) and validation set (b). Discussion

This study identified associations rather than causal determinants of post-treatment recurrence in patients with cervical spondylosis, providing new evidence for individualized risk prediction and long-term management. It is important to distinguish between risk factors for initial onset and predictors of post-treatment recurrence, as these may involve different mechanisms. Many previously identified factors, such as age, occupation, and poor posture, are primarily associated with the occurrence of neck pain rather than relapse after conservative treatment. Recurrence is more likely influenced by disease chronicity, prior episode frequency, and post-remission behaviors. In this study, we specifically focused on predictors of recurrence after remission, which may require different preventive strategies from those targeting initial onset. Our results demonstrated that exercise duration per session, neck length, intervertebral disc space status, disease duration, pre-treatment attack frequency, and physical health (PCS score) were independently associated with recurrence. These findings suggest that recurrence is associated with multiple factors, including patients’ physical condition, disease chronicity, and prior disease activity, rather than being determined by any single cause.

The overall recurrence rate in this cohort was 84.0% (561/668), substantially higher than previously reported in the literature. This high rate may be attributed to several factors. First, our definition of recurrence was broad, encompassing any symptom reappearance requiring medical consultation or physician confirmation, without requiring a minimum symptom-free interval or severity threshold, which may have included mild or transient episodes not counted as recurrence in other studies. Second, retrospective self-reported data may have introduced recall bias, as patients with more severe or recent symptoms may have been more likely to report past episodes, leading to overestimation. Third, our study population may represent a subgroup with more active disease—all participants had received hospital-based conservative treatment and volunteered for a cervical health survey, potentially enriching the cohort with more symptomatic or health-conscious individuals. Despite this high rate, the model maintained good discriminative ability (AUC=0.760) and calibration in the training set, suggesting reliability even in this high-risk population. Future prospective studies with standardized recurrence definitions and objective follow-up are warranted to confirm our findings.

Among these factors, exercise duration per session was significantly associated with reduced recurrence risk and prolonged time to recurrence, consistent with previous studies suggesting that regular exercise may enhance cervical spine stability and musculoskeletal health.13–16 However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Exercise duration was self-reported and measured at the time of the survey, potentially after recurrence had already occurred. Therefore, we cannot conclude that longer exercise duration directly reduces recurrence risk; it may instead serve as a marker of better baseline health, health literacy, socioeconomic status, treatment adherence, or access to rehabilitation, rather than representing an independent therapeutic factor. The observation that exercise frequency was not significant in the model, whereas duration per session was retained, warrants further investigation and may reflect measurement error, collinearity, categorization, or chance. Notably, the effect of exercise on progression from occasional to frequent recurrence was modest, suggesting that additional interventions may be needed for patients with high baseline disease activity. Future studies using objective physical activity monitoring are warranted to clarify these relationships.

Furthermore, longer disease duration and higher pre-treatment attack frequency were associated with increased recurrence risk and earlier relapse. These findings suggest that chronic and recurrent episodes of cervical spondylosis, through prolonged mechanical stress and repeated symptoms, may induce structural and functional changes in cervical tissues, exacerbate degenerative changes, and reduce cervical spine resilience, thereby increasing susceptibility to subsequent episodes, which is consistent with previous studies.9,17–19 Clinically, these results underscore the importance of early intervention and proactive monitoring, particularly for patients with long-standing or frequently recurring disease.

Overall physical health status also emerged as a factor associated with recurrence. Lower PCS scores, which reflect poorer physical condition including reduced muscle strength, flexibility, and postural control, were associated with a higher risk of recurrence.17,20,21 However, this association should be interpreted with caution. PCS scores were measured at the time of the survey, which may have been after recurrence had already occurred. It is possible that lower PCS scores partly reflect current symptoms and functional limitations related to the recurrence episode itself, rather than representing a pre-existing risk factor. This creates a potential risk of outcome-predictor overlap or reverse causation. Nevertheless, the observed association highlights the need for comprehensive rehabilitation programs that address not only cervical symptoms but also general physical fitness and functional capacity to improve long-term outcomes.

Interestingly, intervertebral disc space abnormalities were found to be negatively associated with recurrence, suggesting that structural degeneration does not necessarily translate into a higher risk of relapse. One possible explanation is that the presence of obvious radiographic abnormalities may serve as a “teachable moment”, prompting patients to proactively adopt protective behaviors—such as increasing exercise, optimizing sitting and working postures, or using cervical support measures—thereby indirectly reducing recurrence risk.22 However, this hypothesis is not directly supported by the data presented in our study and requires further validation. Alternative explanations, such as unmeasured confounding, reverse causation, or selection bias, cannot be excluded. Neck length was protective in the primary analysis but showed no significant association with time to recurrence, suggesting that anatomical factors may influence recurrence indirectly, potentially through biomechanical effects on cervical load distribution and posture habits.23,24

In addition, we examined the associations of electronic device use and head-down duration with recurrence after cervical spine treatment and found no significant associations. Several explanations may account for these null findings. First, risk factors for disease onset may differ from those for post-treatment recurrence; although forward-neck posture and excessive screen time are important for initial development,25 their impact on relapse after remission may be weaker, possibly due to differences in underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Second, self‑reported exposure measures are subject to recall bias, which may attenuate true associations. Third, our broad definition of recurrence included many mild and transient episodes, potentially diluting the influence of posture or device use. Finally, categorization of exposure variables may have reduced statistical power and obscured dose–response relationships. Future studies using objective posture monitoring and a stricter recurrence definition are warranted to further clarify these relationships.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, recurrence was self-reported without a predefined symptom-free interval, standardized diagnostic criteria, or uniform follow-up, which may introduce recall bias and outcome misclassification, and the unequal time at risk across participants was not accounted for in binary logistic regression. Second, predictor variables were measured after remission while recurrence was retrospectively recalled, so temporal sequencing cannot be established and only associations can be inferred. Third, self-reported lifestyle factors, disease duration, and pre-treatment attack frequency are subject to recall bias and imprecise categorization, which may have masked dose–response relationships. Fourth, the small number of non-recurrent participants may have affected subgroup analyses. Fifth, conservative treatment details were not systematically collected, limiting assessment of specific treatment effects. Sixth, stepwise variable selection may overestimate model performance, and external validation is needed. Finally, imaging was limited to radiographs; MRI could provide additional soft-tissue evaluation. Prospective studies with objective measures and standardized protocols are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified several factors associated with post-treatment recurrence in patients with cervical spondylosis. Exercise duration, disease chronicity, pre-treatment attack frequency, overall physical health, and certain anatomical characteristics were found to be associated with recurrence risk. However, as discussed above, these associations should not be interpreted as causal determinants. In particular, exercise duration, although associated with reduced recurrence risk in our analysis, cannot be confirmed as an independent protective factor based on the current data, given the potential for reverse causation, confounding, and the self-reported nature of the exposure. Intervertebral disc space abnormalities showed a negative association with recurrence in our analysis, which may be related to patient behavioral responses to radiographic findings, but this finding is speculative and requires further validation. These findings underscore the importance of individualized risk stratification, early intervention, and comprehensive rehabilitation to reduce recurrence and improve long-term cervical spine health. Prospective studies with objective measurements and standardized definitions of recurrence are warranted to confirm these associations and establish causal relationships.
