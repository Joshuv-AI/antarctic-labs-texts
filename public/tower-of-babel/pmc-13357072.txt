Multi-omics identification of an antibody dependent cellular phagocytosis related signature for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Xisheng Yin, Wenmeng Yin, Qin Yan, Shi Zheng, Yantong Li, Xiaolin Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe reduced phagocytic activity of macrophages in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and the presence of anti-phagocytic factors in cancer cells hinder the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in clinical settings. Consequently, our objective was to create a risk assessment model using antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) regulators to serve as prognostic indicators and immunotherapy markers for individuals with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).MethodsThis research incorporated The Cancer Genome Atlas-Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) group, the GSE14520 group, the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) group, and the single-cell dataset GSE166635. Machine learning was used to discover ADCP genes closely linked to prognosis, helping to determine the molecular subtypes of ADCP in HCC samples. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and multivariate Cox regression algorithms were used to develop the ADCP-related signature (ADCPRS). This signature was evaluated using both training and external validation datasets. Furthermore, a nomogram that integrates ADCPRS was created to function as a quantitative instrument for predicting outcomes in clinical settings. A comprehensive multi-omics investigation was performed, integrating genomic, single-cell transcriptomic, and bulk transcriptomic analyses, with the aim of gaining a more profound understanding of the prognostic signature. We assessed how different risk categories respond to immunotherapy and identified drugs tailored to target these specific groups for customized treatment.ResultsWe identified two ADCP subgroups by analyzing 91 genes linked to HCC prognosis. The two ADCP subgroups showed distinct outcomes, pathway activation, immune checkpoint gene expression levels, and immune cell infiltration within the TME. We developed a unified ADCPRS that excels in forecasting clinicopathological characteristics, patient outcomes, and TME stromal activity in HCC. The nomogram incorporating ADCPRS offered a numerical tool for use in medical practice. Notably, the high-risk group presented inflamed TME with an improved response to ICI. In addition, the high-ADCPRS group presented a high sensitivity to the derivative compounds provided by Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) and Profiling Relative Inhibition Simultaneously in Mixtures (PRISM).ConclusionsOur research developed a signature linked to ADCP, which shows promise as a key tool for predicting outcomes, implementing prevention, and enabling personalized treatment in HCC.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), multi-omics, single-cell RNA-seq, tumor immune microenvironment

Highlight box

Key findings

• Integrating bulk and single-cell transcriptomics, we identified two distinct antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) subgroups in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Using machine learning, we developed and validated an ADCP-related signature (ADCPRS). This model accurately predicts prognosis and tumor microenvironment (TME) activity. Notably, the high-risk group exhibited an inflamed TME, predicting an improved response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and high sensitivity to specific therapeutic compounds.

What is known and what is new?

• Tumor-associated macrophages and ADCP evasion significantly hinder immunotherapy efficacy in HCC, yet the specific prognostic value of ADCP regulators remains underexplored.

• We constructed a novel ADCPRS that robustly stratifies HCC patients. Our multi-omics approach links ADCP profiles directly to TME immune infiltration, demonstrating that high-ADCPRS patients possess an inflamed TME and are more likely to benefit from ICI therapies.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings highlight the potential of ADCPRS as a prognostic and predictive biomarker in HCC. While requiring further clinical validation, this model provides a theoretical basis for patient stratification and offers new perspectives on exploring macrophage-targeted immunotherapies.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is recognized as one of the primary contributors to cancer-related deaths worldwide, with a grim outlook. Due to the insidious onset of HCC, at the time of first diagnosis, less than 30% of HCC patients are suitable for radical treatment (1). Several therapeutic options are also available for advanced stages. However, less than one-third of patients benefit from treatment, and a substantial number of patients will develop significant drug resistance (2). Therefore, none of the current therapies significantly improves the prognosis of this disease. Identifying and managing HCC remain highly challenging (3). Therefore, further research is necessary to find better approaches to treat HCC. In recent years, immunotherapy for HCC has achieved remarkable efficacy. In particular, the combination therapy based on an immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) can achieve an objective efficiency of about 30% (4).

Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) is an important biological process in which abnormal cells are “labeled” with antibodies, and immune cells such as macrophages are activated to phagocytize and clear these labeled cells. The therapeutic strategy based on monoclonal antibodies targeting tumor antigens significantly promotes the clearance of tumor cells by inducing macrophage-mediated ADCP responses (5,6). However, tumor cells evade phagocytosis through a series of mechanisms, which remain one of the key obstacles affecting the efficacy of antibody drugs. Recent studies based on clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) screening have identified core molecules such as APMAP in the regulation of ADCP, suggesting that enhancing ADCP activity is expected to significantly improve the efficacy of monoclonal antibody treatment (7). In recent years, breakthroughs have been made in tumor immunotherapy, especially in the application of ICI. The core mechanism lies in relieving the immunosuppression of T cells and achieving a durable and extensive anti-tumor response (8). It is worth noting that tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) have been increasingly confirmed as key regulatory factors affecting T cell function inhibition, monoclonal antibody efficacy, and drug resistance due to their high heterogeneity and plasticity in the tumor microenvironment (TME) (9). The phagocytic activity of TAMs is often negatively regulated by multiple “anti-phagocytic” signals, a phenomenon that further weakens the immunotherapeutic effects including ADCP and ICI (10).

HCC has a unique TME. Multi-dimensional studies have confirmed that TAMs are one of the most abundant immune cells in the TME and are almost present at all stages of liver cancer occurrence and progression. The activity and polarization state of TAMs can significantly affect the immunotherapy response of HCC patients. By regulating the activity of TAMs, it is expected to enhance the sensitivity of HCC to ICI therapies such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) (11,12).

ADCP, as an important mechanism by which monoclonal antibody drugs mediate macrophages to clear tumor cells, its related regulators may affect the overall effect of immunotherapy. Therefore, this study aims to explore the predictive role of ADCP regulatory factors in HCC, develop and validate a multivariate prediction model, and investigate its role in identifying potential candidate patients who may benefit from ICI treatment. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0176/rc).

Methods

Data acquisition and processing

Clinical data and RNA-seq mRNA expression profiles for The Cancer Genome Atlas-Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) cohort were obtained from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The GSE14520 dataset was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Expression data and clinical details pertaining to LIRI-JP were retrieved from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC, https://dcc.icgc.org/). Samples with complete prognostic information and clinical annotations were included in the subsequent analysis. A summary of the clinical characteristics for all analyzed HCC datasets is presented in Table S1. For further analyses, RNA-seq count data were transformed into transcripts per million (TPM). Information regarding copy number variations (CNVs) was obtained from https://xena.ucsc.edu/. CNV data for the TCGA-LIHC cohort and was processed with GISTIC 2.0. Amplification was defined by scores above 0.2 and depletion by scores below −0.2. The IMvigor210 study group was utilized to assess the potential for predicting responses to immunotherapy. This dataset was obtained from http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies/. Single-cell RNA sequencing data for HCC, comprising two tumor samples, which were obtained from the GEO database (GSE166635). ADCP-related genes (ADCPRgenes) were compiled from the research conducted by Roarke A. Kamber et al. (7). A total of 543 ADCPRgenes were identified, adhering to a 5% false discovery rate (FDR) or falling within a 95% confidence interval (CI). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Identification of differentially expressed ADCPR genes

The DEseq2 algorithm (13) was used for differential analysis of ADCPR genes, applying criteria of |log2fold change (FC)| >1 and P value <0.05. The ‘ggplot2’ R package was used to construct the volcano plot, and the ‘ComplexHeatmap’ R package was employed to create the heat map.

Multi-omics analysis utilizing Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA)

The GSCA (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/) (14) platform is an online tool that combines various omics data derived from the TCGA database. The platform was utilized to examine CNV alterations in genes with varying expression levels and to assess the Spearman correlation between gene expression and CNV. We examined gene-specific methylation differences and analyzed the Spearman correlation between gene expression and methylation. Meanwhile, we predicted the relationship between differentially expressed genes and drug sensitivity.

Identification of different ADCP subgroups

To mitigate batch effects, we merged the TCGA and GSE14520 cohorts into a unified dataset utilizing the ‘sva’ R package (15). ADCP regulators were isolated from the combined dataset, and the ‘randomSurvivalForest’ R package was utilized to pinpoint ADCPRgenes associated with prognostic outcomes. Using the ‘ConsensusClusterPlus’ R package, 618 HCC patients were classified based on prognosis-related ADCPR genes (16). Principal component analysis (PCA) was utilized for dimensionality reduction to validate the clustering accuracy. A Kaplan-Meier survival curve was created to evaluate survival rate differences among subgroups. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was utilized to determine the unique biological functions within ADCP clusters (17).

TME analysis between different ADCP subtypes

The ESTIMATE method was used to evaluate the relative abundance of immune and stromal cells in the tumor tissue. The ESTIMATE algorithm’s R script was obtained from the publicly accessible resource site (https://sourceforge.net/projects/estimateproject/). The marker genes associated with immune cells were compiled from the research conducted by Bindea et al. (18). We used transcriptomic data to apply single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) for assessing 28 immune cell types in HCC samples (17). We analyzed the expression levels of immune checkpoint molecules (ICMs) and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes across various subgroups. Additionally, we expanded the ssGSEA analysis to examine the variations in cancer immune cycles among various ADCP clusters (19).

ADCPRS construction and validation

We initially employed univariate Cox regression analysis to identify prognostically significant ADCPR genes in the TCGA-LIHC dataset. Using the ‘glmnet’ R package, we performed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis to identify potential risk genes and eliminate those contributing to model overfitting. We developed an ADCP-related signature (ADCPRS) to predict the overall survival (OS) of HCC patients. Patients were categorized into high and low-risk groups based on the median ADCPRS risk score. Survival outcomes between high- and low-risk groups were evaluated by generating Kaplan-Meier plots to identify significant differences. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the sensitivity and specificity of the ADCPRS in predicting OS in HCC patients. The robustness of the prognostic model was further confirmed through external validation using datasets from the GEO and the ICGC.

Independent prognostic and clinical correlation analysis

We performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses on the TCGA-LHC, GEO, and ICGC datasets to evaluate ADCPRS as an independent prognostic indicator for HCC patient survival. Additionally, we investigated the relationship between ADCPRS and various clinical attributes such as status, gender, grade, and stage, and T. Fisher’s exact test was employed to examine the disparities in clinical phenotypes among the ADCPRS risk categories. Ultimately, we evaluated the ADCPR’s precision and accuracy through the consistency index (C-index) and determined its net clinical benefit via decision curve analysis (DCA).

Nomogram construction

We created a nomogram that integrates ADCPRS and clinical features to improve the model’s prognostic precision and predictive capability, thereby estimating the survival likelihood of HCC patients. A nomogram estimated the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates for individuals with HCC. The nomogram and calibration curves were plotted using the “rms” R package. The accuracy of the nomogram was estimated by calculating the agreement index between the actual observed frequency and the predicted probability. Calibration curves were used to show the properties of the nomogram.

Comprehensive analysis of immune characteristics and ICI therapy response between ADCPRS risk subgroups

The relationship between ADCPRS and immune characteristics was assessed using two primary methods: the ‘CIBERSORT’ R package was employed to quantify the infiltration levels of 22 different immune cell types in the tumor samples. The MCPcounter method was employed to assess the presence of immune and stromal cells in the HCC TME (20). The GSVA method was utilized to assess pathway activity based on hallmark pathway markers. The Spearman coefficient was used to examine the correlation between the ADCPRS risk score and immune cells. We thoroughly examined the mRNA levels of immune checkpoints and pro-inflammatory markers across various ADCPRS risk categories. Furthermore, we acquired the collection of genes linked to the cancer immune cycle from the website (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.created by Xu et al. (21). Mariathasan’s research (22) identified a gene signature positively correlated with clinical outcomes to anti-PD-L1 treatment (atezolizumab), using the ssGSEA method to assess the enrichment scores of these immune signatures. The Pearson correlation method was utilized to examine the relationship between the ADCPRS risk score and immune markers. The IMvigor210 immunotherapy cohort conducted clinical trials with anti-PD-L1 antibodies, and the IMvigor210 dataset was used to examine the capability of riskScore in predicting immunotherapy response. Utilizing the ADCPRS formula, we computed the ADCPRS risk scores for patients in the IMvigor210 cohort and examined their correlation with immune checkpoint genes and immune response.

The prediction of potential small-molecule drugs and resistance to chemotherapy

Based on sensitivity information from Profiling Relative Inhibition Simultaneously in Mixtures (PRISM; https://www.theprismlab) and Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP; https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp.v2.1/), along with the ‘pRRophetic’ R package, we forecasted possible therapeutic options for high-risk patients to facilitate tailored treatment. Medication response was assessed via the area under the curve (AUC), with lower AUC values signifying greater drug sensitivity. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to determine the relationship between compound AUC values and riskScore.Substances showing a correlation coefficient less than −0.5 were associated with reduced AUC values in the high-risk category. Drug responsiveness data and genomic profiles of cancer cell lines were obtained from GDSC (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) and CCLE (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ccle) to validate potential treatments for high-risk patients.

Comparison of variance profiles in high- and low-risk groups

The CNV data was analyzed using Gistic 2.0 on Genepattern’s web platform (https://cloud.genepattern.org/gp/pages/index.jsf). Later, researchers detected substantial increases and losses in chromosome segments and assessed variations in CNV across chromosome arms. The “ggplot2” R package was used to visualize CNV results.

Single-cell analysis and cell-cell interaction analysis

Using the Seurat package (23) for scRNA-seq data analysis, 24,637 cells were retained post-quality control (QC), defined by <15% mitochondrial gene content and genes expressed in at least three cells, with expression levels between 100 and 7,500. Following normalization and PCA on highly variable genes, we utilized the first 40 principal components with a resolution of 0.1 to cluster all cells. Major cell types were annotated based on highly expressed marker genes or known functional genes for cell subtypes and visualized using the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) method. The ssGSEA algorithm assessed the ADCP score for each cell, categorizing those with scores above 0 as the high-risk group. The distinct genes between the two groups were analyzed for enrichment using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) methods. The CellChat R package (24) was employed to deduce, examine, and display intercellular communication among different cell types using scRNA-seq data, as well as to pinpoint distinct ligand-receptor interactions and particular signaling pathways. We further performed differential analysis of the ADCP scores between high-risk and low-risk cell groups, ultimately focusing on hepatocytes, which exhibited the most significant increase in ADCP score. Hepatocytes were subsequently extracted for additional dimensionality reduction and clustering. To infer cellular developmental trajectories, we applied the Vector algorithm (25).

Mapping ADCPscore landscapes by spatial transcriptomics

We from CROST database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/crost/) for HCC space transcriptome samples (sample ID: VISDS000508). To more accurately depict the tissue microenvironment in the spatial dimension, we adopted the Cottrazm algorithm in combination with histological morphological features to correct and adjust the original spatial transcriptome data, and integrated CNV features to assist in determining and precisely depicting the tumor boundary (26). Based on the analysis results of Cottrazm, we identified cell populations of different states in this sample (characterized by spot dimension), including malignant (Mal, spots with clear tumor features) and mixed (Bdy, spots located at the edge/transition zone of the tumor and presenting mixed features. It may simultaneously contain tumor and non-tumor signals), as well as normal (nMal, non-tumor spots consistent with normal tissue features). We utilized the GSVA algorithm to evaluate the spatial distribution of ADCPscore and compare the differences in ADCPscore among different cell states. Meanwhile, we explored the spatial expression patterns of the model genes SORD, KDELR3 and VPS72 in HCC tissue sections. To further clarify the relationship between the spatial structure of tumors and the ADCPscore, we defined the geometric centers of all malignant spots as “tumor cores”. On this basis, we calculated the Euclidean distance from each spot to the tumor core and conducted a correlation analysis between this distance and the corresponding ADCPscore. To present the trend intuitively, we use the loess smooth curve for visual display.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R (4.4.1), with group comparisons conducted via the Kruskal-Wallis, t-test, or Wilcoxon test. Unless specified otherwise, P values below 0.05 were deemed to be statistically significant.

Results

Landscape of expression, genetic mutation, and DNA methylation of differentially expressed ADCPRgenes

A total of 543 ADCPR genes were gathered from prior research (7), and 512 were identified within the TCGA cohort. We conducted a differential analysis of ADCPRgenes within the TCGA-LIHC cohort to identify the differences between HCC and normal samples. A total of 77 differentially expressed ADCPR genes were identified, including 56 upregulated and 21 downregulated genes, based on thresholds of |log2FC|>1 and P value <0.05 (Figure 1A; Table S2). The heatmap displays the top 20 genes with the most significant up- and down-regulation (Figure 1B). We observed an imbalance in the expression of ADCPRgenes, with the majority of genes showing significantly increased expression compared to normal tissues. Subsequently, we identified whether genetic variants affected the expression of ADCP regulators in HCC, and then we performed a multi-omics analysis on differentially expressed ADCPRgenes. Among the 97 HCC samples, MAPIB exhibited the highest mutation frequency, followed by ZDBF2 (Figure 1C).

Further analysis revealed that CNV widely occurs in ADCP regulators, with amplification being the major type of mutation (Figure 1D). CNV in ADCPRgenes on chromosomes was demonstrated in Figure 1E. Spearman correlation analysis further validated that CNV had a positive association with the mRNA levels of the majority of ADCP regulators (Figure 1F). DNA methylation was the primary epigenetic modification, and hypermethylation tended to suppress gene expression. We observed that ZDBF2 was hypermethylated in HCC, while LIMK2 was hypomethylated in HCC (Figure 1G), and both methylation levels were negatively correlated with gene expression (Figure 1H). Overall, our findings indicated that CNV and methylation variants may serve as potential contributors to dysregulated ADCPRgenes expression.

Unsupervised clustering analysis to identify ADCP subtypes in HCC patients

We combined the TCGA and GSE14520 cohorts into a single dataset, correcting for batch effects using the ComBat function from the ’sva’ package (Figure 2). We extracted 327 ADCPRgenes from the integrated dataset and screened 91 prognosis-associated ADCPRgenes based on random survival forest analysis (Figure 2C; Table S3). The 618 HCC samples were divided into two distinct and stable ADCP subtypes according to the expression levels of prognosis-related ADCPRgenes (Figure 2D,2E). The survival analysis indicated a notable survival advantage for ADCPclusterA (Figure 2F). Functional enrichment and tumor immune microenvironment analysis of two ADCP subgroups

Our GSVA analysis of the KEGG pathway revealed that ADCPclusterA exhibited significant metabolic activity, including glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism; xenobiotic metabolism via cytochrome p450; primary bile acid synthesis; tyrosine and histidine metabolism; fatty acid and butanoate metabolism; tryptophan and beta-alanine metabolism; degradation of valine, leucine, and isoleucine; propanoate metabolism; arginine and proline metabolism; alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism; and linoleic acid metabolism, among other processes. In contrast, these signatures were inhibited in ADCPclusterB, indicating inactive metabolism (Figure 3A). Similarly, GSVA analysis of the HALLMARK pathway indicated an upregulation of metabolic markers such as heme metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and bile acid metabolism in ADCPclusterA (Figure 3B). In ADCPclusterB, enrichment of cancer-promoting pathways such as KRAS signaling, TGF-BETA signaling, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling was observed (Figure 3B), indicating a negative prognosis. The findings suggested that ADCPRgenes could play a role in regulating biological activities.

The immune profiles of the subgroups were assessed and compared using the ESTIMATE and ssGSEA algorithms to evaluate immune scores and cell infiltration (Figure 3C). ADCPclusterB exhibited higher immune cell infiltration overall, except for CD56 dim natural killer cells, eosinophils, gamma delta T cells, neutrophils, and type 17 T helper cells, which had lower infiltration levels compared to ADCPclusterA (Figure 3D). Of the 36 immune checkpoints included in the analysis, 29 immune checkpoint genes were statistically different between subtypes and all exhibited upregulation in ADCPclusterB (Figure 3E). In the new generation of “immune checkpoint antibodies”, HLA genes are attracting more attention, and loss of HLA genes will promote cancer progression and lead to immunotherapy resistance (27,28). In this study, most HLA genes were highly expressed in ADCPclusterB (Figure 3F). We then examined the antitumor immune response, finding that most antitumor immune steps were activated in ADCPclusterB, consistent with previous results (Figure 3G). In summary, in the tumor samples of the ADCPclusterB subtype, the infiltration levels of most immune cells and the expression levels of HLA genes were significantly elevated, presenting typical characteristics of an “inflammation-activated” immune microenvironment. However, this subtype is simultaneously accompanied by the synchronous high expression of most immune checkpoint genes, forming a significant immunosuppressive signal and thereby weakening the anti-tumor effect brought about by high immune infiltration. The above results emphasized the close connection between ADCPRgenes and the immune landscape of tumors.

Developing a prognostic model utilizing ADCPR genes in HCC

The ADCPRgenes with differential expression were subsequently subjected to a univariate Cox analysis, leading to the identification of 26 genes linked to OS in HCC (Figure 4A) (Table S4). To prevent multicollinearity and overfitting, we applied LASSO regression to the prognostic genes, identifying four genes with a stronger correlation to HCC prognosis (Figure 4B,4C). ADCPRS (SORD, KDELR3 and VPS72) was constructed by multivariate COX regression, ADCPRS risk score = (−0.105 * SORD expression) + (0.101 * KDELR3 expression) + (0.407 * VPS72 expression) (Figure 4D). We calculated a risk score for each patient using the regression coefficients from the Cox model applied to the expression levels of the three genes. This allowed for the classification of patients into high-risk and low-risk categories based on the median risk score.

We analyzed the expression levels of three genes and evaluated their prognostic significance (Figure S2). SORD expression was down-regulated in tumor tissues, while KDELR3 and VPS72 expressions were up-regulated (Figure S2A,S2C,S2E). Higher SORD expression, based on median levels, correlated with improved OS, disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) (Figure S2B). Conversely, elevated KDELR3 expression correlated with worse OS and DSS in HCC patients (Figure S2D), while increased VPS72 expression was notably associated with reduced OS, DSS, and PF in HCC patients (Figure S2F).

ADCPRS is the independent prognostic factor for survival of HCC patients

We evaluated the potential of the three-gene ADCP signature as an independent prognostic marker for the TCGA-LIHC dataset through univariate and multivariate COX regression analyses, considering clinical factors like Age, Gender, Grade, Stage, and T. The univariate analysis revealed that ADCPRS significantly correlated with OS [hazard ratio (HR) >1, P<0.001] (Figure 6A). Additionally, the multivariate analysis showed that ADCPRS continued to be an independent predictor for OS (HR 1.525, 95% CI: 1.216–1.913, P<0.001) (Figure 6B). Independent prognostic analysis results for the GSE14520 cohort and the ICGC cohort were presented in Figure S3. Given that clinical traits are frequently used in medical practice to predict the outcomes of HCC patients, we analyzed the relationship between the ADCPRS and several clinical features. In the TCGA-LIHC dataset, significant differences in status, grade, stage, and T distribution were observed between the high-risk and low-risk groups (Figure 6C). The DCA graphs (Figure 6D) and C-index charts (Figure 6E) indicated that the risk score exhibited superior predictive accuracy over other clinicopathological features. We developed a nomogram integrating the ADCPRS risk score and clinicopathological features to enhance clinical relevance (Figure 6F) and validated its predictive accuracy through calibration analysis (Figure 6G). The results indicate that the ADCPRS offered a dependable and precise instrument for individualized prognosis forecasting in HCC.

Immune profile in groups with varying risk levels

The CIBERSORT and MCPcounter algorithms were applied to evaluate the abundance of different immune cells. Combined analyses indicated a higher immune infiltration rate in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group. The CIBERSORT algorithm was utilized to analyze the composition of 22 significant immune cells between high- and low-risk groups (Figure S4A,S4B). Significant differences in immune cell infiltration were observed between high-risk and low-risk tumor groups. TME of the high-risk group exhibited a higher abundance of follicular helper T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and M0 macrophages compared to the low-risk group. In the low-risk group, there was a higher prevalence of cell types lacking anticancer properties, like resting mast cells and M2 macrophages (Figure S4C). MCPcounter analysis indicated a significantly higher presence of T cells, CD8 T cells, B cells, monocytes, myeloid dendritic cells, and fibroblasts in the high-risk group, while neutrophils were more abundant in the low-risk group (Figure S4D). We proposed notable differences in the immune microenvironment between high-risk and low-risk categories.

Analysis of key gene markers revealed distinct hallmark pathways distinguishing high-risk from low-risk groups. The high-risk group exhibited significant enrichment in PI3K AKT MTOR signaling, the P53 pathway, NOTCH signaling, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Suggesting that the activation of these oncogenic pathways could contribute to the poor prognosis observed in this group. The low-risk group exhibited the greatest enrichment in signatures associated with fatty acid, bile acid, and xenobiotic metabolism (Figure S4E). Figure S4F illustrated the Spearman correlation between SORD, KDELR3, VPS72, and riskScore with various immune cells. Additionally, we analyzed the relationship between the expression levels of SORD, KDELR3, and VPS72 genes and macrophages. The findings indicated that SORD expression negatively correlated with macrophages (R=−0.26) (Figure S5A), KDELR3 expression positively correlated with macrophages (R=0.3) (Figure S5B), and VPS72 expression showed no correlation with macrophages (Figure S5C). We analyzed the immune profiles of high-risk and low-risk groups using seven immune cell infiltration methods: TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, XCELL, and EPIC (29-32). Figure S6 illustrates immune cell infiltration distribution in high-risk and low-risk groups via a heat map.

In recent years, immunotherapy for HCC has shown promising prospects, especially ICI combination therapy (4). We analyzed the expression levels of typical inhibitory ICMs in both high-risk and low-risk cohorts. Our analysis revealed that every ICM examined was markedly elevated in the high-risk category (Figure S7A). Furthermore, we examined the relationship between the risk score and key pro-inflammatory elements. In the high-risk cohort, IL-1A, IL-1B, IL-18, and IL-8 expression levels were elevated (Figure S7B).

Given the intricate dynamics of immune responses and the TME, measuring immune cell infiltration alone is inadequate to comprehensively describe immune activation and fatigue. Evaluating actions at each phase of the anti-cancer immune cycle offers deeper insights into immune cells’ roles in combating cancer, thereby enhancing immunotherapy direction (33). Consequently, we conducted an additional assessment of the connection between ADCPRS and the stages of tumor immunity. Figure S7C demonstrates that the high-risk group showed significant upregulation in multiple cancer immune cycle activities, including the release of cancer cell antigens, priming, and activation, recruitment of various immune cells (CD8 T cells, Th22 cells, monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, Th17 cells, Treg cells, and MDSCs), immune cell infiltration into tumors, and the recognition and destruction of cancer cells by T cells. The findings suggest that individuals in the high-risk category may be better candidates for immunotherapy due to their inflamed TME. The high-risk group showed increased enrichment scores for gene signatures linked to positive immunotherapy outcomes (Figure S7D). In addition, riskScore was positively correlated with most cancer immune cycle steps as well as with immunotherapy-positive gene signatures (Figure S7E).

Prediction of immunotherapy response

As previously mentioned, there might be connections between riskScore and immunotherapy response. We examined the dataset of BLCA patients receiving anti-PD-L1 therapy to evaluate this more thoroughly. The TCGA model equation was used to calculate the RiskScore for the IMvigor210 cohort, categorizing participants into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median RiskScore. Our analysis indicated that high-risk individuals showed increased PD-L1 and PD-1 expression compared to low-risk individuals (see Figure S8A,S8B). Patients were categorized into two cohorts based on median PD-L1 and PD-1 gene expression levels. The cohort with higher PD-L1 and PD-1 expression exhibited a greater response rate to immunotherapy (Figure S8C,S8D). Figure S8E,S8F demonstrate a significantly higher riskScore in the complete remission (CR) and partial response (PR) cohort compared to the progressive disease (PD) or stable disease (SD) group (P<0.001). Figure S8G indicates that high-risk individuals exhibited higher objective response rates than low-risk individuals. Overall, may serve as a potentially useful reference for treatment stratification and that those with elevated riskScores were more likely to gain greater advantages from immunotherapy.

Evaluation of drug response data identified four compounds from CTRP (BI-2536, clofarabine, GSK461364, and vincristine) as shown in Figure 7A, and one compound from PRISM (irinotecan) as depicted in Figure 7B. Figure 7C-7F demonstrated the estimated AUC values for clofarabine, paclitaxel, volasertib, and irinotecan. The population classified as high-risk individuals may exhibit a higher sensitivity to these related compounds. Discussion

HCC ranks as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally (39). As of now, there is no singular effective treatment approved for HCC. Nonetheless, considerable proof indicates that immunotherapy could be effective in treating HCC. The role of immunotherapy in predicting relevant pathways and determining prognosis holds significant importance in HCC management. Macrophages are crucial in this mechanism, as the majority of tumors contain a high number of these cells. The exact mechanism by which cancer cells evade phagocytosis is not fully understood. Luckily, new research by Kamber and colleagues. Employed an impartial CRISPR/Cas9 screening technique to discover a new group of cancer-specific genes that promote or avoid ADCP (7). Gene markers derived from this set could improve our understanding of how ADCP influences the variety and intricacy of the TME, aiding in the development of tailored treatments. Therefore, we conducted a systematic analysis of ADCP regulators and characterized the influence of ADCP regulators in HCC. This research involved a comparative analysis of ADCPRgenes, revealing that most were overexpressed in HCC, indicating ADCP activation in this condition. Through random survival forest screening, we identified two distinct ADCP subgroups and thoroughly investigated the variances in biological processes and immune microenvironments between these subgroups. By leveraging variably expressed ADCPRgenes, we created and confirmed an ADCPRS to forecast the outcomes for HCC patients.

The metabolic reprogramming of TME is a well-established hallmark of cancer. Our study’s KEGG enrichment analysis revealed the upregulation of multiple metabolism-related pathways in ADCPclusterA, while these processes were relatively inhibited in ADCPclusterB. Additionally, the presence of HCC stem cells [liver cancer stem cells (LCSCs)] has been identified as contributing to treatment resistance in HCC (40). Research identified the glucose-dependent metabolic pathway KCNN4 (potassium calcium-activated channel subfamily N member 4) as a promising target for eradicating LCSCs in HCC (40). Notably, there is an increased flow of glucose towards the glycolytic pathway and enhanced oxidative phosphorylation through fatty acids and glutamate metabolism in HCC (41-43). Inhibition of glycolysis or fatty acid oxidation has been explored as a strategy to mitigate HCC progression. Therefore, targeting metabolic pathways holds promise as a therapeutic approach for patients with HCC. Furthermore, within ADCPclusterB, several oncogenic pathways were enriched, which may account for the poorer survival outcomes observed within this subgroup.

TME in HCC is characterized by multiple metabolic abnormalities, including alterations in lipid metabolism and the accumulation of lipid metabolites, which contribute to tumor development and local immunosuppression (44). Additionally, intense competition for glucose is common in HCC, as both hypoxic cancer cells and antitumor immune cells exhibit high glucose demand, leading to resistance to ICIs (45,46). Metabolite accumulation in the TME significantly diminishes the efficacy of ICIs in HCC by suppressing the immune response (46). Immune infiltrating cells are vital for tumor progression and influence cancer patient prognosis. Further analysis revealed differential immune activity between the two groups within TME; ADCPclusterA exhibited relative immunosuppression, while ADCPclusterB showed an inflamed TME. Enrichment analysis suggested that this difference might be related to the accumulation of metabolites. Upregulation of most immune cells, ICMs, and HLA molecules was observed in ADCPclusterB, along with increased activation of the cancer-immune cycle steps. Proper functioning of the cancer-immune cycle is essential for effective responses to ICIs (47). These findings have implications for precision medicine approaches in selecting therapeutic strategies for HCC.

We developed an ADCPRS (comprising SORD, KDELR3, and VPS72) and validated its prognostic significance and association with the tumor immune microenvironment in HCC using the GSE14520 and ICGC cohorts. Recent studies have demonstrated that SORD functions as a tumor suppressor by promoting necroptosis and directly modulating macrophage polarization within the HCC microenvironment (48). Additionally, VPS72 has been identified as a potential oncogene whose overexpression is strongly associated with poor prognosis and elevated M0 macrophage infiltration (49). Lymphocytes, including CD4+ helper T cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, are crucial to the immune system. The article (50) indicates that T cells can distinguish between antigen concentrations in healthy and cancerous cells, and T-cell receptor-modified T cells have been investigated for use in immunotherapy. MCPcounter analysis indicated elevated infiltration of B lineage cells and CD8 T cells in the high-risk cohort. B lymphocytes can induce the differentiation of CD8+ T cells into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) (51,52). CTL cells recognize tumor antigens and directly eliminate tumor cells, thereby exhibiting a significant anti-tumor effect (53). Macrophages are among the most prevalent immune-related cells and can be broadly categorized into M1 and M2 subtypes. CIBERSORT analysis indicated a notably higher presence of M2 macrophages in the low-risk category. M2-type macrophages have been reported to secrete several types of cytokines and growth factors and to be engaged in extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression, all of which are important typical features that promote tumor growth and metastasis (54). We conducted GSVA enrichment analysis, revealing pathway activation in the high-risk cohort, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, and the P53 pathway. The Pl3K/AKT pathway is intimately associated with cell growth, programmed cell death, and various other cellular functions. Studies have demonstrated that reducing proteins in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway significantly inhibits the growth and migration of HCC cells (55). Metabolic pathways for fatty acids, bile acids, and xenobiotic metabolism were significantly more active in the low-risk cohort. This suggested that cancer-causing pathways were active in the high-risk patient group, whereas those with a low-risk score showed increased metabolic activity.

The introduction of immunotherapy has radically changed the field of cancer care. Recent studies have shown promising outcomes regarding the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs in treating advanced cancers, including HCC, breast cancer, melanoma, and lung cancer (56). Our analysis of immune checkpoints and pathways in both cohorts revealed that high-risk individuals showed greater immune checkpoint activation and higher scores for immune cycle and predicted immunotherapy pathways, indicating an inflamed TME. We propose that high-risk individuals may benefit more from immunotherapy, as validation of our prediction model with the IMvigor210 cohort revealed elevated risk scores in the CR/PR group. Thus, ADCPRS may serve as a potential computational tool to evaluate immunotherapy response.

For patients in the high-risk category of HCC, we identified potentially effective medications, including compounds like clofarabine, paclitaxel, irinotecan, and volasertib. In particular, volasertib may have better effects in high-risk patients. Volasertib is a powerful and specific inhibitor of cell cycle kinases that causes mitotic arrest and cell death by targeting Plk (25), and it demonstrates a tolerable safety profile as a clinical anticancer drug (26).

This study focused on ADCPRgenes, highlighting their crucial role in HCC prognosis, the TME, and responses to immunotherapy and targeted treatments. We investigated the link between ADCPRS and ICI response, identifying drugs that may benefit high-risk HCC patients and potentially facilitating personalized treatment plans. Although our research produced quite encouraging findings, it is important to recognize a few constraints. Although the ADCPRS was assessed and confirmed in both training and external validation sets, a comprehensive multi-center prospective study is needed for further validation. Secondly, further laboratory and animal research is necessary to clarify the biological roles of ADCPRgenes in HCC. Finally, despite our forecasts regarding the responsiveness of ADCPRS risk categories to different small molecule medications, in vitro drug tests and clinical studies are necessary to confirm these predictions. In the future, this signature could potentially be evaluated via standardized PCR assays.

Conclusions

In summary, we curated ADCPRgenes from the Kamber et al study, identified two ADCP-related subgroups, and further characterized their biological behaviors. Subsequently, we created the corresponding risk score model and integrated clinical characteristics, immune infiltration, biological function, immunotherapy, and drug responsiveness to evaluate the model comprehensively. The prognostic model exhibits potential in stratifying HCC patient outcomes and may serve as a bioinformatic reference to inform future personalized immunotherapy strategies.
