FABP4 in lipid metabolism and the tumor microenvironment: mechanisms and therapeutic potential
Authors: Mengyun Wang, Michael Hawanga, Senlin Wan, Chengwei Wu, Changming Liang, Xu Zhang, Dawei Zhang, Fangshuai Hu, Yongchun Liu, Zhenyuan Li, Yabin Xia, Li Xu, Xiaoming Wang, Xiaoxu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4), a key member of the fatty acid-binding protein family, is involved primarily in fatty acid transport, metabolic regulation, and inflammatory signaling. In recent years, numerous studies have shown that abnormal FABP4 expression in various tumors influences the metabolism of lipids and its metabolic products in tumors, significantly regulating tumor progression through processes such as oxidative stress, epithelial‒mesenchymal transition, angiogenesis, and the immune microenvironment. This review focuses on the role of FABP4 in tumors and systematically elucidates the specific mechanisms through which FABP4 regulates lipid metabolism and alters the tumor microenvironment. This review also provides an in-depth analysis of the complex network relationship between FABP4-mediated lipid metabolism and the tumor microenvironment and reveals the critical role of FABP4 in tumor occurrence, development, and metastasis, offering a new theoretical basis and potential targets for tumor therapy.

Background

Lipids are fundamental in life processes, with essential functions such as energy storage, the ability to form biological membranes, and the ability to participate in signal transduction. Their metabolism includes the uptake of exogenous fatty acids, intracellular lipid synthesis, and processes such as the β-oxidation of fatty acids, membrane lipid renewal, and the conversion of signaling molecules, all of which are regulated by factors such as hormone levels and nutritional status. The functions and metabolic pathways of lipids together maintain the physiological balance of organisms. The uptake, synthesis, oxidation, and storage of fatty acids (FAs) are crucial for the survival, proliferation, and metastasis of tumor cells [1]. Among these, the uptake and transport of fatty acids are key components. Tumor cells mainly synthesize FAs from scratch or take up exogenous FAs from the tumor microenvironment (TME), with the uptake of exogenous FAs requiring transport proteins to maintain solubility and mediate transmembrane transport [2].

Fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs) are a class of small-molecule lipid transport proteins with molecular weights of approximately 14 to 15 kDa. They are widely expressed in mammals and can reversibly bind to hydrophobic ligands such as unsaturated long-chain fatty acids. FABPs participate in biological processes such as lipid droplet storage, signal transduction, and mitochondrial oxidation [3], and they play a role in intercellular signal transmission. Ten FABP family members have been identified in the human body and are categorized into types, such as hepatic and intestinal, on the basis of tissue distribution and functional differences. Each member has unique characteristics in terms of ligand preference, expression patterns, and pathological roles [4]. The FABP family plays diverse roles in signaling, inflammatory responses, tissue development, and other areas by precisely regulating lipid metabolism, thus maintaining physiological homeostasis. Dysfunction is closely associated with various conditions, such as obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and tumors [5].

In recent years, FABPs have garnered significant attention in the field of tumor research. As a member of the fatty acid-binding protein family, FABP4 plays crucial roles not only in cell signaling and metabolic regulation but also in the occurrence and development of metabolic disorders, inflammation, and cancer. This review focuses on exploring the potential role of FABP4 in the tumor microenvironment through the regulation of lipid metabolism and its impact on tumorigenesis and progression, as well as analyzing its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

Biological characteristics and functions of FABP4

Molecular structure and ligand-binding characteristics

FABP4, also known as A-FABP or aP2, is a 15 kDa cytoplasmic protein composed of 134 amino acids. FABP4 has a unique molecular structure comprising 10 β-sheets and 2 α-helices, forming a hydrophobic binding pocket that allows it to specifically bind to long-chain fatty acids (such as oleic acid and palmitic acid), eicosanoids, and retinoic acid, among other hydrophobic molecules [6]. FABP4 is highly expressed in adipocytes, macrophages, endothelial cells, and tumor cells, and its biological functions are closely related to lipid metabolism and signal transduction [7]. FABP4 participates in the regulation of mitochondrial β-oxidation, endoplasmic reticulum lipid synthesis, and nuclear receptor (such as PPARγ and RXR) signaling activation through targeted organelle transport mechanisms, making it a key hub connecting the intracellular lipid distribution with nuclear signaling pathways.

Notably, FABP4 can be secreted into the circulatory system, further expanding its role in intercellular lipid signaling. Therefore, FABP4 not only acts as a “molecular chaperone” for fatty acids but is also a core molecule that integrates metabolic homeostasis and the regulation of cellular function (Fig. 1).

Physiological functions and pathological associations

FABP4 plays multiple critical roles in lipid metabolism. Under physiological conditions, it finely regulates lipolysis by interacting with hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL). This interaction modulates HSL’s catalytic activity and lipolytic function [8–10]. Concurrently, FABP4 interacts with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ). This interaction helps coordinate the expression of genes involved in lipid synthesis and storage [11, 12]. Furthermore, FABP4 promotes the uptake of exogenous fatty acids. It achieves this by specifically binding free fatty acids and acting in concert with CD36 [13]. Moreover, FABP4 participates in the targeted transport of fatty acids. It directs fatty acids to specific organelles—either to mitochondria for β-oxidation and energy production, or to the endoplasmic reticulum for lipid synthesis. This compartmentalized targeting maintains a precise balance between energy supply and the reduction of lipotoxicity.

Under pathological conditions, the aberrant overexpression of FABP4 becomes a core driver of metabolic diseases [14]. Its elevated serum level is not only a biomarker for metabolic syndrome [15] but also directly participates in disease progression. In type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), circulating FABP4 promotes disease progression. It acts by inducing systemic insulin resistance, impairing pancreatic β-cell function, and mediating postprandial muscle insulin resistance [16, 17]. In atherosclerosis, FABP4 accelerates lesion formation. It does this by activating adhesion molecules and the STAT-1 signaling pathway, which leads to endothelial dysfunction [18, 19]. These well-defined mechanisms in classic metabolic diseases provide a solid logical foundation. They help us understand how FABP4 drives tumor progression via analogous pathways, such as promoting M2 macrophage polarization, in the tumor microenvironment.

Expression characteristics of FABP4 in tumors

Expression variations across different tumor types

FABP4 participates in lipid metabolic reprogramming by regulating the efficiency of fatty acid uptake in tumor cells, providing an energy foundation for tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [20]. Its expression, function, and clinical value vary significantly across different cancer types: it is highly expressed in colorectal cancer and breast cancer, where it not only facilitates early diagnosis but also indicates poor prognosis. In gastrointestinal tumors, its high expression is additionally associated with macrophage immune infiltration and epithelial–mesenchymal transition, enabling the assessment of tumor malignancy [21]. Moreover, in lung cancer and renal cancer, it may either drive tumor progression and reshape the immune microenvironment or serve as a diagnostic and prognostic target. In contrast, FABP4 expression tends to decrease in thyroid cancer, melanoma, and endometrial cancer. In thyroid cancer, downregulated FABP4 is correlated with abnormal insulin signaling and poor prognosis [22, 23]. In melanoma, its downregulation reduces lipid internalization and is associated with the “obesity paradox” phenomenon [24]. Moreover, in endometrial cancer, downregulated FABP4 promotes tumor development by relieving PI3K/Akt pathway inhibition [25]. All three malignancies demonstrate potential as diagnostic or therapeutic targets (Table 1).

Expression, mechanism of action, and prognostic value of FABP4 in various types of tumors

However, the clinical application of FABP4 as a biomarker [40] faces significant challenges: serum levels are easily confounded by patients’ metabolic status, tissue sampling suffers from bias due to tumor heterogeneity, and standardized expression thresholds are lacking. These issues collectively compromise the specificity of its diagnostic and prognostic utility. Moreover, whether FABP4 functions as a disease driver requires definition on the basis of specific cancer types. In cancers with high FABP4 expression, such as colorectal cancer [33, 34] and breast cancer [29, 30], it directly promotes tumor metabolism and progression, leading to driver characteristics. However, in cancers with downregulated FABP4 expression, including thyroid cancer [22, 23], melanoma [24], and endometrial cancer [25], it does not actively drive disease development but indirectly regulates progression through expression loss or altered metabolic phenotypes, acting more as a regulator rather than a typical driver. These findings indicate that clinical translation requires the precise design of strategies based on FABP4 expression patterns in different cancer types.

Association with tumor lipid metabolism

Tumor cells adapt to proliferation demands through three-stage reprogramming: enhanced lipid uptake [41], directed utilization of de novo lipids [42], and microenvironment regulation by metabolites [43]. During this process, key enzymes involved in lipid metabolism often exhibit abnormal activity or acquire new functions [44, 45]. As a core molecular hub, FABP4 participates in fatty acid uptake and transport processes via multiple mechanisms.

At the uptake level, it promotes the expression of itself and transporters such as CD36 via transcriptional regulation, activating distinct pathways in cholangiocarcinoma [46] and prostate cancer [47], respectively. This simultaneously balances uptake efficiency through antagonistic regulation with that of Nur77 [48], a mechanism particularly prominent in breast cancer. With respect to transport function, mechanisms vary across cancer types. In triple-negative breast cancer, CD36, Nogo-B, and FATP4 form an uptake complex [49], driving progression by increasing fatty acid influx. In colorectal cancer, overexpressed FABP4 intensifies the transport of adipose-derived fatty acids to cancer cells [50], supplying substrates for growth. In breast cancer, FABP4 also functions as a lipid messenger to activate the FABP4/Cebpα pathway in TAMs [51], promoting triglyceride metabolism and fatty acid oxidation. This releases pro-metastatic factors that construct a metastasis-promoting microenvironment.

FABP4-mediated lipid metabolism reprogramming drives tumor progression

Abnormal FABP4-mediated lipid metabolism has a profound effect on the TME through various mechanisms, thus promoting tumor progression and metastasis (Fig. 2). In this process, abnormal lipid metabolism not only induces oxidative stress but also facilitates changes in several areas, such as epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) and angiogenesis [52]. In tumor cells, abnormal lipid metabolism generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and various lipid metabolism intermediates. These products activate inflammatory signaling pathways, accelerating the recruitment and polarization of TAMs and forming an immunosuppressive microenvironment [53], which promotes tumor cell immune evasion. Additionally, lipid metabolism products can stimulate the proliferation and migration of tumor endothelial cells, resulting in the formation of an abnormal tumor vascular network that provides the necessary nutrients and pathways for tumor growth and metastasis. These complex mechanisms collectively influence the tumor microenvironment, further promoting tumor development and spread (Fig. 3).

Bidirectional regulation of oxidative stress: the metabolic hub role of FABP4 in maintaining the malignant phenotype of tumors

The role of FABP4 in ROS homeostasis is context dependent, with its adaptive protective function or pro-oxidative damage determined by its expression level in conjunction with cancer type. As a key lipid metabolism molecule, the core mechanism of FABP4 involves binding and transporting free fatty acids (FFAs). It influences ROS homeostasis by regulating the balance between FFA β-oxidation for energy production and lipid droplet storage within mitochondria [53]. This equilibrium represents a central node in ROS regulation. Specifically, under physiological conditions, FABP4 maintains mitochondrial functional stability in adipocytes and macrophages. It accomplishes this by confining the modest amount of ROS generated during β-oxidation within physiological limits via antioxidant systems while simultaneously mitigating oxidative stress by promoting FFA storage in lipid droplets to reduce lipid peroxidation [54]. Under pathological conditions such as obesity and inflammation, its abnormally high expression promotes fatty acid oxidation [55], exacerbating the mitochondrial burden and leading to excessive ROS production [56], thus disrupting this equilibrium. In the tumor microenvironment, it mediates lipid “metabolic symbiosis” between adipocytes and cancer cells [57], aiding cancer cells in acquiring FFAs for energy while leveraging lipid droplets to evade oxidative damage [51]. It also synergizes with stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) to reduce lipid peroxidation sensitivity through fatty acid desaturation, ultimately shielding cancer cells from oxidative stress-induced ferroptosis [58].

Mechanistically, FABP4 primarily regulates ROS homeostasis through two pathways, exhibiting cancer-specific effects while mediating the bidirectional control of lipid metabolism abnormalities and oxidative stress. On the one hand, it precisely regulates ROS production by mediating fatty acid uptake and mitochondrial β-oxidation, thus shaping the redox state of the microenvironment—as demonstrated in triple-negative breast cancer [59]. This regulation is critical for tumor cell survival. Furthermore, AnxA6, a downstream effector of HIF-1/2α, increases lipid metabolism-driven ROS production by upregulating FABP4 expression, thus promoting the development of oxidative stress adaptation mechanisms in tumors resistant to targeted therapies [60]. Conversely, it promotes metabolic adaptation by activating the antioxidant pathway via nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (NRF2). Bound fatty acids (e.g., oleic acid) activate PPARγ [61]. This crosstalk with the NRF2 pathway enhances antioxidant gene expression while simultaneously activating pERK signaling via the desmoglein 2 (DSG2) receptor to promote NRF2 nuclear translocation. This results in a “lipid–receptor–antioxidant” cascade reaction. For instance, in pancreatic cancer, it downregulates ROS levels via NRF2 [62], whereas in breast cancer, eFABP4 utilizes this pathway to reduce intracellular oxidative stress and drive tumor cell proliferation [63]. Furthermore, in bladder cancer, it synergistically upregulates SLC7A11 with PPARG to resist ferroptosis by increasing glutathione synthesis [64]. In sonodynamic therapy-induced glioma models, it indirectly modulates cellular sensitivity to oxidative stress by inhibiting Ca²⁺-lipid complex dissociation [65]. This bidirectional regulation forms a unique vicious cycle in obesity-associated tumors: lipid metabolism disorders induce oxidative stress adaptation via FABP4, while oxidative stress further drives FABP4 expression and lipid metabolism remodeling. Therefore, targeting FABP4 and its downstream signaling axes (e.g., DSG2, NRF2, and SCD1) to disrupt the coupling between lipid transport and antioxidant pathways may offer novel intervention strategies for metabolism-driven diseases.

Lipid metabolism-driven EMT: key mechanism by which FABP4 promotes tumor metastasis

EMT is a crucial process through which tumor cells acquire mesenchymal characteristics, enhancing their invasive and metastatic capabilities. FABP4 plays a key role in the EMT of tumor cells by regulating lipid metabolism, significantly enhancing the invasive and metastatic capabilities of tumor cells. Through a three-dimensional regulatory network involving interactions with transmembrane receptors, remodeling of the immune microenvironment, and activation of intracellular signaling, FABP4 deeply couples lipid metabolic homeostasis with the EMT process, providing a new interdisciplinary perspective of metabolism‒immune signaling for the study of tumor metastasis mechanisms (Fig. 4).

FABP4 drives EMT via the CD36/TGF-β noncanonical pathway

The interaction between FABP4 and the fatty acid transporter receptor CD36 constitutes one of its core mechanisms driving EMT, resulting in distinct signaling pathways across different cancer types. In glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), FABP4 activates an atypical TGF-β signaling pathway by inducing CD36 expression, thus promoting filopodium formation and invasive capacity in glioma cells [37]. Mechanistically, CD36 acts as a downstream target of FABP4, mediating fatty acid uptake and metabolic reprogramming. This upregulates mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin and vimentin while downregulating E-cadherin, thus driving EMT. In breast cancer, the two proteins synergistically activate the STAT3 signaling pathway, forming a “CD36-STAT3” positive feedback loop [13]. This further enhances the transcription of EMT-related genes while simultaneously increasing tumor cell stemness and invasion.

FABP4 regulates EMT via the macrophage pyroptosis/NLRP3‒IL-1β axis

In obesity-related pancreatic cancer (PC), FABP4 remodels the tumor microenvironment by mediating macrophage pyroptosis. Saturated fatty acids are transported into macrophages by FABP4, inducing pyroptosis through a caspase-1/GSDMD-dependent pathway, releasing the inflammatory factor IL-1β, and activating the NLRP3 inflammasome [66]. IL-1β further acts on cancer cells to upregulate the expression of EMT transcription factors such as Snail and Twist [67], promoting the epithelial‒mesenchymal transition. This enhances cell migration and invasion capabilities. This process has also been observed in a model of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) transitioning to lung cancer (LC). Single-cell analysis revealed that SPP1(+) macrophages promote the progression of IPF to LC, with FABP4 serving as a classifier gene involved in risk prediction [68], revealing the cell interactions and EMT mechanisms involved in the transition from IPF to LC. In gastrointestinal tumors, high FABP4 expression is closely associated with increased macrophage immune infiltration. Analysis using the TIMER2.0 database revealed that FABP4-induced macrophage infiltration can lead to an immunosuppressive microenvironment, promoting the expression of EMT-related genes [21]. This “cell‒cell interaction” model suggests that by coordinating immune cells and stromal cells, FABP4 indirectly drives EMT and tumor progression.

FABP4 regulates invasion through the AKT signaling pathway and EMT-related proteins

In colon cancer, FABP4 promotes EMT by activating the AKT pathway. In colon cancer cells overexpressing FABP4, the level of p-AKT is significantly increased, accompanied by the upregulation of the expression of invasion-related proteins such as Snail and MMP-2/9 and the downregulation of the expression of E-cadherin [50]. Mechanistically, FABP4 enhances fatty acid transport and lipid metabolism (such as triglyceride accumulation and increased ATP production), providing an energy basis for AKT signaling while inducing mesenchymal phenotype transition through metabolic reprogramming. Similarly, in colorectal cancer tissues, FABP4 expression is positively correlated with Snail expression and negatively correlated with E-cadherin expression [40], suggesting that FABP4 drives tumor progression by directly regulating key molecules involved in EMT.

Metabolic regulatory network of angiogenesis: tumor blood supply remodeling mediated by FABP4

FABP4, an intracellular fatty acid-binding protein, is primarily responsible for mediating the intracellular transport and signaling of long-chain fatty acids. In vascular endothelial cells, the expression of this protein is induced and regulated by VEGF-A and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) through the VEGFR2 pathway [69]. Studies have shown that curcumin can synergistically increase the angiogenic ability of human early placental trophoblast cells by upregulating FABP4 and VEGFR2 expression, while the FABP4 inhibitor BMS309403 can block this effect [70], confirming the necessity of FABP4 in this process. In recent years, research has revealed that FABP4 plays a key role in the tumor angiogenesis network. In tumor angiogenesis, the NOTCH1 pathway induces endothelial cells to express FABP4, which provides energy and lipids for angiogenesis by promoting long-chain fatty acid metabolism and resistance to ROS damage [71]. For example, in meningioma research, vascular endothelial cells universally express FABP4, and the expression level of this protein in tumor cells is significantly positively correlated with malignancy (WHO grade III) [72], which not only provides a potential molecular marker for pathological grading but also suggests its critical role in tumor‒endothelial interactions. Moreover, in adipose tissue, the expression of FABP4 is positively correlated with the expression of the proangiogenic long noncoding RNA MALAT1. Both regulate fatty acid metabolism and insulin signaling through the PPARγ pathway [73], potentially synergistically promoting angiogenesis in the tumor microenvironment to support tumor invasion and metastasis.

In the TME, FABP4 drives angiogenesis through multiple mechanisms. On the one hand, it participates in the reprogramming of lipid metabolism in tumor cells, where abnormally active lipid metabolism generates intermediates such as acetyl-CoA and reactive oxygen species, activating the HIF-1α and NF-κB pathways and promoting the transcription of proangiogenic factors such as VEGF and PDGF [74]. On the other hand, in inflammatory signaling and intercellular interactions, studies on uterine leiomyomas have shown that adipocytes mediate the transfer of free fatty acids through FABP4 and secrete factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α, which collaboratively activate the NF-κB pathway in leiomyoma cells to promote inflammation and angiogenesis [75]. Abnormalities in sphingolipid metabolism induce macrophage polarization to the proangiogenic M2 type via the MIF‒CD74 axis, where FABP4 + macrophage subclusters may exhibit increased migratory ability through interaction or signaling with endothelial cells. In a non-small cell lung cancer model, targeting MIF can block this cross-regulation [76], confirming the synergistic role of FABP4 and sphingolipid metabolism in the immune‒angiogenesis network. Additionally, research on colorectal cancer revealed that the proportion of FABP4 + MMP9 + MMP2-TIMP1 + extracellular vesicles in the plasma of obese patients is significantly increased and is positively correlated with tumor vascular density and metastatic potential [77]. These vesicles can serve as biomarkers for evaluating the degree of tumor angiogenesis and treatment response, reflecting the synergy between metabolic abnormalities and angiogenesis. On the basis of the above mechanism, FABP4 is a key target for antiangiogenic therapy in tumors, and the lipid metabolism pathway involving FABP4 is related to the efficacy of antiangiogenic therapy [78]. Research has indicated that FABP4 is a crucial potential target for the ability of rosiglitazone (RGZ) to inhibit breast cancer angiogenesis, with its role possibly involving metabolic pathway regulation, epigenetic mechanisms, and interactions within the immune microenvironment. RGZ targets the PPARγ signaling pathway to regulate FABP4 expression, thus inhibiting tumor angiogenesis [79]. This process closely links abnormal lipid metabolism with angiogenesis regulation, and low FABP4 expression may indicate increased sensitivity to metabolic regulation therapy.

Multiple interactions of FABP4 in the TME

The TME consists of tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells (including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and adipocytes), and extracellular matrix. Lipid metabolism reprogramming and its accompanying signaling cascades not only directly regulate cancer cell phenotypes but also significantly affect the functional states of stromal and immune cells within the TME [80], thus influencing the dynamic equilibrium of the entire TME. As a key regulatory factor in lipid metabolism, FABP4, with its unique lipid-binding and signal transduction properties, is deeply involved in the complex interaction network among multiple cells in the TME (Fig. 5).

Multidimensional regulatory roles in immune cells

With respect to immune cell regulation, the role of FABP4 in macrophage polarization and function shows dynamic bidirectional characteristics depending on the cancer type and microenvironmental context. In non-small cell lung cancer, single-cell technology has revealed a FABP4⁺C1q⁺ macrophage subtype. This group enhances fatty acid synthesis through the AMPK/JAK/STAT axis, conferring stronger antiapoptotic ability and phagocytic function to macrophages, while genes related to proinflammatory cytokines are highly expressed, indicating significant cytokine secretion activity. Notably, this macrophage subtype is closely associated with the clinical efficacy of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy [81], suggesting its critical role in promoting antitumor immune responses. However, in a nonresponsive tumor microenvironment, tumor cells interact with alveolar and M0-type macrophages via LAMC2-ITGA6/ITGB1, inducing the specific expression of OAS1 in CHIT1⁺/FABP4⁺ macrophages and driving macrophages toward immunosuppressive M2 phenotype polarization [82], thus constructing a microenvironment that is resistant to immunotherapy. Additionally, in obesity-related tumors (such as breast cancer and pancreatic cancer), FABP4 regulates lipid metabolism reprogramming through the nuclear receptor NR1H3 [83] or participates in the transfer of saturated fatty acids, inducing macrophage pyroptosis in a caspase-1/GSDMD-dependent manner [66]. Moreover, at the tumor–adipose junction, FABP4 induces the formation of lipid-associated macrophages (LAMs) with high FABP3/4/5 expression [84]. These macrophages exhibit M2-like phenotypes, enhanced lipid accumulation, and increased phagocytic functions, collectively promoting tumor progression and mediating resistance to immunotherapy. In the lung adenocarcinoma microenvironment, FABP4⁺ macrophages (FABP4-Mφ) constitute the dominant cell subtype, whereas SELENOP⁺ macrophages (SELENOP-Mφ) with antitumor functions form synergistic clusters with lymphocytes [85], which jointly participate in the immune surveillance process. The dynamic balance of these macrophage subtypes with different functions may be among the key mechanisms regulating the immune microenvironment in lung adenocarcinoma. In breast cancer, TAMs mediate linoleic acid nuclear translocation through FABP4 to activate C/EBPα, increase the activities of triglyceride synthases such as GPAT1 and DGAT1/2, induce lipid accumulation and fat decomposition processes in TAMs [51], construct a prometastatic microenvironment, and ultimately promote tumor invasion.

The functional transition of T cells from the naïve stage to the activation, effector, and memory stages is highly dependent on the dynamic regulation of lipid metabolism [86]. FABP4, along with CD36, FATP, and SREBPs, constitute the lipid handling network of T cells [87]. The inhibitory effect of FABP4 on T-cell function is reflected in the multilayered synergistic mechanism of infiltration level regulation and signaling pathway interference. In colorectal cancer, its expression level is negatively correlated with CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration [88], whereas in colorectal adenocarcinoma, its low expression state is accompanied by increased infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells [89], suggesting that it promotes tumor escape by creating an immune desert microenvironment. In NSCLC, GZMK⁺CD8⁺ effector memory T (Tem) cells are enriched in treatment-responsive tumors, while the proportion of Tem cells is significantly reduced in nonresponsive tumors [82], suggesting that FABP4 may affect immunotherapy efficacy by regulating Tem cell survival or differentiation. In neuroblastoma, FABP4 inhibits the NF-κB/IL-1α pathway through ubiquitination of ATPB, inducing macrophage polarization to an anti-inflammatory phenotype [90], indirectly blocking T-cell activation signals and forming a “macrophage–T-cell” immune suppression cascade. The aforementioned mechanisms collectively reveal the molecular logic by which FABP4 systematically undermines the antitumor immune response by interfering with T-cell infiltration, functional activation, and interactions with myeloid cells.

Interactions in stromal cells

In the tumor microenvironment, metabolic interactions between stromal cells and tumor cells are key factors driving TME remodeling [91]. As a central molecular hub that regulates lipid metabolism, FABP4 is deeply embedded in this interactive regulatory network, achieving orderly regulation through several multilevel mechanisms.

First, at the level of stromal progenitor cell differentiation regulation, FABP4 + progenitor cells can differentiate into fibroblasts and perivascular cells. This process, through specific interactions with PGF + endothelial tip cells, triggers excessive extracellular matrix deposition and vascular dysfunction [92]. This abnormal stroma‒vascular interaction lays the foundation for a preinvasive microenvironment conducive to tumor progression. Second, in terms of immune stroma remodeling, the infiltration of proinflammatory FABP4 + macrophages in osteosarcoma lung metastases directly mediates the extracellular matrix remodeling process [93]. This immune cell-driven stromal remodeling significantly enhances the invasive capacity of tumor cells by altering the mechanical microenvironment of the tumor.

In the context of metabolic microenvironment regulation, FABP4 mediates metabolic coupling between adipocytes and tumor cells [50, 94]. In gastric cancer models, the infiltration of intratumoral adipocytes and high expression of FABP4 form a synergistic regulatory module that influences immune cell recruitment patterns and tumor cell invasion potential [95], which is an independent predictor of poor prognosis. In breast cancer, elevated levels of FABP4 in beige adipocytes can regulate mammary epithelial cell behavior through a paracrine mechanism [96], thus driving tumor initiation and progression. In the context of fibroblast metabolic reprogramming, in triple-negative breast cancer, CD36 + fibroblasts secrete SLIT3, FBLN-1, PENK, and other protein ligands, upregulating FABP4 in CAFs to drive their metabolic reprogramming [97]. Reprogrammed CAFs then remodel the microenvironment by altering collagen fiber arrangement and growth factor gradients, influencing tumor cell proliferation and migration. Concurrently, FABP4 indirectly inhibits tumor cell growth. In pancreatic cancer, FABP4 expression is regulated by autophagy and is negatively correlated with CAF activation [98]. Furthermore, FABP4 + pancreatic stellate cells can differentiate into CAFs, which participate in matrix evolution and contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [99]. In colorectal cancer, FABP4 upregulation is correlated with lipid metabolism reprogramming. It further modulates PD-L1 expression to establish specific immune infiltration patterns [88], facilitating tumor immune evasion and thus indirectly regulating tumor cell growth. Research on FABP4 in CAFs remains in its infancy. Its multidimensional regulatory mechanisms, cancer-specific variations, and clinical translational evidence require further elucidation.

The aforementioned multidimensional interactions reveal that by integrating the differentiation fate, immune phenotype, metabolic state, and matrix remodeling capability of stromal cells, FABP4 forms a core regulatory hub that links stromal cell metabolic reprogramming with the malignant phenotype of tumor cells.

Cancer treatment strategies that target FABP4 and their clinical translation potential

As a core node in the tumor metabolic regulatory network, FABP4 plays a critical role in cancer development, drug resistance, and metastasis. Strategies that target FABP4 for intervention have significant potential for clinical application. Currently, potential therapeutic strategies targeting FABP4 mainly include small -molecule inhibitors, gene silencing technologies, combination therapies, and tumor microenvironment interventions. The specific progress and challenges are as follows.

In terms of small-molecule inhibitors, current research is quite active. Preclinical studies have yielded multiple positive advances. However, the heterogeneous expression of FABP4 poses a critical challenge for drug application. Specifically, it is overexpressed in adipocytes [100], is induced in endothelial cells under pathological conditions [101, 102], and is expressed specifically in CAFs [97, 98]. This heterogeneity hinders the precise identification and targeting of tumor-associated FABP4, increasing the risk of unintended effects on normal cells and adverse reactions such as metabolic disorders and vascular damage. Nevertheless, the small-molecule inhibitor BMS309403 has demonstrated validated preclinical antitumor efficacy. For instance, in clear cell carcinoma, it reverses the enhanced cell motility induced by adipocyte-conditioned medium [27]. In ovarian cancer treatment, it enhances cancer apoptosis and overall antitumor efficacy when combined with the PARP1/2 inhibitor AZD2281 [31], and in colorectal cancer resistance models, it effectively inhibits tumor growth both as a monotherapy and in combination with cetuximab [103]. Concurrently, small-molecule inhibitors face challenges, including pharmacokinetic limitations, off-target effects [104], and the activation of compensatory pathways. Pharmacokinetic limitations stem from their lipophilic nature, which significantly affects oral absorption by dietary factors and leads to preferential tissue distribution in adipose tissue [105]. Off-target effects manifest not only as interference with FABP5 and FABP3 subtypes but also as high risks of nontumor tissue toxicity. Specifically, inhibiting FABP4 in adipocytes triggers free fatty acid accumulation and metabolic disorders [106], whereas off-target effects on FABP3 in cardiomyocytes cause myocardial injury [107], and targeting regenerating endothelial cells increases the risk of vascular damage [108]. With respect to compensatory pathway activation, mechanisms such as the upregulation of FABP5 [109] can compensate for fatty acid transport and metabolic functions, thus counteracting therapeutic effects. However, breakthroughs have emerged in R&D. The structural analog HTS01037 has demonstrated potential [110]. Novel inhibitors developed through computationally aided drug design [111], such as 14e—optimized with 4-amino and 4-ureidopyridinone moieties—achieve an IC₅₀ as low as 1.57 µM. Silicon-based pharmacokinetic simulations indicate favorable in vivo potential [112, 113]. Nevertheless, no FABP4 inhibitors have yet entered clinical trials. The aforementioned issues of heterogeneous expression, pharmacokinetic deficiencies, off-target effects, and activation of compensatory pathways necessitate further investigation into target selectivity and toxicological safety.

Gene silencing technology downregulates FABP4 expression through siRNA, shRNA, or CRISPR/Cas9, which can delay tumor progression in animal models. For example, siFABP4 can inhibit the proliferation and migration of gastric cancer cells [114], and CRISPR-mediated FABP4 knockout can reduce the metastatic tumor burden in an ovarian cancer mouse model [94]. However, current delivery systems, such as nanocarriers, still require improvement to further increase the precision and efficiency of targeted delivery, thus increasing their translational feasibility in clinical applications.

Combined therapeutic strategies focus on the synergistic effects of FABP4 inhibition with other therapies. Preclinical experiments have confirmed that combining FABP4 inhibitors with chemotherapy drugs (such as carboplatin) or targeted drugs (such as AZD2281) can significantly increase antitumor effects. For example, the combination of olaparib and BMS309403 in BRCA-mutated ovarian cancer can further increase apoptosis, reduce colony formation, and enhance antitumor effects [31]. The combination of cetuximab and FABP4 inhibitors can reverse colorectal cancer resistance [103], whereas in tumor recurrence models, the combined use of FABP4 inhibitors and SCD1 inhibitors can completely prevent tumor regeneration [58], suggesting that a multidrug combination strategy targeting lipid metabolism pathways may become a new direction for preventing tumor recurrence. These studies provide experimental evidence and a theoretical direction for the synergistic application of FABP4-targeted therapy with other therapies. On the one hand, FABP4-targeted therapy may be combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1/CTLA-4 antibodies) to enhance the immune response by modulating the metabolic microenvironment in obesity-related tumors. On the other hand, when combined with lipid metabolism-targeted drugs (such as the fatty acid synthase inhibitor TVB-2640), FABP4-targeted therapy can further strengthen antitumor effects through multitarget interference with the lipid metabolism network.

Focusing on the tumor microenvironment, efforts are directed toward targeting and regulating key cell types that express FABP4 within this environment, including adipocytes and cancer-associated fibroblasts. For example, in clear cell carcinoma, FABP4 released by adipocytes promotes cancer cell migration by increasing ERK phosphorylation [27]. In colorectal and ovarian cancers, FABP4 mediates lipid metabolism interactions between tumors and adipocytes, supporting cancer cell survival in harsh microenvironments [50, 94]. By blocking the metabolic support networks that tumor cells rely on, new intervention targets that inhibit tumor progression can be identified. Additionally, research on non-small cell lung cancer has shown that by polarizing to an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, FABP4 + macrophages resist the efficacy of anti-PD-1 combined with chemotherapy [83]. These findings suggest that FABP4 may be involved in immune evasion by regulating myeloid cell function. Targeting FABP4 could reverse such microenvironment remodeling and increase the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Blocking these intercellular metabolic and immune regulatory networks provides broader targets for intervening in the tumor microenvironment.

Summary and outlook

FABP4 plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of cancer. As a central hub in lipid metabolism and oncogenic signaling networks, it drives cancer progression by regulating tumor metabolic reprogramming, metastasis processes, and the immune microenvironment. Its overexpression is closely associated with poor prognosis in ovarian, breast, and colorectal cancers, making it both a valuable prognostic marker and a potential therapeutic target. Notably, FABP4 has dual oncogenic and tumor-suppressive functions in cancer. In highly expressed cancers such as ovarian and breast cancer, it promotes tumor growth by regulating lipid metabolism to fuel tumors and drive metastasis. Conversely, in cancers with low or downregulated expression, such as thyroid cancer, melanoma, and endometrial cancer, its absence relieves the suppression of tumorigenesis or alters tumor metabolic dependencies, indirectly reflecting tumor-suppressor functions.

As a pivotal hub linking lipid metabolism and the tumor microenvironment, FABP4 drives tumor progression by regulating oxidative stress, the epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and angiogenesis. Its molecular mechanisms involve fatty acid metabolic reprogramming and crosstalk between metabolic and signaling pathways. Currently, despite the promise of FABP4 inhibitors in oncology—particularly for obesity-related tumors, drug-resistant cancers, and recurrence prevention—clinical translation faces significant challenges. Scientific hurdles, including precision drug delivery, optimization of target specificity, and control of treatment-related toxicity, remain critical unresolved issues in this field.

Future research should focus on the specific functions of FABP4 in distinct cell subpopulations across different tumor microenvironments. This requires the integration of interdisciplinary technologies such as spatial omics, structural biology, smart drug delivery, and multiomics approaches (combining metabolomics, proteomics, and genomics) to elucidate in depth the tissue-specific mechanisms of molecular networks and microenvironment interactions. Spatial transcriptomics can precisely map FABP4 expression patterns across distinct cell subpopulations (e.g., cancer cells, CAFs, and immune cells) within the tumor microenvironment and clarify functional differences at specific spatial locations. Multiomics technologies further integrate molecular expression data with metabolic phenotypes to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Building upon this foundation, precisely identifying the core regulatory pathways of FABP4 across different cancer types enables the development of highly targeted and tissue-specific intervention strategies, accelerating its translation from basic research to clinical application. Concurrently, exploring combination regimens with immunotherapy and metabolic targeted drugs offers innovative therapeutic approaches for tumors driven by metabolic abnormalities. This research direction will not only deepen our understanding of tumor metabolic reprogramming but also provide new insights for developing personalized treatment strategies in the era of precision medicine. It will help overcome the specificity limitations of existing targeted therapies, offering patients more effective treatment options.
