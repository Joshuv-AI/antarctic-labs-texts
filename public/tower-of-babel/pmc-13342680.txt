NELFA supports naïve pluripotency and drives 8C-like state in human embryonic stem cells
Authors: Xiaomin Su, Fang Yang, Jitesh Neupane, Zhimin Lu, Han Wu, Mei Gu, Yong Zhang, Siqin Bao, Xihe Li, M. Azim Surani, Baojiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A minority of 8C-like cells (8CLCs) expressing zygotic genome activation genes are captured in naïve embryonic stem cells (ESCs). However, how human ESCs transition into 8CLCs remains unknown. Here, we show that NELFA is dispensable in human primed ESCs but critical for naïve pluripotency, and its overexpression promotes the primed-to-naïve conversion. Notably, NELFA robustly induces 8CLCs that express human ZGA-specific genes in naïve ESCs. NELFA-induced human 8CLCs can contribute to both embryonic and extraembryonic developmental potential in the interspecies human-mouse chimera assay. Importantly, we also discovered that TP53 can activate the human 8CLCs state, and NELFA might be an upstream regulator of TP53. Furthermore, TP53 and NELFA are both essential for the complete transition to the 8CLC state. We also demonstrate that sustained NELFA overexpression in primed ESCs directs neural lineage specification. Thus, NELFA establishes and maintains naïve pluripotency and drives the 8CLC state, providing insights into early human embryogenesis.

Introduction

Mammalian development initiates with a totipotent zygote formed by fertilization. Zygotic or embryonic genome activation (ZGA or EGA) takes place at the mouse 2-cell and human 8-cell stages, a step that governs the first lineage segregation events in early embryogenesis1,2. After ZGA, embryonic cells begin to have distinct cell identities, and they differentiate into inner cell mass (ICM) and trophectoderm (TE) as they reach the early blastocyst stage3,4. Embryonic stem cells (ESCs), trophoblast stem cells (TSCs) and extraembryonic endoderm cells (PrESCs, XEN cells) can all be derived from the ICM, the TE and the primitive endoderm (PrE) from blastocysts, respectively5–8. Furthermore, different types of stem cells have been obtained from preimplantation embryos at different developmental stages, such as expanded potential stem cells, totipotent-like stem cells and totipotent potential stem cells9–13. Establishing these types of stem cell lines provides a platform for studying the mechanisms underlying conversion between different stem cell states.

To study mammalian early development in vitro, mouse and human pluripotent or embryonic stem cells (PSCs, ESCs) have been utilized to investigate early development14–16. Pluripotent stem cell cultures are inherently heterogeneous. In mouse ESCs, a minor subset spontaneously acquires a 2-cell embryo-like molecular and functional signature, designated 2-cell-like cells (2CLCs)17,18. Unlike typical pluripotent stem cells, 2CLCs are characterized by the specific upregulation of genes normally restricted to two-cell embryos, alongside a concomitant downregulation of core pluripotency transcripts such as Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog19. Functionally, this distinct state confers upon 2CLCs an expanded developmental potential, enabling them to contribute not only to the embryo proper but also to extraembryonic (ExEm) tissues, as demonstrated by chimeric assays20–22. Recently, studies reported that a minority of cells in human embryonic stem cells expressing certain features of 8-cell embryos, called 8-cell-like cells (8CLCs), are detected in the presence of specific small molecule induction23–25. 8CLCs show expression of ZGA-specific genes as in 8-cell blastomeres, including ZSCAN4, DUXA, TPRX1, and LEUTX. Also, DPPA3-induced DNA demethylation supports 8CLCs conversion25. Functionally, 8CLCs can contribute to embryonic and extraembryonic lineages in vitro and in vivo26,27. In addition, splicing inhibition can transiently reprogram human pluripotent stem cells into ZGA-like cells (ZLCs), which transition into stable totipotent blastomere-like cells (TBLCs) after long-term passaging26. Distinct from 8CLCs, both ZLCs and TBLCs broadly silence pluripotent genes. ZLCs activate a particular group of ZGA-specific genes, while TBLCs are enriched with pre-ZGA-specific genes26. Notably, during in vitro differentiation, TBLCs recapitulate human pre-implantation development and contribute to both embryonic and extraembryonic developmental potency26. However, the regulatory mechanism of human zygotic genome activation or 8CLCs in human ESCs remains unclear.

Our prior investigations have established a functional role for the maternal negative elongation factor A (NELFA) in driving the conversion of mouse ESCs into the 2C-like state21,28. Furthermore, enforced expression of Nelfa has been shown to markedly increase the efficiency of mouse somatic cell reprogramming21. However, whether NELFA can support naïve pluripotency and/or drive 8C-like state in human ESCs is unknown. In this study, we demonstrate that NELFA overexpression promotes the conversion of human primed ESCs to a naïve state and induces an 8C-like state in human naïve ESCs. Thus, we have uncovered the role of NELFA in pluripotency and the 8C-like state, enabling us to study the human ZGA-like state in vitro.

Results

The influence of NELFA overexpression in human primed ESCs

NELFA was detected in human primed ESCs using an immunostaining (Supplementary Fig. 1a). To investigate if NELFA has a role in human primed ESCs, we generated primed ESCs carrying a Dox-inducible NELFA cDNA overexpression construct (NELFA_OE ESCs) with enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) serving as the reporter (Fig. 1a, b). Following 72 h (72 h) Dox induction, we found that NELFA expression was significantly upregulated in NELFA_OE ESCs compared with that of wild-type (WT) ESCs cultured under primed conditions (Fig. 1c, d). The transient overexpression of NELFA in primed human ESCs exerted no or only subtle effects on the expression of pluripotency genes (OCT4 and NANOG) at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Human ESCs can differentiate towards mesendoderm (ME) fate by canonical WNT and ACTIVIN/NODAL signaling27,29, and also have the competence for definitive endoderm (DE) fates30. Next, we asked whether overexpression of NELFA affects the differentiation ability of human primed ESCs in vitro. To address this, we employed established in vitro differentiation models of human primed ESCs31, as previously reported (Fig. 1e). We found that the pre-mesendoderm (PreME) and ME cells exhibited significant upregulation of early primitive-streak (PS) markers T, EOMES and MIXL1 in primed WT ESC-derived PreME (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 1c), while in primed NELFA_OE ESC-derived PreME and ME cells, early PS markers T, EOMES and MIXL1 were significantly reduced, compared to WT ESC-derived PreME (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 1c). However, the expression of SOX2 was enhanced in primed NELFA_OE ESC-derived PreME compared with primed WT ESC-derived PreME (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Meanwhile, in the 24 h ME induction, there was upregulation of late PS markers TBX6, MESP1 and MESP2 (Supplementary Fig. 1e), which trigger the epithelial-mesenchymal transition, that occurs during gastrulation, which was significantly downregulated in NELFA_OE ESC-derived ME cells (Supplementary Fig. 1e). By prolonging the culture of primed WT ESCs, ME cells gain competence for definitive endoderm fate (Supplementary Fig. 1f). In contrast, primed NELFA_OE ESCs indicates a reduced ability to form a definitive endoderm (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 1f). To assess the intrinsic differentiation potential, we performed undirected differentiation in N2B27 basal medium. RT-qPCR analysis on day 3 of differentiation revealed a general trend consistent with our directed differentiation assays. While most lineage markers followed the expected pattern, with decreased mesodermal markers and some variations in endoderm-associated genes in primed NELFA_OE ESCs compared to primed WT ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 1g). These differences likely arise from variations between the undirected and directed differentiation protocols.

Transcriptome analysis of cells with overexpression of NELFA for 3 days (D3) Dox induction in primed condition showed a decrease in the expression levels of most pluripotency factors LEFTY2, MYC, KLF4, DPPA2 and TFCP2L1, but an increase in neural-specific transcripts PAX3, PAX8, MAP2, SOX9 and SOX1, compared to primed WT ESCs (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Data 1). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that genes upregulated in NELFA_OE ESCs following 3 days of Dox induction were significantly enriched for neurodevelopmental-related gene sets32 (Supplementary Fig. 1h). In contrast, genes upregulated in WT ESCs were predominantly associated with pluripotency-related gene sets25 (Supplementary Fig. 1h). Gene ontology (GO) analysis indicated that the NELFA_OE upregulated genes were highly associated with developmental processes, including tissue morphogenesis, heart and head development (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Data 2). In contrast, the genes upregulated in WT ESCs were mainly related to regulation of secretion, trans-synaptic signaling and metal ion transport (Fig. 1k and Supplementary Data 3). Overall, the combined evidence suggests that overexpression of NELFA represses the differentiation ability of human primed ESC-derived mesendoderm and partially definitive endoderm cells.

NELFA promotes the primed-to-naïve conversion in human ESCs

By analyzing previously published transcriptome data33, we confirmed that NELFA expression is increased by approximately 1.5-fold in human naïve ESCs relative to primed cells (Fig. 2a, b). Many genes and transcription factors have distinct regulatory roles in primed and naïve pluripotency34–36. Therefore, we investigated whether NELFA governs different roles in the primed and naïve pluripotency of human ESCs. To this end, we performed primed-to-naïve reversion using human primed ESCs overexpressing NELFA into naïve 4CL medium25 (Fig. 2c) and examined endogenous NELFA protein localization in human naïve ESCs by immunostaining (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We found that, compared to 4CL_WT ESCs, 4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs not only significantly accelerated the appearance of naïve-like (‘dome-shaped’) colonies on day 7 (Fig. 2d), but also maintained this phenotype on day 18 (Fig. 2d, e). In addition, we quantified the expression levels of pluripotency markers (such as SOX2, OCT4, TFCP2L1) on days 6 and 9 during the transition from primed-to-naïve in 4CL media. These results confirm that 4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs significantly increase the expression levels of pluripotency markers (such as SOX2, OCT4, TFCP2L1) on day 9 compared to 4CL_WT ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Among the analyzed pluripotency-related genes (core pluripotency genes OCT4, NANOG, SOX2, and the core naïve markers DPPA3, DPPA5, TFCP2L1, KLF17), the key naïve genes DPPA3, DPPA5, and TFCP2L1 were significantly upregulated in 4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs compared with 4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 2f), while immunostaining indicated comparable OCT4, NANOG, KLF4 protein levels detected in both 4CL_WT and 4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 2c). Next, we performed RNA-seq on 4CL_WT ESCs and 4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs cultured in 4CL medium. We found that 2,922 genes were upregulated and 806 genes were downregulated in 4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs compared with 4CL_WT ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 2e and Supplementary Data 4). We have integrated our RNA-seq data with publicly available datasets from key naïve pluripotency 4CL conditions25. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering (UHC) and principal components analysis (PCA) indicate that NELFA-overexpressed samples cluster with published naïve-state ESCs (Fig. 2h, i). Comparative analysis showed that 127 naïve pluripotency-related genes25 were induced in the 4CL condition (4CL_WT ESCs and 4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs), while 595 primed pluripotency-related genes25 were downregulated in the 4CL condition compared with primed E8_WT ESCs (Fig. 2j). Thus, NELFA facilitates the primed-to-naïve transition in human ESCs, yet NELFA overexpression alone has a limited capacity to induce naïve pluripotency genes in the absence of 4CL signaling.

NELFA is necessary for primed-to-naïve state conversion

To investigate the functional role of NELFA on primed-to-naïve state conversion of human ESCs, we engineered a piggyBAC-based inducible CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) plasmid system (Fig. 3a). We generated stable human primed ESCs bearing sgRNA and Dox-inducible CRISPRi transgenes carrying EGFP reporter (Fig. 3b) and confirmed the NELFA mRNA expression level by RT-qPCR and protein level by western blot analysis (Fig. 3c, d). We found that repressing the NELFA promoter does not induce human ESCs from a primed to a naïve state in 4CL medium (Fig. 3e). Instead, a severe loss of cell viability was observed by day 6 of Dox induction (Fig. 3e). However, WT ESCs successfully underwent conversion from the primed-to-naïve state (Fig. 3e). Importantly, upon Dox induction, although core naïve pluripotency-related genes such as NANOG, DPPA3 and DPPA5 were slightly induced under 4CL conditions following CRISPRi-mediated repression of the NELFA promoter, their expression levels were significantly lower than those in WT ESCs cultured in 4CL medium (Fig. 3f). We also used a Dox-inducible CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) system to activate endogenous NELFA and confirm the results of exogenous NELFA overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 3a-c). Consistent with exogenous NELFA overexpression, endogenous NELFA activation supports human ESCs primed-to-naïve conversion (Supplementary Fig. 3d). To further investigate the role of NELFA in self-renewal, pluripotency maintenance, and the naïve-to-primed transition of human ESCs, we inhibited NELFA in human primed ESCs and found that it had no effect on self-renewal and pluripotency (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f). Notably, once primed cells are converted to the naïve state by NELFA overexpression, naïve pluripotency remains stable even after Dox withdrawal to terminate NELFA overexpression under 4CL conditions (Fig. 3g, h). However, we found that NELFA inhibition by CRISPRi compromised the self-renewal capacity of human naïve ESCs under 4CL conditions (Fig. 3i, j and Supplementary Fig. 3g, h). These findings indicate that basal NELFA expression sustains self-renewal in human naïve ESCs (Fig. 3g–j). Furthermore, NELFA is also required for the naïve-to-primed transition, as its inhibition compromises this process (Supplementary Fig. 3i). Collectively, NELFA is dispensable in primed ESCs but essential for primed-to-naïve transition, where its basal expression sustains self-renewal in naïve pluripotency. Thus, NELFA influences the establishment and maintenance of the naïve state of human embryonic stem cells.

The effect of NELFA on DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility in human naïve ESCs

These genes, and UHRF1 were also significantly downregulated in 4CL_NELFA_CRISPRa ESCs (Fig. 4b). Next, we performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and compared the DNA methylation profile of human 4CL_WT and 4CL_CRISPRa_NELFA ESCs cultured in 4CL conditions. The levels of CpG methylation in 4CL_CRISPRa_NELFA ESCs are comparable to those of 4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Data 5). These global CpG methylation levels are consistent with previously published data25,40. We also investigated the global level of non-CpG methylation (CHG and CHH sites), which is known to be present in mouse and human ESCs41–43. Interestingly, in NELFA activation, the methylation levels of CHG and CHH were significantly reduced compared to 4CL_WT ESCs levels (Fig. 4f). Thus, no significant global change in 5mC is expected between WT and NELFA_OE ESCs cultured in 4CL. Instead, both lines share an underlying hypomethylated state at pluripotency-related genes (NANOG, ZFP42, KLF5), in contrast to the hypermethylated state observed in human primed ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Globally, pluripotent cells exhibit increased chromatin accessibility44,45. We profiled the chromatin accessibility by assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (ATAC-seq) and found that the chromatin profile heatmaps of ATAC in NELFA activated and repressed ESCs displayed a similar pattern at ATAC-seq summit ± 3 kb compared to 4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 4g). The genomic distribution of the 4CL_CRISPRa_NELFA peaks in ESCs were similar to that in 4CL_WT ESCs, with peaks evenly distributed between promoters, intronic and intergenic regions (Fig. 4h). 4CL_WT ESCs peaks are enriched for transcription from RNA polymerase II promoter and RNA polymerase II core promoter proximal region sequence-specific DNA binding terms (Fig. 4i), while NELFA activation-specific peaks are enriched in developmental process, multicellular organismal development, system development and cell differentiation terms (Fig. 4i).

To investigate molecular changes in NELFA-activated embryonic stem cells, we analyzed ATAC-seq data and found that NELFA activation significantly increases chromatin accessibility in the promoter and gene body regions of the key naïve pluripotent genes DPPA3 and ZFP57 (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Motif enrichment analyses of the NELFA activated peaks indicated potential naïve pluripotent-specific regulators including OCT4, SOX2, NANOG, TFAP2C and OCT4 − SOX2 − TCF − NANOG were enriched in 4CL_CRISPRa_NELFA ESCs cultured in 4CL condition compared with 4CL_WT ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 4c). We also detected some stem cell-related motifs enriched in CRISPRa of NELFA including JUNB, FOSL2 and ATF3 (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Next, to explore whether NELFA directly occupies naïve pluripotency loci, we attempted to test this hypothesis via NELFA ChIP-seq. However, due to the lack of a high-quality, ChIP-grade antibody against NELFA, we were unable to assess its direct genomic binding. As an alternative approach, we employed H3K27ac (H3K27 acetylation) CUT&Tag-seq to determine whether NELFA overexpression potentiates the enrichment of this active histone mark at naïve pluripotency gene loci. We conducted CUT&Tag-seq for H3K27ac in 4CL_WT and 4CL_NELFA OE human naïve ESCs. Upon NELFA overexpression, the resultant gain of H3K27ac peaks remained largely confined to a set of core pluripotency genes (Fig. 4j, k). Despite showing slightly improved chromatin accessibility and histone modifications, NELFA influences naïve pluripotency alongside targeted epigenetic modifications at key loci, rather than through widespread genome-wide epigenetic remodeling.

We induced human 8CLCs in the e4CL medium (with increased doses of trichostatin A and 3-deazaneplanocin A in 4CL) as reported previously25 (Fig. 5b) and found that the ‘dome-shaped’ colonies in e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs were more numerous than in e4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 5c). The expression levels of pluripotency related genes including OCT4 and SOX2 were slightly downregulated while DPPA3 was upregulated in e4CL_NELFA_OE compared with e4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 5d). Importantly, the 8CLCs-related transcripts DUX4, TRIM43 and H3.X/Y were significantly upregulated in e4CL_NELFA_OE compared with e4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 5e). Immunostaining results indicated that TPRX1 and ZSCAN4 positive cells in e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs were increased compared with e4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). We also quantified the 8CLCs related protein levels by western blot analysis and found that the levels of ZSCAN4, DUX and TPRX1 were significantly upregulated in e4CL_NELFA_OE compared with e4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 5c). The above results clearly support our hypothesis that NELFA can induce human 8CLCs.

To explore whether overexpression of NELFA also activates human totipotency related transcripts, we performed RNA-seq on e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs and e4CL_WT ESCs cultured in e4CL medium on day 7. We found that 3,159 genes were upregulated, and 1,044 genes were downregulated in e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs compared to e4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Data 6). Transcriptomic profiling revealed that NELFA overexpression robustly elevated a suite of core totipotency markers encompassing ZSCAN4, DUXA, TPRX1, KLF17, DPPA3, and DPPA5, while suppressing a distinct set of genes including TFAP2A, STAT5A, SALL3, SETD4 and SUSD2 (Fig. 5h). We also integrated transcriptome data from e4CL cultured cells with 4CL and E8 cultured cells, and found that the 8CLCs specific genes including KLF17, DPPA3, TRIM43, TRIM60, TPRX1, ZSCAN4, DUXA, ZSCAN5B, MBD3L2 and ARGFX were only significantly upregulated in e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs cultured in the e4CL condition (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Data 7). Importantly, in e4CL conditions, 30.4% (7/23) of the previously identified twenty-three ZGA marker genes24 were upregulated in e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs compared to e4CL_WT ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 5d and Supplementary Data 8). Notably, 15.9% (1092/6884) of the previously identified human day 3 embryo (h-embryo_E3)-specific genes (6,884 genes)24 were upregulated in e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs compared with e4CL_WT ESCs, while only 4.1% (283/6,884) of these were upregulated in e4CL_WT ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Further analysis of e4CL_WT ESCs and e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs also showed that there are more previously identified 8CLCs marker genes24,25 overlapping with e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs upregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g).

We also performed a GSEA using ZGA marker genes24 and 8CLCs specific genes24,25. Notably, we found that the genes upregulated in e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs (vs. e4CL_WT) are significantly enriched for ZGA and 8CLC gene sets (Supplementary Fig. 5h). We believe these enrichments offer evidence that NELFA overexpression promotes a transcriptional state closely related to the early embryonic and 8CLC-like programs.

Moreover, active histone mark H3K27 acetylation (H3K27ac) levels were significantly increased in NELFA overexpression (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 5c). To investigate how NELFA influences epigenetic regulation under enhanced culture conditions (e4CL), we performed H3K27ac CUT&Tag-seq and ATAC-seq on e4CL_WT and e4CL_NELFA OE ESCs cultured in e4CL medium (Fig. 5j, k). Our analysis revealed that NELFA overexpression causes a specific enrichment of H3K27ac peaks at several 8CLC-associated genomic regions, including genes such as DUXA and SLC34A2 compared to e4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 5l). Furthermore, motif enrichment analysis identified the totipotency-related factor DUXBL as significantly enriched in e4CL_NELFA_OE ESCs compared to e4CL_WT ESCs (Fig. 5m), suggesting a potential role for DUXBL in mediating NELFA-driven 8CLCs induction. Similarly, the ATAC-seq results reflected these findings, showing consistent changes in chromatin accessibility (Fig. 5m, n). Based on these findings, we suggest that NELFA activates the 8 CLCs gene program through facilitating the deposition of active histone marks coupled with increases in chromatin accessibility.

Next, we also used stable human naïve ESCs bearing sgRNA and Dox-inducible CRISPRa transgenes to activate endogenous NELFA and confirmed the results of exogenous NELFA overexpression. We found that pluripotency-related genes OCT4, DPPA3, and DPPA5 were downregulated in NELFA CRISPR activation after 8CLCs induction compared to e4CL_WT ESCs in e4CL medium (Supplementary Fig. 5i). Conversely, 8CLCs specific genes, including DUX4, TPRX1, H3.X/Y, LEUTX, RFPL4A, and TRIM43, were significantly upregulated in NELFA CRISPR activation compared to e4CL_WT ESCs (Supplementary Fig. 5j).

Importantly, to investigate whether NELFA-induced human ESCs have in vivo developmental potential, we conducted an interspecies human-mouse chimera assay. We injected fifteen EGFP-labeled ESCs into an 8C-stage mouse embryo and examined its chimeric contribution ability after 48–60 h of in vitro culture by co-staining with TE and ICM markers. Our results showed that NELFA-induced cells expressed CDX2-positive TE (5/33 recovered embryos, 15.2%) and OCT4-positive ICM (20/31 recovered embryos, 64.5%) markers in chimeric blastocysts (Fig. 5o, p), which was significantly higher than in WT ESCs in e4CL (Fig. 5p and Supplementary Fig. 5k). Altogether, these results confirm that NELFA reliably induces an 8C-like state in human naïve ESCs.

TP53 induces 8C-like state in human naïve ESCs

To further assess whether endogenous TP53 could induce human 8CLCs, we also developed a piggyBAC-based system for inducible CRISPR activation and interference (CRISPRa/i) plasmid system (Fig. 6d). We generated stable human ESCs expressing sgRNA and Dox-inducible CRISPRa/i transgenes. We confirmed the expression of both TP53 mRNA and protein (Fig. 6e, f). Importantly, we found that activating the TP53 promoter significantly induces human 8CLC-related transcripts, including ZSCAN4, DUX4, TPRX1, NELFA, RFPL4A, H3.X/Y, LEUTX and TRIM43 after 72 hours of Dox induction in e4CL medium (Fig. 6g). Conversely, interference of TP53 promoter leads to a significant downregulation of 8CLC-related transcripts such as DUX4, LEUTX, RFPL4A, TPRX1, TRIM43, and ZSCAN4 in e4CL medium (Supplementary Fig. 6f). In conclusion, we found that the endogenous activation of TP53 is also sufficient to induce a human 8C-like state.

To investigate the functional interplay between NELFA and TP53 in regulating 8CLC activation in human naïve ESCs, we generated CRISPRi targeting TP53 in NELFA_OE ESCs under 4CL conditions. CRISPRi of TP53 in NELFA_OE ESCs had no significant effect on naïve pluripotency self-renewal (Fig.6h-j). Even under e4CL induction conditions, TP53 inhibition in NELFA_OE ESCs still led to the upregulation of a subset of 8C-like genes (RFPL4A and TRIM43) (Fig. 6k). Therefore, TP53 may not be the only downstream regulator mediating NELFA overexpression. Furthermore, to explore the role of NELFA in TP53_OE ESCs, we generated a CRISPRi-mediated knockdown of NELFA in TP53-overexpressing ESCs under 4CL culture conditions. In this experiment, we encountered a significant challenge, as both NELFA inhibition and TP53 overexpression are individually known to disrupt the maintenance of naïve pluripotency in human ESCs (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 6a-d). This constraint limited our analysis to a brief 24-hour window of Dox induction, during which we assessed a subset of 8CLC markers under e4CL conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Notably, even under these limited conditions, the combined regulation of TP53 overexpression and NELFA knockdown still resulted in a significant upregulation of several 8CLC markers (LEUTX, RFPL4A, TPRX1 and TRIM43) (Supplementary Fig. 6h). When considered alongside findings from CRISPRi of TP53 in NELFA_OE ESCs, these results collectively suggest that both TP53 and NELFA are necessary for the transition of human naïve ESCs to the 8CLCs state.

Discussion

The establishment of pluripotency is complex, with pluripotency gene expression being essential for maintaining this state50–52. Many genes and transcription factors play divergent roles in primed and naïve pluripotency34–36,53. In this study, we used two closely related and developmentally connected ESC types, naïve and primed ESCs, to elucidate the regulatory role of NELFA in pluripotency and 8CLCs activation in human ESCs. We designed approaches involving exogenous overexpression, endogenous activation, and endogenous silencing to regulate the NELFA gene, thereby elucidating the critical role of NELFA in pluripotency and in the induction of 8CLCs in human ESCs. We show that NELFA plays divergent roles in the pluripotency of human naïve and primed ESCs.

Long-term NELFA overexpression causes human primed ESCs to undergo neural differentiation. This is consistent with a previous study on RNAi-mediated depletion of NELFA in human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells, which promotes survival against hydrogen peroxide-induced cytotoxicity54, suggesting that NELFA plays an important role in the development of human neural cells. However, the underlying mechanism by which NELFA regulates the human ESC-derived neural differentiation process and human in vivo development remains unclear. Therefore, NELFA could serve as a promising focus for research on ESC-derived neural differentiation.

We provide evidence that NELFA is dispensable in primed ESCs but is critical for naïve pluripotency, where its basal expression maintains self-renewal, and its overexpression facilitates the primed-to-naïve conversion. Thus, NELFA influences the establishment and maintenance of the naïve state. Compared to primed pluripotency, naïve pluripotency exhibits a more active epigenetic signature and more open chromatin structures at the level of nucleosomes55–58. NELFA-induced naïve pluripotency involves the upregulation of key naïve genes in this network, supported by limited enrichment of the active histone mark H3K27ac (Fig. 4j, k), and increased ATAC-seq peaks at naïve gene regions (Supplementary Fig. 4b).

Human naïve ESCs contained a small proportion of cells that displayed variable and generally low levels of totipotency genes59–61. However, since the initial discovery of a small number of 8-cell-like cells in human embryonic stem cells in 2022, only limited research has been reported on progress in 8CLCs to date23–26,62. Previous studies have primarily induced 8CLCs using various in vitro culture strategies, but the precise regulatory mechanisms underlying their induction remain unclear. In this study, we induced 8CLCs from human naïve ESCs overexpressing NELFA through the previously reported strategy25. The 8CLCs induced by NELFA in human naïve ESCs exhibit similar molecular features to human 8-cell embryos and contribute to both embryonic and extraembryonic tissues in chimerism with mouse embryos, which is functionally equivalent to mouse 2C-like cells (2CLCs). The role of NELFA in totipotency is conserved in both mice and humans. We demonstrate that NELFA inhibition has no effect on human primed ESCs but abolishes self-renewal in human naïve ESCs (Fig. 3). Our previous study found that NELFA knockout is tolerated in mouse naïve ESCs21. However, its role in mouse primed ESCs remains unknown. Collectively, our results indicate that NELFA’s role in totipotency is conserved, whereas its pluripotency-related roles are divergent between mouse and human. In addition, some media components, such as DZNep and TSA, as well as spliceosome inhibitors, can induce 2CLCs in mouse and 8CLCs in human naïve cells11,24–26. Most other conditions that confer totipotency-like features in mouse ESCs have not yet been evaluated in human cells11,12,20,63.

Notably, we observed that NELFA’s effects on the epigenome are targeted rather than global. The observed reduction in non-CpG methylation (Fig. 4f) and promoter hypomethylation of specific naïve genes (NANOG, ZFP42, KLF5) represents such targeted effects consistent with a naïve state (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Previous studies have shown that non-CpG methylation is almost wholly absent in various somatic cell types and reappears upon their reprogramming as induced pluripotent stem cells64. Consequently, knockdown of the de novo methyltransferases DNMT3A and DNMT3B in human embryonic stem cells leads to a global decrease in non-CpG methylation42. In line with these findings, we demonstrate that NELFA activation significantly reduces DNA methyltransferase activity (Fig. 4a-c) and non-CpG methylation (Fig. 4f). However, it is important to note that the observed reduction in non-CpG methylation upon NELFA activation may be a consequence of broader transcriptional changes rather than a direct epigenetic remodeling event. Therefore, whether non-CpG hypomethylation actively facilitates naïve pluripotency or simply reflects the altered cellular state requires further investigation. Thus, NELFA influences naïve pluripotency associated with targeted epigenetic changes at key loci, rather than through widespread genome-wide epigenetic remodeling.

The p53 signaling pathway is activated in response to DNA damage and replication stress that are closely associated with the transition from mouse ESCs to 2 CLCs49,64,65. The double homeobox (Dux)-family factor required for activation of mouse two-cell (2C)-like cells is also activated by the tumor suppressor p5349. In addition, previous studies demonstrate that cells derived from patients with facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) activate human DUX4 via p53 signaling49. We have discovered that TP53 is required for the induction of 8 CLCs, and TP53 inhibition reduces their activation. Thus, the p53-DUX/DUX4 regulatory axis may be conserved in the activation of human 8CLCs. Altogether, our findings suggest that NELFA may be an upstream regulator of p53, aiding the activation of the human 8CLCs state. Moreover, we found that inhibiting either TP53 or NELFA in human ESCs overexpressing other factors activates a subset of 8CLC marker genes. Collectively, these data show that TP53 and NELFA are both required for the complete transition to the 8CLC state. This reveals that the activation of the 8CLC state in human naïve ESCs is not a straightforward process but is governed by a sophisticated genetic and epigenetic regulatory network.

Our study not only revealed that NELFA supports naïve pluripotency but also demonstrated its contribution to the establishment of the 8C-like state in human embryonic stem cells. The discovery of 8CLCs represents a valuable in vitro model for recapitulating zygotic or embryonic genome activation. This finding complements studies of human embryos, which are available in limited numbers and raise ethical issues. Our approach provides an experimental model to uncover the molecular process of early human embryogenesis.

Methods

Ethics statement

The use of human embryonic stem cells for this study was approved by the Human Biology Research Ethics Committee of the University of Cambridge, UK (HBREC.2019.06) and Inner Mongolia University, China (EC-(2023)−0316). All experiments were conducted under the relevant guidelines and regulations for using human ESCs and in compliance with the ISSCR 2021 guidelines66. Together, this study falls within the existing legislation set by regulatory bodies in the UK and China. This study did not involve the use of human preimplantation embryos.

Human primed ESCs culture

Human primed ESCs cells, including H967 and WIS231 were routinely cultured in mTeSR 1 medium (Stemcell Technologies, 85850) or E8 medium (Thermo Fisher, A1517001) on plates coated with Vitronectin (Thermo Fisher, A31804) or Matrigel (Corning, 354230) according to the manufacturer’s protocol68. Generally, ESCs were passaged every 3-4 days and medium was refreshed every day. For passaging, cells were washed with DPBS (Thermo Fisher, 14200075) once and treated with 0.5 mM EDTA (Thermo Fisher, 15575020) for 5 min. Then, EDTA was removed, and cells were passaged as small clumps. The cells were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. All cell lines were confirmed as mycoplasma negative.

Generation of NELFA overexpression human primed ESCs

Doxycycline-inducible NELFA overexpression human primed ESCs (NELFA_OE ESCs) were generated by Lipofectamine™ Stem Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher, STEM00008) of ESCs with Tet-on-TRE-NELFA-EGFP (2 μg) in addition to the piggyBac transposase-expressing vector (PB-EF1α-transposase) (2 μg), PB-EF1α-rTTA plasmids. Tet-on-TRE, PB-EF1α-transposase, and PB-EF1α-rTTA plasmids were provided by Professor Pentao Liu (The University of Hong Kong). After transfection cells were treated with 3 µg/ml puromycin (MedChemExpress, HY-B1743) for 24–-48 h for selection. The remaining cells were split into 96-wells using mTeSR 1 medium (Stemcell Technologies, 85850) with 10 µM Y-27632 (MedChemExpress, HY-10071). Y-27632 was withdrawn 24 h after splitting, and the medium was refreshed every day. Culture wells containing a single colony were selected and expanded, and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. NELFA expression was induced by adding 1 μg/ml doxycycline (MedChemExpress, HY-N0565) to the culture medium.

Human ESCs primed-to-naïve conversion

To generate 4CL naïve ESCs25, wild-type and NELFA_OE ESCs were washed with DPBS once, dissociated into single cells with TrypLE™ Express (Gibco, 12563029) and plated at a density of 1000–1500 cells cm2 on Matrigel coated plates in mTeSR 1 medium supplemented with 10 µM Y-27632. Twenty-four hours later, the medium was switched to 4CL medium composed of 1:1 mix of Neurobasal medium (Gibco, 21103049) and Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco,12634010) supplemented with 0.5% N2 supplement (Gibco, 17502048), 1% B27 supplement (Gibco, 17504044), 1% Glutamax (Gibco, 35050061), 1% MEM NEAA (Gibco, 11140050), 100 U penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360070), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, 21985023), 10 nM DZNep (Selleck, S7120), 5 nM TSA (Sigma,T8552), 1 μM PD0325901 (Miltenyi biotec,130-103-923), 5 μM IWR-1 (Selleckchem, S7086), 20 ng/mL Human LIF (Peprotech, 300-05), 20 ng/mL ACTIVIN A (R&D, 338-AC), 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid (Sigma, 49752) and 0.2% (v/v) Matrigel). The 4CL culture medium was replaced daily, and the cells were passaged in the form of single cells every 3-4 days. The cells were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2

Generation of human 8CLCs from naïve ESCs

To generate human 8CLCs in stepwise e4CL25, wild-type and NELFA_OE naïve ESCs were dissociated into single cells and plated at 2,000-3000 cells cm2 on Matrigel coated plates in 4CL medium and, replacing it with e4CL medium 24 h later. The composition of e4CL is the same as 4CL except for higher concentrations of DZNep (50 nM) and TSA (20 nM). e4CL medium was refreshed every day until day 6. The cells were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2

Human ESCs in vitro differentiation

Pre-mesendoderm (Pre-ME), ME and definitive endoderm (DE) were induced from wild-type and NELFA_OE ESCs using the aRB27 basal medium, which was composed of Advanced RPMI 1640 Medium (Gibco, 12633012) supplemented with 1% B27 supplement (Gibco, 17504044), 1% Glutamax (Gibco, 35050061), 1% MEM NEAA (Gibco, 11140050), 100 U penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). To induce ME, trypsinized ESCs were seeded on a vitronectin-coated dish at 200,000 cells per well in a 12-well plate and cultured in ME induction medium for 12 h (PreME) and 24 h (ME). ME induction medium contained aRB27 medium supplemented with 100 ng/ml ACTIVIN A (Peprotech, 338-AC), 3 μM CHIR99021 (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-103-926) and 10 μM Y-27632 (MedChemExpress, HY-10071). To induce DE from ME, ME induction medium was replaced with a DE induction medium after washing with PBS once, and cells were cultured for a further 2 days. DE induction medium was composed of aRB27 medium supplemented with 100 ng/ml ACTIVIN A (R&D, 338-AC) and 0.5 μM BMP inhibitor LDN193189 (Sigma, SML0559). To induce spontaneous differentiation, cells were maintained in N2B27 basal medium for 3 days and then harvested for the following assays.

CRISPRa

We designed a piggyBAC-based CRISPRa (puromycin-resistant cassette) Dox-inducible SunTag system that consists of a dCas9 fused to five GCN4 peptides separated by optimized 22-amino-acid linkers and an scFV-sfGFP-VP64 transactivator fusion peptide that can be recruited to the dCas9 through the scFV-GCN4 domains69. The system is completed with piggyBAC gRNA plasmid, which entails a sgRNA cassette driven by an U6 promoter and a Tet-On 3G-IRES2-neomycin resistance cassette driven by an EF1α promoter69. Three sgRNAs targeting the NELFA and TP53 promoters and non-targeting sgRNA controls, were designed using CRISPOR Cas9 gRNA design tool (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/crispor.py). The piggyBAC-based CRISPRa (puromycin-resistant cassette) and sgRNA plasmids (neomycin-resistant cassette), together with a plasmid encoding a hyperactive piggyBAC transposase, were co-transfected into a human primed ESCs using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector transfection device. Stable cell lines with integration of the CRISPRa and sgRNA transgenes were generated after puromycin (MedChemExpress, HY-B1743) and neomycin (Gibco, 10131035) selection for 7–10 days. To activate the promoters, cells were treated with 1 μg/ml Dox in Essential 8 medium for 4–6 days and performed RT-qPCR and western blot analysis. All sgRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 15.

CRISPRi

For CRISPRi, we used the CRISPRa plasmid as the backbone and inserted a KRAB-dCas9-ecDHFR and an IRES-EGFP fragment downstream of the TRE3G promoter using the ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C113-02)69. The resulting plasmid encodes a KRAB-dCas9 transgene under the tight transcriptional control of a Dox-inducible promoter and a protein destabilization degron DHFR. The addition of Dox and trimethoprim (TMP) (MedChemExpress, HY-B0510) allows robust mRNA and protein expression of KRAB-dCas9 CRISPRi machinery that can be tracked by EGFP expression. To generate CRISPRi targeting lines, human primed ESCs were co-nucleofected with the piggyBAC-based CRISPRi (puromycin-resistant cassette) and sgRNA plasmids (neomycin-resistant cassette), as well as a hyperactive piggyBAC transposase plasmid using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector. To ensure stable integration for both the CRISPRi construct and the sgRNA transgenes, cells were selected for 7-10 days of combined puromycin and neomycin treatment after nucleofection. To inhibit the promoters, cells were treated with 1 μg/ml Dox and 10 μM TMP to induce CRISPRi in Essential 8 medium for 4-6 days and performed RT-qPCR and western blot analysis. All sgRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 15.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted with the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74104) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was performed using divalent cations under elevated temperature in 5× NEBNext first strand synthesis reaction buffer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers and M-MuLV reverse transcriptase. Second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Overhangs were converted to blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activity. The library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Colter). Sample clustering was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq platform and paired-end reads (150 bp) were generated.

After sequencing, the clean paired-end reads were aligned to the human reference genome (UCSC GRCh38/hg38) using HISAT2 (v2.0.5)70. Gene-level read counts were generated with featureCounts (v1.5.0-p3)71. Raw counts were used for differential expression analysis with DESeq2 (v1.16.1)72, whereas transcripts per million (TPM) values were calculated from gene length and read counts to quantify gene expression abundance. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was assessed by Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using the clusterProfiler R package. GO terms with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Library preparation, quality control and sequencing for whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS)

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, genomic DNA was extracted from stem cells using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, 69506). Remaining RNA was removed by treating with RNase A. After sample’s DNA testing, positive control DNAs were added into the DNAs, and then they were fragmented into 200–400 bp using Covaris S220 (Covaris). Next, ssDNA fragments were Bisulfite treated. After BS treatment, unmethylated Cytosine will change into Uracil, while methylated Cytosine will stay unchanged. Then, methylation sequencing adapters ligation, size selection and PCR amplification steps were performed to the DNA fragments. The library kit is Accel-NGS Methyl-Seq DNA Library Kit (Swift, 30096). Subsequently, library quality was assessed on the Agilent 5400 system (Agilent) and quantified by qPCR. Pair-end sequencing of sample was performed on Illumina platform (Illumina).

DNA methylation data analysis

Raw reads were initially evaluated with FastQC (v0.11.8), followed by adapter trimming and quality filtering using fastp (v0.23.1). Reads that passed all filtering criteria were retained as clean reads, and their quality was reassessed with FastQC. For methylation analysis, clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (UCSC GRCh38/hg38) using Bismark (v0.24.0) against a bisulfite-converted genome index generated with Bowtie 273. Mapping was performed in directional mode, and only uniquely aligned reads were retained. PCR duplicates were removed according to identical genomic coordinates. Sequencing depth and genome coverage were summarized from deduplicated reads. Cytosine methylation calls were obtained with the Bismark methylation extractor and converted to bigWig files for visualization in Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) genome browser (v2.4.10).

Methylated cytosines were identified by binomial testing based on methylated counts (mC), total counts (mC + umC), and the bisulfite non-conversion rate (r). Sites with FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.05 were classified as methylated. To estimate regional methylation levels, the genome was segmented into consecutive 10-kb bins, within which methylated and unmethylated read counts were aggregated. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified using DSS (v2.12.0)74,75, which models methylation variability with a beta-binomial framework and dispersion shrinkage. DMR-associated genes were defined as genes whose promoter regions (2 kb upstream of the transcription start site) or gene bodies (from transcription start site to transcription end site) overlapped DMRs.

ATAC-seq sample collection and preparation

Cultured human ESCs were collected and centrifuged at 4 °C 500 × g for 5 min. Cell activity was detected with LUNA-FL and counted. 50,000 live cells were centrifuged with 500 × g for 5 min, supernatant was discarded. The precipitation was resuspended with 50 μl lysis buffer, and placed on ice for 5 min, then 1 ml wash buffer was added, mixed, 500 × g centrifuged for 5 min, supernatant was discarded, and nucleus was collected.

ATAC-seq library and sequencing

ATAC-seq was performed as previously reported76. Briefly, nucleus was extracted from samples, and the nucleus pellet was resuspended in the Tn5 transposase reaction mix. The transposition reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Equimolar Adapter 1 and Adapter 2 were added after transposition, PCR was then performed to amplify the library (ATAC-seq_Novogene Kit). After the PCR reaction, libraries were purified with the AMPure beads and library quality was assessed with Qubit. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The library preparations were sequenced on Illumina Novaseq platform at Novogene Bioinformatic Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

ATAC-seq data processing

Raw FASTQ files were processed with fastp (v0.20.0) for adapter trimming and quality filtering. Reads containing poly-N sequences, reads with more than 40% of bases having a Phred quality score <15, and reads shorter than 18 bp after trimming were excluded. Q20, Q30, and GC content were calculated for the filtered reads, which were used in all downstream analyses. Filtered reads were mapped to the human reference genome (UCSC GRCh38/hg38) using BWA-MEM (BWA, v0.7.12). The reference genome and corresponding gene annotation files were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser. Reads aligned to mitochondrial DNA were removed, and only properly paired, deduplicated alignments with MAPQ ≥ 13 was retained for subsequent analyses.

ATAC-seq peak calling

Before peak calling, paired-end reads with fragment sizes <120 bp, corresponding to nucleosome-free open chromatin were extracted. Peaks were then identified with MACS2 (v2.2.7.1)77 in paired-end mode using callpeak -f BAMPE -q 0.05 --call-summits --keep-dup all. A q-value cutoff of 0.05 was applied to all datasets.

ATAC-seq motif analysis, peak annotation and different peak analysis

For motif analysis, peaks were resized to 500-bp intervals centered on summit positions. Motif enrichment was examined with HOMER (v4.11) using findMotifsGenome.pl and the parameters -len 8,10,12,14 -gc -size given -p 2 -S 25 -homer2 -dumpFasta. Peaks were annotated to genomic features and assigned to their nearest genes using ChIPseeker78. Peak-associated genes were subsequently subjected to GO enrichment analysis with GOseq, which accounts for gene length bias. GO terms with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered significant. To define differential peaks, peak sets from different groups were first merged with bedtools merge, and mean reads per million (RPM) values were calculated for each merged region in each group. Regions showing |log2 fold change | > 1 were classified as differential peaks. Genes linked to differential peaks were annotated using ChIPseeker.

CUT&Tag-seq sample preparation

Cultured human ESCs were harvested and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Cell viability and concentration were determined using the LUNA-FL automated cell counter. Subsequently, 500,000 live cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 600 × g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in 500 μl of wash buffer to form a single-cell suspension for the subsequent experiment.

CUT&Tag-seq library and sequencing

CUT&Tag library was generated using a well-established protocol79. Briefly, the cells are bound to Concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads, and the cell membrane is permeabilized by Digitonin. The enzyme pA-Tn5 Transposase precisely binds the DNA sequence near the target protein under the antibody (anti-H3K27ac, Abcam, ab4729) guidance and results in factor-targeted tagmentation. DNA sequence is tagmented, with adapters added at the same time at both ends, which can be enriched by PCR to form the sequencing-ready libraries (Vazyme, TD904). After the PCR reaction, libraries were purified with the AMPure beads and library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The library preparations were sequenced on Illumina Novaseq platform at Novogene Co.,Ltd. (Beijing, China) and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

CUT&Tag-seq data processing

Raw FASTQ reads were filtered with fastp (v0.20.0)80 to remove adapter-contaminated reads, reads containing poly-N sequences, and low-quality reads. Q20, Q30, and GC content were calculated after filtering, and the resulting clean reads were used for downstream analyses. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (UCSC GRCh38/hg38) using BWA-MEM (BWA v0.7.12) with the parameters -k 32 -T 30 -t 4 -M. The reference genome and corresponding gene annotation files were downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser. Only high-confidence alignments with MAPQ ≥ 13 were retained for subsequent analyses.

CUT&Tag-seq peak calling

Peak calling was performed using MACS2 (v2.1.0) in paired-end mode with the following parameters: callpeak -q 0.05 -f BAMPE --call-summits --keep-dup all77. A q-value cutoff of 0.05 was used for all datasets.

CUT&Tag-seq motif analysis, peak annotation and different peak analysis

To examine sequence features within CUT&Tag peaks, peak regions were standardized to 500 bp by centering on summit positions. Enriched motifs were identified with the findMotifsGenome.pl module in HOMER (v4.9.1) using the parameters -len 8,10,12,14 -gc -size given -homer2 -dumpFasta78. Peak annotation was performed with ChIPseeker, which was also used to assign each peak to its nearest gene. GO enrichment analysis of peak-associated genes was carried out with GOseq, with correction for gene length bias. GO categories with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were regarded as significantly enriched. For intergroup comparison, peak sets were consolidated with bedtools merge to generate a unified collection of peak regions. Mean RPM signals were then calculated for each group across each merged region. Peaks showing a > 2-fold difference in mean RPM between groups were defined as differential peaks, and the associated genes were annotated using ChIPseeker.

Chimeric experiments

Mice were kept in the animal house of Inner Mongolia University, China. Mouse handling and experiments were carried out with humane methods in compliance with the guidelines of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Inner Mongolia University (SYXK(MENG)2020-0006). Male (8-10 weeks old) and female ICR mice (6-8 weeks old) were housed in a temperature-controlled room (20-22 °C) under a 12-hour light/dark cycle (06:00-18:00). They had free access to water and food. Both male and female embryos were used in this study without genotyping for sex determination. 8-cell stage embryos were obtained by flushing the oviducts of female ICR mice. Corresponding cells with EGFP reporter expression were dissociated and 10-15 WT or NELFA_OE ESCs cultured in e4CL were injected into each embryo. Following microinjection, 8-cell stage embryos were cultured in G-2TM PLUS (Vitrolife, 10132) for another 48–60 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The chimeric blastocysts were fixed for immunostaining.

RT-qPCR

The total RNA of human ESCs were extracted with the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74104). cDNA was obtained by Reverse Transcription System (Qiagen, 205311). Real-time PCR reactions were set up in duplicate using KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR kit (KAPA Biosystems, KK4601) and repeated with at least three biological replicates with similar experiments. GAPDH expression level was used as an internal normalization control. All samples were conducted using a LightCycler® 96 Instrument (Roche Molecular Systems). Relative expression levels were assessed by the 2-ΔΔCt method. All primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 15.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence staining, samples were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 30 min at room temperature and blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min. Primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer were applied to cells and incubated for overnight at 4 °C. The antibodies used were: 1:200 rabbit anti-BRACHYURY (T) (Abcam, ab209665); 1:200 sheep anti-EOMES (R&D, AF6166); 1:100 goat anti-FOXA2 (R&D, AF2400); 1:100 goat anti-GATA6 (R&D, AF1700); 1:100 goat anti-SOX1 (R&D, AF3369); 1:100 goat anti-SOX2 (R&D, AF2018); 1:100 mouse anti-NESTIN (Santa cruz, sc-71665); 1:100 rabbit anti-KLF4 (Abcam, ab215036); 1:100 rabbit anti-OCT4 (NOVUSBIO, NB100-2379); 1:100 rabbit anti-NANOG (Peprotech, 500-P236); 1:100 sheep anti-DNMT3B (R&D, AF7646); 1:200 mouse anti-NELFA (abcam, ab167675); 1:500 rabbit anti-ZSCAN4 (NOVUSBIO, NBP1-77120); 1:200 rabbit anti-TPRX1 (Proteintech, 25834-1-AP); 1:200 mouse anti-CDX2 (BioGenex, AM392); 1:200 mouse anti-p53 (BD Biosciences, 554293); 1:100 goat anti-SOX17 (R&D, AF1924). Next, the samples were washed three times with PBS, followed by incubation with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, the slides were then mounted in Vectashield with DAPI (Vector Laboratories). Samples were imaged under Leica SP8 upright or inverted scanning confocal microscope or Nikon AX confocal microscope.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using lysis buffer (Solarbio, R0030) containing 0.1 M PMSF (Solarbio, P0100) and 10 g/L phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Scientific, A32957) on ice. After centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, 23227). Protein was separated by 10%-12% SDS-PAGE, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (BIO-RAD, 1620177), blocked with 5% skimmed milk (BD, 232100) for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated first with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, and then with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The antibodies used were: 1:1000 mouse anti-NELFA (abcam, ab167675); 1:1000 rabbit anti-NELFA (Bethyl, A301-910A); 1:1000 rabbit anti-β-ACTIN (CST, 4970S); 1:500 rabbit anti-DUX (HUABIO, ER2001-60); 1:1000 rabbit anti-H3 (Abcam, ab1791); 1:1000 rabbit anti-H3K27ac (Active motif, 39133); 1:1000 goat anti-SOX2 (R&D, AF2018); 1:1000 rabbit anti-OCT4 (NOVUSBIO, NB100-2379); 1:1000 rabbit anti-NANOG (Peprotech, 500-P236); 1:1000 rabbit anti-ZSCAN4 (NOVUSBIO, NBP1-77120); 1:1000 rabbit anti-TPRX1 (Proteintech, 25834-1-AP); 1:1000 mouse anti-p53 (BD Biosciences, 554293). The signals were visualized with Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Scientific, 32209) and detected with the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Statistical analysis

All values are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical parameters including statistical analysis, statistical significance, and n value are reported in the Figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism Software (GraphPad Prism, v8.0.1). Differences between groups were evaluated by an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests was employed. p-value was smaller than 0.05 was considered significant.
