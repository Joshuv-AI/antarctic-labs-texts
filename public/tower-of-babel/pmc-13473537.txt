Integration of gene expression analysis and molecular docking in revealing stress-responsive functions of potato chitinases
Authors: Maryam Faramarzi-Jafarbeiglou, Farhad Nazarian-Firouzabadi, Ali Moghadam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChitin, a long-chain polymer of N-acetylglucosamine units, is a major structural component of the cell walls of many fungi and oomycetes and serves as a strong potent elicitor of plant immune responses. Chitinases are hydrolytic enzymes that degrade chitin, and play important roles in plant defence against pathogens as well as in response to various abiotic stresses. In this study, RNA-seq data were analysed to identify chitinase genes with the high expression levels under drought and disease stress conditions. Candidate genes were further investigated using homology modelling, molecular docking, and experimental validation through qRT-PCR analysis. Among the candidate genes analysed, Class I chitinase exhibited the highest expression under both stress conditions. Class III chitinase showed no significant response to chitin treatment but was significantly upregulated under drought stress. Class IV chitinase was induced under both treatments, with a stronger response to chitin application than to drought stress. In contrast, Class V chitinase was downregulated under drought stress but markedly upregulated following chitin treatment. Molecular docking analysis indicated strong binding of chitin ligands to key catalytic residues of Class V chitinase, supporting its direct role in chitin degradation and suggesting its involvement in the activation of pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) in response to pathogen attack.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55174-9.Keywords: Chitin, Chitinases, Gene expression, Molecular docking, Solanum tuberosumSubject terms: Biotechnology, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), a member of Solanaceae family, is a world-wide staple food crop of industrial importance. In terms of human consumption and animal feed, potato ranks third following wheat and rice, providing a crucial source of calories, vitamins, and minerals for increasing world population. Despite its economic and nutritional values, potato cultivation is vulnerable to diverse biotic and abiotic stresses, including drought and diseases, eventually leading to markedly yield reduction1.

Among different biotic stresses, plant diseases caused by fungal and oomycete pathogens represent a persistent and serious challenge to potato yield, quality and production, worldwide2. Chitinases (EC 3.2.1.14), a major and important group of hydrolytic enzymes, have been widely investigated for their role in plant defense mechanisms3. Such enzymes catalyze the degradation of chitin, a key structural component of fungal cell walls, producing chitin-derived oligomers4. Interestingly, chitinases are broadly distributed across bacteria, fungi, viruses, animals, and higher plants, highlighting their evolutionary and functional significance5. Beyond direct hydrolysis, both chitin and chitinase-derived oligomers act as signaling molecules that trigger immune responses in plants, insects, and mammals6, activating pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) mechanism7.

Biophysical studies have demonstrated that the binding affinity of chitin oligomers increases with chain length. For instance, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) has revealed that chitin oligomers of four to six units exhibited strong exothermic interactions with algal chitinase, whereas shorter oligomers showed little to no binding activity8. Similarly, in potato, reported cultivar-dependent differences in chitin perception: resistant cultivars showed stronger interactions between chitin ligands and the LysM-RLK05 receptor compared to sensitive cultivars9.

In the present study, the structural and functional diversity of potato chitinases was investigated using homology-based modeling and molecular docking. From each class of chitinase gene, the genes with the highest expression under disease and drought stress were selected for protein modeling. Docking analyses with various chitin oligomers were then performed to evaluate binding stability, with the aim of identifying the most effective enzyme–substrate interactions that could contribute to disease resistance mechanisms. Finally, the gene expression levels of each chitinase protein were combined with the results of ligand protein docking to more precisely determine the role of chitinase in biotic and abiotic stress.

Material and methods

To select chitinase genes, RNA-seq data from potato were analyzed using the CLCgenomicswb20 software. From each gene classes, one gene with the highest gene expression level under drought and disease stress conditions was selected. Overall, 31 chitinase genes (Supplementary Table 1) were retrieved from the potato genome using genome-wide in silico based on RNA-seq data analysis (Supplementary Table 2). Subsequently, the sequences of the selected chitinase genes were downloaded from the Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) database (Table 1).

Homology modeling and secondary structure analysis

Three-dimensional (3D) structures of the candidate potato chitinase proteins were generated using the Robetta web server (https://robetta.bakerlab.org/)10,11. Protein sequences in FASTA format were submitted, and the server automatically performed domain parsing and template identification. When suitable templates were available, comparative modeling was applied; otherwise, TrRosetta and RoseTTAFold algorithms were used to generate ab initio models. Multiple candidate structures were produced for each sequence, and the top-ranked models were retained for further analysis.

The quality and stereochemical accuracy of the predicted structures were evaluated using the SAVES v6.0 server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/). This server integrates validation programs such as PROCHECK12, ERRAT13, and Verify3D14. Finally, only models that displayed acceptable stereochemical parameters and satisfactory overall quality scores were kept for downstream analyses.

Secondary structure features of the validated chitinase models were characterized using the PDBsum server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk). This server provides detailed structural annotations, including the distribution of α-helices, β-strands, turns, motifs, hydrogen-bonding networks, and topology diagrams, enabling comparison of conserved structural patterns among different chitinase classes.

In order to predict functional regions, particularly catalytic and ligand-binding sites, the CavityPlus server (http://www.pkumdl.cn) was employed. This server identifies potential binding pockets based on cavity geometry. Predicted cavities were ranked by size, shape, and binding potential. These computationally identified active sites were then cross-referenced with conserved catalytic residues reported in the literature to assign putative functional sites to different classes of potato chitinases. Next, the validated and functionally annotated structural models obtained from this pipeline were subsequently used for molecular docking with chitin, molecules, providing a structural framework for understanding the catalytic mechanisms and biological roles of different chitinase classes in potato.

Protein–ligand docking

Molecular docking was performed to investigate the interaction between the modeled potato chitinases and their natural substrate. N-acetylglucosamine units, ranging from monomers to pentamers (Fig. 1), were constructed using Avogadro v1.2.015. Each ligand structure was geometry-optimized and energy-minimized using the MMFF94 force field to ensure accurate conformations prior to docking.

Chemical structures of chitin oligomers (monomer to pentamer) generated and energy-minimized using Avogadro v1.2.0 server. The repeating monomeric unit of chitin is N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc), shown here as the fundamental building block for the oligomers.

Docking simulations were carried out using AutoDock Vina v1.516, with the predicted active site regions defined as the docking grid. Chitin oligomers were docked into the catalytic pockets of each validated chitinase structure. Multiple binding poses were generated for each ligand, and based on binding affinity scores, the top-ranked conformations were chosen.

The resulting protein–ligand complexes were further analyzed using PyMOL v2.5, which allowed visualization of binding modes and identification of amino acid residues contributing to substrate recognition and catalysis. Hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and key catalytic residues were specifically examined to compare interaction patterns across different chitinase classes. Binding free energy values obtained from AutoDock Vina were used to rank the relative strength of protein–ligand interactions.

Cultivation and treatments

Greenhouse experiments were conducted in pots containing a mixture of vermiculite, perlite, and premixed fertilizer. Potato mini-tubers were planted and maintained under controlled greenhouse conditions (20°C, 14/10 h light/dark photoperiod) Greenhouse humidity was kept at 70% relative humidity.

Drought stress treatment

During plant growth, soil moisture was continuously monitored using a tensiometer. Control plants were regularly irrigated to maintain soil moisture at field capacity. For drought stress treatment, irrigation was withheld at the onset of tuberization, which is considered the most sensitive stage to water deficit stress17. When soil moisture content decreased to approximately 50% of the volumetric water content, leaf samples were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen and immediately stored at − 80 °C for subsequent laboratory analyses.

Chitin induction treatment

For chitin induction, very small superficial grooves were gently made on the leaf surface to enhance uptake and uniform distribution of the treatment. A chitin solution (150 μg mL⁻1) was then applied by spraying onto the potato seedling leaves. Control plants were subjected to the same mechanical treatment and received distilled water instead of the chitin solution. This experimental designed in such a way to ensure that any wound-related responses were equally present in both control and treated leaves, allowing differences in gene expression to be attributed specifically to chitin application. After 48 h post treatment, leaves were covered with nylon and collected for subsequent laboratory analyses.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted using the Dena Zist commercial RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and concentration were assessed by spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis. First-strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using the Thermo Fisher Scientific cDNA synthesis kit following the recommended protocol.

qRT-PCR analysis

For qRT-PCR analysis, three independent biological replicates were used for each treatment, with each biological replicate consisting of pooled leaf samples collected from at least three individual plants grown under identical conditions. In addition, each biological replicate was analyzed in three technical replicates to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the results. Total RNA was extracted, and cDNA was synthesized using standard protocols. Gene expression levels were quantified using specific primers (Table 2) and SYBR Green chemistry. The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt method18 with normalization performed against the expression of elongation factor-1alpha (elfα) as the internal reference gene. The selected reference gene showed consistent expression across all experimental conditions, ensuring reliable normalization of transcript levels.

Result and discussion

Genes expression analysis

In this study, changes in chitinase gene expression were assessed using real-time PCR method. Different classes of chitinase genes exhibited varying levels of expression under chitin inoculation and drought stress. The highest level of chitinase gene expression under both stress conditions was observed for class I (Soltu.DM.10G017450). The lowest expression levels were observed for class III under chitin induction and for class V under drought stress. Recent studies have shown that overexpression of a class I chitinase gene in tea plants, enhances resistance to blister blight fungus19. Similarly in rice plants, class I chitinase gene expression increases in response to infection by late leaf spot and Aspergillus flavus20. In another study, analysis of chitinase gene expression in Arabidopsis leaves under drought and cold stress revealed that class I chitinase genes are highly expressed21. Given the expression of chitinase genes in response to chitin elicitation, it should also be noted that the mechanical wounding applied during chitin treatment may have contributed, at least in part, to the observed induction of chitinase gene expression in a jasmonic acid (JA)-mediated signaling pathways. Therefore, while our experimental design controlled for mechanical injury across treatments, a potential contribution of JA-dependent wound responses to the overall expression profile cannot be completely excluded.

Class III chitinase gene did not show a significantly change in expression under chitin-induced treatment compared to the control, however, its expression was significantly upregulated under drought stress. In cucumber plants, it has been reported that 48 h post infection with Sphaerotheca fuliginea, the expression level of the class III chitinase gene exhibited a slight increase21.

Expression of class IV chitinase gene was significant increased in treated plants relative to the controls under both stresse conditions, however, the level of expression was higher under chitin induction than under drought stress. In mustard leaves, the expression of class IV chitinase genes increased 48 h post inoculation with Alternaria fungus5. Similarley in Arabidopsis plants, the transcript levels of class IV chitinase genes was rapidly accumulated following inoculation with the fungus, suggesting that class IV chitinase genes might be involved in the initial events of hypersensitivity reaction (HR)22. In addition, it has been reported that wounding of plant organs has been shown to stimulate the promoter of chitinase genes mustard leaves. Such an activation is likely attributed to regulatory elements in the promoter responsive to salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, and wounding23. In contrast, there are reports that abiotic stresses do not significantly affect class IV chitinase genes expression, suggesting a limited role for these class of chitinase genes in response to abiotic stresses24. As it can be seen from Fig. 2, Class V chitinase gene showed decreased expression compared to the control under drought stress, but was markedly upregulated under chitin induction (Fig. 2).

Figure 2 shows that class I chitinase gene expression was strongly increased under both pathogen challenge and drought stress treatment. Plus, the expression of class III chitinase gene was upregulated under drought stress but showed no significant induction in response to chitin induction. Class IV chitinase gene did not respond to abiotic stress, whereas class V chitinase gene was markedly upregulated under chitin treatment (Fig. 2).

Based on the above results Soltu.DM.10G017450, Soltu.DM.11G026160, Soltu.DM.10G017290 and Soltu.DM.07G000200 proteins belonging to classes I, III, IV, and V chitinases, respectively, were chosen for further analysis. Soltu.DM.10G017450 (Class I) encodes a 250-amino-acid protein that is targeted to the plasma membrane. Soltu.DM.11G026160 (Class III), comprising 308 amino acids, localizes to both the plasma membrane and the extracellular space. Soltu.DM.10G017290 (Class IV) encodes a shorter, 122-amino-acid protein confined to the extracellular region. In contrast, Soltu.DM.07G000200 (Class V) encodes a longer protein of 377 amino acids, which is also localized to the extracellular space (Table 1). All four chitinases differ in sequence length and subcellular localization, suggesting distinct roles at the cell surface and in the apoplast. Soltu.DM.10G017450 is plasma membrane–targeted, consistent with a GPI-anchored protein involved in wall integrity sensing. Soltu.DM.11G026160 localizes to both the membrane and extracellular space, allowing it to mediate cell-to-cell communication and defense responses. By contrast, the two extracellular proteins show different size classes, implying divergent biological functions. Soltu.DM.10G017290 falls within the size range typical of secreted peptide hormone precursors such as RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTORs (RALFs, ~ 80–150 aa), which regulate growth, reproduction, and stress signaling via receptors like FERONIA. In contrast, Soltu.DM.07G000200 resembles larger secreted enzymes or structural proteins abundant in the plant secretome, including peroxidases, glycosidases, and proteases, which participate in cell-wall remodeling and defense. Together, these findings suggest a functional division: membrane-associated proteins (Classes I and III) are likely to act in cell-surface signaling and anchoring, while secreted proteins (Classes IV and V) contribute to peptide signaling and apoplastic enzymatic activity. Sequence-based analyses will be essential to refine these functional hypotheses and confirm their roles in plant growth and immunity25.

Prediction of 3D structure of potato chitinases

Secondary structure predictions and three-dimensional modeling of the different candidate of potato chitinases were performed using the PDBsum database and the Robetta server, respectively (Figs. 3 and 4). As it can be seen from Fig. 3, structural analysis revealed that Class I and IV chitinases are predominantly characterized by a uniform distribution of α-helices throughout their primary sequences (Figs. 3a, c). Conversely, Class III and V chitinases exhibit more intricate architectures composed of both α-helices and β-sheets. Specifically, the Class III model contains 13 α-helices and 10 β-sheets (Fig. 3b), whereas the Class V enzyme displays a higher density of secondary motifs, comprising 14 α-helices and 18 β-sheets (Fig. 3d).

Each diagram illustrates the distribution of α-helices, β-strands, and connecting loops, highlighting structural variations among different chitinase classes.

Three-dimensional (3D) structural models of different candidate potato chitinase. 3D for class I, III, IV, and V chitinases were predicted using the Robetta server. The models demonstrate conserved α/β fold architectures characteristic of plant chitinases, while highlighting distinct class-specific variations in loop regions and the spatial orientation of the catalytic pockets.

Previous studies have established that GH19 family chitinases are characterized by a high α-helice configuration content and a single-displacement catalytic mechanism. Such an enzymatic activity is dependent on two conserved glutamic acid (Glu) residues within the enzyme active site. Moreover, the catalytic domain typically contains 10 interconnected α-helices, a structural hallmark of Class I and II GH19 chitinases26,27. Our modeling results are consistent with these established features. Notably, the Class I chitinase model was predicted to localize to the plasma membrane, suggesting that they may play a role as plasma membrane-bound receptors. Given that membrane proteins are predominantly composed of α-helical transmembrane domains28, the α-helix–rich structure observed in our study further supports the validity of our predicted models. Conversely, class III and V chitinases are members of the GH18 family, which are characterized by the presence of conserved α/β-barrel domains. Such a fold is typically associated with versatile enzymatic scaffolds capable of catalyzing diverse biochemical reactions. For instance, the α/β-barrel architecture is also observed in plant-derived α(1,4) glucanases. Unlike the GH19 family, GH18 enzymes often utilize a substrate-assisted mechanism involving proton transfer through an intermediate, facilitated by a conserved glutamic acid (Glu) residue at the active site. These established structural benchmarks collectively reinforce the plausibility of our Class III and V potato chitinase models.

The structural quality and integrity of all modeled chitinases were rigorously assessed using Ramachandran plots, which evaluate chitinases protein backbone conformation by quantifying the distribution of amino acid residues within energetically favored versus disallowed regions29. As illustrated in Fig. 5 the proportion of residues in favored regions was 89.7%, 90.5%, 84.4%, and 90% for class IV, III, I, and V chitinases, respectively (Fig. 5). While a high-quality stereochemical model typically requires at least 90% of residues to reside within favored regions, most of the generated models for studied chitinases adhered to this standard30. The class I chitinase proved to be the exception, consistently yielding approximately 84% of residues in favored regions across multiple modeling iterations. This lower structural scores may be attributed to the inherent absence of a chitin-binding domain or specific catalytic motifs within its primary sequence31.

Ramachandran plots validation of the modeled potato chitinases. Analysis was performed using PROCHECK algorithm for (a) Class V chitinase, (b) Class I chitinase, (c) Class III chitinase, and (d) Class IV chitinase. In each model, the majority of residues are distributed within the most favored and additionally allowed regions, thereby substantiating the stereochemical reliability and structural quality of the predicted chitinase proteins conformations.

Molecular docking of chitinases and chitin ligands

Molecular docking is a robust computational and bioinformatics approach to simulate both structural and energetic interactions between biological macromolecules such as proteins and small molecule-ligand substrates32,33. In this study, we assessed the binding affinities of chitin oligomers of varying lengths within the active sites of four classes of potato chitinases. Our docking results indicated that all chitin oligomers successfully interacted with each chitinase classes. Table 2 illustrate the binding energies for each interaction. The variations in binding energies suggest distinct functional roles and substrate affinities among the four potato chitinase classes. Class I chitinase exhibited the highest (most negative) binding energies ranging from − 3.2 to − 8.8 kcal/mol, suggesting a high thermodynamic stability in the enzyme–substrate complex, likely due to a deep, well-defined active site cleft or the presence of specific stabilizing residues that are absent in other classes34. In contrast, class III and class IV chitinases showed significantly lower affinities, as low as − 0.56 kcal/mol which may suggest transient or weak interactions. Such weak interactions could imply that class III and class IV chitinases require longer chitin chains to achieve stable binding or that they function via a different catalytic mechanism35. Class V chitinases demonstrated a more intermediate and consistent range of − 4.88 to − 5.62 kcal/mol. Such a narrow range around − 5.0 kcal/mol likely suggests a specialized, uniform binding mode for class V chitinases regardless of the oligomer length.

Next, the top-scoring conformations of the ligand-receptor complexes were selected for further detailed structural analysis using PyMOL. The specific amino acid residues participating in hydrogen-bond formation for each complex are detailed in Table 3. As it can be seen from Table 3, significant variations in binding architecture were observed across the four chitinase classes. In class I, none of the conserved catalytic residues were engaged in substrate binding, potentially suggesting an inactive or non-productive complex under the simulated conditions. In class III, direct catalytic participation was restricted to the chitin tetramer, where an essential glutamic acid residue-critical for the hydrolytic mechanism-was involved in stabilization.

Gene expression analysis under biotic and abiotic stresses further corroborated our findings. We found that, class I chitinase exhibited high expression levels during both pathogen challenge and drought stress, given that chitin is absent during drought stress, this expression patterns suggest a functional role extending beyond direct chitin degradation or chitin signaling. It has been documented that the activity of class I chitinases depends on glutamic acid, tyrosine, and arginine amino acid residues. While tyrosine residue facilitates substrate binding without directly influencing the reaction rate, glutamic acid and arginine residues are essential for catalysis; their substitution by site-directed mutagenesis has been shown to abolish enzymatic function in homologous barley chitinases36. According to our docking results, none of the chitin oligomers interacted with these residues, a lack of productive binding. The absence of catalytic engagement, combined with robust induction during drought, suggests that class I chitinase may fulfill non-catalytic roles, potentially functioning as a stress-related signaling protein.

There are reports illustrating that certain chitinases can act on alternative polysaccharides, specifically arabinogalactan proteins (AGPs), which function as extracellular signaling molecules37–40. To this end chitinase-mediated modification of AGPs has been shown to enhance their signaling capacity41, and AGPs are known to interact with membrane-bound proteins to relay signals into the cytoplasm42,43. In line with our findings, the predicted plasma membrane localization of the class I chitinase proteins supports the hypothesis that it interacts with AGPs within the apoplastic space, thereby mediating drought or pathogen-related signaling cascades rather than primarily executing direct chitin hydrolysis.

Class III chitinase exhibited a distinct expression profile, characterized by upregulation during drought stress rather than chitin induction. This physiological observation was corroborated by molecular docking, which revealed weak or unfavorable ligand-enzyme complexes; notably, the catalytic glutamic acid participated in binding in only a single instance. Together, these data may suggest that class III chitinase does not function primarily in chitin degradation. Instead, consistent with class I chitinases, its role likely centers on signaling involving AGPs. In the absence of chitin oligomers such as during drought stress, AGPs may preferentially bind to these enzymes to facilitate the amplification of defense-related signals. This competitive binding model is further supported by evidence that mono- and disaccharides can inhibit chitinase-AGP interactions44. By contrast, class IV chitinase remained unresponsive to abiotic stress and appears structurally inactive due to the loss of both its chitin-binding and catalytic domains24. Although it is predicted to localize in the apoplast and showed increased expression following chitin induction, molecular docking failed to demonstrate engagement with catalytic residues or the formation of stable enzyme–substrate complexes. These findings suggest that the role of class IV chitinase has diverged from hydrolysis, likely serving as a signaling molecule rather than a functional enzyme.

Class V chitinase, exhibited a functional profile distinct from the other classes. Our molecular docking simulations demonstrated the direct involvement of conserved glutamic acid and tyrosine residues in ligand binding, particularly with the chitin tetramers (Fig. 6), confirming a definitive catalytic role in chitin degradation. Such an interpretation is further reinforced by expression data, illustrating that class V potato chitinase is strongly upregulated following chitin treatment but remains minimally expressed under drought stress. Classified as chitinase-like proteins, class V chitinases share high structural homology with bacterial chitinases, which typically degrade chitin to serve as carbon and nitrogen sources45. Similarly, potato Class V chitinase likely functions in the hydrolysis of fungal chitin for both defense and nutrient acquisition, while potentially facilitating the recognition and transport of chitin-derived molecules into the cell for downstream processing46.

Conclusion

Potato chitinases play an essential role in plant defense by recognizing carbohydrate signals and activating the MAPK signaling cascade, which induces the expression of stress-responsive genes. Classes I, III, and IV mainly function through interactions with arabinogalactan proteins (AGPs), whereas class V chitinase is primarily activated by chitin. The strong binding affinity between class V chitinase and chitin oligomers, particularly chitin tetramers, highlights its key role in fungal cell wall degradation and pathogen resistance. The findings of present study may suggest that chitin tetramers can serve as effective elicitors to enhance plant resistance against fungal pathogens and may have potential applications in sustainable fungicide formulations.
