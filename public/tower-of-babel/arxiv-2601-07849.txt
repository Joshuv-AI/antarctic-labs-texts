Quantum mechanics provides the physical basis of teleological evolutions
Giuseppe Castagnoli

Abstract
We show that the quantum computational speedup is due to the teleological character of quantum algorithms, their being evolutions toward a goal (the solution of the problem) with an attractor in the very goal they will produce in the future (the solution of the problem again). We also show that, under the quantum cosmological assumption and for the Fine-tuned Universe version of the Anthropic Principle, the physical basis of the teleological character of quantum algorithms applies as well to the evolutions of the living for which the teleological notion was originally conceived.

Introduction

Let us briefly retrace the two and a half millennia history of the teleological notion: attraction exercised by a final cause. Plato and Aristotle applied it the functional structure of the living (Aristotle: teeth were created for the purpose of chewing); of course, at the time, there was no notion of natural evolution. With the advent of this notion, it was applied to the evolutions of the living by a school of natural philosophy. It was eventually dismissed by science (it was classical science) for the alleged absence of a physical basis, even ascribed, for its finalistic character, to the category of beliefs. All this was before the advent of quantum mechanics, but has remained almost unchanged with it. Today, the situation of the teleological notion is somewhat complex. However, to the best of our knowledge, outside the present work, in physics, there are no researches on it. Let us round things up by saying that in both physics and the natural sciences there is a de facto disbelief of the retrocasusality and finalism implied in the notion.

In this work, we shall show that the relatively recent discovery of
the quantum computational speedup
should change this de facto position of science. This is because quantum algorithms turn out to be teleological evolutions: indeed, evolutions toward a goal (the solution of the problem) with an attractor in the very goal they will produce in the future (the solution of the problem again). Since showing this will be simple, the fact that, forty years after the discovery of the quantum speedup, it has never been noticed before could be ascribed to that de facto position of science.

By the way, here and in the following, by quantum speedup we will always mean its certain form, the fact that the quantum algorithm needs fewer computation steps than classically possible. Of course, this is not the case of most quantum algorithms, which need fewer computation steps than their best classical counterpart, but whether the latter is the best classically possible is not known.

We should also note that the teleological character of quantum algorithms—their being evolutions toward a goal (the solution of the problem) with an attractor in the very goal they will produce in the future (the solution of the problem again)—is implicit in the rule in question. Also the notion of finalism acquires a precise definition. The teleological evolution would not be attracted by some scientifically undefinable goal but, very simply, by its own end product (in quantum algorithms, the solution of the problem). We will see further on that the physical definition of teleological evolution must be that of an evolution between an initial and a final measurement outcome.

Until now, this advanced knowledge rule was, so to speak, an empirical rule shown to be satisfied by all major quantum algorithms. In the present work, we should have demonstrated it, incidentally, in a surprisingly simple way. Of course, we would have consequently demonstrated the teleological character of quantum algorithms.

As a matter of fact, we will provide two redundant demonstrations of this rule, which should not be despised given its destabilizing character. Let us outline them.

One is an application of classical logic to what the quantum algorithms does: providing a quantum speedup with respect to what is classically possible.

By the way, we will have to use a quantum description of the quantum algorithms that is not the customary one. The latter is limited to the unitary process of solving the problem, where the quantum speedup resides, and the final measurement of the solution. It is therefore incomplete; the canonically complete description of a quantum process consists of initial measurement, unitary evolution, and final measurement.

We complete this customary quantum description by adding, to the description of the process of solving the problem, that of the upstream process of setting the problem. The result is: an initial measurement of the problem setting in a quantum superposition (or, indifferently, mixture) of all the possible problem-settings selects one of them at random; the unitary computation and measurement of the corresponding solution follow. Note that this quantum description only involves the measurements of two commuting observables (the problem-setting and the solution of the problem); therefore, it should be subject to classical logic.

Having said this, the outline of the first demonstration can be very short. As we shall see in the next section, under the present more complete quantum description of the quantum algorithm, the classical logic description of the quantum speedup turns out to be indeed the advanced knowledge rule.

Thus, in particular, this classical logic description of the quantum algorithm would tell more than its present quantum description; we should conclude that the latter is still incomplete. Indeed, it is simply completed by time-symmetrizing it. We shall see that the time-symmetrization of the more complete quantum description of the quantum algorithm generates the quantum superposition of the causal loops involved in the advanced knowledge rule.

The second demonstration of the advanced knowledge rule simply comes from the fact that the time-symmetrization of the more complete quantum description of the quantum algorithm turns out to be mandatory for another reason independent from the one seen so far. As we shall see, it is to satisfy an atemporal symmetry implicit in this description. As we have seen, this time-symmetrization yields the physical correspondent of the advanced knowledge rule—we have thus a demonstration of the rule.

Naturally, either demonstration of the advanced knowledge rule is also a demonstration of the teleological character of quantum algorithms, their being evolutions toward a goal (the solution of the problem) with an attractor in the very goal they will produce in the future (the solution of the problem again).

A further step, important for the possibility of extending the teleological character of quantum algorithms to the evolutions of the living, is the following generalization.

For simplicity, let us limit ourselves to the case that, in the quantum algorithm, the solution is an invertible function of the problem-setting and is determined with certainty. We can then say that the quantum algorithm unitarily builds up one-to-one correlation between two initially unrelated observables—the problem-setting and the solution of the problem—without ever changing the eigenvalue of the observable selected by the initial measurement—the problem-setting.

We shall show that any building up of quantum correlation of this kind, also outside quantum computation, can occur with quantum speedup and therefore be teleological in character. Note that the “can occur” means that there is certainly the room for achieving a quantum speedup, but, of course, whether it is achieved, in full or in part or not at all, depends on the character of the unitary evolution that builds up the quantum correlation. Let us now go to what the teleological character of quantum algorithms can say to the evolutions of the living.

Naturally, the first observation must be on the enormous difference between quantum algorithms and the evolutions of the living. The former have maximum durations of a few dozen microseconds and a number of quantum bits limited to the order of one thousand; beyond that, in today’s implementations, there is quantum decoherence. The latter have a duration of billions of years and can have the size of the Earth. However, the very context we are dealing with suggests a way out. The difference is enormous in the usual classical way of viewing the macroscopic reality, but disappears under the quantum cosmological assumption that the universe evolves in a unitary quantum way

Let us describe how things should go.

The Fine-tuned universe version of the Anthropic Principle is about the extraordinary theoretical fact that the least change of the value that the fundamental physical constants have in the universe we are in would have developed a relatively trivial universe incapable of producing life.

Under the quantum cosmological assumption, this version of the Principle can be formalized as follows.

We should introduce two commuting observables of the same size: the value of the fundamental constants and the “value of life”; the latter should assume in particular the two values: “existence of sentient life” and “absence of life”.

Of course, at the beginning of time, these two observables must be unrelated: no matter the value of the fundamental constant, the value of life is “absence of life”. At the time of today, the unitary evolution of the universe toward the value of life has built up one-to-one quantum correlation between the value of the fundamental constants since the beginning of time and the value of life, of course, without ever changing the former value.

We can therefore say that the evolution of the universe toward the value of life can occur with quantum speedup and thus be teleological in character; the “can occur” is in the sense that there is certainly the room for its occurring with quantum speedup. Of course, whether the quantum speedup is achieved depends on the character of the unitary evolution. For what was said about quantum random search algorithms, we might think that, if the search for the value of life by the unitary evolution of the universe is random, it should occur with a quantum speedup.

In the universe we are in, the evolution of the universe toward the value of life becomes evolution of the universe toward life; of course, all the above also applies to the latter evolution. We shall see that, as a consequence, the evolution of life on Earth, in principle the trace over all the rest of the evolution of the universe toward life, would inherit the possible teleological character of the evolution of the universe toward life. For consistency, the same would apply to the evolution of species.

Note that the fact that a teleological evolution is also an evolution endowed with a quantum speedup is a novelty for the teleological notion. It could contribute to explaining steps of natural evolutions that are too rapid to be explained by the progressive character of Darwinian evolutions.

Summing up, while the teleological character of quantum algorithms, as we shall see, should be certain, the fact that—under the quantum cosmological assumption—it would extend to the evolutions of the living should be considered, at most, as plausible as the quantum cosmological assumption. Anyway, all this should be quite different from the present widespread belief that the notion of a teleological evolution of the living is an absurdity. At least, it should cast a doubt on this position of science.

Let us eventually note that, so far, holism and finalism have been the enemy of the reductionist and mechanistic vision of science. In the present theory of the quantum computational speedup, they would acquire a physical basis. Of course, this would not touch on the immense practical value of the mechanistic and reductionist view. That the description of the evolution of any system is the trace over all the rest of the quantum description of the evolution of the universe holds only in principle: obviously, in practice, we cannot have such descriptions. However, this contrast between the quantum and the classical view might pinpoint a direction of the research. At a toy model, or theoretical, level, it could be interesting to study how this “tracing over all the rest” conciliates a teleological character of the evolution of a system (standing for the evolution of life on Earth) in the longer term with a classical character in the shorter term. This might even be a further perspective under which to study the possibility of unifying quantum and classical mechanics.

2
Time-symmetric explanation of the quantum speedup

We provide a description of quantum algorithms and their quantum speedups open to the coexistence of causality and retrocausality. Since this is a way of thinking quantum computation that is radically different from the usual one, we provide it first at a conceptual level. All formalizations are postponed in the
Appendix
. The section is divided into subsections corresponding to the various arguments.

Let us first acknowledge the fact that that of the quantum computational speedup has been one of the fundamental discoveries of the past century. Together with that of quantum communication, it has created an entirely new branch of physics. However, for the sake of clarity, we will be completely frank in criticizing what we think are the shortcomings of the current way of viewing quantum computation.

2.1
On the explanation of the quantum computational speedup

We repeat here for convenience the advanced knowledge rule.

In quantum algorithms, it is as if the problem-solver always knew in advance, before beginning her problem-solving action, any of the possible halves of the information that specifies the solution of the problem she will produce and measure in the future and could use this knowledge to produce the solution with fewer computation steps. The number of computation steps needed in the optimal quantum case is that needed in the optimal logical case benefiting from the advanced knowledge in question.

Of course, this rule would offer a unified quantitative explanation of the quantum speedups that is still lacking in mainstream quantum information. Let us discuss this point. To the best of our knowledge, the absence of a unified quantitative explanation has never been acknowledged by the scientific community.

People currently say that there is an explanation of the quantum speedup; it would be that of
quantum parallel computation
, the fact that many computations are performed at the same time in quantum superposition. Indeed, this captures the essential character of quantum computation, but, being a qualitative explanation of the quantum speedup, which is a quantitative feature, cannot be considered its explanation. By the way, what distinguishes science from opinion is, in particular, its quantitative character. The fact that the most popular explanation of the quantum speedup, a quantitative feature, is qualitative in character, would seem to be rather unscientific. The only justification we can see is that this is a way of removing the fact that, forty years after its discovery—an explanation of the quantum speedup is still missing. This is naturally our opinion. However, as a matter of fact, outside our (unconventional) retrocasusal approach to the quantum speedup, in the literature, there is no trace whatsoever of any search for a unifying-quantitative explanation of it.

Of course, as already noted, if the only way of providing the unified quantitative explanation in question is by resorting to retrocausality and finalism, the de facto rejection of these notions by the scientific community might have played a role.

Neither can be considered an explanation the fact that each quantum algorithm naturally explains its quantum speedup. In fact, it would be an explanation completely different for different quantum algorithms. It would be like having different explanations for different throws of a stone without knowing that they all describe a parabola. Obviously, unification is a fundamental objective of any description of physical phenomena.

Summing up, in our judgment, for what concerns the current mainstream explanation of the quantum speedup, it is the case of saying that the emperor is naked.

Of course, the advanced knowledge rule would exactly be that unifying-quantitative explanation. However, to date, it has had a twofold problem: first, although shown to apply to all the major quantum algorithms, it has not been demonstrated; second, it goes against the de facto ban on retrocausality. In this work, we should have demonstrated it, moreover, in a surprisingly simple way. As a matter of fact, we shall be dealing with two independent demonstrations, which is not to be despised because of the destabilizing character of the rule.

Now, we should note that this quantum description of the quantum algorithm is with respect to the customary observer external to the quantum process. To present ends, we should relativize it with respect to the problem-solver who, for the fact of computing the solution of the problem, is part of the process. We can go now to the first demonstration of the advanced knowledge rule. It simply consists in describing in a classical logic way what the quantum algorithm does—computing the solution of the problem with a number of computation steps that is less than the minimum possible in the classical case.

By the way, involving only the measurements of commuting observables (the problem-setting and the solution of the problem) quantum algorithms, and what they do, should be subject to classical logic.

To present ends, we need a quantum algorithm that certainly gives a quantum speedup with respect to what is classically possible. Now, most quantum algorithms yield a quantum speedup with respect to their best classical counterpart, but whether the latter is the best possible is not known. Excluding elementary one-computation-step quantum algorithms whose dimensions do not scale up, we find our choice limited to Grover’s quantum search algorithm

Besides yielding a well determined quantum speedup with respect to what is classically possible, it has the following convenient features: (i) It is a most universal quantum algorithm that can solve any NP problem with a quadratic quantum speedup; more than a single quantum algorithm, it is an entire test bed on which to test the present theory of the quantum speedup. (iv) It is optimal in character; this, of course, constitutes an important reference. By the way, note that the notion of searching for something and finding it is most universal and that this universality is completely represented by Grover algorithm.

We show that the classical logic description of the quantum speedup delivered by Grover algorithm very directly implies the advanced knowledge rule. We begin with the simplest instance of Grover algorithm. As we shall soon see, everything immediately generalizes first to the full fledged Grover algorithm and then to most general forms of quantum correlation (in quantum algorithms, that between the problem-setting and the solution of the problem).

The problem to be solved is as follows. Bob, the problem-setter, hides a ball in a chest of four drawers. Alice, the problem-solver, is to locate the ball by opening drawers. In the worst classical case, she has to open three drawers. If the ball is only in the third drawer opened, she has solved the problem. If not, it must be in the only drawer not yet opened, and she has solved the problem of locating the ball as well.

Instead, Grover algorithm (its simplest instance) always solves the problem by opening just one drawer, in a quantum superposition of the four possible drawer numbers. Obviously, always solving the problem by opening just one drawer would be impossible in the classical case: indeed, we are dealing with a quantum computational speedup.

There is a classical logic equivalent to the fact that the quantum algorithm always solves the problem by opening a single drawer. By the way, since the fact that a single drawer is opened is logically true in itself, we can forget the specification that this is done for a quantum superposition of the four drawer numbers.

The logical equivalence is as follows. Logically, it is as if the problem-solver always knew in advance, before beginning her problem-solving action, that the ball is in a given pair of drawers (one of which, of course, the one with the ball) and took advantage of this knowledge to solve the problem by opening just one of them. In fact, if the ball is in the opened drawer, she has solved the problem; if not, it must be in the other drawer and she has solved the problem of locating the ball as well, in either case having opened just one drawer. This also means knowing in advance half of the information that specifies the solution of the problem that will be produced and measured in the future.

Let us open a parentheses to observe that we are not talking about the quantum algorithm as it were a classical algorithm. In other words, here, classical logic should not be confused with classical physics, which might be a temptation in the present case.

Always knowing in advance that the ball is in a given pair of drawers, in a mutually exclusive way, one of the possible pairs that host the drawer with the ball, is logically necessary to always solve the problem by opening just one drawer. Then the question becomes: from where does Alice, before beginning her problem-solving action, always get that information about the solution? Since, for her, the only possible source of it is the solution she will produce and measure in the future, the only possible answer is that the information in question retrocausally comes to her from her future measurement of the solution. This is formalized in Section (3) of the
Appendix
. However, before looking at it, one should read the description of the time-symmetrization of the quantum algorithm that we shall provide in a moment.

Note that we are dealing with a causal loop. Alice knows in advance one of the possible halves of the information about the solution she will produce and measure in the future (that the ball is in two rather than four drawers) and can use this knowledge to produce the solution by opening just one drawer. There is a causal loop for each way of taking half of the information about the solution. Therefore, we can say that this quantum speedup is logically explained by the mutually exclusive
or
of the causal loops we are dealing with. Of course, this is the advanced knowledge rule for the present instance of Grover algorithm, logically implied by the sheer existence of the quantum speedup. In summary, the classical logic description of the quantum speedup delivered by (the simplest instance of) Grover algorithm implies the advanced knowledge rule.

Also in this case, using the advanced knowledge of half of the information about the solution in an optimal logical way yields the optimal quantum speedup of Grover algorithm. Here we do not care about the fact that most NP problems are classically solved with a number of computation steps exponential in their size, so that a quadratic quantum speedup can be seen as a little advantage. Of course, for the present ends, we are just interested in having a quantum speedup with respect to what is classically possible.

2.3
Completing the ordinary quantum description of the quantum algorithms by time-symmetrizing it

The above subsection also says that the ordinary quantum description of quantum algorithms, which obviously does not describe the advanced knowledge rule, is incomplete. Of course, to bring it in line with their classical logic description, it should be completed. It turns out that this is simply done by time-symmetrizing it.

We describe this operation. For the sake of simplicity, we consider the case that the solution is determined with certainty and is an invertible function of the problem-setting—exactly as in Grover algorithm. We should evenly share between the initial measurement of the problem-setting and the final measurement of the solution the selection of the information that specifies both; of course, in the ordinary quantum description of the quantum algorithm, all the information is selected by the initial measurement. Naturally, there is a plurality of possible ways of evenly sharing: we should take their quantum superposition.

In each time-symmetrization instance, the selection performed by the initial measurement propagates forward in time by the unitary computation of the solution until it becomes the state immediately before the final measurement. Time-symmetrically, that performed by the final measurement propagates backward in time by the inverse of the unitary computation of the solution until replacing the previous outcome of the initial measurement. This backward in time propagation, which inherits both selections, is an instance of the time-symmetrized quantum algorithm. As we can see in Section (3) of the
Appendix
, in the initial measurement outcome of the time-symmetrized instance of the quantum algorithm it is as if the problem-solver knew in advance one of the possible halves of the information about the solution she will produce and measure in the future; she can then use this knowledge to compute the solution with fewer computation steps. This is the physical correspondent of one of the logical causal loops whose mutually exclusive
or
is logically implied by the advanced knowledge rule.

Eventually, we should take the quantum superposition of all the time-symmetrization instances. The time-symmetrized quantum description of the quantum algorithm (i.e. its completed quantum description) becomes the quantum superposition of the causal loops whose mutually exclusive
or
is logically implied by the quantum speedup: So completed, also the quantum description of the quantum algorithm describes the advanced knowledge rule described by its classical logic description—it describes its physical counterpart.

2.4
Second demonstration of the advanced knowledge rule

The second demonstration of the advanced knowledge rule simply comes from the observation that the time-symmetrization of the ordinary quantum description of the quantum algorithm, which provides the (physical counterpart of the) advanced knowledge rule, is mandatory also for another reason, as follows.

In the ordinary quantum description of the quantum algorithm, of course, the unitary computation of the solution never changes the corresponding problem-setting. Therefore, one should be free to postpone the initial measurement of the problem-setting along the subsequent unitary computation of the solution until it is simultaneous with the final measurement of the solution. In this case, we find ourselves with the simultaneous measurements of two maximally entangled observables (the problem-setting and the solution, one an invertible function of the other), and the time-symmetrization we are dealing with must already be in place to represent the perfect (atemporal) symmetry between these two measurements. Of course, there would be no reason why a measurement selected more information than the other. As we have just seen, this time symmetrization generates the quantum superposition of the causal loops whose mutually exclusive
or
is logically implied by the sheer existence of the quantum speedup—the physical counterpart of the advanced knowledge rule. This is indeed the second demonstration of the advanced knowledge rule.

2.5
Origin of the retrocausality

A quantum algorithm is a relatively complex process. A natural question is whether the retrocausality implicit in the classical logic description of its overall result, i.e. the quantum speedup, derives from some more elementary part of it. Of course, in its ordinary quantum description, the unitary computation of the solution is an alternation of generations of quantum state superpositions and quantum interferences. Since the latter have a classical analog, the essentially quantum generation of a quantum state superposition is the suspect.

Let us first provide a very intuitive exposition of the fact that the classical logic description of a state superposition necessarily involves the coexistence of causality and retrocausality. We resort to a prescientific notion of state superposition. It is the legend of the monk seen by his disciples on the two banks of a river at the same time: we should provide a classical logic description of this. Being on one and the other bank is possible in two different times but, at the same time, from the ordinary classical logic standpoint, they should be two mutually exclusive possibilities. Until now, being on the two banks at the same time would seem to be a logical impossibility. However, let us note that it would be impossible in a classical logic description closed to retrocausality. Opening classical logic to the coexistence of causality and retrocausality completely changes the situation

2

By the way, classical logic is neutral for what concerns the notion of causality. Associating it with a causality that always goes in the same time-direction, as customary, must be consequent to the de facto no-retrocausality precept. Classical logic should also go along with the coexistence of causality and retrocausality.
. Indeed, the monk could have gone to one of the two banks along a certain path, then (

This causality zigzag, of course, makes its being on the two banks at the same time logically possible.

Let us now bring the above classical logic description of a state superposition to a quantum state superposition. Think of a photon going in quantum superposition through the two branches of a Mach-Zehnder interferometer. The two branches should be tuned in such a way that the photon always ends up in the same detector, so that we have a (of course, time-reversible) unitary evolution that goes from before the photon enters the interferometer to immediately before its detection.

The classical logic description of this can be as follows. In entering the interferometer, the photon takes, say, the upper branch until it reaches the state immediately before detection (by the detector in question). Of course, this also provides the phase of the photon along the path followed. Then (in causal order), the photon goes backward in time undoing the path just followed until before entering the interferometer. Then, it goes forward in time again taking the lower branch until reaching the detector in question and, this time, being detected. The zigzag path followed in this way by the photon through the interferometer, with the associated photon phases, of course, describes in a classical logic way the fact that the photon goes through the interferometer in a quantum superposition of the upper and lower branches, eventually ending up in that detector. It should be clear that, also in this case, the involvement of retrocausality in the classical logic description is necessary.

We should think that the classical logic description of the sequence of generations of quantum state superpositions and interferences that constitutes the unitary computation of the solution, in the end, simplifies into a description that implies the advanced knowledge rule.

Summarizing, at the root of the logical form of retrocausality we are dealing with there should be the fundamental notion of quantum state superposition, the fact that the classical logic description of its generation necessarily involves retrocausality. By the way, note that this is not an idle speculation as it might even seem. Indeed, we are dealing with a form of retrocausality with huge consequences, such as providing a physical basis to the notion of teleological evolution.

2.6
Generalization

In the second demonstration of the advanced knowledge rule, we have shown that a unitary evolution between two one-to-one correlated measurement outcomes that never changes the eigenvalue selected by the initial measurement is incomplete and must be completed by time-symmetrizing it. Note that we never needed to look inside the unitary evolution, for example
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{1,2}
of Tables I through IV of the
Appendix
. Thus, the need for this time-symmetrization does not only hold for quantum algorithms; there is the following generalization:

The ordinary quantum description of any unitary evolution between two one-to-one correlated measurement outcomes that does not change the eigenvalue selected by the initial measurement is incomplete and is completed by time-symmetrizing it.

Let us call it
the quantum correlation time-symmetrization rule
.

The case where the one-to-one correlation is built up by the unitary evolution characterizes the quantum search algorithm, where this building up occurs with quadratic quantum speedup. However, the possibility of occurring with quadratic quantum speedup holds for any unitary evolution that responds to the rule in question and builds up one-to-one quantum correlation between two initially unrelated observables. Indeed, that this process can occur with quadratic quantum speedup is what the time-symmetrized quantum description of the quantum process says—see the
Appendix
. Of course, in the case where we are not dealing with a quantum algorithm, the notion of advanced knowledge of one of the possible halves of the information about the solution must be replaced by the equivalent notion of quadratic reduction of the dimension of the Hilbert space in which the search for that one-to-one correlation takes place, from
N
to
\sqrt{N}
.

The case in which the one-to-one quantum correlation is not built up by the unitary evolution because it preexists it (more precisely, what would preexist is the maximum entanglement between the observables of the two measurements) is, for example, that of quantum nonlocality. Here, we have a unitary evolution that spatially separates the subsystems of two already maximally entangled observables. With the two measurements performed in the same basis and the spatial separation process preceding the first measurement, this responds to the quantum correlation time-symmetrization rule.

As shown in
[
5
]
, the time-symmetrization of the ordinary quantum description of this form of quantum nonlocality yields a quantum superposition of causal loops that locally explain it and play the role of the hidden variables envisaged by Einstein et al
[
15
]
. Indeed, these causal loops locally go from one to the other measurement outcome via the time the two subsystems were not yet space separate, where they locally tell the subsystem of the not yet measured observable the state to assume in view of the future measurement. Note that this is exactly what Einstein et al required from their hidden variables. That was, of course, without resorting to retrocausality, but under a deterministic view of the measurement process equivalent in this case.

In summary, this application of the quantum correlation time-symmetrization rule both demonstrates the incompleteness of the ordinary quantum description of quantum nonlocality and provides the hidden variables that complete it. Of course, this also means that the incompleteness we are presently ascribing to the ordinary quantum description of quantum algorithms is the same that Einstein and others ascribed to the quantum description of quantum nonlocality.

By the way, the fact that the present explanation of the quantum speedup also demonstrates the incompleteness of the quantum description highlighted by Einstein et al might be destabilizing for the following reason: After highlighting for the first time quantum nonlocality and inspiring fundamental works like Bell’s theorem
[
16
]
and Ekert’s quantum cryptography based on it
[
17
]
, their argument had been dismissed by the physicists’ community because of the alleged absence of a satisfactory way of completing the quantum description by means of local hidden variables.

By the way, if the only possible hidden variables are retrocausal in character as the ones we are dealing with, this dismissal can be ascribed to the already mentioned de facto rejection of retrocausality and finalism (the form it has in philosophy, different from the present trivial form).

2.7
About the teleological character of the evolutions of the living

We have already seen in the
Introduction
that, under the quantum cosmological assumption and for the Fine-tuned Universe version of the Anthropic Principle, the physical basis of the teleological character of quantum algorithms could also apply to the evolution of the universe toward the value of life. This section expands on some facts that, for compactness, were just mentioned, or omitted, in the
Introduction

Let us show that the possible teleological character of the evolution of the universe toward life must be inherited by the evolution of life on Earth. In principle, the latter evolution can be derived from that of the universe toward life by tracing out all the rest. Conversely, we can speak of an image of the evolution of life on Earth in the evolution of the universe toward life. Then, first, evolution times must be the same for the evolution of life on Earth and its image in the evolution of the universe. Second, the amount of physical resources used by the evolution of life on Earth cannot exceed the amount of physical resources used by its image in the evolution of the universe. In fact, the latter resources comprise the former.

In summary, under the quantum cosmological assumption, the evolution of life on Earth could occur with quantum speedup (in the sense that there would be the room for this) and consequently be teleological in character; it might actually occur with quantum speedup if the search for life by the evolution of the universe toward life were random. Then, just for consistency, the evolutions of species would also be the same.

Not to hide behind a finger, let us exemplify what a teleological character of the evolution of life would imply; of course, it will be very destabilizing with respect to the present way of viewing both the evolutions of the living and physical reality.

Let us begin with the evolution of species. It is known that, for many millions of years, the ancestors of the birds were feathered dinosaurs who did not fly. Very light, strong but flexible, cave and air-proof, from the fossils found, even at that time feathers were ideal for flight, very close to those of the future birds. So, why anticipate something designed for flight many millions of years in advance?

The usual explanation, of course, is causal. First, an ancestor of feathers appeared by random DNA mutation, then evolved as a good thermal insulator and perhaps to speed escape from predators; only eventually feathers turned out to be what was needed to conquer the air.

All this could remain true in the teleological view with just one, but very significant, addition: Those random mutations that would have led in the future to the conquest of the air would have more likely occurred. In our possibly biased opinion, this mutual causality explanation of the anticipation in question would be much more convincing than the customary causal one. See our preprint

Another possible consequence is for the fact that, according to the present theory, a teleological evolution is an evolution endowed with quantum speedup. This could contribute to explaining why some steps of the evolutions of the living have been so rapid to defy Darwinian gradualism, see

Let us also recall what, here, is a very interesting observation of the French biologist Rémy Chauvin

As is well known, this biologist merged important contributions to mainstream science with unconventional positions much criticized by colleagues. In addition to highlighting the possibility that some evolutionary changes are teleological in character, another unconventional position was his public interest in parapsychology (he wrote books on the subject). Note the parallel with the present situation. Let us consider the advance knowledge, on the part of the problem-solver, of any of the possible halves of the information about the solution of the problem she will read in the future. Besides being a completely technical notion at the basis of the present teleological notion (the problem-solver is a logical agent), under the quantum cosmological assumption it could well be the physical basis of the parapsychological notion of precognition.

Of course, being under the quantum cosmological assumption, all the above is hypothetical. This is to decouple it from the teleological character of quantum algorithms, which we would consider certain.

Note that a teleological character of the evolutions of the living on the Earth could be further explored by investigating, either theoretically or by means of toy models, the character of the operation of “tracing out all the rest”. We should consider the unitary quantum evolution of a system endowed with quantum speedup, standing for the evolution of the universe toward life, and derive from it the almost autonomous evolution of a subsystem of it, standing for the evolution of life on Earth, by tracing out all the rest. We should look for a way of obtaining an evolution of the subsystem that is teleological (thus quantum) in the longer term and (about) classical in the shorter term. If this were possible, it would go in favor of the quantum cosmological reading of the Anthropic Principle we are dealing with. By the way, this could also be another perspective under which to address the problem of unifying quantum and classical theory.

Another question is how to describe a macroscopic evolution of life endowed with quantum speedup. Of course, classical physics would turn out to be unable to describe not only the processes of the microscopic world but also the teleological evolutions of the macroscopic world.

We quote a precedent in the quantum description of macroscopic natural evolutions. It is about the asymptotic behavior of predator species competing for the same prey

In our case, the canonical way of describing the evolution of life on Earth would be to obtain it from the description of the evolution of the universe toward life by tracing-out all the rest. Since this obviously holds only in principle, we should also model the macroscopic teleological evolutions of species endowed with quantum speedup in a quantum way. In other words, we should accept quantum superposition, entanglement, and correlation between the observables of macroscopic objects.

This might even be a good approximation, since, in the universe we are in, the evolution of life on Earth in its time lapse should have been almost autonomous from the rest of the unitary quantum evolution of the universe toward life. Indeed, if it were completely autonomous, of course, it would be a unitary quantum evolution itself.

4
Conclusions

The present work goes against a long-standing de facto no-retrocausality tradition of physics. More specifically, since causality could well go backward in time along a unitary quantum evolution that is both objectively and ideally time-reversible, we should say that, in the quantum world, the scientific tradition amounts to asserting the mutual exclusivity between the two possible time directions of causality.

Let us first address the reliability of the work, a particular issue because of its unconventionality.

That the classical logic description of the quantum speedup must resort to retrocausality should be obvious. Of course, it should be obvious the fact that always locating a ball hidden in one of four drawers by opening a single drawer is logically equivalent to always knowing in advance that the ball is in a pair of drawers. To the problem-solver, the only possible source of this information is the solution of the problem she will produce and measure in the future: hence retrocausality. We have seen how this immediately generalizes to the full fledged Grover algorithm and a most general form of quantum correlation.

At this point, we should address the legitimacy of applying classical logic to what the quantum algorithm does. Let us recall our two arguments in favor of this choice.

The first is that, only involving the measurements of commuting observables (the problem-setting and the solution of the problem), quantum algorithms (and what they do) should be subject to classical logic.

The other is that all the results obtained under the assumption that quantum algorithms can be described by classical logic are also obtained by time-symmetrizing their ordinary quantum description; this, in turn, is mandatory to satisfy the atemporal symmetry between the simultaneous measurements of two maximally entangled observables implicit in the ordinary quantum description of quantum algorithms.

We would think that the present retrocausal explanation of the quantum speedup is reliable enough to deserve a discussion despite its destabilizing consequences.

The flip side of the destabilizing character of the work is its innovative character. Let us distinguish between what should be certain and what should be plausible. What should be certain: (i) The work provides a unified quantitative explanation of the quantum speedups that is still missing from mainstream quantum mechanics. (ii) It provides a theoretical confirmation of the incompleteness of the quantum description of quantum nonlocality sustained by Einstein and others, also providing the local hidden variables envisaged by those authors to complete it. (iii) It highlights the teleological character of quantum algorithms; given the negative view that the teleological notion has in today’s science and the scope of this notion, this should be the most important result. What should be plausible: It shows that, under a quantum cosmological reading of the Fine-tuned Universe version of the Anthropic Principle, the physical basis of the teleological character of quantum algorithms applies as well to the evolutions of the living.

In conclusion, the present work should show that there is another possible view of physical reality out there, outside the current view. We note that this might not only be a curiosity. A revamping of the abandoned notion of the relation between evolution and functional structure of the living might have a profound impact on today’s evolutionary biology and biology.
