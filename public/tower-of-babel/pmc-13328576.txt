Multi-stage robust and multi-role integrated allocation of emergency resources for hazard-induced disasters
Authors: Xin Wu, Kai Zou, Wenjie Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The allocation of emergency resources is a lifeline in hazard-induced disaster relief, directly determining survival rates, rescue efficiency, and the restoration of social order. Existing allocation models face two limitations: (1) insufficient cross-role integration of funds and resource flows, and (2) limited application of multi-stage robust optimization. To address these issues, this study proposes a tripartite multi-role integrated scheduling framework that jointly considers the objectives and constraints of decision-making (fund allocation), execution (procurement and transportation), and demand roles (affected populations) to optimize economic cost, life-saving utility, and supply–demand balance. We adopt multi-stage adaptive robust optimization to reformulate the allocation model into a feedback framework integrating pre-disaster planning with post-disaster periodic adjustments under demand, procurement, and transportation uncertainties. For model tractability and compatibility with off-the-shelf solvers, we develop a linear reformulation technique that converts the nonlinear robust model into a linear program using duality theory and semi-infinite constraint handling. A case study based on an earthquake scenario shows that multi-stage adjustment and multi-role integration improve allocation optimality, stability, and robustness under multiple uncertainty sources. Sensitivity analyses of key parameters, such as time discretization and historical dependency depth, provide practical guidance for model deployment. Compared with deterministic, static robust, and rolling-horizon methods, the proposed approach shows improvements in allocation safety and overall rescue performance.

Introduction

Accelerating climate change and urbanization have led to increasingly frequent hazard-induced disasters (e.g., earthquakes, floods, typhoons) and public emergencies (e.g., pandemics, safety incidents)1. The IPCC AR6 report indicates that the probability of disasters such as heatwaves, torrential rains, and floods has surged over fivefold compared to pre-industrial levels2. In Q1 2025 alone, direct economic losses from hazard-induced disasters in China reached ¥10.16 billion, with compound disasters (e.g., earthquake-triggered landslides, typhoons compounded by flooding) accounting for over 40%3. These events pose severe threats to socioeconomic stability and public safety. Importantly, disaster losses stem not only from the events themselves but also from inefficient emergency response—delayed decisions or suboptimal resource allocation significantly reduce rescue effectiveness, exacerbating human suffering and economic damage. During the post-disaster “golden 72-h window,” survival rates decline precipitously—from ~ 90% within the first 24 h to 20–30% after 72 h3. This imposes high demands on the timeliness, coordination, and robustness of emergency resource allocation. Effective allocation of emergency resources is essential for minimizing casualties, mitigating property losses, and alleviating human suffering in the aftermath of disasters4.

Disaster management is commonly conceptualized through a four-phase cycle—mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery—a framework established in operations research for structuring disaster operations5. The response phase, particularly the immediate aftermath, demands rapid and coordinated action to save lives and alleviate suffering, and emergency resource allocation is its core operational challenge6. Consequently, optimizing this allocation process has become a major focus within humanitarian logistics, leading to the development of various optimization models for facility location, relief distribution, and casualty transportation, as reviewed in the literature7. The effective and equitable distribution of limited resources directly determines survival rates and the extent of socioeconomic losses6,8,9. Researchers continue to refine these models, applying OR methodologies to enhance the speed, efficiency, and fairness of emergency responses under uncertainty10,11.

Effective post-disaster response depends on the coordinated actions of the decision role (e.g., government bodies allocating funds), the execution role (e.g., logistics units handling procurement and transportation), and the demand role (the affected population)12. While substantial research has focused on optimizing individual segments of this chain—such as fund appropriation models13, supplier selection and procurement models under contracts4,14, and vehicle routing for distribution15,16—these efforts often operate in silos. There is a pronounced lack of models that explicitly coordinate the financial flow (budget al.location), the physical resource flow (procurement, inventory, and transportation), and the information flow (dynamic demand feedback) among all roles. This disconnection between financially constrained sourcing, physically constrained logistics, and dynamically evolving needs creates bottlenecks and hinders efficient information feedback and resource handover. It ultimately leads to suboptimal overall system performance, including fund misallocation, inventory imbalances, and unmet critical demand17–20. An integrated framework that coordinates these processes is therefore essential to bridge the current coordination gap.

Uncertainty is inherent in emergency logistics, manifesting in fluctuating demands, unreliable supply procurement, disrupted transportation networks, and evolving disaster dynamics4,17–19. To address this challenge, a spectrum of optimization methods under uncertainty has been developed and applied. Stochastic Programming (SP) employs probability distributions to model uncertainties, optimizing expected performance. It has been used for integrated pre- and post-disaster management of supplies, coordinating inventory pre-positioning with post-disaster procurement21,22. However, SP’s reliance on accurate distribution data is a key limitation in disasters where historical data is scarce or non-representative. Traditional (Static) Robust Optimization (RO) circumvents the need for distributional knowledge by optimizing against the worst-case realization within a bounded uncertainty set, thus providing guaranteed feasibility. It has been applied to facility location, vehicle routing, and resource allocation under demand uncertainty13,23,24. While ensuring hard safety, this approach is often criticized for excessive conservatism, as the resulting solutions may perform poorly under average or likely scenarios, leading to unnecessarily high costs and inefficient resource utilization—a critical drawback when economic resources are strained24. Compared with SP and RO, Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) represents a middle ground, protecting against a family of distributions (an ambiguity set) rather than a single distribution or all point-wise realizations, thereby balancing robustness and conservatism. DRO models have been formulated for disaster relief logistics with option contracts under demand ambiguity14. To improve adaptability to uncertainty, Two-Stage RO (2 S-RO) and Two-Stage DRO (2 S-DRO) explicitly separate decisions into a “here-and-now” first stage (e.g., pre-positioning) and a “wait-and-see” second stage (e.g., post-disaster distribution), allowing recourse actions. They have been widely adopted for emergency facility location, inventory planning, and post-disaster scheduling13,14,17,24. Despite these advancements, two-stage models depict only a single adaptation point and are less effective in capturing the continuous feedback cycle required over multiple periods (e.g., daily adjustments over 72 h), where later decisions benefit from information revealed earlier. This limitation can lead to suboptimal or infeasible schedules across an extended time horizon.

The paradigm of Multi-Stage Adaptive Robust Optimization (MSARO), with its ability to model sequential decision-dependent recourse under uncertainty, has been successfully applied and increasingly adopted across various fields facing similar dynamic challenges. In energy management, MSARO frameworks have been developed for the adaptive scheduling of industrial oxygen systems and the coordinated expansion planning of electric vehicle charging networks, balancing operational safety with economic efficiency under demand uncertainty25,26. Within supply chain and logistics management, it has been applied to integrated fleet sizing and vehicle routing problems, providing robust and adaptable policies for closed-loop systems27. In the financial sector, multi-stage robust and distributionally robust models are employed for dynamic portfolio selection to manage risk over time28. Furthermore, MSARO principles are advancing in transportation and unmanned systems, such as in robust trajectory planning for UAVs29. These diverse applications illustrate the methodological advantages of MSARO in delivering solutions that are not only robust to worst-case scenarios but also capable of dynamically adjusting to revealed information, thereby improving average-case performance and system adaptability30,31. In contrast, the adoption of MSARO in post-disaster emergency logistics remains limited, representing a salient research gap. Current optimization models for multi-period resource allocation often lack the explicit feedback loop and adjustable decision rules that are hallmarks of MSARO, which would allow funding, procurement, and transportation plans to be systematically revised based on the partial resolution of uncertainty in prior periods.

Building upon the critical analysis above, two intertwined research gaps can be identified. First, there is a lack of a holistic decision-making framework that integrates financial allocation (decision role), physical resource procurement and transportation (execution role), and demand fulfillment (demand role) into a unified and synchronized process. Second, despite its demonstrated effectiveness in managing multi-period sequential decisions under uncertainty in other fields, the methodological power of MSARO remains underexplored within the domain of post-disaster emergency resource allocation. To bridge these gaps, we propose a tripartite “Decision-Execution-Demand” integration model for emergency resource allocation, integrating MSARO and linearization techniques to enhance adaptability, solvability, and applicability. Problem description

Main roles in resource allocation

According to the Emergency Response Law of the People’s Republic of China32, the national emergency management system emphasizes unified leadership, comprehensive coordination, hierarchical responsibility, and local management. The allocation of emergency resources represents a critical challenge in the management of hazard-induced disaster emergencies20. Post-disaster emergency resource allocation involves multiple roles working in coordination to facilitate disaster relief efforts33. Government agencies at all levels serve as the primary decision role, providing unified leadership for disaster response. They are responsible for formulating resource allocation plans, disbursing emergency relief funds, and ensuring the initiation of resource deployment. Their primary objective is to meet disaster relief needs while minimizing economic costs. Procurement and supply departments serve as the intermediate execution role, responsible for procuring and transporting emergency resources at designated local transfer stations (TS). Their tasks are to ensure the timely preparation and accurate delivery of resources to the demand role, maximizing life-saving utility. At the disaster areas (DA), survivors at affected sites constitute the demand role. They require prompt access to emergency resources to maximize survival chances and aim to minimize the gap between resource supply and demand.

Integrated emergency resource allocation

The first 72 h after a disaster are regarded as the “golden window” for emergency rescue. During this period, the decision, execution, and demand roles must collaborate closely to counteract the impacts of hazard-induced disasters, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Prior to or immediately after a hazard-induced disaster, the decision role rapidly assesses the impact and estimates the resource demand of affected populations (such as first-aid kits, instant food, drinking water, etc.). They issue allocation plans and disburse emergency funds to the execution role, dynamically adjusting funding strategies based on real-time feedback regarding resource procurement and transportation. The execution role implements the allocation plan by procuring, temporarily storing, and transporting emergency resources to the demand role, adjusting procurement and transportation strategies in response to actual demands reported by the disaster areas. The demand role—survivors at the disaster areas—cooperate with emergency response teams by reporting their actual demands and waiting orderly for rescue and relief.

It should be noted that while real-world post-disaster response involves multiple independent roles (e.g., government departments, logistics companies, affected communities), each with potentially differing information and incentives, the model presented in this paper adopts a centralized optimization perspective. This perspective synthesizes the core objectives and operational constraints of these distinct roles into a coordinated decision-making framework. This approach allows us to focus on deriving a theoretical benchmark for resource flows under uncertainty.

Multi-stage robust resource allocation model

The deterministic allocation model described above relies on predefined parameters. However, these parameters are difficult to estimate accurately before or during a disaster, which makes the model’s solution vulnerable to uncertainty and potentially infeasible. To adapt to uncertainty, we design a multi-stage robust allocation strategy for emergency resources, integrating pre-disaster long-term planning with post-disaster periodic feedback adjustments, as illustrated in Fig. 3. During the pre-disaster or initial phase, based on parameter estimates from the execution role, the decision role formulates a resource allocation plan for the post-disaster “golden 72-hour window”. During the post-disaster adjustment phase, as each period elapses, demand uncertainties and disaster dynamics are progressively revealed. At the start of each period, the decision and execution roles adjust the current allocation plan using feedback-based decision rules to address newly revealed uncertainties. This framework is formalized using the multi-stage adaptive robust optimization (MSARO) method, involving periodic uncertainty sets and recourse decision rules.

Multi-period uncertainty sets

To reflect uncertainties in disaster response, we consider three sources affecting resource allocation: uncertain resource demand, procurement, and transportation. Actual demands at disaster areas are initially uncertain due to limited post-disaster information. Procurement plans may not be fully executed because of social constraints or disaster impacts at transfer stations. In addition, damaged infrastructure may disrupt planned deliveries from transfer stations to disaster areas.

Linear reformulation of the robust allocation model

The LP reformulation is exact for the ADR-based MSARO formulation and therefore does not introduce additional approximation errors beyond the affine decision rule approximation. Consequently, the robustness and optimality properties of the ADR-based model are preserved.

Experiment analysis and discussion

Case study description

Earthquakes are hazard-induced disasters that threaten human lives and property. This case study considers a major earthquake event causing surface ruptures, landslides, and debris flows that damaged transportation infrastructure. After the disaster, local authorities established an emergency command center as the decision role to coordinate response efforts, including fund allocation. Two municipal transfer stations (TS#1–2) serve as the execution role. The demand role consists of two temporary shelters located in severely affected disaster areas (DA#1–2). Figure 5 shows the geographical distribution and resource flow directions. Table 1 lists the affected populations and transportation distances35. This case study is designed as an illustrative example to examine the behavior of the proposed framework under a controlled post-earthquake setting rather than as a comprehensive empirical validation across multiple disaster scenarios or scales.

Model execution and evaluation metrics.

Parameters are set as = 1 and T = 3, dividing the 72-hour rescue window into three daily periods. Dynamic allocation strategies are updated based on prior-day uncertainty feedback. Monte Carlo simulation generates 1,000 uncertainty samples for testing. For each sample, model-derived allocation strategies are executed to evaluate stability and adaptability. The model is implemented in Python 3.8 using Gurobipy (v11.2) with a runtime limit of 5 min. All tests run on Windows 11 with a 6-core CPU (AMD Ryzen 5 5600G, 3.90 GHz) and 32 GB RAM.

Several metrics are adopted to evaluate model performance: (1) Realized comprehensive objective: mean and standard deviation obtained from Monte Carlo simulations indicate solution optimality and stability; (2) Model scale: numbers of variables and constraints affect computational efficiency; (3) Solution time: reflects computational efficiency and influences response timeliness; (4) Constraint violation rate: proportion of samples violating constraints measures decision reliability. Uncertainty sets are constructed to cover approximately 95% of empirical uncertainty distributions, since real-world uncertainty boundaries are often unknown. Consequently, rare realizations outside the uncertainty sets may occur in simulations. In such cases, allocation decisions may violate certain constraints, such as fund flow, resource flow, and supply–demand balance constraints defined in “Constraints”. Because test samples are randomly generated, violation locations and types are stochastic and cannot be predetermined. While these metrics do not exhaust all evaluation dimensions, they capture key trade-offs relevant to emergency resource allocation under uncertainty.

(5)Discussion on practical applications.

Discussion on practical applications.

From a practical perspective, the proposed framework is intended to support strategic and tactical decision-making in post-disaster emergency resource allocation rather than real-time operational dispatch. Decision makers can use the model to obtain coordinated allocation plans that account for uncertainty propagation and intertemporal dependencies among funding, procurement, and transportation decisions. The results in this section show how feedback-based adjustments contribute to improved supply–demand matching as information becomes progressively available. At the same time, practitioners should note that the performance of such approaches depends on the quality and timeliness of uncertainty information updates, as well as the appropriate selection of planning horizons and model parameters.

Sensitivity analysis of key parameters

This section examines the effect of two core parameters, the number of time periods (T) and the decision dependency depth (Δt), on the model’s overall performance.

Impact analysis of the number of time periods (T).

Impact analysis of the number of time periods (T).

Impact analysis of the number of time periods (T).

Based on the case setup in “Case study description” (Δt = 1), we analyze the effect of varying time periods (T = 3, 4, 6, 9, 12) on model performance metrics. As shown in Fig. 14a, the total number of variables and constraints increases with T, indicating a rise in model complexity. This occurs because each additional time period introduces more decision variables and associated constraints. Figure 14b shows that solution time increases with T, reflecting the higher model complexity. Models with T ≤ 9 are solvable within the time limit. However, for T = 12, the increased scale and computational complexity prevent convergence to the optimal solution within the preset time (denoted as “Timeout”). Finer time granularity (larger T) captures more detailed dynamics but increases computational complexity. Therefore, practical deployment requires balancing response speed with available computational resources. Figure 14c shows that the sample mean and standard deviation of the comprehensive objective f decrease significantly between T = 3 and 6, indicating that more frequent decision adjustments improve scheduling effectiveness. As T increases to 9 and 12, the improvement in mean f diminishes, and its standard deviation stabilizes. For T = 12, both slightly increase due to the non-optimal solution. Figure 14d shows that the proportion of samples violating core constraints (Eqs. 1 and 2) is highest at T = 3. As T increases, the violation rate decreases, albeit at a diminishing rate. Increasing decision adjustment frequency allows for more timely responses to uncertainty, reducing risks like resource shortages and fund depletion, and improving solution feasibility.

Fig. 14Comprehensive performance metrics under different numbers of time periods (T).

Comprehensive performance metrics under different numbers of time periods (T).

Therefore, there is a trade-off between benefits and costs for the number of periods T. T = 6 (one decision period every 12 h) represents the “performance inflection point” for this case study, offering a good balance between optimization, stability, and solving efficiency (within 10 s) while reducing violation risk. Finer partitioning (T > 6) yields diminishing returns, offset by increased computational costs and potential solution failure, making it potentially counterproductive. The timeout (≥ 5 min) observed at T = 12 in Fig. 14b reflects the computational limits of the current MSARO formulation, not its impracticality for real-world applications. In many real-world emergency planning contexts, moderate planning horizons (e.g., T ≤ 6) are commonly adopted, within which the proposed model remains computationally tractable and yields performance improvements.

Figure 15c shows that the sample mean and standard deviation of the comprehensive objective f decrease with increasing Δt, though with diminishing returns. This suggests that incorporating more historical information improves decision stability and optimization. Longer historical windows help the model capture uncertainty propagation patterns, such as persistent demand deviations and route impacts. However, the benefit of excessive historical dependency decreases as distant information becomes less relevant or introduces noise. Figure 15d shows that the sample violation rate decreases with increasing Δt. Leveraging more historical uncertainty information allows the model to better anticipate risks (e.g., procurement difficulties or route issues) and adjust current-period decisions, such as increasing budgets or rerouting transportation. This reduces the likelihood of violating hard constraints. Increasing the historical dependency depth can improve the robustness and safety of dynamic decisions, supporting the effective execution of resource allocation.

Comparative advantages of the proposed model

This section compares the proposed multi-role integrated dynamic allocation model with three widely used methods in emergency resource allocation, under the same simulation conditions. These methods reflect different decision-making paradigms—deterministic myopic planning, static worst-case robustness, and adaptive rolling-horizon decision-making—allowing a focused comparison of the value added by explicitly modeling multi-stage decision dependencies and uncertainty feedback. Specifically: (1) Deterministic Model (DM) ignores uncertainties (as described in “Multi-role integrated emergency resource allocation model”), performing a single optimization based on pre-disaster parameter estimates to generate a fixed allocation scheme. (2) Static Robust Optimization (SRO) uses single-stage robust optimization (Δt = 0), generating a conservative scheme for the worst-case scenario within the uncertainty set (Eq. 17) for the entire rescue period. (3) Rolling Horizon Optimization (RHO) is a widely used dynamic optimization approach. At the start of each period t, it solves an optimization problem based on the latest information and a limited look-ahead horizon, making decisions for the current period only. Other methods, such as stochastic programming, scenario-based robust optimization, and machine learning approaches, may outperform the baselines under certain conditions. However, they typically involve additional assumptions and computational challenges in multi-stage settings, and are beyond the scope of this comparison.

Using the case setup (T = 6) from “Case study description”, we simulated the comparative methods to calculate key metrics, as shown in Fig. 16. In terms of model scale and computational efficiency (Fig. 16a,b), the proposed model and SRO have a larger scale than DM and RHO, resulting in longer solution times. This is due to the introduction of auxiliary variables (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{\beta\:}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\alpha\:}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\alpha\:}}_{t,\tau\:}$$\end{document}) and constraints for robust dual transformation. MSARO also introduces slope variables (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{t}^{1}$$\end{document}) for affine decision rules, which is necessary for multi-period dynamic adaptability and robust feasibility. DM, a simple linear program, is solved the fastest. The computation time reported for the RHO approach in Fig. 16b represents the average solution time for a single decision period. Since the RHO framework resolves the optimization problem at each stage, the total computational burden over the entire planning horizon accumulates with the number of decision periods. Each individual RHO solved within a rolling window has a scale comparable to DM, but the cumulative computational burden across multiple optimization runs is significantly higher than DM over the entire rescue horizon.

Regarding optimization performance (Fig. 16c), DM theoretically yields optimal allocation under its deterministic assumptions but suffers severe deviations in supply-demand gaps due to ignoring perturbations. This results in the highest (worst) comprehensive objective value f, which is not ideal for affected populations. SRO sacrifices optimality for worst-case feasibility, resulting in conservative solutions. RHO adapts to uncertainty through periodic re-optimization but suffers from myopic decision-making, often converging to local optima and limiting optimization potential. In contrast, the proposed model dynamically adjusts decisions based on historical uncertainty information, enabling both reactive adaptation and proactive mitigation of future impacts. It coordinates fund, procurement, and transportation flows across the entire rescue period, considering interdependencies between allocation strategies and the propagation of uncertainty during optimization. This ensures the generated scheme is near-optimal from a multi-period perspective, improving optimality (~ 50%, ~ 44%, and ~ 26% relative to DM, SRO, and RHO) and stability of the comprehensive objective. Table 6 further reports component-wise optimization performance comparisons. The proposed method achieves the smallest supply-demand gap and economic cost while maintaining the highest life-saving utility among all compared methods. This indicates the improvement is mainly due to the reduction in resource mismatch, while utility improvement remains stable compared to DM and better than SRO and RHO.

In terms of constraint reliability (Fig. 16d), DM, ignoring uncertainty, exhibits the highest violation rate (~ 99%), suggesting it frequently leads to fund chain breaks or resource depletion, making the solution impractical. SRO, designed for feasibility within its uncertainty set, achieves a low violation rate (~ 3%) but sacrifices optimization performance. RHO’s violation rate (~ 35%) is higher than SRO’s, and its key weakness is insufficient cross-period coordination: decisions based on current information may lead to violations in subsequent periods due to unanticipated uncertainties. Conversely, the proposed model shows high decision reliability with the lowest violation rate (0.8%). It is constructed based on the predefined uncertainty set and models the dependence of decision variables on historical uncertainties. This ensures current-period decisions satisfy constraints while providing flexibility to handle future uncertainties, avoiding intertemporal violation risks in RHO.

In summary, the SRO approach performs relatively well only in terms of constraint reliability. In comparison, the proposed model achieves comparable performance in terms of model scale and solution time, while yielding substantially lower values in both the constraint violation rate and the comprehensive optimization objective (mean ± standard deviation). These improvements indicate clear advantages in decision safety, economic cost, and solution stability. Compared with DM and RHO, the proposed approach does not exhibit superior computational efficiency. However, under a fully acceptable solving time (on the order of several seconds), it consistently achieves markedly lower constraint violation rates and better comprehensive objective values, demonstrating a favorable trade-off between computational efficiency and decision quality.

Conclusions

Efficient and integrated emergency resource allocation during the post-disaster “golden 72-hour window” is crucial for mitigating disaster losses. Addressing two key gaps in existing research—lack of multi-role coordination mechanisms and limited application of MSARO—this paper proposes an allocation method integrating a “Decision-Execution-Demand” linkage mechanism. This method combines pre-disaster planning with post-disaster feedback adjustments to optimize economic costs, rescue utility, and supply-demand gaps, offering a strategy for complex disaster resource allocation. This study presents a theoretical model for integrated and adaptive emergency resource allocation. However, the model relies on a centralized optimization framework with idealized assumptions, such as perfect information sharing and fully aligned stakeholder objectives. Real-world challenges—including political constraints, information asymmetry, coordination frictions, and conflicting incentives—are not captured. The use of affine decision rules and box uncertainty sets simplifies the model for tractability, but does not capture the full complexity of real-world uncertainty. The empirical analysis is based on a simplified earthquake case study, does not verify generalizability to other hazard types, and abstracts from several operational factors, including network congestion, capacity limits, and multi-modal transportation structures. Future work should: (1) develop decentralized models to account for information asymmetry and individual incentives; (2) incorporate institutional and behavioral constraints; (3) adopt data-driven uncertainty representations and more flexible decision rules; and (4) extend the framework to multi-hazard and recovery-phase scenarios. While this study focuses on an earthquake scenario, the model can be adapted to other sudden-onset crises, such as public health emergencies, with appropriate modifications and consideration of additional operational factors.
