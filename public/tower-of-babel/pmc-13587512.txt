The experience and coping of decision-making dilemma of family caregivers of elderly patients with moderate to severe dementia: a qualitative study
Authors: Ye Ouyang, Xiyi Liu, Ying Chen, Zhenfei Chen, Chunyu Cheng, Naixin Kang, ZiJian Wen, Liping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDementia in older adults is characterized by a prolonged disease course, high disability rate and irreversibility, which impose a heavy burden on patients’ families. Family caregivers often face complex decision-making dilemmas when they make various decisions on behalf of patients. However, the existing literature has not sufficiently examined the current situation and specific features of these decision-making dilemmas among family caregivers of older adults with moderate to severe dementia.MethodsA total of 14 family caregivers of elderly patients with dementia were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Zhanjiang City, Guangdong Province, China, between October 2025 and February 2026. Study participants were selected using purposive sampling combined with the principle of maximum variation. Qualitative data were collected through face-to-face interviews, analysed using thematic analysis, and managed with the assistance of NVivo 15 software.ResultsThis study identified the current status and lived experiences of decision-making dilemmas faced by family caregivers of patients with moderate to severe dementia during the care process. The interviews revealed three core themes: (a) the dilemma of trade-offs between multiple value goals and limited resources; (b) the dilemma of negotiation among medical authority, family cognition and uncertainty; and (c) the dilemma of adjustment between emotional involvement, relational tension and rational decision-making.ConclusionFamily caregivers of patients with moderate to severe dementia often face various types of decision-making dilemmas during the care process. Both society and families should provide timely and appropriate formal and informal support to reduce the intensity of decision-making conflicts and improve the quality of care. Moreover, decision-making models that feature implicit patient participation should be explored to optimize the family caregiving decision-making process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08145-8.Keywords: Dementia, Family caregivers, Decision-making dilemmas, Qualitative research

Introduction

According to a report from the World Health Organization (WHO), 55.2 million people worldwide are living with dementia. The number of affected individuals is projected to increase to 78 million by 2030 and 139 million by 2050 [1]. Statistics [2] show that nearly 17 million people are living with late-onset dementia in China at present, and this figure is expected to increase to 30 million by 2050. The disease affects mainly women, with patients concentrated in the central and eastern regions of the country. In economic terms, the total economic burden of dementia in China accounted for approximately 0.53% of the gross domestic product (GDP) in 2019, indicating a heavy disease burden. Furthermore, the indirect economic burden attributable to dementia was significantly greater than the direct economic burden, which may be related to the care patterns of dementia patients [3].

Because of cultural values as well as the cost of care, around 70% of older adults with dementia in China opt for homecare [4]. In this setting, their most fundamental care needs are met by unpaid informal care from family members, such as their spouses and children [5]; but family caregivers bear heavy psychological and physical strain with long-term and intensive care tasks. With further progression of dementia into the moderate-to-severe stage, the demands and difficulty of care increase drastically, and family caregivers may also face the need to make tough decisions related to care and treatment [6]. Research has suggested that family caregivers of patients with dementia exhibit issues such as decision delay, regret, and isolation in the decision-making process, and they often report moderate to severe decision conflict [7].

A decision-making dilemma is defined as a distressing state of indecision that arises when an individual confronts two or more mutually exclusive yet equally justified courses of action, where each option inevitably requires sacrificing another equally important value [8]. It is conceptually different from decisional conflict: the latter mainly refers to subjective hesitation and cognitive uncertainty caused by inadequate information or unclear personal values in risky contexts [9]; in contrast, the former reflects the adverse outcomes that occur when decisional conflict is not effectively resolved under real-world pressures and continues to intensify. Their decision-making process is not only affected by changes in patients’ conditions but also closely related to family economic status, social support systems, and the accessibility of policies such as long-term care insurance [10–12]. A systematic review [5] revealed a significant positive correlation between the neuropsychiatric symptoms of patients with dementia and caregiver burden and showed that extreme caregiving stress significantly increases the experience of decision conflict [13]. Conversely, active negotiation and listening to the views of other family members or professionals when decision conflicts arise can reduce the level of decision conflict among family caregivers [14–15]. However, existing studies have focused mostly on caregiver burden or caregiving experience and lack an in-depth localized qualitative exploration of decision-making dilemmas as a dynamic, complex and emotionally intertwined process.

Furthermore, although long-term care insurance pilots have been established in many regions across China, the vague institutional access criteria and uneven service quality have made their role in alleviating family decision-making pressure and providing clear referral pathways unclear [16–18]. Moreover, localized explanations of decision-making dilemmas in the Chinese cultural context remain inadequate, and most studies focus on care-related burden, with little attention given to the dynamic changes in caregivers’ decision-making processes at different stages of disease progression [19–20]. Therefore, a systematic understanding of the decision-making process, influencing factors and psychological experiences of family caregivers of patients with moderate-to-severe dementia from the perspective of their subjective experiences has important clinical significance and social policy value.

This study adopted a qualitative research approach to explore in depth the decision-making dilemmas of family caregivers of older adults with moderate-to-severe dementia regarding care model selection, utilization of medical resources, and trade-offs of ethical responsibilities to provide empirical evidence for establishing a supportive decision-making system and improving continuous care services.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted between August 2025 and February 2026, and family caregivers of older adult patients with moderate-to-severe dementia from a tertiary hospital in Zhanjiang City, Guangdong Province, were recruited as study participants. Study participants were selected using a combination of purposive sampling and maximum variation sampling. The collaborative application of these two sampling methods ensured the diversity of participants in terms of gender, age, educational level, caregiving duration, disease type, annual income, self-reported health status, number of decision-makers involved, and other characteristics. The recruitment process was as follows: the researcher obtained study samples through various channels, such as outpatient and inpatient departments; contacted eligible participants by reviewing medical records and verifying patients’ conditions with attending physicians; and briefly explained the research content and obtained informed consent after establishing rapport with participants. Participants were formally enrolled in the study after signing the informed consent form.

The inclusion criteria for patients with dementia were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 60 years and met the diagnostic criteria for dementia in the WHO International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10); (2) met the moderate-to-severe staging criteria of the NIA-AA [21] or had clinical manifestations of moderate-to-severe late-onset dementia specified in the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Alzheimer’s Disease Dementia [22]; and (3) had used relevant medications during the past month and had stable disease conditions. The exclusion criterion included the presence of severe and active comorbidities (e.g., advanced malignancy, end-stage organ failure, or untreated major psychiatric disorders) that could independently determine care decisions or markedly impair communication beyond deficits related to dementia. In contrast, stable chronic conditions commonly observed in older adults (e.g., hypertension, diabetes) were not considered exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria for family caregivers of patients with dementia were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 18 years, was a relative of the patient, and had provided care for no less than 3 months with a daily care time ≥ 6 h; (2 served as the primary or codecision maker in major decisions concerning the patient’s medical treatment and care; (3) had normal language, hearing and comprehension functions with unobstructed communication, and was fully informed of the patient’s condition; and (4) voluntarily participated in the study and provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria for family caregivers of patients with dementia were as follows: (1) had mental illness or other severe diseases that impede the provision of regular care for patients with dementia; (2) had experienced major life stress events (such as divorce, spouse bereavement, or a diagnosis of critical illnesses) within the past 3 months; and (3) provided care services for profit.

The sample size of the interview participants was determined according to the principle of data saturation [23]. Data saturation was defined as the stage at which no new codes or themes were identified across three consecutive interviews. This criterion was achieved after 14 interviews, and participant recruitment was then discontinued. The final sample size agrees with methodological studies indicating that saturation in relatively homogeneous populations is generally reached within 9–16 interviews [24], and is also comparable with other qualitative studies involving dementia caregiving.

Data collection

The semi-structured interview guide was prepared based on a literature review informed by O’Connor’s Decisional Conflict Theory [25], which was used to identify key dimensions, including uncertainty regarding available options, perceived effectiveness, and decision support. The Shared Decision-Making framework [26] was also adopted to guide the exploration of information exchange, deliberation, and power dynamics in family–clinician interactions, together with group discussions and field observations. Pilot interviews were first conducted with the first two participants, and the interview guide was revised accordingly; the data from the pilot interviews were included in the final analysis. Outline of the semistructured interview

The researcher conducted a six-month internship in the relevant departments as a trainee and gained a thorough understanding of the operational mechanisms and staffing conditions of these departments. Face-to-face individual interviews were adopted for the collection of interview data, which were recorded using professional recording devices and mobile phone recording applications. The interviews were conducted in quiet meeting rooms in the hospital’s outpatient building or inpatient department to ensure the absence of any interfering factors. Before the interviews, the researcher explained the purpose and significance of the study to the participants in detail to ensure that they were fully informed of the relevant information and that they voluntarily agreed to participate. Participants were informed that the interview process would be recorded, that their privacy would be properly protected, and that they could withdraw from the interview at any time without any adverse consequences. All the participants signed an informed consent form and completed a brief sociodemographic questionnaire to obtain their demographic background information. The researcher captured nonverbal cues through onsite notes, including facial expressions, emotional states and body movements not explicitly mentioned during the interviews. All interview quotations cited in the study results were translated into English.

A total of 14 participants participated in the interviews for this study, with each interview lasting between 12 and 32 min. All interview recordings and logs were securely stored as digital files on the researcher’s personal computer and USB flash drives.

Data analysis

The interview data were analysed using the thematic analysis approach proposed by Braun and Clarke [27], which consists of six steps: (a) familiarization with the data; (b) generation of initial codes; (c) identification of themes; (d) review of themes; (e) definition and naming of themes; and (f) production of the report. Coding was conducted with the assistance of NVivo 15 software.

First, the research team conducted necessary revisions to the interview transcripts to ensure content accuracy without altering any semantic meaning. Through repeated reading of the interview texts, the research team extracted detailed information while minimizing subjective bias as much as possible. The texts were subsequently uploaded into NVivo 15 software for coding and data integration.During group discussions, themes were discussed together, and all the researchers collaborated to further refine them in order to improve the reliability of the results. Three themes and a total of seven subthemes were finally generated, and all the quotes were descriptively illustrated to enhance the credibility of the findings and to adequately convey the participants’ points of view. All of the quotes were translated from the Chinese language by the research team and were edited professionally by a language editing company. Aside from interviewing, field observation for a long time in the research site could provide a more thorough understanding of caregivers and their care context and thus complement the interview contents.

The credibility and transferability of data were enhanced through the four dimensions of trustworthiness: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability, which are essential indicators of the rigor of qualitative research. We adopted a 3-step verification process during our data collection. First, pilot interviews were conducted prior to the formal interviews to revise the interview questions for the better logic. Second, the interview content must be transcribed and recorded verbatim within 24 h with the reflective notes of the interviews. Third, research team members audited the interview content to assure that the audio tapes and the transcripts are completely consistent.

Reflexivity

The research team included nursing academics experienced in geriatric nursing (L.W., Z.C., N.K.) and nursing students trained in qualitative research methods (Y.O., X.L., C.C., Z.W., Y.C.), who participated in data collection and analysis. As the researchers were nurses engaged in geriatric care, familiarity with the clinical setting and easy access to participants were ensured; however, this background might also introduce bias related to prior assumptions or pre-conceived views. To limit this potential bias, reflective notes were maintained throughout data collection and analysis, and no clinical decisions were discussed during the interviews. The data were further analysed collaboratively by researchers representing different levels of the clinical team, while negative or inconsistent findings and alternative interpretations were actively examined to strengthen credibility. The transcripts were returned to the participants for confirmation to ensure that their statements accurately represented their responses and experiences.

Themes

On the basis of the interview results, three main themes and seven subthemes were identified through thematic analysis, namely, (a) the trade-off between value goals and limited resources; (b) the dilemma of negotiation between medical authority and family cognition; and (c) the adjustment dilemma between emotional tension and rational decision-making. Themes and subthemes of decision-making dilemmas

Theme 1: dilemma of trade-offs between value goals and limited resources

Family caregivers need to weigh their options and make choices among multiple value goals, including prolonging the patient’s life, improving the patient’s quality of life, and maintaining the patient’s dignity. Moreover, they are constrained by limited resources such as manpower, the economy, the care environment and policies, which results in pressure from decision-making trade-offs. This process is not a pursuit of a single goal but a judgement on the priority of different values under restricted conditions. Thus, family caregivers often experience intense psychological pressure, guilt and a sense of responsibility, and any choice may imply the compromise or sacrifice of one important value.

Multiple conflicts of value objectives

Some family caregivers face a direct conflict between prolonging the patient’s life and maintaining their quality of life during the decision-making process. Aggressive treatment may prolong the patient’s life but is often accompanied by suffering or compromised dignity, whereas choosing palliative care can improve the patient’s immediate quality of life but may mean forgoing the chance to extend survival time.

“We are not professionals after all; we don’t know much, and we’re afraid my dad might pass away suddenly because of it… So we were really torn about whether to have a tracheotomy… We mainly wanted his quality of life to be a little better… and hoped he wouldn’t suffer so much.” (L4).

In the long-term care setting, one of the core demands of most family caregivers is to improve patients’ physical comfort and quality of life; however, many choices made in this process may introduce new safety risks or operational uncertainties.

“The good thing is that after suctioning the phlegm, he feels more comfortable and suffers less when there is less phlegm; the bad thing is that I’m afraid I won’t do it properly and hurt him… He can’t tell or express if he’s in pain or injured inside.” (L13).

“If we keep the dialysis catheter, he can receive dialysis later, which is good for his condition; but we’re afraid he will pull it out at home and we won’t notice the bleeding… The main problem is that he doesn’t cooperate—he has dementia and pulls out the tube or touches things randomly, and we can’t control him.” (L12).

Owing to the diverse symptoms and loss of self-care ability of patients with dementia, family caregivers must spend most or all of their time with patients, which restricts their social activities and causes their lives to revolve entirely around patients, thus causing conflicts between their own needs and their caregiving tasks.

“I usually don’t go out; I can’t go anywhere… I can’t go anywhere I want… At most, I come to the hospital to get some medicine.” (L2).

“My son said, ‘Mom, you should take care of your health too… You don’t hire anyone else all day long, and you never have time to go out or do anything you want.’” (L8).

The constraint of limited resources

Economic status is among the key factors influencing the choice of treatment and care plans. Many families, constrained by limited financial capacity, struggle to access higher-quality medical resources or support services and must choose from restricted options. Therefore, care costs become a crucial consideration for the vast majority of family caregivers.

“Families with good conditions and sufficient financial resources may consider going to the provincial capital or Beijing to find good doctors, but we can’t even think about it… We simply can’t consider it or do it. It’s not that we don’t want to, but we don’t have the ability… We’re just wage earners and can’t afford the money at all.” (L14).

“Money is the most important thing. If I had money, I could hire a caregiver to help; if not, I can only bear it all by myself… The caregiver’s salary is 6,000 yuan, plus 1,500 yuan for food expenses, but my mother’s pension is only a little over 4,000 yuan. It’s simply not enough.” (L10).

The physical strength, time and energy invested by family caregivers are important noneconomic resources supporting care provision, and their continuous depletion directly affects care outcomes and patients’ quality of life. When the severity of patients’ disability and dementia increases and care needs increase, family care resources are often insufficient to meet the demand, which not only restricts the choice of care models but also may reduce care quality and even affect the physical and mental health of caregivers.

“I can’t manage him alone at all… He weighs 80kg, and I can’t support him by myself. If I could take care of him alone, I wouldn’t need to ask for help.” (L13).

“Caring for him has left us completely exhausted… I’ve fallen ill several times because of this family burden. I was diagnosed with purpuric dermatitis last October, which is capillary bleeding triggered by emotional stress, so I feel under huge pressure… We’re both in our sixties, and we really can’t keep going like this.” (L10).

For some family caregivers, caregiving is also constrained by environmental and policy limitations. Owing to the unique geographical environment and relatively low level of economic development, the construction of elderly care service facilities and professional institutions in urban areas lags far behind that in the core regions of the Pearl River Delta. These facilities are limited in number and have a narrow service scope, which makes it difficult to meet the diverse care needs of patients with disability and dementia. Moreover, self-built houses dominate local residential patterns. Most of these houses were constructed many years ago and generally lack basic age-friendly facilities such as ramps, handrails and nonslip flooring. Their spatial layout also does not accommodate caregiving needs, creating numerous obstacles to routine care tasks such as turning the patient over, performing transfers, and assisting with washing and personal hygiene.

“We don’t have volunteers here… I see other places have volunteers, which is really good. There are also canteens for elderly people who can’t cook by themselves, but we don’t have any of that here. “ (L2).

“He has no contact with other people all day long; his legs are not mobile… We live in a building with only stairs. Once he comes up, he doesn’t want to go down, and once he goes down, he doesn’t want to come up.” (L7).

Theme 2: negotiation dilemma between medical authority and family cognition

When faced with decision-making scenarios such as choosing treatment and care modalities, family caregivers generally rely heavily on medical authority. Their core goal is to reduce care risks by relying on professional diagnosis and treatment plans. However, constrained by their insufficient medical literacy, biases in their inherent family cognitive patterns, coupled with the complexity of the rapid progression of the patient’s condition and the uncertainty of treatment effects and prognosis, caregivers often face a dual dilemma of negotiation: “doubt about the suitability of following professional advice” and “eagerness to participate in decision-making yet lacking systematic cognitive support”. Such a dilemma essentially represents an adaptive contradiction between the professionalization of medical care and family caregiving, indicating the coexistence of trust in medical authority and fear and uncertainty toward the unknown among family caregivers, and reflecting their internal conflict between caregiving responsibility and the rationality of decision-making under conditions of information asymmetry.

The dependence on medical authority and the cognitive gap

When confronted with decision-making authority in professional medical treatment, family caregivers tend to depend on the diagnostic and therapeutic recommendations and plans provided by attending physicians, due to their limited medical knowledge and lack of independent decision-making capacity. However, this dependence does not arise from complete confidence and trust. Family caregivers often struggle to understand specialized medical terminology and clinical reasoning, are unable to accurately anticipate or evaluate treatment outcomes and potential risks, and therefore may exhibit evident cognitive uncertainty and passive decision-making. Consequently, they remain in a state of “external compliance but internal tension”, passively coping during the implementation and adjustment of medical treatment plan.

“He said it’s Doctor Jia’s medicine, you have to take it, otherwise Doctor Jia won’t prescribe it for you anymore, so he took it… We just take him to see a doctor when he’s ill and do whatever the doctor tells us.” (L2).

“After all, we are not professionals and know nothing… The only thing we can do is quickly look up some information on Baidu… When we talk to doctors, we even feel like we’re being stupid.” (L4).

Moreover, some doctors only present options without providing clear recommendations, resulting in the final decision being left to family caregivers. Although this practice appears to respect caregivers’ decision-making autonomy, it actually exacerbates their decision-making burden because of their lack of professional medical knowledge.

“The doctors didn’t say definitely to transfer. I asked many people, and the other side just said, ‘It’ s up to you; you can come if you want.’” (L3).

Some family caregivers carefully choose appropriate care methods on the basis of doctors’ professional advice. However, owing to their lack of systematic theoretical knowledge and practical skills, they often feel unconfident and hesitant when faced with performing specific operations and may even be afraid to implement necessary nursing measures.

“I looked it up online and know the sputum suction machine is helpful, but I’m still afraid of hurting him because I don’t have the skills to operate it properly; I’m worried I’ll injure his throat.” (L13).

Family cognitive bias and information fragmentation

Family members often form divergent interpretations of dementia-related symptoms in patients on the basis of their own life experiences and simple perceptions. Some family members simply attribute such symptoms to “cognitive impairment”, “mood swings” or other physical illnesses rather than the pathological decline in cognitive function. Even after receiving hospital diagnosis and treatment, they may still discontinue medication for personal reasons because of a lack of scientific understanding of the disease. This may result in inconsistencies between family care practices and the requirements of professional medical treatment and directly interferes with the process of standardized therapy.

“We’ re still wondering if it’s a mental problem or confusion caused by poor sleep… If it’s Alzheimer’s, he would have to take a lot of Western medicines… and taking those medications would take an even greater toll on him. So we’re still observing for now.” (L6).

“My mom thought taking too many medicines was bad and was afraid of dependence. She stopped the pills when she felt he was uncomfortable after taking them… In daily life, people just treat it as a natural part of getting old.” (L5).

The fragmented nature of information acquisition channels further amplifies cognitive biases. To compensate for their lack of knowledge, caregivers obtain information from various sources, such as the internet, relatives and friends, and different doctors. However, such information is often scattered, unsystematic, and even contradictory, resulting in fragmented decision-making and leaving caregivers trapped in a process of repeated trial and error.

“We also ask medical staff we know, or colleagues, friends, and classmates whose elderly family members have had similar experiences, about how to deal with it… But everyone gives different opinions, and we have no authoritative person to offer us clear advice.” (L4).

“We’ve consulted doctors everywhere; basically, we take him to whoever is recommended… If one doctor says a treatment might work a little, we just follow it and see how it goes… We’ve been trying all the time.” (L8).

Therefore, some family caregivers have expressed the need for improved access to information, hoping to establish a more centralized, authoritative and easily understandable information platform. This would help reduce the cognitive confusion and decision-making difficulties caused by fragmented information and simultaneously enable family members and doctors to view issues from the same perspective.

“Currently, no established channels exist to provide such consultation and guidance to patients’ families. The establishment of specialized institutions or professional teams to deliver these services would be highly beneficial and could help mitigate conflicts arising from information asymmetry or differing perspectives between healthcare providers and patients.” (L4).

Assessment and response to unknown risks

The medical decision-making process is always accompanied by multiple unknown factors, such as the dynamic progression of the disease, individual differences in treatment effects, and fluctuations in patients’ physical and psychological responses. Family caregivers who lack professional knowledge cannot predict whether the disease will further progress, nor can they accurately judge how and to what extent the condition will worsen. Such information gaps and cognitive limitations intensify their sense of powerlessness and psychological burden during the caregiving process.

“Since his diagnosis of dementia, we have been completely at a loss. He insists on going home every day, and we do not know how to provide appropriate care. There is currently no specific curative therapy available worldwide for this condition, leaving us entirely helpless… People consider this disease frightening, but do all patients exhibit behaviors such as day–night reversal, verbal aggression, physical aggression, and even coprophagia?” (L9).

“Our main concern is whether he can retain basic self-care abilities… Given our mother’s advanced age, my sister and I are unable to provide independent care. Our only goal is to slow disease progression and avoid rapid decline—a situation that is extremely distressing. In addition, we feel completely powerless in addressing this.” (L11).

Moreover, family caregivers must often carry out difficult risk assessments with regards to their relatives’ care plans and treatment pathways with incomplete information and limited medical knowledge, while bearing the potential consequences alone. This constant anxiety, caused by the lack of clear expectations about disease progression and treatment effects, adds uncertainty to the whole decision-making process. As a result, they often experience repeated hesitation and inner distress when they face critical medical choices.

“It’s definitely extremely difficult. The journey for hospital transfer is so long—what if something happens along the way?… We have to take so many factors into account. What would we do if an emergency occurs halfway?” (L3).

“On the one hand, we hope to actively pursue treatment and continue without giving up, expecting to maintain disease stability through dialysis; on the other hand, due to the patient’s lack of cooperation, enforced treatment would increase risks, placing us in a dilemma.” (L12).

Theme 3: adjustment dilemma between emotional tension and rational decision

Family caregivers become emotionally entangled in long-term caregiving due to their familial relationships. Their emotions are mostly anxiety about the worsening of the patient’s symptoms and distress at the patient’s suffering, as well as helplessness, mental and physical exhaustion due to the high-intensity and long-term caregiving burden. The caregiving situation contains many other emotional relational conflicts, for example, different care ideas and responsibility disputes among family members, and patient rejection of care due to symptoms of cognitive impairment or pain, etc., so caregivers need to coordinate and balance the emotional tension arising from these various conflicts. In the process of decision making, caregivers should not only consider the operability of treatment plans and the suitability of care resources but also the emotional needs of the patients and their psychological endurance, as every choice requires balancing the physical comfort and safety of the patients, the stability of the quality of care, and the alleviation of caregiver pressure themselves.

Emotional burden and ethical dilemma

Due to the sustained high intensity and prolonged pressure associated with family caregiving, caregivers experience a substantial emotional burden. This burden is reflected not only in negative emotions such as anxiety about the patient’s condition, concern about caregiving outcomes, and guilt over perceived inadequacy in providing care; with long-term accumulation, it may also affect their physical health, leading to various adverse conditions such as insomnia, bodily pain and discomfort, or even chronic illness, thereby influencing both physical and psychological well-being. Furthermore, the prolonged physical and emotional strain may impair caregivers’ judgment, patience, and decision-making ability, which can further reduce the quality of care and the rationality of decisions.

“My two younger sisters live abroad and cannot come back easily. I feel really frustrated about this. When my father was ill before, I was the one busy taking care of everything, and I felt just as upset.” (L9).

“For a period of time, due to heavy family responsibilities, I developed an anxiety-related sore throat… The symptoms only improved slightly when my mother’s condition showed some improvement… In October last year, I developed purpuric dermatitis, which caused considerable psychological stress… To be honest, I occasionally felt some dissatisfaction toward my mother; however, upon reflection, these feelings were inappropriate… Observing her current condition has truly been heartbreaking.” (L10).

Long-term intimate caregiving fosters profound emotional attachment between caregivers and patients. This affection serves as a source of caregiving motivation, yet it may also become an impediment to rational decision-making. Family caregivers often struggle between the emotional drive to “spare no effort in treatment” and the rational judgement to “minimize the patient’s suffering” and may even experience self-blame for feeling that they have not done enough.

“You want him to get better and live a long life, but he’ s uncooperative and refuses all the treatments, even shouts at us… So we have to give up and just let him be more comfortable.” (L12).

“At the beginning, I was so annoyed and frustrated… I even visited the nursing home and thought about sending him away. Then, my younger brother told me not to do it. He shouts, my child shouts too, and they said to me: ‘Don’ t send him away, it would be terrible for him.’” (L8).

Power games in relationship interactions

As the disease progresses, patients’ cognitive and communicative abilities gradually decline, but their emotional responses and behavioral choices continue to create direct obstacles to the implementation of care plans. Patients may refuse medication, resist treatment, decline examinations, or express their displeasure regarding certain care arrangements through silence, withdrawal, or irritability. Since patients cannot fully understand the necessity of treatment, reaching an agreement through rational communication proves difficult. Caregivers are often caught in a dilemma between enforcing medical orders and respecting the patient’s wishes.

“The root cause is that he just won’t cooperate—he has dementia, and he pulls out tubes or touches things randomly; we can’t control him… We want him to get better and live a long life, but he refuses all treatment and even scolds us.” (L12).

“It’s not that the doctors can’t do it; the main problem is he won’t cooperate, acting like a naughty child, moving around constantly. We needed to make a dental model, but if he keeps shaking, the denture can’t be fitted properly. The doctors tried twice and failed, and in the end, they said there was nothing they could do.” (L14).

In regard to critical care decisions such as whether to place the patient in a nursing home or have them live with the family, and whether to carry out major treatments, family members often hold completely different positions and demands because of differences in their roles, caregiving concepts, financial situations and practical interests. All the parties are constantly in contention during the decision-making process over the distribution of responsibilities, the right to speak, emotional investment and actual costs. Consequently, decisions regarding care are no longer made solely based on the needs of the patient, but evolve into a complex process in which the power structure within the family, the distribution of responsibilities, and emotional relationships are intertwined, which further increases the difficulty of decision-making as well as the psychological pressure on caregivers.

“The younger brother insisted on adhering to the medical advice, believing that physicians are professionals whose primary objective is to achieve a cure. However, I am concerned that undergoing another surgery under the present conditions would prolong the recovery period and further increase the patient’s suffering.” (L4).

“My father disagreed… My elder brother and younger sister both wanted to send him to a nursing home, but my father refused… In the end, we didn’t send him there.” (L10).

Moreover, some family caregivers stated that they would unconditionally follow doctors’ advice. In this way, they eliminated the possibility of internal decision-making disagreements. Their discourse itself serves as a strategy to avoid power struggles within the family, indicating that disagreements cannot arise when there is no room for choice.

“What is there to struggle over?… This is not something we can decide. We just take him to see a doctor when he is ill and do whatever the doctor tells us.… It’s no use thinking about it; we don’t know anything about medicine anyway.” (L14).

Discussion

The findings from this study indicate that family caregivers face not only one-sided choice dilemmas when making decisions but rather a process of continuous negotiation between three main tensions of value goals, cognitive resources, and emotional relationships. With the local context of Western Guangdong, this paper provides a more detailed analysis of specific behaviors, causes and influencing factors of problems facing family caregivers making decisions for people with moderate-to-severe dementia. This helps close the identified gap in previous research about localized problems in care decision-making of people with moderate-to-severe dementia and provides preliminary real-world evidence of decision making process of the families of older adults with this condition. This paper then uses the three major themes identified to build a conceptual model in which problems in decision-making for this population stem from the continuous reinforcement of three processes: resource constraints, information and knowledge deficits, and emotional and relationship tensions. Limited resources result in reliance on information that lacks coherence and medical professionals, this increases uncertainties over the course and treatment of the disease. Uncertainty creates and aggravates emotional and relationship tensions such as ethical struggles between life-prolonging and comfort care, intra-family conflicts, and patient resistance to interventions. These, in turn, deplete resource mobilization capabilities and compromise consensus building among family members, and consequently intensify limitations in resources. This cycle of reinforcing tensions accounts for why decision making can be so difficult even when solutions might seem straightforward to an outside observer, and when problems do not seem as obvious, but are still there.

This study indicates that family caregivers of individuals living with moderate-to-severe dementia continuously face ethical decisions involving life prolongation versus dignity, and quality of life versus avoidance of harm. This finding is consistent with Determeijer et al. [28], who reported that physical and emotional caregiver burden, financial limitations, and restricted availability of care services are interacting determinants of decision quality. However, in contrast to much of the existing literature that treats financial burden as homogeneous, this study shows that financial pressure is unevenly distributed across different institutional arrangements. Specifically, caregivers’ expressions of inability (“we do not have the capacity”/“we cannot even consider it”) in western Guangdong reflect not only individual financial status but also structural characteristics of the healthcare system. Although China has established a multi-layered system anchored in basic medical insurance, critical illness insurance, and medical assistance, its primary focus remains on acute medical services; consequently, the financial burden of dementia is largely driven by the long-term and continuous costs of informal care [29]. In addition, the scope of reimbursement varies substantially across different insurance schemes, resulting in significant differences in out-of-pocket expenditures and, consequently, varying levels of financial burden and decisional conflict among patients with different coverage types. This mismatch between institutional design and disease characteristics was particularly evident among participants, as the financial strain associated with covering a large proportion of long-term care costs rendered “decision-making” a limited or even unattainable option.

These findings are consistent with previous research on long-term care insurance, which has identified limited coverage and insufficient service provision in less developed regions [16, 18, 29], and has reported that only 33.3% of cities in China include individuals with moderate and severe dementia as eligible beneficiaries [30]. From a regional development perspective, decision-making dilemmas may shift from “which option is best” to “whether any choice is available” when primary care resource constraints are related more to coverage than to quality. While existing studies generally attribute decision-making difficulties to lack of information at the individual level or to psychological burden [31, 32], the present study suggests that when resource scarcity is collectively experienced, decision-making problems extend beyond psychological conflict to structural constraints, an aspect that has been insufficiently addressed in prior research. This finding raises an important policy question: in less developed regions, should greater attention be directed toward the scope of coverage and the allocation of resources within existing care support systems?

Most family caregivers were highly reliant on doctors in this study, but their reliance did not appear to be based on trust but rather on conformity that resulted from inadequate reliable information, consistent with survey data from the National Survey of Dementia [33]: 85% of family caregivers believed memory loss is a normal part of getting old rather than a symptom of dementia, which delayed caregivers from going to see a doctor and getting diagnosed [34]. However, unlike previous research, which only stated the existence of cognitive bias, this study demonstrated that caregivers continued to search for supplementary information in the informal channels, such as the internet and recommendations from their friends and relatives, after receiving professional advice from a doctor [35]. This result implies that conformity serves as a coping strategy when facing inadequate professional information and uncertain situations, rather than being a true expression of trust. Our study supports the research by Seifert et al. [36] of extensive use of the Internet by family caregivers, and, more importantly, it showed that the fragmented information acquisition not only cannot alleviate uncertainty, but the conflicting information may actually increase cognitive anxiety. Our study supports the systematic review of Liu et al. [37] that the research regarding the decision-making support for family caregivers has neglected the interpersonal and social aspects. Moreover, given that the lack of patient education in the Chinese context also implies that these family caregivers have no formal information sources of support, it seems critical to develop a multidimensional and systematic decision-support system to mitigate the social isolation and decision-making pressure of family caregivers by providing a range of services, including clinical, community, Internet, and policy aspects.

Issues of the family caregivers’ decisions also presented some relation-oriented and culture-oriented features, which was consistent with Kong et al.‘s [38] qualitative findings that interplay between the values of the filial piety and the structural constraints profoundly affects the caregiving experiences among rural family caregivers of dementia. However, in addition to describing moral pressures associated with filial piety values as the “moral conflicts between ‘inability to abandon treatment’ and ‘inability to bear the caring burden’ that would cause oscillating in the family caregivers’ decisions of pursuing active treatment and escaping the suffering in advance,” this study further highlights the phenomenon that, in the context of the Chinese family care system, the anticipatory grief experienced by family caregivers was deepened to moral concerns about “inadequate parental care”, which in contrast to the anticipatory grief described in Neumann et al. [39]. As for the institutionalization, our findings showed that it occurred at a much faster pace than reported in Tu et al. [12], and this may be due to the reason that formal caring resources were even more insufficient in the western region of Guangdong and hence the caring burden of the informal caregivers was intensified to the extent of critical point [38], implying that institutionalization might be an act of passive choice that was taken after physical and psychological weariness instead of one that was planned, which was also similar to the experience of ethnic minority caregivers in Manzo et al. [40]. This suggests the interaction of cultural pressure and the structural constraints would create a more intensive caring burden than either of them. Further studies should explore more cultural-sensitive and specific support strategies within the structure of filial piety.

Regarding patients, this study portrays individuals with dementia as implicit actors who actively participate in decision-making processes by resisting care arrangements through behaviors such as removing medical devices, refusing medications, and resisting examinations. This perspective contrasts with previous studies that have predominantly interpreted such behaviors as “implicit agents.” Bogmose et al. [41] argued that individuals with advanced dementia are at risk of dehumanization when caregivers misinterpret their behaviors. The present findings support this view while further indicating that variations in behavioral interpretation directly influence the decision-making experience: when caregivers interpret behaviors such as “he always removes the tube” as “he does not want this intervention,” decision-making shifts from a question of authority to a form of indirect negotiation. Sabatini et al. [42] reported that lower levels of neuropsychiatric symptoms are associated with greater patient involvement; the present study extends this finding by suggesting that even in the presence of such symptoms, patients continue to participate in decision-making through behavioral expressions. This highlights the importance of incorporating behavioral interpretation into care assessment frameworks, thereby promoting a more person-centered and individualized approach to dementia care.

It is important to note that some participants stated, “There are no problems; we simply follow the doctor’s advice.” This expression of “no problem” is itself a meaningful finding. The findings indicate that this response does not mean that decision-making conflict has been resolved; rather, it may serve as a form of self-protection related to several factors. At the cognitive level, a delayed diagnosis leaves caregivers with insufficient time to adapt. At the emotional level, prolonged caregiving may result in decision fatigue [43]. At the family level, China’s “4-2-1” family structure, in which middle-aged couples are expected to support four elderly parents while raising at least one child, makes the sharing of responsibility particularly difficult. Consequently, decision-making authority may be transferred to physicians to avoid family conflict and possible blame afterward [44]. However, transferring decision-making authority does not indicate that decision-making conflict has been truly resolved. A systematic review by Elidor et al. [45] supports that passive decision-making is an important predictor of decision regret, while the participants’ experience of “agreeing outwardly but remaining doubtful internally” further confirms that passive decision-making is not a conflict-free state. Zhang et al. [46] and Biard et al. [47] reported that knowledge transfer can improve confidence in decision-making, whereas the present study further identifies a causal pathway through which insufficient knowledge promotes the transfer of decision-making authority. When caregivers are unable to assume an active decision-making role because of limited cognitive resources, emotional exhaustion, or family pressure, the provision of additional choices may not achieve shared decision-making [48] and may instead increase moral pressure beyond simple compliance [49]. This apparent contradiction indicates that effective decision support should be provided before choices are offered and should first strengthen the basic decision-making capacity of caregivers.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Its qualitative design enabled an in-depth examination of the decision-making difficulties experienced by family caregivers while caring for patients with moderate-to-severe dementia in real-life settings. Qualitative methods are particularly suitable for examining a dynamic, complex, and ambivalent decision-making process. Purposive sampling and maximum variation sampling ensured diversity in participant characteristics, including gender, age, and duration of caregiving, which allowed the phenomenon to be represented from different perspectives. To maintain methodological rigour, the four elements of the trustworthiness framework were followed through three main verification procedures: (i) pilot interviews were conducted to improve the logic of the questions; (ii) interviews were transcribed verbatim and cross-checked within 24 h; and (iii) themes were identified collaboratively through team discussions.

However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited from a tertiary public hospital in Zhanjiang City, and all patients were registered within the formal health care system. Therefore, “hidden” patients who did not seek formal care or depended entirely on informal home care were not included. In addition, the single-site design requires caution when the findings are generalised. Context-specific conditions, including limited geriatric-care resources, insufficient long-term care insurance coverage, and the absence of modifications in owner-built housing for older people, are closely linked to the regional economic and policy environment and may restrict the application of the findings to other settings. Nevertheless, some findings, such as moral tensions and the tendency of caregivers to transfer decision-making authority to physicians during severe emotional distress, may provide useful reference for other regions in China and for low-to-middle income countries with family-based caregiving traditions. Second, because the interviews were retrospective, caregivers’ accounts may have been affected by memory bias or retrospective rationalisation. Some interviews were also less detailed because of the limited time and education levels of caregivers. Third, owing to ethical and practical constraints, the perspectives of patients with dementia were not included, and their role in decision-making was inferred only from caregiver accounts. Future studies could use longitudinal designs to examine changes in dementia-related decision-making over time or adopt dual-perspective designs to investigate the interaction between patients and caregivers during decision-making.

Conclusions

Based on in-depth interviews with family caregivers of patients with moderate-to-severe dementia, this study indicates that the complexity of caregiving and medical decision-making results from the interaction of three structural factors: resource constraints, cognitive gaps, and emotional and relational tensions. The apparently “trouble-free” coping strategy adopted by caregivers, namely complete compliance with medical recommendations, does not provide an effective means of resolving decision-making dilemmas. Instead, it reflects a self-protection mechanism shaped by cognitive overload, emotional exhaustion, and pressure arising from family roles. These findings extend the understanding of decision-making difficulties beyond individual psychological factors by highlighting the interaction between institutional constraints and culturally rooted expectations. This suggests that future research should move beyond a narrow focus on individual decision-making behaviour and examine the changing relationships among health care financing, long-term care policy, and family ethics. At the practical level, multilevel decision-support systems covering the full disease course should be established, and knowledge-sharing tools should be developed for caregivers with low health literacy. Future research may adopt multicentre comparative designs to assess whether these findings are transferable to other settings and use patient-caregiver dyadic approaches to further examine the role of persons with dementia as implicit agents in the decision-making process.
