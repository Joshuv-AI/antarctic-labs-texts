Heavy metals removal from industrial effluent using cocosnucifera shells waste derived activated carbon
Authors: Hadiza Ado Suleiman, Marlia Mohd Hanafiah, Rab Nawaz, Sani Uba, Siti Norliyana Harun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The current study investigated the performance of activated carbon (AC), which was produced from waste cocosnucifera shells and impregnated with potassium hydroxide (KOH), in eliminating heavy metals (HMs) from aqueous solutions and actual tannery wastewater. KOH-modified AC exhibits a porous and amorphous structure. It displayed a 53.442 m2/g surface area that was rich in carboxylic functional groups. It performs very well for removing heavy metals (HMs) from aqueous solutions, as evidenced by the removal of over 80% of Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu from aqueous solutions under both optimized and non-optimized reaction conditions. However, when evaluated for the removal of HMs from actual wastewater, its effectiveness was dramatically reduced, falling from 80% to less than 50% under non-optimized conditions. Its efficiency in actual wastewater was enhanced via optimizing reaction parameters such as contact duration (x1), HM concentration (x2), pH (x3), AC dosage (x4), and reaction temperature (x5) using response surface methods. The maximum sorption capacity of HMs was attained at optimum conditions of 18.10 min, 59.72 mg/L, 9.10, 0.74 g, and 32.76 °C resulting to over 95% removal of HMs. A combination of thermodynamic data, adsorption models, and adsorption isotherms suggested that HMs were chemisorbing on KOH-modified AC. It showed outstanding potential for desorption and reusability when regenerated using hydrochloric acid (HCl).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33358-z.

Introduction

Many pollutants find their way into surface and groundwater resources as a result of rapid industrialization, population growth, and unplanned urbanization. For instance, inorganic pollutants such as heavy metals (HMs) can enter into the aquatic ecosystem through a variety of ways, including mining activities, runoff from agriculture, industrial effluent, and natural processes like weathering of rocks and geological processes1,2. Because HMs are biotoxic and persistent in nature, their build-up in the environment poses a risk to human health3. Human toxicity may arise from consuming high concentrations of HMs from water and other environmental media4,5. Human health problems such as anaemia, cancer, and diabetes can arise from long-term exposure to HMs. Furthermore, the accumulation of HMs can worsen ecological toxicity and affect the chemical, nutritional, and physical characteristics of water6. Since HMs pose significant risks to human health and the ecosystem, they must be removed waste from streams before they may enter freshwater environments like rivers and streams.

Numerous methods, such as adsorption, bioremediation, catalysis, coagulation-flocculation, flotation, electro-flotation, ion exchange, membrane separation, phytoremediation, precipitation, electrodeposition, and solvent extraction, have been employed in investigating the removal of HMs from wastewater at the laboratory or treatment plant scale7–9. Among these methods, adsorption has shown promising results in terms of removing HMs and providing financial advantages. For example, it has been demonstrated that, initial 200 mg/L of Hs concentration, adsorption utilising poly(hydroxyethyl methacrylate) polymer with modified polyfunctional groups may selectively remove 162.2 and 150.3 mg/L of Cu and Cd at pH 510. Activated carbon (AC) has demonstrated favourable characteristics, including a wide surface area, an effective microporous structure, and a large adsorption capacity, among the investigated adsorbents for the removal of HMs11,12. The effectiveness of AC in practical applications and production costs, however, are two major obstacles to the widespread production and use of these adsorbent materials for removing HMs from the waste streams. These limitations necessitate the need to look for alternative sources for the preparation of AC. A variety of waste products, such as coffee, coconut, rice and hazelnut husks, coconut tree, sugarcane bagasse, coir pith, oil palm shells, date palm fibre, orange peel, pine-fruit shell, pine cone, sunflower seed hull, and waste wood, have been used to prepare activated carbon13–16.

The availability of information on the strategic selection of waste materials for AC preparation, performance optimisation through various acid treatments, and response surface approaches is still restricted, despite the increasing expansion of AC applications. Additionally, the majority of the research focuses on the effectiveness of AC in removing HMs from model aqueous solutions, which may not accurately represent real wastewater systems17. Consequently, additional study on real wastewater systems is essential since they are not only contaminated by co-existing pollutants that could affect the performance of the AC but also by non-HM contaminants.

The current research aimed to prepare AC from abundantly available waste materials and improve its performance for the removal of HMs from real wastewater through different acid and base treatments. In the present study, AC was prepared from Cocosnucifera shells waste, which is produced in abundant amounts in developing countries18. The novelty of this study entails the treatment of AC with various acids and bases to examine how these treatments affected the AC’s performance and characteristics in terms of removing HMs in both aqueous and real solutions. The properties of the AC were next ascertained by means of proximate analysis, desorption and regeneration investigations, BET surface area analysis, FTIR, XRD, and SEM. Finally, response surface approach was used to optimize the AC’s performance for the removal of HMs from aqueous solution and the optimized process was then employed for real wastewater. Kinetic experiments were conducted and kinetic models and isotherms were applied to the experimental data to elucidate the adsorption mechanism of HMs on KOH-modified AC. Finally, thermodynamics studies were conducted to acquire mechanistic insight into the adsorption process at various temperatures, as well as to establish the significance of the determined thermodynamic parameters.

Materials and methods

Materials

Calcium carbonate (CaCO3, 98%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 38%), nitric acid (HNO3, 68%), potassium hydroxide (KOH, 85%), phosphoric acid (H3PO4, 85%), sulphuric acid (H2SO4, 98%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 97%), and zinc chloride (ZnCl2, 85%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, USA. The coconut shells were purchased from ‘Yanlemo market Zaria Road, Kano Nigeria. Distilled water was used throughout the experiments. All of the chemical employed in this investigation was of analytical purity. They were used exactly as supplied by with no additional purification.

Adsorbent preparation

AC was prepared from waste Cocosnucifera shells according to a slightly modified method as reported by19. The process flow for preparing AC is illustrated schematically in Fig. 1. First, dust particles were removed from the Cocosnucifera shells by repeatedly washing them in distilled water. They were then dried for four hours at 100 °C in an oven. The dehydrated materials were then pulverized into small particles by crushing them. After that, the precursor was then impregnated with 1.0 M H2SO4, H3PO4, HCl, KOH, NaOH and CaCO3 with impregnation ratio of 1:1.

Process flow for the production, application, and regeneration of activated carbon (AC) made from the waste shells of cocosnucifera.

The impregnated material was then kept for 24 h at room temperature and then placed in a muffle furnace at 600 °C for 2 h. Following vacuum cooling of the furnace to ambient temperature, the resulting products were activated for 6 h using 85% ZnCl2. The product was then washed repeatedly with HCl and distilled water until neutral pH. The sample was then dried at 100 °C, grounded and passed through a 100 μm mesh to obtain a fine homogeneous powder of the AC. After that, the prepared AC adsorbent was kept for further use in a desiccator.

Properties determination of the adsorbent

Various characteristics of the AC were determined before and after their use in wastewater treatment for the removal of heavy metals (HMs). XRD analysis was used to analyse the crystalline structure of the AC. The XRD patterns were acquired using a powder X-ray diffraction spectrometer (PANalytical XPert3 powder) was used to detect the XRD patterns. They were acquired using Cu Ka irradiation (wavelength = 0.15406 nm) in the 20–80°(2θ) range with a 0.003° step size and a 2 s acquisition period per step. The material’s SEM micrographs were taken at 100 kX magnification using a (Phenome proX by Phenome world, Netherlands) operated at 50 kV acceleration voltage. The material’s nitrogen (N2) adsorption-desorption isotherms were obtained using the (Nova 4200e, U.S.A) at −196 °C. Before measurements, the samples were degassed for 12 h at 300 °C. The samples’ specific surface area was determined using the BET technique based on the adsorption data. Desorption data were used to analyse the pore size distribution using the Barret-Joye-Halenda (BJH) method. FTIR analysis was used for determining the AC’s functional groups using Agilent (Agilent, LI-5500, USA).

Determination of HMs

Standards Preparation

To create a calibration curve, a stock baseline solution with a concentration of 1000 mg/L was diluted and standard solutions for each of the heavy metals (HMs) under investigation were created in three to five different (10, 50, and 100 mg/L) concentrations.

Samples Preparation

The samples were prepared by precisely measuring 50 mL of the wastewater into test tubes, followed by the addition of aqua regia solution (a solution of 3:1 by volume of hydrochloric acid to nitric acid)20. Since heavy metals (HMs) are common in the environment, therefore, quality control measures were taken to prevent external contamination of the samples. To minimize the possibility of contamination, all chemicals, reagents, and materials used in sample preparation as well as analysis were maintained as dust-free as possible. Using a stirring hot plate, the samples were digested for two hours at 200 °C until its volume reduced to 20 mL. After cooling, the mixture was passed through a Whatman filter to remove the solid particles. The digested sample (1 mL) was then added to 9 mL of deionized water. The solution was finally subjected to atomic absorption spectrometric analysis to determine the concentration of the selected HMs21.

Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) analysis

Heavy metals (HMs) such as Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni and Cu concentrations in the wastewater before and after the treatment were determined using atomic absorption spectroscopy (Agilent, AA320N) analysis. Standard operating parameters were set at wavelength of 228.8, 22.35, 279.4, 232.0, and 324.7 nm for Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu, respectively with a fixed slit width of 0.7 for each HM.

Adsorption experiments

Preliminary tests

Preliminary adsorption tests were carried out in batch mode to evaluate the effectiveness of various AC and ascertain the impact of various acids and basis treatments on the performance of AC for the removal of heavy metals (HMs) from aqueous solutions. Preliminary tests were carried out in a 100 mL glass vessel. The aqueous solutions (50 mL) of various heavy metals (HMs), such as Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu, were mixed with a specific amount of AC (0.1 g/L) with the initial concentrations of 50 mg/L for each HM. The experiment was run with two distinct pH values—12 being the highest and 2 being the lowest—at two distinct temperatures (25 °C and 45 °C) for a predetermined duration of 60 min. Every experiment was carried out with continuous stirring at 200 rpm. After the end of the tests, the samples were taken out of the reaction medium and their concentration of HMs was examined. Results and discussion

Impregnation of AC

Impregnation of AC is important because it fosters a synergy between the pollutant and carbon. Certain contaminants can be reasonably removed from waste streams using impregnation, which would not be achievable otherwise35. While a few studies on the production of AC from waste cocosnucifera shells have been published36–38, there hasn’t been much research done on how preparation conditions, specifically the impact of impregnation using various acids and bases, affect the process. In the current investigation, the AC produced from waste cocosnucifera shells was impregnated with various acids, such as H2SO4, H3PO4, HCl, and bases, including KOH, NaOH, and CaCO3, at a constant molar concentration of 1.0 mol/L at 600 °C to examine their impact on heavy metal (HMs) adsorption. In this instance, the adsorption studies were carried at constant reaction conditions (pH 7, temperature 25 °C, AC dosage 0.1 g/L, contact time 60 min, and HMs concentration 50 mg/L) to rule out their effect on HMs elimination. The effect of the different acid and basis impregnation of the AC on Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu elimination from aqueous solution is shown in Figure S1. It is evident that regardless of the acids or the basis employed for impregnation, over 95% of the HMs were eliminated except Ni. This result shows that, depending on their cost, any acids or bases can be used to impregnate AC derived from waste cocosnucifera shells. Less than 80% of the Ni was removed by H2SO4 and HCl impregnated AC, suggesting that some acids may improve AC’s selective adsorption but not for all HMs.

Remember that temperature and impregnation ratio are important factors in the adsorptive elimination of pollutants using AC39,40. According to reports, a rise in the adsorption capacity of methylene blue dye from 67.1 to 72.3 mg/g using mangrove-based AC was caused by increasing the impregnation ratio of H3PO3 from 3 to 4. The H3PO3 AC’s adsorption capability showed a decrease upon increasing the impregnation ratio to 540. The impregnation ratio and temperature in the current study, however, were maintained constant at 1:1 and 600 °C, respectively, which explains why there was little variation in the removal effectiveness of the AC impregnated with different acids and bases.

Generally, the bases were most favourable for impregnation as suggested by more 95% removal of all the HMs by KOH impregnated AC in just 60 min of contact time (Figure S1 (supplementary information)). Additional research will elucidate the impact of acids and basis impregnation on yield and other surface features of the AC made from waste cocosnucifera shells. KOH impregnated AC was used in optimisation, adsorption kinetics, and thermodynamic experiments as it was determined to be the best performer for the removal of HMs from aqueous solutions. This could be attributed to; KOH is a stronger base than NaOH, which can lead to more extensive surface modification and introduction of functional groups that enhance metal ion binding, and the larger size of potassium ions (K+) compared to sodium ions (Na+) might influence the surface modification process, leading to differences in adsorption properties31. The mobility of potassium ions might also play a role in the surface modification process, potentially leading to more effective adsorption sites and lastly, KOH treatment introduced different types or amounts of surface functional groups compared to NaOH treatment, which can affect the adsorption properties thereby providing more sites for adsorption35.

It is crucial to note that at this point, KOH-impregnated AC performs exceptionally well at constant reaction conditions, which may differ in a real wastewater environment41. For this reason, further investigation was conducted to study the impact of different reaction conditions on the removal of heavy metals (HMs) using KOH-modified AC and optimize them to increase the removal of HMs. This will also aid in resource optimization as it would take a lot of resources to change the characteristics of the actual wastewater. For example, acids and bases will be needed to bring the wastewater’s pH down to 7, which could be expensive. Therefore, the reaction conditions for efficient adsorptive removal of HMs using KOH-modified AC were optimized using CCD in RSM. The results of the optimization experiments are presented and discussed in detail in the following sections.

Optimization

Regression model

Using the RSM technique, the effectiveness of the KOH-modified AC in adsorptive removal of heavy metals (HMs) such as Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu was evaluated in relation to five common adsorption factors: temperature, pH, AC dose, HMs concentration, contact length, and AC dose. The required experiments were designed using the CCD experimental matrix shown in Table 2 in various combinations of the independent variable values before applying the RSM. Based on the design matrix, a total of 50 adsorption tests were conducted to obtain data on the removal of HMs. Based on this data, the regression models were developed for HMs as expressed in Eq. S1–S5. The regression equations are displayed as coded values, where Yx denotes the removal of (x = Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni and Cu) from aqueous solution. The equations contained only those terms that were statistically significant according to the regression output values shown in Table S2–S6 (supplementary information).

Significance analysis

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) results was used to determine the significance of the regression model terms. The removal of heavy metals (HMs) from aqueous solution was significantly aided by several of the independent variables and their interactions, as seen by the p-values in Tables S2 through S6 (supplementary information). While p-values less than 0.0001 show that the model terms are very significant, meaning that changes in their input values have a very significant impact on the adsorptive removal of HMs, p-values less than alpha values indicate the significance of the model terms. The model fit quality was evaluated using the least square method of the residuals. One of the main presumptions of linear regression models is the normal distribution of the residuals42. Residuals are the disparities between the output values obtained through experimentation and the values predicted by the models that interpret the unexplained variation in the data43,44. If the residuals are not normally distributed, one could challenge the validity of the model inference. Thus, it was essential to verify this assumption. This assumption can be verified in two ways: by plotting the residuals or by evaluating the normal probability plots. According to this investigation, Fig. 2(a–e) shows the normal probability plots of the sample percentile and the values predicted by the regression model. Figure 2(c) & (d) displays the low R2 of 0.3109 and 0.3009 for Mn and Ni, respectively, indicating that the projected values are not normally distributed. The low R2 values could be linked to the fact that adsorption process may be influenced by multiple factors, making it challenging to develop highly accurate models (complexity of the system), and also limited dataset which can affect their predictive power38,41. However, a non-linear model can be utilised to improve the prediction and other approaches, like additional data collection and model refinement (incorporating other variables)39. However, the straight line shown in Fig. 2(a) & (e) with the p-values for Cd and Cu being 0.7198 and 0.8946, respectively, shows an acceptable normal distribution. The Pb removal likewise displayed an adequate normal distribution, as seen in Fig. 2(b). These findings showed that the suggested regression models are a good fit to explain the removal of HMs when it comes to forecasting the removal of Cd, Pb, and Cu using KOH-modified AC. Based on the normal probability, the models may not be as good in predicting the removal of Mn and Ni as they are for the removal of Cd, Pb, and Cu.

In addition, the standards residuals were plotted against the predicted values of the regression models for HMs removal as illustrated in Fig. 2(f–g). Provided that the model fits the data adequately, the residuals are an estimate of the random errors. The regression models’ residuals seem to behave randomly, which indicates that the models do an excellent task of fitting the data. However, the model fits the data poorly in case of Cu (Fig. 2j) because there is a regular pattern in the residuals. The random distribution of the residual around the zero (horizontal line) shows an excellent capturing of the data by the models.

Additional features of the residuals were their lack of systematic positivity throughout the majority of the data points (Fig. 2(f-i)), which suggests good model fitting for Cd, Pb, Mn, and Mn removal. On the other hand, the residuals are consistently positive or negative across a large portion of the data range for Cu removal, it indicates that the model is ill-suited to the data. Given that the proportion of data points that lie outside the valid zone is small compared to the total number of observations, it may be concluded that the modelled and the experimental data are considered to be statistically reliable and accurate45.

To further validate the model fit, the experimental data were compared with values predicted by the regression models as depicted in Fig. 3. Table 3 summarizes the ANOVA output parameters for each model. The high R2 values for Cd and Mn, respectively, of 0.9342 and 0.7944, show that there is no significant difference between the projected and observed values. This indicates that variations in the input variables might account for 93.42% and 79.44% of the variability in the removal of Cd and Mn, respectively. Conversely, it is apparent that the R2 values for Pb, Ni, and Cu removal are lower than 70%, which indicate poor model fitting. Response surface analysis

The 3-dimensional response surface plots of the interaction of five independent variables on heavy metals (HMs) were produced using the polynomial regression equations (Eq. S1–S5 (supplementary information)). The response surface plots for HMs are presented in Figure S2 (supplementary information). Fig S2(a.i.) shows the interaction of AC dosage and pH on Cd removal efficiency using KOH-modified AC. The readiness of more readily accessible adsorption sites on the AC surface, as well as the large surface area that may be enhanced with impregnation, are responsible for the observed rise in Cd removal effectiveness with an increase in AC dosage46. This result is consistent with earlier studies, which reported an improved removal efficiency of HMs with increasing the magnetite-activated carbon composite47. Similar trends can also be observed for other HMs, such as Pb, Mn, Ni, and C. For instance, as demonstrated in Figure S2(bi), Pb removal efficiency consistently increases with increasing AC dosage up to a certain point beyond which the removal efficiency continues to diminish. Similarly increasing the pH of the solution to the range of 5 to 8 increases the HMs removal. This is due to the fact that pH has a significant effect on adsorption because it affects the solubility of metal ions, the concentration of counter ions on the functional group of the adsorbent, and the degree of ionization of the adsorbate during the reaction48. In addition, increasing the pH decreases the competition between protons and metal species49.However, an increase in pH above the optimum range causes the ionisation of functional or active sites, which promotes increased adsorption. Metal cations and the surface of the adsorbent experience less electrostatic repulsion when the pH of the matrix is high. Similar patterns were also seen for the independent variables, which increase the removal effectiveness of HMs when they are operated within their ideal ranges. These variables include temperature, HMs concentration, and contact time. However, moving away in either direction from the optimum decreases the HMs removal efficiency. The optimum values were found to be 18.1 min, 59.72 mg/L, 9.10, 0.74 g/L, and 32.24 °C of contact time, HMs concentration, pH, AC dosage and temperature for more than 95% removal of Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu.

Adsorption kinetics

PFO, PSO, and Elovich models were used to better comprehend the adsorption behaviour and processes of heavy metals (HMs) onto the KOH-modified AC. The kinetics plots of the model fitting to the experimental adsorption data are shown in Fig. 4 (a–c). A summary of the kinetic model parameters is presented in Table 4. Higher correlation coefficients (R2) indicate that the PSO (Fig. 4b) more precisely represented the adsorption processes for Cd, Mn, and Ni. This implies that chemical interactions play a major role in the adsorption50. Furthermore, the PSO rate constant comparison (14.3472–28.6533 mg/g) showed that all HMs had a quicker rate of adsorption, with the exception of Cu. However, comparing the experimental K2 values (2.2 × 10–6 g/mg. min) revealed faster adsorption of Cu on the KOH-modified AC, which is probably because of its smaller ionic radius28. The lower R2 values of the PFO and Elovich models in comparison to the PSO imply that they fit the experimental data poorly.

The increase in equilibrium concentration caused the adsorption capacity for Ni to first rise before progressively plateauing (Fig. 4e). Interestingly, Ni’s adsorption capacity regularly outperformed other metals. Several adsorption isotherm models were fitted to the experimental data, and Table 5 provides a summary of the model parameters. Note that a typical Langmuir scenario would probably happen if the value of 1/n was less than 055,56. In addition, the adsorption of HMs on KOH-modified AC is a chemical process indicated by the n values of less than 140. As demonstrated by the AT and BT values of less than 1, the Temkin isotherm analysis (Fig. 4(g)) further reveals that the HMs adsorption on KOH-modified AC was a chemical process. Small AT and BT values further corroborate the finding that HMs adsorption on KOH-modified AC is a chemical process by indicating that heat sorption does not suggest a physical adsorption.

The adsorption behaviour of HMs on KOH-modified AC was better explained by the Freundlich and Temkin isotherms, demonstrated by their higher R2 values as reported in Table 5, and illustrated in Figures. 4(f) and (g). This suggests that the AC treated with KOH has a heterogeneous surface with varying affinities that is capable of multilayer HMs adsorption57. Studies show that a complex network of interactions controls the adsorption process58. Although the isotherm describes these reactions, further research is necessary to fully understand the chemical processes involved during the HMs adsorption on KOH-modified AC.

Thermodynamics of HMs adsorption

The viability of an adsorption process must be assessed in terms of thermodynamics. To determine whether physisorption or chemisorption occurs during the sorption process, the thermodynamic behaviour of the sorption of heavy metal (HMs) ions onto KOH-modified AC was investigated. The thermodynamic parameters were estimated from the HMs adsorption at different temperatures, such as 298, 305, 311, 316, and 323 K (Fig. 4(d)). The estimated parameters are presented in Table 6. As can be observed, the ∆H values for Cd, Pb, and Ni were greater than o, with the exception of Mn and Cu, indicating that chemosorption is likely involved in the sorption of Cd, Pb, and Ni on the KOH-modified AC. Mn and Cu, on the other hand, displayed a different pattern, with ∆H values less than 0, indicating physical adsorption. The fact that HM adsorption increased with temperature supports the notion that the process is endothermic. Furthermore, positive entropy values for Cd, Pb, and Ni indicate increased unpredictability during the adsorption process at the solid-liquid interface59. This finding is similar to another work published by Arshadi et al.60, who discovered that the elimination of Cd, Co, Cu, and Ni was endothermic with positive entropy. The study also observed that the adsorption of metal on barley straw ash increased with increasing temperature. At low temperatures, there appears to be less efficient HM adsorption on KOH-modified AC, as evidenced by the observed increase in ΔG with rising temperature. The resultant enthalpy and the entropy change after adsorption cannot be correlated because the thermodynamic parameters were calculated using quite distinct HMs61. However, the adsorption of Pb and Mn has shown unusually high ∆H and ∆S values, which could be attributed to the fact that the adsorption process might involve a combination of physisorption and chemisorption (Mixed adsorption mechanisms), leading to deviations from typical chemisorption ranges50–54. The adsorbent-adsorbate interactions with respect to organic pollutants might be more complex than assumed, resulting in unusual thermodynamic parameters (Complex adsorption behaviour)47,53. Moreover, the thermodynamic analysis shows that HMs can be adsorbed by KOH-modified AC, and that the adsorption process is both instantaneous and thermodynamically conceivable.

Comparison between aqueous solution and real wastewater

The performance of the KOH-modified AC in eliminating HMs from aqueous solution was compared to that of actual tannery industrial wastewater. Table 7 shows a comparison of heavy metals (HMs) removal from tannery industrial wastewater and aqueous solution using KOH-modified AC under non-optimal reaction conditions. Compared to 80% in aqueous solutions, the removal efficiency for Cd, Pb, and Ni from actual wastewater was 30.28, 59.72, and 45%, respectively. The existence of impeding compounds may be the cause of the decline in Cd, Pb, and Ni removal efficiency from actual wastewater. A range of inhibitory inorganic and organic chemicals may affect heavy metal adsorptive removal because they can be adsorbed and block the available active sites on the adsorbent’s surface. Furthermore, these substances could compete with the target HMs for the active sites on the surface of the adsorbent, thereby lowering the efficiency of HM removal. Remarkably, the adsorption of HMs from the mixed aqueous solution containing five distinct HMs under the same conditions was the same (80%), indicating that HMs had not been adsorbed selectively by the KOH-modified AC. But when it was tested to remove HMs from actual wastewater, its selectivity increased. As an illustration of the selective adsorption of HMs, the KOH-modified AC was able to remove 80% of the Mn and Cu but only 30–60% of the Cd, Pb, and Ni. The selectivity of Mn and Cu adsorption in real wastewater could be due to disparity in the ionic radius might influence the adsorption process (with certain ions being more favorably adsorbed due to size compatibility with the adsorbent’s binding sites)60. Moreover, ions with lower hydration energies might be more easily adsorbed, as they require less energy to shed their hydration shell and interact with the adsorbent and the formation of complexes with other ions or organic matter in the wastewater might affect the adsorption of Mn and Cu, potentially enhancing their selectivity59.

Fascinatingly, under optimum conditions, the removal of all HMs from actual wastewater decreased by a small fraction in comparison to the aqueous solutions. This observation is crucial because it demonstrates the importance of optimising the reaction conditions for the adsorptive removal of HMs from the actual wastewater. This is also critical not just for optimising resource input and lowering operational expenses, but also for ensuring that the adsorption system works properly in real-world conditions.

However, the selectivity of Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu in the adsorption process was impacted by various factors such as the nature of the adsorbent material, types of pollutants, and chemical characteristics of the HMs. Firstly, the adsorbent composition plays a vital role in ascertaining selectivity, which is attributed to its functional group, surface interaction and pore structure. For instance, as reported by39, adsorbents with nitrogen or oxygen functional groups would have an impact or be selective in the aforementioned HMs in the following trend: Cu > Pb > Ni > Cd > Mn. Moreover, various adsorbents show diverse affinities for diverse metal ions, thereby making it essential to choose the right material for a specific remediation process. In addition, pollutant properties such as the size, charge and electronegativity of the metal ions also impact the adsorption selectivity. The conditions of solutions, such as pH, ionic strength, and presence of competing metal ions, can influence adsorbate selectivity, thereby making it essential to optimise the adsorption conditions for effective metal ions adsorption or desorption. Table 7 shows the removal efficiency for both aqueous solution and real tannery to be > 95%. On the contrary, if there was a decline in the % removal of metals from aqueous solution and real tannery; pre-treatment and optimisation steps could be utilised to remove or reduce competing ions and organic matter before the adsorption process61. Furthermore, speciation models that consider competitive interactions between metal ions, hydrogen, hardness ions, and natural organic matter will be employed to maintain the performance of activated carbon caused by the interaction between competing ions and organic/inorganic pollutants49,53.

Following optimum treatment with KOH-modified AC, the residual concentrations of all HMs in the wastewater are somehow below or similar to the WHO-recommended threshold levels. Additionally, these values are less than those that result from applying various treatment modalities. Following a multivariate analysis of relevant parameters, the developed adsorption system in this work may be scaled up for adsorptive removal of HMs from industrial effluent.

Comparison with literature

It is challenging to compare different adsorbents for the removal of heavy metals (HMS) from wastewater due to differences in raw materials, reaction conditions, and AC preparation procedures. Herein, an effort has been made to compare the adsorbents’ maximal removal capacity for the HMs. Table S7 (supplementary information) compares the performance of AC derived from Cocosnucifera shells and impregnated with KOH with other adsorbents documented in the literature for the removal of HMs from wastewater. More than 98% of the 50 mg/L of all HMs, including Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu, could be removed by the KOH-modified AC. A prior study46 found that the AC made from Cocosnucifera shells impregnated with KMnO4 removed 98, 92, and 96.5% of 5 mg/L of Cd, Pb, and Zn, respectively. This removal effectiveness is marginally less than that of the current work62.

In a different investigation46, the removal of Ni and Cu was documented utilizing AC derived from sludge and optimum parameters of 40 mg/L of HMs concentration, 4 g/L of AC dose, 30 °C, and 100 min of contact time. The maximum adsorption capacities for Ni and Cu were 7.48 and 4.04 mg/g, respectively, which are less than the maximum adsorption capacity for Cu (161.2903 mg/g) found in the current work. The maximal adsorption capacity of the AC obtained from sewage sludge for Ni was found to be higher than the adsorption capacity in the present work. However, the Ni adsorption capacity of the KOH-modified AC in this investigation was 3.6075 mg/g, which was greater than the 0.9744 mg/g adsorption capacity of the AC made from Cocosnucifera shells and impregnated with 0.1 M NaOH (0.1 M)63. Table S7 shows trends that are contradictory. Nonetheless, in comparison to other adsorbents, the KOH modified AC performs well in the removal of HMs from wastewater.

Characteristics of the KOH-modified AC

Since the KOH-modified AC performed better in removing heavy metals (HMs) from both simulated and actual wastewater, a property performance relationship was established via determining its properties. The morphology and surface features, structural characteristics, functional groups, and proximal properties of the KOH-modified AC were all determined. The results are presented and discussed in the following sections.

Morphology and surface characteristics

SEM micrographs of the KOH-modified AC before and after the heavy metals (HMs) adsorption are shown in Fig. 5. The KOH-modified AC had a flat surface with aggregated particles on it both before and after the HMs were adsorbed. Prior to adsorption, the aggregated particles of KOH-modified AC displayed small pores (Fig. 5a), which are favourable for the adsorption of HMs10. Conversely, following the adsorption of HMs, the KOH-modified AC displayed flat surfaces (Fig. 5b), suggesting that the HMs are occupying those small pores. Expanded images sliced from the specified region in Fig. 5(b) illustrate that the KOH-modified AC has a slightly smooth surface texture after the adsorption, but images sliced from the selected sites in Fig. 5(a) show that it had a rough surface before to adsorption.

Functional groups and structural features

The functional groups found on the surface of the KOH-modified AC before and after adsorption were determined using FTIR. The FTIR spectra of the KOH-modified AC are shown in Fig. 6. Table S8 (supplementary information) summarizes peak positions for several functional groups of the KOH-modified Ac before and after adsorption. The C = C bending and C―Cl strching vibrations representing alkenes were located 827.45 cm–1 for the KOH-modified AC before and after the adsorption. Various other functional groups including C = O, —OH, ―CH, and –CO were detected in the FTIR spectra of the KOH-modified AC in both cases, prior to and after the adsorption. These functional groups are commonly reported for coconut materials because of the presence of cellulose, lignin, and hemicellulose64. The C = C bending and C―Cl streching vibrations peak shifted from 827.45 cm1 for KOH-modified AC before to adsorption to 827.47 cm–1 after adsorption, which is significant since it shows that HMs have been adsorbed onto KOH-modified AC. According to Ahmad etl65., the adsorption of metal ions could cause the peaks representing functional groups in the spectra to shift to either larger or smallerfrequencies. The FTIR data shown here are consistent with earlier research that found that HMs adsorption onto plam leaves66, Luffa acutangular65, and Char-modified coconut AC64 produced comparable findings.

Notably, the spectra of the KOH modified AC both before and following the adsorption showed peaks that represented carboxylic groups. The XRD patterns were used to determine the structural characteristics of the KOH-modified AC before and following adsorption. The samples’ XRD patterns are displayed in Fig. 6(c & d). There was no apparent difference in the crystalline structure of the KOH-modified AC prior to versus following HMs adsorption. The KOH-modified AC’s diffraction pattern showed a broad halo at 2θ = 21 and 26°, indicating an amorphous structure. This amorphous structure is likely triggered by minute crystals, or tiny crystallites, that are dispersed randomly throughout the carbon. The absence of finite-size, and long-range crystalline structure was the primary cause of the broad peak that emerged in thesepatterns. Despite the amorphous character of the KOH-modified AC, the lack of a regular structure makes it easier for HMs to enter the interior for adsorption69.

Textural properties

The KOH-modified AC’s textural characteristics, such as its pore size and specific surface area, were determined from the adsorption data using the BJH and BET methods, respectively. Thepore size distribution of KOH-modified AC’s is displayed in Figure S3(a) and Table S10. It is evident that the material is mesoprous because the pores are primarily between 1.5 and 6 nm in size, with an average of 3 nm70. The N2 adsorption isotherm for the KOH-modified AC is presented in Figure S3(b).Type I isotherms, which describe adsorption on microporous and mesoporous materials, are present by the KOH-modified AC. The specific surface area of the KOH-modified AC is 53.442 m2/g. On the other hand, the commercial AC (1069 m2/g) and the AC prepared from dates fronds (510 m2/g) have larger surface areas than the KOH-modified AC. Moreover, the high removal efficiency of heavy metals despite the relatively low surface area suggests that chemical functional groups on the surface of the material may play a more significant role in pollutant removal than surface area alone47. This could be due to certain functional groups on the material’s surface that may form strong chemical bonds with the pollutants enhancing removal efficiency36–38. However, it should be noted that the AC in this work was modified using a fixed concentration of KOH. The surface area of the adsorbent, specifically AC, is said to be greatly impacted by the concentration of the modifying agents. For instance, it has been reported that the surface area of the AC produced from date fronds expanded from 510 m2/g to 1139 m2/g when the H3PO4 concentration was raised from 20% to 60%71. According to another study, increasing the H2PO4 ratio from 3 to 5 resulted in a significant decrease in the surface area of the mangrove-based AC, from 888 m2/g to 729 m2/g40. The surface area decreases as the concentration of acids or alkali is increased in the AC impregantion. This could be because there are too many species present, which causes the pores to widen and therefore reduce the surface area68,72.It is clear that modifying agent concentration is crucial, hence optimising the KOH concentration may improve the surface area of the KOH-modified AC.

Proximate analysis

The KOH-modified AC’s ash, crude fibre, crude fat, moisture, and protein contents were ascertained through proximate analysis. Table S9 (supplementary information) displays the values for these proximate contents. The presence of 43.39% proteins, moisture content (8.87%), and crude protein (6.17%) revealed that the KOH-modified AC was largely composed of fibre. Ash and fats had respective amounts of 0.90 and 0.91%. The low amount of ash (only 0.90%) indicates that the KOH-modified AC is free of impurities, which has improved its efficacy for the removal of heavy metals (HMs). Ash is typically thought of as an impurity. Since ash is thought to disrupt the porous structure, a high ash content could lead to less adsorption. The high carbon content in the form of fibre shows that KOH-modified AC has a high potential to be chosen as an adsorbent for the removal of HMs. The KOH-modified AC’s carbon content (43.39%) was found to be lower than that of the Paulownia wood AC’s carbon content (70.83%). This could be because the current study’s impregnation temperature was higher at 600 °C than the 400 °C used in a prior study to impregnate Paulownia wood AC72. Despite its moderate carbon content, the KOH-modified AC demonstrated greater than 95% removal of Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu from both aqueous solutions and actual wastewater, indicating its effectiveness as an adsorbent. This can be attributed primarily to its functional groups coupled with moderate carbon content and porous structure which has led to significant decline in the physicochemical properties of the ternary effluent as presented in Table S11 (supplementary information).

Desorption capacity

The long-term application potential of adsorbents in water treatment is largely dependent on the regeneration or desorption process73. Furthermore, because the accumulated heavy metals (HMs) may be harmful to aquatic species, the in-situ remediation of contaminated water via adsorption may not be practical on a large scale if the HMs cannot be desorbed from the adsorbent and recovered from the treated wastewater. In the present investigation, the KOH-modified AC was regenerated following every cycle for HM adsorption experiment. In order to perform the regeneration process, the KOH-modified AC adsorbent was used for five consecutive cycles of Cd, Pd, Mn, Ni, and Cu uptake. The adsorption and desorption for each cycle were determined using Eq. (15)74. The wastewater was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 8 min in order to recover the wasted KOH-modified AC. Figure 7(a) displays the desorption efficiency of HMs from the KOH-modified AC adsorbent using H2O. It is evident that the HMs’ desorption efficiency is higher in the first cycle and progressively declines over the course of the following cycles. For instance, as Fig. 7(a) illustrates, the desorption efficiency of Cd was 54.62% in the first cycle and dropped to 48.05% in the fifth. Similarly, following the fifth cycle, the desorption efficiency of Cu dropped from 81.43% to 68.43% (Fig. 7e). Another noteworthy observation is that some HMs have higher desorption capacities than others, which may be because of variations in their affinities for the KOH-modified AC adsorbent24. The presence of residual adsorbed HMs that was not entirely eliminated in the prior desorption cycles may be the cause of the modest decrease in the adsorbents’ removal capability64.

The desorption efficiency of HMs from the KOH-modified AC adsorbent using HCl is shown in Fig. 7(f-j). Similar trends were seen for the desorption efficiency of HMs using HCl as well as for the desorption efficiency of several HMs using H2O31,76. For instance, as shown in Fig. 7(f), the Cd desorption efficiency was 94.96% and progressively dropped to 87.27% over the course of the five cycles. Similarly, as illustrated in Fig. 7(g), the Pb desorption efficiency decreased from 82.97 in the first cycle to 69.16 during the fifth cycle.

More importantly, the desorption efficiency of HMs using HCl was greater than that obtained with H2O. For instance, in the first cycle with H2O, the desorption efficiency of Cd was 54.62% (Fig. 7a) whereas that with HCl was 94.96% (Fig. 7f). Using HCl, the desorption efficiency of Cd decreased to 87.27%, while that of H2O decreased to 48.05%. It is reasonable to say that the desorption efficiency of the HMs from KOH-modified AC adsorbent is greatly influenced by both the kind of HMs and the agents utilised for their desorption77.

Conclusion

In conclusion, activated carbon (AC) prepared from the most common biomass waste—coconut shell waste—and modified with KOH at a biomass to base ratio (1:1 = b/v) has an excellent potential of finding its application as an adsorbent to remove harmful heavy metals (HMs) from both concentrated and diluted wastewater. For certain HMs, such as Cd, Pb, Mn, Ni, and Cu, the KOH-modified AC achieved > 95% removal efficiency and substantially lowered their concentrations below the WHO-recommended allowable limits for wastewater. However, when the reaction conditions are optimized, the KOH-modified AC greatly lowers the cost of the input materials while still performing exceptionally well under both optimized and non-optimized circumstances. Additionally, the KOH-modified AC is reusable and regenerable, making it the ideal adsorbent for large-scale wastewater treatment, particularly for the removal of heavy metals. To enhance the qualities of the KOH-modified AC for HMs removal as well as other environmental applications, the impact of the base (KOH) concentration can be further assessed.
