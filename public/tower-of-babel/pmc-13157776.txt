Influence of general practice education on medical students’ attitude towards this discipline: a mixed studies scoping review protocol
Authors: Lara Schürmann, Lea-Mareen Höft, Julia Karpfinger, Stephan Gerbaulet, Julia Sternal, Christiane Muth, Svetlana Puzhko, Bettina Leeuw, Tim Peters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionThe shortage of general practitioners in many countries remains a persistent issue and is likely to become more severe in the future. Multiple factors influencing the attitude of medical students towards general practice have been identified. The aim of this scoping review is to describe the scope of the evidence on the influence of teaching general practice in medical school (eg, lectures, seminars, internships) on medical students’ attitude towards this discipline and to identify knowledge gaps.Methods and analysisWe will apply a mixed studies scoping review design. Quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies exploring the influence of general practice education in medical school (exposure) on the attitudes (outcome) of medical students (population) will be included. The outcome will be any measured or reported change in medical students’ attitude towards general practice as a discipline. A systematic search in MEDLINE through PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, the Education Resources Information Centre and PsycInfo, as well as forward and backward citation tracking, will be conducted starting from 2015, published in English or German. Titles, abstracts and full texts will be screened and data will be extracted independently by two reviewers. Results will be tabulated and summarised narratively and interpreted according to the framework of the affective domain.Discussion and conclusionBy identifying and linking educational formats with levels of the affective domain, this approach may help educators understand how medical training can influence the attitude towards and interest in primary care and improve the development of professional identity formation and general practice focused curricula.Ethics and disseminationThis scoping review does not require ethical approval. The results will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at national and international conferences.Trial registration numberThe protocol of this scoping review has been registered on OSF (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/UFJCR).Keywords: MEDICAL EDUCATION & TRAINING; GENERAL MEDICINE (see Internal Medicine); Attitude; Education, Medical

Introduction

General practice provides the basis for primary healthcare, including effective management of chronic diseases.1 Studies show that a higher number of general practitioners (GPs) in the health system is associated with the improvement of health outcomes.2 However, there is a shortage of GPs in many countries belonging to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which is expected to increase in the upcoming years since one-third of the physicians are aged 55 years or older.3 For example, in Germany, there are over 4800 vacant GP workplaces leading to a major gap in the healthcare system in 20234 and one-third of the German GPs will retire within the next decade.5

While there is an urgent need to teach the next generation of GPs, many medical students do not consider a career in general practice very appealing. According to a cross-semester survey,6 only 13% of medical students prefer a career as a GP. Studies showed that, in addition to demographic and sociocultural characteristics, there are other important factors like workload, work-life balance and income that may influence the choice of general practice specialisation.7 8

Moreover, it is not only the number of GPs that matters but also their active involvement in the healthcare system and the recognition they receive from other health professionals and policymakers, all of which contribute to the value of general practice for public health. Effective communication between GPs and specialists is also crucial for a well-functioning healthcare system. Therefore, another key role of teaching general practice in medical school is its potential to foster positive attitudes towards general practice among future medical professionals in other specialties.

The experience of learning general practice in medical school7 8 is associated with the attitude towards general practice as a discipline.9 University programmes including electives, clerkships, internships and longitudinal teaching projects in general practice have been shown to increase students’ interest in general practice.10,12 In addition, recent studies show that medical education can enhance the prestige of general practice by increasing practical experience and presenting it as a promising career option.6 13 14

Although many studies have been conducted on the teaching of knowledge (cognitive domain) and skills (psychomotor domain) in medicine, the effect of attitudes (affective domain) on the perception of medical disciplines remains under-researched, despite the recent proliferation of publications on professional identity formation. However, as shown above, this aspect is crucial for attracting the next generation of GPs.

To our knowledge, no systematic or scoping review has yet been published so far on the influence of education in general practice (eg, lectures, seminars, internships) on medical students’ attitude towards this discipline. Since our goal is to conduct a comprehensive review of the available literature and to summarise and disseminate research findings and identify significant gaps in the research,15 we decided to conduct a scoping review. In summary, the objective of this mixed studies scoping review is to describe the scope of evidence on this topic and to answer the review question ‘What is the evidence from qualitative and quantitative studies about the influence of education in general practice on medical students’ attitude towards this discipline?’

Methods and analysis

Protocol and registration

The present protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist16 (online supplemental material file 1). The protocol of this scoping review has been registered on OSF17 (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/UFJCR) and will be updated in case of future changes to the methodology.

Information sources

We will search for eligible literature in the electronic databases MEDLINE through PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, the Education Resources Information Centre and PsycInfo. Forward and backward citation tracking will be conducted for the included literature. The search in the databases is expected to take place in May 2026.

Conceptual framework

The interpretation of results will be guided by Krathwohl’s taxonomy of the affective domain, which conceptualises attitude development in five hierarchical levels: (1) receiving, (2) responding, (3) valuing, (4) organising and (5) characterising (figure 1).18

The five levels of the affective domain according to Krathwohl.18

The Krathwohl framework was chosen because it is one of the most established, influential and widely used frameworks in this field. Furthermore, its five levels make it easy to operationalise, and unlike, for example, the socialisation factors proposed by Cruess and Cruess19 in the field of professional identity formation—which also refers to attitudes—it focuses not on interventions but on the outcomes among learners. We will use this framework to categorise and interpret the outcomes reported in the included studies, mapping educational interventions in general practice to the corresponding levels of attitude development.

Although Bloom and Krathwohl used and coined the term ‘affective domain’ in their theoretical model, the term ‘attitude’ (as a supplement to knowledge and skills) has become established in medical education in recent years. In this paper, we see both terms as synonymous in the context of medical education and use them interchangeably. We will refer to Krathwohl’s definition of the affective domain, that “[…] includes objectives which describe changes in interest, attitudes, and values, and the development of appreciations and adequate adjustment.”18

Eligibility criteria

Population

The population is undergraduate medical students, completing their education to become practising physicians. Studies focusing on students completing their medical graduate or postgraduate study, including medical residents, will be excluded unless it is a retrospective study focusing on the time of medical studies. There will be no restrictions on students’ age or the geographical location of the medical schools.

Exposure and/or Intervention

We will include studies investigating the influence of general practice education in a medical school on the attitude of medical students completing the curriculum to become a practising physician. As shown by our pilot search and our teaching experience, these teaching formats encompass not only didactic teaching but also hands-on experience and may be as follows: lectures, seminars, internships and clerkships (table 1). We will include any other educational activity that incorporates content related to general practice (eg, problem-based learning, mentoring).

Comparator

We will include all kinds of comparators as well as studies without comparators (eg, observational descriptive studies).

Outcome

The outcome of interest is any measured or reported change in the attitude of medical students towards general practice as a discipline as a consequence of educational interventions in general practice (eg, lectures, seminars, clerkships, internships, mentoring, problem-based learning). For quantitative studies, we will extract any reported measures of association between exposure and outcome (eg, ORs, risk ratios, HRs or standardised mean differences). For qualitative studies, we will extract the reported themes related to the outcome of interest. The identified outcomes will be categorised into the five levels of the affective domain (receiving, responding, valuing, organisation and characterisation).18 As shown in table 1, the findings can be categorised according to the different levels of attitude. Level 1 (receiving) corresponds, for example, to an increase in the number of participants or reaching larger groups of learners. Level 2 refers to an increase in active participation, for example, in specific tasks or in elective parts. Level 3 refers to the positive evaluation of individual aspects of general practice, such as the longitudinal care of patients or holistic treatment. Level 4 would be a positive evaluation of general practice as a whole through a combination of several elements from level 3. Finally, level 5 would be the influence on career choice or a long-term positive attitude towards general practice in the workplace.

Study design

Only original (primary) studies will be included. Eligible quantitative studies include interventional (experimental and quasiexperimental) and observational (cross-sectional and longitudinal) studies. Relevant qualitative studies and mixed-methods studies will also be included without any restriction on study design.

Structured reviews (ie, systematic, mapping or scoping reviews) will be screened to identify eligible original studies. Narrative reviews, study protocols, editorials, commentaries, congress abstracts and opinion papers will be excluded. Manuscripts without available full texts will be excluded.

Setting

We will include studies examining medical students’ attitudes throughout their medical school education. Studies focusing on attitudes shaped before medical school (eg, prior internships), after graduation (eg, residencies) or in learning environments outside universities will be excluded.

Time frame

As there have been many changes in (ambulatory) healthcare in recent years (eg, digitalisation, interprofessional collaboration, pandemic) and therefore teaching general practice in medical schools has also changed significantly (eg, changed frameworks and new curricula20,23), we will only include studies published from 2015 onwards in English or German language.

Search strategy

We will search the databases using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MESH) (eg, “general practice”[MESH], “education, medical”[MESH], “students, medical”[MESH]) and keywords (eg, “primary health care”, “medical student*”, “attitude”). The search strategy is based on four concepts: (1) medical students, (2) medical education, (3) general practice and (4) attitudes. The full search strategy used in PubMed is provided in online supplemental file 2.

Selection of sources of evidence

Screening process

The screening will be performed with the help of the Covidence platform.24 After removing the duplicates, two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts for inclusion in the review. Disagreements will be resolved by discussion and consensus and, if needed, by the involvement of a third reviewer. We will perform a stepwise calibration test prior to our screening on a sample of 30 records.25 We aim to achieve at least 80% agreement between reviewers. If the threshold is not met, we will refine the description of our inclusion and exclusion criteria in the screening algorithm and repeat the calibration test until we achieve 80% agreement. The papers included by abstract and title screening will be exported into the electronic library Citavi26 and the full texts will be retrieved. The full texts of potentially eligible records will be assessed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by discussion and consensus. Backward citation searches (via reference lists) and forward citation searches (via citation databases) will be conducted to identify additional eligible studies. We will contact corresponding authors for full texts or missing additional data.

Data extraction

An Excel file will be prepared to extract the data, which will include information on the author, year of the study, research design, country, sample size, setting, participant characteristics (ie, age, sex/gender, year of study), the aim of the study, characteristics of the exposure (ie, teaching formats), outcomes, time of follow-up if applicable, methods of analysis, conflicts of interest and funding sources. The data extraction sheet will undergo a calibration test on five full-text papers to ensure agreement and consistency between reviewers. Two reviewers will independently extract the study characteristics. Discrepancies will be resolved by discussion and consensus. A third reviewer will be involved if needed.

Summary of results

We will conduct a sequential exploratory synthesis in which the qualitative phase precedes and informs the quantitative phase.27 In phase one(qualitative), we will thematically analyse the qualitative studies, using both deductive and inductive approaches following the six-phase framework from Braun and Clarke, to identify overarching themes and develop a typology of teaching formats in relation to levels of attitude based on Krathwohl et al18 as shown in table 1 and the section on outcomes. This typology will serve as the analytical framework for phase two. We will not conduct qualitative analysis of primary data from studies reporting only quantitative results. In phase two (quantitative), the quantitative findings will be mapped onto the typology developed in phase I. Reported measures of association will be tabulated and categorised according to the identified teaching formats and attitude levels. This approach allows us to examine how frequently specific teaching formats are studied and how they relate to different levels of attitude. Integration will occur by comparing and interpreting qualitative themes and quantitative patterns within the shared framework, thereby enabling a comprehensive understanding of how teaching formats are associated with attitudinal outcomes. Based on the interpretation of phases one and two, we will summarise the existing evidence, identify existing gaps in research and phrase conclusions for medical educators in the field of general practice. We expect the manuscript to be finalised by November 2026.

Discussion

This scoping review will comprehensively map qualitative and quantitative evidence on how general practice education influences medical students’ attitudes toward this discipline. While previous studies suggest that exposure to general practice teaching can shape students’ perceptions and career intentions,10 11 the evidence remains fragmented across study designs, settings and outcome measures, and the overall scope of this evidence has not yet been systematically synthesised. By systematically collating this literature, the review will provide an overview of how different educational approaches relate to attitude-related outcomes.

Following the framework of Arksey and O’Malley15 allows us to capture a broad range of study designs and contexts. A key contribution of this review is the application of Krathwohl’s taxonomy of the affective domain18 as an analytical approach, enabling a structured categorisation of attitude-related outcomes. By linking educational formats with levels of the affective domain, this approach may help educators understand how medical training can influence attitudes towards and interest in primary care.

Consistent with the nature of scoping reviews, this study will not assess the methodological quality of included studies or formally evaluate the strength of evidence. In addition, we anticipate substantial heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures which may limit comparability across studies. Differences in healthcare systems and medical curricula across countries may further constrain the transferability of findings. Despite these limitations, the findings will help identify patterns in how general practice education is associated with students’ attitudes and highlight gaps in the current evidence base. This may inform the design of educational interventions and support future research aimed at strengthening interest in primary care careers.

Conclusion

The findings of this mixed-study scoping review will provide an overview of effective educational strategies that promote positive attitudes towards general practice, an essential factor in strengthening primary care. By identifying promising teaching approaches, this review could inform the development of evidence-based curricula that strengthen students’ engagement with primary care, ultimately fostering their interest in pursuing a career in this field. The identified evidence gaps could inform future research.
