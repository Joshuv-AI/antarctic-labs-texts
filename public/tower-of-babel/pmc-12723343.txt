Surface Behavior Regulation of Intermediates on the Copper‐Based Catalysts for Enhancing Electrocatalytic CO2 Reduction to Ethylene
Authors: Peng Luo, Ziyuan Yang, Yuxia Jin, Shasha Li, Yuanchuan Hao, Yun Duan, Wei Huang, Lei Liu, Kai Yan, Abuliti Abudula, Guoqing Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrocatalytic CO2 reduction (CO2RR) on copper‐based catalysts uniquely enables C─C coupling to produce multi‐carbon products such as ethylene and ethanol, owing to their moderate binding affinity toward key intermediates (*CO, *H). However, industrial application is hindered by structural instability, imprecise active‐site control, complex product distributions, and competing side reactions. Such structural strategies include facet engineering, defect creation, oxidation‐state modulation, and interface engineering, which have improved selectivity, yet most focus on static structure–performance relationships, overlooking dynamic surface behaviors of intermediates. This review builds a mechanism‐centered framework along the CO2RR pathway (CO2 activation, *CO enrichment, *CO dimerization) to elucidate how adsorption configuration, spatial distribution, coverage dynamics, and transformation kinetics of intermediates govern selectivity, providing both theoretical guidance and practical strategies for the development of next‐generation Cu‐based CO2RR systems with enhanced selectivity, durability, and industrial applicability.

Introduction

With the rapid development of industrial society, the long‐term reliance on fossil fuels has led to massive emissions of carbon dioxide (CO2), a major greenhouse gas, which has become a key driver of the global climate crisis.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] Besides CO2 capture and storage, the conversion of CO2 into high‐value‐added chemicals has attracted extensive attention, offering a dual benefit of carbon mitigation and energy valorization.[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] In recent years, the electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (ECO2RR) has emerged as a promising approach in the context of carbon‐neutral energy systems due to its low energy consumption, mild reaction conditions, and compatibility with renewable electricity sources.[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] Compared to traditional thermo‐catalytic processes, ECO2RR eliminates the need for high temperature and pressure, and the electrical energy input can be sustainably derived from solar or wind power, thereby enhancing its environmental and practical advantages.[ 11 , 12 ] The ECO2RR process enables the production of a variety of reduced products, including carbon monoxide (CO), methane (CH4), formic acid (HCOOH), ethylene (C2H4), and ethanol (C2H5OH).[ 12 , 13 ] Among these, C2+ products, particularly ethylene, have garnered significant interest owing to their high industrial relevance in the manufacturing of polymers, plastics, and pharmaceuticals. The formation of ethylene involves multi‐step proton‐coupled electron transfer (PCET) and C─C coupling steps, making selective control of this pathway highly challenging.[ 14 , 15 ] Therefore, developing an efficient, stable, and selective ECO2RR route for ethylene production is of substantial scientific and technological importance.

Among currently explored ECO2RR catalysis systems, copper (Cu)‐based materials have attracted widespread interest due to their price advantage and unique ability to catalyze both C1 and C2+ product formation. Cu species exhibit a distinctive adsorption behavior, especially the positive adsorption energy for hydrogen and a negative one for *CO, favoring the selective stabilization of key intermediates and enabling control over reaction pathways. More importantly, Cu‐based catalysts allow for product selectivity modulation through the applied potential—an advantage not commonly observed in other single‐metal catalysis systems.[ 16 , 17 , 18 ] Nevertheless, despite their versatility in generating diverse products, Cu‐based catalysts still suffer from several limitations: their activity and selectivity are highly sensitive to external factors such as pH, electrolyte composition, and applied potential. Under negative potentials, structural reconstruction due to over‐reduction of Cu can lead to the loss of active sites with strong competition from the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), low ethylene selectivity, and insufficient mechanistic understanding of active sites, continuing to restrict their further advancement.[ 19 , 20 ]

To address these challenges, recent research has focused on multiscale optimization of Cu‐based catalysts, mainly encompassing several approaches: 1) surface and structural engineering, which include tailoring the catalyst's surface topology and spatial configuration through multiple approaches including selective exposure of crystal facets (e.g., Cu(100), Cu(110)); construction of low‐coordination sites, defect‐rich regions (e.g., vacancies, steps, edges) and nanostructures with size effects (e.g., clusters, nanoparticles); integration of confinement architectures (e.g., metal organic frameworks (MOFs) and Covalent Organic Frameworks (COFs)); and surface molecular modifications using ligands, hydrophobic coatings or functional polymers. These strategies aim to regulate the adsorption geometry and spatial mobility of *CO and related intermediates, facilitating C─C coupling via well‐defined active regions.[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] 2) Electronic and valence‐state modulations, which include tuning the Cu0/Cuδ+ (0 < δ < 2) ratio, constructing mixed‐valence Cu+/Cu0 systems, and doping with heteroatoms to modulate the catalyst's electron density and Fermi level. These adjustments directly influence the binding strength of *CO, weaken C─O bonds in oxygenated C2 intermediates (e.g., *CH2CHO), thereby enabling precise control over the competition between hydrogenation and dehydroxylation pathways.[ 24 , 25 ] 3) Interface and microenvironment engineering, which leverages heterointerface construction, such as Cu‐Cu2O, Cu‐MO x , Cu‐M (M = Heteroatoms) bimetallics, and local environment optimization, including modulation of pH, interfacial electric field gradients, and the use of alkali metal cations or ionic liquids. Additionally, dual‐site design is used to decouple *CO generation and coupling areas. These strategies improve *CO retention, facilitate intermediate stabilization, and enhance C─C coupling efficiency by spatially separating activation and coupling domains.[ 26 , 27 ] 4) Deoxygenation pathway regulation, which focuses on controlling the transformation of *CO‐derived C2 intermediates (*CH2CHO, *CH2CH2OH) by promoting selective C─O bond cleavage. It involves constructing electron‐rich sites, low‐coordination Cu centers, and oxophilic environments (e.g., Lewis acidic metals or oxygen vacancies), as well as employing alkaline microenvironments to facilitate dehydroxylation. These approaches steer the reaction pathway toward ethylene formation while suppressing ethanol byproducts. While these strategies have demonstrated individual advantages, a comprehensive understanding of their mechanistic roles requires further analysis at the level of key intermediate behaviors.[ 28 , 29 ]

Recent studies have reached a consensus that the adsorption–migration–coupling behavior of *CO intermediates governs the entire ethylene formation process and fundamentally determines the reaction pathway and kinetics.[ 19 , 21 , 23 , 24 ] Initially, CO2 is activated on the Cu surface to form *CO, which subsequently undergoes C─C coupling to form the C2 framework, followed by further hydrogenation or dehydroxylation to produce specific products. Through this process, the surface coverage (θ CO), stability (*CO residence lifetime: τ CO), and mobility of *CO (Diffusion coefficient of *CO species: D CO) critically influence the frequency (C─C coupling probability: P couple) and type of C─C coupling as whether symmetric (*CO‐*CO) or asymmetric (*CO‐*CHO, *CO‐*COH) in turn dictates the final selectivity between ethylene and ethanol.[ 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] Hence, analyzing ethylene selective catalysis through the lens of *CO formation offers a rational indicator to elucidate the structure–activity relationship.

Although several comprehensive reviews on the electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (ECO2RR) have been published in recent years, most have primarily emphasized product distributions or broad categories of catalyst materials.[ 10 , 23 , 27 , 28 , 33 ] However, a systematic summary of the ethylene‐forming mechanism, particularly one centered on the modulation of *CO intermediates remains lacking. To bridge this gap, the present review adopts a *CO‐centric framework that traces the entire reaction trajectory from *CO formation and enrichment to C─C coupling and subsequent hydrogenation/deoxygenation. Within this storyline, we highlight how the interplay among Cu surface structure, electronic configuration, and local microenvironment collectively governs *CO stability, coverage, and transformation behavior, thereby determining the C2H4 selectivity of Cu‐based catalysts (Figure 1 ).

The synopsis of this review includes the key performance metrics of ECO2RR to ethylene, reaction mechanisms, catalyst tuning strategies, and prospects and challenges.

Unlike previous reviews that organize contents by material classes or structural strategies, this work follows the temporal sequence of *CO evolution → formation → enrichment → coupling → subsequent transformation, and projects multiscale optimization tactics, including facet control, defect engineering, electronic modulation, interfacial design, and microenvironment tuning, onto a unified set of quantifiable intermediate‐behavior descriptors (θCO, DCO, τCO, Pcouple), enabling cross‐comparison and combinatorial optimization. The analytical focus is shifted toward five dynamic aspects of intermediate behavior, including adsorption configuration, specific site, spatial distribution, surface coverage, and transformation kinetics according to potential and time as coordinates.

Furthermore, we elucidate symmetric and asymmetric C─C coupling pathways (*CO‐*CO vs *CO‐*CHO/*CO‐*COH) and their associated C─O bond cleavage routes (dehydroxylation/deoxygenation), deriving actionable design criteria from tunable parameters such as electronic structure, coordination environment, local electric field, and interfacial wettability. By mapping diverse structural strategies within the coordinate frame of *CO coverage–migration–coupling–deoxygenation, this review provides a regime‐based rationale for selectivity divergences under different electrolytes and potential windows. Collectively, this *CO‐centric framework not only unifies previously fragmented insights but also establishes a mechanistic foundation for predictive and design‐oriented optimization of Cu‐based catalysts toward selective C2H4 production.

Pathway and Mechanism

In general, ECO2RR is affected by multiple factors, such as the applied overpotential range, pH value near the electrode, and so on. For Cu‐based catalysts, because Cu material exhibits moderate adsorption of H2 and CO2 or CO molecules, the competition between the generation routes toward C1 and C2 products usually occurs. Thus, even based on the assumption of C2H4 as the main target product to design and synthesize the catalyst, a small amount of C1 compounds, e.g., CH4 and formic acid (HCOOH) or other C2 products like ethanol, could unavoidably be generated, and the investigation of various production routes and energy requirements for each step from a kinetic perspective is needed. Figure 2 shows synthesis pathways toward the main product of C2H4 with the generation of C1 by‐products, which include the primary CO2 molecule activation and the parallel pathways. Generally, the ECO2RR process can be divided into three parts: CO2 activation, *CO intermediate transfer and coupling, and final hydrodeoxygenation of C2 intermediates generated.

The possible reaction pathways for ECO2RR to different products over Cu‐based catalysts.

CO2 Adsorption/Activation

CO2, as a stable molecule, requires an excessive energy input to break the C═O bond (750 kJ mol−1) for ECO2RR, and generally a significant overpotential has to be supplied to fulfill bond cleavage through attacking the exposed bent structure after CO2 chemisorption (*CO2 δ− species).[ 16 , 34 ] Here, the chemi‐adsorbed states of CO2 lead to a lower energy barrier for the bond dissociation due to the lower LUMO (Lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) energy level to sensitive electron acceptance. Under the chemisorption states, the primary activation usually occurs through CO2 adsorbing at those Cu surface uncoordinated sites by O─Cu bond or C─Cu bond, and whatever which a kind of adsorption method, CO2 as the linear molecule tends to expose the remote one oxygen, and with the participating of the protons *H, the isolated oxygen will be extremely sensitive to the attacked *H species.[ 19 , 31 , 32 , 35 ] For the generated *COOH, the carbon atom connects hydroxylate and *CO groups respectively, and the unstable states will lead to the breakage of C─OH bond to form *CO. Compared with Fe, Ni, or other metals, Cu expresses a moderate barrier energy for *CO adsorption, allowing the Cu surface to accumulate *CO at a high density without impeding its surface diffusion.[ 5 , 17 , 35 , 36 ] Depending on the surface properties such as roughness, defects, facets, and uncoordinated sites with the nano structuring, doping, and surface modifications, *CO plays a pivotal role in carbon‐chain growth, and its distinct surface configurations exhibit different functions in CO2RR. Operando spectroscopy indicates three predominant *CO configurations: *CObridge, *COatop‐HFB (high‐frequency band), and *COatop‐LFB (low‐frequency band). Owing to differences in adsorption strength and site coordination, these sub‐populations behave differently in subsequent steps. *COatop‐HFB, typically associated with undercoordinated/defective/step sites, undergoes rapid PCET, favoring *CO desorption (CO formation) or deep hydrogenation to CH4, and is thus unfavorable for C─C coupling. In contrast, *COatop‐LFB, characteristic of terrace/(100‐like) sites, correlates positively with C2 formation (especially ethylene) under high *CO coverage and exhibits a lower initiation barrier for C─C coupling. When high *CO coverage is accompanied by co‐adsorbed OH−, the population of *CObridge increases and participates in atop‐bridge dimerization geometries, further promoting carbon‐chain growth along the ethylene pathway.[ 10 , 13 , 33 , 37 , 38 ]

*CO Transfer and Dimerization

The intermediate species adsorbed on the catalyst surface, such as *COOH, can undergo PCET rapidly, or further form *CO, to influence the downstream selectivity between C1 and C2 products.[ 14 , 15 , 37 ] In general, *COOH tends to follow further hydrogenation pathways to form C1‐type intermediates such as *CHO, *COH, and *CH2, which subsequently lead to formate, methane, or CO. In contrast, *CO can proceed via self‐coupling or interact with other surface‐bound species (e.g., *CHO, *COH, *CH2) to initiate C─C coupling, thereby yielding multi‐carbon (C2+) products.[ 38 , 39 ] Meanwhile, *CO is widely regarded as the pivotal intermediate that bridges both C1 and C2 pathways due to its capability to participate in diverse reaction channels.[ 19 , 31 , 40 ] Although the intentional construction of a high *CO as the surface concentration environment may shift the reaction selectivity toward C2+ products, the competitive reaction and the transient of *CO still disturb precise control over intermediate coverage, and thereby eliminating the competing C1 pathways remains a major challenge. Even though the most advanced catalysts designed for ethylene production often exhibit minor selectivity toward C1 products due to the inherently transient and dynamic nature of *CO intermediate, it is still difficult to regulate the reaction route directly. In addition, the surface diffusion behavior also plays a critical role in determining the efficiency of C─C coupling. Consequently, the product distribution in ECO2RR is strongly correlated with the surface coverage of *CO, and the surface modifications or the introduction of in situ growth functional groups can alter the intermediate concentrations and microenvironments, thereby impacting the probability of *CO dimerization.[ 26 , 31 ] Therefore, the ability to precisely regulate both the type and spatial distribution of reaction intermediates, particularly *CO is crucial for enhancing ethylene selectivity. The structure and electronic properties of active sites, along with the surrounding reaction conditions, collectively govern the local *CO environment and ultimately dictate the dominant C─C coupling route.

Hydrodeoxygenation for C2+ Formation

After the initial C─C coupling step that generates the *OCCO intermediate, further PCET leads to the formation of *CO*COH, which serves as a key branching point for product selectivity.[ 41 , 42 ] At this stage, the reaction pathway bifurcates, giving rise to either the C2H4 or ethanol production route depending on the competition between dehydroxylation and further hydrogenation processes.

In a proton‐rich environment, the *CO*COH species can undergo O─H bond cleavage via PCET, resulting in the elimination of H2O and formation of *CCO.[ 43 ] This step is considered a critical deoxygenation event in the C2H4 formation pathway. Subsequent hydrogenation of *CCO yields downstream C2 intermediates, such as *CH2CHO or *CH3CHOH, which may undergo further transformation depending on the local reaction microenvironment and active site properties.[ 44 , 45 ] While the continued hydrogenation can lead to ethanol formation, selective deoxygenation of C2 intermediates, particularly involving O─H bond cleavage, can facilitate the generation of unsaturated species like *CH2CH2, which are the precursors to C2H4 formation. Herein, the ability to remove hydroxyl groups at precise stages is therefore essential for directing the pathway toward C2H4 rather than alcohol products.

Overall, the proton–electron delivery process plays a decisive role across multiple stages of the ECO2RR. The selectivity between C1 and C2 products, as well as between the saturated and unsaturated C2 products, is largely governed by the localization and energy barrier of the PCET processes. Therefore, constructing a proton‐rich yet spatially controlled reaction environment is crucial for enhancing the efficiency and selectivity of C2H4 production over Cu‐based catalysts.

Enhancement of CO2 Adsorption/Activation

The adsorption and activation of CO2 molecules on Cu‐based catalyst surfaces represent the initial steps that govern the subsequent CO2RR pathways. Central to this process is the surface‐assisted electron transfer that generates the CO2•− radical anion, a metastable intermediate that initiates the CO2RR cascade. The formation of CO2•− not only facilitates CO2 activation but may also indirectly suppress the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), by modulating local electron density and surface site occupation. Therefore, achieving efficient CO2 activation requires a delicate balance: optimizing the adsorption energy to ensure stable surface binding, lowering the activation barrier for electron transfer, and enhancing the kinetics of radical intermediate formation. Here, the formation of CO2•− could be through the sequential pathway: CO2•− →*COOH → *CO, culminating in the generation of *CO as the key intermediate.[ 18 , 24 , 25 , 46 , 47 , 48 ] To this end, various catalyst design strategies have been developed, including facet engineering and surface roughening to tune adsorption configurations, as well as defect creation and undercoordinated site exposure to enhance charge transfer capability. Moreover, electronic structure modulation through doping or alloying further contributes to increasing local electron density and improving CO2 affinity. In addition, the supported catalysts benefit from the strong metal–support interaction (SMSI) between the support and the active phase enables the stabilization of ultrasmall copper nanoclusters (Cu NCs) while preserving undercoordinated active sites.[ 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 ] These unique coordination environments can serve as highly active catalytic centers for the initial adsorption, activation, and cleavage of surface‐bound CO2 molecules. In this section, we systematically review these approaches, highlighting their underlying structural principles, mechanistic contributions to CO2 activation, and their demonstrated efficacies in steering the reaction toward desired multi‐carbon products, especially C2H4.

Electronic Structure Optimization

The valence state of Cu at the catalyst surface plays a crucial role in ECO2RR to C2H4, and the exposed positively charged Cu (Cuδ+: 0 < δ < 2) species, encompassing both Cu⁺ and Cu2⁺, have been identified as the key factors influencing CO2 activation (Figure 3 a), *CO formation, and C─C coupling steps.[ 18 , 47 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 ] For instance, the discrepancy of energy band and electron density for Cuδ+ species can determine the adsorbing preference and bonding strength of intermediates at active sites. During ECO2RR, the Cuδ+ species may dynamically evolve into Cu⁺ or metallic Cu0, leading to the changes in the electronic environment that modulate intermediate stabilization. For example, Cu⁺ species tend to bind *CO intermediates at the atop sites, and *CO adsorbed in such isolated atop configurations is more prone to further hydrogenation toward C1 products such as CH4. In contrast, Cu0 preferentially stabilizes *CO in a bridge‐binding configuration, which can facilitate the C─C coupling, and thus favors the formation of C2+ products.[ 10 , 53 ] Recent experimental and theoretical investigations have demonstrated that dynamic evolution between Cu+ and Cu0 states under electrochemical conditions leads to the formation of mixed‐valence Cuδ+ active sites. It is revealed that increasing the amount of Cu+ species can promote the formation of C2H4.[ 57 ] Operando X‐ray absorption spectroscopy and scanning transmission electron microscopy have been adopted to reveal Cu sites' dynamic reducing process, and it is found that Cu+ species exhibit strong resistance to reduction, and can remain and expose on the surface during the ECO2RR process and serve as the active species for ECO2RR to C2H4 (Figure 3b).[ 58 ] Although the enhancement effect of the independent Cu+ species for the ECO2RR to C2H4 has been discussed completely, the effect of Cuδ+ species existed as a mixed state of Cu+/ Cu0, Cu2+/Cu0 or Cu2+/ Cu0 has also attracted the attention,[ 59 ] and aiming at dynamic evolution of Cuδ+ species to enhance the *CO coverage and to reduce the energy barrier at active sites, some strategies have been mentioned such as doping or building an interface,[ 24 , 25 , 60 ] constructing Cu0/Cu+, Cu0/Cu2+ environment to realize the adjustment for *CO concentration and adsorption mode (Figure 3c). In this section, the catalytic mechanism and reaction route at Cu+/ Cu0 or Cu2+/ Cu0 will be discussed. To date, extensive experimental and computational work has demonstrated the CO intermediate behavior at the surface, including Cu0 and Cu+. For instance, Goddard et al.[ 61 ] believed that the presence of a thin suboxide with a mix of Cu+/ Cu0 states can provide subsurface oxygen to form a hydrogen bond with the H2O molecule at Cu+ sites, enhancing *CO generation at Cu0 sites. As such, the *CO adsorbs a physisorbed linear CO2 configuration (l‐CO2), promoting *CO dimerization and suppressing C1‐forming pathways. Meanwhile, Cu+ species not only act as the sites for H2O combination (Figures 3e,g),[ 47 , 48 , 62 ] but also take responsibility for stabilizing *CO species.[ 63 ] Some evidence indicated that *CO species are much more easily adsorbed on the unoccupied Cu+ site than on the Cu0 site, and the synergistic effect of Cu+/Cu0 will promote the following *CO interaction and dimerization (Figure 3f).[ 64 , 65 ]

To date, a series of design strategies have been adopted for gaining the optimized synergistic effect between Cuδ+ and Cu° for ECO2RR to C2H4. For instance, the dense vertical lamellar (DVL) copper catalyst featuring a mixed Cu0/Cu+ interface was synthesized by an in situ electrochemical reduction method. Operando Raman measurements confirmed the coexistence of Cu0 and Cu+ species in the reduced catalyst. DFT calculations further revealed that, at the Cu/Cu2O interface, the formation of *CO rather than HCOO* intermediate is thermodynamically more favorable due to the lower activation barrier, which will facilitate *CO accumulation and subsequent dimerization toward C2H4 formation. In addition, it is found that the vertically aligned nanosheet structure can effectively suppress the catalyst aggregation during the ECO2RR process, thereby preserving the active Cu/Cu2O interfaces. As a result, the high C2H4 selectivity was achieved with Faradaic efficiencies (FEs) of 73.6% in an H‐cell and 84.5% in a flow cell system.[ 67 ] Meanwhile, Boron (B) was selected to modify Cu species for building an electron transmission with Cu and tuning the average oxidation state so that such Cu atoms adjacent to B atoms are more positively charged. As a result, it is found that, after the average copper valence state appeared at +0.35, the motivated C2H4 selectivity of 52% was obtained at −1.1 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). Moreover, DFT calculations and characterizations highlight the decreasing effects for the rate‐limiting *CO + *H →*CHO step, thereby suppressing the C1 reaction pathway.[ 68 ] In addition, through the coordination with cyanamide ligands, an isolated copper framework (denoted as Cuδ+NCN) was synthesized, featuring both metallic Cu0 and cationic Cu+ sites. Here, the linear [NCN]2− anions within the open framework stabilize the Cu0‐Cu+ mixed‐valence system via strong covalent interactions and fast electron delocalization, enabling a well‐balanced and persistent Cuδ+ dual‐site configuration during the ECO2RR. Operando Cu K‐edge XANES and EXAFS analyses revealed that the copper oxidation state lies between 0 and +1, confirming the presence of a mixed‐valence state. As a result, under a high local current density of 400 mA cm−2, the Cuδ+NCN catalyst achieved a FE of 77.7% for C2H4. Spectroscopic evidence further suggested that the coordinated Cuδ+ sites could provide a high *CO surface coverage that favors the C─C coupling (Figure 3e).[ 66 ] Meanwhile, synergistic interactions between the adjacent Cu0 and Cu+ sites can promote CO2 activation (via Cu0) and accelerate the subsequent C─C coupling (via Cu⁺), leading to highly selective ECO2RR to C2H4. Collectively, these findings underscore that tailoring the Cu+/Cu0 ratio and engineering their spatial distribution via interface construction, controlled reduction, doping, and ligand coordination enable precise modulation of *CO adsorption and dimerization behavior.[ 24 , 69 ] Thus, the synergistic electronic and geometric effects of mixed‐valence Cuδ+ sites offer a robust platform for the design of next‐generation Cu‐based electrocatalysts with high selectivity toward C2H4.

Facet Effects

Crystal facets are known to affect the catalytic behavior of metal surfaces due to the presence of low‐coordinated atoms at steps, edges, and kinks. In the case of Cu‐based catalysts for ECO2RR, as Figure 4b shows, both low‐ and high‐index facets exhibit distinct selectivity and activity toward different products.[ 69 , 70 , 71 ] Owing to lower surface energies of Cu(100), Cu(110), and Cu(111) facets, low‐index facets (LIFs) as uncommon exposed facets, could offer edges, steps, and kinks sites for the formation and adsorption of various intermediates, thereby modulating the activity and selectivity toward different products (Figure 4c).[ 70 , 71 ] It is observed that the cubic, rhombic dodecahedral, and octahedral morphologies belong to Cu(100), Cu(110), and Cu(111), respectively.[ 72 , 73 , 74 ] At CO2‐saturated 0.1 m KHCO3 environment, the Cu catalysts within the mixed facets always display a wide product distribution range from C1 to C2. It is found that the CH4‐dominated selectivity is always achieved on the Cu(111) facet, mainly occupied catalyst. In contrast, the Cu(110) and Cu(100) facets prefer the ECO2RR to C2H4.[ 23 , 75 ] To further verify the facet‐dependent effect, researchers have synthesized Cu cubes (exposing Cu(100) facet) and octahedra (exposing Cu(111) facet) to compare their ECO2RR selectivity. As a result, the Cu cubes with more Cu(100) facet exposure showed a C2H4 FE of 57%, and in comparison, Cu octahedra displayed a high CH4 FE up to 51% in 1 m KOH electrolyte.[ 76 ] Huang's research further revealed the reason for C2H4 preference at the Cu(100) facet. As shown in (Figure 4a,e), compared with other facets, the energy barrier could be decreased by the *CO coverage so that the coupling of two *CO species becomes the limiting step, and it only requires less energy for dimerization at higher *CO coverages.[ 77 ] Thus, constructing Cu (100) facet at the catalyst surface seems to be the key to reaching the higher selectivity toward C2H4. Several strategies to achieve this objective have been implemented (Figure 4d).[ 23 , 75 ] For instance, through utilizing surfactant to adjust facet growth of Cu precursor, successful growth of Cu with[ 78 ] facets on the six equal faces of Ag nanocubes was realized (Figure 4f,g). For the Ag65‐Cu35 JNS‐100, it realized high FEs of C2H4 and C2+ production at 54% and 72%, respectively.[ 79 ] Herein, the excellent ECO2RR performance should be attributed to the *CO coverage. Especially, the Janus structure consisting of Ag and Cu species provided abundant *CO coverages on the Cu(100) facet to effectively induce C─C coupling. Meanwhile, a Cu x O y /CN catalyst was obtained by using the nitrogen‐containing Cu‐MOF precursor pyrolysis method.[ 80 ] With the assistance of the CN layer coating, the Cu(100) facet adsorbs and stabilizes *CO more easily, thereby improving *CO coverage and finally facilitating the following C─C coupling reaction. A similar enhancement was achieved by developing hexagonal boron nitride (h‐BN)‐coated Cu(100) facets, wherein the FE of C2+ products increased to 83.4% at 1.2 A cm−2 in a strongly acidic electrolyte (pH = 1), with C2H4 production maintained at ≈60% FE even under a diluted 10% CO2 feed.[ 81 ] Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (in situ ATR‐FTIR) demonstrated that, compared with the bare Cu nanoparticle catalyst, an eight‐fold of *CO concentration can be covered on the Cu(100) facet. In contrast, in a recent study, by modifying the Cu(100) facet with dodecanethiol (DDT), a hydrophobic long‐chain thiol, it significantly lowered the activation barrier of the rate‐determining *CO‐*CHO coupling step, thereby increasing surface *CO coverage and promoting an asymmetric C─C coupling pathway.[ 82 ] Moreover, the DDT layer provides a hydrophobic interface that suppresses *H attack on *CO, thereby mitigating CH4 formation. Consequently, the catalyst delivered a high Faradaic efficiency of 72% for C2H4.

Besides experimental evidence, DFT calculations can further confirm the energy barriers of *CO pathway for ECO2RR to C2H4 on different facets. It is found that *CO intermediate will be competing to form (*COH) or formyl (*CHO) on Cu(111) or Cu(100) facet. On Cu(111) facet, the energy barrier of only 0.21 eV is required for the preference of *COH formation.[ 84 , 85 ] Meanwhile, ab initio molecular dynamics simulations indicated that, a solvation environment, the surrounding liquid phase supplying ─OH group for building hydrogen bonds with *COH is beneficial for the subsequent hydrogenation to generate CH4.[ 86 ] However, in comparison to *COH generating methane though CH x hydrogenated, two *CHO focus more on coupling and experience following hydrogenated to form C2H4 on the Cu(100) facet. In addition, the kinetic calculations from *CO to *CHO have two possible pathways on the Cu(100) facet, i.e., (i) Tafel process (*CO + *H → *CHO), with ΔG of ≈0.75 eV and (ii) Heyrovsky reaction (*CO + H2Oaq + e− → *CHO + OH− aq), with ΔG# of ≈0.6 eV.[ 86 , 87 ] Regardless of the reaction pathway, the *CHO formation barrier required is much lower than that for the hydrogenation of *CO to *COH since the latter hydrogenation demands a high barrier of 1.07 eV,[ 88 ] Additionally, the coupling of two *CHO species to form *OCCO exists, and Cu(100) facet contains fourfold sites to form a binding with *OCCO intermediates by two carbon atoms. The free energy for *OCCO adsorption significantly declines on Cu(111) facet. Thus, combined with the higher *CO coverage on at Cu (100) facet, it could result in considerable *CO dimerization. Overall, at a higher *CO coverage, the Cu(100) facet could not only determine the competing route of primary *CO from *COH for C1 (CH4) to *CHO, but also ensure the low enough energy barrier for the dimerization step, achieving the formation of C2H4.[ 89 , 90 , 91 ]

In addition to Cu(100) facet, high‐index facets like Cu(311) and Cu(511) have also demonstrated superior C2H4 selectivity due to their abundant low‐coordinated sites and step/kink structures.[ 83 , 92 ] By modifying catalyst synthesis parameters such as the composition of additives, the nature of substrate materials, and the temperature of thermal treatment, high‐index facets could be constructed for the target catalysts. Generally, high‐index facets are considered to consist of multi‐crystalline facets, like [n(100) × m(111)], thence, their functions are similar to those of low‐index facets, achieving the final selectivity eventually.[ 91 ] Owing to introducing sufficient kinks and atomic steps as surface defects, high‐index facets always expose low‐coordinated sites, which could remain sensitive for intermediate adsorption. For instance, at oxide‐Cu catalyst within series exposed facet, C2H4 selectivity of Cu‐based catalyst could exhibit a strong positive correlation with the (311) facet rather than (100), (110), or (111) facet.[ 83 , 92 ] Here, because of the feasible protonation process, the (311) facet is prone to reconstruct, leading to the generation of smaller Cu nanoparticles followed by the further generation of the irregular sites with high surface energy, which plays an important role as the active sites to enhance *OCCO forming at *CO coupling step, leading to a better C2H4 generation performance (Figure 4h). According to theoretical calculations, Cu(511) facets can exhibit the same function as the multi‐crystalline facet (Cu S‐[3(100) + (111)]). Experimental results also proved that the Cu(511) facet is stable when it appears at concave octahedral Cu2O (o‐Cu2O) nanocrystals.[ 83 ] Moreover, for a series of Cu2O NCs with various morphologies obtained through controlling nanocrystal growing, only concave octahedral Cu2O (o‐Cu2O) nanocrystal with (511) high‐index facets showed superior selectivity toward C2H4 (>70%) during the ECO2RR process. Here, the synergistic effect of the improved *CO concentration and the shorter route for C1 intermediates led to the C2H4 selectivity improvement. Especially, the significant *CO coverage induced dimerization happened by decreasing the adsorption barrier of two neighboring *CO on (511) facet, indirectly suppressing the HER. In essence, *CO level is the key factor to deciding the ending C2H4 selectivity during ECO2RR, and thus, constructing high‐index facets on Cu‐based catalyst to enhance the enrichment ability for *CO coverage should be beneficial for the C2H4 generation.

Defect Effect

Defect engineering has emerged as a powerful strategy in tuning the performance of Cu‐based catalysts for ECO2RR. Surface defects such as grain boundaries, steps, edges, and oxygen vacancies not only alter the electronic environment but also modulate the adsorption and reaction pathways of key intermediates. Grain boundaries, steps, edges, and unsaturation coordinated Cu located at the surface are always regarded as the defect sites, working as the active centers for the combination with various intermediates.[ 93 , 94 ] Thus, by constructing abundant defect sites, one can optimize the electronic structure of nearby atoms, modifying the catalyst surface valence, regulating the adsorption preference and concentration for intermediates, and switching reaction routes, which eventually determine the type and yield of the eventually.[ 95 ] Despite being collectively categorized as defect sites, different types of defect sites can promote *CO enrichment through distinct mechanistic pathways. For example, grain boundaries show significant improvement in *CO adsorption by the construction of local adsorption strength gradients. In a previous study, by reducing Cu3(HITP)2 (Cu‐based MOF) with the introduction of Ketjen Black as the conducting regent, a Cu‐based catalyst with rich grain boundaries was obtained, which achieved a remarkable 60–80% C2+ FEs across a wide potential range from −1.1 to −1.5 V versus RHE.[ 96 ] Here, the rich grain boundaries and multi‐facets on the reduced Cu nano‐crystallites precisely caused the obvious performance improvement since the geometric effect enhanced *CO adsorption environment at boundary sites (Figure 5e). Especially, the coexistence of adsorption sites with strong and neighboring weak adsorption sites can satisfy the extended square principle (Figure 5b), resulting in *CO intermediates constantly covered on the under‐coordinated surface square area for C─C coupling. Some special structural Cu‐based catalyst also performs boundary sites. For example, by using a kind of star decahedron Cu nanoparticles (SD‐Cu NPs) with twin boundaries (TBs) and multiple stacking faults, an adequate C2H4 FE of 52.43% ± 2.72% was achieved.[ 97 ] Here, the surface stacking fault and twin defects increase CO binding energy so that plenty of *CO species can be generated for the formation of *CHO intermediates, which more readily promote the formation of C2H4 over CH4 during the competition between C1 and C2 formation routes. Except for enhancing the coverage of *CO, those boundary sites can also enhance the CO2 activation. For example, by electrochemically reducing Cu(II) carbonate basic (Cu2CO3(OH)2) nanoribbon precursor (Figure 5h,i),[ 98 ] Cu nanoribbons with abundant grain boundaries were generated, which can activate CO2 to promote *CO intermediates formation and adsorption. Accordingly, the subsequent formation of *OCCO and *OCCOH is consistent with accelerated *CO dimerization, leading to the generation of C2H4 with excellent selectivity (Figure 5a).[ 98 , 99 , 100 ]

Some experimental works have supplied evidence that the optimal density of edges and steps exists, and these edges and steps always act as the active sites to participate in the primary CO2 activation with the formation of ideal *CO coverage for the following C─C coupling step. Meanwhile, the edge and step sites also contribute significantly to ECO2RR selectivity by facilitating the intermediate adsorption.[ 78 , 101 , 103 , 104 ] First, theoretical calculations provide evidence that engineering nanocavities to expose edge and step sites is a feasible strategy to enhance the surface *CO concentration. Chemical‐kinetics simulations (CKS) with an unchanged intrinsic rate constant K show that nanocavities redirect the near‐surface mass‐transfer flux of free CO, generating local enrichment within the cavity and increasing the surface θCO. Compared with planar Cu, the cavity geometry breaks the uniform concentration profile along the surface normal and creates a localized CO hotspot; at higher cavity densities, θCO reaches ≈1.3× that of planar Cu (Figure 5g). Within this geometric mass transfer framework, a higher θCO increases the likelihood of C─C coupling, shifting the C2/C1 partition toward C2 products. Consistent with simulation, a CV‐activated, cavity‐rich electrode exhibits a C2H4/CH4 selectivity ratio of 4.41, ≈16‐fold higher than planar Cu, evidencing nanocavity‐induced *CO enrichment.[ 101 ] In parallel, works resolving edge/step density on Cu nanocrystals demonstrate that edge/step‐rich facets act as primary CO2‐activation sites and help establish an optimal θCO for downstream coupling. Experimental results also corroborate this conclusion. Meanwhile, the edge and step sites also contribute significantly to ECO2RR selectivity by facilitating the intermediate adsorption.[ 78 , 101 , 103 , 104 ] For instance, Anna's group designed a series of Cu nanocrystals possessing different edge sites to figure out unambiguous structure/property relations for ECO2RR over Cu‐based catalysts.[ 104 ] Among all shaped crystals, the cube‐shaped one showed higher activity than the sphere‐shaped one (Figure 5c,d). Distinct from traditional plane‐oriented enhancement on *CO coverage, 44‐nm‐side‐length Cu NC cubes exhibited the best performance with a 50 % FE of C2H4 owing to the optimal balance between plane‐ and edge‐sites on the catalyst. Here, the plane‐ and edge‐sites are the key sites to facilitate the adsorption and stabilization of ECO2RR reaction intermediates (i.e., *COOH) for C2H4 generation and inhibit HER. Meanwhile, statistical analysis of the surface atom density also suggests that the edge sites play a key role in ECO2RR to C2H4. For example, a kind of Cu(OH)2‐D/Cu foil was fabricated using a Cu foil coated with Cu(OH)2 via a chemical deposition method and subsequently reduced to construct edge sites.[ 101 ] Experiencing Cu(110) and Cu(100) growth at stepped sites, it is found that *CO adsorption and dimerization will occur more easily since the energy barrier is significantly reduced. As a result, maximum FEs of 58% and 87% for C2H4 and C2+ products, respectively, were obtained with a high partial current density of 217 mA·cm−2 toward C2+ products at only −0.54 V versus RHE (Figure 5f). Oxygen vacancies can promote CO2 activation by facilitating C═O bond cleavage through phase evolution and ionic redistribution to build oxygen vacancies, which has already been proved for enhancing CO2 activation and *C1 adsorption to generate C2+ products when applying the doping strategy.[ 102 , 105 ] The oxygen vacancies are beneficial for the breakage of C═O bond for CO2 molecules and the adsorption preference for *CO intermediates, affecting the final production toward C2H4. For instance, Lan et al.[ 106 ] designed a kind of Bi doping Cu‐based catalyst using Bi–Cu MOF as the precursor, and found that the optimal catalyst, named as Bi‐Cu x O‐3 with rich oxygen vacancies, presented a satisfying C2H4 FE of 50.95 % with a stable performance over 60 h. Here, benefit from sufficient oxygen vacancies, Bi‐CuO/Cu2O presented effective charge transfer with *COOH in comparison with CuO/Cu2O (0.78 |e −| vs 0.42 |e −|), indicating that oxygen vacancies can enhance intermediate adsorption and reduce the activation energy for key steps. Likewise, by applying La atom as the additive, oxygen vacancies enriched Cu‐based catalysts were also constructed. Unlike massive CH4 as the main product over the unmodified Cu catalyst, the La‐doped Cu‐based catalyst, i.e., La2‐xCuO4‐δ, performed an optimal activity at 51.3 mA cm−2 with a high selectivity of 41.5 % for C2+ production.[ 107 ] Here, it is considered that the facilitated adsorption and activation ability for the key reaction intermediates and the controlled dimerization of *CO will enhance the selectivity toward C2H4.

Generally, surface‐exposed grain boundaries, steps, edges, and undercoordinated Cu atoms are typically regarded as defect sites. Owing to the synergy effect of different defects, the behavior of the adsorbed CO2 molecules can be adjusted, leading to the generation of the target product like C2H4. As stated above, oxygen vacancies can enhance the breakage of C═O bond, which is crucial for the primary activation of CO2 toward C2H4 formation. Meanwhile, the step and boundaries supply active sites for *CO adsorption, leading to *CO enrichment on the surface to ensure the following C─C coupling step at a lower energy barrier condition. Therefore, by virtue of its simplicity and precise controlling of surface behaviors, defect engineering should be an effective way to create effective catalysts for ECO2RR to C2H4.

Alloying

Alloying copper with secondary metal elements has re‐emerged as a promising strategy for enhancing the performance of electrocatalysts in ECO2RR.[ 108 , 109 , 110 ] Through careful modulation of the electronic structure and local coordination environment of catalysts, the introduction of foreign metals can substantially alter intermediate adsorption behavior, reaction kinetics, and product selectivity (Figure 6a,b). These effects are generally attributed to the electronic interactions at the Cu‐M interface, geometric strain, and the formation of dual active sites.[ 111 , 112 ] Depending on the metal component introduced, alloying strategies can be broadly categorized into three mechanistic classes: i) electronic structure regulation, ii) *CO intermediate enrichment, and iii) tandem or cooperative catalysis via bifunctional sites.[ 108 , 113 , 114 ]

For instance, non‐noble metal doping, such as Zn, offers a pathway to fine‐tune the electronic structure of Cu without introducing excessive cost. As reported,[ 119 ] a representative CuZn bimetallic catalyst can be synthesized via carbonization of a CuZn‐based metal–organic framework (MOF) on conductive porous carbon. The resultant catalyst contained both alloy and segregated Cu and Zn phases, where the intimate Cu–Zn interface significantly enhanced the FEs of C2 products, achieving a fivefold increase (25%) over the bare Cu. This performance was ascribed to the electronic modulation of the Cu catalyst by Zn and local enrichment of *CO intermediates (Figure 6c), which favored C─C coupling. In contrast, CuZn bimetallic catalysts with phase segregation showed reduced selectivity, emphasizing the importance of alloy homogeneity.[ 78 , 109 ]

Noble metal alloying further extends the utility of this approach. In a single‐atom alloy (SAA) configuration, a nanocavity‐enriched CuPd catalyst exhibited outstanding C2H4 selectivity, reaching FEs of 75.6% for C2H4 and 85.7% for total C2+ products (Figure 6g).[ 115 ] Here, the incorporation of isolated Pd atoms modified the electronic structure of adjacent Cu sites, effectively lowering the activation barrier for C─C coupling and increasing the proportion of linearly adsorbed *CO, which is considered an intermediate geometry strongly associated with C2H4 production (Figure 6e).[ 117 ]

In addition to modifying the electronic structure of the Cu site, some alloying elements, such as Ag, can serve to enrich *CO intermediates on the catalyst surface.[ 111 , 112 ] A nanoporous CuAg alloy catalyst with a low Ag content (<10%) was synthesized via electrodeposition using 3,5‐diamino‐1,2,4‐triazole as an additive.[ 120 ] The optimized composition (6% Ag) achieved (6% Ag) achieved an FE of ≈60% for C2H4 and 25% for ethanol at −0.7 V versus RHE, with a high total current density of ≈−300 mA cm−2. Here, the enhanced C2+ selectivity should be attributed to the Ag‐induced stabilization of the Cu2O surface layer and the increased local *CO coverage (Figure 6d,f), which can collectively promote C─C bond formation.[ 117 ]

Building on the success of bimetallic alloys, ternary alloy systems have been explored to decouple CO generation and C─C coupling steps spatially (Figure 6i).[ 113 ]

A trimetallic Ag/Au/Cu nano‐frame catalyst demonstrated exceptional C2H4 selectivity with FEs reaching 69.5% in an H‐type cell and 77.2% in a flow cell.[ 116 ] Here, Ag/Au alloy domains facilitated *CO generation, whereas the defect‐rich Cu sites act as coupling centers. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that the free energy barrier for *COOH formation on Ag3Au(100) is only 0.31 eV, and the transfer of *CO to neighboring Cu sites is thermodynamically favorable (ΔG = −0.47 eV), highlighting the tandem catalytic synergy (Figure 6h,j).

Despite these promising developments, several challenges persist in alloy catalyst design. Especially, precise control over elemental distribution and phase uniformity remains difficult, and dynamic surface reconstruction under electrochemical conditions can lead to metal leaching or phase segregation, thereby compromising long‐term activity. Furthermore, inappropriate choice or excessive loading of the secondary metal may inadvertently favor the competing HER, reducing overall ECO2RR selectivity. These limitations underscore the need for advanced synthetic strategies, in situ characterization techniques, and theoretical modeling to rationally design robust and selective Cu‐based alloy electrocatalysts.

Size‐Dependent

In the preceding sections, we have comprehensively discussed the role of Cu facets, oxidation states, and defect structures as catalytically active centers for the ECO2RR.[ 54 , 69 , 94 ] Although these structural features critically influence the catalytic performance of Cu‐based materials, bare Cu catalysts still face several challenges under reaction conditions. For instance, Cu species are prone to reduction under applied potentials, leading to significant alterations in surface electronic structure. Moreover, the aggregation of Cu atoms during the reaction can result in the loss of initially exposed active sites, ultimately degrading the catalytic performance.[ 16 ] In addition, the relatively high overpotential required for ECO2RR remains a major obstacle to practical application (Figure 7 a). To address these limitations, recent studies have explored the design of supported Cu catalysts. By leveraging strong metal–support interactions (SMSI), it is possible to stabilize ultrasmall Cu nanoclusters (Cu NCs) and concurrently preserve undercoordinated active sites.[ 118 , 121 ] For example, graphdiyne (GDY), featuring a π‐conjugated framework rich in acetylenic linkages, offers excellent electronic conductivity and anchoring capability, thereby effectively immobilizing Cu species and preventing their aggregation (Figure 7b).[ 122 ] Similarly, supports such as SiO2 can influence the particle size and dispersion of Cu nanoparticles by regulating their nucleation and growth on the support surface. It has been reported that the supported single‐atom or sub‐nanometer Cu species tend to favor the formation of C1 products, whereas larger Cu clusters and nanoparticles promote the generation of C2+ products.[ 121 ] From a structural perspective, supported catalysts typically exhibit robust solid–solid interfacial contact between the active Cu phase and the support. These interfaces are not merely passive physical contact; instead, they actively facilitate the dispersion, anchoring, and stabilization of metal species.[ 123 ] Importantly, Cu atoms located at the interface often exhibit coordination environments distinct from those in the bulk, which, due to support‐induced electronic perturbations, may serve as highly active catalytic centers. Furthermore, these interfaces can mediate electronic redistribution, localizing charge transfer, interfacial polarization, and even strain effects. Such phenomena synergistically enhance the activation and accumulation of key reaction intermediates (e.g., *CO or *OCCO), thereby enriching Cu⁺ sites with *CO species and promoting the subsequent C─C coupling step.[ 38 , 63 ] Therefore, in the supported Cu‐based catalysts, both particle size effects and interfacial interactions operate via distinct yet complementary mechanisms to collectively govern the catalytic performance and product selectivity in ECO2RR. For instance, Philipp and co‐workers[ 124 ] systematically investigated the influence of support materials on the size stability and CO2 reduction performance of Cu nanoparticles. Operando studies on ≈220 nm Cu nano‐cubes supported on carbon revealed severe morphological degradation under ECO2RR conditions, including (100) facet roughening, pore formation, and Cu atom loss, accompanied by significant CuO x reduction (Figure 7f). These changes can suppress C2+ product (e.g., C2H4 and ethanol) formation and increase the CH4 selectivity. In contrast, Cu nano‐cubes of similar size deposited on Cu foil maintained structural integrity and stabilized Cu+/Cu0 interfacial sites, resulting in a markedly enhanced C2/C1 product ratio. These results highlight that the structural evolution of Cu under electrochemical conditions is strongly governed by the interaction strength between the metal and its substrate. In another strategy, researchers constructed Cu–N–C catalysts by infiltrating Cu2+ into porous N‐doped carbon frameworks derived from ZIF‐8, followed by a high‐temperature treatment.[ 125 ] Here, by precisely tuning the pulse electrolysis parameter Δt‐c, the Cu particle size was controllably adjusted with sub‐nanometer resolution. It was observed that larger particles (>2.5 nm) predominantly yielded H2 and CO while smaller particles with more undercoordinated sites exhibited enhanced H and CO2 adsorption. However, excessive *H binding in ultra‐small clusters hindered *CO adsorption, shifting selectivity toward H2. That is, by modulating particle size, the selectivity could be tuned across H2, CH4, and C2H4 pathways (Figure 7c).

Meanwhile, a MOF‐derived Cu catalyst, CuPz2‐Act‐30, exhibited excellent C2H4 selectivity, reaching a FE of 70.2% at −1.03 V versus RHE.[ 126 ] In situ Raman and ATR‐FTIR spectroscopy demonstrated that electrochemical activation generated highly dispersed Cu/Cu2O nanoclusters that can enrich *CO surface coverage, thereby facilitating C─C coupling. DFT calculations identified the C2H4 formation pathway (*CO + *CO + *H → *CO‐COH) with a low energy barrier (ΔG = 1.05 eV), lower than that of the CH4 pathway, consistent with the observed product distribution (Figure 7d,e).

Furthermore, theoretical studies evaluated the activity of sub‐nanometer Cu clusters (<2 nm) using a revised particle swarm optimization (RPSO) algorithm combined with DFT and microkinetic modeling.[ 127 ] Several symmetric configurations (Ih, D5h, Oh, Wulff) have been analyzed, and a geometric–electronic structural descriptor integrating generalized coordination number (GCN) and surface strain (Δd) was proposed to accurately predict intermediate binding energies. A contour map of C2H4 generation activity as a function of GCN and Δd revealed a volcano‐type relationship, with ∼1.0 nm Cu clusters exhibiting the highest predicted C2H4 yield due to an optimal balance between edge/corner site density and reaction energetics (Figure 7g). These theoretical predictions align well with experimental observations and provide guidance for the rational design of highly efficient sub‐nano Cu‐based catalysts.

Interface Establishment

In supported Cu‐based catalysts, the interface between the active Cu phase and the support plays a pivotal role in modulating catalytic ECO2RR behavior.[ 34 , 50 , 111 ] For instance, the interfacial effects between the active phase and the support can facilitate electron redistribution, thereby preventing oxidation and loss of the active species. Alternatively, interfacial interactions within the catalytic layer can establish ordered diffusion channels, efficiently transporting and enriching reactants or intermediates at the interface, thus promoting their accumulation on the active phase and lowering the corresponding reaction energy barriers. Therefore, for Cu‐based catalysts, constructing copper interfaces with oxides or hetero‐phases not only enhances the stability of Cu+/Cu0 species but also induces interfacial effects‐such as electronic perturbation, strain modulation, and cooperative adsorption of key intermediates‐which collectively steer the product selectivity toward C2+ hydrocarbons.[ 6 , 17 , 111 ] Beyond simple stabilization, such interfaces are recognized as chemically active regions capable of manipulating the local electronic structure, intermediate adsorption, and elementary reaction steps (Figure 8a ). A growing body of evidence has demonstrated that engineering Cu‐based interfaces with oxides or hetero‐phases can not only improve the stabilization of Cu+/Cu0 species but also induce interfacial effects such as electronic perturbation, strain modulation, and synergistic adsorption of key intermediates, ultimately steering product selectivity toward C2+ hydrocarbons.[ 26 , 41 , 47 ] For instance, coupling Cu active centers with a CeO2 support yielded a Cu‐CeO2 nanorod catalyst exhibiting remarkable selectivity toward C2H4.[ 128 ] Here, under ECO2RR conditions, initially deposited CuO clusters undergo partial reduction to form metallic Cu clusters partially decorated with Cu+ at the Cu/CeO2 interface. These interfacial mixed‐valence Cu+/Cu0 sites act as the stabilized active centers that effectively lower the energy barrier for C─C coupling (Figure 8c). At −1.1 V versus RHE, the Cu‐CeO2 nanorod catalyst achieved a FE of 50.0% for C2H4, significantly outperforming a CeO2‐supported Cu single‐atom catalyst. In another case, interface and phase engineering enabled the stable integration of CuO nanoparticles with Al2CuO4 nanosheets, forming a dual‐phase catalyst enriched with CuO/Al2CuO4 interfaces.[ 129 ] These sites synergistically stabilized *CO intermediates and promoted their coupling toward C2H4 formation. Operando ECO2RR at −1.0 V yielded a C2H4 FE of 82.4% (Figure 8b), maintained over 100 h of continuous operation. Spectroscopic and mechanistic analysis suggested that the CuO/Al2CuO4 interface can not only enhance *CO coverage but also facilitate the surface migration, thus accelerating *OCCO intermediate formation (Figure 8d,e).

Heterointerface construction has also proven effective when combining Cu with transition metal oxides. A representative NiOOH/Cu hybrid catalyst exhibited sustained C2H4 FE exceeding 50%, attributed to interfacial *CO stabilization.[ 130 ] In situ surface‐enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) revealed the enhanced Cu–CO vibrational features relative to pristine Cu or NiOOH, indicating preferential CO adsorption at the interface. DFT calculations further confirmed that the NiOOH/Cu interface strengthens *CO binding and lowers the dimerization barrier toward *OCCO, accounting for the high C2+ productivity (Figure 8f). To further elucidate the impact of interfacial valence distribution, Xie et al.[ 131 ] applied interface boundary engineering to construct Cu catalysts with tunable Cu0/Cu+ densities at Cu(111)/Cu2O(111) phase junctions. Across a range of CO2/N2 feed concentrations (2.5–100% v/v), catalysts with abundant Cu0/Cu+ interfaces achieved a C2+ FE of (51.9 ± 2.8) % and partial current density of (34.5 ± 6.4) mA.cm−2 under dilute CO2 (5% v/v). Importantly, the selectivity toward C2 products positively correlates with the density of Cu0/Cu+ interface boundaries. Kinetic analysis identified the *COOH formation step rather than *CO2‐generation (Figure 8g,h) as the rate‐determining step under a low CO2 concentration, which highlights the unique role of interface structure in steering reaction pathways. Bottom–up synthetic control of interfacial valence states also enables precise tailoring of C2 selectivity. Liu et al.[ 132 ] developed convex Cu@Cu2O spheres with tunable Cu0/Cu+ interfaces via hydrothermal treatment. Here, it is found that the product distributions evolved significantly with synthesis time: 2 h samples favored CH4, 7 h samples favored HCOOH, whereas the 4 h convex spheres achieved 90.5% total C2+ selectivity (59.3% C2H4, 31.2% EtOH). In situ FTIR and DFT studies indicated that the superior performance should be attributed to the well‐coupled Cu0‐Cu+ sites, which enriched the surface in *CO and stabilized key *OCCO intermediates.

Additional strategies for exploiting oxide‐derived interfacial effects have also been explored. Zhu et al.[ 133 ] reported a silica‐modulated CuO catalyst (m‐SiO2/CuO) wherein strong interfacial interactions suppress CuO reduction and stabilize Cu2+. The optimized catalyst reached a partial C2H4 current density of 600 mA·cm−2 with 55.5% FE. Operando ATR‐FTIR revealed that Cu0/Cu2+ interfaces facilitated *CHO and *OC‐CHO formation, which are critical precursors for C2H4 generation. Similarly, Sargent and co‐workers[ 134 ] designed a Cu‐SiO x catalyst, in which the silica framework acts as a structural modulator to create Cu‐SiO x step sites. Since these sites exhibit enhanced *CO coverage and reduced energy barriers for *OCCOH formation, in a membrane electrode assembly (MEA), the optimized Cu‐SiO x catalyst achieved >60% C2H4 FE at 300 mA·cm−2, with stable performance over 55 h under varied CO2 concentrations. Taken together, these studies underscore the fundamental and practical importance of interfacial design in Cu‐based ECO2RR catalysts. From tuning valence state distributions to manipulating electronic structures and local strain fields, the rational construction of reactive Cu–support interfaces offers a versatile platform for selectively steering C─C coupling toward C2+ products.

*CO Intermediates Transfer

Although the concentration of *CO intermediates can largely determine the selectivity toward C2H4 in the ECO2RR process, the subsequent coupling of *CO is a highly complex process involving multiple electron and proton transfer steps as well as diverse coupling pathways such as *CO‐*CO, *CO‐*CHO, and *CO‐*COH. The intermediates *CO, *CHO, and *COH, as the primary species in the coupling process, are particularly susceptible to protonation, which can divert them toward undesired C1 products before successful C─C coupling occurs.[ 10 , 38 ] This challenge is further compounded by the complex surface environment of Cu‐based catalysts, which impedes the efficient dimerization of *CO or its coupling with hydrogenated derivatives, ultimately limiting the C─C coupling efficiency toward C2+ products in ECO2RR. In addition, the overpotential and selectivity of Cu‐based catalysts are constrained by the inherent trade‐off between the initial *CO surface coverage and the C─C coupling rate, as governed by the Brønsted–Evans–Polanyi (BEP) linear relationship.[ 135 , 136 ] Therefore, once CO2 molecules are successfully activated and *CO intermediates are generated, two key aspects: 1) enhancing the diffusion of *CO intermediates to ensure their efficient transport from activation sites to coupling‐active sites, and 2) improving the stability of *CO, *COH, and *CHO species to prevent premature protonation into C1 products prior to the coupling process on the catalyst should be considered.[ 10 , 13 , 29 ]

On Cu‐based catalyst surface, the initial surface concentration of *CO intermediate is governed by multiple factors, including crystal facets, surface defects, electronic structures, and the surrounding electrolyte environment. These factors not only play critical roles in the activation and dissociation of CO2 but also significantly influence the behavior of *CO during its migration across the surface. For example, specific facets such as Cu(100) and defect sites such as steps and edges provide active sites that define particular reaction pathways, while the local electric field gradients arising from the electronic structure of the catalyst and the electrolyte environment modulate electron distribution, thereby affecting *CO adsorption, desorption, and diffusion.[ 90 , 137 , 138 , 139 ] Given that these fundamental mechanisms have been widely discussed in previous sections and the literature, in this review, it will primarily focus on the strategies for enhancing transport efficiency. For the tandem catalysts, the construction of multiple complementary active sites enables the spatial separation of *CO activation and transport, thus enhancing the overall migration efficiency.[ 140 , 141 ] Meanwhile, the steric hindrance effect also plays an important role. The strategies on this involve the introduction of specific molecular or surface structures, which could selectively suppress competing *CO reaction pathways other than C─C coupling, thereby further improving the selectivity of ECO2RR toward C2+ products.[ 142 , 143 ] The application of surface molecular engineering to stabilize active centers can also enhance the adsorption and stabilization of key C─C coupling precursors, such as *CHO and *COH. This prevents their premature protonation into C1 products and facilitates their effective transport to C─C coupling sites, thereby improving the overall selectivity toward C2+ products.[ 144 , 145 ]

Tandem Catalysis Establishment over Cu‐Based Catalysts

In the ECO2RR, the PCET process is intrinsically complex, involving multiple steps of intermediate generation, migration, and transformation.[ 14 , 15 , 37 ] Due to the varying binding energies of intermediates at different catalytic sites, it is largely constrained by linear scaling relationships among adsorbates. To overcome these limitations and enable precise control over key reaction pathways, the strategy of tandem catalysis has been proposed.[ 140 , 141 , 146 ] This approach spatially decouples the CO2 reduction process into multiple steps, assigning each step to a distinct active site, thereby disrupting the energy correlation between intermediates and enhancing the selectivity toward C2+ products.[ 21 , 28 ] Unlike conventional doping or synergistic strategies, tandem catalysts often incorporate function‐specific external components into Cu‐based catalysts to achieve the localized enrichment and the ordered migration of key intermediates such as *CO.[ 40 , 141 ] In these systems, certain sites are designated for initial CO2 activation and *CO formation while others are optimized for C─C coupling and further reduction (Figure 9 a,b). Representative tandem catalysis system includes the introduction of noble metals (e.g., Ag), Cu coordination polymers (Cu‐CPs), metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), and metalloporphyrin complexes (MPcs), each serving as functional domains for directing the spatial transport of *CO intermediates.[ 75 , 126 ] In Ag‐based tandem catalysis systems, Ag always exhibits excellent CO‐forming ability. For example, in Cu@Ag/C catalysts, Ag and Cu domains are physically separated on the same support,[ 146 ] where Ag catalyzes the initial CO2‐to‐CO conversion, and the resulting *CO intermediates are enriched in the vicinity of the adjacent Cu sites for subsequent C─C coupling. This spatial arrangement led to a FE of 50.41% for C2H4 in an H‐type cell and 58.03% in a flow cell. Meanwhile, when AgI is employed as the Ag precursor, the released iodide ions can interact with CuO species to form a stable I−‐rich interface, which will prevent the spontaneous reduction of CuO and stabilize Cu+/Cu0 mixed valence states during the ECO2RR. This interfacial stabilization enhances *CO binding and lowers the C─C coupling barrier, thereby boosting C2+ selectivity. In addition, the segmented gas diffusion electrode (s‐GDE) design could further strengthen spatial control of intermediate transport. In this configuration, Ag and Cu catalytic layers can be integrated into distinct segments of the same GDE, allowing CO2 to be selectively reduced to CO at the Ag layer and then transported via the gas phase to the downstream Cu layer.[ 147 ] Similarly, a hierarchically interfaced Cu–Ag tandem electrocatalyst was further designed. By sputtering Ag onto an NC‐modified Cu nanowire array, Cu and Ag were integrated in series to spatially decouple CO2 activation to *CO from the subsequent *CO coupling steps.[ 148 ] Specifically, CO2 is first reduced to CO on the outer Ag layer, after which CO spills over to the adjacent NC‐modified Cu nanowire array. At the Cu/NC interface, on the one hand, the linear *CO adsorption and surface residence are enhanced; on the other hand, H2O dissociation and local PCET are promoted, thereby strengthening C─C coupling and stabilizing the key intermediate *OCCOH. At a current density of 519.0 mA cm−2, FEs of 53.5% for C2H4 and 87.5% for C2+ products are achieved, with a C2+/C1 ratio of 10.42, which significantly outperforms the electrode without the NC layer and the non‐tandem electrode. As such, the increased *CO coverage and residence time on Cu sites can enhance the coupling efficiency. When this approach was applied to a Cu/Fe‐N‐Cs‐GDE tandem catalysis ECO2RR system, a remarkable 90% C2+ FE at j(C2+)>1 A cm−2 was achieved, validating the potential of spatially resolved tandem design (Figure 9d).

Cu coordination polymers (Cu‐CPs) also serve as effective tandem platforms. Zhang et al.[ 151 ] developed a PDI‐Cu/Cu catalyst by integrating isolated Cu–N coordination sites with Cu nanoclusters, where CO2 can be initially reduced to *CO at the atomically dispersed Cu–N sites and then transferred to the Cu cluster surface for C─C coupling. Structural control studies showed that this tandem configuration doubled the C2H4 FE (31.4%) compared to a non‐tandem PDI/Cu reference (15.5%) at −1.9 V. Meanwhile, for a MOF‐based system, Yan et al.[ 152 ] assembled a tandem catalyst comprising a Cu(II)‐MOF supported on Cu foil (CF), in which the MOF was constructed using 1,3,5‐tris(1‐imidazolyl) benzene (L1) and 4,4′‐biphenyldicarboxylic acid (L2) ligands, forming a highly porous framework with abundant unsaturated Cu sites (Figure 9c). These sites can effectively activate CO2 and cleave C═O bonds to form *CO, which is confined within the MOF pores and directed to downstream Cu(220) crystal planes on the CF substrate. Here, the CF not only provides structural support but also serves as the primary active surface for C─C coupling and C2 product formation. As a result, the system achieved a C2H4 FE of 48.6% with negligible CH4 generation under −1.11 V. In a metalloporphyrin‐based tandem system, an in situ electropolymerized cobalt porphyrin (EP‐CoP) layer was deposited on Cu‐based catalysts, creating a CO‐generating interface.[ 149 ] Here, the CoN4 sites within EP‐CoP can catalyze CO2‐to‐CO conversion, and the resulting *CO is guided to the underlying Cu surface for dimerization. DFT calculations confirmed that CoN4 moieties enhance *CO coverage on Cu, facilitating C─C coupling (Figure 9e,f). The EP‐CoP/Cu system delivered a current density of 726 mA cm−2 at −0.9 V, with a C2 product FE of 76.8% and a C2H4 FE of 43%. Meanwhile, a Cu‐porphyrin/phthalocyanine organic framework (CuPOF) demonstrated a dual‐active‐site configuration for the tandem catalysis,[ 150 ] in which Cu(II)‐porphyrin (CuPor) and Cu(II)‐bipyridine (CuBpy) moieties were co‐assembled on a CNT scaffold, forming an atomically dispersed ultrathin CuPOF membrane. Here, the CuPor and CuBpy units catalyze CO2‐to‐*CO formation, followed by confined‐space migration to adjacent Cu2O nanoparticles, where C─C coupling and chain propagation occur. As a result, this catalyst achieved a C2H4 FE of 71.0% at −1.1 V, outperforming most reported systems (Figure 9g).

In summary, tandem catalysis provides a promising pathway to circumvent the limitations imposed by linear scaling relationships with spatially decoupling intermediate formation, transport, and coupling. Its success hinges on the rational design of generation–migration–coupling sequences across discrete active centers. Future efforts should focus on quantifying intermediate migration dynamics, tuning the microenvironment for directional transport, and integrating in situ spectroscopic techniques with theoretical modeling to enable comprehensive control over C2+ product selectivity in ECO2RR.

Steric Hindrance Effect

As a representative transition metal, Cu can be spatially regulated through molecular or structural modifications to enhance its catalytic stability and selectivity in ECO2RR.[ 16 , 21 ] Beyond conventional electronic modulation using electron‐donating or electron‐withdrawing ligands, spatial confinement strategies, particularly those involving surface‐anchored organic molecules or morphological engineering, can effectively govern the transport, accumulation, and transformation of reaction intermediates.[ 31 , 38 ] In several systems, the introduction of bulky or spatially extended ligands onto Cu surfaces leads to the formation of sterically crowded microenvironments that restrict proton (*H) access. This suppresses undesired hydrogenation pathways and instead promotes *CO‐*CO coupling. Notably, even ligands lacking explicit acid–base functional groups can influence *CO transport kinetics by altering the geometry and accessibility of surface active sites. These steric effects result in the localized *CO‐enriched domains that stabilize key intermediates and favor the formation of multi‐carbon (C2+) products.[ 153 ] In addition to ligand‐induced confinement, structural strategies, such as the design of ordered porous frameworks and tailored surface topologies, have shown significant potential in directing intermediate transport. For instance, it is found that a 3D ordered porous Cu2O (3DOP Cu2O) architecture with interconnected and uniform microchannels can facilitate the accumulation and guide the migration of *CO intermediates while minimizing competitive *H adsorption.[ 154 ] This spatial separation effectively reduces the probability of C1 byproduct formation and enhances the selectivity toward C─C coupled products (Figure 10a,b). In situ spectroscopic studies further reveal that such confinement structures can elevate the local pH, thereby favoring *CO‐*CO dimerization over hydrogen evolution.[ 154 , 155 ]

Importantly, the pathways of generating CH4 and C2H4 can be controlled by the steric hindrance of the ligand side groups, and the C2H4 generation pathway is more sensitive than that for CH4.

Further modification can be performed by adding the functionalized groups at the ligand parts. For instance, dipropynen groups have been selected to modify NHC (N‐heterocyclic carbenes) ligand to construct copper nanoclusters precisely, and the unique coordination pattern between the Cu atom and ligand groups facilitates the exposure of catalytic sites.[ 157 , 158 ] Besides the influence of steric hindrance has also been proven, it is also found that two kinds of copper nanoclusters within different spatial configurations named Cu17a and Cu17b showed an obvious variation of product distributions caused by the steric hindrance, Here, the minimized steric hindrance in Cu17a caused superior CO2 adsorption and activation capabilities compared to Cu17b, as evidenced by its lower free energy for CO2 adsorption and subsequent intermediate formation. The surface ligands growing in the structure of Cu17a facilitate substrate binding and lower the energy barriers for *COOH and *CO formations (Figure 10c), thereby enhancing the CO2‐to‐CO conversion process. Moreover, the energetically favorable dimerization of *CO and *CHO intermediates promotes the C─C coupling toward C2H4 formation, while the hydrogenation pathway toward CH4 remains kinetically hindered. These theoretical findings correlated well with the experimental results, highlighting the greater tendency of Cu17a for multi‐carbon product formation and especially its higher selectivity toward C2H4.

Generally, tailoring catalyst architecture and surface properties can dramatically boost ECO2RR toward multi‐carbon (C2+) products by promoting *CO‐*CO coupling while suppressing undesired C1 pathways. For example, a 3D ordered porous Cu2O cubic‐octahedron (3DOP Cu2O‐CO) with interconnected uniform channels was designed to accumulate *CO intermediates during the ECO2RR, mitigating *H attack that would generate C1 by‐products, and thus facilitating C─C coupling.[ 142 , 147 ] In situ spectroscopy and simulations reveal that the 3DOP architecture elevates the local pH, further favoring *CO‐*CO coupling over H2 evolution (Figure 10a). As a result, at an industrially relevant current density (j) of −1.2 A cm−2, 3DOP Cu2O‐CO delivered an 81.7% C2+ FE with a C2+ partial j of −0.89 A cm−2, corresponding to a C2+ production rate of 2.92 mmol h−1 cm−2.

Surface Molecular Engineering

In the ECO2RR, the coupling of *CO intermediates is a pivotal step toward the formation of multi‐carbon (C2+) products. However, *CO species is not only limited by the surface diffusion rate but also prone to protonation by *H, leading to undesired C1 byproducts such as CO and formate. Therefore, the thermodynamic and kinetic stabilization of *CO prior to C─C coupling plays a critical role in determining whether the reaction proceeds along a C1 or C2 generation pathway.[ 38 , 88 , 156 ] Although Cuδ+ sites have been widely recognized for their ability to stabilize *CO intermediate and facilitate C─C coupling, Cu+ species are easily reduced to metallic Cu0 under highly negative potential, thereby compromising the stabilizing function.[ 22 , 31 ] Given that *CO dimerization can proceed via multiple parallel pathways such as *CO‐*CO, *CO‐*CHO, and *CO‐*COH. Some surface molecular engineering strategies aiming to enhance the transport to promote the coupling of *CO, such as the in situ introduction of functional groups or molecular ligands, have attracted considerable attention for their ability to stabilize Cu sites. Some works prove that metal could be stabilized by motifs (M‐N x /M‐O x ) support‐engineering framework through establishing defect/heteroatom‐rich supports, regulating charge redistribution, and suppressing migration/reconstruction under cathodic bias, which aligns with the interface‐establishment strategy.[ 9 , 22 , 159 ] Furthermore, specific ligands or functional groups exhibit preferential interactions with the targeted intermediates (e.g., *CHO, *COH), thereby regulating *CO adsorption geometry (bridge vs atop) to steer reaction pathways, then enhancing their stability while minimizing the introduction of competing adsorption sites that could divert the reaction toward C1 products.[ 153 , 154 ] Collectively, these approaches represent the promising routes for selectively promoting C2+ product formation in ECO2RR (Figure 11a,b). For example, a molecular‐level in situ modification strategy using tannic acid (TA) was employed to reconstruct Cu‐based catalysts, successfully steering the ECO2RR toward C2H4 formation with a remarkable C2H4 partial current density of 497.2 mA cm−2 and a FE of 63.6%, which is ≈6.5 times higher than that of the pristine Cu catalyst in a flow cell configuration.[ 80 , 160 ] In situ X‐ray absorption spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy revealed that hydroxyl groups in TA stabilized the Cuδ+/Cu0 interfacial structure during the ECO2RR, thereby facilitating C─C coupling. More importantly, these hydroxyl functionalities formed hydrogen bonds with *COH intermediates, enhancing their stability and promoting C2H4 generation. In addition, TA molecules also tuned the binding strength of *CO and *COH intermediates through hydrogen bonding, effectively promoting *CO‐*COH dimerization and boosting C2H4 selectivity (Figure 11c).

The types of key intermediates generated are also influenced by the nature of surface‐modifying molecules. For example, a Cu catalyst modified with an amino acid exhibited a stabilizing effect on CHO* intermediates. In comparison, the total FE for C x H y products (including C2H4 and C3H6) reached 34.1% at −1.9 V on the glycine‐modified Cu NW film, nearly double that of the bare Cu NW film electrode (17.8%).[ 159 ] This enhancement is attributed to the excellent adsorption stability of the amino acid on the Cu surface, where the *CHO intermediate is stabilized through interactions with the ─NH3 + group of the adsorbed zwitterionic glycine. Computational studies revealed that glycine adsorbs on Cu(110) via its ─NH2 end while its ─NH3 + group interacts with *CHO in a hydrogen‐bond‐like manner, lowering the reaction barrier to approx≈0.20 eV, thereby promoting the formation of hydrocarbons.

The stabilization of *CO intermediate also facilitates its subsequent transport and coupling processes, thereby enhancing the conversion of CO2 toward C2+ products. In this regard, constructing the metal organic frameworks (MOFs) composed of organic ligands coordinating with metal ions or metal clusters is an effective way to achieve this purpose.[ 75 , 126 ] For example, a polycarboxylic acid ligand (H6TDPAT) 2,4,6‐tris(3,5‐dicarboxylphenylamino)‐1,3,5‐triazine, including an extended‐size nitrogen heterocyclic was selected to construct Cu‐TDPAT MOF structure,[ 80 ] and the optimized catalyst attained an C2H4 FE of 44% at 500 mA cm−2, with a partial current density of C2H4 of 220 mA cm−2. The traditional methods like magnetron sputtering or co‐calcination with organic compounds can successfully make nitrogen‐doped carbon (CN) coated into Cu catalysts, which shows a stabilization effect for *CO. In situ Raman and IR spectroscopy further confirmed the mechanism of CN coating for the stabilization of *CO intermediates. DFT calculations revealed that the Cu‐Cu2O/CN structure has stronger *CO adsorption ability due to the synergistic function between abundant Cu⁺ sites and the CN layer, thereby facilitating C─C coupling.[ 160 ]

Furthermore, the use of MOF structure enables the successful construction of noble‐metal‐doped Cu/Cu2O catalysts, yielding unexpected improvements in ECO2RR performance. For example, the Cu‐BTC MOF framework allowed single‐atom Pd incorporation, which effectively shifted the product distribution from C1 to C2 products (Figure 11d ).[ 161 ] Here, the Pd dopants played a dual role in stabilizing both *CO and *CHO intermediates, thereby promoting asymmetric coupling and significantly enhancing C2H4 selectivity. Under a current density of −400 mA cm−2, the Pd‐SA Cu/Cu2O catalyst achieved a C2+ FE exceeding 70%, representing a fivefold increase over Cu catalysts enriched with Cu(111) facets (Figure 11e,f). Similarly, a pyrazolate‐stabilized asymmetric Ni/Cu site constructed on the Cu1Ni‐BDP MOF framework exhibited remarkable structural stability under harsh electrochemical conditions.[ 162 ] It is found that the Cu1Ni clusters showed moderate *CO binding energy, balancing intermediate stabilization and C─C coupling acceleration. Notably, symmetric *COH‐*COH and *CH2‐*CH intermediates exhibited low Gibbs free energy barriers (ΔG = −0.08 and −0.01 eV, respectively), both regarded as rate‐limiting steps in C2+ product formation (Figure 11g). As a result, the enhanced stabilization of *CO and *COH intermediates successfully improved C2H4 selectivity, with the catalyst achieving 52.7% C2H4 selectivity at −1.3 V versus RHE and maintaining stable operation at 500 mA cm−2 for 25 h.

*CO Coupling Enhancing

As stated above, in the ECO2RR, the adsorbed *CO intermediate is widely regarded as a pivotal species for the generation of multi‐carbon (C2+) products since *CO can be stably bound to the catalyst surface and migrate to adjacent copper (Cu) active sites to undergo coupling, thereby forming C─C bonds and enabling carbon chain growth. Coupling between *CO species can proceed via two pathways: a symmetric route (dimerization of two *CO to form a *CO‐*CO intermediate) and an asymmetric route (cross‐coupling between *CO and a partially hydrogenated species such as *CHO or *COH).[ 163 ] The efficiency of this C─C coupling step is largely governed by the surface concentration of *CO on the catalyst, and especially higher *CO coverage lowers the kinetic barrier for coupling and increases the probability of C─C bond formation.[ 77 , 88 , 89 ] In addition, the catalyst must possess an appropriate electronic structure and spatial arrangement of active sites to stabilize key intermediates and support the approach of two *CO species for coupling. To promote *CO coupling, two major strategies have emerged in recent years. The first one is to construct heterogeneous interfaces or dual‐site catalysts by pairing Cu with another material (such as a metal oxide or the second metal) to create synergistic adjacent sites. In such systems, one site can generate or supply *CO intermediate while a neighboring Cu site facilitates their coupling, collectively lowering the C─C bond formation barrier and thereby improving selectivity toward C2+ products. Dual‐site synergy has achieved significant enhancements in multi‐carbon product yields, but requires careful design to ensure interface compatibility and long‐term stability.[ 21 , 28 , 40 ] The second strategy involves microenvironment modification, whereby the local reaction environment at the catalyst interface (e.g., local pH, ion distribution, hydrophobicity) is tuned to enrich *CO and stabilize intermediates, thereby increasing the local *CO coverage. This approach can boost C─C coupling without significantly altering the catalyst's bulk composition, but its effectiveness often depends on fine control of electrolyte conditions and operating parameters. Notably, *CO coupling produces oxygenated C2 intermediates (e.g., *CH2CHO and *CH2COH) that serve as branching points toward C2H4 and ethanol.[ 39 , 83 , 95 ] In particular, *CH2CHO tends to yield C2H4 via ─OH removal (C─O bond cleavage), whereas further hydrogenation of *CH2COH produces C2H5OH.[ 164 ] Therefore, controlling the C─O dehydroxylation pathway largely dictates the final product distribution. Various strategies have been proposed to promote C─O bond cleavage. For example, tuning the catalyst's electronic properties to create an electron‐rich surface can weaken the C─O bond in C2 intermediates, facilitating their deoxygenation.[ 29 , 111 , 165 , 166 ] Introducing oxophilic sites or oxygen vacancies on the catalyst can help capture and remove *OH groups, thereby promoting the dehydration of *CH2CHO to yield C2H4.[ 164 ] Additionally, employing alkaline electrolytes or otherwise increasing the local concentration of OH− in the reaction environment can accelerate the removal of ─OH (as H2O), further favoring the C2H4 generation pathway. Collectively, these approaches provide new avenues to enhance C2H4 selectivity in ECO2RR while allowing better control over ethanol production.

Microenvironmental Modulation

Compared to conventional strategies that focus on tuning the intrinsic properties of catalysts[ 18 , 23 , 39 , 93 , 111 , 122 ] or electrolytes,[ 20 , 58 ] recent efforts have increasingly emphasized the modulation of the local reaction environment, which is commonly referred to as the microenvironment, to enhance the selectivity of the ECO2RR. Representative approaches include adjusting the applied potential,[ 41 , 68 ] tuning the local pH,[ 154 ] engineering surface hydrophobicity,[ 54 ] and modifying the composition and distribution of electrolyte ions.[ 167 , 168 ] Among these strategies, surface wettability can regulate the relative affinity of CO2 and electrolyte species at the catalyst interface, thereby altering the formation and adsorption balance between *CO and *H intermediates, ultimately influencing product distribution.[ 169 , 170 ] In addition, catalysts with unconventional architectures or tailored surface features can generate localized electric field gradients at the nanoscale, promoting the accumulation of *CO intermediates and finally facilitating C─C coupling pathways.[ 68 , 171 ] Meanwhile, the regulation of local pH also plays a critical role in steering product selectivity. For example, constructing weakly alkaline domains near the catalyst surface can suppress the formation of *H intermediates, thereby minimizing the premature hydrogenation of *CO into undesired C1 products but favoring the generation of C2+ species.[ 154 , 172 ] Although the advantages of microenvironment engineering in enhancing ECO2RR activity and selectivity have been well documented, the underlying mechanisms, particularly the enrichment of *CO and its correlation with coupling pathways, remain insufficiently understood at the intermediate level. This section, therefore, aims to provide a mechanistic overview of how microenvironmental modifications influence ECO2RR pathways and intermediate dynamics.

Surface Wettability

The introduction of hydrophobic microenvironments near Cu‐based catalysts has emerged as a powerful strategy to enhance the ECO2RR toward C2H4 production. In aqueous media, such hydrophobic interfaces promote the formation of a stable gas–liquid–solid boundary, which increases the local CO2 concentration, reduces the accessibility of water to the catalyst surface, and ultimately improves C2H4 selectivity (Figure 12a ).[ 169 , 173 ] Especially, this approach has proven particularly effective for improving the local accumulation of *CO intermediates, which are crucial for C─C coupling processes.[ 174 , 175 ] Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of polymer‐based hydrophobic modifications in creating triple‐phase boundaries on Cu‐based catalysts. By blocking the transport of H2O molecules to the catalyst surface, polymers such as polyacrylic acid (PAA), Nafion (containing mixed hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains), and fully hydrophobic fluorinated ethylene propylene (FEP) can enhance both the activity and selectivity of ECO2RR (Figure 12c).[ 175 , 176 ] Among these, FEP‐coated catalysts have shown exceptional performance, with C2+ partial current density exceeding 600 mA cm−2 and FE of ≈77% at −0.76 V versus RHE. Importantly, this performance enhancement is attributed not only to the suppression of the HER by the hydrophobic barrier but also to an increase in *CO chemical adsorption, as revealed by in situ infrared spectroscopy. The high hydrophobicity of FEP selectively allows CO2 vapor to penetrate the electrode, leading to a locally enriched *CO concentration near the coupling sites, thus facilitating C─C coupling reactions (Figure 12b).

Similarly, Cu‐based catalysts coated with perfluorosulfonic acid (PFSA) ionomers have exhibited strong hydrophobic behavior. Catalysts with 3D confined interfacial biphasic hydrophobic (CIBH) architectures have achieved C2H4 FEs of 65–75% with a peak partial current density of 1.34 A cm−2, and cathodic energy efficiency of 46.3%.[ 179 ] Exceptional performance has been ascribed to the surface metal–organic molecule‐hydrophobic group assemblies, which not only prevent water contact but also establish highly efficient CO2 transport channels. Notably, CO2 diffusion concentration at the surface was reported to increase by 400‐fold, creating a *CO‐rich environment that drives C─C coupling process. Long‐chain alkanethiol coatings have also been employed to increase C2H4 yields. These organic modifiers can form hydrophobic layers on the Cu surface, enriching *CO near the active sites and stabilizing catalytically favorable Cu facets. Hierarchical CuO nanoelectrodes modified with thiol functional groups have achieved C2H4 FE of up to 79.5%. In situ spectroscopic analyses showed that thiol layers enhanced CO2 transport to the catalyst and increased surface coverage of *CO intermediates. Crucially, the thiol groups can help stabilize the Cu(100) facet, which is particularly conducive to C─C coupling. DFT calculations have further revealed that the thiol‐treated Cu(100) surface exhibits a reduced energy barrier for *CO‐*CHO coupling and promotes CO dimerization, explaining the enhanced C2H4 selectivity. Additional studies have highlighted how tuning local wettability through alkanethiol modification to shift the contact angle from ≈43° (hydrophilic) to ≈156° (superhydrophobic), which alters ethanol/C2H4 selectivity. Meanwhile, as CO2/CO supply increases and H availability diminishes, the relative influence of *CO coupling becomes saturated, and proton availability emerges as the key factor controlling C2H4 formation.[ 169 , 180 ] Under optimally tuned hydrophobicity, C2+ product FE up to 86.1% and the partial C2+ current of 321 mA cm−2 were achieved. Beyond molecular and polymeric surface modifications, engineering catalyst layer architecture into porous hydrophobic structures also improved CO2 utilization and C─C coupling.[ 177 ] A notable example involves superhydrophobic OH‐Cu catalysts that enhance gas diffusion and CO2 transport to active sites. In acidic electrolytes (pH = 1), these catalysts achieved a remarkable 87% FE for C2+ products at a partial current density of −1.6 A cm−2. Here, the hydrophobic scaffold effectively prevents electrode flooding and maintains a stable CO2 supply, thus enabling efficient C─C coupling reactions. Kinetic analyses indicated that C2 formation in this acidic system followed a second‐order reaction, consistent with a *CO‐*CO coupling rate‐determining step (Figure 12d).

Without relying on surface molecular modifications, hydrophobicity was constructed solely based on the intrinsic properties of the Cu2O material. Liu et al.[ 178 ] have developed Cu2O spheres with multilevel pore distributions, including 8–10 nm mesopores and macropores surpassing 60 nm. Through the formation of a “gas‐liquid‐solid” three‐phase interface, macropores create a different water concentration containing gradient microenvironment from the interior to the exterior (Figure 12e). In addition, the three‐phase interface establishes a highly hydrophobic microenvironment for suppressing HER, whereas the hollow mesopores in the interface provide abundant mass transfer channels to decide product distribution between CO and C2H4. At a low current density of 100 mA cm−2, the CO FE reached 96.97%. In situ experiments and theoretical calculations showed that the pure Cu2O phase is unfavorable for the adsorption of *CO intermediates at lower potentials, while the weak protonation inhibits HER, thereby promoting CO production. Conversely, after applying a higher negative potential, pure Cu2O has been reduced and formed the Cu0/Cu+ interface, and as such, strong protonation generates locally elevated concentrations of *CO and *COOH intermediates, which enhance C─C coupling and deep hydrogenation, ultimately improving selectivity toward C2+ products.

In summary, hydrophobic surface engineering, whether through polymer coatings, molecular monolayers, or porous architecture, plays a critical role in enhancing ECO2RR selectivity toward C2H4 by creating CO2‐rich but H2O‐lean reaction environments. These strategies elevate local *CO concentrations, stabilize key catalytic facets, and suppress undesired HER pathways, thereby promoting efficient C─C coupling. Mechanistic insights from in situ spectroscopy and DFT calculations consistently support the central role of the hydrophobic interface in modulating reaction pathways, underscoring its importance as a design principle for next‐generation ECO2RR catalysts.

Internal Electric Field

As stated above, the ECO2RR to C2H4 is highly sensitive to the microenvironment surrounding the catalyst. Among the key tunable parameters, the local electric field plays a pivotal role by polarizing reactants and intermediates, concentrating key species, and even modulating the electronic structure of the catalyst (Figure 13 a).[ 26 , 27 , 137 , 138 , 139 ] Recent efforts have focused on harnessing this effect through three main strategies: designing nanostructured catalysts with high‐curvature “tip effect” to amplify local fields, constructing heterojunction interfaces to generate internal electric fields, and tailoring the electrolyte via selective cation species or ionic liquids to regulate the electrical double layer (EDL).[ 181 , 182 , 183 ] These approaches have been demonstrated to significantly enhance the C─C coupling of *CO intermediates on Cu‐based catalysts, thereby improving C2H4 production (Figure 13b,c).[ 138 , 139 ]

For Cu‐based catalysts, the sharp tip structures and high curvature features could dramatically amplify the local electrostatic field by concentrating free electrons at the regions of highest curvature, where the field intensity can exceed that of flat surfaces by several orders of magnitude.[ 181 ] This so‐called “lightning‐rod effect” leads to two key consequences: (i) local enrichment of cations (such as K+) and CO2 molecules near the tip and (ii) a reduction in the activation barriers for *CO adsorption and coupling.[ 185 , 186 , 187 ] For example, an ordered Cu nanoneedle array catalyst achieved a FE of 59% for combined C2H5 and C2H5OH at −1.2 V, significantly outperforming catalysts with irregular or planar surfaces. Finite element simulations confirmed that the aligned needle architecture generates highly intensified local fields, driving K+ enrichment on the Cu surface. This effect in turn stabilizes *CO adsorption and enhances the C─C coupling step, directly promoting the formation of multi‐carbon products. These findings highlight that nano‐architecture Cu into high‐curvature morphologies can profoundly tailor the reaction microenvironment and product selectivity. Moreover, specific nanostructures featuring the confined nanocavities or gaps have shown similarly pronounced effects on *CO coupling.[ 171 ] Here, within 1–2 nm nanogaps, overlapping electrical double layers can amplify the local electric field strength inside the cavity, shifting the reaction pathway from asymmetric *CHO‐*CO coupling toward direct *CO‐*CO dimerization. In other words, the stronger electric field in the compact layer reduces the coupling energy barrier, increases *CO surface coverage, and facilitates the coupling between two *CO intermediates, ultimately boosting C2H4 formation (Figure 13d,e).

Enhancement of the local electric field not only promotes CO2 reduction but also suppresses the competing HER. For example, systematic investigations using Cu x O nano‐bipyramidal catalysts revealed a clear correlation among tip curvature, local field intensity, and CO2RR performance (Figure 13h).[ 171 , 184 , 188 ] Here, catalysts with the smallest tip curvature (e.g., L‐Cu x O‐HC) exhibited the strongest local fields and achieved a C2H4 FE of ≈60%.[ 185 ] The intensified field promotes the preferential adsorption of K+ cations near the catalyst surface, reducing local H+ coverage, effectively suppressing HER, and creating a favorable microenvironment for C─C coupling in ECO2RR (Figure 13f,g). In heterojunction catalysts, the introduction of an internal electric field at the interface provides another effective route for optimizing C2H4 selectivity. Doping Cu‐based catalysts with metallic or semiconducting materials can generate internal fields at the interface due to charge redistribution, which modulates surface electronic properties and *CO behavior. For instance, at the Cu‐Ag interface, the work function difference drives electron transfer from Cu to Ag, creating an electron‐depleted Cu surface with Cu+‐like characteristics that can strengthen *CO adsorption.[ 181 ] Studies have shown that an optimally Ag‐doped Cu catalyst (≈6% Ag) can achieve ≈60% C2H4 FE at −0.7 V. This enhancement is attributed to the electronic effects at the Ag/Cu interface, where the charge transfer lowers the energy barrier for CO–CO coupling and substantially increases *CO surface coverage, thus promoting C2H4 formation.

Similarly, the pronounced interface effects have been observed in metal‐oxide heterojunctions. At the Cu/Cu2O interface, charge accumulation or dipole formation leads to the stabilization of Cu+ species, thereby enhancing *CO surface coverage.[ 63 , 189 ] Leveraging this mechanism, researchers have developed an oxygen‐rich ultrathin CuO/Cu catalyst that forms abundant Cu/Cu2O heterojunctions under ECO2RR conditions. Here, the polar interface and preserved Cu⁺ sites stabilize the *OCCOH intermediate, a key precursor for C─C coupling. As a result, this catalyst achieved an impressive C2H4 FE of 84.5% in neutral electrolyte, maintaining excellent performance over 55 h of continuous operation.

Local pH

The local reaction pH, as a critical microenvironmental parameter, also plays a decisive role in governing the surface coverage of *CO intermediates on Cu‐based catalysts since the relative concentrations of *CO and *H directly determine the competition between C─C coupling and protonation pathways.[ 138 , 154 ] Under the conditions where the local pH at the Cu/electrolyte interface is low, an excess of protons in the electrolyte can promote the protonation of *CO intermediates, steering the reaction toward the formation of C1 products such as CH4 or H2, thereby disfavoring C2H4 production. In contrast, when the local pH becomes more alkaline, the increased surface coverage of OH− and the reduced proton availability will effectively suppress H2 evolution and CH4 formation, allowing *CO to accumulate on the catalyst surface and facilitating its participation in C─C coupling process (Figure 14a ).[ 190 , 191 ] Therefore, without altering the bulk electrolyte pH, internal modifications to the catalyst itself, such as constructing nanostructures or introducing surface coatings, could offer an effective strategy to regulate the local pH, tune the relative concentrations of *CO and *H intermediates, and thereby significantly promote *CO‐*CO dimerization, ultimately enhancing the yield of C2H4 (Figure 14e).[ 77 , 192 ]

By retaining oxidized Cu species (e.g., Cu+ or Cu2O) within Cu‐based catalysts, oxide‐derived Cu (OD‐Cu) materials can not only modulate the local reaction environment but also significantly influence intermediate concentrations and reaction pathways during ECO2RR.[ 194 , 196 ] For example, an ultrathin CuO‐derived nanoplates array (DVL‐Cu) exhibited remarkable C2H4 selectivity and stability, achieving a C2H4 FE up to 84.5%, maintaining a stable operation for ≈55 h in a neutral KCl electrolyte flow cell, and delivering a full‐cell C2H4 energy efficiency of 27.6% at 200 mA cm−2 in a membrane electrode assembly (MEA) electrolyzer.[ 196 ] Characterizations revealed that the Cu/CuO interface provides a locally alkaline environment and an optimized interfacial electronic structure, which together suppress the reductive dissolution of Cu+ species and reduce *H availability, thereby effectively suppressing the competing HER. Mechanistic analysis further indicated that the Cu/Cu2O interface stabilizes key intermediates such as *COOH and *CO‐*COH, lowers the C─C coupling energy barrier, and synergistically promotes *CO‐*CO dimerization under the elevated local pH conditions, ultimately enhancing C2H4 formation. This explains why oxide‐derived Cu electrodes consistently outperform smooth Cu surfaces, and why their intrinsic interfacial effects and optimized microenvironment effectively enhance *CO coverage and C2 product selectivity.

In addition, thin organic layers can be employed to modify Cu surfaces, acting as built‐in microenvironment regulators that control local proton and CO2 accessibility at the interface without altering the bulk electrolyte pH.[ 195 ] For instance, Cu‐based catalysts modified via in situ grafting of polyaromatic thin films achieved a C2+ FE up to 75% in neutral media and retained ≈65% C2+ FE even under strongly acidic electrolyte conditions (pH ≈ 1). Here, these polyaromatic coatings regulated interfacial hydrophilicity and ion transport, reducing proton flux to the Cu surface and maintaining a locally elevated pH at the coupling sites, thereby enabling effective *CO accumulation and facilitating C─C coupling (Figure 14c,d).

Beyond constructing homogenous or heterogeneous metal interfaces, introducing specific functional groups onto the Cu surface can further combine the local pH effects with electronic and chemical tuning of intermediate adsorption. For example, Cu nanowire catalysts coated with a monolayer of fluorinated graphdiyne (GDY) achieved a C2H4 yield of 40.9% and a C2+ product selectivity of 73.15% ± 2.5%.[ 193 ] Studies showed that the fluorinated GDY not only modulates the Cu surface electronic structure through its electron‐withdrawing effects but also promotes the formation of *OH surface coverage, generating a locally alkaline interface favorable for further *CO adsorption (Figure 14b). Moreover, a high *Cu‐OH/*CO ratio can assist in directing *CO protonation at optimal sites to generate *CO‐*CHO rather than over‐hydrogenating *CO to CH4. This work highlights that metal–organic interfaces can synergistically regulate both the electronic structure and the local alkaline microenvironment, effectively steering the reaction selectivity toward C2‐generating pathways.

Deoxygenation Regulation

Depending on the type of *CO dimerization, including symmetric or asymmetric one, the resulting *CH2CHO or *CH2CH2OH intermediates follow distinct C2 product‐generating pathways, either undergoing further hydrogenation to ethanol or dehydroxylation to C2H4. Critically, the stability of the C─O bonds within these coupled intermediates and products determines whether the subsequent hydrogenation proceeds smoothly with C─OH removal, ultimately dictating whether C2H4 or C2H5OH is favored (Figure 15a ).[ 83 , 95 , 120 ] In the competition between C2H4 and C2H5OH formation, the introduction of specific metal dopants (e.g., Al, Gd, and Rh) has been shown to effectively modulate the C─O bond stability in *CO coupling products, thereby steering the reaction pathway.[ 197 , 198 ] For example, Al doping, owing to its Lewis acidity, can enhance the coordination to C─O bonds in *CH2CHO and *CH2CH2OH intermediates on the Cu surface, significantly stabilizing these bonds, suppressing C─O cleavage, and promoting the hydrogenation pathway toward *OC2H5 and ultimately ethanol.[ 199 ] In contrast, Gd doping enriches the electron density at Cu sites, strengthening Cu‐*O‐CHCH2interactions and lowering the energy barrier for C─O bond dissociation, thus promoting the dehydroxylation process that leads to C2H4. In a Gd‐Cu2O catalysis system, this electronic effect can significantly facilitate the cleavage of the weakened C─O bonds in *CO dimer products. Similarly, doping with other electron‐rich atoms (e.g., Ce and Rh) exhibits analogous effects, enhancing C─O bond scission in coupled intermediates under proton‐assisted conditions.[ 200 , 201 ] Notably, single‐atom Rh doping not only tunes the Cu─O binding strength but also optimizes the C─O bond cleavage pathways within *OC‐COH intermediates.[ 202 ] The optimal Rh‐Cu catalyst achieves an C2H4 FE of ≈61% and an C2H4‐to‐ethanol FE ratio of ≈4.5. DFT calculations and operando spectroscopic studies revealed that Rh incorporation enhances Cu─O interactions, making the C─O bond in *OC‐COH more susceptible to cleavage upon surface proton attack, thus promoting dihydroxylation (Figure 15b).

In addition, constructing confined composite Cu architecture has proven effective in directing *CO coupling products toward the C2H4 generating pathway. For instance, a Cu‐based catalyst embedded within a cyanamide framework can leverage the isolated Cu0‐Cu+ dual sites, which tightly bind *CO intermediates and facilitate electronic delocalization, thereby promoting the dehydroxylation step of the *CHCOH intermediate.[ 66 , 203 ] Similarly, the quasi‐1D coordination polymer Cu(OH)BTA (Cu atoms with deprotonated 1,2,3‐benzotriazole) provides a rigid molecular scaffold that stabilizes isolated dual Cu sites under CO2RR conditions, preventing site aggregation and maintaining precise Cu–Cu distances (≈5.7 Å) optimal for C─C coupling.[ 203 ] In this system, the confined geometry not only enhances *CO adsorption in an atop configuration but also enables a low‐barrier (0.82 V) coupling of *CHO with *CO to form *OCCHO, while suppressing side pathways such as CH4 formation. Operando spectroscopic evidence and DFT calculations confirm that both cyanamide‐confined Cu0‐Cu+ sites and Cu(OH)BTA dual sites direct electron density toward adsorbates rather than catalyst reduction, ensuring structural stability and persistent high C2 selectivity (Figure 15c,d). These examples highlight a unifying principle: spatially confined, electronically complementary Cu dual sites can simultaneously stabilize key intermediates and lower the activation barriers for C─C coupling or dehydroxylation, enabling precise control over the ethylene‐to‐ethanol selectivity balance.

Additionally, a grain boundary‐enriched Cu‐based catalyst can achieve directional production of C2H4 by selectively protonating the coupling intermediate *CH2CHO.[ 164 , 204 , 205 ] Here, the adsorption strength of Cu atoms toward *OH groups regulates the stability of *CH2CHO and controls the cleavage of the C─O bond. Low‐coordinated amorphous Cu sites with strong OH− adsorption can facilitate the C─O bond dissociation in *CH2CHO, thereby promoting the formation of C2H4. In a 25 cm−2 MEA configuration, the Cu‐3 catalyst achieved a remarkable C2H4 FE of 63.4 ± 1.5% at a high current of 12.5 A. Additionally, DFT calculations have been employed to further investigate the impact of C─O bond stability on product distribution. On Cu(100) surfaces, the competition between C2H4 and ethanol during the ECO2RR will be significantly influenced by C─O bond cleavage. *CO‐*CO coupling typically occurs first due to its favorable kinetics. However, at high *CO coverage, alternative coupling routes such as *CO‐*CHO or *CO‐*COH will become more prevalent, though kinetically hindered. Beyond coupling, *CO‐derived species may undergo C─O bond dissociation. For example, the cleavage of the C─O bond in the reaction *CH2CH2OH → *CH2CH2 + *OH is thermodynamically favorable, leading the reaction pathway to bifurcate toward C2H4 formation (Figure 15e,f). Furthermore, during the final protonation steps of *CH2CH2OH, the transition‐state barrier for C─O bond dissociation remains relatively low. When the carbon bonded to the oxygen atom is fully hydrogenated to form species such as O‐(CH2‐CH x ) or HO‐(CH2‐CH x )‐, the O─C bond cleavage will exhibit notably low activation barriers. Thus, the facile elimination of the O─H group from these intermediates also contributes to the preferential formation of C2H4.

Summary and Outlook

This review summarizes the reaction pathway for the ECO2RR to C2H4, covering the initial CO2 adsorption and activation, the formation of *CO intermediates, and the subsequent C─C coupling steps. Given the pivotal role of *CO behavior on the catalyst surface in determining product distribution, this review places particular emphasis on mechanistic strategies that enhance the generation and accumulation of *CO. Especially, constructing mixed‐valence interfaces (e.g., Cu+/Cu0) to generate structural heterogeneity can effectively modulate the local electronic structure and adsorption geometry, thereby facilitating CO2 activation and *CO formation. Meanwhile, tuning surface crystallography, such as exposing high‐index facets, can increase the surface *CO coverage, lower the dimerization energy barrier, and steer the reaction pathway toward C2H4 production. In addition, defect engineering represents another important strategy, in which the introduction of grain boundaries, steps, edges, and oxygen vacancies can precisely alter the coordination environment and electronic properties of Cu surfaces, thereby enhancing *CO adsorption strength and lowering the energy barrier for C─C coupling.

From the standpoint of multiphase catalytic interfaces, alloying Cu with secondary metals also offers a means of electronic modulation at the active sites. This modification can enhance *CO binding strength and promote both its surface retention and spatial migration, supporting efficient C─C coupling. Meanwhile, Cu catalysts supported on the substrate provide another way through particle size control or the construction of metal–support interfaces to promote *CO generation and stabilization by regulating charge transfer and confinement effects. In addition, interfacial engineering, such as forming oxide‐metal heterojunctions or valence‐tuned phase boundaries, also contributes to the stabilization of Cu+/Cu0 species and facilitates C─C coupling by providing a favorable local environment for *CO accumulation.

In addition to increasing *CO concentration, other strategies have been developed to enhance its spatial transport and suppress competitive HER. Tandem catalysis can decouple CO2‐to‐*CO conversion, intermediate migration, and C─C coupling across discrete sites, often integrating function‐specific domains such as Ag, Cu‐MOFs, or Cu‐porphyrin frameworks to direct *CO flow and prevent undesired side reactions. Meanwhile, spatial confinement strategies achieved through the use of organic ligands, porous frameworks, or hydrophobic coatings can create localized *CO‐rich regions while excluding *H intermediates, thereby enhancing *CO dimerization efficiency. In addition, surface molecular engineering, including the incorporation of functional ligands, dopants, and conjugated frameworks, can provide additional stabilization to *CO and related intermediates via hydrogen bonding, electronic tuning, or steric effects. These modifications prevent premature protonation and support directional transport and efficient dimerization of *CO into C2+ products such as C2H4.

Extensive efforts have been devoted to enhancing ECO2RR to C2H4 by modulating the surface energetics of catalysts to promote initial CO2 activation and enrich surface *CO intermediates, thereby facilitating the C─C coupling. However, the majority of these strategies rely on the construction of complex multi‐site architecture, in which the three key steps of CO2 activation, *CO accumulation, and C─C bond formation are often carried out by distinct catalytic sites. Meanwhile, the reaction remains fundamentally constrained by intrinsic linear scaling relationships between the adsorption energies of key intermediates. These correlations among series intermediates often couple the energetics of CO2 activation, *CO accumulation, and C─C bond formation, making it difficult to independently optimize each elementary step on a single catalytic surface. Although targeted enhancement of a single specific step can indeed lead to improved C2H4 selectivity, the underlying mechanistic understanding of how these individual steps are interconnected remains insufficient. For example, tandem catalysts are frequently designed with spatially separated active centers, where one site activates CO2 and the other mediates C─C coupling via *CO intermediates. However, the precise migration pathways, diffusion mechanisms, and kinetic coupling of *CO or other intermediates between these sites are rarely elucidated. This knowledge gap hinders the rational integration of synergistic effects and limits the further optimization of multi‐step reaction sequences. Future research should aim not only to develop multifunctional catalytic systems capable of simultaneously enhancing all three critical steps but also to establish a unified understanding of intermediate transport across active domains. In particular, elucidating how *CO migrates between distinct catalytic motifs and how its spatial and temporal distribution affects downstream selectivity will be the key to achieving precise control over product formation. Such insights will ultimately enable the design of advanced catalysts that balance site‐specific activity with efficient intermediate transfer, paving the way for scalable and selective CO2‐to‐C2+ conversions.

Although the surface concentration of *CO intermediates is widely recognized as a key factor in enhancing C2H4 production during the ECO2RR, it may also unintentionally promote the formation of competing C2 products such as ethanol. This complexity arises from the partially overlapping reaction pathways of C2H4 and ethanol, particularly beyond the initial C─C coupling step. Specifically, the common intermediate, typically identified as *CH x CHO (e.g., *CH2CHO), can either undergo deoxygenation to yield C2H4 or further hydrogenation to form ethanol. Therefore, directing the reaction pathway of such C─C intermediates toward selective deoxygenation becomes a critical challenge in achieving high C2H4 selectivity. Given that Cu‐based catalysts intrinsically exhibit moderate binding affinities for both C2H4 and ethanol, they are capable of facilitating both transformation routes. Recent studies have proposed strategies to steer intermediate selectivity, including tuning specific crystallographic facets (e.g., Cu(100) vs Cu(111)) to modulate adsorption geometries and electronic structures, or adjusting the local ratio of surface *OH and *H species to influence the deoxygenation versus hydrogenation preference of C─C coupled intermediates. However, most of these insights remain primarily theoretical or computational in nature and lack direct experimental validation, particularly regarding the fate of oxygen atoms during the critical post‐coupling steps. To bridge this gap, future investigations should focus on simultaneously integrating theoretical modeling with advanced experimental characterization to identify and optimize the spatial configuration and electronic environment of key *CH x CHO‐like intermediates. In particular, in situ and operando vibrational spectroscopic techniques like X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), surface‐enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (SEIRAS), and surface‐enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) can offer powerful tools for probing the dynamic evolution of adsorbed species under true electrochemical conditions. These methods can help unravel the competitive pathways leading to C2H4 versus ethanol formation and clarify the molecular mechanisms governing O‐retention and O‐elimination processes. Establishing such mechanistic insights will be essential for the rational design of Cu‐based catalysts that enable precise control over C─C intermediate transformation and selective synthesis of C2 products.

Also, the current limitations of electrode architectures and reactor configurations continue to constrain the further enhancement of C2H4 selectivity in ECO2RR. One of the fundamental challenges lies in the inherently complex mass transport processes between the electrolyte environment and the catalyst surface. In aqueous systems, the slow diffusion rate of CO2 and the development of nonuniform local concentration gradients significantly hinder the sustained delivery of reactants to active sites, thereby limiting catalytic efficiency and selectivity. These transport‐related bottlenecks remain a major obstacle to the practical, scalable deployment of ECO2RR technologies for C2H4 production. In response to these challenges, in recent years, it has been witnessed the emergence of advanced reactor designs such as gas diffusion electrode (GDE) systems and membrane electrode assembly (MEA) configurations, which offer alternative platforms for enhancing ECO2RR performance. Especially, GDE systems utilize hydrophobic layers to construct a well‐defined gas–liquid–solid triple‐phase boundary, enabling direct CO2 gas access to the catalyst surface without needing dissolution in bulk electrolyte. This architecture significantly enhances catalytic site utilization and operational stability under elevated current densities, and some GDE‐based systems have achieved industrially relevant current densities exceeding 500 mA cm−2 with notable C2H4 selectivity. However, GDEs also suffer from several critical drawbacks, including mechanical instability, poor long‐term sealing of the gas–liquid interface, and uneven electrolyte contact across the catalyst layer. These issues can result in catalyst degradation, flooding of the gas channels, and partial deactivation of active sites due to local over‐wetting or dedication. Meanwhile, MEA systems allow CO2 gas to diffuse directly to the catalyst layer through a gas diffusion backing, bypassing transport through the liquid electrolyte entirely. This design facilitates a high surface concentration of CO2, which is particularly favorable for initial activation and subsequent C─C coupling. Nevertheless, the membrane component of the MEA, typically an ion‐conducting polymer such as Nafion or an anion exchange membrane, remains expensive and technically demanding to fabricate. Moreover, under high current operation, gaseous products like C2H4 tend to adsorb or accumulate at the membrane catalyst interface, impeding their timely removal and complicating downstream product separation. In most systems, CO2 reduction proceeds exclusively at the cathode, while the anode employs the energy‐intensive oxygen evolution reaction (OER) to close the circuit, which raises the overall cell voltage and, consequently, diminishes device‐level efficiency. Coupling CO2RR with value‐added anodic oxidations, such as selective oxidation of low‐carbon alcohols/polyols, biomass‐derived substrates, or small oxygenates, offers a pathway to lower the operating cell voltage, reshape interfacial electric fields and local microenvironments at both electrodes, and co‐produce high‐value chemicals at the anode alongside C2+ products at the cathode, thereby improving system‐level energy and carbon efficiency.[ 206 ] A representative demonstration that paired CO2 electrolysis with methanol oxidation; this system‐level integration lowered the required cell voltage while stabilizing favorable interfacial field distributions, highlighting practical routes to simultaneous energy savings and value‐added co‐production. However, such paired‐electrolysis systems also face several challenges. The anodic oxidation reactions often exhibit complex multistep kinetics and narrow potential windows, making it difficult to achieve both high selectivity and stability under the strongly reducing conditions required for CO2RR. In addition, the two half‐reactions typically have mismatched optimal pH and potential ranges, complicating electrolyte design and long‐term cell balance. These incompatibilities, together with possible crossover of oxidized species, may compromise overall durability and scalability despite the lower nominal cell voltage.

Lastly, considerable advances have been achieved in the development of Cu‐based electrocatalysts at ECO2RR, but still lacking a fundamental disconnection still persists between mechanistic understanding at the microscopic scale and performance evaluation at the macroscopic level, thereby impeding the rational design of high‐efficiency systems. On the microscopic front, theoretical modeling frequently employs a variety of descriptors, such as adsorption energies of key intermediates (ΔG), electronic structure parameters (εd), surface geometry, charge distribution (ΔE), and environmental responsiveness for rationalizing catalytic behavior.[ 127 , 207 ] However, the selection criteria for these descriptors vary significantly across multiple studies and often lack transferability across different crystal facets, structural motifs, and reaction conditions. Moreover, intrinsic linear scaling relationships among intermediate adsorption energies impose thermodynamic constraints that couple multiple reaction steps, thereby preventing independent optimization through a single descriptor. This ambiguity in the structure–function interaction not only limits the predictive power of mechanistic models in guiding catalyst development but also exacerbates the difficulty in correlating microscopic mechanisms with macroscopic performance. At the macroscopic level, the performance evaluation standard remains inconsistent. Faradaic efficiency, partial current density, and energy conversion efficiency those key metrics that are often reported in isolation across diverse reactor configurations, including H‐cells, gas diffusion electrodes (GDEs), and membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs), thereby posing significant challenges in data comparability and reproducibility. The lack of standardized testing conditions further disconnects theoretical insights from practical implementation, making it difficult to validate mechanistic predictions under realistic working conditions. Therefore, bridging this mechanistic‐performance gap is essential for accelerating the translation of Cu‐based CO2RR systems toward industrial relevance. A unified framework that couples robust, transferable descriptors with standardized evaluation regulations is urgently needed to enable the coherent integration of mechanistic modeling, catalyst design, and system‐level benchmarking.

Given these limitations, it becomes increasingly clear that achieving high C2H4 selectivity at commercially viable current densities requires not only the rational design of highly active and selective catalysts, but also their seamless integration with reactor systems optimized for mass transport, phase management, and long‐term operational stability. Future efforts should focus on the co‐development of catalyst materials and high‐throughput electrochemical platforms that enable efficient gas delivery, robust interface control, and scalable system operation, ultimately bridging the gap between laboratory‐scale performance and industrial feasibility.
