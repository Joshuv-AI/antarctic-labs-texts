Quasi‐BIC and Reflective Mode Coupling Drives SHG Beyond the Q Factor Limit
Authors: Wenkai Yang, Hao Qin, Boqing Liu, Chuanyu Wang, Yilin Tang, Jiri Janousek, Xueqian Sun, Alex Y Song, Yuerui Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSecond harmonic generation (SHG) is a widely used nonlinear optical process for frequency conversion and quantum information processing. However, existing approaches to enhance SHG in metasurfaces that are based on quasi‐bound state in the continuum (quasi‐BIC) resonances are often constrained by the limited second‐order nonlinear susceptibilities of materials and the practical challenge of achieving high quality (Q) factors. Here, an alternative strategy is proposed to enhance SHG by increasing the absorptance (A) at the quasi‐BIC resonance. Using temporal coupled mode theory (TCMT), an analytical expression is derived to link SHG intensity with both the resonance Q factor and absorptance, showing that strong SHG can be achieved even with finite Q when absorptance is properly optimized. To validate this concept, a 3R phase of molybdenum disulfide (3R‐MoS2) metasurface is designed and fabricated by spectrally aligning a reflective resonance with a transmitted quasi‐BIC mode, thereby enhancing absorptance at the target wavelength. The metasurface exhibits an SHG conversion efficiency of ∼3 × 10−5 at 9 GW cm−2, corresponding to more than 40‐fold enhancement over an unpatterned flake. Despite the reduced damage threshold, the experimental results agree well with the theoretical model and establish a general design framework for absorptance‐engineered metasurfaces for SHG.Keywords: absorptance, dual‐resonance metasurface, nonlinear optics, quasi‐BIC, second harmonic generation

Introduction

Second‐harmonic generation (SHG) is a pivotal nonlinear optical process with applications spanning frequency conversion [1], ultrafast pulse optics [2, 3, 4], optical sensing [5], and quantum information processing [6, 7, 8]. Conventional III–V semiconductors such as GaAs and AlGaAs offer large second‐order nonlinear susceptibilities (χ(2)> 100 pm/V). However, their practical application in integrated photonics remains limited by several intrinsic drawbacks, including strong absorption in the visible spectral range and severe diffraction losses [9, 10, 11, 12]. Specifically, the centrosymmetric crystal orientation of epitaxial films often necessitates complex phase‐matching geometries, and parasitic scattering from subwavelength nanostructures can redirect a large fraction of the generated harmonic emission into nonfunctional diffraction orders [12, 13, 14]. 2D van der Waals (vdW) materials have emerged as a promising alternative, owing to their unique optical and structural properties [15]. Their atomically thin profiles, high refractive indices, and pronounced optical anisotropy enable strong light–matter interaction at subwavelength scales [16]. Among these, the more widely studied hexagonal (2H) phase transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), which are centrosymmetric in their bulk form, lack electric‐dipole allowed second order nonlinear processes [17]. In contrast, TMDs, due to broken inversion symmetry, possess strong second‐order nonlinearities with χ(2) coefficients, and the rhombohedral (3R) phase stacking configuration enables SH polarization accumulation for coherent SHG in bulk thick samples [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. However, as the demands for compact and efficient nonlinear optical components continue to rise, the SHG performance attainable from an unpatterned 3R phase of molybdenum disulfide (3R‐MoS2) film becomes insufficient. In the absence of resonant field enhancement, the incident pump field interacts only weakly with the material, resulting in limited mode confinement and relatively modest SHG output compared with resonantly engineered nanostructures [26].

Building on these considerations, nonlinear optical metasurfaces have emerged as powerful platforms for manipulating light‐matter interactions at subwavelength scales, providing viable solutions to the fundamental limitations of conventional bulk crystals. A variety of strategies have been developed to enhance nonlinear optical interactions in nanophotonic structures, including plasmonic field localization [27, 28, 29], dielectric Mie resonances [14, 30], and multipolar Fano interference [31, 32, 33, 34]. More recently, nonlinear metasurfaces supporting multiple resonant modes or multimode coupling were explored to enhance nonlinear optical processes, such as the simultaneous excitation of multiple quasi‐bound state in the continuum (quasi‐BIC) resonances and degenerate bound states in the continuum (BIC) modes [35, 36, 37]. While these approaches provide varying degrees of near‐field enhancement, plasmonic platforms suffer from severe ohmic losses, and conventional dielectric resonances often fail to provide the extreme field confinement required for efficient nonlinear frequency conversion [14, 27, 28, 29, 38]. In contrast, dielectric metasurfaces supporting BICs have emerged as a particularly effective route toward strong field enhancement [13, 39]. An ideal BIC is a non‐radiative eigenmode embedded within the radiation continuum, exhibiting theoretically infinite quality (Q) factors. Introducing slight structural asymmetries transforms these modes into quasi‐BICs, which allow controlled coupling to external radiation channels while maintaining high Q factors [36]. Such quasi‐BIC modes have enabled record‐high local field intensities and substantial enhancements of SHG in recent metasurface demonstrations [40, 41, 42, 43]. Motivated by the common assumption ISHG∝Q2 [44, 45], many efforts have focused on maximizing the resonance Q factor through increasingly precise fabrication and structural optimization [38, 46, 47]. However, ultrahigh Q resonances inherently exhibit weak external coupling, leading to low absorptance of the incident pump light and consequently limiting the overall SHG efficiency. Achieving strong nonlinear conversion, therefore, requires not only a high‐Q resonance to enable field buildup but also sufficient absorptive coupling to efficiently funnel optical energy into the resonant mode.

In this study, a theoretical framework based on temporal coupled mode theory (TCMT) is developed to demonstrate that SHG efficiency depends jointly on the resonance Q factor and the absorptance [48, 49]. This framework identifies absorptance as a critical, yet often overlooked, parameter in the design of nonlinear metasurfaces. To validate the model, we implement a dual‐resonance design strategy that couples a high Q quasi‐BIC with a radiatively bright reflective resonance, as schematically illustrated in Figure 1, within a dielectric metasurface based on 3R‐MoS2. By spectrally coupling the high Q quasi‐BIC mode to the reflective resonance, the absorptive coupling is substantially enhanced without significantly compromising the resonance Q factor, yielding an SHG intensity approximately 40‐fold higher than that of an unpatterned 3R‐MoS2 flake at the quasi‐BIC wavelength. These results are in good agreement with the TCMT predictions and establish a broadly applicable design framework for nonlinear metasurfaces. Overall, our findings highlight the joint and complementary roles of the Q factor and absorptance in optimizing nonlinear frequency conversion efficiency, offering a general strategy for the design of high‐performance metasurfaces across diverse material platforms.

Results and Discussion

TCMT Analysis of Q Factor and Absorptance Contributions to SHG Intensity

Metasurface nonlinear measurements are typically performed under single‐port, single‐mode excitation, where the residual pump transmitted through the metasurface is reflected by a short‐pass filter positioned beneath the substrate, so TCMT provides a physically consistent framework for our experimental configuration. Numerical Modeling of Dual Resonance Interactions in 3R‐MoS2 Metasurface

To experimentally validate the theoretical model described by Equation (5), an array of “H” shaped nanostructures was designed and patterned into a 300 nm thick 3R‐MoS2 flake, which provides strong second‐order nonlinear susceptibility (χ(2)) and high SHG efficiency [26, 51], on a sapphire (Al2O3) substrate, as shown in Figure 2a. Each unit cell features a lattice period (P) of 494 nm in both x and y directions to minimize lattice‐induced interference and maintain uniform coupling. The “H” shaped resonator has a lower arm with fixed dimensions L1 = 275.5 nm in length and w1 = 142.5 nm in width. The upper arm is defined by L2 and w2 accordingly, where the degree of asymmetry is quantified by the asymmetry parameter α, such that α = (L1 − L2)/ L1 = (w1 − w2)/ w1, as shown in Figure 2b. The total height of the structure is denoted by H = P/2 + (1 − α)w1. The “H” shaped structure offers straightforward tunability of asymmetry parameters independently along both the x and y axes, where broken structural symmetry enables the realization of a BIC, which is the emergence of a high Q factor resonance. At the same time, this design simultaneously brings the reflective resonance as close as possible to the quasi‐BIC position. By tuning the asymmetry parameter α, this enables the modulation of the position of the reflective resonance and thereby enhances the absorptance at the quasi‐BIC wavelength, leading to a pronounced enhancement of SHG intensity. This approach provides an ideal platform to validate the accuracy of our theoretical model described by Equation (5).

Based on these design parameters, a metasurface model has been established, and its linear and nonlinear optical response under x‐polarized excitation has been calculated using finite element method (FEM) simulations. As shown in the transmittance mapping Figure 3a, increasing the asymmetry parameter α from 0 to 0.12 leads to the quasi‐BIC resonance and the reflective resonance gradually approaching and eventually overlapping (spectra of different asymmetry parameter α have been shown in Figure S2). Under this overlapping situation, quasi‐BIC is capable of maintaining the field confinement when the reflective resonance improves the in‐coupling (see Section S4 and Figure S3). Moreover, the extracted Q factor, shown in Figure 3b, increases sharply as the asymmetry parameter α decreases, confirming that the resonance marked by the circle indeed corresponds to a quasi‐BIC mode.

Figure 3c (absorptance mapping) shows that as the asymmetry parameter α increases, the quasi‐BIC and reflective resonances overlap, and the absorptance at the quasi‐BIC wavelength concomitantly rises. The absorptance reaches its maximum at α = 0.052, as highlighted by the white circle, and subsequently decreases. In comparison, the SHG intensity mapping in Figure 3d shows that, although the Q factor decreases with increasing α, the SHG intensity still rises with increasing absorptance, reaching its maximum at α = 0.0405 (yellow circle). Notably, even though the absorptance continues to increase between α = 0.0405 and α = 0.052, the improvement in SHG intensity from higher absorptance is outweighed by the loss due to the reduced Q factor. As a result, the SHG intensity begins to decrease when α exceeds 0.0405. This trend further confirms that the enhancement of SHG depends not only on the Q factor but also on the magnitude of the absorptance A. Based on these results, we extracted the Q factors, absorptance values A, and FEM‐simulated SHG intensities for several different α values from the simulation mapping. Using Equation (5), the corresponding SHG intensities were then calculated from the extracted Q and A values and compared with the simulated SHG intensities. All results are expressed as ratios relative to the case of α = 0.0405, as shown in Figure S4. The close quantitative agreement between the FEM‐simulated SHG intensities and those calculated from Equation (5) confirms that the TCMT‐derived expression captures the essential physics of the nonlinear process. This consistency across independently computed datasets provides strong validation of the model and demonstrates its predictive reliability at the simulation level.

Characterization of Optical Performance in 3R‐MoS2 Metasurface

To further validate the accuracy of Equation (5), a series of dual‐resonance samples was fabricated and characterized based on the structure design with an asymmetry parameter of α = 0.1, following the fabrication and measurement procedures described in the Methods section. The array of “H” shaped nanoresonators is arranged over a 52 µm × 52 µm area on a sapphire substrate. Optical microscopy (Figure S5a) reveals a uniform periodic pattern across the sample, while scanning electron microscopy (Figure S5b) confirms accurate definition and consistent geometry of the fabricated structures.

The linear optical response of sample A was characterized under normal incidence near its resonance wavelength. The measured transmittance and reflectance spectra (Figure 4a) verify that the quasi‐BIC and reflective resonances are spectrally overlapped with an extracted Q factor of approximately 65.2 at 1010 nm and an absorptance of 46.2%. To more accurately reproduce the experimental conditions, the simulation model was refined by adjusting certain structural parameters of the designed metasurface and incorporating additional factors such as surface roughness and sidewall inclination of the unit cell, which can lead to modest shifts in resonance wavelength and variations in Q. The overall degree of structural roughness was quantified using a parameter β, as illustrated in Figure S6a. The results show that introducing these experimentally relevant parameters produces a slight blue shift of the quasi‐BIC resonance and a corresponding decrease in Q, consistent with the measured response (see Figure S6c,d). The final simulation results in Figure 4b verify that the quasi‐BIC and reflective resonances retain the designed spectral overlap in the fabricated sample. Consequently, the measured Q and absorptance values remain within the operating regime in which our TCMT‐derived scaling relation accurately describes the nonlinear response. This guarantees that Equation (5) can be meaningfully validated using the experimental data.

The SHG response of the fabricated sample was then characterized to enable direct comparison with the predictions from Equation (5) and thereby validate the model. As shown in Figure 5a, taking the x‐direction of the Sample A unit cell as 0°, the angle‐resolved SHG intensity map reveals that sample A exhibits broken‐asymmetry BICs and enhanced SHG only for x‐polarized excitation, consistent with the design. Furthermore, with an incident power of 0.525 mW, we measured the SHG intensities for x‐polarized excitation near the resonance wavelength of sample A. The results, presented in Figure 5b, show that the strongest SHG response occurs at an excitation wavelength of 1010 nm, corresponding to the quasi‐BIC resonance in Figure 5a, and is slightly blue‐shifted toward the region of higher absorptance. However, because the SHG intensity also depends on the Q factor of the quasi‐BIC resonance, the maximum SHG does not appear at the wavelength of the highest absorptance. To further quantify the enhancement provided by the metasurface design, the SHG intensity of sample A at 1010 nm was compared with that of a thick 3R‐MoS2 flake of the same thickness under identical excitation conditions. As shown in Figure 5c, sample A exhibits a pronounced enhancement, yielding an SHG intensity approximately 40 times higher than that of the unpatterned flake. Under x‐polarized excitation at 1010 nm, the incident power has been progressively increased to determine the damage threshold of sample A. The SH emission is readily observable by the naked eye at an excitation power of 5 mW (Figure S7).

As shown in Figure 5d, the SHG intensity deviates from the expected quadratic dependence on power at around 17 mW (9 GW cm−2), at which point the measured absolute SHG conversion efficiency of sample A is 0.003%. A comparison with previous studies has been summarized in Table S1. Worth noting that, due to the heat accumulation induced by excessive field enhancement and slow heat dissipation, the structure's performance is limited by a relatively low damaging threshold (See Section S5). This structure is expected to be more robust with the improved damage threshold achieved through engineering methods, such as substrate thermal engineering [52], optimized pulsed excitation conditions [53], and improved device‐level heat dissipation [54]. Therefore, while this metasurface does not exhibit the highest Q factor and damage threshold, it still delivers competitive SHG efficiency, highlighting the importance of considering absorptance together with Q during the designation.

Equation (5) has been experimentally verified by fabricating five additional samples (B–F), with the results supported by the corresponding transmittance, reflectance, and absorptance spectra (Figure S8). For each sample, the Q factor and absorptance A at the resonance wavelength were extracted, and the SHG intensity at the corresponding resonance was measured under the same incident power. The measured SHG intensities were then compared with those calculated from the extracted Q and A values using Equation (5). All results are expressed as ratios relative to the SHG intensity of sample A (Figure S9). The measured and calculated values exhibit the same overall trend, with only minor deviations, thereby confirming the validity of Equation (5). These small differences can be attributed to slight variations in sample thickness and to the fact that the two resonances do not occur at exactly the same wavelength in all fabricated structures. The agreement substantiates the central conclusion of our model that SHG enhancement is simultaneously dictated by the resonance quality factor and its absorptance, as described by ISHG∝Q2(1−1−A)2. Therefore, in the pursuit of enhanced SHG, it is essential to optimize not only for a high Q factor but also for a high absorptance A.

Conclusion

In this work, a general theoretical framework is established for the nonlinear metasurfaces. By deriving an explicit scaling relation from TCMT, this formula reveals that SHG intensity is jointly governed by the resonance quality factor Q and absorptance A. This relation is rigorously validated through a combination of numerical simulations and experimental measurements. Both verify that efficient nonlinear enhancement requires not only strong field confinement but also effective coupling of pump energy into the resonant mode. Guided by this insight, a dual‐resonance 3R‐MoS2 metasurface has been realized by overlapping a quasi‐BIC mode and a reflective resonance. This co‐optimization strategy leads to more than a 40‐fold enhancement of SHG efficiency compared to an unpatterned reference flake. This result thus demonstrates the practical impact of the proposed model. While the strong field confinement associated with dual resonances may lower the optical damage threshold, this can potentially be overcome. Overall, this work offers a deeper understanding of resonance‐mediated nonlinear optical processes, introducing a versatile and fabrication‐tolerant design strategy, even applicable to a broad range of nonlinear materials, higher‐order harmonic generation processes, and integrated photonic platforms.

Methods

Numerical Simulation

For the theoretical calculations, we use the COMSOL based on the finite element method (FEM) to simulate the linear and nonlinear response from the metasurfaces [55]. During the simulation, we create a unit cell of metasurface, which is modeled by introducing periodic boundary conditions on the sidewalls of the unit cell in the x‐ and y‐directions and adding perfectly matched layers (PML) above and below the unit cell in the z‐direction, to describe the infinitely periodic surface. The unit cell consists of multiple rectangular pillars made of 3R‐MoS2, which are placed on a sapphire (aluminum oxide, Al2O3) substrate block, as shown in Figure 2a. The model structure consists of PML, port1, air, 3R‐MoS2 structure, sapphire, port2, and PML from top to bottom. PML is used to absorb light scattered or transmitted from the metasurface, while the port is used to input and detect light flux. We use the refractive index and extinction coefficient of the MoS2 bulk from a work presented in Ref. [22].

For the nonlinear simulation part, the second‐order nonlinearity is taken into account via the nonlinear polarization term PiNL(2ω0)=ε0∑jkχijk(2)EjEk with the nonlinear susceptibility χijk(2) from the Ref. [56]. For χyyy(2) we adopt the dispersion model reported by Faust and Henry to account for the presence of the A and B excitons of MoS2 [57] the value of the χyyy(2) is around 200pm/V. The simulations are performed in two calculation steps, starting with the plane wave excitation at the fundamental frequency, followed by the nonlinear polarization with PiNL(2ω0) at the second harmonic within the 3R‐MoS2 metasurface. The pump is incident from the airside (port1), s‐polarised in the x‐direction, and the pump intensity is 9 GW cm−2. Moreover, only the emitted zeroth diffraction order (kx = ky = 0) in the transmission or reflection direction is considered in this work.

Fabrication Methods

In order to fabricate the 3R‐MoS2 metasurface on the sapphire (Al2O3) substrate, and follow the simulation result. We mechanically exfoliated vdW material 3R‐MoS2 onto the substrate. The thickness of the transferred flakes was then measured by a surface profilometer. After cleaning, we used plasma to ensure that the photoresist remains on the flake surface. Then we spin‐coated 200 nm thick Arp 6200 09 positive resist and deposited 15 nm thick gold (Au) (to dissipate the charges during the lithography process) on the selected flake. The flake was subsequently patterned into a metasurface using EBL. After developing the sample, we deposit a 35 nm thick layer of Al (for dry etching mask), using an e‐beam evaporator. The Al cap structure will be shown after a lift‐off process. The ‘H’ shape rectangular pillars were then dry‐etched by employing inductive‐coupled plasma of SF6 + CHF3 + Ar, and then Al caps from the top of the ‘H’ shape rectangular pillars were removed by the Al etch type A. Finally, we employed SEM to examine the quality and dimensions of the 3R‐MoS2 metasurfaces.

Linear Measurements

The linear characterization process utilized a customized optical setup that seamlessly integrated a fiber‐coupled near‐infrared (NIR) spectrometer (Ocean Optics NIR Quest+2.2) with broadband illumination (Thorlabs OSL2IR). Polarization control was achieved by incorporating a high‐extinction‐ratio polarizer within the excitation path. To eliminate background interference before the light entered the spectrometer, a controllable pinhole was implemented. In reflectance mode, a 10x objective (NA = 0.26) focused the incident beam to ensure full‐field illumination of the metasurface, with reflectance normalized with respect to that of a gold substrate. For transmission measurements, the white light source was focused through a 20x objective (NA = 0.4), while a 50x objective (NA = 0.42) collected transmitted signals through the metasurface and sapphire substrate. The absorption curve was quantitatively derived through synchronized reflectance and transmission measurements.

Non‐Linear Measurements

Angle‐resolved and wavelength‐dependent SHG characterizations employed a tunable femtosecond laser (Spectra‐Physics Mai Tai HP) integrated with a custom‐built horizontal microscope coupled to a CCD (Andor iDus 420). Samples were optically excited through a 20× apochromatic NIR objective (NA = 0.4), while transmitted SHG signals were collected via a 50× apochromatic NIR objective (NA = 0.42). Polarization control was implemented using a broadband half‐wave plate (𝜆/2) in the excitation path, with alignment relative to the metasurface's symmetry axes verified by a visible‐range polarizer. Neutral density (ND) filters were systematically deployed to prevent CCD overexposure. The diagram of the measurement setup can be found in Figure S10.
