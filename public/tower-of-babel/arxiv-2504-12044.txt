Anatomy of the simplest renormalon
Marcos Mariño

Abstract
Perhaps the simplest IR renormalon occurs in the ground state energy of a superrenormalizable model, the scalar O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) theory in two dimensions with a quartic potential and negative squared mass. We show that this renormalon, found previously in perturbation theory at next-to-leading order (NLO) in the 1 / N 1/N expansion, gives indeed the correct asymptotic expansion of the exact large N N solution of the model, and we determine explicitly the complete trans-series of non-perturbative corrections to the perturbative result. We also use this framework to study the O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) -invariant two-point function of the scalar field. As expected, it is IR finite in perturbation theory, but it is afflicted as well with an IR renormalon singularity and is not Borel summable. The pole mass is purely non-perturbative and its trans-series can be also fully determined at NLO in 1 / N 1/N .



1
Introduction

Factorial divergence due to renormalons is a generic feature of the perturbative approach to
quantum field theory (QFT). Mathematically,
renormalons appear as singularities of the Borel transform of the perturbative series. Since their inception in the late 1970s, they have been
interpreted as smoking guns for nonperturbative effects which do not have a semiclassical origin.

Renormalons have been mostly studied in renormalizable, asymptotically free theories.
When they arise in the correlation function of products of operators, their presence has been related
Parisi (1979)
to
the existence of non-trivial condensates in the operator product expansion (OPE)
Shifman et al. (1979)
. However, renormalons also appear in more
general observables without an OPE description. A particularly simple example was discovered in
Mariño and Reis (2020)
,
in the ground state energy of the two-dimensional scalar field theory with a
O(N)
symmetry, a quartic potential,
and a negative squared mass. This theory is super-renormalizable, but it is well-known that its IR physics is subtle. This is because
the
O(N)
symmetry cannot be spontaneously broken in two dimensions, due to the Coleman–Mermin–Wagner
theorem
Coleman (1973)
;
Mermin and Wagner (1966)
. However, as explained by Jevicki a long time ago
Jevicki (1977)
, the conventional
perturbation theory in this model can be obtained by expanding around a “false” vacuum in which the
O(N)

symmetry is broken, and the resulting perturbative series for the ground state energy is free of IR divergences.
It was shown in
Mariño and Reis (2020)
that this series is factorially divergent and non-Borel summable due to an
IR renormalon, by working at next-to-leading order (NLO) in the
1/N
expansion,
but perturbatively in the coupling constant, as usual in renormalon analysis
Beneke (1999)
. This behaviour is in stark contrast
to the quartic scalar theory with a positive squared mass, and to the
N=1
theory
with a negative mass, where in both cases the perturbative series is Borel summable
Eckmann and Epstein (1979)
;
Serone et al. (2018)
;
Serone et al. (2019)
;
Sberveglieri et al. (2019)
.
In the theory with a positive squared mass, the ground state energy at NLO in
1/N
is
even convergent
Sberveglieri et al. (2021)
.
For this reason, the non-Borel summability found in the
O(N)
theory was interpreted
in
Mariño and Reis (2020)
as a manifestation of the absence of Goldstone bosons in two dimensions.

It is perhaps useful to compare this situation with the double-well potential
in quantum mechanics. In that case, the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
symmetry
can not be spontaneously broken, yet we do perturbation theory by expanding around one of the
two “false” vacua. The resulting
perturbative series is factorially divergent and non-Borel summable, but we can cure the problem
by taking into account the non-perturbative effects due to instantons. When these effects are
added, in the form of a trans-series, one can reproduce
the exact ground state energy obtained from the spectral theory of the Schrödinger operator
(see e.g.
Mariño (2021)
for
an exposition and references).

Figure 1:
In the double-well potential in quantum mechanics (left), the two perturbative vacua
are false vacua, and expanding around them leads to a non-Borel summable perturbative series. In the
scalar field theory with a quartic Mexican hat potential and
O(N\geq 2)
symmetry (right), the perturbative
vacua are also false vacua in
d=2
, and they also lead to a non-Borel summable perturbative expansion. In the
quantum mechanical case, the relevant non-perturbative effects are instantons, while in the two-dimensional field theory, they are IR renormalons.

In the case of our two-dimensional QFT, one can find exact results for the observables, order by order in
the
1/N
expansion, by using non-perturbative large
N
techniques. In contrast to the perturbative large
N

methods based on summing up “bubble” diagrams, their non-perturbative counterpart produces actual functions
of the ’t Hooft coupling. This non-perturbative approach was used long ago in
Coleman et al. (1974)
to calculate the effective potential at
large
N
, and to show that there is no spontaneous symmetry breaking at large
N
. A more detailed large
N

analysis
Sberveglieri et al. (2021)
shows that there is a non-perturbative mass gap, and one can compute various physical
quantities at NLO in
1/N
, like the ground state energy.

The perturbative and non-perturbative large
N
results are obtained by expanding around different
large
N
vacua, and they are very different, even as mathematical entities. The first one is a formal
power series in the ’t Hooft coupling,
while the second one is a well-defined, real function of that coupling. As in quantum mechanics,
we expect that the formal series gives the asymptotic expansion of the well-defined function, at weak coupling.
We also expect that the exact result can be obtained by adding a series of
non-perturbative corrections to the perturbative series,
and performing an appropriate resummation in which renormalon singularities cancel. In this
paper we verify these expectations in detail. Mathematically, we “decode” the exact, non-perturbative answer as a trans-series, and we verify in particular that the perturbative series found in
Mariño and Reis (2020)
is indeed the correct asymptotic expansion of the non-perturbative large
N
result.

This sort of decoding at large
N
is not new and has been studied in other
two-dimensional models, starting with the beautiful paper
Beneke et al. (1998)
on the
two-point function of the non-linear sigma model, which builds on the
pioneering work of
David (1984)
. Similar analyses have been done recently for the
supersymmetric sigma model
Schubring et al. (2021)

and the Gross–Neveu model
Mariño and Miravitllas (2025)
;
Liu (2024)
. We believe that the example of the
O(N)
quartic theory is
interesting for various reasons. First of all, it is in many ways technically simpler than previous studies,
and provides perhaps the simplest example of a QFT renormalon and its associated
trans-series. Second, it concerns a quantity, the ground state energy, in which the trans-series
cannot be calculated in principle with the OPE approach, and might serve as a testing ground
for more general techniques. Finally, this example illustrates an IR sensitive dependence
whose underlying physics is different from the one in asymptotically free theories. In particular,
since there is no dimensional transmutation, the ground state energy has a non-trivial dependence
on the coupling constant, both at the perturbative and the non-perturbative level.

We also calculate the
O(N)
-invariant two-point function of the scalar field at NLO in

1/N
, both in perturbation theory and in the exact
1/N
expansion. We show explicitly that, in the perturbative calculation,
IR divergences cancel when we sum all diagrams, as expected from the
study of the non-linear sigma model
Elitzur (1983)
;
David (1981)
. As a result, we find a
closed form, integral expression for the
all-loop perturbative series. It displays as well an IR renormalon behavior, with the same set
of Borel singularities as the ground state energy. In particular, it is not Borel summable, in contrast
to what happens in e.g. the
N=1
theory with positive squared mass. Our calculation also shows
that the pole mass vanishes at all orders in perturbation theory, at NLO in
1/N
, but can be calculated
as a non-trivial trans-series
in the non-perturbative large
N
vacuum.

The paper is organized as follows. In section
2
we focus on the perturbative analysis of the observables and the
renormalon singularities. We review and slightly extend the results of
Mariño and Reis (2020)
and we present a detailed computation of the two-point function of the

\Phi
field at NLO in
1/N
and at all loops, and its renormalon behavior. In section
3
we review the standard large
N
non-perturbative calculations of the quantities considered in section
2
, and we present complete results for the trans-series corrections in the case of the ground state energy and the pole mass. We conclude with some observations and open problems. The Appendix contains technical details
complementing the computations performed in the bulk of the paper.

in the counterterm. The simplest choice is the one made in

This is equivalent to renormalize

\int{{\rm d}^{2}k\over(2\pi)^{2}}{1\over k^{2}+a^{2}}=-{1\over 4\pi}\log\left({a^{2}\over m^{2}}\right),

(2.7)

as well as

\int{{\rm d}^{2}k\over(2\pi)^{2}}\log(k^{2}+a^{2})={a^{2}\over 4\pi}\left(1-\log\left({a^{2}\over m^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.8)

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{\Phi}(0)\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}(0)\rangle^{2}=\langle\xi(x)\xi(0)\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\langle\eta_{j}(x)\eta_{j}(0)\rangle.

The perturbative self-energy at NLO in the

\gamma
and the dimensionless quantity

\mathfrak{p}^{2}={p^{2}\over m^{2}}.

(2.50)

We will now calculate
\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)
as a formal power series in
\gamma
,
at all loops. This is done with the IR regulator (

), and we will take
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
at the end of the calculation. As we will see, the contributions of the
\xi
and the
\eta

Let us first consider the two-point function of the
\eta

(2.51)

\displaystyle S_{\eta}(p^{2})

\displaystyle+{1\over N}\left\{-{1\over p^{2}+\chi_{(0)}}+{1\over\left(p^{2}+\chi_{(0)}\right)^{2}}\int{{\rm d}^{d}q\over(2\pi)^{d}}\left({{\cal S}_{\tilde{\chi}}(q^{2})\over(p+q)^{2}+\chi_{(0)}}-{{\cal S}_{\tilde{\chi}}(q^{2})\over q^{2}+\chi_{(0)}}\right)\right\}+{\cal O}(N^{-2}).

Both
S_{\eta}(p^{2})

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\kappa^{2}\phi_{(0)}^{2}\over p^{2}+\chi_{(0)}+\kappa\phi_{(0)}^{2}}{\Pi(p^{2},\chi_{(0)})\over p^{2}+m^{2}+\gamma m^{2}\log\left({p^{2}\over m^{2}}\right)}+{\cal O}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle 1\over p^{2}}{\gamma\over\mathfrak{p}^{2}}\left(\log(\epsilon)+\int_{0}^{\infty}{{\rm d}y\over{\sqrt{(y+\mathfrak{p}^{2}+\epsilon)^{2}-4y\mathfrak{p}^{2}}}}{1\over y+1}\right)

where

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{p}^{2},

(2.63)

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{0}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\log(\epsilon)+\int_{0}^{\infty}{g_{0}(y)\over{\sqrt{(y+\mathfrak{p}^{2}+\epsilon)^{2}-4y\mathfrak{p}^{2}}}}{\rm d}y,

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{g_{n}(y)\over{\sqrt{(y+\mathfrak{p}^{2}+\epsilon)^{2}-4y\mathfrak{p}^{2}}}}{\rm d}y-\int_{0}^{\infty}{g_{n}(y)\over y}{\rm d}y,\qquad n\geq 1.

The second term in the last line comes from the last term in (

for

which cancels the divergence of

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{p}^{2}{1+\gamma\log(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over\mathfrak{p}^{2}+1+\gamma\log(\mathfrak{p}^{2})}-\left({2\log(1+\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over 1+\mathfrak{p}^{2}}+{\mathfrak{p}^{2}\log(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over 1+\mathfrak{p}^{2}}\right)\gamma

\displaystyle-\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\left({{\rm P}\over t-\mathfrak{p}^{2}}+{1\over t}\right)\left(\frac{t}{t+1+\gamma\log(t)}-\frac{t}{t+1}\right){\rm d}t

\displaystyle-2\gamma\int_{\mathfrak{p}^{2}}^{\infty}\log\left({t\over\mathfrak{p}^{2}}-1\right)\left(\frac{t}{t+1+\gamma\log(t)}-\frac{t}{t+1}\right)^{\prime}{\rm d}t.

As in previous occasions, this expression has to be understood as a formal power series in
\gamma
, in which
we first expand the integrand in
\gamma
and then we integrate term by term. We find, at the very first orders,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\gamma^{n}

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{p}^{2}\over\mathfrak{p}^{2}+1}-\left({2\log(1+\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over 1+\mathfrak{p}^{2}}+{\mathfrak{p}^{2}\log(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over(1+\mathfrak{p}^{2})^{2}}\right)\gamma+{\cal O}(\gamma^{2}).

We also have

\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})=(-1)^{n}{\mathfrak{p}^{2}\log^{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over(\mathfrak{p}^{2}+1)^{n+1}}+(-1)^{n+1}{\cal J}_{n-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{2}),\qquad n\geq 2.

(2.68)

By using the expression (
2.66

\lim_{\mathfrak{p}^{2}\to 0}\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})=0,\qquad n\geq 0.

(2.69)

This shows that the pole mass

of the
\Phi
field, which is defined as usual by

,
vanishes up to order
1/N
in the large
N
expansion, and at all orders in powers of the coupling constant
\gamma
.
Although there are no Goldstone bosons in two dimensions, the
\Phi
particles are massless in perturbation theory.

We can use (
2.66
) to determine the nature of the perturbative expansion (
2.67
)
for the self-energy. We first note that the obstruction to make sense of the integrals in (
2.66
) is the pole in the integrand
at
t=t_{\star}(\gamma)
, as in the case of the ground state energy (
2.32
). We can then deform the integration contour slightly
above or below the positive real axis, so as to avoid this pole. The resulting integrals are the lateral resummations of the formal power series
\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)
:

\displaystyle s_{\pm}\left(\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\right)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{p}^{2}{1+\gamma\log(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over\mathfrak{p}^{2}+1+\gamma\log(\mathfrak{p}^{2})}-\left({2\log(1+\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over 1+\mathfrak{p}^{2}}+{\mathfrak{p}^{2}\log(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\over 1+\mathfrak{p}^{2}}\right)\gamma

(2.70)

\displaystyle-\gamma\int_{{\cal C}_{\pm}}\left({{\rm P}\over t-\mathfrak{p}^{2}}+{1\over t}\right)\left(\frac{t}{t+1+\gamma\log(t)}-\frac{t}{t+1}\right){\rm d}t

\displaystyle-2\gamma\int_{\mathfrak{p}^{2}}^{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{\pm}}\infty}\log\left({t\over\mathfrak{p}^{2}}-1\right)\left(\frac{t}{t+1+\gamma\log(t)}-\frac{t}{t+1}\right)^{\prime}{\rm d}t.

The Stokes discontinuity can be easily computed from (
2.70
). It is given by the trans-series

\left(s_{+}-s_{-}\right)\left(\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})\right)(\gamma)=2\pi{\rm i}{\gamma\over\gamma+t_{\star}(\gamma)}\left(t_{\star}(\gamma)\pm{t_{\star}^{2}(\gamma)\over t_{\star}(\gamma)-\mathfrak{p}^{2}}\right),

(2.71)

where the
\pm
sign corresponds to the cases
\mathfrak{p}^{2}>t_{\star}(\gamma)
and
\mathfrak{p}^{2}<t_{\star}(\gamma)
, respectively. It follows that there are Borel singularities at all positive integers, as in the case of the ground state energy. The first exponential correction, which controls the leading contribution to the large order behavior of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})
, is simply
2\pi{\rm i}\,{\rm e}^{-1/\gamma}
, and independent of
\mathfrak{p}^{2}
.

The Borel singularities above are IR renormalons in the self-energy of the model, similar to the ones which are found in asymptotically free theories (see e.g.
Mariño and Miravitllas (2025)
for a recent detailed analysis of the two-point function in the Gross–Neveu model). One difference however is that, in
the asymptotically free case, the dependence on the momentum can be absorbed in the running coupling constant, and the power series for the two-point function has numerical coefficients. In this two-dimensional model, each coefficient
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})
is a complicated function of
the momentum (see e.g. (
A.8
)). This is sometimes called parametric resurgence, since the resurgent structure depends on a parameter, in this
case the momentum
\mathfrak{p}^{2}
. The parametric dependence in this case is very simple, and in particular
the position of the singularities is momentum-independent.

3.1
The non-perturbative large
N
vacuum

Let us now consider the other solution to the large
N
vacuum equations, in which
\phi_{(0)}=0
. In
Coleman et al. (1974)
this was
regarded as the actual vacuum, in which no spontaneous symmetry breaking occurs, as
required by the Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem. We will denote by

\chi_{(0)}=M^{2}

(3.1)

the vev of the auxiliary field in this vacuum, at leading order in
1/N
. From (
2.24
) we find that it satisfies the equation

M^{2}=-{m^{2}\over 2}-{\kappa\over 8\pi}\log\left({M^{2}\over m^{2}}\right).

(3.2)

This is the analogue of the “gap equation” obtained in the non-linear sigma model and the Gross–Neveu models.
As shown in
Sberveglieri et al. (2021)
, this equation can be solved as follows. Let us define the dimensionless quantity

x={M^{2}\over m^{2}}.

(3.3)

Then, (
3.2
) gives

x=-{1\over 2}-{\gamma\over 2}\log(x),

(3.4)

which can be solved explicitly in terms of Lambert’s function,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle={\gamma\over 2}W\left({2\over\gamma}{\rm e}^{-1/\gamma}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}{(-2\ell)^{\ell-1}\over\ell!}{\rm e}^{-\ell/\gamma}={\rm e}^{-1/\gamma}-{2\over\gamma}{\rm e}^{-2/\gamma}+{6\over\gamma^{2}}{\rm e}^{-3/\gamma}+\cdots

(3.5)

This is a purely non-perturbative quantity, which is in addition strictly positive for any
\gamma>0
(in comparing with

Sberveglieri et al. (2021)
we should note that here we are already making a choice of scheme, determined by (
2.7
)).
The vev of
\chi
corresponds to a vev of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{2}
: it follows from (
2.10
) that integrating out
\chi
sets it
equal to

\chi=\mu^{2}+{\kappa\over 2N}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{2}.

(3.6)

We conclude that, at large
N
,

\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}\rangle=0,\qquad{1\over N}\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{2}\rangle={1\over 4\pi\gamma}\left(1+2x\right),

(3.7)

and we note that the first, perturbative
term in the vev of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{2}
agrees with the “classical” vev (
2.4
).
The ground state energy at leading order in the
1/N
expansion is then obtained by
evaluating (
2.22
) at this non-perturbative vacuum. After using (
3.4
) we find,

{\cal E}_{(0)}={E_{(0)}\over m^{2}}=-{1\over 32\pi}{1\over\gamma}+{x\over 8\pi}+{x^{2}\over 8\pi\gamma},

(3.8)

so already at leading order in the
1/N
expansion we have non-perturbative corrections to the ground state energy.

Let us now consider the
1/N
correction to this quantity. The propagator of the

\tilde{\chi}
particle in the non-perturbative vacuum can be obtained
by using standard techniques:

\Delta^{-1}(k^{2},M^{2})=\kappa^{-1}+\Pi(k^{2},M^{2}),

(3.9)

where
\Pi(k^{2},M^{2})
is given in (
2.15
). Then, the
1/N
correction to (
3.8
)
is given by the one-loop contribution of the
\tilde{\chi}
particle
Sberveglieri et al. (2021)
:

{\cal E}_{(1)}={1\over 2m^{2}}\int{{\rm d}^{d}k\over(2\pi)^{d}}\log(1+\kappa\Pi(k^{2},M^{2})).

(3.10)

It is useful to introduce the notations of
Campostrini and Rossi (1992)
and write

1+\kappa\Pi(k,M^{2})=1+zF(y),\qquad F(y)={1\over y\xi}\log\left({\xi+1\over\xi-1}\right),

(3.11)

where

y={k^{2}\over M^{2}},\qquad\xi(y)={\sqrt{1+{4\over y}}}

(3.12)

and

z(\gamma)={\gamma\over x(\gamma)}.

(3.13)

We note that, in the perturbative regime
\gamma\ll 1
,
z\gg 1
and is exponentially large. In terms of the variables in (
3.11
) we have

8\pi{\cal E}_{(1)}(\gamma)=x\int_{0}^{\infty}\log\left\{1+{z\over y\xi}\log\left({\xi+1\over\xi-1}\right)\right\}{\rm d}y.

(3.14)

The integral in (
3.14
) is clearly UV divergent. We will regularize it by using the sharp momentum cutoff (SM) scheme introduced in

Campostrini and Rossi (1992)
;
Campostrini and Rossi (1993)
, which is perhaps the most useful one for
1/N
calculations in two-dimensional models.
We first write

\displaystyle 8\pi{\cal E}_{(1)}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=x\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\log\left(1+{z\over y\xi}\log\left({\xi+1\over\xi-1}\right)\right)-{z\over y\xi}\log\left({\xi+1\over\xi-1}\right)\right\}{\rm d}y

(3.15)

\displaystyle+\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\log\left({\xi+1\over\xi-1}\right){{\rm d}y\over y\xi}.

It is easy to see that the first line is finite, so the UV divergence is in the term in the second line. The
SM regularization goes as follows: we first introduce an UV
cutoff in the integrals,
\Lambda
, and we expand the integrand around
y\to\infty
. We then
subtract the part of the expansion responsible for the UV divergence. Since the
subtracted part might lead to an IR divergence when we integrate it, we introduce as well an IR
cutoff
\mu
(not to be confused with the mass appearing in (
2.2
)). The value of
\mu
parametrizes
the choice of scheme in the SM regularization,
and we will choose it in such a way
that we match the normal-ordering scheme used in perturbation theory.
When applied to the second line in (
3.15
) this method gives the regulated value

\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}\gamma\left\{\int_{0}^{\Lambda^{2}/M^{2}}\log\left({\xi+1\over\xi-1}\right){{\rm d}y\over y\xi}-\int_{\mu^{2}/M^{2}}^{\Lambda^{2}/M^{2}}{\log(y)\over y}{\rm d}y\right\}={\gamma\over 2}\log^{2}\left({M^{2}\over\mu^{2}}\right).

(3.16)

To match the scheme of perturbation theory, we consider the integral in the l.h.s. of (
2.7
). In the SM scheme this integral is regularized
as follows

\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}{1\over 4\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\Lambda^{2}/M^{2}}{{\rm d}y\over y+a^{2}/M^{2}}-\int_{\mu^{2}/M^{2}}^{\Lambda^{2}/M^{2}}{{\rm d}y\over y}\right)=-{1\over 4\pi}\log\left({a^{2}\over\mu^{2}}\right),

(3.17)

which agrees with the r.h.s. of (
2.7
) precisely when
\mu=m
. We conclude that the renormalized vacuum energy is given by

8\pi{\cal E}^{r}_{(1)}(\gamma)=x\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\log\left(1+{z\over y\xi}\log\left({\xi+1\over\xi-1}\right)\right)-{z\over y\xi}\log\left({\xi+1\over\xi-1}\right)\right\}{\rm d}y+{\gamma\over 2}\log^{2}(x).

(3.18)

We can use the gap equation in the form (
3.4
) to write the last term as a quadratic polynomial in
x
. Finally, the change of variable

{\rm e}^{\zeta}={\xi+1\over\xi-1}

(3.19)

leads to the useful form

\displaystyle 8\pi{\cal E}^{r}_{(1)}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=x\int_{0}^{\infty}2\sinh(\zeta)\left\{\log\left(1+{z\zeta\over 2\sinh(\zeta)}\right)-{z\zeta\over 2\sinh(\zeta)}\right\}{\rm d}\zeta

(3.20)

\displaystyle+{1\over 2\gamma}+{2x\over\gamma}+{2x^{2}\over\gamma}.

3.2
Trans-series for the ground state energy

Perhaps the most significant bonus of working at the non-perturbative large
N

vacuum is that, order by order in
1/N
, one obtains actual

functions
of the coupling constant (after regularization), and not just
formal power series. In particular,
{\cal E}^{r}_{(1)}(\gamma)
, as defined by the integral expression (

),
is a perfectly well-defined function of
\gamma
. It is plotted in Fig.
6
as a function of
\gamma
, and it can be easily
expanded in a series in
z
around

, as noted in
Sberveglieri et al. (2021)
, leading to a convergent series. In the theory
with a positive squared mass, this series is an expansion in
g/m^{2}
, and it is a
bona fide

perturbative expansion. However, in the theory with a negative squared mass considered in this paper,

z
is exponentially large. It can be easily checked that, in fact,
the regime of small
z
is never realized when
\gamma>0
,
and in particular
it is unrelated to the conventional perturbative regime. We then have to study the behavior
of
{\cal E}^{r}_{(1)}(\gamma)
when
\gamma
is very small and
z\gg 1
.

Although the expression

perturbative vacuum considered in section

, one expects that the asymptotic expansion
of the function (

A similar relationship can be found for the self-energies in e.g. the nonlinear sigma model and the Gross–Neveu models:
the asymptotic expansion of the functions obtained in the non-perturbative vacua agree with the conventional perturbative expansion,
up to a change of scheme. This was first
argued in
Campostrini and Rossi (1992)
and later established with Mellin transform techniques in
Beneke et al. (1998)
for the non-linear sigma model.
It was recently verified in detail in
Mariño and Miravitllas (2025)
in the case of the Gross–Neveu model. The method
used in
Beneke et al. (1998)
makes it possible to
obtain as well the explicit trans-series of non-perturbative corrections,
realizing explicitly in this way the renormalons as signals of non-perturbative physics
in the perturbative series.

The relation (
3.21

with some effort

2.33
) matches
correctly the exact result. Our goal now is to derive
analytically the asymptotic result (
3.21
), and to calculate as well the non-perturbative corrections. In other words,
we want to obtain the full trans-series expression of the exact result (
3.20
).
Our approach is therefore conceptually similar to the one in
Beneke et al. (1998)
;
Mariño and Miravitllas (2025)
, although the trans-asymptotic
analysis involves more elementary tools. As we will see, although (
3.20
) is deceptively simple, it leads to a
trans-series including an infinite number
of non-perturbative corrections, each of them being a non-trivial asymptotic series in the coupling
\gamma
.

Let us consider the function of
z
given by

{\cal I}(z)={1\over z}\int_{0}^{\infty}2\sinh(\zeta)\left\{\log\left(1+{z\zeta\over 2\sinh(\zeta)}\right)-{z\zeta\over 2\sinh(\zeta)}\right\}{\rm d}\zeta,

(3.22)

so that

8\pi{\cal E}^{r}_{(1)}=\gamma{\cal I}(z(\gamma))+{1\over 2\gamma}+{2x\over\gamma}+{2x^{2}\over\gamma}.

(3.23)

We now use the elementary result

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left({\rm e}^{-t(a+b)}\left(1+at\right)-{\rm e}^{-tb}\right){{\rm d}t\over t^{2}}=b\left(\log\left(1+{a\over b}\right)-{a\over b}\right)

(3.24)

to write

{\cal I}(z)={1\over z}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}\zeta\int_{0}^{\infty}\left({\rm e}^{-t\zeta z-2t\sinh(\zeta)}\left(1+zt\zeta\right)-{\rm e}^{-2t\sinh(\zeta)}\right){{\rm d}t\over t^{2}}.

(3.25)

The integrals over
\zeta
appearing here can be computed in closed form in terms of Bessel, Anger and Struve
functions. We first have (
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik (1996)
, 3.336, 1)

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\nu\zeta-\beta\sinh(\zeta)}{\rm d}\zeta={\pi\over\sin(\pi\nu)}\left({\bf J}_{\nu}(\beta)-J_{\nu}(\beta)\right),

(3.26)

where

{\bf J}_{\nu}(\beta)={1\over\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(\nu\theta-\beta\sin\theta){\rm d}\theta

(3.27)

is the Anger function, and
J_{\nu}(\beta)
is the Bessel function of the first kind. The result (
3.26
) is valid when
\nu
is not an integer. When
\nu=0
we have (
Paris and Kaminski (2001)
, section 5.1.3)

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\beta\sinh(\zeta)}{\rm d}\zeta={\pi\over 2}\left({\bf H}_{0}(\beta)-Y_{0}(\beta)\right),

(3.28)

where
{\bf H}_{0}(\beta)
is the Struve function and
Y_{0}(\beta)
is the Bessel function of the second kind.
Let us define

\displaystyle{\cal R}(\nu,t)

\displaystyle={\pi\over\sin(\pi\nu)}\left({\bf J}_{\nu}(2t)-J_{\nu}(2t)\right),

(3.29)

\displaystyle{\cal R}(t)

\displaystyle={\pi\over 2}\left({\bf H}_{0}(2t)-Y_{0}(2t)\right).

We will need the series expansion around
t=0
of
{\cal R}(\nu,t)
for generic
\nu

Paris and Kaminski (2001)
:

{\cal R}(\nu,t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}(\nu)t^{k}+t^{\nu}\sum_{k\geq 0}b_{k}(\nu)t^{2k},

(3.30)

where

a_{k}(\nu)={(-1)^{k}\over 2}{\Gamma\left({\nu\over 2}-{k\over 2}\right)\over\Gamma\left(1+{\nu\over 2}+{k\over 2}\right)}={(-2)^{k}\over\prod_{i=0}^{k}(\nu+k-2i)},\qquad b_{k}(\nu)={\Gamma\left(-\nu-k\right)\over k!}.

(3.31)

Using the above formulae, as well as the change of variables

t={\nu\over z}

(3.32)

we obtain the representation

{\cal I}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{{\cal R}\left(\nu,{\nu\over z}\right)-\nu\left({\partial{\cal R}(\nu,t)\over\partial\nu}\right)_{t=\nu/z}-{\cal R}\left({\nu\over z}\right)\right\}{{\rm d}\nu\over\nu^{2}}.

(3.33)

We can write this integral as a series in
1/z
, by simply using the series expansion
(
3.30
). However, when we do this, each separate term in the series will be given by a singular integral. The singularities are due to the form of the functions
a_{k}(\nu)
,
b_{k}(\nu)
, and occur at integers on the positive real axis. We can however regulate these singular integrals by deforming the integration contour as in (
2.38
). We will then write:

{\cal I}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}{\cal I}^{\pm}_{n}(\sigma)z^{-n},

(3.34)

where we have introduced the variable

\sigma=\log(z).

(3.35)

We have to distinguish two different cases. When
n\geq 3
, the integrals
{\cal I}^{\pm}_{n}(\sigma)
have the following structure

{\cal I}^{\pm}_{n}(\sigma)=\int_{{\cal C}_{\pm}}{\rm e}^{-\sigma\nu}{\cal F}_{n}(\nu){\rm d}\nu+\int_{{\cal C}_{\pm}}{\cal H}_{n}(\nu){\rm d}\nu,

(3.36)

where the functions
{\cal F}_{n}(\nu)
,
{\cal H}_{n}(\nu)
can be obtained from the functions
a_{k}(\nu)
,
b_{k}(\nu)
.
The functions
{\cal F}_{n}(\nu)
vanish when
n
is odd, and when
n=2k
,
k\geq 2
, they are given by

{\cal F}_{2k}(\nu)={1\over k!}\nu^{\nu+2k-2}\Gamma(-\nu-k)(\nu+2k),\qquad k\geq 2.

(3.37)

The functions
{\cal H}_{n}(\sigma)
are given by

{\cal H}_{n}(\nu)=na_{n}(\nu)\nu^{n-2},\qquad n\geq 3,

(3.38)

In obtaining these results we have integrated by parts in (
3.33
).

The cases
n=0,1,2
are special, and caution has to be exercised in the integration by parts due to the
singular behavior at
\nu=0
of each separate contribution to the integral (
3.33
). It is therefore convenient to regulate the integrals by introducing an explicit lower cutoff
\epsilon>0
, and taking the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
at the end. We then find,

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{\pm}_{0}(\sigma;\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\epsilon}^{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{\pm}}\infty}\left\{{\rm e}^{-\sigma\nu}\nu^{\nu-1}\Gamma(-\nu)+{\gamma_{E}+\log(\nu)-\sigma\over\nu^{2}}\right\}{\rm d}\nu+{a_{0}(\epsilon)\over\epsilon}+{\rm e}^{-\sigma\epsilon}\epsilon^{\epsilon-1}\Gamma(-\epsilon),

(3.39)

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{\pm}_{1}(\sigma;\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\epsilon}^{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{\pm}}\infty}{a_{1}(\nu)\over\nu}{\rm d}\nu+2\left(-1+\gamma_{E}+2\log(2)-\sigma\right)+2\log(\epsilon),

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{\pm}_{2}(\sigma;\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\epsilon}^{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{\pm}}\infty}\left\{{\rm e}^{-\sigma\nu}\nu^{\nu}(\nu+2)\Gamma(-\nu-1)+2a_{2}(\nu)\right\}{\rm d}\nu.

We note that

a_{0}(\nu)={1\over\nu},\qquad a_{1}(\nu)={2\over 1-\nu^{2}},\qquad a_{2}(\nu)={4\over\nu(\nu^{2}-4)}.

(3.40)

To obtain the results in the first two lines of (
3.39
) we have to use (
A.15
) in Appendix
A.2
.

We can now give explicit expressions for the
\epsilon\to 0
limit of the above integrals. An elementary result that is useful to perform
explicit calculations is that, if
f(\nu)
has only simple poles at
\nu=\nu_{n}
,
n=1,\cdots,s
, on the real positive axis, then we have

\int_{{\cal C}^{\pm}}f(\nu)\,{\rm d}\nu={\rm P}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\nu){\rm d}\nu\mp\pi{\rm i}\sum_{n=1}^{s}{\rm Res}_{\nu=\nu_{n}}f(\nu).

(3.41)

In the case
n=0
one finds

{\cal I}^{\pm}_{0}(\sigma)=\int_{{\cal C}_{\pm}}{\rm e}^{-\sigma\nu}{\cal F}_{0}(\nu){\rm d}\nu-{\sigma^{2}\over 2}+\sigma-\sigma\log(\sigma)-{3\log^{2}(\sigma)+\pi^{2}\over 6},

(3.42)

where

{\cal F}_{0}(\nu)=\nu^{\nu-1}\Gamma(-\nu)+{1\over\nu^{2}}+{\gamma_{E}+\log(\nu)\over\nu}

(3.43)

is regular at
\nu=0
. In the case
n=1
, use of (
3.41
) gives

{\cal I}_{1}^{\pm}(\sigma)=-2\sigma+2(\gamma_{E}+2\log(2)-1)\mp\pi{\rm i}.

(3.44)

Finally, the integral with
\nu=2
can be written as

{\cal I}^{\pm}_{2}(\sigma)=\int_{{\cal C}_{\pm}}{\rm e}^{-\sigma\nu}{\cal F}_{2}(\nu){\rm d}\nu-2\log(\sigma)+\int_{{\cal C}_{\pm}}{\cal H}_{2}(\nu){\rm d}\nu,

(3.45)

where

{\cal F}_{2}(\nu)=\nu^{\nu}(\nu+2)\Gamma(-\nu-1)-{2\over\nu},\qquad{\cal H}_{2}(\nu)={8\over\nu(\nu^{2}-4)}+2{{\rm e}^{-\nu}\over\nu}

(3.46)

are manifestly regular at
\nu=0
.

Let us now recapitulate our results so far. We have expressed the main contribution to

{\cal E}^{r}_{(1)}(\gamma)
as an infinite series (
3.34
), in which each term
{\cal I}_{n}^{\pm}(\sigma)

is given by an integral. Since
\sigma>0
, the integrands decay rapidly at infinity, and

{\cal I}_{n}^{\pm}(\sigma)
are well defined functions of
\sigma
. We seem to have an ambiguity due to the
two choices of contour, and leading to two different functions
{\cal I}^{\pm}_{n}(\sigma)
. These two
choices differ by the contribution of residues at the poles of the integrand, which is a purely
imaginary quantity. However, as we show in Appendix
A.2
, the difference cancels as we
sum over all
n\geq 0
with the factor
z^{-n}
. This is exactly as in the example studied in
Beneke et al. (1998)
,
and is a manifestation of the general mechanism of cancellation of renormalon ambiguities
discovered in
David (1984)
.
Finally, since
z^{-1}\sim{\rm e}^{-1/\gamma}
, the
{\cal I}_{n}^{\pm}(\sigma)
with
n\geq 1
give non-perturbative
corrections to the perturbative result.

We want to extract now formal power series from the above integrals. Let us denote

h_{n}={\rm P}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\cal H}_{n}(\nu){\rm d}\nu

(3.47)

and

h_{n,k}={\rm res}_{\nu=k}{\cal H}_{n}(\nu),\qquad n\geq 2,\quad k=1,\cdots,n.

(3.48)

Then, by using (
3.41
) we have

\int_{{\cal C}_{\pm}}{\cal H}_{n}(\nu){\rm d}\nu=h_{n}\mp\pi{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}h_{n,k}.

(3.49)

We will extend this definition to
n=0,1
by setting
h_{0}=0
and

h_{1}=2(\gamma_{E}+2\log(2)-1),\qquad h_{1,1}=1.

(3.50)

We consider now the asymptotic expansion of the following functions at large
\sigma
,

{\cal I}_{n}^{\pm}(\sigma)\pm\pi{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}h_{n,k}\sim\mathfrak{I}_{n}(\sigma),\qquad\sigma\to\infty.

(3.51)

We note that the
\mathfrak{I}_{n}(\sigma)
do not depend on the choice of integration contour,
since the two choices differ in exponentially small terms
{\rm e}^{-k\sigma}
,
k\geq 1
. The

\mathfrak{I}_{n}(\sigma)
are formal asymptotic series in
1/\sigma
, involving as well logarithmic
terms of the form
\log^{l}(\sigma)/\sigma^{k}
. The explicit
form of these series can be easily obtained from the expressions above, and one finds in this way the formal trans-series

\mathfrak{I}(\sigma,z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathfrak{I}_{n}(\sigma)z^{-n}

(3.52)

However, in our original theory, the perturbative parameter is not
\sigma^{-1}
, but
\gamma
, and we have to rewrite (
3.52
) in terms of it. One would expect that the resulting asymptotic expansions will have terms of the form
\gamma^{k}\log^{l}\gamma
, but this does not happen: they are formal power series in
\gamma
, involving at most a logarithmic term
\log(\gamma)
. We will now derive these explicit expansions. Let us note that

\sigma=\sigma_{0}+{2x\over\gamma},

(3.53)

where
\sigma_{0}
was introduced in (
2.44
). To recover the conventional trans-series we have to do a formal expansion of
\mathfrak{I}_{n}(\sigma)
around
\sigma=\sigma_{0}
.

The first step is to verify that we reproduce the perturbative sector, i.e. that

8\pi\mathfrak{E}_{1}(\gamma)=\gamma\mathfrak{I}_{0}(\sigma_{0})+{1\over 2\gamma}.

(3.54)

This can be easily checked analytically by using the expression
(
2.43
) and the calculation of (
A.17
) in the Appendix
A.2
.
This gives a proof of the asymptotic statement (
3.21
). Note that the ambiguities due to
renormalon singularities that appear in the resummation of the perturbative series, will
cancel against some of the explicit ambiguities appearing in (
3.49
).

Let us consider the trans-series corrections.
The functions
\mathfrak{I}_{n}(\sigma_{0})
for
n
odd are trivial. We now show that the formal
series
\mathfrak{I}_{2k}(\sigma_{0})
, and their derivatives w.r.t.
\sigma_{0}
, can be expressed as
formal power series in
\gamma
(except for
k=1
, where we have a
\log(\gamma)
term). To do this we just
reverse the argument that led to (
2.43
). We find, for the first two derivatives of
\mathfrak{I}_{0}(\sigma_{0})
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{0}(\sigma_{0})

\displaystyle={1\over\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\zeta/\gamma}{\rm d}\zeta\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\log(t)\over 1+t}\zeta}-1\right]{{\rm d}t\over 1+t}-{1\over\gamma},

(3.55)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}^{\prime\prime}_{0}(\sigma_{0})

\displaystyle=-{1\over\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\zeta/\gamma}{\rm d}\zeta\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta{\rm e}^{-{\log(t)\over 1+t}\zeta}{{\rm d}t\over(1+t)^{2}}-1,

Here and in subsequent formulae, the integral over
\zeta
has to be understood as a formal Laplace transform.
For
\mathfrak{I}_{2}(\sigma_{0})
we find

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}_{2}(\sigma_{0})

\displaystyle={1\over\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\zeta/\gamma}{\rm d}\zeta\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[{\zeta+2(1+t)\over\zeta+1+t}{\rm e}^{-{\log(t)\over 1+t}\zeta}-2\right]{{\rm d}t\over 1+t}

(3.56)

\displaystyle+2\log(\gamma)-2(\gamma_{E}+\log(2)).

For the remaining cases we have the single formula,

\mathfrak{I}^{(p)}_{2k}(\sigma_{0})={(-1)^{p}\over\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-\zeta/\gamma}{\rm d}\zeta\int_{0}^{\infty}{\zeta^{2k-2+p}(\zeta+2k(1+t))\over\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\zeta+i(1+t))}{\rm e}^{-{\log(t)\over 1+t}\zeta}{{\rm d}t\over(1+t)^{k+p}}+h_{2k}\delta_{p0},

(3.57)

which is valid for
2k+p\geq 3
. Note that these formulae give the formal series in
\gamma

through their explicit Borel transforms. These are obtained as integrals over
t
which are convergent when
|\zeta|<1
, and
can be easily computed as formal series in
\zeta
around
\zeta=0
. The lateral resummations of the resulting
series in
\gamma
reconstruct the functions
{\cal I}_{n}^{\pm}(\sigma_{0})
:

{\cal I}_{n}^{\pm}(\sigma_{0})=s_{\pm}\left(\mathfrak{I}_{n}\right)(\gamma)\mp\pi{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}h_{n,k}.

(3.58)

With the above results we can promote the asymptotic expansion (
3.21
) to a full trans-series asymptotics for the NLO ground state energy.
We will write it as

{\cal E}_{(1)}^{r}(\gamma)\sim\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}^{(k)}(\gamma)x^{k},

(3.59)

where
\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}^{(0)}(\gamma)=\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}(\gamma)
is the perturbative series (
2.33
).
We have chosen
x
as the non-perturbative parameter, since it is the natural one coming from the gap equation,
but one could also use
{\rm e}^{-1/\gamma}
. The coefficients of
\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}^{(k)}(\gamma)
as series in
\gamma

will be given by integrals akin to (
2.34
). We find, for the first non-perturbative correction,

8\pi\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}^{(1)}(\gamma)=\mathfrak{I}_{1}(\sigma_{0})+2\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{0}(\sigma_{0})+{2\over\gamma}\mp\pi{\rm i},

(3.60)

where the third term in the r.h.s. comes from the second term in the last line of (
3.20
). Explicitly,

\displaystyle 4\pi\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}^{(1)}(\gamma)=-{1\over\gamma}-\log(\gamma)-1+\gamma_{E}+2\log(2)+\sum_{n\geq 1}(-\gamma)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\log^{n}(t)\over(1+t)^{n+1}}{\rm d}t\mp{\pi{\rm i}\over 2}

(3.61)

\displaystyle=-{1\over\gamma}-\log(\gamma)-1+\gamma_{E}+2\log(2)+{\pi^{2}\over 6}\gamma^{2}+{\pi^{2}\over 2}\gamma^{3}+\left(\pi^{2}+\frac{7\pi^{4}}{60}\right)\gamma^{4}+\cdots\mp{\pi{\rm i}\over 2}.

This result can be verified numerically by considering the asymptotic behavior of the function

{\cal E}^{r}_{(1)}(\gamma)-s_{\pm}\left(\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}\right)(\gamma).

(3.62)

As usual in these representations, the choice of sign for the last term in (
3.60
),
\mp\pi{\rm i}/2
, must be correlated
to the choice of lateral Borel resummation for the perturbative series, so that the ambiguity associated to the first
renormalon cancels.

As a final illustration, let us consider the second trans-series. It is given by

8\pi\gamma^{2}\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}^{(2)}(\gamma)=\mathfrak{I}_{2}(\sigma_{0})+2\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{1}(\sigma_{0})+2\mathfrak{I}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\sigma_{0})+2\gamma\mp\pi{\rm i}.

(3.63)

Explicitly, one finds

\displaystyle 8\pi\gamma^{2}\mathfrak{E}_{(1)}^{(2)}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=-2(\log(2)+\gamma_{E}+3)+2\log(\gamma)+\gamma+{2\pi^{2}\gamma^{2}\over 3}+{5\pi^{2}\gamma^{3}\over 3}

(3.64)

\displaystyle+{\pi^{2}\over 30}(95+14\pi^{2})\gamma^{4}+{\cal O}(\gamma^{5})\pm\pi{\rm i}.

3.3
The non-perturbative two-point function

N
. The wavy line corresponds to the large

propagator of the
\tilde{\chi}
field.

Another quantity that can be easily computed in the non-perturbative large
N
vacuum at NLO in
1/N
is the self-energy of the
\Phi
field. Its calculation is very similar to what is done in the non-linear sigma model and the Gross–Neveu models in
Campostrini and Rossi (1992)
, see
Sberveglieri et al. (2021)
;
Liu (2025b)
for further details. One finds

\Sigma_{(1)}(p)=\int{{\rm d}^{d}k\over(2\pi)^{d}}{\Delta(k^{2},M^{2})\over(p+k)^{2}+M^{2}}-{\Delta(0,M^{2})\over 2}\int{{\rm d}^{d}k\over(2\pi)^{d}}{{\rm d}^{d}q\over(2\pi)^{d}}{1\over(q^{2}+M^{2})^{2}}{\Delta(k^{2},M^{2})\over(q+k)^{2}+M^{2}}.

(3.65)

This is non-perturbative and
will produce an actual function of
\gamma
, after renormalization. We recall that
\Delta(k^{2},M^{2})
is given by (
3.9
).
The first and second terms in the r.h.s. of (
3.65
) correspond to the two diagrams shown in Fig.
7
. We can simplify the second term by noting that,

\displaystyle{\partial\Delta^{-1}(k^{2},M^{2})\over\partial M^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\int{{\rm d}^{d}q\over(2\pi)^{d}}{1\over(q^{2}+M^{2})^{2}}{\Pi(k^{2},M^{2})\over(q+k)^{2}+M^{2}}

(3.66)

\displaystyle=-{2\over k^{2}\xi^{2}}\left(\Pi(k^{2},M^{2})+{1\over 8\pi M^{2}}\right),

and one finds the useful expression

\displaystyle\Sigma_{(1)}(p)

\displaystyle=\int{{\rm d}^{d}k\over(2\pi)^{d}}{\kappa\over 1+\kappa\Pi(k^{2},M^{2})}{1\over(p+k)^{2}+M^{2}}

(3.67)

\displaystyle-\Delta(0,M^{2})\int{{\rm d}^{d}k\over(2\pi)^{d}}{\kappa\over 1+\kappa\Pi(k^{2},M^{2})}{1\over k^{2}\xi^{2}}\left(\Pi(k^{2},M^{2})+{1\over 8\pi M^{2}}\right)

We also note that, in this case,

\Delta(0,M^{2})=\left({1\over\kappa}+{1\over 8\pi M^{2}}\right)^{-1}={2\kappa\over 2+z}.

(3.68)

After some massaging one finds

\displaystyle\Sigma_{(1)}(p)

\displaystyle={\kappa\over 4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[{1\over 1+zF(y)}\left({1\over{\sqrt{(1+y+v)^{2}-4vy}}}-{z-2\over z+2}{1\over y+4}\right)-{4\over z+2}{1\over y+4}\right]{\rm d}y

(3.69)

\displaystyle+{\kappa\over 2\pi}{1\over 2+z}\int_{0}^{\infty}{{\rm d}y\over y+4},

where
y
is the variable introduced in (
3.12
) and

v={p^{2}\over M^{2}}={\mathfrak{p}^{2}\over x}.

(3.70)

The first line of (
3.69
) is finite, but the integral in the second line is UV divergent. We renormalize
it by using again SM regularization. Its finite part is obtained from (
3.17
) or directly from (
2.7
).

After all these calculations, we find a renormalized self-energy which can be written in terms of
dimensionless quantities as

{\Sigma^{r}_{(1)}(p)\over m^{2}}=S(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)+\mathsf{m},

(3.71)

where

S(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)=\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}{{\rm d}y\over 1+zF(y)}\left({1\over{\sqrt{(1+y+v)^{2}-4vy}}}-{1\over{\sqrt{y^{2}+4y}}}\right),

(3.72)

and

\mathsf{m}=\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[{1\over 1+zF(y)}\left({1\over{\sqrt{y^{2}+4y}}}-{z-2\over z+2}{1\over y+4}\right)-{4\over z+2}{1\over y+4}\right]{\rm d}y-\gamma{2\log(4x)\over 2+z}.

(3.73)

The function (
3.72
) satisfies the property

S(-x;\gamma)=0,

(3.74)

where the evaluation point
\mathfrak{p}^{2}=-x
corresponds to
p^{2}=-M^{2}
or
v=-1
.
The non-perturbative two-point function of the
\Phi
field at NLO in
1/N
is then given by

{1\over m^{2}}S^{-1}_{\rm NP}(p^{2})=\mathfrak{p}^{2}+x+{1\over N}(S(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)+\mathsf{m})+{\cal O}(N^{-2}).

(3.75)

Due to the property (
3.74
), the pole mass
m^{2}_{p}
is given by

{m^{2}_{p}\over m^{2}}=x+{1\over N}\mathsf{m}+{\cal O}(N^{-2}),

(3.76)

i.e.
\mathsf{m}
gives the
1/N
correction to the pole mass. A similar calculation of the pole mass
for the non-linear sigma model and the Gross–Neveu model was done in
Campostrini and Rossi (1992)
. We have seen that the pole mass
vanishes in perturbation theory, at this order in
1/N
, so
\mathsf{m}
must be purely non-perturbative. In addition, the asymptotic expansion of the exact function
S(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)
, at small
\gamma
, must be given by the formal power series (
2.67
), i.e.

S(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma)\sim\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\gamma).

(3.77)

We have checked this asymptotic behavior numerically. Using the results in
Liu (2025b)
for the theory
with a positive squared mass, it is possible to verify (
3.77
)
Liu (2025a)
.

In contrast to what happens in asymptotically free theories, where the
1/N
correction to the pole mass is proportional to the dynamically generated scale
Biscari et al. (1990)
;
Campostrini and Rossi (1992)
;
Forgacs et al. (1991)
, here
\mathsf{m}
is given by a (resummed) trans-series and in particular it has a non-trivial dependence on the coupling constant
\gamma
. Let us give some details on this, although the derivation is similar to the one of the ground state energy. If we change variables as we did in (
3.20
), we find

\displaystyle\mathsf{m}

\displaystyle=2\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{{1\over z\zeta+2\sinh(\zeta)}\left[\sinh(\zeta)-{z-2\over z+2}(\cosh(\zeta)-1)\right]-{2\over z+2}\tanh(\zeta/2)\right\}{\rm d}\zeta

(3.78)

\displaystyle-\gamma{2\log(4x)\over 2+z}.

We now use the integration trick

{1\over a}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-at}\,{\rm d}t

(3.79)

to write

\displaystyle\mathsf{m}

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\left(1-{2\over z}\right){\cal R}\left(\nu,{\nu\over z}\right)-{1\over z}\left({\partial{\cal R}(\nu,t)\over\partial t}\right)_{t=\nu/z}-{z-2\over z+2}{1\over\nu}-{4\over z+2}{{\rm e}^{-4\nu/z}\over\nu}\right]{\rm d}\nu

(3.80)

\displaystyle+m(\gamma,z)

where

m(\gamma,z)=-\gamma{4\log(2)\over z+2}+{2\over z}{2\gamma+z\over z+2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}m_{n}(\gamma)z^{-n}.

(3.81)

As in the case of the ground state energy, we can calculate the trans-series expansion of this quantity by doing an expansion of the integrand in
1/z
. One obtains in this way

\mathsf{m}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathsf{m}^{\pm}_{n}z^{-n},

(3.82)

where

\mathsf{m}^{\pm}_{n}=\gamma\int_{{\cal C}_{\pm}}\left({\rm e}^{-\sigma\nu}{\cal M}_{n}(\nu)+{\cal N}_{n}(\nu)\right){\rm d}\nu-2\delta_{n1}\log(\sigma)+2^{n+1}(-1)^{n}\gamma\sigma+m_{n}(\gamma).

(3.83)

The functions
{\cal M}_{n}(\nu)
,
{\cal N}_{n}(\nu)
are regular at
\nu=0
but have poles at non-negative integers. They are given as follows. For
n=1
we have

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{1}(\nu)

\displaystyle=-2\left(\nu^{\nu}\Gamma(-\nu)+{1\over\nu}\right),\qquad{\cal N}_{1}(\nu)

\displaystyle={2\over\nu}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-\nu}\right)-{2\over 1+\nu}.

(3.84)

For
n\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{n}(\nu)

\displaystyle=-\nu^{\nu+n-1}c_{n}b_{[{n+1\over 2}]}(\nu),

(3.85)

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{n}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\nu^{n-1}\left((\nu-n)a_{n}(\nu)-2a_{n-1}(\nu)\right)-2^{n+1}(-1)^{n}{1-{\rm e}^{-\nu}\over\nu},

where
c_{n}=2
if
n
is odd, and
c_{n}=n
if
n
is even. We recall that
a_{k}(\nu)
,
b_{k}(\nu)
were introduced in
(
3.31
). With the above results we can write a trans-series expansion for
\mathsf{m}
, of the form

\mathsf{m}\sim\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathfrak{M}_{n}(\gamma)x^{n},

(3.86)

and the formal power series
\mathfrak{M}_{n}(\gamma)
can be calculated by using the same techniques that we used
in the case of the ground state energy. One finds for example, for the first correction,

\mathfrak{M}_{1}(\gamma)=-{2\over\gamma}-2\log(\gamma)+2(\gamma_{E}+2\log(2))+2\sum_{n\geq 1}(-\gamma)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\log^{n}(t)\over(1+t)^{n}}{\rm d}t,

(3.87)

which is almost identical to the first correction to the ground state energy in (
3.61
).

4
Conclusions and open problems

In this paper we have studied the renormalon appearing in the ground state energy of the quartic scalar theory with negative mass squared.
This renormalon was discovered in
Mariño and Reis (2020)
from a purely perturbative approach, and its connection to the
non-perturbative result obtained e.g. in
Sberveglieri et al. (2021)
in the large
N

expansion was not completely clear. Similar results for the self-energies of solvable two-dimensional models
Campostrini and Rossi (1992)
;
Beneke et al. (1998)
;
Mariño and Miravitllas (2025)
suggested that the
perturbative result of
Mariño and Reis (2020)
should give the asymptotic expansion of the exact ground state energy obtained in the non-perturbative vacuum.
We have verified this in detail and found in addition the complete series of trans-series corrections to the non-perturbative result. This shows that the
IR renormalons obtained in
Mariño and Reis (2020)
are the smoking gun for this infinite series of non-perturbative corrections. An additional lesson of this exercise is that
the extraction of the asymptotic perturbative series and its corrections from a non-perturbative result might be far from obvious. In that sense, the perturbative
expansion around the “false” vacuum discussed in
Jevicki (1977)
;
Mariño and Reis (2020)
is a powerful method to obtain asymptotic results.

In addition, we have studied the
O(N)
-invariant two-point function of the
\Phi
field at the perturbative and the non-perturbative level. We have
extended Jevicki’s approach in
Jevicki (1977)
and verified IR finiteness once all diagrams are added. As in asymptotically
free theories, the self-energy has IR renormalon singularities at NLO in
1/N
which can be analyzed in detail. The corresponding pole mass
vanishes at all orders in perturbation theory but it is given by a non-perturbative trans-series which can be explicitly computed. Although we have not
worked out the non-perturbative corrections to the full two-point function, which become more complicated,
the techniques introduced recently in
Liu (2025b)
could be used to
find them explicitly. This is yet another example where the perturbative calculation, although intricate due to the presence IR divergences, gives an easier
access to the asymptotic series than the non-perturbative result.

Our result makes it clear that the physical origin of all these non-perturbative corrections is the
structure of the true vacuum,
summarized in (
3.7
):
in this vacuum, the condensate associated to
\Phi
vanishes (in contrast to what is assumed in
perturbation theory), while the condensate of the
operator
\Phi^{2}
develops a non-perturbative correction to its classical value. Perhaps the
most interesting problem open by this investigation would be to reproduce the
non-perturbative corrections directly from an OPE/condensate calculation, as it was done
recently in
Mariño and Miravitllas (2025)
for the self-energy of the Gross–Neveu model. Similar calculations
have been done in the past in the somewhat
inverse situation, in which one wants to reproduce
results in the spontaneously broken theory by using the OPE with condensates in
the disordered phase
Taylor and McClain (1983)
;
Novikov et al. (1985)
. Here, we would like
on the contrary to go back to the disordered phase, and to turn on in addition a
non-perturbative condensate for the
\Phi^{2}
operator. It is likely that the recent
results of
Liu (2025b)
will be very helpful for such a calculation, at least in the case
of the two-point function, in which we can use the OPE. In the case of the
ground state energy we cannot use the OPE directly, and we would need a
generalization of the ideas and techniques of
Shifman et al. (1979)
to reproduce the trans-series
obtained in this paper. This remains an important and challenging problem.
In this respect, it is interesting to note that, in the first non-perturbative correction (
3.61
), the infinite
sum in the first line is
precisely what would one obtain by inserting an appropriate “condensate” in the ring diagrams
of the perturbative series.
Thanks to formulae like (
3.57
), it might be possible to decode as well the higher corrections in terms of
diagrams with condensates.

Our analysis shows that, although the renormalons in the perturbative series of the ground state energy
are “smoking guns” for non-perturbative corrections, they do not capture them in any detail. As already
shown in
Mariño and Reis (2020)
, the singularities of the Borel transform of the perturbative series are poles,
and their associated trans-series are trivial. In contrast, the non-perturbative corrections involve non-trivial series.
For example, at the first non-perturbative order
x\approx{\rm e}^{-1/\gamma}
, the non-perturbative correction is given by
(
3.61
), while the renormalon only captures the ambiguous term
\mp\pi{\rm i}/2
. This situation is typical of the
large
N
analysis of
Beneke et al. (1998)
;
Mariño and Miravitllas (2025)
;
Di Pietro et al. (2021)
, and also of the standard renormalon analysis at large number of
flavours
N_{f}
in QCD. It is another instance of the “weak” resurgence scenario in
Di Pietro et al. (2021)
. However, as pointed out
in
Di Pietro et al. (2021)
, this might be an artifact of the large
N
expansion, and perhaps at finite
N
we
have a “strong” resurgence scenario, i.e. it might happen that at finite
N
the resurgent
structure of the perturbative series encodes all the needed corrections. This has been recently shown to be the
case for the perturbative series of the ground state energy in the Lieb–Liniger model
Bajnok et al. (2026)
, which is a non-relativistic
version of the
O(2)
quartic model studied here, as noted in
Jevicki (1977)
;
Mariño and Reis (2020)
.

Finally, there have been proposals for a semiclassical understanding of renormalon corrections,
see e.g.
Bhattacharya et al. (2026)
for a recent one. In our view, this and previous proposals remain at the very least vague,
and they have not made any contact with the -by now extensive- collection of explicit renormalon trans-series
obtained both at large
N
and finite
N
in many two-dimensional models
(see e.g.
Reis (2022)
;
Serone (2024)
for reviews in the integrable case). The example in this paper (together with
the self-energy of the Gross–Neveu model studied in
Mariño and Miravitllas (2025)
) is perhaps the simplest case where a
proposal for a semiclassical theory of renormalons could prove its worth.

A.1
Perturbative calculation of the two-point function

We want to calculate the integrals appearing in the expression (
2.63
) for
{\cal G}_{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2};\epsilon)
,
as
\epsilon\to 0
. To do this, we adapt a trick from
Beneke et al. (1998)
. We recall that the Mellin transform of a function
f(y)
is defined as

{\cal M}[f(y);s]=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{s-1}f(y){\rm d}y.

(A.1)

In
Oberhettinger (1974)
, 2.64, we find

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{s}}{\sqrt{(x+y+\epsilon)^{2}-4xy}}{\rm d}y=(x+\epsilon)^{s}B(s+1,-s){}_{2}F_{1}\left(s+1,-s,1;{x\over x+\epsilon}\right).

(A.2)

We also have the expansion

\displaystyle(x+\epsilon)^{s}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(s+1,-s,1;{x\over x+\epsilon}\right)=

(A.3)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{\displaystyle x^{s}\over\Gamma(-s)}\sum_{k\geq 0}{(-s)_{k}\over k!^{2}\Gamma(s+1-k)}\left\{\log(\epsilon)-\log(x)-2\psi(k+1)+\psi(-s+k)+\psi(s+1-k)\right\}\left({\epsilon\over x}\right)^{k}.

In the limit
\epsilon\to 0
we can just keep the term
k=0
in the above series. We then get,

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}y\frac{y^{s}}{\sqrt{(x+y+\epsilon)^{2}-4xy}}=-x^{s}\left\{\log(\epsilon)-\log(x)+2\gamma_{E}+\psi(-s)+\psi(s+1)\right\}+{\cal O}(\epsilon).

(A.4)

Therefore, we can calculate the integrals appearing in (
2.60
), in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, by an inverse
Mellin transform:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{g_{n}(y)\over{\sqrt{(y+\mathfrak{p}^{2}+\epsilon)^{2}-4y\mathfrak{p}^{2}}}}{\rm d}y=-\left(\log(\epsilon)-\log(\mathfrak{p}^{2})+2\gamma_{E}\right)g_{n}(\mathfrak{p}^{2})

(A.5)

\displaystyle-\int_{c-{\rm i}\infty}^{c+{\rm i}\infty}\mathfrak{p}^{s}\left(\psi(-s)+\psi(s+1)\right){\cal M}\left[g_{n}(y);-s\right]{{\rm d}s\over 2\pi{\rm i}}+{\cal O}(\epsilon).

We note that the divergent part, proportional to
\log(\epsilon)
, leads to the result in (
2.64
).
The inverse Mellin transform in the second line can be computed in various ways.
One possibility is to do a sum over residues,

\displaystyle-\int_{c-{\rm i}\infty}^{c+{\rm i}\infty}\mathfrak{p}^{2s}\left(\psi(-s)+\psi(s+1)\right){\cal M}\left[g_{n}(y);-s\right]{{\rm d}s\over 2\pi{\rm i}}

(A.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}{\rm Res}_{s=m}\left(\mathfrak{p}^{2s}\left(\psi(-s)+\psi(s+1)\right){\cal M}\left[g_{n}(y);-s\right]\right).

It is however possible to obtain a very useful, closed integral formula by using general properties of the Mellin transform.

{\cal M}\left[-xf^{\prime}(x);s\right]=s{\cal M}\left[f(x);s\right],

(A.9)

and the following convolution formula: if
F_{i}(s)={\cal M}\left[f_{i}(t);s\right]

(A.10)

We also note that

\psi(s)+\psi(1-s)=2\psi(s)+\pi\cot(\pi s),

(A.11)

as well as the inverse Mellin transforms

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{-1}\left[\pi\cot(\pi s);x\right]

\displaystyle={\rm P}{1\over 1-x},

(A.12)

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{-1}\left[{\psi(s)\over s};x\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\gamma_{E}-\log\left({1\over x}-1\right),&\text{if $x<1$}\\
0,&\text{if $x>1$}.\end{cases}

One obtains in this way,

\displaystyle\int_{c-{\rm i}\infty}^{c+{\rm i}\infty}\mathfrak{p}^{-2s}\left(\psi(s)+\psi(1-s)\right){\cal M}\left[g_{n}(y);s\right]{{\rm d}s\over 2\pi{\rm i}}
