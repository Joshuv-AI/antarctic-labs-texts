On performance bounds for topology optimization
Anna Dalklint, Rasmus E. Christiansen, Ole Sigmund

Abstract
Topology optimization has matured to become a powerful engineering design tool that is capable of designing extraordinary structures and materials taking into account various physical phenomena. Despite the method’s great advancements in recent years, several unanswered questions remain. This paper takes a step towards answering one of the larger questions, namely: How far from the global optimum is a given topology optimized design? Typically this is a hard question to answer, as almost all interesting topology optimization problems are non-convex. Unfortunately, this non-convexity implies that local minima may plague the design space, resulting in optimizers ending up in suboptimal designs. In this work, we investigate performance bounds for topology optimization via a computational framework that utilizes Lagrange duality theory. This approach provides a viable measure of how “close” a given design is to the global optimum for a subset of optimization formulations. The method’s capabilities are exemplified via several numerical examples, including the design of mode converters and resonating plates.



1
Introduction

Topology optimization has seen significant advancements since its inception in 1988 via the seminal paper by
Bendsøe and Kikuchi [10]
. The technique can be used to design remarkably sophisticated structures involving various physical phenomena such as acoustics (
Dühring et al. [20]
,
Dilgen and Aage [19]
,
Christiansen et al. [18]
), plasticity (
Maute et al. [35]
,
Wallin et al. [50]
), contact (
Strömberg and Klarbring [46]
,
Bluhm et al. [11]
), fluids (
Borrvall and Petersson [13]
,
Alexandersen et al. [2]
) and photonics (
Jensen and Sigmund [29]
,
Wang et al. [51]
). Via computational and technical developments, the method has even matured to allow the design of high-resolution structures involving billions of design degrees-of-freedom (
Aage et al. [1]
,
Liu et al. [33]
). For a comprehensive review of the applicability of topology optimization, see
Sigmund and Maute [43]
. In this work, we limit ourselves to the density-based approach to topology optimization, although we acknowledge that other approaches exist, cf.
Sigmund and Maute [43]
.

Even though the aforementioned applications of topology optimization truly illustrate its power as a design tool, questions remain regarding its efficacy in the sense of design
optimality
. Traditionally, the topology optimization problems are solved in a staggered fashion, i.e. the state variables, e.g. the mechanical displacement or electric field, are given implicitly via the equilibrium equations. This allows the efficient use of gradient-based optimizers such as the method of moving asymptotes (MMA,
Svanberg [47]
) by computing the sensitivities using the adjoint method (
Tortorelli and Michaleris [48]
). Nonetheless, these optimization problems are rarely convex, wherefore the design space contains local minima that may obstructs the optimizer’s search for the optimal design. So-called global optimizers such as Genetic Algorithms, Particle Swarms or Simulated Annealing do not guarantee global optima either, cf.
Sigmund [42]
. Historically, the topology optimization community has addressed this issue in various ways. For certain “simple” problems, such as compliance minimization, a comparison can be made to state-of-the-art solutions, which can be argued to be at least nearly-optimal from an engineering perspective. Also, certain problems are naturally bounded, such as those involving efficiency metrics, or can be analytically bounded. For those problems, topology optimization has proven to yield near-optimal designs, cf.
Christiansen [17]
,
Sigmund and Torquato [44]
,
Guest and Prévost [26]
,
Andreassen et al. [3]
and
Wang and Sigmund [53]
. For problems lacking natural or analytical bounds, it is common to resort to heuristic methods wherein the results of a number of optimization runs, each using different initializations of the design variables, are compared. The hope is that the superior design in this set is at least close to the global optimum. Unfortunately, nothing guarantees such a claim. In such cases, the topology optimization community would greatly benefit from a measure of how far from optimality a specific design is; in general topology optimization lacks
performance bounds
.

Under certain simplifying assumptions, there can exist analytical bounds to physical problems. One example is the Hashin-Shtrikman bound (
Hashin and Shtrikman [27]
), which expresses the limits of isotropic linear elastic composite material properties. Another is the limit of the effective thermal expansion coefficients in relation to the effective elastic moduli of two-phase materials (
Rosen and Hashin [39]
). In free-space optics, other examples include the optical diffraction limit (
Born and Wolf [12]
) or bounds on the local density of state (
Miller et al. [36]
). Of course, the fundamental bounds of mass, energy and momentum conservation also exist. There has recently been a surge of research that explicitly addresses the development of
computational
bounds. These bounds are, as the name implies, based on numerical simulations, in contrast to analytical or physics-based bounds. In general, the idea behind the computational bounds emanates from traditional Lagrange duality theory; the Lagrange dual function always bounds the primal objective function (
Boyd and Vandenberghe [15]
). It turns out that the tightest possible bound is found by solving the dual problem to optimality, and this is where the power of the method resides, since the dual problem is guaranteed to be convex even when the primal problem is not.

Of course, the success of the computational bounds relies on the fact that the dual problem can be formulated and solved in an efficient manner, and that its solution does not coincide with the trivial bound.
Gertler et al. [23]
argues that many photonic inverse design problems can be posed as quadratically constrained quadratic programs (QCQP), for which the dual (i.e. bound) problem coincides with a convex semidefinite program (SDP) obtained using a so-called semidefinite relaxation technique (
Shor [41]
,
Luo et al. [34]
). Indeed, many topology optimization problems involve objectives and constraints that are, or can be reformulated as, quadratic functions in the state field. Unfortunately, the solution times of SDPs typically scale with
n^{3.5}
or
n^{4}
for
n
degrees-of-freedom, wherefore the application of SDPs to “large-scale” optimization problems is still hindered (
Gertler et al. [23]
,
Luo et al. [34]
). However, there exists promising recent research that utilizes the sparsity characteristics of the involved matrices to drastically speed up the solution times (
Gertler et al. [23]
).

Although the idea of utilizing Lagrange duality to bound optimization problems is far from new, it has only recently been applied to bound inverse design problems. The methodology was exemplified by
Angeris et al. [7]
,
Angeris et al. [6]
to compute bounds for photonic design optimization problems with mode-matching objectives. Following this paper, bounds to various photonics design problems were developed, such as maximum far-field power-bandwidth (
Kuang and Miller [32]
), mode converters (
Angeris et al. [5]
), maximum reflectivity contrast between frequencies (
Shim et al. [40]
) and radiative Purcell enhancement (
Molesky et al. [37]
). Recently, the approach was generalized to objectives that are quadratic functions in the field by

Angeris et al. [5]
and
Gertler et al. [23]
.

Even though the computational bounds have found success in many photonic design problems, their application to other physics remains unexplored. One reason for this is that the so far developed formulations, which target 2D photonics, i.e. scalar-fields, only use Green’s function solutions and/or finite difference discretizations of the physics. Many vector-field physical problems, such as elasticity, cannot be accurately and readily solved by finite difference schemes, at least not for arbitrarily complex geometries and boundary conditions. Instead, one usually employs the finite element method in such contexts (
Bathe [8]
). The finite element method is also the traditional approach to topology optimization. This somewhat obstructs the direct translation of the previously developed computational bounds to a general topology optimization context.

In this work, we develop a framework for calculating computational performance bounds in the context of traditional density-based topology optimization. We compute the performance bounds for two different design problems: 1) photonic mode converters and 2) elastic resonating plates, and then solve these problems using the traditional topology optimization approach assessing the achieved performance. Our framework is based on the work by
Angeris et al. [5]
, which relies on a QCQP reformulation of the primal optimization problem, such that a SDP dual problem is obtained. We exemplify how the framework can be extended to problems wherein the finite element method is employed. Also, in contrast to
Angeris et al. [5]
, we introduce additional cross-correlation constraints in the formulation which tightens the bounds.

2
Preliminaries

We seek performance bounds to non-convex optimization problems on the form

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}\underset{\text{\boldmath$z$},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}{\text{max}}\ \ f(\text{\boldmath$z$})\\[10.0pt]
\text{s.t.}\ \ \begin{cases}\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\text{\boldmath$z$}=\text{\boldmath$b$},\\[5.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mathcal{B},\end{cases}\end{array}\end{cases}

(1)

where the set
\mathcal{B}
defines the box constraints,

\text{\boldmath$z$}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the
finite element
discretized complex state field, whereas
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the real discretized design density field, where
n
is the number of degrees-of-freedom. The equality constraint in (
1
) stems from the PDE that
z
must fulfill (for e.g. elasticity
A
is the stiffness matrix,
z
the displacement field and
b
is the external force), where
\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is Hermitian and
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We follow
Angeris et al. [5]
and further limit (
1
) to objectives which are ratios of
quadratic
functions, i.e.

f(\text{\boldmath$z$})=\frac{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$P$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$p$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+r}{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$q$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+s},

(2)

where
\bar{(\,\cdot\,)}
denotes the conjugate transpose,

\text{\boldmath$P$},\text{\boldmath$Q$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are real symmetric matrices,

\text{\boldmath$p$},\text{\boldmath$q$}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Existing frameworks for performance bounds of inverse problems (e.g.
Angeris et al. [5]
) relies on certain characteristics of the optimization problem. This explains why (
1
) is somewhat simplified in comparison to a conventional topology optimization problem; only the PDE and box constraints are included, no additional constraints such as a volume constraint is present. As mentioned in the introduction, the techniques utilized herein to compute the performance bounds rely on a QCQP reformulation of (
1
), wherefore the incorporation of arbitrary constraints is not straightforward, as will be obvious later on. Nonetheless, ideas of how to include a volume constraint exist (
Kuang [31]
). However, in our numerical examples, the volume constraint is not explicitly needed, wherefore we can simplify the formulation by excluding it. Incorporation of more complex constraints, such as stress constraints or eigenfrequency constraints, still remains an unexplored avenue.

Certain properties are also required for the equilibrium constraint
\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\text{\boldmath$z$}=\text{\boldmath$b$}
for it to conform to the performance bound framework. Firstly, it should be possible to split the matrix
A
into two matrices; one independent of
\theta
and one dependent of
\theta
, such that
\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})=\text{\boldmath$C$}+\text{\boldmath$D$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
. Here it is worth emphasizing that
\text{\boldmath$C$}=\text{\boldmath$0$}
is a valid choice. Secondly, the design dependency should enter
\text{\boldmath$D$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
in a specific way, namely
\text{\boldmath$D$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})=\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$d$})
. We acknowledge that these restrictions are rather harsh, and strictly limit the applicability of the methodology to a subset of design problems. In a traditional finite element setting, the aforementioned conditions basically reduces the design problem space to dynamic problems where the design density variables
\theta
dictates the mass distribution, since the mass matrix can be made diagonal using lumping techniques, such that
\theta
distributes point masses in the degrees-of-freedom. There exists ideas of how to extend the method to problems where the above conditions do not hold, i.e. static problems (cf.
Angeris [4]
), but we leave the exploration of such problems to future work.

2.1
Real field reformulation

In the following, we base the derivations on the aforementioned assumptions. The first task is to reformulate the complex-field equilibrium constraint in (
1
) into its real-field equivalent, which allows the use of traditional optimization solvers. To this end, a usual complex split can be utilized such that

\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\text{\boldmath$z$}=\text{\boldmath$b$}\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}))&-\Im(\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}))\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}))&\Re(\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}))\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$b$}),\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{\boldmath$b$})\end{bmatrix},

(3)

where
\Re(\,\cdot\,)
and
\Im(\,\cdot\,)
denote the real and imaginary part operations, respectively. By inserting the assumption
\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})=\text{\boldmath$C$}+\text{\boldmath$D$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
in (
3
), we obtain

\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$})&-\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$})\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$})&\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$})\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})&\text{\boldmath$0$}\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$0$}&\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$d$}))&-\Im(\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$d$}))\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$d$}))&\Re(\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$d$}))\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$z$})\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{\boldmath$z$})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$b$})\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{\boldmath$b$})\end{bmatrix},

(4)

which is denoted

\left(\text{\boldmath$C$}^{\prime}+\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime})\text{\boldmath$D$}^{\prime}\right)\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\prime}=\text{\boldmath$b$}^{\prime}.

(5)

Here it is noted that
\bar{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, i.e. the number of density design variables has doubled to account for the complex-valued nature of the original problem. To retain the original design parametrization, the additional constraints

\theta^{\prime}_{n+j}=\theta^{\prime}_{j},\quad j=1,2,...,n,

(6)

must be appended to (
1
). The optimization problem in (
1
) is to this end restated as

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}\underset{\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\prime},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}{\text{max}}\ \ \displaystyle\frac{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$P$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$p$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+r}{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$q$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+s}\\[15.0pt]
\text{s.t.}\ \ \begin{cases}\left(\text{\boldmath$C$}^{\prime}+\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime})\text{\boldmath$D$}^{\prime}\right)\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\prime}=\text{\boldmath$b$}^{\prime},\\[4.0pt]
\theta^{\prime}_{n+j}=\theta^{\prime}_{j},\quad j=1,2,...,n,\\[5.0pt]
{\theta}_{j}^{\prime}\in[-1,1],\quad j=1,2,...,n.\end{cases}\end{array}\end{cases}

(7)

It is emphasized that the so-called cross-correlation constraints in
(
6
) are neglected by
Angeris et al. [5]
. Based on our numerical investigations, we have found that these constraints are of critical importance to obtain useful bounds. Of course, in cases where the physics can be described by real fields, the necessity of these constraints vanish.

3
Topology optimization

In our topology optimization, we solve (
1
) staggered, i.e. introduce the implicit dependency
\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})=\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})^{-1}\text{\boldmath$b$}
, such that (
3
) is solved explicitly in each design iteration. The topology optimization problem the optimizer sees therefore reads

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}\underset{\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}{\text{min}}\ \ f(\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})),\\[8.0pt]
\text{s.t.}\ \ \text{\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mathcal{B}.\end{array}\end{cases}

(8)

Throughout this paper, it is assumed that the box constraints are defined as
\mathcal{B}=\{\text{\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\theta_{j}\in[-1,1],\ j=1,2,...,n\}
. We emphasize that we bound the density design variables between
[-1,1]
for numerical reasons that will become apparent when deriving the bounds. For convenience, we further assume that the design variables
\theta
scale a material property (e.g. the relative permittivity or the mass density) linearly between two values
a
and
a_{o}
, i.e.

a\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}\left(\theta_{j}\left(a-a_{o}\right)+\left(a+a_{o}\right)\right),\ \ j=1,...,d,

(9)

although other choices as valid as we will discuss later on.

The topology optimization problem in (
8
) is solved using the gradient-based optimizer MMA (
Svanberg [47]
). To this end, the sensitivities of the objective function with respect to
\theta
are necessary. Via the adjoint method, we find

\frac{\partial f(\text{\boldmath$z$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}))}{\partial\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}=-\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$\mu$})^{T}&\Im(\text{\boldmath$\mu$})^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\Re(\text{\boldmath$A$})}{\partial\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}&-\frac{\partial\Im(\text{\boldmath$A$})}{\partial\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}\\[10.0pt]
\frac{\partial\Im(\text{\boldmath$A$})}{\partial\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}&\frac{\partial\Re(\text{\boldmath$A$})}{\partial\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$z$})\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{\boldmath$z$})\end{bmatrix},

(10)

where
\Re(\text{\boldmath$\mu$}),\Im(\text{\boldmath$\mu$})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the adjoint variables obtained from the solution of the linear system

\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$A$})&\Im(\text{\boldmath$A$})\\[10.0pt]
-\Im(\text{\boldmath$A$})&\Re(\text{\boldmath$A$})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\text{\boldmath$\mu$})\\[10.0pt]
\Im(\text{\boldmath$\mu$})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\Re(\text{\boldmath$z$})}\\[10.0pt]
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\Im(\text{\boldmath$z$})}\end{bmatrix},

(11)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial\Re(\text{\boldmath$z$})}=\frac{2\text{\boldmath$P$}\Re(\text{\boldmath$z$})+2\Re(\text{\boldmath$p$})}{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$q$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+s}-\left(2\text{\boldmath$Q$}\Re(\text{\boldmath$z$})+2\Re(\text{\boldmath$q$})\right)\frac{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$P$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$p$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+r}{\left(\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$q$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+s\right)^{2}},\\[15.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial\Im(\text{\boldmath$z$})}=\frac{2\text{\boldmath$P$}\Im(\text{\boldmath$z$})+2\Im(\text{\boldmath$p$})}{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$q$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+s}-\left(2\text{\boldmath$Q$}\Im(\text{\boldmath$z$})+2\Im(\text{\boldmath$q$})\right)\frac{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$P$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$p$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+r}{\left(\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$z$}+2\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$q$}}\text{\boldmath$z$})+s\right)^{2}}.\end{array}

(12)

3.1
Differences to a conventional topology optimization approach

As already discussed, our topology optimization problem in (
8
) does in some ways differ from a conventional topology optimization formulation. Firstly, no penalization scheme is used when interpolating the material properties in (
9
). Of course, it is acknowledged that penalization schemes are frequently incorporated in a topology optimization context, with popular choices including SIMP (
Bendsøe [9]
) and RAMP (
Stolpe and Svanberg [45]
). Nothing hinders the introduction of such a scheme in our formulation, but to keep the presentation as clean as possible we refrain from using any penalization. Lastly, we do not use any filtering technique. This is the case since the introduction of a filter inherently results in a loss of spatial locality of the design variables, as they become functions of their neighbors. This complicates the computation of the bounds, wherefore we leave the inclusion of filtering in the bounds to future work. Nonetheless, since the design space of a filtered topology optimization problem is a subset of the design space of the original topology optimization problem in (
8
), it is emphasized that bounds to (
8
) are still valid as non-tight bounds to a filtered topology optimization problem. We will utilize this property in the numerical results.

4
Performance bounds

The derivation of our performance bounds follows closely those of
Angeris et al. [5]
. For clarity, we repeat most of those derivations here.

4.1
Reformulation of fractional objective

The first task is to reformulate our objective function in (
2
) such that it is not a fraction. The reason why this is done will become apparent later; it is to obtain an optimization problem on standard QCQP form. To this end, (
5
) is multiplied by the parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

\left(\text{\boldmath$C$}^{\prime}+\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime})\text{\boldmath$D$}^{\prime}\right)\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}=\alpha\text{\boldmath$b$}^{\prime},

(13)

where
\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}=\alpha\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\prime}
(or equivalently
\text{\boldmath$y$}=\alpha\text{\boldmath$z$}
). By plugging in
\text{\boldmath$z$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}/\alpha
in (
2
) we find

f(\text{\boldmath$y$})=\frac{\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\text{\boldmath$P$}\text{\boldmath$y$}+2\alpha\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$p$}}\text{\boldmath$y$})+\alpha^{2}r}{\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$y$}+2\alpha\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$q$}}\text{\boldmath$y$})+\alpha^{2}s}.

(14)

The idea is now to let
\alpha
scale the input excitation
b
, such that the denominator of (
14
) equals one. To this end,
\alpha
is introduced to (
7
) as an auxiliary design variable. The optimization problem together with the equality constraint becomes

(\mathbb{OPT})\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}\underset{\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime},\alpha}{\text{max}}\ \ \bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\text{\boldmath$P$}\text{\boldmath$y$}+2\alpha\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$p$}}\text{\boldmath$y$})+\alpha^{2}r\\[10.0pt]
\text{s.t.}\ \ \begin{cases}\bar{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$y$}+2\alpha\Re(\bar{\text{\boldmath$q$}}\text{\boldmath$y$})+\alpha^{2}s=1,\\[5.0pt]
\left(\text{\boldmath$C$}^{\prime}+\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime})\text{\boldmath$D$}^{\prime}\right)\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\prime}=\text{\boldmath$b$}^{\prime},\\[4.0pt]
\theta^{\prime}_{n+j}=\theta^{\prime}_{j},\quad j=1,2,...,n,\\[5.0pt]
{\theta}_{j}^{\prime}\in[-1,1],\quad j=1,2,...,n.\end{cases}\end{array}\end{cases}

(15)

which can be shown to be equivalent to (
7
) if
\text{\boldmath$A$}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
is invertible, cf.
Angeris et al. [5]
.

4.2
Variable elimination

Next, we show that it is possible to eliminate the density design variables
\theta
from the optimization problem, i.e. obtain an optimization problem that solely uses
\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}
and
\alpha
as design variables. To this end, (
5
) is rearranged and rewritten on component-wise form such that

\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}-\alpha b_{j}=-\theta_{j}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime},\quad j=1,2,...,2n,

(16)

where e.g.
\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*}^{\prime}
denotes the
j
th row of
\text{\boldmath$C$}^{\prime}
. By taking the absolute value on both sides of the above, it can be identified that

|\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}-\alpha b_{j}|=|-\theta_{j}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}|=|\theta_{j}^{\prime}||\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}|\leq|\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}|,\quad j=1,2,...,2n,

(17)

since
\theta_{j}^{\prime}\in[-1,1]
yields
|\theta_{j}^{\prime}|\leq 1
. For later convenience, both sides of (
17
) are squared, which renders

|\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}-\alpha b_{j}|^{2}\leq|\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}|^{2},\quad j=1,2,...,2n.

(18)

The field
\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}
satisfies (
13
) for a given
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}
if and only if it satisfies the above inequality constraints in (
18
), cf.
Angeris et al. [5]
. Therefore, by enforcing the above constraints in our optimization problem,
\theta
is ultimately eliminated.

It is possible to compress the number of constraints in (
18
). Inserting the definitions of the matrices/vectors in (
4
) in (
18
), we obtain two constraints for each degree-of-freedom
j=1,2,...,n
, i.e.

\begin{array}[]{ll}|\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})-\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})-\alpha\Re({b}_{j})|^{2}\leq|\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})-\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})|^{2},\\[10.0pt]
|\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})+\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})-\alpha\Im({b}_{j})|^{2}\leq|\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})+\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})|^{2}.\end{array}

(19)

By combining the above constraints for each
j=1,2,...,n
, expanding the squares and introducing
\text{\boldmath$x$}=[\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$}),\,\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$}),\,\alpha]^{T}
, the quadratic constraints

\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$A$}}^{\prime}_{j}\text{\boldmath$x$}\leq 0,\ \ j=1,2,...,n,

(20)

are obtained, where

{\text{\boldmath$A$}}^{\prime}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\text{\boldmath$A$}_{11}&\text{\boldmath$A$}_{12}&\text{\boldmath$A$}_{13}\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$A$}_{12}^{T}&\text{\boldmath$A$}_{11}&\text{\boldmath$A$}_{23}\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$A$}_{13}^{T}&\text{\boldmath$A$}_{23}^{T}&\text{\boldmath$A$}_{33}\end{bmatrix},

(21)

and

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{\boldmath$A$}_{11}=\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})^{T}\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})+\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})^{T}\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})-\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})-\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}),\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$A$}_{12}=-\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})^{T}\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})+\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})^{T}\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})+\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})-\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}),\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$A$}_{13}=-\Re(b_{j})\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})^{T}-\Im(b_{j})\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})^{T},\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$A$}_{23}=\Re(b_{j})\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})^{T}-\Im(b_{j})\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})^{T},\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$A$}_{33}=\Re(b_{j})^{2}+\Im(b_{j})^{2}.\end{array}

(22)

4.2.1
Cross-correlation constraints

The next task is to reformulate the constraints in (
6
) such that the explicit dependency of
\theta
is avoided. By rearranging (
16
), the relationship

\theta_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha b_{j}-\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}}{\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}},\quad j=1,2,...,2n,

(23)

is found. It is emphasized that (
23
) is well-defined as long as
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}\neq 0
, which should never happen in practice. If
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}=0
, the associated
\theta_{j}
does not affect the analysis, i.e. this
\theta_{j}
is ill-defined in the first case. Inserting the above in (
6
) renders

\left(\alpha b_{n+j}-\text{\boldmath$C$}_{n+j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}\right)\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}=\left(\alpha b_{j}-\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime}\right)\text{\boldmath$D$}_{n+j,:}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\prime},\quad j=1,2,...,n,

(24)

which is equivalent to

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\alpha\Im(b_{j})-\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})-\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})\right)\left(\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})-\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})\right)=\\[10.0pt]
\left(\alpha\Re(b_{j})-\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})+\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})\right)\left(\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$y$})+\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$y$})\right),\quad j=1,2,...,n,\end{array}

(25)

where (
4
) was used. Similarly to (
19
), we can rewrite (
25
) on the quadratic format

\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}_{j}\text{\boldmath$x$}=0,

(26)

where

{\text{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\text{\boldmath$B$}_{11}&\text{\boldmath$B$}_{12}&\text{\boldmath$B$}_{13}\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$B$}_{21}&\text{\boldmath$B$}_{11}&\text{\boldmath$B$}_{23}\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$0$}&\text{\boldmath$0$}&0\end{bmatrix},

(27)

and

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{\boldmath$B$}_{11}=-\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}+\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$B$}_{12}=\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}+\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$B$}_{13}=\Im(b_{j})\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})-\Re(b_{j})\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$B$}_{21}=-\Re(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}-\Im(\text{\boldmath$C$}_{j,*})\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})^{T}\\[10.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$B$}_{23}=-\Im(b_{j})\Im(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})-\Re(b_{j})\Re(\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j,*})\end{array}.

(28)

4.3
The QCQP

All the above derivations allows us to rewrite (
15
) to the form

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}\underset{\text{\boldmath$x$}}{\text{max}}\ \ \text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$x$},\\[5.0pt]
\text{s.t.}\ \ \begin{cases}\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$Q$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$x$}=1,\\[5.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$A$}}^{\prime}_{j}\text{\boldmath$x$}\leq 0,\ \ j=1,...,n,\\[5.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}_{j}\text{\boldmath$x$}=0,\ \ j=1,...,n,\end{cases}\end{array}\end{cases}

(29)

where we introduced

{\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}=\left[\begin{matrix}\text{\boldmath$P$}&\text{\boldmath$0$}&\Re(\text{\boldmath$p$})\\[5.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$0$}&\text{\boldmath$P$}&\Im(\text{\boldmath$p$})\\[5.0pt]
\Re(\text{\boldmath$p$})^{T}&\Im(\text{\boldmath$p$})^{T}&r\end{matrix}\right]\ \ \text{ and }\ \ {\text{\boldmath$Q$}}^{\prime}=\left[\begin{matrix}\text{\boldmath$\text{\boldmath$Q$}$}&\text{\boldmath$0$}&\Re(\text{\boldmath$q$})\\[5.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$0$}&\text{\boldmath$Q$}&\Im(\text{\boldmath$q$})\\[5.0pt]
\Re(\text{\boldmath$q$})^{T}&\Im(\text{\boldmath$q$})^{T}&s\end{matrix}\right],

(30)

such that

{\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime},{\text{\boldmath$Q$}}^{\prime},{\text{\boldmath$A$}}^{\prime}_{j},{\text{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1,2n+1}
and
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. The above optimization problem in (
29
) is a QCQP, and we note that it is convex if and only if
{\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}
,
{\text{\boldmath$Q$}}^{\prime}
,
{\text{\boldmath$A$}}^{\prime}_{j}
and
{\text{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}_{j}
,
j=1,...,n
are positive semidefinite matrices, i.e. in general it is non-convex.

4.4
Semidefinite relaxation

The optimization problem in (
29
) can be rewritten to a SDP using the trick that

\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{trace}\left(\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}{\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$x$}\right)=\text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$x$}\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}\right)=\text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right),

(31)

where
\text{\boldmath$X$}=\text{\boldmath$x$}\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}
is a rank one symmetric positive semidefinite matrix. The QCQP in (
29
) is therefore equivalent to the SDP

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}\underset{\text{\boldmath$X$}}{\text{max}}\ \ \text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right),\\[5.0pt]
\text{s.t.}\ \ \begin{cases}\text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$Q$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right)=1,\\[5.0pt]
\text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$A$}}^{\prime}_{j}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right)\leq 0,\ \ j=1,...,n,\\[5.0pt]
\text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}_{j}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right)=0,\ \ j=1,...,n,\\[5.0pt]
\text{rank}(\text{\boldmath$X$})=1,\\[5.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$X$}\succeq 0,\end{cases}\end{array}\end{cases}

(32)

where
\text{\boldmath$X$}\succeq 0
constrains
X
to the set of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices. In general, the SDP in (
32
) is a non-convex optimization problem due to the rank-one constraint on
X
, cf.
Luo et al. [34]
. However, by simply dropping this constraint, i.e. utilizing the semidefinite relaxation technique (
Shor [41]
), we obtain the convex optimization problem

\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}\underset{\text{\boldmath$X$}}{\text{max}}\ \ \text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$P$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right),\\[5.0pt]
\text{s.t.}\ \ \begin{cases}\text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$Q$}}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right)=1,\\[5.0pt]
\text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$A$}}^{\prime}_{j}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right)\leq 0,\ \ j=1,...,n,\\[5.0pt]
\text{trace}\left({\text{\boldmath$B$}}^{\prime}_{j}\text{\boldmath$X$}\right)=0,\ \ j=1,...,n,\\[5.0pt]
\text{\boldmath$X$}\succeq 0.\end{cases}\end{array}\end{cases}

(33)

The solution to (
33
) will always bound the solution of (
7
) from above, since it can be shown to be equivalent to the dual problem of (
29
), cf.
Vandenberghe and Boyd [49]
.

While we via the aforementioned steps have gained a convex optimization
problem, we have lost computational efficiency. The number of design variables increased from
n
to
n^{2}
, which causes not only an increase in computational time to solve (
33
), but also a drastic increase in the memory demand since
X
is dense. It is however emphasized that the solution to (
33
) can be obtained more efficiently by solving the dual problem, which we do, see
Angeris et al. [5]
for more details.

4.5
What information does the bounds provide?

The solution to (
33
) bounds the solution to the primal problem in (
7
). However, when solving (
33
), we also obtain the solution variables
X
. The question is if and how we can relate
X
to
x
, and thereby the physical field
z
and density design field
\theta
. If a clearly interpretable relationship can be identified, the bound problem could be used to not only bound the primal problem objective, but also to generate designs that might prove useful as e.g. initial guesses to the topology optimization problem, cf.
Angeris et al. [7]
, and expanded on in the following.

Indeed, the transformation of the QCQP in (
29
) to the SDP
in (
32
) includes the explicit definition
\text{\boldmath$X$}=\text{\boldmath$x$}\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}
. However, while the relaxation gave us a convex optimization problem, it also resulted in a loss of this exact relation between
X
and
x
, since the rank-1 constraint was neglected. In general, the design variable matrix
X
will instead have a rank
(\text{\boldmath$X$})=r>1
, i.e.

\text{\boldmath$X$}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\text{\boldmath$\psi$}_{j}\text{\boldmath$\psi$}_{j}^{T},

(34)

where
(\lambda_{j},\text{\boldmath$\psi$}_{j})
are the non-trivial eigenpairs of
X
arranged in descending order, such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq...\geq\lambda_{r}>0
. In the extraordinary case that rank
(\text{\boldmath$X$})=1
, the relation
\text{\boldmath$X$}=\lambda_{1}\text{\boldmath$\psi$}_{1}\text{\boldmath$\psi$}_{1}^{T}=\text{\boldmath$x$}\text{\boldmath$x$}^{T}
is of course retained, but this can only occur if the primal optimization problem is convex; i.e. in practice it will never happen for the family of optimization problems we are interested in. Therefore, the simplest idea is to approximate
x
via the rank-1 approximation of
X
, i.e. assume that
\text{\boldmath$x$}\approx\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\text{\boldmath$\psi$}_{1}
, cf.
Luo et al. [34]
. An approximation of the density design field
\theta
can thereafter be obtained from (
23
). It is however emphasized that this
x
will be infeasible in our original QCQP in (
29
) in practice, and similarly for
\theta
in (
7
). To obtain feasible density design variables
\theta
, we can employ heuristics and simply map
\theta
to
[-1,1]
, e.g. if
\theta_{j}>1\mapsto\theta_{j}=1
or if
\theta_{j}<-1\mapsto\theta_{j}=-1
.

5
Objective functions

5.1
Normalized overlap

A specific optimization problem we will address in the subsequent numerical examples is that of the maximized normalized overlap between the fields
z
and
c
,
||\text{\boldmath$c$}||_{2}=1
, in the region specified by the indices given by the set
S\subseteq\{1,2,...,n\}
. In other words, we are searching for a structure that gives us a specified output response
c
. In this case, the objective function in (
2
) reduces to

f(\text{\boldmath$z$})=\frac{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$P$}\text{\boldmath$z$}}{\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$Q$}\text{\boldmath$z$}},

(35)

where
\text{\boldmath$P$}=\text{\boldmath$R$}\text{\boldmath$c$}\bar{\text{\boldmath$c$}}\text{\boldmath$R$}^{T}
,
\text{\boldmath$Q$}=\text{\boldmath$R$}\text{\boldmath$R$}^{T}
,
\text{\boldmath$p$}=\text{\boldmath$q$}=\text{\boldmath$0$}
,
r=s=0
and the matrix
\text{\boldmath$R$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is diagonal with

R_{jj}=\begin{cases}1,\ \ \text{if}\ \ j\in S\\[10.0pt]
0,\ \ \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\ \ \text{for}\ \ j=1,...,n.

(36)

5.2
Normalized magnitude

By instead equating
\text{\boldmath$P$}=\text{\boldmath$R$}\text{\boldmath$c$}\bar{\text{\boldmath$c$}}\text{\boldmath$R$}^{T}
,
\text{\boldmath$Q$}=\text{\boldmath$0$}
,
\text{\boldmath$p$}=\text{\boldmath$q$}=\text{\boldmath$0$}
,
r=0
and
s=1
the objective in (
2
) becomes

f(\text{\boldmath$z$})=\bar{\text{\boldmath$z$}}\text{\boldmath$P$}\text{\boldmath$z$},

(37)

which can be interpreted as the output overlap magnitude
|\bar{\text{\boldmath$c$}}\text{\boldmath$z$}|^{2}
in region
S
. If
\text{\boldmath$c$}=\text{\boldmath$1$}
, the objective function instead simply promotes a maximized field magnitude squared in the region defined by
S
.

The solution to the bound optimization problem and thus the bound value will depend on the discretization of the physics. To illustrate this, we plot the objective function of the topology and bound optimization problems versus the number of free design variables when using

While the bound (black curve) is respected for each topology optimized design when evaluated on the meshes that they where optimized on (red curve), it is seen that the coarse topology optimized designs evaluated on the fine mesh (blue curve) actually surpass the predicted bound, which at a first glance should not be possible. This is however not surprising, yet a key point, when considering that the bounds have been computed for a particular problem size (mesh resolution), and thus are only guaranteed to be valid for said mesh resolutions. This example illustrates that one must exercise great care when computing bounds for a particular problem; the bounds are mathematical/computational in nature and thus relates to the particular optimization problem being solved and only make physical sense if the physics is sufficiently resolved.

Although the design in the bottom panel of Fig.
5
exhibits almost perfect mode conversion and transmittance (
T\approx 0.9996
), it consists of large intermediate density regions wherein
\theta\in(-1,1)
. This hinders the physical realization of the design as it is challenging if not impossible to realize intermediate
\varepsilon_{r}
values at such small length scales. Therefore, to end this section, we implement a “conventional” topology optimization approach with penalization, filtration and thresholding, such that intermediate density regions are penalized. The filtering is a traditional convolution filter (
Bruns and Tortorelli [16]
,
Bourdin [14]
)

\tilde{\theta}_{j}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k\in N_{j}}H_{jk}\theta_{j}}{\displaystyle\sum_{k\in N_{j}}H_{jk}},

(41)

with a cone shaped kernel such that
H_{jk}=\text{max}(0,r-\Delta_{jk})
, where
N_{j}
is the set of nodes
k
for which the node-to-node distance
\Delta_{jk}
to node
j
is smaller than
r
. The thresholding is performed via an approximate Heaviside projection around zero (
Wang et al. [52]
)

\bar{\theta}_{j}=H_{\beta}(\tilde{\theta}_{j})=\frac{\text{tanh}(\beta\tilde{\theta}_{j})}{\text{tanh}(\beta)}.

(42)

The penalization follows a so-called pamping scheme (
Jensen and Sigmund [28]
), wherein intermediate density regions are penalized by introducing a density dependent attenuation, i.e.

\varepsilon_{r}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\theta}_{j}(\varepsilon_{r}-1)+(\varepsilon_{r}+1))-i\eta(\bar{\theta}_{j}+1)(1-\bar{\theta}_{j}),

(43)

where
\eta=1
is a scaling parameter. In this way, intermediate densities
\theta\in(-1,1)
are penalized since the objective is to maximize the output field magnitude, i.e. the optimizer will tend to avoid attenuating materials.

We use a filter radius
r
of three times the element length. The Heaviside projection slope parameter
\beta
is initially
\beta=1
and increased
\beta\leftarrow 2\beta
every 50th iteration until
\beta=8
. The topology optimized design obtained using the conventional framework is depicted in Fig.
11
. In a post-processing evaluation without pamping (
\eta=0
in (
43
)), the Fig.
11
design has a transmittance of
T\approx 0.9685
, i.e. by obtaining a realizable design we only drop approximately
3\%
in performance. We note that this performance drop can be reduced significantly by allowing added design freedom using a finer finite-element discretization (
Christiansen [17]
) and possibly continuation in
\eta
.

7
Conclusions

In this work, we have presented a framework for computing performance bounds to a class of topology optimization problems. We have developed and extended the framework by
Angeris et al. [5]
which relies on the finite difference method, to incorporate cross-correlation constraints for stability and finite element discretization methods for generality. The computational framework is tested on two topology optimization problems; 1) a photonic mode converter and 2) an elastic resonating plate. To the author’s knowledge, the latter problem pioneers the investigation of computational performance bounds for both vector-field and linear elastic inverse design problems.

The numerical examples illustrate the power of computational performance bounds in topology optimization. By having access to the bounds, we know when the topology optimization produces “optimal” designs, and when we should rerun the optimization with another initial guess for the design variables. We can also verify that our topology optimization framework is able to generate near optimal designs for certain problems. However, the numerical examples also illustrate the current limitations of the computational bounds. Using the proposed solution method, we are limited to small problem sizes and certain optimization problems.

7.1
Outlook

Although this work has exemplified the usage of computational bounds in topology optimization, several open questions remain regarding their generality and efficiency. To end this paper, we therefore pose a handful of these questions and provide suggestions on how to go about (hopefully) to answer them:

1.

What if the design dependency is not present in the “mass” matrix, but rather in the “stiffness” matrix, i.e. the differential operator? In other words: Is it possible to bound static problems using a modified version of the presented framework?

In such cases, the locality of the density design variables
\theta
is lost, i.e. a density design variable does in general not influence solely a single degree-of-freedom, wherefore the proposed framework for computing the bounds is not directly applicable. In such cases, the second-order differential equation could be converted into two coupled first order differential equations, such that design locality is preserved. For elasticity problems, we believe that this would require a mixed finite element formulation, wherein both the displacements and the stresses are treated as unknown variables. The investigation of such an approach is clearly an interesting subject for future work.

2.

Can arbitrary constraints possibly be included in the optimization problems and the bound?

The presented framework requires that the constraint is (or can be rewritten as) a quadratic (or linear) function in the state field, i.e.
z
.

3.

Can you guarantee that the bounds are tight, i.e. define the best possible optimization outcome?

As also discussed by
Angeris et al. [5]
, we cannot guarantee the tightness of the computational bounds presented herein due to the numerics involved. Numerical inconsistencies can occur due to e.g. the scaling of the problem. For example, we found it harder (in addition to more time-consuming) to solve the bound problem when considering elasticity in comparison to photonics. We attribute this issue to the large contrasts (condition numbers) that exists for the stiffness and mass matrices in the former case. In other words, the influence of the design variables is smaller in the elasticity problem. Our experience is that the computations of the bounds then become more sensitive to what we believe to be e.g. scaling and round-off errors.

4.

How can the computation of the bounds be made more efficient in relation to both CPU time and memory usage?

As also discussed by
Gertler et al. [23]
, there is currently a growing interest in investigating the speed-up of SDP:s. For example, sparsity properties characterized by chordal graph structures can be exploited to speed-up the solution times; there already exists open-source software that utilizes such properties, cf. sparseCoLO (
Kim et al. [30]
). More recent work (
Yurtsever et al. [54]
) shows promising results for solving the “max-cut” problem with very large SDP:s (number of entries in
X
up to
10^{13}
). In future work, it would be very interesting to implement and test this algorithm.

5.

Are computational bounds worth to pursue in topology optimization or is the formulation too limited for (most) practical problems?

We argue that the state-of-the-art computational bound frameworks are still too rudimentary for practical usage for state-of-the-art topology optimization problems. Exceptions to this statement could however be “simple” or small dynamical problems (such as the numerical examples included in this work). Nonetheless, if the existing frameworks are extended to be applicable to static, non-local problems, and their efficiency is increased to allow for larger problem sizes, we believe the computational bounds could be immensely useful for the topology optimization community.
