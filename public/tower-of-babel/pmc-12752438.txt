Human papillomavirus (HPV) genotypes extended prevalence in the female population from a city in Northern Chile
Authors: Valeria Escobar, Alejandro Catalán, Verónica Callejas, Roylester Araya, José Rojas, Jonathan García, Armando Alday, Tania Martin, Karol Santoro, Claudia Campillay-Véliz, Christian A Muñoz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer is primarily associated with the presence of human papillomavirus (HPV), with high-risk genotypes HPV-16 and HPV-18 being the focus of vaccination programs in developing countries such as Chile. Preventive screening for cervical cancer in women aged 25 to 64 years remains centered on cytological techniques and is primarily performed based on clinical suspicion of cervical lesions. However, extended screening for HPV genotypes using DNA amplification methods is not routinely applied to the Chilean female population yet. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of high- and low-risk HPV genotypes in women without known risk factors in a city in northern Chile.MethodsCervicovaginal brushing samples were obtained from 390 women from Antofagasta city, Northern Chile, aged between 25 and 64 years; genomic DNA was extracted, and multiplex real-time PCR analysis was used to identify a larger group of high- and low-risk HPV genotypes.ResultsAmong 390 samples, HPV prevalence was 36.9%, of which 54.9% were high-risk genotypes, 18.7% were low-risk genotypes, and 26.4% showed mixed infection with both high- and low-risk genotypes. High-risk genotypes 16, 58, 39, and 31 were the most frequently identified among HPV-positive samples. Furthermore, a significant association was observed between HPV presence and both age and suspicion of cervical alteration, and women testing positive for other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) were more likely to acquire HPV.ConclusionsImplementing a screening program that incorporates extended HPV genotyping in Chile, including testing for high-risk genotypes 16, 18, 31, 39, and 58, is crucial to optimize control, early detection, and vaccination efforts for Chilean circulating HPV genotypes that are not covered by the actual vaccine, thus contributing to a more effective reduction in the burden of disease associated with the virus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04179-z.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Genotyping, High-risk HPV, Low-risk HPV, HPV prevalence

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a slowly progressive neoplastic process originating in the epithelial tissue of the cervix. This progression is characterized by severe dysplasia, which is confined to the epithelial surface, to invasive carcinoma involving breach of the basement membrane [1]. Cervical cancer is the second most frequent cancer among women worldwide [2], and the principal etiological agent is the acquisition and persistence of human papillomavirus (HPV) [3]. The risk of acquiring HPV infection is strongly associated with sexual behaviors, including early initiation of sexual activity and a high number of sexual partners [4–6]. Nevertheless, the incidence of persistent infection leading to malignancy remains low, with only a small proportion of HPV-infected individuals developing cervical cancer [7]. Thus, it is estimated that approximately 90% of HPV infections are transient, efficiently controlled by the host immune system, and clear spontaneously within two years [8–10]. However, the precise determinants influencing progression to persistence and, ultimately, cervical cancer are not fully understood. Evidence suggests that additional risk factors such as tobacco use, immunosuppression, multiparity, and co-infection with other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) may contribute to disease progression [11]. Globally, STIs with HPV were associated with 570,000 cases of cervical cancer and over 311,000 cervical cancer deaths in 2018 [12].

HPV is subdivided into numerous genotypes, which are classified based on their oncogenic potential [13]. High-risk HPV (hrHPV) genotypes, such as HPV-16 and HPV-18, are strongly associated with the development of cervical cancer and its precursors, while low-risk HPV (lrHPV) genotypes (for example, HPV-6 and HPV-11) are typically linked to benign lesions, such as genital warts, and have a low probability for cervical carcinogenesis [14, 15]. To date, over 200 HPV genotypes have been identified, with at least 14 considered high-risk for cervical cancer. This classification is essential for understanding the significance of HPV genotyping in cancer prevention and screening strategies [16].

The most prevalent genotypes, HPV-16 and HPV-18, account for approximately 70% of invasive cervical carcinomas [17]. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a community-acquired a pooled HPV prevalence of about 11% among asymptomatic women and non-sex workers in both urban and rural settings [18]. In contrast, the pooled prevalence in female sex workers was 39.5–42.6% [19, 20].

In Chile, sexually transmitted infections that are subject to epidemiological surveillance include syphilis, gonorrhea, hepatitis, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), but not HPV [21]. Although HPV is recognized as a primary cause of cervical cancer, preventive screening for HPV has not been incorporated into current national screening guidelines [22]. Instead, these guidelines recommend cervical cancer screening for women aged 25 to 64 years, primarily through cytological methods such as the Pap test (Pap smear) or exfoliative cervical cytology, to assess cervical cancer risk [23, 24]. However, the diagnostic sensitivity of these cytological techniques is limited, with reported false-negative rates ranging from 30% to 50% [24–27]. Also, the Chilean regulations stipulate that Pap test should be performed on women aged 25 to 64 every three years. However, cervical cancer screening coverage in Chile is low and even lower in the country’s northern regions [28]. Additionally, northern regions of Chile, such as the Antofagasta Region, have the highest mortality rates compared to the national average [29], which could be related to a range of social (transient or non-resident populations and the growth of sex work linked to mining lifestyles) and environmental contaminants associated with other cancer types [30]. Furthermore, the HPV immunization strategy was incorporated as a health policy in 2014, using a tetravalent vaccine (capable of conferring protection against high-risk genotypes HPV-16 and HPV-18), and only introducing the nonvalent vaccine as part of the strategy in 2024 [31], although its implementation has still been limited.

Data regarding HPV prevalence in Chile remains limited; nonetheless, some research has shown that women under 25 years old (a group excluded from the cervical cancer screening target population) and receiving treatment from the cervical cancer program have an 84.8% HPV prevalence, with 70.3% attributable to hrHPV genotypes (HPV-16 being the most prevalent) and 5.5% associated with lrHPV genotypes [32]. In the general female population of Santiago, Chile, the prevalence of HPV was 12.8%, with high-risk genotypes representing 64.6% and low-risk genotypes representing 35.4% [33].

The prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes in northern Chile is currently unknown. A preliminary personal report conducted in 2023 on a limited female sample at the Clinical Hospital of the University of Antofagasta (HCUA), Antofagasta, Chile, revealed a 14.7% prevalence of HPV, with 4.6% positive for the high-risk HPV-16 genotype and only 0.6% positive for the high-risk HPV-18 genotype (unpublished data). However, due to assay limitations, other non-HPV 16/18 hrHPV genotypes could not be identified. Considering these gaps, this study aims to determine the prevalence of a several high-risk and low-risk HPV genotypes among the general women population in Antofagasta, northern Chile.

Methods

Study population

A descriptive, cross-sectional, and inferential study was carried out. A total of 390 women from Antofagasta city, Northern Chile, aged between 25 and 64 years, were recruited from July 2023 to January 2024 and invited to participate in the study through a screening campaign for the detection of STIs. The sample size was representative for the general women population and calculated using the finite population correction factor with an expected prevalence of 0.5, considering a 95% confidence level and an estimation error of 0.05, using the formula reported by Pourhoseingholi et al., 2013 [34]. The inclusion criteria were focused on the age group considered for HPV screening in Chile, as well as on individuals with no history of cervical abnormalities or other factors associated with an increased risk of HPV infection. While the exclusion criteria were: women younger than 25 or older than 64 years, pregnant women, those with a previous diagnosis of cervical cancer, women with a previous diagnosis or ongoing treatment for sexually transmitted infections, women currently menstruating or experiencing pathological metrorrhagia, those who did not observe a sexual abstinence period of at least 24 h prior to sample collection, and women undergoing intravaginal treatment for vulvovaginitis.

All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of Antofagasta (CEIC-UA), approval number CEIC REV384/2022. Written informed consent detailing the risks and benefits of participation was obtained from all study participants. Participants also filled out an epidemiological survey to identify possible risk factors for STIs. Prior to sample collection, participants were instructed to avoid vaginal hygiene, and the use of vaginal medications for at least 24 h. Additionally, they were required to abstain from sexual intercourse and antibiotic treatment for at least 24 h and to confirm that they had not undergone any previous cervical or uterine clinical procedures, such as vaginal, cervical, or endometrial biopsies, or polyp removal.

Sampling

Cervicovaginal brushing samples were collected from participants who met all the inclusion and exclusion criteria and provided informed consent by signing an informed consent form. The samples were collected at the facilities of the University of Antofagasta Clinical Hospital (HCUA), Antofagasta, Chile. Briefly, swab samples were obtained by inserting a cervical brush with central bristles into the external cervical orifice, gently pressing against the cervix while rotating the brush 360° five times, and subsequently dragging the brush along the vaginal fornix and walls. The brushes were then rinsed ten times in a collection vial, sealed with a cap, and labeled. Samples were promptly transported at 4 °C to the Molecular Diagnostic Laboratory of the HCUA.

During sample collection, visual observation by speculoscopy was performed by the clinical professional, and possible cervix alterations were recorded on an examination form. These findings were categorized as either healthy cervix (without lesions or alterations) or cervix with alterations. Alterations included suspicious findings such as: visible inflammation or lesions, benign cervicopathy, metrorrhagia (presence of bleeding), leukorrhea, and sinusorrhagia [35]. For variable dependence analysis, only the binary classification of healthy versus altered cervix (including all alterations) was considered.

Genomic DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from cervicovaginal samples using the PureLink™ Genomic DNA Purification Mini kit (Thermo Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 200 µL of lysis buffer containing proteinase K and RNase was added to 200 µL of samples and incubated at room temperature for two minutes, followed by incubation at 55 °C for ten minutes. Afterwards, 200 µL of 100% ethanol was added, mixed by vortexing, and the lysate-ethanol mixture was transferred to a centrifugation column with a silica membrane. The column was centrifuged at 10,000 x g for one minute at room temperature. The column was subsequently washed twice, first with 500 µL of wash buffer 1 and then with 500 µL of wash buffer 2. Each wash followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 1 min at room temperature. Finally, membrane-bound DNA was eluted by adding 50 µL of elution buffer and centrifuging at 16,000 × g for 1.5 min. The quantity of DNA was measured using nano-UV spectrometry with a UV nanophotometer, and DNA purity was assessed by calculating the absorbance ratios at 260/280 nm and 260/230 nm. DNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until further use.

HPV genotyping and STI assay

Genomic DNA samples were subjected to multiplex real-time PCR analysis to identify an extended group of HPV genotypes. Specifically, 19 high-risk genotypes (HPV-16, −18, −26, −31, −33, −35, −39, −45, −51, −52, −53, −56, −58, −59, −66, −68, −69, −73, and − 82) and 9 low-risk genotypes (HPV-6, −11, −40, −42, −43, −44, −54, −61, and − 70) were identified and genotyped using the Anyplex™ II Human Papillomavirus HPV-28 Detection kit (Seegene Inc, Taewon Blgd, 91, Seoul, Korea), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay was performed in two multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) reactions: the first reaction detected 14 high-risk genotypes (Set 1), while the second reaction detected 5 high-risk genotypes and the remaining 9 low-risk genotypes (Set 2). The assay incorporated an endogenous internal control to evaluate potential PCR inhibition or inadequate genomic DNA extraction. Additionally, the kit employed an uracil-DNA glycosylase (UDG)-deoxyuridine triphosphate (dUTP) system to prevent cross-contamination between amplification products.

For each reaction, 5 µL of DNA sample was added to each well of a 96-well PCR plate containing 15 µL of the respective reaction master mix, resulting in a final reaction volume of 20 µL. RNase-free water was used as a negative control, while three distinct positive controls (HPV-28 PC1, PC2, and PC3), including five clones from Set 1 and five clones from Set 2, respectively, were included for each master mix. An assay was considered invalid if the positive control did not amplify in any cycle threshold (Ct) value. Tests were validated when all positive controls amplified below a Ct threshold of 45; samples were considered HPV-positive when amplification occurred below Ct 45 for any study genotype, together with amplification of human beta-globin gene as endogenous internal control.

Additionally, genomic DNA samples were used for the PCR amplification of four STI pathogens (Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Mycoplasma genitalium, and Trichomonas vaginalis) utilizing the Allplex™ CT/NG/MG/TV Assay (Seegene), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay enables the simultaneous detection of all four pathogens and includes an endogenous internal control. For this assay, 8 µL of DNA sample was added to each well of a 96-well PCR plate containing 12 µL of reaction master mix, resulting in a final volume of 20 µL. RNase-free water was used as a negative control, and a mixture of pathogen clones plus the endogenous internal control served as a positive control. An assay was validated when all positive controls amplified below a Ct of 45 and no amplification occurred in the negative control. Samples were considered positive when amplification occurred below Ct 45 for any of the four pathogens, as well as human beta-globin gene as the endogenous internal control.

All assays were conducted on a CFX96™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (BIO-RAD) thermal cycler, with results visualized and analyzed using the Seegene Viewer software platform.

Statistical analysis

A frequentist descriptive analysis was performed to summarize the sample data. The chi-square test was used to assess the independence hypotheses between two variables and to test for associations between categorical variables. When the sample size was insufficient for reliable chi-square analysis, Fisher’s exact test was employed. Also, statistical analysis was performed using multivariable logistic regression to examine factors associated with HPV positivity in a cohort of women aged 25 to 64 years in a population screening context. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, version 9.5.0., and SPSS Statistics software, version 21.

Results

A total of 390 cervical samples were collected from a representative woman sample. Thus, the prevalence of HPV infection in this cohort was 36.9% (n = 144), while 63.1% (n = 246) tested negative for HPV (Fig. 1A). Among the HPV-positive samples, 79 (54.9%) correspond to high-risk HPV genotypes, 27 (18.7%) correspond low-risk genotypes, and 38 (26.4%) exhibited a mixed infection with both high- and low-risk HPV genotypes (Fig. 1B). In total, high-risk HPV genotypes (including mixed infections) were detected in 117 samples, whereas low-risk genotypes were identified in 65 samples.

With respect to the distribution of single and multiple infections, samples positive for high-risk HPV genotypes were predominantly infected by a single genotype (65.8%), followed by dual infections (22.7%) and triple infections (10.1%) (Fig. 1C). In contrast, low-risk HPV infections were primarily multiple infections (85.1%), with dual infections accounting for 14.8% of cases (Fig. 1D).

Analysis of HPV genotype distribution indicated a diverse and heterogeneous pattern among the HPV-positive samples. The four most common hrHPV genotypes detected were HPV-16 (n = 26), HPV-58 (n = 21), HPV-39 (n = 20), and HPV-31 (n = 18). Furthermore, other high-risk genotypes detected included HPV-66 (n = 14), HPV-51 (n = 12), HPV-56 (n = 12), and HPV-52 (n = 10), while HPV-18 was observed in only 9 samples, and genotypes HPV-26 and HPV-69 were not detected (Table 1). Also, a multiplex PCR panel was utilized to identify lrHPV genotypes within the study population. Thus, the most prevalent lrHPV genotypes detected were HPV-6 (n = 22), HPV-42 (n = 17), HPV-43 (n = 11), HPV-54 (n = 11), and HPV-61 (n = 10) (Table 2).

Given the public health impact of HPV and other STIs, the prevalence of HPV and STI coinfection was determined in the study sample. The most frequently identified STI pathogen was Chlamydia trachomatis, and a 4.4% prevalence of coinfection with HPV and other STIs was recorded (Table S1 and Table S2). Furthermore, the probability that women with positive STI results also tested positive for HPV was 0.71 (Table S1). Also, a logistic regression model was performed, which showed that the presence of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), cervix alterations, and age group are significant factors associated with HPV positivity. Although the overall fit of the model was statistically significant (χ²=43.104, p < 0.001), its statistical power was moderate (Nagelkerke R²=0.143). In terms of variables, it was shown that women who were not diagnosed with a sexually transmitted infection (STI) were considerably less likely to be HPV positive (OR = 0.237; IC 95%: 0.094–0.594; p = 0.002), which represents a 76.3% reduction in the probability of HPV infection. On the other hand, a cervix with alterations increased the probability of a positive HPV result by 60.5%. (OR = 1.605; IC 95%: 1.023–2.521; p = 0.040). Meanwhile, age range also had a significant correlation, with the 51–55 age group showing a significant reduction in HPV infection risk (OR = 0.132; 95% CI: 0.034–0.517; p = 0.004) when compared to the reference group (61–65 years) (Table 5).

Discussion

In Chile, two women die daily from cervical cancer, with 99% of these cases linked to Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection. According to studies by the Public Health Institute of Chile, 14% of women aged 15–69 in Santiago are infected with HPV, and 45–61% of them are infected with multiple genotypes [23]. In Antofagasta, cervical cancer ranks first in cancer incidence and mortality covered by the explicit health guarantee policy, with an adjusted mortality rate (AMR) of 9.6 per 100,000 women, as reported in the National Cancer Plan 2018–2028 [36].

The 2024 guidelines from Chilean health institutes have recently introduced extended genotyping of high-risk HPV using molecular methods such as real-time PCR as a desirable component in primary care centers for cervical cancer prevention. However, the majority of these centers currently lack laboratories capable of conducting molecular analyses for comprehensive HPV genotyping [37]. Extended genotyping enables the identification of a broader array of viral genotypes, thereby providing a more accurate diagnosis that reflects the genetic diversity of the HPV genome [38]. By distinguishing viral genotypes into high- and low-risk categories, clinicians can establish more precise risk stratification, enabling early and personalized interventions that improve women’s health outcomes. This methodological advance has significantly enhanced the efficiency of cervical cancer screening programs and contributed to epidemiological research, supplying critical insights into the dynamics and behavior of HPV infection and its link to cervical carcinogenesis [39].

In this study, the prevalence of HPV in cervical-vaginal samples collected from a representative cohort of women aged 25 to 64 in Antofagasta, Chile, without sexually attributed risk factors, was 36.9%. Surprisingly, this prevalence is notably close to rates reported in a similar-sized cohort of female sex workers, where Hott et al. (2022) documented an HPV prevalence of 45% [40]. Likewise, the reported prevalence is comparable to the global HPV prevalence among sex workers (39.5%), where HPV 16, 52, 53, 58, and 89 were the most common genotypes [20]. Interestingly, individuals screened and found to be infected with other STIs were more likely to be co-infected with HPV. The association between STIs and HPV-related cervical alterations has been previously reported, highlighting the association of Chlamydia trachomatis with the persistence of high-risk HPV genotypes [41]. A more extensive examination of the relationship between the two pathogens seems required, as C. trachomatis was the main pathogen found.

Regarding extended HPV genotyping, sample type significantly impacts diagnostic accuracy; cervicovaginal samples are preferred for women, as genotyping from urine samples has demonstrated lower sensitivity and specificity compared to cervical brush samples [42].

Additionally, variable dependence analysis revealed a statistical association between HPV infection and age, with the 25–40-year-old age group exhibiting a 42% HPV positivity rate among the studied patients. HPV most commonly affects young adults, which may relate to early sexual initiation, having multiple sexual partners, and insufficient sexual health education regarding the risks of unprotected intercourse. These behavioral risk factors, coupled with non-sexual external factors such as tobacco use, lack of vaccination, and/or immunodeficiencies, may contribute to increased susceptibility to HPV infection in this population [43, 44], as observed in the high prevalence among the youngest age group (25–40 years old). In this regard, to prevent STI and HPV infection in the younger population, the Chilean Ministry of Public Health (MINSAL) introduced a national sexual and reproductive health program within the public health system. Central to this strategy was vaccination, initiated in 2014 with the tetravalent HPV vaccine (Gardasil™), targeting both low-risk genotypes 6 and 11 and high-risk genotypes 16 and 18. This vaccine comprises virus-like particles formed via self-assembly of HPV L1 major capsid protein pentamers [45]. In 2019, the immunization campaign was expanded to include boys after initially focusing on girls in the fourth and fifth grades of schooling (around 9–10 years old). The HPV vaccination has demonstrated substantial effectiveness, offering protection against high-grade cervical lesions persisting for up to 17 years post-vaccination [46]. Supporting this, another study reported an incidence rate ratio of 0.14 (95% CI: 0.04–0.53) for cervical cancer among vaccinated versus unvaccinated women, underscoring the importance of early vaccination [47]. In 2024, Chile transitioned to the nonavalent HPV vaccine Gardasil™ 9, approved in 2014, which covers five additional oncogenic genotypes: 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58. Concurrently, the dosing schedule for children in fourth grade was reduced from two doses to a single dose [48]. Notably, the nonavalent vaccine targets high-risk HPV genotypes, which are responsible for approximately 90% of HPV-related cancers worldwide [49]. Unfortunately, the number of vaccinated participants in the study was marginal, limiting the study’s analysis, so it seems necessary to conduct a follow-up study on this group of patients to determine whether the genotypes detected in the general female population persist or are effectively eradicated as a result of these public health measures.

Among the HPV-positive samples collected from women with non-associated risk factors, 54.9% correspond to high-risk genotypes and 26.4% to mixed infections. The most frequently detected high-risk genotype was HPV-16, which is recognized as the most potent oncogenic type, causally linked to cancers at multiple anatomical sites, including the cervix, vulva, vagina, penis, anus, oral cavity, oropharynx, tonsils, and larynx [50]. This was followed by HPV-58, which has been associated with a high prevalence among squamous cell carcinoma cases; HPV-39; and HPV-31, both of which are implicated in anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers [50–52].

It is important to note that the tetravalent vaccines administered in Chile since 2014 do not confer protection against high-risk genotypes such as HPV-31, HPV-39, and HPV-58. This prompts concerns regarding the immunization status of the female population and the efficacy of these vaccines in protecting against other oncogenic genotypes. These findings suggest the need to consider vaccination with the nonavalent HPV vaccine for women under 26 years of age, in line with the recommendations of the CDC Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) for catch-up vaccination in previously unvaccinated individuals, as well as shared clinical decision-making regarding vaccination for persons aged 27 to 45 years [45].

The prevalence of high-risk genotypes in single infections was 65.8%, which was in line with previous findings in Chile, as reported by Melo et al. (2014), with a prevalence of 70.3% for single high-risk genotype infections in women under 25 years of age [32], while Farias et al. (2025) observed a prevalence of 71% in a cohort from Coquimbo [53]. Likewise, these findings are comparable to those found in sex workers, with a prevalence of 63% of single infections caused by high-risk genotypes [40]. Regarding the absolute frequency of individual genotypes observed in our study, these were similar to those documented in southern Chile, where HPV-58 was the second most frequent genotype in women under 25 years of age exhibiting irregular post-injury follow-up [32]. Consistent patterns were also found in the northern area of ​​Santiago in 2021, with HPV-58 ranked as the second most frequent genotype, with an absolute frequency of 9.8% [40].

Furthermore, this study identified a statistical association between cervical alterations and HPV infection. Unfortunately, colposcopy or follow-up data were not obtained for patients with positive HPV results, making it necessary to compare these parameters in light of identifying possible associations between cervical abnormalities and the presence of HPV and high-risk HPV genotypes. Also, since colposcopy is recommended by international guidelines to classify cervical lesions [54, 55], it would be reasonable to establish an ongoing monitoring program for those patients that exhibiting cervical visual alterations who also test positive for HPV, combined with colposcopy for prevention of future cervical lesions or cervical cancer. Furthermore, the above could be supported, since the high-risk genotypes found in our study group—in the absence of sexual risk factors and exhibiting cervicovaginal alterations but not classified as premalignant lesions—were comparable to those reported in women with both low- and high-grade premalignant lesions [56], except for genotype 58. The multivariable logistic regression analysis has shown that age range, cervical alterations, and the existence of STIs are factors strongly linked to HPV positivity in the research group. However, the model accurately classified only 66.4% of the cases but was more successful at classifying negative cases (87.8%) than HPV-positive women (29.9%). Therefore, the model’s low explanatory power suggests the need to incorporate other variables in future analyses to improve the prediction of HPV infection risk and to develop more effective intervention strategies for HPV detection and prevention.

The relevance of identifying non-HPV 16/18 hrHPV genotypes as part of extended screening is underscored by the distinct oncogenic potentials of high-risk HPV types and their variable distribution across cervical precancers and cancers worldwide. For example, HPV-58 is among the most common oncogenic types in East Asia but is relatively rare globally [52, 57]. However, in Chile, HPV-58 has been reported as the second most prevalent genotype among the female population after HPV-16 [32, 40]. Furthermore, patients with HPV-58-positive cervical cancer have been demonstrated to present an increased risk of poorly differentiated tumors and uterine corpus invasion, characteristics that may be associated with a poorer prognosis [58].

Similarly, HPV-31 is part of the high-risk phylogenetic cluster responsible for most invasive cervical cancer cases [59]. HPV-31 shares approximately 70% DNA sequence similarity with genotype HPV-16, and this evolutionary relatedness is relevant because viral carcinogenicity is linked to genetic variability. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and methylation changes within HPV-31 have been frequently associated with the development of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 and cervical cancer [59–61]. Persistent infection with HPV-31 is strongly linked to squamous intraepithelial lesions, and this risk is further increased when HPV-31 co-occurs with phylogenetically related types such as HPV-16 and HPV-18 [62]. In addition, research on HPV-39 has shown that a single nucleotide polymorphism in the E6, E7, and L1 oncogenes can alter both the secondary and tertiary structures of viral proteins, thereby influencing carcinogenic potential, viral function, and immune responses. Such changes may ultimately impact the efficacy of current vaccines due to differences in antigenic epitopes [63]. Therefore, the inclusion of extended genotyping in population-based HPV screening and vaccination programs is essential for accurately stratifying risk and informing public health strategies tailored to regional genotype prevalence.

Moreover, the prevalence of multiple infections among high-risk genotypes was 34.2% in our study. This result is comparable to a study conducted in China, which examined patterns of multiple HPV infections and genotype competition and reported a multiple infections prevalence of 25.2% [64]. Similar studies on the East Coast of the United States and in New Mexico identified co-infection rates of 19% and 41.1%, respectively [65, 66]. Data from the Netherlands showed an even higher multiple infection prevalence of 55.2%, which may be influenced by regional risk factors and common transmission dynamics [67]. Additionally, Su et al. demonstrated that being infected with more than one oncogenic genotype increases the risk of developing cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions [64]. However, the increased risk appears to be additive rather than synergistic, suggesting that each HPV genotype individually correlates with lesion development [68].

In our study group, 18.7% of HPV genotypes were classified as low risk for cervical cancer development, with 85.1% of being single infections. Among the low-risk genotypes, HPV-6 and HPV-42 were the most frequent, followed by HPV-43 and HPV-54. These findings contrast with those from a study with sex workers, where HPV-54 was the most prevalent low-risk genotype, followed by HPV-83 and HPV-84 [40].

In summary, the prevalence of HPV observed in this study was comparable to that reported among women engaged in sex work and was unexpectedly high in a population of women without recognized sexual risk factors.

Conclusions

These findings underscore the necessity for preventive HPV testing with extended genotyping among Chilean women, including those under 25 years of age, as well as the need to evaluate the protective effect of early vaccination and consider revaccination with the nonavalent vaccine in this age group. This importance of extended genotyping is further supported by the presence of genotypes beyond the commonly monitored HPV-16 and HPV-18 (specifically, genotypes HPV-31, HPV-39, and HPV-58) which were detected at relevant frequencies within the studied sample from Antofagasta, Chile.
