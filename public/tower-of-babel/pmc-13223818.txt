Spatiotemporal screening and functional analysis of gut microbiota associated with abdominal fat deposition in breeder broiler hens
Authors: Yu Zhang, Xiuping Wang, Yurong Tai, Ranran Zhu, Yuxiang Jiang, Zihan Xu, Ziyi Lian, Jiying Yu, Xuemei Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explored the regulatory mechanisms of gut microbiota on abdominal fat deposition in breeder broiler hens. Using Wenchang chickens as models, abdominal fat thickness (AFT) was measured via ultrasound at early (22 weeks) and late (45 weeks) laying periods, and hens were divided into high (Group H) and low (Group L) fat deposition groups. Following 24 h of fasting, serum samples were analyzed for lipid metabolism indicators, and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing was performed on ileal/cecal digesta. Serum tests showed significantly higher triglycerides (TG) and cholesterol (TC) levels in Group H than in Group L at both stages (all P < 0.01). 16S rRNA sequencing indicated that microbiota structural variations were mainly driven by temporal (4.64%, P = 0.001) and spatial (43.64%, P = 0.001) factors, far exceeding those related to fat deposition phenotypes (0.96%, P = 0.035). Unlike stable ileal microbiota, cecal microbiota showed obvious temporal dynamics and a higher proportion of fat deposition-related differential KEGG orthologs (KOs), suggesting a more prominent regulatory role in host fat deposition. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that Group L’s cecal microbiota network matured functionally and structurally from early to late laying periods, while Group H showed distinct network degradation, indicating that early laying cecal microbiota structure affects its late-stage evolution. Differential analysis identified Butyricicoccus as significantly enriched in the cecum of Group L (P < 0.01), and this genus showed a negative correlation with the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway (r = -0.445, P < 0.05). In contrast, the abundance of Peptococcus was significantly increased in Group H (P < 0.05), and this genus exhibited a positive correlation with the aforementioned pathway (r = 0.6342, P < 0.001). Collectively, these findings indicate that the cecal microbiota at the early laying period plays an important role in regulating abdominal fat deposition in broiler breeder hens. Notably, Butyricicoccus and Peptococcus were identified as key genera that modulate host lipid metabolism via the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway, providing novel insights into the microbial regulatory mechanism and potential targets for controlling abdominal fat deposition.

Introduction

The global poultry industry, particularly chicken production, plays a crucial role in meeting the increasing demand for animal protein worldwide (Bouzari et al., 2025; Li et al., 2021). Excessive abdominal fat deposition in chickens compromises meat quality by inducing an oily texture (Wu et al., 2023b). This reduction in quality contradicts the prevailing preference of modern consumers for healthier, low-fat products (Liu et al., 2022). Furthermore, in breeder broiler hens, excessive abdominal fat deposition was found to impair reproductive performance, leading to reduced egg production and fertilization rates, thus jeopardizing the sustainability of the production chain (Chen et al., 2023b).

The gut microbiota, a complex and dynamic microecosystem, significantly modulates host lipid metabolism through various pathways, including the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and their influence on lipid metabolism-related genes and blood lipid profiles (Duan et al., 2023; López-Montoya et al., 2022). Therefore, modulating the gut microbiota was considered an effective strategy for intervening in abdominal fat deposition in chickens. However, previous studies primarily focused on the finishing stage, while the laying period received relatively little attention. The laying period represents a unique physiological stage in the poultry life cycle, characterized by significant hormonal fluctuations and energy redistribution to support rapid ovarian development and high-intensity egg-laying activity (Wang et al., 2021d). This stage can be subdivided into the early and late laying periods.

Unlike the finishing stage, which primarily focused on growth and fat deposition, the laying period in breeder broiler hens represented a unique physiological state with distinct metabolic priorities centered on reproduction. During the early laying period, hens achieved sexual maturity and initiated egg production, which demanded rapid ovarian development and substantial lipid mobilization for yolk formation. This involved a marked increase in hepatic lipoprotein synthesis and secretion to meet the high energy and nutrient requirements (Shi et al., 2023). As hens progressed into the late laying period, sustained high-intensity egg production imposed a considerable and prolonged physiological burden on lipid metabolic pathways. This continuous mobilization of energy and nutrients led to elevated hepatic oxidative stress and a decline in antioxidant capacity, collectively impairing yolk precursor synthesis and overall metabolic homeostasis (Kim and Kang, 2022; Wu et al., 2023a). Given these dynamic metabolic shifts, the gut microbiota was also closely associated with energy redistribution throughout the laying period. However, comprehensive spatiotemporal observations and regulatory mechanisms of gut microbiota during the laying period remain limited and require further investigation.

Furthermore, current studies have rarely explored the spatiotemporal dynamics of gut microbiota across different intestinal segments and laying stages. The structural and functional differences in gut microbiota between hens with high and low abdominal fat deposition, as well as the underlying regulatory mechanisms, remain poorly understood.

The present study aimed to clarify the spatiotemporal dynamics and regulatory mechanisms of abdominal fat deposition and intestinal microbial communities in breeder broiler hens during the laying period. We hypothesized that gut microbiota exhibits spatiotemporal specificity and differs significantly between hens with high and low abdominal fat deposition. To address this, we utilized Wenchang chickens as an experimental model, measured abdominal fat thickness (AFT) and serum lipid metabolism indicators, and performed 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing on ileal and cecal digesta at early (22 weeks) and late (45 weeks) laying periods, categorizing them into high and low fat deposition groups. These findings were expected to provide novel insights into the microbial regulation of host abdominal fat deposition and offer potential targets for optimized fat management in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in China and the Beijing Guidelines for Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics. The protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University (Approval No.: SKLAB-2012-04-07).

Experimental design

This study used a longitudinal design with the same hen population sampled at two physiological stages: early laying at 22 weeks (Stage E) and late laying at 45 weeks (Stage L). These two time points were selected to enable temporal and spatial comparison of gut microbiota across the laying cycle. Relative to continuous repeated sampling, this design also mitigates ethical concerns and animal stress associated with repeated euthanasia for intestinal sampling, while allowing clear characterization of stage-specific fat deposition and microbial profiles within the identical population.

At both Stage E and Stage L, the entire flock underwent in vivo ultrasonic measurement of AFT. Based on population-level statistical analysis, individuals with an AFT value exceeding the population mean plus 2 standard deviations (Mean + 2 SD) were categorized into the high abdominal fat deposition group (H), while those below the mean minus 2 standard deviations (Mean – 2 SD) were assigned to the low abdominal fat deposition group (L). Specifically, these groups were designated as early-stage high (HE), early-stage low (LE), late-stage high (HL), and late-stage low (LL) abdominal fat groups, respectively.

Following group stratification, blood samples were collected for serum biochemical analysis to assess lipid metabolism indicators. Concurrently, ileal and cecal digesta samples were collected for gut microbiota analysis. For 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, samples were uniformly labeled based on their respective laying period and abdominal fat deposition phenotype: cecal digesta from the early laying HE group were labeled CHE, from LE as CLE; ileal digesta from HE as IHE, and from LE as ILE. Similarly, for the late laying period, cecal digesta from HL were labeled CHL, from LL as CLL; ileal digesta from HL as IHL, and from LL as ILL. Subsequently, 16S rRNA gene sequencing data were analyzed to compare microbial community structure and function across groups, aiming to identify key microbial taxa influencing abdominal fat deposition.

The comprehensive experimental design and analytical workflow are visually summarized in Fig. 2.

Animal care

A total of 1,142 Wenchang broiler breeder hens (Stage E) and 1,517 hens (Stage L) were enrolled, all from Hainan (Tanniu) Wenchang Chicken Co., Ltd., hatched in the same batch, and selected for similar initial bodyweights (1.5–2.0 kg). Hens were individually housed in temperature-controlled houses (22-25 °C, 50-60% RH) under a consistent photoperiod (16L:8D, 20 lux). All hens received restricted feeding with a standard basal diet (85 g/hen/day for Stage E, 86 g/hen/day for Stage L; see Supplementary Table 1 for details), with feeding ingredients sourced from Hainan (Tan Niu) Wenchang Chicken Co., Ltd., and premix from Beijing Sinouk Institute of Biological Technology. Clean water was available ad libitum. Routine vaccinations were administered. No antibiotics or other medications known to affect gut microbiota were used during the experiment. Hens were observed twice daily for health, with moribund individuals excluded.

Ultrasonic measurement of AFT in vivo

AFT was measured in vivo using a full-digital color Doppler ultrasound diagnostic system (Model DCU10; Xuzhou Kaixin Electronic Equipment Co., Ltd., Xuzhou, Jiangsu, China) equipped with a 7.5 MHz linear array probe. Three detection sites (left, middle, right) caudal to the keel edge were measured (Li et al., 2023b), and the average AFT was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA).

Blood collection and serum biochemical analysis

Serum concentrations of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) were analyzed using commercially available assay kits (Beijing Sinouk Institute of Biological Technology, Beijing, China) with corresponding catalog numbers as follows: TC (HY‑50061), TG (HY‑50062), HDL (HY‑50070), and LDL (HY‑50071). All measurements were conducted on an Indiko Plus fully automated clinical chemistry analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (Kirkpatrick et al., 2019).

Humane euthanasia

Following a 12-hour feed withdrawal (with ad libitum water), body weight (BW) was measured. Hens were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation, in accordance with AVMA Euthanasia Guidelines and the approved animal ethics protocol (Approval No.: SKLAB-2012-04-07). This procedure was performed as previously described (Ripplinger et al., 2024).

Determination of abdominal fat deposition phenotypes

Abdominal adipose tissue, including fat pads surrounding the proventriculus, gizzard, small intestinal mesentery, cloaca, and subcutaneous adipose tissue, was aseptically isolated following a standardized anatomical protocol. Abdominal fat weight (AFW) was determined using a high-precision electronic balance (Model PL2002; Mettler Toledo, Zurich, Switzerland). Abdominal fat percentage (AFP) was calculated as:AFP=(AFW/BW)×100%

Collection of gut microbiota samples

Approximately 2 g of digesta samples were collected from the terminal ileum (5 cm proximal to the ileocecal junction) and the entire left cecum of each hen under aseptic conditions. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent DNA extraction (Guo et al., 2023).

Gut microbiota DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from ileal and cecal digesta using a QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA quality was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). PCR products were purified, quantified, and ligated to Illumina adapters for library construction. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Subsequently, raw 16S rRNA gene sequencing reads were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic and Pear, followed by assembly into contigs with Flash and Pear (minimum overlap 10 bp, mismatch rate 0.1). Chimeric sequences were removed using UCHIME. High-quality sequences were clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at 97% similarity using UPARSE. Taxonomic annotation was performed by aligning representative OTU sequences to the Silva, RDP, and Greengene databases using the RDP Classifier (confidence threshold 0.7). Subsequent analysis included alpha and beta diversity to assess microbial richness, diversity, and community structure, alongside comprehensive comparisons of microbial composition and functional predictions (Wu et al., 2025).

Data statistical analysis

The experimental unit was the individual hen. Statistical comparisons of abdominal fat phenotypes and serum biochemical indicators between H/L groups were performed using SPSS Statistics 29.0. Data normality and variance homogeneity were assessed by Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Unpaired t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were used as appropriate, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for multiple comparisons. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to assess factor effects on microbial community structure. Statistical significance: P < 0.05 (significant), P < 0.01 (highly significant), P < 0.001 (extremely significant). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R (v4.3.1) with the ggplot2 package.

Results

Population distribution of abdominal fat thickness

To verify adherence to the restricted feeding protocol, daily feed intake per pen was recorded throughout the experiment. The average actual daily feed intake per hen was 84.7 ± 1.2 g during the early laying period and 85.8 ± 1.5 g during the late laying period, which were consistent with the preset feeding standards (85 g/hen per day for the early laying period and 86 g/hen per day for the late laying period). These results confirm that nutrient intake was uniform across all experimental groups, with no significant deviations that could confound the interpretation of abdominal fat deposition phenotypes.

Frequency distribution analysis of AFT across the entire flock revealed an approximately normal distribution during both the early and late laying periods (Fig. 1A, B). A greater dispersion of AFT values was observed in the late laying period compared with the early laying period (Table 1). Hens were stratified into high (H) and low (L) abdominal fat deposition groups based on population AFT data (Mean ± 2 SD). The early laying period comprised high-AFT (HE, n = 22) and low-AFT (LE, n = 22) groups, while the late laying period included high-AFT (HL, n = 10) and low-AFT (LL, n = 24) groups. This disparity in sample sizes for the late laying period (HL, n = 10; LL, n = 24) directly reflects the biological reality that extreme phenotypes, identified by our stringent Mean ± 2 SD selection criteria, occur less frequently in a natural population. By taking anatomical measurement of abdominal fat volume as the gold standard, ultrasonographic images and phenotypic observation were compared between the H and L groups across different laying stages. Significant differences in abdominal fat deposition were observed in each period (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 1), further supporting the reliability of ultrasonic in vivo detection for AFT traits.

Phenotypic characterization of divergent abdominal fat deposition phenotypes

No differences in BW were detected between the H and L groups during both the early and late laying periods (P > 0.05; Fig. 1G). In the early laying period, AFT, AFW, and AFP in the HE group were higher than those in the LE group (P < 0.001; Fig. 1E, F, H). Similarly, these three parameters were elevated in the HL group compared with the LL group during the late laying period (P < 0.001; Fig. 1E, F, H).

To investigate the differences in AFT between the early and late laying periods of Wenchang chickens in the HL group and LL group, AFT values of individuals from the two groups were compared across the two laying periods (Fig. 1I). The HL group exhibited a larger magnitude of AFT variation between the early and late laying periods (P = 0.002), while the LL group showed relatively limited AFT variation over the same laying periods (P = 0.24).

Serum lipid profiles of divergent abdominal fat phenotypes

To characterize lipid metabolic profiles in hens with divergent abdominal fat deposition, serum concentrations of TG, TC, HDL, and LDL were quantified (Fig. 1J). During the early laying period, compared with the LE group, the HE group exhibited elevated serum TC (P = 0.003) and TG (P < 0.001) as well as LDL (P < 0.001) concentrations, while HDL concentration was decreased (P = 0.009). In the late laying period, the HL group maintained higher serum TC and TG levels (both P < 0.001), lower HDL concentrations (P = 0.014), and elevated LDL levels (P = 0.002) compared with the LL group. These results demonstrate that hens with high abdominal fat deposition displayed dyslipidemia during both laying periods, supporting a strong association between abdominal fat deposition and lipid metabolic dysregulation.

To characterize the temporal dynamics of lipid metabolism between the H and L groups, temporal changes in serum TG, TC, HDL, and LDL levels were compared across the early and late laying periods (Fig. 1J). In the L group, serum TC and TG concentrations remained unchanged from the early to late laying period (P = 0.157 and P = 0.552, respectively), whereas HDL concentrations decreased (P < 0.001) and LDL concentrations increased (P = 0.006). In the H group, serum TC and TG concentrations increased during the late laying period (P < 0.001 and P = 0.001, respectively). Unlike the L group, the H group showed no reduction in HDL concentrations (P = 0.199), but LDL concentrations rose markedly (P < 0.001). These findings demonstrate that the H group exhibited more severe and persistent lipid metabolic dysregulation.

Correlation analysis of serum lipid metabolism

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to explore associations between serum lipid parameters and abdominal fat phenotypes, with results visualized as a correlation heatmap. During the early laying period (Supplementary Fig. 2A), serum TG levels were positively correlated with both AFP (r = 0.3957, P = 0.01) and AFW (r = 0.4384, P = 0.0041). However, no correlations were detected between serum lipid parameters and abdominal fat phenotypes in the late laying period (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Collectively, differences in abdominal fat-related phenotypes existed among individuals in the chicken population during both laying periods without external intervention. Moreover, serum lipid metabolic trends were consistent with abdominal fat phenotypes in the H and L groups.

Gut microbiota differences in hens with differential abdominal fat deposition

To investigate differences in gut microbiota structure among individuals with divergent abdominal fat deposition, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing was performed on ileal and cecal digesta samples. Given the sample size limitations of the HL group, we specifically focused on the robustness of statistical methods in our microbiota analysis. Comparative analysis of all microbial diversity, composition, and functional predictions employed statistical approaches suitable for non-normally distributed and unbalanced data (e.g., PERMANOVA, Mann-Whitney U test, LEfSe analysis), and were subjected to multiple comparison corrections. Phylum-level compositional stacked bar plots (Fig. 3A) showed that the ileal microbiota was predominantly dominated by Firmicutes, with a relative abundance ranging from 86.7% to 96.3%. In contrast, cecal microbiota had Firmicutes with a relative abundance of 27.2%–43.0%, while Bacteroidetes accounted for 45.5%–64.8%. Collectively, these two phyla constituted 88.2%–92.6% of the total cecal microbiota. Genus-level compositional stacked bar plots (Fig. 3B) demonstrated that Lactobacillus was the dominant genus in ileal microbiota, with a relative abundance of 65.8%–84.5%. In cecal microbiota, Bacteroides had the highest relative abundance (21.5%–32.0%), followed by Rikenella (8.5%–13.2%).Fig. 3Comparative analysis of gut microbiota structure in broiler breeder hens during the laying period.Fig 3 dummy alt text(A) Stacked bar plots showing the relative abundance of gut microbiota at the phylum level across all groups (ILE, IHE, CLE, CHE, ILL, IHL, CLL, CHL). Volcano plots (Fig. 4E–H) visualized differentially abundant KEGG orthologs (KOs) of microbial enzyme genes in ileal and cecal microbiota between the L and H groups. Four secondary metabolic pathways linked to obesity severity—digestive system (K09154), carbohydrate metabolism (K09101), energy metabolism (K09102), and lipid metabolism (K09103) (Zhang et al., 2023) —were highlighted, with KOs involved in these pathways colored distinctly in the plots for intuitive identification.

The H group showed the lowest ileal microbial differentiation (1897 differential KOs), significantly fewer than the L group (5984). Similarly, cecal differential KOs in the H group (3193) were considerably lower than in the L group (5467). Moreover, both the overall abundance of differential KOs and the proportion of enzymes in the four pathways were higher in cecal than in ileal microbiota.

Co-occurrence network dynamics of cecal microbiota in fat-divergent breeder broiler hens during egg-laying

Co-occurrence networks of gut microbiota were constructed for each experimental group based on 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing data. All networks displayed a typical modular architecture and scale-free property, indicating that a small subset of core microbial taxa dominates community connectivity. Key topological metrics differed significantly across groups, reflecting distinct ecological features of the microbial communities.

Network topological parameters were compared across experimental groups based on gut microbiota co-occurrence network analysis (Supplementary Table 2). Results demonstrated network structural divergence between the L and H groups. Among the four pairwise comparisons (ILE-IHE, CLE-CHE, ILL-IHL, CLL-CHL), the CLE vs. CHE comparison exhibited the most pronounced differences. Specifically, the CLE group’s microbial network contained more nodes (569) and connected components (56), but had lower graph density (0.007), longer network diameter (16), greater average path length (5.43), higher modularity (0.71), and lower average clustering coefficient (0.368). These features indicate a loose network architecture with high modularity and distinct functional differentiation within the microbiota. In contrast, the CHE group’s microbial network had fewer nodes (544) and connected components (43), with marginally higher graph density (0.008), shorter network diameter (18), shorter average path length (6.17), lower modularity (0.75), and higher average clustering coefficient (0.332), suggesting a more tightly interconnected network with enhanced overall integration. These structural discrepancies are intuitively illustrated in the co-occurrence network diagrams (Fig. 5): the CLE network shows a modular, sparse distribution, whereas the CHE network has densely interconnected nodes. Discussion

Breeder broiler hens in the H group exhibited significantly elevated abdominal fat thickness, weight, and percentage, accompanied by pronounced dyslipidemia. This metabolic perturbation was characterized by higher serum TC and TG concentrations and reduced HDL levels. Such excessive abdominal fat accumulation, as observed in the H group, posed a significant challenge in poultry production, impairing feed conversion efficiency, increasing costs, and negatively affecting meat quality and reproductive performance (Tian et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2024). The observed lipid profiles in the H group further resembled features of hyperlipidemia or metabolic syndrome in other models (Oh et al., 2023), underscoring the broader metabolic implications of this phenotype.

TG metabolism played a pivotal role in regulating abdominal fat deposition, particularly during the early laying period. Specifically, the H group exhibited significantly higher serum TG concentrations compared with the L group during this stage. This observation aligned with the physiological demands of early laying, where hens underwent active lipid synthesis to met the substantial lipid requirements for yolk formation (Wang et al., 2023). We proposed that dysregulation of TG metabolism or excessive TG production in the H group at this critical stage led to surplus TG being transported to abdominal adipose tissue for storage, consequently increasing AFW (Zhang et al., 2018).

Gut microbiota analysis identified distinct spatiotemporal differences in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio between the high and low abdominal fat groups. In the ileum during the early laying period, the F/B ratio was significantly lower in the ILE group than in the IHE group, which was mainly attributed to the increased abundance of Bacteroidetes. By contrast, in the cecum at the same developmental stage, the CLE group had a markedly higher F/B ratio relative to the CHE group. These observed differences were particularly relevant given the general understanding that an increased F/B ratio was associated with host obesity. However, our findings underscored that the F/B ratio was a relatively coarse metric, and its fluctuations, as seen in our study, reflected dynamic shifts in specific bacterial populations that might exert unique regulatory effects on energy metabolism depending on the physiological state or gut segment (Houtman et al., 2022). Therefore, our results suggested that relying solely on the F/B ratio to assess an individual's obesity status is insufficient; it was essential to consider the specific temporal node and spatial location of microbial sampling for a comprehensive understanding.

The regulation of host fat deposition by the gut microbiota exhibited significant temporal and spatial specificity. Temporal specificity was reflected in the diurnal rhythmic variations in microbial composition and function (Choi et al., 2021), as well as the dynamic impact of the microbiota on lipid metabolism across distinct host life stages (Choi et al., 2021; Han et al., 2025; Jiao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2022). Spatial specificity was manifested in the divergent roles of foregut and hindgut microbiota in driving fat deposition (Lai et al., 2024). Therefore, the present study analyzed ileal and cecal microbiota across temporal dimensions. Results revealed that the ileal microbiota structure was relatively stable, whereas the cecal microbiota underwent significant compositional shifts over time, indicating that these two gut segments represented distinct microenvironments. From a functional perspective, the cecal microbiota also exerted a significantly greater regulatory role in host glycolipid and energy metabolism compared with the ileal microbiota. Consequently, subsequent analysis in this study focused on the cecal microbiota.

The ecological and structural characteristics of the gut microbiota exerted a profound influence on host lipid absorption and energy homeostasis. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that the microbial network in the CLE group displayed distinct functional differentiation and enhanced metabolic flexibility (Fu et al., 2023). In contrast, the microbial network of the CHE group was characterized by relative simplicity of metabolic pathways and diminished ecological resilience (Fu et al., 2023). This observation was consistent with and supported by existing literature. In a porcine model, microbial network modularity was positively correlated with fat accumulation efficiency, as a highly modular network facilitated compartmentalized nutrient utilization and thereby enhanced metabolic adaptability (Jiang et al., 2024). Similarly, murine studies demonstrated that a high-fat diet induced a more rigid microbial network structure, which was directly associated with an obese phenotype—specifically manifested as elevated serum LDL-C levels and exacerbated hepatic lipid deposition. Furthermore, analysis of cecal microbiota dynamics in the L and H groups provided additional support for these findings: the microbial network in the L group exhibited a healthy successional trajectory, whereas the H group displayed signs of ecological and functional degradation (Aggarwal et al., 2024). This phenomenon was relatively rare in gut microbiota studies of other species, suggesting that obese breeder broiler hens might possess unique gut microbiota ecological characteristics during the laying period. It also indicated that the microbial network structure established in the early laying period exerted a decisive impact on the long-term metabolic homeostasis of the host.

Therefore, the present study compared differences in gut microbiota structure and function between the CHE and CLE groups. Results demonstrated that the CLE group exhibited a significantly higher abundance of Butyricicoccus compared with the CHE group. Accumulating evidence supported the beneficial effects of Butyricicoccus and its metabolite butyrate on host health. For instance, a lower abundance of Butyricicoccus was frequently observed in obese individuals (Li et al., 2024). A study confirmed that Butyricicoccus improved host metabolism by regulating the gut microbiome and metabolic functions, while simultaneously enhancing gut immune defenses by activating immune cells and promoting the production of anti-inflammatory factors, thereby collectively maintaining gut health (Zhao et al., 2025). Moreover, in weaned piglets, the presence of Butyricicoccus was recognized as a marker of intestinal health (Gardiner et al., 2020).

As a well-characterized butyrate-producing probiotic genus, the increased abundance of Butyricicoccus in the CLE group suggested that the cecum of hens with low abdominal fat deposition during the early laying period might have enhanced butyrate biosynthesis (Liu et al., 2023). Furthermore, the abundance of Butyricicoccus was significantly negatively correlated with the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway, suggesting that this genus might inhibit this pathway through butyrate production, thereby reducing host abdominal fat deposition.

Butyrate might play a core regulatory role through multiple molecular mechanisms in this process. It was known to activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key hub of cellular energy metabolism, which typically inhibited gluconeogenesis. Additionally, butyrate could inhibit the activity of key transcription factors (e.g., ChREBP and FOXO1) and act as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, thereby reducing the expression of gluconeogenesis-related genes at the transcriptional and epigenetic levels (Wu et al., 2022). These actions collectively decreased hepatic de novo glucose output, helping to maintain glucose homeostasis and limit carbon sources for fat synthesis. Furthermore, butyrate could exert systemic regulation of energy metabolism by enhancing mitochondrial oxidative capacity and inhibiting hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α)-mediated glycolytic gene expression (Wang et al., 2021a). Collectively, these findings indicated that butyric acid improved lipid metabolism via activation of AMPK, which subsequently inhibited hepatic lipogenesis (Zhao et al., 2021).

The glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway played a central and dual role in regulating poultry fat deposition, with distinct functional branches tailored to avian metabolic characteristics. Glycolysis directly fueled de novo lipogenesis by generating key precursors for triacylglycerol (TAG) synthesis, the primary form of fat storage in chickens (Göransson et al., 2023). In high-fat-deposition chicken breeds, studies consistently showed upregulated glycolytic activity in hepatic and adipose tissues, which synergized with enhanced lipid synthesis to accelerated TAG accumulation and increased abdominal fat content (Chen et al., 2023b). Notably, in poultry, glycolytic flux was tightly coupled to yolk lipid synthesis during the laying period, where excess glycolytic intermediates were preferentially shunted to hepatic lipid pathways to support egg production, creating a unique metabolic trade-off between reproduction and fat storage (Cui et al., 2024). Conversely, the gluconeogenesis branch acted as a critical counterbalance to fat deposition by diverting lipid intermediates toward glucose production. In chicken liver—the central metabolic hub for avians—gluconeogenic activity was dynamically regulated by hormonal and microbial signals (Yang et al., 2023). Reduced gluconeogenic activity not only promoted abdominal fat accumulation but also ensured sufficient lipid partitioning to the ovaries for yolk formation, especially in breeder hens where energetic demands for egg production were high (Weaver et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025). Research further demonstrated that gluconeogenesis inhibition exacerbated fat deposition by limiting glycerol catabolism, a major gluconeogenic substrate in chickens, leading to re-esterification and fat storage (Weaver et al., 2025).

Notably, poultry-specific metabolic features distinguished this gut microbiota-glycolysis/gluconeogenesis regulatory axis, highlighting its unique relevance to breeder broiler hens. In contrast to mammals, where de novo fatty acid synthesis primarily occurred in adipose tissue, chickens exhibited distinct species-specific lipid metabolism characteristics: the liver served as the central site for de novo fatty acid biosynthesis, accounting for approximately 70%–90% of the total synthetic capacity, while the contribution of adipose tissue was merely about 5% (Emami et al., 2021). While chickens do possess discrete white adipose depots (e.g., abdominal fat pads, mesenteric fat, and intramuscular fat depots), their lipid metabolism was centered on hepatic function—liver served as the primary organ for lipid synthesis, transport, and metabolic regulation, with lipids packaged into very-low-density lipoprotein for delivery to peripheral adipose tissues (Jia et al., 2024b). This evolutionary adaptation elevated the gut-liver axis to a central regulatory hub for avian fat deposition, rendering cecal microbiota-derived metabolites such as butyrate particularly influential in modulating host lipid homeostasis (Marroncini et al., 2024).

Butyricicoccus-derived butyrate exerted two key poultry-specific effects aligned with the physiological demands of breeder hens: first, it enhanced intestinal nutrient absorption by improving villus height, crypt depth, and digestive enzyme activity—adaptations critical for meeting the elevated energy and nutrient requirements of egg production (Wang et al., 2021b); second, it modulated the AMPK-PPARα signaling pathway, which was functionally specialized in poultry. Specifically, PPARα in chickens exhibited higher tissue-specific expression in the liver and intestine compared with mammals, with direct roles in regulating fatty acid oxidation and energy homeostasis (Chen et al., 2021). Additionally, the inherent energetic trade-off between egg production and fat deposition in breeder hens linked this metabolic crosstalk to reproductive performance: butyrate-induced modulation of glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways not only restricted excessive abdominal fat accumulation but also ensured appropriate lipid partitioning to the ovaries for yolk formation (Chen et al., 2023a).

No correlations were detected between the relative abundance of Butyricicoccus and serum TG, TC, HDL, and LDL concentrations in the current study. This finding could be interpreted in two respects. Rather than modulating systemic serum lipid profiles directly, Butyricicoccus appeared to regulate abdominal fat deposition indirectly via mediating host glycolysis and gluconeogenesis pathways, with the underlying regulatory mechanisms described in detail in the preceding section. On the other hand, the physiological metabolic focus of hens during the early laying period shifted from the growth stage to the reproductive stage, which induced rapid ovarian development and massive synthesis and deposition of yolk precursor substances, thereby imposing high-intensity physiological demands on hepatic lipid metabolism (Wang et al., 2021c). Previous studies have confirmed that during the sexual maturation process of poultry, both body weight and liver weight showed an upward trend, and biochemical indicators such as serum total protein, albumin, globulin, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) also exhibited obvious fluctuations; meanwhile, the morphological and histological characteristics of the liver and ovary further confirmed that sexual maturation promoted yolk precursor synthesis and derived from the F-test. Furthermore, the reproductive development and hepatic lipogenesis of hens during the early laying period were regulated by a complex and sophisticated endocrine system. The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis was the core system regulating the laying performance of laying hens (Shi et al., 2024). As a key signaling molecule for yolk formation, estrogen could participate in the regulation of hepatic lipid synthesis by modulating factors such as miR-19b-3p(Jia et al., 2024a). Osteocalcin (OCN) and its uncarboxylated form (ucOCN) could induce hepatocyte lipophagy by activating the ADPN-AMPK/PPARα-mTOR signaling pathway, thereby reducing the risk of fatty liver hemorrhage syndrome (FLHS) in laying hens (Tu et al., 2024). In addition, adiponectin and its receptors AdipoR1 and AdipoR2 played roles in energy balance and the reproductive system, and the adiponectin receptor agonist AdipoRon could promote the secretion of reproductive hormones and gonadal development through the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (Li et al., 2023a) . These studies collectively indicated that the endocrine system exerted a strong dominant role in reproductive and metabolic processes during the early laying period. In summary, the endocrine system exerted a powerful overall regulatory effect on reproductive physiology and lipid metabolism processes during the early laying period. This high-intensity, global endogenous homeostatic regulation created significant physiological background interference, which could mask the specific regulatory effects of the intestinal flora on lipid metabolism, thereby resulting in no correlations between flora abundance and various lipid indicators.

While this study provided valuable insights into gut microbiota and abdominal fat deposition in breeder broiler hens, several limitations warranted consideration. Our analysis was restricted to two discrete time points, limiting a continuous understanding of microbial dynamics. Furthermore, 16S rRNA sequencing offered inferred functional insights, not direct evidence of metabolic activity. We identified correlations, but establishing causality required further experimental validation.

Conclusions

This study reveals the spatiotemporal characteristics of gut microbiota in regulating abdominal fat deposition in broiler breeder hens. Cecal microbiota during the early laying period plays a critical role in host lipid metabolism. Low-fat hens display a functionally mature cecal microbial network enriched in Butyricicoccus, which is negatively correlated with the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway. By contrast, high-fat hens show degraded cecal microbial networks and elevated Peptococcus, which is positively correlated with this pathway. These results highlight the key role of the early laying period and identify Butyricicoccus and Peptococcus as signature genera modulating fat deposition via glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, providing a novel target for fat control in poultry breeding.
