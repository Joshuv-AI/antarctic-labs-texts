Comparative and integrated application of flow cytometry and next-generation sequencing for minimal residual disease monitoring in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Jungao Huang, Chaoqiang Zheng, Yulan Liu, Changhao Li, Xiaoqin Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMinimal residual disease (MRD) detection has emerged as a important prognostic biomarker in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), While both flow cytometry (FCM) and next-generation sequencing (NGS) are established MRD detection methods, their comparative performance, concordance, and optimal integration strategy remain incompletely defined in real-world clinical practice. To evaluate the concordance, diagnostic performance, and prognostic value of FCM versus NGS for MRD monitoring in AML patients achieving morphological complete remission. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 210 AML patients treated between January 2014 and December 2023 who underwent simultaneous FCM and NGS MRD assessment during remission. MRD positivity was defined as ≥ 0.1% leukemia-associated immunophenotype cells by FCM or ≥ 0.01% variant allele frequency by NGS. Primary outcomes included relapse-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS). Secondary outcomes included concordance analysis and subgroup-specific performance. Among 210 patients (median age 54 years, 56.2% female), FCM detected MRD in 119 patients (56.7%) while NGS identified MRD in 128 (61.0%), with an overall concordance of 93.81% (197/210; κ = 0.872, 95% CI: 0.812–0.932). Using NGS as reference standard, FCM demonstrated sensitivity of 91.41% (117/128), specificity of 97.56% (80/82), positive predictive value of 98.32% (117/119), and negative predictive value of 87.91% (80/91). Among 13 discordant cases (6.2%), 11 were FCM-negative/NGS-positive, predominantly harboring NPM1 mutations (8/11, 72.7%), with median VAF of 0.35% (range 0.12%-0.98%). During median follow-up of 18 months, 54.5% (6/11) of FCM-negative/NGS-positive patients relapsed versus 50% (1/2) of FCM-positive/NGS-negative patients. Integrated assessment stratified patients into three distinct risk groups: dual-negative (n = 80, 2.5% relapse rate, 11.2% mortality), single-positive (n = 13, 53.8% relapse rate, 23.1% mortality), and dual-positive (n = 117, 95.7% relapse rate, 57.3% mortality) (P < 0.001 for all comparisons). For relapse prediction, the combined approach achieved an area under the curve of 0.965 (95% CI: 0.935–0.989) with 98.4% sensitivity and 97.5% negative predictive value, superior to either method alone. FCM and NGS demonstrate high concordance for AML MRD detection while providing complementary information that enhances risk stratification. The integrated application of both methods improves prognostic assessment, with dual-negative status identifying patients suitable for treatment de-escalation and any MRD positivity warranting intensified monitoring. Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) represents a highly heterogeneous hematologic malignancy characterized by clonal expansion and differentiation arrest of hematopoietic stem or progenitor cells [1]. Despite significant advances in AML treatment, including the development of novel targeted therapies and improvements in allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) techniques, disease relapse remains the primary cause of treatment failure [2]. Approximately 50–70% of AML patients who achieve complete remission (CR) will experience disease recurrence [3, 4]. This high relapse rate underscores the critical need for sensitive methods to detect subclinical disease persistence, termed minimal residual disease (MRD).

Minimal residual disease (MRD), defined as the persistence of leukemic cells below the threshold of morphological detection following achievement of CR, has emerged as a important prognostic biomarker in AML [5–7]. The presence of MRD correlates with disease relapse and has become instrumental in risk stratification, treatment optimization, and relapse prediction [8, 9]. Accurate detection and monitoring of MRD are therefore important for improving clinical outcomes in AML patients.

Currently, two primary technologies dominate AML MRD detection: multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) and molecular biology-based approaches. Flow cytometry (FCM), through the detection of leukemia-associated immunophenotypes (LAIP) or “different from normal” (DfN) patterns, can identify residual leukemic cells at sensitivities of 0.01–0.1% [10, 11]. This technique offers several advantages, including rapid turnaround time, broad applicability, and provision of immunophenotypic information, making it widely adopted in clinical practice [12].

The advent of high-throughput sequencing has introduced next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based MRD detection methods. NGS-MRD, by quantifying variant allele frequencies (VAF) of specific gene mutations, can achieve detection sensitivities of 0.01 − 0.001% [13, 14]. This technology not only detects known driver mutations but also reveals clonal evolution patterns, providing useful insights into leukemia molecular pathogenesis and resistance mechanisms [15, 16]. However, both FCM and NGS MRD detection methods have inherent limitations. FCM relies on cell surface marker expression and may be affected by immunophenotypic drift during treatment [17]. NGS, despite its higher sensitivity and specificity, requires known molecular markers at diagnosis, has longer turnaround times, and incurs higher costs. Therefore, investigating the concordance, complementarity, and clinical value of the integrated application of these techniques is of clinical importance.

Given the complementary strengths and limitations of FCM and NGS, this study addresses three key questions: First, what is the real-world concordance between these methods in a large, unselected cohort? Second, what clinical significance do discordant results carry? Third, can integrated assessment improve risk stratification beyond either method alone? To answer these questions, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to compare the diagnostic performance of FCM and NGS in AML MRD monitoring, evaluate their concordance, analyze the clinical significance of discordant results, and assess the prognostic value of their integrated application, thereby providing evidence-based guidance for optimizing AML MRD monitoring strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at Ganzhou People’s Hospital between January 2014 and December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. PJB2025-146-01), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported following STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Sample size determination

Based on previous multicenter studies reporting concordance rates of 70–85% between FCM and NGS, we calculated that a minimum of 200 patients would provide 80% power to detect a kappa coefficient ≥ 0.70 with α = 0.05, assuming equal distribution of MRD-positive and negative cases. We included additional patients to account for potential subgroup analyses.

Cytogenetic and fusion gene detection

Cytogenetic abnormalities were identified using conventional G-banding karyotype analysis (metaphase cytogenetics) on bone marrow aspirates, with a minimum of 20 metaphases analyzed per sample. Fusion genes were detected using multiplex RT-PCR panels covering common AML-associated fusions (RUNX1-RUNX1T1, CBFB-MYH11, PML-RARA, and others) followed by confirmation with FISH when indicated. The NGS panel used in our study targeted point mutations and small indels but was not designed to detect fusion genes or large structural variants.

Flow cytometry MRD detection

Ten-color multiparameter flow cytometry was performed for MRD assessment. Bone marrow samples were processed within 2 h of collection. Mononuclear cells were prepared using red blood cell lysis and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. The antibody panel included CD45, CD34, CD117, CD33, CD13, CD56, CD7, CD19, and HLA-DR, among others (e.g., CD11b, CD15, CD64, CD123). Detection was performed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, acquiring at least 200,000 nucleated cells per sample.

MRD positivity was defined as detection of ≥ 0.1% cells with leukemia-associated immunophenotype (LAIP) or “different from normal” (DfN) phenotype. Data analysis was performed using BD FACSDiva software, with independent interpretation by two experienced technicians. Discordant cases were adjudicated by a senior hematopathologist.

Next-generation sequencing MRD detection

NGS-based MRD detection was performed using a targeted sequencing panel covering commonly mutated genes in AML. DNA was extracted from bone marrow mononuclear cells using standard protocols. The same gDNA sample was used for detecting all mutations within our targeted sequencing panel. Library preparation was performed using the Agilent SureSelect Custom Panel targeting 42 genes recurrently mutated in myeloid malignancies. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 550 platform using 150 bp paired-end reads.

Sequence alignment was performed using BWA-MEM, and variant calling was conducted using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) following established best practices [18]. The bioinformatics pipeline was validated internally following CAP/CLIA guidelines, similar to previously published NGS-MRD assays [14]. Unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were incorporated to distinguish true variants from PCR errors [19].

MRD positivity was defined as detection of known driver mutations with variant allele frequency (VAF) ≥ 0.01%. Only mutations detected at initial diagnosis were tracked for MRD monitoring to ensure specificity. Quality control included analysis of germline variants and technical replicates.

The same comprehensive NGS panel was applied at both diagnosis and MRD timepoints. However, for the purpose of MRD positivity determination, we tracked only driver mutations detected at diagnosis. Following the 2021 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) MRD Working Party recommendations, mutations associated with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), specifically isolated DNMT3A, TET2, or ASXL1 (DTA mutations) without other AML-defining mutations, were excluded from MRD assessment as these may persist during remission without indicating residual leukemia. Germline mutations (variant allele frequency approximately 50%) were also excluded. Molecular markers tracked for MRD included NPM1 mutations, FLT3-ITD/TKD, IDH1/2, KIT, TP53, NRAS/KRAS, and other established AML driver mutations. For patients harboring both NPM1 and FLT3-ITD mutations at diagnosis (n = 73, 34.7%), both markers were tracked simultaneously, and MRD positivity was defined if either marker was detected above the 0.01% VAF threshold. Notably, no patients in our cohort had DTA mutations as their sole detectable genetic alteration; all patients harboring these mutations also carried additional pathogenic AML-defining mutations.

Error correction was achieved through multiple approaches: [1] High sequencing depth (≥ 5000× coverage) to ensure statistical confidence in variant calling; [2] Unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were incorporated during library preparation to distinguish true variants from PCR or sequencing errors; [3] Bioinformatic filtering using strand bias analysis, mapping quality scores (≥ 30), and base quality scores (≥ 20); [4] Variants were required to be present in both forward and reverse reads.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) or mean ± standard deviation (SD), as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Concordance between FCM and NGS was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, with values > 0.80 indicating excellent agreement.

Diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), were calculated using NGS as the reference standard. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the predictive performance of MRD detection for both relapse and mortality, with area under the curve (AUC) values calculated. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with log-rank tests for group comparisons. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between January 2014 and December 2023, 487 adult AML patients were treated at our institution. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 210 patients with simultaneous FCM and NGS MRD assessments were included in the final analysis. The flowchart illustrating the patient selection process, including the applied inclusion and exclusion criteria, is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The study cohort had a median age of 56 years (IQR 48–63), including 92 males (43.8%) and 118 females (56.2%). At diagnosis, the median white blood cell count was 28.4 × 109/L (IQR 4.3–65.1 × 109/L), median hemoglobin was 74 g/L, and median platelet count was 59.5 × 10^9/L. The median bone marrow blast percentage was 65% (IQR 40.2–82). Cytogenetic analysis revealed abnormal karyotype in 76 patients (36.2%) and chromosomal normal in 134 patients (63.8%). Fusion genes were detected in 9 patients (4.3%). Molecular profiling identified NPM1 mutations in 140 patients (66.7%), FLT3-ITD in 91 (43.3%), WT1 mutations in 45 (21.4%), IDH1 mutations in 42 (20.0%) and TP53 mutations in 29(13.8%). The gene mutation profiles of 210 patients detected by next-generation sequencing are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. Twenty-four patients (11.4%) underwent allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.(Table 1).

Diagnostic performance analysis

Using NGS as the reference standard, FCM demonstrated a sensitivity of 91.41% (117/128), specificity of 97.56% (80/82), positive predictive value of 98.32% (117/119), and negative predictive value of 87.91% (80/91). The overall agreement rate between the two methods was 93.81% (197/210), with a Cohen’s Kappa coefficient of 0.872 (95% CI: 0.812–0.932), indicating high concordance. (Table 2and supplement Fig. 3).

Analysis of discordant cases

Thirteen patients (6.2%) showed discordant results between FCM and NGS. Among the 11 FCM-negative/NGS-positive cases, 8 (72.7%) harbored NPM1 mutations, 5 (45.5%) had DNMT3A mutations, and 4 (36.4%) carried FLT3-ITD mutations. The median VAF detected by NGS in these cases was 0.35% (range 0.12%-0.98%), approaching the FCM detection threshold. The 2 FCM-positive/NGS-negative cases both exhibited abnormal immunophenotypes, but mutations detected at diagnosis had cleared at MRD assessment. Notably, 7 of 13 discordant cases (53.8%) experienced disease relapse during follow-up, including 6 FCM-negative/NGS-positive and 1 FCM-positive/NGS-negative case, suggesting potentially superior sensitivity of NGS in detecting molecular-level MRD.

MRD status and clinical outcomes

With a median follow-up of 18 months (range 1–98 months), the entire cohort had a median RFS of 12 months and median OS of 16 months. Disease relapse occurred in 123 patients (58.6%), and 76 patients (36.2%) died. When stratified by FCM MRD status, the MRD-negative group (n = 91) showed a relapse rate of 9.9%, mortality rate of 9.9%, median RFS of 17 months, and median OS of 18 months. The MRD-positive group (n = 119) demonstrated a relapse rate of 95.8%, mortality rate of 56.3%, median RFS of 8 months, and median OS of 14 months. Both RFS (P < 0.001) and OS (P < 0.001) differed significantly between groups. When stratified by NGS MRD status, the MRD-negative group (n = 82) had a relapse rate of 4.9%, mortality rate of 11.0%, median RFS of 18 months, and median OS of 18 months. The MRD-positive group (n = 128) showed a relapse rate of 93.0%, mortality rate of 52.3%, median RFS of 8 months, and median OS of 14 months. Similarly, both RFS (P < 0.001) and OS (P < 0.001) differed significantly between groups (Supplement Table 2; Fig. 2and Supplemnet Fig. 4).

Assessment To evaluate the clinical value of integrated application, patients were categorized into three groups: dual-negative (FCM-/NGS-, n = 80), single-positive (FCM+/NGS- or FCM-/NGS+, n = 13), and dual-positive (FCM+/NGS+, n = 117). The dual-negative group demonstrated favorable outcomes with a relapse rate of 2.5%, mortality rate of 11.2%. Conversely, the dual-positive group showed the poorest prognosis with a relapse rate of 95.7%, mortality rate of 57.3%. Subgroup analyses

In patients with FLT3-ITD mutations (n = 91), the concordance rate between FCM and NGS was 91.2%, slightly lower than the overall cohort. Among NPM1-mutated patients (n = 140), NGS detected a higher MRD-positive rate (65.7%) compared to FCM (55.0%), suggesting potentially superior sensitivity of NGS in detecting NPM1-mutated MRD. Among 24 patients who underwent allogeneic HSCT, none of the pre-transplant MRD dual-negative patients (n = 8) experienced relapse, while 31.3% of MRD-positive patients (n = 16) relapsed, highlighting the importance of MRD status in transplant patient prognostication.(Table 3).

Subgroup Analysis of MRD Detection by Mutation Status and Treatment

Using WHO 2022 criteria, we retrospectively identified 46 (21.9%) patients meeting AML with myelodys-plasia-related changes (AML-MR) criteria, including ༔ patients with MDS-related cytogenetic abnormalities and 42 patients with AML-MR defining mutations (ASXL1: 12, RUNX1: 14, SRSF2: 4, U2AF1: 7, BCOR: 13, STAG2:6). Notably, RUNX1 mutations are classified as AML-MR defining under the WHO 2022 classification, whereas the ICC 2022 classification categorizes AML with mutated RUNX1 as a distinct entity rather than an MR-defining alteration. In this AML-MR subgroup, MRD concordance between FCM and NGS was 97.8% (45/46), consistent with the overall cohort. The relapse rate in AML-MR patients was 65.2% versus 57.3% in non-AML-MR patients (P = 0.398). The integrated FCM-NGS approach maintained predictive value in AML-MR patients with an AUC of 0.941 for relapse prediction. (Supplement Table 4)

We also performed a sensitivity analysis comparing MRD concordance and prognostic outcomes using WHO 2022 risk stratification. The key findings remained consistent: Concordance rate: 93.5% (WHO 2022) vs. 93.8% (WHO 2016)and AUC for relapse prediction: 0.962 vs. 0.96.(Supplement Table 5).

Additionally, sensitivity analysis by treatment era (2014–2018 versus 2019–2023) demonstrated consistent concordance patterns between FCM and NGS across both periods (91.3% versus 95.2%, P = 0.38), suggesting that the introduction of FLT3 inhibitors during the later period did not substantially affect the comparative performance of these MRD detection methods.(Supplement Table 6).

For relapse prediction, both FCM and NGS demonstrated good diagnostic performance with AUC values of 0.940 (95% CI: 0.907–0.970) and 0.937 (95% CI: 0.902–0.971), respectively. The combined approach further improved the AUC to 0.965 (95% CI: 0.935–0.989), achieving a sensitivity of 98.4% and NPV of 97.5%.

For mortality prediction, the diagnostic performance was moderate but clinically meaningful. FCM showed an AUC of 0.747 (95% CI: 0.673–0.816) with a sensitivity of 88.2% and specificity of 61.2%. NGS yielded a slightly lower AUC of 0.713 (95% CI: 0.636–0.785), while the combined approach achieved an AUC of 0.748 (95% CI: 0.674–0.817). Notably, all methods maintained high NPV values (88.8–90.1%) for mortality, suggesting that MRD-negative status effectively identifies patients at low risk of death.

The better performance for relapse prediction compared to mortality prediction (AUC approximately 0.2 for all methods) likely reflects that relapse is the direct consequence of residual leukemic burden, while mortality is influenced by multiple factors including salvage therapy effectiveness, transplantation eligibility, and patient comorbidities.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study provides insights into the comparative performance and clinical utility of FCM and NGS for MRD detection in AML patients. While the prognostic significance of NPM1 and FLT3-ITD mutations for MRD monitoring is well established, our study contributes novel findings in several aspects: [1] real-world concordance data from simultaneous FCM and NGS testing in a Chinese AML population, where cytogenetic profiles may differ from Western cohorts; [2] a practical three-tier risk stratification system (dual-negative, single-positive, dual-positive) that extends beyond single-method MRD assessment; [3] detailed characterization of discordant cases providing mechanistic insights into the complementary nature of these technologies; and [4] comprehensive mutation spectrum analysis across 42 genes allowing assessment in diverse molecular subtypes. Our findings demonstrate excellent concordance between these two methodologies (93.81% agreement,

Regarding the representativeness of our cohort, the normal karyotype (NK) AML frequency (63.8%) in our study is higher than the 40–50% typically reported in both Western and Chinese populations [20, 21]. This discrepancy is most likely attributable to selection bias introduced by our inclusion criteria requiring achievement of morphological complete remission (CR). It is well-established that cytogenetics is the strongest predictor of CR achievement, with patients harboring adverse-risk cytogenetics (complex karyotype, monosomal karyotype, -5/del(5q), -7, etc.) demonstrating significantly lower CR rates (30–47%) compared to those with normal or favorable-risk karyotypes [22]. Consequently, by selecting only CR-achieving patients for MRD assessment, our cohort would naturally be enriched for NK-AML patients who respond more favorably to standard induction chemotherapy. These selection considerations should be acknowledged when extrapolating our findings to unselected, newly diagnosed AML populations.

The high concordance rate between FCM and NGS observed in our study aligns with recent multicenter investigations, validating both techniques as reliable MRD detection methods [1, 5, 23]. The excellent agreement (κ = 0.872) exceeds the threshold for clinical reliability and surpasses concordance rates reported in several previous studies, which ranged from 70–85% [24, 25]. This improved agreement likely reflects several factors: standardization of FCM protocols with 10-color panels and ≥ 200,000 events acquired, implementation of high-sensitivity NGS with ≥ 5000× coverage, simultaneous sampling eliminating temporal variations, and experienced operators following standardized interpretation criteria [26].

Using NGS as the reference standard, FCM demonstrated adequate diagnostic metrics with sensitivity of 91.4% and specificity of 97.6%. The high positive predictive value (98.3%) indicates that FCM-positive results reliably identify true MRD positivity, minimizing false-positive classifications that could lead to unnecessary treatment intensification. However, the relatively lower negative predictive value (87.9%) suggests that FCM-negative results warrant cautious interpretation, particularly in high-risk patients where false-negative results could delay therapeutic intervention.

The 13 discordant cases (6.2%) in our cohort provide valuable insights into the complementary nature of these technologies. Among the 11 FCM-negative/NGS-positive cases, the predominance of NPM1 mutations (72.7%) corroborates previous observations that NPM1-mutated AML may exhibit immunophenotypic instability during treatment, potentially compromising FCM sensitivity [27, 28]. These discordant cases suggesting that NGS captures molecular MRD at levels below FCM resolution. Critically, 54.5% of these FCM-negative/NGS-positive patients experienced relapse, underscoring the clinical relevance of molecular MRD detection even when immunophenotypic assessment appears negative [29].

The two FCM-positive/NGS-negative cases likely represent scenarios where immunophenotypic aberrations persist despite molecular clearance, possibly reflecting residual differentiated leukemic cells without self-renewal capacity or regenerating normal hematopoiesis with transient phenotypic abnormalities [30]. The lower relapse rate in this subgroup (50%) compared to dual-positive patients suggests that isolated immunophenotypic positivity may carry different prognostic implications than molecular persistence.

Our analysis reveals that MRD status by either method predicts clinical outcomes, with notable differences in relapse rates. The FCM MRD-negative group demonstrated a relapse rate of only 9.9%, while NGS MRD-negative patients showed an even lower rate of 4.9%. This better performance of NGS in identifying truly MRD-negative patients likely reflects its higher analytical sensitivity and ability to track specific molecular markers rather than potentially variable immunophenotypes [15, 16].

The integrated assessment using both methods provided improved risk stratification. Dual-negative patients exhibited favorable outcomes with a 2.5% relapse rate, establishing this group as low-risk and potentially suitable for de-escalated post-remission therapy [8]. Conversely, dual-positive patients faced a 95.7% relapse rate, identifying a population requiring intensive surveillance and aggressive intervention strategies. The intermediate-risk single-positive group (53.8% relapse rate) represents patients who would benefit from close monitoring and individualized treatment decisions based on additional clinical factors.

The better performance of MRD detection for relapse prediction (AUC ~ 0.94) compared to mortality prediction (AUC ~ 0.74) merits careful consideration. This differential likely reflects the direct mechanistic relationship between residual leukemic burden and disease recurrence, whereas mortality represents a multifactorial endpoint influenced by salvage therapy efficacy, transplantation eligibility, treatment-related toxicity, and patient comorbidities [31, 32]. The high NPV for mortality (88.8–90.1%) across all methods suggests that MRD-negative status reliably identifies patients with favorable survival prospects, supporting its use in treatment de-escalation strategies.

Our subgroup analyses reveal important mutation-specific patterns that should inform MRD monitoring strategies. The lower concordance rate in FLT3-ITD positive patients (91.2% vs. 93.81% overall) may reflect the known instability of FLT3-ITD during treatment and potential emergence of FLT3-ITD negative subclones [33]. The higher NGS positivity rate in NPM1-mutated patients (65.7% vs. 55.0% by FCM) supports preferential use of molecular monitoring in this subset, particularly given the established prognostic significance of NPM1 MRD kinetics [34].

The transplantation subgroup analysis provides evidence supporting pre-transplant MRD assessment, with no relapses observed among dual-negative patients versus 31.3% in MRD-positive patients. This finding supports current guidelines recommending MRD-guided transplantation timing and suggests that achieving dual-negative status before transplantation should be a therapeutic goal when feasible [35].

The interpretation of NGS-based MRD results requires careful consideration of mutations associated with clonal hematopoiesis. Following ELN guidelines, we excluded isolated DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 (DTA) mutations from MRD assessment, as these are commonly associated with age-related clonal hematopoiesis and may persist during remission without indicating residual leukemia. In our AML-MR subgroup analysis (n = 46, 21.9%), the integrated FCM-NGS approach maintained good predictive value (AUC = 0.941), though the concordance rate was consistent with (97.8%) the overall cohort. Importantly, AML-MR defining mutations such as ASXL1, RUNX1, and SRSF2 may persist as clonal hematopoiesis even after leukemia clearance, potentially complicating NGS-based MRD interpretation. We recommend that in patients with AML-MR or those harboring CHIP-associated mutations, MRD status should be interpreted in conjunction with FCM results and other clinical parameters rather than relying solely on NGS findings.

Based on our findings, we propose a risk-adapted MRD monitoring strategy that leverages the complementary strengths of both technologies. Initial assessment should employ both FCM and NGS when feasible, with dual-negative status identifying patients suitable for treatment de-escalation and reduced monitoring frequency. For resource-limited settings, prioritization should consider mutation status, with NGS preferred for NPM1 or FLT3-ITD positive patients and FCM potentially sufficient for patients lacking trackable molecular markers.

The rapid turnaround time and lower cost of FCM support its use for frequent monitoring during early treatment phases when MRD kinetics influence therapeutic decisions. NGS may be reserved for critical decision points such as pre-transplantation assessment or evaluation of suspected relapse, where its higher sensitivity and molecular precision provide clinical value.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. First, the retrospective single-center design may limit generalizability, particularly given that our cohort characteristics (63.8% normal karyotype AML) differ from Western and Chinese cohorts. Second, the heterogeneous treatment regimens, including the introduction of FLT3 inhibitors (midostaurin, gilteritinib) during the later study period (2019–2023), preclude definitive conclusions about MRD-guided therapy optimization, although sensitivity analysis by treatment era showed consistent concordance patterns (91.3% versus 95.2%, P = 0.38). Third, the exclusion of patients without simultaneous FCM and NGS testing may introduce selection bias. Fourth, patients were originally diagnosed using WHO 2016 criteria; although retrospective reclassification per WHO 2022 yielded similar results (concordance: 93.5% versus 93.8%; AUC for relapse: 0.962 versus 0.965), the classification system evolution should be acknowledged. Fifth, NGS detection of large FLT3-ITDs (greater than 150 bp) may have slightly reduced sensitivity due to read length limitations. Sixth, our NGS panel focused on established AML mutations and may miss emerging clones with different molecular profiles. Finally, AML-MR defining mutations may persist as clonal hematopoiesis even after leukemia clearance, potentially complicating NGS-based MRD interpretation in this subgroup, emphasizing the importance of integrating FCM results for comprehensive assessment.

Future prospective studies should evaluate standardized MRD-guided treatment algorithms, incorporating both FCM and NGS at predefined timepoints. Investigation of novel technologies, including digital PCR for enhanced sensitivity and single-cell sequencing for clonal architecture assessment, may further refine MRD detection. The integration of MRD dynamics with other prognostic factors through machine learning approaches could enable more precise risk prediction models.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that FCM and NGS provide complementary information for MRD assessment in AML, with high concordance but distinct advantages that support integrated use. The high negative predictive value of combined assessment identifies low-risk patients, while the detection of any MRD positivity warrants intensive monitoring and consideration of treatment modification. Our findings support a personalized approach to MRD monitoring that considers mutation profile, treatment phase, and resource availability. As therapeutic options for AML continue to expand, optimized MRD detection strategies will become increasingly important for tailoring treatment intensity to disease biology and achieving improved outcomes while minimizing toxicity. The implementation of dual-method MRD assessment, particularly at key therapeutic decision points, represents a useful approach in precision medicine for AML management.
