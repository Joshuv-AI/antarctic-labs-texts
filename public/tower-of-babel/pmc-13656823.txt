Advances in Indocyanine Green Near-Infrared Fluorescence for Extrahepatic Biliary Identification in Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy for Acute Cholecystitis: A Narrative Review
Authors: Zhihui Jin, Xusheng Yang, Yuntian Liu, Lei Song, Xuewen Chen, Yue Wang, Xiaoping Cheng, Lu Liang, Bihui Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, a minimally invasive procedure with rapid recovery, remains the standard surgical intervention for acute cholecystitis. Acute inflammation of the gallbladder can cause severe edema, dense adhesions, and anatomical distortion within Calot’s triangle, frequently obscuring tissue planes and increasing the risk of iatrogenic bile duct injury, a significant surgical complication. Indocyanine green fluorescence serves as a valuable intraoperative navigation tool. Its hepatobiliary metabolic characteristics enable real-time near-infrared visualization of extrahepatic bile ducts, thereby compensating for the limitations of visual and tactile identification during routine cholecystectomy. This narrative review compiles published clinical evidence regarding the application of indocyanine green fluorescence in acute cholecystitis operations. We summarize its advantages in inflamed operative fields and categorize practical application experiences across varying grades of inflammation. Current data indicate enhanced biliary visualization and reduced operative time; however, robust high-level trials confirming a reduction in bile duct injury risks remain lacking. Additionally, we discuss emerging technical advancements, such as artificial intelligence and multispectral imaging. This article aims to evaluate the practical value of indocyanine green near-infrared fluorescence for the recognition of extrahepatic bile ducts in laparoscopic cholecystectomy performed on patients with acute cholecystitis.

Introduction

For symptomatic cholelithiasis and acute cholecystitis, laparoscopic cholecystectomy, a minimally invasive procedure with rapid recovery, is a standard treatment for gallbladder disease (cholelithiasis) and acute cholecystitis [1]. Compared with open surgery, laparoscopic cholecystectomy has a higher risk of bile duct injury (BDI) [2]. Once BDI occurs, it can lead to severe complications, including bile leakage, biliary stricture, recurrent obstructive jaundice, cholangitis, biliary cirrhosis, and even hepatic failure [3,4]. In acute cholecystitis, intense inflammation of Calot’s triangle often leads to severe tissue edema, friability, and dense fibrotic adhesions that obscure important biliary landmarks and blur anatomical planes [5,6]. Moreover, biliary variants exist in approximately 15% to 20% of individuals and are even harder to recognize when inflammation is present [7,8]. Together, these factors undermine the reliability of conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy and are a major substrate for increased BDI in acute inflammation [4–6]. Indeed, evidence suggests that the rate of BDI during laparoscopic cholecystectomy for acute cholecystitis can reach 1.49%, markedly higher than the 0.09% reported for elective surgery [2]. To address this persistent clinical challenge, intraoperative adjunct imaging techniques have gained traction, among which near-infrared (NIR) indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence cholangiography has attracted particular attention in recent years [9,10]. After intravenous administration, ICG is rapidly taken up by hepatocytes and excreted into bile, and NIR illumination emits fluorescence which allows real-time dynamic visualization of the extrahepatic biliary system. By exploiting physiological biliary excretion rather than relying solely on static anatomical appearance, this approach can extend biliary mapping beyond the limitations of conventional dissection and may still provide a clear “roadmap” even in the presence of inflammation and adhesions [9,11]. Although concerns remain regarding whether acute inflammation may affect ICG kinetics or fluorescence penetration, a growing body of clinical evidence supports high identification rates and procedural safety in difficult cholecystectomy, anatomical variants, and reoperative biliary surgery [9,11,12].

Although studies evaluating ICG fluorescence imaging in acute cholecystitis surgery are increasing, evidence regarding the stratification of ICG use according to disease severity remains limited. In this narrative review, we summarize the current evidence for ICG fluorescence imaging for extrahepatic biliary identification during acute cholecystitis surgery. We present the challenges, imaging principles, clinical effectiveness, and severity-adapted applications of ICG, as well as considerations for standardization and future directions. We combine these observations and aim to provide conceptual rationale and practical guidance for precise and safe application of ICG fluorescence imaging in acute cholecystitis surgery. The primary objective of this narrative review is to sort existing evidence for ICG fluorescence used in acute cholecystitis surgery. Unlike previous broad reviews, we organize all relevant observations under Tokyo Guidelines 2018 (TG18) inflammatory grading and analyze ICG performance separately for mild, moderate, and severe cases [3]. This article aims to review the role of ICG NIR fluorescence for extrahepatic biliary identification during laparoscopic cholecystectomy in patients with acute cholecystitis.

Clinical Challenges and Safety Considerations in Intraoperative Identification of the Extrahepatic Biliary Anatomy in Acute Cholecystitis

Risk factors for iatrogenic BDI

The extrahepatic biliary system is often unable to be identified surgically due to local changes that aggravate the anatomy and diminish landmark recognition. Biological variation represents a high-risk substrate for BDI. Clinically relevant anatomical variations include accessory hepatic ducts, abnormal drainage of the right posterior sectoral duct, and unusual cystic duct insertion patterns. Preoperative imaging seldom detects these variations from the start, especially when emergency laparoscopic cholecystectomy is performed [7,8]. Epidemiologic results suggest that in the 15% to 20% of patients that harbor biliary variants, the risk of misidentification in the intraoperative period is increased [7]. Chronic inflammation can further enhance these challenges, with edema, fibrosis, and large adhesions of Calot’s triangle potentially obscuring the junction between the cystic duct and the common hepatic duct (CHD) [5,6]. The limitations in surgical exposure and working space are nontrivial risks. Surgical exposure is restricted in patients with obesity, previous abdominal surgery, or severe bowel distension. Poor lighting and inadequate tissue retraction further limits visualization. Surgeons may accidentally clip or divide the bile ducts, and thermal injury from electrocautery can cause permanent ductal damage [3,13]. Even experienced surgical teams are vulnerable if the critical view of safety (CVS) is not achieved [8]. Therefore, careful preoperative assessment, a cautious intraoperative approach, and timely conversion to open surgery when safety cannot be ensured are key strategies to mitigate these risks.

Limitations of Conventional Identification in an Acutely Inflamed Field

Conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy relies heavily on the surgeon’s visual assessment and instrument tactile feedback to interpret the relationships between the cystic duct, common bile duct (CBD) and CHD. In acute cholecystitis, however, this approach is severely challenged in 2 ways. Visually, inflammation increases adhesions, renders tissues more fragile, reduces color contrast, and, together with inflammatory exudate, obscures normal tissue planes, increasing the risk of anatomical misidentification. As a result, the CBD can be mistaken for a fibrotic cystic duct [5]. In acute cholecystitis, the gallbladder wall adheres strongly to the liver bed and adjacent extrahepatic bile ducts. Rough blunt dissection or excessive electrocautery can tear fragile tissue and cause hemorrhage. Operators may misidentify an adherent CHD or CBD as a cystic duct and divide these structures, leading to severe complications postoperatively [4]. Examples of failure scenarios include ligation and transection of an aberrant hepatic duct (eg, the right posterior sectoral duct) misidentified as the cystic duct, delayed bile leakage resulting from unrecognized biliary injury, and blind dissection with an electrocautery hook in severely adherent areas, causing thermal injury to the lateral wall of the CHD and subsequent postoperative biliary stricture [14]. Collectively, these observations underscore the intrinsic shortcomings of conventional identification in acute inflammation and highlight the rationale for intraoperative adjunct navigation.

Long-Term Hazards and Clinical Management Burden of BDI

BDI is more common than regular surgical complications and can cause a series of events, including acute perioperative crises and long-term morbidities. BDI may present as bile leakage or biliary peritonitis if it is not recognized and treated promptly after surgery. If it is not properly diagnosed and treated, BDI can lead to generalized peritonitis and sepsis. Severe cases can result in multiple organ dysfunction and high mortality risk [3,15]. BDI requires frequent monitoring of liver function and inflammation markers. Radiologic and endoscopic interventions are commonly used for biliary decompression. The most frequently performed procedures include percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage and stent placement during endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography to control sepsis and optimize conditions for definitive surgical repair [16]. Even after technically successful reconstruction, some patients experience persistent long-term sequelae, such as recurrent cholangitis, maldigestion, and malabsorption, and a significant decline in quality of life. In severe cases, secondary biliary cirrhosis may develop and progress to liver failure [4,17]. Effective prevention of BDI extends beyond operative technique alone and relies on multidisciplinary team decision-making, precise risk assessment, and individualized approaches [13,18]. For patients at high risk, such as older individuals and those with diabetes mellitus or hypoalbuminemia, initial percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PTGBD) can be considered to mitigate acute inflammation, followed by interval elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy, thereby potentially reducing BDI risk and improving overall outcomes. This risk-stratified, individualized approach epitomizes the core principles of contemporary precision biliary surgery.

Principles and Distinct Advantages of ICG Fluorescence Imaging

Pharmacokinetic Basis and Optical Properties of ICG

ICG is a water-soluble tricarbocyanine dye with a favorable safety profile. Following intravenous administration, ICG rapidly binds to circulating plasma proteins, primarily albumin and lipoproteins. It is then selectively taken up by hepatocytes and excreted unchanged into the bile. This efficient hepatobiliary pathway is the basis for functional cholangiography [19]. Optically, ICG absorbs and emits peak intensity in the NIR spectrum (780–810 nm absorption and 810–850 nm emission). NIR photons can penetrate tissues to a depth of approximately 5 to 10 mm. Because they are less absorbed by endogenous chromophores, such as hemoglobin, NIR imaging provides intraoperative images with a high signal-to-noise ratio. Fluorescence intensity strongly depends on hepatic function, and impaired uptake and delayed excretion in patients with liver dysfunction can reduce the signal and cause poor image quality. The ICG retention rate at 15 minutes may be a useful preoperative reference for anticipating imaging performance [19]. At commonly recommended doses (0.25–0.5 mg/kg), ICG is generally well tolerated, and severe hypersensitivity reactions are rare [9,20].

Functional Visualization Superior to Conventional Anatomical Identification

Traditional laparoscopic cholecystectomy relies on surgeons recognizing fixed anatomical landmarks. Such anatomical recognition fails in acute cholecystectomy and other inflamed operative fields, in which edema, adhesions, and distortions of tissue planes disrupt normal anatomy. By contrast, ICG guidance does not “read” morphology but rather defines bile ducts through its excretory function. Figure 1 shows that the bile contour of the cystic triangle is clearly visualized under ICG fluorescence on the left side, while the anatomical layers are blurred on the right side under normal white light due to tissue edema and adhesion. Only conduits receiving and transporting ICG-laden bile emit fluorescence intraoperatively to provide an objective, on-demand functional roadmap of biliary course and continuity [9,11]. This advantage is important for complex variants. When ductal trajectories are difficult to see under standard white light, ICG fluorescence can outline the lumen and directionality of the biliary tract, leading to safer dissection and prevented injury [9]. In reoperative biliary surgery, in which scar tissue can obscure normal anatomy, ICG navigation has been reported to achieve an success rate of 92.31%, which can lead to reliable identification and reduce the risk of inadvertent injury [11]. A bile leakage can also be detected intraoperatively. Abnormal fluorescence extravasation around the gallbladder bed or near an anastomosis can serve as a warning sign of occult bile leakage, with greater sensitivity than visual inspection alone [21,22]. Collectively, function-guided navigation can contribute to significant improvement of the biliary surgery safety profile.

Kinetic characteristics of ICG Fluorescence and Intraoperative Guidance

Intraoperative visualization of the biliary system after ICG injection follows a fairly predictable time course, and a clear understanding of the timing is crucial to optimize imaging timing. After intravenous injection, hepatic parenchymal fluorescence is usually visible in 1 to 2 minutes. Extrahepatic biliary signal increases and usually peaks in 10 to 30 minutes [20]. Liver function has the largest effect on fluorescence kinetics. Cirrhosis decreases hepatocellular uptake and biliary excretion of ICG and leads to delayed or weak ductal signals [19]. Injection dose and timing are other variables to be calibrated. Underdosing may lead to low signal, and overdosing can increase background fluorescence and decrease duct-to-liver contrast. Preoperative intravenous injection in a time window ranging from 30 minutes to 24 hours before surgery is common practice but should be tailored to patient characteristics and surgical context [23]. Integrating kinetic principles into perioperative planning can help identify the most informative imaging window and maximize the navigation value of fluorescence.

Practical limitations of ICG fluorescence imaging

Although ICG fluorescence imaging offers several advantages, its real-world performance is limited by multiple practical constraints. For instance, NIR light penetrates only 5 to 10 mm of tissue. Inflammatory exudate, edema, necrosis, and thickened tissue layer all contribute to weak signal intensity [10,12]. Hepatic dysfunction, biliary obstruction, and acute cholangitis can delay or reduce the biliary excretion of ICG. Performance differs between acute cholecystitis severity grades, and optimal dosing and timing are not universally validated [12]. Moreover, ICG imaging provides functional rather than high-resolution structural detail, and interpretation still depends on surgical experience [10,12]. These factors must be considered when implementing ICG in clinical practice.

Clinical Effectiveness and Emerging Evidence of ICG Fluorescence Imaging in Surgery for Acute Cholecystitis

Evidence-based appraisal of biliary identification accuracy

A growing number of studies have shown that ICG fluorescence imaging can improve the biliary identification rate in acute cholecystitis. The rate of CHD and CBD identification is between 85% and 95% with ICG guidance. This rate is much higher than the rate obtained with standard white light laparoscopy [24,25]. ICG fluorescence imaging can also reduce the operative time. Results from the retrospective analysis of 1389 patients treated with laparoscopic cholecystectomy show that ICG fluorescence imaging can improve the surgical procedure and shorten the operative time. Importantly, ICG performance is not invariant and appears strongly affected by inflammation severity. In mild acute cholecystitis (TG18 grade I), where edema is small, ICG often provides near-ideal biliary contours. In grade II and III, moderate and severe, cholecystitis, the surgical field contains serosal thickening, inflammation fluid, and necrotic tissue. These substances scatter and absorb NIR light, weaken fluorescent signals, and reduce image resolution [3,25].

Most data supporting ICG in acute cholecystitis are based on retrospective cohorts and case series. Some randomized controlled trials have reported improved visualization of the biliary anatomy and shorter operative times; however, high-quality evidence demonstrating a definitive reduction in BDI or bile leakage is still lacking. Retrospective studies suggest positive trends, but they need to be confirmed in adequately powered prospective studies.

Positioning of ICG Among Adjunct Modalities and its Synergistic Value

Compared with conventional intraoperative cholangiography, ICG fluorescence cholangiography is a more practical technique because it eliminates radiation exposure, does not require additional procedural steps, reduces resource utilization, and provides continuous real-time visualization of the biliary anatomy [26]. In contrast to intraoperative ultrasound, ICG provides an intuitive “global” view of biliary anatomy with a flatter learning curve, which may help surgeons to implement it quickly. However, intraoperative ultrasonography provides more detailed structural information, including duct wall thickness, vascular relationships, and intraluminal pathology. Recent technological trajectories favor integration rather than replacement. Recent studies have explored a combined approach using ICG fluorescence imaging and intraoperative ultrasonography, in which ICG is used to identify the overall course of the biliary tree, followed by targeted ultrasonographic examination of high-risk regions, such as Calot’s triangle and the distal CBD. Such coordination could maximize safety by leveraging complementary strengths [27]. This integrative paradigm aligns with the broader direction of precision biliary surgery.

Navigational Relevance of ICG in Complex Surgical Scenarios

In routine cholecystectomy, ICG fluorescence imaging may serve primarily as an adjunct, but in some complex high-risk cases, its role may be crucial to maintaining safety. Impacted gallbladder neck stones compress the CBD and move its natural position. ICG can trace the displaced CBD wall. This allows surgeons to avoid misclassifying a compressed CBD as a thickened cystic duct and potentially damaging the duct [28]. In patients with complex biliary anatomical variants, ICG fluorescence imaging may assist in tracking the course of the bile ducts, improving orientation within Calot’s triangle, clarifying ductal anatomy, and facilitating diagnostic decision-making and operative strategy selection [9]. In patients with obesity, a thick abdominal wall and abundant adipose tissue can limit surgical exposure and reduce visualization under white light. Since NIR light can penetrate tissues to a small depth, ICG fluorescence may still provide a more stable navigation reference for biliary identification in a confined working space [9]. ICG also aids reoperations and suspected gallbladder malignancy. Fluorescence locates intact bile ducts hidden in scar tissue or tumor tissue. In some cases, the tool distinguishes benign inflammatory adhesions from malignant infiltration to guide intraoperative decision-making [29]. Together, these applications suggest that ICG fluorescence imaging has become a valuable—and in some cases even necessary—navigation tool for challenging biliary surgery.

A Practical, Severity-Adapted Guide for ICG Fluorescence Imaging in Acute Cholecystitis

General Stratification of Acute Cholecystitis per TG18

Acute cholecystitis presents diverse clinical and pathological manifestations. The internationally recognized TG18 classification system divides this disease into grade I (mild), grade II (moderate), and grade III categories [3]. These 3 grades differ greatly in terms of the extent and severity of inflammations in Calot’s triangle. Therefore, both the clinical utility and technical implementation of ICG fluorescence imaging should be tailored to TG18 severity classification to optimize dosing, timing, and intraoperative applications.

Application for Mild Acute Cholecystitis (TG18 Grade I)

In mild acute cholecystitis (TG18 grade I), there can be edema and adhesions in Calot’s triangle, but they are generally small, and the most important anatomical landmarks often remain identifiable. ICG provides additional benefits beyond emergency navigation in simple cases, including standardizing surgical workflows and allowing resident training. Published data show that the biliary visualization rate is 95% after injection of ICG under grade I inflammation, with clear duct lines and strong fluorescent signals [1]. There are also several practical advantages. First, ICG can speed technical efficiency. Experienced surgeons can see the real-time biliary roadmap, immediately confirm anatomical relationships, and thus reduce unnecessary dissection and possibly shorten CVS time. Second, ICG may flatten the learning curve. For junior surgeons, objective fluorescence feedback can be a useful tool to internalize 3-dimensional biliary anatomy and CVS principles and improve operative confidence and safety habits. Third, ICG can help detect other occult variants. Even mild inflammation variants (present in about 15%–20% of individuals) can be overlooked with white light alone; ICG fluorescence may reveal patterns such as accessory hepatic ducts or low cystic duct insertion [7,8]. Together, the routine use of ICG in TG18 grade I acute cholecystitis may lead to more standard, higher-quality laparoscopic cholecystectomy and more structured surgical training.

Application in Moderate Acute Cholecystitis (TG18 Grade II)

Moderate acute cholecystitis (TG18 grade II) planes in Calot’s triangle are often obliterated by dense fibroids and marked edema, making this one of the highest risks for BDI. In performing such technically demanding laparoscopic cholecystectomy, ICG fluorescence may become a helpful adjunct and a key determinant of whether dissection can proceed safely. Serosal thickening and inflammation weakens fluorescent signals, but NIR light (800 nm) can penetrate 5 to 10 mm of tissue to track duct routes [25,30]. At this grade, surgeons should not wait for a “perfect” image; rather, the active interpretation of the fluorescence pattern is required. For example, in severe adhesions, surgeons should first identify the most stable signal of the CHD and track distally toward the CBD and near the cystic duct. Here, the functional nature of ICG guidance provides additional clues. If the cystic duct is completely blocked by an impact stone, it can fail to fluoresce, a signal which can localize the diseased segment and discourage blind dissection at the gallbladder neck. In such cases, a fundus-first (retrograde) cholecystectomy may be a safer bailout strategy [28,31]. Multiple retrospective studies show that, in patients with TG18 grade II, ICG-guided surgery reduces the BDI rate below 0.5% [9,32], underscoring the potential role of ICG fluorescence imaging in overcoming difficult gallbladder surgery.

Application in Severe Acute Cholecystitis (TG18 Grade III) and Special Scenarios

Patients with grade III acute cholecystitis often develop organ dysfunction and critical systemic conditions. Their overall surgical risk increases significantly [33]. PTGBD is usually performed first to control infection and stabilize vital signs, and terminological laparoscopic cholecystectomy is delayed until inflammation subsides. In the acute phase, ICG fluorescence imaging may not be sufficient. Hemodynamic instability and accompanying hepatic dysfunction may delay or reduce ICG uptake and biliary excretion, affecting fluorescence quality [19]. In addition, tissue necrosis and sloughing associated with gangrenous cholecystitis, as well as purulent coating, can markedly dampen signal detectability [22]. However, this limitation may not make ICG irrelevant. In fact, ICG guidance may be useful during the interval laparoscopic cholecystectomy after PTGBD when dense fibrosis and scarring obscure normal anatomy [10]. After preoperative ICG administration, fluorescence imaging may help identify extrahepatic biliary ducts through fibrotic tissue planes [11]. Fluorescence bile leakage directs fistula tracts and can guide surgeons to choose appropriate reconstructive procedures, such as fistula repair, duct-to-duct anastomosis, or Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy [9]. Therefore, in the management of severe acute cholecystitis, fluorescence imaging may be an important adjunct to enhance safety of staged surgery.

A Multidimensional Reappraisal of the Clinical Value of ICG Fluorescence Imaging in Acute Cholecystitis

Perioperative Outcome Improvements Supported by Clinical Evidence

The impact of ICG fluorescence imaging on perioperative outcomes has been investigated in clinical studies. Table 1 summarizes retrospective studies comparing ICG-assisted laparoscopic cholecystectomy with standard laparoscopic cholecystectomy. These studies demonstrate several potential advantages of ICG fluorescence imaging. Xu et al [34] reported shorter operating times in patients receiving ICG guidance. The average procedure time decreased to 55 minutes compared with 60 minutes in the control group. More patients had intraoperative blood loss below 5 mL in the ICG group (73.4% vs 65.6%). These results reflect improved surgical efficiency through clearer biliary visualization [34]. Broderick et al [35] further confirmed that the risk of postoperative complications in the ICG group decreased to 0.5% (vs 0.9% in the conventional group), which meets the standard risk reduction indicator in evidence-based medicine. A systematic review by Serban et al summarizing global clinical data showed a consistent protective effect of ICG fluorescence against iatrogenic biliary damage in all inflammatory grades of acute cholecystitis [36]. The authors also highlighted the considerable heterogeneity in ICG dosage and timing protocols across existing clinical trials, which aligns with the present discussion regarding personalized preoperative administration based on liver function status.

The clinical impact of ICG fluorescence guidance is supported by an accumulating evidence base and has been linked to improvements in several perioperative endpoints. A randomized controlled trial including 440 patients reported that ICG navigation significantly reduced operative time [1]. In elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy, ICG also appeared to accelerate biliary identification; the time to identify the CBD was shorter with ICG (22 min) than under conventional conditions (32 min). In a separate analysis of 1389 laparoscopic cholecystectomy cases, mean operative time was 75.57 minutes in the ICG group vs 104.9 minutes in the non-ICG group [9]. Collectively, these findings suggest that ICG may not only expedite biliary mapping but may also streamline overall procedural efficiency. ICG performs well in reoperative biliary cases with extensive scar tissue. Published data show a 92.31% success rate for accurate duct recognition. Better visualization lowers the chance of accidental tissue damage, and patients achieve shorter hospital stays, by 2 to 4 days on average [11]. Importantly, potential benefits may extend beyond the index operation. A multicenter retrospective analysis indicated that, during difficult cholecystectomy performed under ICG guidance, the need for reintervention due to bile leakage or biliary stricture was lower than in control cohorts [32]. Taken together, observational data suggest that ICG may enhance intraoperative decision-making, shorten operative time, and improve biliary identification. Trends toward reduced downstream complications and reintervention burden have been reported but require confirmation in high-quality randomized controlled trials.

Shift to Objective Image-Guided Surgical Decision-Making

ICG also changes the way surgeons make intraoperative decisions. Traditional cholecystectomy relies heavily on the surgeon’s personal experience. Surgeons often make subjective decisions to achieve the CVS, which may be difficult to obtain reliably in the setting of severe inflammation. ICG fluorescence imaging improves decision models using objective real-time visualization. During interval cholecystectomy after PTGBD, residual fibrosis and edema still obscure tissue layers. Fluorescence clearly marks the junction of cystic and extrahepatic bile ducts, and surgeons can place clips effectively instead of performing blind dissection [10,11]. Image-informed, individual decision-making has been associated with improved safety. The TG18 guidelines recommend routinely considering ICG fluorescence navigation in patients with acute cholecystitis [3]. Preoperative intravenous administration within a specified window (ie, 2–6 hours before surgery with commonly used preparation) has been described as a standard approach, and injections in excess of 30 minutes before surgery may also allow adequate visualization in some settings [20]. With NIR imaging systems becoming available, ICG has become an optional part of precision-oriented biliary surgical workflows, signifying the transition to functional real-time image-guided biliary surgery.

Toward Standardized Operative Protocols for ICG Fluorescence Imaging

Individualized Preoperative Dosing and Timing

Dosage and injection timing affect intraoperative imaging quality. Clinicians should adjust these 2 factors according to liver function, body weight, and inflammation severity of each patient. We found intravenous injection of 10 to 25 g of ICG via a peripheral vein 30 minutes before surgery allowed the highest optimal visualization rate of biliary tract under 4K laparoscopy [23]. For standard high-definition laparoscopy, rather than 4K laparoscopy, a commonly used ICG protocol involves intravenous administration of 2.5 mg of ICG within 1 hour before surgery or fluorescence imaging [37]. In patients with borderline or overt hepatic impairment (eg, Child-Pugh class B or C), earlier administration (eg, 2–4 hours before surgery) and/or dose reduction may be considered to allow delayed ICG and excessive hepatic background fluorescence [19]. Pharmacokinetics-informed, individual preoperative planning can help to guide fluorescence navigation.

Integrated Intraoperative Operating Workflow

Effective fluorescence navigation depends on integration with a standard laparoscopic technique rather than isolated use. Surgeons start dissection under white light to expose tissue and identify basic anatomical landmarks. Fluorescence is switched on at key judgment stages. Examples include cystic duct identification and safe dissection planes on the gallbladder bed. Fluorescent signals provide functional evidence supporting surgical decisions [38]. A white-light-first approach preserves the surgeon’s conventional anatomical assessment, while fluorescence imaging supplements this process with objective physiological information to enhance surgical safety. In addition to duct mapping, intraoperative fluorescence imaging can help outline tissue planes and provide additional information on perfusion of the biliary tract and adjacent tissues, which may inform intraoperative decisions in some cases [30]. In difficult cholecystectomy with dense adhesions and poor anatomic definition, fluorescence should be used early and proactively. Before splitting the cystic duct, fluorescence must be confirmed along the cystic duct trajectory, and its relationship with the extrahepatic bile duct at the junction must be understood to achieve the CVS [31].

At the end of the operation, fluorescence imaging can serve as a final safety assessment after completion of the key dissection steps. It may also provide a reliable method for detecting occult bile leakage. Surgeons can evaluate 3 critical areas under fluorescence guidance: the gallbladder bed, the extrahepatic bile duct trunk, and Calot’s triangle. The team searches for abnormal fluorescent effusions on the surgical field. Local fluorescence pooling can indicate a small leak that is easily missed under white light alone. When detected intraoperatively, such leakage can prompt immediate, targeted repair, which could reduce postoperative bile-related complications and the need for reintervention [21,22]. In addition to cholecystectomy, fluorescence assessment is used to assess biliary structures on hepatic resection surfaces to support completeness and safety [21]. Therefore, incorporating an ICG-based biliary integrity check as a standard closing step (especially in acute cholecystitis surgery) may help consolidate a safety workflow and conform to precision surgery principles.

Future Directions

Artificial Intelligence–Assisted Intraoperative Interpretation

ICG fluorescence clearly describes the biliary structures during surgery. However, surgeons still depend on personal experience to judge fluorescent signals. Severe inflammation, large adhesions, and variants of anatomy increase the risk of incorrect image interpretation. Artificial intelligence (AI)-augmented fluorescence navigation could help the surgeon move from manual recognition to machine-assisted understanding. Deep learning systems such as U-Net and Mask R-CNN are very accurate in medical image segmentation of abdominal organs, including the liver and pancreas [39–41]. In the biliary area, early studies have applied AI models to intraoperative images to automatically detect and segment structures such as the cystic duct and CHD, comparable to expert judgment and providing real-time navigation support [42]. However, current approaches face several challenges. In particular, the reliable identification of key dissectible planes, such as loose connective tissue planes that guide safe separation, remains challenging in complex cases. Differentiating biliary structures under the most demanding conditions can also be difficult. However, early AI-augmented fluorescence navigation may reduce the likelihood of iatrogenic injury and provide standard anatomical references for trainees, potentially speeding the progress toward more precise and safer biliary surgery.

Multispectral Fluorescence Imaging for Intraoperative Tissue Differentiation

The surgical field of acute cholecystitis mixes bile leakage, inflammatory fluid, and necrotic tissue, which are similar dark shapes under white light or single NIR imaging. Surgeons cannot distinguish different tissues easily, which leads to incomplete cleaning and residual infections. Multispectral fluorescence imaging addresses this problem by collecting reflected and emitted signals over multiple narrow bands (eg, 5–8 channels in 700–900 nm) and applying spectral unmixing algorithms to obtain more component-specific discrimination. Mechanically, bile, because of bilirubin chromophores, exhibits characteristic absorption around 720 and 810 nm; exudate, which is mostly water, shows a significant increase of reflectance beyond 850 nm; and necrotic tissue may exhibit larger low-reflectance signatures, consistent with protein denaturation. Animal experiments using simulated bile-leak models reported a bile identification specificity of 91.5%, outperforming conventional single-band ICG imaging (73.2%) [43]. If integrated into existing laparoscopic platforms, multispectral fluorescence imaging could help surgeons visualize bile ducts and characterize surrounding pathology for better debridement decision-making.

Novel Targeted Fluorescent Molecular Probes

Traditional ICG relies on liver uptake and biliary excretion to produce signals. It represents only biliary excretion function rather than specific duct structure. Inflammation, liver damage, and biliary blockage weaken its image quality. Next-generation probes of biliary ducts may be based on nanocarrier platforms. These approaches may include encapsulation of ICG within poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles, surface conjugation with anti-epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) antibodies, or the development of pH-sensitive probes activated within the alkaline biliary environment (approximately pH 7.5–8.5) [45]. Near-infrared II (NIR-II; 1000–1700 nm) fluorescent nanoprobes may provide theoretical advantages such as deeper penetration and less scattering than NIR-I imaging. Such properties may improve visualizations for larger tissue thickness (eg, obesity or severe inflammation) or deeper extrahepatic duct segments difficult to distinguish with current approaches [46]. Most of these concepts are still under development, but their trajectory from “functional fluorescence” toward “molecularly targeted recognition” suggests a roadmap for precision navigation in biliary surgery.

Conclusions

ICG NIR fluorescence improves functional intraoperative visualization. It improves detection of the extrahepatic bile duct and decreases operation time. The clinical performance of ICG differs significantly among mild, moderate, and severe acute cholecystitis grouped by TG18 grading. Current evidence is based on retrospective studies, and there is no standardized perioperative administration of ICG. AI, multispectral imaging, and new fluorescent probes are expected to further refine this navigation. High-quality large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to validate the clinical benefits and standardization of ICG fluorescence imaging in acute cholecystitis. Most reviews address ICG without stratifying patients by severity of inflammation. In this review, we used the TG18 grading system to organize the relevant evidence and evaluate the advantages and limitations of ICG across different disease stages. This approach distinguishes our work from previous reviews and summaries. Overall, ICG represents a valuable tool that can improve the safety and precision of biliary surgery.
