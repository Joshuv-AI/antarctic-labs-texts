Probiotic-driven modifications in structural properties enhance protein digestibility and amino acid availability in yogurt
Authors: Caiziyu Yu, Shusen Li, Xiyu Qin, Julong Liu, Chenyuan Wang, Qianqian Huang, Erna Sun, Ziqiang Li, Qiruonan Shen, Nan Shang, Xiaoyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Probiotic fortification can remodel yogurt gel matrix, thereby modulating protein digestion and absorption, yet precise mechanisms and strain-specific effects remain insufficiently characterized. In this study, yogurts added with Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis MN-Gup (MN-Gup) and Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12 (BB-12) were compared with a non-probiotic yogurt (Ctrl) to elucidate structure-digestion-absorption relationships. Both probiotic yogurts increased amino acid levels in post-meal plasma. The addition of probiotics reduced the gel strength of yogurt and resulted in a more porous and loose microstructure. Consequently, probiotic yogurt exhibited accelerated protein hydrolysis, greater release of low-molecular peptides (<1000 Da), and an increase in free amino acids during digestion. MN-Gup typically showed stronger effects in protein hydrolysis and amino acid utilization compared to BB-12. These findings demonstrate that specific Bifidobacterium strains reshape the yogurt gel structure to improve protein digestibility and postprandial amino acid absorption, providing a theoretical basis for the development of functional probiotic yogurt.

Introduction

Probiotics, defined as viable microorganisms that boost the well-being of the consumer if taken in adequate amounts, function effectively as a primary starting culture and an additional functional ingredient in typical yogurt formulations to create probiotic yogurt (Li et al., 2025; Nyanzi et al., 2021). Probiotic yogurt is widely consumed for health-promoting properties, providing rich essential nutrients such as proteins, minerals, and lipids, while supporting the intestinal microbiota, immune function, gastrointestinal health, and reducing the risk of chronic diseases (Hasegawa & Bolling, 2023). Besides bringing health benefits, the addition of probiotics mediates protein changes in yogurt, potentially playing a key role in modulating the digestibility and bioavailability of yogurt proteins (Li et al., 2025). However, this modulation is highly strain-dependent, as different strains alter the microenvironment of the yogurt matrix in their own unique ways due to their specific metabolic activities (such as proteolytic capacity and extracellular polysaccharide production) (Hassan et al., 2003b; Li et al., 2025), resulting in distinct digestive outcomes. Therefore, understanding how different probiotics regulate the protein digestion and absorption process is critical for improving the nutritional value of yogurt.

Bifidobacteria represent a fundamental component of the human gut microbiota and are being widely utilized in the production of functional foods (Yousefvand et al., 2024). Although previous study have demonstrated the role of Lactobacillus in promoting protein digestion in yogurt (Bai et al., 2020), the specific impact of Bifidobacteria on protein digestion and absorption in yogurt remains insufficiently understood. Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis MN-Gup (MN-Gup) is a potential developing strain that has received increased interest from academia and industry in recent years. Previous investigations have shown that MN-Gup is capable of regulating gut microbiota composition and maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Moreover, yogurt supplemented with MN-Gup has shown preventive and therapeutic potential against obesity, highlighting its promise for the development of functional yogurts targeting specific health needs (Niu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). In contrast, Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12 (BB-12) is one of the most extensively documented probiotic Bifidobacterium strains worldwide. It has a long history of safe use in infant formula, dietary supplements, and fermented dairy products, and is characterized by strong acid and bile salt tolerance as well as good mucus adhesion capacity (Jungersen et al., 2014; Uttarwar et al., 2024). Beyond its technological advantages in improving yogurt texture and flavor, BB-12 has been clinically demonstrated to modulate gut microbiota balance and enhance host immune function. Taken together, MN-Gup and BB-12 represent two Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis strains with distinct origins and functional characteristics, providing an ideal comparative model to investigate how Bifidobacterium species influence yogurt protein digestion and nutritional outcomes.

The existing research has confirmed that the addition of probiotics can indeed change the physicochemical properties and structural characteristics of yogurt. Bai et al. (2020) found that probiotic Lactobacillus casei Zhang, as an auxiliary culture in stirred yogurt production, could enhance the gel structure of yogurt and improve rheological properties, texture, and other technical characteristics. It has also demonstrated that the addition of BB-12 and its derived postbiotic powders to yogurt improved rheological properties, water-holding capacity, and microstructural characteristics, thereby influencing the physicochemical properties and structural quality of the yogurt (Yousefvand et al., 2024). Furthermore, different structures of dairy products have shown significant differences in protein digestion and absorption. Lamothe et al. (2017) found that different processing methods had a significant impact on the composition parameters and physical characteristics of dairy matrix, thus affecting the release of nutrients in dairy products. Barbé et al. (2013) employed a mini pig model to investigate the influence of gelation upon milk protein digestion and absorption, and found that milk gelation decreased cumulative absorption by extending the time that milk was retained in the stomach and delayed the absorption of amino acids. For yogurt, the gel network structure formed in the fermentation process is an important factor affecting proteolysis and gastric emptying, especially the size, hardness and porosity of the particles, which affect the accessibility of protein to protease, thus further affecting the amino acids availability (Aryana & Olson, 2017).

Therefore, how the structural modifications are induced by Bifidobacterium, and then specifically translated into alterations in protein digestion and absorption characteristics has not been systematically elucidated.

Therefore, the present research sought to evaluate the effects of Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis MN-Gup and BB-12 on the structural characteristics of yogurt and to elucidate how these probiotic-driven structural changes influence protein digestion and amino acid availability. By combining in vivo mouse experiments with static in vitro digestion models, this work aims to provide mechanistic insight into the structure-digestion-absorption relationship of probiotic yogurt and offers a scientific basis for the rational design of yogurt with improved protein nutritional quality.

Materials and methods

Samples and reagents

Raw milk was initially homogenized at 55–60 °C under a pressure of 150–250 bar, followed by heat treatment at 95 °C for 300 s. After cooling to 42 °C, the sterilized milk was divided into three groups for fermentation. All groups were inoculated with commercially available yogurt strains containing Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus (International Flavors & Fragrances Inc., New York, NY, US). Two groups were co-inoculated with probiotics Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. Lactis MN-Gup (MN-Gup, which is from BaMa longevity village, Guangxi, China, CGMCC No. 15578) and BB-12, respectively. After fermentation, yogurts were stirred and cooled to 4 °C. The stirred yogurt with only the starter was the control group (Ctrl), while the yogurts with the additional two probiotics was the MN-Gup group (MN-Gup) and BB-12 group (BB-12), respectively. The probiotics in the products were counted, and the corresponding probiotic content in both probiotic yogurts was 5 × 109 CFU/100 g. In addition, all chemical reagents used in this study were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China), unless otherwise specified.

Determination of quality-related physicochemical parameters of yogurts

pH and acidity

The pH values were determined by a pen acidity meter at ambient temperature. According to the previous study (Aktar, 2022), after mixing 10 g of precisely weighed yogurt samples with 20 mL distilled water, acidity value was computed after titrating with a standard solution of 0.1 mol/L NaOH. Measurements were done in duplicate from triplicate samples.

Water-holding capacity(WHC)

According to the study by Senaka Ranadheera et al. (2012), the water holding capacity (WHC) of yogurt was determined by centrifugation of a 30 g yogurt sample. WHC was calculated as follows:WHC%=W−W0W1−W0×100%where: W = weight of the sediment after centrifugation and discarding the supernatant, W0 = weight of the empty centrifuge tube, and W1 = total weight of the yogurt sample added in centrifuge tube.

Particle size

Determinations of particle size in yogurt samples were carried out using a Mastersizer 3000 Laser Particle Size Analyzer (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK), following the method reported by Zou et al. (2025).

Texture profile

Yogurt samples were removed from the refrigerator and analyzed at room temperature. Textural attributes of the yogurt samples including firmness, springiness, adhesiveness, and cohesiveness were analyzed by a Brookfield CT3 texture analyzer (Brookfield Engineering Laboratories, MA, USA) equipped with TA5 probe and TA-RT-KIT fixture. The speed prior to, throughout, and following the test was 1.00 mm/s, with an Auto-10.0 g trigger force.

Rheological properties

The research of Jiao et al. (2023) was referenced and modified on this basis. The rheological properties of yogurt samples were measured using an AR1500 ex rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle). The changes in the apparent viscosity of yogurt samples with shear rates ranging from 0.1 to 100 s−1 were measured under static conditions at 25 °C. Frequency sweep tests were conducted at a temperature of 25 °C and a strain of 0.1% to determine the variations in the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G″) with increasing frequencies from 0.1 to 10 Hz.

Extraction and quantification of exopolysaccharides (EPS)

The extraction of EPS was performed based on the method described by Bai et al. (2020). Briefly, yogurt samples (100 mL) were mixed with 20% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged (10,000×g, 30 min, 4 °C) to precipitate caseins. The supernatant was neutralized to pH 6.8 with 4 M NaOH, boiled for 30 min, and centrifuged again to remove residual insoluble proteins. Subsequently, an equal volume of chilled absolute ethanol was added to the supernatant to precipitate EPS overnight at 4 °C. The collected EPS pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of sterile distilled water, sonicated for 2 h to ensure uniform dispersion, and dialyzed (8 kDa MWCO, Solarbio, Beijing, China) against distilled water for 72 h with daily water changes. Finally, the purified EPS was quantified using the phenol‑sulfuric acid method. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm, and the EPS content was expressed as glucose equivalents using a standard curve (10–400 mg/L).

Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6J mice aged six weeks (HFK Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were kept in a particular pathogen-free environment with a light/dark cycle of 12 h to 12 h. All the animal-based experiments were conducted following the guidelines of the Administration for Experimental Animal Affairs (Ministry of Science and Technology, China) and in accordance with the NIH (National Research Council) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental procedures were approved by Beijing Gushi Test Technology Co., LTD Laboratory Animal Welfare and Animal Experimental Ethical Inspection (GSCS-2025-025). After all mice were fasted for 16 h, three to five drops of orbital venous blood were collected using EDTA tubes after anesthetizing the mice. No additional harm was caused during the blood collection procedure, and all mice remained active after completion. After that, the animals underwent a 7-d adaptation period, animals were fasted overnight (16 h) and gavaged with different yogurt samples (0.01389 mL/g). Following a 2-h post-gavage period (enough time for completing whole intestinal digestion of proteins, reported by Song et al. (2024)), all mice were euthanized by gradual CO2 exposure (progressive filling at 30–70% chamber vol/min until breathing stopped, then 2–3 min of 100% CO2), after which cervical dislocation was performed. After euthanasia, blood samples and ileal chyme were collected. The ileum was perfused twice with 0.1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline containing protease inhibitor cocktail (P2714), and perfusates were collected in tubes (Wada et al., 2017). All samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

In vitro simulated digestion

In vitro simulation of yogurt gastrointestinal digestion was performed following the standardized INFOGEST protocol (Brodkorb et al., 2019). Prior to in vitro digestion, sample protein concentrations were standardized to 3.0 g/100 mL using deionized water. Gastric digestion was conducted by incubating the mixture of simulated gastric fluid (SGF) and yogurt at 37 °C for 120 min in a water bath. Enzymatic reactions were then terminated by adjusting the pH to 7.0 with 1 M NaOH, followed by heat inactivation in a boiling water bath for 5 min and immediate cooling on ice. Intestinal digestion was performed by incubating the mixture of simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) and gastric digesta at 37 °C for 120 min, and digestion was terminated by immersing the samples in a boiling water bath for 5 min followed by immediate ice cooling. Following digestion, the samples were separated into microcentrifuge tubes and kept at −80 °C until additional examination. Digestions were performed in triplicate.

Sample analysis

Amino acids determination

For animal samples, 20 μL of plasma or ileal perfusate was mixed with 80 μL of cold methanol for protein precipitation to extract free amino acids. For in vitro digestion samples, the free amino acids were extracted after freeze-dried and thorough grounded. All extraction methods were referenced from previous study (Qin, Yu, et al., 2025). Amino acid analysis was performed using an UPLC-TripleTOF 7600 (AB SCIEX, USA) equipped with a BEN Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μL, Waters, USA).

CLSM observation

The microstructure of yogurt and in vitro digested samples were visualized using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM, Zeiss LSM900, Oberkochen, Germany). The instrument parameters were set as follows: argon excitation wavelength 488 nm, helium‑neon excitation wavelength 633 nm, and observation through a 63× oil immersion lens. Fast Green aqueous solution (1 mg/mL) and Nile Red ethanol solution (1 mg/mL), stored at room temperature under light-shielding conditions, served as fluorescent dyes for proteins and lipids respectively. A total of 50 μL Fast Green was added to 1 mL of in vitro digestion samples or yogurt samples, thoroughly mixed, and then incubated for light avoidance over 5 min. Subsequently, Nile Red (5 μL) and 40 μL of stained sample were dropped onto a concave slide, then covered with cover glass for observation (Gantumur et al., 2024).

Protein hydrolysis

Protein hydrolysis was analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Protein extraction from digestion samples and SDS-PAGE experiment was performed as described in prior study (Qin, Yu, et al., 2025). The resulted gel was stained by Coomassie Brilliant Blue dyeing solution for 20 min on a shaking bed, followed by decolorization overnight (Krebs et al., 2021).

Quantification of free amino groups

To quantity the free amino groups released during in vitro digestion, the ortho-phthalaldehyde (OPA) method was utilized. The samples were pretreated with TCA. In brief, 5% TCA solution was added to 0.5 mL of the digested sample to precipitate proteins and longer peptides. The sample was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 30 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was filtered through a needle filter and a 4 mm, 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (GE Healthcare Life Science, UK). The resulting 10 μL supernatant was mixed with 200 μL freshly prepared OPA reagent (Rutherfurd, 2019). After a 15-min incubation in the dark, the absorbance values were measured at 340 nm. Results were calculated based on a serine standard curve, with TCA solution used as the blank control.

Peptidomics analysis

Approximately 20 mg of the lyophilized digests were subjected to defatting, protein removal, and desalting procedures, strictly following the protocol delineated by Qin, Wu, et al. (2025). The eluted sample was dried under a rotary evaporator, and then redissolved in 0.1% acetonitrile and centrifugally filtered through an Ultracel-3 membrane to remove peptides larger than 3 kDa. NanoLC separation was achieved with nanoAcquity nano HPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Detection and analysis methods were performed according to previous methods (Song et al., 2024). Bioactive peptides were searched against the MBPDB database (https://mbpdb.nws.oregonstate.edu/) (Nielsen et al., 2024). The peptide fingerprint was used for protein peptide sequence alignment analysis using Peptigram (http://bioware.ucd.ie/peptigram/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). One-way ANOVA was used to assess differences across groups, considering results with P < 0.05 as statistically significant. Post hoc multiple comparisons between groups were conducted using Tukey's test. Significance levels are indicated by *, **, ***, and ****, corresponding to P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.005, and P < 0.0001, respectively. Figures were produced with GraphPad Prism version 9.5, and the error bars in each figure represent the standard error of the means.

Results

Amino acids concentrations in plasma and ileal chyme

The contents of amino acids in plasma represent absorption efficiency of proteins in yogurt after digestion by the body. The composition and levels of plasma amino acids in mice were determined both before feeding and after gavage with yogurts containing different probiotics. It is noteworthy that the plasma amino acid levels in mice were considerably higher in the MN-Gup and BB-12 groups following yogurt consumption than in the Ctrl group (Fig. 1A). Additionally, a statistical analysis was performed on the amounts of total amino acids (Fig. 1B), essential amino acids (Fig. 1C), and branched-chain amino acids (Fig. 1D) in postprandial plasma of mice. According to the results, the MN-Gup and BB-12 groups had considerably higher levels of total, essential and branched-chain plasma amino acids than the Ctrl group. Specifically, the postprandial plasma total amino acid levels in the MN-Gup group and BB-12 group were 257.66 ± 8.95 μg/mL and 262.25 ± 6.52 μg/mL, respectively, representing increases of 23.23% and 25.32% compared to the Ctrl group (209.26 ± 6.36 μg/mL). The postprandial essential amino acid levels in the MN-Gup group (119.31 ± 2.49 μg/mL) and BB-12 group (123.50 ± 2.84 μg/mL) were 26.44% and 30.88% higher than those in the Ctrl group (94.36 ± 3.19 μg/mL), accordingly. The postprandial branched-chain amino acid levels in the MN-Gup group (20.15 ± 1.26 μg/mL) and BB-12 group (20.93 ± 1.14 μg/mL) were correspondingly 19.09% and 23.70% higher than those in the Ctrl group (16.92 ± 0.63 μg/mL).Fig. 1Heatmap depicting changes in levels of plasma amino acids pre- and post-meal in Ctrl, MN-Gup and BB-12 groups (A); (B), (C) and (D) showed concentrations of total amino acids, essential amino acids and branched-chain amino acids in plasma after yogurt intake; (E), (F) and (G) were the amounts of total, essential and branched-chain amino acids in ileal chyme. To corroborate the findings regarding plasma amino acid concentrations, the amounts of residual amino acids in ileal chyme of the mice were quantified. Following meals, the ileal chyme of mice in the MN-Gup and BB-12 groups had considerably less total amino acids (Fig. 1E), essential amino acids (Fig. 1F), and branched-chain amino acids (Fig. 1G) than the Ctrl group. This finding suggested a higher net absorption of amino acids since amino acids levels in the ileal chyme were comparatively lower following consumption of MN-Gup and BB-12 yogurt. In conclusion, both probiotics, MN-Gup and BB-12, promoted the amino acid absorption in yogurt.

Physicochemical characteristics of yogurts

The physicochemical characteristics of three varieties of yogurt were examined in order to investigate the causes of the variations in postprandial plasma amino acid levels in yogurt with various probiotics. There was no significant difference in the titratable acidity of the three yogurts, and all were within the appropriate range, indicating that their fermentation process and end-point acidity were consistent (Fig. 2A). After ruling out acidity as a dominant factor affecting the structure of yogurt, the impact of different probiotic fortifications on the structural characteristics of yogurt was further investigated. Fig. 2B showed the comparison of water-holding capacity of different yogurts. The water-holding capacity of MN-Gup yogurt was significantly higher than that of the Ctrl yogurt, suggesting that MN-Gup yogurt may have a more stable gel network. The particle size distribution of the three yogurts was bimodal, indicating the presence of particles of different sizes in the samples (Fig. 2C). The macroscopic structural characteristics such as texture and rheology of yogurt were further analyzed. Fig. 2E-H described the textural characterization of different yogurts. The springiness of MN-Gup and BB-12 yogurts was significantly higher than that of the Ctrl yogurt (Fig. 2F), and the cohesiveness of BB-12 yogurt was significantly higher than that of the other two yogurts, indicating that the intermolecular force in BB-12 yogurt was stronger than that in the Ctrl yogurt without probiotics and MN-Gup yogurt. No significant differences were detected in hardness or adhesiveness. All yogurts exhibited typical shear-thinning behavior (Fig. 2I), and the apparent viscosity of probiotic yogurts was lower than that of the Ctrl yogurt at the same shear rate. Dynamic rheological measurements showed that G′ exceeded G″ across the entire frequency range for all samples, confirming the dominance of elastic behavior. Although G′ and G″ values were comparable among samples, Ctrl yogurt generally exhibited higher moduli, whereas BB-12 yogurt showed lower values. Overall, probiotic fortification did not affect yogurt fermentation characteristics but significantly modulated yogurt structure at both micro- and macro-scales, including water-holding capacity, particle size, texture, and rheological behavior.

Furthermore, the EPS content in different probiotic yogurts was determined (Fig. 2K). The addition of probiotic strains significantly enhanced EPS production during fermentation. Specifically, the EPS concentrations in the MN-Gup (317.13 ± 4.55 mg/L) and BB-12 (281.45 ± 6.66 mg/L) groups were 2.41 and 2.14 times higher than that of the Ctrl group (131.65 ± 1.53 mg/L), respectively. Notably, the MN-Gup strain exhibited the strongest EPS-producing capability among the tested groups.

Microstructure changes of yogurts digestion

In order to further investigate the structural changes responsible for different plasma amino acid content following yogurt ingestion with different probiotics, the microstructures of three types of yogurt and their evolution during in vitro digestion were characterized by CLSM (Fig. 3). Before digestion, the MN-Gup and BB-12 yogurt exhibited a looser protein network structure compared to the Ctrl group, with more pores. During the initial stage of gastric digestion (G15), the protein aggregates in yogurt maintained a gel network structure. At this point, the Ctrl group's protein aggregates remained more clustered, while those in the MN-Gup group became more fragmented with a more open network structure. Subsequently, as the digestion time increased, the protein networks in each group of yogurt were gradually hydrolyzed, the protein matrix decreased, and the fat globules originally wrapped by the protein network gradually released into the aqueous phase. By the end of the in vitro simulated gastric digestion (G120), the aggregated protein network structure was hardly observed in the MN-Gup group, and the proteins were hydrolyzed into smaller fragmented curds during digestion, suggesting the formation of a relatively soft curd structure during gastric digestion, whereas larger and more compact aggregates were still observed in the Ctrl group. Although the curd structure of the BB-12 group was not as loose as that of the MN-Gup group, it was still significantly better than that of the Ctrl group. Protein hydrolysis of yogurts digestion

The hydrolysis rate and degree of protein in yogurt supplemented with different probiotics during in vitro digestion were further investigated to determine the specific effects of these probiotics on protein digestion. SDS–PAGE profiles (Fig. 4A–C) showed that, prior to digestion (G0), probiotic-supplemented yogurts exhibited weaker casein bands (25–35 kDa) and relatively stronger β-lactoglobulin (17 kDa) and α-lactalbumin (11 kDa) bands compared with the Ctrl group. This indicated the higher hydrolysis of casein in probiotic yogurt during fermentation. Additionally, the bands at 17 kDa and 11 kDa, apart from β-lactoglobulin and α-lactalbumin, also contained small molecular weight proteins resulting from partial casein hydrolysis, which explained the stronger intensity of these bands. With progressing gastric digestion, the intensity of all protein bands gradually decreased and completely disappeared after 15 min of intestinal digestion (I15). Notably, protein degradation occurred more rapidly in the MN-Gup group, where αS1- and αS2-casein bands were markedly weakened at 15 min of gastric digestion (G15) and were barely detectable at 120 min (G120), indicating accelerated digestion of αS1- and αS2-casein in the MN-Gup group. Although protein hydrolysis in the BB-12 group was less pronounced than in the MN-Gup group, its protein bands were substantially weaker than those of the Ctrl group at the end of gastric digestion. The release of free amino groups during digestion is shown in Fig. 4D. During gastric digestion (0–120 min), free amino group content increased gradually, with the MN-Gup group becoming significantly higher than the other groups after 90 min. Upon transition to the intestinal phase (120–240 min), all samples showed a sharp increase in free amino group release, which then approached a plateau. Throughout intestinal digestion, the MN-Gup group released the highest amount of free amino groups, followed by the BB-12 group, both remaining significantly higher than the Ctrl group. Ultimately, the contents of free amino groups released by MN-Gup yogurt after digestion was 18.68 ± 0.54 mmol/L, which was 1.35 times that of the Ctrl group (13.79 ± 0.04 mmol/L). And the contents of free amino groups released by BB-12 yogurt after digestion was 15.18 ± 0.23 mmol/L, which was 1.1 times that of the Ctrl group. These results indicate that MN-Gup yogurt achieved the highest degree of protein hydrolysis during digestion, while BB-12 yogurt also enhanced protein hydrolysis, particularly during the intestinal phase.

Peptidomic analysis

The peptides released from yogurt supplemented with different probiotics after in vitro gastrointestinal digestion were analyzed to characterize the protein digestion in yogurt. The types and abundances of peptides in digestion samples from the Ctrl, MN-Gup, and BB-12 groups were further analyzed. A total of 1015 peptides were detected in the Ctrl group, 1085 in the MN-Gup group, and 1078 in the BB-12 group (Fig. 5A). The MN-Gup group contained 11 unique peptides, while the BB-12 group had 5. The bioactivity of these unique peptides in the digestion samples were identified. Among them, ALKALPMHIR, derived from β-lactoglobulin, was exclusively found in the MN-Gup group and exhibited cell growth-promoting activity (Jacquot et al., 2010). In the BB-12 group, FQSEEQQQTEDELQDK was identified as a β-lactoglobulin-derived bioactive peptide with potential to enhance calcium uptake (Cao et al., 2017). PCA analysis revealed a clear separation between the MN-Gup and Ctrl groups, indicating distinct peptide release patterns (Fig. 5B). Although the total ion intensities of peptides did not differ significantly among the three groups after gastrointestinal digestion (Fig. 5C), probiotic supplementation markedly altered peptide size distribution. The proportion of small molecular peptides (<1000 Da) was significantly higher in the MN-Gup (36.10%) and BB-12 (35.20%) groups than in the Ctrl group (26.37%). Differential peptide analysis further demonstrated pronounced differences in peptide abundance among groups (Fig. 5D-F), confirming that probiotic addition significantly modulated peptide release during digestion. The volcano plot showed that, compared with the Ctrl group, 123 peptides exhibited lower ion intensities and 155 peptides exhibited higher ion intensities in the MN-Gup group (Fig. 5D). Compared with the Ctrl group, 106 peptides showed lower ion intensities and 141 peptides showed higher ion intensities in the BB-12 group (Fig. 5E). Analysis of peptide origin revealed that yogurts supplemented with probiotics generated a greater number and higher abundance of small peptides derived from major milk proteins, particularly αS1-casein, αS2-casein, κ-casein, and β-casein (Fig. 5G–H). The number of small peptides derived from αS1-casein and κ-casein in the digestion samples of the MN-Gup and BB-12 groups was significantly higher than that in the Ctrl group, and the number of small molecular peptides derived from αs2-casein in the digestion samples of the MN-Gup group was significantly higher than that in the Ctrl group. Furthermore, the MN-Gup and BB-12 groups' digestion samples had substantially greater ion intensities of small molecular peptides produced from β-casein and αS1-casein than the Ctrl group. Overall, peptides release during digestion was promoted in the two groups of yogurt supplemented with probiotics, and the MN-Gup group performed better.

Discussions

To clarify the impact of adding two probiotic strains, Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis MN-Gup and BB-12, during fermentation on the protein nutritional value of yogurt, a systematic approach was adopted, employing both an in vivo and a static in vitro digestion model to investigate the protein digestion and absorption in yogurt supplemented with different strains. The results demonstrated that fortifying yogurt with probiotics MN-Gup and BB-12 significantly enhanced protein digestibility and postprandial amino acid absorption. Our findings highlighted a structure-mediated mechanism, whereby probiotics actively modulate the yogurt gel network, leading to improved nutritional outcomes.

The addition of Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis MN-Gup and BB-12 significantly enhanced the postprandial absorption of amino acids in yogurt. The nutritional quality of dietary proteins is widely recognized to depend on the efficiency of amino acid absorption after digestion (Liu et al., 2022), and postprandial plasma amino acid levels are commonly used as an indicator of protein digestion and absorption (Lee et al., 2024). Based on previous evidence showing that mice complete protein digestion and absorption within approximately 2 h (Song et al., 2024), plasma amino acids were quantified at this time point. It was found that the total amino acids and essential amino acids levels in the plasma of mice fed with two types of probiotic yogurt were significantly higher compared to those in the control yogurt group. To further support these findings, amino acid contents in ileal chyme were analyzed. Reduced amino acid retention in the ileal chyme of probiotic yogurt–fed mice indicated enhanced net absorption in the upper gastrointestinal tract (Qin, Wu, et al., 2025). Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that proteins in probiotic-fortified yogurts were absorbed more rapidly and efficiently. Notably, the improved absorption of branched-chain amino acids is particularly relevant, given their critical roles in muscle protein synthesis and energy metabolism (Arciero et al., 2015).

The alterations in the structural characteristics of yogurt induced by probiotics provide a fundamental basis for changes in protein digestion and absorption. The initial gel texture of yogurt directly determines its disintegration behavior in the stomach. Highly elastic gels often exhibit ″weak gel″ characteristics, combining elasticity with moderate fluidity, and are therefore more susceptible to gradual dissociation under gastric acid conditions and require less mechanical force for breakdown than hard and brittle gels with low cohesiveness (Faridah et al., 2023; Gan et al., 2022; Khin et al., 2021). Texture and rheological analyses showed that yogurt supplemented with MN-Gup exhibited higher springiness and consistently lower G′ and G″ values than the Ctrl group, while BB-12–supplemented yogurt also displayed increased elasticity, reduced viscoelastic moduli, and the highest cohesiveness. These changes indicate a softer gel with weakened intermolecular interactions in probiotic yogurts. Such structural modifications are likely associated with probiotic-driven remodeling of the yogurt gel microstructure during fermentation. Both MN-Gup and BB-12 strains significantly increased the EPS content in yogurt, with the MN-Gup group exhibiting the highest EPS levels. This finding corroborates previous studies indicating that Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis strains can produce extracellular polysaccharides during fermentation (Prasanna et al., 2014; Sadeghi et al., 2024; Salazar et al., 2009), which could interfere with casein–casein interactions by associating with casein micelles or dispersing in the serum phase, thereby limiting the formation of a dense protein network (Amatayakul et al., 2006; Hassan et al., 2003a). Although this limitation effects slightly reduced the macroscopic mechanical strength, the abundant EPS acted as a hydrophilic filler in the yogurt matrix, which explained enhanced WHC in MN-Gup yogurt and increased average particle size in both probiotic yogurts. Consistent with this finding, CLSM observations revealed that probiotic yogurts exhibited a more loosely packed and porous protein network compared with the Ctrl yogurt. Given that protein digestion is strongly influenced by food matrix structure, such porous networks are expected to facilitate enzyme diffusion and hydrolytic reactions. Accordingly, the addition of MN-Gup and BB-12, particularly MN-Gup, resulted in a softer and more open gel structure, which may promote gastric hydrolysis and subsequently enhance protein release and absorption.

The probiotic-induced modifications in yogurt microstructure further affected protein digestion behavior, ultimately leading to differences in amino acid release and absorption. Once yogurt forms an acid-induced gel network, it does not undergo additional gastric coagulation; therefore, its initial gel structure largely determines gastric digestion kinetics (Horstman & Huppertz, 2023; Sumi et al., 2023). Structural characteristics such as mechanical strength and network porosity directly influence the accessibility of proteolytic enzymes to the protein matrix. Yogurt supplemented with MN-Gup exhibited typical weak-gel characteristics, including higher springiness and lower G′ and G″ values, indicating a looser and more porous protein network. This structure was more susceptible to disruption under gastric conditions, facilitating pepsin penetration and exposure of cleavage sites (Ye, 2021; Ye et al., 2019), and thereby accelerating protein hydrolysis during the gastric phase. Similarly, BB-12–supplemented yogurt also showed enhanced protein hydrolysis compared with the control, which can be attributed to its more digestible gel network. In addition, probiotics exerted a pre-hydrolysis effect on milk proteins during fermentation (Hadjimbei et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2020), promoting subsequent gastric proteolysis and the generation of small peptides and free amino acids. Certain strains of Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis can produce proline-specific peptidases, helping compensate for the limited ability of conventional digestive enzymes to cleave peptide bonds adjacent to proline, which is the second most abundant amino acid in casein (Savijoki et al., 2006). Accordingly, probiotic yogurts released a higher abundance of low-molecular-weight peptides (<1000 Da), consistent with previous reports that co-cultured fermented milk produces more small peptides after digestion (Wang et al., 2023). These peptides are nutritionally relevant, as dipeptides and tripeptides are absorbed more efficiently via the PepT1 transporter than free amino acids (Adibi & Morse, 1971; Daniel, 2004). Furthermore, the degree of protein hydrolysis and amino acid release in the MN-Gup and BB-12 groups was higher than that in the control, increasing the availability of amino acids for intestinal absorption and participation in physiological processes such as protein synthesis (Arciero et al., 2015). Overall, the looser microstructure of yogurt fortified with MN-Gup and BB-12 facilitated protein hydrolysis and enhanced the generation of absorbable peptides and amino acids, in agreement with the in vivo observations.

Although this study offers significant insights into the structure-digestion-absorption relationship of probiotic yogurt, several limitations should be acknowledged. The Fundamental mechanisms of proteolysis and intestinal amino acid transport systems are highly conserved across mammals, so the mouse model can effectively demonstrated differences in protein digestion and postprandial amino acid absorption (Bröer, 2008). However, due to the structural differences in the gastrointestinal tract between mice and humans can lead to variations in gastric emptying and transit times, inherent physiological and anatomical disparities limit the direct extrapolation of these results to human nutrition (Stamatopoulos et al., 2021). Therefore, rigorous clinical trials are required to validate the translational relevance of these findings. Furthermore, as the in vivo experiment in the present study was based on an acute single-dose administration protocol, the bioavailability of amino acids following long-term feeding was not evaluated. Future studies are warranted to further investigate the dynamic interactions between long-term probiotic yogurt intake and continuous amino acid metabolism. Finally, from a microbiological perspective, as the precise genetic determinants driving the strain-specific metabolic differences of MN-Gup and BB-12, such as specific EPS biosynthesis gene clusters, remain uncharacterized, future multi-omics investigations are necessary to definitively unravel the molecular basis of these functional benefits.

Conclusions

In the present study, by establishing an in vivo mouse model combined with a static in vitro digestion model, the effects of supplementing yogurt with MN-Gup and BB-12 on yogurt structure, protein digestion, and amino acid absorption were systematically investigated. The results demonstrated that both probiotics reduced yogurt gel strength and increased porosity in the microstructure, while inducing pre-hydrolysis of proteins to promote digestion into low molecular peptides and amino acids. This process enhanced the release of amino acids into the bloodstream, with MN-Gup showing superior efficacy in these aspects. These findings highlight the advantages of probiotic yogurt with specific health benefits in protein digestion and absorption, providing valuable insights for probiotic strain screening and functional yogurt development. In order to further enhance product quality and usefulness, subsequent studies should focus more on examining how specific probiotics influence milk proteins structure in complex food matrices.
