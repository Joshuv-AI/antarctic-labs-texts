Abstract
. Determining the asymptotic independence ratio of random regular graphs is a key challenge in the area of sparse random graphs. Due to the interpolation method, very good upper bounds are known, which are actually known to be sharp for sufficiently large degrees. This paper provides explicit lower bounds for given degrees d d , beating the previous best bounds for d ≥ 10 d\geq 10 . The starting point is a second moment argument that we can boost by arguing that the obtained independent set has a certain spatial Markov property. One can then exploit this property by making local modifications to the independent set, resulting in substantial improvements. Our approach can also be used to prove the existence of other objects in random regular graphs. To demonstrate this, we consider the problem of decomposing random regular graphs into stars.

Introduction

This paper is concerned with random regular graphs: let
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
be a uniform random graph among all
d

\{1,\ldots,N\}

1.1.
Labelings and entropy

Suppose that
\mathcal{X}
is a finite set of labels. Given a
d
-regular graph
G
, an
\mathcal{X}
-labeling of
G
is a mapping
\ell\colon V(G)\to\mathcal{X}
. If
G
is finite, one can consider the local statistics of a labeling. For instance, we may define the
edge statistics
of
\ell
as the distribution
\pi
of the pair
\big(\ell(u),\ell(v)\big)
, where
uv
is a uniform random (directed) edge of
G
. Note that

\pi
is an exchangeable distribution on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
(i.e., it is invariant under exchanging the two coordinates). In particular, the two marginals of
\pi
coincide; let us denote this marginal distribution by
\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}}
. (Since
G
is regular,
\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}}
is simply the distribution of
\ell(v)
for a uniform random vertex
v
.) We denote the probabilities defining
\pi
and
\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}}
as follows:

\displaystyle p_{x,y}

\displaystyle=p_{y,x}=\pi\big(\{(x,y)\}\big);

\displaystyle p_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}p_{x,y}=\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}}\big(\{x\}\big).

Later on we will need the
Shannon entropy
of
\pi
and
\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}}
defined as follows:

(2)

H(\pi)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}h\big(p_{x,y}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad H(\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}h\big(p_{x}\big),\\
\text{where }h(x)=\begin{cases}-x\log x&\text{if }x\in(0,1];\\
0&\text{if }x=0.\end{cases}

Many fundamental problems in graph theory can be phrased as questions about whether a graph has a labeling with certain edge statistics. For instance, the independence ratio of
G
is at least
\alpha
if and only if
G
has
\{0,1\}
-labeling with edge statistics

p_{1,1}=0\quad\text{and}\quad p_{0,1}=p_{1,0}\geq\alpha.

Recall that
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
denotes the uniform random graph among all
d
-regular simple graphs on the vertex set
\{1,\ldots,N\}
. This is closely related to the random multigraph
\mathbb{G}^{\textrm{conf}}_{N,d}
produced by the so-called
configuration model
.
The exponential rate of the expected number of labelings of
\mathbb{G}^{\textrm{conf}}_{N,d}
with edge statistics close to a given
\pi
can be seen to be equal to

\Sigma(\pi)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{d}{2}H(\pi)-(d-1)H(\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}}).

A second moment argument (supplemented by an Azuma-type concentration result) gives the following result.

Claim
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}}
denote the set of exchangeable distributions on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
. We say that
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies the
second moment condition
if

(3)

\Sigma(\nu)\leq 2\Sigma(\pi)\text{ for every coupling $\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}$ of $\pi$ and $\pi$.}

If
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies the second moment condition (
3
), then, for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. has an
\mathcal{X}
-labeling with edge statistics
\varepsilon
-close to
\pi
.

Next we explain how one can boost this result by considering larger neighborhood statistics. We start with defining the notion of
tree-indexed Markov chains
. Let
T_{d}
denote the
d
-regular infinite tree. A
T_{d}
-indexed Markov chain is a random labeling
X\colon V(T_{d})\to\mathcal{X}
with the property that, conditioned on the label
X_{v}
of a vertex
v\in V(T_{d})
,
X
is conditionally independent on the
d
subtrees obtained after deleting
v
. (Similar properties are often referred to as
spatial Markov properties
, while the authors of
[
1
]
call such random labelings
vertex-Markov processes
.) It is easy to see that the distribution
\mu
of
X
is uniquely determined by its marginal on two neighboring vertices, which can be considered as some
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}}
. For
R\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu_{R}
denote the marginal of
\mu
corresponding to an
R
-ball of
T_{d}
. It is not hard to show that the following stronger version of the second moment method also holds true; see
[
1
, Thm 1.15]
.

Claim
.

Let
\mu
be a
T_{d}
-indexed Markov chain with edge marginal
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}}
. If
\pi
satisfies the second moment condition (
3
), then, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
R\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. has an
\mathcal{X}
-labeling with
R
-neighborhood statistics
\varepsilon
-close to
\mu_{R}
.

In essence, the second moment condition guarantees the existence of a labeling that satisfies the spatial Markov property in the sense of local statistics. Therefore, we know what our labeling looks like in larger neighborhoods. Then we can use (randomized) local algorithms to produce new labelings: the new label of a vertex
v
is computed based on the original labels in a neighborhood of
v
. The point is that we can compute the local statistics of the new labeling using the large-neighborhood statistics of the original one. Next we demonstrate this approach in the context of independent sets.

1.2.
Independent sets

We already pointed out that an independent set can be thought of as a
\{0,1\}
-labeling with no adjacent
1
’s. So in the edge statistics of the labeling we have
p_{1,1}=0
, while
p_{0,1}=p_{1,0}=p_{1}
is the density of the independent set that we usually denote by
\alpha
, and hence
p_{0,0}=1-2\alpha
.

Using the method outlined in Section
1.1
, we will find a “Markovian independent set” with some density
\alpha
. Then one can design local algorithms that produce a larger independent set with density
\hat{\alpha}>\alpha
. In this paper we use a very basic algorithm. We call a vertex
full-zero
if its label is
0
and the labels of all its neighbors are also
0
. Consider the induced subgraph over the full-zero vertices. We will see that the connected components are finite with probability
1
. Consequently, one may choose an independent set inside each finite component, and add these to our original independent set. It follows that the original Markovian independent set can be augmented by all one-vertex components and at least half of the remaining full-zero vertices. Since our starting independent set is Markovian, we can easily work out the density
\hat{\alpha}
of our new independent set by computing the probability that a vertex is full-zero and the probability that it is an isolated full-zero vertex, see Section
4
for details.

One could, of course, consider more sophisticated algorithms to augment the Markovian independent set, resulting in a slightly higher density. However, the improvements would be minor, so we decided to stick with this basic method.

1.3.
Further applications

To summarize, our approach is to use a stronger version of the second moment method to find some “typical” Markovian labeling, then perform certain local modifications. Note that the new (possibly non-Markovian) labeling may
be a different “type of object”
than the original Markovian labeling. In particular, we can start with the same Markovian independent set and create other objects, and hence proving the existence of more complex structures in random regular graphs. (For instance, suppose that we want to find large induced subgraphs with degree bound, say,
2
. It is not hard to design a local algorithm that produces such a subgraph from an independent set.)

In this paper we present one such application, concerning so-called
star decompositions
. In Section
5
we demonstrate how our approach can be used to prove that, with high probability, the edge set of a random
d
-regular graph can be decomposed into
k
-stars for certain pairs
d,k
.

Organization of the paper

Section
2
gathers the necessary facts about invariant and typical processes on the infinite tree. In Section
3
we examine the second moment condition for independent sets, while in Section
4
we discuss how the Markovian independent set can be augmented. Finally, Section
5
is concerned with star decompositions.

2.
Typical processes

When studying labelings or colorings of random regular graphs, a useful tool is to study processes on the limiting graph, which is the
d
-regular infinite tree
T_{d}
. It is well known that
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
converges to
T_{d}
locally (Benjamini–Schramm convergence) as
N\to\infty
. This is due to the fact that
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
is essentially large-girth with high probability: for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
the number of cycles of length at most
\ell
is
o(N)
.

Processes

By an invariant process on
T_{d}
we mean a random coloring/labeling, the distribution of which is invariant under the automorphisms of
T_{d}
. The so-called

.

Suppose that

2.2.
Entropy

Given an exchangeable distribution
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, we write
\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}}
for its marginal on any of the two coordinates, and define

(4)

\Sigma(\pi)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{d}{2}H(\pi)-(d-1)H(\pi_{\mathrm{vtx}}).

See (
2
) for how Shannon entropy
H(\cdot)
is defined. The motivation for this definition is that
\Sigma(\pi)
gives the exponential rate of the expected number of
\mathcal{X}
-labelings of
\mathbb{G}^{\textrm{conf}}_{N,d}
with edge statistics close to
\pi
. In particular, if
\Sigma(\pi)<0
, then
\mathbb{G}^{\textrm{conf}}_{N,d}
may have such a labeling only with exponentially small probability (in
N
). This fact is what we usually refer to as the
first moment method
in random regular graphs.

When we wish to compute (the exponential rate of) the second moment of the number of such labelings, then we need to determine the expected number of pairs of labelings (one red and one blue), and hence consider
\Sigma(\nu)
for distributions
\nu
over

\mathcal{X}^{4}=\big({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathcal{X}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{X}}\big)\times\big({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathcal{X}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{X}}\big).

We will use the following stronger form of the
second moment method
.

Lemma 2.3

(Backhausz–Bordenave–Szegedy, Thm 1.15 of
[
1
]
)
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a tree-indexed Markov chain on
T_{d}
with the property that for any coupling
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
of
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}}
we have

(5)

\Sigma(\nu)\leq 2\Sigma(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}).

Then
\mu
is (strongly) typical.

Remark 2.4
.

We have to be a bit careful how we think of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
. Given labels
i,j,k,\ell\in\mathcal{X}
, they have the following roles when we write
({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}i},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}j},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}k},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ell})\in\mathcal{X}^{4}
:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}i}

\displaystyle:\quad\text{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}red} label of the {first} vertex;}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}j}

\displaystyle:\quad\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}blue} label of the {first} vertex;}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}k}

\displaystyle:\quad\text{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}red} label of the {second} vertex;}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ell}

\displaystyle:\quad\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}blue} label of the {second} vertex.}

We need to consider distributions
\nu
over
\mathcal{X}^{4}
that is invariant under exchanging the first and second vertex
5
5

5

Note that
\nu
does not have to be invariant under exchanging the two colors.
and its marginal for any color is
\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}
. We will use the notation

p_{ij,k\ell}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\nu\big(\{(i,j,k,\ell)\}\big).

Note that the comma separates the labels of the first and second vertex of the edge. With this notation, the property that
\nu
is exchangeable can be expressed as

(6)

p_{ij,k\ell}=p_{k\ell,ij}\quad(\forall i,j,k,\ell\in\mathcal{X}).

As for the condition on the marginals, we have

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{j,\ell\in\mathcal{X}}p_{ij,k\ell}

\displaystyle=p_{i,k}\quad(\forall i,k\in\mathcal{X});

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,k\in\mathcal{X}}p_{ij,k\ell}

\displaystyle=p_{j,\ell}\quad(\forall j,\ell\in\mathcal{X}).

Remark 2.5
.

Consider the independent coupling
\nu^{\star}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}\times\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}
for which
p_{ij,k\ell}=p_{i,k}p_{j,\ell}
(that is, the red and blue labels are independent). We clearly have

\Sigma(\nu^{\star})=2\Sigma(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}).

Therefore, the second moment condition (
5
) can also be written as
\Sigma(\nu)\leq\Sigma(\nu^{\star}),\forall\nu
.

Note that if we choose
\nu
to be the distribution of two identical copies of
\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}
(that is, the blue and red labels are always the same), then
\Sigma(\nu)=\Sigma(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}})
. Therefore, if the second moment condition holds, then we necessarily have the “first moment condition”
\Sigma(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}})\geq 0
.

2.3.
Factors

As we mentioned in the introduction, many previous lower bounds on
\alpha^{\star}_{d}
used
(randomized) local algorithms
to construct independent sets. These algorithms can be applied to any graphs of essentially large girth, not only to random regular graphs. In the language of graph limits, such local algorithms are equivalent to the so-called
FIID (factor of IID) processes
. See
[
2
]
for the precise definition and some basic facts about these processes.

It is easy to see that FIID processes are (strongly) typical. In fact, one can start from a typical process and apply a factor mapping; the resulting process is always typical. More specifically, suppose that
X\colon V(T_{d})\to\mathcal{X}
is an invariant process on
T_{d}
, and let
Z\colon V(T_{d})\to[0,1]
be an IID process (also independent from
X
). In this context, by a factor mapping we mean an
\Aut(T_{d})
-equivariant measurable mapping
\Psi\colon\big(\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\big)^{V(T_{d})}\to\mathcal{Y}^{V(T_{d})}
. Clearly,
Y\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\Psi(X,Z)\colon V(T_{d})\to\mathcal{Y}
is also invariant under
\Aut(T_{d})
. Moreover, it is an easy fact that if
X
is typical, then so is
Y
.

Essentially, it suffices to consider
block factors
, where the new label of a vertex
v
is determined by the original labels on
B_{R}(v)
. In essence,
Y_{v}
is computed based on the original labels
X_{u}
and the IID labels
Z_{u}
for
u\in B_{R}(v)
, and each vertex has to use the same measurable “rule”.

In our setting, we will start from some typical Markovian labeling
X_{v}
, and obtain
Y_{v}
using a fixed local algorithm based on the
X
-labels around
v
, possibly using some independent random choices along the way. The resulting labeling is, therefore, also typical.

3.
Second moment for independent sets

In this section we apply the machinery presented in Section
2
to independent sets.

Definition 3.1
.

We define an
invariant independent set
as an invariant process on
T_{d}
such that each label is
0
or
1
with no adjacent
1
’s. If, in addition, the process has the Markov property (see Definition

4.
Augmented independent sets

In this section we discuss the local improvements that one can make to an independent set with the spatial Markov property. Let us start from a Markovian independent set of density
\alpha
(see Definition
3.1
). If we have two neighboring vertices, then the conditional probability that one has label
0
conditioned on the other having label
0
is equal to

p\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1-2\alpha}{1-\alpha}.

In the introduction we defined a
full-zero vertex
as a vertex whose label, as well as all its neighbors’ labels, are
0
. Clearly, the probability that a vertex is full-zero is

(11)

(1-\alpha)p^{d}.

Furthermore, the probability that a vertex is an isolated full-zero vertex (that is, a full-zero vertex with no full-zero neighbor) is

(12)

(1-\alpha)p^{d}\big(1-p^{d-1}\big)^{d}.

Now consider the induced subgraph over all full-zero vertices. We will need that the connected components of this subgraph are finite (almost surely). Note that the conditional probability that a given neighbor of a full-zero vertex is also full-zero is equal to
p^{d-1}
. Therefore, if the condition

p^{d-1}\leq\frac{1}{d-1}

holds, then the corresponding Galton–Watson process dies out almost surely, and hence we have finite components. This condition is always comfortably satisfied whenever
\alpha
satisfies the second moment condition. Therefore, we may assume that the components are finite.

It is easy to design a randomized local algorithm that selects an independent set inside each finite component with size at least half of the component.
6
6

6

The components are trees and hence bipartite. Take a “canonical” proper 2-coloring and choose the larger color class. If they have the same size, choose the one in which the sum of IID labels is larger.
Let us add these to the original independent set. Of course, the resulting set remains to be independent. What can we say about its density? We augmented the original set with all the one-vertex components (i.e., isolated full-zero vertices) and at least half of the remaining full-zero vertices. Therefore, using (
11
) and (
12
), the new density is at least

(13)

\hat{\alpha}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\alpha+(1-\alpha)p^{d}\frac{1+\big(1-p^{d-1}\big)^{d}}{2}.

We summarize our findings in the next theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
be an integer and
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, and set
p=(1-2\alpha)/(1-\alpha)
. Suppose that the function
f_{d,\alpha}\colon[0,\alpha]\to\mathbb{R}
attains its maximum at
\alpha^{2}
, and
(d-1)p^{d-1}\leq 1
. Then there exists a typical independent set on

with density
\hat{\alpha}
as in (

5.
Star decompositions

To further demonstrate the strength of our approach, in this section we consider the following problem.
Given an integer
d/2<k<d
, can the edges of
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
be partitioned into edge-disjoint stars, each containing
k
edges? We restrict ourselves to those
N
for which the number of edges (
Nd/2
) is divisible by
k
. If such a partition exists with probability
1-o_{N}(1)
, then we say that
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.) has a
k
-star decomposition.

The case
d=4,k=3
was settled (affirmatively) in
[
7
]
. Later, that method was extended in
[
6
]
, and a sufficient condition was given for general pairs
d,k
. Both papers used a second moment method and checking the required conditions lead to very technical computations.

The problem is closely related to the asymptotic independence ratio
\alpha^{\star}_{d}
of
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
. More specifically, if
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. has a
k
-star decomposition, then

\alpha^{\star}_{d}\geq 1-\frac{d}{2k}\text{, or equivalently: }k\leq\frac{d}{2(1-\alpha^{\star}_{d})}.

This gives a necessary condition for the existence of a
k
-star decomposition. It was conjectured in
[
6
]
that this is essentially the only requirement. This was confirmed for most degrees (in an asymptotic sense) in
[
13
]
. In that paper we showed that the existence of large independent sets with an additional property (called
thinness
) actually implies the existence of star decompositions provided that the graph has some properties regarding the edge density of its induced subgraphs. We will use this general result here. First we define the notion of thin sets in graphs.

Definition 5.1
.

Given a positive integer
\hat{d}<d
, we say that a set
A\subset V(G)
is
\hat{d}
-thin in
G
if every
v\in V(G)\setminus A
has at most
\hat{d}
neighbors in
A
.

Then we have the following sufficient condition for the existence of a
k
-star decomposition.

Lemma 5.2

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular graph on
N
vertices and let
k>d/2
. Set

\alpha_{d,k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=1-\frac{d}{2k}.

For
U\subseteq V(G)
let
e[U]
denote the number of edges of
G
inside
U

A\subset V(G)
satisfies the following conditions for a positive integer
\hat{d}<k
and a real number
0<c<1
.

(i)

A

\alpha_{d,k}

(ii)

A

(iii)

For any set

(iv)

For any set

e[W]\leq(k-\hat{d})|W|.

Then

If we choose the parameters properly, then conditions (iii) and (iv) actually hold true a.a.s. for random regular graphs. In fact, one can simply check the numerical condition of

13

to check (iii) and (iv) in specific cases. We omit the details here as this is not the main focus of the current paper. Instead, we focus on the first two conditions.

To find a set
A
satisfying (i) and (ii), we start from a Markovian independent set of density
\alpha

p\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(1-2\alpha)/(1-\alpha)
is the conditional probability that a vertex has label

\binom{d}{\ell}(1-p)^{\ell}p^{d-\ell}.

For any vertex

(1-\alpha)\sum_{\ell=\hat{d}+1}^{d}(\ell-\hat{d})\binom{d}{\ell}(1-p)^{\ell}p^{d-\ell}.

Therefore, we have proved the following.
