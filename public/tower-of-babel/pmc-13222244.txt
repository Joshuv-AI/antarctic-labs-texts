EIT-Guided Personalized PEEP Improves Postoperative Lung Ultrasound Scores in Patients Undergoing Metabolic Bariatric Surgery: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Qi Xia, Yanan Yan, Rong Yu, Yurong Liu, Qi Wang, Zhichao Xu, Zhiping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are prevalent after metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) (Ann Surg 242:326–341, 2005, Best Pract Res Clin Anaesthesiol 24:211-25, 2010). Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is a cornerstone of lung-protective ventilation (Anesthesiology 123:66–78, 2015), but the optimal approach for titrating PEEP in patients with obesity is unknown (Trials 18:202, 2017). Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) enables individualized, real-time PEEP selection (Intensive Care Med 35:1132-7, 2009, Thorax 72:83–93, 2017). However, the influence of an EIT-based personalized PEEP strategy on early postoperative lung aeration, as quantified by the lung ultrasound score (LUS), remains incompletely characterized.MethodsIn this single-center, randomized controlled trial, we enrolled 120 patients scheduled for MBS. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either EIT-guided personalized PEEP (n = 60) or standard PEEP of 5 cmH2O (n = 60). The primary outcome was the change in LUS score from preoperative baseline (24 h prior to surgery) to post-extubation assessment (30 min following extubation, prior to PACU discharge). Secondary outcomes included respiratory mechanics intraoperatively, oxygenation index, and PPCS incidence within 48 h.ResultsBaseline characteristics, including preoperative oxygenation index, were well-balanced between the two groups (all P > 0.05). The median [IQR] PEEP level in the experimental group was 10 (Anesthesiology 111:979-87, 2009, Br J Anaesth 114:483–90, 2015) cmH2O. The EIT-guided group showed a greater improvement in LUS score compared with the fixed PEEP group (1.68 ± 1.02 vs. 1.25 ± 1.01; between-group mean difference: 0.43; 95% CI, 0.06 to 0.80; t = 2.162; P = 0.033). Furthermore, the EIT-guided group demonstrated a higher postoperative oxygenation index (407.58 ± 25.68 vs. 393.15 ± 30.21 mmHg; t = 2.820; P = 0.006) and greater dynamic lung compliance (revealing a significant main effect of group and a group-by-time interaction; P < 0.05). The effect size for the primary outcome was moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.395; 95% CI, 0.033 to 0.755). A sensitivity analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test confirmed the robustness of this primary finding (p = 0.031). There were no significant differences in PPCs incidence in the first 48 h or in hemodynamic safety parameters.ConclusionIn patients undergoing MBS, an EIT-based personalized PEEP strategy produces a statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in early postoperative lung aeration and respiratory function. This is evidenced by superior changes in LUS score, oxygenation index, and dynamic compliance without any reduction in hemodynamic safety when compared with 5 cmH2O of fixed PEEP. However, no significant difference was observed in the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications within 48 h.Keywords: Electrical impedance tomography, Personalized PEEP, Metabolic bariatric surgery, Lung ultrasound, Postoperative pulmonary complications

Introduction

PPCs, particularly atelectasis, are a major clinical concern for patients with obesity undergoing metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS), as they can lead to prolonged hospitalization and increased healthcare costs [1, 2]. The pathophysiology of obesity includes a reduced functional residual capacity and increased chest wall elastance. These conditions are further exacerbated by pneumoperitoneum and mechanical ventilation, creating an environment of high risk for lung collapse [7, 8].

Although lung-protective ventilation strategies using low tidal volumes and PEEP have become the standard of care [3], a fixed (one-size-fits-all) PEEP level (typically 5 cmH₂O) does not account for the substantial variability in respiratory mechanics among patients with obesity [4, 9]. EIT is non-invasive bedside technology that allows for individualized PEEP application instead. By providing real-time monitoring of regional lung ventilation, EIT allows clinicians to tailor PEEP to the level that results in the least amount of alveolar collapse and over-distention [5, 6, 10].

EIT-guided PEEP has shown some promise for improving oxygenation and respiratory mechanics intraoperatively in multiple clinical situations [11, 12], however, high-quality evidence in the MBS population is limited. Notably, no prior study has used the sensitive, and bedside available, metric of LUS pre-to-post difference as a primary outcome of the EIT-guided PEEP effect on early lung recovery. This study was developed to fill that gap by testing the hypothesis that EIT-guided personalized PEEP would improve the LUS difference score more than fixed PEEP in individuals undergoing MBS.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The Ethics Committee approved this prospective, assessor-blinded, randomized controlled trial (Approval No. [XYFY2025-KLI62-01]), which is registered with chictr.org.cn ChiCTR2500103728. Patient recruitment commenced on 2025-06-15. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and all procedures were in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

We enrolled 120 patients scheduled for elective laparoscopic MBS (sleeve gastrectomy or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass). Inclusion criteria were: age 18–65 years; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status II or III; body mass index (BMI) > 30.0 kg/m²; absence of severe chronic pulmonary disease (FEV₁/FVC < 0.70 on preoperative pulmonary function tests); ability to cooperate with bedside LUS and EIT monitoring; and intact skin on the chest and back for electrode placement. Exclusion criteria included: a history of chronic pulmonary disease (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease) or prior cardiothoracic surgery; severe cardiac, hepatic, renal, cerebral, or psychiatric comorbidities; an active respiratory infection in the 4 weeks preceding surgery; any contraindication for EIT (e.g., skin breakdown at electrode sites, implanted electronic devices); pregnancy or lactation; anticipated inability to complete follow-up; and severe thoracic or spinal deformity or spinal deformity.

Randomization and Blinding

Participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the EIT-guided PEEP group (EG group) or the fixed PEEP group (CG group) using a computer-generated block randomization sequence. Sealed, opaque envelopes ensured allocation concealment. Due to the nature of the intervention, the attending anesthesiologists could not be blinded. However, the personnel performing all LUS examinations and collecting postoperative outcome data remained blinded to group assignment throughout the study.

EG group (EIT-guided Group): PEEP was titrated employing a Dräger PulmoVista 500 EIT system. The optimal PEEP was defined as the level at which collapse/overdistension curves were balanced based on real-time EIT monitoring.

CG group (Fixed PEEP Group): A PEEP of 5 cmH2O was maintained throughout the procedure.

Intervention and Anesthesia Protocol

All patients received a standardized general anesthetic. Standard monitoring included ECG, HR, non-invasive blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), and a BIS. Arterial blood pressure (ABP) was monitored with a radial artery catheter. Prior to anesthesia induction, all patients received intravenous dexamethasone (5 mg). Anesthesia induction was achieved with intravenous etomidate (0.3–0.6 mg/kg), propofol (2–3 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) to perform orotracheal intubation. Anesthesia maintenance was achieved with the inhalation of sevoflurane (1–2%) and continuous infusion of propofol (4–10 mg/kg/h), remifentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg/min), and rocuronium (0.3–0.6 mg/kg/h), with target-controlled infusion concentrations of all medications adjusted to achieve BIS values between 40 and 60. The rocuronium infusion was discontinued during wound closure.

Mechanical ventilation was initiated in volume-controlled mode after intubation. Ventilator settings followed established recommendations for patients with obesity: tidal volume (VT) was set at 6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight; respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide pressure at 35–45 mmHg; and the inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio was set at 1:2. The fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) was set at 80% and titrated to maintain SpO2 >95%. Recruitment maneuvers were not performed routinely. After intubation, the EG group received personalized PEEP as determined by EIT, while the CG group received a fixed PEEP of 5 cmH2O for the remainder of the surgery [14, 15].

Hemodynamic stability was maintained within ± 20% preoperative baseline values with the use of phenylephrine, urapidil, or atropine as required. Tracheal extubation was performed following the patient’s return of consciousness, with demonstration of adequate tidal volume and minute ventilation. Patients were discharged from the PACU after achieving a modified Aldrete score of ≥ 9 [13].

EIT-Guided PEEP Titration and LUS Examination

EIT-Guided PEEP Titration

In the intervention group, after induction of general anesthesia with controlled ventilation and tracheal intubation, a 16-electrode EIT belt (Dräger PulmoVista 500) was placed around the patient’s thorax at the level of the 4th to 5th intercostal space. Electrode-skin contact quality was verified, and impedance calibration was performed. The PEEP titration protocol commenced from a baseline of 0 cmH2O. Each PEEP level was held for 5 min or sufficient time to achieve hemodynamic and respiratory stability. After stabilization at a PEEP of 0 cmH₂O, PEEP levels were increased incrementally (0 → 5 → 10 → 15 → 20 cmH₂O). Importantly, no recruitment maneuvers (RM) were performed during the PEEP titration process. The optimal PEEP was identified as per the physiological principle of balancing aerated against overinflated (overdistension). At PEEP increases, collapsed alveoli will sequentially aerate (reflected in a decreasing collapse curve, CL LP), but beyond a certain level, overdistension will be observed in the same alveoli (reflected in an increasing overdistension curve, CL HP). Consequently, the optimal PEEP was considered the inflection point where the collapse and overdistension curves cross each other to provide a simple measure of determined PEEP [5, 16]. The cross point signifies the airway pressure that maximizes alveolar recruitment while minimizing the risk of ventilator-induced lung injury from overinflation; this identified PEEP level was then applied and maintained for the surgical duration. Lung Ultrasound (LUS) Examination and Scoring

Two experienced anesthesiologists, blinded to group allocation, performed all LUS examinations using a high-frequency (5–12 MHz) linear array transducer. A standardized 12-zone protocol was applied, which divided each hemithorax into 3 regions (anterior, lateral, and posterior) mandated by drawing anterior and posterior axillary lines and then divided into an upper and lower part within each region [17]. Each zone was scored as follows: 0 (normal ventilation): absence of B-lines or ≤ 2 isolated B-lines; 1 (mild loss of aeration): ≥3 well-defined B-lines; 2 (moderate loss of aeration): multiple coalescent B-lines; 3 (severe loss of aeration): confluent B-lines (“white lung”) or evidence of subpleural consolidation. The total LUS scores thus ranged from 0 to 36. The LUS examination was performed preoperatively (24 h prior to procedure) and postoperatively (30 min after extubation, pre-PACU discharge). The primary outcome measure was determined as a change in total LUS score (ΔLUS = preoperative score - postoperative score). Figure 1A presents a lung ultrasound image that was obtained within 30 min after extubation from a patient undergoing MBS. The image reveals three distinct B-lines (indicated by arrows), corresponding to a regional LUS score of 1. The lung ultrasound score was chosen as a sensitive, bedside-accessible indicator of early postoperative lung aeration. While ΔLUS reflects immediate changes in regional lung ventilation, it should be interpreted as a physiological endpoint rather than a direct measure of long-term clinical outcomes.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure of the study was the change in LUS scores from the preoperative assessment to 30 min after extubation (prior to PACU discharge).

Secondary outcome measures were intraoperative airway plateau pressure (Pplat) and dynamic lung compliance (Cdyn), oxygenation index (PaO2/FiO2 ratio) at 30 min after extubation, the incidence of PPCs within 48 h postoperative, and hemodynamic safety measures (the incidence of vasopressor use). The incidence of PPCs within 48 h was considered a composite outcome, which was independently assessed by a blinded senior pulmonologist daily and categorized according to established criteria by the European Society of Anesthesiology (ESA) [18]. The ESA definition of PPCs included:

Incidence was calculated as (the number of patients with ≥ 1 PPC within 48 h/ total number of patients) × 100%.

Data Collection

Preoperative data included demographic information, BMI, history of hypertension, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), smoking history, and type of surgery, and preoperative arterial blood gas analysis. Intraoperative data included PEEP, VT, Pplat, Cdyn, and dosages of anesthetic agents, and post-extubation arterial blood gas analysis. VT, Pplat, and Cdyn were measured and recorded at six time points: after intubation (T1), after intervention (T2), after pneumoperitoneum establishment (T3), 1 h after surgery (T4), after pneumoperitoneum release (T5), and before extubation (T6). Data collected postoperatively included skin adverse events at the sites of the EIT electrodes, and these were assessed in the PACU and then 24 h post-operatively. These adverse events were classified as Grade 0 (none): no reaction; Grade 1 (mild): transient erythema/itching at one site; Grade 2 (moderate): erythema/edema at multiple sites ± tiny unruptured blisters (< 0.5 cm); Grade 3 (severe): vesiculation; skin breakdown; or infection requiring treatment.

Sample Size Calculation

Using PASS software (v15.0.5), the sample size was calculated a priori using a prior pilot study (n = 20). An independent-samples t-test, SD of 0.85, for ΔLUS with the expected mean difference of 0.6, a two-sided α of 0.05, and 90% power (1-β) required 88 subjects (44 per group). Accounting for an estimated 20% dropout rate, the theoretical sample size was 110. To enhance power and increase robustness, we enrolled a total of 120 patients (60 each group).

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and were compared using independent-samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (IQR), and were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables are presented as n (%), and compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Our primary outcome (ΔLUS) was compared using the independent samples t-test, with effect size (Cohen’s d) and sensitivity analysis (Mann-Whitney U test). A simple linear regression was used to analyze the relationship between group assignment, and ΔLUS. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed, with p-values for interaction being reported for subgroup analyses. Data which were measured repeatedly (e.g. respiratory mechanics) were analyzed with repeated-measures ANOVA with Greenhouse-Geisser correction if the assumption of sphericity was violated (Mauchly’s test). A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

The study enrolled 120 patients, all of whom completed the trial protocol as shown in the CONSORT diagram (Fig. 2). Primary Outcome

The change in LUS score (ΔLUS) was greater in the EG group compared to the CG group (1.68 ± 1.02 versus 1.25 ± 1.01; mean difference = 0.43, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.80, t = 2.162, P = 0.033), with a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.395, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.76). The robustness of this primary finding was confirmed by a sensitivity analysis performed with the Mann-Whitney U test (P = 0.031). The primary outcome data are provided in Table 2.

Secondary Outcomes

Post-operative oxygenation index was higher in the EG group than in the CG group (407.58 ± 25.68 vs. 393.15 ± 30.21 mmHg, t = 2.820, P = 0.006), as detailed in Table 2.

The analysis of respiratory mechanics confirmed a significant main effect of time for plateau pressure (p < 0.05, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected), and post-hoc tests demonstrated greater values at T3 and T4 compared to T1 and T2 (all p < 0.001), although there was no significant group effect or interaction. The repeated-measures ANOVA for dynamic lung compliance demonstrated a significant main effect of time (p < 0.001) and group (p < 0.001), and a significant time-by-group interaction (p = 0.022). Post-hoc simple effects analyses indicated the EG group had higher dynamic compliance than the CG group at all six intra-operative time points (all p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between groups in the incidence of PPCs within 48 h (10.0% vs. 11.7%, P = 0.769) or vasopressor use (73.3% vs. 66.7%, P = 0.426) (Table 2). It is important to note that the study was not powered to detect differences in PPCs, a less frequent outcome. Therefore, the similar PPCs incidence between groups should be interpreted with caution and does not preclude potential clinical benefits that might be observed in larger trials.

Subgroup Analysis

Univariate analysis confirmed a statistically significant difference in LUS score change between the two groups. Subsequent simple linear regression analysis, with the ΔLUS as the dependent variable and group assignment as the independent variable, yielded a statistically significant overall model (F = 4.675, P = 0.033). This result indicates that the intervention significantly affected the change in LUS score. To further explore whether this effect was consistent across populations with different characteristics, a subgroup analysis was conducted. Subgroup analyses by ASA classification, gender, OSA, smoking history, hypertension, diabetes, and surgical type showed similar treatment effects (all interaction P > 0.05). Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The treatment effect was most pronounced in the OSA, no smoking, and sleeve gastrectomy subgroups. Safety and Tolerability of EIT Monitoring

The EIT electrode system demonstrated excellent skin tolerability. The majority of patients (86.7%, 52/60) exhibited no skin reactions (Grade 0). Adverse skin events (Grade ≥ 1) occurred in 13.3% (8/60) of individuals; all reported events were mild to moderate (Grade’s 1–2) and self-limiting, with no severe events (Grade 3) reported.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrates that an EIT-guided, individualized PEEP strategy improves early postoperative lung aeration and respiratory function after MBS. The primary outcome, that is, the magnitude of LUS score improvement, showed greater increase in the EIT guided group than in the fixed PEEP group, with a moderate effect size. This physiological improvement was further evidenced by better secondary outcomes in the EIT-guided group, namely a higher postoperative oxygenation index and greater intraoperative dynamic lung compliance.

Our results are both physiologically plausible and clinically relevant. The EIT-guided strategy resulted in a higher median PEEP (10 cmH₂O) than the fixed level of 5 cmH₂O, individualized to counteract the lung collapse pathology associated with obesity [7, 8, 19]. The consistent improvement in dynamic lung compliance across all intraoperative time points within the EG group indicates that the tailored PEEP levels optimized overall lung mechanics and ventilation efficiency. This is consistent with the personalized ventilation goal for this ventilated population [4, 20]. This mechanistic advantage was directly correlated to enhanced gas exchange, supported by the enhanced oxygenation index just after extubation.

The moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.395) observed for the primary outcome warrants careful interpretation. Although the between-group difference in ΔLUS reached statistical significance, this moderate effect size suggests that the improvement primarily reflects short-term alveolar recruitment achieved by intraoperative personalized PEEP, rather than a durable lung protection effect extending beyond the immediate postoperative period. This interpretation is supported by concurrent improvements in secondary outcomes—higher postoperative oxygenation index and greater intraoperative dynamic lung compliance—which are direct measures of acute respiratory mechanics and gas exchange. Importantly, this short-term physiological benefit did not translate into a reduction in the incidence of PPCs within 48 h. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, the study was powered to detect a difference in ΔLUS, a sensitive but surrogate endpoint, and not for the rarer outcome of PPCs. Second, the 48-hour follow-up period may be insufficient to capture complications that develop later. Third, the standardized broad definition of PPCs remains susceptible to conditions less closely associated with the effect of intraoperative ventilation [18, 21, 22]. Thus, the similar PPC rates between groups do not negate the physiological benefits observed but rather highlight that improvements in early aeration do not automatically confer robust, long-term lung protection.

The similar safety profile between groups, despite higher PEEP levels delivered by the EG, is an important finding as it allays concerns for hemodynamic compromise and supports the practice of a personalized, physiology-driven approach in those cases of EIT technology monitoring [23].

There are limitations to our study. First, the primary outcome of LUS scoring, while highly sensitive and practical for bedside use, has inherent limitations. The LUS score is semi-quantitative and operator-dependent, although we adopted blinded assessment and a standardized approach. Also, the LUS score captures primarily subpleural lung aeration and not representation of changes occurring in the deep lung parenchyma [17, 24].

Second, the EIT-guided titration protocol itself has limitations. The stepwise titration approach, while practical, may not identify the ideal PEEP for all patients or fully capture immediate physiological changes [25].

Third, the single center design may affect the generalizability. Also, the inability to blind the intervention team presents a potential source of performance bias, despite our blinding of outcome assessors.

Fourth, this was a single-center study conducted in a setting with specialized expertise in both EIT and lung ultrasound. The generalizability of our findings to centers without similar technological access or experience may therefore be limited. This acknowledges a key clinical reality: titration strategies based on real-time ventilator parameters such as driving pressure or dynamic compliance are more readily available and feasible for widespread adoption. While more complex, our EIT-guided protocol serves as a proof-of-concept for the physiological benefits of personalized PEEP. The improvements in dynamic compliance and oxygenation we observed provide a physiological target and rationale for simpler titration methods. Thus, our findings do not replace but rather inform and validate the ongoing development of intraoperative ventilation protocols that use commonly available metrics (e.g., driving pressure minimization or compliance optimization) to achieve personalized PEEP. Future research should directly compare EIT-guided PEEP with these pragmatic, parameter-driven protocols to determine the optimal balance between precision and practicality for improving outcomes in patients undergoing MBS.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing MBS, personalized PEEP guided by EIT improved early postoperative lung aeration, gas exchange, and respiratory mechanics compared with fixed PEEP, without increasing hemodynamic risk. While this study was not powered to detect differences in patient-centered outcomes such as the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications within 48 h, our findings contribute to the physiological rationale for precision ventilation in patients with obesity. Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to determine whether these physiological benefits translate into improved patient-centered clinical outcomes.
