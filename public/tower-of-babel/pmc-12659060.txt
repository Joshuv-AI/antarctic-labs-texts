Extracellular vesicles for delivery of functional telomerase
Authors: Yingying He, Yumeng Cui, Lijuan Wang, Wen Yang, Ruping Wang, Yanghua Li, Yue Ma, Yanli Lin, Xiaojie Wu, Long Cheng, Chengjiang Zhou, Youliang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTelomerase, essential for maintaining chromosomal telomere integrity and preventing cellular senescence, represents a promising therapeutic target. However, the inherent risks associated with existing treatments for telomerase deficiency-related diseases necessitate the development of safe and precise targeted delivery systems capable of reaching specific cell populations. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), nanoscale membrane-bound particles naturally secreted by cells, mediate intercellular communication by transporting bioactive molecules, including proteins and nucleic acids. We hypothesized that EVs could function as intrinsic vehicles for telomerase delivery.MethodsTo investigate this, we demonstrated the effective encapsulation of functional telomerase components, specifically TERT and TERC, within EVs. Furthermore, we observed that enhanced TERT expression led to increased telomerase activity within the EVs, as confirmed by TRAP assay results.ResultsThese telomerase-loaded EVs, leveraging their inherent targeting capabilities and unique vesicular structure, efficiently delivered the active enzyme to recipient cells. In aged mice, delivery of telomerase via EVs restored endogenous telomerase activity, preserved telomere integrity, and attenuated senescence across multiple tissues. We observed that the internalized telomerase modulated the expression of senescence-associated markers, thereby delaying cellular aging and promoting cellular proliferation.ConclusionsThese findings support the feasibility of EV-mediated intercellular telomerase delivery, suggesting a potential avenue for developing therapeutic interventions for pathologies associated with telomerase deficiency.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02524-1.Keywords: Extracellular vesicles, Telomerase, TERT, Cellular senescence

Introduction

Telomeres are specialized nucleoprotein structures located at the ends of linear chromosomes, serving to protect genomic DNA from degradation and prevent inappropriate recombination events [1–3]. During each cell division, telomeres undergo progressive shortening, which eventually leads to replicative senescence in most somatic cells [4–7]. Counteracting this shortening, telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein complex, extends telomeres by adding telomeric repeats through the reverse transcription of its intrinsic RNA template. The catalytic core of telomerase consists of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), with auxiliary proteins such as dyskerin (DKC1) and NOP10 ribonucleoprotein (NOP10) playing roles in stabilizing the complex and facilitating its function [8–11]. Insufficient telomerase activity is linked to various degenerative diseases, including dyskeratosis congenita, aplastic anemia, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, which originate from impaired telomere maintenance, resulting in genomic instability and age-related pathologies characterized by stem cell exhaustion [12–16]. Although current therapeutic strategies aim to restore telomerase function through gene therapy and small-molecule activators, these approaches face challenges such as off-target effects, suboptimal delivery efficiency, potential tumorigenicity, and difficulties in achieving sustained and precise expression within target tissues [17–23]. Consequently, these limitations have spurred the investigation of alternative delivery mechanisms, with extracellular vesicles (EVs) emerging as promising candidates due to their inherent biocompatibility and potential for targeted delivery.

Extracellular vesicles, which include exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies, are secreted by nearly all cell types via distinct cellular pathways [24–27]. These vesicles encapsulate a variety of bioactive molecules that mirror the profiles of their donor cells, thereby enabling essential functions such as gene regulation, immune modulation, and tissue repair [28–33]. The lipid bilayer of EVs not only determines their biological functions but also facilitates targeted delivery by enabling membrane traversal [24, 34–36]. This membrane provides excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, effectively protecting the encapsulated cargo from enzymatic degradation [29, 37–39]. Furthermore, specific membrane proteins on EVs facilitate targeted delivery by binding to receptors on recipient cells, enabling precise uptake through receptor-mediated endocytosis, direct membrane fusion, or other internalization mechanisms [30, 40–42]. This sophisticated delivery system allows EVs to efficiently transport functional molecules directly into the cytoplasm or nucleus of target cells [43–46]. Despite these promising attributes, current EV-based delivery platforms are predominantly limited to single-molecule cargo, which restricts their therapeutic versatility [47–50]. Considering the complexity of many disease processes, there is a critical need to expand the cargo capacity of EVs to accommodate complex multi-component assemblies [49, 51].

The capacity of EVs to mediate intercellular transfer of telomerase represents a particularly compelling area of investigation. Several studies have suggested that EVs can transport TERT mRNA, TERT protein, or even catalytically active telomerase holoenzymes, potentially modulating telomere dynamics in recipient cells [52–54]. However, findings concerning telomerase transfer via EVs remain inconsistent across different studies. While some report significant telomerase activity following EV treatment [55], others fail to detect functionally relevant levels of TERT or TERC within EVs. These discrepancies may arise from methodological variability in EV isolation, quantification, and cargo profiling. Consequently, there is a clear need for more rigorous validation of telomerase transfer via EVs, alongside the development of well-defined EV formulations with thoroughly characterized telomerase content and activity.

In this study we investigate the potential of EV-mediated delivery systems to transport the complete telomerase complex to target cells. By leveraging the natural intercellular communication capabilities of EVs, we propose that these systems could overcome the limitations associated with single-molecule delivery, thereby restoring telomere maintenance and introducing novel therapeutic strategies for diseases linked to telomerase deficiency and aging-associated disorders. Our specific aim is to demonstrate the feasibility of delivering not just individual components, but the entire telomerase holoenzyme complex, which includes TERT, TERC, and associated regulatory proteins.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HeLa cells (CCL-2, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection, 293FT cells (R70007, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) were purchased from Invitrogen, and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, 342436, BNCC, Beijing, China) were acquired from the Bena Cell Collection. Both HeLa and 293FT cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, C11965500BT, Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, FSP500, ExCell Bio, Suzhou, China). In contrast, HUVECs were maintained in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM, 1001, Sciencell, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% endothelial cell growth supplement, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin (1001, Sciencell, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All cell cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Cell transfection and generation of stable cell lines

To generate stable HeLa cell lines with modified telomerase activity, we employed three distinct genetic manipulation techniques: plasmid transfection for overexpression, lentiviral transduction for knockdown, and CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing for knockout of telomerase components (TERT and/or TERC).

For telomerase overexpression, HeLa cells, seeded at 70% confluency in 6-well plates, were transfected with either pCDH-Flag-TERT (for TERT overexpression) or pcDNA3.1-hTR (for TERC (hTR) overexpression). Cells transfected with the corresponding empty vectors, pCDH and pcDNA3.1, served as negative controls. Transfection was performed using FuGENE® HD Transfection Reagent (Promega, E2311). Briefly, 4 µg of plasmid DNA and 10 µL of FuGENE® HD were diluted in 150 µL of Opti-MEM (Gibco, 31985062), incubated for 15 min at room temperature to allow complex formation, and then added to the cells. Following overnight incubation at 37 °C in a humidified CO₂ incubator, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing puromycin (Invitrogen, ant-pr-5) and G418 (Gibco, 10131027) for selection of successfully transfected cells.

For gene knockdown experiments, lentiviral vectors expressing shRNAs targeting human TERT and TERC, constructed using the pLent-U6-puro backbone, were utilized. An empty pLent-U6-puro vector, lacking any shRNA insert, served as the negative control. Lentiviruses were produced by transfecting 293 T cells with the lentiviral vector and a third-generation packaging system consisting of psPAX2 and pMD2.G helper plasmids. Viral supernatants were then harvested, filtered, and concentrated via ultracentrifugation. Viral titers, determined by qPCR, were approximately 1 × 10⁸ TU/mL. For transduction, HeLa cells, seeded at ~ 70% confluency in 24-well plates, were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere with lentiviruses diluted in complete culture medium supplemented with 8 µg/mL polybrene. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing puromycin to select for successfully transduced cells. Knockdown efficiency of TERT and TERC was subsequently validated by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis.

TERC gene knockout was achieved using a CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing approach. HeLa cells were co-transfected with a plasmid encoding Cas9 nuclease and a separate plasmid encoding a guide RNA targeting human TERC. Following transfection, cells were selected with puromycin and blasticidin to enrich for successfully transfected populations. Single-cell clones were then isolated by limiting dilution, and TERC knockout was confirmed by both qRT-PCR and genomic PCR.

Mice

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the Beijing Institute of Biotechnology (approval no. IACUC-DWZX-2024-P020) and in accordance with the Basel Declaration. All animals were housed in pathogen-free, ambient temperature (20–25 °C), 45–55% humidity, and 12 h dark/light cycle conditions and cared for in accordance with the International Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care policies and certification.

Isolation and identification of EVs

Culture supernatants from 293FT and HeLa cells were collected and centrifuged at 3000×g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove intact cells and debris. The clarified supernatant was then filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane and transferred to a new tube. EVs were isolated using the EXODUS H-600 system (Huixin, Shenzhen, China). Subsequently, 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, C10010500BT, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to elute the EVs from the isolation chip [56]. Whole blood was collected from C57BL/6J mice via ocular enucleation and transferred into sterile tubes. After allowing the samples to clot at room temperature for 20 min, serum was separated by centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cellular components. The clarified serum was then immediately subjected to a sequential ultracentrifugation protocol at 4 °C to isolate extracellular vesicles (EVs). Specifically, the serum was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min, followed by 10,000 × g for 10 min, and finally at 120,000 × g for 90 min using a Beckman Coulter Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The resulting EV pellet was gently washed once with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended for further analysis.

EV size distribution and concentration were determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)(Zetaview-PMX120-Z, Particle Metrix, Germany), and their morphology was confirmed via transmission electron microscopy (HT7700, HITACHI, Japan). Western blot analysis was conducted to detect EV markers (CD9, Alix, and TSG101) and to confirm the absence of the endoplasmic reticulum marker calnexin, ensuring sample purity. Additionally, Single Particle Interferometric Reflectance Imaging Sensing (SP-IRIS) analysis was employed to identify EV markers CD63, CD81, and CD9, with anti-mouse IgG serving as the negative control [57, 58]. The zeta potential and polydispersity index (PDI) of isolated EVs were determined via dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK). Prior to analysis, EV samples were diluted in filtered phosphate-buffered saline to achieve a suitable concentration. These diluted samples were then loaded into folded capillary zeta cells. Zeta potential measurements, reflecting the surface charge of the EVs, were recorded to assess their colloidal stability. The PDI, indicative of particle size distribution uniformity, was also measured. A PDI value below 0.3 was considered indicative of acceptable monodispersity for biological applications. All measurements were conducted in triplicate at a controlled temperature of 25 °C.

EVs uptake assay

For EVs uptake studies, EVs were labeled with PKH67 fluorescent dye (MINI67, Sigma-Aldrich, St, Louis, MO, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, and the labeled vesicles were purified by ultracentrifugation to remove excess dye. HUVECs were then incubated with PKH67-labeled EVs for 24 h. In parallel, cell membranes were labeled with PKH26 fluorescent dye (MINI26, Sigma-Aldrich, St, Louis, MO, USA), and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst33342 (C1022, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cellular uptake was visualized using confocal microscopy.

EV treatment of recipient cells

EV samples were standardized to a concentration of 1 × 1010 particles/mL. HUVECs and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEF) were treated by adding 200 µL of the EV suspension to 1 mL of DMEM containing 10% FBS. Cells were passaged at a 1:2 upon reaching 95% confluence, with EV treatment conducted for two successive passages prior to further experimentation.

Detection of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) [59]

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. Prior to incubation with the probe, cells were washed to remove any residual medium. Subsequently, cells were incubated with a 10 µM DCFH-DA working solution, prepared by diluting a stock reagent 1:1000 in serum-free medium, at 37 °C for 20 min to allow for cellular uptake of the probe. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to eliminate any unincorporated DCFH-DA. ROS accumulation was then visualized by measuring fluorescent signals using laser scanning confocal microscopy.

Apoptosis detection

Following collection by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min, cells were gently resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline and quantified. Subsequently, a quantity of 100,000 to 500,000 cells were transferred to a fresh tube and subjected to another centrifugation step at 1000 × g for 5 min, after which the supernatant was carefully aspirated. The resulting cell pellet was then resuspended in 195 µL of binding buffer. For apoptosis assessment, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL of propidium iodide staining solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were added to the cell suspension. The mixture was gently mixed and incubated for 10–20 min in the dark at room temperature (20–25 °C). Finally, the stained cells were promptly analyzed via flow cytometry.

Hydrogen peroxide-induced cellular oxidative stress [60]

To induce oxidative stress and subsequent senescence, HUVECs were first pre-treated with EVs under standard culture conditions. Subsequently, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 125 µM. This H2O2 exposure was maintained for 4 h to trigger oxidative stress. Following this induction period, the H2O2-containing medium was then removed and replaced with fresh, H2O2-free culture medium to allow for observation of post-stress cellular responses.

EV administration via tail vein injection in aged mice

EVs were isolated from HeLa cells co-overexpressing TERT and TERC by differential ultracentrifugation and quantified by nanoparticle tracking analysis. Sixteen-month-old male and female C57BL/6 mice were randomized into treatment groups (n = 6 per sex) and acclimated for one week. EVs were suspended in sterile PBS at 2.0 × 1011 particles/mL. Each mouse received 100 µL EV suspension via lateral tail vein injection. Injections were administered every 48 h over a 12-week period (total of 42 doses). All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and adhered to national guidelines.

Animal behavioral experiments

To comprehensively evaluate the effects on emotional state, cognitive function, and locomotor activity, a behavioral test battery was administered, including the Morris Water Maze (MWM), Novel Object Recognition Test (NOR), Open Field Test (OFT), Elevated Plus Maze (EPM), and Tail Suspension Test (TST).

Morris water maze (MWM)

The MWM was employed to assess spatial learning and memory. This involved a circular pool (diameter: 1.5–2.0 m; depth: 25–30 cm) filled with opaque water, the temperature of which was maintained at 20–24 °C. A transparent platform (diameter: 10 cm) was submerged 1–2 cm below the surface in one quadrant, with distal visual cues placed around the pool to aid spatial navigation. Prior to the acquisition phase, mice underwent a pre-training session for acclimation. Subsequently, during the 5-day acquisition phase, mice were subjected to four trials per day, initiated from varying starting points. Each trial concluded either when the platform was located or after a maximum duration of 60 s. Escape latency and swim path were recorded via an automated video tracking system. On the sixth day, a probe trial was conducted, wherein the platform was removed, and mice were allowed to swim freely for 60 s. Memory retention was evaluated by quantifying the time spent in the target quadrant and the number of platform site crossings.

Novel object recognition test (NOR)

The NOR test, conducted over two days in a square open field box (40 × 40 × 40 cm) with transparent walls under ambient lighting, was used to assess recognition memory. Mice were initially habituated to the empty arena for 5 min on Day 1. On Day 2, two identical objects were placed symmetrically 5 cm from the walls, and the mice were allowed to explore them for 10 min. On Day 3, one familiar object was replaced with a novel object, and exploration behavior was recorded for another 10 min. The discrimination ratio (DR) was calculated as the time spent exploring the novel object divided by the total exploration time. A DR value exceeding 0.5 indicates a preference for novelty, indicative of preserved memory function.

Open field test (OFT)

Locomotor and anxiety-like behaviors were evaluated using the OFT. Mice were individually placed in an open field arena (50 × 50 × 40 cm) for 5 min, and video tracking software was used to record total distance traveled, time spent in the center zone, and rearing behavior. A reduction in center zone exploration suggests increased anxiety, while the total distance traveled provides an index of general locomotor activity.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The EPM was further employed to assess anxiety-like behavior. The maze consisted of two open arms (30 × 5 cm) and two closed arms (30 × 5 × 15 cm) arranged in a plus configuration and elevated 50 cm above the ground. Mice were placed at the center of the maze, facing an open arm, and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The time spent in open versus closed arms, and the number of entries into each, were recorded. Increased exploration of the open arms is considered indicative of reduced anxiety.

Tail suspension test (TST)

The TST was used to assess depressive-like behavior. Mice were suspended by the tail from a horizontal bar (height: 40–50 cm) using adhesive tape affixed ~ 1 cm from the tip of the tail. Each session lasted 6 min, during which immobility time was recorded. An increased duration of immobility is interpreted as reflecting behavioral despair, whereas increased struggling time suggests active coping behavior.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (15596018, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (4368814, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) with random or oligo (dT) primers. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using Thunderbird SYBR qPCR Mix (QPS-201, TOYOBO, Shanghai, China) on a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), with ribosomal protein L13 (RPL13) serving as the internal control. The relative expression levels of TERT, TERC, and aging-related genes (p16 and p21) were calculated using the 2−ΔCt method.

Edu incorporation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using an EdU incorporation assay. Cells were seeded on Nunc™ Lab-Tek™ II chambered glass slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and cultured to the desired confluence. EdU (10 µM) was added to the culture medium and incubated for 4 h to allow incorporation into replicating DNA. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, and incubated with Click Additive Solution containing Alexa Fluor 594 (C0085S, Beyotime EdU cell proliferation detection kit, Shanghai, China) for 30 min in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (ZLI-9600, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) before fluorescence imaging [61].

Western blot analysis

Cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (23227, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Proteins (equal amounts) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE (CW0027S, CWBIO, Jiangsu, China) and transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Merck millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-GAPDH (diluted 1:10,000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China), anti-TERT (diluted 1:1000, ab32020, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-Dyskerin (diluted 1:1000, #53234, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p16 (diluted 1:1000, #2947, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p21 (diluted 1:1000. #2947, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-TSG101 (diluted 1:1000, #72312, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-Alix (diluted 1:1000, #2171, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-CD9 (diluted 1:1000, #13174, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-γ-H2A (diluted 1:1000, #12349, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), CD63 (diluted 1:1000, sc-5275, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA), anti-Calnexin (diluted 1:1000, ab92573, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), and anti-p53 (diluted 1:5000, 60283-2-Ig, Proteintech Group, Danvers, MA, USA). Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). GAPDH served as the loading control.

Immunofluorescence

HeLa cells were plated in chamber slides and allowed to adhere. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and then washed three times with PBS (5 min per wash). Permeabilization was performed with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by further PBS washes. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubating the slides in 20% serum at 37 °C for 30 min. Primary antibodies (1:50, anti-TERT, sc-393013, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA) diluted in antibody dilution buffer were applied, and cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After three PBS washes, cells were incubated with a 488-conjugated secondary antibody (diluted 1:200) for 60 min at room temperature, followed by additional washes. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI before fluorescence imaging.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

Cells in a 12-well plate were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 1 mL of fixative solution at room temperature for 15 min, and then washed three times with PBS (3 min per wash). SA-β-gal staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (C0602, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After incubation with the staining solution at 37 °C overnight, the senescent cells were visualized using an optical microscope [62].

Telomeric repeat amplification protocol (TRAP) assay [63]

The TRAP assay was performed using the TRAPeze® Telomerase Detection Kit (S7710, Sigma-Aldrich, St, Louis, MO, USA) as per the manufacturer’s protocol.

For cell-based TRAP assays, cultured cells were initially trypsinized, and the trypsin activity was subsequently quenched by the addition of complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum. Following quenching, cells were washed twice with cold PBS and pelleted via centrifugation. To preserve telomerase integrity during cell lysis, the cell pellet was resuspended in a CHAPS-based lysis buffer (at 3–4 times the volume of the cell pellet) supplemented with an RNase inhibitor (1:200 dilution) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (1:100 dilution). The resulting lysate was incubated on ice for 30 min and then clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. For each TRAP reaction using cell lysates, 100 ng of total protein from samples expected to exhibit high telomerase activity was used. The PCR amplification was performed for 27–33 cycles, with the specific cycle number depending on the anticipated level of telomerase expression. Following amplification, a 10 µL aliquot of the PCR product was subjected to polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis to visualize the amplified products. A similar procedure was employed for TRAP assays using EVs. EVs were lysed using the CHAPS buffer under identical conditions as described for cells, and equal amounts of EV-derived protein were then subjected to the TRAP assay.

Mouse tail and cellular DNA isolation

Mouse tail tissue (0.5 cm segment) and cultured cells were processed in parallel. A 0.5 cm segment of mouse tail tissue was initially placed into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and kept on ice. Cells were harvested and transferred into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes, then kept on ice. To each tube, 400 µL of digestion buffer and 1 µL of 25 mg/mL Proteinase K were added. Samples were incubated overnight at 50 °C with occasional gentle inversion. The following day, 200 µL of saturated NaCl solution was added, and tubes were vigorously shaken before incubation on ice for 10 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was carefully collected. To the supernatant, 1 mL of 100% ethanol was added with thorough mixing, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of double-distilled water (ddH2O) and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h to ensure complete DNA dissolution.

In vivo biodistribution analysis of EVs

To track the in vivo biodistribution of EVs, purified EVs were labeled with the near-infrared fluorescent dye DIR (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, EVs were incubated with DIR at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, ultracentrifugation was performed to remove any unbound dye, effectively isolating the labeled EVs (EVs-DIR). These EVs-DIR were then resuspended in sterile PBS and administered to mice via tail vein injection at a single dose of 2.0 × 1010 particles. The in vivo distribution of EVs-DIR was monitored via whole-body fluorescence imaging at predetermined time points using an in vivo imaging system (Tanon ABL, Shanghai, China). At the study endpoint, mice were euthanized, and major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, brain, thymus, and gastrointestinal tract, were harvested. These organs were rinsed with PBS and then subjected to ex vivo fluorescence imaging to quantitatively assess tissue-specific EV accumulation [64, 65].

Telomere length quantification

Average telomere length was determined using the Absolute Human and Mouse Telomere Length Quantitative qPCR Assay Kit (8918, ScienCell, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Genomic DNA was extracted using standard protocols, quantified, and adjusted to an optimal concentration. The qPCR reaction mix was prepared as per the kit instructions, with cycling parameters comprising an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 52 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s). Data were analyzed following the manufacturer’s guidelines [66].

Immunohistochemical staining

Harvested mouse tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin at room temperature for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Five-micrometer sections were cut on a rotary microtome, mounted onto silane-coated slides, and dried overnight at 37 °C. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using the two-step rabbit detection kit (PV-6001, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and endogenous peroxidase activity quenched. After antigen retrieval, slides were blocked and incubated with primary antibody (anti-p16, 1:100, #2947, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p21, diluted 1:100. #2947, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA),) at 4 °C overnight. Sections were then treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, developed with DAB substrate, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Following dehydration and mounting, stained sections were examined under a bright-field microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently, with each experimental condition having at least three biological replicates. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (Version X, La Jolla, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were made using an independent samples t-test, while one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among multiple groups. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

EVs contain active telomerase complexes

To determine whether EVs can encapsulate intact telomerase complexes, EVs were isolated from HeLa cells, 293FT cells, and mouse serum. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed that the isolated EVs exhibited the characteristic bilayer membrane structure and typical cup-shaped morphology (Fig. 1A). Single Particle Interferometric Reflectance Imaging Sensor (SP-IRIS) analysis further validated the presence of canonical EV surface markers CD63, CD81, and CD9 (Fig. 1B). Consistent with established EV dimensions, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) demonstrated that the EV size distribution predominantly ranged from 100 to 200 nm (Fig. 1C). Western blotting confirmed the expression of EV markers Alix, TSG101, CD63, and CD9, while the absence of the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin verified the purity of the samples. The observed marker expression indicates minimal contamination from intracellular components and suggests that most isolated EVs are exosomes derived from the endosomal pathway (Fig. S1A-C).

Importantly, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) detected TERC within EVs from HeLa cells, 293FT cells, and mouse serum, and agarose gel electrophoresis validated the integrity of the isolated TERC (Fig. S1D-F, Fig. 1D). Additionally, Western blot analysis demonstrated the presence of TERT in EVs from HeLa cells, 293FT cells, and mouse serum (Fig. 1E). To assess whether EVs contain functionally active telomerase complexes, we performed telomeric repeat amplification protocol (TRAP) assays. This widely used method detects telomerase activity by leveraging its ability to extend a synthetic oligonucleotide (TS primer) with telomeric repeats. These extended repeats are subsequently amplified via PCR, producing a characteristic ladder of DNA fragments. The presence and intensity of this ladder directly correlate with the level of telomerase enzymatic activity. Applying this assay to EV preparations isolated from HeLa cells, 293FT cells, and mouse serum, we observed ladder patterns indicative of telomerase activity (Fig. 1F). Although the signals were weaker compared to those obtained from whole-cell lysates, the presence of the TRAP-specific ladder in EV lanes provides compelling evidence that EVs encapsulate catalytically competent telomerase complexes. These results establish a solid experimental basis for further investigating EV-mediated telomerase delivery. These findings provide a robust experimental foundation for exploring EV-mediated telomerase delivery.

TERT overexpression enhances telomerase activity in EVs

Given that telomerase activity is contingent upon the synergistic interaction between TERT and TERC, donor cells were genetically manipulated to modulate the expression of these components [67]. Specifically, we engineered HeLa cell lines to either overexpress or knock down TERT, TERC, or both concurrently. qRT-PCR analysis confirmed the successful modulation of TERT and TERC mRNA levels in the respective cell lines (Fig. 2A and B, Fig. S2A-F). To generate a TERC-knockout cell line and a derivative stably overexpressing TERT within this knockout background, CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing was employed. The resulting engineered cell lines were then subjected to qRT-PCR analysis, which confirmed the successful ablation of TERC transcripts in the knockout cells (Fig. 2C). Western blot analysis further corroborated these findings, revealing significantly elevated TERT protein levels in TERT-overexpressing cells and a marked reduction in TERT-knockdown cells (Fig. 2D and Fig. S2G). Immunofluorescence analysis also demonstrated robust TERT expression in TERT-overexpressing and TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells (Fig. 2E).

Following the genetic modifications, EVs were isolated from the donor cells, and their morphology, size distribution, and enrichment of EV markers (Alix, TSG101, CD9) were confirmed to be consistent across samples via TEM, NTA, and Western blotting (Fig. 2F, Fig. S3A-O). To further evaluate the quality and stability of the isolated EVs, we performed dynamic light scattering (DLS) to determine the polydispersity index (PDI). The DLS results d showed a relatively narrow size distribution (Fig. S3P), indicative of high homogeneity within the EV preparations. Complementing this, zeta potential measurements (Fig. 2G) yielded values within the range typically associated with good colloidal stability. These findings collectively support the structural integrity and suggest a reliable delivery potential for the EV-based system.

qRT-PCR analysis revealed that EVs from TERC-overexpressing and TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells had significantly increased TERC mRNA levels compared to controls. Conversely, TERC mRNA was almost undetectable in EVs from TERC-knockout cells (Fig. 2H and I). Moreover, Western blotting indicated an upregulation of TERT protein in EVs derived from TERT-overexpressing and TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells, while TERT was downregulated in EVs from TERT-knockdown cells (Fig. 2J and K). TRAP assays demonstrated that EVs from TERT-overexpressing and TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells exhibited significantly enhanced telomerase activity relative to control EVs. In contrast, EVs from TERC-overexpressing cells did not exhibit significant changes in telomerase activity. Conversely, EVs from TERT-knockdown cells showed reduced telomerase activity, whereas those from TERC-knockdown cells were largely unaffected. Consistent with these observations, telomerase activity was virtually undetectable in EVs isolated from the TERC-knockout cells (Fig. 2L-N). Taken together, these results suggest that TERT overexpression in donor cells leads to an amplified functional telomerase complex within EVs. Moreover, the data indicate that TERC is essential for the assembly of a functional telomerase holoenzyme within EVs, irrespective of TERT abundance in the donor cells.

EVs deliver telomerase to recipient cells, enhancing telomerase activity and telomere extension

We next assessed whether EVs can transfer the telomerase complex to telomerase-deficient recipient cells and thereby restore telomerase function. Primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEF), which inherently exhibit low telomerase activity, were used as recipient models [68, 69]. Initially, TRAP assays demonstrated that donor HeLa and 293FT cells possess significantly higher telomerase activity compared to HUVECs and MEFs (Fig. 3A). Using fluorescence microscopy with PKH67-labeled EVs, we observed efficient uptake of EVs by both HUVEC and MEF cells (Fig. 3B and C). Subsequent Western blot analysis indicated a notable increase in TERT protein levels in HUVECs treated with EVs from TERT-overexpressing or TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3D) and in MEFs treated with EVs from TERT-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3E). Enhanced telomerase activity in HUVECs and MEFs was further confirmed through TRAP assays following treatment with EVs derived from TERT-overexpressing or TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3F and G). Quantitative PCR-based telomere length analysis revealed significant telomere extension in HUVECs treated with EVs from TERT-overexpressing, TERC-overexpressing, or TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells, with the most pronounced effect in the TERT/TERC co-overexpression group (Fig. 3H). Similarly, MEFs treated with EVs from TERT-overexpressing cells exhibited extended telomeres (Fig. 3I). Conversely, telomere length in HUVECs remained unchanged after treatment with EVs from TERC-knockout cells compared with control groups (Fig. 3J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the delivery of the telomerase complex via EVs can enhance telomerase activity and promote telomere elongation in recipient cells.

EV-mediated delivery of telomerase counteracts cellular senescence and promotes proliferation

The biological impact of telomerase transfer via EVs was assessed by treating HUVECs and MEFs with EVs derived from cells overexpressing TERT, telomerase RNA component TERC, or both TERT and TERC. Senescence associated β-galactosidase staining demonstrated a notable reduction in senescent cell populations in HUVECs treated with EVs from cells overexpressing TERT, TERC, or both, compared to those treated with PBS or control EVs (Fig. 4A and B). Similarly, MEFs showed a significant decrease in β-galactosidase-positive cells after treatment with EVs from TERT-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4C and D). These findings were corroborated by qRT-PCR analysis, which revealed downregulation of the senescence marker p16 mRNA in both HUVECs and MEFs treated with EVs from TERT-overexpressing and TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4E and F). Western blot analysis further confirmed the reduction in protein levels of senescence-associated markers p16, p21, and p53 in HUVECs treated with EVs from TERT-overexpressing and TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells, with the most significant effects observed in the TERT/TERC co-overexpression group (Fig. 4G). MEFs treated with EVs from TERT-overexpressing cells also exhibited decreased levels of p21 and p53 proteins (Fig. 4H).

To evaluate the effect of EV-delivered telomerase on cellular proliferation, EdU incorporation assays were conducted. Both HUVECs and MEFs showed a substantial increase in EdU-positive cells after treatment with EVs from TERT-overexpressing, TERC-overexpressing, and TERT/TERC co-overexpressing cells, with the highest proliferation rates in the TERT/TERC co-overexpression group (Fig. 4I-L).

To further investigate intracellular oxidative stress, a known hallmark of senescence [70], reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurements were conducted. These measurements demonstrated that EVs derived from TERT/TERC-overexpressing cells significantly reduced ROS levels in both HUVECs and MEFs, thereby supporting their anti-senescent effects (Fig. S4A and B). Furthermore, apoptosis assays performed under both basal and H2O2-induced stress conditions revealed no significant increase in apoptotic cell death across any treatment groups, indicating that these telomerase-enriched EVs are non-cytotoxic (Fig. S4C and D). To assess their protective capacity against oxidative DNA damage, cells were subsequently exposed to hydrogen peroxide after EV treatment. Western blot analysis confirmed that EVs from TERT/TERC-overexpressing cells markedly reduced γ-H2A.X expression, which is indicative of effective mitigation of DNA damage (Fig. S4E).

To investigate the contribution of telomerase holoenzyme integrity to the anti-senescent properties of EVs, HUVECs were treated with EVs isolated from control cells, TERC knockout cells, TERT-overexpressing cells, and cells engineered with both TERC knockout and TERT overexpression. Subsequent senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining revealed that EVs derived from TERC knockout cells did not significantly alter the proportion of β-gal-positive senescent cells in HUVECs compared to PBS or vector controls. However, while EVs from TERT-overexpressing cells reduced the percentage of β-gal-positive cells, EVs from cells with simultaneous TERC knockout and TERT overexpression exhibited a significantly higher percentage of β-gal-positive cells than those from TERT-overexpressing cells alone (Fig. S5A and B). This indicates that the absence of TERC diminishes the anti-senescent efficacy of EV-delivered telomerase components. Consistent with these findings, EdU incorporation assays demonstrated that treatment of HUVECs with EVs from TERC knockout cells did not significantly affect cell proliferation. Crucially, HUVECs treated with EVs from cells with combined TERC knockout and TERT overexpression showed a significantly reduced proliferation index compared to cells treated with EVs from TERT-overexpressing cells alone (Fig. S5C and D).

Our preliminary TRAP assay results indicated a substantial reduction in telomerase activity in EVs derived from shTERT cells, confirming the functional impairment of telomerase cargo (Fig. 2M). To further investigate the functional role of telomerase within EVs, we treated HUVECs with EVs isolated from TERT-silenced donor cells. Subsequent analysis revealed that, compared to HUVECs treated with control EVs, cells exposed to TERT-deficient EVs exhibited a significantly increased proportion of β-galactosidase-positive senescent cells and a decreased proportion of EdU-positive proliferating cells (Fig. S5E–H).

Collectively, these results suggest that the anti-senescent and pro-proliferative effects of EVs are compromised upon TERT depletion in donor cells, thus supporting the intercellular transfer of functionally active telomerase via EVs. The observed attenuation of EV-mediated senescence suppression and proliferation promotion following TERT disruption suggests that the biological effects of EVs are largely dependent on the presence of a functional telomerase complex. Consequently, these findings provide compelling evidence that the EV-mediated delivery of an active telomerase complex can effectively mitigate cellular senescence and promote proliferation in recipient cells.

EV-mediated telomerase replenishment attenuates molecular and histological hallmarks of aging in vivo

To establish the age-related baseline of circulating telomerase-bearing EVs, EVs were isolated from the serum of young (2 months) and aged (16 months) C57BL/6 mice. Immunoblotting analysis of EVs isolated from young mice serum confirmed the enrichment of canonical exosomal markers Alix, TSG101, and CD9, along with the telomerase holoenzyme components TERT, DKC1, and TCAB1. In contrast, EVs isolated from aged mice serum exhibited a marked reduction in the abundance of all three telomerase-associated proteins (Fig. 5A). Consistent with the observed proteomic differences, TRAP assays demonstrated robust telomerase activity in EVs from young mice serum, while EVs from aged mice serum exhibited a largely abrogated telomerase activity (Fig. 5B). These findings demonstrate an age-dependent decline in both the abundance and catalytic competence of telomerase-positive serum EVs, thus providing a rationale for subsequent supplementation studies.

To assess the therapeutic potential of telomerase-loaded EVs, aged (16-month-old) male and female mice were administered PBS, empty-vector EVs, or telomerase-loaded EVs every 48 h for 12 weeks. To determine the in vivo biodistribution of EVs, fluorescently labeled EVs were injected into mice via different routes, and in vivo imaging was performed. Compared to PBS, both intraperitoneal and intravenous injections of EVs resulted in detectable fluorescence signals. Intravenous injection, in particular, led to a marked accumulation of EVs in the brain (Fig. S6A), prompting its selection as the administration route for subsequent experiments. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs 48 h post-injection corroborated the systemic delivery of EVs, revealing their predominant localization in the liver, spleen, and lung, which is consistent with uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system (Fig. S6B). Time-course in vivo imaging following intravenous injection of PBS, empty vector-containing EVs, or telomerase-enriched EVs demonstrated a consistent biodistribution pattern across 6, 12, 24, 48 and 72 h post-injection (Fig. S6C), supporting the anticipated organ-level distribution of EVs after systemic administration [64]. To further evaluate the functional impact of the administered EVs and their biodistribution in vivo, a battery of behavioral tests was conducted in the aged mice, including the Morris water maze test for spatial learning and memory, the novel object recognition test for recognition memory, the open field test for exploratory behavior and anxiety-like behavior, the elevated plus maze test for anxiety-like behavior, and the tail suspension test for depression-like behavior. The results demonstrated that aged mice treated with telomerase-enriched EVs exhibited significantly improved spatial learning (as indicated by shortened escape latency and increased platform crossings), enhanced recognition memory, increased center exploration and reduced immobility time in the open field test, elevated open arm entry in the plus maze, and prolonged climbing time in the tail suspension test (Fig. S6D-J). These findings collectively suggest that telomerase-enriched EVs effectively mitigate cognitive decline, reduce anxiety and depression-like behaviors, and improve overall behavioral performance in aged mice.

To further validate the systemic functional impact of telomerase-enriched EVs, we performed quantitative PCR analysis to assess telomere length and senescence-associated gene expression across multiple organs. Telomerase-loaded EVs significantly enhanced telomere length in multiple organs, including the heart, liver, kidney, and intestine, compared to both control groups (Fig. 5C). Concomitantly, transcript levels of the senescence markers p16 and p21 were substantially reduced across the same tissues (Fig. 5D and E). These findings were corroborated at the protein level through immunoblotting of representative organs, which demonstrated a pronounced down-regulation of p21 and p53 following telomerase-loaded EV administration (Fig. 5F). Immunohistochemical staining further illustrated the anti-senescent effect of telomerase-bearing Evs (Fig. 5G and H, Fig. S7A and B). Specifically, nuclear p16 and p21 immunoreactivity was markedly diminished in brain, liver, stomach, intestine, and spleen sections from treated mice compared with PBS or empty-vector EVs cohorts.

Taken together, these data indicate that systemic delivery of telomerase-competent EVs reinstates telomerase activity, preserves telomere length and mitigates senescence-associated molecular pathology in aged mice, supporting their therapeutic potential for age-related decline.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates EVs can encapsulate and functionally transport telomerase holoenzyme complexes, establishing their capacity as natural vehicles for intercellular telomerase transfer. We observed that overexpression of TERT in donor cells significantly enhances telomerase activity within EVs. Subsequently, these EVs effectively deliver functional telomerase to recipient cells, increasing intracellular telomerase activity and inducing telomere elongation, thus counteracting cellular senescence and promoting proliferation. Importantly, systemic administration of these EVs to aged mice resulted in significant telomere elongation across multiple organs and a concomitant reduction in the expression of canonical senescence markers in vivo. Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of the EV–telomerase axis in maintaining telomere homeostasis and attenuating senescence, positioning telomerase-loaded EVs as a promising biotherapeutic platform for counteracting cellular and systemic age-related decline.

To further elucidate the functional requirements for telomerase packaging, we examined the effect of co-expressing key telomerase components. The simultaneous overexpression of both TERT and TERC yielded the highest telomerase activity, highlighting the importance of assembling a complete telomerase holoenzyme for optimal function. Indeed, regardless of TERT expression levels, a functionally competent telomerase holoenzyme complex fails to assemble in the absence of TERC. These findings demonstrate that the integrity of the complete telomerase complex, rather than TERT alone, is essential for exerting biological activity within EVs. Furthermore, our data suggest that EV-mediated delivery of the complete telomerase complex initiates telomerase activity more rapidly compared to administering individual components that require intracellular assembly. This accelerated activation underscores the potential therapeutic significance of EV-mediated telomerase delivery, particularly in the context of conditions associated with telomerase deficiency.

Mechanistic studies have revealed the potential of EV-mediated telomerase transfer, yet translational applications necessitate precise control over both delivery and dosage. Building on established EV drug delivery platforms [71–73], the telomerase-specific engineering strategies, such as controlled molecular loading [74, 75], facilitate personalized treatment strategies tailored to different cell types. This precision in control substantially mitigates side effects associated with telomerase dosage imbalance. Furthermore, EVs are characterized by excellent biocompatibility and the ability to cross the blood-brain barrier [76, 77], a selective permeability barrier that typically restricts drug penetration to the brain [78–80]. The unique structure and composition of EVs allow for the delivery of telomerase to target cells within the brain, effectively delaying the progression of related diseases [81, 82].

A significant challenge for the clinical translation of EVs lies in their inherent heterogeneity, impacting batch-to-batch consistency, reproducibility, and targeted delivery [27]. To address this, our study utilized the EXODUS ultrafast isolation system to enrich for EVs, followed by comprehensive characterization of their morphology, size distribution, and expression of canonical EV markers. Looking ahead, future translational studies could benefit from more sophisticated EV subpopulation isolation techniques or the creation of synthetic EV mimetics to improve precision, purity, and targeting specificity [83, 84]. Scalability is another crucial factor [85], while our current method provided enough EVs for preclinical research, achieving large-scale clinical applications will necessitate the development of stable EV-producing cell lines, optimized culture conditions, and scalable bioreactor-based production platforms [86]. Ultimately, advancements in EV bioengineering and manufacturing will be crucial to support Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant production for clinical use.

The potential oncogenicity associated with telomerase activation constitutes the most pressing concern [47, 87]. Specifically, constitutive overexpression of TERT achieved through viral vectors or genome-integrating approaches has been implicated in insertional mutagenesis, sustained proliferative signaling, and tumorigenesis [88]. However, EV-mediated delivery offers a fundamentally different approach. Our system employs EVs to transfer a pre-assembled, catalytically active telomerase holoenzyme into recipient cells, circumventing the need for genetic manipulation or integration into the host genome. This delivery mechanism is inherently transient and self-limiting, as it does not depend on de novo expression of telomerase subunits. Furthermore, the recipient cells’ intracellular environment likely exerts additional regulatory control over telomerase activity, thereby mitigating the risk of uncontrolled proliferation. In line with this, our in vivo data demonstrated that telomerase-enriched EVs modestly improved telomere function and reduced cellular senescence without inducing abnormal cell growth or tumor development during the observation period. The non-integrative, temporally restricted, and physiologically regulated nature of EV-mediated telomerase delivery, combined with the innate targeting capabilities and biocompatibility of EVs, suggests that this approach may provide a safer and clinically translatable alternative to traditional telomerase activation strategies.

The demonstrated capability of EV delivery technology to precisely control dosage and efficiently traverse tissue barriers has paved new avenues for treating a variety of diseases [84, 89, 90]. Future research should prioritize optimizing the EV-telomerase delivery system, further elucidating its mechanism of action, and assessing its safety and efficacy in clinical applications. Collectively, these findings establish EVs as a versatile platform for telomerase-based therapeutics.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the feasibility of utilizing extracellular vesicles (EVs) as efficient nanocarriers for functional telomerase delivery. EVs derived from engineered donor cells successfully encapsulate and transfer catalytically active telomerase complexes to recipient cells, enhancing telomerase activity, preserving telomere length, and attenuating cellular senescence. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of telomerase-enriched EVs as a novel anti-aging strategy and provide a foundation for EV-based interventions in telomerase deficiency–related disorders.
