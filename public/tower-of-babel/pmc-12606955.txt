Question

Can primary care practices identify and support family care partners through a simple portal based intervention?

Findings

Between 45.9% (Organization 1) and 64.9% (Organization 2) of patients receiving a pre-visit questionnaire completed it. Of these, 16.9% (Organization 1) and 22.8% (Organization 2) reported care partner responsibilities. 1,105 care partners completed the Caregiver Intensity Index (CII), with the majority reporting moderate levels of intensity. Between 16.2% (Organization 1) and 19.5% (Organization 2) of care partners completing the CII accessed a curated list of care partner resources.

Meaning

This first-of-its-kind demonstration suggests a simple portal questionnaire, with the CII linked to resources, and supported with in-clinic materials, may successfully identify, engage, and support care partners, with minimal changes to clinician workflow.

Introduction

Millions of adults in the United States help a family member or friend manage their health and well-being [1]. These care partners, often family members or other trusted adults, provide critical support for tasks such as dressing and bathing, managing medications and medical equipment, appointment transportation and attendance, and helping ensure follow-up with care plans [2–4]. Some research suggests that helping another person in this way has a protective health effect for care partners and can provide a sense of purpose and fulfillment [5–8]. Conversely, other research indicates a link between caregiving responsibilities and poorer mental and physical health [9–13].

Both care partners and those they assist can benefit from psychosocial support, but identifying care partners is challenging, as is linking them with needed services [14–16]. Their interactions with the healthcare system on behalf of patients rarely result in support for themselves [17, 18]. Adding time to a busy clinical visit to discuss caregiving responsibilities and available resources is often not feasible [19]. Identifying care partners (both patients with caregiving responsibilities and care partners accompanying patients to the visit) before or during clinical visits and connecting them to resources without adding to clinical work could be beneficial to patients, care partners, and clinicians.

Use of patient portals

Most healthcare organizations in the U.S. use an electronic health record (EHR) that offers a secure patient portal [20]. Patients and care partners can use these portals to access their medical information, complete care management tasks such as prescription renewals and scheduling appointments, and securely message clinicians. Portals are increasingly used to collect information from patients through routine questionnaires [21, 22]. These questionnaires allow clinicians and staff to query patients before a visit about their needs and concerns, including any caregiving responsibilities [23, 24].

In addition to gathering information from patients, portals can be used to offer interventions and resources outside of the clinic visit [25]. Portal-based interventions may generate improvements in outcomes, such as health knowledge, self-efficacy, and decision-making [25, 26]. While prior research has generally focused on preparing patients to manage their own health, the benefits of portal-based interventions may extend to other aspects of health management, including caregiving responsibilities. Despite the increased use of and the value placed on communication through patient portals, there are no published studies of attempts to use the portal to identify and support patients with caregiving responsibilities.

Given the potential impact of caregiving responsibilities on health, understanding the care partner status of patients, areas of strength and difficulty, and being prepared with resources or referrals is an appropriate role for clinicians [27–31]. However, there is little evidence to guide the implementation of such an intervention, including the feasibility of delivery in routine care. Here we report on an evaluation of a multi-site demonstration project to identify care partners through a pre-visit questionnaire and provide digital resources designed to support them in understanding caregiver intensity and connecting with local resources.

Methods

Setting

The demonstration was conducted within 5 primary care practices at two healthcare organizations as previously described (See Appendix 1for a description of participating clinics) [24]. Organization 1 launched the intervention at 3 clinics on September 30, 2022, and Organization 2 launched on May 17, 2023 in one clinic, and June 1, 2023 in the other. Both organizations use Epic as their electronic health record vendor, with its accompanying “MyChart” patient portal.

Intervention components

Pre-visit questionnaire

Three days before a scheduled visit, patients were sent a portal notification inviting them to log in to the portal and complete a pre-visit questionnaire (Fig. 1). The pre-visit questions were adapted from a previously tested questionnaire [23] with input from clinic staff, clinicians, and patient and family advisors, and contained the following: the identity of the person completing the questionnaire (patient, someone completing on behalf of the patient); any important life changes (free text limited to 200 characters at Organization 1 and 255 at Organization 2); the top one or two things to be addressed in the upcoming visit (free text limited to 80 characters at Organization 1 and 255 at Organization 2); caregiving responsibilities (yes, no, don’t know); relationship to the person for whom they were providing care (a spouse, a parent, an adult child, a friend, a neighbor, someone else [Organization 1 only]); and receipt of caregiver help (Appendix 2) [23].

Identifying, engaging and supporting care partners through the patient portal in primary care practices

Respondents reporting caregiver responsibilities were provided an embedded link to the ARCHANGELS Caregiver Intensity Index™ (CII) [32]. The CII is designed as a self-assessment instrument of caregiver intensity across multiple dimensions. Individuals who complete the CII receive a numeric score between 0 and 100, a color (clear = low intensity [score range 0–25]; yellow = moderate intensity [score range 25–54]; red = high intensity [score range 55–100]), and the top two drivers and top two buffers of their caregiver intensity. A care partner’s top buffers and drivers of intensity are determined based on their responses to 25 questions, covering 12 domains, using a 5-point Likert scale. Those completing the CII are directed to an ARCHANGELS microsite with resources specific to the care partner’s drivers of intensity and tailored to the community in which they receive care. The project team collaborated with each organization to ensure the resource page was comprehensive. Patients completing the pre-visit questionnaire were offered the opportunity to provide their score and color in the questionnaire for clinician viewing prior to the visit.

All patients aged 18 years and older at Organization 1 and 21 years and older at Organization 2 received the pre-visit questionnaire through the patient portal three days prior to a scheduled visit. The pre-visit questionnaire was excluded from specific visit types that already required a large number of e-check-in questionnaires (e.g., Medicare Annual Wellness Visits, non-Medicare Health Maintenance Exams) and from telehealth visits due to concerns that an incomplete questionnaire could prevent a patient from starting the video visit.

Additional intervention components

Each organization was provided with in-clinic posters and wallet-sized handouts (referred to as “Care Cards™”) containing a scannable QR code connecting them to the CII, and staff/clinician tip sheets for discussing the intervention with patients. In addition, two EHR macros were developed: one for clinicians to incorporate information about the CII in the patient post-visit summary and another enabling them to integrate pre-visit questionnaire responses into their notes. Finally, the project team conducted training sessions with staff and clinicians from participating practices to demonstrate how to access the pre-visit questionnaire within the EHR, identify workflow adjustments, and provide an overview of the CII and in-clinic materials.

The intervention was approved and deemed exempt from full review by the institutional review boards at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and the University of Michigan and a waiver of informed consent was obtained. We developed structured query language to extract EHR data, which each site adapted to account for organizational differences in their EHRs. The results reporting adhered to Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) guidelines, a framework for reporting results of quality improvement projects (Appendix 3).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics, response rates, and characteristics of caregiving activities. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. For the continuous measure of the CII, mean and standard deviation (STD) were reported. Given the limited sample size and potential skewness in the CII distribution, median and range values were also calculated to provide a more comprehensive summary.

Chi-square tests were performed to examine differences in distributions between groups (patients completing pre-visit questionnaires vs care partners completing on behalf of patients) across demographic variables including age, gender, and primary language. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, N.C.). Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-sided α = 0.05 threshold level.

Results

As shown in Fig. 2, 19,407 patients at Organization 1 received the invitation to complete the questionnaire, and 8,905 completed it at least once (response rate = 45.9%). At Organization 2, 12,047 patients received an invitation to complete the questionnaire, and 7,819 completed it at least once (response rate = 64.9%). Across both organizations, a majority of respondents were female, older than age 45, and had English as a preferred language (Table 1). Respondents did not differ from non-respondents in age distribution, sex, or primary language.

Caregiver Intensity Index TM(CII)

Across the two organizations, 1,105 participants completed the CII [573 at Organization 1; 532 at Organization 2] either via the link in the pre-visit questionnaire or using a QR code on in-clinic materials or after visit summaries(Table 4). Most respondents completed the CII through the link provided in the pre-visit questionnaire [540/573 (94.2%) at Organization 1; 487/532 (91.5% at Organization 2)], representing 36.9% percent of participants reporting care partner responsibilities in the questionnaire (540/1464) at Organization 1 and 27.3% (487/1783) at Organization 2. Fewer participants accessed the CII through in-clinic materials. Approximately 5% of participants completed the CII using the QR code on a care card [29/573 (5.1%) at Organization 1; 23/532 (4.3%) at Organization 2], with the remaining participants accessing through the clinic poster or after-visit summary [4/573 (1.0%) at Organization 1; 22/532 (4.1%) at Organization 2].

Participants completing the Caregiver Intensity Index

The majority of participants completing the CII scored in the moderate intensity range [364/573 (63.5%) Organization 1; 315/532 (59.2%) Organization 2]. Approximately sixteen percent [90/573; 15.7%] of respondents completing the CII at Organization 1 scored in the high intensity range, as did 25.4% [135/532] at Organization 2 (Fig. 3). Participants’ CII scores varied by the channel used to access the CII. Across the two sites, 21.9% [225/1027] of participants completing the CII through the pre-visit questionnaire link scored in the high-intensity range; the proportion scoring in the red was highest (51.9%) for those using the care card QR code [27/52] and 42.3% [11/26] for those using the after visit summary or poster QR code (Data not shown).

Caregiver intensity index scores

Across both organizations, the most common drivers of caregiving intensity were the need for respite or a break from caregiving duties—“no time for you”- (156/573 [27.2%] at Organization 1; 153/532 [28.8%] at Organization 2) and the need for mental health support—“feeling stressed out or depressed” (104/573 [18.2%] at Organization 1; 100/532 [18.8%] at Organization 2). The top factor helping to moderate, or buffer, intensity at both organizations was “Knowing how to manage expenses related to caregiving” (210/573 [36.7%] at Organization 1; 168/532 [31.6%] at Organization 2). At Organization 1, the second most common buffer of caregiving intensity was “knowing what to expect” (72/573 [12.6%]) while at Organization 2 it was “having the support and encouragement of family” (88/532 [16.5%]). Of participants completing the CII, 16.2% and 19.5% accessed support by clicking on at least one of the curated resources (93/573 at Organization 1; 104/532 at Organization 2).

Discussion

In this paper, we report on a demonstration project designed to identify, engage, and support care partners in primary care clinical settings. Findings suggest that caregiving responsibilities are common among patients in primary care settings, with approximately 1 in 5 patients reporting they helped another person with their health or well-being. The relatively high response rate to the questionnaire and the use of the embedded link to access the CII suggests that the intervention is acceptable to patients and mirrors earlier reported findings from clinicians and staff indicating that the intervention is easy to implement and not disruptive to clinical workflow [24].

While millions of Americans are providing critical help and support to patients every day, they are often not well supported in care delivery, and successful efforts to identify and support them remain rare [16, 33]. Many models may require new workflows and staff for successful implementation, making them difficult to scale beyond high performing health systems with a committed focus on care partners. Our intervention – a simple questionnaire using an available functionality in a widely used electronic health record system, paired with an easily accessible external assessment tool and in-clinic materials – can be implemented with minimal financial costs or workflow changes for healthcare organizations. Once implemented, information about care partners is readily available to clinicians and staff, and patients and care partners can access local resources outside of a clinical visit.

Findings from this demonstration project point to an important equity issue. The COVID-19 pandemic helped to accelerate a shift in care from in-person to virtual modalities [34–36]. While in-person care has since returned to pre-pandemic levels, asynchronous communication and portal-based care have not [37]. Studies suggest that when care moves online, some groups, including older adults, those with lower digital literacy, and patients speaking a language other than English, are left behind [38–40]. Our findings suggest that when care partners at our participating organizations use the portal on behalf of patients, those patients are significantly older and less likely to have English as a first language. Without care partners’ use of the portal on their behalf, clinicians and staff would miss the opportunity to digitally engage with these vulnerable patients.

Similar to national data, self-identified care partners in our demonstration who completed the CII were generally experiencing a moderate level of caregiving intensity [41]. The intervention, which provides care partners who complete the CII with a list of local and state-level resources, may be particularly helpful for this group to the extent that feeling heard and acknowledged is protective for care partner strain. Further, care partners experiencing a moderate level of intensity may have a greater capacity to independently explore resources. In contrast, those with a high level of intensity may be cognitively overloaded by stress and need more “hands-on” immediate intervention, such as a warm handoff to a social worker or community resource specialist. However, it is worth nothing that those using the QR codes on care cards or posters were more likely to score in the highest levels of intensity, suggesting in-clinic materials may prompt patients to discuss their caregiving responsibilities with clinicians or staff during a visit.

Across both organizations, care partners participating in the intervention cited similar drivers and buffers of the intensity of their caregiving responsibilities and experiences. This data could provide invaluable information for organizational leaders seeking to support care partners in their patient population. With information about the key intensity drivers, those developing care partner support programs can target those resources to maximize the benefit for the largest number of their patients. Such careful targeting has become increasingly important in a time of limited resources and increasing health system strain [42].

While our demonstration has important strengths, including implementation at multiple primary care clinics and the high level of patient and care partner participation, there are several limitations to note. First, both organizations were academic medical centers. Implementation experiences and results may differ in community settings and specialty clinics – as well as for subpopulations who are managing specific disease-related symptoms or challenges. Second, both organizations use the same electronic health record system and patient portal. Further work should explore the capabilities of other electronic health record systems to easily implement the pre-visit questionnaire and the usability of such questionnaires for patients and care partners. Third, by using the patient portal to distribute the pre-visit questionnaire, patients without portal accounts were by definition unable to access the questionnaire with the embedded link to the CII. However, in-clinic materials allowed all patients to complete the CII. Fourth, our demonstration project had a relatively small number of patients speaking a language other than English, reflecting the demographics of portal users overall. However, we attempted to increase participation among this group by allowing the pre-visit questionnaire to be completed by someone other than the patient. Finally, participants had to navigate away from the portal to the ARCHANGELS website to complete the CII, requiring some level of comfort with technology, which may have presented a barrier to CII completion. It is possible that some patients were unable or reluctant to navigate to an external website (Appendix 4).

Conclusion

Millions of individuals in the United States rely on the help of friends and family members to manage their health and well-being [1]. Our first-of-its-kind demonstration suggests that a simple questionnaire, deployed through a readily available HIT functionality, coupled with the CII, and supported with in-office materials, may successfully identify, meaningfully engage, and support care partners with minimal workflow changes for healthcare organizations [24]. This approach may also offer an opportunity to digitally engage more vulnerable patients. As national efforts to innovate and improve care for older adults gain traction, such as the GUIDE model for dementia care, or the Age-Friendly Health System movement, incorporating this portal-based intervention for care partners offers an additional opportunity to engage and support this critical group [43, 44].
