General obesity index or central obesity indicators: which is a better predictor of body fat percentage in the first trimester?
Authors: Anqi Xiong, Shiqi Luo, Yan Wang, Yonghong Wang, Xiujing Guo, Xinyuan Li, Biru Luo, Shujuan Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare general and central obesity indicators in their association with body fat percentage (BFP) in early pregnancy.MethodsA retrospective study was carried out involving 10,267 women with singleton pregnancy who completed their first prenatal examination and body composition assessment at a tertiary women and children’s hospital between January 2020 and December 2021. Data extracted from the hospital information system included personal information, BFP, the general obesity indicator (BMI), and central obesity indicators such as waist circumference (WC), hip circumference (HC), waist-hip ratio (WHR), and waist-height ratio (WHTR). The associations between BFP and obesity indices were calculated by Pearson correlation. The predictive performance of the general obesity index and central obesity indicators for estimating BFP was evaluated by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and its area under the curve (AUC).ResultsWHTR, WC, BMI, HC, and WHR were significantly correlated with BFP (p < 0.001 for all) in early pregnancy. The AUC values of WHTR, WC, BMI, HC, and WHR for predicting BFP were 0.89, 0.86, 0.85, 0.82, and 0.81, respectively. The combined effects of BMI, WC, HC, WHR, and WHTR on BFP were the largest (AUC = 0.92).ConclusionsAll anthropometric obesity indices in early pregnancy were related to BFP. The most accurate and cost-effective approach for estimating BFP in early pregnancy was the combined use of WHTR, WC, BMI, HC, and WHR. With limited time and measurement conditions, WHTR may be the optimal obesity indicator to estimate BFP, followed by WC and BMI.Keywords: Anthropometry, Body composition, Early pregnancy, Body fat percentage

Introduction

Maternal obesity in early pregnancy has profound effects on maternal and fetal health. It has been widely reported that obese pregnant women may have higher risks of pregnancy complications such as cardiac dysfunction, gestational diabetes mellitus, and preeclampsia compared with normal-weight women [1–3], which may lead to the increased incidence of intrapartum complications, such as a higher risk of cesarean section, prolonged labor, increased incidence of induced labor and stillbirth [4–6]. In addition, obesity during early pregnancy has potential effects on offspring health, not only in utero and the newborn, but also in childhood, adolescence, and adulthood [7–9]. Hence, the accurate identification of maternal obesity in the first trimester has great significance in improving gestational health and preventing adverse pregnancy outcomes.

BMI (body mass index) is currently an extensively used measure of adults’ nutritional status. However, BMI could not reveal detailed information about hidden fat, which is a prediction of obesity and important to overall health [10], and women with high muscle mass may be incorrectly classified as obese [11]. Additionally, the World Health Organization (WHO) defines obesity as a condition in which BFP is increased to a certain extent with afflicted health and well-being [12], thus BFP could enhance the understanding of fat distribution and the diagnosis of obesity [13]. However, BFP estimation needs professional equipment and specific techniques such as Computer Tomography (CT) scan, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Bioelectric Impedance Analysis (BIA), and Dual Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA), which may result in high costs and are not feasible to use in certain clinical settings [14]. Despite the widespread use of smart scales, published research suggests that these devices may not provide accurate body composition measurements [15].

Anthropometry provides the systematic collection and correlation of measured data for individual humans [16]. It is frequently used in roughly estimating body fat by both researchers and clinical practice due to its universally applicable, economical, and noninvasive characteristics [17, 18]. In addition to BMI as a general obesity index, central obesity anthropometric indicators such as waist circumference (WC), waist-hip ratio (WHR), waist-height ratio (WHTR) can also be adopted to crudely evaluate fat distribution [19, 20], and hip circumference (HC) could measure both adipose tissue and lower body muscle mass, reflecting body fat and obesity to some extent [21]. Given the infeasibility of the measurement of BFP and the facility of the above-mentioned anthropometric indicators of obesity, it may offer an easier and more efficient way to reflect BFP by assessing the latter data including BMI, WC, HC, WHR, and WHTR. However, few studies have attempted to do such and further analyze which indicator is more strongly associated with BFP-defined obesity. Therefore, by using BIA, which is one of the most commonly used method in this field [22, 23], this study aimed to compare the general obesity index and central obesity indicators in their association with body fat percentage in early pregnancy.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was conducted from January 2020 to December 2021 at West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University. Health records of 10,267 pregnant women (see Fig. 1) who met the following criteria were searched and extracted from the hospital information system in this study: (a) aged at least 18 years, (b) singleton pregnancy, (c) anthropometric measurements taken in the first trimester, (d) delivered at the target hospital. Individuals were excluded if they (a) had pregnancy complications (i.e., pre-existing diabetes mellitus or hypertension, thyroid disorders, virus infection, tuberculosis), (b) delivery gestation<37 weeks or ≥ 42 weeks, (c) having any mental disorders, (d) with missing data. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University.

Data collection, measures, and variables definition

Data collection

Data included in our study was generated in the first trimester. Data collection was limited to the first trimester and retrieved from the hospital’s computerized database for research purposes only, without any identifiable personal information. The extracted data include (1) Sociodemographic characteristics: maternal age (years), ethnicity (categorized as Han or Minorities), height (cm), weight (kg), pregnancy (number of pregnancies), and parity (number of live births). (2) Clinical obstetric and neonatal information: gestational weight gain (kg), birth weight (g), fetal sex (divided into boy or girl). (3) Anthropometric information: WC (cm), HC (cm), BFP (%).

Measures of anthropometric variables

First-trimester anthropometric measurement (<13 weeks) is part of the routine prenatal care in the targeted hospital. All assessors received standardized training based on the procedures recommended by the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry to ensure accuracy and minimize inter-observer variability [24]. The equipment used to assess anthropometric information was a professional BIA device (InBody Body Composition Analyzer, BioSpace Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea). The InBody device uses eight tactile electrodes to measure voltage drops in the upper and lower body, applying six frequency bands (1, 5, 50, 250, 500, and 1000 kHz) to obtain 30 impedance values from five body segments. Participants were measured wearing light clothing, barefoot on the metal footplates, and holding the hand electrodes. Each variable was measured twice with the mean value recorded. Pre-measurement instructions included: avoiding vigorous exercise for 12 h, avoiding caffeinated foods for 4 h, and limiting liquid intake to ≤ 1% of body weight within 2 h [25].

Computation and categorization of variables

BMI was viewed as a general obesity index and calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m²). Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated as WC (cm) divided by HC (cm), and waist-to-height ratio (WHTR) was determined by dividing WC (cm) by height (cm). WC, HC, WHR, and WHTR were regarded as central obesity indicators.

According to the definition of BFP from the American Council on Exercise (ACE), women with BFP >32% were categorized as obese, and those with BFP ≤ 32% as non-obese [26]. Central obesity was defined as WC ≥ 85 cm or WHTR >0.5, and general obesity as BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² [27, 28].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or median (interquartile range, IQR) if not normally distributed; categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Kolmogorov – Smirnov test was conducted to assess normality. For comparisons between groups (BFP ≤ 32% vs. BFP > 32%), the independent-samples t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the χ² test was used for categorical variables. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used for collinearity diagnosis of the covariates. Pearson correlation analysis was adopted to evaluate correlations between obesity indices and BFP.

The predictive performance of BMI, WC, HC, WHR, and WHTR for estimating BFP (>32%) was evaluated by a receiver-operating characteristics (ROC) analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The highest value for the sum of sensitivity (Sen) and specificity (Spe) was adopted to determine the optimal cutoff value, with the Youden index calculated by the equation of (Sen + Spe − 1). We also conducted ROC analysis to assess the combined effects of BMI, WC, HC, WHR, and WHTR for estimating BFP (>32%). A χ² test was applied to compare the prevalence of obesity defined by BFP with that defined by WHTR, WC, and BMI.

Discussion

BFP stands as the optimal indicator for fat distribution [12, 13], yet its clinical utility is hampered by prohibitively high measurement costs [14]. In this study, we used BIA to assess and compare the estimation of BFP with commonly used anthropometric methods for general and central obesity. Based on the results of this study, we found that the combination of WHTR, WC, BMI, HC, and BMI to assess BFP in early pregnancy was the most accurate and cost-effective method, and that the central obesity indicator WHTR may be the optimal single obesity estimator of BFP, followed by WC and BMI.

The prevalence of obesity has increased rapidly in China over the past forty years [29]. A study based on WHTR to determine diet-related risk factors for central obesity showed that the prevalence of central obesity (WHTR >0.5) was 48.4% among married women in China [30]. Additionally, the results of a body measurement of adults in Shanghai, China, showed that 12.2% and 28.1% of women were general obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2) and central obesity (WC ≥ 85 cm), respectively [31]. In a large-scale cross-sectional survey of about 16 million participants among 243 cities in China, about 9.6% of women were obese (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2) [32]. In our study, the incidence of obesity diagnosed by WHTR, WC, and BMI was 33.48%, 15.37%, and 1.72%, respectively, all lower than other regions in China. The prevalence of obesity as defined by BFP was 43.18%, which was higher than that diagnosed by BMI, WC, and WHTR, suggesting that commonly used anthropometric measurements may underestimate true obesity. However, WHTR, WC and BMI can be more easily measured than BFP. Previous studies have confirmed the similar performance of these three as indicators of obesity [33, 34], and they were closely related not only to each other, but also to BFP, which were attractive as evaluation indicators for screening obesity.

WHTR, which possesses unified diagnostic criteria and predictive power for obesity, has been widely accepted and applied [30]. It is an effective anthropometric indicator that can better reflect the distribution of visceral fat to detect obese people with lower BMI [35]. Many studies use WHTR as a perfect indicator of fat distribution to diagnose central obesity to predict other diseases such as hypertension [36], metabolic syndrome [37], and diabetes [38]. In this study, WHTR had the strongest correlation with BFP with the largest AUC of 0.89 in predicting BFP, and was strongly correlated with WC, BMI, HC, and WHR, indicating that WHTR was reliable in estimating maternal BFP and superior to commonly used BMI in predicting obesity-related diseases, especially for pregnant women with little change in height.

Consistent with published studies [36, 39, 40], this study found that WC in comparison to BMI was a better indicator of BFP and obesity-related disease, and also had a good correlation with other anthropometric estimators. Although WC is better than BMI in distinguishing between high muscle mass and excess fat [36], the main problem with this indicator is that changes in WC do not coincide with changes in body weight, i.e. WC is not suitable for estimating obesity based on weight gain [41, 42]. Moreover, the incidence of high WC always peaks later than BMI [41]. The WHO recommends that using both WC and BMI can better diagnose the relative risk of obesity [12]. BMI, as a standard indicator for evaluating general obesity, is also the most commonly used indicator associated with many obesity-related diseases [43, 44]. The study found that BMI was strongly related to BFP and other anthropometric indicators, suggesting that BMI is still a reliable predictor of obesity in early pregnant women [13].

WHR and HC are also commonly applied to measure central obesity. Our study showed that of all the included anthropometric indicators, WHR had the weakest association with BFP and the smallest area under the ROC curve, which is inconsistent with previous studies, one study suggested that WHR had little association with visceral fat [13, 45], and other studies showing that WHR was a better predictor of obesity [46, 47]. The differences from published studies may be related to the study’s focus population and measuring equipment. WHR may have underestimated obesity in women with larger HC and overestimated abdominal obesity in women with higher WC [13, 48]. HC deserves attention as a key component of the WHR concept, which reflects the concentration of fat in the lower body and is a more specific measure of subcutaneous adipose tissue [49, 50]. Studies have shown that a larger HC than WC predicted a lower incidence of obesity-related diseases in women [48]. Our results showed that HC has a weak association with BFP, possibly because larger HC may be attributed to greater muscle mass in the gluteofemoral region [51].

The use of specialized instruments such as DXA and BIA when economically and environmentally feasible is the best option for measuring BFP [22, 23]. However, in the absence of direct BFP measurements, obesity indicators such as WHTR, WC, BMI, HC, and WHR can be used as estimators of BFP. Our findings suggest that while WHTR, WC, and BMI individually demonstrated the strongest predictive value for BFP, integrating all five indices yields superior accuracy, highlighting a comprehensive anthropometric approach as both precise and cost-effective when adequate measurement time is available. Pregnant women in early gestation with elevated values across these indices may be at greater risk for BFP-defined obesity and related metabolic complications. Clinically, these findings highlight the practicality of WHTR and combined anthropometric indices as simple, low-cost, and scalable tools for obesity screening in early pregnancy. Pregnant women in early gestation with values above the identified cutoff points (BMI ≥ 21.45 kg/m2, WC ≥ 78.05 cm, HC ≥ 90.73 cm, WHR ≥ 0.87, or WHTR ≥ 0.48) may indicate higher risk for BFP-defined obesity and related metabolic complications. Such measures are particularly advantageous in primary care and resource-constrained settings where advanced instruments such as BIA are unavailable. Integrating these indicators into routine first-trimester assessments could enable the timely identification of women at elevated risk for gestational complications, allowing for early, targeted interventions. The emphasis on clinical applicability and cost-effectiveness strengthens the translational value of this work, supporting its incorporation into evidence-based prenatal screening and public health strategies.

Strength and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first to identify reliable measures of BFP-defined obesity among Chinese pregnant women, based on a large sample and a direct comparison of widely used obesity indices; it provides ROC-derived cutoffs specific to this population; and it emphasizes clinically actionable, cost-effective tools that can be readily incorporated into prenatal care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study relied on retrospective data, which may introduce selection bias and limits the ability to infer causal relationships, while unmeasured and unadjusted confounders such as physical activity level, dietary intake, and pregnancy-specific hormonal changes could further affect the results. Additionally, some measures were self-reported, potentially leading to inaccuracies, underscoring the need for objective assessments or biomarkers in future prospective research.

Second, anthropometric data were collected only during the first trimester, without accounting for dynamic changes in body composition throughout pregnancy. Prospective longitudinal studies are warranted to examine how such changes influence BFP and obesity risk.

Third, the ROC-derived cutoff points for obesity indices have not been externally validated in independent cohorts, which may limit their applicability. Future research should validate these cutoffs in larger, demographically diverse populations.

Fourth, this study specifically focused on pregnant women due to the unique physiological changes during gestation, the influence of maternal adiposity on pregnancy outcomes, and the importance of early identification of at-risk women; however, the overwhelming majority of participants were of Han ethnicity (97.95%) and recruitment was confined to a single country, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic groups and to pregnant women in different countries.

Finally, while BIA offers advantages of non-invasiveness, portability, and feasibility for large-scale studies, its estimates of body fat percentage may differ slightly from gold-standard methods such as DXA; further validation studies comparing multiple measurement techniques in pregnant populations are recommended.

Conclusion

Combining general and central obesity indicators may provide the most precise and cost-effective evaluation of BFP in early pregnancy. When measurements and time were limited, the possible optimal obesity indicator for estimating BFP was WHTR, followed by WC and BMI. The effect of anthropometric changes during pregnancy on BFP needs further study.
