Knowledge, attitudes and practices of Tunisian paramedic students regarding antibiotics and factors associated with self-medication: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Asma Ben Mabrouk, Rim Ben Salem, Jihene Chelli, Rebeh Bougossa, Houcem Elomma Mrabet, Rabeb Jouirou, Fatma Larbi, Chawki Loussaief
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the knowledge, attitudes and practices of students at the School of Health Sciences and Techniques of Monastir regarding antibiotic use and self-medication and to identify associated factors.DesignDescriptive and analytical cross-sectional knowledge, attitude and practice study.SettingSchool of Health Sciences and Techniques of Monastir, Tunisia, during the 2023–2024 academic year.Participants324/500 students (64.8% response rate) recruited through inclusion criteria (enrolled students with informed consent).Primary and secondary outcome measuresKnowledge assessment, prevalence of self-medication and factors associated with self-medication (OR with 95% CI).ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 21.6±2.4 years. Targeted educational strategies along with behaviour modification approaches are needed to raise awareness about microbial resistance and limit self-medication.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global public health threat. Developing countries are particularly vulnerable because of the unregulated availability of antibiotics, inadequate control of their dispensing and limited antimicrobial stewardship resources.1 The major threat is a post-antibiotic era where previously treatable infections become difficult or even impossible to manage.2

Antibiotic self-medication is one of the behavioural factors contributing to the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance.2 The WHO defines self-medication as the use of medicinal products by individuals to treat self-recognised disorders or symptoms without prior consultation with a healthcare professional.3 This practice is particularly common in low- and middle-income countries, where the prevalence of antibiotic self-medication has been reported to reach 80%–90% in some settings.4 Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that this practice is even more common among students in health-related fields.5–7

In Tunisia, the increasing rates of antimicrobial resistance are increasing along with a substantial consumption of antibiotics.8 Although antibiotic self-medication has been reported in approximately 20% of the population,9 available data remain limited and heterogeneous.

The knowledge, attitudes and practices of Tunisian health sciences students regarding antibiotics and antibiotic self-medication remain insufficiently characterised despite their future role in healthcare delivery, patient education and antimicrobial stewardship. This knowledge gap is important because health sciences students are not only potential users of antibiotics but also future healthcare professionals who may contribute to shaping antibiotic-use behaviours and combating AMR. Characterising their knowledge and practices is therefore essential for identifying educational needs and informing targeted antimicrobial stewardship interventions.

Accordingly, this study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes and practices of students at the School of Health Sciences and Techniques of Monastir regarding antibiotics and antibiotic self-medication, and to identify the factors associated with this practice.

Methodology

Study type

We conducted a descriptive and analytical cross-sectional study of the type Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices (KAP) among students enrolled at the School of Health Sciences and Techniques of Monastir, in accordance with the STROBE declaration for cross-sectional studies recommendations.

Location and period of study

The survey was carried out at the School of Health Sciences and Techniques of Monastir, Tunisia, during the 2023–2024 academic year. This school had 617 students enrolled for the 2023–2024 academic year, with 220 in the first year, 205 in the second year and 192 in the third year, evenly distributed among the different fields of study. 35 students were enrolled in master’s programmes.

Study population and sample

Inclusion criteria

All students enrolled in the School of Health Sciences and Techniques of Monastir for the 2023–2024 academic year, who gave their informed consent, were included in the study.

Criteria for non-inclusion

Students who refused to complete the questionnaire were not included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Students who agreed to participate in the study but did not complete the entire questionnaire were excluded.

Sample size

The minimum sample size was calculated using the following Cochrane formula, with a margin of error of 5%, a 95% CI and an estimated prevalence of antibiotic self-medication in Tunisia of 20%.9 Applying this formula, the minimum sample size was 246 participants.

Data collection

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire, where each participant was informed of the purpose of the study and the implications of their participation. The questionnaire was distributed in paper form in classrooms, and a Google Forms link was sent to absent students.

Measuring instrument

The questionnaire was derived from existing literature5–7 and tailored by the authors to ensure comprehension among the participant cohort. It was initially administered to a pilot group of approximately twenty students to evaluate instructional clarity and item accessibility. The questionnaire was not validated. It consisted of four main sections, including 34 closed-ended and multiple-choice questions.

Section 1: Socio-demographic characteristics

Section 1: Socio-demographic characteristics

This section included eight questions on the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants, including age, gender, place of residence, level of education, type of social security coverage and history of chronic diseases.

Section 2: Knowledge of antibiotics and bacterial resistance

Section 2: Knowledge of antibiotics and bacterial resistance

This section included 15 questions that aimed to assess students’ understanding of antibiotics and bacterial resistance.

First, students were asked to self-assess their level of knowledge about antibiotics by attributing a mark out of 10 and to indicate whether they had received previous training on the subject. Then, the questions focused on antibiotic indications, intake practices, treatment duration and drug safety. Finally, the section explored students’ perceptions of antimicrobial resistance and its main causes.

Section 3: Antibiotic Use Practices

Section 3: Antibiotic Use Practices

This section consisted of six questions about antibiotic use practices, these drugs management, their storage methods and the practices of checking the package leaflet and the expiry date.

Section 4: Self-Medication Practices

Section 4: Self-Medication Practices

This section included five questions to assess students’ self-medication with antibiotics. It explored the use of antibiotics without a prescription, the picked molecules, the indications and the reasons for this practice.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS V. 23 software. Qualitative variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, while quantitative variables were reported as means±SD after checking for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.

The following statistical tests were applied:

Bivariate analyses: Performed using the χ2 test for qualitative variables and Student’s t-test for quantitative variables.

Bivariate analyses: Performed using the χ2 test for qualitative variables and Student’s t-test for quantitative variables.

Multivariate analyses: Binary logistic regression was used. The variables included in the final model were those with a p value <0.2 in bivariate analysis and a p value <0.05 was used for final selection.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Ethical considerations

Informed consent was collected for each student. The data collected was anonymous and confidential, in accordance with the ethical guidelines.

The approval of the Ethics committee in Mahdia was acquired, reference: CEM-20251209.

The average age of the participants was 21±2 years. Most students did not have chronic conditions requiring medical follow-up (n=307, 94.8%). Student perceptions of antibiotic resistance

Nearly two-thirds of students (210/324, 64.8%) agreed that antibiotic resistance is a major concern, whereas 68 students (21.0%) perceived resistance as uncommon in the Tunisian context. On the other hand, 14.2% (n=46) thought that resistance can be bypassed by ‘more powerful’ molecules.

Regarding the perceived causes of this resistance, the most frequently identified factors were unnecessary use of antibiotics (n=243, 75%), use without a prescription (n=222, 68.5%) and premature discontinuation of treatment (n=146, 45.1%). The other mentioned factors were the frequent antibiotic use (34%), the repetitive use of the same molecule (12%) and veterinary prescription (2%).

Antibiotic use practices

Among the students included in our study, 51.2% (n=166) received antibiotics in the past year. When questioned, 54% (n=175) of students systematically consulted the antibiotic leaflet, 43.5% (n=141) did so occasionally and 2.5% (n=8) never consulted it. Similarly, 76.9% (n=249) always checked the expiry date, 20.7% (n=67) did so sometimes and 2.5% (n=8) never. More than half of the students (n=173, 53.4%) reported keeping the remaining antibiotics for future use and 50.6% of students (n=164) reported keeping antibiotics at home for use in emergencies.

As for stopping antibiotics, 55.2% (n=179) of students continued to take antibiotics until the end of the full course of treatment, as prescribed by the doctor, 39.8% (n=129) stopped antibiotics after symptoms disappeared, 12% (n=39) stopped antibiotics after a few days, regardless of results, and 5% (n=17) of students stopped when antibiotics were finished.

Self-medication practices

The frequency of self-medication in the study population was 60.5% (n=197). The main reasons for self-medication were a positive experience with antibiotics in the past (n=89, 45.2%), use of previous prescriptions for similar symptomatology (n=81, 41%) and considering the symptoms as mild (n=80, 40.6%). The other reported reasons were consultation fees (15%), lack of time (8%) and not having medical insurance (2%).

Antibiotics used for self-medication were mainly amoxicillin (n=154, 78.2%), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (n=91, 46.2%), azithromycin (n=32, 16.2%) and fluoroquinolones (n=10, 5.1%).

The main symptoms justifying self-medication with antibiotics were fever (n=324, 100%), followed by dental conditions (n=232, 71.6%) and sore throat (n=230, 71%). Colds, coughs and nasal congestion led 63.9% (n=207) of participants to self-medicate. Antibiotics were used to treat urinary symptoms in 48.2% of participants and diarrhoea in 37.6%.

The main sources of antibiotics were advice from pharmacists (n=96, 48.7%), advice from friends or family members (n=48, 24.4%), followed by the internet and social media (n=5, 2.5%)

Discussion

Self-medication with antibiotics is a major health problem since it contributes to the emergence of bacterial resistance. When access to antibiotics without a prescription is easy, the role of healthcare workers is crucial in raising awareness among the population. In this context, we conducted an analytical cross-sectional study with 324 students from the School of Health Sciences and Techniques of Monastir, enrolled during the 2023–2024 academic year. The frequency of self-medication with antibiotics was 60.5%. Keeping leftover antibiotics (OR=2.6 (1.532–4399)), having antibiotics for emergency use (OR=3.3 (1.905–5723)) and prior training in antibiotic use (OR=1.9 (1.135–3263)) were significantly associated with self-medication with antibiotics.

As a result of the self-assessment of antibiotic therapy, almost half of the students (n=157, 48.5%) rated their knowledge as moderate and only 37.7% of students (n=122) had received specific training on the proper use of antibiotics during their university studies, which could partly explain an insufficient level of knowledge. Most students (n=296, 91.4%) correctly answered that antibiotics are effective against bacterial infections. However, only 55.9% knew these infections were the exclusive target, indicating a partial understanding. In addition, a significant proportion of students (n=130, 40.1%) were unaware of their ineffectiveness against viral infections. These results are consistent with those of other studies that have shown similar confusion among students.10–12 In our study, 50.9% of students (n=165) expressed a preference for broad-spectrum antibiotics, a higher proportion than the one reported in a study conducted in China (13.6%).11 This preference seems to be linked to the desire to ensure a wider coverage in the event of diagnostic uncertainty.11 When it came to dose, most surveyed students (87.3%) rejected the idea that a higher dose of antibiotics led to faster recovery. These findings were consistent with the results of a study conducted in China, where only 8.3% of students believed that higher doses improved antibiotic effectiveness.11 However, the issue of increasing doses remained debated in the literature. On the one hand, several studies highlighted the risks associated with high doses, including the selection of ultra-resistant strains and toxicity for the patients.13 On the other hand, there were arguments in favour of increasing the dosage, including exceeding the minimum inhibitory concentration, reducing the risk of the emergence of resistant mutants and improving efficacy in infections with high bacterial density, such as abscesses or severe pneumonia.13 Hence, it is crucial to prescribe the dose adapted to the infection in question, considering the severity, the site, the involved microorganism and, above all, the patient history. A majority of students (74.7%, n=242) considered intravenous administration to be more efficient than oral intake, a trend also observed in China (51.7%) despite the lack of clinical justification.11 However, several studies have shown that the oral route is as effective and safe as the intravenous route for certain molecules such as fluoroquinolones and rifampicin. This route also has significant advantages, including a reduction in hospital stay and costs.14–16 The question of the appropriate duration of antibiotic treatment elicited varied responses among students. In our study, the majority (n=203, 62.7%) felt that it should depend on the type of infection, in line with current recommendations.17 However, a significant proportion of students opted for fixed durations: 21.9% recommended a regimen of 4 to 6 days and 10.5% suggested a duration of 7 days or more. A personalised prescription, based on the guidelines as well as on the clinical course, remains essential.18 In our study, the majority (n=278, 85.8%) of students reported that an antibiotic would be less effective if it were reused to treat the same infection. This finding is consistent with the results of other studies.19 20 When asked about the safety of antibiotics, 23.5% (n=76) of students considered their use to be risky, 29% (n=94) considered them safe, while almost half (n=154, 47.5%) remained unsure. These perceptions contrast with those reported in a study conducted at a teaching hospital in southern India, where a large majority (78.4%) of students believed that antibiotics were safe.21 In our study, the most frequently reported side effects were bacterial resistance (n=222, 68%), allergic reactions (n=199, 61%) and digestive disorders, including nausea and vomiting (n=109, 33.8%). These results are in line with the findings of similar studies.10 22 Almost two-thirds of students (n=210, 64.8%) considered antibiotic resistance to be a problem of concern. In Europe, 80% felt that this problem was already present in their own hospital,23 and in the Democratic Republic of Congo, 87% blamed excessive antibiotic prescribing.24 The results of our study revealed that the main causes of microbial antibiotic resistance identified by the students were unnecessary antibiotic use (n=243, 75%), self-medication (n=222, 68.5%) and premature discontinuation of treatment (n=146, 45.1%). These results were similar to those reported in the literature.21 25

In our study, 51.2% of students reported using antibiotics in the past year. This rate is significantly lower than that reported in Malaysia by Haque et al, where 93.2% of students had used it including 39.3% as self-medication.26 In China, a multicentre study (2018) revealed that 57% of students had bought antibiotics in pharmacies during the year, 97% of them without a prescription.27 These variations could be explained by cultural, regulatory or educational differences between countries. In our study, consultation of antibiotic leaflets was frequent but remained irregular in nearly half of the students. In comparison, a study conducted in China reported that 72.2% of students consistently read the leaflet.10 Similarly, in Libya, 68.4% said they consulted the leaflets.12 In addition, 76.9% (n=249) of students reported routinely checking the expiry date of antibiotics before use. The study by Saddique et al reported that 93% of respondents systematically checked the expiry date.28 As future healthcare professionals, verifying the expiry date must be a reflex before any drug intake or administration. In our study, 53.4% (n=173) of students reported keeping the remaining antibiotics for future use. This trend was observed in other studies; in China, where antibiotic storage seemed to be widespread, 63.1% of students kept antibiotics at home, of whom 29.6% self-medicated in case of illness and 23% used them prophylactically.10 Regarding the storage of antibiotics for emergency use, our study showed that 50.6% (n=164) of students adopted this practice. In Italy, only 17.7% of students kept antibiotics for emergency use.7

The prevalence of antibiotic self-medication varies considerably across regions and populations. In our study, it was 60.5%, higher than the average of 47.2% reported by the WHO EMRO region.29 This rate also exceeded that observed in several academic studies, notably in Libya (42.9%),12 Saudi Arabia (58.4%)30 and Iran (43.6%).31 The highest prevalence was in Africa, with 93.8% in Uganda5 and 90.7% in the Democratic Republic of Congo.4 In Europe, rates were significantly lower, at 16% in Italy,7 0.43% in Sweden32 and 4.5% in Denmark,33 where regulations are strict, and antibiotics cannot be purchased over the counter. In our study, the main cause of self-medication was previous positive experiences with antibiotics when presenting the same symptomatology, a result also observed in Iran.31 The perception of the disease as mild was also common and was reported in studies conducted in Iran (54%)31 and Rwanda (50.7%).34 The medical consultations fee, a factor mentioned by 15.2% of students, was also reported in studies conducted in Rwanda (26.1%),34 Ethiopia (24.4%)35 and Malaysia.26 Therefore, it is important to address the problem of health insurance among students to facilitate access to medical consultations. Beta-lactams, particularly amoxicillin, were among the most commonly used antibiotics in self-medication, as reported by several studies.12 20 22 34 36 Our results confirmed this trend, with 78.2% of students reporting using amoxicillin, followed by amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (46.2%) and azithromycin (16.2%). However, fluoroquinolones, used by only 5.1% of respondents, were well below the rates reported in other countries: up to 51.7% in Pakistan.36 This could be explained by the high prevalence of digestive infections in these areas. In our study, fever was the main indication for self-medication with antibiotics, which is consistent with the results obtained in Pakistan (79.3%).37 Sore throat is also a common reason for antibiotic use, with a prevalence of 63% in our study. This indication was widely reported in other international studies.5 10 Colds, coughs and nasal congestion were cited in studies conducted in Pakistan, China and Italy.7 10 22 These infections that are mainly of viral origin do not justify antibiotic intake. Such a practice only adds additional risks of side effects and selection of resistant bacteria. Urinary symptoms and diarrhoea were among the causes of self-medication reported in Rwanda and Ghana.20 Pharmacists were the main source of antibiotics used for self-medication in most studies. In our survey, 48.7% of students had obtained antibiotics directly from pharmacies, a proportion that remains lower than the rates observed in Rwanda (72.46%),34 China (89.4%)10 and Ethiopia (79.5%).35 Although pharmacists are not licensed to ‘prescribe’ antibiotics, this practice is widely common and attributed to lack of time, consultation fees and, occasionally, difficulties accessing healthcare facilities. The advice from relatives had also been an important source of self-medication, comparable to that reported in Ethiopia (14.1%) but lower than the figures found in China (35.6%) and among future health professionals in Saudi Arabia (46.8%).30 Only 2.5% of students in our study said they had relied on information from the web to self-medicate, significantly lower than the 22.5% reported in Saudi Arabia and the 10.5% reported in Libya.12 In Tunisia, although regulations exist, their implementation remains insufficient, and anyone can buy antibiotics without a medical prescription. In addition, the commercialisation of generic drugs has made antibiotics more affordable, contributing to their excessive consumption.38 Strict monitoring is needed to limit access to these medications.

In our study, a previous training about antibiotics was associated with self-medication, with an OR of 1.925. This observation is supported by a study conducted in China, where self-medication was more common among students with prior knowledge about antibiotics.11 Previous training seems to provide a false sense of security and an overestimated ability to self-diagnose and self-medicate.6 38 39 In fact, while knowledge acquisition is an essential part of improving antibiotic use, the quality of training provided remains the key element to limiting self-medication. Training should emphasise the importance of consulting before every drug intake and highlight antimicrobial resistance as a global threat. However, adequate training needs to be associated with behaviour modification interventions to change the adopted practices and help overcome social pressure. In fact, paramedics are a cornerstone in the stewardship programmes and should be involved in advice about rational antibiotic use.

Keeping antibiotics after a course of treatment and keeping antibiotics for emergency treatment were also associated with self-medication. These factors were described in the literature.40 This could be explained either by the fact that people who self-medicate with antibiotics tend to keep these medications available and accessible to use when needed, or by the fact that the medication availability makes it easier for students to use for the mildest symptoms. Dispensing the exact prescribed dose of antibiotics could limit leftover availability.

Our study has many strengths. It fills a gap in national data on antibiotic self-medication among paramedic students, an underexplored domain in a crucial public health context marked by the rise of bacterial resistance. In addition, the analytical results highlight clear determinants of self-medication, paving the way for targeted awareness-raising and training actions. Finally, the use of a self-administered questionnaire may limit social desirability. On the other hand, our survey has some limitations. The cross-sectional design does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between the factors studied and self-medication with antibiotics. In addition, data collection via a self-administered questionnaire, without direct control, may lead to reporting bias, as some participants may have consulted external sources or recollection bias, leading to an underestimation of certain practices. Although inspired by the published literature and adapted to the study context, the questionnaire was piloted but not validated, which constitutes a limitation of our study. Finally, the single-centre sample limits the generalisation of the results to all health sciences students in Tunisia.

Our results highlight the need for a comprehensive strategy to limit antibiotic self-medication among health students. Strengthening training, combined with targeted awareness campaigns, could improve the knowledge and attitudes of future professionals. Regulating access to antibiotics, as well as providing better information for the public, is also essential. Finally, a collaborative approach involving health actors, institutions and training structures is essential to promote the responsible use of antibiotics and curb the emergence of resistance.
