Rituximab-Conjugated DART Nanoformulation for CD20-Targeted Drug Delivery in Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma
Authors: Md Musavvir Mahmud, Byoungjae Kong, Daiheon Lee, Eduar Fernando Pinzon Burgos, Alonso Heredia, Jung Soo Suk, Anthony J Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTo develop rituximab (RTX)-conjugated decreased nonspecific adhesivity receptor-targeted (DART) nanoparticles for CD20-targeted paclitaxel (PTX) delivery in non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and evaluate their targeting, therapeutic activity, formulation stability, and immune-associated cellular responses.MethodsPTX-loaded PLGA-PEG nanoparticles were conjugated with RTX (RTX-DART/PTX) or control IgG (IgG-DART/PTX). Physicochemical properties and colloidal and frozen-storage stability were characterized. CD20-dependent cellular association and intracellular localization were evaluated in Raji lymphoma cells using flow cytometry, competitive blocking, and confocal microscopy. Cytotoxicity was assessed following short-term treatment and media replacement. Calreticulin (CRT) surface exposure and macrophage polarization-associated markers were evaluated in vitro. Anti-tumor efficacy was assessed in a systemic luciferase-expressing Raji xenograft model.ResultsRTX-DART/PTX exhibited a size near 100 nm, low polydispersity, near-neutral surface charge, and 7–8% PTX loading. DART nanoparticles maintained their colloidal properties in serum incubation for up to 72 hours, while frozen storage at −20°C in 10% sucrose for 3 weeks preserved physicochemical properties and biological activity. RTX-DART showed greater CD20-dependent cellular association and intracellular localization than IgG-DART and free RTX blocking reduced nanoparticle association. Under short-exposure conditions, RTX-DART/PTX produced greater cytotoxicity than free PTX and IgG-DART/PTX. RTX-DART/PTX also enhanced CRT surface exposure, while PTX-containing treatments altered macrophage polarization-associated markers and IGF1 secretion in vitro. In vivo, RTX-DART/PTX reduced systemic tumor bioluminescence and improved survival relative to PBS and IgG-DART/PTX.ConclusionThese findings support RTX-DART/PTX as a proof-of-concept CD20-targeted nanomedicine strategy for systemic NHL. Further validation in additional lymphoma models, immunocompetent or humanized systems, and pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies is required.

Introduction

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) comprises a diverse group of lymphoid malignancies that arise from B cells, T cells, or NK cells, with B-cell NHL representing the majority of cases. NHL remains a substantial public health burden and continues to drive the need for improved systemic therapies. The American Cancer Society estimates ~79,000 new diagnoses and ~20,000 deaths in 2026 in the United States, reflecting both the prevalence of disease and the persistent mortality burden.1 Current frontline management for many B-cell NHL subtypes relies on chemotherapy and immunotherapy regimens incorporating anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, with rituximab (RTX)-based combinations such as R-CHOP serving as foundational standards of care.2,3 Despite these advances, a meaningful proportion of patients experience relapses or refractory disease, and systemic toxicities associated with cytotoxic therapy continue to limit treatment intensity and contribute to morbidity.

Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems have been widely studied to improve the therapeutic index of anticancer agents by altering pharmacokinetics, enabling formulation of hydrophobic payloads, and potentially reducing off-target exposure.4–8 However, achieving consistent and efficient drug delivery remains a major translational barrier.9 Moreover, the design of many nanotherapeutics strategies was based on passive accumulation via enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which is variable across different tumors and is most relevant to solid lesions rather than disseminated hematologic malignancies. However, these strategies may not be effective against malignant cells distributed across blood and lymphoid compartments, in which exposure is strongly shaped by circulation and clearance.10,11 Consequently, there is continued interest in developing strategies that increase effective cell-specific therapeutic delivery through antigen-directed interactions, particularly for systemic B-cell cancers. Recent studies further support the potential of antigen-directed polymeric nanocarriers for hematologic malignancies. For example, CD7-targeted polymersome enhanced vincristine delivery to CD7-positive T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, resulting in increased cellular uptake, improved antileukemic activity, and prolonged survival relative to non-targeted or conventional vincristine treatment.12,13

Decreased nonspecific Adhesivity Receptor-Targeted (DART) nanoparticles represent a promising engineered nanotherapeutics strategy designed to reduce nonspecific interactions while preserving receptor-specific binding, thereby improving functional delivery performance.14–18 Building on this platform, we engineered RTX-conjugated DART nanoparticles to target CD20, which is a clinically validated B-cell surface antigen and a long-standing therapeutic target in NHL.19–22 CD20 is a non-glycosylated transmembrane protein expressed on normal and malignant B cells and is commonly retained on B-cell NHL, which has supported its broad clinical deployment as a therapeutic target. The clinical success of RTX in combination regimens provides strong validation that CD20 can be used to increase selective nanoparticle association to improve effective cytotoxic payloads in NHL.23 In this context, CD20-directed nanoparticle association is expected to enhance cell-associated delivery during limited exposure windows, which can be more consequential than relying on passive distribution alone.24,25

The present study extends the DART design strategy to CD20-positive lymphoma by incorporating rituximab into the established PLGA-PEG nanoparticle architecture. Although rituximab-functionalized nanocarriers and CD20-targeted PTX formulations have previously been explored, DART nanoparticles are specifically engineered to minimize nonspecific adhesivity while preserving receptor-mediated cellular association, a design feature that may be particularly relevant in disseminated disease where nanoparticle-cell interactions occur during limited systemic exposure.22,26 In this study, rituximab was incorporated into the established PLGA-PEG DART platform to determine whether CD20 binding could be translated into improved cytotoxic and antitumor activity while maintaining formulation stability. PTX was selected because Phase II studies demonstrated antitumor activity in patients with relapsed or refractory NHL, despite its absence from contemporary standard treatment regimens, and because PTX-loaded DART nanoparticles had previously demonstrated successful formulation and therapeutic activity in other tumor models. Together, these considerations provided a disease-relevant and platform-validated basis for evaluating CD20-targeted PTX delivery in systemic NHL.

Here, we report the development of RTX-conjugated DART nanoparticles encapsulating PTX (RTX-DART/PTX) as a promising strategy to target CD20 and enhance anti-lymphoma efficacy. We first establish the physicochemical properties of RTX-DART/PTX and their long-term storage conditions and then assess their CD20-dependent cellular association and functional cytotoxicity in human B lymphoma Raji cells. Next, we assess immunomodulation effects of RTX-DART/PTX, including surface expression of calreticulin, a hallmark of immunogenic cell death (ICD), and macrophage polarization phenotype markers. Finally, we test the therapeutic performance of RTX-DART/PTX in a systemic Raji lymphoma xenograft model using longitudinal bioluminescence imaging and survival outcomes. Collectively, these studies test the hypothesis that CD20-targeted DART nanoparticles can enhance PTX delivery and efficacy in disseminated lymphoma while maintaining formulation robustness and translational practicality.

Materials and Methods

Poly (D, L-lactide-co-glycolide) Resomer® RG 502 H (PLGA) (MW 7–17kDa, 50:50 LA:GA), Dichloromethane (DCM), 2-iminothiolane hydrochloride (2-IT) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Methoxy poly (ethylene glycol)-b-poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (mPEG-PLGA) (MW 5,000:10,000 Da, 50:50 LA:GA), Poly(lactide-co-glycolide)-b-poly(ethylene glycol)-maleimide (PLGA-PEG-Mal) (MW 10,000:5,000 Da, 50:50 LA:GA) and Poly(lactide-co-glycolide)-Cyanine 5 (PLGA-Cy5) (Mn 45,000–55,000 Da, 50:50 LA:GA) were purchased from Akina Inc., USA Poly Vinyl Alcohol (PVA) (25,000 Da, 88% hydrolyzed) was purchased from Polysciences, USA. Paclitaxel (PTX) was purchased from TargetMol, USA. Rituximab (RTX) Biosimilar and human IgG1 isotype (IgG) were purchased from Bio X Cell, USA.

Preparation of IgG and RTX Conjugated Nanoparticles

At first, both RTX and IgG were thiol-modified utilizing the reaction of free amines and 2-IT as described previously.14,18 Briefly, the antibodies were mixed with 140 times molar excess of 2-IT in a thiolation buffer made with 100 mM phosphate buffer, 150 mM NaCl and 5 mM EDTA (final pH adjusted to 7.2 with 0.1N HCl). The reaction was conducted for 2 hours at room temperature, followed by a purification with Zeba Spin Columns (7 kDa molecular weight cutoff). The purified thiolated antibodies were used immediately to avoid potential disulfide formation between the thiol groups. Thiolated RTX and IgG were labeled as RTX-SH and IgG-SH, respectively.

Single emulsion-solvent evaporation method was used to formulate all types of nanoparticles used in this study. Briefly, the polymers (PLGA, mPEG-PLGA, PLGA-PEG-Mal) and drugs were dissolved in DCM to prepare the organic phase. The amount of polymer and drugs as well as volume of DCM and PVA are listed in Table T1. The aqueous phase was prepared using 5% (w/v) PVA in water. The organic phase was added to the aqueous phase dropwise to form the oil-in-water emulsion. The emulsion was sonicated in an ice bath using an ultrasonication probe (Sonics Vibracell, USA) at 40% amplitude for 3 min with a 20s on-off pulser. The sonicated emulsion was immediately transferred to magnetic stirring and kept stirring for 4 hours at room temperature to ensure complete evaporation of DCM. The nanoparticles were purified and washed by centrifugation (Beckman Coulter Avanti J-25I, USA) at 65,000g for 30 mins (3 times). The nanoparticles were resuspended in thiolation buffer to conjugate with thiolated antibodies.

PLGA-PEG nanoparticles containing maleimide functional groups were mixed with thiolated antibodies (1.2 times excess RTX-SH or IgG-SH to maleimide) in a buffer consisting of 100 mM phosphate buffer and 150 mM NaCl with a pH of 7.2. The reaction was allowed overnight at 4°C. The reaction was performed immediately following the PLGA-PEG-Mal nanoparticle formulation to avoid any hydrolysis of the maleimide groups. After the reaction, unconjugated thiolated antibodies were purified by centrifugation at 65,000g for 30 mins with DI water (2 times). Finally, the nanoparticles were resuspended in water for further characterizations. Information regarding nanoparticle yield, batch concentration and antibody conjugation are listed in Table T2 and T3.

Characterization of Physicochemical Properties

Hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI) and ζ-potential (surface charge) of the nanoparticles were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and laser Doppler anemometry using Zetasizer NanoZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). Size measurements were performed at a scattering angle of 173° at 25°C and reported as number average mean. The surface charge on the particles was calculated using the Smoluchowski equation (calculated by the instrument software) and reported as the mean ζ-potential. Nanoparticle morphology was assessed using transmission electron microscopy (FEI Tecnai T12 TEM) operated at 80 kV.

Quantification of Antibody on Nanoparticle Surface

A standard curve was prepared by using a known concentration of the antibody being quantified. The number of nanoparticles in a batch was determined by Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Quantification of PTX Loading

For PTX loading measurements, nanoparticles were lyophilized, followed by dissolving in acetonitrile (ACN) and passing through a 0.2 µm filter to separate drugs from polymers. The filtered solution was analyzed at an ultraviolet absorption peak of 228 nm on high-performance liquid chromatography (Arc HPLC, Waters, USA) equipped with a reverse-phase C18 column (Waters XBridge 3.5µm, 4.6×50 mm). Cell Lines and Cell Culture

Human Raji lymphoma cells expressing GFP and luciferase were obtained from the Translational Laboratory Shared Resources (TLSR) at the University of Maryland School of Medicine. Cell line authentication and validation were performed utilizing short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and mouse antibody production (MAP) testing. The cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11875–093) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; GeminiBio, 100–106H-500), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 µg/mL of streptomycin (Pen-Strep; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15140122) (complete RPMI). Cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were subcultured twice per week and counted using a hemacytometer after staining with trypan blue.

Flow Cytometric Analysis of CD20 Surface Expression and Nanoparticle Cellular Association

Flow cytometry was employed to evaluate both the baseline surface expression of CD20 on Raji cells and the targeted cellular association of the formulated nanoparticles. All sample acquisitions were performed using a Cytek Aurora Cytometer equipped with SpectroFlo software (version 3.3.0), and the resulting data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.10.0). For all preparation steps, confluently cultured Raji cells were harvested by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Although both assays evaluated fluorescence intensity, the preparation protocols were distinctly tailored to each analytical objective. To evaluate CD20 surface expression, 1×105 cells were washed twice with cold FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% (v/v) FBS and 2 mM EDTA). The cells were then resuspended in cold FACS buffer and stained with either an APC-conjugated anti-human CD20 antibody (BioLegend, 375505) or an APC-conjugated isotype control antibody (BioLegend, 400221) at a 1:200 dilution. This receptor labeling was conducted via a 30-minute incubation on ice in the dark, followed by two washes with cold FACS buffer to remove unbound antibodies. In contrast, to analyze nanoparticle cellular association, a higher cell density (5 × 105 cells) was washed twice with PBS, resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium, and pre-incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. The cells were then treated with either Cy5-labeled IgG-DART or RTX-DART at a final concentration of 5 µg/mL for 1 hour at 37°C. To confirm the specific interaction between RTX-DART and the CD20 receptor, a competitive binding assay was conducted by pre-incubating the cells with an excess of free rituximab (500 µg/mL) for 30 minutes to block CD20 binding sites prior to the addition of RTX-DART. Following the 37°C incubation, the cells were washed four times with cold PBS before the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Cy5 was analyzed.

Confocal Microscopy and Quantitative Image Analysis

To evaluate the cellular uptake and intracellular localization of the nanoparticles, Raji cells were incubated with Cy5-labeled IgG-DART or RTX-DART at a concentration of 5 μg/mL for 1 hour at 37°C. Following the initial incubation, the cells were washed thoroughly with cold PBS to remove unbound nanoparticles and further incubated in fresh media for an additional 2 hours at 37°C. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The plasma membrane and nuclei were counterstained with Wheat Germ Agglutinin, Alexa Fluor™ 488 Conjugate (WGA-AF488; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, W11261) and DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 62248), respectively. Images were acquired using a spinning disk confocal system on a Nikon Ti2 inverted microscope equipped with dual Hamamatsu sCMOS cameras for high-resolution imaging. For objective quantitative analysis of nanoparticle uptake, the acquired confocal images were processed using ImageJ software (Fiji, version 1.54p, NIH). To eliminate user bias, the “Triangle” thresholding algorithm was applied to define precise cellular boundaries based on the WGA channel, and the “Fill Holes” function was subsequently utilized to ensure the entire intracellular area was selected. The Cy5 fluorescence intensity within these defined single-cell regions of interest (ROIs) was then measured. For statistical analysis, the single-cell intensities were averaged per image to yield a representative mean value for each field of view (n = 10 independent images per group; approximately 60–120 cells analyzed per image). Spatial distribution was analyzed by generating a cross-sectional line profile using the ROI manager in ImageJ, plotting the relative fluorescence intensity (RFI) of each channel across a defined axis to confirm intracellular localization.

In vitro Cytotoxicity Assay

For the in vitro cytotoxicity assay, Raji cells were seeded in U-bottom 96-well plates (COSTAR, 7007) at a density of 2×104 cells per well and incubated for 24 hours to allow for cell stabilization and entry into the logarithmic growth phase prior to treatment. Following this stabilization period, to administer the therapeutics, the culture medium was removed following centrifugation (400 × g, 5 min, 4°C), and the cells were resuspended in medium containing various formulations: free PTX, IgG-DART/PTX, RTX-DART/PTX, or RTX-DART. To mimic the nanoparticle clearance mechanism, the treatment medium was removed via centrifugation after 2 hours of incubation and replaced with fresh culture medium. Following an additional 24 hours of incubation, cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo, Cat# CK-04). Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm, and percent cell viability was calculated relative to the absorbance of the untreated control wells.

Stability of DART Nanoparticles

To evaluate the colloidal stability of DART nanoparticles under serum-containing conditions, 1 mg of nanoparticles was dispersed in 5 mL of PBS alone or PBS supplemented with 10% or 50% (v/v) FBS and incubated at 37°C under continuous agitation.8 At predetermined time points of 1, 2, 4, 6, 24, 48 and 72 hours, aliquots were collected and analyzed by DLS to determine hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index.

To evaluate stability over a certain period, the nanoparticles were stored in 10% sucrose solution at −20°C for 3 weeks. The particles were thawed and all the physicochemical properties were compared with freshly prepared nanoparticles to confirm stability. Similarly, in vitro cell uptake and cytotoxicity were also compared with freshly prepared particles to assess any difference in biological activities.

Immunogenic Cell Death Assay

To investigate whether RTX-DART/PTX induces immunogenic cell death (ICD), the cell surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT) was evaluated. Briefly, Raji cells were seeded in U-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells per well and treated with free PTX, IgG-DART/PTX, RTX-DART/PTX, or mitoxantrone dihydrochloride (MTX; Sigma-Aldrich, M6545). MTX, a well-known ICD inducer, was included as a positive control. After 24 hours of incubation at 37°C, the cells were harvested via centrifugation and washed twice with FACS buffer. To detect surface CRT, the cell pellets were incubated with a primary anti-calreticulin antibody (Abcam, ab22683) diluted 1:400 in FACS buffer for 1 hour at 4°C. Following three washes with FACS buffer, the cells were stained with an APC-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A865) at a 1:200 dilution for 1 hour at 4°C in the dark. After three final washes with FACS buffer, the fluorescence intensity of the cells was analyzed using a flow cytometer.

Generation and Polarization of Bone Marrow-Derived Macrophages (BMDMs)

Bone marrow cells were harvested by irrigating the femurs and tibias of 6- to 8-week-old BALB/c mice with complete RPMI medium. The collected cell suspension was gently triturated, passed through a 40-µm nylon mesh strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 352340), and spun down at 450 × g for 10 min at 4°C. To remove erythrocytes, the resulting pellet was treated with 1× RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend, 420301) for 5 min on ice. The lysis reaction was neutralized by adding an excess volume of cold PBS. Following another centrifugation step, the cells were resuspended in complete RPMI medium containing 20 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; PeproTech, 315–02). For macrophage differentiation, the cells were seeded into 100-mm petri dishes and maintained for 7 days, with fresh M-CSF supplemented every 48 hours. The successful generation of unpolarized (M0) BMDMs was validated via flow cytometric analysis utilizing an APC-conjugated anti-mouse F4/80 antibody (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17–4801-82). To induce M2 macrophage polarization, the M0 BMDMs were subsequently stimulated with 20 ng/mL of interleukin-4 (IL-4) for an additional 48 hours.

Macrophage Reprogramming Assay

To evaluate the repolarization of M2 macrophages, the previously differentiated M2 macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells per well. The cells were then treated with either free PTX or RTX-DART/PTX and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C. Following incubation, the cell culture supernatants were collected and stored for subsequent cytokine analysis, while the remaining cells were harvested via centrifugation. To prevent non-specific binding, the cell pellets were pre-incubated with an Fc receptor blocking antibody (anti-mouse CD16/32; BioLegend, 101301) diluted 1:100 in FACS buffer for 20 min at 4°C. Subsequently, the cells were stained with a cocktail of fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark, including FITC-labeled anti-mouse F4/80 (1:200; BioLegend, 123107), Pacific Blue-labeled anti-mouse CD86 (1:200; BioLegend, 105022), and APC-labeled anti-mouse CD206 (1:200; BioLegend, 105021) antibody. After three washes with FACS buffer, the surface marker expression levels were analyzed using a flow cytometer. Additionally, the secretion of IGF1, an M2 macrophage marker, in the collected supernatants was quantified using a Mouse IGF1 ELISA kit (Proteintech, KE10032) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vivo Anti-Cancer Efficacy Study in Systemic Human Lymphoma Xenograft Mouse Model

NSG-SGM3 mice were bred in-house and utilized for in vivo experiments at 6 weeks of age or older. All animals were housed and maintained at the animal facility of the University of Maryland School of Medicine. All animal procedures were designed and conducted in strict accordance with the protocols approved (IACUC# AUP-00000470) by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Maryland School of Medicine. For the in vivo anti-cancer efficacy study, a systemic human lymphoma xenograft mouse model was established. NSG-SGM3 mice were inoculated with an intravenous injection of 3×106 luciferase-expressing Raji cells via the tail vein. Four days post-inoculation, following the confirmation of systemic lymphoma development by live-animal bioluminescence imaging, animals were randomly assigned to three separate groups (n = 7). The sample size (n = 7 per group) was determined based on an a priori power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.80) to detect a significant difference in therapeutic efficacy, considering the expected effect sizes from previously established similar models. The primary endpoints of this study were defined as the longitudinal suppression of systemic tumor burden and overall survival. The groups were systemically treated with PBS, IgG-DART/PTX, or RTX-DART/PTX at a dose of 10 mg/kg on a weekly administration schedule. Throughout the course of the treatments, the tumor burden was monitored by quantifying the bioluminescence signal using the IVIS imaging system. To ensure animal welfare, mice were monitored daily for signs of distress, and body weights were recorded closely. In accordance with humane endpoint criteria, animals were euthanized if they exhibited >20% loss in initial body weight, severe lethargy, hind limb paralysis, or a moribund state. Euthanasia of mice was conducted in a manner consistent with the recommendations of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. The IACUC-sanctioned method used for euthanasia was carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation followed by cervical dislocation once breathing had ceased and mice were unconscious.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). To ensure robust statistical evaluation, all in vitro experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3), and in vivo studies were performed with seven mice per treatment group (n = 7). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.4.3, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The selection of appropriate statistical tests was based on the experimental design, the number of comparison groups, and the presence of repeated measurements. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was utilized. For evaluating differences between two groups across multiple conditions (eg, varying nanoparticle concentrations), multiple unpaired t-tests were applied, followed by the Holm-Šídák correction for multiple comparisons to strictly control the family-wise error rate. For experiments comparing three or more groups at a single time point (eg, immunogenic cell death and macrophage reprogramming assays), an ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For in vivo longitudinal studies, changes in body weight over time were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. To appropriately account for missing data points resulting from subject mortality over the course of the efficacy study, the longitudinal quantitative region-of-interest (ROI) bioluminescence data (total flux) were evaluated using a mixed-effects analysis (using Restricted Maximum Likelihood, REML), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Animal survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and statistically compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. For all analyses, statistical significance was defined a priori at an alpha level of 0.05, and exact annotations are denoted as follows: ns (not significant); *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Physicochemical Properties of Formulated Nanoparticles

We first assessed the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles to verify successful formulation and to establish baseline comparability between targeted and control constructs. Figure 1A represents a simplified schematic of the three different types of nanoparticles investigated in this study. The hydrodynamic diameters (number mean) of the PLGA-PEG nanoparticles were found to be 94.6 ± 3.0 nm, whereas the sizes of IgG-DART and RTX-DART were slightly larger, 104.0 ± 4.7 nm and 103.2 ± 3.5 nm, respectively. The intensity distributions of the nanoparticles are shown in the Figure S1. The PDI of all the groups was below 0.2, suggesting there was no significant aggregation of nanoparticles. Surface charge is reported as ζ-potential, which was found to be near neutral charge (between 0 and −5 mV). The morphology of the nanoparticles studied via TEM revealed well-dispersed spherical-shaped particles, as shown in Figure 1B-D. Drug loading of all the nanoparticle groups was between 7–8% as measured with HPLC. On average, approximately 7 IgG and 8 RTX antibodies were conjugated on the surface of the IgG-DART and RTX-DART, respectively. CD20-Targeted RTX-DART Nanoparticles are Efficiently Internalized by NHL Cells

Having established the specific binding capability of RTX-DART to CD20 on Raji cells, we next sought to visually confirm whether these bound nanoparticles undergo efficient intracellular internalization, a prerequisite for successful paclitaxel delivery. Confocal laser scanning microscopy was utilized to track the spatial distribution of Cy5-labeled nanoparticles (Figure 3A, left). Cells treated with the non-targeted IgG-DART exhibited a basal level of nanoparticle fluorescence. In comparison, incubation with RTX-DART resulted in a significantly enhanced accumulation of the nanoparticles within the cellular boundaries demarcated by the WGA membrane stain. Quantitative single-cell image analysis further corroborated these observations, revealing a profound and statistically significant increase in the mean fluorescence intensity of Cy5 in the RTX-DART group compared to the IgG-DART control (Figure 3A, right). To rule out the possibility of nanoparticles merely adhering to the outer cell surface, we performed a cross-sectional fluorescence line profile analysis on a representative single cell (Figure 3B). The line scan clearly demonstrated that the strong Cy5 signal (RTX-DART) was localized entirely between the two distinct WGA fluorescence peaks representing the plasma membrane. Taken together, these results provide direct spatial evidence that CD20 targeting not only facilitates binding but actively drives the robust intracellular internalization of DART nanoparticles in human Raji NHL cells. Stability of DART Nanoparticles

The short-term colloidal stability of DART nanoparticles was evaluated following incubation in PBS and serum-containing media at 37°C. No appreciable changes in hydrodynamic diameter or PDI were observed following incubation in either 10% or 50% FBS for up to 72 h, with values remaining comparable to those measured in PBS. These findings indicate that exposure to serum proteins did not induce detectable changes in nanoparticle hydrodynamic properties under the tested conditions (Figure S2).

To evaluate long-term storage stability, RTX-DART nanoparticles were stored at −20 °C in 10% (w/v) sucrose for 3 weeks and characterized after thawing. Post-thaw measurements showed that the hydrodynamic diameter was not affected due to the storage (Figure 5A), with PDI remaining around 0.16 for both fresh and stored particles (Figure 5B). Other key characteristics of nanoparticles, including ζ-potential, drug loading and antibodies per nanoparticle, were also unaffected by the long-term storage (Table T5). To assess whether long-term storage impacted CD20-mediated association, cellular association in Raji cells was quantified by flow cytometry. Stored RTX-DART exhibited a comparable association relative to fresh RTX-DART (Figure 5C). Finally, in vitro cytotoxicity assays showed that stored RTX-DART showed similar cytotoxicity compared to fresh RTX-DART (Figure 5D). Overall, −20 °C storage in 10% sucrose for 3 weeks preserved the physicochemical properties and functional performance of RTX-DART. Nanoparticle-Induced Immunogenic Cell Death (ICD)

Immunogenic cell death (ICD) was evaluated by quantifying cell-surface calreticulin (CRT) expression in Raji cells using flow cytometry, reported as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Compared to untreated controls, mitoxantrone (MTX; positive control) induced a pronounced increase in CRT MFI, confirming induction of an ICD-associated response. Representative flow cytometry histograms showed a rightward shift in CRT fluorescence following RTX-DART/PTX treatment compared with free PTX and IgG-DART/PTX, while MTX exhibited the greatest shift in CRT fluorescence (Figure 6A). Treatment with free PTX also increased CRT MFI compared with control. Importantly, PTX delivery via CD20-targeted nanoparticles RTX-DART/PTX resulted in significantly greater CRT surface expression than free PTX (Figure 6B), indicating enhanced ICD-associated CRT exposure. In contrast, the non-targeted nanoparticle control IgG-DART/PTX and free PTX exhibited significantly lower CRT MFI than RTX-DART/PTX. Collectively, these data demonstrate that CD20-targeted DART-mediated PTX delivery enhanced CRT surface exposure compared to free drug and non-targeted nanoparticle. Nanoparticles Induce Macrophage Reprogramming

Nanoparticle-mediated macrophage reprogramming was assessed by flow cytometry quantification of the M1-associated marker CD86 (reported as MFI), along with measurement of IGF1 secretion in supernatants of mouse macrophage cultures. As expected, M2-polarized macrophages exhibited elevated CD206 (Figure 7A) and reduced CD86 relative to the M0 condition (Figure 7B). Treatment of M2-polarized macrophages with free PTX or RTX-DART/PTX reduced CD206 surface expression compared with the untreated M2 condition (Figure 7A). In addition, all PTX-containing conditions increased CD86 expressions relative to M2-polarized macrophages, indicating a shift toward an M1-like surface phenotype (Figure 7B). Consistent with these surface marker changes, high IGF1 secretion under M2-like conditions was significantly decreased following PTX treatment (Figure 7C). RTX-DART/PTX also reduced IGF1 relative to the M2-polarized control, with IGF1 levels remaining significantly above the free PTX group (Figure 7C). Collectively, these data demonstrate that PTX delivered either as free drug or in DART formulations modulates canonical M2/M1-associated markers and suppresses M2-associated IGF1 production, with the magnitude of IGF1 suppression differing between free and nanoparticle-delivered PTX. In vivo Anti-Lymphoma Efficacy in a Systemic Raji Xenograft Model in NSG-SGM3 Mice

The safety and treatment tolerability were assessed by tracking body weight over time. Across the groups, the body weights remained largely stable with the PBS group exhibiting the most noticeable decline during later stages of disease progression (Figure 8A). All treated groups, including IgG-DART/PTX and RTX-DART/PTX, maintained comparatively higher body weight percentages, suggesting acceptable systemic tolerability under the weekly 10 mg/kg dosing regimen. IVIS bioluminescence imaging (BLI) confirmed rapid establishment and progression of lymphoma following intravenous inoculation of luciferase-expressing Raji cells in NSG-SGM3 mice. BLI revealed a marked increase in whole-body radiance in the PBS group (control group) over time, with a strong systemic signal evident by days 12–16 post-inoculation (Figure 8B). Mice treated with IgG-DART/PTX showed a trend toward reduced tumor-associated signal relative to PBS, but substantial radiance was still apparent at later time points, indicating continued disease progression. In contrast, RTX-DART/PTX produced the most pronounced suppression of bioluminescent signal throughout the study window, with visibly lower systemic radiance compared to both PBS and IgG-DART/PTX, consistent with improved control of disseminated tumor burden (Figure 8C). Quantification of total flux on day 14 further supported these observations, showing reduced whole-body bioluminescence in the RTX-DART/PTX group compared with controls, with statistical significance indicated in the plot (Figure 8C). Consistent with tumor control, Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated improved survival outcomes in the RTX-DART/PTX cohort relative to PBS and the non-targeted IgG-DART/PTX control, with statistical differences annotated on the plot (Figure 8D). Together, these data indicate that CD20-targeted RTX-DART/PTX enhances in vivo anti-lymphoma efficacy in a systemic Raji xenograft model, leading to lower disease-associated bioluminescence and extended survival compared with non-targeted and vehicle controls. Discussion

A central challenge in translating advanced therapeutics is that therapeutic success depends less on prolonged residence in a localized tumor mass and more on efficient cell-level engagement during transient exposure in circulation and lymphoid compartments.27–29 In this context, CD20 targeting of the RTX-DART/PTX provides a significant delivery advantage under clearance constraints, rather than relying on passive accumulation mechanisms that dominate many solid-tumor nanomedicine paradigms.30,31 The CD20 receptor-blocking experiment establishes that enhanced association is driven by CD20-specific binding and not by nonspecific adhesion or formulation differences, thereby supporting a mechanistic link between ligand presentation and cell engagement.14 Confocal microscopy provided complementary spatial evidence, showing significantly greater Cy5 fluorescence within WGA-defined cellular boundaries following treatment with RTX-DART compared with IgG-DART. Cross-sectional line-profile analysis further localized the Cy5 signal between the plasma membrane peaks, supporting intracellular localization rather than exclusive retention at the cell surface. Together, the flow-cytometry and confocal findings indicate that rituximab conjugation increases CD20-dependent cellular association and intracellular localization of nanoparticle-associated material. These complementary association and localization findings become functionally consequential in the short-exposure, clearance-mimicking cytotoxicity design, which better reflects the time-limited drug exposure expected in vivo and is therefore more stringent than conventional continuous-incubation assays.32 Under these conditions, the improved cell killing observed with RTX-DART/PTX compared with free PTX and non-targeted IgG-DART/PTX supports the interpretation that CD20 targeting increases the amount of PTX-loaded nanoparticle material associated with and retained by Raji cells during the exposure window, thereby increasing effective intracellular drug action. The in vivo suppression of systemic tumor burden and improved survival further reinforce the translational relevance of this mechanism by demonstrating that the CD20 targeting advantage persists when distribution and clearance dominate exposure, which is the critical therapeutic challenge in systemic lymphoma.

RTX-DART/PTX increased cell-surface calreticulin exposure relative to free PTX and the non-targeted control, indicating that CD20-targeted PTX delivery enhanced this ICD-associated cellular stress response. PTX has previously been reported to induce calreticulin exposure and other immune-associated stress signals, providing biological context for the observed effect.33–35 However, calreticulin exposure alone is insufficient to establish immunogenic cell death, which requires additional molecular and functional validation such as assessment of ATP and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release, type I interferon signaling, annexin A1 (ANXA1).36,37 Accordingly, the present data support enhanced calreticulin surface exposure associated with targeted PTX delivery but do not demonstrate a complete scenario of immunogenic cell death. PTX and RTX-DART/PTX also altered polarization-associated surface markers and reduced IGF1 secretion in murine bone marrow-derived macrophages in vitro. Because CD20 targeting is intended to increase association with CD20-positive lymphoma cells rather than macrophages, these responses are most reasonably attributed to macrophage exposure to PTX, including PTX released from the nanoparticle formulation. Given the differences in species and immune context between the in vitro macrophage assay and the in vivo xenograft model, further studies will be required to determine whether these macrophage-associated effects are reproduced within the tumor microenvironment in vivo.

From a translational standpoint, the significance of this DART platform lies in how its architecture supports targeted delivery while maintaining practical formulation attributes. Polymeric nanoparticle systems have been widely explored for the delivery of hydrophobic small molecules because they enable encapsulation, protection, and controlled release of payloads within a biodegradable core while presenting a functionalized surface for biological interactions.38–41 In an antibody-functionalized PLGA-PEG nanoparticle format, the targeting ligand is displayed at the particle interface, while the bulk therapeutic payload is contained within the polymer matrix, allowing payload loading and release behavior to be tuned through formulation parameters such as polymer composition, drug-to-polymer ratio, and processing conditions.39–42 This separation of targeting function and payload compartment provides flexibility for optimizing delivery without directly modifying the antibody component. In addition, translational handling is an important consideration for antibody-functionalized nanocarriers, as storage and processing conditions can impact both colloidal stability and ligand functionality. RTX-DART maintained its physicochemical properties and, importantly, retained targeting-associated function after long-term storage at −20 °C in sucrose. This is important because freeze-thaw stresses can potentially impair both colloidal stability and interfacial protein function, and sucrose-based cryoprotection has been shown to preserve PLGA nanoparticle stability and functional performance during freeze-thaw challenges.43–46 Preserving both stability and targeting capability supports manufacturability and cold-chain handling in a way that is directly relevant to clinical translation.

Another important implication of this work is the broader applicability of the DART platform across different diseases. DART nanoparticles were developed as a platform strategy to reduce nonspecific interactions while preserving receptor-mediated binding, and this design principle has been successfully applied to Fn14-targeted systems in multiple solid tumor settings. Prior studies have demonstrated that Fn14-directed DART nanoparticles for glioblastoma, triple-negative breast cancer, and brain metastasis models improved tumor localization and therapeutic response.16,17,47 The present work extends the DART platform to hematologic malignancy by conjugating RTX to target CD20, a clinically validated B-cell surface receptor in non-Hodgkin lymphoma. These studies established that the DART formulation platform can be adapted to different disease-relevant receptors while maintaining its core design advantages, including reduced nonspecific adhesivity and efficient receptor-mediated association.

In interpreting the in vivo outcomes, it should be noted that disseminated Raji-based xenografts in highly immunodeficient hosts are generally considered a stringent efficacy setting, because disease progresses rapidly and therapeutic exposure is dominated by systemic distribution and clearance rather than sustained retention within a single lesion.48–50 Therefore, the magnitude of tumor growth suppression and survival extension observed with RTX-DART/PTX is consistent with the level of control that has been reported for other PTX-based therapeutic approaches in related Raji lymphoma models.22,25,51 The key point of this RTX-DART/PTX strategy is that the therapeutic benefit is observed in a disseminated setting where cellular-level engagement under clearance constraints is likely to be a decisive determinant of response. At the same time, the current NSG-SGM3 Raji NHL model is not suitable to evaluate the full contribution of adaptive immune mechanisms because NSG-based backgrounds lack functional T and B cells for intact antitumor immunity.52,53 This is particularly relevant for CD20-targeted therapies, as RTX-mediated anti-tumor activity involves immune effector mechanisms such as complement-dependent cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, which depend on functional complement and immune effector cells that are substantially impaired in NSG-SGM3 mice.54 As a result, the in vivo efficacy observed in this model primarily reflects direct cytotoxic and delivery-related effects and may underestimate the full therapeutic potential of CD20-targeted RTX-DART/PTX nanoparticles in an immunocompetent setting. The in vitro calreticulin and macrophage findings therefore provide a rationale for further investigation in models with intact immune function. Future studies in immunocompetent or humanized lymphoma models, including evaluation of rational combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-1 antibodies, will be required to determine whether these immune-associated cellular responses contribute to antitumor efficacy in vivo.

An important limitation of the present in vivo study is the absence of free PTX, drug-free RTX-DART, and rituximab-alone treatment groups. Although the formulation-matched IgG-DART/PTX control enables comparison between the targeted and non-targeted formulations, the current experimental design does not fully distinguish the respective contributions of nanoparticle-mediated PTX delivery, CD20 targeting, and rituximab-associated therapeutic effects. Therefore, the in vivo findings are limited to demonstrating greater therapeutic activity of RTX-DART/PTX relative to PBS and IgG-DART/PTX under the conditions evaluated. Additional mechanistic control groups would be required to determine the contribution of each formulation component.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides proof-of-concept evidence that rituximab-conjugated DART nanoparticles can enable CD20-targeted delivery of paclitaxel in systemic non-Hodgkin lymphoma. RTX-DART/PTX retained its physicochemical properties and biological activity following frozen storage, exhibited CD20-dependent association with Raji cells, and enhanced cytotoxicity following short-term treatment compared with free paclitaxel and non-targeted nanoparticles. In the systemic Raji xenograft model, RTX-DART/PTX reduced tumor-associated bioluminescence and improved survival relative to vehicle and non-targeted controls. RTX-DART/PTX also enhanced cell-surface calreticulin exposure, while paclitaxel-containing treatments altered macrophage polarization-associated markers and IGF1 secretion in vitro; however, these findings do not establish complete immunogenic cell death or in vivo immune remodeling. Further studies in additional lymphoma models, immunocompetent or humanized systems, and pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies are required to define the therapeutic scope, immune contributions, and translational potential of this platform.
