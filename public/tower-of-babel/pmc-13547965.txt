GmERF109 Positively Enhances Soybean Resistance to Phytophthora sojae by Transcriptionally Activating GmG4DT‐Like to Promote Glyceollin I Accumulation
Authors: Xin Fang, Rui Liang, Yiling Cheng, Hong Gao, Chuanzhong Zhang, Jingcan Sun, Junjun Song, Gengpu Zhang, Meng Liu, Yuxin Zhao, Yan Sun, Xiran Wang, Xiaoyu Chen, Kexin Ma, Shuo Wang, Kexin Li, Jiapeng Tong, Junjiang Wu, Pengfei Xu, Shuzhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhytophthora root rot, a devastating disease caused by Phytophthora sojae, poses a significant threat to worldwide soybean ( Glycine max ) production. Therefore, enhancing crop resistance to this pathogen is a major breeding objective. However, the signalling mechanisms underlying the response of soybean plants to P. sojae infection, and the networks and targets of key transcription factors TFs, are not yet fully understood. Here, we reveal the mechanisms and function of GmERF109, which differs in expression between soybean cultivars resistant and susceptible to P. sojae race 1 and encodes an AP2/ERF transcription factor. Molecular evaluation and disease resistance analysis show that GmERF109 is a nucleus‐localized transcription factor that positively regulates soybean resistance to P. sojae. We also demonstrate that GmERF109 targets and activates the expression of GmG4DT‐like, a gene whose role in the biosynthesis of the phytoalexin glyceollin was confirmed through overexpression and RNA interference (RNAi) analyses. GmG4DT‐like also enhances P. sojae resistance. GmG4DT‐like and GmERF109 greatly increased the content of the glyceollin I isomer. Overall, our results suggest that GmERF109 enhances glyceollin accumulation by positively regulating the expression of its target gene GmG4DT‐like, thereby improving soybean resistance to P. sojae. These findings provide novel insights into soybean resistance to Phytophthora root rot and will be useful in efforts to create resistant soybean cultivars.Keywords: AP2/ERF transcription factor, disease resistance, glyceollin, Phytophthora sojae, soybean

Introduction

The adaptation of plants to unfavourable environmental conditions, such as biotic stresses, involves complex networks of regulatory mechanisms centered around transcription factors (TFs). Many of these TFs modulate gene expression in response to pathogen infection, thereby facilitating plant survival and adaptation (Ashapkin et al. 2020; Jia et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025). Among these, AP2/ERF (APETALA2/Ethylene Responsive Factor) TFs are a plant‐specific superfamily whose members generally contain one or two AP2/ERF domains involved in sequence‐specific DNA binding to a GCC‐box (Feng et al. 2005; Nakano et al. 2006; Zhang et al. 2025). Accumulating evidence has shown that AP2/ERF TFs play pivotal roles in plant responses to fungal, bacterial, oomycete, and viral infections (Gao et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2024).

Mechanistically, AP2/ERF TFs modulate plant defence responses by regulating pathogenesis‐related (PR) genes, hormonal signalling pathways, and physical barriers (Ma et al. 2024; Su et al. 2025). For instance, GmERF113 positively regulates resistance to Phytophthora sojae through the activation of PR1 and PR10‐1 expression in soybean ( Glycine max ) (Zhao et al. 2017). Gossypium barbadense GbERF1‐like transactivates the promoters of key lignin biosynthesis genes GhHCT1 and AtPAL3, thereby enhancing resistance to Verticillium wilt (Guo et al. 2016). SlERF2 promotes the accumulation of salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) to enhance the immune response to Stemphylium lycopersici in tomato (Yang, Liu, et al. 2021; Yang, Sun, et al. 2021). Furthermore, as a major component of direct chemical defence, the production of diverse phytoalexins is also orchestrated by AP2/ERF transcription factors (Meraj et al. 2020; Zhan et al. 2022; Zeng et al. 2025). For example, NbERF‐IX‐33 regulates phytoalexin production to confer resistance of Nicotiana benthamiana to Phytophthora infestans (Imano et al. 2022). ERF72 enhances resistance to the necrotrophic pathogen Botrytis cinerea by positively regulating the biosynthesis of the phytoalexin camalexin in Arabidopsis (Li, Liu, et al. 2022; Li, Wu, et al. 2022).

The major phytoalexins in soybean are daidzein, genistein, and glyceollin isomers I, II and III, of which glyceollins are biosynthesized from daidzein precursors (Lyne et al. 1976). Glyceollins can effectively suppress the growth of P. sojae and are therefore considered a key component of soybean defence (Graham et al. 2007; Jahan et al. 2020; Lygin et al. 2013). The glyceollin biosynthetic pathway requires phenylalanine ammonia‐lyase (PAL), 4‐coumarate‐CoA ligase (4CL), chalcone synthase (CHS), chalcone isomerase (CHI), isoflavone synthase (IFS), and prenyltransferases including 4‐dimethylallyltransferase (G4DT) and 2‐dimethylallyltransferase (G2DT) (Moy et al. 2004; Akashi et al. 2009). G4DT and G2DT produce the direct precursors of glyceollin I and glyceollin II, respectively (Akashi et al. 2009; Xie et al. 2025). Recently, the final cytochrome P450 cyclase genes required for completing the glyceollin biosynthetic pathway have been identified (Xie et al. 2025; Khatri et al. 2025). The transcriptional regulation of glyceollin biosynthesis has also been partially elucidated. The NAC family protein GmNAC42‐1 positively regulates glyceollin biosynthesis by directly binding the promoters of IFS2 and G4DT (Jahan et al. 2019). The MYB family protein GmMYB29A2 regulates soybean resistance to P. sojae through the glyceollin pathway (Jahan et al. 2020). The heat shock transcription factor GmHSF‐1 directly binds the promoters of HIDH (2‐hydroxyisoflavanone dehydratase) and I2′H (isoflavone 2′‐hydroxylase) to activate glyceollin biosynthesis (Lin et al. 2023). GmWRKY72 negatively regulates glyceollin biosynthesis by directly binding the promoters of several biosynthetic genes, including IFS2, PTS1, P6αH, and CHR. The abscisic acid (ABA)‐regulated JAZ1 protein also suppresses glyceollin accumulation by interacting with GmNAC42‐1 and GmMYB29A1 (Lin et al. 2023, 2025). However, whether AP2/ERF TFs regulate glyceollin biosynthesis and thereby contribute to resistance against P. sojae remains poorly understood.

In a previous study, we demonstrated that the AP2/ERF TF GmAP2 functions as a negative regulator in response to P. sojae infection (Zhang et al. 2021). Here, we focused on the function of GmERF109 (a down‐regulated AP2/ERF TF gene from the GmAP2‐OE transgenic hairy root transcriptome) in the response to P. sojae infection. Molecular, biochemical, and physiological analyses revealed that GmERF109 enhances soybean resistance to P. sojae by activating the expression of a target gene, GmG4DT‐like, which we further demonstrated to be sufficient for promoting glyceollin I accumulation, thereby enhancing disease resistance. These findings expand our understanding of the transcriptional regulatory network controlling glyceollin biosynthesis and demonstrate that AP2/ERF TFs contribute to soybean resistance against P. sojae through this pathway, providing insights that may facilitate crop improvement.

Results

GmERF109 Expression Differs Between Resistant and Susceptible Soybean Cultivars

Our previous study identified GmAP2 as a negative regulator of soybean resistance to P. sojae. RNA‐seq analysis of GmAP2‐overexpressing transgenic soybean hairy roots revealed a significantly downregulated ERF family TF (Zhang et al. 2021), which we designated as GmERF109. To investigate whether GmERF109 participates in the response to P. sojae infection, we measured the expression levels of GmERF109 in five susceptible and five resistant soybean cultivars. In susceptible cultivars, GmERF109 expression showed no significant induction or only modest, transient increases, with peak levels at 3 or 6 h post‐inoculation (hpi) generally remaining below 2‐fold relative to 0 hpi (Figure 1A–E). In contrast, GmERF109 was rapidly and strongly induced in all tested resistant cultivars, with transcript levels peaking at 12 hpi and reaching approximately 2 to 8‐fold increases compared to 0 hpi (Figure 1F–J). This differing expression pattern between resistant and susceptible cultivars indicates that GmERF109 is likely involved in soybean defence responses against P. sojae.

GmERF109 Is a Nucleus‐Localized TF That Positively Regulates Resistance to P. sojae

To elucidate the role of GmERF109 in regulating soybean resistance to P. sojae, we generated two types of transgenic soybean plants using the susceptible cultivar Dongnong 50, that is, GmERF109‐overexpressing lines (GmERF109‐OE) driven by the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter, and GmERF109 RNA interference lines (GmERF109‐RNAi). The GmERF109‐OE lines were validated by immunoblot analysis, which confirmed the accumulation of GmERF109 protein (Figure S1A), and by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR), which revealed significantly elevated transcript levels of GmERF109 (Figure S1B). The GmERF109‐RNAi lines were verified using a QuickStix Kit for LibertyLink bar strips (Figure S1C), and RT‐qPCR analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in GmERF109 expression compared with the wild type (WT) (Figure S1D). To assess the contribution of GmERF109 to P. sojae resistance, transgenic and WT plants were subjected to pathogen inoculation assays using P. sojae race 1. Disease symptoms were markedly attenuated in GmERF109‐OE plants, whereas GmERF109‐RNAi plants exhibited substantially more severe rotting symptoms than the control plants (Figure 2A). Consistent with these phenotypic observations, quantitative analysis revealed that the relative abundance of P. sojae and lesion size were significantly reduced in GmERF109‐OE lines, whereas GmERF109‐RNAi plants exhibited the opposite trend (Figure 2B,C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GmERF109 positively regulates soybean resistance to P. sojae.

To determine the subcellular localization of GmERF109, we expressed a GmERF109‐green fluorescent protein (GFP) fusion protein in Arabidopsis protoplasts. A GFP signal was observed in protoplast cells harbouring the 35S pro :GFP vector, whereas the GmERF109‐GFP fluorescence signal was localized specifically within the nucleus (Figure 2D). GmERF109 localization to the nucleus is consistent with it functioning as a TF.

To verify the transcriptional activation function of GmERF109, we conducted a transient expression assay in yeast using a GAL4‐responsive reporter system. Transformed yeast cells carrying DBD‐P53 + T‐antigen (pGBKT7‐53 + pGADT7‐T, positive control), BD‐GmERF109 (pGBKT7‐GmERF109), and DBD‐GmWRKY31 (pGBKT7‐GmWRKY31) (Fan et al. 2017)—the latter of which was previously shown to possess transcriptional activation capacity—all grew well on synthetic dropout medium lacking tryptophan, histidine, and adenine (SD [−Trp/−His/−Ade]) and displayed α‐galactosidase (α‐Gal) activity. In contrast, empty‐vector control cells (pGBKT7, negative control) showed no α‐Gal activity (Figure S2). Collectively, these results confirm that GmERF109 functions as a transcriptional activator.

GmERF109 Positively Regulates the Expression of Glyceollin Biosynthesis Genes

To identify genes regulated by GmERF109, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) on GmERF109‐overexpressing (OE) transgenic plants and wild‐type (WT) controls. In total, 226 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (fold change > 2.0, FDR < 0.01), with 154 genes up‐regulated and 72 down‐regulated in the OE plants (Figure 3A,B and Table S1). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the up‐regulated DEGs revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in pathways integral to glyceollin biosynthesis, such as ‘Flavonoid biosynthetic process’, ‘Phenylpropanoid metabolic process’ and ‘Trihydroxypterocarpan dimethylallyltransferase activity’ (Figure 3C). Furthermore, RT‐qPCR validation confirmed that the transcript levels of key genes in these pathways—including GmG4DT‐like, GmG4DT, GmCHR6, Gm4CL3, GmCHI1B2, GmCHS7, and GmIFS—were markedly elevated in GmERF109‐OE plants but significantly reduced in GmERF109‐RNAi lines compared to the WT. Notably, GmG4DT‐like exhibited the most pronounced transcriptional change among all transgenic lines (Figure 3D).

GmG4DT‐like Is a Direct Target of GmERF109

To identify potential targets of GmERF109, we analysed the promoter regions of GmG4DT‐like, GmG4DT, GmCHR6, Gm4CL3, GmCHI1B2, GmCHS7, and GmIFS for the presence of a GCC‐box. Among the analysed promoters, only GmG4DT‐like was found to contain a GCC‐box, located at position −166 bp (Figure 4A and Table S2). We next used chromatin immunoprecipitation–quantitative PCR (ChIP‐qPCR) assays to examine the binding of GmERF109 to GmG4DT‐like promoter regions in the roots of GmERF109‐OE transgenic plants, which express Myc‐tagged GmERF109. The region in the GmG4DT‐like promoter containing the GCC‐box was significantly enriched following immunoprecipitation with anti‐Myc (Figure 4B), but there was no enrichment of the b region, which lacks a GCC‐box. Similarly, we found no enrichment in two promoter regions of the housekeeping gene Elongation Factor 1 (GmEF1; Figure 4B), which also lack GCC‐boxes. GmEF1 is frequently selected as an internal control gene in soybean studies due to its stable and ubiquitous expression across different tissues and developmental stages, and its minimal responsiveness to external treatments. The ChIP‐qPCR results indicated that GmERF109 significantly associates with the GCC‐box‐containing region of the GmG4DT‐like promoter, whereas regions lacking the GCC‐box showed no enrichment. Together, these ChIP‐qPCR results indicate that GmERF109 associates with the GCC‐box‐containing region of the GmG4DT‐like promoter.

To functionally validate the regulation of GmG4DT‐like by GmERF109, we performed a transient dual‐luciferase reporter assay in N. benthamiana leaves. The effector construct featured GmERF109 driven by the 35S promoter (35Spro:GmERF109‐Myc). For the reporter, we used two constructs: one with the luciferase (LUC) gene driven by the wild‐type GmG4DT‐like promoter (pGmG4DT‐like:LUC), and another with a mutated GCC‐box in the promoter (pGmG4DT‐like‐mut:LUC; Figure 4C). Co‐expression of GmERF109 with the wild‐type reporter led to a marked increase in LUC activity compared to the empty vector (EV) control. However, this activation was abolished when the GCC‐box was mutated (Figure 4D,E). These results demonstrate that GmERF109 positively regulates GmG4DT‐like expression via the GCC‐box element in its promoter.

GmG4DT‐like Positively Regulates P. sojae Resistance by Increasing Glyceollin I

We investigated the potential role of GmG4DT‐like in soybean responses to P. sojae by comparing the phenotypes of control (EV), GmG4DT‐like overexpression (GmG4DT‐like‐OE), and GmG4DT‐like‐RNAi hairy roots following inoculation with P. sojae zoospores. GmG4DT‐like‐OE transgenic hairy roots were verified by fluorescence screening (Figure S3A) and RT‐qPCR analysis (Figure S3B), whereas GmG4DT‐like‐RNAi transgenic roots were identified using a QuickStix LibertyLink bar strip assay (Figure S3C) and further confirmed by RT‐qPCR (Figure S3D). At 72 hpi, GmG4DT‐like‐OE hairy roots showed almost no visible lesions (Figure 5A), whereas GmG4DT‐like‐RNAi lines developed more extensive rot and water‐soaked lesions than EV controls (Figure 5A). Consistently, the relative abundance of P. sojae in infected roots revealed a significant reduction in the GmG4DT‐like‐OE lines and a significant increase in the GmG4DT‐like‐RNAi lines relative to EV plants (Figure 5B). Together, these findings demonstrate that GmG4DT‐like acts as a positive regulator of resistance to P. sojae.

GmG4DT catalyses the key prenylation step in glyceollin I biosynthesis via two conserved catalytic motifs (Motif 1: NQxxDxxxD and Motif 2: KDxxDxEGD; Akashi et al. 2009; Sukumaran et al. 2018). To test whether GmG4DT‐like positively regulates resistance to P. sojae by boosting glyceollin I accumulation, we first compared the amino acid sequences of GmG4DT‐like and GmG4DT. Sequence alignment revealed that GmG4DT‐like exhibits 82.6% amino acid identity to GmG4DT and contains both of the aforementioned conserved catalytic motifs (Figure 5C).

Subcellular localization analysis further revealed that GmG4DT‐like is localized in the nucleus and cytoplasm (Figure 5D). We then measured glyceollin I levels in EV, GmG4DT‐like‐OE, and GmG4DT‐like‐RNAi transgenic soybean hairy roots under P. sojae infection, and the results showed that glyceollin I was significantly elevated in GmG4DT‐like‐OE transgenic hairy roots but lower in GmG4DT‐like‐RNAi transgenic hairy roots compared to EV (Figure 5E). Furthermore, glyceollin I was confirmed to inhibit the growth of P. sojae race 1 (Figure 5F,G). Collectively, these results indicate that GmG4DT‐like positively regulates resistance to P. sojae by enhancing glyceollin I accumulation.

GmERF109 Enhances Resistance to P. sojae by Transcriptionally Activating GmG4DT‐Like to Promote Glyceollin I Accumulation

To determine whether GmERF109 enhances resistance to P. sojae by promoting glyceollin I accumulation, we first quantified glyceollin I levels in transgenic plants following P. sojae infection. We found significantly more glyceollin I accumulation in GmERF109‐OE lines compared to WT plants, while GmERF109‐RNAi lines exhibited the opposite effect (Figure 6A). Given that other TFs are also known to regulate glyceollin I synthesis, we sought to determine if they are part of the GmERF109 regulatory network. We measured the transcript levels of several known regulatory genes (GmG4DT, GmG4DT‐like, GmNAC42‐1, GmMYB29A2, GmMYB29A1, GmHSF6‐1, GmWRKY72, and GmJAZ1‐9) in WT and transgenic lines at 12 hpi with P. sojae. Compared to WT, only the expression of GmG4DT‐like, GmG4DT, and GmNAC42‐1 changed significantly in the GmERF109 transgenic lines. Notably, in GmERF109‐OE lines, their expression levels were 23.6, 3.0, and 1.9 times higher than in WT, respectively (Figure 6B). This suggests that GmERF109 primarily promotes glyceollin I accumulation by strongly upregulating its target gene, GmG4DT‐like.

To further characterise this regulatory relationship, we analysed the expression of both GmERF109 and GmG4DT‐like in five resistant and five susceptible soybean cultivars in response to P. sojae. In susceptible cultivars, both genes showed no or only weak and transient induction (Figure 6C–G). In contrast, within resistant cultivars, GmERF109 was rapidly and strongly induced, with its expression pattern closely paralleling that of GmG4DT‐like. Both genes reached 2‐ to 8‐fold higher expression levels at 12 hpi (Figure 6H–L). This coordinated and stronger induction in resistant cultivars suggests that regulation of GmG4DT‐like by GmERF109 is a key component of an effective defence pathway against P. sojae. Collectively, our results demonstrate that GmERF109 enhances resistance to P. sojae by transcriptionally activating GmG4DT‐like to promote glyceollin I accumulation.

Discussion

GmERF109 Is a Nucleus‐Localized AP2/ERF TF That Positively Regulates Soybean Resistance to P. sojae

TFs of the AP2/ERF family have been widely documented as key regulators of plant defence against diverse pathogens (Li, Liu, et al. 2022; Li, Wu, et al. 2022; Thilakarathne et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2024). For example, overexpression of OsERF83 in rice enhances resistance to the fungal pathogen Magnaporthe oryzae (Tezuka et al. 2019). In Physcomitrium patens, the moss‐specific TF PpERF24 is induced upon fungal infection and confers enhanced resistance to both B. cinerea and Colletotrichum gloeosporioides (Reboledo et al. 2022). In soybean, AP2/ERF family members have also been implicated in resistance to P. sojae. GmERF5 positively regulates the expression of PR1‐1 and PR10‐1 during the response to P. sojae (Dong et al. 2015), and GmERF1 enhances resistance to P. sojae through salicylic acid signalling (Zhang et al. 2025). Consistent with these previous studies, our results showed that GmERF109 was strongly induced by P. sojae infection in resistant soybean cultivars, whereas its induction was weak or absent in susceptible cultivars, suggesting that GmERF109 plays an important role in the defence response to P. sojae (Figure 1). Further phenotypic analyses demonstrated that GmERF109 positively regulates soybean resistance to P. sojae (Figure 2A–C). AP2/ERF TFs often mediate disease resistance by binding to cis‐acting elements such as the GCC‐box in the promoters of defence‐related genes, thereby regulating their transcription. For instance, GmERF3 binds to the GCC‐box and activates the transcription of PR genes in response to pathogen attack (Zhang et al.2009). In agreement with this mode of action, we found that GmERF109 is predominantly localized in the nucleus (Figure 2D) and can bind to the GCC‐box in the promoters of its downstream target genes (Figure 4). Together, these findings demonstrate that GmERF109 functions as a nucleus‐localized AP2/ERF TF and positively regulates soybean resistance to P. sojae, further supporting the important role of AP2/ERF family members in soybean defence against Phytophthora root rot.

GmG4DT‐Like Enhances Resistance to P. sojae by Promoting Glyceollin I Biosynthesis

Secondary metabolites play crucial roles in plant–microbe interactions, where they primarily participate in responses to pathogen infection (Liu et al. 2025; Zernova et al. 2014). Glyceollins are major pathogen‐induced phytoalexins in soybean that confer resistance to P. sojae (Chen et al. 2019; Cheng et al. 2015). Consistent with this, our results show that glyceollin I effectively inhibits the growth of P. sojae race 1 (Figure 5F,G). To investigate the role of GmG4DT‐like in glyceollin biosynthesis, we compared it to the well‐characterized key enzyme, GmG4DT. GmG4DT is a member of the UbiA prenyltransferase superfamily that catalyses glyceollin I biosynthesis via two conserved motifs (Motif 1: NQxxDxxxD and Motif 2: KDxxDxEGD), which are essential for the enzymatic activity of this superfamily (Akashi et al. 2009; Sukumaran et al. 2018). Our analysis revealed that GmG4DT‐like not only shares a high amino acid homology of 82.6% with GmG4DT but, critically, also possesses these two conserved catalytic motifs (Figure 5C). Additionally, we determined that the GmG4DT‐like protein is localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure 5D). Most importantly, our phenotypic validation provides direct evidence for the function of GmG4DT‐like. In transgenic hairy roots, overexpression of GmG4DT‐like significantly increased glyceollin I content and enhanced resistance to P. sojae, while its suppression had the opposite effects (Figure 5A,B,E). Taken together, the combined evidence from sequence homology, conserved functional motifs, and phenotypic analysis strongly supports that GmG4DT‐like enhances resistance to P. sojae by promoting glyceollin I biosynthesis.

GmERF109 Functions as a Key Activator in the Glyceollin I Biosynthesis Network

The biosynthesis of glyceollin is governed by a complex transcriptional network involving multiple TF families, including NAC, MYB, WRKY, and HSF (Jahan et al. 2019, 2020; Lin et al. 2023, 2024). Among these regulators, GmNAC42‐1 and GmMYB29A2 act as positive regulators by binding to the promoters of IFS2 and GmG4DT to activate their expression and thereby promote glyceollin I accumulation (Jahan et al. 2019, 2020). Conversely, GmWRKY72 and GmJAZ1 function as negative regulators that interact with GmNAC42‐1 to repress its activation of GmG4DT, thereby dampening glyceollin I biosynthesis (Lin et al. 2024, 2025). These previous studies collectively indicate that glyceollin I biosynthesis is controlled by a multilayered transcriptional network; however, involvement of AP2/ERF TFs in this pathway has not been clearly established. In GmERF109‐OE lines, GmG4DT‐like transcript levels increased 23.6‐fold, whereas GmNAC42‐1 and GmG4DT were induced only modestly (1.9‐fold and 3.0‐fold, respectively), and GmMYB29A2, GmMYB29A1, GmHSF6‐1, GmWRKY72, and GmJAZ1‐9 showed only minor or no significant changes (Figure 6B). These results indicate that GmG4DT‐like is a preferentially responsive downstream target of GmERF109 and suggest that GmERF109 may regulate glyceollin I biosynthesis through a pathway distinct from previously characterized regulatory branches. Moreover, the expression of GmERF109 was tightly coordinated with that of its direct target GmG4DT‐like across both resistant and susceptible cultivars upon P. sojae infection (Figure 6C–L), wherein GmERF109 acts as a key positive regulator that rapidly and strongly activates GmG4DT‐like, driving glyceollin I accumulation and enhancing resistance to P. sojae.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which GmERF109 acts upstream of GmG4DT‐like to promote glyceollin biosynthesis, thereby contributing to the differential immune responses observed between soybean cultivars (Figure 7). These results provide mechanistic insights into cultivar‐specific defence against P. sojae. Future studies could also investigate whether this regulatory pathway contributes to broad‐spectrum resistance against fungi, oomycetes, and viruses and facilitate the development of associated molecular markers.

Proposed model for the role of the GmERF109–GmG4DT‐like regulatory in the response of soybean to Phytophthora sojae infection. P. sojae induces the expression of GmERF109. GmERF109 enhances glyceollin I accumulation by positively regulating the expression of its target gene GmG4DT‐like, thereby improving soybean resistance to P. sojae in resistant soybean, while opposite effects may be observed in susceptible soybean due to significantly lower GmERF109 expression.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that GmERF109 is a key TF that enhances soybean resistance to P. sojae by directly activating GmG4DT‐like, a gene involved in phytoalexin glyceollin I biosynthesis. Our results establish the GmERF109–GmG4DT‐like regulatory pathway as an important component of the molecular mechanism underlying soybean defence, providing valuable insights and genetic resources for breeding P. sojae‐resistant soybean cultivars.

Experimental Procedures

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

A total of 10 soybean ( G. max ) cultivars were used in this study, including five cultivars susceptible to P. sojae race 1 (Williams, Dongnong 50 [DN50], Zhonghuang 901, Kendou 65, and Beidou 40) and five resistant cultivars (Williams 82, Suinong 10 [SN10], Changnong 28, Jiyu 353, and Shengdou 264). All soybean cultivars were provided by the Key Laboratory of Soybean Biology, Ministry of Education of China (Harbin, China). The susceptible cultivar DN50 was used for genetic transformation and generation of transgenic hairy roots, whereas the resistant cultivar SN10 served as the source material for gene isolation. Soybean seeds were germinated and grown in a controlled growth chamber under conditions of 25°C, 70% relative humidity, and a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod. Plants of Arabidopsis thaliana were grown for subcellular localization and bimolecular fluorescence complementation experiments in a growth chamber set to 22°C with an 8‐h light/16‐h dark cycle. N. benthamiana plants used for dual‐luciferase reporter assays were cultivated at 22°C with the same photoperiod. P. sojae race 1 (PSR01), originally isolated from diseased soybean plants in Heilongjiang Province, China, was maintained on V8 juice agar plates and cultured at 25°C for 7 days prior to use.

Total RNA Isolation and RT‐qPCR Analysis

Root tissues were processed for total RNA isolation using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), with subsequent DNase I treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. First‐strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 2 μg of purified RNA as template with Superscript II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative real‐time PCR analysis was conducted on the LightCycler 96 System (Roche) using SYBR Green PCR master mix (TaKaRa). Expression levels were calculated using the comparative C t method and normalized to two reference genes: GmEF1 (GenBank accession NM_001248778) and GmTUB4 (XM_028361182.1). Primer sequences used in this study are provided in Table S3.

Generation of Transgenic Soybean Plants and Hairy Roots via Agrobacterium‐Mediated Transformation

To generate overexpression constructs, the full‐length coding sequence of GmERF109 was inserted into the pCAMBIA3301‐Myc vector, which contains the bar gene as a selectable marker, while the GmG4DT‐like coding sequence was cloned into pCAMBIA3301‐GFP. For RNA interference (RNAi), specific cDNA fragments of GmERF109 and GmG4DT‐like were amplified and individually ligated into the pFGC5941 vector. The overexpression and RNAi constructs for GmERF109 were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404 as described by Holsters et al. (1978). These constructs were subsequently used for the stable genetic transformation of the susceptible soybean DN50, following the protocol of Paz et al. (2004). For hairy root transformation, the overexpression and RNAi constructs for GmG4DT‐like were introduced into Agrobacterium rhizogenes K599. Transgenic soybean hairy roots were then generated according to the method established by Kereszt et al. (2007).

Assessment of Soybean Disease Responses

To evaluate the phenotypic response of soybean to P. sojae infection, we conducted controlled inoculation experiments following previously established protocols with slight adaptations (Zhang et al. 2010; Ward et al. 1979). Soybean root tissues were exposed to P. sojae zoospore suspensions prepared at approximately 1 × 105 spores mL−1. Disease manifestations on root systems were monitored and documented photographically using a Nikon B7000 imaging system at multiple time intervals after inoculation. The relative abundance of P. sojae biomass within infected soybean hairy roots was quantified through transcript analysis of the P. sojae TEF1 gene (EU079791).

Subcellular Localization

For the analysis of subcellular localization, the open reading frames (ORFs) of GmERF109 and GmG4DT‐like were individually inserted into the pBinGFP2 vector, where expression was driven by the CaMV 35S promoter, yielding the constructs 35S:GmERF109‐GFP and 35S:GmG4DT‐like‐GFP. These constructs were then co‐transformed into A. thaliana protoplasts with a nuclear marker, P2300‐35S‐H2B‐mCherry (RFP), using the polyethylene glycol (PEG)‐mediated method described by Yoo et al. (2007). The transformed protoplasts were incubated at 25°C under light for 16–20 h. Fluorescence signals were imaged using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope with a 40× objective lens. Sequential scanning was employed to minimize crosstalk between channels. The excitation/emission wavelengths were set as follows: 488/500–550 nm for GFP, 561/580–650 nm for mCherry/RFP, and 633/660–720 nm for chlorophyll autofluorescence.

Transactivation Assays

The GmERF109 ORF was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector and transformed into the Y2HGold yeast strain. The empty pGBKT7 vector served as the negative control, while pGBKT7‐GmWRKY31 (Fan et al. 2017) and a pGBKT7‐p53/pGADT7‐T co‐transformation were used as positive controls. After selection on SD/−Trp medium, yeast cells were spotted onto SD/−Trp/−His/−Ade selective medium containing X‐α‐Gal (20 μg/mL). Growth and colony colour were recorded after incubation at 30°C for 3–5 days. Transcriptional activation was assessed by observing cell growth on the selective medium and colony colour.

RNA‐Seq Analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the root tissues of both GmERF109‐OE transgenic plants and wild‐type (WT) plants for transcriptomic analysis. Indexed sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The libraries were then pooled and subjected to paired‐end sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. The resulting clean reads were aligned to the soybean reference genome (https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/) using TopHat software (Trapnell et al. 2009). Gene‐level read counts were quantified using HTSeq‐count in union mode. Differential expression analysis was subsequently performed using the DESeq package (Anders and Huber 2010). Genes were identified as DEGs if they met the criteria of a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute log2(fold‐change) > 1. The GO enrichment analysis was implemented using EasyGO (http://bioinformatics.cau.edu.cn/easygo/).

ChIP Assays

ChIP assays were conducted using root tissues from 30‐day‐old GmERF109‐OE and WT soybean plants, following the protocol established by Saleh et al. (2008) with modifications. Briefly, harvested roots were crosslinked with 1% (w/v) formaldehyde in MC buffer (0.1 M sucrose; 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.0; 10 mM NaCl). Five rounds of fixation were conducted, each consisting of vacuum infiltration for 3 min; after the last fixation round, 1.25 M glycine solution was added to achieve a final concentration of 0.125 M to stop the crosslinking reaction. To isolate nuclei, the samples were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in an extraction buffer containing 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 M NaCl, 10 mM β‐mercaptoethanol, 1 M hexylene glycol, 0.1 mM PMSF, and 0.1% (w/v) protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The suspension was filtered and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The pellet was washed five times using Wash Buffer I (10 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.0; 0.1 M NaCl; 10 mM β‐mercaptoethanol; 1 M hexylene glycol; 1 mM MgCl2; 0.5% [v/v] Triton X‐100; 0.1 mM PMSF) and twice using Wash Buffer II (10 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.0; 0.1 M NaCl; 10 mM β‐mercaptoethanol; 0.1 mM PMSF). For chromatin shearing, the resulting nuclear pellet was lysed in a sonication buffer containing 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 M NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, 0.5% (v/v) Sarkosyl, 0.1 mM PMSF, and 0.1% (w/v) protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysed nuclei were sonicated in an ice‐water bath for 15 cycles (30 s on and 30 s off per cycle) to shear the chromatin to an average fragment size of 500 bp. The sheared chromatin sample was diluted 1:1 (v/v) with IP buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5; 150 mM KCl; 50 mM MgCl2; 10 μM ZnSO4; 1% [v/v] Triton X‐100). To minimize nonspecific background, the soluble chromatin was precleared with unconjugated agarose beads, followed by immunoprecipitation with 30 μL of an agarose‐conjugated anti‐Myc‐tag monoclonal antibody (Abmart, #M20012S) at 4°C overnight. The precipitated DNA complexes were dissolved in 20 μL elution buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; 10 mM EDTA; 0.5% [w/v] SDS) and treated with proteinase K (Merck) and DNase‐free RNase (Biotopped). Finally, the purified DNA was quantified by qPCR using the SYBR Premix ExTaq Mix (TaKaRa). The relative enrichment was calculated as the ratio of immunoprecipitated DNA to input DNA (IP/input). qPCR was conducted with primers listed in Table S3.

Dual‐Luciferase Assay to Determine Transient Transcription

To investigate the transcriptional activation of the GmG4DT‐like promoter by GmERF109, a dual‐luciferase reporter assay was conducted in N. benthamiana leaves. Two reporter constructs were generated using the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector: a wild‐type promoter construct, in which the native promoter of GmG4DT‐like was fused upstream of the firefly luciferase gene (pGmG4DT‐like: LUC), and a mutant promoter construct (pGmG4DT‐like‐mut: LUC), in which the GCC‐box motif in the GmG4DT‐like promoter was disrupted by site‐directed mutagenesis, with the GCC‐box sequence mutated to AAAAAA. The coding sequence of GmERF109 was cloned into a vector under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter to create the effector construct (35Spro:GmERF109‐Myc). Both the effector and reporter constructs were separately transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. After growing to a logarithmic phase (OD600 = 0.8), the bacterial cultures were harvested, resuspended in infiltration buffer, and mixed in a 1:1 volume ratio for co‐infiltration into the leaves of 3‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants using a needleless syringe. Then, the plants were kept in continuous darkness for 72 h. After incubation, a solution of 1 mM luciferin containing 0.01% Triton X‐100 was sprayed evenly onto the infiltrated leaf surfaces. In vivo luminescence was immediately captured using a Tanon 5200 chemiluminescence imaging system. Subsequently, leaf discs from the same infiltrated areas were collected to measure luciferase activity using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). The relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly luciferase (LUC) activity to Renilla luciferase (REN) activity (LUC/REN).

Glyceollin I Analysis

The extraction and quantification of glyceollin I were performed using a high‐performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS) system. Briefly, the samples were ground to powder in liquid nitrogen. Approximately 1 g of the powder was extracted with 4 mL of 1% HCl‐methanol (v/v) by vortexing for 1 min and sonicating in an ice‐water bath for 30 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 11,000 rpm for 30 min. This extraction process was repeated twice. All supernatants were combined, adjusted to a final volume of 25 mL, and filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane filter prior to LC–MS/MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (3.5 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm) using a 1260 II HPLC system (Agilent Technologies). The column temperature was maintained at 35°C, and the autosampler was set to 4°C. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (solvent B) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The solvent gradient programme was set as follows: 0–2 min, 20% B; 2–6 min, 20%–90% B; 6–9 min, 90%–95% B; 9–9.1 min, 95%–20% B; 9.1–15 min, 20% B. The injection volume was 2 μL. LC‐grade methanol and acetonitrile were purchased from Merck. Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted using a 6420 Triple Quad mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies) operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The ion source parameters were set as follows: drying gas temperature, 350°C; drying gas flow, 10 L/min; nebulizer pressure, 45 psi; capillary voltage, +4 kV (positive mode). The specific MRM parameters for glyceollin I were precursor ion m/z 339.1, product ions m/z 255.0 (quantitative ion) and m/z 199.1 (qualitative ion), fragmentor voltage 130 V, and collision energies 20 eV and 50 eV, respectively. Quantification was performed using the external standard method (Bebrevska et al. 2007). An authentic glyceollin I standard (Aktin Chemicals; Cat. No. APC‐1670, purity ≥ 98%) was used to construct a calibration curve with a concentration range of 250 to 10,000 ng/mL.

In Vitro Antifungal Activity Assay Against P. sojae

Glyceollin I (Biosynth, Cat. No. XG161768) was dissolved in methanol to create a stock solution, which was subsequently sterilized by filtration through a 0.42 μm membrane filter. For the antifungal activity test, the sterile stock solution was added to V8 medium to prepare test plates with final glyceollin I concentrations of 50 μg/mL and 100 μg/mL. V8 medium plates supplemented with an equivalent volume of methanol served as the negative control. A 5 mm diameter mycelial plug was taken from the edge of an actively growing P. sojae colony and inoculated onto the centre of each prepared plate. All plates were incubated in the dark at 30°C for 72 h. The inhibitory effect of glyceollin I was assessed by measuring the colony diameter. Each treatment was performed in triplicate, and the experiment was repeated three times.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD from at least three independent biological experiments, each performed in technical triplicate. For qualitative assays (western blotting, subcellular localization, dual‐luciferase assays, and antimicrobial activity assays with glyceollin I), experiments were independently repeated three times with comparable results, and a representative result is shown. Statistical analyses were performed using a two‐tailed Student's t‐test, with significance denoted as *p < 0.05 or **p < 0.01.
