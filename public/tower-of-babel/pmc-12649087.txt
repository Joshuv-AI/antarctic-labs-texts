Association between spouse health and cognitive function in older Chinese adults: a moderated mediation of frailty and activity engagement
Authors: Junhao Huo, Yuyan Ding, Jiandong Bai, Yali Wang, Jinxin Zhang, Shuangshuang Yang, Lili Cui, Ying Yang, Junru Wang, Kai Liu, Jiahui Zhang, Juan Cao, Liqun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe accelerating global ageing phenomenon has given rise to a growing concern regarding cognitive decline in older adults. Research shows that spouse health is closely linked to individual well-being, as the physical and emotional health of one partner significantly affects the other. Although the connection between spousal health and older adults’ cognitive function has been recognized, there is still limited research on this specific impact. This study explores how spousal health is linked to cognitive function in older adults by investigating frailty as a mediator and activity engagement as a moderator.MethodsThis study utilized data from the 2017–2018 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), which included a sample of 4,652 Married older adults, all aged 65 and above, living with their spouses. Cognitive function was evaluated using the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE), frailty was measured with a frailty index, and activity engagement was gauged through various activity measures. By using mediation and moderated mediation analyses via the PROCESS macro in SPSS.ResultsAmong the 4,652 participants, 209 (4.49%) had cognitive impairment. Higher spouse health scores (indicating poorer spouse health) were associated with lower cognitive function and lower activity engagement (p < 0.001), and positively associated with frailty (p < 0.001). The frailty index was also negatively associated with cognitive function (p < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed that frailty significantly mediated the association between spousal health and cognitive function (β = -0.071, SE = 0.011, 95% CI: -0.094 ~ -0.050), but direct effects were not significant. In moderated mediation analysis, activity engagement moderated the association between frailty and cognitive function, with lower participation amplifying the negative impact (low: β = -0.064; high: β = -0.051; p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study shows a significant association between spousal health and cognitive function among older adults, with frailty serving as a fully mediator in this association. Crucially, activity engagement is associated with a weaker impact of frailty on cognitive abilities, with greater involvement linked to less pronounced cognitive decline. These findings emphasize the dual protective roles of a spouse’s health and activity engagement in sustaining cognitive health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06453-z.Keywords: Spouse health, Cognitive function, Frailty, Activity engagement, Moderated mediation effect, Older adults

Background

The accelerating global aging and the sustained decline in cognitive function among older adults have become significant challenges for public health systems, urgently requiring an integrated response from medical, social, and policy resources [1]. Cognitive impairment typically starts with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), which can progressively develop into more severe conditions like dementia [2]. According to WHO projections, approximately 78 million people worldwide will be affected by dementia by 2030, and this figure is expected to surge to 152 million by 2050 [3]. However, existing pharmacological treatments can only alleviate symptoms and improve cognitive function to a certain extent, without effectively halting the progression of the disease or reversing the damage [4]. An important yet often overlooked factor in mitigating cognitive decline is the role of a spouse. Spouses often share common living environments, lifestyles, and emotional interdependence, with mutual support in caregiving and emotional domains potentially associated with lower cognitive decline [5–7]. Variations in one partner’s physical and cognitive function can significantly impact the other partner’s quality of life [7]. Supportive dyadic coping between spouses enhances psychological well-being and marital quality, especially in the context of cognitive challenges such as mild cognitive impairment [8, 9].

Frailty is a multidimensional syndrome that encompasses physical, psychological, cognitive, and social deficits, and has been identified as a key factor associated with cognitive decline [10, 11]. Growing evidence indicates that deterioration in spousal health may contribute to frailty development in partners through increased caregiving burden, emotional distress, reduced physical activity, shared lifestyle risks, and chronic physiological stress. For instance, Potier et al. reported elevated frailty levels and inflammatory biomarkers among spousal caregivers of cognitively impaired individuals, reflecting chronic stress responses [12]. Dassel and Carr demonstrated that dementia caregiving accelerates frailty progression in spouses [13]. Chiu and Lin found that worsening spousal health was associated with increased allostatic load, cardiovascular dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances, particularly among older female [14]. Compared to other potential mediators such as depression or physical inactivity, frailty offers a comprehensive framework that captures the cumulative vulnerability arising from various stressors [15].

In addition, activity engagement, which reflects engagement in social, cultural, and recreational activities, may serve as a protective factor that mitigates the adverse impact of frailty on cognitive function. According to the WHO Active Aging framework, active engagement in social activities can enhances emotional well-being, cognitive stimulation, and physical activity, thereby helping to buffer against negative health outcomes in older adults [16, 17]. These studies collectively emphasize the interconnection between spouses, cognitive function, activity engagement, and frailty in the aging process. This study investigates whether frailty mediates the association between spousal health and cognitive function, while also exploring how varying levels of activity engagement may moderate this indirect link, as depicted in Fig. 1. Methods

Study design and participants

Using data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), coordinated by the Center for Healthy Aging and Development Studies at Peking University. Clinical trial number: not applicable. By virtue of being the earliest nationwide longitudinal study centered on older adults in a developing context, the CLHLS yields critical insights into the evolving landscape of aging in China, thereby serving as An indispensable reference for examining and addressing emergent demographic challenges. Since its inception in 1998, the survey has been conducted over eight waves, with the latest data collected in 2017–2018. This survey includes older adults aged 65 and above as well as their children aged 35 to 64, covering 22 provinces in China. As a large-scale study, it provides a broad overview of intergenerational dynamics and offers deeper insights into the aging process nationwide. The sampling design of CLHLS adopts multiple random sampling methods [18]. The survey gathers detailed data on respondents’ health status, socioeconomic characteristics, family background, psychological traits, lifestyle, and demographic information. By securing approval from Peking University’s Biomedical Ethics Committee (IRB00001052-13074) and obtaining written informed consent from participants, the CLHLS survey demonstrates a clear commitment to conducting ethically sound and responsible research. The dataset is accessible to the public at https://sites.duke.edu/centerforaging/.

In our study, from the 2017–2018 CLHLS wave (15,874 participants), 6,074 older adults who were married and living with their spouse were identified. We excluded participants with missing data on cognitive function (164 participants), activity engagement (160 participants), spouse health status (257 participants), and covariates (841 participants), resulting in a final analytical sample of 4,652 participants (Fig. 2).

Sample selection flowchart

Spouse health

Spousal health was measured using a self-report question that asks, “How would you rate your spouse’s current health status (for remarriage, refer to the last partner)?” Responses were provided on a five-point scale, where 1 represents very good and 5 represents very poor, with higher scores indicating worse health.This single-item indicator is an important tool for health assessment, with high reliability and validity, and is widely applied in national health survey programs [19–21].

Cognitive function

Using the widely validated modified Chinese MMSE provides a robust and reliable assessment of cognitive function, thereby enhancing the accuracy and dependability of the study’s findings on cognitive impairment [22, 23]. The MMSE consists of 24 items that measure various cognitive domains, including time and place orientation, immediate and delayed memory, attention and calculation, language, and visuospatial abilities. Its validity and reliability have been confirmed in earlier studies [23–25]. The MMSE yields scores from 0 to 30, with higher scores signifying better cognitive function. Given that the general education level of older adults in China is typically low, those scoring below 24 are classified as having cognitive impairment, while other participants are considered to have no cognitive impairment [26–28]. This scoring range demonstrates reliable diagnostic performance, with a specificity of 80% to 100% and a sensitivity of 80% to 90%, making it an effective method for diagnosing cognitive impairment among older adults in China [26, 28]. According to previous studies, answers of “unable to answer” are considered “incorrect” [28].

Frailty index

Using at least 30 deficit items in the frailty index is recommended because it improves both the accuracy and reliability of the assessment, resulting in a more robust evaluation of frailty [29, 30]. The study utilized a frailty index based on 40 health deficits, constructed via a validated method, to ensure a reliable and accurate assessment of frailty among participants. These deficit items can be selected based on various dimensions, such as health deficits, functional abilities, physical health status, and cognitive abilities. This approach helps ensure the comprehensiveness and accuracy of the frailty index while minimizing the impact of bias from a single dimension. The CLHLS provides rich data on the health, social, and lifestyle aspects of older Chinese adults [31, 32]. Each health deficit item was dichotomized or trichotomized according to its severity. For example, toileting independence was assessed using a scoring system where 0 indicates no assistance, 0.5 represents partial assistance, and 1 signifies full assistance. Mapping health deficits onto a scale from 0 to 1 standardizes their assessment and allows for a more detailed evaluation of their impact on quality of life. The frailty index is computed by summing an individual’s deficit scores and dividing by the total number of deficits, resulting in a standardized measure of frailty severity. By integrating physical, cognitive, and emotional dimensions, this method provides a comprehensive assessment of frailty, ensuring all aspects of an individual’s health status are considered. According to the thresholds established in previous research, individuals with a frailty index of less than or equal to 0.25 are considered frail [33].

Activity engagement

Activity engagement was assessed using multiple items from the CLHLS questionnaire that reflect involvement in a range of activities, including practicing Tai Chi, dancing, visiting friends, outdoor pursuits, gardening, reading, playing games, watching TV, and other forms of engagement. Participants were asked how often they engaged in different activities, with their answers rated on a scale from 1 (never) to 5 (almost every day) to assess activity engagement. The total score can range from 6 to 30, reflecting the frequency of engagement in social activities. More active involvement in activities is indicated by a higher score. In a previous study, activity engagement was one of the key variables in model, influencing the cognitive status of older adults [34].

Covariates

To minimize confounding bias when examining the association between spouse health and cognitive impairment, this study employed a variable selection method based on Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) [35, 36]. This approach integrates both theory-driven and data-driven variable selection, which enhances the robustness and credibility of causal inference results [37]. By constructing a Directed Acyclic Graph and applying the d-separation principle, the study identified the minimal sufficient adjustment set to block potential confounding pathways, thereby enhancing the validity of the observed association between spouse health and cognitive impairment [38, 39]. The results suggested that, to provide an accurate assessment of how spousal health relates to cognitive impairment, the smallest necessary set of covariates for adjustment comprises the following factors: gender (0 = female, 1 = male), age (0 = ≤ 85 years old, 1 = >85 years old), smoking (0 = no, 1 = yes), recent physical exercise (0 = no, 1 = yes), recent alcohol consumption (0 = no, 1 = yes), residency (0 = city, 1 = Town/rural), self-reported chronic diseases, and CES-D-10 score (continuous variable). In subsequent analyses, we included these eight variables as covariates in the model to control for bias from these confounding factors. The number of chronic diseases was calculated by statistically assessing the prevalence of six chronic conditions in participants, including hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, and others [40]. Depression levels were measured using the CES-D scale, where scores range from 0 (infrequent or not at all) to 3 (frequent or constant). The CES-D-10 quantifies depression severity on a scale from 0 to 30, with higher scores reflecting greater levels of depressive symptoms [41].

Statistical analyses

Data analysis for the study was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software package (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Using DAGitty version 3.1(DAGitty v3.1) enabled the systematic construction. Data analysis of the causal model Data analysis for the study was conducted using SPSS 26.0 and DAGitty 3.1 software. Means with standard deviations were used to present continuous variables, whereas percentages were used for categorical variables. Significant differences in covariates were assessed using chi-square and independent t-tests. To understand the connections between spousal health, cognitive function, frailty, and activity engagement, correlation analysis was utilized. Using the PROCESS macro, the study conducted mediation analysis (Model 4) to evaluate whether frailty helps explain the association between spousal health and cognitive function, and to explore whether activity engagement modifies this pathway. (Model 14) to assess how activity engagement modifies this indirect pathway [42, 43]. The bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method (with 5,000 repetitions) was employed to compute 95% confidence intervals [44]. The study evaluated mediation and moderated mediation effects using confidence intervals and considered them significant only if 0 was not included. The covariates controlled for in the analysis included age, gender, residency, smoking status, physical exercise, alcohol consumption, chronic diseases, and CES-D score. Setting a p-value threshold below 0.05 confirms that the study’s findings are statistically significant.

Correlation analysis

Table 2 shows significant bivariate correlations among spouse health, the frailty index, activity engagement, and cognitive function. Spouse health was negatively associated with cognitive function (r = −0.039, p < 0.001) and activity engagement (r = −0.058, p < 0.001), suggesting that better spouse health is related to higher cognitive function and greater activity engagement. Additionally, spouse health was positively associated with the frailty index (r = 0.126, p < 0.001), indicating that poorer spouse health is related to greater frailty. Cognitive function was found to be negatively related to the frailty index (r = −0.341, p < 0.001), but positively associated with activity engagement (r = 0.249, p < 0.001). Additionally, the frailty index showed an inverse association with activity engagement (r = −0.353, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Despite extensive research on older adults’ cognitive status, the association between spousal health and cognitive function has not been extensively studied. Utilizing data from the 2017–2018 CLHLS, the study investigates the association between spousal health and cognitive function in older adults by assessing both direct effects and those mediated by frailty, while also examining how activity engagement moderates these connections.

The initial hypothesis proposed that frailty mediates the association between spousal health and cognitive function. The findings showed that while spousal health had no significant direct association with cognitive function, frailty fully mediated their association. Cognitive decline is not solely attributed to individual factors but is also indirectly influenced by the health status of family members. Poor spousal health may be associated to caregiving stress, emotional burden, or physiological strain, which in turn may coincide with greater frailty [45]. This finding reinforces established theories on family health connectivity and emotional interdependence, demonstrating that family members’ health statuses are interrelated and associated with cognitive outcomes in older adults, highlighting the need to account for familial health dynamics in efforts to maintain cognitive function [17].

Our second hypothesis analysis shows that activity engagement significantly moderates the association between frailty and cognitive function. Higher activity engagement was linked to a weaker negative association between frailty and cognitive function. Active activity engagement in older adults supports emotional well-being, stimulates cognitive function, and encourages physical activity, working together to reduce the negative impact of frailty and promote better overall cognitive health [46]. This result emphasizes the role of activity engagement in health protection and underscores the importance of building a multi-level social support system to promote health and cognitive function [47]. The outcomes of this study suggest a potential association between activity engagement and cognitive outcomes among older adults, where promoting activity engagement may be linked to more favorable cognitive outcomes [48].

In the moderated mediation analysis, activity engagement moderates the effect of spousal health on cognitive function, with frailty as the mediator. Lower levels of activity engagement were associated with a stronger negative association between frailty and cognitive function, whereas higher activity engagement was linked to a weaker association, highlighting its protective role in supporting cognitive health in older adults. This finding is supported by research on the correlation of frailty between older married couples, which shows that frailty is interdependent and consistent between spouses [16]; when one spouse experiences frailty, the likelihood of the other spouse becoming frail also increases [49]. The moderation effect of activity engagement is further evidenced by the simple slope analysis, which shows that, regardless of whether activity engagement is above or below the average level, the negative association between frailty and cognitive function persists. This suggests that an individual’s health status is not isolated, but embedded within a broader family health context [50]. Previous longitudinal studies have highlighted the importance of both structured and unstructured forms of activity engagement. Engagement in social activities has consistently been associated with reduced frailty and improved cognitive function among older adults, particularly in China, underscoring a bidirectional association between activity engagement and physical as well as cognitive health [51, 52]. Activities that encourage interactions within family and community environments, such as regular visits from family members and involvement in community gardening or hobby groups, have demonstrated protective effects against cognitive decline [53, 54]. Therefore, governments should consider providing financial subsidies to support both structured community-based programs and informal activities, creating inclusive environments that facilitate sustained social interaction. Older adults can maintain high levels of activity engagement, enhance emotional well-being and self-efficacy, and ultimately mitigate the progression of frailty and associated cognitive impairments [53]. Although the study has certain advantages in terms of sample size, variable selection, and statistical methods, several limitations remain. First, this study’s reliance on cross-sectional data restricts its ability to establish definitive causal association s, thereby limiting the strength of any cause-and-effect inferences. Second, the assessment of spouse health primarily relies on self-report measures, which may introduce subjectivity and bias. Third, while the study controlled for several covariates, unmeasured confounding variables may still affect the robustness of the results.

Conclusions

The study demonstrates that cognitive health in older adults is associated with multiple factors, including spousal health, frailty, and activity engagement—highlighting the importance of considering these interconnected elements when assessing and addressing cognitive decline in later life. The association between spousal health and cognitive function is entirely mediated by frailty, while the association between frailty and cognitive function varied depending on levels of activity engagement. These results suggest that improving family health dynamics and promoting activity engagement may help enhance cognitive health among aging populations. Integrating efforts to strengthen family health connections and encourage activity engagement may play a role in preventing cognitive impairment in older adults, providing a useful reference for developing policies and interventions in the context of global aging.
