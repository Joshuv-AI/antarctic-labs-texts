The effect of pain on fear of movement in the early period after open heart surgery according to the fear-avoidance model: the role of moderating factors
Authors: Muaz Gülşen, Sevban Arslan, Emine Ceren, Hülya Binokay, Ömer Faruk Doğan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative pain and fear of movement are critical factors that can adversely affect recovery outcomes. The interplay among these factors can significantly influence mobilization and rehabilitation outcomes. This study aims to examine the correlation between pain and fear of mobility during the initial phase following open-heart surgery, taking into account the influence of moderating variables.MethodsThis cross-sectional and correlational study, conducted within the framework of the Fear-Avoidance Model, involved 203 patients in a Cardiovascular Surgery Intensive Care Unit. Data were collected using a “Patient Information Form,” the “Numeric Rating Scale” and the “Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia.” These data collection tools were administered to patients at specific time points (12, 24, and 36 hours). The data collection process lasted approximately 30 minutes for each patient.ResultsA weak, positive connection was identified between pain and fear of movement at 12 h post-surgery. A comparable correlation was discovered between pre-mobilization discomfort and mobility apprehension at the 24-hour mark. At 24 h post-mobilization, the association between pain and fear of movement shifted to a moderate, negative correlation after mobilization. All demographic and health-related variables, with the exception of body mass index (BMI), were determined to have no moderating influence on the link between pain and fear of mobility. At 24 h post-surgery, post-mobilization BMI influenced the correlation between pain and fear of mobility. In patients with a lower BMI, the fear of mobility diminished as pain intensified. The identical relationship was noted prior to mobilization at 36 h following surgery.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the direction and strength of the correlation between pain and fear of movement vary across the early postoperative period, with body mass index functioning as a significant moderating variable. The significance of pain management and early mobilization in the postoperative phase is emphasized. Thus, personalized patient management planning by nurses and healthcare experts is essential for improving patient comfort and facilitating the recovery process.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Open-heart surgery is a significant surgical intervention conducted to address cardiac ailments and mend blood vessels [1]. This intervention is life-saving, although the postoperative healing phase frequently presents challenges [2, 3]. Pain can impede patients’ mobility, thereby prolonging the rehabilitation process [1, 2].

The correlation between pain and anxiety of mobility can significantly influence postoperative mobilization in patients [1, 2]. Nonetheless, comprehending the interplay between these two factors continues to be a significant study focus, especially in persons with cardiovascular disease [1]. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols advocate for the prompt movement of patients post-cardiac surgery, indicating that this expedites recovery and diminishes the likelihood of problems. Nonetheless, whereas these guidelines promote patient mobilization, they also emphasize that pain and anxiety of movement provide considerable obstacles to this process [4]. Patients with limited mobility may experience problems like muscle weakness, lung infections, deep vein thrombosis, and pneumonia, which adversely affect the rehabilitation process [1, 3, 5]. This results in extended hospitalizations and markedly diminishes patients’ quality of life [6–8]. At this point, comprehending the connection between pain and fear of movement is crucial [2]. Recognizing moderating factors that affect this relationship can empower all healthcare workers, especially nurses, to enhance the efficacy of pain and mobility treatment [2].

The current literature comprises several investigations within the musculoskeletal and physiotherapy domains investigating the correlation between pain and movement apprehension. Nevertheless, research in the domain of cardiovascular surgery frequently examines pain, movement, and fear of movement as distinct variables, lacking a comprehensive evaluation of the interplay among these factors [9–17]. Research examining the correlation between pain and fear of movement is few, and existing studies frequently neglect to consider potential moderating variables [1, 2].

Aim of the study

This study seeks to examine the correlation between pain and fear of mobility in patients post-open-heart surgery, along with the moderating factors that affect this association. The findings aim to furnish scientific proof for patient management post-cardiovascular surgery, aiding in the formulation of appropriate solutions for nurses and other healthcare workers to address pain and movement apprehension.

Study questions

What are the levels of pain and fear of movement experienced by patients after open-heart surgery at 12, 24, and 36 h?

Are patients’ demographic and health characteristics associated with pain and fear of movement at 12, 24, and 36 h?

How does the relationship between pain and fear of movement change at 12, 24, and 36 h after open-heart surgery?

Are patients’ demographic and health characteristics factors that influence the relationship between pain and fear of movement at 12, 24, and 36 h?

Method

Type of study

This study aimed to investigate the association between pain and fear of movement, structured according to the Fear-Avoidance Model [18] and utilizing a cross-sectional and correlational approach. The study was conducted in compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) standards [19].

The Fear-Avoidance Model (FAM) posits that an individual will evade movement when they perceive pain as a danger, perhaps resulting in functional restrictions.18 In this study, pain was identified as the “triggering variable,” whereas fear of movement was designated as the “outcome variable.” Patients’ demographic and health-related features were assessed as “moderating variables” that may affect this association. Theoretical basis for the use of the FAM in the acute phase: Although the model was initially developed to explain responses to chronic pain, several recent studies have shown that it may also be applicable in the acute postoperative period [20–25]. Therefore, in this study, the FAM was employed as a theoretical framework to evaluate patients’ psychological responses to pain during the early mobilization process.

Setting and timeframe of the study

The study was conducted in the Intensive Care Unit of the Cardiovascular Surgery Clinic at the City Hospital in Adana, Turkey, between June 14, 2024, and March 14, 2025.

To improve the consistency and reliability of the data, patients who underwent open heart surgery by a single surgeon were included in the study. This selection aims to reduce variability in postoperative pain intensity by minimizing differences in surgical technique and experience. In the study, no specific pain education was given to the patients before mobilization. In addition, a single surgeon subjected patients to standardized surgical procedures and consistent early postoperative care protocols, thus ensuring homogeneity of the sample and increasing internal validity. This approach is thought to allow a more accurate analysis of the relationship between the independent variable, pain intensity, and the dependent variable, fear of movement. In addition, the selection of patients operated by a single surgeon provided order and convenience in the data collection process.

Patients transferred to the intensive care unit after surgery receive an early postoperative care protocol. This protocol establishes a standard of care and treatment.

Pain is evaluated in conjunction with vital signs according to the protocol. Postoperative pain evaluation occurs every 15 min for the initial hour, every 30 min during the subsequent hour, and then every 60 min for the next two hours. Subsequent evaluations of patients with stable vital signs are performed routinely every two hours. During the initial four-hour interval, the surgeon assesses the overall status of patients exhibiting stable vital signs, normal blood gas readings (monitored hourly throughout this period), and the absence of problems. Patients are extubated if considered appropriate by the surgeon after this assessment. According to the protocol, patients transferred from the operating room to the intensive care unit receive a single intramuscular dose of 100 mg/2 ml Aldolan, provided that hypotension is not present. Additionally, starting from the time of intensive care unit admission, all patients are routinely administered 1 gram of intravenous paracetamol every eight hours (totaling 3 g per day). No additional opioids or other analgesics were administered prior to mobilization. Pain levels were routinely assessed before each mobilization, and no need for analgesia beyond the standard protocol was identified. Although the study protocol stipulated the exclusion of patients experiencing pain severe enough to prevent mobilization, no such cases were encountered during data collection. All patients were mobilized as planned without requiring further pain management.

Patient mobilization following surgery is not conducted on the first postoperative day (day 0). Patient mobilization, as assessed by the surgeon, occurs on the morning of the first postoperative day. Mobilization typically occurs between 08:00 and 10:00 AM on postoperative day one. The subsequent mobilization occurs 12 h later, between 20:00 and 22:00. Mobilization is conducted under the surgeon’s supervision by the nurse in charge of the patient’s care, accompanied by support workers, for an average duration of 15 min. In the absence of issues during the protocol’s implementation, the patient will be re-evaluated by the surgeon 48 h post-surgery (post-op day 2), and upon the surgeon’s agreement, will be transferred to the ward. This process is regularly executed by the surgeon.

Professor Ö.F.D., a member of the research team and the clinic’s education director, indicated that the evaluation of pain intensity and fear of movement conducted at the fourth hour in the current protocol may not accurately represent the patient’s genuine pain intensity and fear of movement, as the patient is influenced by anesthesia, analgesic medication (Aldolan-Paracetamol), and the post-extubation phase. This study evaluated pain severity and fear of movement after 12, 24, and 36 h post-surgery, based on postoperative care and expert judgment (Fig. 1).

Data collection process

Population and sample of the study

The study population comprises patients who received open-heart surgery in the Cardiovascular Surgery Intensive Care Unit of a city hospital in Adana, Turkey, over the past year. Hospital records indicate that 298 people got open-heart surgery conducted by the same specialist in 2023. The sample size was calculated using a recognized formula for population sample size, employing the numbers t = 1.96 and d = 0.05, as advised by Salant and Dillman (1994) [20]. The calculations indicated that a minimum of 168 patients must be included in the study, ensuring a 95% confidence interval and a ± 5% sampling error [26]. To mitigate potential losses during data collection, an attrition rate of 20% was accounted for [27], resulting in a sample size of 203. The study was concluded upon achieving the specified sample size.

Patients aged 18 or older who received elective open-heart surgery for the first time by the authorized surgeon, were capable of verbal communication, were extubated within four hours post-surgery, and consented to participate in the study were included. Patients with neurological or orthopedic conditions potentially impacting postoperative mobility, those with psychiatric disorders or undergoing psychiatric treatment, patients experiencing postoperative complications (including hypotension, arrhythmia, respiratory failure, pneumonia, or severe pain limiting movement), individuals diagnosed with chronic pain preoperatively, and patients with a history of long-term analgesic use were excluded from the study.

Data collection tools

The “Patient Information Form” the “Numeric Rating Scale” and the “Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia” were used for data collection.

Patient information form (PIF)

The variables in this form were established by the researchers, aligned with the study’s objectives, research questions, and target demographic, and corroborated by existing literatüre [1, 2]. This was executed to enhance the reliability and validity of the study. The form comprises sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, marital status, and educational level, as well as clinical variables including smoking and alcohol use, presence of chronic diseases, body mass index, ASA score, type of surgery, and presence of drains or tubes.

Numeric rating scale (NRS)

This scale is used to assess the severity of pain through self-report. Patients were asked to rate their current pain intensity on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain) by stating or marking a number that best reflects their pain. The NRS is a valid and widely used instrument in clinical settings due to its simplicity and ease of use [28].

Tampa scale of kinesiophobia (TSK)

Developed by Kori et al. and published by Vlaeyen and colleagues, the TSK is a 17-item scale designed to measure fear of movement and re-injury [29]. It utilizes a 4-point Likert-type scoring system, with several items reverse-scored to calculate the total score. The total score ranges from 17 to 68, with scores of 37 and above indicating high levels of kinesiophobia [29]. The Turkish validity and reliability of the scale were confirmed by Tunca Yılmaz et al., reporting a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81 [30]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.77.

Data collection

Before initiating the data collection process, the researcher furnished participants with comprehensive information regarding the study’s goal, methods, and potential impacts, and secured their informed consent. The research was executed in compliance with ethical standards.

In the context of the study, patients admitted to the clinic prior to surgery were given the PIF to assess their sociodemographic attributes and health condition, and the TSK to evaluate their fear of mobility.

During the postoperative phase, the pain management protocol incorporated into postoperative care was routinely applied in the intensive care unit (Refer to 2.2). In compliance with this procedure and the clinical assessments conducted by cardiovascular surgery specialist Ö.F.D., a member of the study team, the NRS and TSK were administered to patients at the bedside 12 h postoperatively. In accordance with the critical care protocol, patients were assessed by the surgeon at the bedside 24 h postoperatively. The surgeon stated that the patient had no contraindications for mobilization, and the initial mobilization was conducted in the intensive care unit after 24 h. The NRS and TSK were re-administered to patients prior to and following the initial mobilization. Likewise, patients underwent re-evaluation by the same surgeon at 36 h, and a second mobilization was conducted if they were deemed appropriate for mobilization. The NRS and TSK were re-administered prior to and after to the second deployment. The data gathering method averaged 30 min per subject. All measures were conducted utilizing the face-to-face interview technique.

Various steps were implemented during the data collection process to mitigate non-response bias. Participants were guaranteed that their anonymity would be safeguarded, so alleviating privacy worries. This approach lowered the non-response rate. The questionnaire was conducted anonymously to mitigate common method bias, and reverse-coded items were used to diminish social desirability bias in responses.

Study variables

Independent Variables: Data including the demographic and health status of patients undergoing open-heart surgery and pain intensity.

Dependent Variable: TSK score.

Data analysis

Data statistical analysis was conducted utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) software. Categorical data were summarized with counts and percentages, whereas numerical variables were summarized using averages and standard deviations. The Chi-Square test was employed to compare categorical variables between groups, whereas the Independent Samples T-Test was utilized for numerical variables when assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U Test was applied. When comparing multiple groups, One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was employed if the assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis Test was utilized. In instances of substantial disparity, Tukey or Games-Howell Tests were employed for pairwise comparisons where assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the Bonferroni-adjusted Mann-Whitney U Test was utilized. The Pearson Correlation was employed to analyze the relationship between numerical variables when assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the Spearman Correlation was utilized. The Jamovi 2.5.3 software [31] was utilized to examine the moderating influence of body mass index on the correlation between pain and anxiety of movement. The statistical significance level was established at 0.05 for all tests. The biostatistics expert (H.B.) conducted the data analysis for the study team.

Ethical principles of the study

Written permissions were obtained from the Cukurova University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Meeting Number/Decision Number/Date: 145/71/14.06.2024) and the Health Sciences University Adana City Training and Research Hospital Chief Physician. Before starting the research, the purpose of the study was explained to the participants and both verbal and written consents were obtained. For the reliability of the study, care was taken to ensure that the participants volunteered to participate in the study. It was stated that the participants had to volunteer for the reliability of the research results, that the information given to the questions was accurate and that it was very important for the reliability of the research results, that the information received would only be used for this study, that the personal information received would not be shared with other persons or institutions in any way, that the data would be kept confidential, and that no disruption would occur in the medical treatment in line with the data received. In this context, the principles of “Informed Consent”, “Respect for Autonomy”, “Confidentiality and Protection of Confidentiality” were fulfilled. In addition, the research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles stated in the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

This study examined the temporal change in pain and fear of movement experienced by patients in the early period after open-heart surgery, the relationship between these two variables, and the moderating factors that affect this relationship. It was found that there was a positive relationship between pain and fear of movement at 12 h postoperatively, but this relationship changed to a negative direction with mobilization. In addition, it was determined that body mass index only played a moderating role in the relationship between pain and fear of movement after mobilization at 24 h and before mobilization at 36 h.

In this study, it was determined that patients felt the highest level of pain and were most afraid of moving during the bedside measurements taken at 12 h postoperatively. However, in subsequent measurements, it was observed that both pain intensity and fear of movement gradually decreased. In our study, acute pain caused by surgical intervention was most intense in the early postoperative period (12 h), and the perception of pain and fear of movement gradually decreased over time due to wound healing, desensitization to pain, and increased mobilization. These findings are consistent with previous studies [2, 6, 8, 32–35], which have also reported similar patterns of postoperative pain and recovery. In addition, according to the Fear-Avoidance Model, patients are afraid to move due to pain and therefore tend to avoid movement [18]. Avoidance behavior in response to acute pain may initially be considered a normal and protective physiological reaction. However, in some individuals, this fear may persist throughout the recovery process, leading to movement limitations and, consequently, negatively impacting the rehabilitation process. The decrease in pain and fear of movement observed in subsequent measurements indicates a decrease in patients’ pain levels and adaptation processes to pain, and it can be said that these fears decrease over time with pain control.

In this study, no significant relationship was found between gender, marital status, education level, smoking and alcohol use, presence of chronic disease, ASA score, and type of surgery with pain and fear of movement. This finding contradicts the results reported in the literature that some demographic factors are effective on pain perception and fear of movement [1–3]. As stated in the study by Makkad et al. (2023), the reason for this contradiction is that psychological and physiological factors such as individuals’ pain threshold and pain perception may show personal differences independently of demographic characteristics [10]. In this case, it may have led to the effect of demographic factors on pain perception not being observed in the same way in every individual [36, 37].

A weak, positive correlation was found between pain and fear of movement in the measurements taken at 12 and 24 h postoperatively, when the patients were not mobilized. However, after the first mobilization at 24 h, this relationship was observed to reverse in the negative direction, and the level of the relationship increased from weak to moderate. This finding supports that mobilization in the early period after cardiovascular surgery may be effective in reducing fear of movement [2]. It has also been stated in the literature that mobilization alleviates fear of movement by reducing pain in the postoperative period [1–3]. However, a systematic review by Zeng et al. (2024) found that some studies reported that mobilization reduced pain intensity, while others found that it increased it. As stated by Zeng et al. (2024), this contradiction is attributed to individual differences in patients’ pain management [3]. In addition, it was determined that patients who gained experience after mobilization at 24 h postoperatively had a weak negative correlation between pain and fear of movement in the measurements taken before and after mobilization at 36 h. This result shows that over time, patients’ fears that pain will increase with movement decrease and that they begin to perform the movement more safely. The literature also emphasizes that pain intensity and fear of movement decrease significantly as the postoperative process progresses [6, 8, 34, 35].

When Table 5 is examined, it was determined that the only moderating variable affecting the relationship between pain and fear of movement in the measurements taken after mobilization at 24 h and before mobilization at 36 h postoperatively was body mass index. The moderating effect of small body mass index values was significant, while the moderating effect of large body mass index values was not significant. In both measurements (after mobilization at 24 h and before mobilization at 36 h), it was observed that fear of movement decreased as pain increased in individuals with low body mass index (Fig. 2). This finding is consistent with some studies in the literatüre [36, 37]. However, while it is generally expected that increased pain will increase fear of movement, the study shows that this relationship is reversed in individuals with low body mass index. This is suggested to be because individuals with low body mass index lack mechanical support due to less fat tissue and may perceive pain more intensely [36, 37]. However, it is not an expected situation that individuals who feel more pain have less fear of movement [3, 36]. In the literature, it has been reported that fear of movement also increases when pain perception is high [3]. Therefore, the findings of the study suggest that the decrease in fear of movement in individuals with low body mass index should be explained by different physiological or psychological mechanisms [3]. Individuals with low body mass index may not have avoided movement despite pain because they are more physically active. Alternatively, even if their pain perceptions are high, they may be less likely to develop fear of movement. On the other hand, it is noteworthy that this moderating effect was not significant in individuals with large body mass index in the study. It is suggested that fear of movement in obese individuals may be related to physical capacity and functional limitations rather than directly to pain [3, 36].

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the relationship between pain and fear of movement in the early period following open-heart surgery may vary over time. The findings suggest that body mass index (BMI) may play a potential moderating role in this relationship; specifically, fear of movement associated with pain appears to be more pronounced in patients with a lower BMI. The results are consistent with the underlying Fear-Avoidance Model, which posits that individuals who perceive pain as threatening may develop avoidance behaviors, potentially leading to long-term functional limitations. Our findings indicate that this process may be influenced not only by psychological but also by individual physiological factors such as BMI. Therefore, for nurses and healthcare professionals, it is important to develop postoperative strategies that address not only pain management but also early mobilization and patient education tailored to individual risk factors (e.g., low BMI), in order to enhance patient comfort and support recovery.

Strengths and limitations

This study examines the relationship between pain and fear of movement in patients undergoing open-heart surgery, within the framework of Zale and Ditre’s (2015) Fear-Avoidance Model [18]. Scientific rigor was enhanced by the use of validated and reliable measurement tools. The findings offer clinically relevant insights for healthcare professionals, particularly regarding the interplay between pain management and early mobilization in the postoperative period. Despite these strengths, the study has certain limitations. Its generalizability is restricted by the single-center design and the absence of long-term follow-up to assess the enduring effects of kinesiophobia. Future research should consider multicenter studies involving diverse surgical populations and extended follow-up periods to better capture the long-term implications of fear of movement. Larger sample sizes would also allow for the evaluation of additional variables, such as body mass index, and help identify specific risk groups. Moreover, the study did not directly assess the role of nursing care in shaping patients’ pain perception and fear of movement. Variations in nurse-patient communication, pain management strategies, and mobilization practices may influence patient outcomes. Subsequent studies should incorporate the impact of nursing interventions and institutional care protocols when exploring pain and movement-related fears in surgical patients. One notable limitation is the exclusion of psychological and psychiatric variables, which are central components of the theoretical model employed. Future studies are encouraged to adopt a biopsychosocial perspective to better elucidate the mechanisms influencing postoperative recovery. Additionally, the presence of preoperative chronic pain was not directly assessed in this study. Given that chronic pain has been associated with heightened postoperative pain perception and increased fear of movement, further research should address its potential role in shaping postoperative experiences.
