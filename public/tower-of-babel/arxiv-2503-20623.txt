Collaborative Storytelling and LLM: A Linguistic Analysis of Automatically-Generated Role-Playing Game Sessions
Alessandro Maisto

Abstract
Role-playing games (RPG) are games in which players interact with one another to create narratives. The role of players in the RPG is largely based on the interaction between players and their characters. This emerging form of shared narrative, primarily oral, is receiving increasing attention. In particular, many authors investigated the use of an LLM as an actor in the game. In this paper, we aim to discover to what extent the language of Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit oral or written features when asked to generate an RPG session without human interference. We will conduct a linguistic analysis of the lexical and syntactic features of the generated texts and compare the results with analyses of conversations, transcripts of human RPG sessions, and books. We found that LLMs exhibit a pattern that is distinct from all other text categories, including oral conversations, human RPG sessions and books. Our analysis has shown how training influences the way LLMs express themselves and provides important indications of the narrative capabilities of these tools.

Introduction

Despite the introduction of writing and the so-called
Gutenberg Galaxy
, which revolutionized human narrative, many kinds of stories remain characterized by orality. Children’s stories, jokes, anecdotes, and other everyday narrations are often expressed orally due to their occasional and informal nature. However, a contemporary form of shared narrative, primarily oral, is receiving increasing attention: the Role-Playing Game (RPG).

RPG narratives have two interesting features
Tychsen 2006
;
Cover 2014
:

•

Collaborative Storytelling: the narrative is cooperative, with stories built by a group of people. One person acts as the ”moderator” or Game Master (GM), a neutral narrator who guides the story, manages the setting, and facilitates interactions between the Player Characters (PCs). Each PC, portrayed by a player, represents an independent actor within the story.

•

Primarily Oral Narrative: although the GM may use written materials (such as rulebooks or pre-planned adventures), and player characters have their abilities defined on character sheets, the core of the narrative unfolds orally. The GM describes the situations and impersonates Non-Player Characters (NPCs), while the players describe their PCs’ actions and dialogue, interacting with each other and the environment to achieve their goals.

Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have become widespread in RPGs because of their ability to generate stories and portray characters as virtual dialogue agents. Indeed, LLMs have proven useful in supporting GMs not only by generating random events and stories but also by making decisions for NPCs independently of the GM, adding unpredictability to their stories.

However, while an informal oral record characterises RPG sessions, the language of LLMs is often formal and may exhibit features typically associated with written narrative.

The objective of this paper is to discover to what extent the LLM language exhibits oral or written features. We will quantitatively and qualitatively analyze a dataset of automatically generated RPG sessions in which four LLM instances interact to generate a completely new story. We will conduct a linguistic analysis of the lexical and syntactic features of the generated texts and compare the results with analyses of oral conversations, transcripts of human RPG sessions, and books.

The paper is structured as follows: in the second section, we will briefly present the related works and the features of spoken and written discourses. In Section three, we describe our experiment and its results. Finally, in section five, we will extract our conclusions.

2
Related Works

Artificial Intelligence (AI), and particularly LLMs have been applied to the domain of RPGs in many different ways. Early work explored AI’s ability to play text-adventure games, with research focusing on generating interactive worlds and automated quest generation
Fan et al. 2020
;
Ammanabrolu et al. 2019
. These systems aimed to create engaging and dynamic game experiences by leveraging AI’s ability to understand and respond to player choices in a text-based environment.
More recent research has shifted towards using LLMs as virtual GMs in more complex RPG systems like
Dungeons & Dragons
(D&D). These models can generate stories, portray NPCs, and even make independent decisions for NPCs to enhance unpredictability
Callison-Burch et al. 2022
;
Zhu et al. 2023
. Several studies have investigated the potential of AI to participate in collaborative storytelling.
Branch et al. 2021
explored how human actors and AI narrators can co-create stories, while
Zhang et al. 2023
focused on building cooperative embodied agents using LLMs. These works highlight the potential of AI not just to generate stories but to actively participate in their creation alongside human players.

Beyond RPGs, research in story generation has explored various techniques to create engaging and coherent narratives.
Louis and Sutton 2018
used deep learning to model character interactions in D&D transcripts, while
Park et al. 2023
developed generative agents that simulate human behaviour in interactive narratives. Furthermore, researchers have tried to use common-sense reasoning to guide automated story generation and improve narrative coherence
Peng et al. 2022
. Despite the progress, challenges remain in developing AI systems that can truly capture the nuances of human storytelling and social interaction in RPGs. One key challenge is ensuring that AI-generated language aligns with the informal oral register typical of RPG sessions because the output of LLMs often exhibits features associated with written text.

Differences between orality and writing have been widely studied since the last century. The fundamental differences between oral and written cultures have been investigated by
Ong 1982
. The main interest of Ong was in primary oral cultures (i.e. groups of people that never known the writing). Although our interest lies not in oral cultures but solely in the differences between written and spoken narrative, some characteristics of orality highlighted by Ong may prove relevant. In particular, Ong defines oral thinking and expression as ”additive rather than subordinate”, with a prevalence of coordination mechanisms; as ”aggregative rather than analytic”, rich in epithets and parallel or opposite terms; ”redundant”; ”empathetic and participatory rather than objectively distanced”; ”homeostatic”, strongly anchored in the present; ”situational rather than abstract”, characterized by concreteness rather than abstraction.

Regarding the characteristics of spoken discourse,

Ochs 1979
and

Chafe 1984
identified a large list of features that in some cases correspond to the features of orality by Ong. He affirmed that written languages are characterized by a high degree of ”integration” in opposition to the ”fragmentation” of spoken languages. Instead, spoken languages are characterized by ”involvement”, in opposition to the ”detachment” typical of written languages. Chafe’s list of features directly refers to ”integration” and ”involvement”. Nevertheless,
Tannen 1982
remarks that those notions ”operate on different levels and are not mutually exclusive”.
Tannen 1982
proposed a comparative analysis of spoken and written versions of narratives, demonstrating ”(1) that features which have been identified as characterizing oral discourse are also found in written discourse, and (2) that the written short story combines syntactic complexity, expected in writing, with features that create involvement, expected in speaking”.

2.1
Spoken and Written Discourse Features

As
Tannen 1982
pointed out, written narratives can combine features of spoken discourse and formal writing, especially in short stories. A single discourse type can combine the features of ”integration” with the features of ”involvement”.
Tannen 1982
suggests that literary language, in general, ”uses features of involvement to create the kind of knowing that
Havelock 1963
shows to be basic to oral performance: subjective knowing, established through a sense of identification between audience and performer or audience and characters in the text.”

Tannen 1982
also reports some recurring hypotheses regarding written and spoken discourse.
Olson 1977
, for example, affirmed that spoken discourse is context-bound, while written discourse is de-contextualized. Cohesion is lexically established in writing but relies on paralinguistic and non-verbal channels in speech. However,
Tannen 1982
affirmed that the former characteristic is related to the genre of discourse (casual conversation versus expository prose), while the latter is ”a necessary concomitant of spoken and written modes.”

Similarly, Ochs observed that spoken discourse relies on simple morphosyntactic structures acquired early in life, such as coordination, while Chafe highlighted the prevalence of subordinate clauses and prepositional phrases in written discourse.

Oral expression is aggregative. This means that, for example, a noun is often accompanied by the same adjective, resulting in lexical or syntactic parallelisms. It is also empathetic and participatory, focusing on the speaker (through the use of the first person) or on emphatic particles. For example, ”reports of speaker’s mental processes” or ”Devices for monitoring communication channels” can be automatically analyzed only if we rely on specific semantic databases (for the tag of concepts related to mental processes or actions, agents, etc.) or if we have transcriptions that report non-verbal behaviours.

In the next section, we will associate the computationally verifiable features with standard automatic text analysis metrics to compare the language produced by a Large Language Model (LLM) with transcribed spoken discourse and written texts.

3
Experiment

To analyze the characteristics of the language produced by Large Language Models (LLMs), we analyzed a dataset of automatically generated texts and positioned them along a hypothetical continuum from spoken to written language. To do this, we compiled a diverse set of texts (sub-corpora), selecting eight different texts for each of the following categories:

•

Conversations
: Transcribed spoken conversations from the British National Corpus (BNC)
Leech 1993
;

•

Academic speeches
: Transcribed spoken academic discussions from the English as a Lingua Franca in the Academia (ELFA) corpus
Mauranen 2006
;

•

Human RPG sessions
: Transcriptions of D&D sessions from the online web series Critical Role (
https://criticalrole.fandom.com/
);

•

Books
: Classic fantasy novels published between 1932 (Conan the Barbarian) and 1997 (Harry Potter and the Philosopher’s Stone) (see Table
2
).

The sub-corpora were selected to facilitate a comparative analysis of RPG sessions with both spoken and written language corpora, specifically those representing conversational and narrative genres. The British National Corpus (BNC) was chosen due to its established reputation within the scientific community and its extensive collection of transcribed dialogues, providing a representative sample of everyday spoken English. In contrast, the ELFA corpus comprises academic speech, which exhibits distinct linguistic characteristics compared to the conversational language in the BNC. Notably including longer and more syntactically complex utterances. This corpus allows for an examination of spoken language used in a more formal and structured setting. Finally, the
book
sub-corpus consists of classic works of fantasy literature. This selection ensures domain consistency with the RPG sessions, enabling a comparison of the LLM-generated language to established examples of narrative prose within the fantasy genre.

The generated session begins with a manually introduced prompt:
”Please start the adventure!”
. The Game Master then produces an answer, which serves as the introduction to the adventure. The first player to respond is always Grog, who receives the Game Master’s answer as input. The second player, Pike, receives the concatenated responses of the Game Master and Grog as input. Similarly, Vax receives the concatenated responses of the Game Master, Grog, and Pike. After the third player provides their response, the entire sequence of answers is passed back to the Game Master. The new response from the Game Master is then passed to Grog, and the session loop continues in this manner.

The loop is interrupted by the system when it reaches 200 interactions per player or if the API reaches the token limit.

3.1
Qualitative analysis

A qualitative analysis of the LLM-generated data will yield a comprehensive understanding of the narrative mechanisms employed by these models.

Analysis of the generated sessions reveals a notable presence of Non-Player Characters (NPCs). These characters, typically controlled by the GM in traditional tabletop role-playing games, interact with PCs to guide the narrative trajectory. By influencing the PCs’ actions, the GM can effectively steer the narrative flow.

While Prompt 3 (”Legions of Thyatis”) explicitly defined the names and roles of certain NPCs, Prompts 1 (”Dungeon Master Rulebook Adventure”) and 2 (”Horror on the hill”) provided no such specifications. In these instances, the LLM exhibited a tendency to repeat generic character designations across sessions. Notably, the name ”Grimbold” was assigned to the innkeeper in both Session 1 and Session 2, as well as to the blacksmith in Sessions 3 and 6. Furthermore, each Grimbold served a consistent narrative function: warning the PCs of impending danger. This repetition suggests the LLM may have been influenced by readily available online resources, as ”Grimbold Ironfist” is a common NPC archetype in Dungeons & Dragons campaigns.

The antagonist varied across sessions, except in the final two, where the prompt explicitly designated this role. Similarly, the LLM exhibited considerable variability in its generation of key objects, suggesting a degree of creativity in this domain. While an ”(ancient) book” appeared in three sessions, a diverse array of other objects were also introduced, including a crystal, a map, a tapestry, a talking statue, a wooden box, a fragment of ancient knowledge, and a magic stone.

However, divergences between the LLM and human GM become more apparent in the dialogue generated for individual characters, as we will see below.

This observation aligns with the findings of the quantitative analysis, which we will present in the next subsection.

3.2.1
Lexical Features

We analyzed a variety of lexical features. First, we calculated the
D-value

Malvern and Richards 1997
, a validated measure of lexical diversity
McCarthy and Jarvis 2010
. The D-value is an alternative to the Type-Token Ratio and is less sensitive to text length. It works by calculating the probability that two randomly selected words are different.

We also calculated the
lexical range
(LR) as a measure of the lexical complexity of the texts. As suggested by
Kormos 2011
, we used the dictionaries of the Nation’s Range program
Heatley et al. 2002
, which calculates the percentage of words in a text that belong to the most frequent 1,000 (LR1) and 2,000 (LR2) words of ”A General Service List of English Words”
West 1953
, and the percentage of words belonging to ”The Academic Word List” (LR3) by
Coxhead 2000
.

Although lexical diversity and range are not explicitly included in the list of features associated with orality, they can effectively capture differences between spoken and written texts. For instance, the use of formulas and repetitions in spoken discourse can affect the lexical diversity index and the lexical range of a text. To account for this, we also calculated the lexical repetitions found in the texts.
Lexical repetitions
were calculated by summing the uni-grams, bi-grams, and tri-grams repeated within a 2, 5, and 10-word window.

Concreteness
ratings were based on the MRC Psycholinguistic Database
Coltheart 1981
, which provides concreteness values ranging from 100 to 700 for a large set of words. Concreteness is a specific feature of spoken discourse
Chafe 1984
. To obtain a weighted concreteness index, we divided the sum of the concreteness values for all retrieved words in a text by the product of the number of tokens in the text and 100.

Frequencies of
deictics
,
attributive adjectives
, and
emphatic particles
were calculated by identifying the number of words in the text that belong to specific lists. These lists were compiled by collecting the most frequent elements of each category. We manually collected 69 deictics, 365 attributive adjectives, and 106 emphatic particles. As shown in Table
1
, emphatic particles and deictics are features of spoken discourse, while the presence of attributive adjectives characterizes written texts.

3.2.2
Syntactic Complexity

Syntactic complexity measures were calculated using the coreNLP Java package
Manning et al. 2014
, which provides comprehensive syntactic tagging of texts. We calculated the
number of subordinate
and
relative clauses
using the dependency relation tags provided by coreNLP, corresponding to the Universal Dependency tags ”csub,” ”ccomp,” ”advcl,” ”acl,” ”xcomp,” and ”parataxis.” The tags ”advcl:relcl” and ”acl:relcl” specifically identify relative clauses. A large number of subordinate and relative clauses is characteristic of written text, as noted by
Chafe 1984
, and is closely related to the use of complex morphosyntactic structures, which
Ochs 1979
includes in their list of written features.

Syntactic complexity is also indicated by a high
average distance from the main element
of the sentence (the root node in the dependency graph, tagged as ”ROOT”) and by a high
graph depth
. The distance from the root is calculated as the average number of words separating the root from each word in the sentence. The depth of the graph is calculated as the maximum number of nodes separating the root from any other node.

Additionally, we calculated the number of
noun modifiers
(”nmod”) as another measure of syntactic complexity.

3.2.3
Analysis of Verbs

As shown in Table
1
, a key difference between writing and speech lies in the use of
verb tenses
. We used the results of the syntactic parsing to calculate the percentage of verbs in the present tense (tagged as ”VB,” ”VBZ,” and ”VBP”), past tense (”VBD”), and gerund or participle forms (”VBG,” ”VBN,” and ”VBG”). We also counted the number of
first-person pronouns
(”I,” ”me,” ”mine,” ”myself”) as a feature of spoken discourse.

3.2.4
Frequency of Connectives

As suggested by
Kormos 2011
, we counted the number of connectives as a measure of text
cohesion
. Connectives are important devices for signaling relationships within a text
Halliday and Hasan 2014
and can provide insights into the textual quality of writing
Cameron et al. 1995
. Following
Halliday and Hasan 2014
, we focused on four types of connectives: additive, causal, temporal, and logical. As noted by
Kormos 2011
, causal cohesion is particularly relevant when narrating events. We categorized connectives as either positive, extending the information provided in the text, or negative, restricting the information. We selected the 20 most frequent positive and negative connectives for each class and calculated the relative frequency of each class per 1,000 words of text.

Next, we constructed a weighted cohesion score for each text. This score was calculated by summing the normalized frequencies of each connective class, with weights assigned to reflect the perceived contribution of each class to overall cohesion. Causal connectives received the highest weight (2), followed by logical and additive connectives (1.5) and temporal connectives (1).

To understand the distribution of cohesion scores across each corpus, we calculated the standard deviation. To further analyze the relationship between connective frequency and variability, we computed the logarithm of the standard deviation, using the weighted sum of frequencies as the base. This compressed the values and highlighted the differences among the analyzed classes.

Starting with the lexical features, the differences between writing and speech are evident. Taking into account that the D-value is partially influenced by text length, we found a particularly high value in the traditional speech corpora (

For LR3, the values increased from 0.013 for both speech corpora to 0.03 for human RPG and book, to 0.04 for LLM RPG. 3.4
Discussion

The results of our experiment do not provide a definitive answer regarding the language register that LLMs adopt in their narrative strategies. From a lexical perspective, the D-value, a measure of lexical richness, highlights the differences between human and LLM-generated D&D sessions. The D-values of the LLM sessions are much closer to those of books, likely reflecting the nature of the LLMs’ training data, which primarily consists of written text. This influences the LLMs’ ability to accurately reflect the style of a real RPG session. The analysis of lexical ranges supports this hypothesis: while we found similar LR1 values in Critical Role, books, and LLM-generated texts, the LLMs made more extensive use of terms included in the LR2 and LR3 dictionaries, indicating a greater reliance on less frequent and more academic vocabulary. Additionally, the concreteness value of Critical Role reflects its similarity to written texts, possibly due to the planned nature of the Dungeon Master’s speech, while the results for the LLMs may indicate a lack of planning inherent in their generative process. Finally, the most significant lexical finding is the high number of repetitions in the LLM-generated texts.

From a syntactic perspective, we first note that the Critical Role transcripts are much more similar to books than to the other speech categories. The syntactic data yielded two different and somewhat contradictory results: considering the sentence length and depth of the dependency graphs, we found that LLMs exhibit a pattern distinct from all other text categories. Specifically, they produce the longest sentences and the lowest distance from the root node. This indicates that the sentences produced by the LLMs are longer than those produced by humans, and their main element (the root) tends to be located near the center of the sentence. Regarding subordinate clauses, LLM-generated texts contain more subordinate clauses than books but a lower ratio of relative clauses, exceeding the values achieved by both in the Critical Role transcripts. Additionally, both LLMs and books exhibit a greater number of noun modifiers compared to the speech.

The analysis of verb tenses reveals a key difference between LLMs and the other texts. The LLMs almost exclusively used the present tense, occasionally replaced by participles, but rarely used the past tense. In Critical Role, the present tense is also dominant but interspersed with the past tense. This difference may stem from the LLMs’ focus on the immediate actions and events of the narrative, while Critical Role includes numerous references to the characters’ backstories and past events.

Finally, the analysis of textual cohesion presents some peculiarities, potentially due to the limited number of connectives included in the dictionary. For example, books exhibit a lower cohesion value compared to Critical Role or ELFA, possibly because more frequent connectives are often replaced by synonyms in books to avoid redundancy. Additionally, as Ong suggests, coordination is typically associated with orality. However, temporal connectives are most frequent in books, likely due to the greater importance of temporal connections between events in written narratives. In contrast, the LLMs obtained a very low cohesion value, primarily due to the lack of logical and causal connectives in their generated texts
Maisto 2024
. Nevertheless, when calculating the correlation among the frequency of connectives in each text category, we found a strong correlation among all the spoken corpora but only a moderate correlation between books and LLM-generated texts.

As we commented for the qualitative analysis, the GM descriptions in LLM and human sessions are similar, while the PC dialogues in the human sessions reflect the spontaneous nature of spoken discourse.

The Critical Role (CR) transcripts differ from the other speech corpora in that the GM’s language exhibits characteristics more akin to written language (Books), while the PC dialogues retain features typical of spoken language. For instance, in terms of lexical analysis, CR often falls between the speech corpora and Books, with exceptions observed in indicators such as deictic frequency and Lexical Range 3. Syntactically, CR displays average sentence length. However, the prevalence of short utterances in PC dialogues contributes to a lower proportion of subordinate and relative clauses, as well as a reduced Distance of the ROOT value. Cohesion analysis further confirms average values for CR.

Conversely, the LLM-generated dialogues present more irregular values across these linguistic measures. This irregularity may stem from the unique nature of the LLM-generated language, which blends features of both written and spoken discourse. While the dialogues are rich in descriptive language, complex sentences, and noun modifiers, they also exhibit extensive use of the present tense and a notably low degree of cohesion.

We decided to analyze the linguistic features of CR and LLM dialogues by dividing each session into two parts: the first containing only the DM dialogue lines (CR-DM and LLM-DM) and the second containing the PC dialogues (CR-PC and LLM-PC). 4
Conclusion

The results of our experiment offer insights into the narrative strategies of these LLMs with a similar architecture and training data to LLaMa3. Lexically, they demonstrate a rich vocabulary, with lexical diversity comparable to written texts, likely due to their extensive training data. They generate complex sentences with subordinate clauses and noun modifiers, approaching the syntactic complexity of the written text. However, analysis of concreteness and lexical repetition suggests that these LLMs generate text in a less planned manner than humans. Syntactically, these models produce longer sentences with a lower distance from the root node than human-generated text, indicating a unique sentence structure. They utilize more subordinate clauses than books, but fewer relative clauses, and more noun modifiers than speech. These findings suggest that they possess a distinct syntactic signature. A key difference between narratives generated by these models and humans lies in verb tenses. LLMs predominantly use the present tense, rarely employing the past tense. This contrasts with human narratives, which utilize a mix of present and past tenses to establish temporal context and character background. The analysis of textual cohesion reveals that LLMs exhibit low cohesion, with limited use of logical and causal connectives. This may contribute to less nuanced and persuasive narratives compared to human-generated stories. From this point of view, an LLM cannot replace the human GM in an RPG, but it can be effectively employed to simulate a non-player character upon precise instructions, introducing unpredictability to the narrative and ensuring greater variety to the story. Overall, these LLM models show potential for generating narratives, but their strategies differ significantly from human approaches
Lieto 2021
. They appear better suited for generating formal written text, with limitations in replicating spoken language nuances and constructing temporally and textually complex narratives.

Further research is needed to understand the impact of training data on LLM narrative strategies, develop methods for improving textual cohesion and verb tense usage, and investigate reinforcement learning techniques for training LLMs to generate more human-like narratives, including affective analysis with systems like
Lieto et al. 2021
.
