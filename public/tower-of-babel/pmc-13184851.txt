Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy After Cardiac Surgery
Authors: Sophia B See, Ian YH Chan, Jacky YK Ho, Randolph HL Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTakotsubo cardiomyopathy (TTC) is an episode of transient left ventricular (LV) dysfunction affecting 2.1% of patients with suspected acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.Case SummaryA 61-year-old woman underwent elective aortic valve replacement for severe aortic regurgitation. An entry tear at the aortic cannulation site necessitated hemiarch replacement. On postoperative day 3, the patient presented with cardiogenic shock, displaying deteriorating LV function with global hypokinesia. Coronary angiography revealed minimal coronary artery disease, whereas computed tomography revealed LV apical ballooning. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support was initiated. Echocardiography and computed tomography showed normalization of LV function and resolution of apical ballooning 2 weeks later, permitting extracorporeal membrane oxygenation weaning. The patient was discharged in good health 1 month postoperatively.DiscussionCardiac surgery complicated by TTC is rarely described. This case highlights prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time and surgical stress as potential TTC triggers.Take-Home MessageClinicians should be mindful of TTC risk after cardiac surgery.Key Words: aortic valve, cardiomyopathy, complication, hemiarch replacement, postoperative, stress cardiomyopathy, valve replacement

History of Presentation

A 61-year-old woman with a history of smoking, severe aortic regurgitation, NYHA functional class I-II heart failure, and a preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 45% was referred to our institution for an elective aortic valve replacement (AVR) (Figure 1).

Past Medical History

Two years prior, she presented with dyspnea, where echocardiography revealed severe global left ventricular systolic impairment with left ventricular dilation, and an ectatic ascending aorta measuring 4.1 cm. The chest radiograph also showed cardiomegaly.

Procedural Details

AVR was performed through a median sternotomy using standard cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) via aortic and venous cannulation, monitored by transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). The left ventricle (LV) was vented with the right superior pulmonary vein. After aortic cross-clamping, aortotomy was performed, and myocardial protection was administered with direct coronary cardioplegia. The native aortic valve was resected and replaced with a 25-mm mechanical AVR On-X valve (Artivion Inc). CPB was uneventfully weaned. CPB time was 94 minutes, and aortic cross-clamp time was 68 minutes for the valve replacement procedure.

TEE during decannulation identified an entry tear at the aortic cannulation site with propagation of dissection retrogradely and antegradely. In light of this, the surgical team decided to proceed with hemiarch replacement. Systemic heparin was readministered in conjunction with CPB re-establishment via the right common femoral artery and double-stage venous cannulation. The ascending aorta was transected and replaced with a size 26 J-graft (Japan Lifeline Co, Ltd) with meticulous reinforced anastomoses. CPB and cross-clamp times were 142 minutes and 70 minutes, respectively, for the hemiarch replacement procedure. Vital signs and electrocardiogram monitoring remained stable throughout both operations. Postoperative recovery was initially unremarkable, with successful weaning from ventilation on postoperative day 1.

Investigations and Management

The patient developed deterioration of hemodynamics with elevated lactate levels on the second postoperative day. A computed tomography (CT) aortogram confirmed residual arch and descending thoracic aortic intramural hematoma extending to the aortic bifurcation with a false lumen break just above the right renal artery branch. On postoperative day 3, after a period of anxiety, the patient developed nonsustained supraventricular tachycardia and atrial fibrillation requiring multiple cardioversions. Echocardiography showed deterioration of LVEF to 10%, with global hypokinesia most pronounced in the apical and middle regions. CT thorax confirmed LV apical ballooning indicative of Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TTC) (Figure 2). Subsequently, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support through right femoral arterial and venous cannulation was initiated to provide mechanical circulatory support.

Outcome and Follow-Up

During ECMO, the initial echocardiogram showed the presence of a dilated LV with spontaneous echocardiographic contrast. The patient was anuric and required commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. After 2 weeks of ECMO support, follow-up CT thorax demonstrated normalization of left ventricular shape and wall thickness with gradual LVEF improvement, which allowed ECMO weaning (Figure 3). The patient was on renal replacement therapy for another 2 weeks after ECMO. Eventually her renal function recovered, and she was discharged 1 month after the operation. She has since maintained a state of good health thereafter.

Discussion

TTC comprises 2.1% of patients with suspected acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.1 It occurs predominantly in postmenopausal women, often after an episode of emotional or physical stress.2,3

TTC is observed in 0.9% of patients after cardiac surgery under CPB.4 The exact mechanism remains unclear, but it is hypothesized to be due to the emotional or physical stress of surgery. The consequential surge in plasma catecholamines from sympathetic activation causes direct myocardial toxicity and transient left ventricular dysfunction. This effect is mediated by intense β-adrenergic receptor stimulation via the Gs protein-adenylyl cyclase-cyclic adenosine monophosphate−protein kinase A pathway.2

Our case illustrates a rare occurrence of TTC after AVR, complicated by iatrogenic aortic dissection necessitating hemiarch replacement. Our patient demonstrates characteristics aligning with most TTC diagnostic criteria proposed internationally.3 Her demographic predisposition, with reduced estrogen levels, likely impairs cardioprotective effects, including vasodilation and coronary blood flow.5 This reduces the ability to decrease oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby increasing her vulnerability to TTC. On the second postoperative day, she presented with cardiogenic shock that was speculated to be precipitated by emotional stress, displayed as an episode of anxiety. This is reflected in her presentation of tachyarrhythmia, as evidenced by her electrocardiogram abnormalities. Repeated TEEs demonstrated left ventricular hypokinesia of the apical and midventricular segments, concurrent with hyperkinetic basal segments, creating the characteristic ballooning appearance of myocardial stunning. TTC is a stress-induced apical ballooning in the absence of underlying coronary artery disease. Although patients with low cardiac output syndrome typically present with global left ventricular, right ventricular, or biventricular hypokinesia without apical ballooning after prolonged CPB, low cardiac output syndrome is not mutually exclusive from TTC and can instead manifest from TTC. The resolution of her LV wall motion abnormalities within 2 weeks underscores its transient nature, differentiating it from permanent ischemic injury as seen in myocardial infarction. This conclusion was also reinforced by the absence of significant coronary artery occlusions revealed by coronary angiography. Pheochromocytoma was considered unlikely given the absence of a detectable adrenal mass as well as lack of episodic hypertension or tachyarrhythmia after weaning from ECMO. However, it remains in the latest diagnostic criteria as a risk factor for TTC regardless. In addition, her presentation was distinguishable from iatrogenic cardiomyopathy due to adequate cardioplegia provision and delayed onset of myocardial stunning, appearing 2 days postoperatively rather than immediately at CPB weaning. Ultimately, these findings confirmed the diagnosis of TTC.

What makes this case exceptional is the complexity of her condition and, hence, procedures. Valvular and aortic replacement procedures complicated by TTC have rarely been documented. Most reported cases of TTC after cardiac surgery involved mitral and tricuspid valve repairs, accounting for 86%.6 In contrast, our patient underwent open AVR that required additional re-establishment of CPB due to her intraoperative complication, demanding emergent hemiarch replacement. We suspect increased vulnerability to TTC with complex valve procedures due to the extended duration of CPB. Studies have shown that cardiac surgeries operated under CPB increase plasma catecholamine concentrations by 3-fold.7 In addition, there is evidence of acute myocardial β-adrenergic receptor dysfunction due to desensitization and reduced signal transduction during aortocoronary surgery under CPB, regardless of preoperative protection by β-adrenergic antagonists.8 Similarly, there is documentation of hemodynamic improvement after withdrawal of extrinsically administered inotropes in patients with postoperative TTC.9 Another possible mechanism could be direct injury to cardiomyocytes. Elevated catecholamine levels decrease myocyte viability by triggering calcium overload through cyclic adenosine monophosphate pathways. Concurrently, the generation of oxygen-derived free radicals disrupts sodium and calcium transporters, leading to excessive calcium influx and cellular dysfunction.10 This combined effect drives the pathogenesis of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury. Endogenous catecholamines, coupled with exogenous administration, transiently impair myocardial contractility, which may have exacerbated the postoperative myocardial stunning exhibited in our patient.

Conclusions

Valvular and aortic replacement surgeries may act as potential triggers for TTC. This case study highlights the need for increased clinical suspicion of TTC risk after open valvular replacement surgery, warranting increased vigilance and strategies to minimize catecholamine exposure and surgical stress.
