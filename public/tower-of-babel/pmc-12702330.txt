Protocol for quantifying coccidian parasite mechanics and rupture force using advanced micromanipulation-based piercing
Authors: Jana El Husseiny, Pierre-Henri Puech, Aurélien Dumètre, Julien Husson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCoccidian parasites can rely on their mechanical properties to infect their hosts. Here, we detail a microindentation-based technique for quantifying the wall mechanics of coccidian oocysts. We describe steps for micropipette fabrication, calibration of flexible microindenters, system assembly, and quantifying indentation and rupture forces. We then present procedures for automated data analysis using open-source software and programming languages. The protocol enables measurement of wall rupture forces and is applicable to study the integrity of parasites under various physiological conditions.Subject areas: Biophysics, Microbiology, microscopy

Before you begin

This protocol outlines an advanced microindentation technique for quantifying the mechanical properties of the oocyst wall of coccidian parasites, specifically its bending stiffness (N/m) and rupture forces. The method is adapted from the cell indentation system designed by Husson.1 This approach operates across a wide force range (typically in the nN to μN range) and is particularly suitable for weakly or non-adhesive, asymmetric or inhomogeneous biological objects with cellular dimensions (∼5–30 μm).

Figure 1 shows the main components of the microindentation setup. This configuration is essentially identical to the one described in Husson1 with the primary modification being the use of a needle-shaped indenter. The microindentation and piercing technique enables controlled piercing of the multilayered polymeric oocyst walls of coccidian parasites and accurate measurement of rupture forces while allowing precise control of parasite orientation. This capability supports a comprehensive understanding of the structure’s mechanical characteristics and integrity when exposed to biochemical or biophysical stresses that may influence parasite infectivity.

The ability of this technique to explore different orientations is particularly valuable for studying asymmetric biological structures, as it allows for a complete assessment of wall strength variations that may be critical to the organism’s survival. Furthermore, the ability to perform these measurements on non-adherent biological objects significantly expands the range of microorganisms that can be studied, including coccidian parasites.

By combining force measurements with real-time visual analysis, this protocol enables researchers to correlate structural deformations with specific force thresholds, providing insights into the wall mechanical properties, such as its elasticity and breaking points. This level of detail is crucial for deciphering how microorganisms withstand environmental pressures and how their walls might respond to potential therapeutic interventions.

The consistency and reliability of the measurements, together with its relative ease of use for trained experimentalist, make it an invaluable tool for comparative studies across different species or strains of microorganisms. It also enables assessment of the effect of treatments or genetic modifications on wall integrity.

Innovation

The microindentation technique provides key advantages over other biophysical indentation techniques, such as atomic force microscopy (AFM), which generally requires adhesive cells and can restrict object orientation. In contrast, the approach described here enables mechanical characterization of non-adherent and asymmetrically shaped organisms and supports controlled variation of sample orientation.

This protocol integrates custom-fabricated, needle-shaped glass microindenters with automated image-guided control to improve precision over existing micromanipulation and indentation techniques. Each microindenter is calibrated for bending stiffness, enabling reliable force measurements in the nanonewton to micronewton range. The system combines optical tracking with real-time feedback regulation of a piezoelectric stage to achieve spatial and temporal resolution during indentation and allowing detection of discrete rupture events associated with different wall layers. In parallel, an open-source Python-based computational pipeline performs automated signal filtering, peak detection, and force quantification from time-resolved datasets. By unifying microtool fabrication, calibration, mechanical testing, and data analysis, this method offers a reproducible, accessible, and versatile alternative to atomic force microscopy for investigating microorganism wall biomechanics and their responses to environmental stressors or therapeutics.

Expected outcomes

The microindenter (also called microneedle herein) deflection vs. time obtained using this protocol are converted to force measurements by multiplying the microindenter deflection by the calibrated bending stiffness of the microindenter. Parasite indentation is calculated by subtracting the microindenter deflection from the piezoelectric stage displacement. If an infinitely stiff object were brought into contact with the microindenter, the piezoelectric displacement would be entirely transmitted to microindenter deflection. However, when a deformable object such as a parasite is pressed against the microindenter tip, the piezoelectric displacement is partitioned between parasite indentation and microindenter deflection. As a result, the parasite indentation is obtained by a straightforward subtraction of the microindenter deflection to the piezoelectric displacement. This relationship between piezoelectric displacement, microindenter deflection, and target indentation is further explained in Husson.1

As illustrated in Figure 3, we obtain in fine a series of images capturing real-time rupture events for each wall component, synchronized with corresponding force/indentation vs. time curves that provide a comprehensive visualization of force dynamics throughout the indentation process. In panel A, the force–time curve shows the gradual buildup of force as the oocyst is indented, punctuated by distinct rupture events corresponding to mechanical failure of structural components. Panels B–G provide synchronized microscopy snapshots at key time points. The sequence begins before contact (B), followed by proximal wall contact (C) and its rupture (D), after which the microindenter continues until distal wall contact (E) and rupture (F). Finally, the glass–glass contact between the microindenter tip and the inner holding pipette wall is reached (G), corresponding to the sharp linear increase in force. Panel H summarizes proximal wall rupture forces across multiple measurements, highlighting the reproducibility and variability of the observed mechanical thresholds.

Glass-glass contact cannot be determined with sufficient accuracy through visual inspection alone. However, this event is clearly identifiable in the deflection vs. time curve. When the needle tip contacts the inner wall of the holding micropipette, friction causes the tip to interlock with the micropipette wall. Consequently, the microindenter tip suddenly begins moving at the constant translation velocity of the piezoelectric stage (0.5 μm/s). Glass-glass contact typically occurs after the distal wall of the parasite has been pierced and therefore does not interfere with our measurements. The algorithm controlling piezoelectric translation is programmed to halt when a maximum deflection, set in the interface of the software, is reached, preventing microindenter breakage.

Measuring both force and indentation vs. time enables precise quantification of indentation and rupture forces for both proximal and distal walls of the oocyst after a meticulous calibration of the microneedle bending stiffness.

These force curves reveal critical mechanical behaviors, beginning with initial contact between the microindenter and oocyst wall, progressing through elastic deformation of the structure and yield points, culminating in distinct rupture events marked by sudden force drops. The synchronized presentation of visual time points and force curves allows us to correlate specific structural events with quantitative force measurements, offering nuanced insights into the oocyst wall mechanics. By tracking the differential strengths between proximal and distal walls and examining rate-dependent mechanical properties, the protocol provides an unprecedented understanding of cellular structural integrity.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For data acquisition, analysis and post-processing, we chose to use open source software and programming languages. We use the last version of Micro-manager (version 1.4.22) and Fiji (version 2.16.0), this latter being a meta-package of ImageJ, with specialized plugins available either on Fiji/ImageJ website, programmer’s website or GitHub repository. For Python, we use the latest release of Anaconda Python distribution (version 3.10.18), which contains all the needed packages for data science applications, together with the Jupyter notebook system utilized in this protocol. The complete analysis code is openly available on GitHub (https://github.com/phpuech/FindPeaks). Installation can be performed directly with the provided requirements.txt files to recreate the Python environment.

A Python Jupyter notebook process has been designed to analyze time-series data from the experimentally obtained tab-separated values files, specifically focusing on deflection measurements over time. As illustrated in Figure 4, the pipeline begins by importing the time-resolved microindenter deflection and piezo displacement data (tab-separated format) and computing indentation over time by subtracting deflection from piezo displacement. To reduce noise and improve peak detection, it applies a mean filtering technique through the mean_filter_adaptive function, smoothing the deflection data. Once the data is processed, the function identifies peaks using the find_peaks method from the scipy.signal module. The method allows for adjustable parameters, such as peak prominence, which can be varied based on the shape of the peaks, and height constraints, where the maximum height is determined by the highest value recorded before the retraction of the microindenter. Following detection, rupture forces are computed by multiplying the calibrated microindenter deflection by its bending stiffness, and the resulting forces and rupture positions are stored in a pandas DataFrame, which also includes metadata such as the file path and folder name. For visualization, the code generates matplotlib/seaborn plot displaying the original deflection data, the smoothed signal, and the identified peaks, which are marked as red dots. To enhance readability, the plot is refined using seaborn.despine(), and appropriate labels and titles are added. The final plot is saved as a high-resolution.png file in a specified output folder. Ultimately, the code returns the DataFrame containing peak information and saves it as a.xlsx file, for further processing with ad-hoc plotting and statistical analysis secondary Jupyter-based code. Of note, we mainly use non parametric statistical tests since the data distribution is often observed to be non-Gaussian.

Limitations

This microindentation technique, while precise, has several limitations.

For Eimeria oocyst parasites, temperature control is less critical as they survive at lower temperatures. However, when working with other cell types that require physiological conditions, maintaining 37°C might be challenging. The inefficient heating of the large open dish and the resulting temperature gradients can cause unwanted objects motions. Temperature control represents an additional experimental challenge that would require substantial modifications to the current setup. Implementation would necessitate choosing between a simple heating plate approach versus full system thermalization, each presenting distinct complications. A heating plate would create temperature gradients and potential thermal drift, while complete system thermalization would require protecting sensitive electronics from heat exposure and potentially moisture if humidity is also controlled. Additionally, temperature elevation increases the risk of bubble formation in the tubing linking the micropipette holder and the reservoir, which would block the applied aspiration. Thermal expansion of system components could also introduce positional drift that would compromise the precision required for microindentation measurements. The current protocol prioritizes mechanical reproducibility at ambient temperature, though future developments could address these environmental control challenges.

The setup is sensitive to environmental factors like vibrations and air currents. The use of a damping table or any vibration isolation system is highly recommended. The proximity of vents or aspiration systems as for air conditioning may introduce mechanical noise.

The integrity of the microindenter is critical for accurate force measurements, and any damage can lead to calibration errors. Standard microindenter calibration is based on using a commercial force probe. In case of limited budget, an alternative method consists of using the weight of small pieces of wire or paper as known forces. In a previous study,7 we determined the density of a lightweight paper by measuring the mass of pieces whose surface area was measured under a microscope. We then calibrated reference microindenters by measuring their vertical deflection under known weights of paper pieces. This alternative technique, however, is less accurate and much more time-consuming than using a commercial force probe.

The protocol presented here is optimized for specific coccidian parasites, and adaptation may be required for other microorganisms and cell types, especially regarding their diameter and the force applied to pierce them. When manipulating non-ovoidal parasites, several key parameters require adjustment. Researchers should adapt the pipette diameter to target locally spherical regions of the parasite to ensure optimal sealing and prevent leakage that would reduce aspiration efficiency. The approach geometry is equally critical aligning the holding micropipette axis with the indentation tip minimizes lateral torque that could cause the parasite to rotate or even escape from the pipette during manipulation. Regarding aspiration pressure, the holding force follows the relationship F=ΔP×π×Rpipette2, where ΔP is the pressure difference and Rpipette is the internal pipette radius. Therefore, when using pipettes of different diameters, the aspiration pressure must be adjusted accordingly to ΔP=F/(π×Rpipette2) to maintain equivalent holding force. This calculation assumes a tight seal between the parasite and pipette opening; any leakage will reduce the effective aspiration force below the theoretical maximum. For irregularly shaped parasites, researchers may empirically optimize these parameters through preliminary trials, as the contact geometry will influence both sealing efficiency and may also impact the mechanical response during indentation.

Along with this, adjustment of the indenter’s bending stiffness may be necessary when working with particularly fragile or mechanically stiff cells. This can be achieved by selecting indenters with appropriate geometries to either decrease or increase stiffness as required.

Finally, the technique measures only one oocyst at a time, requiring multiple measurements for statistical significance (typically 25–30 per experimental condition), potentially introducing sample-to-sample variability. Moreover, the manual nature of oocyst selection and positioning limits experimental throughput.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Glass enters inside the capillary during trimming, leading to a broken opening and aspiration system leakage (related to Step 2).

Potential solution

Ensure the cut is as straight as possible from all directions. This will help maintain the integrity of the capillary and prevent unwanted glass intrusion. If the section of the capillary becomes too close to the desired one or is larger, pull another needle and restart the process.

Problem 2

The pipette bends irregularly or breaks during heating (related to Step 3, 6, and 7).

Potential solution

Reduce power or heating duration. Proceed with shorter, more gentle, and more numerous heating steps. If issues persist, restart with a new capillary. This approach allows for more controlled shaping of the pipette.

Problem 3

Errors in calibration of the microindenter, specifically in Kref (reference microindenter bending stiffness), propagate to the calculated Kmi (related to Step 12).

Potential solution

Ensure accurate knowledge of the microindenter’s bending stiffness. Regularly calibrate the force transducer used to calibrate reference microindenters according to the manufacturer’s specifications. This will ensure and maintain the accuracy of force measurements in the entire experiment set.

Problem 4

Bubbles get stuck in the micropipette (in particular in very thin and/or the curved regions), syringe, 4-way valve, or meniscus of the pipette (related to Step 14, 15, and 16).

Potential solution

Gently flick the syringe with your fingernail to dislodge bubbles. If passing to a positive pressure by raising the water reservoir does not stop aspiration, it’s likely due to a bubble in the micropipette holder or tubing. In such cases, consider replacing the micropipette or cutting its back a few millimeters with a diamond tip pen.

Problem 5

Objects, biological or not, such as preparation remnants, adhere to the side walls of the pipette (related to Step 19).

Potential solution

Increase BSA concentration in the buffer to 2%–3%. This enhances surface passivation and reduces unwanted adhesion. Be careful then with air bubbles, which are a bit more stabilized hence prone to form.

Problem 6

The oocyst wall and microindenter are not in the same focus during acquisition, causing the microindenter to deflect or slide on the wall (related to Step 26).

Potential solution

Monitor the focus before acquisition to ensure both objects are aligned. Make slight focus adjustments during acquisition if necessary. This ensures accurate interaction between the microindenter and the sample.

Problem 7

Poor indenter deflection signal quality due to setup vibrations (related to Step 25).

Potential solution

Avoid or reduce vibrations for better signal quality by using damping tables and not touching the setup during measurements, especially when using soft microindenters and high-magnification objectives. Avoid tension in wires, particularly in heavy camera cables. Minimize objects with fans present or touching on the insulating table. Consider enclosing the setup in an acoustic insulation case, though this may reduce accessibility. Avoid proximity to air conditioning and open windows in the room.

Problem 8

The notebook cannot read the input file, or the columns “time” or “deflection” are not detected (related to the “expected outcomes” section).

Potential solution

Verify that the file path is correct and that the file exists. Ensure the tab-separated format is consistent. Check the file headers for typos, extra spaces, or mismatched names, and rename columns as needed.

Problem 9

Peaks are flattened or missed after smoothing, spurious peaks appear due to noise, or true peaks are not detected during peak identification (related to the “expected outcomes” section).

Potential solution

Adjust the smoothing window and modify find_peaks parameters such as prominence, height, width, and distance. Always visually inspect the plots to confirm that detected peaks correspond to true events and not artifacts, especially when peak values are low.
