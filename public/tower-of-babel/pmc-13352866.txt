Biosynthetic pathway construction and production enhancement of phenazine-1-carboxylic acid derivatives in Pseudomonas chlororaphis H18
Authors: Sijia Xu, Chaozhi Wang, Chenxi Gong, Jinbei Shi, Xiao Zhang, Mohd Sadeeq, Wei Huang, Peng Xiong, Feifei Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhenazines represent a promising class of natural products with diverse biological activities, including antitumor, antiparasitic and antibacterial properties. Among them, phenazine-1-carboxylic acid (PCA)—a registered biopesticide—stands out for its broad-spectrum antifungal efficacy, low environmental toxicity and potential as a lead structure for developing anti-tumor and antituberculosis agents. However, the chemical synthesis of PCA derivatives is often hindered by harsh reaction conditions, toxic byproducts and poor selectivity, limiting their sustainable production. In contrast, biosynthesis emerges as a green and scalable alternative, yet efficient microbial platforms for structurally diverse PCA derivatives remain underdeveloped.ResultsTo address this gap, we engineered Pseudomonas chlororaphis H18 into a versatile cell factory for PCA derivative biosynthesis. We first characterized the substrate promiscuity of the S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)-dependent methyltransferase Pcm2, demonstrating its capacity to catalyze methylation and ethylation of many aromatic carboxylic acids. This catalytic versatility broadens the scope of PCA derivatives that can be accessed through biosynthesis. By integrating the pcm2 gene into the P. chlororaphis H18 genome, we established de novo pathways for two key derivatives: phenazine-1-carboxymethyl (PCM) and 2-hydroxy-phenazine-1-carboxymethyl (2-OH-PCM) for the first time, with PCM having been reported to exhibit superior fungicidal activity against the tobacco brown spot pathogen, Alternaria alternata, compared to PCA. To enhance titers of PCM and 2-OH-PCM, we implemented a systematic metabolic engineering strategy comprising: (1) increasing intracellular SAM supply, (2) overexpressing key genes, (3) knocking out the negative regulators and (4) disrupting competitive pathway. The engineered strain H18A4 produced 124.6 mg/L PCM and 130.8 mg/L 2-OH-PCM in KB medium. To further increase production of PCM and prevent PCA hydroxylation, we disrupted the phzO gene to block conversion to hydroxylated derivatives, increased pcm2 gene copy number and optimized the fermentation medium. The resulting engineered strain H18A6 produced 1.37 g/L PCM in fed-batch fermentation—a marked advance toward scalable, bio-based production of this high-value compound.ConclusionsThis study fills a technical gap by establishing a novel biosynthetic route for diverse PCA derivatives, overcoming the limitations of chemical synthesis and expanding the scope of sustainable natural product production. And the integrated metabolic engineering strategy (spanning enzyme characterization, pathway construction, metabolic flux optimization and process refinement) validated herein offers a reusable template for engineering microbial cell factories for complex high-value natural products. Background

Phenazine-1-carboxylic acid (PCA) is an important nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compound widely found in the metabolites of many microorganisms, such as Streptomyces and Pseudomonas [1–3]. PCA exhibits significant antimicrobial activity against various pathogenic fungi, including rice sheath blight, watermelon wilt, pepper blight, wheat take all disease, watermelon anthracnose, and rapeseed rot, yet remains safe for humans, animals, and the environment. Its practical utility is proven by the commercial bio-fungicide Shenqinmycin, a registered PCA-based product in China [4–6]. Driven by the quest for new anti-tumor and anti-tuberculosis drugs and eco-friendly fungicides, researchers have turned to PCA—a typical carboxyl-substituted phenazine. Extensive chemical modifications of its core structure have yielded many highly bioactive PCA derivatives [7–10].

While chemical methods often rely on costly catalysts and harsh conditions, generating toxic byproducts, biosynthesis presents an eco-friendly alternative with its milder processes and reduced environmental impact [8, 11]. Pseudomonas has emerged as a prominent chassis cell for producing phenazine derivatives due to its rapid growth, genetic tractability, and scalable fermentation. In Pseudomonas, phenazine biosynthesis commonly utilizes PCA as a central precursor, and the underlying mechanisms of the PCA biosynthesis have been sufficiently understood [6, 12, 13]. However, the biosynthetic pathways for many high-value phenazine derivatives remain uncharacterized, which presents a major obstacle to their engineered production in Pseudomonas [14–16]. Phenazine-1-carboxymethyl (PCM) has been reported to exhibit enhanced fungicidal activity against the tobacco brown spot pathogen, Alternaria alternata, compared to PCA [17]. The biosynthetic pathways for methylated derivatives of PCA remain uncharacterized [14]. Introducing methyl groups can profoundly alter the biochemical and biophysical properties of these valuable molecules, yet achieving precise and chemo-selective methylation using chemical methods is challenging [7]. Conventional reagents like methanol are not only toxic but also environmentally harmful (Fig. 1A). As a sustainable alternative, S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)-dependent methyltransferases offer a solution, enabling highly specific methyl group transfer under mild conditions [18–20]. Therefore, we pursued established artificial biosynthetic pathways for methylated PCA derivatives.

The unconventional bacterium Pseudomonas chlororaphis H18 is an attractive platform strain for biotechnological engineering [4, 21]. It possesses several distinguishing features: high tolerance to environmental stresses and product toxicity, rapid growth, easy genetic manipulation. Owing to this combination of attributes, it has been used to produce diverse PCA-derived compounds, such as 1-hydroxyphenazine (1-OH-PHZ), 1-hydroxyphenazine N′ 10-oxide, and 1-methoxyphenazine (1-OCH3-PHZ). In this study, P. chlororaphis H18 was engineered as a platform for PCA derivative synthesis. We first determined the substrate scope of the SAM-dependent methyltransferase Pcm2 from Burkholderia lata ATCC 17,760, which led to the synthesis of a series of PCA derivatives [15]. Subsequently, the pcm2 gene was introduced into the P. chlororaphis H18 strain, enabling the production of PCM and 2-hydroxy-phenazine-1-carboxymethyl (2-OH-PCM) from the mutant’s fermentation broth. Finally, we optimized the production of these compounds through various strategies, including overexpressing key genes, knockout of negative regulators and the competitive pathway, increasing the copy number of pcm2 gene, enhancement of SAM supply and medium optimization. The resultant strain H18A6 produced 1.37 g/L PCM in fed batch fermentations. The strategies used in this study are listed in Fig. 1B.

Materials and methods

Materials

Restriction enzymes and DNA polymerase were purchased from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, USA) and Takara Biomedical Technology (Dalian, China), respectively. A one-step cloning kit was obtained from Vazyme Biotech (Nanjing, China). DNA extraction kit and plasmid preparation kit were purchased from Omega Bio-Tek. Chemicals were sourced from Macklin (Shanghai, China) or Bide Pharmatech Ltd (Shanghai, China). All reagents used were of analytical grade.

Strains, plasmids and cultivation conditions

The strains, plasmids used in this work were listed in Table S1. Escherichia coli DH5α, BL21(DE3) and S17-1 (λpir) were cultured at 37 ℃ in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium. P. chlororaphis H18 and its mutants were grown in King’s B (KB) medium (20 g/L tryptone, 10 mL/L glycerol, 1.5 g/L K2HPO4, and 1.5 g/L MgSO4•7H2O, pH 7.0). For fermentation, P. chlororaphis H18 or mutants were inoculated into 3 mL of the KB medium as a seed culture at 30 ℃ with 200 rpm of shaking for 24 h. Then the seed culture (1 mL inoculum) was inoculated into 50 mL KB or PPM medium (22 g/L tryptone, 20 g/L glucose, 5 g/L KNO3, pH 7.0) in 250 mL flasks at 30 ℃ with 200 rpm for 48 h. When required, kanamycin (Kan, 100 µg/mL) or ampicillin (Amp, 100 µg/mL) was used in the media. The DNA sequencing and PCR primers were completed by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China).

In vitro enzymatic assays

The enzymatic activity of Pcm2 was measured using a whole-cell catalysis system. The reaction mixture (100 µL) contained PCA or PCA derivative (0.5 mM), SAM (50 µM), and E. coli BL21(DE3) cells expressing Pcm2 in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5) at 30 °C for 1 h. At least three independent biological replicates were performed for each assay. The reaction was quenched by an equal volume of CH3OH. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 5 min, the supernatants were filtered and the substrate conversion was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) at 250 nm and high-resolution mass spectrometer (HRMS). HPLC analysis was performed on an Agilent 5TC-C18(2) column (250 × 4.6 mm) with a mobile phase consisting of acetonitrile and water (containing 0.1% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The gradient program was as follows: 30–50% acetonitrile from 0 to 5 min, 50% from 5 to 10 min, 50–70% from 10 to 15 min, 70–80% from 15 to 18 min, 80% from 18 to 21 min, 80 − 30% from 21 to 23 min, and 30% from 23 to 29 min.

To test the synthesis of PCA ethyl ester derivatives, reactions were catalyzed by enzymes V140TAtHMT and Pcm2. The reaction mixtures contained 0.5 mM phenazine derivatives, 6 mM ethyl iodide (EtI), 50 µM SAH and purified enzymes (0.1 mg/mL V140TAtHMT and Pcm2, respectively) in a final volume of 100 µL. Activity assays, initiated by the addition of enzymes, were performed at 30 ℃ and 220 rpm overnight. All assays were performed in at least three independent biological replicates. 100 µL of methanol was added to stop the reaction. The supernatants were filtered, and determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and HRMS. The genes were listed in Table S2.

Gene knockout and overexpression

Gene knockout and overexpression in P. chlororaphis H18 were performed via homologous recombination using the plasmid pK18mobsacB as described previously [4]. For gene knockout, approximately 500 bp upstream and downstream homologous arms of the target gene were independently amplified by PCR and subsequently cloned into the plasmid pK18mobsacB using Gibson Assembly. For gene overexpression, the expression cassette, containing the overexpressed gene along with its promoter, was fused with the downstream and upstream fragments of the chromosomal insertion site. These fragments were then assembled into the plasmid pK18mobsacB. The resulting recombinant plasmids were first verified by sequencing and introduced into E. coli S17-1 (λpir). And then these plasmids were transformed into P. chlororaphis H18 through conjugative transfer. Eventually, positive clones, in which the target gene was either knocked out or replaced via double crossover homologous recombination, were selected and confirmed by PCR amplification and DNA sequencing.

Fed-batch fermentation

Batch fermentation was conducted in a 5 L fermenter. The primary seed culture was prepared overnight in test tubes using KB medium. The 1% primary seed culture was then transferred to 200 mL of KB medium in a 500 mL shake flask, and cultured at 30 ℃ at 200 rpm for 12 h. The secondary seed culture was transferred to a 5 L fermenter containing 2 L of PPM medium. The dissolved oxygen level was maintained at 30% by automatically adjusting the agitation speed, varying from 400 to 1000 rpm. The fermentation temperature was kept at 30 ℃. The pH was controlled by adding NH3•H2O and was monitored with the pH electrode. And glucose (400 g/L) was fed to maintain a residual glucose concentration of 1 g/L.

Results

Characterizing the substrate promiscuity of methyltransferase Pcm2 in vitro

Phenazines are a large class of nitrogen-containing heterocycles that can be either naturally derived or chemically synthesized. The potential pesticide PCM is a chemically synthesized methylated phenazine derivative (as shown in Fig. 1A), and its biosynthetic pathway has not been previously described [8, 11]. A comparative analysis of the chemical structures of PCA and PCM suggests that methylation of the carboxylic acid group in PCA is a key structural modification. Based on this insight, we directly designed a biosynthetic pathway for PCM. The SAM-dependent methyltransferase Pcm2, from Burkholderia lata ATCC 17,760, has been reported to catalyze the methylation of carboxyl groups on the phenazine ring (phenazine-1,6-dicarboxylic acid to phenazine-1,6-dicarboxylic acid methyl ester or 6-carboxyphenazine-1-carboxamine to 6-carboxymethyl phenazine-1-carboxamine) [14, 15]. We therefore proposed that Pcm2 is also capable of converting PCA into PCM, positioning it as a suitable biocatalyst for pathway engineering. To facilitate the detection of Pcm2 activity, we expressed Pcm2 protein in BL21(DE3) and verified its activity by whole-cell catalysis. When we incubated Pcm2 with PCA in the presence of SAM (Fig. 2A), HPLC analysis showed an absorption peak for a new compound at 14.1 min, which matched the retention time of the PCM standard. And in positive ion mode, the compound exhibited an m/z of 239.0815 ([M + H]+), suggesting a molecular formula of C14H11N2O2 (calculated value: 239.0825, error: 4.18 ppm), which is consistent with the formula of PCM (Fig. 2B, C). These results indicate that Pcm2 is functional in BL21(DE3) and can catalyze the methyl esterification of PCA.

To further understand the catalytic properties of Pcm2, we evaluated its substrate specificity for various aromatic carboxylic acid compounds using whole-cell catalysis (such as quinoxaline-5-carboxylic acid, 8-quinolinecarboxylic acid, isoquinoline-1-carboxylic acid, quinoxaline-6-carboxylic acid, 2-quinoxalinecarboxylic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid, protocatechuic acid). As shown in Table S3 and Figures S1-8, Pcm2 displayed broad substrate specificity. In addition, many methyltransferases exhibit broad substrate tolerance for SAM analogs, suggesting their potential to catalyze diverse alkylation reactions if provided with appropriate alkyl donors [22–24]. This catalytic promiscuity offers a valuable opportunity to expand the structural and functional diversity of bioactive molecules. However, S-adenosyl-L-ethionine (SAE) is not commercially accessible and has to be synthesized as needed. To overcome this limitation, we produced SAE in vitro using ethyl iodide (EtI) as the alkyl donor via the engineered halide methyltransferase V140TAtHMT from Arabidopsis thaliana [25]. We subsequently evaluated the capability of Pcm2 to utilize SAE and PCA for the synthesis of PCA ethyl ester (PCE) (Fig. 2D and Figure S9). Consistent with the promiscuity observed in related methyltransferase enzymes, Pcm2 also demonstrated activity toward the biosynthesis of PCE and other alkylated derivatives (Table S3 and Figures S1–9).

In vivo construction of biosynthetic pathway for PCA derivatives in P. chlororaphis H18

As previously reported, the strain P. chlororaphis H18 is a plant rhizosphere isolate that naturally produces PCA, 2-hydroxyphenazine (2-OH-PHZ) and 2-hydro-phenazine-1-carboxylic acid (2-OH-PCA) [4]. The basic biosynthetic pathway of phenazines has already been clearly resolved, and the proteins PhzABCDEFG convert chorismate to PCA [4]. The FAD-dependent monooxygenase PhzO converts PCA into 2-OH-PCA and 2-OH-PHZ, and 2-OH-PCA is an intermediate that undergoes non-enzymatic decarboxylation to form 2-OH-PHZ [26, 27]. For de novo PCM biosynthesis, we first integrated a synthetic pcm2 gene into the P. chlororaphis H18 genome by replacing pykF, generating strain H18A1 (Fig. 3). In addition to PCM, an unexpected product with an [M + H] + m/z of 255.0775, which is 16 more than PCM, suggesting a molecular formula of C14H11N2O3. It is consistent with the formula of 2-OH-PCM (Figure S10). 2-OH-PCA remained relatively stable under our experimental conditions, while 2-OH-PCM was successfully detected as a methylated product. The concentration of PCM and 2-OH-PCM in H18A1 fermentation broth is relatively low (37.3 mg/L PCM and 42.5 mg/L 2-OH-PCM), which limited their industrial application (Fig. 4A).

Increasing PCA derivatives production through metX and metH overexpression

While the biosynthesis of methylated natural products commonly utilizes established model organisms such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and E. coli, this strategy is frequently constrained by the intracellular availability of SAM [28–31]. Despite metabolic engineering efforts to enhance SAM pools, its consumption in multi-step pathways often emerges as the principal constraint on production yield, as demonstrated in the biosynthesis of vanillate and methyl anthranilate [32]. The concentrations of PCM and 2-OH-PCM in strain H18A1 fermentation broth were relatively low with significant amounts of the intermediate PCA accumulating in the culture medium (as shown in Figure S10A). We hypothesized that the reaction was limited by a shortage of SAM, the essential methyl donor in methyltransferase catalysis. To improve the fermentation titer without adding induction agents or antibiotics, we overexpressed the metX and metH genes (encoding homoserine O-acetyltransferase and methionine synthase, respectively) under the strong PrpsJ12 promoter in strain H18A1, generating strain H18A2. This strategy aimed to enhance the metabolic flux from L-homoserine to methionine. After 48 h fermentation, this intervention increased the PCM and 2-OH-PCM titers to 78.2 mg/L and 85.7 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 4A). These values represent an approximately twofold increase over the control. This result confirms that the strategy effectively redirected accumulated L-homoserine toward methionine biosynthesis, highlighting the importance of an adequate intracellular SAM supply for efficient PCM and 2-OH-PCM production.

Combinatorial gene overexpression and knockout for PCA derivatives production improvement

To enhance the production of PCM and 2-OH-PCM, we engineered strain H18A2 to increase the flux toward the phenazine substrate pool. Based on previous research, we targeted key enzymes that boost the shikimate pathway and chorismate supply: phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) synthetase (PpsA), transketolase (TktA), quinate/shikimate dehydrogenase (AroE), and 3-deoxy-D-arabino-heptulosonate-7-phosphate synthase (PhzC) [4, 33]. The genes lon, prsA, and rsmE are known as negative regulators in P. chlororaphis, while prnA encodes a key enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of the secondary metabolite pyrrolnitrin [4]. To this end, we replaced the native genes lon, prsA, rsmE, and prnA genes in H18A2 with the ppsA, tktA, aroE and phzC genes from the P. chlororaphis H18 genome, generating the new strains H18A2-1, H18A2-2, H18A2-3 and H18A2-4, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4B, these individual modifications led to increases in the production of PCM and 2-OH-PCM, ranging from 2% to 19%. Building on these results, we constructed strain H18A3, which combines the overexpression of all four genes. HPLC analysis revealed that this engineering strategy boosted titers, achieving 101.66 mg/L PCM and 115.41 mg/L 2-OH-PCM in KB medium, representing a 30% enhancement over the parental H18A2 strain (Fig. 4B). In addition, the gacA gene is a positive global regulator, controlling phenazine biosynthesis in P. chlororaphis 30–84 and promoting 1-OH-PHZ production in P. chlororaphis H18 [4, 34]. Thus, we hypothesized that it also positively regulates the synthesis of PCM and 2-OH-PCM in P. chlororaphis H18. To test this, we introduced an additional copy of the gacA gene into strain H18A3, replacing the gltR gene. The resulting mutant, H18A4, showed a 20% increase in PCM (124.6 mg/L) and 2-OH-PCM (130.8 mg/L) titer, confirming the positive regulatory role of the gacA gene (Fig. 4B).

Further improvement of PCM production by deleting the competitive pathway

The PCA derivative PCM plays an important role in the biocontrol of plant diseases, and exhibits stronger bacteriostatic and fungistatic activity than PCA against some pathogens, as previously reported [17]. In Pseudomonas, monooxygenase PhzO converts PCA into 2-OH-PCA, which is then spontaneously decarboxylated to form 2-OH-PHZ [27]. Therefore, to prevent the competitive consumption of PCA by PhzO and enhance the conversion of PCA to PCM, we knocked out the phzO gene from strain H18A4, generating strain H18A5 (Fig. 5A). This strategy successfully abolished 2-OH-PHZ and 2-OH-PCM production and significantly increased PCM titers to 270.1 mg/L (Fig. 5B and C). To assess whether the expression of the pcm2 gene was the rate-limiting factor in PCM production after deleting the phzO gene, a second copy of pcm2 was inserted into the prnB site of the strain H18A5 genome to generate the strain H18A6. The strain H18A6 fermentation result showed that PCM production was further enhanced to 309.6 mg/L, representing a 15% increase in comparison with the strain H18A5 (Fig. 5C). This result indicated that the catalytic efficiency of Pcm2 was a rate-limiting factor for PCM production under fermentation conditions. Therefore, we propose that the protein engineering and high-throughput screening strategies to obtain improved Pcm2 variants with higher catalytic activity could further enhance the conversion efficiency.

Medium optimization and fed batch fermentation to improve PCM production

To further improve the production of PCM, we tested KB and PPM medium. The HPLC analysis showed that PPM was better for PCM production, in which the PCM titer (381.7 mg/L) was 23% higher than that in KB medium (Fig. 5C). In addition, betaine has been reported to be a key compound that promotes SAM biosynthesis for the production of vanillic acid and is also inexpensive [32]. Therefore, betaine was added to the fermentation medium to promote SAM biosynthesis. When betaine was added at a concentration of 2.5 g/L, the PCM production reached 421.9 mg/L, 10% higher than that in the control group (Fig. 5C). The production of PCM was subsequently scaled up via fed-batch fermentation. The strain H18A6 was cultivated in a 5 L fermenter with 2 L of PPM liquid medium, and glucose was fed during the process to ensure an adequate carbon supply. Product synthesis continued to increase until 60 h, at which point the highest titer reached 1.37 g/L for PCM. Discussion

Pseudomonas spp. are recognized as effective biocontrol agents due to their ability to produce diverse bioactive phenazines and other metabolites that protect plants against phytopathogenic fungi. Among these, P. chlororaphis and P. fluorescens have been commercialized by companies from Europe (Cedomon BioAgri AB, Uppsala, Sweden) and the USA (AtEze, Turf Science Laboratories) [1, 2]. P. chlororaphis H18 offers several advantages for industrial applications, including robust growth, easy genetic manipulation, and a well-established safety profile [4, 21]. The PCA derivative PCM exhibits enhanced antimicrobial activity against plant pathogens and holds promise for agricultural and pharmaceutical applications. However, efficient biosynthetic platforms for methylated phenazine derivatives have remained largely unavailable. In this study, we engineered P. chlororaphis H18 to achieve de novo production of PCM and 2-OH-PCM, two methylated PCA derivatives that are not naturally produced by this strain. Our results demonstrate that systematic metabolic engineering—enhancing precursor supply, strengthening cofactors, and eliminating competitive pathways—can overcome the limitations of natural phenazine biosynthesis and enable high-titer production of non-natural derivatives.

Pcm2 from Burkholderia lata has been reported to methylate phenazine-1,6-dicarboxylic acid or 6-carboxyphenazine-1-carboxamine [14, 15]. We found that it also accepts PCA as a substrate, converting it to PCM, and exhibits broad specificity toward various aromatic carboxylic acids. This suggests that Pcm2 recognizes the carboxyl group rather than the specific scaffold, making it a versatile catalyst for methylating diverse carboxyl-containing precursors. Notably, we demonstrated that Pcm2 can also utilize SAE to synthesize PCA ethyl ester, suggesting that this system may be extendable to other alkylated derivatives. The discovery of such a versatile catalyst is particularly valuable in the context of the broader field, which has increasingly shifted from understanding natural pathways to engineering them for non-native product formation.

Over the past two decades, research on phenazine biosynthesis has evolved from foundational pathway elucidation to biosynthetic pathway engineering across diverse bacterial genera [15, 33, 35–37]. A landmark study by McDonald et al.. elucidated the core PCA pathway in P. fluorescens, identifying ADIC as the key branch-point intermediate and delineating its conversion to DHHA and subsequently to PCA [37]. The same PCA biosynthetic pathway operates in P. chlororaphis. In contrast to previous efforts that focused on identifying core pathways and optimizing native phenazine production in Pseudomonas strains, our study equips P. chlororaphis H18 with a heterologous enzyme, Pcm2, to create non-native derivatives. Two major bottlenecks limited PCM production in our strain. The first bottleneck was an insufficient supply of the methyl donor, SAM. In our initial H18A1 strain, PCA accumulated while PCM titers remained low, suggesting that SAM availability rather than Pcm2 activity was the primary limitation. Overexpression of metX and metH enhanced SAM biosynthesis and led to an approximately twofold increase in titers, confirming this hypothesis. This result aligns with recent studies demonstrating that SAM availability is a key constraint in methylated natural product biosynthesis [28–31]. Having addressed the methyl donor supply, the second bottleneck was the availability of the PCA precursor. By replacing native negative regulators (lon, prsA, rsmE, prnA) with genes driving upstream flux (ppsA, tktA, aroE, phzC), we increased flux into the DAHP/PCA precursor pool. Single-gene replacements yielded modest improvements (2–19%), but combining all four resulted in a 30% increase over the parental strain, indicating that multiple flux control points act synergistically. And deleting phzO to block hydroxylated byproduct formation increased PCM titers to 270.1 mg/L, confirming that competitive pathways must be eliminated to maximize flux to the desired product. Nevertheless, introducing a second pcm2 copy increased titers by an additional 15%, revealing that Pcm2 catalytic efficiency remained rate-limiting even after pathway optimization. This result suggests that enzyme engineering, rather than further transcriptional upregulation, is the next frontier for improvement.

Finally, through medium optimization and fed-batch fermentation, we raised PCM titers to 1.37 g/L, representing a significant advance in PCM biosynthesis. The absence of inducers or antibiotics during fermentation, combined with the well-established safety profile of P. chlororaphis, makes this platform attractive for industrial use. Notably, Pcm2 also utilized SAE to synthesize PCA ethyl ester [25], suggesting that protein engineering of this enzyme could further expand the product scope toward other alkylated derivatives. Future work should therefore focus on directed evolution or rational design of Pcm2 to unlock a wider range of value-added phenazine derivatives.

Conclusion

Chemical synthesis of PCA derivatives is limited by sustainability and selectivity issues, whereas microbial biosynthesis lacks efficient platforms for diverse compound production. In this study, we first characterized the substrate promiscuity of the SAM-dependent methyltransferase Pcm2 from Burkholderia lata ATCC 17,760, which catalyzes the methylation of various aromatic carboxylic acids and can also utilize SAM analogues to produce ethylated derivatives. And then we established novel de novo biosynthetic pathways of PCM and 2-OH-PCM in P. chlororaphis H18. To improve their production, we employed several strategies, including enhancing intracellular SAM supply, overexpressing key enzymes and knocking out negative regulators. To further increase PCM yield and reduce the diversion of PCA to competing pathways, we disrupted the phzO gene to block conversion to hydroxylated derivatives, increased pcm2 copy number and combined with medium optimization. The final engineered strain, H18A6, achieved a PCM titer of 1.37 g/L in fed-batch fermentation. This study provides both a novel biosynthetic platform for PCA derivatives and a validated metabolic engineering framework for constructing high-performance microbial cell factories dedicated to natural product synthesis.
