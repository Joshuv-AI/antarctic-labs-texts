Brain-inspired cognitive state modeling of complex English discourse reading: an LLM-parameterized hierarchical semi-Markov approach
Authors: Bing Xie, Yongjin Hu, Linlin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionComplex English discourse reading requires readers to integrate lexical access, syntactic parsing, local meaning construction, cross-sentence inference, and discourse monitoring over time.MethodsWe developed a brain-inspired, large-language-model-parameterized hierarchical hidden semi-Markov model that encodes lexical-, sentence-, and discourse-level representations and maps contextual embeddings to state-emission, transition, and duration parameters. Discourse-structure and cognitive-load constraints were incorporated into global decoding.ResultsExperiments on 1,200 English reading passages achieved a macro-F1 of 0.860, a boundary accuracy of 0.83, a duration MAE of 0.52, and a discourse-consistency correlation of 0.76.DiscussionThe results suggest that duration-aware latent-state modeling can provide an interpretable computational account of adaptive discourse comprehension while preserving explicit temporal structure.

Introduction

Complex English discourse comprehension is not reducible to word recognition or sentence-level interpretation. During continuous reading, readers build and revise layered mental representations, including surface encoding, propositional meaning, and situation models. Local cohesion, global coherence, and knowledge-based inference jointly shape this process (Graesser et al., 1994; Kintsch, 1988; van Dijk and Kintsch, 1983; Zwaan and Radvansky, 1998). For English reading research, a computational model that follows these evolving representations is valuable because it links cognitive theory with process diagnosis, reading assessment, and the analysis of discourse-level difficulty.

Several issues remain unresolved. First, comprehension unfolds as a temporal process. Event shifts in characters, time, space, causality, and goals can reorganize the reader's situation model, so the relevant cognitive state depends on earlier and later discourse context rather than on the current word alone. Second, many experimental studies still rely on short or isolated texts, which limits their ability to describe state persistence in coherent discourse. Third, eye movements and reading times provide online evidence of processing, but conventional analyses often emphasize local predictors and are less suited to estimating state boundaries and duration intervals (Demberg and Keller, 2008; Just and Carpenter, 1980; Levy, 2008; Rayner, 1998; Smith and Levy, 2013). Finally, lexical access, syntactic parsing, discourse representation, and eye-movement control have often been modeled separately, even though readers coordinate these processes during actual comprehension (Engbert et al., 2005; Kuperberg and Jaeger, 2016; Reichle et al., 1998).

Prior work offers useful but incomplete foundations. Cognitive theories of discourse comprehension clarify how coherence, inference, and prior knowledge guide meaning construction, yet they do not by themselves yield a unified latent-state decoding model. Probabilistic sequence models, including hidden Markov and hidden semi-Markov models, provide a principled way to infer unobserved states and state durations (Bishop, 2006; Rabiner, 1989; Yu, 2010), but their parameters are often fixed or only weakly tied to discourse semantics. Transformer-based language models provide strong contextual representations (Brown et al., 2020; Devlin et al., 2019; He et al., 2021; Vaswani et al., 2017), and recent model-brain studies indicate that large language models can serve as computational hypotheses about human language processing (Caucheteux and King, 2022; Goldstein et al., 2022; Pereira et al., 2018; Schrimpf et al., 2021; Wehbe et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2024). At the same time, brain-inspired accounts of efficient coding and predictive processing emphasize hierarchical, temporally constrained inference (Barlow, 1961; Clark, 2013; Friston, 2010; Rao and Ballard, 1999), which is closely aligned with the Research Topic's concern with adaptive large models.

This study therefore develops a large-language-model-parameterized hierarchical hidden semi-Markov model for complex English discourse reading. The model organizes discourse at lexical, sentence, and discourse levels; defines lexical activation, syntactic integration, semantic connection, cross-sentence inference, and discourse monitoring as duration-constrained latent cognitive states; and uses contextual embeddings from a large language model to generate state-emission, transition, and duration parameters. Discourse-structure consistency and cognitive-load constraints are then introduced during global decoding. The contribution is threefold. First, the model integrates local and discourse-level comprehension processes into a shared latent state space. Second, it connects large-model contextual representation with explicit cognitive-state duration, making the model more sensitive to the temporal persistence of comprehension. Third, it offers a brain-inspired framework in which large language models serve as parameter generators for interpretable cognitive trajectories. More broadly, the convergence of artificial intelligence, cognitive science, and neuroscience has motivated brain-inspired computational frameworks for formulating and testing hypotheses about human cognition (Hassabis et al., 2017; Lake et al., 2017; Kriegeskorte and Douglas, 2018; Richards et al., 2019).

Cognitive modeling of complex discourse reading

Overall system framework

The proposed framework treats complex discourse reading as a computational chain that connects hierarchical text input, contextual semantic encoding, state-parameter generation, and constrained decoding. The input layer organizes each passage into lexical, sentence, and paragraph units. The representation layer extracts contextual meaning and inter-sentence relations at each reading position. The inference layer then decodes the cognitive-state trajectory under discourse-structure and cognitive-load constraints. Formally, the multi-granular input of a passage is defined in Equation 1:

This representation provides a unified input basis for subsequent modeling, ensuring that each reading position is within a clear lexical, sentence, and text structure.

The central component of the system is the contextual encoder. Rather than assigning fixed probabilities to cognitive states, the model uses a large language model to encode local lexical cues, inter-sentence cohesion, and discourse organization around the current reading position. These representations are mapped to the conditional parameters required by the semi-Markov module. State emission, transition, and duration distributions are therefore adjusted by the current discourse environment, which helps keep cognitive-state inference aligned with the text being read.

In this architecture, hierarchical text input provides the observable reading sequence, large-model encoding supplies context-sensitive semantic evidence, the hierarchical hidden semi-Markov module represents state persistence and switching, and structural constraints produce a globally coherent cognitive trajectory. LLM-parameterized hierarchical hidden semi-Markov modeling

A large language model is introduced as a parameter generator within the hierarchical hidden semi-Markov framework. In this design, latent cognitive states are not governed by a static probability table. Instead, the contextual representation at each reading position determines the emission strength, transition bias, and expected state duration. This choice reflects the assumption that reading cognition is adaptive: the same lexical or syntactic cue may trigger different processing demands when it appears in different discourse contexts. Given the whole reading sequence, the contextual representation of position t is obtained as shown in Equation 4:

In formula (4), ht represents the contextual semantic representation of the t-th reading position, LLM(·) represents the large language model encoding function, x1, x2, …, xt, …, xT represents the hierarchical input sequence of the entire text on the reading time axis, and T represents the total number of reading positions. The left side of formula (4) gives the semantic encoding result of position t, and the right side explains that the result is jointly determined by the entire sequence context. The result obtained thus ht simultaneously preserves local lexical clues, inter-sentence dependencies, and textual organization information, providing a unified condition for subsequent parameter generation.

This indicates that the explanatory strength of the state for the observation is no longer a fixed setting, but is adjusted according to the semantic environment of the current position. In the dataset, the observed response at position t is a five-dimensional vector whose k-th entry is the proportion of annotation decisions at that position assigned to state k, so each entry lies between zero and one and the five entries sum to one. The emission mapping matrix of state k maps the 256-dimensional state representation to five output logits, the bias vector has five entries, and the normalization function in Equation 5 is applied across the five state categories. Thus, the cognitive state switching process is written as a dynamic evolutionary process constrained by the discourse environment.

Because cognitive processing in reading often persists over several units rather than changing at every position, state duration is explicitly modeled in addition to transition probability, as shown in Equation 7:

In formula (7), p(dt = l | zt = k) represents the conditional probability that position t is in state k with a duration of length l, dt represents the state duration span, vk represents the duration weight vector of state k, γk,l represents the bias term of state k at length l, and Lmax represents the maximum duration. This modeling allows the model to not only identify the state to which the current position belongs, but also to characterize the state maintenance interval. The five state categories and their annotation guidelines are defined a priori from discourse-comprehension theory, whereas all emission weights, transition weight vectors, duration weight vectors, and bias terms are estimated from the annotated training set by optimizing the joint objective. No transition path, duration length, or state ordering is hard-coded into the parameterization. The transition matrix is initialized uniformly, and the duration parameters start from a shared flat prior, so any directional structure in the decoded trajectories reflects parameter values learned from the data rather than fixed architectural assumptions. To verify this property, we ran a sensitivity analysis in which the duration term, the discourse-structure consistency constraint, and the ordering-based initialization were removed or randomized one at a time, and the decoded trajectories were re-estimated on the test set.

All three types of state parameters are driven by ht through a unified mechanism. Therefore, the implicit cognitive states in the model retain the ability of hierarchical hidden semi-Markov structures to express temporal processes, while also absorbing the modeling advantages of large language models for complex discourse semantic dependencies. Experimental design

Corpus sources and task definition

The experimental corpus was constructed to support cognitive-process modeling of English reading. It includes TOEFL iBT academic reading passages, IELTS academic reading passages, Cambridge CAE/CPE reading passages, and continuous English expository and narrative passages. After screening, 420, 360, 240, and 180 texts from these four sources were retained, yielding 1,200 valid passages. From 1,386 candidate texts, 186 were removed because of incomplete structure, residual question stems, duplicate content, or paragraph-boundary problems. All retained texts preserve complete discourse structures, and lexical, sentence, and paragraph boundaries were checked to ensure stable hierarchical alignment.

Discourse complexity was defined along two dimensions: linguistic complexity and textual complexity. Linguistic complexity was measured through sentence length, syntactic embedding, lexical difficulty, and dependency density. Textual complexity was measured through referential-chain span, inter-sentence cohesion, logical-relation density, and thematic-progression distance. This operationalization follows established work on discourse coherence, inference, readability, and cohesion analysis (Graesser et al., 2004; McNamara and Kintsch, 1996), while also reflecting the predictive and expectation-based nature of online language comprehension (Kuperberg and Jaeger, 2016; Levy, 2008). Each of the eight complexity measures is computed per passage and converted to a z-score with the mean and standard deviation of the full corpus. The linguistic-complexity score of a passage is the unweighted mean of its four linguistic z-scores, and the textual-complexity score is the unweighted mean of its four textual z-scores. Passages are assigned to the low, medium, and high groups by tertiles of the corresponding score computed within each source collection, which keeps the three groups comparable across sources, and the resulting group sizes are reported in Table 1.

The data were divided at the text level, so that no passage appeared in more than one split. The final training, validation, and test sets contained 840, 180, and 180 texts, respectively. The split achieved a text-level separation rate of 100.0%, a topic-stratification retention rate of 96.8%, and a post-split length-distribution divergence of 0.027. Although the corpus covers several standardized reading sources and complexity levels, it remains centered on controlled academic reading materials. Its generalizability to classroom reading, open online discourse, and multimodal reading environments therefore requires further validation.

Four tasks were defined on the basis of this corpus. Cognitive-state recognition identifies the dominant latent state at each reading position. State-boundary detection locates the start, end, and switching points of cognitive states. Duration fitting estimates how long each state persists. Discourse-comprehension consistency evaluates the match between the decoded cognitive trajectory and the passage's organizational structure. All four tasks operate on the manual annotations described in Section 3.2, and every label count reported in this paper refers to the manual annotations rather than to decoded model outputs. Cognitive state annotation and data preprocessing

A cognitive-state annotation scheme was developed for complex discourse reading. The reading position served as the basic unit, and each position received one dominant state label aligned across lexical, sentence, and discourse levels. The five state categories correspond to the model definition: lexical activation, syntactic integration, semantic connection, cross-sentence inference, and discourse monitoring. The annotation criteria focused on the processing function of the position within the comprehension sequence rather than on surface linguistic form alone.

A reading position is one lexical unit together with its aligned sentence and paragraph units. Lexical activation is assigned when the dominant demand at the position is word recognition or initial meaning access, operationalized through low lexical frequency or ambiguity with routine syntactic and discourse roles. Syntactic integration is assigned when the dominant demand is building or revising the phrase structure of the current sentence, operationalized through embedding depth, non-canonical order, or structural ambiguity at the position. Semantic connection is assigned when the dominant demand is integrating the position into the local propositional meaning of the current sentence. Cross-sentence inference is assigned when comprehension of the position requires information stated in earlier sentences or the recovery of an implicit relation not encoded within the current sentence. Discourse monitoring is assigned when the dominant function is checking global coherence, updating the situation model, or regulating the reading process at paragraph or passage boundaries. When more than one function plausibly applies to a position, annotators select the state whose processing demand sits highest in the hierarchy, with ties resolved toward the function supported by explicit textual cues. During arbitration the third annotator applies the same criteria to each disputed position, and a final label is accepted only when it matches the choice of the third annotator and at least one of the two original annotators. Cohen's kappa is computed on the five-category labels over all annotated positions, and the category-specific values are one-versus-rest agreements for each state.

The annotation rules emphasized hierarchical consistency, sequence continuity, and boundary plausibility. Hierarchical consistency required annotators to consider local lexical information, intra-sentence structure, and discourse context together. Sequence continuity required neighboring labels to follow the normal progression of reading unless a clear semantic or structural shift occurred. Boundary plausibility required state onsets and offsets to align with sentence structure and discourse transitions. Three researchers trained in English reading research and computational linguistics completed the annotation. Trial annotation covered 120 texts and 51,480 reading positions; disagreements were used to refine the guidelines. In the formal stage, two annotators worked independently, cross-checked each text, and submitted unresolved cases to a third annotator. Trial-stage Cohen's kappa was 0.842. In the formal review sample of 180 texts and 77,220 positions, the overall agreement rate was 94.7%, the position-level disagreement rate was 7.6%, and the arbitration rate was 6.4%. Kappa values for lexical activation, syntactic integration, semantic connection, cross-sentence inference, and discourse monitoring were 0.881, 0.857, 0.841, 0.819, and 0.806, respectively.

Data preprocessing proceeded through text cleaning, hierarchical segmentation, reading-position alignment, and context construction. Text cleaning removed 4,127 noisy symbols. Sentence boundaries were identified with spaCy 3.7.2 and the en_core_web_trf 3.7.2 model. Position alignment linked word-level units with sentence and paragraph units, and manual correction was applied to word-boundary and paragraph-boundary mismatches, with correction rates of 3.8% and 2.6%. Context windows included the two preceding sentences, the current sentence, and the two following sentences, with a maximum input length of 512 tokens and a window step of 64. The paragraph-alignment success rate was 99.3%. After preprocessing, each sample followed the structure reading position, hierarchical representation, and state label. The formal review sample of 180 texts and 77,220 positions is exactly the test split reported in Table 1, and every lexical unit in the test set received one cognitive-state label, which is why the reported category counts sum to 77,220. All agreement and arbitration statistics are computed on this test split. The train, validation, and test split was fixed before guideline refinement began, and annotation, arbitration, and label finalization on the test set proceeded independently of model development, hyperparameter tuning, and error analysis, with no test label modified after model evaluation started.

Baseline models and training settings

To identify the source of performance gain, the comparison models were arranged in a progressive sequence. The baseline classifier tests how far local representation alone can support position-wise state discrimination. The sequence-labeling model adds adjacent-label dependence. The hidden-state model without duration modeling tests latent-state inference after removing explicit duration constraints. The proposed model adds large-language-model parameterization and semi-Markov duration modeling. This design separates gains from representation quality, sequence dependence, latent-state inference, and duration modeling, which is consistent with standard probabilistic modeling practice (Bishop, 2006; Rabiner, 1989; Yu, 2010) and with recent large-model approaches to contextual encoding (Devlin et al., 2019; He et al., 2021; Vaswani et al., 2017).

In terms of training implementation, all models used the “reading position—hierarchical representation—state label” structure preprocessed in Section 3.2 as a unified input and shared the same data partitioning and evaluation protocol to control biases caused by non-model factors. The large language model's encoding part used DeBERTa-v3-base as the context representation, with an output dimension of 768. The hidden state space of the hierarchical hidden semi-Markov module was consistent with the five cognitive states defined in Section 2.2, with a state representation dimension of 256. Transition parameters and duration parameters were jointly learned within this space. During training, the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) was used, with an initial learning rate of 2 × 10−5, a weight decay coefficient of 0.01, a batch size of 16, and a maximum training epoch of 30. The first 10% of training steps were used for linear warm-up, followed by cosine annealing to update the learning rate. To ensure experimental reproducibility, all models were trained using a fixed random seed, the same data partitioning, the same input window length, and a consistent early stopping criterion. To suppress parameter oscillations, the gradient clipping threshold was set to 1.0, the dropout ratio was set to 0.1, and early stopping was triggered when validation macro-F1 did not improve for four consecutive evaluation periods. For semi-Markov duration modeling, the maximum duration L was set to 12 reading units to ensure that the state interval modeling covered the main cognitive duration span while avoiding excessively long periods from interfering with decoding stability.

All baseline models were trained under the same hardware environment, random seed, data split, batch construction method, and input-window length. The platform used an NVIDIA RTX 4090 24GB GPU, an Intel Core i9-13900K CPU, 64GB RAM, CUDA 12.1, PyTorch 2.1.2, and Transformers 4.38.2. The random seed was 42. Each model was trained 3 times independently. The proposed model required an average of 3.8 h per run; its standard deviations across runs were 0.004 for macro-F1, 0.006 for boundary accuracy, and 0.012 for duration MAE. These controls make the comparison depend primarily on model structure rather than incidental training conditions.

Evaluation metrics

The evaluation system was designed around the four tasks and therefore covers category discrimination, boundary localization, duration fitting, and discourse-level consistency. Two principles guided metric selection. First, each metric had to correspond directly to a task objective. Second, the metrics together had to reflect the dynamic nature of reading cognition, so that the evaluation would not be reduced to single-position classification accuracy.

For cognitive-state recognition, macro-F1 was used as the main metric. Precision and recall were calculated for each state and then averaged across states. This metric is appropriate because the five cognitive states are not equally frequent; macro-F1 prevents high-frequency categories from masking weaker performance on less frequent but cognitively important states.

For state-boundary detection, boundary accuracy measured whether the predicted start, end, and transition points matched the annotated boundaries within a unified tolerance range. Because state boundaries connect local recognition with the overall cognitive trajectory, higher boundary accuracy indicates a more stable segmentation of the reading process.

For duration modeling, mean absolute error (MAE) measured the difference between predicted and annotated state duration. A lower MAE indicates a closer fit to the persistence interval of the cognitive state. This metric is especially important for evaluating the semi-Markov component, since ordinary sequence labeling can identify state categories without explaining how long those states are maintained.

For discourse-comprehension consistency, Pearson's correlation coefficient measured the alignment between the decoded cognitive trajectory and discourse organization. The metric captures whether changes in state intensity and switching density correspond to the progression of the text. It therefore evaluates the explanatory value of the trajectory at the whole-sequence level.

Results

Cognitive state recognition

Using the same data split, input structure, and evaluation protocol, the proposed model was compared with the baseline classifier, sequence-labeling model, and hidden-state model without duration modeling. The next analysis examines the two states most closely associated with deep discourse comprehension: cross-sentence inference and discourse monitoring. The analysis considers changes in discourse complexity and context-span expansion under the same test set and evaluation criteria. Figure 6 shows that both high-level states become harder to identify as discourse complexity increases. They are consistent with the view that human high-level reading states depend on accumulated context and global coherence, but they do not establish that dependence, and the connection to model-brain studies remains indirect because the present study contains no neural data or model-brain alignment analysis.

Causal, cross-architecture, and pooling analyses

Causal parameter generation

To align the parameter generator with the incremental left-to-right nature of human reading, we built a causal variant in which the encoder applies a causal attention mask and the context window is restricted to the current sentence and the four preceding sentences. On the test set, the causal variant reaches a macro-F1 of 0.838 against 0.860 for the bidirectional model, a boundary accuracy of 0.802 against 0.830, and a duration MAE of 0.58 against 0.52. The degradation concentrates in the higher-level states: cross-sentence inference falls from 0.832 to 0.791 and discourse monitoring falls from 0.824 to 0.783, whereas lexical activation changes only from 0.901 to 0.894 and syntactic integration moves from 0.882 to 0.866. Mean boundary detection latency, measured as the delay in reading positions between the annotated boundary and the first decoded position of the new state, rises from 0.9 to 1.6. This pattern quantifies the cost of removing future context: high-level states rely more on upcoming disambiguating information, while low-level states remain stable under strictly incremental encoding, which matches the temporal profile of online reading.

Alternative LLM architectures

To test whether the parameter-generation mechanism is model-agnostic, we replaced the encoder with representatives of three further paradigms: the bidirectional BERT-base, native causal decoders from the Qwen 3 and Gemma 4 families at two sizes each, and the state-space Mamba 3 at two sizes.

Table 6 reports the results on the test set, with the context window fixed at two preceding and two following sentences for the bidirectional models and at four preceding sentences for the causal and state-space models. All architectures reach macro-F1 between 0.818 and 0.867, and the ranking tracks representational capacity rather than architectural family: the two four-billion-parameter decoders reach 0.867 and 0.864, DeBERTa-v3-base reaches 0.860, and Mamba 3 at 1.3 billion parameters reaches 0.845. Widening the context window of DeBERTa-v3 from one to two surrounding sentences raises macro-F1 from 0.847 to 0.860, while a further widening to four surrounding sentences yields 0.862, and the causal decoders show the same saturation pattern. These results show that the gains reported in this paper stem from the hybrid parameter-generation architecture rather than from a specific encoder, and that moderate context windows capture most of the usable discourse signal.

Subword-to-word pooling strategies

To assess how subword aggregation affects state modeling, we replaced mean-pooling with four alternatives: attention-weighted pooling with a learned query per state, layer-wise scalar mixing across the encoder layers, element-wise max-pooling, and first-and-last subword concatenation followed by a linear projection. Layer-wise scalar mixing performs best, raising macro-F1 from 0.860 to 0.871 and boundary accuracy from 0.830 to 0.845, and lowering duration MAE from 0.52 to 0.48. Attention-weighted pooling reaches a macro-F1 of 0.866 and first-and-last concatenation reaches 0.863, and both outperform mean-pooling on every metric, whereas max-pooling falls below mean-pooling on boundary accuracy at 0.818. The pattern indicates that naive averaging dilutes the morphological and syntactic signals carried by individual subwords and by lower encoder layers, and that preserving layer-specific and position-specific subword information sharpens state boundaries and duration estimates. The main-text results are retained with mean-pooling for comparability with the original protocol, and the scalar-mixing configuration is reported as the recommended setting.

In Figure 7, the lexical, sentential, and discourse support shares are the normalized layer contributions defined for Figure 4b, averaged within one hundred equal discourse-position bins across the test set. The hierarchical center of a state is the mean layer index of its dominant support, with the lexical, sentence, and discourse layers indexed as one, two, and three. Discourse stages are contiguous ranges of the normalized position axis delimited by changes in the modal dominant state, and the state band in Figure 7a reports the modal dominant state within each bin across all test passages rather than a single passage or an illustrative trajectory. Because the support curves, the state band, and the duration values all derive from the same annotations and decoded sequences, their mutual agreement reflects the internal coherence of the model outputs rather than independent cognitive validation. The category-specific F1 values in Figure 7b are included to show where recognition errors concentrate along the trajectory, and they describe recognition performance rather than an intrinsic property of the states. Across the test set, the discourse-comprehension consistency correlation was 0.76.

Discussion

The findings indicate that large-model contextual representations become more useful for reading-process modeling when they are constrained by a cognitive state architecture. The LLM encoder supplies rich semantic and discourse information, but the hierarchical semi-Markov layer gives this information temporal form. This combination is important for the Research Topic because it treats a large model not as a static text classifier but as a computational platform for modeling adaptive cognition.

The results also clarify why duration matters. Lexical activation is brief and locally triggered, whereas cross-sentence inference and discourse monitoring persist across longer spans. This pattern resembles the temporal organization emphasized in predictive-processing and efficient-coding accounts, where higher-level representations integrate information over broader contexts. The proposed model operationalizes this idea through explicit state-duration parameters rather than through post hoc interpretation.

At the same time, the model should not be overread as a direct neural model. It does not claim biological equivalence, nor does it include neuronal plasticity or embodied sensorimotor interaction. Its contribution is narrower: it provides a bridge between discourse-comprehension theory, probabilistic state modeling, and large-model contextual encoding. This positioning is consistent with the special issue's interest in using large models to generate and test hypotheses about adaptive cognition.

Several limitations remain. The corpus is based mainly on standardized English reading materials, and the state labels depend on expert annotation. The main experiments rely on a bidirectional encoder with a symmetric context window, and the causal analysis in Section 4.6 shows that strictly incremental encoding lowers the recognition of higher-level states, so online deployment of the framework requires the causal configuration. Direct behavioral and neurocognitive evidence is needed to validate the decoded trajectories against actual reading processes.

Conclusion

This study proposed a brain-inspired, LLM-parameterized hierarchical hidden semi-Markov model for modeling cognitive states in complex English discourse reading. The proposed model unifies state category, boundary, persistence, and cross-level coupling within a single framework. The experimental results confirm stable improvements across all four evaluation tasks. The results suggest that large language models can be used as adaptive parameter generators for cognitive-process modeling, provided that their representations are coupled with interpretable temporal structure. The study is still limited by its reliance on standardized reading materials and annotation-based state labels. Future work will incorporate eye-tracking and neural evidence, test online and reader-specific adaptation, and explore whether the model can serve as a cognitive-core module for embodied language-learning agents in more open environments.
