Recent advances in HPV biotechnology: understanding host-virus interactions and cancer progression – a review
Authors: Saba Yousaf, Anum Shehzadi, Muhammad Ahmad, Ahmad Asrar, Ishtiaq Ahmed, Hafiz MN Iqbal, Mohammed Hussen Bule
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer ranks as the fourth most common cancer among women globally, posing a significant mortality risk. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) is the primary instigator of cervical cancer development, often alongside coinfection with other viruses, precipitating various malignancies. This study aimed to explore recent biotechnological advances in understanding HPV infection dynamics, host interactions, and its role in oncogenesis. The gathered data shed light on HPV biology, host-virus interplay, viral coinfections, and cellular transformations leading to HPV-associated cancers. Recent years have seen the introduction of diverse vaccination strategies, including live attenuated, subunit, and DNA-based vaccines, complemented by innovative nanotechnology and plant-based products. Despite rich data addressing research inquiries, urgent calls echo for the implementation of contemporary screening and therapeutic modalities at clinical levels. Moreover, extensive public awareness campaigns are imperative to alleviate the burden of HPV-related diseases, emphasizing the necessity for proactive intervention strategies in combating this global health challenge.Keywords: carcinogenesis, coinfection, Epstein–Barr virus, herpes simplex virus, host-interactions, immune response

With an estimated 660 000 new cases in 2022, cervical cancer ranks fourth among cancers that affect women worldwide1,2. Approximately 94% of the 350 000 cervical cancer-related fatalities that year happened in countries with low or middle incomes. South-East Asia, Central America, and sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) have the greatest incidence and fatality rates of cervical cancer. The disparities in access to immunization, screening, and treatment facilities, risk factors like HIV prevalence, and socioeconomic factors including sex, gender prejudice, and poverty are all linked to regional variations in the incidence of cervical cancer3. Compared to the overall population, women who carry HIV have a six-fold increased risk of developing cervical cancer, and HIV is thought to be the cause of 5% of all instances of cervical cancer. Cervical cancer strikes younger women more frequently than other cancers, 20% of children whose mothers die from cancer do so as a result of cervical cancer4.

HPV is a DNA tumor virus belonging to the family Papillomaviridae. More than 200 human and animal papillomavirus genotypes have been characterized and sequenced. Among these subtypes, 40 are considered high-risk because they have the potential to cause diseases such as cervical cancer, genital warts, and other cancers of the anogenital region5. HPVs types can be categorized into high-risk (HR-HPV) and low-risk (LR-HPV). LR-HPV variants encompass types 6, 11, 4, 40, 42, 43, 44, 54, 61, 70, 72, 81, and 89 while HR-HPV variants consist of types 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 68, 73, and 826. HR-HPV strains, including HPV 16 and 18, among others, have been recognized as primary contributors to several cancer types. Notably, HPV 16 and 18 are prominently associated with the majority of HPV related cancer instances. LR HPV variants typically do not lead to cancer, yet certain strains may manifest as genital, anal, oral, or throat warts; in some instances, particularly when affecting the larynx, they can induce respiratory papillomatosis, potentially resulting in respiratory difficulties6. Clinical signs of HPV infection can range from benign lesions from low-risk oncogenic HPV to severe cancers from high-risk oncogenic HPV such as those of vulvovaginal, oropharyngeal, cervical, and the head and neck7,8.

About 5% of cases of human cancer primarily result from oncogenic HR-HPV types9. Recent data indicates that infections and cancers associated with HPV increase healthcare expenses and endanger human life. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), an average of 47 200 new cases of cancer are discovered each year, and HPV infection is responsible for 80% of these occurrences10. Currently, our comprehension of the natural history, carcinogenic properties, screening, and preventive measures against HPV infection is relatively advanced. Nevertheless, HPV infection rates remain elevated, especially in underdeveloped nations where the occurrence and prevalence of cervical cancer persist at high levels. Various factors, including low socioeconomic status, insufficient community awareness, and inadequately implemented screening and immunization initiatives, contribute to this trend. Hence, ongoing discourse on HPV infection and associated diseases must persist, with experts and the general public worldwide refocusing their attention on this issue10.

This review delves into the host interaction strategy, the evolution of the HPV virus, the cellular alterations induced by viral invasion, and the onset of cancer. Additionally, it addresses the distinctive features of coinfection with bacteria and viruses like Epstein–Barr, HSV, and HIV, offering insights into cancer progression. Furthermore, it briefly touches upon various aspects of the HPV vaccine, therapeutic advancements, and potential alternatives for HPV-related cancer treatment. The objective is to inform the scientific community about the latest HPV traits and emerging therapeutic options gaining traction within the medical field.

Unveiling the biological dynamics of human papillomavirus (HPV)

Typically, HPV results in a temporary infection, which often resolves gradually in affected individuals. However, in a minority of cases, persistent and prolonged infection can lead to severe and enduring health complications. HR-HPV primarily causes malignant lesions, while LR-HPV tends to result in small, benign hyperproliferative lesions10. HPV can be classified into five types, including alpha, beta, gamma, mu, and nu, based on their genome sequences, phylogenetic and clinical features. HPV is categorized into five primary types determined by11 the nucleotide sequences of the genome, alongside their phylogenetic and clinical attributes. These sequences are denoted by the Greek letters alpha, beta, gamma, mu, and nu. The alpha papillomavirus genus serves as the primary causative agent for various cancers, including cervical, genital, extragenital, and oral cancers. Among the high-risk clades within the alpha genus, α-9 stands out as particularly significant, with HPV-16 emerging as a major contributor to severe viral infections. In addition to sexual behavior, factors such as reproductive cycles, heredity, immune function, nutritional status, and habits like smoking and drinking may also influence the severity of viral pathogenesis and the development of malignancies12,13.

Mechanisms of immune evasion by HPV

HPV uses several techniques to sidestep the host’s immune system and go lengthy periods without being noticed. Virus antigens are mainly located far from immune surveillance on the surface of epithelial cells. Furthermore, the virus does not result in viremia, necrosis, or cell death—all of which would normally set off an inflammatory response. HPV-OPCs are linked to the lymphoepithelial organs of the oropharyngeal area, which are essential for immune response. These tissues have systems in place to control overreactions from the immune system and preserve the proper ratio of tolerance to immunity14. The reticulated epithelium of the deep crypts, which is the primary location of HPV-OPC formation, is well expressed of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), an essential protein for immune evasion. Innate immunity and acquired immunity work together to support the body’s defenses against HPV infection15.

Using pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs) such as toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs), and RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs) to recognize and react to the virus, the innate immune system is the first line of defense against HPV. While HPV18 E7 suppresses the cGAS/STING pathway in HeLa cells, HPV16 E7 downregulates TLR9 in epithelial cells, reducing the interferon response. HPV16 E7, on the other hand, promotes autophagy-dependent STING degradation, indicating that HPV16 and HPV18 have distinct immune evasion strategies. Furthermore, the C5a/C5aR1 pathway in particular, which is part of the complement system, is linked to HPV-related carcinogenesis. Macrophage urokinase cleaves C5 to C5a, causing persistent inflammation and inhibiting the invasion of CD8+ T cells. The NLRP3 molecular complex activation and the creation of an inflammasome help HPV evade the immune system even further2.

The oncogenesis of Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is driven by the viral proteins E6 and E7, which disrupt normal cellular processes and lead to the development of cancer. HPV, particularly high-risk types like HPV-16 and HPV-18, infects epithelial cells in areas such as the cervix and oropharynx. The virus can either remain as a separate entity in the nucleus or integrate into the host’s genome. When integration occurs, it often results in the overexpression of the E6 and E7 proteins due to the disruption of viral regulatory genes16,17.

The E6 protein contributes to cancer development by targeting the tumor suppressor protein p53 for degradation through its interaction with a cellular protein known as E6-associated protein (E6-AP). p53 plays a critical role in controlling the cell cycle and initiating apoptosis in cells with damaged DNA. By degrading p53, E6 allows cells with potential genetic errors to survive and continue dividing. Additionally, E6 can activate telomerase, an enzyme that helps maintain telomere length, thus enabling cells to bypass the normal limits on cell division and avoid aging18,19.

The E7 protein contributes to oncogenesis by inactivating the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), another important tumor suppressor. Normally, pRb regulates the cell cycle by binding to and inhibiting E2F transcription factors, preventing the premature progression of the cell cycle. E7 binds to pRb, leading to its degradation, which in turn frees E2F to drive the cell cycle forward unchecked, leading to uncontrolled cell proliferation. The loss of both pRb and p53 functions results in significant genetic instability, allowing for the accumulation of mutations that can lead to cancer20.

HPV also employs strategies to avoid detection by the immune system, further contributing to its oncogenic potential. For example, the E5 protein can reduce the presentation of viral antigens on the cell surface by downregulating MHC class I molecules, making it harder for the immune system to recognize and destroy infected cells. This ability to evade immune surveillance allows the infected cells to persist and increases the likelihood of malignant transformation. Through these combined actions, HPV disrupts normal cellular regulation, inhibits programmed cell death, and fosters an environment conducive to cancer development21.

Deciphering HPV-host interplay

Exploring this dynamic relationship holds the key to unlocking new insights into HIV pathogenesis and treatment and informs the development of targeted therapies and preventive measures against HIV infection22. Such inquiries are crucial for grasping the biological intricacies of viruses and gaining insight into their genomic alterations that drive infection progression and resistance to host immune responses. These findings are pivotal for advancing targeted therapeutics and vaccines aimed at combating viral clinical manifestations23. The interaction between HPV and the host begins with the virus entering undifferentiated cells within the squamous epithelium of the ectocervix, as shown in Figure 1 below. This process is often triggered by significant mechanical stressors such as heat, radiation, or chemical exposure that the body undergoes24,25.

Viral DNA is incorporated into the host genome to cause cervical and other HPV-associated malignancies, upset cellular homeostasis, and encourage oncogene activation.

Cells that experience shock and allow viral entry to retain their capacity to multiply and differentiate. Viral particles exploit this characteristic to replicate their genome within the host cells. The viral cycle comprises three phases: productive, maintenance, and differentiation-dependent phases26. Critical infections typically last for 12–18 months and often resolve spontaneously with adequate host immune responses. However, in cases of weak immunity, insufficient viral elimination leads to persistent infection and viral accumulation, culminating in malignancy27. To initiate its cycle, the virus penetrates the stem cells located within the basal epithelium. This process relies on a complex interplay between the capsid protein housed within the virus and the receptor proteins present on the surface of the host cell. Upon internalization, the host cell initiates intricate endocytic mechanisms in response to specific ligands28.

Upon entry, the virus utilizes the E1 and E2 genes to maintain a low number of genomic copies. Viral DNA replication relies on certain proteins derived from the host cell29. Furthermore, the expression of genes E1, E2, E5, E6, and E7 during viral replication assists in maintaining the viral genome and inducing further proliferation, leading to HPV infection within the cellular epithelium30–32. As a result, numerous infectious virions are generated, initiating circulation within the epithelium. The expression of the E4 gene also promotes the replication process. Subsequently, late genetic expression triggers the assembly of viral capsid proteins L1 and L2, facilitating the assembly of capsid proteins for the release of new viral copies from the infected epithelial layer. This phase is referred to as the productive phase of the virus life cycle33.

During the maintenance phase, HPV virions infect cells within the epithelial layers and become exposed to extracellular spaces through microlesions. The binding of the virus to the basal layer triggers the activation of keratinocytes at the wound site. Here, the virus utilizes the host DNA machinery to replicate and produce a limited number of virion copies34. These virus particles are subsequently presented to the immune system, eliciting a protective response against low viremia before further viral replication and persistence. This phase facilitates the maintenance of viruses within the host body. During this stage, the expression of E6, E7, E1, and E2 genes contributes to episomal maintenance and DNA replication capabilities35.

During host cell replication, the virus genome undergoes replication, integrating itself into the rolling-circle mode of DNA replication to amplify thousands of viral copies within the host cell epithelium. Additionally, viral proteins disrupt normal cell cycle regulation in the host, thereby subverting regulatory pathways and altering the cellular signaling environment to promote further viral replication36. Unlike the maintenance phase, the virus transitions into the differentiation-dependent phase, characterized by an accelerated replication mode and excessive translation of viral proteins. The delayed expression of viral proteins in differentiated cells is a significant factor contributing to delayed antigenic expression in host immune cells. This delay plays a crucial role in impeding immune responses against viremia and perpetuating the long-term infectious strategy employed by HPV37–39.

E7 assumes a pivotal role in replication competencies within differentiated cells. During this phase, the virus undergoes extensive replication, generating thousands of copies within a single host cell. This replication strategy serves as the primary mechanism for the virus to evade the host’s humoral and cellular responses. Additionally, E1, E4, and E5 proteins contribute to late viral functions during the differentiation stages. E6 and E7 proteins are integral in orchestrating complex cellular protein formations that regulate cell cycle functions and confer genomic stability to viral particles. Furthermore, capsid proteins L1 and L2 are synthesized during this stage, aiding in the release of viral assemblies and facilitating virus shedding through the epithelial layers into the cellular environment with minimal rates of lysis or necrosis40.

The previously mentioned stages of replication enable the virus to perpetuate itself, evading inflammatory responses and persisting stealthily. This phenomenon significantly contributes to the prolonged presence of viruses in women, even beyond 6–12 months postinfection41. Notably, HPV 16 is associated with extended periods of infection. Typically, after 12–18 months, the immune system of the host initiates systemic viral clearance. In the cervical system, infection lasts for 12–18 days following exposure to high-risk HPV, often resolving spontaneously in most cases. However, when the immune system is compromised and incapable of combatting the consequences of viral load, persistent infection ensues. This is particularly prevalent in women with compromised immune systems, where the virus persists, leading to long-term infection that may ultimately progress to malignant lesions, such as cervical cancer42. Studying HPV-driven cellular changes is crucial for understanding the mechanisms of cervical cancer development and for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies against HPV-associated malignancies, impacting global public health outcomes.

HPV-driven cellular changes

Head and neck cancers, as well as squamous cell carcinomas, vaginal, and cervical cancers, are all brought on by HPV, as described in Figure 2 below. The scientific community is constantly investigating the various elements that lead to the development of cancers associated with HPV43. These considerations encompass several aspects: comprehending the latent stages of HPV, elucidating the mechanisms underlying the development of immunity against reinfection, and investigating weakened immune responses to HPV infections, particularly about reduced expression of inflammatory proteins. Additionally, researchers are delving deeper into the distinction between persistent and transient infections induced by HPV44. Most HPV infections resolve within a span of 2 years, but roughly 10% of individuals experience persistent HPV infection, which can progress to lesions and malignancies. The development of carcinogenic changes is linked to compromised host immune responses, as the virus induces genomic alterations in host cells through repeated cycles of replication45,46.

The development of head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC) and cervical cancer is aided by viral integration, which disrupts the host cell cycle and oncogene expression.

HPV-16 has emerged as the primary instigator of HPV-induced malignancies, including anogenital, oropharyngeal, and, notably, cervical cancers47. Carcinogenicity is facilitated by the activation of oncogenic viral proteins E6 and E7, which disrupt cellular regulatory networks48. These oncogenes instigate gradual genetic mutations in the host, which can persist unnoticed for extended periods49.

In both benign and malignant HPV lesions, HPV triggers an increased demand for cellular nutrients, leading to the overexpression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) and its targeted genes, ultimately inducing hypoxia. This gene dysregulation prompts angiogenesis, which in turn sustains HPV-induced malignancy by providing nutrients for HPV proliferation21,50. The identification of oncogenic proteins and genes within HPV has enabled researchers to engage in bioinformatics investigations and develop vaccination and therapeutic strategies against HPV infections51. These studies, which encompass screening, diagnostics, and treatment-related endeavors, offer promising avenues for comprehending and addressing HPV-related infections and cancers52. Recent research has unveiled vaccination options for cervical cancer prevention, which also demonstrate prophylactic efficacy against HPV-associated HNSCCs. Investigations are also ongoing to elucidate the etiology of beta-HPV and its association with nonmelanoma skin cancer. In addition to vaccination, the local excision of tumors, combined with immune-modulating antiviral and antitumoral medications, is often proposed as an alternative to surgical intervention53.

Furthermore, these vaccinations have the potential to inhibit the development of HNSCCs associated with HPV. Research is also underway to elucidate the origins of beta-HPV, which has been associated with nonmelanoma skin cancer54. Before considering surgical intervention, local tumor excision, often accompanied by immune-modulating antiviral and antitumoral medications, is commonly recommended in conjunction with vaccination. Most research indicates a strong correlation between cervical cancer and HPV infection55,56.

Continuous scientific research endeavors aim to discover new medicines for the treatment of cervical cancer, as it ranks among the most prevalent illnesses caused by HPV. Following closely are HNSCCs, the second-most common HPV-induced cancers originating from the host’s gastrointestinal tract57. Distinct prognostic responses, current therapies, and underlying carcinogenic mechanisms differentiate HPV-related HNSCCs from non-HPV-related cases58. The coinfection dynamics of HPV with other viral agents are further illustrated by the identification of concurrent squamous epithelial lesions and human papillomavirus in individuals with HIV. Recent studies have established a connection between primary vulvovaginal cancer and HPV infections through clinical observations of affected individuals.

Recent studies provide data on the associations and incidences of penile cancer and primary oropharyngeal malignancies following HPV infection in individuals. It is imperative to integrate a significant research domain for these carcinogenic investigations into a systematic meta-analysis59. Drawing from the current pool of study findings, we have endeavored to encompass most characteristics of HPV-induced malignancies and occurrences. Moreover, research has established links between HPV coinfection and other viruses within the host body. Each year, ~0.5 million new cases of cervical cancer are diagnosed globally. According to the WHO, HPV is one of the leading causes of these cancers60. Surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy are commonly effective treatments for early-stage cervical cancer, with a 5-year survival rate. However, the prognosis for individuals with late-stage cervical cancer is relatively poor. High-risk HPVs have been identified as a significant risk factor in most cases of cervical and HNSCC carcinomas, as well as other cancer predispositions50.

In particular, 70% of instances of oropharyngeal cancer have been documented to be HPV-positive. Beta-HPV-associated carcinoma advancement has also been seen in several skin cancers, but it is unclear what causes HPV to relate to cutaneous cancer. Like those with HIV, persons taking immunosuppressive medications have a higher risk of contracting opportunistic infections caused by HPV61. Additionally, HPV types 5 and 8 are responsible for almost 90% of squamous cell carcinomas. Moreover, the HPV-beta subtype has been linked to nonmelanoma skin cancer (NMSC), a common form of cancer in individuals worldwide24.

Numerous radiation exposures, oxidative stress, exposure to ultraviolet (UV) rays, and other epigenetic variables like obesity, anxiety, and chronic illnesses may be among these causes52. The survival rates of these people may be increased if they are not addicted to alcohol or tobacco. Despite being a vital disease tool, there are currently no screening methods for reliable early HPV identification27.

In comparison to HPV-negative tumors, most of these carcinomas with HPV positivity have better prognoses and are more responsive to chemotherapy. Early-stage laser treatment options, radiation, chemotherapy, and surgical removal are crucial components of the critical management of such cancers14. While ongoing research into less invasive treatments is ongoing, immune regulatory drugs like imiquimod, and photodynamic therapy have also demonstrated efficacy in treating HPV-induced carcinogenesis45. To learn more about HPV-dependent oncogenesis, thorough investigations on tonsillar, oropharyngeal, and cervical malignancies have been done. Confronting HIV coinfections

Studies show that HPV infection frequently persists for a long time and raises the possibility of coinfection with other viruses and bacteria, such as HSV, EBV, HIV, and others. The importance of HPV as a risk factor associated with human malignancy has been extensively explored. To properly understand the process, research is still being done on the connections and etiological underpinnings of HPV coinfection with other viral illnesses. Due to HPV persistence, patients whom HIV specifically impacts are at significant risk of developing anogenital dysplasia. HIV patients frequently have HPV and HIV coinfections63.

Furthermore, high-risk HPV entities frequently cause people to coinfect with HIV and HPV, which can result in cervix and anogenital cancer. Scientists are focusing on individuals who have HIV and HPV coinfections to demonstrate basic cellular mechanisms and create therapy possibilities64. The immune system susceptibility that HIV patients develop opens the door for an abundance of HPV and the development of carcinogenic processes in such patients, which is the leading cause of coinfection. Although there are now no approved vaccinations for the complete prevention of HIV, effective therapeutic vaccines for HPV are being researched and may benefit from concerns over coinfection mitigation. Infected individuals are also being checked for the development of malignant lesions through recently authorized screening and diagnostic technologies11,65,66. Examining HPV-EBV coinfection is crucial for understanding potential synergistic effects in oncogenesis and immune modulation, shedding light on mechanisms underlying the development of malignancies like cervical cancer and aiding in the development of targeted therapeutic interventions.

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is more common in people with HIV because it is caused by the same virus that is frequently linked to cervical cancer. It is possible to transfer HIV and HPV unintentionally and not realize you have either of them. It may take years to experience signs of HPV. HIV impairs immunity, making it more difficult for an HPV infection to gradually go away. Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), an aberrant precancerous cell formation in the cervix, is more likely to occur if this occurs. Cancer patients may experience complications from their therapy due to a compromised immune system67. Standard therapies like chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or immunotherapy may not work as effectively for these people. Treatments for cancer and antiretroviral therapy (ART) may interact, influencing the effectiveness and toxicity levels of the administered medications. HIV-positive individuals frequently require dose modifications or other medications because they have a reduced tolerance to the negative effects of cancer treatments. Coinfection can result in worse clinical outcomes because of increased recurrence rates and more aggressive cancer growth. Research has indicated that people with HIV-positive cancer may have a poorer chance of survival when compared to those with HIV-negative cancer of the same kind21.

Navigating HPV-EBV coinfection

Coinfection of HPV and EBV (Epstein–Barr Virus) in patients with concurrent oral cavity malignancies has been extensively studied. Nasopharyngeal, stomach, and lymph epithelial carcinomas have all been linked to EBV and comparable coinfections68. These studies showed that while many patients had signs of coinfection with at least two subtypes of the mentioned viruses, not all patients had dual viral infections. Research has also demonstrated that patients with cancer of the oral cavity who have a higher frequency of tumor cells that have differentiated rather than just single virally infected cells are at a higher risk of contracting viral infections, including EBV and HPV69. Scientists could conclude when they realized that infectious diseases with HPV and EBV were found within malignant and benign cells in a range of carcinomas. The hypothesis that individuals with additional cancers linked to HPV may also be coinfected was sparked by this finding. Researchers are studying the biological process underlying this coinfection to develop effective medications for possible future treatment options25,70.

HSV and HPV: coinfection insights

Based on several recent studies, patients with HR-HPV often coinfect with the herpes simplex virus (HSV). There are substantial incidence rates for these coinfections since HSV has an undiscovered seroprevalence in nearly 80% of the population of the world. The primary risk factors for this type of infection have been high-risk sexual behavior, prior HPV infections, and HSV infection. According to a study, people with type 2 diabetes (DM2) also had coinfections with HSV-1, HPV, and EBV71,72. Microbiota components, which generally regulate the human body functions, were found to have changed due to the spread of diseases. As previously indicated, even little modifications to this microbiota, consisting of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa, can result in coinfection with many different viral entities. The oral, respiratory, vaginal, and gastrointestinal tracts where bacteria support normal body functions—are the most often affected areas by these microbiota-associated viral illnesses46,73.

Synergy between HPV and bacterial infections

As was already mentioned, studies have shown that changes in a patient’s microbiome can result in both bacterial and HPV infections co-occurring. Another study found that Streptococcus anginosus bacteria and HPV coinfections can occasionally be an infectious risk factor for developing oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC)74. Similar findings were made by a study that examined 13 significant indicators for bacterial vaginosis in cervical samples that revealed coinfection with HPV. These studies have established a link between bacterial vaginosis and the potential danger of chronic lesions at squamous intraepithelial cells of the cervix. Due to this coinfection with a virus and bacterial vaginosis, patients have also developed high-grade cancerous lesions. Further studies and additional data collection are necessary in this case to determine the etiology of this coinfection, forecast the basic biological systems involved, and identify effective treatment approaches35.

HPV diagnostics

A quick, easy, and low-cost method of testing for HPV might reduce the death rate and treatment costs by identifying those who are at a higher risk of acquiring cancer and putting them under closer observation. The market’s availability of commercial HPV tests is restricted by their frequently high cost, time-consuming nature, and requirement for complex equipment9. Novel technologies, such as isothermal amplification techniques, lateral flow assays, CRISPR-Cas-based systems, microfluidics, or lab-on-a-chip devices—which are perfect for point-of-care testing in decentralized settings—are being implemented for HPV diagnostics to address these challenges10.

Isothermal nucleic acid amplification is a real-time fluorescent assay developed in the USA for detecting HPV directly from clinical samples without needing DNA extraction. It processes a dry brush sample or the assay transport media in under an hour. This method boasts several advantages, including speed, efficiency, portability, and strong specificity. It can detect nucleic acids or gene expression in living cells. By amplifying nucleic acids at a constant temperature, this technique can diagnose pathogens quickly, with signal reading times within a few hours75.

The Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats/CRISPR-associated protein technology, or CRISPR-Cas changed not just genome editing but also molecular biology in general. Remarkably, CRISPR-Cas technology has been applied recently to cure cervical cancer linked to HPV infection7. On a panel of 20 clinical samples, 92.3% sensitivity and 100% specificity were obtained in comparison to standard PCR. Although the CRISPR-Cas technology suggests that it provides strong single-nucleotide sequence resolution, very precise gRNA design would be needed to discriminate between distinct genotypes. Even though HPV is a small virus, up to 1000 distinct variations have been found within individual samples, and there is a significant amount of intra-host sequence diversity within highly conserved sections10,76,77.

Microfluidic technologies, sometimes known as ‘lab-on-a-chip’, provide a chance to develop tools or chips that might rival traditional methods in chemical and biological research. These inexpensive devices can handle microliters of solution in a high-throughput manner via capillary tubes, which eliminates the need for samples and reagents5. Crucially, every reaction and cleaning procedure is carried out in sealed reaction systems, which are perfect for avoiding cross-contamination. The topic of LOC technology is expanding quickly in both HPV analysis and DNA diagnosis. Microfluidic devices are seen to be the most promising options for environments with limited resources since they need little equipment and are inexpensive, simple to use, and disposable. However, there is still room for improvement. For example, there is comparatively poor measurement sensitivity due to substantial sample retention in paper fluidic channels and greater detection limits associated with traditional optical detection78.

Therapeutic vaccinations against HPV infection could produce immune responses on the humoral and cellular levels. Scientists have developed a few vaccine delivery systems, including DNA-based vaccines, and vaccines using protein and peptide-based subunits and conjugate vaccines and live attenuated-vector vaccines. The FDA has authorized several of these HPV prevention vaccinations. These vaccinations, which come in bivalent, quadrivalent, and nonvalent varieties, are marketed and include VLPs or virus-like particles40. L1 capsid proteins essentially self-assemble to form these VLP-based proteins. They primarily target the two HPV subtypes known to cause the majority of cancers, HPV-16 and HPV-18. L1-VLP, a monovalent vaccination, is one of these that has recently been released on the market. Postvaccination follow-ups have revealed that this vaccine significantly reduces the risk of illness starting79.

An extra HPV vaccination technique is a DNA-based vaccine. Proteases that encode for specific gene sequences with tumor suppressor properties are among the naturally occurring chemicals used to build DNA vaccines53. These treatments can be taken on their own or combined with other plant-based medications that include metabolites including triterpenoids, flavonoids, and tannins with anticancer properties80. The use of HPV and associated cancer therapies is expanded by DNA vaccination, which causes patients to develop immune responses specific to antigens81. Furthermore, DNA-based vaccinations are easier to make, less costly, safer, and more effective. They can also trigger more targeted immune responses. Due to their potential to disrupt cancer-causing cellular pathways, plant metabolites, and other immunomodulatory drugs are frequently used with DNA vaccines in experimental treatments47.

There are several methods for producing attenuated vaccines. Several popular techniques include transferring the pathogen via several cell cultures or animal embryos. Every technique for transferring a virus through an organism other than a human results in a virus that is still detectable by the immune system but is not able to grow rapidly in a human host. The resultant vaccine virus will not reproduce to the point where it may infect a human, but it will still elicit an immune response that can guard against infection in the future. Subunit and conjugate vaccines only include fragments of the diseases they are meant to protect against. Subunit vaccines stimulate the immune system by using only a portion of the target pathogen. This can be accomplished by separating a particular protein from a pathogen and delivering it separately as an antigen. Genetic engineering is used in the production of the HPV vaccination. There are now two different forms of HPV vaccines; one protects against two strains of the virus, while the other covers four. However, both are produced in the same manner, isolating a single viral protein for each strain. Virus-like particles, or VLPs, are produced when these proteins are expressed. These VLPs incite an immune response that offers long-term protection against HPV but do not carry any genetic information from the viruses and cannot cause disease.

Implementation of vaccines

According to the WHO, 133 countries have integrated HPV vaccines into their National Immunization Programs (NIPs). Of these, school-based delivery was the most widely used strategy, being implemented in 69 countries (64%) and 32 countries (30%) that were able to achieve vaccination coverage above 70% due to effective communication plans, while the coverage fell below 50% in nearly 24% of the countries that adopted the strategy. The facility-based delivery method was implemented by less than 20% of the nations that accepted it, and 36% of those countries were able to reach coverage levels above 70%. In contrast, 41% of those countries had unsuccessful implementations, resulting in vaccine coverage levels below 50%. The most effective technique is the mixed delivery approach, which is only used in 16% of countries but has a rate of coverage of >70% in 53% of them since immunization is easily accessible in these nations rastructure, are economically viable, environmentally sound, and capable of attaining the maximum level of vaccination coverage. The data clearly shows that school-based vaccination is the most often used method globally (~64%), with 32 out of 69 (~46%) nations using this technique to achieve immunization coverage >70%. It could be necessary to use a mix of strategies to guarantee admission for all females who qualify. It is imperative to guarantee the involvement of policy-formulating committees, regulatory authorities, political agencies, and financial agencies. HPV vaccinations should be made available in NIP methodically and openly as a component of a comprehensive and coordinated plan. Parents have a significant influence on the decisions made by young people; thus it is necessary to educate them about the advantages and significance of the HPV vaccine. Health professionals should also get coordinated screening and vaccination training2.

About already present HPV infections and related malignancies, it does not have a therapeutic impact. It is imperative to successfully address the constraints of these vaccines, including storage concerns, costly formulations, and distribution conditions, to develop a more effective vaccination program in the future83. Several HPV vaccines were accessible in the US that the FDA has authorized. These can consist of Cervarix, Gardasil 9, and Gardasil 9. In addition to offering considerable additional defense against HPV-52, 45, and 31 types, these vaccinations also provide immunity against viral subtypes 18, 16, and 11. Moreover, only high-income countries can receive these vaccinations; in contrast, middle-income countries and low-income countries, where the sickness burden is truly highest, have fewer options to obtain vaccines. To effectively regulate clinical care, we must increase vaccine accessibility in high-prevalence areas and broaden our knowledge of the diagnostic and therapeutic options available for OHPV-persistent malignancies84.

Resistance to treatment

Treatment resistance is a significant obstacle in the management of cancer and restricts the effectiveness of the therapy. Most fatalities from cervical cancer are attributable to treatment failure and subsequent disease recurrence. Therefore, creating biomarkers and tailored therapeutics would benefit from a knowledge of the processes and molecules linked to treatment resistance. The factors behind cervical cancer therapy resistance include apoptosis, DNA repair pathway, EMT, methylation, stemness, and TME’s. Radioresistant, chemoradiation, and chemoresistant cervical cancers have all been shown to have differential methylation of their genes. Growing research indicates that cervical cancer treatment resistance is influenced by the DNA repair system, particularly when DNA-damaging drugs are used. The epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) controls both treatment resistance and progression; nevertheless, the majority of research focusing on EMT have not yet provided clear guidance. To combat treatment resistance in cervical cancer, further research is necessary to justify therapeutically interfering with EMT75.

The clinical adoption of HPV biotechnology, particularly in the context of cancer prevention and treatment, faces several significant challenges. One of the primary barriers is the cost and accessibility of HPV vaccines, such as Gardasil and Cervarix, especially in low-income countries and middle-income countries (LMICs). These vaccines are often expensive, making it difficult for public health programs to provide widespread coverage. Research suggests that financial constraints and unequal distribution of healthcare resources contribute to low vaccination rates in these regions, exacerbating the global burden of HPV-related cancers. To address this, international health organizations, such as GAVI and the WHO, have initiated programs to subsidize vaccine costs and promote generic vaccine production to increase accessibility. However, overcoming these economic challenges remains critical for equitable access to HPV biotechnology32.

Vaccine hesitancy also presents a significant challenge to the adoption of HPV vaccines. Studies have identified concerns about vaccine safety, misconceptions about the vaccine’s impact on adolescent sexual behavior, and general distrust in vaccines as contributing factors to low uptake. Public health campaigns that provide accurate information about the safety and cancer-preventing benefits of HPV vaccination are essential to counter these myths. Additionally, engaging healthcare providers to educate parents and patients has been shown to significantly improve vaccination rates20. Another issue is the low rate of screening for HPV-related cancers, particularly in LMICs where access to medical infrastructure is limited. Cervical cancer screening, for instance, remains underutilized, leading to delayed diagnosis and higher mortality rates. Innovative solutions, such as mobile screening units and self-sampling HPV tests, have been proposed to improve accessibility and promote early detection. Ensuring that these technologies reach underserved populations is essential for reducing the incidence and impact of HPV-related cancers22.

The integration of emerging biotechnologies, such as CRISPR gene editing and immunotherapies, also faces challenges related to high costs and regulatory hurdles. While these advanced therapies hold great promise for treating HPV-related cancers, the lack of long-term efficacy data and the complexity of regulatory approval processes have slowed their clinical adoption. Large-scale clinical trials are needed to gather the necessary data on safety and efficacy, while policymakers must streamline approval processes to facilitate the use of these innovative therapies in clinical practice. Additionally, fostering public-private partnerships can help scale up production and make these treatments more widely accessible18.

Equity remains a central issue in the adoption of HPV biotechnology. There are stark disparities in vaccination and cancer treatment availability between high-income countries and LMICs. Research has shown that while high-income nations have established robust vaccination programs, LMICs struggle with limited healthcare infrastructure and resources. Addressing these disparities requires global collaboration through international aid programs, pharmaceutical partnerships, and nongovernmental organizations to ensure equitable access to vaccines and cancer treatments85.

Lastly, adherence to HPV vaccination schedules poses a challenge, particularly in regions with limited healthcare follow-up systems. Many patients fail to complete the full vaccination course, reducing the vaccine’s effectiveness. Mobile health (mHealth) solutions, such as reminder systems and community-based vaccination initiatives, have been suggested to improve adherence and ensure full vaccination coverage. By addressing these challenges with targeted strategies, the clinical adoption of HPV biotechnology can be significantly improved, ultimately reducing the global burden of HPV-related cancers32,68,86.

Numerous research that has identified prospective targets to improve therapy efficacy for cervical cancer are included in our evaluation. Expanding patient-based data and conducting efficient and economical clinical trials might help close the gap between lab and bedside research. In-depth follow-up research is also required to track treatment results and comprehend resistance mechanisms. Techniques such as combination therapy and predictive biomarker-based patient selection may be able to address the treatment resistance associated with conventional therapy. Research on the processes behind treatment resistance will provide new perspectives on successful cancer care techniques68.

Advancing HPV- immunization focus

Future immunization campaigns and therapies will continue to emphasize ontogenetic targeting vigorously. Researchers are using nanotechnology to develop future combination therapy strategies and vaccines due to multidrug resistance and secondary adverse effects of therapies that have been discovered. These nano-based medications can improve efficacy while reducing the number and size of dosages needed86. The HPV vaccine effectiveness is further increased by the nano-delivery vehicles, making nano-delivery and drug production methods the most effective treatment for lesions and cancers caused by HPV.

Along with preventing infections, these medications also provide a modicum of resistance against tumor growth by making cells more sensitive to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Comparable to HeLa cells, the cell lines for adenocarcinoma (A549), breast cancer (MCF7), melanoma (A375), and skin papilloma (A375) have also been examined for their potential to inhibit cancer growth and exhibit cytotoxic effects on plant-based components such as tannins, flavonoids, and triterpenoids3. Therefore, they can be used both alone and in conjunction with other anticancer medications that have been demonstrated effective in the management of HPV-associated malignancies. Examined against regular cell lines, the toxicity profile of plant chemicals is also modest4.

Furthermore, these plant components have demonstrated their ability to inhibit the proliferation of several cell lines and mice models. Additionally, specific plant-derived polysaccharides have been studied for their immunoregulatory properties; they have been shown to have positive anticancer effects through phagocytosis and increased serum hemolysin levels54,87. These predictions imply that plant-based materials may be used to create anticancer medications, vaccinations, and herbal remedies that aid in the treatment of cancer patients who have been exposed to the HPV virus. Utilizing state-of-the-art Nano-biotechnology methods, it is imperative to keep an ongoing focus on developing more potent vaccinations against HPV infection and distributing community awareness and education programs that offer both preventive and therapeutic guidance globally41. However, these initial efforts are pretty modest. Researchers are combining immunotherapy and pharmaceuticals produced from plants to create a multimodal treatment. Scientists have a new avenue to explore when looking for cures for HPV and related illnesses thanks to these plant-based ingredients and nanotechnology1.

An effective campaign on HPV oncogenesis requires a multifaceted approach, incorporating educational outreach, digital engagement, community involvement, and data-driven strategies. One essential component is audience segmentation, which tailors messaging to specific groups such as adolescents, parents, and healthcare providers. Research shows that campaigns targeting healthcare professionals, such as the CDC’s ‘You Are the Key’ initiative, increase their confidence in recommending the HPV vaccine, leading to higher vaccination rates. Additionally, clear educational messaging that explains the link between HPV and various cancers is crucial. Studies emphasize the importance of simple, nontechnical language in dispelling misconceptions and increasing understanding of HPV’s role in cancer prevention12.

The use of digital and social media platforms is another pivotal strategy. Younger audiences, in particular, are highly engaged with online content, and campaigns that incorporate infographics, videos, and interactive tools on platforms like Instagram and TikTok have been shown to increase awareness and engagement with HPV vaccination messages. In addition, influencer partnerships can amplify campaign messages by leveraging the credibility and reach of trusted figures. For instance, campaigns in India that featured Bollywood celebrities resulted in higher awareness and vaccine acceptance among parents, particularly in urban areas. Implementing school-based vaccination programs has proven to be one of the most effective methods for achieving high vaccine uptake. Rwanda’s school-based HPV vaccination program, which achieved over 93% coverage within its first few years, serves as a global model, demonstrating the success of integrating vaccine administration into routine health services for adolescents. Furthermore, engaging community leaders and peer educators is critical in reaching marginalized groups. Community-based interventions, like those seen in Peru, where local health workers played a key role in educating parents, have led to significant improvements in vaccine uptake in rural areas68.

To ensure campaign effectiveness, it is essential to incorporate data-driven monitoring and evaluation systems. Australia’s National HPV Vaccination Program, for example, used a national registry to track vaccination coverage and adapt public health strategies accordingly. This approach not only enables real-time adjustments but also provides evidence for continued advocacy and policy development. Additionally, multilingual and culturally relevant materials are essential in reaching diverse populations, as seen in Canada, where HPV vaccine information is provided in multiple languages to cater to its multicultural population.

Finally, addressing vaccine hesitancy through myth-busting campaigns is crucial. Research indicates that misinformation about the HPV vaccine, particularly around its safety and its alleged encouragement of risky sexual behavior, continues to be a barrier to vaccine uptake. Campaigns that confront these myths with evidence-based information, such as the UK’s recent efforts, have been successful in reducing hesitancy and increasing vaccination rates28.

Future directions

Even if the creation of a targeted therapeutic HPV vaccination has advanced slowly, therapy should be advanced by attempting to enhance the immunogenicity of T-cell response through diverse delivery methods, vaccine composition, delivery routes, and adjuvant types. For the time being, at least, careful planning of clinical trials assessing various side effects and dosage of treatments, as well as molecular investigation of expected effects in the desired target tissues, are required to develop the discipline. Comprehending the basic biology of the tumor might facilitate the creation of more potent therapeutic vaccinations10. Additionally, to increase the therapeutic vaccines’ clinical effectiveness, more clinical studies are required. Subsequent clinical studies should examine the possibility of a synergistic effect of therapeutic vaccinations and antigens expressed by both viruses and neo-antigens in HPV-related cervical cancer. Furthermore, a possible therapeutic option that may complement or perhaps replace the existing courses of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy is the CRISPR/Cas genome editing technology. The vast amount of research on CRISPR/Cas suggests that this novel approach to cancer treatment is the way of the future. Additionally, a few cutting-edge techniques with encouraging outcomes include radioimmunotherapy and CAR-T therapy. Before being applied in clinical settings, these techniques must still be optimized for effectiveness, safety, and specificity. We think the future of targeted gene therapy for cervical cancer is promising as long as programmable Cas nuclease structure and function are further understood51.

Current knowledge on HPV oncogenesis and vaccination highlights several significant gaps that require further investigation. One critical area is the long-term efficacy of HPV vaccines, particularly their ability to prevent noncervical cancers, such as oropharyngeal and anal cancers, in males. While the vaccines have been effective in reducing cervical cancer rates, more research is needed to assess their impact on these other cancer types. Additionally, the durability of vaccine-induced immunity remains an open question, particularly concerning the necessity for booster doses as time progresses. Current studies suggest lasting immunity for over a decade, but longer-term data are still required to confirm this43.

Moreover, there are persistent disparities in vaccine uptake, especially among marginalized communities, such as low-income, rural, and ethnic minority populations. Barriers like misinformation, healthcare access issues, and cultural differences contribute to lower vaccination rates in these groups, which requires tailored interventions. Addressing vaccine hesitancy is another crucial area, with digital misinformation playing a significant role in deterring vaccination2. More studies are needed to explore how misinformation spreads online and how public health campaigns can effectively counter it. Additionally, the impact of sex-neutral vaccination and its potential to reduce HPV transmission and noncervical cancer rates in males should be further explored, as current research has focused predominantly on female. Finally, optimizing postvaccination screening protocols in vaccinated populations remains under-researched, and there is a need to adjust guidelines to maximize cancer prevention while minimizing healthcare burdens.

Recommendations

Numerous viral and malignant diseases have a high coinfection and carcinogenic potential with HPV. Communities are at risk from this kind of biological manifestation. Better research and tests are effectively planned, carried out, and implemented in high-income nations to design medications and carry out vaccination procedures against diseases linked to HPV. In middle-class and low-income nations, where patients already endure extreme financial and health-related strain, this level of study is not practical. As a result, wealthy countries must make sure that approved medications and vaccines are accessible in afflicted areas since these areas are unable to manage their healthcare needs on their own. These areas could become the epicenter of an HPV-related disease that spreads via globalization if they are ignored. Furthermore, scientists, educators, physicians, and researchers must collaborate closely and exchange data and novel scientific insights across a more comprehensive network. In addition to raising public awareness of strategies to lessen and alter the burden of disease associated with HPV, this will contribute to the more significant cause of developing cutting-edge scientific treatment alternatives. To alleviate the global burden of HPV-related healthcare, better treatment options and heightened public awareness of the disease’s causes and prevention are anticipated in the future.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study delved into the intricate landscape of cervical cancer, propelled by the pervasive threat of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Through the exploration of recent biotechnological breakthroughs, we have illuminated the complex dynamics of HPV pathogenesis, unveiling novel insights into host-virus interactions and the mechanisms underlying oncogenesis. As we stand at the forefront of innovative vaccination approaches and technological advancements, there emerges a pressing need for the translation of this knowledge into tangible clinical interventions. Urgent action is warranted to integrate contemporary screening methodologies and therapeutic innovations into healthcare systems worldwide. Simultaneously, a concerted effort towards heightened public awareness is indispensable in curbing the relentless tide of HPV-related diseases, underscoring the imperative for proactive strategies to mitigate this global health crisis.
