Global trends in macrolide and lincosamide resistance in Streptococcus species: a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ali Shivaee, Fatemeh Kazemi, Tahereh Navidifar, Narges Golab, Elnaz Parvizi, Maryam Mofid, Mohammad Sholeh, Masoumeh Beig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe widespread use of macrolides and lincosamides, particularly for treating infections caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (S. pyogenes) (group A Streptococcus (GAS)), and S. pneumoniae has led to a concerning rise in antibiotic resistance. This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the weighted pooled resistance rates of macrolides and lincosamides in clinical isolates of S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, and S. agalactiae (group B Streptococcus (GBS)) based on time, geographical areas, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and guidelines.MethodWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus for studies published between 2015 and 2023. Study quality was assessed via the Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist. Resistance rates were calculated with a random-effects model, supplemented by meta-regression, subgroup analyses, and identification of outliers. Statistical analyses were performed in R using the metafor package.ResultsThis review analyzed 276 studies across 62 countries. Pooled resistance was highest for clarithromycin (57.6%) and azithromycin (55.8%), followed by erythromycin (41.5%) and clindamycin (29.5%), all with substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 98%, p < 0.001). Resistance differed significantly across various countries and continents (p < 0.001). Resistance patterns varied notably by species, with S. pneumoniae exhibiting the highest resistance to all tested macrolides and lincosamides, followed closely by S. agalactiae. In contrast, S. pyogenes consistently showed the lowest resistance levels across all antibiotics examined. A significant upward trend in resistance was observed over time for erythromycin (p = 0.019) and clindamycin (p = 0.003). According to the meta-regression, the erythromycin resistance rate increased over time (r = 0.094, p = 0.005).ConclusionMacrolide and lincosamide resistance among key Streptococcus species are alarmingly high and rising globally, with marked species-specific and regional differences. Our findings underscore the urgent need for standardized susceptibility testing protocols, targeted surveillance by species, and enhanced antibiotic stewardship efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11884-5.Keywords: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, Macrolides, Lincosamides, Erythromycin, Clarithromycin, Azithromycin, Clindamycin, Antimicrobial resistance, Systematic review and meta-analysis

Introduction

Streptococcus, a genus of Gram-positive bacteria, is responsible for many infections, including pharyngitis, pneumonia, skin and soft tissue infections, and invasive diseases such as sepsis and meningitis [1]. Antibiotic resistance in Streptococcus species, particularly S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes (group A Streptococcus (GAS)), and S. agalactiae (group B Streptococcus (GBS)), is an increasing global concern [2, 3]. S. pneumoniae has developed significant resistance to penicillin, macrolides, and fluoroquinolones, making the treatment of pneumonia, meningitis, and invasive infections more difficult [4]. S. agalactiae is showing rising resistance to macrolides and clindamycin, which reduces alternative options for patients allergic to β-lactams and complicates prophylaxis in pregnant women [2]. Although S. pyogenes remains universally susceptible to penicillin, resistance to macrolides and tetracyclines is increasing, limiting empirical treatment choices in some regions [5]. This growing resistance in these clinically important pathogens threatens effective infection management, increases the risk of treatment failure, and underscores the need for ongoing surveillance, antibiotic stewardship, and the development of new therapeutic strategies. The rising resistance to macrolides and lincosamides in these species, which are often used as alternatives to beta-lactams, poses a significant threat to public health, particularly in regions with high infection rates and limited access to alternative antibiotics [2, 6, 7]. These antibiotics inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by targeting the 50S ribosomal subunit and are critical in treating various bacterial infections [8–13]. Clindamycin is particularly effective against Gram-positive cocci, including streptococci, as well as anaerobic bacteria, making it a versatile option for treating polymicrobial infections [13, 14]. Clinically, it is widely used for managing skin and soft tissue infections, respiratory tract infections, and osteomyelitis caused by Streptococcus spp., particularly in patients with beta-lactam allergies [14]. Furthermore, lincosamides, including clindamycin, are recommended as a second-line treatment for S. agalactiae infections, especially in pregnant women who are allergic to beta-lactams [13]. However, increasing resistance in Streptococcus spp. undermines the effectiveness of these antibiotics, highlighting the need for continued surveillance and judicious use. Resistance to macrolides and lincosamides results from multiple genetic and biochemical adaptations. A primary mechanism of macrolide resistance is modification of the antibiotic target site, specifically ribosomal RNA, by methylation mediated by erm genes (e.g., ermB), which reduces antibiotic binding affinity [15]. Efflux pumps, such as those encoded by the mef(A) gene, actively expel macrolides, further contributing to resistance [15]. Similarly, lincosamide resistance mechanisms include mutations in the lnu and lsa genes, which modify the antibiotic’s target site, and erm gene–mediated mechanisms that result in altered patterns of cross-resistance [16]. The increased use of macrolides and lincosamides during the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a corresponding increase in macrolide- and lincosamide-resistant Streptococcus strains, underscoring the need for prudent antibiotic use [17].

Cross-resistance between macrolides and lincosamides is particularly concerning, as it reduces effective treatment options for resistant Streptococcus strains and increases the risk of treatment failure with other antibiotic classes, including streptogramin B [16]. This underscores the urgent need for prudent antibiotic stewardship and strict medical guidelines to limit the development of resistance. In addition to genetic adaptations, geographic variability in resistance rates further complicates the management of infections. For example, different rates of clindamycin resistance have been reported in S. agalactiae and S. pyogenes, highlighting the importance of regional surveillance [10]. In some regions, resistance rates have reached alarming levels, necessitating alternative antibiotics or combination therapies to ensure effective treatment [18]. The prevalence of macrolide and lincosamide resistance in Streptococcus spp. has been extensively studied in regions such as Spain, India, Taiwan, Poland, and China [19–23]. These studies show that resistance rates are increasing worldwide, highlighting the need for ongoing surveillance to monitor resistance patterns and understand the genetic factors that influence resistance [24–26].

Effective management strategies are urgently needed to address the evolving epidemiology of Streptococcus spp. and the rising prevalence of resistant strains. However, no comprehensive and up-to-date synthesis currently exists that integrates recent global data. Therefore, this study systematically reviewed and meta-analyzed publications from 2015 to 2023 to quantify the prevalence of macrolide and lincosamide resistance in S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, and S. agalactiae.

By addressing these objectives, this study aimed to fill important knowledge gaps in the global epidemiology of macrolide and lincosamide resistance in Streptococcus species. The findings provide evidence-based insights to guide the prioritization of high-burden regions for targeted surveillance, inform updates to antimicrobial stewardship and prescribing guidelines, and support the development of future clinical trials and molecular studies investigating resistance mechanisms and transmission dynamics.

Methods

This study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [27] to ensure a rigorous and comprehensive data synthesis on macrolide and lincosamide resistance in Streptococcus species. In addition, registration in the Prospero database (ID: CRD42024557756) underscores our commitment to methodological transparency and rigor.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

To ensure the reliability and relevance of the data, studies were included if they reported quantitative resistance rates for macrolides and lincosamides in S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, or S. agalactiae, specified sample size, and were published as full-text articles in English. Resistance rates had to be quantitatively reported, preferably according to recognized standards such as Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) or European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines. Studies involving clinical isolates from human patients were included. For S. agalactiae, eligible sources encompassed maternal, neonatal, carrier, and invasive infections, provided all other inclusion criteria were met. To ensure robustness, only studies providing sufficient data to calculate both the number of resistant isolates and the total sample size were included. Eligible publications were restricted to those published between May 2015 and December 2023 to capture recent and relevant resistance trends.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria encompassed non-English publications, non-human isolates, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, opinion pieces, and studies with incomplete data. Details of the eligibility criteria, including the rationale for exclusions, are available in Fig. 1 of the included studies. Studies with limited methodological rigor, including case reports, small case series, single-arm or cohort studies, pharmacokinetic studies, and other designs lacking prevalence data, were excluded. Publications with incomplete or unclear data, such as missing resistance proportions or insufficient methodological details, were removed to ensure reliability. When multiple reports originated from the same dataset, only the most complete and recent version was retained to avoid duplication. Studies involving environmental or veterinary isolates were also excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

We systematically searched four major databases—PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus—for studies published between May 2015 and December 2023. These databases were selected for their extensive and complementary coverage of biomedical literature, ensuring the inclusion of a broad and representative range of relevant studies. Search strategies were customized for each database to align with its specific indexing system and search functionalities (details provided in the Supplementary Appendix). Key search terms included “macrolide”, “lincosamide”, “antibiotic resistance”, and “Streptococcus” (S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, S. agalactiae), along with corresponding MeSH terms, to ensure thorough coverage of the topic.

Boolean operators and relevant filters were applied to ensure an accurate representation of clinical studies involving human isolates. Full details of the search strategy and specific search strings for database are provided in the supplementary table.

Selection process

Search results were imported into EndNote (version 20), where duplicate entries were removed. Three authors (ASh, EP, and NG) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts to assess study eligibility. Any disagreements during the selection process were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third author (TN), who provided the final decision.

Data collection process

Two authors (MM and MB) independently extracted data to ensure accuracy and resolved discrepancies through consensus.

Data items

The extracted data included the first author(s), publication year, country of origin, AST method, clinical sample source, number of resistant isolates, and total sample size. AST data were obtained from studies assessing resistance in S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, or S. agalactiae. Only isolates from clinical human infections were included, while environmental or colonizing isolates were excluded.

AST methods were classified as MIC-based, disk diffusion, automated systems, or multiple/combined approaches. Studies were further categorized by interpretive guidelines, including CLSI, EUCAST, Antibiotic Committee of the French Society of Microbiology (CA-SFM), or multiple/unspecified standards. Pooled resistance proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the influence of testing methods and guideline types. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Study risk of bias assessment

Each study was evaluated for quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist [28], which included criteria such as study design, sample description, measurement validity, and statistical rigor. Studies meeting both the inclusion criteria and achieving an acceptable quality score were retained for the quantitative synthesis. Studies with significant methodological flaws, such as small sample sizes or incomplete resistance data, were excluded from the final analysis.

Four authors (M-B, E-P, M-M, and N-G) independently conducted the assessments, with disagreements resolved by consulting three authors (M-Sh, T-N, and A.S.H) for a final determination.

Effect measures

This meta-analysis evaluated the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance by determining the proportion of resistant isolates reported in the included studies. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression were performed to examine potential sources of heterogeneity in resistance rates, such as geographic region, differences in antibiotic susceptibility testing methods and guidelines, and temporal trends in resistance over time.

Synthesis methods

A random-effects model was used to account for study variation, with the DerSimonian–Laird estimator used to calculate heterogeneity (τ2). Given the inclusion of a large number of studies spanning diverse geographical regions, we anticipated notable heterogeneity in the pooled estimates. To account for this, all meta-analyses were conducted using random-effects models, which incorporate between-study variability into the overall estimates. Furthermore, subgroup analyses based on geographic region, Streptococcus species, and antibiotic type were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was further assessed using the Q test and the I2 statistic. When heterogeneity was detected (τ2 > 0), meta-regression analysis was used to explore temporal effects on resistance rates. Outliers and influential variables were identified using studentized residuals and Cook's distances. Studies with studentized residuals exceeding thresholds based on Bonferroni correction or high Cook's distances were flagged as influential. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot symmetry, rank correlation, and regression tests. The standard error of the observed results was used as a predictor. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.1) using the metafor package (version 3.8.1) [29–35].

Reporting bias and certainty assessment

Funnel plot asymmetry was examined using rank correlation and Egger's regression tests to assess potential reporting bias. In addition, the Fail-Safe N and Trim-and-Fill methods were used to address publication bias and increase the reliability of the results. These rigorous analytical approaches strengthened the credibility and robustness of the study's conclusions.

Result

Search and study selection process

The search of online databases identified 9,992 records. After removing 3,996 duplicates, 5,996 articles underwent initial screening, of which 5,047 were excluded for reasons including irrelevance to Streptococcus or antimicrobial resistance, reviews or conference abstracts without full text, non-English publications, case reports, single-arm studies, and studies on veterinary or environmental isolates. Subsequently, 949 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 673 were excluded because they lacked prevalence data, focused solely on genetic or molecular characterization without resistance profiles, discussed virulence or treatment outcomes without susceptibility testing, lacked laboratory antimicrobial testing, were non-English full texts, were pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic studies, were reviews or editorials, or contained duplicate/overlapping data. Ultimately, 276 [15, 36–310] studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Supplementary Table 1 provides detailed characteristics of the included studies and extracted data. The PRISMA flow chart outlines the screening and selection process, as shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2.

Study characteristics

The included studies originated from 62 countries (Kenya, Croatia, China, Japan, Peru, Iran, India, Romania, Serbia, Vietnam, Germany, Poland, Taiwan, Mexico, Canada, Zimbabwe, Tanzania, South Africa, Morocco, Tunisia, Thailand, Sri Lanka, United States, Russia, Norway, Brazil, Malaysia, South Korea, Ethiopia, Portugal, Cambodia, Greece, Italy, France, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Spain, Chile, Belgium, Slovenia, Nepal, Nigeria, Iraq, Algeria, Lebanon, Bulgaria, Ghana, Indonesia, Egypt, Australia, Argentina, Burkina Faso, Bosnia & Herzegovina, Palestinian Territories, Oman, Philippines, Niger, Cameroon, Israel, Iceland, Uganda, Jordan) from six continent (Africa, Europe, Asia, Americas, Multiple continents, Oceania). These studies, spanning from 2015 to 2023, employed various AST methods and guidelines.

Overview of antibiotic resistance prevalence

The analysis of erythromycin resistance was based on 294 reports from 267 studies (as some studies provided multiple reports), with 60,502 resistant isolates among 173,854 investigated isolates, was 0.415 (0.379, 0.452), and heterogeneity between reports was significant (I2 = 99.24%, p < 0.001).

The proportion of clarithromycin resistance through 24 reports from 23 studies with 10,353 resistant isolates among 31,896 investigated isolates was 0.576 (0.477, 0.668), and heterogeneity between reports was significant (I2 = 99.39%, p < 0.001). The proportion of azithromycin resistance through 45 reports of 41 studies, with 8,270 resistant isolates among 16,549 investigated isolates, was 0.558 (0.473, 0.639), and heterogeneity between reports was significant (I2 = 98.25%, p < 0.001). The proportion of clindamycin resistance through 168 reports from 157, with 22,735 resistant isolates among 78,785 investigated isolates, was 0.295 (0.247, 0.347), and heterogeneity between reports was significant (I2 = 99.07%, p < 0.001) (Table 1). A forest plot showing the observed outcomes and the estimate based on the random-effects model is shown in Fig. 2.

Prevalence of erythromycin resistance

A total of 173,854 isolates from 267 studies were included in the analysis of erythromycin resistance. The estimated average proportion, based on the random-effects model, was 0.415 (95% CI: 0.379–0.452). This value differed significantly from zero (z = − 4.432, p < 0.001). The Q-test confirmed high heterogeneity (Q(293) = 38,326.232, I2 = 99.25%, p < 0.001). Figure 2 shows the forest plot with the pooled estimate. Trim-and-fill analysis yielded the same estimate (0.415, 95% CI: 0.379–0.452). No studies had studentized residuals greater than 3.760, indicating no potential outliers. Cook’s distances showed no overly influential studies. The regression test suggested funnel plot asymmetry (p < 0.001), whereas the rank correlation test did not (p = 0.287) (Table 2).

Prevalence of clarithromycin resistance

A total of 31,896 isolates from 23 studies were included in the analysis of clarithromycin resistance. The estimated average proportion, based on the random-effects model, was 0.576 (95% CI: 0.477–0.668), with no significant difference from zero (z = 1.510, p = 0.131). The Q-test indicated substantial heterogeneity (Q(23) = 3,742.227, I2 = 99.39%, p < 0.001). Figure 2 presents the forest plot of observed outcomes and the pooled estimate. The trim-and-fill method produced the same proportion (0.576, 95% CI: 0.477–0.668). Studentized residuals revealed two potential outliers [221, 306] with values greater than 3.078. Removal of these studies did not alter the pooled estimate (0.576, 95% CI: 0.477–0.668). Cook’s distances indicated no overly influential studies. Funnel plot asymmetry was not detected by the rank correlation (p = 0.703) or regression test (p = 0.442).

Prevalence of azithromycin resistance

A total of 16,549 isolates from 43 studies were included in the analysis of azithromycin resistance. The estimated average proportion, based on the random-effects model, was 0.558 (95% CI: 0.473–0.639). This estimate did not differ significantly from zero (z = 1.343, p = 0.179). According to the Q-test, the true effects appeared heterogeneous (Q(44) = 2,515.576, I2 = 98.25%, p < 0.001). A forest plot of the observed outcomes and the random-effects model estimate is presented in Fig. 2. Application of the trim-and-fill method did not change the pooled estimate (0.558, 95% CI: 0.473–0.639). Examination of studentized residuals identified one potential outlier [221] with a value greater than 3.261. Excluding this study did not affect the pooled estimate (0.558, 95% CI: 0.473–0.639). Cook’s distances indicated that no study was overly influential. Funnel plot asymmetry was not detected by either the rank correlation test (p = 0.145) or the regression test (p = 0.710).

Prevalence of clindamycin resistance

A total of 78,785 isolates from 157 studies were included in the analysis of clindamycin resistance. The estimated average proportion, based on the random-effects model, was 0.295 (95% CI: 0.247–0.347), which was significantly different from zero (z = − 7.121, p < 0.001). The Q-test indicated considerable heterogeneity (Q(167) = 17,889.344, I2 = 99.07%, p < 0.001). The forest plot in Fig. 2 presents the observed outcomes and pooled estimate. The trim-and-fill method adjusted the proportion to 0.406 (95% CI: 0.348–0.467). Studentized residuals identified one potential outlier [149] with a value above 3.617; excluding this study did not change the estimate (0.406, 95% CI: 0.348–0.467). Cook’s distances suggested that the same study was overly influential. Funnel plot asymmetry was indicated by the regression test (p < 0.001) but not by the rank correlation test (p = 0.145).

Subgroup analysis

This section provides an in-depth summary of subgroup analyses related to antibiotic resistance. The full dataset is provided in Supplementary Table 3. The study explored variations in resistance rates across different regions, the impact of various AST methods, temporal trends, and the influence of study quality on reported findings.

Subgroup analysis based on country

Country-level subgroup analysis revealed significant overall differences for all four antibiotics (p < 0.001). For clindamycin, the lowest prevalence was observed in Kenya (0.7%) and the highest in Vietnam (87.2%). Erythromycin resistance ranged from the lowest prevalence in Croatia (1.9%) to the highest in Vietnam (88.5%). Azithromycin resistance varied significantly, with Germany showing the lowest prevalence (2.7%) and China the highest (91.8%). Clarithromycin resistance also differed significantly, with Germany reporting the lowest prevalence (2.7%) and China the highest (89.8%) (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis based on continent

Continent-level subgroup analysis showed significant differences for all four antibiotics (P < 0.001). For azithromycin, resistance was highest in Asia (69.7%) and lowest in Africa (8.2%), with intermediate estimates in the Americas (46.4%) and North America (36.9%). Clarithromycin followed a similar pattern, peaking in Asia (74.3%) and lowest in Africa (21.8%) and the Americas (23.1%). Erythromycin resistance was greatest in Asia (61.4%) and lowest in the Americas (19.4%), with Europe (27.0%) and Africa (22.0%) also below Asia. Clindamycin resistance varied widely across continents, from a high in Asia (56.0%) to a low in Africa (7.9%), with Europe at 17.3% and the Americas at 8.9% (Fig. 4A).

Subgroup analysis based on AST method

Across antibiotics, resistance estimates differed by AST method for azithromycin (P < 0.001), clarithromycin (P = 0.004), and erythromycin (P < 0.001), but not for clindamycin (P = 0.076). For azithromycin, MIC produced the highest pooled resistance (84.7%), whereas disk diffusion yielded the lowest (27.8%); automate (61.3%) and multiple methods (63.2%) were intermediate. Clarithromycin showed a similar pattern with the highest estimate by MIC (84.7%), followed by automate (53.3%), disk diffusion (42.8%), and multiple methods (37.0%). For erythromycin, automate returned the highest estimate (64.2%), MIC was moderate (36.5%), and disk diffusion (25.0%) and multiple methods (26.6%) were lower (Fig. 4B).

Subgroup analysis based on AST guidelines

By guideline category, significant between-group differences were observed for all four antibiotics: azithromycin (P = 0.003), clarithromycin (P = 0.013), erythromycin (P = 0.004), and clindamycin (P = 0.004). For azithromycin, pooled resistance was 66.1% with EUCAST, 59.7% with CLSI, and 22.0% when guidelines were not defined. Clarithromycin resistance was 64.4% (CLSI) versus 47.6% (EUCAST) and 19.3% (not defined). For erythromycin, estimates were highest with CLSI (35.2%) and lower with ND (23.2%), EUCAST (15.5%), and multiple-guideline reporting (8.6%). For clindamycin, CLSI yielded 35.2% compared with 15.5% (EUCAST), 8.6% (multiple guideline), and 23.2% (not defined). Overall, these findings indicate that both the AST method and the interpretive guideline materially influence pooled resistance estimates, with MIC-based testing and CLSI reporting generally producing higher proportions than disk diffusion and EUCAST (Fig. 4C).

Subgroup analysis based on quality assesment

Resistance estimates varied significantly across risk-of-bias categories for three antibiotics. Studies classified as low risk reported the highest resistance rates for azithromycin (59.7%), erythromycin (43.5%), and clindamycin (33.0%). In contrast, studies with some concerns showed markedly lower resistance for erythromycin (29.9%) and clindamycin (4.2%), while studies at high risk reported the lowest rates for azithromycin (3.5%), clindamycin (4.2%), and erythromycin (7.9%). Overall, the pattern suggests that lower risk-of-bias assessments are associated with higher reported resistance rates, particularly for macrolides (Fig. 4D).

Subgroup analysis based on species

Significant variations in resistance prevalence were observed across bacterial species for azithromycin, erythromycin, and clindamycin. For S. pneumoniae, resistance was highest for azithromycin (66.0%) and clarithromycin (61.7%), followed by erythromycin (52.1%) and clindamycin (37.3%). S. agalactiae exhibited the highest clarithromycin resistance (61.9%) and notable resistance to azithromycin (52.5%) and erythromycin (34.5%), with clindamycin at 30.9%.

In contrast, S. pyogenes showed comparatively lower resistance levels, with clarithromycin at 40.0%, azithromycin at 33.8%, erythromycin at 18.0%, and clindamycin at 9.7%.

Overall, macrolide resistance was most pronounced in S. pneumoniae and S. agalactiae, while S. pyogenes consistently exhibited the lowest resistance rates across antibiotics (Fig. 4E).

Subgroup analysis based on year

Significant temporal differences in resistance prevalence were observed for erythromycin and clindamycin. Erythromycin resistance increased from 36.4% in 2015–2019 to 45.4% in 2020–2023 (p = 0.019), while clindamycin resistance rose from 22.1% to 35.9% over the same periods (p = 0.003).

Although azithromycin resistance showed a slight decline (from 55.6% to 53.7%, p = 0.564) and clarithromycin resistance increased (from 49.1% to 62.2% p = 0.253), these changes were not statistically significant (Fig. 4F).

Meta-regression

Meta-regression revealed distinct temporal patterns in resistance trends. Azithromycin showed a slight but non-significant decline over time (r = –0.084, p = 0.270, 95% CI [–0.233, 0.065]) (Fig. 5A), whereas clarithromycin (r = 0.121, p = 0.164, 95% CI [–0.049, 0.292]) (Fig. 5B), erythromycin (r = 0.094, p = 0.005, 95% CI [0.029, 0.159]) (Fig. 5C), and clindamycin (r = 0.096, p = 0.080, 95% CI [–0.011, 0.203]) (Fig. 5D) all exhibited upward trends. Notably, the increase in erythromycin resistance was statistically significant, while the rises in clarithromycin and clindamycin were not.

Publication bias assessment

Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s and Begg’s tests. Egger’s test indicated significant bias for erythromycin (p < 0.001) and clindamycin (p < 0.001), whereas no significant bias was observed for clarithromycin or azithromycin. The robustness of these findings was further supported by the Fail-Safe N and Trim-and-Fill methods, with the adjusted pooled proportion for clindamycin estimated at 0.406 (95% CI: 0.348–0.467) (Table 2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 276 studies from 62 countries (2015–2023) reveals a concerning global burden of macrolide and lincosamide resistance in S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, and S. agalactiae. Erythromycin resistance, reported in 294 datasets, averaged 41.5% (I2 = 99.24%, p < 0.001), with clarithromycin (57.6%) and azithromycin (55.8%) showing the highest rates—reflecting widespread use and likely cross-resistance within the macrolide class. Clindamycin resistance, though lower (29.5%), remains clinically significant, especially for macrolide-allergic patients.

Geographical variation was striking: Oceania topped the list (72.7%, one report), followed by Asia (61.4%, 137 reports), where Vietnam reached 88.5% and Lebanon just 7.9%. The Americas (19.4%) and Africa (22.0%) had the lowest pooled rates, though local extremes existed—from Peru’s 32.6% to Ghana’s 2.6%. Lower rates in the Americas may reflect strong stewardship and circulation of susceptible clones, while Africa’s low rates could stem from limited AST access and reduced macrolide use.

These patterns underscore an urgent need for prudent prescribing, reinforced stewardship, and harmonized global surveillance to prevent further erosion of macrolide and lincosamide efficacy.

Clindamycin resistance was highest in Asia (56%, 73 reports), especially in Vietnam (87.2%, three studies) and China (80.8%, 27 studies). The high levels of resistance may be due to multidrug-resistant (MDR) clones capable of clonal spread. Selected strains may pose a threat if they cause infections, acting as a reservoir of resistance genes. For example, S. pneumoniae serotype 15A, a serotype commonly associated with MDR in respiratory isolates, exhibits resistance to clarithromycin, clindamycin, and doxycycline. This resistance profile has been linked to the globally recognized Sweden15A-25 clone (sequence type ST63) [311].

Subgroup analyses demonstrated notable regional variations in azithromycin resistance rates. Moreover, Asia had the highest prevalence of resistance to azithromycin (69.7%, 31 reports), particularly in China (91.8%, 13 reports) and Iran (76.6%, three reports). In contrast, Africa had the lowest prevalence (8.2%, six reports).

In contrast to other regions, Africa exhibited the lowest resistance rates to macrolides, azithromycin (8.2%) and clarithromycin (21.8%), and the lincosamide clindamycin (7.9%).

This notably low resistance may reflect limited availability and usage of these antibiotic classes in many African healthcare systems. Empirical treatment in resource-constrained settings frequently relies on older, more readily accessible antibiotics such as penicillins, which likely limits selective pressure for resistance to macrolides and lincosamides. In The Gambia, restricted clindamycin access and high cost may explain low resistance [312]. Furthermore, a review of antibiotic consumption in West Africa shows that “Access” antibiotics account for the majority of use, with much lower use of “Watch” agents, highlighting a pattern of underuse for these newer antibiotic classes [313].

In the Americas, clindamycin resistance ranged widely, from 27.6% in Peru to 5.0% in Argentina, though limited data (two studies from Peru, one from Argentina) highlight the need for broader surveillance.

Regional differences in resistance prevalence should be interpreted with caution. Some of the observed variation, such as the differences between Asia and Africa, may reflect genuine epidemiological patterns driven by antimicrobial usage practices, public health policies, and pathogen distribution. However, these results may also be influenced by methodological factors, including the unequal number of studies from each continent, uneven representation of countries, and variability in study design, AST methods, and interpretive guidelines.

This meta-analysis included a large number of studies from diverse geographical regions, which inevitably introduced heterogeneity into the pooled estimates. Although random-effects models, subgroup analyses, and the AST method were applied to account for and explore this variability, residual heterogeneity may still influence the precision and interpretation of regional estimates.

Subgroup analysis by species identified S. pneumoniae as the most prevalent species. Among 155 studies assessing erythromycin susceptibility (2015–2023), the pooled resistance prevalence was 52.1%. This aligns with Fu et al.’s meta-analysis of 24 studies from China (2000–2016), which reported a markedly higher rate of 94.4% in pneumococcal isolates [314]. Similarly, Mather et al. found 64% erythromycin resistance in S. pneumoniae from paediatric acute otitis media cases across 48 studies (1980–2017) [315]. while Hosseini et al. reported 34% prevalence in nasopharyngeal carriage in Iran (up to 2015) [316]. Variations may reflect geographic differences, antibiotic usage, laboratory methods, timeframes, and population or sampling differences (e.g., clinical vs. carriage).

Our analysis also showed species-specific variations in resistance, though estimates for less-represented species should be interpreted cautiously due to small sample sizes, which may bias results and reduce precision. More high-quality, balanced studies are needed to confirm these patterns. Our species-based analysis revealed notable differences in resistance rates among Streptococcus species. These findings may reflect true species-specific trends; however, they should be interpreted cautiously, due to small sample sizes, which may bias results and reduce the precision of pooled resistance rates.

Further high-quality studies with larger and more balanced datasets are needed to confirm these species-specific patterns and provide more reliable estimates.

From 82 reports (2015–2023), S. pneumoniae was the leading species for clindamycin resistance, with a pooled prevalence of 37.3%. Meta-analyses in China reported even higher rates—92.3% [314] and 64% [317] —including findings from nasopharyngeal carriage specimens. Clindamycin, preferred over erythromycin for penicillin-allergic pregnant women due to erythromycin’s poor placental transfer and high resistance rates [177, 318], showed a 30.9% prevalence in S. agalactiae. Regional variation was substantial: 33.6% in North China and 65.1% in Northwest China [319], versus 19.6% in African maternal rectovaginal colonization [320], underscoring the need for local resistance-based prescribing.

For azithromycin, 45 reports yielded a pooled prevalence of 55.8%, with S. pneumoniae again highest (66%, 24 reports, 2015–2023). It is indicated for skin and respiratory infections [321, 322].

No meta-analysis on S. pneumoniae azithromycin resistance has been published in the past decade. In S. pyogenes, azithromycin is an option for penicillin-intolerant patients; in southern Taiwan, resistance rose from 19.3% to 61.0% between 2000–2019 [21], emphasizing the need for ongoing surveillance. Resistance patterns in Streptococcus spp. are influenced by AST methods and guidelines, with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)-based/automated systems and CLSI criteria often yielding higher rates than disk diffusion or EUCAST/CA-SFM. In this meta-analysis, erythromycin resistance (81 studies) averaged 34.5% [177], ranging from low levels in Africa [114] to 65.4% in China (2000–2020) [319] and 25% among pregnant women in China (2000–2016) [323]. Variations likely reflect consumption patterns, prescribing shifts, and resistant strain spread, with upward trends in China pointing to a need for stewardship.

Clindamycin resistance (97 studies) had a pooled prevalence of 29.5% (I2 = 99.07%, p < 0.001), increasing to 40.6% after adjusting for publication bias. Rates varied with population, sampling, protocols, and infection sites. Although not first-line, intravenous clindamycin is IDSA-recommended for inpatient treatment of S. pneumoniae community-acquired and aspiration pneumonia [324].

Current clinical guidelines—including those from the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)—recommend penicillins as first-line therapy for streptococcal infections. Macrolide use is limited to patients with confirmed susceptibility or documented β-lactam allergy, particularly in regions with high resistance rates [325, 326]. Similar recommendations are reflected in the Canadian Paediatric Society (CPS) and British Thoracic Society (BTS) guidelines [327, 328], which emphasize the use of first-line β-lactams and caution against empiric macrolide monotherapy in regions with high resistance prevalence.

In this meta-analysis, clarithromycin susceptibility was assessed in 24 studies, with a pooled prevalence of 57.6%. Due to the high prevalence of macrolide resistance in Streptococcus spp., clarithromycin is no longer recommended as monotherapy [329, 330]. Al-Salloum et al. [331] reported higher S. pneumoniae eradication rates with clarithromycin + beta-lactam compared to azithromycin + beta-lactam. Between 2015–2019 and 2020–2023, resistance to clarithromycin, erythromycin, and clindamycin increased, with a significant rise for erythromycin only; azithromycin resistance declined slightly but not significantly. Although initially considered beneficial for COVID-19 pneumonia [332], later trials showed no benefit and possible increased mortality when combined with hydroxychloroquine [333–335]. Widespread overuse of azithromycin during the pandemic was reported [336, 337], followed by a decline after national evidence-based guidelines were introduced [336].

This highlights the importance of stewardship programs in mitigating the risk of resistance and ensuring the rational use of antibiotics. A retrospective study by Nixon et al. examined trends in macrolide resistance among S. pyogenes isolates in the Northern Territory between 2012 and 2023. Among the 33,519 S. pyogenes isolates identified, resistance to clindamycin and erythromycin remained consistently low (1–2%) from 2012 to early 2020. However, a significant increase in resistance was observed in 2020, with peaks of 6.0% for clindamycin-resistant S. pyogenes and 12.2% for erythromycin-resistant S. pyogenes in 2021. By early 2023, resistance rates in S. pyogenes had almost returned to pre-2020 levels. The temporary spike in resistance highlights the need for continuous genomic surveillance to rapidly detect emerging resistance patterns and guide timely public health interventions [338]. Studies show contrasting resistance trends: in Canada (2007–2016), S. pneumoniae maintained high susceptibility to clarithromycin and clindamycin [311]; in Gaza, erythromycin resistance increased from 76.5% to 91.6% and clindamycin from 61.2% to 73.4% post-COVID [339]; in Romania, erythromycin resistance declined from 52.55% to 29.11% and clarithromycin from 49.12% to 27.03% [340], reflecting the effects of overuse versus targeted stewardship.

Conclusion

This review and meta-analysis, based on 276 studies from 62 countries (2015–2023), shows that macrolide and lincosamide resistance in S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, and S. agalactiae is a serious global problem, with higher rates in many developing countries. Resistance patterns vary by region, making it important to understand local trends to guide research, improve antibiotic use policies, and limit the spread of resistant bacteria.

Clindamycin has lower resistance rates than the macrolides studied, but careful use is needed to prevent resistance from increasing. Learning from regions with low resistance, such as parts of Europe, Africa, and the Americas, can help shape better strategies. A combined effort involving regular surveillance, focused research, and strong antibiotic stewardship programs is essential to protect the effectiveness of existing treatments and safeguard public health.
