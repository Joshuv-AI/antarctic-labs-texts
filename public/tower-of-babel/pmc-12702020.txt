GPTRC: A large-scale dataset for evaluating question-answering capabilities and limitations of large language models
Authors: Akarsh Tripathi, Tanisha Gupta, Ashutosh Kumar Dubey, Ravita Chahar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA Generative Pre-Trained Transformer (GPT) Responses Corpus (GPTRC), a large-scale dataset designed to facilitate comprehensive analysis of ChatGPT’s performance was presented in this paper. The dataset consists of 50,134 question-response pairs generated by GPT-3.5 Turbo model. It covers broad range of topics and question formats for the evaluation of the Large Language Models (LLMs) strength and limitations. It is also comparable to human-generated text. The records in the dataset includes metadata like thinking time, response time, response length, and extracted keywords. These metadata are helpful for benchmarking studies. The dataset was curated through a combination of web-scraped and manually generated questions, which were submitted to ChatGPT via OpenAI’s API to capture raw, unfiltered responses. The question types considered are both factual and reasoning-based. It also covers multilingual record for studying LLMs capabilities in terms of coherence, relevance, adaptability, and processing efficiency. The GPTRC dataset is a transparent, reproducible, and openly accessible resource for analyzing LLM behavior across varied domains. It supports researchers aiming to benchmark LLMs on multiple fronts such as accuracy, efficiency, linguistic richness, and consistency. The comprehensive metadata allows researchers to draw nuanced insights into model performance under different conditions. By facilitating fine-grained analysis, this dataset serves as a foundational resource for improving LLM robustness and guiding the development of future AI systems.Keywords: Large language models (LLMs), ChatGPT-3.5 Turbo, Question-answering dataset, Natural language processing (NLP), Benchmarking AI models, Generative AI evaluation

Background

Large Language Models (LLMs) have rapidly gained popularity among everyday applications due to their ability to respond in a human-like fashion [[1], [2], [3]]. The integration of LLM into real-world applications is growing from service automation to personalized recommendation systems [4,5]. Due to this, the demand for rigorous evaluation and benchmarking of these models has intensified [[6], [7], [8]].

The most of the existing works rely completely on narrow benchmark which generally evaluates models on a small set of domain or task [[9], [10], [11]]. This paper presents a comprehensive dataset designed to bridge the gap by capturing real user-model interactions. The dataset comprises a substantial corpus of user-submitted questions and AI-generated responses, covering a wide range. This diversity enables the evaluation of the model behavior across multiple domains and enhances the generalizability of analytical findings. Fine-tuning of LLMs is increasingly becoming a standard practice for handling a wide range of everyday tasks. These tasks span from relatively simple interactions, such as responding to customer queries, to more complex applications like generating personalized recommendations. As a result, determining which LLM is most suitable for a specific task has emerged as a significant challenge, particularly given the diversity of available models and their varying capabilities [[12], [13], [14], [15]].

A number of parameters are needed in addition to questions and answers to evaluate a model and compare it to its counterparts. Each LLM has its own capabilities and limitations. So, there is the need of large corpus to fit the need. The name of the dataset developed is Generative Pre-Trained Transformer (GPT) Responses Corpus (GPTRC). It contains 50,134 records contain questions and their answers (Each record represents a distinct question), as well as specific criteria that offer the metadata against the generated responses. The metadata (described in detail in Section 3) is of paramount importance when comparing various LLMs.

Data Description

The dataset comprises 50,134 records each representing a separate question. This dataset was developed using ChatGPT 3.5. The main components of the dataset are question number, questions, keywords, unfiltered answer, thinking time (in milliseconds) and response time (in seconds). A question bank was crafted combining questions scraped from various sources, including manual collection. To collect responses from the LLM model a python script was used. OpenAI GPT 3.5 Turbo model’s API was integrated in the script to prompt the model with questions one by one. This script was then run on a virtual machine in a cloud-based environment. The question bank in the dataset is divided into three broad categories: Analytical, Logical, and Factual, each requiring a distinct answering style and reasoning pattern. The dataset contains a total of 50,134 questions distributed as follows—Analytical (16,640), Logical (16,841), and Factual (16,653). Logical questions form the largest portion, followed closely by Factual and Analytical, indicating a well-balanced dataset. The categorization was performed entirely based on the linguistic and contextual characteristics of each question, ensuring diversity in question complexity and intent.

Analytical questions focus on analysing the subject of the question carefully before reaching a conclusion and generating a response. Whereas, logical and factual questions are more straightforward. Factual questions focus on real life facts, e.g. “The sun rises from the East.” is a universal truth and an axiom. Each category of question tests a different aspect of the model’s ability to understand, contextualize and respond in a unique way. Factual questions test the existing knowledge base of the LLMs, whereas Logical questions are aimed at determining whether the model can generate responses that are both relevant and accurate within the context of a given question. Analytical questions, on the other hand, are more challenging and do not have straightforward answers; instead, they require the model to explore the depth and complexity of the question before formulating a response.

Each question is assigned a question_no which enables unique identification of a question. This also provides the ease of accessing any specific question to the researchers.

The question attribute specifies the actual question. It is encoded in the standard UTF-8 format and is not limited to the English language. Rather, it also contains few instances of Hindi text written in the ‘Devanagari’ script. This diversity adds to the value of this dataset and provides opportunity to the researchers to analyze the working of the GPT 3.5 Turbo model with different languages. All the questions vary in terms of their category, that is, the questions do not correspond to any specific industry or type. Questions present as a part of this dataset are either scraped from various search engines available on the internet or have been self-generated.

The keywords attribute of each record specifies the keywords used in the question corresponding to that record. This field helps keep track of the context of the question. In addition to that, it also helps in figuring out the category of the question and may help researchers to extract records based on specific topics or subjects. It is also helpful in verifying the relevancy of the generated response.

The unfiltered answer attribute is where the unprocessed answer that was generated by the ``GPT-3.5-turbo'' LLM is saved. Responses, just like the question field, are encoded using the UTF-8 standard and may contain chunks of Hindi text written in the ``Devanagari'' script. Because these answers have not been processed in any way, it is possible to conduct an exhaustive study of the model's output in a variety of scripts and languages.

The thinking time attribute keeps a record of the amount of time, in milliseconds, that it takes for the model to process each question. It offers information regarding the processing speed of the ``GPT-3.5 turbo'' model when it is tasked with providing an answer to a certain inquiry.

The response time (in seconds) field stores the overall amount of time, measured in seconds, that it took to receive the response when making use of the API. It considers the amount of time necessary to communicate the question, run the query via the language model, and obtain the response. The effectiveness and responsiveness of the model in terms of providing results is shown by this metric.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data collection and preparation

To generate this corpus of GPT responses, first a set of questions was prepared. Questions for this question bank were collected through various means, through scraping the internet as well as from manual sources. This question bank is diverse, i.e. it incorporates questions in multiple domains irrespective of language or script. Once the question bank was ready, a script was prepared which runs against the prepared question bank and uses OpenAI’s completion API to prompt the LLM against each question iteratively. On each iteration, it captures the response as well as other important aspects of the response as metadata. The metadata generated comprises of the following parameters:

Each parameter offers specific insights into the dataset. Thinking time refers to the duration reported by OpenAI’s API, indicating how long the LLM takes to process the input and begin generating a response. In contrast, total response time measures the entire duration from the moment the prompt is submitted to the completion of the iteration, including the time taken to receive and record the response.

The process iteratively evaluates and checks if a subsequent row (record) exists in the dataset. If yes, it evaluates the current response against the defined thresholds. This iteration continues until all records are processed. Once the dataset has been fully traversed, the system isolates and analyzes the rows where one or more of the thresholds were exceeded. These outliers indicate potential weaknesses in the LLM’s performance with respect to accuracy, fluency, or response efficiency.

Dataset-driven evaluation of LLMs

Only a few open-access datasets currently exist that allow detailed analysis of specific LLM models. The following datasets are discussed to illustrate how LLM performance is validated.

1.Large language models comparison dataset [16]: This dataset provides a comprehensive, quantitative framework for validating LLM performance across multiple dimensions. By including attributes such as context window, generation speed, and latency, it allows researchers to evaluate model efficiency and responsiveness, two critical factors in real-time deployment scenarios. Benchmark metrics like Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) and Chatbot Arena scores further establish a standardized measure of model competency in academic knowledge and interactive dialogue, respectively. Moreover, the inclusion of price per million tokens, compute power, and energy efficiency enables cost–performance trade-off analyses, which are crucial for determining production feasibility. Such multi-attribute evaluation supports a holistic assessment of LLMs, helping researchers select models that balance accuracy, speed, and operational cost. However, the dataset’s reliance on vendor-reported statistics and point-in-time measurements may limit longitudinal validity, requiring researchers to periodically update or cross-verify values with independent benchmarks before drawing conclusions.

Because each task has ground-truth SAS code and runnable data, researchers can assess not only semantic similarity using NLP metrics but also end-to-end fidelity, does the code run and produce the right statistical results? The dataset further enables correlation studies between automatic metrics and human judgments, ablation across tasks like regression, ANOVA, Generalized Linear Model (GLM), survival, and comparative benchmarking across models under identical prompts and data. The major strengths include reproducibility, multi-criteria human evaluation, and fine-grained error analysis like syntax vs. logic vs. results. Limitations are that it is SAS-centric (generalization to R/Python must be tested), human scoring is labor-intensive and may carry rater bias mitigated but not eliminated by procedures, and results can be prompt-sensitive. StatLLM provides a rigorous, execution-based benchmark to substantiate claims about LLM reliability in statistical analysis beyond text matching alone.

However, most existing studies focus primarily on generalizing the questions and evaluation parameters to benchmark specific LLMs, often overlooking model-specific behavioral nuances and interaction patterns [[22], [23], [24]]. Traditional benchmarking datasets are usually designed to test knowledge recall or code generation accuracy in isolation, which, while useful, fails to capture the holistic behavior of LLMs in interactive or conversational settings [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. As a result, these benchmarks may not provide a complete picture of how models perform in real-world, multi-turn scenarios where factors such as consistency, reasoning depth, and adaptability play a crucial role [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]].

In contrast, the GPTRC dataset introduces a more comprehensive and structured evaluation protocol that establishes ground rules and trust-based factors before arriving at conclusions regarding model performance. GPTRC goes beyond simple question-answer validation by incorporating additional behavioral and linguistic features such as grammatical accuracy, creativity, relevance to context, response speed, and even the probability of machine-like behavior. These features allow evaluators to capture subtle differences in how LLMs process, generate, and refine their responses, thereby enabling a more realistic and human-centered assessment.

The GPTRC dataset offers a comprehensive and well-structured resource designed to support diverse experimental setups, including the integration of intelligent learning algorithms and AI-based approaches for advanced model assessment. For instance, some models may demonstrate high grammatical accuracy but exhibit limited creativity, whereas others may generate diverse and contextually rich responses at the cost of increased hallucination rates. By systematically quantifying these trade-offs, the GPTRC dataset enables reproducible and actionable insights for researchers and developers seeking to optimize model performance. Its carefully designed structure facilitates comparability of results across studies, helping establish standardized benchmarks for LLM assessment. Moreover, GPTRC functions not only as a dataset but also as a practical evaluation framework by incorporating metrics for model correctness, human-likeness, and trustworthiness, making it a valuable resource for advancing research in LLM evaluation.

Another key significance of the GPTRC dataset is that it introduces a new perspective for evaluating LLMs using specific, well-defined parameters. These parameters, treated as metadata, which provide considerable information about the LLMs, their internal thought process and how fast they reach to the conclusion on any specific topic. This approach is particularly effective in contexts where questions are categorized into distinct perspectives, namely Analytical, Factual, and Logical. These three broad categories, combined with the associated metadata parameters, enable researchers and developers to systematically benchmark and enhance their LLMs, leading to more reliable and robust conclusions regarding model performance and capabilities.

Additionally, datasets like GPTRC become of utmost importance in judging the capability of LLMs on a broad level. The parameters as basic as “thinking_time” and “response_completion_time” as mentioned in the dataset help researchers to analyse the performance of any LLM straightforward as these parameters are neither difficult to calculate nor they are too variable for each LLM. Thus, even fine-tuned LLMs, when evaluated using such a diversified question bank, are likely to normalize their performance in alignment with the benchmark established through this dataset. The question bank not only consists of multiple categories, but also has occurrences of varied languages which again helps in diversifying the sample space for the LLM benchmarking. Keywords from the questions and the length of the answers are some other major parameters that are discussed as a part of this dataset as they provide key insights regarding the enablement of valid benchmarking. These parameters allow us to explore factors like relevance and accuracy of the answers generated by any LLM. These along with other statistical parameters places the GPTRC dataset in a unique position to act as a benchmark for many LLMs to come.

•Keyword Coverage Ratio – Measures whether the LLM response adequately covers the key points expected in the answer. This ensures that responses remain relevant and on-topic.

Limitations

Although the number of questions that have been scraped from the internet for the creation of the dataset is seemingly large, the quality of the questions cannot be guaranteed in terms of abstractness or vagueness. ChatGPT has been programmed to reject any questions that require real life experience, subjective judgment, or sensitive real-world scenarios, some responses in the dataset may be duplicated or generic. Due to the open-ended and unstructured nature of web-scraped content, there is the chance of topic imbalance and limited control over linguistic quality. However, this has been implemented by the OpenAI team to uphold the ethical standards required in the development of AI tools, and there is no workaround other than processing the received responses through an additional filtering mechanism.
