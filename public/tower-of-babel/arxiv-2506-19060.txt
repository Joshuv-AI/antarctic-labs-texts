Diophantine Approximation on Abelian Varieties; a Conjecture of M. Waldschmidt
Lior Fishman, David Lambert, Keith Merrill, David Simmons

Abstract
. Following the work of Waldschmidt, we investigate problems in Diophantine approximation on abelian varieties. First we show that a conjecture of Waldschmidt for a given simple abelian variety is equivalent to a well-known Diophantine condition holding for a certain matrix related to that variety. We then posit a related but weaker conjecture, and establish the upper bound direction of that conjecture in full generality. For rank 1 elliptic curves defined over a number field K ⫋ ℝ K\subsetneqq\mathbb{R} , we then obtain a weak-type Dirichlet theorem in this setting, establish the optimality of this statement, and prove our conjecture in this case.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background on Diophantine Approximation

In its most general form, the field of
Diophantine approximation
usually seeks to quantify in a precise manner the density of some subset
\mathcal{Q}
within its closure
M
, with respect to some notion of complexity on the points of
\mathcal{Q}
. Specifically, adapting language from
[
4
,
5
]
,
we consider a Diophantine tuple to consist of: (1) a complete metric space
(M,\operatorname{dist})
; (2) a countable subset
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq M
; and (3) a
height function

H:\mathcal{Q}\to(0,\infty)
. When addressing questions in the field, we must specify the tuple
(M,\operatorname{dist},\mathcal{Q},H)
. Given a function
\psi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, we say that
x\in M
is
\psi
-
approximable
if there exists a constant
C_{x}
and an infinite sequence
\mathcal{Q}\ni r_{n}\to x
satisfying

\operatorname{dist}(r_{n},x)<C_{x}\psi(H(r_{n}))\quad\text{ for all }n.

Traditional questions in the field concern characterizing the functions
\psi
for which (almost) all points are
\psi
-approximable, or for a fixed function
\psi
, to describe in some manner the set of points which are
\psi
-approximable.

Consider this definition concretely in the case of
(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|,\mathbb{Q},H)
where
H(\frac{p}{q})=q
. We call a function
\psi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
a
Dirichlet function
if for every
x
, there exists a constant
C_{x}
such that
\left|x-\frac{p}{q}\right|<C_{x}\psi\left(H\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\right)
. In this language, the corollary to Dirichlet’s seminal theorem below can be restated as saying that the function
\psi(t)=\frac{1}{t^{2}}
is a Dirichlet function for irrationals in
\mathbb{R}
(and that the constant
C_{x}
can be taken to be 1 for every
x
).

More generally, one can consider the problem of “simultaneous approximation,” which seeks to approximate a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by rationals expressed in the form
\frac{\mathbf{p}}{q}
, where
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\gcd(\mathbf{p},q)=1
. The height function in this case is generally taken to be
H\left(\mathbf{p}/q\right)=q
. It is common, in this setting, to “clear denominators” and consider the expression
\left\|q\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{p}\right\|
instead. This has led to consideration of the more general notion of
Diophantine approximation on matrices
, where an
m\times n
matrix
A
is evaluated in terms of the quality of “integer approximations”
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, where the quality is measured in terms of the tradeoff between the quality of approximation
\|A\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}\|
and the height
\|\mathbf{q}\|
. The first result along these lines is Dirichlet’s theorem in Diophantine approximation:

Theorem 1.1

(Dirichlet 1842)
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix and fix
Q\geq 1
. Then there exist
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that

\|A\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}\|<Q^{-n/m},\;\;\;\;\|\mathbf{q}\|\leq Q.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix. Then there exist infinitely many pairs
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}
such that

\|A\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}\|<\|\mathbf{q}\|^{-n/m}.

Beyond Dirichlet’s theorem, we can ask whether the quality of approximation is improved by some factor of the form
\|\mathbf{q}\|^{\varepsilon}
with
\varepsilon>0
:

Definition 1.3
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix. The
Diophantine exponent
of
A
is

\omega(A):=\limsup_{\|\mathbf{q}\|\to\infty}\frac{-\log\min_{\mathbf{p}}\|A\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}\|}{\log\|\mathbf{q}\|}

Equivalently,
\omega(A)
is the supremum of
\omega
such that there are infinitely many
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}
such that

\|A\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}\|\leq\|\mathbf{q}\|^{-\omega}.

The corollary to Dirichlet’s theorem implies that
\omega(A)\geq n/m
for all
m\times n
matrices
A
. Such a matrix is called
very well approximable
if
\omega(A)>n/m
. A matrix is called
badly approximable
if there exists a constant
C
such that

\|A\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}\|\geq C\|\mathbf{q}\|^{-n/m}

for every
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}
with
\mathbf{q}\neq\textbf{0}
.

It is worth mentioning that these results about matrices don’t fit nicely in the language of a Diophantine tuple introduced above. We have decided to frame the introduction this way, however, because it is the most natural way to understand the goal of this paper, which is to describe the approximation of arbitrary points on an abelian variety by rational points, with respect to a Riemannian metric and a natural height function, discussed next.

1.2.
Background on Abelian Varieties

As mentioned in the abstract, the goal of this paper is to study Diophantine approximation in the context of abelian varieties, and in line with the previous section, we need to specify the tuple in which we’re working. Note that our description of abelian varieties is in line with our needs to describe the set as a metric space (in particular a Riemannian manifold), and hence differs in language from other popular approaches, but the description is equivalent when focusing on the real or complex locus of the variety.

An abelian variety is a projective variety
\mathcal{A}
together with a group law
+:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
which is a homomorphism. The operator
+
is always abelian
[
11
, p.2, p.44]
and the pair
(\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}),+)
is isomorphic as a complex Lie group to
(\mathbb{C}^{g}/\Lambda,+)
for some
g\in\mathbb{N}
and some lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{g}

[
11
, p.2, p.44]
.

Now let
K
denote a number field with a fixed real embedding, let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian variety defined over
K
, and let
\mathcal{A}(K)
denote the set of points in
\mathcal{A}
whose coordinates are in
K
. We have an inclusion

\mathcal{A}(K)\subseteq\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}).

The real locus
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
is virtually a torus of the same dimension
g
(when viewed as a real manifold), and carries a Riemannian metric, which we denote by
\operatorname{dist}
. We now have three of the four pieces of our set-up: we will study the density of the set
\mathcal{A}(K)
in its closure inside
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
, with regards to the Riemannian metric. What remains is to describe the height in this situation.

The construction of a height function in this setting goes back to Néron and Tate, independently, and a succinct introduction can be found in
[
7
, III. Section 1]
. While the full construction is well beyond the scope of this paper, the upshot is that if we choose a symmetric, invertible, ample line bundle over
\mathcal{A}
(see
[
10
, Cor. 7.2]
for a proof that such a choice of line bundle is always possible), then the corresponding height function
\hat{h}
has zeros exactly consisting of the
K
-rational torsion points, and in fact defines a positive definite quadratic form on the finite-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{R}\otimes\mathcal{A}(K)

[
7
, III, Section 1, Theorem 1.3]
. We view the
K
-rational points modulo torsion as a lattice in
\mathbb{R}\otimes\mathcal{A}(K)
.

For a more concrete discussion, fix an embedding into some projective space
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, and define a preliminary height (note this is not the final form of the height function we will use in what follows) of any point
P=[x_{0},x_{1},...,x_{N}],x_{i}\in K
, to be

H(P)=\left(\prod_{\nu\in M_{K}}\max\{|x_{0}|_{\nu},...,|x_{N}|_{\nu}\}^{[K_{\nu}:\mathbb{Q}_{\nu}]}\right)^{1/[K:\mathbb{Q}]}.

Here
M_{K}
denotes the set of standard absolute values on
K
, namely those absolute values on
K
whose restriction to
\mathbb{Q}
yields one of the usual absolute values (either the archimedean absolute value
|\cdot|_{\infty}
or one of the
p
-adic absolute values), and
[K:\mathbb{Q}]
denotes the degree of the field extension.

The
canonical height
of
P\in\mathcal{A}(K)
is defined to be

\hat{h}(P)=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\log(H(nP)).

See
[
15
, Chapter VIII, Section 9]
for details.

As before, the goal is to
quantify
the density of
\mathcal{A}(K)
inside the closure, and it seems reasonable that “how dense” this set is should depend in some sense on how many
K
-rational points there are.

The celebrated Mordell–Weil theorem states that the set
\mathcal{A}(K)
is a finitely-generated abelian group, and therefore, by the Fundamental Theorem of Finitely-Generated Abelian Groups, it can be written as

\mathbb{Z}^{r}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\text{tors}},

where
r
is called the
rank
of the group, and
\mathbb{Z}_{\text{tors}}
is the finite subgroup consisting of the torsion points. It is natural to conjecture that the optimal Dirichlet function for this setting should depend in some way on the rank of the abelian variety.

In
[
16
]
, M. Waldschmidt makes the following conjecture. Let
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
denote the identity component of
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
. For a real number
h
, let

\hat{\eta}_{\mathcal{A}}(h)=\inf\left\{\delta:\text{ for any }\zeta\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0},\text{ there exists }\gamma\in\mathcal{A}(K)\text{ with }\hat{h}(\gamma)\leq h\text{ and }\operatorname{dist}(\zeta,\gamma)<\delta\right\}.

Conjecture 1.4

(
[
16
, Conjecture 1.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a simple abelian variety of dimension
g
over a number field
K
embedded in
\mathbb{R}
. Denote by
r
the rank of the Mordell–Weil group
\mathcal{A}(K)
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
h_{0}>0
(which depends only on the abelian variety
\mathcal{A}
, the real number field
K
, and
\varepsilon
) such that, for any
h\geq h_{0}
,

\hat{\eta}_{\mathcal{A}}(h)\leq h^{-(r/2g)+\varepsilon}.

In line with the distinction between Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
, we posit a related conjecture.
Given
\kappa>0
, we say that a point
P\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
is
\kappa
-approximable if there exists a constant
C
and infinitely-many
Q\in\mathcal{A}(K)
such that

\operatorname{dist}(P,Q)\leq C\left(\hat{h}(Q)\right)^{-\kappa}.

Analogously to the case of matrices discussed above, we define the Diophantine exponent of
P\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
to be

\sigma(P):=\limsup_{\hat{h}(Q)\to\infty}\frac{-\log\operatorname{dist}(P,Q)}{\log\hat{h}(Q)}\geq\kappa,

and we define the
Diophantine exponent
of the abelian variety to be

\sigma_{\mathcal{A},K}:=\inf\{\sigma(P):P\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}\setminus\mathcal{A}(K)\}.

We can now state the main result of this paper, and a related conjecture.

Theorem 1.5
.

Waldschmidt’s conjecture
1.4
is equivalent to the statement that a certain matrix (determined by the abelain variety
\mathcal{A}
and the number field
K
, and defined below in Section
2.2
), is not very well approximable in the sense of Definition
1.3
.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a simple abelian variety of dimension
g
,
K
a number field with a fixed real embedding, and let
r
denote the rank of the Mordell–Weil group
\mathcal{A}(K)
. Then

\sigma_{\mathcal{A},K}=\frac{r}{2g}.

A few comments about these conjectures are in order. Firstly, the reason for the restriction to the set
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
is a necessary one, as it may be that even if
r\geq 1
, the closure of
\mathcal{A}(K)
may not coincide with
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
. For example, consider the elliptic curve
y^{2}=x^{3}-12x-1
, with
LMFDB label 110160.cd1
, considered as a curve defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. This curve is rank 1 and torsion-free (i.e.
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}
), with a possible generator of the Mordell–Weil group given by (5,8). Because the discriminant of this curve is positive, the real locus of the curve has two connected components, and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
contains no rational points. Therefore, in this case,

\overline{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{Q})}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}\subsetneqq\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})

The first equality in this case follows directly from our Theorem
3.1
below.

Secondly, it’s not known whether the closure of
\mathcal{A}(K)
always includes
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
. This result is known when the inequality
r\geq g^{2}-g+1
is satisfied (
[
16
]
), which includes all eliptic curves of rank at least 1 (since
g=1
in this case). Both conjectures
1.4
and
1.6
would imply that
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{A}(K)}
. One could alternatively define
\sigma_{\mathcal{A},K}
to only consider those points
P\in\overline{\mathcal{A}(K)}
, but we have opted for our formulation because of the relationship with conjecture
1.4
, and to establish a nontrivial upper bound in Lemma
2.3
below. In the event that
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}\not\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{A}(K)}
, we simply have
\sigma_{\mathcal{A},K}=0
by definition.

The notion of equivalence in the setting of abelian varieties defined over
K
is that of an
isogeny
, which is a surjective map with finite kernel, defined over the number field
K
. Morever, every isogeny
\mathcal{A}_{1}\to\mathcal{A}_{2}
comes paired with a dual isogeny in the reverse direction,
\mathcal{A}_{2}\to\mathcal{A}_{1}
(c.f.
[
11
, Ch. 2, Section 8, p. 80]
). While the isogeny and its dual have some deeper relations, for our purposes it is enough to know simply that the relation of having an isogeny between varieties is symmetric.

Lemma 1.7
.

The Diophantine exponent is an isogeny invariant.

Proof.

Fix an ample divisor
D
on
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, and consider its associated Néron–Tate height
\hat{h}_{2,D}
. Let
\phi\in\text{Mor}_{K}(\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{R}),\mathcal{A}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. Define
\hat{h}_{1,\phi^{*}(D)}
to be the Néron–Tate height associated with the pullback
\phi^{*}(D)
of
D
to
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. Note that the pullback of an ample line bundle by an isogeny is ample (
[
8
, Theorem 1.2.13]
). Let
Q\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(K)
with
\hat{h}_{1,\phi^{*}(D)}(Q)>0
and such that
d_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}(P,Q)<C\left(\hat{h}_{1,\phi^{*}(D)}(Q)\right)^{-\kappa}
. There is a constant
c_{1}>0
, dependent only on
\text{deg}(\phi)
and
D
, such that
\hat{h}_{2,D}(\phi(Q))\leq\text{deg}(\phi)\hat{h}_{1,\phi^{*}(D)}(Q)+c_{1}

[
15
, Chapter VIII, Theorem 5.6]
. As
\phi
lifts to an invertible linear map
d\phi
between the tangent spaces
T_{0}\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
T_{0}\mathcal{A}_{2}

[
1
, Chapter 8, Section 2, Subsection 27]
, it only distorts distances by a constant,
|d\phi|
.

\displaystyle\text{d}_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}(\phi(P),\phi(Q))

\displaystyle\leq|d\phi|\text{d}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}(P,Q)

\displaystyle<|d\phi|C(\hat{h}_{1,\phi^{*}(D)}(Q))^{-\kappa}

\displaystyle\leq|d\phi|C\deg(\phi)^{\kappa}(\hat{h}_{2,D}(\phi(Q))-c_{1})^{-\kappa}.

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}(\hat{h}_{2,D}(\phi(Q)))^{-\kappa}

where
\tilde{C}>0
exists and is finite due to the fact that the kernel of
\phi
is finite and there is an infinite set of
Q
with
\hat{h}_{1}(Q)>0
implied by
\kappa
-approximablility on
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and because there is only a finite number of rational points with height below any given threshold
[
15
, Theorem 5.11]
. This shows that

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2},K}\leq\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1},K},

and applying the argument again with the dual isogeny and the roles of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
reversed establishes the opposite inequality.
∎

2.
Main Theorem

2.1.
A problem in inhomohegenous approximation

We can give some insight on Waldschmidt’s conjecture using inhomogeneous Diophantine approximation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recall the definitions of badly approximable and very well approximable matrices given in Definition
1.3
above. Eventually, we will show that Waldschmidt’s conjecture for a pair
(\mathcal{A},K)
is equivalent to a certain matrix being not very well approximable. We first mention an equivalent formulation of a stronger condition on the matrix, but we omit the proof because it’s essentially the same as that of Theorem
2.3
below.

Recently, a paper by paper by Moshchevitin and Neckrasov
[
12
]
established results about inhomogeneous approximations which are more general than what we need here, but for the ease of the reader we provide independent proofs of these statements. The methods we use are different than those employed in
[
12
]
, and the language they employ is much more general. We give the way to recover our Theorems
2.1
and
2.3
from their work below.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{m\times n}
. Then
A
is badly approximable if and only if there exists a constant
C
such that for all sufficiently large
Q
and all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, there exist
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
0<\|\mathbf{q}\|\leq Q
satisfying

Q^{\frac{n}{m}}\|A\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|\leq C.

Before proving Theorem
2.3
, we recall some standard notation for viewing Diophantine approximation via homogeneous dynamics:
Consider the unimodular lattice

\Lambda_{A}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&A\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}

and the flow on the space of lattices given by

g_{t}:=\begin{pmatrix}e^{t/m}I_{m}&0\\
0&e^{-t/n}I_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix that is
\psi
-approximable
1
1

1

See the in-line definition at the start of Section
1.1
and
r
be the function corresponding to
\psi
in
[
6
, Theorem 8.3]
.
There is an unbounded sequence of times
t_{k}
at which
t\mapsto e^{r(t)}\|g_{t}\Lambda_{A}\|
has a local minimum smaller than 1.
Furthermore, the set
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is exactly the set of local minima of
t\mapsto\|g_{t}\Lambda_{A}\|
.

Proof.

Consider the function
\Delta(t):=-\log(\min_{\mathbf{x}\in g_{t}\Lambda_{A}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\|\mathbf{x}\|)
.
For any particular
\mathbf{x}\in\Lambda_{A}
, the function
t\mapsto\|g_{t}\mathbf{x}\|
has an interval on which it is proportional to
e^{\frac{t}{m}}
and an interval on which it is proportional to
e^{-\frac{t}{n}}
, and the intersection point
t(\mathbf{x})
of these intervals is a unique minimum for
t\mapsto\|g_{t}\mathbf{x}\|
.
If
\mathbf{x}\in\Lambda_{A}
is fixed, then the set
\{t\geq 0:\|g_{t}\mathbf{x}\|=e^{-\Delta(t)}\}
is an interval containing
t(\mathbf{x})
. This is due to the fact that all vectors with lengths that are contracting have lengths proportional to
e^{-\frac{t}{n}}
, and all vectors with lengths that are expanding have lengths proportional to
e^{\frac{t}{m}}
. Since
g_{t}\Lambda_{A}
is a lattice, the set of lengths of its vectors is discrete.
Therefore,
\Delta
is piecewise linear, with regions of slope
-\frac{1}{m}
alternating with regions of slope
\frac{1}{n}
.
By
[
6
, Theorem 8.5]
, there are arbitrarily large positive times
t_{\ell}
at which
\Delta(t_{\ell})\geq r(t_{\ell})
.
Given one of these times,
t_{\ell}
, if it lies in an interval where
\Delta(t)=\Delta_{0}-\frac{t}{m}
, one may replace
t_{\ell}
with the time of the previous maximum, say
t_{k}
.
This is because

m\Delta(t_{k})+t_{k}=m\Delta_{0}=m\Delta(t_{\ell})+t_{\ell}\geq mr(t_{\ell})+t_{\ell}\geq mr(t_{k})+t_{k}

(for the last inequality see
[
6
, Eq. (8.2b)]
).
Similarly, if
t_{\ell}
lies in a region where
\Delta(t)=\Delta_{0}+\frac{t}{n}
, one may replace
t_{\ell}
with the time of the next maximum, say
t_{k+1}
, since

n\Delta(t_{k+1})-t_{k+1}=n\Delta_{0}=n\Delta(t_{\ell})-t_{\ell}\geq nr(t_{\ell})-t_{\ell}\geq nr(t_{k+1})-t_{k+1}

(here the last inequality follows from
[
6
, Eq. (8.2a)]
).
So, the times at which
\Delta
has a maximum are also where
r-\Delta
has a minimum.
Exponentiating, one has that
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an unbounded sequence of local minima of
t\mapsto e^{r(t)}\|g_{t}\Lambda_{A}\|
.
∎

In the proof of the theorem below, we will need to prove that a certain set is nonempty. We will do this by establishing something much stronger, namely that is a winning set for a modification of a game introduced by Schmidt in
[
14
]
. This modification, known as the
hyperplane absolute game

[
2
]
, is played as follows (see the discussion in
[
2
]
for a nice overview of the development of various modifications of Schmidt’s original game). Fix a parameter
0<\beta<\frac{1}{3}
. Bob, the first player, chooses a point
\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a radius
\rho_{1}
, thus defining a closed ball
B(\mathbf{x}_{1},\rho_{1})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Alice, the second player, then chooses an
(m-1)
-dimensional affine hyperplane
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and deletes an
\varepsilon_{1}\rho_{1}
-neighborhood of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
from Bob’s ball
B(\mathbf{x}_{1},\rho_{1})
, where
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\beta
. For convenience, we denote the
\varepsilon_{1}\rho_{1}
-neighborhood of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
by
A_{1}
. Bob then picks a point
\mathbf{x}_{2}
and a radius
\rho_{2}\geq\beta\rho_{1}
satisfying

B(\mathbf{x}_{2},\rho_{2})\subseteq B(\mathbf{x}_{1},\rho_{1})\setminus A_{1}.

Alice then chooses a new affine subspace
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, and the game proceeds as before. A set
\mathcal{S}
is said to be
\beta
-
hyperplane absolute winning
, written HAW, if Alice has a strategy such that, no matter how Bob plays, she can guarantee that the intersection point
\mathbf{x}_{\infty}
defined by

\{\mathbf{x}_{\infty}\}:=\bigcap_{i}B(\mathbf{x}_{i},\rho_{i})\textup{ satisfies }\mathbf{x}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{S}.

We need two important facts about hyperplane absolute winning sets, both proven in
[
2
, Prop. 2.3]
: (1) HAW sets have full Hausdorff dimension; and (2) the countable intersection of HAW sets is again HAW.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})
. Then
A
is not very well approximable if and only if for all
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
c>0
, such that for all sufficiently large
Q
and all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, there exist
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
\|\mathbf{q}\|<Q
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
satisfying

(2.1)

\|A\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|<\frac{c}{Q^{\frac{n}{m}-\varepsilon}}.

Proof.

Since
A
is not very well approximable, neither is its transpose
A^{T}
.
As before, we define
A^{T}
to be
\nu
-VWA
if the Diophantine exponent
\sigma\left(A^{T}\right)
satisfies
\sigma\left(A^{T}\right)>\nu>\frac{m}{n}
(this is the same as in Definition
1.3
, but the dimensions are reversed).
Fix
\sigma>\frac{m}{n}
. As
A^{T}
is not
\sigma-
VWA,
it is in particular not
\sigma
-approximable, and therefore by
[
6
, Theorem 8.3]
we have
\Delta((g_{t}\Lambda_{A})^{*})<r(t)
for all
t
sufficiently large, where
r(t)=\theta_{\sigma}t:=\frac{n\sigma-m}{(\sigma+1)mn}\ t
and
\Lambda^{*}=\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\;\forall\mathbf{r}\in\Lambda\}
is the polar body of
\Lambda
. By the definition of
\Delta
given in Lemma
2.2
above, this means that
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in(g_{t}\Lambda_{A})^{*}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}(e^{r(t)}\|\mathbf{x}\|)>1
, i.e. that
\lambda_{1}((g_{t}\Lambda_{A})^{*})>e^{-r(t)}
, where
\lambda_{i}(\Lambda)
denotes the
i
th Minkowski minimum of the lattice
\Lambda
.
By a theorem of Mahler
[
9
, end of section 3]

\lambda_{m+n}(g_{t}\Lambda_{A})\asymp\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}((g_{t}\Lambda_{A})^{*})}
.
This gives us an upper bound:
\lambda_{m+n}(g_{t}\Lambda_{A})\asymp\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}((g_{t}\Lambda_{A})^{*})}<e^{r(t)}=e^{\theta_{\sigma}t}
, and thus on the codiameter of
g_{t}\Lambda_{A}
(as the codiameter is
\asymp
to
\lambda_{m+n}
). That is, there is a uniform constant
c_{2}>0
such that the codiameter is no larger than
c_{2}e^{\theta_{\sigma}t}
. Then there exists
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}
such that

\|g_{t}(A\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})-g_{t}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{0})\|<c_{2}e^{\theta_{\sigma}t}.

Fix
Q
large enough, and set

t=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n}+\theta_{\sigma}}\log\left(\frac{Q}{c_{2}}\right).

Then one has that

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{t}{n}}\|\mathbf{q}\|

\displaystyle<c_{2}e^{\theta_{\sigma}t}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{q}\|

\displaystyle<c_{2}e^{\left[\theta_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{n}\right]t}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{q}\|

\displaystyle<Q.

Similarly,

\displaystyle e^{\frac{t}{m}}\|A\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|

\displaystyle<c_{2}e^{\theta_{\sigma}t}

\displaystyle\|A\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|

\displaystyle<c_{2}e^{\left[\theta_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{m}\right]t}

\displaystyle\|A\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|

\displaystyle<cQ^{\left[\theta_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{m}\right]/\left[\frac{1}{n}+\theta_{\sigma}\right]}

\displaystyle\|A\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|

\displaystyle<\frac{c}{Q^{\frac{n}{m}-\varepsilon}},

where
\varepsilon=\frac{n}{m}-\frac{1}{\sigma}
and
c=c_{2}^{1+\frac{n}{m}-\varepsilon}
.

We will prove the reverse direction by contraposition. Assume that
A^{T}
is
\sigma
-VWA for some
\sigma>\frac{m}{n}
. Define
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{n}+\sigma)
,
\varepsilon(\mu)=\frac{n}{m}-\frac{1}{\mu}
,
\theta_{\sigma}=\frac{n\sigma-m}{nm(\sigma+1)}
, and
\theta_{\mu}=\frac{n\mu-m}{nm(\mu+1)}
. Note that
\frac{m}{n}<\mu<\sigma
,
\varepsilon>0
, and
0<\theta_{\mu}<\theta_{\sigma}
.
Consider the set

D_{c,\mu}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\forall Q_{0}>0\;\exists Q>Q_{0}\;\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\;\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\,\|A\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|>\frac{c}{Q^{\frac{n}{m}-\varepsilon(\mu)}}\right\}.

We will show that this set is hyperplane absolute winning.

To show
D_{c,\mu}
is hyperplane absolute winning, we exhibit a winning strategy for Alice (player II) in the hyperplane absolute game on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
D_{c,\mu}
as her target set.
Alice begins by finding the sequence of times
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
at which
\|(g_{t}\Lambda_{A})^{*}\|
and
e^{\theta_{\sigma}t}\|(g_{t}\Lambda_{A})^{*}\|
are simultaneously locally minimized, which is possible by Lemma
2.2
. Passing to a subsequence if necessary, Alice ensures that
\left(\theta_{\mu}-\frac{1}{m}\right)(t_{k+1}-t_{k})=-\frac{n+m}{nm(\mu+1)}\left(t_{k+1}-t_{k}\right)<2\log(\beta)
.
For each
k
, Alice chooses a vector in
(g_{t_{k}}\Lambda_{A})^{*}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
of minimal length, say

\mathbf{r}_{k}=(\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k})
. Since
t_{k}
is a local minimum of
t\mapsto e^{r(t)}\|g_{t}\Lambda_{A}\|
, we have
\|\mathbf{r}_{k}\|=\|\mathbf{a}_{k}\|=\|\mathbf{b}_{k}\|<e^{-\theta_{\sigma}t_{k}}
.

Recall that
(g_{t_{k}}\Lambda_{A})^{*}=((g_{t_{k}}u_{A})^{T})^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}
. Thus for all
(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d})\in g_{t_{k}}\Lambda_{A}
we have
\mathbf{a}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{b}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Now for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
let
H_{k,i}^{\prime}=\{(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}:\mathbf{a}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{b}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{d}=i\}
and
H_{k,i}=\{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{0})\in H_{k,i}^{\prime}\}
. Note that the distance between
H_{k,i}
and
H_{k,i+1}
is
1/\|\mathbf{a}_{k}\|>e^{\theta_{\sigma}t_{k}}>e^{\theta_{\mu}t_{k}}

Let
\theta_{\mu}=\frac{n\mu-m}{mn(\mu+1)}=\frac{\varepsilon}{n(1+(n/m)-\varepsilon)}>0
. When Bob’s ball has radius between
2c\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{m}-\theta_{\mu}\right)t_{k}\right)\beta^{-1}
and
2c\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{m}-\theta_{\mu}\right)t_{k}\right)\beta^{-2}
,
Alice deletes a hyperplane neighborhood of radius
2c\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{m}-\theta_{\mu}\right)t_{k}\right)
around
e^{-\frac{t_{k}}{m}}H_{k,i}
, where
i
is chosen so that
H_{k,i}
is the closest hyperplane to the center of Bob’s ball (if there are two such hyperplanes, she chooses the smaller index).
If Bob’s ball has radius larger than the prescribed interval, Alice finds the smallest
k^{\prime}\geq k
such that the hyperplane neighborhood of radius
2c\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{m}-\theta_{\mu}\right)t_{k}\right)
around a multiple of
e^{-\frac{t_{k}}{m}}H_{k,i}
includes the center of Bob’s ball and deletes this hyperplane neighborhood (if no such hyperplane neighborhood exists, she chooses a hyperplane neighborhood that does not intersect Bob’s ball).
This concludes Alice’s strategy.
Since
\exp(\theta_{\mu}t_{k})<\exp\left(\theta_{\sigma}t_{k}\right)<\text{dist}(H_{k,i},H_{k,i+1})
the hyperplane neighborhoods Alice wishes to delete shrink faster than the distance between them.
The inequality
-\frac{n+m}{nm(\mu+1)}\left(t_{k+1}-t_{k}\right)<2\log(\beta)
ensures that Bob’s ball will eventually be smaller than the distance between the hyperplane neighborhoods Alice considers for deletion at stage
k
(and that it need not shrink too quickly).
So, eventually, there will be at most one hyperplane neighborhood that Alice wishes to delete that intersects Bob’s ball.
It remains to show that Alice’s strategy is a winning strategy.
Suppose
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\notin D_{c,\mu}
.
At some sufficiently large stage
k
, one has that for
\mu>\frac{m}{n}
there are
\mathbf{q}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
\mathbf{p}_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
with
\|\mathbf{q}_{k}\|<Q_{k}=ce^{(\theta_{\mu}+\frac{1}{n})t_{k}}
such that
\|A\mathbf{q}_{k}-\mathbf{p}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|<cQ_{k}^{-(\frac{n}{m}-\varepsilon(\mu))}
. Consider the point
\mathbf{y}_{k}:=g_{t_{k}}(\mathbf{z}_{k},0)^{T}=(e^{\frac{t_{k}}{m}}\mathbf{z}_{k},0)^{T}
, and its distance to the lattice
\Lambda_{k}:=g_{t_{k}}\Lambda_{A}
.

\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{y}_{k},\Lambda_{k})\leq\max\{e^{\frac{t_{k}}{m}}\|A\mathbf{q}_{k}-\mathbf{p}_{k}-(\mathbf{z}_{k},\mathbf{0})\|,e^{-\frac{t_{k}}{n}}\|\mathbf{q}_{k}\|\}<c\exp(\theta_{\mu}t_{k}).

Therefore

\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{y}_{k},H_{k,i}^{\prime})<c\exp(\theta_{\mu}t_{k})\text{ for some $i\in\mathbb{Z}$.}

Say for example
\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{y}_{k},(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d}))<c\exp(\theta_{\mu}t_{k})
for some
(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d})\in H_{k,i}^{\prime}
. Then
\mathbf{a}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{b}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{d}=i
, so
\mathbf{a}_{k}\cdot(\mathbf{c}+\frac{\mathbf{b}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{k}\|^{2}}\mathbf{a}_{k})=i
, i.e.,
\mathbf{c}+\frac{\mathbf{b}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{k}\|^{2}}\mathbf{a}_{k}\in H_{k,i}
. Since
\|\mathbf{a}_{k}\|=\|\mathbf{b}_{k}\|
, we have
\|\frac{\mathbf{b}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{a}_{k}\|^{2}}\mathbf{a}_{k}\|\leq\|\mathbf{d}\|<c\exp(\theta_{\mu}t_{k})
. So
\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{c},H_{k,i})<c\exp(\theta_{\mu}t_{k})
and thus

\operatorname{dist}(e^{t_{k}/m}\mathbf{z}_{k},H_{k,i})<2c\exp(\theta_{\mu}t_{k}).

Therefore, by the above equation, the distance between
\mathbf{z}_{k}
and
e^{-t_{k}/m}H_{k,i}
is less than
2c\exp\left(\left(\theta_{\mu}-\frac{1}{m}\right)t_{k}\right)
, in which case Alice would have deleted the hyperplane-neighborhood
N\left(e^{-t_{k}/m}H_{k,i},2c\exp\left(\left(\theta_{\mu}-\frac{1}{m}\right)t_{k}\right)\right)
containing
\mathbf{z}_{k}
, a contradiction.
Thus, the intersection of Bob’s balls must lie in
D_{c,\mu}
, and Alice’s strategy is a winning strategy. So
D_{c,\mu}
is HAW.
Since we can write

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\forall\varepsilon>0\;\forall C>0\;\forall Q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\;\exists Q>Q_{0}\;\exists\mathbf{q},m\in\mathbb{Z},|n|<Q,|A\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{z}|>\frac{C}{Q^{\frac{n}{m}-\varepsilon}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{c\in\mathbb{Q}}\bigcap_{\mu_{j}\searrow\frac{m}{n}}D_{c,\mu},

where we choose a countable sequence
\mu_{j}
which tends to
\frac{m}{n}
, we see that
D

is also HAW, and so nonempty, as was to be shown.
∎

.

As stated, one can recover both Theorems
2.1
and
2.3
from

]
. In the case of Theorem
2.3
, applying their Theorem 1A with
f(T)=\frac{1}{T^{1+\varepsilon}}
, establishes that if a matrix is not VWA, then the set of
\gamma
which satisfy Equation (
2.1
) is all of
\mathbb{R}^{m}

f

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

Both of the above theorems are strictly about properties of a matrix and solutions to some inhomogeneous approximation problem in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We now need to connect this back to a statement about approximation on the abelian variety itself. This section is largely taken from
[
16
]
, but we reproduce the pertinent facts here for ease of the reader.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian variety defined over a number field
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and let
T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbb{R})
denote the tangent space at the identity. In this case,
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
is a real lie group, and we let

\exp_{\mathcal{A}}:T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})

denote the usual exponential map. The image of this map is precisely
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
, and the kernel
\Omega_{\mathcal{A},\mathbb{R}}
is a discrete subgroup of
T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbb{R})
of rank
g
over
\mathbb{Z}
. We fix a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{g}
of
\Omega_{\mathcal{A},\mathbb{R}}
.

For each
1\leq j\leq r
, let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
\exp_{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})=\beta_{j}
, where
\{\beta_{1},....,\beta_{r}\}
denote a (maximal)
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent set in
\mathcal{A}(K)\cap\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
.

The following result connects distances on the abelian variety to distances in
\mathbb{R}^{g}
:

Lemma 2.5

(cf.
[
16
, Lemma 5.1]
)
.

Let
\theta
be a positive number and let
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}\beta_{1}+\mathbb{Z}\beta_{2}+...+\mathbb{Z}\beta_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{A}(K)\cap\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
. The the following are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for any
Q\geq 1
and any
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
, there exists
\beta\in\Gamma
with
\hat{h}(\beta)<Q
and

\operatorname{dist}(\beta,\zeta)\leq CQ^{-\theta/2}.

(2)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for any positive integer
Q
and any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbb{R})
, there exist rational integers
q_{1},....,q_{r}
and
p_{1},...,p_{g}
satisfying

(2.2)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq r}|q_{j}|\leq Q,\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|q_{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}+...+q_{r}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}+p_{1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}+...+p_{g}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{g}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|\leq CQ^{-\theta}.

Waldschmidt’s conjecture
1.4
is that the statement (
1
) holds with
\theta=r/g-\varepsilon
for any simple abelian variety defined over a real number field
K
and any
\varepsilon>0
. We can rewrite the equation in (
2.2
) in the following manner. Let the matrix
H\in M_{g\times r}(\mathbb{R})
have columns given by
\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},....,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}\}
and the matrix
J\in M_{g\times g}(\mathbb{R})
have columns given by
\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},....,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{g}\}
. Then we can write (
2.2
) as:

\left\|H\textbf{q}+J\textbf{p}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|\leq CQ^{-r/g+\varepsilon},

where
q
is the column vector of the numbers
q_{1},...,q_{r}
and
p
is the column vector of the numbers
p_{1},...,p_{g}
. Multiplying the lefthand side by the inverse of the matrix
J
on the left, which will change the norm by only a bounded amount, we see that

\left\|J^{-1}H\textbf{q}+\textbf{p}+J^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|\leq CQ^{-r/g+\varepsilon}.

This is the same statement as in Theorem
2.3
with

m=g,\ n=r,\ A=J^{-1}H,\text{ and }\boldsymbol{\gamma}=J^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}.

Combining Lemma
2.5
with Theorem
2.3
shows that Waldschmidt’s conjecture is true of some simple abelian variety
\mathcal{A}
defined over
K
if and only it the matrix
J^{-1}H
as constructed above is not VWA.

This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any simple abelian variety
\mathcal{A}
of dimension
g
and any number field
K
as above, we have

\sigma_{\mathcal{A},K}\leq\frac{r}{2g},

where, as usual,
r
denotes the rank of the Mordell-Weil group
\mathcal{A}(K)
.

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
\sigma_{\mathcal{A},K}>\frac{r}{2g}
. Then there exists
\mu>\frac{r}{g}
such that for every
P\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})^{0}
, there exists a constant
C
and infinitely many
Q\in\mathcal{A}(K)
satisfying

\operatorname{dist}(P,Q)<C\left(\hat{h}(Q)\right)^{-\mu/2}.

As in the proof of Lemma
2.5
in
[
16
]
, and using the notation we introduced above, if we write
Q=q_{1}\beta_{1}+...+q_{r}\beta_{r}
, then we have that

\left\|q_{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}+...+q_{r}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}+p_{1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}+...+p_{g}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{g}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|\asymp\operatorname{dist}(P,Q)<C\left(\hat{h}(Q)\right)^{-\mu/2}

for some choice of rational integers
p_{1},....,p_{g}
and where
\exp_{\mathcal{A}}\boldsymbol{\xi}=P
. Rewriting this as before, this implies

\left\|J^{-1}H\textbf{q}+\textbf{p}+J^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|\leq C^{\prime}\left(\hat{h}(Q)\right)^{-\mu/2}.

Because the height function
\hat{h}
defines a positive definite quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}\otimes\mathcal{A}(K),
we have, up to constant, that

\hat{h}(Q)=\hat{h}\left(q_{1}\beta_{1}+...+q_{r}\beta_{r}\right)\asymp\max_{1\leq i\leq r}|q_{i}|^{2}.

Therefore, for some possibly new constant
C^{\prime}
, we have that

(2.3)

\left\|J^{-1}H\textbf{q}+\textbf{p}+J^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|\leq C^{\prime}\max_{1\leq i\leq r}|q_{i}|^{-\mu},

for all points
J^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{g}
. However, this is never the case:

Theorem

(
[
3
, Theorem 1]
)
.

For any matrix
A\in\mathcal{M}_{g\times r}(\mathbb{R}),
the Hausdorff dimension of the set

\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{g}:\ \exists c>0\text{ s.t. }\left\|A\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{x}\right\|\geq\frac{c}{\max_{i}|q_{i}|^{r/g}}\ \forall\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r},\ \forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}\right\}

equals
g
. In particular, this set is always nonempty.

Letting
A=J^{-1}H
and
\mathbf{x}=J^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}
as usual, and recalling that
\mu>\frac{r}{g}
, shows that this result is incompatible with equation (
2.3
) holding for all points
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{g}
. Therefore,
\sigma_{\mathcal{A},K}\leq\frac{r}{2g},
as needed.

∎

As a consequence of Lemma
2.6
, our conjecture
1.6
is weaker than Waldschmidt’s conjecture
1.4
, in that a proof of
1.4
would immediately imply that
1.6
is true as well.

3.
Rank 1 Elliptic Curves

In this section, we establish further results in the case of a rank 1 elliptic curve defined over
K
.

Let
d_{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote a Riemannian metric on
E(\mathbb{R})^{0}
and let
\hat{h}
be the Néron–Tate height defined on
E(K)
associated with
d_{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
(see, for instance,
[
15
, Chapter 8, Section 9]
).

For rank 1 elliptic curves defined over
K
, we can actually establish Conjecture
1.6
:

Theorem 3.1
.

If the rank of
E
over
K
is exactly 1, then we have equality:

\sigma_{E,K}=\frac{1}{2}.

Explicitly, there exists a constant
C
depending only on the curve
E
such that for every
P\in E(\mathbb{R})^{0}\setminus E(K)
, there exist infinitely many points
Q\in E(K)
satisfying

d_{E}(P,Q)<C\left(\hat{h}(Q)\right)^{-1/2}.

Moreover, the function
C\left(\hat{h}(Q)\right)^{-1/2}
cannot be improved (in a sense defined below) if the inequality in (
3.1
) is to remain true for all points
P\in E(\mathbb{R})^{0}\setminus E(K)
.

In fact, the proof shows that for any elliptic curve
E
defined over
K
with rank
at least
1, we have

\sigma_{E,K}\geq\frac{1}{2},

with equality in the case that the rank is exactly 1.

Before we prove Theorem
3.1
, we revisit the general discussion of abelian varieties from the introduction, but in this concrete setting.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve defined over
K
and let
\Lambda
be the associated complex lattice (see
[
15
, Chapter 6, Theorem 5.1]
). Let
\wp
denote the Weierstrass
P
-function associated to the lattice
\Lambda
, and let
\wp^{\prime}
be its derivative. It is well-known that
(see e.g.,
[
15
, Chapter 6, Theorem 3.6]
) that
the map

z\mapsto[\wp(z):\wp^{\prime}(z):1]

defines a complex analytic isomorphism of complex Lie groups between
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
and
E(\mathbb{C})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

As
E
is defined over
K
and hence over
\mathbb{R}
, this map restricts to the usual exponential map

\exp_{E}:\mathbb{R}\to E(\mathbb{R})\subseteq P^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\quad z\mapsto[\wp(z):\wp^{\prime}(z):1]

whose image is the connected component of the origin,
E(\mathbb{R})^{0}
, and which intertwines the group law of addition on
\mathbb{R}
with the group law on the set
E(\mathbb{R})
(since the curve is defined over
K
, the group law preserves real coefficients, hence restricts to a group law on
E(\mathbb{R})
). The kernel of this map is given by
\mathbb{Z}\omega
for some nonzero period
\omega
. This map distorts the Riemannian metric on
E
only by a bounded constant.

Let
P
be a point in
E(\mathbb{R})^{0}\setminus E(K)
and let
Q
be a generator of the free summand of the Mordell–Weil group
E(K)
(we need only one such point because we assume that
E
has rank 1). As in the discussion of Waldschmidt’s conjecture above, let
\theta
denote a preimage of
Q
under
\exp_{E}
, and
\gamma
a preimage of
P
.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have
\frac{\theta}{\omega}\notin\mathbb{Q}
and there do not exist integers
a,b
for which
a\frac{\theta}{\omega}+b=\frac{\gamma}{\omega}
.

Proof.

If it were that
\frac{\theta}{\omega}=\frac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
q\theta=p\omega
, and hence

[q]Q=[q]\exp(\theta)=\exp(q\theta)=\exp(p\omega)=[p]\exp(\omega)=[p]O=O,

contradicting that
Q
has infinite order.

Similarly, if there existed integers
a,b
for which
a\frac{\theta}{\omega}+b=\frac{\gamma}{\omega}
, then we would have that
a\theta+b\omega=\gamma
, and hence

P=\exp(\gamma)=\exp(a\theta+b\omega)=[a]\exp(\theta)+[b]\exp(\omega)=[a]Q+[b]O=[a]Q,

contradicting that
P\in E(\mathbb{R})\setminus E(K)
(actually, it is enough that
P
not belong to the orbit of
Q
; it could be a torsion rational point, for instance). So no such integers can exist.
∎

Because the exponential map is an isometry, we have reduced the problem of approximating
P
by the orbit
[q]Q
to one of approximating
\gamma=\varphi(P)
by points of the form
q\theta=\varphi([q]Q)
in
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\omega
. In other words we are interested in how small expressions of the form

|\gamma-q\theta+p\omega|

can be in terms of the size of
|q|
. Dividing this expression through by
|\omega|
, we see that this is precisely a problem in
inhomogeneous Diophantine approximation
on the real line. We have the following classical theorem of Minkowski:

Theorem

(Minkowski; see for instance
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\frac{\theta}{\omega}
be any irrational number and let
\frac{\gamma}{\omega}
be any number for which
\frac{\gamma}{\omega}=a\frac{\theta}{\omega}+b
has no solutions in integers
a,b
. Then there exist infinitely many pairs of integers
(q,p)
for which

|q|\left|q\frac{\theta}{\omega}+p-\frac{\gamma}{\omega}\right|<\frac{1}{4}.

Given the results of Lemma
3.2
, we see that for infinitely many
