Association between oral health and physical performance in Brazilian older adults: SABE cohort study
Authors: Fabiola Bof de Andrade, Luara Murta Torres, Yeda Aparecida de Oliveira Duarte, Jair Licio Ferreira Santos, Enrico Colosimo, Eduardo Bernabe, Wael Sabbah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral health is recognized as integral to general health and impaired dentition status may affect physical performance among older adults. This study evaluated the longitudinal association between clinical and self-reported oral health measures and physical performance (outcome) in Brazilian older adults.MethodsThis was a longitudinal study that used data from the second (year 2006), third (year 2010) and fourth (year 2015) waves of the Health Well-being and Aging Study conducted in Brazil. Physical performance, evaluated using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), was the dependent variable. Independent variables of interest were the number of teeth, presence of periodontal pocket, use of dental prostheses, and poor perceived oral health. The association between oral health measures and physical function was analyzed using generalized estimating equations with an ordinal regression model.ResultsIn the total sample, every additional tooth was associated with a greater chance of achieving a higher score on the SPPB test. Individuals wearing dental prostheses had higher chances of having higher scores than those not wearing them. In the analyses for the dentate sample, the presence of a periodontal pocket was not associated with SPPB and the increase in the number of teeth increased the chance of achieving a higher score.ConclusionA greater number of teeth, and using dentures, were associated with higher physical performance. Periodontal disease was not associated with the outcome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05250-1.

Background

Healthy aging is a multifaceted process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables well-being in older age. Maintaining physical performance is essential for preserving functional ability, and enabling healthy aging [1]. Physical performance is a multidimensional concept. It is an objective measure of whole-body function that measures muscle strength and body organs and systems (e.g. bones, balance and other neurological inputs, cardiovascular aspects) [2]. There is no consensus on the best tool of physical performance for use in clinical or population settings, but the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) is the most widely used and recommended [1–4]. In particular, it offers a comprehensive assessment through a series of tasks including balance tests, walking speed, and chair stands, providing a reliable measure of lower extremity function, besides predicting the risk of disability [5–7] and mortality [8]. As such, understanding the factors that contribute to the preservation of physical performance is essential for preventing its decline and enabling healthy aging.

Oral health is increasingly acknowledged as an integral component of general health [9], yet its potential influence on physical performance, particularly lower extremity function, remains underexplored [10]. Emerging evidence highlights a complex interaction between oral health and systemic health outcomes, with periodontal diseases and tooth loss being associated with chronic conditions [11] and nutritional deficiencies [12]. These factors are known to affect musculoskeletal function [13–15] and could potentially impact functional mobility and, consequently, intrinsic capacity.

Few longitudinal studies have directly examined the association between oral health status and the physical performance of lower extremity function and the findings are heterogeneous [16–18]. Some studies found that, among older adults, poor perceived oral health [16] and edentulism [18] at baseline were associated with poor SPPB at follow-up. On the other hand, analysis of two cohorts of older adults (70 + years) in the UK and USA identified that, of various oral health measures, only dry mouth at baseline was associated with an 8-year decline in chair stand speed in the USA sample, while tooth loss and difficulty eating were significant in the UK sample [17].

More studies from alternative settings, using multiple measures of oral health status repeated over time and controlling for common determinants of oral health and physical performance later in life might shed light on this research area. Therefore, this study evaluated the longitudinal association between clinical and self-reported oral health measures and physical performance (outcome) in Brazilian older adults.

Methods

Study population

This 9-year longitudinal study used panel data from the second (year 2006), third (year 2010) and fourth (year 2015) waves of the Health Well-being and Aging Study (SABE) conducted in Brazil. SABE is prospective study, started in 2000, designed to evaluate the health and ageing process in the Latin America and the Caribbean. The Brazilian sample was drawn from the City of São Paulo and was representative of community-dwelling older adults, aged 60 years or older, living in the urban region. In each subsequent wave, a new representative sample of older adults aged 60 to 64 years was included to ensure the continued representation of this age group within the study’s population over time. A comprehensive description of the study design and the sampling methodology, including sample size calculation, has been described elsewhere [19, 20].

For every wave of the SABE study, the Research Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health at the University of São Paulo granted ethical approval (reference numbers: 67/99 for the 2000 wave, 83/06 for the 2006 wave, 23/10 for the 2010 wave, and 3,600,782 for the 2015 wave), confirming compliance with ethical guidelines and procedures. Before initiating interviews in each phase, participants provided written informed consent.

This study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).

In the present study, only individuals with complete data for all variables of interest in each wave were analyzed. The flowchart (Supplementary Fig. 1) presents the study sample included in each wave.

Measures

Physical performance (main outcome) was evaluated using the SPPB in every wave [4]. The battery is split into three parts: balance, gait speed, and lower limb strength. Each test has its cut-offs defined by the instrument [21]. In the balance test, participants were instructed to stand with their eyes open and maintain three different positions for 10 s each: feet side by side, semi-tandem (semi-heel-to-toe), and tandem (heel-to-toe). A score of 1 was given if they could hold the side-by-side position for 10 s but not the semi-tandem position. A score of 2 was assigned if they sustained the semi-tandem position for 10 s but struggled to maintain the full tandem position for more than 2 s. Participants scoring 3 maintained the full tandem position for 3 to 9 s, while those scoring 4 held it for the full 10 s. For the gait speed test, participants walked a 3-meter course at their usual pace, and the shortest time of two walks was recorded. A score of 1 was given for times ≥ 6,52 s, 2 for times between 4.66 and 6,52 s, 3 for times between 3,62 and 4.65 s, and 4 for times ≤ 3.62 s. In the Chair Stand Test, participants crossed their arms over their chest and were instructed to stand up from a chair and sit down as quickly as possible for five repetitions. Scores were assigned based on execution times: 1 for > 16.7 s, 2 for 13.7 to 16.69 s, 3 for 11.2 to 13.69 s, and 4 for ≤ 11.19 s. A score of zero was assigned if the participant could not complete five chair stands or if the execution time exceeded 60 s [21]. Each test is scored from 0 to 4, leading to a total possible score of 12 for the entire battery, with higher values indicating better physical performance [21].

Oral health measures (independent variables) included the number of teeth, any periodontal pocketing ≥ 4 mm (no, yes), use of removable and/or fixed dental prostheses (no, yes) and poor perceived oral health (no, yes). Clinical oral health examinations were performed by trained dentists using standardized criteria according to the World Health Organization protocol [22]. Periodontal pocketing was evaluated using the Community Periodontal Index on ten index teeth [22]. Perceived oral health was measured through the 4 items on physical function (eating, chewing, swallowing and speaking due to problems with teeth or dentures) of the GOHAI [23]. Each question employed a 5-point Likert scale to assess the regularity with which participants experienced the above physical problems over the preceding year. Participants who reported experiencing any of these problems always or often were categorized as having poor perceived oral health.

Covariates included in the study were: sociodemographic variables (baseline age [age when the participant began participating in the study], sex [male, female], and schooling [years of education]); lifestyle factors (smoking habits [non-smoker, current smoker, ex-smoker], participation in physical activity [inactive, active], and alcohol use [no, yes/but not at risk of alcoholism, yes/at risk of alcoholism]); and general health indicators (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, cognitive performance, and Body Mass Index (BMI) [calculated as the individual’s weight in kilograms divided by their height in meters squared]). All the chronic diseases were self-reported. The assessment of physical activity was determined by whether individuals participated in a minimum of 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activities or 75 minutes of high-intensity activities per week [24], as measured by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire [25]. Cognitive abilities were evaluated using the Mini-Mental State Examination, adapted for and validated within the study context [26]. The evaluation of alcohol use employed the Short Michigan Alcoholism Screening Test to distinguish between consumption levels that posed a risk of alcoholism and those that did not. The score ranges from 0 to 10 and those scoring 2 or more points are classified as “at risk for alcoholism [27]. Chronic conditions reported by participants were confirmed through diagnoses previously made by medical professionals.

Data analysis

We first presented the distribution of the variables in each wave and then compared SPPB scores at every wave according to confounders and oral health measures. The Asymptotic Generalized Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test was used to test linear trends when the covariate was categorical with ordered groups. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for categorical covariates with unordered groups, and the Mann-Whitney test was used to evaluate differences between means when the covariate was binary. For continuous covariates, Kendall’s tau test was used to identify the correlation with the outcome. For comparisons between included and excluded participants in the analysis, the Chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables.

We estimated the association between oral health measures and physical function using generalized estimating equations (GEE), with an ordinal proportional odds regression model, that treated the SPPB score as an ordered variable ranging from 0 to 12. The models with periodontal pockets were fitted using data on dentate participants only. The estimates were presented as proportional odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). In the models, an OR grader than 1 for a categorical variable means that the individuals in the category had a higher chance of getting a higher score than the ones in the reference category [28]. GEE yield population-averaged effects of exposure on outcomes while accounting for repeated measurements for each participant [28]. We used a categorical indicator to represent time (coded as 0 for wave 1, 1 for wave 2 and 2 for wave 3). Adjusted models included controls for all confounders (sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors and general health indicators). The interaction between each oral health indicator and the time indicator was evaluated by adding their product to the confounder-adjusted model. All predictors were time-varying except age, sex and schooling. All the analyses were done using R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Data for the complete sample included 3254 observations from 1926 individuals. Of them, 342 had data on three waves, 644 on two waves, and 940 on one wave. Overall, the mean SPPB score was 8.29 (SD: 2.16) at wave 1, 8.79 (SD: 2.36) at wave 2 and 8.27 (SD: 2.14) at wave 3. Supplementary Table 1 shows the distribution of individuals included or not in each wave by oral health status.

The mean number of teeth was 6.53 (SD: 8.59), 7.96 (SD: 9.20) and 9.41 (SD: 9.68) at waves 1, 2 and 3, respectively. The proportion of participants with dental prosthesis was 82.1, 79.0 and 78.1% in each wave whereas the proportion of participants reporting poor perceived oral health was 33.0, 32.6 and 23.1% in waves 1, 2, and 3, respectively. In all waves, the mean SPPB score was lower for individuals with fewer teeth, those with periodontal pocket, and poor perceived oral health (Table 2). Discussion

In this analysis of longitudinal data, we examined the association between a number of oral health indicators and decline in physical performance over nine years among older Brazilian adults. Greater number of teeth and wearing dentures were significantly associated with maintaining physical performance. Poor perceived oral health and the presence of periodontal pockets were not associated with the SPPB score after adjusting for relevant variables.

This study has a few limitations worth mentioning. The use of observational data does not support conclusions about causality; however, the use of longitudinal data shows temporality. Self-reported data on behaviors, socioeconomic factors and perception of oral health function could be subjected to reporting bias. The analysis could have included a wider range of behaviors and early life factors. However, the study included sufficient confounders considering the scope and objectives of the paper. Additionally, the use of index teeth for the evaluation of periodontal disease can underestimate periodontal status, limiting the evaluation of the association [29]. On the other hand, the study strength is in using longitudinal data to demonstrate the impact of poor oral health on change in physical performance over nine years. The analysis also used several clinically assessed oral health indicators and accounted for their change over time in relation to decline in physical performance.

It is worth noting that in the three known longitudinal studies that assessed association between oral health and physical performance of lower extremity function [16–18], the use of repeated measures of oral health was only reported in one study [17]. The findings of this study, particularly those related to number of teeth, are consistent with the findings from earlier studies among British [17] and Italian [18] populations that demonstrated that having more teeth was associated with preserving physical performance of lower extremity function [17, 18]. However, unlike the current analysis, the results in these two populations accounted for the exposure (number of teeth) only at baseline.

Among the Italian older adults, aged 65 years or more living in the community and nursing homes in Northeastern Italy, participants with 20 + teeth at baseline were less likely to have limited lower extremity function (SPPB < 9) 7 years later (although not after 5 years of follow-up) than those with no teeth. The analysis of two cohorts of community-dwelling older adults (aged 70 years or older) in the UK and USA found that of all oral health measures evaluated (number of teeth, % sites with periodontal pocketing, % sites with loss of attachment, poor self-rated oral health, difficulty eating and dry mouth), only dry mouth measured at baseline was positively associated with the 8-year decline in chair stand speed [17]. However, this association was only found in the USA sample. Further analysis of the UK sample revealed that increasing tooth loss and difficulty eating, but not deteriorations in self-rated oral health and dry mouth, were associated with declines in chair stand speed. One longitudinal study of community-dwelling older adults, aged 65 years and older, from a rural area in South Korea, evaluated the association between self-reported oral health and physical performance measured by the SPPB [16]. The study found that poor perceived oral health at baseline, measured using the General Oral Health Assessment Index (GOHAI), was associated with incidence of poor physical performance, evaluated using the SPPB, after 2 years.

The impact of oral health, particularly the number of remaining teeth, and use of denture to restore functional dentition, on physical performance, could be attributed to a nutritional pathway. Having fewer teeth could affect individuals’ ability to chew and lead to inadequate intake of essential nutrients [12], which could affect physical performance. Inadequate nutritional intake associated with tooth loss is also linked to several chronic conditions that could in turn affect physical performance [30]. Interestingly, in the current analysis, use of denture was positively associated with maintaining physical performance highlighting the importance of maintaining a functional dentition to improve ability to eat. While the impact of use of denture on physical performance was not assessed in the earlier longitudinal studies, it was reported in a cross-sectional study where it showed an association with nutritional intake and with grip strength [31]. In line with these findings, among older Japanese adults, those dissatisfied with dentures and unable to chew hard food had higher frailty scores (worst score) at the four-year follow-up. Moreover, each additional tooth reported was associated with a 1.5% lower frailty score at the follow-up [32].

Aside from the nutritional pathway, poor oral health could impact physical performance through a psychological pathway. Having fewer teeth could lead to embarrassment, avoiding talking and eating with others [33]. On the long run, this could lead to social isolation which impacts health in general and physical performance in old age [34, 35]. It is worth noting that in the current analysis, a compound variable reflecting impact on speaking and eating was associated with poorer physical performance, although the association was fully attenuated after accounting for other oral health indicators.

Biomarkers linked to periodontal diseases is another potential pathway linking oral health to decline in physical performance among older adults [36]. While the association between periodontitis and physical performance was demonstrated in an earlier longitudinal study among older Americans [17], in the current analysis, periodontitis was not associated with physical performance. The impact of periodontitis through inflammatory pathways may require a longer follow-up period and the use of alternative measures that are not based on index teeth, as these may underestimate periodontal status [29].

The association between poor perceived oral health and decline in physical performance could also be a result of adverse socioeconomic factors and other common risk factors affecting both conditions. In the current study, the analysis accounted for education as an indicator of socioeconomic factors, but there are other adverse conditions in early life and adult life that could explain part of the observed association [37].

The findings of this study, particularly that the association between number of teeth and use of denture with decline in physical performance, support the theory of nutritional pathway between oral health systemic conditions [12]. These findings highlight the importance of integrating oral health promotion measures with interventions aiming at tackling decline of physical performance among older adults. Future research should use intervention study design to test the impact of improving oral health and restoring functional dentition on physical performance among older adults.

This study examined the association between change in a number of oral health indicators and change in physical performance among older adults over nine years, and demonstrated that greater number of teeth, and using dentures, were associated with higher physical performance. On the other hand, periodontal diseases were not associated with physical performance. The study highlights the importance of dentition as a potential risk factor for decline in physical performance among older adults.
