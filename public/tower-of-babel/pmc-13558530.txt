Physiological temperature enhances proliferative capacity and protein production characteristics of chick satellite cells
Authors: Dong Bin Kim, Hyo Jin Lee, Hye Won Lee, Ho Gun Jang, Sang-Hyon Oh, Jong Hyuk Kim, Seung Yun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCulture temperature is a critical factor influencing cellular metabolism and protein production. This study investigated the effects of culture temperature on the proliferation, differentiation, and nutritional profile of chick satellite cells. Cells isolated from chick embryos were cultured at 37 °C and 40 °C to evaluate their bioprocessing efficiency and protein characteristics. The results demonstrated that culturing cells at 40 °C significantly enhanced cell proliferation, as evidenced by increased cell numbers and a shortened population doubling time, without inducing chronic thermal toxicity. Regarding myogenic status, immunofluorescence analysis showed no significant differences in terminal differentiation markers between the two conditions. However, transcriptional analysis revealed that 40 °C upregulated the expression of myoblast determination protein and decreased the expression of paired box-7 compared to 37 °C. Furthermore, while the total protein content showed no significant differences between the two temperatures, the amino acid composition revealed qualitative distinctions: cells cultured at 37 °C exhibited higher levels of glycine and alanine, whereas those cultured at 40 °C showed higher levels of branched-chain amino acids, such as valine, isoleucine, and lysine. These findings suggest that 40 °C is an optimal culture temperature for the efficient expansion of chick satellite cells, providing a practical strategy for producing cell-derived protein materials.Keywords: Alternative protein, Cell-derived protein, Chick satellite cell, Culture temperature

Introduction

Proteins, generally sourced from animals and plants, are one of the three major macronutrients in food products, alongside lipids and carbohydrates (Maleky & Ahmadi, 2025). Protein deficiency is associated with severe health consequences, including stunted growth, anemia, and vascular and immune dysfunction (Wu, 2016). Consequently, an adequate supply of dietary protein, often obtained from meat, is fundamental for maintaining human health, particularly for preserving muscle mass and physical function in older adults (Coelho-Júnior et al., 2022).

However, global meat consumption is rapidly increasing owing to rising global income and population expansion (Godfray et al., 2018). Traditional agricultural production is nearing saturation, and the available land is insufficient to meet this accelerating food demand (Hemathilake & Gunathilake, 2022). Conventional livestock farming is also facing significant limitations in meeting the demand, and elevated meat consumption can potentially exacerbate global food security issues (Post, 2012). Therefore, it is necessary to explore alternatives beyond traditional livestock farming to ensure a stable protein supply.

In light of these challenges, the development and utilization of alternative proteins are crucial for establishing a sustainable food system that addresses both the increasing meat demand and environmental concerns. Research on alternative protein sources is actively progressing (Sha & Xiong, 2020), and the market is projected to grow to 97 million tons annually by 2035 (Li et al., 2024). Among various alternative proteins, those derived from animal satellite cells offer environmental advantages, including reduced land and water usage and low greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (Hadi & Brightwell, 2021). Moreover, animal cell-derived proteins facilitate protein production without conventional animal farming and slaughter. Furthermore, these proteins are a promising alternative because their flavor, taste, and nutritional profile are comparable to those of conventional meat (Zhang et al., 2022).

Despite the increasing interest, previous studies using satellite cells from livestock, such as chicken, cattle, and swine, have primarily focused on cell biology and the development of cultivated meat (Kim et al., 2025). Numerous studies have focused on developing scaffold technology and optimizing tissue engineering for three-dimensionally structured satellite cells to mimic the texture and structure of conventional meat (Bomkamp et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). While some recent studies have reported that physiological temperatures (approximately 41 °C) enhance the proliferation of avian satellite cells, these investigations have largely been confined to basic cellular kinetics and early myogenic expression rather than evaluating the nutritional quality of the end-product (Gregg et al., 2023; Harding et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2025). To advance cell-derived protein systems, it is essential to rigorously validate how thermal conditions quantitatively regulate myogenic differentiation, and how this regulation affects the amino acid profile, which determines cellular stability and potential nutritional value. However, comprehensive studies integrating quantitative molecular validation with amino acid profiling remain scarce.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the comprehensive effects of a physiological culture temperature (40 °C) on chick satellite cells (CSCs). Specifically, we evaluated cellular proliferation capacity, the transcriptional expression patterns of myogenic markers, and the amino acid composition of the cultured cells to assess their potential nutritional value. By elucidating these aspects, this study provides fundamental data for optimizing culture conditions and understanding the nutritional profile of cell-derived proteins.

Materials and methods

Isolation of muscle satellite cells

The use and treatment of animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Gyeongsang National University (approval no. GNU-250911-E0203). Muscle satellite cell isolation and culture were performed as described by Kim et al. (2023). Fertilized eggs were obtained from Gyeongsang National University, and cells were isolated from 15-day-old chick embryos. After 15 days of incubation, leg muscles were harvested from the embryos. The muscles were washed with 1X Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) (Welgene, Gyeongsan, Republic of Korea) and homogenized. Minced muscle tissue was treated with a final concentration of 1 U of collagenase D (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, US), 2 U dispase II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), and 2.5 mM calcium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, US) per gram of tissue. The mixture was incubated in a shaking incubator for 30–60 min to ensure thorough enzyme mixing. To obtain a uniform cell suspension and prevent potential contamination, the minced tissue was sequentially filtered through cell strainers with pore sizes of 100, 70, and 40 μm. The filtered tissue was centrifuged at 79 × g for 5 min and the supernatant was collected. The collected supernatant was centrifuged again at 1561 × g for 5 min to obtain a cell pellet. To separate fibroblasts, the pellet was suspended, plated on uncoated cell culture dishes, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 5 min. The supernatant, containing the satellite cells, was then collected and cultured in collagen-coated cell culture dishes at both 37 and 40 °C. Subculturing was performed after detachment using 1X trypsin–EDTA solution (Welgene, Gyeongsan, Republic of Korea) when cell confluence exceeded 80%.

Chromosomal karyotype analysis

To confirm whether satellite cells extracted from chick embryos were derived from chicken, chromosomal analysis was performed to examine their karyotype patterns.

Chromosome isolation

Chromosome isolation was performed according to the method described by Kiazim et al. (2021), with slight modifications. CSCs were cultured in Ham’s F-10 nutrient mixture (Welgene, Gyeongsan, Republic of Korea) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, US), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, US). The cultures were maintained in an incubator set to 37 and 40 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 to 48 h. The cells were subcultured approximately 10 times and subculturing was performed when the cells reached a monolayer covering over 80% of the bottom of the culture flask. The cells were detached by subculturing in a 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution. To induce metaphase arrest, colcemid (Gibco, NY, US.) was added at a concentration of 0.2 µg/mL 1 h before the end of the culture period. The cells were then harvested using a cell scraper and centrifuged at 6.75 × g. For cell swelling, harvested cells were exposed to 0.06 M potassium chloride (Daejung, Siheung, Republic of Korea) at room temperature for 15 min. Fixation was performed by treating the cells thrice with a fixative composed of methanol (Samchun, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and acetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, US) in a 3:1 ratio. Finally, the specimens were prepared by placing three to four drops of the cell suspension onto chilled slides.

GTG-banding

GTG-banding was performed using dried specimens on a heated plate using the method described by Sohn et al. (2003) with slight modifications. The specimens were briefly immersed in 0.1% trypsin–EDTA for 2–3 s and rinsed with cold 1X DPBS lacking Ca2+ and Mg2+. Subsequently, the specimens were stained with 0.04% Leishman stain (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, US) for 5 min, and observed under a light microscope.

Morphological analysis

Proliferation and differentiation patterns of the CSCs at different temperatures were observed using a phase-contrast inverted microscope. CSCs were cultured for proliferation using Ham’s F-10 basal medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S), and 2 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (Welgene, Gyeongsan, Republic of Korea). For the differentiation assay, when cell confluence reached 80% on the culture dish, the basal medium was replaced with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Cytiva/Hyclone, MA, US) supplemented with 2% horse serum (HS) (Gibco, NY, US) and 1% P/S for the first three days. From day 4 onwards, the medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% HS and 1% P/S. Subculturing was performed when cell confluence reached 80% in the cell culture dish. Subsequently, the differentiation and proliferation patterns of the cells were examined using a phase-contrast inverted microscope (EVOS M5000, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, US).

Cell proliferative capacity

Cell number

To evaluate the proliferative capacity of cells at different culture temperatures, cell counting was performed at 48-h intervals using a hemocytometer. Cell viability was assessed simultaneously using the trypan blue (Gibco, NY, US) exclusion method.

Population doubling time

Population doubling time (PDT) was measured to investigate the effects of culture temperature on CSCs, using the method of Lotfy et al. (2014) with modifications. CSCs were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells per cell culture dish and cultured at temperatures of 37 and 40 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Proliferation measurements were initiated from passage 4 (P4) to ensure the purity and stability of the isolated primary cells. Subculturing was performed at 48-h intervals. Cell viability

Cell viability was evaluated to assess the effects of culture temperature on CSCs using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). CSCs were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per culture dish and maintained at 37 and 40 °C. At passage 3 and passage 9, cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the control group at 37 °C.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed, as described by Zhan et al. (2022), to investigate the effects of culture temperature on cell proliferation and differentiation. For assessing proliferative capacity, paired box-7 (Pax-7) (NK max, CA, US) was used as the primary antibody, and Alexa Fluor™ 488 goat anti-mouse IgG1 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, US.) was used as the secondary antibody. To evaluate differentiation potential, the myosin heavy chain (MHC) was targeted using MF20 (DSHB, IA, US) and myogenin (MyoG) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, US) as primary antibodies. The secondary antibodies used were Alexa Fluor™ 488 goat anti-mouse IgG1 and Alexa Fluor™ 647 goat anti-mouse IgG2b (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, US).

Staining for proliferation was performed 12 h after subculturing, whereas differentiation staining was carried out between days 3 and 7 after switching to the differentiation medium upon observation of the myotubes. The cells were washed with 1X DPBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Bylabs, Hanam, Republic of Korea) in 1X DPBS for 20 min at room temperature. Permeabilization was achieved with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Generay Biotech, Shanghai, China) in 1X DPBS for 20 min, followed by two 5-min washes with 1X DPBS. Blocking was performed with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Hanlab, Cheongju, Republic of Korea) in 1X DPBS for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted 1:500 in 2% BSA in 1X DPBS and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing, the secondary antibodies were diluted 1:1000 in 2% BSA in 1X DPBS and incubated for 2 h at room temperature, followed by two more washes with 1X DPBS. Hoechst (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, US) was diluted 1:10000 in 1X DPBS, applied for 5 min at room temperature, and washed with 1X DPBS. Finally, samples were examined using a phase-contrast inverted microscope, and images were processed using ImageJ 1.x (NIH, MD, US) software to adjust brightness and contrast (Rueden et al., 2017). Additionally, the fusion index was calculated using ImageJ software by determining the percentage of nuclei within MHC-positive myotubes relative to the total number of nuclei observed in the field of view.

Extraction of RNA and real time quantitative PCR

For gene expression analysis, cells were cultured at 37 °C and 40 °C, and harvested at 24 h for the proliferation phase, and at 48 h for the differentiation phase. Total RNA was extracted using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The purity and concentration of the extracted RNA were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (K lab, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). The relative mRNA expression levels of each gene were analyzed using the light cycler 480 Instrument II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland).

Bicinchoninic acid protein assay

The protein content of muscle tissue was determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay (BCA assay) with Pierce™ BCA Protein assay kits (Thermo Fisher, MA, US.). Bovine serum albumin was used to prepare a standard curve with concentrations of 0, 25, 125, 250, 500, 750, 1000, 1500, and 2000 μg/mL. A 20 μL sample was mixed with 160 μL of BCA working reagent and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm to quantify the protein concentration.

Amino acid composition analysis

The amino acid content of muscle tissue was measured using a Biochrom 30 Plus amino acid analyzer (Biochrom, Cambridge, UK). Tissue samples (0.5 mg) were diluted in 2 mL 5% trichloroacetic acid (Daejung, Siheung, Republic of Korea) and vortexed for 4 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 13,523 × g for 20 min at 20 °C. The supernatant was collected and mixed with 4 mL n-hexane (Daejung, Siheung, Republic of Korea), followed by vigorous shaking by hand for 10 min. After centrifugation at 1561 × g for 10 min, the lower phase was collected. The sample was then filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane filter into a vial for the analysis of free amino acids using the amino acid analyzer.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the collected data were statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS Version 27 (International Business Machines Corporation; IBM, NY, USA). Statistical significance was determined using one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Student-Newman–Keuls (SNK) method for post hoc analysis and independent sample t-tests. For the two-way ANOVA, the main effects of temperature, passage number, and their interaction were evaluated. Differences between samples were considered significant at p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001 and were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Cell karyotype and origin verification

Chickens have a chromosomal complement of 2n = 78, consisting of 38 pairs of autosomes and two sex chromosomes (ZZ for males and ZW for females). Their karyotype is known to exhibit 10 pairs of large chromosomes (macrochromosomes), with the remainder appearing as dot-like microchromosomes (Huang et al., 2023). Irrespective of the culture temperature, the chromosomes of the satellite cells obtained from chick embryos showed the same pattern as reported earlier, with 8–10 pairs of large chromosomes and the presence of microchromosomes, confirming their chick origin (Fig. 1). This finding confirmed that the isolated cells were of chick origin, and suggested that the temperatures used did not lead to chromosomal abnormalities.

Effects of temperature on proliferation capacity

Morphological observation revealed that CSCs cultured at 40 °C exhibited a denser morphology and a greater proliferative tendency (Fig. 2). Quantitative growth measurements showed that CSCs cultured at 40 °C maintained stable proliferation without significant degradation as passaging progressed. Specifically, cell numbers were significantly greater in the 40 °C group compared to the 37 °C control at passages 6, 7, 8, 10, and 11 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3). Consistently, the PDT of cells cultured at 40 °C was significantly shorter at passages 6, 7, 10, and 11 compared to those at 37 °C (Fig. 3). In contrast, the 37 °C group displayed signs of growth retardation and increased kinetic variance from passage 6 onwards (Fig. 3). Cells cultured at 40°C exhibited significantly higher metabolic activity at both early (passage 3) and late (passage 9) passages, as determined using the CCK-8 assay, further supporting the enhanced proliferative capacity observed in the cell counting and PDT analyses. (Fig. 3). These results demonstrate that prolonged 40 °C exposure does not induce chronic thermal toxicity, which supports the stable proliferation and shorter PDT observed during extended culture (Fig. 2, 3).

To evaluate the myogenic status of the CSCs, Pax-7 protein expression was assessed via immunofluorescence staining, and both Pax-7 and myoblast determination protein (MyoD) expression were measured at the transcriptional level using RT-qPCR. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a higher Pax-7 protein ratio in the 40 °C group compared to the 37 °C group (Fig. 4). Conversely, RT-qPCR showed that Pax-7 mRNA levels were significantly higher at 37 °C, while MyoD mRNA expression was upregulated at 40 °C (Fig. 6). Although Pax-7 protein abundance and mRNA expression showed different patterns, mRNA expression does not necessarily correlate with protein abundance because these are regulated at different biological levels. Therefore, the protein and transcript data obtained in this study should be interpreted as complementary indicators of myogenic status rather than directly corresponding measurements. Taken together, these observations suggest that the 40 °C condition potentially mediates a balance between the competing processes of cell cycle progression and tissue-specific differentiation, supporting stable CSC proliferation while facilitating a transcriptional shift toward myogenic commitment (Ruijtenberg & van den Heuvel, 2016). This expression pattern is consistent with the canonical regulatory pathway of myogenesis, in which Pax-7 maintains the progenitor state, whereas MyoD marks the transition toward myogenic commitment (Buckingham & Relaix, 2015; Collins et al., 2009; Yin et al., 2013).

These temperature-dependent differences align with the physiological characteristics of poultry, which require an optimal growth environment close to the chicken’s normal body temperature to prevent severe impairments in growth performance and physiological functions caused by environmental heat stress (Ahmad et al., 2022; Halevy et al., 2006). Previous studies reported that culture temperatures near this physiological range are associated with mitochondrial metabolic efficiency and heat-shock protein expression, which support the ATP production required for cell proliferation (Halevy, 2020). The stable proliferation and myogenic commitment observed at 40 °C in our study are associated with metabolic mechanisms where pathways such as mTOR govern G1/S phase transition and cell cycle progression (Laplante & Sabatini, 2012).

Although Pax-7 mRNA expression was higher at 37 °C, the CSC proliferation rates and PDT results at 40 °C demonstrate that this physiological temperature optimizes growth. Providing a physiological temperature environment at 40 °C mitigates the cellular senescence and kinetic variance typically observed during extended in vitro expansion. This provides a practical strategy for optimizing chick satellite cell culture conditions, which contributes to cell-derived protein production systems.

Effects of temperature on myogenesis and protein production profile

Myogenin (MyoG) acts as a critical marker for evaluating the degree of myogenic differentiation, supporting the expression of myosin heavy chain (MHC), a major protein component of myofibrils (Adhikari et al., 2021; Fumoto et al., 2024). Quantitative analysis of the fusion index and immunofluorescence staining for MyoG and MHC revealed no statistically significant differences between the 37°C and 40°C groups (Fig. 5). RT-qPCR analysis also revealed no statistically significant differences in MyoG mRNA expression between the two temperatures (Fig. 6). This result further supports the immunofluorescence findings that differentiation capacity was not significantly altered by culture temperature.

Transcriptional analysis revealed distinct expression patterns between the two conditions. Pax7 mRNA levels were significantly higher at 37 °C, whereas MyoD expression was significantly higher at 40°C (Fig. 6). This indicates that incubation temperature influences the regulatory state of the myogenic differentiation program. Pax7 is associated with the maintenance of satellite cell stemness, whereas MyoD acts as a regulatory factor in the transition to differentiation (Olguin & Olwin, 2004; Zammit, 2017). Despite no statistically significant differences in terminal differentiation markers (MyoG and fusion index) between the two conditions (Fig. 5, 6), these differences at the transcriptional level indicate that 40 °C and 37 °C reach the final differentiation state through distinct transcriptional pathways centered on MyoD and Pax-7, respectively.

BCA analysis indicated that total protein content showed no statistically significant differences between the 37 °C and 40 °C conditions, nor compared to actual chick leg muscle tissue (Fig. 7). However, as the amino acid profile is a primary determinant of the nutritional quality and functional metabolic properties of a protein source (Wu, 2009), further amino acid content analysis was conducted, revealing qualitative differences in protein composition between the two conditions (Fig. 8). Glycine and alanine levels were higher at 37 °C, whereas valine, isoleucine, and lysine levels were higher at 40 °C (Fig. 8, 9). The higher levels of glycine and alanine at 37 °C are associated with mechanisms related to metabolic homeostasis and cell maintenance, as glycine is linked to cellular integrity and alanine to metabolic adaptation under altered growth conditions (Hatazawa et al., 2018; Koopman et al., 2017). Conversely, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) such as valine and isoleucine, along with lysine, were elevated at 40 °C. Previous studies have reported that branched-chain amino acids are associated with signaling pathways involved in protein metabolism, including the mTOR pathway (Buel et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2022; Neinast et al., 2019; Rehman et al., 2023). Specifically, these essential amino acids are known to be primarily responsible for stimulating muscle protein anabolism (Volpi et al., 2003). While these BCAAs may suggest a shift toward an environment conducive to myogenic progression, further studies are required to determine whether these compositional differences are associated with changes in protein synthesis or other metabolic processes. In conclusion, while total protein accumulation does not differ between the two temperature conditions or actual tissue, 40 °C is characterized by a BCAA-rich metabolic environment, while 37 °C is characterized by a glycine- and alanine-rich metabolic environment, demonstrating that these distinct molecular and metabolic backgrounds are associated with the final myogenic differentiation results.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effect of culture temperature on the proliferation and differentiation of chick satellite cells (CSCs). The results demonstrated that culturing CSCs at 40°C promoted stable proliferation and reduced population doubling time compared to the 37 °C condition, without inducing chronic thermal toxicity. Furthermore, molecular analyses revealed that, compared to 37 °C, the 40 °C condition showed decreased expression of Pax-7 while upregulating the expression of MyoD, a key marker for differentiation. This suggests that the 40 °C environment adequately stimulates the differentiation process toward the myogenic lineage. In terms of the amino acid profile, while the levels of glycine and alanine were higher at 37 °C, the CSCs exhibited a rich content of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), which are essential for muscle protein synthesis. Based on these findings, 40 °C appears to be a favorable culture temperature for the efficient expansion of chick satellite cells. This physiological temperature condition, combined with a BCAA-enriched amino acid profile, may provide a useful basis for producing cell-derived protein materials with distinct amino acid characteristics.
