Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a common malignant tumor of the urinary system that originates from the epithelial cells of the proximal renal tubules [1]. RCC has three common pathological types: clear cell RCC (ccRCC), papillary RCC, and chromophobe RCC [2]. Approximately 70–75% of RCC cases are attributed to ccRCC, making it the most frequent subtype [3]. The main feature of ccRCC lies in the abnormal activation of the HIF-VEGFR-mTOR signaling pathway caused by von hippel-lindau (VHL) mutations, which in turn leads to metabolic disorders, increased angiogenesis and deterioration of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [4]. Currently, the treatment methods for ccRCC include nephrectomy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy [5]. Although immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors can activate anti-tumor immunity and generate positive responses in some patients, due to the high heterogeneity of TME, most patients find it difficult to benefit from it in the long term [6, 7]. Meanwhile, although targeted therapy has improved the disease control rate and patient survival period, it also has certain side effects [8]. For instance, tyrosine kinase inhibitors therapy is often accompanied by significant adverse reactions such as hypertension, hand-foot syndrome, vomiting and diarrhea [9]. Given the limitations of existing therapies and the issue of drug resistance, exploring new therapeutic targets and drugs is crucial for improving the prognosis of patients with ccRCC.

Mitochondrial metabolism, as a crucial biological energy hub, mainly involves the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, fatty acid oxidation (FAO), the electron transport chain (ETC), and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [10]. In tumor biology, these processes are widely recognized as playing significant roles in the establishment and maintenance of malignant phenotypes, profoundly influencing the TME [11]. TCA cycle and OXPHOS activity potentially fuels cancer progression, notably metastasis [12]. Lung metastasis in orthotopic breast cancer mice significantly enhanced TCA cycle flux, as measured quantitatively [13]. Mutations in TCA cycle enzymes can also lead to abnormal accumulation of various metabolites, causing dysregulation of signal transduction and promoting cancer progression [14]. Additionally, RCC is fundamentally a metabolic disease characterized by the reprogramming of energy metabolism [15–17]. This is particularly evident in the diversion of metabolic flux toward glycolysis [18–20], impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics and OXPHOS, and disrupted lipid metabolism [18, 21–24]. The Warburg effect stands as one of the earliest sources of evidence for metabolic reprogramming in ccRCC [25]. This effect manifests itself in the fact that even under oxygen-sufficient conditions, most cancer cells primarily generate energy through glycolysis, whereas most normal cells rely on mitochondrial OXPHOS. This tumor-specific metabolism not only meets the energy demands of rapid proliferation but also provides essential precursors for biosynthesis [26]. Against this backdrop, recent studies have further revealed MMRGs as key regulators of renal cancer stem cell metabolism, influencing multiple biological behaviors including self-renewal and differentiation potential [27, 28]. Although evidence indicates the critical role of MMRGs in ccRCC development, systematic investigations into their specific regulatory networks, functional contributions to malignant phenotype establishment, and causal relationships remain limited. Therefore, this study explores the role of mitochondrial metabolism in the pathogenesis of ccRCC, aiming to provide new insights into the metabolic basis of this disease.

This study aims to integrate multiple bioinformatics methods to develop a ccRCC prognostic model drawing on mitochondrial metabolism and address disease heterogeneity by identifying molecular subtypes of ccRCC. By correlating immune infiltration characteristics with biological functions, this study seeks to identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets for personalized ccRCC management.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The TCGA-KIRC cohort (72 normal, 537 tumor samples) served as the training set, with transcriptomic data acquired from TCGA and matched clinical metadata extracted from UCSC Xena. For validation, the E-MTAB-1980 dataset comprising 100 ccRCC tumor profiles was retrieved from ArrayExpress. From the research of Braun et al. [29], we obtained a dataset of 309 ccRCC patients showing different responses to anti-PD-1 receptor inhibitors (ccRCC-32472114). Additionally, the keyword “mitochondrial metabolism” was searched in the MSigDB database, yielding 1234 mitochondrial metabolism-related genes (MMRGs).

Screening of prognostic genes for CcRCC

Differential analysis between tumor and normal tissues in the TCGA-KIRC cohort was conducted via the limma package (|logFC| > 1, FDR < 0.05). Intersecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with MMRGs yielded differentially expressed MMRGs (DEMMRGs). Subsequently, we adopted a multi-stage analysis strategy to screen for prognostic genes in ccRCC: univariate Cox regression (survival package), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (glmnet package), and multivariate Cox regression (survival package).

Subsequent differential analysis between risk groups (limma package) identified enrichment pathways via GO/KEGG analyses (clusterProfiler package).

Nomogram based on clinical information and risk score

A nomogram is constructed based on diagnostic genes to predict the risk of developing ccRCC. The clinical utility of the model is evaluated using calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA).

Immune infiltration analysis

This study utilized ssGSEA and CIBERSORT to analyze the infiltration of immune cells in different groups. The ESTIMATE algorithm was employed to evaluate the ESTIMATE score, Immune score, Stromal score, and Tumor purity index of different groups. Drawing on the ccRCC-32472114 cohort, we classified disease stable (SD), disease progression (PD), and not evaluable (NE) as not response (NR), and complete response (CR) and partial response (PR) as response (R), to analyze the response rate of patients in different risk groups to anti-PD-1 blockade therapy. The tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) algorithm quantified immune evasion potential, while differential expression of immune checkpoints was assessed across risk strata.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and drug sensitivity analysis

Mutation spectra analysis of TCGA-KIRC characterized the top 20 recurrently mutated genes in high/low-risk cohorts, visualized through waterfall plots (GenVisR package). For pharmacogenomic profiling, half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of ccRCC-relevant chemotherapeutics were quantified utilizing pRRophetic. CellMiner database screening identified anti-tumor agents associated with prognostic genes, with gene expression-drug sensitivity correlations interrogated via Spearman’s analysis.

Molecular typing of ccRCC

Consensus clustering (ConsensusClusterPlus package) stratified ccRCC into distinct molecular subtypes based on prognostic genes, with clustering stability assessed via cumulative distribution function (CDF) and delta area curve analyses.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

This study employed normal renal epithelial cells HK-2 and human renal carcinoma cell line 786-O for experimentation. Under strict aseptic conditions, both cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription with PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix. RNA and cDNA concentrations were quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. RT-qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 5 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system, with primer sequences detailed in Table 1. GAPDH served as the internal reference gene, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCT) method.

Data analysis

Data analysis and visualization were conducted utilizing R software (version 4.3.1). Inter-group comparisons employed the wilcoxon test, while correlations were quantified using the pearson correlation coefficient, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Screening of MMRGs in ccRCC

Differential analysis was performed using the training set (|logFC| > 1, FDR < 0.05), identifying a total of 1896 DEGs (Fig. 1A). The intersection of DEGs and MMRGs yielded 175 DEMMRGs (Fig. 1B). Further univariate Cox analysis identified 41 ccRCC candidate prognostic genes (Fig. 1C), and their expression patterns were compared between Normal and Tumor groups (Fig. 1D). The results revealed that ACLY, FABP5, PLIN2, and TRIB3 were significantly upregulated in the Tumor group, while 37 genes were significantly upregulated in the Normal group. Subsequently, correlation analysis was performed on this gene set (Fig. 1E), showing that TRIB3 and FABP5 were negatively correlated with most genes, while ACLY expression demonstrated a positive association with ETNK2 but an inverse correlation with FDX1. Additionally, TMB analysis revealed that ACLY had the highest mutation frequency at 10%, followed by CYP4A11, NDUFS1, NNT, and PCK1 (Fig. 1F).

Construction and validation of a prognostic model for ccRCC

To identify the optimal prognostic genes for ccRCC, this study employed LASSO regression analysis to screen candidate prognostic genes, identifying 16 potential prognostic genes (Fig. 2A-B). Further, six prognostic genes were selected using multivariate Cox analysis (Fig. 2C), and K-M survival curve analysis confirmed that these six genes were significantly associated with survival differences in patients with ccRCC (Supplementary Figure S1). K-M survival curve analysis demonstrated significantly superior overall survival in low risk versus high risk patients (Fig. 2D). ROC curve analysis showed that the prognostic model had robust prognostic performance in both the training and validation sets (Fig. 2E-F). Furthermore, the risk score distribution and corresponding survival status plots clearly demonstrated patient outcomes distribution in the dataset (Fig. 2G-H). RT-qPCR results further confirmed that the six MMRGs exhibited significant expression differences between normal renal epithelial cells (HK-2) and human renal carcinoma cells (786-O) (Fig. 2I). In 786-O cells, ACLY exhibits high expression, while the remaining genes show a trend of low expression.

Gene enrichment analysis

Gene enrichment analysis was performed to investigate the biological characteristics between high and low risk groups of ccRCC patients. High risk group exhibited activated hematopoietic cell lineage, ribosome biogenesis, and proteasome (Fig. 3A). Low risk group was enriched in erbb signaling pathway and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 3B). Subsequent enrichment analysis of DEGs between the high and low risk groups was performed. GO analysis revealed (Fig. 3C) that the upregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processe (BP) including acute inflammatory response, granulocyte chemotaxis, and neutrophil chemotaxis; cellular component (CC): collagen-containing extracellular matrix, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, and collagen trimer; Molecular function (MF): extracellular matrix structural constituent, chemokine activity, and cytokine receptor binding. Conversely, the downregulated DEGs were enriched in BP such as organic anion transport, monocarboxylic acid transport, and carboxylic acid transport; in CC including the apical plasma membrane, apical part of cell, and brush border; and in MF such as carboxylic acid transmembrane transporter activity, sodium ion transmembrane transporter activity, and active transmembrane transporter activity. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 3D) indicated that the upregulated DEGs were enriched in pathways including the Chemokine signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway. The downregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in Renin-angiotensin system, PPAR signaling pathway, and Carbon metabolism.

Construction and validation of a ccRCC prognostic nomogram

Analysis of riskscores based on distinct clinical characteristics revealed statistically significant differences for stage (Fig. 4A). Specifically, riskscores were significantly higher in stage III + IV compared to stage I + II, in M1 compared to M0, in N1 compared to N0, and in T3 + T4 compared to T1 + T2. Univariate Cox analysis results demonstrated that the riskscore, stage, M, N, and T were all significantly associated with prognosis (Fig. 4B). Multivariate Cox analysis validated the riskscore as an independent prognostic predictor (Fig. 4C). To predict patient survival probability, this study developed a nomogram model integrating the riskscore with key clinical parameters (Fig. 4D). Calibration curves and DCA rigorously validated the model’s predictive accuracy, confirming clinical utility for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prognostication (Fig. 4E-F).

Immune infiltration analysis of high and low risk groups in ccRCC

To investigate the immune microenvironment characteristics between high risk and low risk groups, the following analyses were conducted. ESTIMATE analysis demonstrated significantly higher Immune/ESTIMATE Scores in high risk patients versus elevated Tumor Purity in low risk counterparts (Fig. 5A). Significantly higher TIDE scores characterized the high risk group (Fig. 5B). The ssGSEA immune infiltration analysis indicated (Fig. 5C) that Mast cells and Type II IFN response showed significantly higher immune infiltration levels in the low risk group, whereas aDCs, B cells, and DCs exhibited opposite patterns. CIBERSORT analysis (Fig. 5D) indicated significantly elevated infiltration of plasma cells, T cell CD8, Tregs, and Macrophages M0 in high risk versus low risk patients, while the high risk group had significantly lower infiltration levels of T cells CD4 memory resting, Macrophages M2 and Mast cells resting. Analysis of immune checkpoint molecule expression (Fig. 5E) revealed that most immune checkpoints, including CCL5, CD14, and CCL19, showed significantly higher expression levels in high risk groups compared to low risk groups. Furthermore, in the ccRCC-32472114 cohort, survival in low risk patients was notably prolonged (Fig. 5F), and their response rate to PD-1 blockade immunotherapy was significantly exceeded that in high risk patients (Fig. 5G).

TMB and drug sensitivity analysis

The TMB results indicate that the frequency of TTN mutations was higher in high risk patients (17%) than in low risk patients (12%) (Fig. 6A-B). Subsequently, drug sensitivity analysis for two chemotherapeutic agents commonly used in ccRCC showed (Fig. 6C-D) that enhanced drug sensitivity was observed in low risk cohort: doxorubicin/paclitaxel IC50 significantly below high risk levels. In addition, correlation analysis revealed (Fig. 6E) that ACLY expression was negatively correlated with the sensitivity to Vinorelbine, and ETNK2 expression, showed a significant negative correlation with the sensitivity to Palbociclib, Perifosine and PX-316.

Molecular subtypes of ccRCC

In this study, ccRCC was classified into two molecular subtypes by consensus clustering based on prognostic genes (Fig. 7A), and the robustness of the typing was confirmed by CDF and delta area curve analysis (Fig. 7B-C). K-M survival analysis showed (Fig. 7D) that the survival rate of patients in group2 was significantly lower than that of group1. Differential expression analysis showed (Fig. 7E) that group1 had significantly higher expression levels of ACLY, ETNK2, HADH, and HSD11B2, while the opposite was true for G0S2. ESTIMATE analysis showed (Fig. 7F) that group2 had significantly higher ESTIMATE/Stromal/Immune Score than group1, while the opposite was true for Tumor Purity. ssGSEA immune infiltration analysis (Fig. 7G) showed that most of the immune cells and immune functions in group2 had significantly higher levels of immune infiltration than those in group1. CIBERSORT analysis (Fig. 7H) demonstrated significantly elevated infiltration of plasma cells, T cells CD4 memory activated and Macrophages M2 in group2 versus group1.

Discussion

Despite available treatments for ccRCC, persistent clinical challenges such as poor metastatic prognosis and high recurrence rates compel novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets to advance patient management [30]. The critical role of mitochondrial metabolism in cancer is widely recognized [31, 32], but its specific regulatory network, function and causal relationship with tumor malignancy characteristics in ccRCC have not been systematically described. In this study, we integrated multiple bioinformatics analysis to conduct a comprehensive study on the role of mitochondrial metabolism in ccRCC.

The six prognostic genes identified in this study demonstrated significant survival differences in both high and low risk groups of ccRCC, suggesting their critical role in disease progression. Adenosine triphosphate citrate lyase (ACLY) knockdown attenuated ccRCC proliferation [33]. This is consistent with our study that in the TCGA-KIRC cohort, the expression level of ACLY was significantly higher in the ccRCC group than in the Normal group. Ethanolamine kinase 2 (ETNK2) can promote gastric cancer liver metastasis by inhibiting the tumor protein (p53) and B-cell Lymphoma 2 (Bcl2) expression [34]. Ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) is involved in the reduction of mitochondrial cytochromes, and it is an upstream regulator of protein thiooctylation in the TCA cycle [35], which is lowly expressed in ccRCC, and its knockdown increased cell invasion in ccRCC cells [36]. Hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (HADH) is an important subunit of mitochondrial trifunctional protein (MTP), and its abnormal expression affects tumour development and prognosis [37], which is lowly expressed in ccRCC tissues and cells, and its overexpression not only reduces migration and invasion of ccRCC cells, but also increases expression of apoptotic proteins and cleavages Caspase3 [38]. These are consistent with our findings that ETNK2, FDX1 and HADH are lowly expressed in ccRCC and associated with poorer OS. In addition, low expression of HSD11B2 and G0S2 was also associated with malignant progression of tumours. For example, knockdown of hydroxysteroid 11-beta dehydrogenase 2 (HSD11B2) enhances proliferative, invasive, and angiogenic capacities in oral cancer cells [39]. Low expression of G0/G1 switch 2 (G0S2) is associated with a significantly increased risk of recurrence in estrogen receptor positive (ER+) breast cancer patients [40]. Furthermore, ccRCC has been widely recognized as a metabolic disease [41]. These six MMRGs, serving as prognostic markers for ccRCC, suggest that metabolic reprogramming plays a pivotal role in ccRCC progression. ACLY as a central metabolic enzyme in cancer, can play a key role in cancer metabolism by depriving cytoplasmic citrate in promoting glycolysis [42, 43]. ETNK2 has been reported to enhance phosphatidylethanolamine synthesis in non-small cell lung cancer, suggesting its potential involvement in lipid metabolism regulation [44, 45],. FDX1 is involved in multiple metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, and amino acid metabolism, demonstrating broad metabolic regulatory functions [46]. HADH, as a key enzyme in fatty acid oxidation, influences tumor progression by regulating fatty acid metabolism [37]. HSD11B2 is associated with cortisol metabolism [47]. G0S2 participates in lipid metabolism regulation by inhibiting adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) activity [48]. Although the metabolic functions of these genes are gradually being elucidated, how they influence the initiation and progression of ccRCC by regulating mitochondrial metabolism remains to be further explored.

RCC is a tumor characterized by significant immune infiltration [49–51]. Its TME features profoundly influence disease biology and may affect systemic treatment response [52–55]. Recent studies indicate that specific metabolic pathways play crucial roles in regulating angiogenesis and inflammatory responses [56, 57]. Among these, mitochondrial metabolism not only plays a key role in the occurrence and development of tumors but also regulates the immune response within the TME [58]. For instance, the suppressive function of Treg cells is regulated by cellular metabolism, although they enhance glucose uptake during proliferation, their suppressive function depends on OXPHOS and mitochondrial metabolism [59]. This study stratified ccRCC patients through the MMRGs risk model, revealing the different immune infiltration characteristics among patients in the high and low risk groups. Immune infiltration analysis further demonstrated elevated infiltration levels of CD8 T cells and Tregs in high risk patients relative to the low risk group, an observation aligning with prior studies linking increased infiltration of these cell types in ccRCC to unfavorable clinical outcomes [60–62]. Additionally, our study demonstrated that the expression of immune checkpoint molecules programmed cell death 1 (PDCD1) and lymphocyte activating 3 (LAG3) was significantly upregulated in high risk ccRCC patients. This phenomenon suggests that inhibitors targeting the PDCD1 and LAG3 pathways may have therapeutic potential for ccRCC. It has been reported that pembrolizumab, a humanized anti-PDCD1 monoclonal antibody, may serve as the initial therapeutic option for patients with advanced ccRCC with good anti-tumor activity and acceptable safety [63]. Notably, clinical evidence indicates that combining LAG3 inhibitors with PDCD1 inhibitors may more effectively enhance anti-tumor immune responses compared to single inhibition of LAG3 [64]. In summary, the metabolic and immune interactions within the ccRCC TME are highly complex. Deepening our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms by which MMRGs modulate immune cell function will facilitate the development of more precise combined metabolic and immune therapeutic strategies.

Gene enrichment analysis in this study identified significant enrichment of differentially upregulated genes within the Wnt signaling pathway between high and low risk ccRCC patient groups, indicating a potential key role for this pathway in ccRCC pathogenesis. It was reported that although transient activation of the Wnt signaling pathway promotes tissue repair and regeneration after kidney injury, its excessive and uncontrolled activation leads to permanent kidney injury and significantly increases the risk of developing RCC [65]. In ccRCC, elevated levels of Wnt1 expression have been shown to correlate with increased tumour size, advanced stage and vascular infiltration in patients [66]. In addition, Wnt10A expression was significantly upregulated in ccRCC cell lines and tissues compared to normal tissues, and its high expression level co-existed with nuclear localisation of β-catenin and cell cycle protein D1, constituting an independent risk factor for RCC development and progression [67]. Further studies found that Wnt signalling pathway regulation is associated with mitochondrial metabolism and permeabilisation [68]. For example, inhibition of Wnt3α decreased mitochondrial metabolism in adipocytes [69]. Oncogenic Wnt signalling in colorectal cancer cells can regulate mitochondrial metabolism via glycolysis, which generates anabolic precursors necessary for rapid cancer cell growth [70].

Despite the comprehensive analysis of MMRGs of ccRCC in this study, several limitations remain. Firstly, although ccRCC subtypes based on molecular features showed significant differences in the degree of immune infiltration and the activation status of signalling pathways, the potential of these subtypes as a clinical tool for predicting therapeutic response still oughts to be validated by clinical studies. Second, the core molecular mechanisms of MMRGs driving ccRCC progression, especially the interactions between key prognostic genes and the Wnt signalling pathway in disease development, are still poorly known. Therefore, future studies should integrate in vitro cellular molecular experiments with in vivo models to deeply elucidate the clinical application value of prognostic genes in ccRCC.

Conclusion

MMRGs are not only related to the occurrence and progression of ccRCC tumors, but may also interact with immune infiltration and the Wnt signaling pathway. Targeting prognosis genes could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for ccRCC.
