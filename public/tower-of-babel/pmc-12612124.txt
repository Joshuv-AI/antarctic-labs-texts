Assessment of bacterial contamination, physical quality, and internal pH (albumen and yolk) of table eggs in Khartoum North
Authors: Sahar Hemeda, Mogahid Abdallh, Bashir Ibrahim, Elrasheed Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTable eggs are a vital dietary component owing to their nutritional value; however, bacterial contamination, particularly of eggshells, poses significant public health risks. In Sudan, these risks are exacerbated by inadequate hygiene, poor storage conditions, and limited regulatory oversight, which compromise egg safety in local markets. This preliminary, exploratory, cross-sectional, laboratory-based study aimed to evaluate bacterial contamination, assess physical characteristics, examine the pH of albumen and yolk, and investigate the associations among these factors in table eggs from Khartoum North. A total of 46 samples (three eggs per sample, totalling 138 eggs) were randomly collected from five types of retail outlets in Khartoum North in September 2020. Eggshell, albumen, and yolk samples were tested for aerobic bacterial contamination and total viable bacterial counts (TVC) using standard microbiological methods. Physical characteristics, including yolk colour, shell thickness, albumen height, shell strength, Haugh unit (HU), and albumen weight, were assessed using a Digital Egg Tester 6000, whereas pH levels were measured using a HI98107 pH meter. ANOVA and Pearson’s correlation were used to evaluate relationships, and Bayesian statistics were applied to estimate the pH values. Eggshell contamination by Staphylococcus spp. is widespread. (89.1%), Corynebacterium spp. (23.9%) being the most prevalent, followed by Escherichia coli (19.5%) and Salmonella spp. (15.2%). No bacteria were isolated from the albumen or yolk. A significant positive correlation was observed between the TVC and albumen pH (r = 0.540, P < 0.001), whereas other internal traits showed weak, non-significant correlations. The physical classification identified Grade AA (32.6%), A (34.8%), and B (32.6%) eggs. Weak correlations were found between shell strength and HU (r = 0.155, 95% CI: −0.141 − 0.427, P = 0.302) and between egg weight and HU (r = − 0.175, 95% CI: −0.434–0.121, P = 0.244). ANOVA revealed no significant differences in shell strength (F = 2.622, P = 0.084) or thickness (F = 0.115, P = 0.892) across grades. Bayesian estimates showed slight pH increases from Grade AA to B in yolk (6.363–6.555; 95% CrIs: 6.135–6.791) and albumen (8.463–8.604; 95% CrIs: 8.013–9.071), but non-significant differences. However, the overlapping CrIs indicated that these differences were not significant. The presence of Staphylococcus and E. coli on eggshells indicates inadequate hygiene. Bacterial load is linked to albumen pH, indicating that external microbes influence internal composition. Quality variations were observed based on egg grade and source, with contamination undermining the internal defences. Albumen pH is an indicator of freshness. Monitoring the shells and conducting physicochemical tests could enhance the traditional methods. Policymakers must enforce standards to ensure the safety of eggs. Given the preliminary and exploratory nature of this study, coupled with the limitations of a small and uneven sample size, the findings should be interpreted with caution. These results are meant to generate hypotheses, highlighting the need for further research with larger, more representative samples, balanced sampling strategies, and more comprehensive methods. Introduction

Table eggs are one of the most affordable and widely consumed animal protein sources, providing approximately 75 kcal per egg, a well-balanced amino acid composition, and high digestibility. Eggs are universally accepted across different cultural and religious contexts, further solidifying their role as a staple food globally1. Despite their nutritional benefits, eggs are highly susceptible to microbial contamination, particularly on the eggshell surface, which poses significant food safety risks. Freshly laid eggs are generally sterile; however, eggshells are immediately exposed to microorganisms after they are laid. Although this contamination is common and expected, it does not pose a significant risk if the eggshell remains intact, preventing bacteria from penetrating the internal components (the albumen and yolk). The eggshell and cuticle provide a natural barrier against microbial invasion. However, cracks, dirty surfaces, improper handling, and storage under unhygienic conditions can compromise this protective barrier, allowing microorganisms to breach the shell membrane and contaminate the internal contents2,3. Microbial penetration can occur indirectly when bacteria on eggshells breach the shell membrane4. The primary foodborne pathogens associated with eggs are members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, including Salmonella spp., Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella spp., all of which can cause spoilage and foodborne infections in consumers3,5–7. Furthermore, other pathogenic organisms, including Campylobacter spp., Listeria monocytogenes, and additional E. coli strains, have been detected in unwashed eggs and shell egg processing facilities7,8. Recent studies have also shown that many of these egg-associated pathogens exhibit antimicrobial resistance (AMR). For example, a global survey by9 reported resistance in Salmonella spp, E. coli, and Staphylococcus aureus isolates from table eggs across multiple regions, while a study in Bangladesh by10 identified multidrug resistance genes Gene encoding methicillin resistance in Staphylococcus aureus (mecA), the Gene encoding TEM-type β-lactamase (confers resistance to β-lactam antibiotics) (blaTEM), and the Gene encoding tetracycline efflux pump (confers resistance to tetracyclines)(tetA), Genes encoding vancomycin resistance in Enterococcus spp., (vanA/C), and enterotoxin-producing S. aureus from both eggshells and egg contents. These findings indicate that the risks posed by contaminated eggs extend beyond the presence of bacteria alone, with resistant strains complicating treatment options and amplifying the public health burden. The growing consumer demand for raw or undercooked egg-based foods, such as tiramisu and milkshakes, exacerbates these risks and underscores the need for effective contamination control7. In Sudan, bacterial contamination of table eggs has been extensively reported, particularly in Khartoum, the capital city. Studies have revealed that Escherichia coli is the most frequently isolated contaminant, followed by Proteus spp., Citrobacter spp., and Salmonella spp. Other bacteria of lower incidence include Klebsiella spp., Shigella spp., Serratia spp., Yersinia spp., Vibrio spp., Streptococcus spp., Staphylococcus spp., Bacillus spp., and Aeromonas spp11 Notably, eggs collected from retail stores and households exhibited high contamination levels, with Bacillus spp. and Staphylococcus spp. being the most predominant isolates12. These findings highlight the significant public health risks associated with poor hygiene practices, inadequate storage infrastructure, and weak regulatory enforcement in Sudan.

In addition to microbial contamination, egg quality is a critical determinant of consumer acceptance and food safety. Egg quality encompasses internal and external characteristics that influence their suitability for consumption13. The internal quality is determined by the composition and freshness of the egg albumen and yolk, as well as possible inclusions such as blood or flesh spots. Conversely, external quality is evaluated based on shell strength, integrity (e.g., cracks), cleanliness, colour, and shape14,15. Environmental factors, such as temperature, humidity, and carbon dioxide (CO₂), significantly influence the internal and external egg quality. Storage duration also plays a pivotal role in maintaining egg quality. Over time, prolonged storage results in a decline in albumen quality, which is quantitatively assessed using the Haugh Unit (HU)16. HU, widely regarded as the gold standard for freshness evaluation, relates the height of the thick albumen to the egg weight, allowing for egg classification into quality grades (AA, A, B, and C)12. Additionally, the pH of eggs, particularly the albumen and yolk, is widely used as an indicator of freshness and age. Freshly laid eggs exhibit a relatively low albumen pH (approximately 7.6–8.0), which increases over time owing to the loss of CO₂ through the porous eggshell, thereby transitioning the albumen to a more alkaline state. However, there remains disagreement among scientists regarding whether pH should be categorised as a physical or chemical property. This ambiguity arises because pH reflects a chemical process (CO₂ loss, altering the hydrogen ion concentration) and is a measurable physical parameter of the albumen of eggs during aging and storage. Regardless, pH changes are widely recognised as reliable indicators of the freshness and storage conditions of eggs11.

Justification

Establishing a connection between bacterial contamination of eggshells and internal egg quality traits, such as albumen and yolk pH, is of scientific significance because it offers a more comprehensive understanding of egg safety and freshness. Previous studies have demonstrated that storage conditions and bacterial metabolism can elevate albumen pH, hasten the reduction in Haugh units, and compromise egg quality7,17–20. Consequently, evaluating microbial load alongside internal traits enables the assessment of both external hygiene status and internal freshness, providing practical indicators that extend beyond the mere presence or absence of pathogens.

Rationale

From a public health perspective, this integration is particularly pertinent in Sudan, where eggs are widely consumed but predominantly sold unrefrigerated in small grocery outlets. Systematic food safety monitoring is limited, and existing research has seldom combined microbial and internal quality assessments. By generating baseline data that link eggshell contamination with internal egg characteristics, this study enhances food safety surveillance by identifying low-cost, field-feasible indicators, such as TVC and albumen pH, which could be incorporated into market inspections and retailer training programs to better protect consumers in resource-limited settings in the future.

To address these issues, this exploratory study was designed to generate baseline data on the safety and quality of table eggs in Khartoum, North. The objectives of this study were (1) to evaluate the prevalence of bacterial contamination in retail table eggs in Khartoum North,

(2) to assess the eggs’ physical characteristics and albumen and yolk pH levels; and.

(3) To examine the correlations between bacterial contamination and egg quality traits, including internal pH and external features.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Khartoum North (locally known as Bahri), the third-largest city in Sudan and a key industrial and commercial hub within the Khartoum metropolitan area. It has an estimated population of approximately 1.6 million people21. Khartoum North is administratively divided into three localities: Bahri, Bahri North, and Rural Bahri. The retail market in this region is diverse and extensive, comprising 2079 retail outlets, including grocery stores, supermarkets, malls, and distribution centres22. This urban environment represents a major food supply centre with a high demand for table eggs, making it a pertinent setting for assessing egg quality and bacterial contamination in retail outlets.

Bahri hosts the majority of Sudan’s commercial egg-producing farms and serves as a central distribution point for table eggs across the wider Khartoum metropolitan area. Given the similar socio-environmental and regulatory conditions across the three cities in the capital, the findings may offer a realistic overview of food safety challenges in Sudan’s largest urban centre.

Study design

This exploratory, cross-sectional, laboratory-based study was conducted in September 2020 in Khartoum North Locality, Khartoum State, Sudan. Khartoum North is divided into three administrative units: A (North Bahri), B (Bahri), and C (Rural Bahri). This study was designed as an exploratory assessment to generate preliminary data on bacterial contamination, physical characteristics, and albumen and yolk pH levels of table eggs in retail outlets across these units. Sampling was proportionally distributed across various retail outlet types within administrative units to ensure their representativeness.

Sampling procedure

The study was conducted across various retail outlets in Khartoum North Locality, which is divided into three administrative units (A, B, and C). The total number of retail outlets in Khartoum North was 2,07922. To ensure representativeness, the sampling was proportionally distributed based on the percentage of each outlet type relative to the total population of outlets. The distribution of outlet types was as follows: grocery stores comprised 93.6% (1,945/2,079), and modern supermarkets comprised 3.3% (69/2,079), 1.8% (37/2,079), and 1.3% (28/2,079). Consequently, grocery stores received the majority of the samples (43/46, 93.5%), while other outlet types were sampled according to their smaller representation: modern supermarkets (1/46, 2.2%), malls (1/46, 2.2%), and distribution centres (3/46, 6.5%). This proportional approach ensures that the sample reflects the local retail structure.

The eggs were obtained directly from retail outlets at the time of purchase, ensuring that they reflected market conditions at the point of sale. The sampled eggs were commercially available table eggs sourced from different suppliers and brands sold at these outlets. For each sample, three eggs were randomly selected by hand while wearing clean gloves, ensuring that there was no preference for size, position, or batch within the outlet. The eggs were placed individually in sterile plastic bags and open carton filler flats. Sterility was maintained, and gloves and hands were sterilised by handling each sample. The eggs were transported by car to the laboratory under ambient temperature conditions, reflecting real-world storage and transport practices while minimising contamination risk.

The samples were transported to the Food Hygiene and Safety Laboratory within two hours of collection25. The samples were not stored after delivery to the laboratory.

Bacterial contamination assessment

Given the limited baseline data on egg microbiology in Sudan, this study targeted a broad spectrum of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria to establish an initial microbial profile. Priority was given to Enterobacteriaceae such as Salmonella spp., Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., and Proteus spp. Owing to their known roles in foodborne illnesses and frequent association with egg contamination in regional and international studies in Sudan4, India26, Thailand5, and Iran27. Additional genera, including Staphylococcus spp. and Listeria spp., were included because of their relevance to food safety. This broad-spectrum approach aligns with the exploratory nature of this study and supports baseline surveillance in low-data settings.

Sample preparation

One hundred thirty-eight eggs were collected and numbered for bacterial examination of the outer shell, albumen, and yolk. The shell samples were assessed using the washing method described by Reu et al.7. Each intact egg was placed in a plastic bag containing 10 mL of quarter-strength Ringer’s solution and rubbed through the bag for 1 min to ensure thorough rinsing. Subsequently, the dilution method described by Damena was used28. The eggs were removed, and a further tenfold dilution was prepared up to 10 −5 by transferring 1 ml aliquots into tubes of dilution blanks containing 9 ml sterile normal saline solution as a diluent. The tubes were vortexed between transfers to ensure uniform homogeneity, as described previously. on nutrient agar to determine the total count of aerobic bacteria and on violet red bile glucose agar (356–4584, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) to enumerate the Enterobacteriaceae. Plates were incubated for 3 and 1 d at 30 °C, according to the standard protocol described in29, for better growth of aerobic and gram-negative bacteria. Bacterial counts were log-transformed before statistical analysis.

The upper half of each egg was wiped twice with alcohol swabs. A sixteen-gauge needle was inserted horizontally into the upper portion of the egg, above the estimated location of the yolk. An albumen sample was collected using a syringe and needle. Once removed from the egg, the albumen was deposited in a sterile Petri dish. A cotton swab was dipped into the albumen and spread onto a Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) plate using the same technique as for the outer shell sample. Using the needle hole as a starting point, the upper half of the eggshell was deconstructed, and the remaining albumen was dispensed into a Petri dish, while preventing the yolk from exiting the remaining shell “cup”, expelling from its membrane, or contacting the exposed outer membrane and shell interface. The yolk was then carefully deposited into a sterile Petri dish, and the remaining shell “cup” was saved. The yolk membrane was breached using sterile forceps and the wooden end of a cotton swab. The cotton swab was then dipped into the yolk and applied to a TSA plate using the technique described by Spitzer30. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Visible colonies were enumerated using a digital Colony Counter (Stuart Colony Counter). Isolation and identification of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria were performed using the diagram from Cowan and Steel’s Manual for the Identification of Medical Bacteria31.

Bacterial identification

Bacterial contaminants were isolated and identified using a structured approach based on the Cowan and Steel Manual for the Identification of Medical Bacteria31. The samples were first cultured on general and selective media to facilitate the growth of a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Initial differentiation was performed using Gram staining, followed by a series of biochemical tests tailored to each bacterial group. This approach allowed the identification of multiple bacterial genera, including Staphylococcus, Escherichia, Salmonella spp, Enterobacter spp, Bacillus spp, Corynebacterium spp, Listeria spp, Legionella spp, Vibrio, Yersinia, Klebsiella spp, Haemophilus spp, Streptococcus spp, Clostridium spp, Kurthia spp, and Brochothrix spp, ensuring a comprehensive microbiological assessment of eggshell contamination.

Colonies isolated on TSA, a general-purpose medium supporting a wide range of bacterial species, were first subjected to Gram staining to differentiate between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Based on the Gram staining results, the identification was performed using a structured approach tailored to the specific characteristics of each bacterial group.

For Gram-positive bacteria, the identification process began with an initial categorisation based on shape, motility, spore formation, and oxygen requirements. Key tests at this stage include Gram staining, catalase and oxidase tests, and anaerobic growth assessment. Genera such as Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus, Clostridium, Listeria, and Corynebacterium were identified during this stage. The second stage involved detailed differentiation based on cultural and biochemical characteristics. Staphylococcus spp. were isolated using Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA). Colonies that appeared yellow due to mannitol fermentation were initially considered presumptive Staphylococcus aureus. However, as other species, such as S. haemolyticus, S. simulans, S. warneri, and S. xylosus, can also produce yellow colonies, further biochemical tests were conducted for a definitive identification. These include catalase, coagulase, oxidase, and urease tests, which ensure accurate differentiation between S. aureus and other Staphylococcus species. Blood Agar was used to differentiate Listeria spp. (weak beta-haemolysis) from Streptococcus spp. (variable haemolysis). Clostridium spp. were cultured under anaerobic conditions on Reinforced Clostridial Agar. Advanced confirmation tests, such as urease production, nitrate reduction, and sugar fermentation pattern analysis, were performed to achieve precise identification. Motility tests were conducted using motility medium and the hanging drop method, with positive motility indicated by diffuse growth or darting movement.

For Gram-negative bacteria, identification began with an initial classification based on shape, motility, and glucose metabolism (oxidation or fermentation). Selective and differential media play a crucial role in this process. For example, MacConkey Agar was used to differentiate lactose fermenters, such as Escherichia coli (pink colonies), from non-lactose fermenters, such as Salmonella spp. Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar was used for Salmonella spp., where red colonies with black centres indicated hydrogen sulfide production. Cefsulodin-Irgasan-Novobiocin (CIN) agar was used to isolate Yersinia spp., characterised by distinctive red “bullseye” colonies. The second stage focused on biochemical assays, such as hydrogen sulfide production, indole, Simmon’s citrate, methyl red (MR), and Voges-Proskauer (VP) tests, to differentiate species. Enrichment media, including Buffered Peptone Water (BPW) and MacConkey Broth, were used to recover sub-lethally injured bacteria and promote growth before plating.

Biochemical tests were pivotal in confirming the isolates for both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial identification. These tests included hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) production, oxidase, catalase, coagulase, and indole tests, as well as fermentation assays to characterise metabolic activity. This structured, multistage approach ensured the accurate identification of bacterial species present in the samples.

Physical characteristics of table eggs

One hundred thirty-eight eggs were assessed using a Digital Egg Tester (DET6000, Japan), and egg weight, shell thickness, shell strength, albumen height, and HU were measured. Eggs were categorised into grades AA, A, B, and C according to the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) standards32, which classify eggs based on Haugh Unit values ranging from 0 to 130. The ranking criteria were as follows: AA (HU ≥ 72), A (HU 71–60), B (HU 59 − 31), and C (HU ≤ 30). The use of the DET6000 to assess these parameters followed the established methodologies validated in previous studies33–35. Visual inspection was not performed.

Albumen and yolk pH values of the table eggs

Yolk and albumen pH values were determined using a Hanna Instruments HI98107 pH tester, as outlined by the New South Wales Food Authority (NSWFA)36. The pH meter was calibrated before each measurement to ensure accuracy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Frequency distributions summarised the data on bacterial contamination. One-way ANOVA was used to assess the differences in physical characteristics (e.g. shell strength and shell thickness) across egg grades (AA, A, and B). Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used to evaluate the relationships between contamination levels and physical traits (e.g. shell thickness and HU). Bayesian methods were used to estimate the mean, standard deviation, and 95% credible intervals of pH values across egg grades.

Results

Bacterial contamination

This study found that all 46 eggshell samples were contaminated with Staphylococcus spp. (89.1%), and Corynebacterium spp. (23.9%) were the most common pathogens detected in eggshells, followed by Staphylococcus spp. Other bacteria, such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp., were found at lower percentages (19.2% and 15.5%, respectively) (Table 1). The bacterial contamination on the outer shell ranged from 3.3 log10 CFU/eggshell to 5.11 log10 CFU per egg, suggesting varying levels of microbial contamination across the samples. No bacterial contamination was observed in the albumen or yolk.

Albumin and yolk pH

Bayesian estimates of pH showed slight variations across the grades. The pH of egg yolks was as follows: Grade AA had a mean of 6.363 (95% CI: 6.135–6.591), Grade A had a mean of 6.548 (95% CI: 6.334–6.762), and Grade B had a mean of 6.555 (95% CI: 6.319–6.791). For egg albumen pH, the means were: Grade AA at 8.463 (95% CI: 8.013–8.914), Grade A at 8.471 (95% CI: 8.047–8.894), and Grade B at 8.604 (95% CI: 8.138–9.071). The 95% credible intervals (CrIs) indicate the probability range within which the true mean pH is likely to fall based on the observed data. Unlike traditional confidence intervals, CrIs offer a direct probabilistic interpretation of the parameter uncertainty. These findings suggest a slight increase in pH from Grade AA to Grade B, particularly in egg albumen. Figure 3 further illustrates this pattern, showing the distribution of albumen pH across grades, with Grade B eggs displaying the highest variability. However, the 95% credible intervals for pH overlapped across grades, indicating that the ranges of plausible pH values for each grade were not entirely distinct, and the observed differences in mean pH values may not have been statistically significant. The low error variance further suggests that the pH measurements were stable and consistent (Table 7.).

Discussion

This study generated baseline data on the safety and quality of the table eggs sold in Khartoum North. Three key findings were obtained. First, bacterial contamination was detected in 100% of the eggshells, with Staphylococcus spp. and Corynebacterium spp. being the most prevalent. Second, while most eggs were classified as Grades AA and A, internal quality traits such as HU and albumen pH varied significantly across grades and retail types, reflecting differences in the freshness and storage conditions of the eggs. Third, a significant positive correlation was observed between the eggshell TVC and albumen pH, suggesting that eggs with higher microbial contamination may also exhibit a weakened internal protective capacity. Together, these findings highlight the prevalence of eggshell contamination in Sudanese retail markets and the potential value of incorporating simple physicochemical measures, such as albumen pH, into food safety surveillance.

This study demonstrated that 100% of the eggshell samples collected from retail outlets in Khartoum North were contaminated with at least one bacterial species. Staphylococcus spp. was the most prevalent isolate (89.1%), followed by Bacillus spp. (26.1%), and Corynebacterium spp. (23.9%), whereas other organisms such as Escherichia coli (19.2%) and Salmonella spp. (15.5%), and Vibrio spp. (17.3%) occurred less frequently than in other countries. These findings highlight the widespread and diverse nature of microbial contamination in table eggs sold in Sudan.

The microbial profile identified in this study was consistent with previous research findings from the same region. Mustafa et al.33. reported bacterial contamination in 75% of retail eggs, primarily involving Bacillus spp. and Staphylococcus spp. Similarly, Eltaib et al.34. documented a wide range of pathogens in animal-source foods, including Salmonella spp., Listeria monocytogenes, Campylobacter jejuni, Escherichia coli, and Staphylococcus aureus. The alignment of these findings with our results underscores the diversity of bacterial communities present on eggshells in local retail markets. This widespread occurrence suggests contamination from environments associated with human and animal handling2,37,38. Notably, the ability of Staphylococcus spp. to produce heat-stable enterotoxins that withstand cooking temperatures poses a significant food safety risk39. Emerging evidence further shows that S. aureus isolated from table eggs can harbour multiple AMR genes (e.g. mecA, blaTEM, tetA, and vanA/C) and enterotoxin-producing capacity, amplifying their threat to consumer health10.

Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp. are widely recognised as indicators of faecal contamination. In this study, E. coli was detected more frequently, likely due to its ubiquitous presence in poultry intestines and persistence in production and retail environments. Its detection strongly suggests that the eggs were exposed to faecal material either during cloacal passage or through unhygienic handling39. In contrast, Salmonella spp. were recovered at a lower rate, which diverges from reports in other regions where it is a leading cause of egg-related foodborne illnesses40. This discrepancy likely reflects the sporadic nature of Salmonella spp. contamination, as it is not uniformly present in all herds, whereas E. coli indicates routine faecal exposure to this pathogen. Differences in detection methods and limited sample sizes may also contribute to low recovery rates41. The detection of multiple pathogens in this study underscores the involvement of several contamination pathways, most likely linked to inadequate sanitation practices and housing system differences that affect bacterial loads42–44. However, as eggs were sourced at the retail level, it was not possible to assess flock-level management practices and housing conditions, thereby limiting the ability to trace the source of contamination.

Notably, despite extensive external contamination, no bacteria were detected in either the yolk or albumen. This finding supports the hypothesis that internal contamination typically occurs through trans-shell penetration under compromised conditions rather than being present at the time of laying45. However, the high prevalence of surface pathogens underscores the potential for inward migration if shells are cracked, dirty, or stored under suboptimal conditions17, thereby emphasising the critical importance of maintaining a hygienic environment throughout the supply chain. It should be noted that in this study, bacterial contamination was expressed as colony-forming units per egg (CFU/egg) rather than CFU per unit surface area (CFU/cm²). Although this approach aligns with several comparable studies, it limits direct comparability with datasets that normalise contamination by surface area. Internal egg quality varied significantly across grades and retail outlets, reflecting both biological freshness and the supply chain practices. Grade AA eggs consistently showed the highest HU values and albumen height, whereas Grade B eggs had the lowest values, and Grade C eggs were absent from all outlets. The retail distribution further emphasised these differences: grocery stores offered a mix of AA and B grades, reflecting variability in handling and storage, whereas supermarkets and distribution centres sold eggs confined to single grades, suggesting stricter procurement standards or shorter storage times.

These patterns highlight how retail type and consumer market dynamics shape egg quality at the point-of-sale. The predominance of high-grade eggs in Khartoum North reflects both supplier strategies and consumer expectations in an urban setting, where the demand for superior-quality products is high. This aligns with previous reports that urban markets often display a skewed distribution favouring high-grade eggs46, and with more recent evidence from Botswana, where supermarket and vendor-sourced eggs also showed marked variation in internal and external quality traits14.

A key finding of this study was the significant positive correlation between the eggshell TVC and albumen pH. Despite the sterility of the internal contents, which indicates no internal contamination, this association is both biologically plausible and important. While this study offers valuable insights, the observed correlation between TVC and albumen pH primarily serves as a hypothesis-generating finding and does not directly aid in food safety risk management without data on internal contamination levels. Previous research has reported lower albumen pH in contaminated eggs18, but our data show alkalinization, which may be explained by three interacting mechanisms. First, ambient storage raises albumen pH and diminishes freshness indicators, such as the HU and yolk index, and elevated TVC has been linked to poor post-lay handling and retail storage17–19,47. Second, common isolates in our samples, notably Staphylococcus spp., can produce alkaline metabolites such as ammonia that diffuse into the albumen and alter the pH; these bacteria can persist under alkaline conditions48. Third, prolonged or suboptimal storage may amplify the effect of surface bacterial loads on internal chemistry, leading to the accumulation of alkaline byproducts and a reduction in the antimicrobial function of the albumen20. Although this correlation does not demonstrate bacterial migration into the egg in our dataset, it underscores that albumen pH is responsive to both storage conditions and external microbial pressure. Measuring albumen pH is inexpensive and rapid; thus, it could serve as a useful, low-cost screening tool to complement microbiological monitoring in low-resource food safety programs19.

In contrast, the correlations between bacterial contamination and other internal egg quality traits were weak and statistically insignificant. Contamination did not seem to influence traits such as egg weight, albumen height, or yolk percentage, which is consistent with the findings of a previous study14. A weak negative trend was observed between egg weight and HU. Although not significant, this trend is consistent with previous studies, indicating that as hens age, egg weight increases, whereas albumen height decreases, leading to lower HU scores44. The HU formula adjusts for weight but may overcompensate for larger eggs, resulting in sharper declines in albumen height measurements. Another study45 confirmed a significant negative correlation between egg weight and HU, suggesting that larger eggs often exhibit reduced albumen quality compared to smaller eggs. Although our findings are exploratory, they align with these biological patterns.

Weak positive correlations were observed between shell thickness and shell strength, suggesting that thicker shells may slightly enhance fracture resistance. Although these associations were not significant, they aligned with mechanical principles and previous studies46. Similarly, weak positive correlations were observed between egg weight, shell thickness, and shell strength. Although these associations are still exploratory, their consistency with biological patterns indicates structural relationships that warrant further investigation in larger samples. The modest positive relationship between egg weight and shell strength further implies that larger eggs may maintain structural integrity through mechanisms beyond mere thickness, possibly reflecting the shell composition or microstructure. As only white-shelled eggs were analysed, this pattern may reflect breed- or type-specific characteristics49,50. Previous research has shown differences in shell strength between brown and white eggs; brown eggs may have thinner shells but greater strength than white eggs50, with diet and hen genetics further influencing shell quality51. Finally, the negative correlation between egg weight and shell thickness observed in this dataset is consistent with studies reporting thinner shells in larger eggs49,52. Notably, eggshell thickness decreases in larger eggs, particularly in extra-large eggs, which are more susceptible to reduced breaking strength and cracking52.

No variation in shell thickness and strength was observed across different grades, indicating that these factors are not primary determinants in the grading process. This finding aligns with our ANOVA results, which showed no significant differences in shell thickness or strength among the grades. This observation supports previous reports suggesting that shell quality is more significantly influenced by the age and nutritional status of hens than by external grading standards53,54. Although shell integrity is crucial for consumer safety, its relative uniformity across grades in our dataset implies that other traits, such as HU and albumen height, are more critical for distinguishing egg quality55.

In contrast, the pH of the albumen showed a slight increase across the lower egg grades, a trend consistent with well-documented biological changes during storage. As carbon dioxide diffuses through the shell, the albumen becomes more alkaline, serving as a recognised indicator of freshness loss54,56,57. Conversely, yolk pH remained relatively stable across grades, indicating greater resistance to change over time. This distinction highlights albumen pH as a sensitive indicator of storage history, whereas yolk pH may be more reliable for industrial applications that require internal consistency58. This is consistent with recent systematic reviews emphasising HU and pH as the most practical and validated parameters for assessing egg quality16. The divergence between albumen and yolk pH patterns underscores the necessity of monitoring both parameters to gain a comprehensive understanding of egg quality.

Despite these important findings, several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting our results. First, sample size and sampling distribution: Although the sample size was calculated based on the number of retail outlets, it may not fully capture the variation in egg quality across different geographic areas, production systems, or retail conditions. Additionally, most samples were collected from grocery stores, resulting in an uneven distribution of outlet types. This skewed sampling reduces the external validity of the study and limits the generalisability of the findings, including bacterial prevalence, egg quality traits, and observed correlations, to other regions or sales points, such as bakeries and local markets. Sampling bias may have led to the underestimation or overestimation of contamination levels or quality variations. Therefore, conclusions regarding contamination and egg safety should be interpreted as being specific to the sampled retail context and not broadly representative of the national patterns. Future studies should use more balanced and geographically diverse sampling strategies to improve external validity and enable robust comparisons across outlet types.

Second, the lack of measurements for the eggshell surface area impeded the normalisation of the TVC per unit area, such as CFU/cm². This limitation affects precision, as reporting total bacterial counts alone may lead to either overestimation or underestimation of contamination, depending on the size of the egg. Consequently, the accuracy of the correlation between shell contamination and internal egg quality traits may be compromised in this study. Furthermore, although reporting contamination as CFU/egg is valid, it reduces comparability with international datasets that express contamination as CFU/cm².

Third, missing information on hen age: Hen age is a well-known factor that significantly affects egg quality traits, such as egg weight, shell thickness, and HU. Without data on the age of the birds producing the eggs, it is challenging to discern whether the observed variations arise from biological aging or external influences, such as handling and storage. The lack of flock-level production information limits the depth and interpretability of our findings regarding the determinants of internal egg quality.

Fourth, the limited characterisation of E. coli isolates poses significant challenges. Although the presence of E. coli on eggshells suggests potential faecal contamination and raises public health concerns, the absence of phenotypic characterisation hampers our ability to evaluate the clinical significance of these isolates. Certain strains, such as enteropathogenic or enterohemorrhagic E. coli, present substantial health risks, especially in the context of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Without pathotyping or AMR profiling, it remains unclear whether the detected E. coli represents a minor hygiene issue or a significant threat to foodborne illness, thereby limiting the robustness of our microbial risk assessment and its public health implications. This limitation reduces the direct public health impact of the study and underscores the need for future research that includes pathogen-specific and AMR analyses of the data. Furthermore, given recent global reports indicating widespread antimicrobial resistance among egg-associated pathogens, including Salmonella spp., E. coli, and S. aureus, addressing this gap is crucial for ensuring food safety and improving public health10.

Fifth, the study used univariate and Bayesian methods suitable for small samples but did not apply multivariate analyses or multiple comparison corrections, limiting the ability to explore complex interactions. Future studies with larger datasets should apply advanced models to obtain deeper insights.

Finally, the absence of visual inspection of external egg quality limited our ability to explore the relationship between shell condition and bacterial contamination. Without evaluating physical defects such as cracks, dirt, or stains, we missed an important factor that could explain variations in the microbial load. This lack of inspection restricts our understanding of how shell damage or cleanliness affects contamination levels, weakening the potential to correlate external quality traits with microbiological safety and thereby limiting insights into contamination sources and pathways.

Conclusion

This study investigated the microbial safety and quality of table eggs in Khartoum, North Sudan. The presence of Staphylococcus spp. on eggshells highlights the need for improved hygiene throughout the supply chain. Although no internal contamination was detected, the bacterial load on the shell was correlated with albumen pH, suggesting that external microbes may influence the internal chemistry. Internal quality traits varied across grades and retail types, reflecting the impact of storage conditions on these parameters. The correlation between the total viable count on eggshells and albumen pH suggests that contaminated eggs may have compromised their defences. Consequently, albumen pH serves as a useful measure for assessing the freshness and contamination risk of eggs in resource-limited settings. These findings underscore the urgent need for policymakers to implement targeted strategies to enhance food safety systems, reduce contamination risks, protect consumer health, and boost confidence in the safety and quality of eggs available in Sudanese markets. These strategies involve incorporating physicochemical assessments, such as albumen pH and HU, into routine monitoring as rapid tools to complement microbiological testing in resource-limited settings; enforcing biosecurity standards and hygienic handling practices, including developing vendor training programs on proper refrigeration and storage of eggs; pilot testing these approaches in local markets; and conducting consumer education campaigns on safe egg handling. Such initiatives could improve food safety monitoring and public health protection in resource-limited environments and support sustainable and cost-effective enhancements in egg safety and quality.

However, the lack of pathogen-specific and antimicrobial resistance profiles limits the direct application of these findings to public health risk management. Furthermore, due to the preliminary and exploratory nature of this study, along with the small and uneven sample sizes, these findings should be interpreted with caution. They are intended to generate hypotheses, emphasising the need for further research involving larger and more representative samples, balanced sampling strategies, and more comprehensive methods to confirm these findings. Such studies will aid in validating and expanding these observations and deepening our understanding of bacterial contamination and egg quality.
