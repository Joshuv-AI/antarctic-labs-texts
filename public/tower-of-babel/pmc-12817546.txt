Serum bilirubin concentrations in peritoneal dialysis patients are correlated with the initial onset of peritonitis
Authors: Jingyi Xie, Xiaoqin Liu, Le Wang, Shuwang Ge, Ying Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to explore the varying association of different bilirubin levels on the prognosis of peritoneal dialysis patients, with particular emphasis on the relationship between the initial occurrence of peritonitis and bilirubin levels, which has not been well elucidated in existing literature.Materials and methodsThis single-center retrospective study enrolled end-stage renal disease patients who underwent peritoneal dialysis catheter placement at Tongji Hospital from January 1, 2009, to December 31, 2023. Follow-up was conducted until October 31, 2024. To explore the relationship between the development of peritonitis and bilirubin levels, we utilized Cox proportional hazards regression models, Spearman correlation analysis, and restricted cubic spline plots.ResultsThis study included 426 patients, with the sum of 106 episodes of peritonitis were documented, of which 57 were first-time occurrences, making for an incidence rate of 0.09 episodes per patient-year. Patients with total bilirubin (TBil) levels below 3.69 µmol/L (P = 0.008, HR = 1.919, 95% CI: 1.188–3.099) or direct bilirubin (DBil) levels below 1.46 µmol/L (P < 0.001, HR = 2.969, 95% CI: 1.755–5.023) exhibited a greater likelihood of peritonitis occurrence compared to those in the higher-level groups.ConclusionSerum bilirubin, especially total and direct bilirubin, exhibits association with the risk of initial peritonitis occurrence in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD). Low levels of total and direct bilirubin are associated with an increased risk of the initial onset of peritonitis in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Bilirubin, Peritoneal dialysis, Peritonitis, Pathogenic microorganism, ISPD guidelines

Background

In the therapeutic landscape of chronic kidney disease (CKD), peritoneal dialysis (PD) plays a vital role as a critical renal replacement therapy [1, 2], offering life-sustaining support to numerous patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). However, peritonitis remains one of the most prevalent complications of PD, significantly impacting patient quality of life and potentially leading to dialysis failure, increased technical failure rates, and higher mortality [3, 4]. Investigating the factors influencing the incidence of peritonitis is crucial for improving patient outcomes.

Bilirubin, a byproduct of hemoglobin metabolism, is categorized into direct bilirubin (DBil) and indirect bilirubin (IBil). Traditionally, bilirubin has been primarily recognized as an important biomarker for assessing liver function [5]. However, as an endogenous antioxidant, bilirubin demonstrates anti-inflammatory properties under specific conditions and may confer protective effects on the kidneys [6–9]. For PD patients, elevated total bilirubin (TBil) levels have been related to a higher risk of treatment failure in cases of peritonitis [10], while high TBil and IBil levels were significantly linked to increased 5-year mortality [11]. Conversely, excessively low TBil levels have been correlated with residual renal function loss in PD patients [12], and TBil exhibited a U-shaped relationship with 3-year mortality, with the lowest mortality rates observed in patients maintaining concentrations between 0.5 and 0.6 mg/dL [13]. These conflicting results highlight the complex biological effects of bilirubin.

Given the intricate prognostic implications of bilirubin on PD patients and the discrepancies among existing studies, especially the lack of knowledge regarding the relation between the first occurrence of peritonitis and bilirubin levels, this study aimed to explore the correlation between the initial occurrence of peritonitis and bilirubin at the time of catheter placement through a retrospective analysis, thereby providing a scientific basis for enhancing patient prognosis.

Methods

Population and study design

This retrospective analysis focused on a cohort of ESRD patients treated with peritoneal dialysis at a single medical center from January 1, 2009, to December 31, 2023, catheter placement at Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (HUST), with follow-up until October 31, 2024. All patients received continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD). The exclusion criteria were: (1) PD treatment duration < 3 months, (2) age < 18 years, (3) lack of baseline data, (4) peritonitis within 30 days of catheter placement, (5) hemodialysis for more than three months or a history of renal transplantation, (6) patients with viral hepatitis, and (7) aspartate aminotransferase (AST) or alanine aminotransferase (ALT) > 40 IU/L.

Clinical data collection

Baseline laboratory results were collected from 7 days before peritoneal dialysis (PD) catheter placement to 7 days after placement, with repeated measurements averaged. Data extraction was conducted using the YIDUCloud platform and included demographic information, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia), medications (proton pump inhibitors, antibiotics, immunosuppressants), and laboratory markers (white blood cell counts, percentage of mononuclear cells, hemoglobin, serum albumin, creatinine, glucose, ferritin, parathyroid hormone, plasminogen activity, direct bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, total bilirubin, serum phosphorus, serum calcium, serum magnesium, serum sodium, serum chloride, and serum potassium).

Outcome measures

The first occurrence of peritonitis was the primary outcome, while secondary outcomes included bacterial peritonitis and culture-negative peritonitis. Peritonitis diagnosis required at least two of the following criteria [14]: (1) clinical features consistent with peritonitis, such as turbidity in the effluent and/or abdominal pain; (2) effluent leukocyte counts exceeding 100/µl or 0.1 × 109/L (with at least 2 h of abdominal retention), with a polymorphonuclear leukocyte (PMN) proportion greater than 50%; and (3) positive bacterial culture of the effluent. Cases meeting criteria (1) and (2) but with negative bacterial cultures were defined as culture-negative peritonitis. Positive bacterial cultures in the effluent indicated bacterial peritonitis.

Follow-up endpoints included any of the following: first occurrence of peritonitis, renal transplantation, death, conversion to hemodialysis, transfer to another center, loss to follow-up, or follow-up until October 31, 2024.

Statistical analysis

Using R version 4.4.2 and SPSS 25.0, statistical analyses were performed. Trends in the relation between serum bilirubin and the first occurrence of peritonitis were analyzed using restricted cubic spline (RCS) plots. The relationship between DBil and clinical variables was assessed using Spearman’s correlation analysis.

Patients were divided into two groups based on the Youden index derived from ROC analysis of TBil, DBil, and IBil: (1) low direct bilirubin (LDBIL) is defined as a DBIL level below 1.46 µmol/L, and high direct bilirubin (HDBIL), characterized by a DBIL level of 1.46 µmol/L or higher; (2) low indirect bilirubin (LIBIL) is defined as a IBIL level below 2.02 µmol/L, and high indirect bilirubin (HIBIL), characterized by a IBIL level of 2.02 µmol/L or higher; (3) low total bilirubin (LTBIL) is defined as a TBIL level below 3.69 µmol/L, and high total bilirubin (HTBIL), characterized by a TBIL level of 3.69 µmol/L or higher. These values are calculated from single-center data. If verified in external data, they will achieve broader clinical applicability.

Continuous variables were described using the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and the median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with between-group differences assessed using chi-square tests.

Using Log-Rank tests and Kaplan-Meier curves to analyze the influence of bilirubin grouping on the first occurrence of peritonitis during follow-up. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to examine the relation between serum bilirubin and the first occurrence of peritonitis. Model 1 of the multivariate analysis adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 added comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia), medications (proton pump inhibitors, antibiotics, immunosuppressants), and clinical variables (leukocyte counts, monocyte percentages, glucose, and ferritin) to Model 1. Results were reported using hazard ratios (HR) and corresponding two-sided 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A P-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using subgroup analyses to account for potential interactions. Subgroups included age (< 60 or ≥ 60 years), hypertension (yes or no), sex (male or female), and diabetes (yes or no). Considering those other outcomes (renal transplantation, conversion to hemodialysis, and death) might influence the relationship between the first occurrence of peritonitis and serum bilirubin, a competing risk model (Fine-Gray model) was incorporated into the multivariate Model 2.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

This study ultimately enrolled 426 patients (Fig. 1), with a median follow-up duration of 782.5 days. 106 episodes of peritonitis were recorded, of which 57 were initial episodes, making for an incidence rate of 0.09 episodes per patient-year. This includes bacterial peritonitis at 0.05 episodes per patient-year, culture-negative peritonitis at 0.03 episodes per patient-year, and fungal peritonitis at less than 0.01 episodes per patient-year. Table 1 summarized baseline characteristics. Patients had a mean age of 42.51 ± 12.66 years. Among them, 208 (49.17%) were male, 319 (75.41%) were hypertensive, and 24 (5.67%) had diabetes. Patients in the LTBIL group showed lower laboratory indices, suggesting potential malnutrition or poorer overall health status.

DBil was negatively correlated with serum chloride (r = -0.104), serum sodium (r = -0.097), and white blood cell count (r = -0.096), P < 0.05.

Correlation analysis between clinical variables and direct bilirubin concentration

Possible linear correlation between first occurrence of peritonitis and serum bilirubin levels

In this study, restricted cubic spline plots were used to assess whether the nonlinear correlation was between serum bilirubin levels and the first occurrence of peritonitis. The results showed (Fig. 2) that there was an overall correlation between the first occurrence of peritonitis and TBil (P = 0.035), but this correlation appeared to be linear (P = 0.821). Similarly, there was an overall correlation for DBil (P = 0.001), which also appeared to be linear (P = 0.205). The first occurrence of peritonitis was not significantly correlated with IBil (P = 0.190).

Association of serum bilirubin with first occurrence of peritonitis

Analysis of Kaplan-Meier curves (Fig. 3) demonstrated that the first occurrence of peritonitis differed significantly between patients grouped by TBil (P < 0.05) and those grouped by DBil (P < 0.01). The cumulative incidence was lower in the high-level groups.

Compared to the control group, univariate Cox regression analysis (Table 3) indicated that the risk of peritonitis was higher in the LDBIL (HR = 2.969, 95% CI: 1.755–5.023) and LTBIL groups (HR = 1.919, 95% CI: 1.188–3.099), P < 0.01. This result was consistent in multivariate Model 1 (HR = 2.931, 95% CI: 1.729–4.968; HR = 1.893, 95% CI: 1.172–3.058) and Model 2 (HR = 2.598, 95% CI: 1.129–5.978; HR = 2.552, 95% CI: 1.328–4.905), P < 0.05. For bacterial peritonitis, the difference was also significant (HR = 8.316, 95% CI: 1.811–38.184; HR = 2.552, 95% CI: 1.328–4.905), P < 0.01.

Sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analyses (Table 4) revealed that in the subgroup below 60 years of age and in the female subgroup, both LDBIL (HR = 2.865, 95% CI: 1.16–7.076; HR = 13.122, 95% CI: 2.637–65.292) and LTBIL (HR = 2.791, 95% CI: 1.342–5.805; HR = 4.496, 95% CI: 1.402–14.422) were related with a higher risk of peritonitis, P < 0.05. LTBIL was also a risk factor in the hypertensive subgroup (HR = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.24–6.414) and the non-diabetic subgroup (HR = 2.625, 95% CI: 1.25–5.514), P < 0.05. Interaction was between DBil and diabetes (P = 0.035), and between TBil and hypertension (P = 0.015).

In the competing risk model analysis, after controlling for competing risk events (renal transplantation, conversion to hemodialysis, and death), with covariates included in Model 2, LDBIL was a significant independent predictor of peritonitis (HR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.496–4.379, P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study was designed to determine how serum bilirubin levels at the time of catheter placement are related to the first occurrence of peritonitis in peritoneal dialysis patients. Through long-term follow-up of 426 peritoneal dialysis patients, 57 first episodes of peritonitis were recorded. LTBil and LDBil were significantly related with an increased risk of the first episode of peritonitis. Competing risk modeling further confirmed that LDBil increased the risk of peritonitis development.

In this study, DBil was positively correlated with albumin, hemoglobin, the percentage of monocytes, serum calcium, and plasminogen activity, and negatively correlated with serum chloride, white blood cell count, and serum sodium. These correlations suggest that serum bilirubin levels may reflect the patient’s overall nutritional status, immune function, and metabolic status. Notably, patients in the low serum bilirubin group had lower hemoglobin and plasminogen activities and higher parathyroid hormone levels, which may imply the potential deficiencies in iron metabolism, coagulation processes, and mineral balance.

Studies have shown that elevated bilirubin levels are associated with better outcomes in several infectious diseases, such as experimental endotoxemia [15] and infections after shoulder and knee arthroplasty [16]. The possible reason is that bilirubin, the end product of heme degradation, acts as an antioxidant, clearing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reducing oxidative stress [17]. This antioxidative property is crucial in combating infections by reducing inflammation and enhancing immune responses [18, 19]. In animal experiments, bilirubin increased the survival rate of mice with sepsis by inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [20]. Bilirubin has also been shown to inhibit the pro-inflammatory signaling, such as NF-κB [21], thus dampening the inflammatory response and promoting faster recovery from infections.

This study demonstrated a statistically significant relationship between LDBil and LTBil and an elevated risk of peritonitis. Conversely, no such association was observed for IBil. IBil, being water-insoluble, typically requires binding to albumin for transport in the bloodstream [22]. Compared to DBil, IDBil is lower water solubility may limit its accessibility and effectiveness as a primary active form of bilirubin. Changes in DBil have been more significant in predicting hyperlipidemia [23], cardiovascular mortality [24], and adverse outcomes following thrombolysis in ischemic stroke [25]. However, IBil has shown superiority in predicting postoperative survival in non-small cell lung cancer [26], motor subtypes of Parkinson’s disease [27], and sarcopenia [28]. The differences and complexities between these two forms warrant further investigation through additional studies.

Sensitivity analyses revealed a significant impact of LTBIL and LDBIL on the risk of peritonitis in specific subgroups, including individuals under 60 years old, females, those with hypertension, and individuals without diabetes. Research indicates that liver function declines with age, which may result in decreased efficiency of bilirubin metabolism and accelerate the aging process of red blood cells [29]. Younger individuals tend to have relatively lower bilirubin levels, and the association between low bilirubin levels and peritonitis is more obvious. Among people with low bilirubin levels, females outnumber males [29, 30], which contributes to observed gender differences in the relationship with peritonitis. In addition, interactions between DBil and diabetes, as well as between TBil and hypertension, were also found. Previous studies have shown that bilirubin exerts protective effects by inhibiting the generation of ROS in vascular cells of patients with hypertension and diabetes [31–35].

Despite the valuable insights gained regarding serum bilirubin and peritonitis, the study has limitations, including its single-center design and small sample size. Second, despite considering multiple covariates, the results may be affected by potential confounders not included in the analysis. Although we excluded patients with liver function abnormalities that might affect bilirubin levels, other conditions, such as hemolytic anemia and Gilbert’s syndrome, can also cause elevated bilirubin levels. Therefore, in clinical practice, the conclusions of this article should be comprehensively considered. Future studies should expand the sample size and adopt a multicenter, prospective design to comprehensively assess the association between the risk of peritonitis and serum bilirubin levels, as well as the specific mechanisms by which bilirubin contributes to infection defense in peritoneal dialysis patients.

Conclusion

Low levels of total bilirubin and direct bilirubin at the time of catheter placement in peritoneal dialysis patients were significantly related with an increased risk of the peritonitis first occurrence, particularly in cases of bacterial peritonitis.
