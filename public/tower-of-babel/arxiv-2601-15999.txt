Graph Topology Identification Based on Covariance Matching
Yongsheng Han, Raj Thilak Rajan, and Geert Leus

Abstract
Graph topology identification (GTI) is a central challenge in networked systems, where the underlying structure is often hidden, yet nodal data are available. Conventional solutions to address these challenges rely on probabilistic models or complex optimization formulations, commonly suffering from non-convexity or requiring restrictive assumptions on acyclicity or positivity. In this paper, we propose a novel covariance matching (CovMatch) framework that directly aligns the empirical covariance of the observed data with the theoretical covariance implied by an underlying graph. We show that as long as the data-generating process permits an explicit covariance expression, CovMatch offers a unified route to topology inference. We showcase our methodology on linear structural equation models (SEMs), showing that CovMatch naturally handles both undirected and general sparse directed graphs - whether acyclic or positively weighted - without explicit knowledge of these structural constraints. Through appropriate reparameterizations, CovMatch simplifies the graph learning problem to either a conic mixed integer program for undirected graphs or an orthogonal matrix optimization for directed graphs. Numerical results confirm that, even for relatively large graphs, our approach efficiently recovers the true topology and outperforms standard baselines in accuracy. These findings highlight CovMatch as a powerful alternative to log-determinant or Bayesian methods for GTI, paving the way for broader research on learning complex network topologies with minimal assumptions.



I
Introduction

Graph topology identification (GTI) remains a critical problem in graph signal processing (GSP), where systems are modeled as networks, yet their actual underlying structure is often invisible. Examples of such systems include brain functional connectivity networks and social networks. In these applications, while the direct graph structure is not observable, nodal data is typically available. For instance, in academic networks
[
20
]
, the advisor-advisee links may not be visible, yet we can analyze collaborative patterns to uncover these connections. Similarly, in brain networks
[
22
]
, neural signals provide indirect clues about the connectivity. Therefore, the primary challenge in graph topology identification lies in deducing the hidden graph structure from these nodal observations, a task that is fundamental for analyzing and understanding the interactions within these networks.

A widely used approach to represent such hidden structures is the linear structural equation model (SEM)
[
25
,
7
,
9
,
12
]
.
Note that although nonlinear versions of the SEM exist, we solely focus on the linear version in this paper, and will drop the term “linear” from now on.
Under known exogenous inputs, it has been shown that, with diagonal input coupling and sufficiently rich exogenous signals, the underlying directed network can be uniquely recovered
[
9
]
.
However, when the exogenous variables remain unobserved, the SEM is often coupled with a directed acyclic graph (DAG) constraint, which is widely used for causal inference
[
26
,
35
]
.
Initially, it was shown that a score‑based search for the optimal DAG is NP‑hard
[
13
]
. At that juncture, identifiability was an open problem, which hindered early progress in both theory and practice, thereby confining most efforts to small-scale graphs.
This deadlock was first broken in the case of non-Gaussian disturbances by the so-called linear non-Gaussian acyclic model (LiNGAM), which established that the underlying DAG can be uniquely identified
[
34
]
.
For the Gaussian case, this breakthrough was presented in
[
27
]
, showing that DAG structures are indeed identifiable under certain conditions and further proposing a greedy search method to recover the underlying graph.

The above foundational works paved the way for subsequent developments, resulting in more efficient algorithms such as those in
[
38
,
10
,
31
]
, where the acyclicity constraint is replaced by powerful smooth characterizations that enable gradient-based optimization. These methods have achieved impressive performance, handling larger graphs with faster runtimes and lower estimation errors.
Nevertheless, many real-world networks deviate from the DAG assumption, and learning more general directed graphs remains challenging. Existing approaches usually rely on a greedy search or additional metrics to guide optimization
[
3
,
37
]
, leaving considerable space for further improvements.

In addition to directed graphs, undirected graphs have also been studied within the SEM framework, and various methods have been proposed to address structure learning. For instance, approaches based on spectral templates (SpecTemp) leverage the eigen-decomposition of the data covariance matrix to estimate the underlying undirected topology
[
33
]
. These methods do not assume a specific graph filter or restrict its functional form, and thus often require a large number of samples.
Another eigen-based method is the covariance matching (CovMatch) framework introduced in
[
17
]
, which streamlines the estimation process by converting the graph estimation task into a binary variable inference problem. However, CovMatch formulated in
[
17
]
only enforces the hollowness constraint (no self-loops) and does not use additional priors such as sparsity, so accurate recovery typically requires very large sample sizes even SEM structure is explicitly considered.
Building on this idea, in this work we extend CovMatch to more general settings, demonstrating that it effectively handles both undirected graphs and general directed graphs with only a sparsity constraint on the number of active edges. Our proposed approach easily integrates additional constraints when needed and, even without explicit constraints, can still recover diverse types of graphs, including DAGs and positively weighted directed graphs.

I-A

Contributions

In this work, we propose a novel framework for GTI based on CovMatch. The overarching idea is remarkably simple: as long as the data-generating model permits an explicit expression for the covariance, our approach provides a pathway toward estimating the underlying graph. The key contributions are as follows:

Unified covariance matching formulation

We introduce a versatile framework that aligns the empirical covariance with the theoretical covariance derived from an underlying SEM. Although this paper focuses on an SEM, the proposed approach inherently accommodates any model whose covariance can be computed explicitly.

General sparse directed graphs

To the best of our knowledge, our method is the first to handle general sparse directed graphs through a covariance matching paradigm for an SEM. It accurately infers network structures that go beyond the usual DAGs or undirected graphs.

Robustness without prior knowledge

A notable feature is that our approach relies solely on the graph’s inherent sparsity and requires no additional priors. No assumptions regarding acyclicity or positive edge weights are required, yet empirical results show strong performance across diverse network types.

Scalability to larger graphs

We demonstrate that the proposed method can address relatively large graphs—even those that are not especially sparse—thanks to its binary or orthogonal variable reparameterization and efficient solution strategies. This scalability is particularly useful in real-world applications where network size is large.

Taken together, these contributions demonstrate that CovMatch can serve as a powerful method for GTI. By leveraging the generality of the covariance structure, our framework opens the door to new research directions in learning complex undirected and directed graphs.

I-C

Graph Signal Processing

This subsection introduces the key concepts and notation from graph signal processing (GSP) that underpin our framework. We begin by defining the basic elements of a graph, its corresponding graph shift operator (GSO), and the notion of a graph signal.

Graphs

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
denote a graph, where
\mathcal{V}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
is the set of nodes and
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
is the set of edges. For a
directed
edge from node
j
to node
i
, we write
(j,i)\in\mathcal{E}
. In the
undirected
case, whenever
j
and
i
are connected, both
(j,i)
and
(i,j)
belong to
\mathcal{E}
. We do not consider self-loops, so
(i,i)\notin\mathcal{E}
.

Graph shift operator (GSO)

A graph can be represented by a shift operator
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, which shares the same support as
\mathcal{E}
. Specifically,
S_{ij}\neq 0
only if
(j,i)\in\mathcal{E}
or
i=j
. Common choices include the adjacency matrix or (normalized) Laplacian. In this work, we adopt the adjacency matrix, and since we exclude self-loops,
\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{S})=\mathbf{0}
.

Graph signals

A graph signal is formed by assigning a scalar value to each node, resulting in an
N\times 1
vector
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N}]^{\top}
. The ordering of entries in
\mathbf{x}
corresponds to the ordering of nodes in
\mathbf{S}
. GSP often assumes a relationship between the graph signal
\mathbf{x}
and the GSO
\mathbf{S}
. While many such relationships exist, the next section introduces the SEM, which links
\mathbf{x}
directly to
\mathbf{S}
via a set of linear dependencies.

II
Graph Topology Identification

Graph topology identification (GTI) aims to recover the underlying matrix
\mathbf{S}
(e.g., an adjacency matrix or more general graph shift operator) from data observations. Concretely, let
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be a collection of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
drawn independently from a parametric distribution

p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{S})
,
where
\mathbf{S}
embodies the unknown network structure. Depending on the specific modeling assumptions,
\mathbf{S}
may represent an undirected graph, a directed graph, or a more specialized structure (e.g., a DAG).

Characterising
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{S})
typically requires assumptions on the data-generating mechanism. In this paper, we focus on an SEM as an illustrative example for our study. Nonetheless, it is important to note that our approach has the potential to be applied to any model whose covariance structure can be computed explicitly.

II-A

Structural Equation Model

In a structural equation model (SEM), each random vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is generated according to the equation
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
, where
{\bf S}
represents the weighted adjacency matrix of the graph and
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denotes unknown exogenous variables or, equivalently, a noise term. By rearranging the terms in the equation, one obtains
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1}\,\mathbf{e}
. For convenience, we define
\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1}
, such that each entry of
\mathbf{x}
can be interpreted as a weighted combination of the entries of
\mathbf{e}
, specifically
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{e}
.

If
\mathbf{e}
is zero mean with covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}
, then
{\bf x}
is zero mean with covariance

\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-\top}=\mathbf{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\mathbf{H}^{\top}.

(1)

A particular instance arises when
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}=\mathbf{I}
, which leads to
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top}
, simplifying to

\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{H}^{2}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}

when
\mathbf{S}
is symmetric (undirected graph).

We construct
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{T}]
and
\mathbf{E}=[\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{T}]
, where
T
is the number of samples. The matrix form of the SEM then becomes

\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{E}.

(2)

II-B

Maximum Likelihood Estimation and Its Limitations

In the special case where the exogenous variables are Gaussian, i.e., the noise is Gaussian, we obtain
p({\bf x}|{\bf S})=\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}})
, where
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}
is parameterized by
{\bf S}
as in (
1
).
In other words, we obtain

p(\mathbf{x}|{\mathbf{S}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\bigl|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}\bigr|^{1/2}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\Bigr).

(3)

The negative log-likelihood (up to additive constants) for the set of vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
(or the matrix
{\bf X}
) thus yields the classical objective function
[
21
]

f(\hat{\bf S})=\log\det\bigl(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}\bigr)+\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}),\quad

(4)

where

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}

denotes the sample covariance matrix. Note that here we use a hat on
\hat{\bf S}
and
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}
to indicate that they are optimization variables. This is in order to distinguish them from the ground truth
{\bf S}
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}
. The same will hold for other symbols throughout this manuscript.

Direct optimization of (
4
) is difficult, as the log-determinant term introduces strong non-convexity. A common workaround is to drop this component, leading to a signal matching (SigMatch) objective
\|\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}

[
5
]
.
Such treatment is widely adopted in DAG settings, where
\log\!\det(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}})
is equal to
\log\!\det({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{e}})
and thus not related to
\hat{\mathbf{S}}

[
27
]
. When
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}
is known, the log-determinant term reduces to a constant and can consequently be omitted
[
38
]
.
For general graphs, however, eliminating the log–determinant term does not guarantee convergence to the correct solution, even in the undirected case (see Appendix
-A
).

In this work, our aim is to develop strategies that bypass the complexities of optimizing the log-determinant term, without resorting to overly simplistic approximations. This preserves the potential to recover the correct network structure. In the next two sections, we present our covariance matching (CovMatch) framework for achieving this balance and derive a tractable optimization problem that can handle both undirected and directed graphs.

III
Covariance Matching

Instead of directly working with the likelihood, we adopt a covariance matching (CovMatch) approach. The motivation stems from the fact that, for a zero-mean Gaussian distribution, the covariance matrix fully characterizes the underlying distribution. Consequently, a natural idea is to align the sample covariance
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}=\tfrac{1}{T}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
with the theoretical covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}
. Recalling from (
1
) that

\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\mathbf{H}^{\top},\quad\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1},

(5)

we aim to solve

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{*}=\arg\min_{\hat{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathscr{H}}\|\hat{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\|_{F}^{2},

(6)

where
\mathscr{H}
denotes the feasible set of
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
implied by the relevant graph structural assumptions (e.g., prohibiting self-loops, enforcing sparsity, requiring a DAG, guaranteeing positive weights, or imposing symmetry). By focusing on second-order statistics directly, this formulation provides a flexible alternative to log-determinant-based methods and can readily incorporate additional constraints as dictated by the application context.

Although the above objective remains non-trivial to solve in practice, it is worth noting that in the absence of any structural constraints, the best we can do is to estimate
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
such that it exactly reconstructs
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
. Moreover, as
T\to\infty
, if the correct
\mathbf{H}
(or equivalently
\mathbf{S}
) is identified, the objective converges to zero.
This observation motivates a different perspective in which we reverse the roles of the objective and the constraints:

\begin{array}[]{c@{\hspace{0.4em}}c@{\hspace{0.4em}}c}\text{Classical}\hfil\hskip 3.99994pt&\hfil\hskip 3.99994pt&\text{CovMatch}\\[3.99994pt]
\begin{aligned} \min_{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}\;\;&\big\|\hat{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\\
\text{s.t.}\;\;&\hat{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathscr{H}\end{aligned}\hfil\hskip 3.99994pt&\ \rightarrow\hfil\hskip 3.99994pt&\begin{aligned} \min_{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}\;\;&r_{\mathscr{H}}(\hat{\bf H})\\
\text{s.t.}\;\;&\hat{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\end{aligned}\end{array},

(7)

where
r_{\mathscr{H}}(\hat{\bf H})
represents a regularization term related to the constraint
\hat{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathscr{H}
.
In this formulation, instead of directly optimizing
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
to minimize the covariance fitting term, we collect all matrices
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
that exactly fit the sample covariance matrix and then select the one that best satisfies our desired constraints. Note that applying that same reversal of objective and constraints in the maximum likelihood formulation leads to an equivalent outcome (see Appendix
-B
).

Inspired by the CovMatch perspective, we next examine what can be inferred from the equality

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}},

(8)

which holds when the objective is forced to be zero. We show how to map this condition into a more structured, lower-dimensional variable estimation problem, thereby simplifying the subsequent optimization. Specifically, this section handles the covariance matching objective for the undirected and directed cases separately, while deferring structural constraints to the next section. For clarity, we focus on a special case, i.e.,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}=\mathbf{I}
, and relegate the discussion on the general scenario to Appendix
-C
.

III-A

Undirected Graphs via Eigenvalue Decomposition

When the graph is undirected and the noise covariance is
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}=\mathbf{I}
, our goal is to enforce
\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{2}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
. Since
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
is symmetric, an eigenvalue decomposition (EVD) is the natural choice of decomposition. Hence, rather than estimating
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
directly, we parameterize it via its eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Specifically, assuming
\hat{\mathbf{H}}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\lambda}})\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}
, we obtain
\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{2}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\lambda}}^{2})\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}
.
Similarly, let

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}

be its EVD. Matching
\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{2}
to
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
implies that we can set

\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}

and

\hat{\bm{\lambda}}^{2}=\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}
.
However, enforcing
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}^{2}=\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}
introduces a sign ambiguity for each eigenvalue. To resolve it, we define a binary vector
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}
as an estimate of
\operatorname{sign}(\bm{\lambda})
and rewrite

\,\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=\operatorname{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\,\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}
, where
(\cdot)^{1/2}
denotes the positive square root.
As a result, we obtain

\hat{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}\bigr)\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top},

(9)

and substituting
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
into

\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}

yields

\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\bigr)\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top},

(10)

where we make use of the fact that
\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{-1}=\hat{\mathbf{q}}
.
Ultimately, the estimation task reduces to determining
\hat{\mathbf{q}}
, which captures the sign ambiguity of each eigenvalue. Note that when
T\rightarrow\infty
, we can only
identify a valid ground truth
{\bf U}

(i.e., the eigenvectors of the ground truth
{\bf H}
) when
{\bf H}
has no two non-zero eigenvalues that are negatives of each other, i.e.,

\nexists\,i\neq j:\ \lambda_{i}=-\lambda_{j}

(see Appendix
-D
).

III-B

Directed Graphs via Singular Value Decomposition

A similar method is applied in the directed graph scenario, where

\hat{\mathbf{H}}=(\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{S}})^{-1}

may be non-symmetric, yet we still enforce

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\,\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
.
Now, let

\,\hat{\mathbf{H}}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\lambda}})\,\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}

be its singular value decomposition (SVD), and let

\,\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}

be again its EVD. Then matching

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\,\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\lambda}}^{2})\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}
to

\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}
means we can select
\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}

and

\,\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}
.
We now obtain

\hat{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}\bigr)\,\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top},

(11)

and substituting
\hat{\bf H}
into

\,\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}

yields

\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\bigr)\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}.

(12)

Unlike the undirected case, singular values are nonnegative by design, so there is no separate sign ambiguity. Instead, any remaining freedom is subsumed in the orthogonal matrix factor
\hat{\mathbf{V}}
, creating a rotation ambiguity. On the one hand, this makes the directed scenario more challenging, but on the other hand, when
T\rightarrow\infty
,
we can now always identify a valid ground truth
{\bf U}

(i.e., the left singular vectors of the ground truth
{\bf H}
) and hence no further assumptions are required as in the undirected case (see Appendix
-E
).

IV
Incorporating Structural Constraints

Thus far, we have focused on matching the model covariance to the sample covariance without imposing explicit structural requirements.
In practice, however, domain knowledge often dictates additional constraints: for example, no self-loops in neuronal connectivity graphs
[
29
]
, sparse interactions in large-scale networks, where each node only connects to a limited subset of others
[
8
]
, directed acyclicity in causal inference models
[
26
]
, or positive edge weights
[
33
]
.
In this section, we illustrate two representative constraints, each of which can be implemented via an appropriate penalty function
h(\hat{\mathbf{S}})
.

IV-A

No Self-Loops

A common requirement in certain graph structures is the absence of self-loops, i.e., the diagonal entries of the adjacency matrix
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
should be zero.
Translating this hard constraint into an objective-based formulation, we can introduce the penalty term

h_{\mathrm{diag}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})=\|\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})\|_{2}^{2},

(13)

thereby encouraging the diagonal to be small without strictly forcing it to be zero.

For the undirected graph problem, a diagonal constraint is often sufficient. Intuitively, we only need to resolve
N
binary variables
\hat{q}_{i}
in
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
as defined in (
10
), and enforcing
\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})=\mathbf{0}
supplies
N
equations. In Appendix
-D
, we provide a rigorous proof of when and why this condition leads to a successful recovery when
T\to\infty
.

In the directed case, however, the impact of the diagonal constraint diminishes as the size of the graph increases. This is because the relevant orthogonal matrix
\hat{\bf V}
in
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
has
\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
degrees of freedom, far exceeding the
N
equations imposed by
\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})={\bf 0}
.

IV-B

Sparsity

Many real-world graphs exhibit a limited number of connections between nodes. To promote sparsity, we simply introduce an
\ell_{1}
-type penalty on
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
:

h_{\mathrm{sparse}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})=\|\hat{\mathbf{S}}\|_{1},

(14)

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the elementwise absolute sum. This penalty, similar in spirit to the Lasso in sparse regression
[
15
]
, shrinks small coefficients toward zero while retaining larger ones, thus favoring a solution with fewer nonzero entries.

Beyond a global
\ell_{1}
penalty, one may also add a term that limits the number of active edges per node. For instance, augmenting the objective with a function of the form
\max_{i}\|[\hat{\mathbf{S}}]_{i,:}\|_{1}
(or
\max_{j}\|[\hat{\mathbf{S}}]_{:,j}\|_{1}
) bounds the total connection strength emanating from (or incoming to) each node, thereby controlling the maximum number of nonzero edges per row or column in a relaxed sense.

IV-C

Combining Constraints with CovMatch

Combining the structural constraints (
13
) and (
14
) leads to a purely structural objective of the form

h(\hat{\mathbf{S}})=h_{\mathrm{diag}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})+\alpha h_{\mathrm{sparse}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}),

(15)

where
\alpha\geq 0
adjusts the relative importance of sparsity over hollowness (no self-loops). In fact, additional constraints can readily be incorporated within the same framework, such as enforcing positive edge weights, bounding node degrees, or imposing a DAG structure. However, even without these extra constraints, our method already performs very well; in the experiments, we illustrate this point using the DAG case as an example in Section
VII-B
.

For an undirected graph,
combining
h(\hat{\mathbf{S}})
with the covariance matching constraint (
10
), we obtain the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}

\displaystyle h\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}\bigr)

(16)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\bigr)\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}.

Similarly, for a directed graph, combining
h(\hat{\mathbf{S}})
with the corresponding covariance matching constraint (
12
) yields

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{V}}:\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{I}}

\displaystyle h\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}\bigr)

(17)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{V}}\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\bigr)\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}.

In the next sections, we will study these two problems in more detail.

V
Covariance Matching for Undirected SEM

We first focus on the undirected graph setting.
Combining the purely structural objective
h(\hat{\mathbf{S}})
from (
15
) with the covariance matching constraint from (
10
) yields

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{diag}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})+\alpha h_{\mathrm{sparse}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})

(18)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\bigr)\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}.

Here, the only additional constraints we consider are the hollowness and sparsity requirements.

V-A

Hollowness Constraint

Substituting the expression for
\hat{\bf S}
from (
18
) into
h_{\mathrm{diag}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})
from (
13
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top})-\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|({\mathbf{U}}_{\bf x}\circ{\mathbf{U}}_{\bf x})\text{diag}({\bm{\lambda}}_{\bf x}^{-1/2})\hat{\bf q}-\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2},

where
\circ
is the Hadamard product.
This equivalence follows from

\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{A}\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d})\,\mathbf{A}^{\top})=(\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{d}
,
applied with
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathbf{d}=\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\circ\hat{\mathbf{q}}
.

Defining
\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\circ\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\bigr)
, the hollowness penalty reduces to

\|\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}.

(20)

Minimizing this term over
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}

hence results in an unconstrained binary quadratic program (UBQP)
[
19
]
.

V-B

Sparsity Constraint

Substituting the expression for
\hat{\bf S}
from (
18
) into
h_{\mathrm{sparse}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})
from (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\bigr)\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\|_{1}

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|((\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\bigr)\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top})\odot\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}})\hat{\bf q}-\mathrm{vec}({\bf I})\|_{1},

where
\odot
is the Khatri-Rao product.
Let
\mathbf{M}=(\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top})\odot\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}
, then the sparsity penalty simplifies to

\|\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{I})\|_{1}.

(22)

To minimize this sparsity cost, let
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}}_{+}
be an auxiliary vector that upper bounds every
absolute deviation in (
22
).
Replacing each
\ell_{1}
term by two linear inequalities then yields the following mixed–integer
linear program (MILP)
[
14
]

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{q}},\,\mathbf{t}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{t}

(23)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle-\mathbf{t}\;\leq\;\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{I})\;\leq\;\mathbf{t},

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}\geq\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{i}\in\{-1,1\},\quad i=1,\dots,N.

V-C

Conic Mixed–Integer Reformulation

Combining the hollowness and sparsity costs, our composite objective is

\min_{\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}\quad\|\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{I})\|_{1}.

(24)

This composite objective combines a squared
\ell_{2}
penalty with an
\ell_{1}
penalty, both of which admit conic representations. Specifically, the squared Euclidean norm can be embedded in a rotated second–order cone (RSOC)
[
2
]
, while the
\ell_{1}
norm corresponds to a non-negative orthant cone after the introduction of auxiliary variables. Hence the entire problem can be written as a conic mixed–integer program (CMIP)
[
6
]
.

To see this formally, introduce a scalar
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a vector
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}}_{+}
such that

\|\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq s
and
-\mathbf{t}\leq\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{I})\leq\mathbf{t}.

The quadratic bound is equivalent to the
RSOC condition
\bigl(s,\tfrac{1}{2},\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{1}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{rSOC}}
with

\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{rSOC}}=\;\Bigl\{(u,v,\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,2uv\;\geq\;\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr\},

(25)

because setting
v=1/2
yields the scalar inequality

s\;\geq\;\|\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2}
. The
element-wise bounds based on
\mathbf{t}
on the other hand can be formulated as

\bigl(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{I})\bigr)\;\in\;\mathcal{K}_{1}
with
\mathcal{K}_{1}
the
\ell_{1}
cone given by

\mathcal{K}_{1}=\Bigl\{(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}}:-u_{i}\leq v_{i}\leq u_{i}\Bigr\}.

Collecting these ingredients, the undirected SEM inference task is
equivalent to the following CMIP problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{q}},\,s,\,\mathbf{t}}

\displaystyle s+\alpha\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{t}

(26)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\bigl(s,\tfrac{1}{2},\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{1}\bigr)\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{rSOC}},

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{I})\bigr)\in\mathcal{K}_{1},

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{i}\in\{-1,1\},\quad i=1,\dots,N.

Modern solvers
[
4
,
16
]
handle
a CMIP via branch-and-bound enhanced with conic dual bounds and
cutting planes to achieve global optimality.

VI
Covariance Matching for Directed SEM

We now turn to the directed graph setting, where
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
(and thus
\hat{\bf H}
) may be non-symmetric. Combining the structural objective
h(\hat{\mathbf{S}})
from (
15
) with the covariance matching constraint (
12
) leads to

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{V}}:\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{I}}

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{diag}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})+\alpha h_{\mathrm{sparse}}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})

(27)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{V}}\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}.

A semidefinite relaxation approach can then be applied using the Russo-Dye theorem
[
30
]
. While this method often provides a good solution, it requires solving a nontrivial convex optimization problem, which is challenging for large
N
.

VI-A

Riemannian Gradient Descent on the Orthogonal Group

An alternative is to treat
\hat{\mathbf{V}}
as a point on the orthogonal group
\mathcal{O}(N)
and minimize the objective via Riemannian gradient descent.
We initialise
\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{(0)}
with a random orthogonal matrix
[
23

, i.e., a sample that is uniform on

in the sense of the left– and right–invariant Haar measure. The same initialization is used whenever a random orthogonal matrix is required later (Algorithms
2
,
3
,
5

To do Riemannian gradient descent, at each iteration we compute the Euclidean (sub)gradient of the objective in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and then project it onto the tangent space of the orthonormal manifold
[
1
]
(for details on derivative computation in Euclidean space, see Appendix
-F

However, while convex optimization techniques can circumvent local minima, manifold optimization methods typically do not enjoy such benefits. A straightforward remedy is to try multiple initial guesses, hoping that at least one of them leads to a good local minimum. Since the unitary manifold is pretty large, most random initializations are ineffective though, rendering this naive multi-start approach prohibitively inefficient.

VI-C

Sampling on the Orthogonal Group

A key step in our algorithms involves sampling orthogonal matrices in a manner that is not uniformly distributed over the orthogonal group, but instead biased toward moderate rotations. Equivalently, given
\mathbf{V}
we perturb it by a moderate random rotation: sample an orthogonal
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
concentrated near
\mathbf{I}
and set
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}
. Thus the problem reduces to specifying how to generate
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
near
\mathbf{I}
.

Starting from a random orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{O}(N)

[
23
]
, write

\mathbf{U}=\exp\!\bigl(\log(\mathbf{U})\bigr).

(29)

When
\det(\mathbf{U})>0

\{\exp(\tau\,\log(\mathbf{U})):\,\tau\in[0,1]\}
is the shortest geodesic connecting
\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{U}

[
24
]
. Hence, drawing
\tau\in[0,1]
and setting
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=\exp(\tau\,\log(\mathbf{U}))
samples exactly along this geodesic, shrinking all rotation angles by a factor
\tau
. When
\det(\mathbf{U})<0
, we flip the sign of the first column of
\mathbf{U}
to make the logarithm real, compute
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=\exp(\tau\,\log(\mathbf{U}))
, and then again flip the first column of
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
so that
\det(\mathbf{U}^{\prime})<0
remains possible. This preserves orthogonality and allows sampling from either component of
\mathcal{O}(N)

VIII
Conclusion

In this study, we presented CovMatch, a unifying framework that reconstructs network structure by re-expressing the sample covariance under analytically tractable constraints and encoding prior graph knowledge directly in the optimization objective. Using the linear structural equation model as a running example, we derived the optimization problem for both undirected and directed graphs. In the undirected setting, the problem can be solved with off‑the‑shelf solvers, whereas in the directed setting, we propose an algorithm based on Riemannian gradient descent.

Comprehensive experiments on synthetic data reveal that CovMatch attains near-zero NSE when the underlying adjacency is sparse. Remarkably, in the directed case, our method achieves lower asymptotic error than widely used DAG-specific algorithms such as NOTEARS and DAGMA, despite not imposing an acyclicity prior. Real-world validation on the T–cell protein‐expression benchmark further confirms the practical utility of the approach.

-A

Signal Matching Limitation

Consider the signal matching approach

\min_{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}\frac{1}{T}\|\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{S}}\,\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\top},\,\operatorname{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})=\mathbf{0}.

(33)

Assume the data follow the SEM, i.e., for each column
\mathbf{x}_{t}
of
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}]
,

\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{e}_{t}
with
\mathbf{S}
hollow and symmetric, and
\mathbf{e}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
i.i.d. across
t
.
One might hope that a large sample size would make
\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{S}
the optimal solution. However, we show below that the true
\mathbf{S}
cannot minimize (
33
) even
T
goes to infinity.

Proof.

To justify our claim, we first examine the objective function as
T\to\infty
.
Defining the sample covariance as
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}:=\tfrac{1}{T}\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
, we obtain the following trace form for the objective:

\frac{1}{T}\bigl\|\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{X}\bigr\|_{F}^{2}=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}})-2\,\operatorname{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}})+\operatorname{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}).

(34)

When
T
goes to infinity, by the SEM relationship, we have
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\to(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}
. We therefore define

\displaystyle f(\hat{\mathbf{S}})

\displaystyle:=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\bigl\|\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{X}\bigr\|_{F}^{2}-\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}})

(35)

\displaystyle=-2\,\operatorname{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2})+\operatorname{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\top})

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that
\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{S}
is indeed optimal for
f(\hat{\mathbf{S}})
. We then aim to derive a contradiction by examining the derivative structure.

From standard matrix calculus (see
[
28
]
), the derivative under a symmetry constraint becomes

\frac{df}{d\hat{\mathbf{S}}}\;=\;\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}}\;+\;\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}}\;-\;\operatorname{Diag}\!\bigl(\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}}\bigr).

(36)

Clearly, if any off-diagonal entry of
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}}
is nonzero, we could move a small step along the corresponding symmetric zero-diagonal direction to further decrease
f
.
Hence,
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}}
must be a diagonal matrix.

From
f(\hat{\mathbf{S}})=-2\,\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}\bigr)+\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\bigr)

and the symmetry of
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}
, the partial derivative is

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}}=-2(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}+2\,\hat{\mathbf{S}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}=2(\hat{\mathbf{S}}-\mathbf{I})\,(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}.

(37)

Evaluating at
\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{S}
gives

\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}}\right|_{\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{S}}=-4(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-2}=-4(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1}.

(38)

For this to be a diagonal matrix,
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1}
must be a diagonal matrix, which means the true
\mathbf{S}
should also be diagonal. However, since we assume no self loops, this means only the trivial (zero) graph can be a solution.

This contradicts the goal of recovering a meaningful adjacency matrix, proving that naive node-domain fitting fails to identify
\mathbf{S}
even with infinite samples.
∎

-B

Maximum Likelihood Estimation for Covariance

We can interpret the constraint in the CovMatch problem (
7
) as the solution to the unconstrained classical least squares problem on the left-hand side of (
7
). We show now that this constraint can also be viewed as the solution to the unconstrained maximum likelihood problem, where we minimize

f(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}})=\log\det\bigl(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}\bigr)\;+\;\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\bigr).

(39)

Using standard matrix calculus
[
28
]
and the symmetry of
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}}\Bigl(\log\det\bigl(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}\bigr)\Bigr)\;=\;\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1},

(40)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}}\Bigl(\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\bigr)\Bigr)\;=\;-\,\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}.

Thus,

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}}\;=\;\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\;-\;\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}.

(41)

Setting
\partial f/\partial\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}
implies

\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}

(42)

So both the least squares cost and the maximum likelihood cost on the left-hand side of (
7
) lead to the same CovMatch problem on the right-hand side of (
7
).

-C

Covariance Matching for Colored Noise

We now move to the general scenario in which
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}
does not need to be the identity matrix.

For an undirected graph, although one could directly match
\hat{\mathbf{H}}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\,\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}
to
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
, solving that problem can be cumbersome. Thus, rather than matching
\hat{\mathbf{H}}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\,\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}
to
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
directly, we can enforce

(\hat{\mathbf{H}}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}})^{2}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}},

(43)

which mirrors the reasoning used in the simpler identity-noise case.

For the left-hand side of (
43
), we introduce
\hat{\mathbf{U}}
and
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}
via the eigenvalue decomposition (EVD):

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\lambda}})\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}.

(44)

We then obtain

(\hat{\mathbf{H}}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}})^{2}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\lambda}}^{2})\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}.

(45)

For the right-hand side of (
43
), the EVD leads to

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{xe}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{xe}})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{xe}}^{-1}.

(46)

Note that
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{xe}}
is not necessarily orthogonal because
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}
is generally non-symmetric.
By setting
\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{xe}}
and enforcing
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}^{2}=\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{xe}}
, we once again face a sign ambiguity. Introducing
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}
and writing

\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=\operatorname{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\,\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{xe}}^{1/2}

(47)

resolves this ambiguity. Substituting
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{U}}
back into (
44
) gives

\hat{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{U}_{\!\mathbf{xe}}\,\operatorname{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\,\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{xe}}^{1/2}\bigr)\,\mathbf{U}_{\!\mathbf{xe}}^{-1}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}^{-1},

(48)

resulting in

\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\,\mathbf{U}_{\!\mathbf{xe}}\,\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{xe}}^{-1/2}\bigr)\,\operatorname{diag}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})\,\mathbf{U}_{\!\mathbf{xe}}^{-1}.

(49)

Accordingly, the expression for
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
in problem (
18
) is replaced by the one derived above.

Similarly, for a directed graph, we want to enforce

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}\,\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}.

(50)

To do this, we write the Cholesky decomposition
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}
and let

\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}^{1/2}
be the principal square root of the noise covariance matrix.
Then (
50
) is actually equal to

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\;\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}^{1/2}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{H}}\;\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}^{1/2}\bigr)^{\!\top}=\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}.

(51)

Hence, there exists an orthogonal matrix
\hat{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathcal{O}(N)
such that

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\;\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}^{1/2}\,\hat{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}.

(52)

Substituting
\hat{\mathbf{H}}=(\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{S}})^{-1}
and rearranging gives

\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{e}}^{1/2}\,\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}.

(53)

To incorporate this result into the optimization framework, simply replace the expression for
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
in problem (
27
) with the formula derived above.

Both substitutions preserve the optimization problem structure; all algorithms remain applicable.
Hence, our method naturally extends to the colored-noise setting.

Proof of Identifiability for Undirected Graph

Theorem 1
.

Let the true GSO be
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1}
.
Let the EVD of
\mathbf{H}
be
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{U}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda})\mathbf{U}^{\top}
. Further, consider the following two conditions:

(i)

The matrix
\mathbf{H}
has no two eigenvalues that are negatives of each other, i.e.,
\nexists\,i\neq j:\ \lambda_{i}=-\lambda_{j};

(ii)

The binary linear system

(\mathbf{U}\circ\mathbf{U})\,\operatorname{diag}(|\bm{\lambda}|^{-1})\,\hat{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{1},\qquad\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},

(54)

has a unique solution (since
\operatorname{sign}(\bm{\lambda})
is always a solution, the unique solution is equal to
\operatorname{sign}(\bm{\lambda})
).

Now, let us formulate the optimization problem

\begin{split}&\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}}\|\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{1}\|_{2}^{2},\\
&\text{where}\quad\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\circ\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}})\,\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}).\end{split}

(55)

By solving the optimization problem (
55
), we obtain a sign vector
\mathbf{q}^{*}
.
Then the resulting estimator
\mathbf{S}^{*}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{q}^{*})\,\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}

satisfies
\mathbf{S}^{*}\to\mathbf{S}
as
T\to\infty
.

Proof.

At
T=\infty
, we have

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{H}^{2}=\mathbf{U}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{2})\mathbf{U}^{\top}.

(56)

On the other hand, we can write the EVD of
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
as

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}})\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}.

(57)

Observing that both (
56
) and
(
57
) are EVD forms of the same covariance
matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
, and using Assumption (i), if

\lambda_{i}^{2}=\lambda_{j}^{2}
then necessarily
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
.
Hence every eigenspace of
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}}
coincides with an
eigenspace of
\mathbf{H}
, and we can choose the EVD such that

\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{U},\qquad\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}=|\bm{\lambda}|.

(58)

Using
\mathrm{diag}\big(\mathbf{A}\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{v})\,\mathbf{A}^{\top}\big)=(\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}
, for any
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\circ\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}})\,\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\hat{\mathbf{q}}

(59)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\!\Big(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}\hat{\mathbf{q}}\big)\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\!\Big(\mathbf{U}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(|\bm{\lambda}|^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{q}}\big)\,\mathbf{U}^{\top}\Big)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{U}\circ\mathbf{U})\,\operatorname{diag}(|\bm{\lambda}|^{-1})\,\hat{\mathbf{q}}.

By Assumption (ii), the equation

(\mathbf{U}\circ\mathbf{U})\,\operatorname{diag}(|\bm{\lambda}|^{-1})\,\hat{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{1}

has the unique binary solution
\operatorname{sign}(\bm{\lambda})
.
Hence the minimizer of (
55
) satisfies

\mathbf{q}^{*}=\operatorname{sign}(\bm{\lambda})
for
T=\infty
.

Finally, the estimate
\mathbf{S}^{*}
then converges to the ground truth
\mathbf{S}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{q}^{*})\,\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}

(60)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}\,\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{sign}(\bm{\lambda}))\,\operatorname{diag}(|\bm{\lambda}|^{-1})\,\mathbf{U}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}\,\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{-1})\,\mathbf{U}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}^{-1}=\mathbf{S}.

∎

In the directed case, problem (

\mathcal{J}(\hat{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\hat{\mathbf{V}})

\displaystyle=\Big\|\mathrm{diag}\!\Bigl(\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\Bigr)\Big\|_{F}^{2}

(64)

\displaystyle+\alpha\Big\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\Big\|_{1}.

To apply Riemannian methods, we need to compute the Euclidean gradient of
\mathcal{J}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
For notational convenience, let

\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top},

then

\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{S}}=-\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,(\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top})
.

Using the Frobenius product identity
\langle A,BC\rangle=\langle AC^{\top},B\rangle
, for any scalar function
f
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=\big\langle\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}f(\hat{\mathbf{S}}),\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{S}}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}f(\hat{\mathbf{S}}),\,-\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\big(\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\big)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=-\,\Big\langle\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}f(\hat{\mathbf{S}})\,\big(\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\big)^{\!\top},\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{V}}\Big\rangle,

hence
\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{V}}}\,f(\hat{\mathbf{S}})=-\,\big(\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}f(\hat{\mathbf{S}})\big)\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}).

Therefore, for the diagonal penalty, using
\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\|\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{X})\|_{F}^{2}=2\,\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{X})
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{V}}}\Big\|\mathrm{diag}\!\big(\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\big)\Big\|_{F}^{2}

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{V}}}\Big\|\mathrm{diag}\!\big(-\hat{\mathbf{S}}\big)\Big\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\,2\,\mathrm{Diag}(\hat{\mathbf{S}})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=-\,2\,\mathrm{Diag}\!\Big(\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\Big)\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}).

For the sparsity penalty, using the elementwise subgradient
\partial\|\mathbf{X}\|_{1}=\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{X})
(with
\mathrm{sign}(0)\in[-1,1]
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{V}}}\Big\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\Big\|_{1}

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{V}}}\big\|-\hat{\mathbf{S}}\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\in\ \mathrm{sign}\!\big(\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\big)\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=-\,\mathrm{sign}\!\Big(\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{V}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\Big)\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}),

Since
\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)
is discontinuous and often induces pronounced oscillations in the objective, in practice we optimize with a Huber-type smooth surrogate for
\|\cdot\|_{1}

[
18
]
and revert to
\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)
only once the loss has stabilized.
Concretely, if the objective shows no improvement for 30 consecutive inner iterations
l
in Algorithm
4
, we consider it stabilized and replace the Huber-like gradient by the discrete

\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)
function.

-G

Candidate Set Update

To maintain diversity, candidates are admitted in ascending order of
objective value, but only if they are sufficiently different from those
already selected. Specifically, let
\delta>0
be a distance threshold.
We traverse the refined set
\mathcal{R}
in order of increasing

\mathcal{J}(\hat{\mathbf{V}})
and add a matrix
\hat{\mathbf{V}}
to the
candidate set
\mathcal{C}
only if

\min_{\hat{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathcal{C}}\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}-\hat{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}>\delta.

(67)

This process continues until either
L^{\prime}
candidates are collected or

\mathcal{R}
is exhausted. The resulting
\mathcal{C}
therefore contains
the
L^{\prime}
best solutions while avoiding near-duplicate matrices.

In practice, we adapt
\delta
depending on the optimization dynamics
discussed in Appendix
-F
: when the loss has not yet
stabilized we set
\delta=0.5
, whereas after stabilization we use

\delta=0.01
.
