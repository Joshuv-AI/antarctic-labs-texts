Geotechnical improvement of dune sand embankments using rubber crumb and brick powder: RSM vs. ANN-GA hybrid optimization
Authors: Fatima Zohra Benamara, Chiraz Kechkar, Messaouda Boumaaza, Ahmed Belaadi, Mohamed Sahraoui, Mostefa Hani, Ibrahim M H Alshaikh, Sarra Mezaouri, Lina Zaidi, Djamel Ghernaout, Yazid Chetbani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing need for efficient and sustainable construction materials has prompted the investigation of local and recycled resources to improve the characteristics of poor soils. This research aims to enhance dune sand (DS)—a plentiful yet geotechnically weak material—by integrating rubber crumb (RC) and brick powder (BP), with the objectives of soil stability and waste valorization. Experimental formulations were created with RC at concentrations of 10%, 30%, and 50%, and BP at concentrations of 1%, 2%, and 3%. A response surface methodology (RSM) and artificial neural network (ANN) techniques were utilized to examine the influences of RC and BP content on three primary responses: maximum dry density (MDD), internal friction angle (ϕ), and cohesion (C). Ideal conditions with 40.7% RC and 3% BP were achieved through optimization utilizing RSM and ANN-GA, greatly enhancing compaction and shear strength for geotechnical applications. According to Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), a high CR content raises energy consumption (E-Energy) and greenhouse gas emissions (E-CO₂), primarily as a result of rubber recycling. However, the reduction in sand mining and the diversion of tyre waste balance these effects. Therefore, the combination of RC with BP is turning out to be a viable and efficient infrastructure solution, especially in dry regions.Keywords: Dune sand stabilization, Rubber crumb recycling, Brick powder additive, Geotechnical optimization, Response surface methodology (RSM), Artificial neural network (ANN), Sustainable civil engineering, Life cycle assessment.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Stabilization technologies have been widely employed in civil engineering projects, including building foundation bases, highway embankments, and road construction, to enhance soils’ engineering qualities by applying the right stabilizers1–4. Although cement and lime are typically utilized for this purpose, a lot of study has recently concentrated on using waste materials as stabilizers because of their strong pozzolanic properties with soil particles5–7. This approach enhances the natural soil’s engineering qualities while also adding to their disposal issue and environmental contamination.

The development of industrial activities generates a lot of waste, which pollutes the environment8. The use of industrial waste in the fields of civil engineering and public works, which are major consumers of natural resources, makes it possible to reduce costs, protect the environment, and improve the mechanical characteristics of the structure7,9. Dune sand (DS) presents a solution to the problem of depletion of natural resources and is presented as a material of the future that can replace other types of sand due to its abundance in arid regions10.

Dune sand can be effectively used as a foundation material, especially in arid regions where conventional construction materials are scarce or expensive11. With proper stabilization, dune sand can be effectively used as landfill material, especially in arid regions where conventional construction materials are scarce or expensive. However, dune sand has limitations in terms of stability and bearing capacity due to its low cohesion and high permeability12,13.

Various techniques have been developed to improve the mechanical and physical characteristics of dune sand, such as the mechanical compaction technique, which results in the bearing capacity increasing13,14. The use of rigid and granular inclusions can be used to reinforce dune sand and improve its bearing capacity. Geosynthetics, such as geogrids and geotextiles, can be used to reinforce dune sand and thus improve the stabilization of embankments and supporting walls15. To enhance dune sand’s geotechnical characteristics, materials such as cement have improved the strength parameters of dune sands13,16. The addition of bentonite and kaolinite to dune sand improved the load-bearing capability and reduced foundation settling17.

Recent studies have shown that additives derived from solid waste, such as plastics and used tires, are commonly incorporated as stabilizing18 or improving materials in construction mixtures11,19–21. Used tires are not biodegradable and contain harmful chemical compounds, making them a major source of pollution. Every year, millions of tires are reaching the end of their life, requiring proper management to avoid fire risks and soil contamination. Used tires also pose public health risks. They can provide a reproduction site for parasites and release toxic gases in the event of a fire22. The use of rubber waste not only addresses environmental concerns related to tire disposal but also provides a cost-effective solution for soil improvement in geotechnical applications. Anbazhagan and Manohar23 used rubber crumb as an additive to improve the strength properties, energy absorption capacity, and ductility. Ravichandran et al.24 reported that the optimum percentage (10%) of rubber crumb (RC) significantly improved the CBR value. Anvari et al.25 focuses on using granulated rubber to enhance the shear strength of fine-grained sandy soils. The results indicate that granulated rubber can effectively increase the weak soil’s shear strength and reduce settlement. Benjelloun’s work26 showed that 20% rubber aggregates increased the friction angle and shear strength of sand-rubber mixes, contributing to better bearing capacity. The lightness of the rubber reduces the density of the sand mix and consequently the reduction of the total weight of the embankment27; this can be advantageous in poor soils or in case of high-water tables. Tire chips reduce the overall density of the mix. Applications like lightweight fill for retaining structures may benefit from this28. Research indicates that mixtures can achieve higher shear strength, with internal friction angles increasing from 34° for sand alone to about 67° when combined with tire chips18. Reddy et al.29 demonstrated that shear resistance improved with the rubber chip percentage, which contributes to increased shear strength. This effect is particularly observed at low rubber content2. In the geotechnical field, used tires can be used after recycling to create new shapes (shredded, aggregates, and crumb) adapted to soil particles30.

The viability of incorporating rubber and brick waste in road embankments as a soil stabilizer for poor soil is the main topic of this study because there aren’t many studies on the usage of this material for geotechnical purposes. This study explores the enhancement of dune sand, a common but geotechnically poor material, by adding RC and BP to encourage soil stabilization and waste valorization.

Determining the ideal amount of stabilizer to add to the soil while keeping the right proportions is one of the biggest challenges in stabilizing embankments. Optimization approaches must be used to generate trustworthy results31. Traditional approaches, which modify every variable separately, necessitate a significant number of experiments in addition to a significant investment of time and money in analysis32. Numerous optimization strategies are available, including Taguchi’s method33–35, response surface methodology (RSM)36,37, and genetic algorithms38–40. RSM is a useful modeling method that provides pertinent alternatives for engineering project decision-making41. These limitations of traditional methods can be overcome advantageously by fitting experimental datasets using first- or second-order modeling based on RSM, in combination with a central composite experimental design (CCD).

The RSM’s primary concept is to use a set of tests intended to provide the best possible result. According to earlier research, the RSM method can provide reasonable solution alternatives to help decision-assessment for specific problems in engineering, which makes it useful for determining the ideal mix proportions42–44. To determine the right amounts of stabilizers to add to the soil, the RSM approach has been applied rather infrequently for soil stabilization. This study’s primary goal is to use RSM to calculate the ideal input amounts of RC% and BP% that might be provided for the mixture’s (DS + RC + BP) maximum dry unit weight, angle of internal friction, and cohesive performance.

It is important to highlight that several of the previous research investigations on the optimization of reinforcement parameters for embankment stabilization only used parametric studies rather than mathematical models using advanced optimization techniques. To solve this issue, sophisticated predictive techniques seem highly helpful. To create a relationship between the design parameters and the maximum dry unit weight, internal friction angle, and cohesion, prediction techniques like the response surface method are advised45,46. RSM has been successfully applied in numerous civil engineering domains. It was utilized for experimental estimates, material behavior simulation, structural issue optimization, and concrete mix proportion estimation47.

Recently, the incorporation of recycled materials in geotechnical engineering has accelerated as part of wider initiatives to advance sustainable construction methods. Rubber crumb (RC), sourced from end-of-life tires, provides significant mechanical advantages for soil stabilization, particularly in improving cohesion and resilience. Nevertheless, the environmental sustainability of recycled content utilization cannot be comprehensively evaluated without examining its effects over the entire material lifecycle. Life cycle assessment (LCA) functions as an effective instrument for a thorough analysis of greenhouse gas emissions, energy usage, and resource efficiency linked to recycled additives. While RC aids in garbage valorization and alleviates landfill pressure, its manufacturing entails energy-intensive procedures, including shredding, grinding, and conditioning, potentially increasing its carbon footprint substantially. Consequently, incorporating LCA into the assessment framework is crucial to reconcile mechanical performance enhancements with environmental accountability and to ascertain if the utilization of RC represents a genuinely sustainable approach for soil enhancement48–50.

Several studies have separately explored the use of recycled rubber (RC) or brick powder (BP) in improving fine or clay soils, highlighting their respective effects on bearing capacity, density or durability51,52. To the best of our knowledge, however, no study has looked at the combined impact of RC and BP on dune sands, either in terms of the best formulation or the ways in which these two materials interact with sand. This work is notable for its novel integration of rubber crumb (RC) and brick powder (BP) to enhance dune sand, a geotechnical method that is predominantly under-researched in the current literature, which generally examines these materials separately. The research seeks to improve the mechanical properties of dune sand, including its shear strength and compaction ability, by incorporating these two recycled additions. A comprehensive experimental campaign with a central composite design is paired with a novel hybrid optimization framework that combines desirability function modelling using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with Genetic Algorithm (GA) optimization powered by Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). This new synergistic approach enables accurate modelling and prediction of critical geotechnical parameters: maximum dry density (MDD), internal friction angle (ϕ) and cohesion (C). By using thorough error metrics to assess model robustness and predictive reliability, the methodology’s superior applicability and efficacy for advanced engineering design are demonstrated. This study not only proposes novel predictive frameworks but also develops a data-driven optimization technique for practical soil stabilization in the field. It provides a substantial and innovative contribution to sustainable geotechnical engineering in arid regions, harmonizing mechanical efficiency with ecological responsibility.

Materials and methods

Materials

The materials used in this research include brick powder waste (BP), rubber granules (RC), and dune sand. The dune sand used in this investigation originates from the southeast Algerian town of Oued-Souf. The soil is dune sand, which is classified as class D1 for road construction. According to the technical guide for the construction of embankments and subgrade layers53, this type of soil is porous, non-cohesive, and poorly graded.

In this study, two waste-based additives—brick powder (BP) made from construction waste and rubber crumb (RC) from old tires—are added to dune sand (DS) to improve its geotechnical qualities. Dune sand is found in abundance in arid zones, but it has poor mechanical performance and low cohesiveness. An eco-friendly way to utilize trash is to use the rubber crumb (RC), a by-product of the tire retreading industry, to fortify the dune sand (DS) in this study (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis using RSM modeling

RSM analysis uses a mathematical and statistical technique for experimental design and optimization processes to optimize input factors or variables in various applications and minimize the number of experimental trials55–58. RSM has the advantage of organizing experimental procedures to allow for the analysis of pertinent data, which sets it apart from other statistical procedures. RSM typically entails conducting a number of trials in accordance with a meticulously planned experimental strategy. ANOVA is a statistical technique that determines each independent variable’s significance on the output by figuring out Its contribution by percentage59–62. To comprehend the influence of many independent factors and their interactions on the answer, numerous researchers have incorporated ANOVA into their investigations63–67.

CCD is one of the experimental plan parts of RSM68,69. The CCD approach can assess how many experiments are needed to determine factors and response optimization. The factorial, axial (or star), and central points are the three points of this CCD approach70. This study used the Central Composite experimental design with two factors to examine how the input variables affected the MDD, c, and φ of the dune sand embankments. Furthermore, the RC (A) and BP (B) are the two input variables. Thirteen processes were developed through experimental manufacturing employing CCD based on Design Expert. Results and discussions

Proctor test

Changes in maximum dry density (MDD) and optimum moisture content for compaction (OMC) show that dune sand, on its own, has the highest compactness and requires a small amount of water to reach its optimum density, reflecting an initially dense structure (Fig. 9).

Proctor curves for different mixtures.

The gradual addition of rubber crumb (RC) modifies this behavior: a small proportion of RC (10%) improves or stabilizes the maximum density while requiring slightly more water, due to a filling effect within the sand matrix. Conversely, higher levels of RC (30–50%) significantly reduce MDD and greatly increase OMC, indicating a more porous and lighter overall structure. Several studies confirm that incorporating RC into soil leads to a gradual decrease in MDD followed by an increased, or at least maintenance, of OMC as the rubber content increases77,78. The optimum compromise is often observed for dosages of 10 to 30% RC, which makes it possible to reconcile compactness, stress absorption, and ease of laying, while higher contents lead to a continuous fall in density79,80.

The addition of brick powder (BP) at a low dose (1%) acts as a binder, making it possible to maintain or even slightly increased density; however, a higher addition (2–3%) accentuates the loss of compactness, particularly when combined with high RC contents, due to BP’s ability water retention and opening up the material structure. The literature shows that low BP percentages (≤ 10–15%) can increase or preserve MDD through a binding and pore-filling effect, contributing to a reduction in porosity81. On the other hand, higher additions (> 15–20%) increase the OMC and significantly reduce the compactness of the treated soil, due to the powdered brick’s porous nature82.

A judicious dosage of RC and BP is therefore crucial for optimizing the compactness of stabilized sand, as excessive quantities lead to a marked drop in density and require more water for processing. The overall analysis shows that the addition of RC and BP influences the compaction characteristics of dune sand by increasing OMC and reducing MDD, especially at high content. However, moderate proportions—between 10 and 30% RC coupled with 1–2% BP—offer an optimal compromise, maximizing density while limiting the amount of water required, thus guaranteeing good compactness and mechanical stability. For sub-base or backfill applications, it is therefore recommended to use these balanced dosages to ensure that the material performs well and is easy to install. Conversely, too much RC or BP should be avoided, as it leads to a significant drop in compactness and a significant increase in OMC, adversely affecting load-bearing capacity and rigidity, as well as practical management of moisture on site. Precise control of the dosage of these additives remains essential to ensure the quality and durability of stabilized soils.

Direct shear test

Figure 10 illustrates the shear characteristics (cohesion and angle of internal friction) change for various combinations made of RC, BP, and dune sand. DS has a high internal friction angle (38°) and very low cohesiveness (4 kPa), which are traits of clean, well-compacted sands with a dense but incohesive structure, according to this figure.

Shear parameters for various mixtures.

When RC is added gradually, the angle of internal friction first decreases significantly, reaching about 29.6° for the DS + 10% RC mix. At the same time, cohesiveness increases by up to 10 to 17 kPa, depending on the content. The rubber’s low stiffness and surface compliance, which interfere with the sand granules’ natural interlock, are responsible for this reduction in friction. The flexible nature of RC, on the other hand, produces a so-called “related” cohesion in the mixture, resulting in increased cohesion83,84. This advantageous effect is related to the RC’s function as a filler, which promotes improved interlocking between the rubber particles and the sand grains, strengthening the material’s apparent cohesiveness85. The mechanical interlocking of the rubber granules in the granular matrix and the anchoring of the angular brick powder particles on the RC’s deformable surface, which increases the effective contact surface and dissipates energy under load, can both account for the increased cohesion. The SEM image of the rubber crumb (Fig. 4b) shows particles that are “irregularly shaped” with a “rough-textured” surface. This high surface roughness, a result of mechanical shredding, creates significantly more friction at the interface between rubber and sand particles compared to the smooth, rounded surfaces of sand grains. This increased friction resists particle rearrangement and contributes to the overall shear strength.

In addition to stabilizing or even slightly increasing the angle of internal friction, which can reach up to 38°, the addition of brick powder (BP) at modest doses (1–2%) results in a noticeable improvement in cohesiveness, reaching between 19 and 30 kPa for the DS + 50%RC + 2%BP mix. This beneficial development in mechanical parameters can be explained by the fact that BP functions as an efficient binder at these low doses, filling voids and strengthening intergranular adhesion86. The SEM images of the brick powder reveal a “slight porosity” and a very fine, layered particle structure (Fig. 5c). These fine particles can adhere to the surfaces of the larger sand and rubber grains, acting as a physical glue rsult from pozzolanic Reactions, the brick powder’s aluminosilicate composition can react with the calcium in the dune sand to form C-S-H and C-A-H gels. Although not directly visible in these specific SEM images, this chemical bonding would coat the particles and weld their contact points, creating true cohesion within the matrix. This mechanism transforms the granular material into a weakly cemented one. According to EDX-SEM examination (Figs. 4 and 5), the angular and microcracked surface of the brick powder permits mechanical anchoring and micro-bridging, while the roughness and deformability of the rubber granules encourage their interlocking in the interstices. The observed increase in cohesion in spite of the lack of a hydraulic binder can be explained by these physico-mechanical interactions.

Numerous recent experiments, most notably those of Chamma et al.87, have proven that a moderate dosage of RC (up to 30%) and BP (1–2%) work in concert to maximize the cohesiveness and internal friction of the stabilized material. A more porous structure that decreases shear characteristics because of greater water absorption and decreased density might result from large concentrations of these additives, such as more than 3% BP or more than 50% RC. Therefore, a balanced and modest dosage of these components is necessary to guarantee optimal mechanical performance88. A balanced dosage (RC 10–30%, BP 1–2%) maximizes both cohesion and the angle of internal friction, guaranteeing optimum mechanical performance for sand stabilization, which is also reported in many recent scientific publications on the subject.

Assessment of rubber crumb and brick powder content

It is evident that a significant decrease in maximum dry density (MDD) occurs as the amount of rubber content (RC) is increased (Fig. 11a). The ability of rubber particles to fill voids in the sandy matrix or integrate into the structural skeleton affects the maximum dry density (MDD), cohesion and internal friction angle of the final mixture. At low percentages (10%), RC acts as a filler, while at higher percentages (30–50%), it creates a more porous and lighter structure, significantly reducing MDD. For instance, when the RC is raised to 50% without BP, the MDD of the pure mix (0% RC, 0% BP) drops to 14.6 kN/m³. This decline is a result of the rubber’s low intrinsic density and elastic properties, which lessen the blend’s compactness. However, this loss of density is generally compensated for by the steady addition of brick powder (BP), with the MDD increasing consistently for all proportions of RC as we move from 0% to 3% BP. For instance, adding 3% BP at 50% RC increases the MDD from 14.6 to 15.9 kN/m³, showing greater intergranular contact with the fines and better compaction due to the filling effect.

Changes observed in MDD for φ and c dune sand fills: (a) MDD, (b) φ, and (c) C.

When 10% RC alone is added, the angle of internal friction (φ), which is high in natural sand at 38°, lowers to 29.6°, showing a significant decrease in sliding resistance associated with the rubber’s lubricating and elastic properties (Fig. 11b). But raising BP at these same RC levels progressively overcomes this resistance. For instance, φ increases from 32.9° (without BP) to 40.9° (with 3% BP) at 50% RC, showing that brick powder increases and restores internal friction by improving particle cohesion and creating a denser microstructure.

While RC and BP are added, cohesiveness (c), which was initially low at 4 kPa for pure sand, climbs significantly to 10 kPa with 10% RC alone and to 34 kPa when the maximum proportion is reached (50% RC, 3% BP) (Fig. 11c). This development emphasizes the two additions’ synergistic effect, which strengthens the mix’s cohesiveness when combined. This improvement is most likely due to bonding and interlocking mechanisms caused by the rubber particles and brick powder.

Life cycle assessment

The use of recycled mineral waste, particularly brick powder from demolition debris and rubber crumb from end-of-life tires, has become a feasible approach for promoting sustainability in geotechnical engineering. These materials provide a method to decrease the extraction of non-renewable resources while also helping to alleviate the environmental impact linked to construction and industrial waste streams. Their incorporation into dune sand embankment systems enhances essential soil characteristics, such as resistance to shear, cohesion, and compaction efficiency95–97.

The assumption that waste-derived additives are environmentally superior to natural alternatives requires further examination. The conversion of waste into construction-grade materials involves processes such as particle size reduction, classification, and potential pre-treatment, which are energy-intensive and can significantly contribute to indirect greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. The transportation of these materials over extended distances from collection or processing centers to construction sites may increase their environmental impact. Comparative environmental assessments indicate that, in specific scenarios, the total energy consumption and can equal or surpass those of natural materials when assessed comprehensively98–100.

A comprehensive life cycle analysis indicates that the anticipated sustainability benefits of using recycled inputs may be diminished or completely negated when considering both upstream and downstream effects. This encompasses the embodied energy and greenhouse gas emissions linked to material processing and logistics, as well as the potential energy consumption during blending and compaction in field applications. Previous LCA studies have shown that although diverting materials from landfills and conserving virgin resources are valuable goals, they do not automatically ensure environmental benefits, especially when recycling processes are mechanized, decentralized, or dependent on fossil-fuel energy systems101–103.

There is a need to assess recycled material interventions using a multi-criteria sustainability framework that incorporates mechanical performance, energy consumption, carbon emissions, and cost-effectiveness throughout the entire product system boundary. This study uses a life cycle assessment (LCA) to assess the ecological effects of incorporating BP and RC into dune sand stabilization systems104,105. This approach seeks to guide material selection and formulation strategies that enhance geotechnical functionality and environmental performance, thus aiding in the creation of technically robust and ecologically sustainable infrastructure solutions.
