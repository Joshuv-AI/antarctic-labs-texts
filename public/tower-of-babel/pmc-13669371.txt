Global inequities and fragmentation in guidelines for endometriosis: a scoping review

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the availability of endometriosis care guidelines from multiple governmental and professional organisations, their global applicability and recommendations have been insufficiently examined. This scoping review examines endometriosis guidelines worldwide, evaluating their availability, target audiences and populations, methodologies, and clinical recommendations, building on a previous review of region-specific endometriosis care guidance.MethodsWe searched PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus from inception through October 2025 without language, publication date, or geographic location restrictions. Additional grey literature searches were conducted through March 2026. Eligible guidelines addressed individuals with suspected or confirmed endometriosis of any age, including adolescent-specific guidance and recommendations incorporating adenomyosis.ResultsA total of 47 guidelines were included, comprising 28 English-language and 19 non-English-language guidelines from multiple regions. The findings reveal marked regional inequities: most guidelines originate from high-income settings, particularly in Europe (15/47, 32%), with substantial gaps in Africa (2%) and the Eastern Mediterranean (4%). Variation in inclusivity and methodological rigour was observed, including underrepresentation of adolescents (77%), individuals with lived experience (30%), and gender-diverse people (19%), as well as inconsistent approaches to grading evidence. There was also limited consideration of primary care physicians (49%), who play a key role in early diagnosis and management. Gaps in alignment across guidelines on key diagnostic and therapeutic pathways include recommendations on histological confirmation, biomarker use, aromatase inhibitors, repeat surgery, fertility care, non-medical interventions, and self-care interventions.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the need for a global normative guideline, greater standardisation in guideline development methods, context-specific guidance that incorporates the needs and rights of end-users, and coordinated efforts to strengthen equitable, evidence-based care.

Introduction

Endometriosis is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting approximately 10% of people of female sex of reproductive age (~190 million) worldwide1. It is characterised by the presence of endometrium-like tissue outside the uterus and is commonly associated with chronic, often severe pelvic pain, abnormal uterine bleeding, infertility, fatigue, bowel symptoms, and mental health conditions, which can substantially impair quality of life2–4. Despite this substantial burden on the health and well-being of affected individuals, endometriosis has not been adequately prioritised in global health policy, and no globally applicable care guidance exists5,6.

Advances in diagnostic approaches and increasing awareness, driven by targeted national policies, care guidance, and patient advocacy, exist, but prolonged diagnostic delays persist across healthcare systems and continue to challenge effective management7,8. Delays in diagnosis of 12 years or more, driven by non-specific clinical presentations, symptom normalisation, lack of non-invasive diagnostics and limited access to specialised care9–11, have been reduced to 6 years in some high-income settings12,13. However, diagnostic delays remain a contentious issue, and even the more recent data are considerably longer than the one-year-or-less target recommended by the United Kingdom All Party Parliamentary Enquiry into endometriosis by 203014. These delays contribute to higher hospitalisation rates and increased healthcare use and costs for pain management, particularly in settings with limited access to timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary care15,16. The health, well-being, and economic burden associated with endometriosis across the life course are likely underestimated, as existing cost data are limited and largely derived from high-income countries17. However, the scale of the problem is significant: non-cancerous gynecological conditions collectively account for over 5% of global years lost to disability (YLDs), with endometriosis the leading individual contributor (0.51%), accounting for nearly half the combined burden of HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria (1.08%)18.

Normative guidelines, developed through systematic evidence review and appraisal of benefits and harms, are essential for standardising care, supporting evidence-based decision-making, and reducing unwarranted variation in clinical practice, non-medical and self-care interventions19. Although many governmental and professional bodies have issued guidelines for endometriosis care over the past two decades, their global applicability, the strength of their approaches, and their recommendations have been insufficiently examined.

This scoping review expands on prior work, including a review of the national availability of endometriosis care guidance6 and three reviews of diagnostic and management recommendations from a limited set of English-language guidelines from high-income countries20–22. It systematically maps and compares endometriosis guidelines across all WHO regions. It also evaluates methodological approaches, target populations and audiences, and clinical recommendations to identify key gaps and inform the future development of globally applicable normative guidance. The guidelines reveal marked regional inequities and variation in inclusivity, methodological rigour, and recommendations, highlighting the need for more standardised and equitable global guidance.

Methods

Search strategy

We conducted a scoping review of the published literature up to October 2025 using PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus (Supplementary Table 1). The search was limited to human guidelines and used combinations of title, abstract, keyword, and subject heading terms in PubMed (MeSH), EMBASE (Emtree), and Scopus: (“Endometriosis” OR “Adenomyosis”) AND (“Practice Guideline” OR “Guideline” OR “Clinical Practice Guideline” OR “Practice Bulletin” OR “Green-top” OR “Living Guideline” OR “Position Statement” OR “Consensus”). Additional guidelines, particularly those in non-English publications, were identified through grey literature sources and manual hand-searching, including the Guidelines International Network, Google Scholar, and websites of professional societies and government health agencies, up to March 2026. Grey literature searches combined “endometriosis” with country names and guideline-related terms to identify national endometriosis guidance not captured in bibliographic databases. No restrictions were applied on language, date of publication, or source location. We performed searches in English and the primary languages of each country, with non-English documents screened using Google Translate and, where possible, checked by co-authors and others fluent in those languages. The review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping review23, and was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (Supplementary Table 2)24. The review protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/ZE64P).

Selection criteria

Titles and abstracts of identified records were independently screened by DGG and GDM, followed by a full-text review of eligible documents. Guidelines were included if they addressed individuals with suspected or confirmed endometriosis (of any age), including adolescent-specific guidance or recommendations integrating adenomyosis. Eligible documents comprised clinical guidelines, consensus statements, recommendations, or care pathways developed in international, national, or regional contexts. We included clinical practice guidelines and consensus documents issued or endorsed by national governments, government–professional society collaborations, national professional societies, multinational or regional professional societies, and international professional societies or expert consensus groups. Documents were excluded if they were outdated (i.e., superseded by a more recent edition), commentaries, narrative reviews, summaries without detailed recommendations, or committee opinions. When multiple guidelines were identified for a country, the most recent, authoritative, and comprehensive source was selected; if both professional society and government guidelines were available, both were recorded.

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers (DGG and LWR), with any discrepancies resolved through discussion. Non-English documents (n = 19; e.g., Spanish, Portuguese, Chinese, and others) were translated using Google Translate, and the extracted data were subsequently reviewed and, where applicable, verified by multilingual collaborators. Information was collected from each eligible document across three categories: Guideline characteristics (issuing body and year, country or region, title, document type, scope, first and last edition, language, and originality); methodological characteristics (target population, target audience, methodology used, and evidence grading system); and recommendation domains (diagnosis, imaging, surgical management, medical treatment, fertility, and non-pharmacological care). Adaptation was defined as a guideline that explicitly used one or more existing clinical practice guidelines as a primary source and modified or contextualised their recommendations for local use. These included documents stating that they were “adapted from,” “based on,” or “derived from” a named guideline, or that followed a formal adaptation framework.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data were synthesised using a descriptive approach, including narrative summaries, frequency tables, and thematic mapping. Comparisons were made across regions, scopes, and methodological characteristics to highlight similarities and variations among guidelines. Clinical recommendations were categorised into the predefined domains, and each guideline was assessed for the presence or absence of recommendations within these domains. Results were presented in summary tables (characteristics of included guidelines, methodology, and recommendations covered) and visualised through figures, including a world map of endometriosis guidelines. This review used Microsoft Excel 365 for data synthesis and visualisation, including Figs. 3 and 4. The world map and heatmaps were generated in R (version 4.5.0) using the ggplot2 package.

Results

A total of 5347 records were identified from PubMed (n = 974), Embase (n = 2365), and Scopus (n = 2008). After removing 2105 duplicates, 3242 records were screened, and 91 full-text documents were assessed for eligibility (Fig. 1). Seventy documents were excluded as newer editions had superseded them or were commentaries, narrative reviews, summaries without detailed recommendations, or committee opinions. Twenty-one documents were included from database searches, and 26 from grey literature and hand searching, yielding 47 final guidelines (28 in English and 19 in non-English languages across multiple regions, including Spanish, Portuguese, Chinese, Thai, Russian, and others). Of the 47 documents, 12 were national governmental, 6 were government–professional society collaborative, 20 were national professional society, 6 were multinational or regional professional society, and 3 were international professional society.Fig. 1PRISMA-ScR flow diagram showing the identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion of guidelines.

Guidelines characteristics

A total of 38 national guidelines were identified, all published between July 2010 and March 2026 (Supplementary Data 1). Countries in the European Region had the greatest proportion of national guidelines (n = 16), comprising 13 clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) and 3 related documents (2 consensus and 1 clinical care pathway). Of these, 11 were original, and 5 were adaptations of existing guidelines. Eight countries in the Americas published 10 guidelines – 5 original CPGs and 1 consensus statement from North America, and 4 CPGs from South America (3 original, 1 adapted). Seven countries in the Western Pacific Region published original national-level guidelines (5 CPGs and 2 consensus). The South-East Asia Region published 2 original CPGs, and one country in the African Region published an adapted CPG. In the Eastern Mediterranean Region, 2 countries published guidelines: one was an original CPG, and the other was an adapted, formulary-focused clinical guidance document. In addition, 9 international or regional guidelines, all original publications, were identified, including 2 CPGs and 7 consensus statements. Overall, 39 guidelines were original developments, while 8 were adaptations, most commonly drawing on the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE), the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), or the American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (ACOG).

Figure 2 shows the global distribution of national guidelines for endometriosis. Most guidelines were published in Europe, followed by the Western Pacific region, with limited coverage in Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East. Most were original CPGs, with fewer related documents, such as consensus statements. Several non-English guidelines and adaptations of existing recommendations were also identified, primarily in Europe and South America. Methodological characteristics

Across the 47 endometriosis guidelines, all targeted reproductive-aged patients, and 77% also included adolescents (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 2). Most (83%) addressed specific clinical subgroups, such as pregnancy-associated disease or menopausal status; however, only a minority (19%) explicitly acknowledged gender-diverse individuals. All guidelines were directed at gynaecologists or fertility specialists, and 85% recommended a multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach. In contrast, fewer than half (49%) explicitly included primary care clinicians, such as general practitioners (GPs). Evidence of patient and public involvement (PPI) was reported in only 30% of endometriosis guidelines (n = 14).Fig. 3Target population and intended users of included guidelines (n = 47).

Target population and intended users of included guidelines (n = 47).

Methodological approaches varied across the guidelines (Fig. 4, Supplementary Data 3). Among original guidelines (n = 39), 82% used a hybrid model combining evidence review (systematic or narrative) with expert consensus, while 8% conducted a systematic or evidence-based review alone, and 10% relied solely on expert consensus without reporting a supporting evidence review. Regarding evidence grading, 26% of the original guidelines applied the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to assess evidence quality and the strength of recommendations25. A further 44% used national or organisational grading systems (i.e., frameworks developed by countries or professional bodies, including adapted or alternative approaches to GRADE), whereas 31% issued ungraded or consensus-based recommendations without formal appraisal of the underlying evidence. Among adapted guidelines (n = 8), 63% reported conducting a literature review combined with consensus processes to contextualise recommendations, and 50% applied national-level grading systems.Fig. 4Methodology and evidence grading among the original guidelines (n = 39).

Methodology and evidence grading among the original guidelines (n = 39).

Clinical domain recommendation

Most guidelines covered a broad range of clinical domains, including diagnosis (91%), medical management (85%), surgical management (83%), fertility care (81%), and non-pharmacological interventions (74%). A minority had more limited scopes: the Saudi Arabian Council of Health Insurance (CHI)26 focused only on the medical management of endometriosis; the United States’ Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound (SRU)27 and the European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR)28 consensus on routine pelvic imaging for endometriosis; the Asia Pacific Initiative on Reproduction (ASPIRE)29 consensus provided a detailed evaluation of the structure of care in endometriosis and criteria for networks of expertise; the Asia-Pacific Endometriosis Expert Panel (APEX)30 addressed diagnosis, imaging, and care pathways; and the Australasian Certificate of Reproductive Endocrinology and Infertility Consensus Expert Panel on Trial Evidence (ACCEPT)31 focused on the management of endometriosis-associated infertility.

Most guidelines (>77%) addressed core diagnostic domains, including clinical assessment, pelvic examination, empirical therapy, avoidance of routine first-line laparoscopy, and referral to specialist care (Fig. 5). However, variation was observed in recommendations regarding histological confirmation and the diagnostic value of biomarkers such as cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), particularly among European and North American guidelines.Fig. 5Diagnosis recommendations across endometriosis guidelines by WHO region and issuing body (n = 47).

Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) was consistently recommended as the first-line imaging modality, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reserved for suspected deep endometriosis (DE) or severe disease when advanced ultrasound was not feasible in almost all guidelines (94%), except ACCEPT, CHI, and the Brazilian Ministry of Health (MoH) guideline (Fig. 6). Inconsistencies were evident in guidance on specialist imaging and in explicit recognition that imaging alone cannot exclude endometriosis, especially among national guidelines from the Western Pacific, North America, and Europe.Fig. 6Imaging recommendations across endometriosis guidelines by WHO region and issuing body (n = 47).

Most guidelines (>70%) – particularly those from Europe, South America, and the Western Pacific – largely converged on recommending laparoscopy when medical therapy fails, cystectomy for endometrioma, symptom- or fertility-based surgical indications, and referral to expert surgeons or MDTs (Fig. 7). However, inconsistencies remained, particularly regarding repeat surgery and the preference for excision over ablation.Fig. 7Surgical recommendations across endometriosis guidelines by WHO region and issuing body (n = 47).

Most guidelines (>77%) converged on recommending non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for pain relief and hormonal therapy as first-line management, primarily combined oral contraceptive pills (COCPs) and progestins. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues with add-back therapy were also commonly recommended (79%) as second-line options for patients with persistent symptoms or intolerance to first-line treatment (Fig. 8). Some variation was noted in recommendations for post-surgical hormonal treatment, with the use of the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) addressed less consistently. A limited number of guidelines (45%) recommend aromatase inhibitors for refractory disease.Fig. 8Medical recommendations across endometriosis guidelines by WHO region and issuing body (n = 47).

Most European and South-East Asian guidelines showed strong convergence in fertility-related recommendations, particularly in endorsing Assisted Reproductive Technology/In Vitro Fertilisation (ART/IVF), reserving surgery mainly for moderate-to-severe disease, considering ovarian reserve in decision-making, and promoting individualised fertility planning or MDT referral (Fig. 9). In contrast, greater regional variation was observed in recommendations on early fertility referral, avoiding routine surgery before ART, and providing fertility counselling, particularly among guidelines from North America, parts of Europe, and the Western Pacific region.Fig. 9Fertility recommendations across endometriosis guidelines by WHO region and issuing body (n = 47).

Patient education (40%) and psychological support (30%) were the most frequently recommended non-pharmacological interventions across the included guidelines (Fig. 10). Some guidelines acknowledged the potential therapeutic value of pelvic floor physiotherapy (19%), modifiable behavioural measures (e.g., dietary or physical activity changes; 36%), and complementary therapies (e.g., acupuncture, yoga, traditional Chinese medicine; 26%) but did not issue specific recommendations due to insufficient evidence32–36. Notably, there was variation in the inclusion of complementary and behavioural therapies, with some guidelines mentioning these interventions briefly and others not addressing them at all.Fig. 10Non-pharmacological recommendations across endometriosis guidelines by WHO region and issuing body (n = 47).

Given that many patients with endometriosis also have adenomyosis, recommendations on adenomyosis appeared in 10 endometriosis guidelines (21%), although it was not a primary focus. However, because adenomyosis is a distinct condition, some guidelines, such as ESHRE, did not include recommendations for it.

Discussion

This review identified 38 national guidelines, mostly from high-income countries in Europe and the Western Pacific. Substantial gaps – particularly in Africa, Latin America, and the Eastern Mediterranean – highlight fragmentation and inconsistency in national endometriosis care guidance. Several national guidelines were adaptations of pre-existing frameworks, most commonly drawing on guidance from ESHRE, NICE, or ACOG. Of the 9 regional or multinational guidelines identified, most were consensus statements led by Asia–Pacific or multinational expert groups, while only 2 were full CPGs from Europe, underscoring the limited availability of globally applicable guidance for the diagnosis and management of endometriosis.

This review provides a multilingual analysis of 47 guidelines across all WHO regions, documenting domain-specific clinical content, methodological variation, and global inequities that have not been previously described. A scoping review of the national availability of endometriosis care guidance reported that ~18% of WHO-recognised Member States have governmental, professional society, or collaborative government–society guidelines, while many rely on informal advocacy or social media sources6. Another review summarised diagnostic and management recommendations from 3 major international guidelines (ESHRE, RANZCOG, and NICE) but did not evaluate wider global variations20. Earlier work reviewed 7 predominantly high-income, English-language guidelines published before 201621, while another review compared treatment recommendations from 8 guidelines using standardised, evidence-graded tables22. However, these previous reviews did not evaluate guidelines across all WHO regions.

All included guidelines addressed reproductive-aged individuals, but only 77% considered adolescents, despite the known early onset of symptoms and diagnostic delays beginning in adolescence37. 83% of guidelines also addressed key clinical subgroups, such as pregnancy and menopause, but only a small number (19%) explicitly acknowledged gender-diverse individuals, highlighting persistent gaps in inclusivity. 70% of guidelines did not include the needs, preferences, and priorities of people with lived experience, particularly those from underserved communities. Systematic reviews have demonstrated that gender-diverse individuals who experience endometriosis often face even further delayed diagnosis and inadequate care due to cis-normative assumptions and the absence of inclusive clinical guidance38,39. All guidelines included gynaecologists as a key clinical care provider, and most endorsed multidisciplinary care; however, fewer than half explicitly addressed primary care clinicians, despite their pivotal role in early symptom recognition and referral, as highlighted by recent evidence emphasising the central importance of first-contact management pathways20,40.

PPI was reported inconsistently across endometriosis guidelines, with only 30% of the most recent guidelines (e.g., RANZCOG, NICE, ESHRE, HSE/NWIHP, AWMF/DGGG, ASPIRE, APEX, and WES) documenting patient participation in their development. This highlights the need for greater integration of patient perspectives to strengthen patient-centred care and shared decision-making.

Methodological heterogeneity was evident across guidelines. Although hybrid evidence–consensus models predominated in the original guidelines, grading frameworks were applied inconsistently, with roughly one-quarter of guidelines using the GRADE approach (e.g., RANZCOG, KSE, and ESHRE). This inconsistency reflects broader concerns about variability in transparency and rigour in guideline development41. In addition, several countries adopted existing guidelines rather than undertaking original development, most commonly using literature review combined with consensus processes to contextualise recommendations. Collectively, this variation highlights limited standardisation in the strength and clarity of recommendations, reducing their clinical applicability and comparability across health systems.

The variation in how adenomyosis is addressed across guidelines appears to reflect differences in guideline scope rather than disagreement about its clinical importance. Although adenomyosis frequently coexists with endometriosis and shares clinical features, it is increasingly recognised as a distinct condition with different diagnostic and management considerations. Accordingly, most guidelines limit their recommendations to endometriosis, whereas a minority, including the RANZCOG33, TEAS42, and PTGO43 guidelines, incorporate adenomyosis to better reflect the overlap between these conditions observed in clinical practice.

Over 77% of guidelines endorse a symptom-based diagnostic approach, discourage diagnostic laparoscopy as a first-line investigation, support empiric medical therapy, and outline referral pathways to specialist care for timely diagnosis. TVUS is commonly recommended as the initial imaging modality, with MRI reserved for suspected deep or severe disease when advanced ultrasound is unavailable; however, these distinctions are often difficult to establish from clinical presentation alone. Overall, these recommendations promote earlier, less invasive clinical pathways rather than routine surgical confirmation20. Such approaches are intended to minimise diagnostic delay, facilitate earlier symptom relief, and reduce the risk of disease progression44. Despite this broad convergence, inconsistencies were observed in recommendations on histological confirmation and the use of biomarkers such as CA-125. Most guidelines note uncertainty in the evidence supporting the use of biomarkers for the diagnosis of endometriosis, given their low sensitivity and specificity, which limits their ability to reliably confirm or exclude the disease32,33,45. Consequently, biomarkers are not recommended as standalone diagnostic tests for endometriosis. In contrast, histological confirmation remains important when lesions are identified. For instance, ESHRE, NICE, and RANZCOG recommend that lesions suspected at laparoscopy be confirmed histologically, while recognising that negative histology does not definitively exclude endometriosis32–34.

Some inconsistencies were observed in surgical recommendations, particularly regarding repeat ovarian surgery and the management of ovarian endometriomas, with excision often prioritised over ablation. Evidence suggests that repeat ovarian surgery may reduce ovarian reserve46, and that ablation may be associated with higher recurrence rates than cyst excision; however, high-quality comparative data remain limited47. For example, SASREG48, CENETEC49, PTGO43, and FOGSI50 state that both excision and ablation are effective for managing pain and infertility, while some American and Western Pacific guidelines do not specify a preferred technique. Although several American and European guidelines also do not explicitly address hysterectomy, most recommend it only for patients with persistent symptoms who no longer wish to conceive, emphasising that all visible disease should be excised at the time of surgery and recognising that hysterectomy does not constitute a definitive cure for endometriosis32–34.

Medical management of endometriosis showed strong convergence across most guidelines, with COCPs and progestins recommended as the preferred first-line hormonal therapy and GnRH analogues with add-back therapy advised as second-line options when initial treatment fails – consistent with high-quality evidence. NSAIDs or other analgesics are suggested, either alone or in combination with hormonal therapy, to alleviate endometriosis-related pain, although this remains a weak recommendation due to limited supporting evidence32,33. Most guidelines do not specifically address prescription opioids32,34,35; however, the RANZCOG guideline recommends that, if indicated, opioids be restricted to acute pain, prescribed at the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible duration, and not used routinely for chronic endometriosis-associated pain33. The LNG-IUS is also widely recommended for pain control, particularly when long-term, localised hormonal suppression is appropriate, although some guidelines did not explicitly include it34,51,52. For individuals with persistent symptoms despite standard medical or surgical treatment, only a minority of guidelines (45%) recommend aromatase inhibitors (e.g. letrozole, anastrozole) – usually in combination with other hormonal agents – reflecting the limited evidence base. Their use may be off-label for endometriosis, and differences in regulatory approval across jurisdictions may partly explain variation in recommendations. Several guidelines also recommend postoperative hormonal therapy for individuals who are not planning immediate pregnancy to reduce disease recurrence and pain; however, supporting evidence for this practice remains heterogeneous and limited53,54.

Most guidelines (>65%) advise offering ART/IVF for endometriosis-related infertility, incorporating ovarian reserve assessment when planning treatment, and adopting an individualised approach to fertility planning or MDT involvement. Despite this, notable variation persists across regions, particularly in guidance on early referral to fertility services, although early specialist referral is recommended for individuals with endometriosis-associated infertility20. Inconsistencies were also identified in recommendations advising against routine surgery before ART. While surgery for ovarian endometriomas may reduce ovarian reserve, evidence for benefit before ART remains uncertain55. In addition, available evidence for the use of hormonal therapies, such as oral contraceptives or GnRH analogues, before ART to improve live birth outcomes, remains limited46,56.

The majority of guidelines advise clinicians to discuss non-medical strategies to support quality of life and psychological well-being in endometriosis patients, with HAS/CNGOF57, MINSAL/SOCHOG58, MoH–New Zealand59, and ASPIRE29, explicitly supporting the use of psychological support, patient education, and complementary therapies like acupuncture and yoga. Other guidelines, including ESHRE32, RANZCOG33, NICE34, HSE35, and KSE36 – provide general support for this approach, but refrain from recommending interventions such as acupuncture, physiotherapy, exercise, diet, self-care or other complementary therapies due to insufficient evidence.

The ASPIRE consensus29 evaluated, through rigorous consensus methodology, how endometriosis care should be structured (although other guidelines allude to this), and what criteria should define a network of expertise for managing endometriosis in the Asia Pacific.

This scoping review maps the availability of endometriosis guidelines across all WHO regions and systematically characterises methodological variation and inconsistencies in clinical recommendations. Although we used a rigorous search strategy, the limited availability of non-English guidelines in electronic databases required reliance on grey literature sources and Google Translate, which may not have captured all relevant documents or search terms. Non-English documents were also translated using Google Translate for data extraction and, where possible, reviewed by multilingual collaborators; however, machine translation may have introduced minor inaccuracies or loss of contextual nuance60. Although methodological characteristics were examined in detail, formal quality appraisal (e.g., AGREE II) was not undertaken; thus, findings reflect the presence and content of recommendations rather than their rigour or overall quality. The use of a “presence versus absence” coding framework may have oversimplified recommendations, as items coded as present can vary in detail and strength. When multiple guidelines were available within a country, the most recent and comprehensive source was selected; this approach might have introduced selection bias by excluding subspecialty or complementary guidelines that provide more detailed recommendations. This review maps guideline availability and content but does not assess implementation, equitable uptake, or patient outcomes; guideline availability is not a proxy for quality of care. Variation in the inclusion of adenomyosis likely reflects differences in guideline scope and evolving clinical recognition rather than omission or lack of importance. Direct comparison of guidelines may be limited by differences in healthcare systems, resource availability, and implementation contexts across countries, particularly between high- and low-income settings.

The marked variation in guideline development methods and recommendations identified in this review, together with the underrepresentation of Africa and the Eastern Mediterranean Region, supports the value of a global normative guideline. Such a framework could promote methodological consistency, broader regional representation, and equitable recommendations, while allowing adaptation to diverse health system contexts and resource settings.

The absence of national or regional endometriosis guidelines may contribute to inconsistent clinical practice, delayed diagnosis, fragmented referral pathways, and inequitable access to evidence-based care, particularly in resource-limited settings. In these contexts, clinicians often rely on international recommendations or individual clinical experience, which may not align with local healthcare infrastructure or resource availability. A global guideline adaptable to local contexts could establish minimum standards of care, strengthen training and referral pathways, and help reduce inequities in endometriosis care across diverse health systems61,62. The findings of this scoping review may guide future efforts by identifying regions with limited guideline coverage, highlighting variations in recommendations, and informing priorities for guideline development and implementation. Greater collaboration among international professional bodies, regional organisations, policymakers, and patient representatives may support the development of harmonised, contextually appropriate guidance across diverse healthcare settings63,64.

Underserved populations, including Indigenous peoples, gender-diverse individuals, people living with disabilities, and those in rural and remote areas, may face additional barriers to timely endometriosis diagnosis and care due to geographic, cultural, and socioeconomic factors. These populations are often underrepresented in endometriosis research and guideline development, which may limit the extent to which recommendations reflect their needs, preferences, and experiences65,66. Greater inclusion of these perspectives is essential to support equitable, inclusive, and patient-centred care.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates substantial regional variation in the availability, methodologies, and recommendations of endometriosis guidelines worldwide. These findings highlight the need for greater harmonisation of guideline development and implementation, while ensuring recommendations remain responsive to regional healthcare contexts and population needs. Expanding guideline development in underrepresented regions, particularly in the WHO African and Eastern Mediterranean regions, and broadening target audiences to include primary care and adolescent health providers could improve access to timely diagnosis and management.

Improved collaboration between international professional bodies and relevant organisations could help reduce inconsistencies in guidance, strengthen education and training, support timely updates to recommendations, and enhance patient support. Further research is needed to better understand the causes and disease mechanisms, including robust evidence confirming the effectiveness of aromatase inhibitors and self-care interventions for managing symptoms of suspected or confirmed endometriosis. Additional research is also needed to advance non-invasive diagnostics, develop non-hormonal, precision-based treatments, and establish more effective clinical pathways to expedite earlier diagnosis and enable personalised care. A high-quality, standardised, inclusive, and global guideline is essential to address these gaps and improve the quality, accessibility, affordability, and availability of care for individuals living with endometriosis worldwide.
