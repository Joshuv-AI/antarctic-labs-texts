Salt-reducing pickering emulsions from Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. byproducts: preparation and saltiness-enhancing mechanism
Authors: Zi-Hui Cai, Zhi-Wu Yang, Wan-mei Zhou, Tian-Yu Ma, Dong-Qing Ye, Yu-Jie Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, the average salt intake per capita in China far exceeds recommended levels, leading to a range of health issues. As a representative seasoning, Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. (ZBM) has been demonstrated to enhance salty taste perception. However, the production of the pericarp generates substantial waste of by-product resources annually. This study presented a novel, simple, and green one-pot method for producing edible pickering emulsions from ZBM by-products. β-Cyclodextrin (β-CD) served as both an extraction enhancer and solid particle for emulsions innovatively. The optimized emulsion (6:4 oil-water ratio, 5% w/w β-CD) exhibited inherent saltiness while effectively delaying the oxidative degradation of hydroxy-α-sanshool (HAS) and inhibiting lipid oxidation. At the molecular level, main compounds primarily interacted with TMC4 through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. The development of this new flavouring products is not merely the efficient usage of ZBM by-product resources, but also of great significance to low-salt diets.Keywords: Pickering emulsions, Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. by-products, β-Cyclodextrin, Saltiness enhancement

Introduction

Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim (ZBM), a traditional Chinese condiment, was often used for sacrifices, warding off evil spirits and spices in ancient times (Hao et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2020). By now the Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim peel (ZBMP) is widely used as a flavouring and is gradually becoming one of the most prominent flavouring (Cui et al., 2023). However, the escalating industrial production of ZBM pericarps generates substantial agricultural by-products, specifically seeds (ZBMS, exceeding one million metric tons annually) and leaves (ZBML), which are typically discarded or relegated to low-value processing (Hou et al., 2019). This represents a significant waste of biomass, considering both by-products are rich in bioactive constituents. ZBMS is an excellent source of proteins, dietary fibers, and essential unsaturated fatty acids (Hou et al., 2019), which exhibit lipid-regulating and immunomodulatory properties (Guo et al., 2023). Concurrently, ZBML possesses a higher abundance of flavonoids (renowned for their antioxidant activity) and a lower content of pungent unsaturated fatty amides compared to the pericarp (Chen et al., 2019; Fengyuan He et al., 2016; Premathilaka et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2022).

The pungent amides in ZBM, particularly hydroxyl-α-sanshool (HAS), are the predominant compounds responsible for its characteristic numbing flavour (Luo et al., 2022). In the context of contemporary public health, reducing dietary sodium intake is a critical strategy to mitigate cardiovascular disease risks. Previous research has demonstrated that piperine (Moss et al., 2023), capsaicin (Han et al., 2025), ZBM oleoresin (Zhang et al., 2020), and essential oil (Liu et al., 2025) effectively enhance the human perception of saltiness. Most of them are pungent compounds or contain abundant pungent compounds. Consequently, it is hypothesized that the HAS and related amides abundant in ZBML could exert a similar salt-enhancing effect, offering a promising avenue to valorize these leaves as functional, sodium-reducing dietary ingredients.

Despite this potential, the industrial application of ZBM by-products is severely hindered by the extreme oxidative instability of HAS. For instance, related pungent compounds have been reported to degrade completely within four hours of ultraviolet exposure (Luo et al., 2022). Furthermore, conventional extraction methods often rely on volatile organic solvents. While emerging green alternatives like deep eutectic solvents (González-Rivera et al., 2021), supramolecular solvents (Cai et al., 2023), ionic liquid (Wang et al., 2022) and switchable solvents (Sed et al., 2018) have reduced toxicity, they still face regulatory hurdles for direct human consumption. Therefore, developing a food-grade, oxidation-resistant extraction and delivery system is imperative. β-Cyclodextrin (β-CD) presents a highly viable alternative. There have been recent studies using β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) for the extraction and encapsulation of plant active ingredients. Non-polar guest molecules can be incorporated into the hydrophobic cavity to form inclusion complexes using host-guest interaction, thereby delaying the oxidation of unstable compounds to some extent. At the same time, due to their external hydrophilicity, they can be easily dispersed in water, thus improving the extraction effect of different polar compounds. Due to their advantages of being cheap, safe and degradable, they are used as food additives and encapsulants in a wide range of food, biomedical and environmental applications (Li et al., 2022). The effect of using different types of CDs on the formation of food-grade Pickering emulsions has been compared, and β-CD was selected as the optimal CDs (Cheng et al., 2023).

While previous research has explored β-CD either as an extraction enhancer or an emulsion stabilizer independently, its integrated dual application within a single system remains unexplored. To address this gap, this study proposes a novel “one-pot” strategy. By utilizing β-CD to simultaneously extract lipophilic active compounds from ZBML and in-situ synthesize an edible pickering emulsion with ZBMS oil, this approach eliminates the need for toxic solvents and complex purification steps, strictly adhering to green chemistry principles. Specifically, the primary objectives of this study are to: (1) achieve the green extraction and enrichment of numbing components from ZBML; (2) prepare and structurally characterize the ZBM by-product-based edible pickering emulsion; (3) systematically evaluate its physical emulsion stability and the chemical oxidative stability of its components; and (4) verify the saltiness-enhancing capacity of ZBML and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism via receptor interactions.

Materials and methods

Materials

ZBML are selected from 18 varieties of ZBML provided by the Sichuan Academy of Forestry as the best variety (Zanthoxylum piperitum ‘Liujin’), and ZBMS are the seeds of the best local pepper variety – ‘Gong jiao’. Standards (all purity = 98%) for HPLC quantitative analysis were bought from Weikeqi Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China. Activated clay, β-CD, Methyl Tridecanoate (purity≥97.0%) were bought from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Acetonitrile (chromatographic grade) was from J&K Scientific Ltd., Beijing, China.

Phytochemicals determined by UPLC-MS/MS in Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. leaves

UPLC-QqQ-MS/MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system equipped with an autosampler and coupled to an Agilent 6470 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. The mass spectrometer was equipped with an Agilent Jet Stream (AJS) electrospray ionization source, operating in either positive or negative ion mode for the detection of all target compounds. Data acquisition and processing were executed using MassHunter software (Version B.10).

Chromatographic separation was efficiently achieved on a C18 column maintained at a constant temperature of 30 °C. The binary mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (solvent A) and 0.2% (v/v) aqueous formic acid (solvent B). The gradient elution was performed at a constant flow rate of 0.4 mL/min with the following program: 0–1.0 min, 5–7% A; 1.0–2.0 min, 7–14% A; 2.0–5.0 min, 14–30% A; 5.0–6.0 min, 30% A; 6.0–7.0 min, 30–90% A; and 7.0–8.0 min, 90–10% A. The post-run equilibration time was set to 3 min, and the injection volume was 5 μL. And the operational parameters for the electrospray ionization were optimized as follows: drying gas flow rate, 5 L/min; gas temperature, 300 °C; cell accelerator voltage, 4 V; nebulizer pressure, 45 psi; capillary voltage, 3500 V (for both positive and negative ion modes); and nozzle voltage, 0 V. Furthermore, the specific multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) conditions for each target compound were systematically optimized using the MassHunter Optimizer tool. The developed method demonstrated high sensitivity, with limits of quantification (LOQs) ranging from 0.0001 to 0.0781 μg/mL. Furthermore, the spiked recoveries ranged from 96.71% to 106.54%, demonstrating excellent accuracy. These validation results indicated that the proposed method was highly reliable and suitable for the quantitative analysis of active components in ZBML.

Phytochemicals determined by GC–MS in Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. seeds oil

ZBMS were dried and crushed, then passed through a 60-mesh sieve and extracted for eight hours using soxhlet extractor method to collect the gross oil. After refining by hydration degumming, sodium hydroxide deacidification, white clay decolorization and deodorization by distillation, the refined oil meets the standard and was placed in a 4° refrigerator.

ZBMS oil was analyzed using an Agilent 7890B GC/MS system coupled with a DB-5MS column (30 m * 0.25 * 0.25um; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) (see Table 1). The split ratio was 20:1, 1 mL/min of helium was used as carrier gas. The temperature of the transfer line, the ion source and the syringe were 250 °C. The heating procedure was as follows: an initial temperature of 40 °C for three minutes, a temperature increases to 180 °C at a rate of 20°/min for two minutes, and finally a temperature increases to 280 °C at a rate of 5°/min for 10 min. Methyl tridecanoate was utilized as an internal standard and the relative area was calculated using area normalization.

Extraction of Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. leaves with β-CD aqueous solution

Firstly, based on previous research conducted (Cai et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024), the parameters of the extraction process should be optimized, including the solid-liquid ratio and the number of extractions, in order to obtain a saturated ZBML extract. Due to the limited aqueous solubility of β-CD, 5%–8% β-CD was added to water and placed in a magnetic stirrer to heat and stir until a clear and transparent solution was formed (Blagojević et al., 2023). Then ZBML powder crushed through a 40-mesh sieve was added to the β-CD aqueous solution, mixed and sonicated for 15 min, centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. The supernatant was subjected to the same treatment with a second fresh batch of material, separated and the extraction continued until the solution was saturated. The supernatant after each extraction was centrifuged and filtered into HPLC analysis. All the experiments were measured three times in duplicate.

Synthesis and characterization of pickering emulsions

Preparation of pickering emulsions

The prepared ZBML extract was briefly mixed with pretreated ZBMS oil. The mixture was then stirred at 15000 rpm for 3 min using a high-speed homogenizer to prepare a homogeneous pickering emulsion based on ZBM by-products (Fig. 1). In previous experiments, it was found that the emulsion with β-CD aqueous solution saturated at room temperature was not stable, so the solution was heated to obtain a higher concentration of β-CD aqueous solution for configuration. Then, the oil-water ratio (2:8–7:3 v/v), mass ratio of β-CD in aqueous solution (2%–8% w/w) and the number of extraction times (1–4 times) were varied, and then the emulsions synthesized with different parameters were placed at room temperature for 30 days to investigate their stability and thus screen the suitable preparation parameters.

Schematic diagram of the synthesis process of ZBM by-products based Pickering preparation.

Droplet size

Pickering emulsions prepared with different oil-water ratios and mass fractions of β-CD were observed and captured by optical microscopy. Then, ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA) was used to select no less than one hundred representative droplets to measure the particle sizes and then averaged to obtain the particle size at that scale.

Morphology observation

Nile blue A (0.1 wt%, aqueous solution) was added to the prepared ZBML extract to stain the β-CD, while Nile red (0.01 wt%, 1,2-propanediol solution) was added to the ZBMS oil to stain the oil. The stained emulsion was placed under Zeiss inverted microscope and Leica-TCS-SP8 laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscopy (LSCFM, Leica Biosystems, Germany) for observation. When under LSCFM, it is observed and photographed in both bright field and fluorescent field (488 nm and 633 nm as excitation wavelengths).

Rheology analysis

The dynamic and steady rheology properties of pickering emulsions synthesized from aqueous solutions of β-CD at different concentrations were measured using rheometer (MCR302, Anton Paar, GmbH, Graz, Austria). The emulsions were placed on parallel plates for frequency scans within an angular frequency of 0.1–100 rad/s and for viscosity scans from a shear rate of 0.01–100 s−1.

Oxidative stability measurements

Pickering emulsion prepared by different extraction times of cyclodextrin solution and pure seed oil were collected in test tubes and stored in an electric bath at 55 °C for 14 days to accelerate oxidation. Lipid hydroxides (primary and secondary lipid reaction products) of emulsions are periodically determined to gain the peroxide value (PV) and thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS) according to previous methods (Li et al., 2022; Shao & Tang, 2014). The hydroxides were quantified by establishing external standard curves with benzene hydroperoxide and 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane, respectively. The absorbance of the final processed liquid was measured with a UV spectrophotometer at 510 nm and 532 nm wavelengths, separately. And all the experiments were measured three times in duplicate.

Stability of hydroxy-α-sanshool

Similarly, the emulsions prepared from β-CD aqueous solution with different extraction times and powders of ZBMP and ZBML were also sealed and placed in a metal bath at 55 °C for 14 days. Every two days, the emulsion was taken out and 70% ethanol was added to vortex for dissolving the compound. After centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered and entered into HPLC to determine the content change of HAS. Whereas, the powder of ZBMP and ZBML were extracted by ultrasound assisted extraction with 70% ethanol for 10 min and then centrifuged and filtered to determine HAS content.

Cell culture conditions and treatments

HepG2 cells were cultured in high glucose medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% foetal calf serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in a cell culture incubator environment of 37 °C and containing 5% CO2. The effect of β-CD aqueous solution, ZBMS oil, β-CD aqueous solution with ZBML extract, blank emulsion and pickering emulsion on cell viability was determined using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). HepG2 cells were transferred to 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. The cells were then transferred to a 96-well plate. After incubation, the cells were treated with different concentrations of samples (20, 40 and 60 μg/mL), respectively. Cells in the negative control group were added to DMEM medium without foetal calf serum, and three double pores was set up in each group and co-incubated for 24 h. The original medium or drug solution were discarded, and then 100 μL of 1 mg/mL MTT solution was added to each well. It was put back into the cell culture box for 4 h. The upper solution was discarded and 150 μL of DMSO was added to each well, followed by shaking for 10 min. Preparation of compound flavorings and electronic tongue analysis

As shown in Fig. S1, we set up high salt (10% NaCl aqueous solution), low salt (5% NaCl aqueous solution) as a control group, and pickering emulsions made by adding low levels of NaCl with different times of β-CD aqueous solution extractions (sample A–D), pickering emulsions without salt (sample C), the final composite seasoning, and commercially available seasonings as an experimental group to determine a total of eight indices: bitterness, astringency, freshness, sourness, richness, bitter aftertaste, astringent aftertaste, and saltiness. Each sample was reconstituted by adding distilled water and stirred for three minutes. The mixture was then diluted tenfold by adding a pre-configured reference solution, and the supernatant was gained after centrifugation and measured with an electronic tongue (INSENT, JAPAN, SA402B). All the experiments were measured three times in duplicate.

In addition, in order to meet the market demand, some simple compounding of the preparation is generally required. So, we added ZBMS oil to the aqueous solution of β-CD containing ZBML extract at an oil-water ratio of 6:4, followed by the compounding of 5% w/w NaCl, 0.3% w/w sucrose, and 6% w/w soy sauce. The mixture was homogenized and mixed at high speed for 3 min at 15,000 rpm to finally obtain a homogeneous edible pickering condiment.

Molecular docking of the compound with TMC4

Salty taste signaling was primarily mediated by two pathways: epithelial sodium channel (ENaCs), transient receptor potential vanillic acid (TRPV1) (Le et al., 2022). However, a growing body of evidence indicates that these two pathways were insufficient to fully explain all salty taste perception. Recently, Transmembrane Channel-like 4 (TMC4), a novel chloride channel, has been identified as a key protein regulating the intensity of salty taste. Consequently, TMC4 has emerged as a new molecular target for elucidating the mechanism of salt-taste enhancing peptides derived from various food sources.

Based on the method of Niu et al. (2024), the TMC4 structure was generated by homology modeling using TMC1 as the template. A semi-flexible docking strategy was adopted to simulate the formation of stable complexes, which was essential for elucidating mechanisms of action and for the identification of potential lead compounds. Docking calculations were conducted using AutoDock Vina version 1.1.2 (Trott et al., 2009) and protein structures were preprocessed with PyMOL version 2.4 by removing water molecules and non-essential ligands, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms. The ligand structure was energy-minimized using ChemDraw version 20.0. During each docking run, nine conformations were generated, with the conformation exhibiting the lowest binding energy and highest clustering frequency selected as the most probable binding mode. Visualization of docking results was carried out using PyMOL version 2.4 to display ligand–protein binding conformations and molecular interactions. Discovery Studio was employed to conduct a visual analysis of the optimized docking conformation, generating a two-dimensional interaction map to illustrate key binding features such as hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and π–π stacking.

Results and discussion

The active ingredient of Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. leaves and seeds oil

The chromatogram of HPLC and the total ion chromatogram (TIC) diagram of GC–MS are shown in Fig. S2. Regarding ZBML, flavonoids are the most important components. Rutin, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, and quercetin reached 0.034 mg/g, 3.595 mg/g, 0.305 mg/g, and 14.196 mg/g, respectively, while HAS was only 0.158 mg/g (50% ethanol extraction). Flavonoid components have been shown to have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-aging and other biological activities, as well as good biochemical effects on a variety of diseases (Shen et al., 2022). And the flavonoid components in the prepared O/W emulsions preferred to be distributed in the aqueous phase. Also, the composition in ZBMS oil is as reported, the main composition is unsaturated fatty acids (Linolic acid and 9-octadecenoic acid) with a relative content of 94.199%. It also contains α-Linolenic acid, which has a variety of health benefits, including improving intelligence, regulating blood lipids, preventing obstruction and other effects (He et al., 2022). Therefore, the by-products of ZBM contain a wide variety of active ingredients, most of which have excellent activity. The preparations produced from these by-products have the potential to become healthy condiments.

Extraction experiment

The extraction of β-CD is shown in Fig. 2, where the first extraction rate reached 1.70 times that of water, which is also slightly lower than that of 50% ethanol. This result may be due to the fact that β-CD itself acts as a thickener and the viscosity of its aqueous solution is inevitably higher than that of water and 50% ethanol, thus affecting the mass transfer process and the extraction. However, for the extraction of chlorogenic acid, the aqueous solution of β-CD was better than water and 50% ethanol, which also suggests that the aqueous solution of β-CD is not yet saturated and that the inner cavity of β-CD is not embedded with enough hydrophobic active components. Considering the cyclic countercurrent extraction method can effectively reduce the amount of solvent use and the composition, and also further enrich the content of active ingredients in the extract to make it closer to the flavour of the peel. Then three more extractions were carried out with fresh plant material, and it can be seen that the extraction rate decreased significantly in the fourth extraction compared to the third, indicating that the extracts obtained from the three extractions were basically saturated. Finally, the total extraction amount reached 4.890 and 2.187 times higher than that of water and 50% ethanol, resulting in an extract with 1/3–1/7 of the HAS content in the ZBMP extracted by the organic solvent. Blagojević et al. demonstrated that utilizing β-CD enhanced extraction yields by 40–50% compared to conventional water extraction (Blagojević et al., 2023). Consequently, employing sequential extractions in our study substantially enriched the concentration of active phytochemicals.

Effect of extraction times on the extraction yield using aqueous β-CD solution.

Emulsion stability

The observation results of pickering emulsions prepared with different oil-water ratios placed at room temperature for 4 weeks were shown in Fig. 3, all of which are uniform and stable emulsions when they are just prepared. Pickering emulsions consisting of 2:8, 3:7 and 4:6 oil-water ratios started to cream after only one day, while emulsions prepared with oil-water ratios of 7:3 showed delamination from the very beginning. And pickering emulsions prepared with an oil to water ratio of 5:5 showed slight creaming after one week, i.e., a small portion of water precipitated out. Finally, an oil-water ratio of 6:4 was chosen as the optimum, which is also in agreement with previous results reported in the literature (Cheng et al., 2023). The effect of different β-CD concentrations on the stability of the emulsion was not as strong as that of the oil-water ratio. The aqueous solution of β-CD below 5% w/w was obviously difficult to completely wrap around the oil-water interface to stabilize the emulsion because the amount of cyclodextrin was too low, and delamination phenomenon gradually appeared with the extension of time. However, the high concentration of β-CD greatly increased the viscosity of the preparation, which caused a certain obstacle for its application. Therefore, we chose 5% w/w as the optimal concentration of β-CD, which is slightly higher than the 0.025 mol/L concentration selected in previous studies (Cheng et al., 2023). On the other hand, extract solutions with different number of extractions had little effect on the stability of pickering emulsions. As the number of extractions increased, the color of the preparation gradually deepened and the total amount of enriched extracts rose gradually.

Microstructure of the emulsion

After the macroscopic stability observation, the specific particle size and optical microscopy diagram are shown in Fig. S3. The photographs taken using LSCFM in bright field and fluorescence are shown in Fig. 4A, which further complements the optical microscope photographs. Closely packed but not completely homogeneous droplets were formed within the emulsion, and the oil phase and β-CD were stained by Nile Red and Nile Blue A, respectively. Under the excitation of fluorescence, all the green fluorescence, i.e. Nile Red, is distributed within the droplets, which indicates the formation of a typical oil-in-water emulsion. As previously reported in the literature, the reddish-coloured β-CD particles are tightly distributed on the surface of oil droplets, forming a dense interfacial layer wrapped around the oil droplets, which significantly reduces the interfacial tension of the emulsion, and reduces the contact and merging of the oil droplets, thus making the emulsion more stable.

In addition, in order to further clarify the intrinsic mechanism of the optimal formulation scheme, the particle size of emulsions prepared with different oil-water ratios and β-CD concentrations was observed (Fig. S3). Changing the ratio of oil to water does not seem to change the particle size of the emulsions much, ranging from 14.18 to 23.73 μm. However, an increase in the proportion of oil also leads to an increase in particle size, which significantly accelerates the aggregation and merging of droplets and ultimately leads to emulsion delamination in a short period of time. And as the proportion of β-CD increases, the emulsion particle size decreases from 110.04 μm to 10.76 μm. The emulsion particle size obtained from 2% w/w β-CD aqueous solution preparation reaches about five times that of 3% w/w. The particle size of pickering emulsions prepared with β-CD concentrations exceeding 3% w/w apparently changed little, and the stability of the emulsions steadily improved. This is due to the fact that more β-CD adsorbs on the surface of the oil droplets to form an interfacial layer, effectively preventing the droplets from contacting and thus preventing them from precipitating, which in turn improves the stability of the emulsion.

Rheological properties of pickering emulsions

The rheology properties of emulsions have a significant impact on the processing characteristics, storage stability and application aspects of emulsions. As in Fig. 4B–C, the apparent viscosity of the emulsion increased significantly as the concentration of β-CD increased, probably due to the accumulation of particulate layers that formed a stronger network. At the same time, the viscosity of the emulsion decreases with increasing shear rate, exhibiting a significant shear-thinning behavior of the pseudo-plastic fluids. The storage modulus (G') of the emulsions are higher than the loss modulus (G″) with or without the addition of ZBML extracts, and the curves do not cross in the sweep frequency range. These phenomena are common in some pickering emulsions synthesized from solid particles (Yang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023), demonstrating that the final prepared emulsion is typically an elastic gel-like emulsion.

Stability of emulsion components

Oxidative stability

Fats and oils in the light, oxygen, moisture, temperature, metal ions and other factors will often undergo oxidative rancidity, resulting in the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids into hydroperoxides, and then decomposition of small molecules of aldehydes, ketones, acids, etc., and ultimately resulting in a decline in the value of oil and are no longer suitable for consumption. In addition to this, secondary oxidation products and polymers generated by the continued oxidation of hydroperoxides have been determined to be chemically toxic and can accumulate in the body, damaging the liver and causing irreversible damage to human health. The formation of primary and secondary lipid oxidation products in the emulsion was measured to assess the lipid oxidation stability of the emulsion (Fig. 4D–E). Pure ZBMS oil was used as a control and it was seen that both PV and TBRAS values gradually increased during storage. However, with the increase in the number of extractions, the content of active ingredients (mainly flavonoids) in the emulsions gradually increased, and the rate of increase in the PV and TBRAS values of the emulsions obviously slowed down gradually. With the exception of the secondary oxidation rate for one-time extraction (probably due to partial destabilization of the emulsion at elevated temperatures), the rate of lipid oxidation of the emulsions was significantly lower than that of the pure oils during the first ten days. The final value after 14 days was inversely proportional to the time of extractions. Moreover, the emulsion enriched with ZBML extract produced significantly fewer oxidation products and showed a significant improvement in lipid oxidation stability compared to the pure oil.

Stability of numbing ingredients

HAS is the most abundant alkyl amide in ZBM, and this class of compounds has been reported to be the main compounds that determine the numbness of ZBM (Wu et al., 2020). These compounds are easily oxidized due to the presence of three conjugated double bonds. The oxidative stability of HAS in the emulsions was determined along with lipid oxidation and was shown in Fig. 4F–G. Similar to the results for lipid oxidation stability, the time of extractions was proportional to the oxidative stability of HAS. After two weeks, the content of HAS in emulsions prepared by three- and four-times extraction of β-CD aqueous solution still managed to be around 60% of the initial content. Also, for HAS in ZBML, the rate of oxidative degradation was much higher than that of peels and emulsions during the first two days when HAS decreased substantially, probably due to the fact that the content of HAS in ZBML was not high. And the rate of degradation increased substantially when its environmental conditions were changed instantaneously. Even though the HAS content of the emulsion after two weeks was almost identical to the HAS content remaining in the ZBML, the emulsion greatly reduced the rate of HAS degradation during the first week of storage. The pickering emulsions prepared in this study also have higher HAS stability compared to conventional storage.

We believe that these superior properties of the emulsion may be attributed, on the one hand, to the fact that the flavonoid constituents in the ZBML have some antioxidant effects and inhibit the rate of lipid oxidation. On the other hand, the tight interfacial layer formed by the β-CD particles on the surface of the oil droplets isolates the lipids from some external pro-oxidant factors, which is partly responsible for the high lipid stability of the emulsion. All these indicate the storage stability of the edible formulation.

Safety evaluation of pickering emulsion

In order to verify its safety, the effect of different components of pickering emulsion on cell proliferation was determined. The results are shown in Fig. S4, where the aqueous solution of β-CD did not have any effect on cell proliferation. Single ZBMS oil, its synthetic emulsion and aqueous β-CD solution with ZBML extract were also non-toxic to the cells, with cell survival rates above 80%. Whereas the final pickering emulsion had a slightly lower cell survival rate than the other treatment groups, probably due to the compounding effect of multiple ingredients in the formulation, but the cell survival rate was still close to 80%. Therefore, the final edible preparation obtained by compounding the ZBML extract obtained from aqueous β-CD extraction with ZBMS oil proved to be non-cytotoxic and safe for consumption.

Flavouring effect of pickering preparation

Since the base solution is composed of potassium chloride and tartaric acid, which has a certain degree of acidity and saltiness, an acidity below ‘-13’ is considered to be odorless. As shown in Fig. 5A and Table S1, the indicators of all the samples are basically free of acidity, which is in line with the basic taste of the public. In Fig. 5C, the sample C obtained from the initial preparation showed higher umami flavour and lower bitterness compared to the other groups. What is more interesting is that its own salinity reached 6.47, and the salinity obtained when compounded with low-concentration NaCl solution was higher than that of the low-concentration group, which was similar to that of high-concentration NaCl solution, indicating that it has a certain saltiness in itself without adding salt. While some other flavour could play a role in the saltiness instead, the initial preparation of pickering emulsion had extremely low values of bitterness and astringency. In addition, water and β-CD in the composition are basically colorless and odorless, and ZBMS oil has not been reported to have a salty flavour, so it is likely that the ZBML are playing a salty taste. And it is possible that the salty flavour and certain numbness of ZBML, which are mostly used in Chinese folklore to make cakes as a food supplement, indicate that the emulsion can replace part of the salt to exert a salty flavour, reduce people's daily intake of salt, and lower the risk of disease. But nowadays a single seasoning can not meet the market demand, more for the composite seasoning to achieve a variety of flavouring effects. Therefore, after adding a small amount of salt, sugar and other auxiliary materials to the emulsion, we get the final composite finished seasoning (Fig. S5). Mechanism of saltiness enhancement

Molecular docking was performed between TMC4 and the primary compounds in ZBML (rutin, hyperoside, quercitrin, isoquercitrin, hydroxyl-α-sanshool, and linalool), which were selected based on our earlier experimental results. These compounds included the primary flavonoids and amides found in ZBML, as well as the main constituents of the essential oil, for reference. The final results indicate that rutin (−9.1 kcal/mol) exhibited the highest binding energy with TMC4, followed by hyperoside (−8.7 kcal/mol), quercetin (−7.5 kcal/mol), isoquercetin (−6.9 kcal/mol), HAS (−5.6 kcal/mol), and linalool (−5.5 kcal/mol). Previous studies have demonstrated that binding energy correlates solely with binding affinity rather than with the ability to enhance saltiness (Niu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). Despite some limitations, the molecular-level approach still holds significant reference value for verifying whether there is a saltiness enhancement effect. For example, linalool has been confirmed as the primary component of ZBM essential oil without any salty taste-enhancing effect, consistent with the lowest binding energy results (Liu et al., 2025). Therefore, given that irritant amides such as piperine and capsaicin have been demonstrated to possess salt-enhancing effects, HAS is highly likely to exhibit the same efficacy. Zhang et al. (2020) also reported a saltiness-enhancing effect of ZBM oleoresin, with the high content of hydroxyl-α-sanshool suggesting its potential contribution. Additionally, several flavonoid compounds, which possessed higher binding energy and were more abundant in ZBML compared to hydroxyl-α-sanshool, were also likely to play significant roles in the saltiness enhancement process. Molecular docking further revealed that various potential salt-enhancing compounds primarily bind to TMC4 through hydrogen bonds with key residues Glu525 and Gln527, as well as hydrophobic interactions with Ile426 and Pro529 (Fig. 6).

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully established an innovative, green “one-pot” strategy for the high-value valorization of ZBM by-products. By strategically employing β-CD as both an extraction enhancer and an interfacial stabilizer, a highly stable, food-grade pickering emulsion was fabricated in-situ. This dual-functional system overcame significant industrial bottlenecks by effectively mitigating the oxidative degradation of highly susceptible compounds, including HAS and unsaturated fatty acids. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the preparation possessed an intrinsic saltiness-enhancing capacity, functioning effectively as a sodium-reducing agent with a competitive sensory profile. Mechanistically, this enhancement was primarily driven by the robust interactions of leaf-derived flavonoids and amides with the TMC4 chloride channel.

Ultimately, the innovation of this work is in the utilization of by-product resources and the innovative one-pot method of green extraction and preparation. From pretreatment to extraction and preparation to final product development, it helps to promote the efficient usage of ZBM industry resources and the development of high-value and diversified product, and ultimately promotes the economic growth of the entire ZBM industry.
