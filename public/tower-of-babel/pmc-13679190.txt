Paper integrated aptamer–CRISPR sensing enables multiplexed small-molecule sensing in a low-cost device
Authors: Anindita Sen, Douglas James Bock, Tegan Foster, Rachel Fleming, Justin M. Hodgkiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CRISPR/Cas12a-based diagnostics achieved attomolar sensitivity for nucleic acid detection, yet their extension to small-molecule targets remains constrained by the need for solution-phase aptasensor–CRISPR coupling and laboratory infrastructure incompatible with resource-limited settings. Here we report a Paper-integrated Aptamer–Cas12a Sensing platform (PACSense) in which the complete aptasensor–CRISPR/Cas12a signal transduction cascade is air-dried within a paper substrate, enabling a one-pot, amplification-free small-molecule detection workflow that is initiated by sample addition alone. Two orthogonal sensing modalities are demonstrated: first, a signal-OFF bivalent aptamer channel for adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP), achieving solution- and paper-phase limits of detection of 336 nM and 446 nM respectively, and second, a signal-ON complementary strand-displacement channel for cocaine, which to our knowledge represents the first coupling of the NC195 aptamer to CRISPR/Cas12a sensing, with solution- and paper-phase LODs of 0.605 µM and 16.15 µM respectively. Both channels are operated simultaneously on a single multiplexed paper chip with a cross-channel geometry that routes sample passively to four spatially distinct reaction zones, enabling independent and simultaneous detection confirmed by fluorescence imaging under a 495 nm blue LED. Air-dried chips retain 76% of initial fluorescence signal after 30 days of ambient storage without cold chain, with a 93% signal retention measured from the functional equilibration baseline. The complete assay workflow is completed within 20 minutes using a reusable sodium acetate hand warmer for incubation and a 3D-printed smartphone-compatible reader, designed to align with ASSURED criteria for point-of-care diagnostics. PACSense establishes paper-integrated aptasensor–CRISPR sensing as a viable route to multiplexed, laboratory-instrument-free small-molecule diagnostics at the point of need.

Introduction

Equitable access to rapid, affordable diagnostics remains a critical unmet need in global health. The World Health Organisation ASSURED framework – defining point-of-care (POC) diagnostic criteria as affordable, sensitive, specific, user-friendly, rapid and robust, equipment-free, and deliverable to end-users provides a practical benchmark for tools intended to operate outside laboratory settings.1,23–25

Paper-based microfluidic devices have emerged as a compelling platform for meeting these criteria: first demonstrated by Martinez and colleagues as patterned cellulose substrates capable of performing colorimetric assays without instrumentation,2 they exploit passive capillary-driven fluid flow, support air-dried reagent storage, and can be manufactured at low cost from widely available materials.3,4 However, conventional paper-based lateral flow assays predominantly rely on antibody-based capture and are largely restricted to protein or pathogen targets. Extending paper diagnostics to small-molecules – including metabolites, drugs of abuse, and environmental contaminants – requires bioreceptors capable of recognising low-molecular-weight targets with high affinity and conformational selectivity.5

Aptamers, single-stranded oligonucleotides selected through the systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) process,6 fulfil this bioreceptor role: they can be raised against small-molecules that are poorly immunogenic, are chemically synthesised with high reproducibility, exhibit excellent thermal stability, and undergo a conformational switch upon target binding that provides a direct transduction signal.7–10 The ATP-binding DNA aptamer11 and the cocaine-binding NC195 aptamer12 represent two of the most extensively validated small-molecule aptamers and serve as canonical proof-of-concept targets for new biosensing platforms.

Recognition, however, is only half of the sensing problem. The conformational switch of an aptamer must still be transduced into a measurable signal, and direct aptamer readouts generate no intrinsic amplification. Their sensitivity is bounded by the affinity of the aptamer itself – a significant limitation for the modest-affinity receptors typical of small-molecule targets. One of the most promising routes for transducing signals from aptamers with amplification is CRISPR/Cas12a. Upon recognising a duplexed DNA activator, Cas12a causes indiscriminate single-stranded DNA trans-cleavage,13 a catalytic turnover process in which one activation event cleaves many fluorophore-quencher reporter molecules. This property underpins nucleic acid diagnostics such as SHERLOCK14 and DETECTR,15–17 and offers, in principle, a mechanism by which a small-molecule binding event could be reported with enzymatic gain.

Because Cas12a is RNA-guided and requires a nucleic acid activator, direct small-molecule detection is intrinsically impossible without a molecular interface. Coupling aptamers to CRISPR/Cas12a resolves this limitation: target binding modulates the availability of a CRISPR activator sequence, transducing a small-molecule binding event into a reportable nucleic acid signal. Xiong and colleagues pioneered this concept by coupling functional DNA molecules including aptamers to Cas12a for solution-phase small-molecule sensing.18 Li et al. subsequently demonstrated an accelerated bivalent aptamer strategy in which two ATP aptamer units are bridged by a linker strand that directly activates Cas12a, achieving rapid target binding and an ON-mode lateral flow readout.19 Despite these advances, all reported aptamer–CRISPR sensors for small-molecules operate in solution phase, requiring pipetting, tubes, and thermocycler or water bath incubation incompatible with resource-limited settings.

While air-dried CRISPR reagents can be stabilised on paper for nucleic acid detection,20,21 extending this to small-molecules introduces a fundamentally different challenge: the aptamer must retain its conformational switching behaviour on paper, where surface adsorption to cellulose fibres, restricted molecular diffusion, and altered ionic microenvironments can impair the structural rearrangement required for CRISPR modulation. No prior work has demonstrated the complete aptasensor–CRISPR signal transduction cascade – from small-molecule binding through aptamer conformational switch to Cas12a activation – operating end-to-end within a paper substrate.

Here we address this gap with PACSense, a Paper-based Aptamer–Cas12a Sensing platform fabricated from laser-cut chromatography paper, in which the complete aptasensing and CRISPR/Cas12a reaction cascade is air-dried within a single paper disc and performed in a one-pot format upon sample rehydration. Using ATP and cocaine as proof-of-concept small-molecule analytes, we demonstrate and discuss its detection sensitivity and selectivity against structurally related interferents, and chip-to-chip stability over 30 days at room temperature. We further demonstrate simultaneous multiplexed detection of ATP and cocaine analytes using a three-layer paper microfluidic chip that directs sample flow to spatially distinct reaction zones. Reader hardware comprises a reusable sodium acetate hand warmer as a thermostatically appropriate incubation source and a 3D-printed enclosure with a battery-powered blue LED and emission-filtered viewing window, enabling naked-eye and smartphone-image readout with no laboratory infrastructure. Collectively, the PACSense platform advances CRISPR-based small-molecule diagnostics toward a genuinely ASSURED point-of-care format.

Materials and methods

PACSense platform overview

The PACSense platform comprises three integrated components: an air-dried paper chip carrying the DNA aptamer and CRISPR/Cas enzyme mix, a reusable HotHands sodium acetate hand warmer (HeatMax Inc., USA) as a portable incubation heat source, and a 3D-printed fluorescence reader for signal visualisation by naked eye and smartphone camera. The complete platform requires no laboratory infrastructure beyond a battery-powered LED and operates within the temperature range generated passively by the hand warmer, satisfying laboratory-instrument-free requirements for point-of-care deployment in resource-limited settings.

Portable heating source

A commercially available reusable HotHands sodium acetate hand warmer was used as the incubation heat source (Fig. S1A). Activation by snap-triggered crystallisation of a supersaturated sodium acetate solution generates exothermic heat in the range of 37–45 °C, providing the thermally stable environment required for Cas12a trans-cleavage activity without electrical heating infrastructure. The paper chip was placed directly on the surface of the activated hand warmer for the duration of the 20-minute assay incubation period. Following incubation, the chip was transferred to the 3D-printed reader for fluorescence readout.

Fabrication of the portable fluorescence reader

A portable fluorescence reader was designed in-house using Onshape software and fabricated by 3D printing in black PETG to minimise internal light reflection. The enclosure measures 100 × 100 × 67 mm and comprises a main body and a flat base plate with a perimeter lip to prevent ambient light ingress during imaging. A 0.12 W, 495 nm blue LED from Lumimax 5 mm round blue diffused LED (manufacturer part number: LEDDC-5BLU) is mounted through a side through-hole at a 45° angle relative to the chip plane to direct excitation light across the chip surface while minimising backscatter into the imaging path. The top surface features a recessed bay (50 × 50 mm, recessed 5 mm) that seats an orange-tinted acrylic cover glass (32 × 32 × 3 mm) serving as a long-pass emission filter, transmitting FAM fluorescence emission (∼520 nm) while rejecting scattered 495 nm excitation light. The recessed bay design ensures the smartphone camera (Apple iPhone 17 Pro) lies flush against the enclosure top surface, providing a fixed and reproducible chip-to-camera distance for consistent image capture. Flash was disabled and exposure locked to manual settings for all imaging sessions. The complete CAD design files for the reader enclosure are provided as SI Fig. S1B. The 3D printable file can be accessed via: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21970694.

Fabrication of the paper chip

Chromatography-grade filter paper (Whatman 113, GE Healthcare) was selected as the base substrate due to its efficient capillary pressure-driven fluid transport, high wet strength, and excellent biocompatibility with oligonucleotide and protein reagents. Chip geometries were designed in Inkscape (version 1.3.2, 2023-11-25) and transferred to a laser cutter (Creality Falcon 2 Pro, 22 W, fully enclosed) for cutting. Single-zone chips comprised a circular reaction head (6 mm diameter) with a linear sample inlet tail to facilitate directional capillary wicking of sample to the reaction zone. A reagent volume of 10 µL was deposited onto each single-zone reaction head (Fig. S2A).

Multiplexed chips comprising four reaction zones were fabricated as a three-layer laminated assembly (Fig. S2B). The bottom layer consists of a 100 µm polyester film coated on one side with a hydrophilic coating ARflow 93025 for fluid flow. The hydrophilic layer has the Whatman 113 chromatography paper as reaction zone/substrate to facilitate the reaction. The middle layer consists of a double-coated tape with an acrylic hybrid pressure-sensitive adhesive on both sides of a clear polyester PET film from Adhesive Research Ltd ARcare 90106NB patterned to define the fluidic channels connecting the central sample inlet to the four circular reaction heads, with channel length and width optimised empirically to produce symmetric simultaneous flow to all four zones. The top layer consists of a transparent plastic sheet with a laser-cut central sample inlet hole. The three layers were aligned and laminated by pressure to produce a sealed chip directing fluid introduced at the inlet exclusively through the defined channels to each reaction head. A reagent volume of 5 µL was deposited onto each reaction head of the multiplexed chip. All designs can be accessed via: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21970767.

One-pot aptasensor–CRISPR/Cas12a solution-phase assay

One-pot aptasensor–CRISPR/Cas12a assays were performed sequentially within a single tube. The aptasensing reaction was carried out in 20 µL aliquots containing 13 µL ATP binding buffer (10 mM Tris–HCL pH 7.9, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2), 2 µL aptamer–linker mix (50 nM, aptamer : linker ratio 1 : 2), and 5 µL of ATP solution or buffer control, and incubated at 37 °C for 3–5 minutes to allow bivalent aptamer–target binding. The CRISPR/Cas12a detection reaction was subsequently initiated by addition of a 10 µL master mix containing 0.06 µM EnGen® Lba Cas12a (New England Biolabs, catalogue no. M0653), 0.06 µM crRNA, 1× NEB r2.1 buffer (New England Biolabs), and 1.6 µM dual-quenched hairpin reporter probe (5′-6-FAM-CTCTCATTT-ZEN-TTTTTTTGAGAG-IABkFQ-3′; IDT PrimeTime™), adjusted to volume with nuclease-free water. The combined reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 20 minutes. Fluorescence was measured using a Bio-Rad real-time PCR system CFX Opus 96 (FAM channel, excitation 495 nm, emission 520 nm). All oligonucleotide sequences are provided in SI Table S1.

Air-drying of paper-based aptasensor–CRISPR/Cas12a reagents

Paper-based assays used the same reagent composition as solution-phase assays but at 10× concentration to compensate for partial adsorptive loss of oligonucleotides and Cas12a protein to cellulose fibres upon immobilisation.21 Reaction buffer was omitted from the air-dried reagent mixture to prevent premature introduction of Mg2+ prior to sample addition; magnesium ions were instead introduced during rehydration via the ATP binding buffer to ensure optimal aptamer folding and Cas12a activity conditions. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, Mw 89 000–98 000, 10% w/v final concentration) was included as a stabiliser to form a protective glassy matrix preserving enzymatic and oligonucleotide activity during ambient storage.

For single-zone chips, 8 µL of the 10× reagent mixture was deposited onto each paper reaction disc by pipetting. For multiplexed chips, 5 µL was deposited onto each of the four reaction heads. Air-drying was performed at room temperature with a small fan to accelerate evaporation; complete drying was confirmed visually within 15–30 minutes. Dried chips were assembled into the three-layer multiplexed format where applicable, sealed individually in pouches with silica gel desiccant, and stored at room temperature in the dark until use. Assays were initiated by applying the target sample or buffer directly to the chip inlet at 37 °C, placing the chip on the activated hand warmer, and incubating for 20 minutes prior to fluorescence readout under 495 nm LED illumination.

Fluorescence image analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all paper chip fluorescence images were recorded at a fixed timepoint of 20 minutes and captured using a Thermo Fisher Dual LED Blue/White Transilluminator with amber long-pass emission filter (∼580 nm cut-on; 495 nm excitation, FAM emission 520 nm). This instrument was used as the reference imaging system for all analytical performance data reported in Sections 3.2–3.7. Images were captured using an Apple iPhone 17 Pro (flash disabled, manual exposure locked) placed directly above the transilluminator viewing surface. The prototype 3D-printed reader was evaluated separately as a field-deployable readout alternative in Section 3.9, using the same smartphone and ImageJ quantification approach described below.

Smartphone fluorescence images captured were analysed using FIJI/ImageJ (version 1.54g, public domain, NIH). The green channel was isolated from RGB images prior to analysis. A fixed circular region of interest (ROI) was drawn over each reaction zone and the mean grey value recorded. Background correction was applied by subtracting the mean grey value of an equivalent ROI drawn over an adjacent blank paper region, yielding the corrected mean grey value (arbitrary units, a.u.) used for all quantitative comparisons. Limit of detection (LOD) values were calculated as 3σ/slope, where σ is the standard deviation of blank (0 µM target) measurements across n = 3 independent chip replicates and slope is derived from the linear regression of the calibration curve within the linear dynamic range.

Results and discussion

Sensor design and working principle

PACSense is a paper-integrated aptamer–Cas12a sensing platform in which aptamer and CRISPR/Cas chemistries are air-dried within laser-cut Whatman 113 chromatography paper discs. Using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as a stabilising agent, we demonstrate a single-step, one-pot detection workflow that is initiated by sample addition alone and completed within 20 minutes; aptamer–target binding triggers the CRISPR reaction, with fluorescence visible within 5–10 minutes (Scheme 1).

Design and working principle of the PACSense platform. (A) Schematic of the signal-OFF ATP detection channel: in the absence of ATP, the bivalent aptamer linking strand activates the Cas12a–crRNA complex, driving trans-cleavage of the dual-quenched FAM hairpin reporter and producing high fluorescence; ATP binding sequesters the linking strand, inhibiting Cas12a and reducing fluorescence. (B) Schematic of the signal-ON cocaine detection channel: cocaine binding to the NC195 aptamer displaces a pre-hybridised 20-nucleotide activator strand, which activates Cas12a and generates fluorescence. (C) Sketch of the laser-cut three-layer multiplexed paper chip showing the central sample inlet and four circular reaction zones connected by symmetric capillary channels. (D) Assay workflow starting with sample introduction – incubation to final read out with the portable fluorescence reader, comprising a 3D-printed enclosure with a 495 nm blue LED and orange acrylic emission filter, with a reusable sodium acetate hand warmer as the incubation heat source and smartphone camera readout.

The platform implements two orthogonal aptasensor–CRISPR coupling strategies: (i) a signal-OFF channel for adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP), in which target binding sequesters the bivalent aptamer that otherwise activates Cas12a trans-cleavage activity, and (ii) a signal-ON channel for cocaine, in which target binding to the NC195 aptamer displaces a pre-hybridised 20-nucleotide complementary activator strand, releasing it to activate Cas12a. For ATP, we adopted the bivalent aptamer architecture of Li et al.,19 in which two copies of the classical Huizenga–Szostak ATP aptamer11 are bridged by a linker strand bearing an 8-base complementary segment and a poly-T5 connector. Isothermal titration calorimetry establishes that this bivalent construct binds ATP with Kd = 12.59 ± 0.87 µM, essentially unchanged from the monovalent parent (Kd = 11.59 ± 0.32 µM); the bivalent design confers no affinity advantage but pre-organises the aptamer in an extended conformation that accelerates target binding from approximately 30 minutes to 3 minutes.19 Circular dichroism and gel analyses in the same study confirm that ATP binding drives the conformational switch that sequesters the linker strand, rendering it unavailable to activate Cas12a – the basis of the signal-OFF response employed here. Rapid switching is a prerequisite for a point-of-care assay, and the bivalent architecture was therefore selected specifically for its kinetic rather than thermodynamic properties.

For cocaine, we selected NC195, a high-affinity aptamer generated by high-stringency in vitro selection.20 NC195 binds cocaine with Kd = 39 ± 8 nM approximately three orders of magnitude tighter than the classical MNS-4.1-family cocaine aptamers12 that underpin most reported cocaine biosensors – and exhibits markedly improved specificity against structurally related alkaloids and common cutting agents, addressing the well-documented cross-reactivity of the earlier receptors.20 Mechanistically, NC195 operates through the structure-switching principle first established for the original cocaine aptamer,12 in which target binding stabilises a folded conformation and displaces a pre-hybridised complementary strand. We exploited this displacement behaviour to generate a signal-ON channel by designing a 20-nucleotide activator strand complementary to the cocaine-binding region, which is released upon target binding and becomes available to activate Cas12a. To our knowledge, NC195 has not previously been coupled to any CRISPR-based sensing format.

In both channels, Cas12a trans-cleavage activity is reported by a cleaved FAM-Iowa Black hairpin DNA probe,30 with signal read out using a 3D-printed enclosure housing a 495 nm LED and an orange-tinted acrylic emission filter, providing both naked-eye and smartphone camera detection. A reusable sodium acetate hand warmer serves as the incubation heat source, eliminating the need for any electrical or laboratory infrastructure. A multiplexed three-layer paper chip routes sample from a single central inlet through passive capillary flow to four spatially distinct reaction zones, enabling simultaneous multi-analyte detection on a single disposable substrate.

Solution-phase characterisation of the ATP aptasensor–CRISPR system

Prior to paper integration, the analytical performance of the ATP aptasensor–CRISPR/Cas12a system was characterised in solution to establish a baseline against which paper-phase performance could be benchmarked. Fluorescence measurements were performed across an ATP concentration range of 0–5000 µM (n = 3 independent replicates per concentration), and a monotonically decreasing signal response was observed with increasing ATP concentration, consistent with the expected signal-OFF detection mode (Fig. 1A). The fluorescence signal reached a plateau below 1000 µM, indicating effective saturation of the aptamer binding sites and full inhibition of Cas12a trans-cleavage at higher target concentrations. A linear relationship between fluorescence intensity and ATP concentration was established over the range 0–500 µM (R2 = 0.95, Fig. 1A, inset), with the limit of detection (LOD) calculated as 3σ/slope, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank signal (n = 3), yielding an LOD of 336 nM (0.33 µM). The modest R2 reflects the inherently non-linear kinetics of Cas12a indiscriminate trans-cleavage activity, which produces a compressed response at higher analyte concentrations as aptamer-mediated inhibition approaches saturation; this behaviour is consistent with previously reported aptamer–CRISPR fluorescence sensors for small-molecules.19,26–29 The solution-phase LOD of 336 nM for ATP lies more than an order of magnitude below the aptamer Kd discussed in Section 3.1. Direct aptamer transduction methods, which report signal in proportion to the fraction of aptamer occupied, are generally constrained to detection limits at or above the Kd, since a measurable bound population is required to generate signal. Under the conditions tested here, the present system detects ATP below the apparent aptamer Kd, which we interpret as reflecting the catalytic signal amplification intrinsic to CRISPR/Cas12a: each target-modulated activation event governs the trans-cleavage of many reporter molecules, so that even the small fraction of aptamer engaged at sub-Kd analyte concentrations is transduced into a detectable fluorescence change. We do not interpret this as a decoupling of detection sensitivity from binding affinity; target occupancy, binding kinetics, the assay equilibrium and the amplification reaction remain interconnected, and the apparent detection limit reflects their combined effect under the specific conditions used. Rather, these results indicate that Cas12a amplification can extend the accessible detection range below the apparent aptamer Kd, which is advantageous when working with the modest-affinity aptamers typical of small-molecule targets.

Inset: linear range over 0–500 µM (R2 = 0.95), used to determine the solution-phase limit of detection of 336 nM (3σ/slope). The solution-phase LOD of 0.33 µM is comparable to previously reported fluorescence-based aptamer–CRISPR platforms for ATP detection,19 confirming that the bivalent aptamer–Cas12a coupling strategy is functional under the buffer conditions used in this work and that successful aptamer conformational switching is achieved within the one-pot reaction format. Critically, these solution-phase experiments validate the aptamer–CRISPR signal transduction cascade as the foundation for subsequent translation into the paper matrix, where additional challenges of surface adsorption, restricted diffusion, and reagent co-localisation must be overcome.

Paper-based ATP detection and analytical performance

Translating the solution-phase assay onto paper required addressing the three challenges anticipated for aptasensor–CRISPR systems in a cellulose matrix: adsorptive loss of oligonucleotides and protein to paper fibres, the altered ionic microenvironment governing aptamer folding and Cas12a activity, and preservation of the aptamer conformational switch through drying and rehydration. Three design measures were introduced to counteract these effects. First, to compensate for adsorptive losses, all reagents were deposited at ten-fold higher concentration than the corresponding solution-phase assay; lower reagent loadings produced weak (5×) or undetectable fluorescence (1×), and 10× was empirically determined as the minimum concentration yielding robust signal on Whatman 113 paper. Second, to preserve correct aptamer folding and enzymatic activity, magnesium ions were excluded from the air-dried reagent mixture and introduced only at the point of rehydration via the binding buffer, preventing premature Mg2+-dependent reactions during drying and ensuring optimal ionic conditions were established only once the sample was applied.22 Third, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was incorporated as an air-drying stabiliser, forming a protective glassy matrix that maintains the structural integrity of the aptamer and Cas12a during dehydration and rehydration, thereby preserving the conformational switching behaviour required for target-dependent CRISPR modulation. Together, these measures enabled the complete aptasensor–CRISPR cascade to operate within the paper matrix.

With these optimisations in place, fluorescence signals from ATP-loaded paper chips were quantified by corrected mean grey value analysis of transilluminator images (ImageJ, n = 3 independent chips per concentration). A signal-OFF dose–response was observed across the full ATP titration range of 0–5000 µM, consistent with solution-phase behaviour (Fig. 2A and B). All reported concentrations refer to the sample ATP concentration applied to the chip inlet.

A linear response was established over the range 0–200 µM ATP (R2 = 0.92, Fig. 2B, inset), with the LOD calculated as 3σ/slope, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank signal across n = 3 independent chip replicates, yielding a paper-phase LOD of 446 nM. The narrowing of the linear range relative to solution phase (0–500 µM) is consistent with the compressed dynamic range characteristic of surface-immobilised CRISPR reporters, where restricted diffusion of the cleaved hairpin probe fragments within the paper matrix accelerates apparent signal saturation at lower analyte concentrations.22

Critically, the paper-phase LOD of 446 nM represents only a 1.3-fold increase relative to the solution-phase LOD of 336 nM. The 1.3-fold increase in LOD relative to solution phase is accompanied by the elimination of pipetting, tube handling, cold-chain and thermocycler incubation. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of aptamer conformational switching coupled to CRISPR/Cas12a signal transduction operating entirely within a paper matrix, without any solution-phase pre-incubation steps.19 The result confirms that the bivalent aptamer retains its target-binding and CRISPR-modulating function after air-drying onto Whatman 113 chromatography paper and rehydration with sample, establishing the feasibility of the one-pot paper-integrated aptasensor–CRISPR format at the core of the PACSense platform.

Selectivity of the ATP aptasensor

The selectivity of the ATP aptasensor–CRISPR system was evaluated against guanosine 5′-triphosphate (GTP) and adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP) as the most structurally and biologically relevant interferents. GTP was selected as the most challenging structural analogue, sharing the triphosphate moiety and differing only in the nucleobase, while AMP represents the principal metabolic degradation product of ATP and a likely co-present species in biological samples. The comprehensive selectivity of the ATP aptamer against the broader nucleotide panel – including ADP, CTP, and UTP – is well established in the literature9,19 and was not duplicated here. Selectivity experiments were performed in solution at an ATP concentration of 500 µM, with GTP and AMP tested at 1000 µM – twice the ATP concentration – to provide a conservative assessment of cross-reactivity under conditions that favour interference.

As shown in Fig. 3, ATP at 500 µM produced a marked decrease in fluorescence signal relative to the blank, consistent with aptamer-mediated inhibition of Cas12a trans-cleavage. In contrast, GTP and AMP at 1000 µM both produced fluorescence signals comparable to the blank control (∼150 a.u.), confirming that neither analogue inhibits Cas12a activity and that the bivalent aptamer does not undergo the conformational switch required for CRISPR modulation in their presence. These results confirm that the selectivity of the ATP aptasensor is preserved within the one-pot CRISPR reaction format. Equivalent selectivity was confirmed on the paper platform, with paper chips loaded with GTP and AMP showing no visually discernible signal reduction relative to the no-target control under LED illumination (Fig. 3, inset), demonstrating that aptamer selectivity is maintained after air-drying and rehydration on Whatman 113 chromatography paper.

Only ATP produces a marked decrease in fluorescence, confirming aptamer-specific inhibition of Cas12a trans-cleavage; GTP and AMP signals remain comparable to the blank. Proof-of-concept cocaine detection and ON-sensor integration

Having established paper-based detection of ATP through a signal-OFF mechanism, we next sought to demonstrate that the PACSense architecture is not restricted to a single target or a single sensing modality. Extending the platform to a second analyte necessarily introduces two changes simultaneously – a different aptamer–target pair and a different signal transduction logic. Importantly, these two changes are not independent design choices but follow naturally from the established architecture of each aptamer. The bivalent ATP aptamer intrinsically produces a signal-OFF response, because target binding sequesters the linking strand that activates Cas12a,19 whereas the cocaine aptamer NC195 was originally characterised in a strand-displacement format12 that intrinsically yields a signal-ON response, in which target binding releases a complementary strand. Both the signal-OFF aptamer–linker strategy18,19 and the signal-ON strand-displacement strategy12,18 is individually well-validated in solution-phase aptamer and aptamer–CRISPR sensing. Demonstrating both modalities on the PACSense platform therefore does not require independent validation of all four target-logic permutations; rather, it confirms that the paper-integrated platform accommodates the two most natural and established implementations of these distinct aptamer architectures. Cocaine was selected as the second analyte to provide a target structurally and mechanistically unrelated to ATP, and its strand-displacement aptamer conveniently exercises the complementary signal-ON modality.

The cocaine-binding aptamer NC195 has been extensively characterised in fluorescence displacement assays,12 however its coupling to CRISPR/Cas12a sensing has not previously been reported. In the fluorescence displacement format, a complementary strand pre-hybridised to NC195 is released upon cocaine binding and conformational switching of the aptamer. Here, we extended this principle to CRISPR activation by designing a 20-nucleotide activator strand complementary to the cocaine-binding region of NC195, of sufficient duplex length and stability to activate the Cas12a–crRNA complex upon release. This produces a signal-ON response mode orthogonal to the ATP channel, in which increasing cocaine concentration generates increasing Cas12a trans-cleavage activity and rising fluorescence (Fig. 4A and B).

Solution-phase characterisation was performed across a cocaine concentration range of 0–500 µM (0, 10, 20, 80, 100, 200, and 500 µM; n = 3 independent replicates per concentration). A linear increase in fluorescence with cocaine concentration was observed over the range 0–100 µM (R2 = 0.92, Fig. 4A, inset), with the LOD calculated as 3σ/slope, yielding 0.60 µM. Signal response plateaued above 200 µM, consistent with saturation of the strand displacement equilibrium at higher cocaine concentrations. The solution-phase LOD of 0.60 µM is comparable to previously reported aptamer-based cocaine sensors12 and confirms that the 20-nucleotide activator strand design produces sufficient thermodynamic drive for cocaine-induced displacement and subsequent Cas12a activation within the one-pot reaction format. The thermodynamic context for the cocaine channel differs instructively from that of the ATP channel. The solution-phase LOD achieved here (0.60 µM) lies above, rather than below, the intrinsic Kd of the receptor discussed in Section 3.1, which suggests that sensitivity in this channel may not be limited by aptamer affinity but by the thermodynamics of the strand-displacement step. Because cocaine must compete against a pre-hybridised 20-nucleotide activator duplex, we propose that the effective sensitivity of the sensor is governed by the stability of that duplex rather than by the aptamer–cocaine interaction itself: a duplex that is too stable would raise the cocaine concentration required to drive displacement, elevating the LOD above the aptamer Kd. We emphasise that this interpretation is proposed rather than experimentally established, as the present study does not compare activator-strand lengths, melting temperatures, or displacement equilibria. Nonetheless, it identifies a plausible route to improved performance – tuning the length and melting temperature of the activator strand to lower the displacement threshold – and suggests that the nanomolar affinity of NC195 may remain substantially untapped in the current design. That the sensor nevertheless achieves sub-micromolar detection in a one-pot, amplification-free format confirms the viability of the signal-ON architecture, while identifying activator-strand optimisation as a target for future development.

Further analytical validation of the cocaine aptasensor – specifically paper-phase selectivity against structurally related compounds and testing in complex biological or forensic matrices is beyond the scope of the present proof-of-concept study. The solution-phase characterisation presented here confirms the functional coupling of NC195 to CRISPR/Cas12a and establishes the proof-of-concept for the signal-ON channel, while paper-phase dose–response and limit of detection are established in Section 3.6. Integration of the cocaine aptasensor alongside the ATP channel in a simultaneous multiplexed format is demonstrated in Section 3.7.

Paper-based cocaine detection and analytical performance

To establish the analytical performance of the cocaine channel directly on the paper device, a dose–response was recorded on air-dried paper chips under the same conditions used for the ATP channel, with corrected mean grey values quantified from transilluminator images at the 20-minute endpoint (n = 3 independent chips per concentration; Fig. 5). The signal-ON response increased with cocaine concentration and saturated above approximately 100 µM, consistent with the competitive strand-displacement mechanism of the NC195 activator system. A linear response was observed over 0–80 µM (y = 1.30x + 7.70, R2 = 0.95), from which a paper-phase limit of detection of 16.15 µM was determined (3σ/slope, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank, n = 3).

Corrected mean grey values (relative fluorescence intensity, RFU) from air-dried paper chips as a function of cocaine concentration (0–500 µM), recorded from transilluminator images at a fixed 20-minute endpoint. The response saturates above ∼100 µM. Top: representative fluorescence images of the reaction zones at each concentration. Inset: linear region over 0–80 µM (y = 1.30x + 7.70, R2 = 0.95), from which the paper-phase limit of detection of 16.15 µM was determined (3σ/slope). Data points represent the mean ± standard deviation of three independent chips n = 3 per concentration.

This paper-phase LOD is approximately 27-fold higher than the solution-phase value (0.605 µM), a substantially larger solution-to-paper shift than observed for the ATP channel (1.3-fold). We attribute this difference to the distinct transduction mechanisms of the two channels. The signal-OFF ATP channel operates by direct sequestration of the Cas12a activator upon target binding, whereas the signal-ON cocaine channel depends on a competitive strand-displacement equilibrium in which cocaine must displace a pre-hybridised activator strand. This displacement step is expected to be more adversely affected by the paper matrix – where restricted molecular diffusion, adsorption of oligonucleotides to cellulose, and the fixed 20-minute reaction endpoint all discourage a bimolecular competitive equilibrium – than the simpler sequestration event underlying the ATP channel. The larger paper-phase penalty for the cocaine channel is therefore consistent with the displacement-limited behaviour of the signal-ON architecture, and further indicates activator-strand design, rather than aptamer affinity, as the principal route to improved cocaine sensitivity. While the larger paper-phase penalty is consistent with a displacement-limited mechanism, this interpretation is proposed rather than experimentally dissected in the present work (see Limitations).

Multiplexed simultaneous detection on a consolidated paper chip

The multiplexed chip was designed to deliver a single sample to four reaction zones simultaneously. A cross-channel geometry laser-cut into Whatman 113 chromatography paper directs flow from a central inlet to each zone, and lamination between transparent plastic layers confines fluid to the defined channel. The central inlet connects symmetrically to four circular reaction heads via channels of equal length and width, ensuring equivalent fluid path resistance to each zone. Passive capillary wicking following sample addition at the inlet was visualised using a red food dye tracer, confirming simultaneous and symmetric flow to all four reaction heads within seconds of sample introduction, with no preferential filling of any individual channel (Fig. 6A). This symmetric wicking behaviour demonstrates that the chip geometry delivers equivalent sample volumes to each reaction zone without active pumping or user intervention, a prerequisite for reliable, multiplexed quantification.

Multiplexed simultaneous detection on a consolidated paper chip. (A) Photograph of the dye wicking experiment showing symmetric, simultaneous capillary flow from the central inlet to all four reaction zones. (B) Quantification of corrected mean grey values for each zone (mean ± SD, n = 3 independent chips). The cocaine ON-sensor and positive control zones generate strong fluorescence, while the ATP OFF-sensor zone is statistically indistinguishable from the negative control (one-way ANOVA, F(3,8) = 16 537.9, p < 0.0001; Tukey post hoc). Inset: fluorescence image of the multiplexed chip under 495 nm LED illumination following addition of a mixed sample containing 500 µM ATP and 500 µM cocaine; zone 1 (top) = negative control, zone 2 (right) = cocaine ON-sensor, zone 3 (bottom) = positive control, zone 4 (left) = ATP OFF-sensor.

The four reaction zones were assigned as follows: negative control (buffer only, no CRISPR reagents); cocaine ON-sensor channel (NC195 aptamer with pre-hybridised 20-nucleotide activator strand and Cas12a–crRNA); positive control (Cas12a–crRNA with free activator strand, no aptamer); ATP OFF-sensor channel (bivalent aptamer–Cas12a crRNA). The positive control zone, containing constitutively active CRISPR without aptamer-mediated inhibition, establishes the maximum achievable fluorescence signal for each chip and serves as an internal reference for normalisation. The negative control zone, containing no CRISPR reagents, confirms the absence of non-specific fluorescence from the sample or paper matrix. Although a dedicated reaction-matched negative-control zone was not included on the multiplexed chip; the single-channel no-target conditions (Section 3.6), in which the complete sensing chemistry produced only background paper autofluorescence, indicate that the assembled Cas12a/crRNA/reporter chemistry does not self-activate in the absence of target. The negative-control zone additionally reports paper autofluorescence in the absence of CRISPR reagents.

A mixed sample containing 500 µM ATP and 500 µM cocaine was introduced at the central inlet of the assembled chip and incubated on the sodium acetate hand warmer for 20 minutes prior to fluorescence readout. As shown in the chip images (Fig. 6B, inset), the cocaine ON-sensor zone and positive control zone produced clearly visible fluorescence under 495 nm LED illumination, while the ATP OFF-sensor zone and negative control zone remained dark. Quantification of corrected mean grey values by ImageJ analysis (Fig. 6B; n = 3 independent chips; mean ± SD) confirmed these observations: the cocaine ON-sensor zone (122.0 ± 2.0 a.u.) and positive control zone (194.8 ± 1.0 a.u.) generated fluorescence signals 54.6-fold and 87.2-fold above the negative control (2.2 ± 0.3 a.u.) respectively, while the ATP OFF-sensor zone (2.4 ± 0.2 a.u.) was statistically indistinguishable from the negative control, consistent with ATP-mediated inhibition of Cas12a trans-cleavage in the OFF channel. One-way ANOVA showed significant differences between zones (F(3,8) = 16 537.9, p < 0.0001), with Tukey post hoc analysis indicating that the ATP OFF-sensor zone was not significantly different from the negative control (p > 0.05). These results demonstrate that the signal-OFF ATP and signal-ON cocaine channels can be operated simultaneously on a single chip, each producing its expected response at the concentration combination tested (500 µM ATP and 500 µM cocaine). Cross-interference was not assessed across the full analytical range of the two channels (see Limitations), and the multiplexed result is therefore presented as a proof-of-concept demonstration of simultaneous operation.

Reagent stability under ambient storage conditions

The shelf life of air-dried PACSense chips was evaluated by storing fabricated chips at room temperature with desiccant in the dark and measuring relative fluorescence signal intensity at intervals over a 30-day period (days 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 30). Signal intensity was quantified as relative fluorescence intensity (% of day 0) by corrected mean grey value analysis of transilluminator images using ImageJ. As shown in Fig. 7, signal decay followed a biphasic pattern: an initial decrease of approximately 18% was observed within the first four days of storage, after which signal intensity entered a stable plateau maintained between 80% and 83% through to day 20, before declining slightly to 76% at day 30.

Reagent stability under ambient storage conditions. Relative fluorescence intensity (% of day 0) of air-dried PACSense chips stored at room temperature with desiccant over 30 days. An initial decrease of approximately 18% within the first four days (attributed to adsorptive equilibration) is followed by a stable plateau (80–83%) through day 20 and a slight decline to 76% at day 30. Dashed lines indicate the day 0 baseline – red (100%) and the day 4 equilibration baseline – blue (82%), from which 93% signal retention is maintained at day 30. At each time point, three independent chips were measured (mean ± standard deviation); all chips were prepared from a single air-dried batch.

The rapid initial signal loss may be attributable to irreversible adsorption of a fraction of air-dried aptamer and Cas12a molecules to cellulose fibres during the drying and initial equilibration phase, a phenomenon reported in paper-immobilised biomolecular systems.21 If adsorptive equilibration is largely complete by approximately day 4, the remaining functionally active reagent pool would be expected to produce a stable signal with minimal further loss – a behaviour characteristic of systems where initial surface saturation stabilises the remaining free reagent fraction. The extended plateau spanning days 4–20 indicates that PVA-stabilised air-drying helps preserve the aptamer–CRISPR reagent assembly against loss of signal under ambient storage conditions. As the contributions of adsorption and equilibration were not directly investigated here, this biphasic interpretation remains a proposed rather than an established explanation.

As chips would not be used immediately after fabrication, the day 4 equilibration baseline better reflects the reagent pool available at the point of use. Normalised to this baseline, signal retention at day 30 is 93% under ambient storage without cold chain; against the day 0 baseline, 76% signal retention was maintained at day 30. The day 30 signal remained clearly distinguishable from background, consistent with retention of sufficient Cas12a trans-cleavage and aptamer binding function to support target discrimination over the 30-day storage period. While the study confirms retention of fluorescence signal over 30 days, it did not assess post-storage analytical performance; calibration and limit of detection were not re-determined after storage. These results indicate that PVA-stabilised air-drying on Whatman 113 chromatography paper supports ambient-temperature storage of the complete aptasensor–CRISPR reagent assembly, consistent with the ASSURED criteria for field-deployable diagnostics.1

Comparison of the prototype reader against laboratory transilluminator

Analytical performance data reported in previous sections were collected using a Thermo Fisher Dual LED Blue/White Transilluminator with amber long-pass emission filter (∼580 nm cut-on; 495 nm excitation, FAM emission 520 nm) as the reference fluorescence imaging instrument. To assess whether the 3D-printed prototype reader (Fig. S1C and D) could reproduce the positive/negative discrimination of this reference instrument without laboratory infrastructure, paired fluorescence measurements were performed on identical PACSense chips imaged sequentially on both instruments. The orange acrylic emission filter of the prototype reader was selected to approximate the amber filter of the transilluminator, facilitating direct instrument comparison. Chips loaded with 500 µM ATP (positive) and buffer only (blank) were prepared in triplicate (n = 3 independent chips per condition), incubated on the hand warmer for 20 minutes, and imaged on the transilluminator followed immediately by the prototype reader. Mean grey values were extracted from all images using ImageJ as described in Section 2.7.

At both conditions tested, the prototype reader produced systematically lower absolute intensity values than the transilluminator, consistent with differences in optical path geometry, emission filter transmission characteristics, and smartphone camera sensor response. The signal-to-background ratio was nonetheless preserved across instruments – 2.4× for the transilluminator and 2.2× for the prototype reader – and both blank and positive chips were correctly and unambiguously discriminated by both readout systems under identical assay conditions (Fig. 8).

Comparison of the prototype reader with a laboratory transilluminator. Corrected mean grey values (mean ± standard deviation, n = 3) for blank and ATP-positive (500 µM) chips imaged on the transilluminator and on the prototype reader; the dashed line indicates the line of identity (y = x). Inset: representative fluorescence images of blank and positive chips captured on both instruments, showing equivalent visual discrimination. The signal-to-background ratio was 2.4× for the transilluminator and 2.2× for the prototype reader.

These results indicate that the prototype reader reproduces the positive/negative discrimination of the laboratory transilluminator for the two conditions tested, supporting its feasibility as an equipment-minimised readout for the assay. This comparison was, however, limited to a blank and a single positive concentration (500 µM ATP); it therefore establishes agreement for qualitative discrimination at these conditions but does not, on its own, demonstrate equivalence of quantitative sensitivity, linearity, selectivity, or long-term stability between the two readout formats. Validation across a full concentration range, and across different smartphones and ambient lighting conditions, remains to be carried out and is noted among the limitations of the present study. Combined with the hand warmer incubation source and single-step sample addition workflow, the prototype reader nonetheless supports the operation of PACSense as a laboratory-instrument-free assay in resource-limited settings.

Limitations of the present study

This work establishes the PACSense platform as a proof-of-concept and, in doing so, defines several limitations that frame its current applicability and the priorities for future development. First, the cocaine channel is now characterised on paper by dose–response and limit of detection. However, its selectivity against structurally related compounds and common interferents was not evaluated in the present strand-displacement/Cas12a configuration, nor was it validated in complex biological or forensic matrices. The cocaine results should therefore be regarded as a proof-of-concept demonstration rather than a fully validated assay.

Second, simultaneous operation of the two channels was demonstrated only at a single high-concentration combination (500 µM ATP and 500 µM cocaine). A full cross-interference study, in which the concentration of one analyte is varied in the presence of the other across the analytical range, was not undertaken. The absence of cross-talk is therefore established only for the condition tested.

Third, the 30-day storage study tracked retained fluorescence signal rather than post-storage analytical performance. Calibration and limit of detection were not re-determined after storage. All stability chips derived from a single preparation batch, so inter-batch reproducibility was not assessed.

Fourth, validation of the 3D-printed reader against the laboratory transilluminator was limited to blank and 500 µM ATP conditions (n = 3). It therefore supports agreement for positive/negative discrimination at those conditions only, not equivalence of sensitivity, linearity, selectivity, or stability. Robustness across different smartphones and lighting conditions also remains to be assessed.

Fifth, the proof-of-concept experiments used relatively high analyte concentrations. The mechanistic explanation proposed for the larger paper-phase limit of detection of the cocaine channel-attributed to the thermodynamics and kinetics of activator-strand displacement in the paper matrix-was not experimentally dissected, as we did not vary activator-strand length, melting temperature, or displacement equilibria.

Addressing these points, in particular paper-phase selectivity and real-matrix validation of the cocaine channel, cross-interference characterisation, post-storage analytical validation, and optimisation of the activator-strand design, represents the natural next stage in developing PACSense toward a field-deployable diagnostic.

Conclusion

We have presented PACSense, a paper-integrated aptamer–Cas12a sensing platform that demonstrates, to our knowledge for the first time, the complete aptasensor–CRISPR signal transduction cascade operating end-to-end within an air-dried paper matrix without solution-phase pre-incubation steps. Using ATP and cocaine as proof-of-concept small-molecule analytes, the platform achieved solution-phase LODs of 336 nM and 0.60 µM respectively, with paper-phase detection yielding LODs of 446 nM for ATP and 16.15 µM for cocaine. Two orthogonal sensing modalities were implemented within the same platform architecture: a signal-OFF bivalent aptamer channel for ATP, adapting the strategy of Li et al.19 and a signal-ON complementary strand-displacement channel for cocaine representing, to our knowledge, the first coupling of the NC195 aptamer to CRISPR/Cas12a sensing. The ATP sensor operates below its apparent aptamer Kd (12.6 µM), which we interpret as reflecting the catalytic amplification of Cas12a extending detection below the apparent affinity limit, while noting that occupancy, kinetics, equilibrium and amplification remain interconnected; the cocaine sensor operates above the Kd of NC195 (39 nM), which we propose reflects the competitive strand-displacement architecture, in which the displacement equilibrium rather than the aptamer may become the sensitivity-limiting step. Together these observations suggest design considerations for aptamer–CRISPR coupling: signal-OFF architectures that directly sequester the activator appear to exploit the full amplification benefit of Cas12a, whereas signal-ON displacement architectures may require the activator duplex to be thermodynamically matched to the target–aptamer interaction if the affinity of the receptor is to be fully realised. Both channels were operated simultaneously on a single multiplexed paper chip, demonstrating independent detection with clear visual discrimination under 495 nm LED illumination at the single concentration combination tested.

From a diagnostic engineering perspective, PACSense addresses several practical barriers to field deployment of CRISPR-based small-molecule sensing. PVA-stabilised air-drying of the complete reagent assembly within Whatman 113 chromatography paper supports ambient-temperature storage with 76% signal retention at 30 days and 93% retention measured from the day 4 functional equilibration baseline, reducing cold chain requirements. A reusable sodium acetate hand warmer replaces thermocycler or water bath infrastructure for assay incubation, and a 3D-printed enclosure with orange acrylic emission filter provides fluorescence readout by smartphone camera; comparison against a laboratory transilluminator showed agreement for positive/negative discrimination at the conditions tested. The complete workflow from sample addition to readout is achieved within 20 minutes, and is designed to align with the ASSURED criteria for point-of-care diagnostics in resource-limited settings.

The current work establishes proof-of-concept for the PACSense platform architecture and identifies clear directions for further development. Further validation of the cocaine aptasensor channel – including paper-phase selectivity characterisation and real-matrix testing in relevant biological or forensic samples – remains to be completed. Optimisation of the NC195 activator strand length and melting temperature is expected to improve cocaine detection sensitivity. Extension of the platform to additional small-molecule targets of clinical, environmental, or food safety relevance would further demonstrate the modularity of the aptasensor–CRISPR coupling strategy. Finally, integration of quantitative image analysis directly into a smartphone application would provide a fully self-contained diagnostic tool suitable for community health workers and field responders operating without laboratory support. Together, these advances would position PACSense as a broadly applicable, laboratory-instrument-free platform for multiplexed small-molecule diagnostics at the point of need.
