Viral helicase and methyltransferase promote genotype one-hepatitis e virus internal ribosome entry site-like element activity
Authors: Shiv Kumar, Samriddhi Mehta, Jyoti Gupta, Geetika Anand, Preeti Hooda, Akash Kumar, Deepak Sehgal, Milan Surjit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis E virus (HEV) is a leading cause of acute viral hepatitis. Our earlier study reported the presence of an 87-nucleotide-long internal ribosome entry-site-like element (IRESl) in the genotype 1 (g1)-HEV genome, which mediated cap-independent translation of the viral ORF4 protein. RNA-protein interactome analysis of the HEV IRESl revealed its association with multiple host proteins, including translation regulatory proteins, which controlled its function. Role of HEV-encoded proteins in modulating the viral IRESl activity remains unknown. In the present study, we investigated the role of viral proteins in modulating the activity of the HEV IRESl element. Luciferase-reporter assay using a bicistronic vector revealed the ability of viral Helicase and MeT-Y-domain proteins in upregulating the HEV IRESl activity. Further studies confirmed direct interaction of the viral Helicase and MeT-Y-domain with the HEV IRESl. Collectively, these findings unravel the positive role of HEV Helicase and MeT-Y-domain proteins in modulating the viral IRESl activity.

Introduction

Proteins are synthesized in a eukaryotic cell through a cap-dependent or cap-independent translation process. Many RNA viruses are exclusively dependent on the cap-independent translation process to synthesize their proteins. Prominent examples include the Polio virus, Hepatitis A virus and the Hepatitis C virus. Here, viral translation is predominantly mediated by an RNA regulatory element located in the 5’- end of the viral genome, called the internal ribosome entry site (IRES), which initiates translation by cap independent assembly of the eIF4F complex or direct recruitment of the 40S ribosomal subunit1,2. Some RNA viruses such as the Dengue virus, utilizes IRES mediated translation only under conditions of stress3–5. There is no consensus among the IRESs regarding requirement of factors essential for their optimal activity. However, each IRES depends on one or more host factors for optimal function. These host factors are called IRES-trans-acting factors (ITAFs). Most ITAFs are cellular RNA processing factors that act by directly binding with the IRES RNA. They may help in maintaining the IRES function by stabilizing its conformation, by facilitating IRES interaction with other RNA/proteins leading to assembly of the translation initiation complex6,7. Notably, La protein (Sjorgen syndrome antigen B) enhances the IRES activity of many viruses such as polio virus (PV, Equine Myocarditis virus (EMCV), Hepatitis C virus (HCV), Hepatitis A virus (HAV) and Coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3)8–10; Poly RC-binding protein (PCBP2) enhances the IRES activity of PV, CVB3, HAV and HRV (Human rhinovirus)11; Polypyrimidine tract binding protein1 (PTBP1) enhances the IRES activity of PV, CVB3, HAV and FMDV (Foot and Mouth disease virus)12; Many heterogenous ribonucleoproteins (HNRNPs) enhance the IRES activity of HCV, PV, EV-A71 (Enterovirus A-71)13. Although ITAFs are generally known to enhance the IRES activity, few of them also inhibit the IRES activity; for example, G3BP stress granule assembly factor (G3BP1) inhibits CVB3 and FMDV IRES activity, KH-type splicing regulatory protein (KHSRP) inhibits the activity of EV-A71, Gemin5 is also known to inhibit the FMDV IRES activity14,15. Inhibitory activity of these ITAFs is neutralized by viral factors so that IRES-mediated translation of viral RNA is not interrupted. For example, Gemin5 inhibits FMDV IRES activity by competing with PTB for binding to domain V of FMDV IRES. In turn, viral LPRO cleaves Gemin5 to counter its inhibitory effect16. Although many cellular ITAFs are known, role of virus-encoded factors in modulating the function of its IRES, if any, remains unexplored.

Hepatitis E virus is a positive strand RNA virus that causes acute viral hepatitis, acute liver failure, acute on chronic liver failure and extra hepatic problems such as neurological anomalies, arthritis, pancreatitis and glomerulonephritis17–19. The disease worsens in pregnancy with mortality rate up to 20-25%20. Recent reports suggest the occurrence of chronic HEV infection in immunosuppressed individuals with HIV, hematological malignancies or transplant recipients17.

There are 8 different genotypes of HEV, classified on the basis of affected species and geographical area. genotype1 (Asia) and Genotype 2 (Africa and Mexico) exclusively affect humans, whereas Genotype 3 (Europe and North America) and genotype 4 (Asia) are zoonotic with wide host range including pig, chicken, rabbit, deer, ferret and bat. Genotypes 5, 6,7 and 8 primarily infect animals and in some cases, non-human primates. Genotype 5 and 6 are known to infect wild boars while genotype 7 and 8 are infections of camels, dromedary camels and Bactrian camels, respectively21. Regardless of the disease burden, molecular details of HEV life cycle are not well characterized owing to multiple reasons such as lack of efficient cell culture system and animal models, limited knowledge of involved host factors and production of quasi enveloped virus particles.

The HEV genome is approximately 7.2 Kilo-bases long, with three open reading frames (ORF1-3) along with 5’- and 3’- untranslated regions (5’-UTR and 3’-UTR) which are 27 and 68 nucleotides long, respectively21. HEV ORF1 encodes a non-structural polyprotein that consists of methyl-transferase, Y domain, PCP: papain-like cysteine protease; HV: hypervariable region; Pro: proline-rich domain; X: X-domain; Hel: helicase; RdRp: RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. ORF1-3 are translated by the cap-dependent translation process. Many of the ORF1 encoded proteins play essential role in viral replication22. A bicistronic subgenomic mRNA encodes both the ORF2 and ORF3 proteins of hepatitis E virus. ORF2 encodes the capsid protein, which is the major structural protein of the HEV23. ORF3 is the smallest of the sequences and encodes a 13 kDa protein. It plays important role in host invasion although its function details are still obscure. A fourth ORF, named ORF4, is present only in the genotype 1 (g1)-HEV24. We earlier reported the presence of an 87-nucleotide-long internal ribosome entry-site-like (IRESl) element in the g1-HEV genome, which was located within the ORF1 coding region. This IRESl controlled cap-independent translation of the viral ORF4 protein, which played an essential role in g1-HEV replication. Our subsequent study identified the host protein interactome of the HEV IRESl, which further confirmed that HEV IRESl is a bonafide internal translation initiation-site25. Here, we evaluated the role of HEV-encoded proteins in modulating the HEV IRESl activity. Using a bicistronic reporter assay to measure the activity of HEV IRESl and the yeast three hybrid assay and streptavidin pull-down assay to measure RNA-protein interactions, we show that viral Helicase and MeT-Y-domain proteins directly associate with and enhance the activity of the HEV IRESl. Significance of these findings in understanding the function and regulation of HEV IRESl is discussed.

Although HEV ORF1 is functionally divided into multiple independent domains, its processing into individual protein domains is not confirmed. Partial processing of the ORF1 polypeptide has been reported in independent studies26–28. The predicted MeT and Y-domain overlap with each other and an earlier report suggested MeT to extend upto 410 amino acids, covering the entire Y-domain29. Since both MeT and Y-domain enhanced internal translation mediated by the HEV IRES, we wondered whether the overlapping region between the two domains is responsible for their translation-enhancing function. Therefore, region spanning amino acids 1-410 of the ORF1 polypeptide (denoted MeT-Y), containing both MeT and Y-domain was also tested for its ability to modulate the HEV IRESl activity. As expected, expression of MeT-Y enhanced the activity of HEV IRESl (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1). Next, we tested whether HEV IRESl activity was affected by g1-HEV replication in Huh7 cells where the ORF1 polypeptide was produced by cap-dependent translation of the viral genomic RNA. A replication-deficient mutant of g1-HEV (g1-HEV-GAA mutant) was used as a control to ensure that the observed effect was attributed to viral replication. Huh7 cells were transfected with in vitro synthesized, capped g1-HEV or g1-HEV-GAA mutant genomic RNA, followed by transfection of the pRL-FF/Luc reporter plasmid. Enhanced Luciferase activity was observed in cells expressing the g1-HEV at 24 hours and 48 hours post-transfection of the reporter plasmid (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 2k, l). Replication of g1-HEV was measured by RT-qPCR analysis of an aliquot of the above cells (Fig. 1e). Replication of g1-HEV was further confirmed by immunofluorescence analysis of parallelly prepared samples using anti-ORF2 antibody. As expected, ORF2-positive cells were detected only in wild type g1-HEV genomic RNA transfected cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a, 3b).

Many of the IRES regulatory proteins act by binding to the IRES RNA and our earlier study showed the role of several host factors in controlling the function of HEV IRESl by associating with it25. Therefore, we investigated whether viral MeT, Helicase and Y-domain proteins associated with the HEV IRESl to enhance its function. A yeast three hybrid assay was performed to monitor the RNA-Protein interactions in an in vivo set up30. Eighty-seven nucleotides corresponding to the HEV IRESl was cloned into p3HR2 vector downstream of the MS2-RNA sequence. pGADT7 vector expressing the HEV proteins was used to produce the different HEV proteins in fusion with the GAL4 activation domain (AD), reported earlier31. Corresponding empty vectors and bait RNA or prey proteins expressing plasmids were used to check the background levels of HIS3 and LacZ reporters in YBZ1 yeast strain. Co-transformants expressing MeT-Y, MeT, Y-domain and Helicase along with HEV IRESl activated both HIS3 and LacZ reporters, indicating interaction between them (Fig. 2). No growth was observed in colonies expressing p3HR2 and MeT-Y/MeT/Y-domain/ Helicase or p3HR2 HEV IRESl and pGADT7, suggesting that the interaction was not due to self-activation of HIS3 and LacZ reporters by the prey or bait (Fig. 2). We also tested the interaction between HEV IRESl and viral PCP, V-domain, X-domain, ORF4, RdRp, ORF3 and ORF2 proteins. None of them showed any interaction, supporting that the observed interaction between HEV IRESl and MeT-Y/MeT/Y-domain/Helicase is specific (Fig. 2). Specificity of the interaction between the HEV IRESl and MeT, Y-domain, and Helicase was evaluated by testing the interaction of these proteins with the HCV IRES and FMDV IRES. Mapping the region of interaction between the HEV IRES RNA-MeT-Y and HEV IRES RNA-Helicase proteins

Next, series of overlapping deletions of the MeT-Y region were tested to identify the minimal region required for its interaction with the HEV IRESl. 262-330 amino acids region within the MeT-Y was sufficient to interact with the HEV IRESl (Fig. 5a). Analysis of the 262–330 amino acid region revealed the presence of seven basic amino acid residues (K277, H280, H285, R290, R306, R312, K317). These residues were mutated to alanine and interaction of the mutant protein [MeT-Y (1-330) Mut] with the HEV IRESl RNA was tested. No interaction was observed between the mutant protein and HEV IRES RNA (Fig. 5a). Western blot analysis of [MeT-Y (1–330)] and [MeT-Y (1–330) Mut] protein expressing YBZ1 cell lysate using the anti-HA antibody (both proteins contain an HA-tag at the N-terminus, immediately following the GAL4-AD) confirmed that both proteins were expressed at equal levels (Fig. 5b). These data indicate that both MeT and Y-domain interact with the HEV IRES through the same region involving seven basic amino acids (K277, H280, H285, R290, R306, R312, K317). Note that these seven amino acids are conserved in g1-HEV isolates (Fig. 5c). Deletions of the Helicase domain was similarly tested, which identified that 103–199 amino acids within the Helicase region were sufficient for interaction with the HEV IRES (Fig. 6a). Western blot analysis of full length and truncated Helicase protein expressing YBZ1 cell lysate using the anti-HA antibody (both proteins contain an HA-tag at the N-terminus, immediately following the GAL4-AD) confirmed that both proteins were expressed at equal levels (Fig. 6b). Subsequently, we attempted to identify the RNA motif within the HEV-IRESl which mediated its interaction with MeT-Y and Helicase protein domains. HEV IRESl contains three stem loops and our previous study reported the key nucleotide mutations required to dissolve these stem loops (Fig. 7a). Our earlier study also showed that stem-loops B and C of the HEV IRESl are critical for its activity24. In order to evaluate the importance of stem-loops A, B and C in binding with MeT-Y and Helicase, interaction of HEV IRESl mutants with MeT-Y, helicase and their deletion mutants were tested by Y3H assay. HEV IRESl mut A interacted with the full-length MeT-Y and MeT-Y (262–330) deletion proteins like the wild type RNA (Fig. 7b). HEV IRESl mut B, C and BC completely lost the ability to interact with MeT-Y whereas HEV IRESl mut AC weakly interacted with MeT-Y (Fig. 7b). A similar interaction pattern was observed in the case of full-length Helicase and Helicase 103-199 aa fragment (Fig. 7c). Discussion

Many cellular proteins are known to regulate the activity of an IRES element by acting as ITAFs. However, involvement of HEV encoded proteins in controlling HEV IRESl-driven translation is unknown. In an earlier study, we identified and characterized an IRESl element in the g1-HEV genome. Using a combination of point mutations, bicistronic reporter assay and viral replication assay, we had demonstrated cap-independent, internal translation of the ORF4 protein in g1-HEV infected cells, driven by the HEV IRESl element. Subsequently, we identified the host interaction partners of the HEV IRESl, showed the presence of HEV IRESl in polysome fraction and demonstrated the essential role of several host translation factors in mediating cap-independent translation via the HEV IRESl. These studies clearly proved that the HEV IRESl is a bonafede IRES.

Data obtained in the current study shows that viral Helicase and MeT-Y domain interacts with the HEV IRESl and upregulates its ability to initiate translation. A stretch of basic amino acids in the MeT-Y domain region was found to mediate its interaction with the HEV IRESl. This region (262-330 aa) overlaps between Methyltransferase and Y-domain of g-1 HEV. HEV Methyltransferase and Y-domain proteins are produced by proteolytic processing of the ORF1 polypeptide. No clear information exists regarding the actual size of Methyltransferase and Y-domain proteins29. An earlier report suggested Y-domain to be an extension of the Methyltransferase29. However, the core Methyltransferase domain spans from 22 to 112 amino acids in the ORF1 peptide. Methyltransferase has also been shown to interact with the viral X-domain protein (a product of ORF1 polypeptide processing) through its 30–90 amino acid region31. In contrast, HEV IRESl interaction region is located between 260 and 330 aa region, which is also part of the Y-domain. Hence, it remains to be confirmed whether HEV IRESl associates with MeT or Y-domain or MeT-Y. Clear understanding of ORF1 polypeptide processing will help in resolving this paradigm.

The Y-domain sequence is present in the HEV, Rubella virus and Beet necrotic yellow vein virus35. Point mutations within the Y-domain region (C336A, C337A, W413A) abolish HEV replication36. Further, in silico analysis reveals the conservation of Y-domain sequence throughout the course of evolution and suggests RNA binding activity as one of its molecular functions37. Therefore, it is possible that Y-domain binds to and stimulates the activity of HEV IRESl. Additional experiments showing generation of Y-domain fragment in g1-HEV infected cells and identification of its N-terminal-end may clarify the involvement of Y-domain in driving the HEV IRESl-mediated translation.

HEV IRESl also associates with the viral Helicase protein and overexpression of the viral Helicase enhances the HEV IRESl activity. HEV Helicase belongs to the Helicase Superfamily1 (SF1) and possess RNA 5’-triphosphatase, NTPase and RNA unwinding activities35,38,39. It contains seven signature motifs, of which motifs I and III are crucial for its function. Helicase motif I, IV, V and VI have alternative motifs to carry out enzymatic functions of the protein40. HEV IRESl interaction could be mapped to 103-199 aa of the Helicase protein, which contains motifs V and VI. Note that, Helicase motifs V and VI of the West Nile Virus have been proposed to bind to nucleic acids41. Many RNA Helicases are known to be important for IRES activity. DExxH-box RNA helicases that belong to the Helicase superfamily 2 (SF2), such as DHX9 and DHX29 have been shown to control the IRES activity of HCV and FMDV. Both SF1 and SF2 family Helicases contain two core helicase domains, which mostly share common sequence motifs42. They primarily differ in their N-and C-terminal sequences. In contrast, SF3 helicases contain only 3 conserved motifs (motif I, II and III). Considering the similarity between the SF1 and SF2 RNA helicases, it is likely that HEV Helicase stimulates HEV IRESl activity in a manner analogous to that of the cellular RNA Helicases.

In summary, the current study demonstrates the ability of HEV MeT-Y-domain and Helicase proteins to associate with the HEV IRESl element and stimulate its activity. Since HEV IRESl drives the translation of the viral ORF4 protein, which is an essential component of the g1-HEV replication machinery, MeT-Y-domain and Helicase promote viral replication by stimulating the activity of the IRESl. This positive feedback mechanism could be an apt strategy for survival and spread of the virus.

Yeast three hybrid assay

Yeast three hybrid assay (Y3H) was utilized to identify RNA-protein interactions between HEV IRES RNA and viral proteins using YBZ1 yeast strain30. YBZ1 competent cells were prepared using Lithium chloride and self-activation was determined by transformation of 2µg of p3HR2 HEV IRES (or p3HR2) and pGADT7 each in YBZ1 cells25. Cotransformants were plated on LU- media for three days in humidified chamber at 30 °C. After three days, 8 random colonies were replica plated on LUH- and 3-AT (0.1–1mM) supplemented LUH- plates and observed for growth for four consecutive days in humidified chamber at 30 °C. There was very little or no colony growth on LUH- and 3-AT supplemented media. Further identification of HEV IRESl RNA interacting viral proteins was performed by transformation of 2µg each of p3HR2 HEV IRESl (and mutants) and viral protein (and mutants) clones in pGADT7 with p3HR2 (or pGADT7) empty vectors. Evaluation of LacZ reporter activity was carried out using Ortho-Nitrophenyl-β-galactoside (ONPG) as colorimetric substrate with three random colonies for each cotransformant, as described earlier25.

Mammalian cell-culture, transfection, in vitro transcription, western blot assay, RT-qPCR, immunofluorescence assay

Huh7 cells are as described previously24. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 50 I.U./mL Penicillin and Streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were maintained in antibiotic free medium before starting the experiments. For plasmid transfection, cells were seeded at 70–80% confluency in DMEM + 10% FBS and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Next day, cells were transfected with the desired plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA), at 1:1 ratio, following manufacturer’s instruction. 6-8 hours post-transfection, culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM+10% FBS.

Capped-g1-HEV or g1-HEV-GAA mutant RNA was synthesized by in vitro transcription and transfected into Huh7 cells, as described (24). For biotinylated-RNA pull down assay, pJET1.2 HEV IRESl and pJET1.2 Control RNA plasmids were linearized by restriction digestion with NcoI enzyme and column purified. One microgram of purified linearized DNA was used for in vitro transcription reaction using Maxi-script T7 transcription kit, following manufacturer’s instruction (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). 0.2mM Biotin-16-UTP (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) and 0.3mM unlabeled-UTP were added to the reaction mixture to produce biotinylated RNA. Non-biotinylated RNAs were transcribed in parallel in the presence of 0.5mM unlabeled UTP. In vitro transcribed RNA was purified as per the instruction of the manufacturer of Maxi-script kit.

For western blot analysis of MeT and Helicase proteins, pGADT7, pGADT7-Helicase (1-101), pGADT7-Helicase (103-245), pGADT7-Helicase (1-245), pGADT7-MeT-Y, and pGADT7-MeT-Y (1-330) Mut plasmids were transformed into YBZ1 cells along with the p3HR2 plasmid. The co-transformants were cultured on SD (LU-) agar plates. A single colony was picked and inoculated in SD (LU-) medium (5mL) and incubated at 30°C, 200 rpm for 20 hours. A secondary culture (10 mL) was initiated by adding 10% of the primary culture to fresh SD (LU-) broth and incubating it overnight under the same conditions. The secondary culture was harvested by centrifugation, washed with PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4) and resuspended in 100 µl Z buffer (60mM Na2HPO4.7H2O, 0.40mM NaH2PO4.H2O, 0.10mM KCl, 1mM MgSO4, 50mM β-mercaptoethanol). Zymolyase (2.5U) was added to the cell suspension (100μL) and it was incubated at 37 °C for 3 hours to generate spheroplasts. The spheroplasts were pelleted by centrifugation at 1,000g for 1 minute and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in Laemmli buffer (4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 10% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.004% bromphenol blue, 125 mM Tris HCl, pH 6.8.), incubated at 95°C for 5 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 12,000g for 5 minutes. For western blot assay of Huh7 cells, 48 hours post-transfection, whole cell extract was prepared from plates transfected with various plasmids, by directly lysing the cells in Laemmli buffer by incubation at 95 °C for 5 minutes. For all samples, 50 µg of whole cell lysate or 1µg of purified MeT, Helicase and ORF2 proteins were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gel along with a pre-stained protein ladder. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membrane, blocked with 5% non-fat dried milk in 0.05%PBS-T (Phosphate buffer saline with 0.05% Tween-20) for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 40C with primary antibodies [anti-HA (1:2500), anti-GAPDH (1:20000), anti-MeT (1:2000), anti-Helicase (1:2000), anti-His (1:2000)]. The blots were washed with PBST, incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by visualization of the bands using a commercially available enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (BioRad, USA) and images were acquired using a gel documentation system (Chemidoc MP, BioRad, USA). For RT-qPCR analysis, total RNA was isolated from Huh7 cells using Tri reagent (MRC, USA) and RT-qPCR was performed to measure g1-HEV genomic RNA and GAPDH RNA levels, as described (25). Immunofluorescence assay was done to detect the expression of g1-HEV ORF2 protein by staining the cells using anti-ORF2 rabbit polyclonal primary antibody, as described (24). Anti-ORF2 antibody has been validated earlier (24).

Dual luciferase reporter assay

Dual luciferase reporter assay was performed using HEK 293T cells at 70% confluency. Dual luciferase reporter plasmid (pRL-FF/Luc87, 1μg/well) and viral protein expressing constructs (mammalian vector constructs, 1µg/well) were cotransfected into HEK293T cells using lipofectamine 2000 at 1:1 (wt/v) ratio. After 8 hours of incubation, media was changed and cells were incubated for next the 48 hours in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (fetal bovine serum). Cells were washed with ice cold PBS and used for luciferase assay using the dual glow Luciferase assay kit, following manufacturer’s protocol (Promega, Wisconsin, USA). The Firefly-Luc values were divided by that of the Renilla-Luc and graphs were plotted in GraphPad prism or Microsoft Excel. Values are mean (±SD) of three independent experiments done in triplicates.

Biotinylated-RNA pull down assay

For biotinylated RNA pulldown assay, 2 µg of the purified proteins were added with biotinylated control RNA [control RNA (B+)], non-biotinylated HEV IRESl RNA [HEV IRESl RNA (B−)]. Biotinylated HEV IRESl RNA [HEV IRESl RNA (B+)] was added to the binding buffer [1% Triton X-100, 0.5% NP-40, 150mM NaCl, 2mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 20mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1X complete protease inhibitor cocktail, 40 units RNase inhibitor] or mock (protein without RNA) and the reaction mix was placed on a flip-flop rocker for 1 h at 4 °C. Streptavidin magnetic beads were washed twice in binding buffer and added to the RNA-protein mixture, followed by incubation for 1 h at 4 °C. Streptavidin bound RNA-protein complex was subsequently washed twice in ice cold low salt wash buffer [1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 150mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0)] and once in high salt wash buffer [1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 250 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA (pH 8.0) 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0)] followed by Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0). Fifty percent of the beads were re-suspended in the elution buffer (1% SDS, 0.1M NaHCO3), incubated for 5 min at 4 °C on a rocker followed by collection of the supernatant to detect RNA species on by fractionating on the formaldehyde-agarose gel electrophoresis. Rest of the beads were mixed with 2X Laemmli buffer, incubated at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by collection of the supernatant for Western blot analysis using different antibodies.
