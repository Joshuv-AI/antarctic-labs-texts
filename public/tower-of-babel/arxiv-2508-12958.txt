The S S -functional calculus for the Clifford adjoint operator

Abstract
. In this paper, we work within the framework of modules over the Clifford algebra ℝ d \mathbb{R}_{d} . 1.
Introduction

Quaternionic and Clifford operators are essential in several areas of mathematics and physics, including quaternionic quantum mechanics, vector analysis, differential geometry, and hypercomplex analysis, for more details and references see Section
6
. The spectral theory for Clifford operators differs from the classical complex theory. This distinction arises from the noncommutativity of the spectral parameter
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and the operator
T
, and manifests itself in the definition of the spectrum, which is related to the bounded invertibility of the second order operator

(1.1)

Q_{s}[T]:=T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2},\qquad\text{with }\operatorname{dom}(Q_{s}[T]):=\operatorname{dom}(T^{2}).

As discussed in
[
26
,
27
]
and detailed in
[
17
,
18
]
, the
S
-spectrum of
T
is then defined as

(1.2)

\sigma_{S}(T):=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\notin\mathcal{B}(V)\big\},

where
\mathcal{B}(V)
denotes the set of bounded, everywhere defined operators on the Clifford module
V
. In this context, for every
s\notin\sigma_{S}(T)
, the two
S
-resolvent operators, both left and right, are defined as

(1.3)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T):=Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,T):=(\overline{s}-T)Q_{s}[T]^{-1}.

These resolvent operators are now crucial for defining the
S
-functional calculus, which is the Clifford analog of the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus in
[
33
]
. Roughly speaking, the
S
-functional calculus is defined as

(1.4a)

\displaystyle f(T):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s),\qquad\text{or}

(1.4b)

\displaystyle f(T):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}f(s)ds_{J}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T),

where
U
is a suitable open set containing the
S
-spectrum of
T
, and
J
is some arbitrary imaginary unit of the Clifford algebra. Since in a Clifford algebra one distinguishes between left and right slice hyperholomorphic functions, we need two different versions of the
S
-functional calculi, (
1.4a
) for left slice hyperholomorphic functions
f
, and (
1.4b
) for right slice hyperholomorphic functions.

Depending on whether the operator
T
is bounded or unbounded, and depending on the decay properties of the function
f
, the integrals (
1.4
) have to be interpreted differently. Precise definitions of the several cases are given in the subsections of Section
4
. In particular, since the
S
-spectrum for unbounded operators is in general not bounded, one has to adapt the integration path accordingly, or impose decay conditions on the function
f
, in order to ensure the convergence of the integrals (
1.4
), see Definition
4.5
or Definition
4.10
.

Only a regularization procedure, called
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, then allows to extend (
1.4
) to functions for which the integrals diverge. In the complex setting, the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus was first introduced by A. McIntosh in
[
46
]
, with further developments and applications discussed in
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
34
]
. It is a key instrument in spectral theory, particularly for differential operators, and it has applications in various fields as parabolic evolution equations, Kato’s square root problem, elliptic systems, and Schrödinger operators, see
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
.

In the Clifford setting, the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for left slice hyperholomorphic functions is already well established in
[
2
,
15
,
17
]
. It is given by

(1.5)

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T),

where both, the regularizer
e(T)
and the regularized operator
(ef)(T)
are interpreted as the integral (
1.4a
). Detailed properties of this calculus are examined in
[
44
]
, based on the universality property of the
S
-functional calculus established in
[
19
]
. The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus defines in general unbounded operators, while in
[
23
]
, boundedness has been characterized using suitable quadratic estimates.

However, it was unknown until the recent paper
[
22
]
, how the regularization formula (
1.5
) adapts for right slice holomorphic functions. The key idea is to look at the right
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus as

(1.6)

f(T):=\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}},

where it is a priori not known if
(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}
is closable. Hence the closure leads to a multivalued operator
f(T)
in the sense of Definition
3.3
.

The primary aim of the present article is to investigate the
S
-spectral theory of the adjoint operator
T^{*}
of a densely defined right-linear operator
T:\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\rightarrow V
, acting in a Hilbert module
V
over the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{d}
. In particular, we prove in Theorem
3.5
that the
S
-spectrum (
1.2
) of the adjoint operator coincides with the one of the original operator

\sigma_{S}(T^{*})=\sigma_{S}(T),

and also that the respective
S
-resolvent operators of
T^{*}
and
T
are connected via

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T^{*})=S_{R}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)^{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,T^{*})=S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)^{*}.

We will also investigate the various versions of the
S
-functional calculus (
1.4
) of
T^{*}
. Starting with bounded operators in Theorem
4.4
, continuing with unbounded operators in Theorem
4.6
and heading to the
\omega
-functional calculus of unbounded bisectorial operators in Theorem
4.12
. In all the cases, we will show the connection

(1.7)

f(T^{*})=f^{\#}(T)^{*},

between the functional calculus of
T^{*}
and
T
, using the function

f^{\#}(s):=\overline{f(\overline{s})}.

We also prove in Theorem
4.15
, that the formula (
1.7
) holds for the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), if one interprets both sides accordingly as unbounded or multivalued operators.

Additionally, we present some preliminary results in Section
2
in a more general setting for Banach modules, which are of independent interest. The most important one of them is Theorem
2.4
, which characterizes the
S
-spectrum in terms of a first order operator. More precisely, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, with the right multiplication operator

(1.8)

(\mathcal{I}^{R}s)(v):=vs,\qquad v\in V,

we can write the
S
-spectrum as

\sigma_{S}(T)=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}\notin\mathcal{B}(V)\big\}.

Note that since
T
is right-linear and
\mathcal{I}^{R}s
is left-linear, their difference turns out to be
\mathbb{R}
-linear only. The idea of such a first order representation of the
S
-spectrum was already discussed in
[
17
, Theorem 3.1.8]
, where a similar result was proven, but only on slices
\sigma_{S}(T)\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}
.

In the final Section
5
of this article, we present the multiplication operator
M_{h}
with a Clifford valued function
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
as an application of the
S
-functional calculus. In Theorem
5.9
, we compute its
S
-spectrum, which turns out to not be the essential range as in the complex case, but rather the rotation of the essential range around the real axis

\sigma_{S}(M_{h})=[\operatorname{essran}(h)].

This fact is a consequence of the axial symmetry of the
S
-spectrum. Moreover, in Theorem
5.11
we explicitly calculate the action

f(M_{h})=M_{f\circ h}

of the
S
-functional calculus on the multiplication operator. This formula means that the functional calculus
f(M_{h})
is again a multiplication operator with the new function
f\circ h
.

2.
Preliminaries on Clifford Algebras and Clifford modules

In this section we will fix the algebraic and functional analytic setting of this paper. The underlying algebra will be the real
Clifford algebra

\mathbb{R}_{d}
over
d

imaginary units

e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
, which satisfy the relations

e_{i}^{2}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\qquad i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,d\}.

More precisely,
\mathbb{R}_{d}
is given by

\mathbb{R}_{d}:=\Big\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}e_{A}\;\Big|\;s_{A}\in\mathbb{R},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\},

using the index set

\mathcal{A}:=\big\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\;\big|\;r\in\{0,\dots,d\},\,1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq d\big\},

and the
basis vectors

e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}\dots e_{i_{r}}
. Note that for
A=\emptyset
the empty product of imaginary units is the real number
e_{\emptyset}=1
. Furthermore, we define for every Clifford number
s\in\mathbb{R}_{d}
its
conjugate
and its
absolute value

(2.1)

\overline{s}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(-1)^{\frac{|A|(|A|+1)}{2}}s_{A}e_{A}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|s|^{2}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}^{2}.

An important subset of the Clifford numbers are the so called
paravectors

\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:=\big\{s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{d}e_{d}\;\big|\;s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{d}\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

For any paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we define the
imaginary part

\operatorname{Im}(s):=s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{d}e_{d}.

Also for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, the conjugate and the modulus in (
2.1
) reduce to

\overline{s}=s_{0}-s_{1}e_{1}-\dots-s_{d}e_{d}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|s|^{2}=s_{0}^{2}+s_{1}^{2}+\dots+s_{d}^{2}.

The sphere of
imaginary units
is defined by

\mathbb{S}:=\big\{J\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;J_{0}=0,\,|J|=1\big\}.

Any element
J\in\mathbb{S}
satisfies
J^{2}=-1
and hence the corresponding
hyperplane

\mathbb{C}_{J}:=\big\{x+Jy\;\big|\;x,y\in\mathbb{R}\big\}

is an isomorphic copy of the complex numbers. Moreover, for every paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
we consider the corresponding
(d-1)
–sphere

[s]:=\big\{s_{0}+J|\operatorname{Im}(s)|\;\big|\;J\in\mathbb{S}\big\}.

A subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is called
axially symmetric
, if
[s]\subseteq U
for every
s\in U
.

Next, we introduce the notion of slice hyperholomorphic functions
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
, defined on an axially symmetric open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Definition 2.1

(Slice hyperholomorphic functions)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be open, axially symmetric and consider

\mathcal{U}:=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;\big|\;x+\mathbb{S}y\subseteq U\big\}.

A function
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
is called
left
(resp.
right
)
slice hyperholomorphic
, if there exist continuously differentiable functions
f_{0},f_{1}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
, such that for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{U}
:

i)

The function
f
admits for every
J\in\mathbb{S}
the representation

(2.2)

f(x+Jy)=f_{0}(x,y)+Jf_{1}(x,y),\quad\Big(\text{resp.}\;f(x+Jy)=f_{0}(x,y)+f_{1}(x,y)J\Big).

ii)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the
compatibility conditions

(2.3)

f_{0}(x,-y)=f_{0}(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{1}(x,-y)=-f_{1}(x,y).

iii)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the
Cauchy-Riemann equations

(2.4)

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{0}(x,y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{1}(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{0}(x,y)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{1}(x,y).

The class of left (resp. right) slice hyperholomorphic functions on
U
is denoted by
\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U)
(resp.
\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
). In the special case that
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are real valued, we call
f

intrinsic
and the space of intrinsic functions by
\mathcal{N}(U)
.

Next, we turn our attention to modules over
\mathbb{R}_{d}
. For a real Banach space
V_{\mathbb{R}}
with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the corresponding
Banach module

V:=\Big\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\;\Big|\;v_{A}\in V_{\mathbb{R}},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\},

and equip it with the
norm

(2.5)

\|v\|^{2}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\qquad v\in V.

For any vector
v=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\in V
and any Clifford number
s=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{A}}s_{B}e_{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{d}
, we establish the left and the right scalar multiplication

\displaystyle sv:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(s_{B}v_{A})\otimes(e_{B}e_{A}),

(left-multiplication)

\displaystyle vs:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(v_{A}s_{B})\otimes(e_{A}e_{B}).

(right-multiplication)

We recall the well known properties of these products, see for example
[
44
, Lemma 2.1]
:

\displaystyle\|sv\|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}|s|\|v\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|vs\|\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}|s|\|v\|,\qquad\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}_{d},

\displaystyle\|sv\|

\displaystyle=\|vs\|=|s|\|v\|,\hskip 137.42685pt\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

If
V_{\mathbb{R}}
is also a real Hilbert space, i.e. equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, we also make
V
a
Hilbert module
with the inner product

(2.6)

\langle v,w\rangle:=\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}\langle v_{A},w_{B}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{e_{A}}e_{B},\qquad v,w\in V.

The sesquilinear form (
2.6
) is now right-linear in the second, and right-antilinear in the first argument, i.e. for every
u,v,w\in V
,
s\in\mathbb{R}_{d}
, there holds

(2.7)

\begin{split}\langle u,v+w\rangle&=\langle u,v\rangle+\langle u,w\rangle,\hskip 42.67912pt\langle v,ws\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle s,\\
\langle v+w,u\rangle&=\langle v,u\rangle+\langle w,u\rangle,\hskip 42.67912pt\langle vs,w\rangle=\overline{s}\langle v,w\rangle.\end{split}

Moreover, there also holds

(2.8)

\overline{\langle v,w\rangle}=\langle w,v\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle v,sw\rangle=\langle\overline{s}v,w\rangle.

The norm (
2.5
) is then connected to the inner product (
2.6
) by

\|v\|^{2}=\operatorname{Sc}\langle v,v\rangle,\qquad v\in V.

Remark 2.2
.

In Hilbert modules over
\mathbb{R}_{d}
, we consider two inner products that serve different purposes. The full inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is used for instance in the Riesz representation theorem, while the
\mathbb{R}
-linear scalar part
\operatorname{Sc}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
gives raise to the norm
\|v\|^{2}=\operatorname{Sc}\langle v,v\rangle
.

For any Clifford Banach module
V
we will denote the set of
bounded right-linear operators
by

\mathcal{B}(V):=\big\{T:V\rightarrow V\text{ right-linear}\;\big|\;\operatorname{dom}(T)=V,\,T\text{ is bounded}\big\},

and the set of
closed right-linear operators
by

\mathcal{K}(V):=\big\{T:V\rightarrow V\text{ right-linear}\;\big|\;\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\text{ is right-linear},\,T\text{ is closed}\big\}.

In difference to complex Banach spaces, in Cliffordian Banach modules, the spectrum is connected to the bounded invertibility of the second order operator (
1.1
), which suggests the following definition of
S
-spectrum.

Definition 2.3

(
S
-Spectrum)
.

For every
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
, let us define the
S
-resolvent set
and the
S
-spectrum

(2.9)

\rho_{S}(T):=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{S}(T):=\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\rho_{S}(T).

Moreover, for every
s\in\rho_{S}(T)
we define the
left
and the
right
S
-resolvent operator

(2.10)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T):=Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,T):=(\overline{s}-T)Q_{s}[T]^{-1}.

Although the
S
-resolvent set is defined via the invertibility of the second order operator
Q_{s}[T]
defined on
\operatorname{dom}(T^{2})
, we can use the right multiplication operator
\mathcal{I}^{R}s
from (
1.8
) to characterize it via the invertibility of a first order operator, defined on
\operatorname{dom}(T)
.

Theorem 2.4
.

For every
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
, the
S
-resolvent set (
2.9
) can be characterized by

(2.11)

\rho_{S}(T)=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)\big\},

and for every
v\in V
the respective resolvents are connected via

(2.12)

Q_{s}[T]^{-1}=(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})^{-1}(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}.

Proof.

For the inclusion
\text{“}\subseteq\text{“}
in (
2.11
) let
s\in\rho_{S}(T)
, i.e.
Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
. Then there holds for every
v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)

\displaystyle(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})Q_{s}[T]^{-1}(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)v

\displaystyle=(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})Q_{s}[T]^{-1}(Tv-vs)

\displaystyle=(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})(TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}v-Q_{s}[T]^{-1}vs)

\displaystyle=T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}vs-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}v\overline{s}+Q_{s}[T]^{-1}vs\overline{s}

(2.13)

\displaystyle=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v=v.

Moreover, for every
v\in V
, there is

\displaystyle(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v

\displaystyle=\big(T^{2}-(\mathcal{I}^{R}s)T-T(\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})+(\mathcal{I}^{R}s)(\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})\big)Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v

\displaystyle=T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}vs-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}v\overline{s}+Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v\overline{s}s

(2.14)

\displaystyle=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v=v.

From (
2.13
) it now follows that
T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s
is injective, and from (
2.14
) that
T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s
is surjective. Altogether we know that
T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s
is bijective and by the closed graph theorem also
(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
.

For the inverse inclusion
\text{“}\supseteq\text{“}
in (
2.11
), let
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with
(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
. In the
first step
we show that also
(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
. To do so, let us choose
J\in\mathbb{S}
with
s\in\mathbb{C}_{J}
, and another imaginary unit
I\in\mathbb{S}
, with
IJ=-JI
, possible because of
[
26
, Proposition 2.2.10]
. We then consider the
\mathbb{R}
-linear operator

R_{s}(v):=-(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}(vI)I,\qquad v\in V.

Then for every
v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
, there is

\displaystyle R_{s}((T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})v)

\displaystyle=R_{s}(Tv-v\overline{s})=-(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}\big((Tv-v\overline{s})I\big)I

(2.15)

\displaystyle=-(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}\big((T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)(vI)\big)I=-(vI)I=v,

where in the third equation we used that due to
IJ=-JI
there is
\overline{s}I=Is
and hence
v\overline{s}I=vIs=\mathcal{I}^{R}s(vI)
. Moreover, for every
v\in V
we also have

\displaystyle(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})R_{s}(v)

\displaystyle=TR_{s}(v)-R_{s}(v)\overline{s}=-T(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}(vI)I+(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}(vI)I\overline{s}

(2.16)

\displaystyle=(-T+\mathcal{I}^{R}s)\big((T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}(vI)\big)I=-(vI)I=v,

where in the second line we used
Is\overline{s}=sI
, which is again due to
IJ=-JI
. Now, from the identity (
2.15
) it follows that
T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s}
is injective and from (
2.16
) that
T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s}
is surjective. Hence
T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s
is bijective and by the closed graph theorem then
(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
.

In the
second step
, we derive for every
v\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{2})
the identity

\displaystyle(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})v

\displaystyle=(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)(Tv-v\overline{s})=T^{2}v-Tvs-Tv\overline{s}+v\overline{s}s

\displaystyle=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})v=Q_{s}[T]v.

Hence
Q_{s}[T]=(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})
is the product of two bijective operators, and hence bijective itself. This shows that
s\in\rho_{S}(T)
, and that

Q_{s}[T]^{-1}=(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})^{-1}(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}.\qed

Remark 2.5
.

Note that although the right hand side of the resolvent identity (
2.12
) consists of the product of two only
\mathbb{R}
-linear operators, their product equals the pseudo resolvent
Q_{s}[T]^{-1}
and hence is right-linear again.

Remark 2.6
.

In the complex setting, the resolvent condition
(T-\lambda)^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
is equivalent to
T-\lambda
being bijective. This is due to the closed graph theorem, which concludes the boundedness of the inverse operator automatically. This is true also in the Clifford case for the operator
Q_{s}[T]
. Indeed, the proof of Theorem
2.4
tells, if
Q_{s}[T]
is bijective, then also
T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s
and
T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s}
are both bijective and their inverses are bounded by the closed graph theorem. Hence also

Q_{s}[T]^{-1}=(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s})^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)

is a bounded operator. This shows, the bijectivity of
Q_{s}[T]
alone already gives the boundedness of its inverse. Recall that the topology of
V
as a Banach module over
\mathbb{R}_{d}
and as a real Banach space is the same. Hence the closed graph theorem applies to right-linear and
\mathbb{R}
-linear operators both.

The next proposition however states that if
Q_{q}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
for one point
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, then
Q_{s}[T]
is closed for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
with
\rho_{S}(T)\neq\emptyset
. Then

Q_{s}[T]\text{ is closed for every }s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

Proof.

By assumption, there exists some
q\in\rho_{S}(T)
. With the corresponding operator
Q_{q}[T]
we can rewrite the operator
Q_{s}[T]
, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, as

\displaystyle Q_{s}[T]

\displaystyle=Q_{q}[T]+2(q_{0}-s_{0})T+|s|^{2}-|q|^{2}

(2.17)

\displaystyle=Q_{q}[T]\big(1+2(q_{0}-s_{0})TQ_{q}[T]^{-1}+(|s|^{2}-|q|^{2})Q_{q}[T]^{-1}\big).

Next note that
Q_{q}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
due to
q\in\rho_{S}(T)
, and
T
is closed, which means that the product
TQ_{q}[T]^{-1}
is everywhere defined and closed. Hence
TQ_{q}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
by the closed graph theorem. Altogether, this shows that the operator in the large bracket on the right hand side of (
2.17
) is a bounded operator. Since also
Q_{q}[T]
is closed (because
Q_{q}[T]^{-1}
is closed), the representation (
2.17
) gives a decomposition of
Q_{s}[T]
into a closed and a bounded operator. So
Q_{s}[T]
is closed as a consequence.
∎

The next proposition states that for a densely defined operator
T
with nonempty resolvent set, also the operator
Q_{s}[T]
is densely defined.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
with
\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T)=V
and
\rho_{S}(T)\neq\emptyset
. Then

\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T^{2})=V.

In particular,
Q_{s}[T]
is densely defined for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Proof.

Let us start with
v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
. Since
\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T)=V
by assumption, there exists a sequence
(w_{n})_{n}\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
, such that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}w_{n}=Tv.

Let us now choose some arbitrary
q\in\rho_{S}(T)
. Then
TQ_{q}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
, and there also converges

(2.18)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}TQ_{q}[T]^{-1}w_{n}=TQ_{q}[T]^{-1}Tv.

Since
v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
, we can rewrite it as

v=Q_{q}[T]Q_{q}[T]^{-1}v=TQ_{q}[T]^{-1}Tv-2q_{0}Q_{q}[T]^{-1}Tv+|q|^{2}Q_{q}[T]^{-1}v.

Plugging in the limit (
2.18
) for the first term on the right hand side, gives

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}TQ_{q}[T]^{-1}w_{n}-2q_{0}Q_{q}[T]^{-1}Tv+|q|^{2}Q_{q}[T]^{-1}v

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}Q_{q}[T]^{-1}(Tw_{n}-2q_{0}Tv+|q|^{2}v).

This proves that
v\in\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(Q_{q}[T]^{-1})=\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T^{2})
, and since
v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
was arbitrary, we get

\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T^{2}).

However, since
\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T)=V
is dense, this inclusion shows that also
\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T^{2})=V
.
∎

3.
The adjoint of a Clifford operator

In this section let
V
be a Hilbert module over the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{d}
. We will introduce the adjoint operator
T^{*}
of a densely defined unbounded operator
T:\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\rightarrow V
, and collect some of its main properties. In particular, in Theorem
3.5
we prove that the
S
-spectra of
T^{*}
and
T
coincide. For further results see also
[
20
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
T:\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\rightarrow V
be a densely defined
\mathbb{R}
-linear operator. Then we define the
adjoint operator

T^{*}:\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})\subset V\rightarrow V
as the unique operator with the property

(3.1)

\operatorname{Sc}\langle T^{*}w,v\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}\langle w,Tv\rangle,\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(T),w\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{*}),

and with the domain

(3.2)

\operatorname{dom}(T^{*}):=\big\{w\in V\;\big|\;\exists v_{w}\in V:\operatorname{Sc}\langle v_{w},v\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}\langle w,Tv\rangle,\,v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\big\}.

Then
T^{*}
is again a closed,
\mathbb{R}
-linear operator. The next lemma proves that for right-linear operators
T
, the adjoint operator is again right-linear, and there exists a characterization of the action (
3.1
) and the domain (
3.2
) in terms of the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
instead of its scalar part
\operatorname{Sc}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, see also Remark
2.2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T:\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\rightarrow V
be a densely defined right-linear operator. Then the operator
T^{*}
from Definition
3.1
is right-linear, its domain can be characterized by

(3.3)

\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})=\big\{w\in V\;\big|\;\exists v_{w}\in V:\langle v_{w},v\rangle=\langle w,Tv\rangle,\,v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\big\},

and it satisfies the identity

(3.4)

\langle T^{*}w,v\rangle=\langle w,Tv\rangle,\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(T),\,w\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{*}).

Proof.

For the inclusion
\text{“}\subseteq\text{“}
in (
3.3
), let
w\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})
. Note that any component
s_{A}
of a Clifford number
s=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}e_{A}
can be written as
s_{A}=\operatorname{Sc}(s\,\overline{e_{A}})
, see e.g.
[
21
, Eq.(2.2)]
. This allows us to write for every
v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)

\displaystyle\langle T^{*}w,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Sc}\big(\langle T^{*}w,v\rangle\overline{e_{A}}\big)e_{A}=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Sc}\langle T^{*}w,v\,\overline{e_{A}}\rangle e_{A}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Sc}\langle w,Tv\,\overline{e_{A}}\rangle e_{A}=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Sc}\big(\langle w,Tv\rangle\overline{e_{A}}\big)e_{A}=\langle w,Tv\rangle.

The identity (
3.5
) now on the one hand proves that
w
is contained in the right hand side of (
3.3
) and also proves the identity (
3.4
).

For the inclusion
\text{“}\supseteq\text{“}
in (
3.3
), let
w\in V
such that there exists some
v_{w}\in V
with

\langle v_{w},v\rangle=\langle w,Tv\rangle,\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(T).

Then there clearly also is

\operatorname{Sc}\langle v_{w},v\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}\langle w,Tv\rangle,\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(T),

and we have proven that
w\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})
, as defined in (
3.2
).

Finally, we want to verify that
T^{*}
is right-linear. With the identity (
3.4
), the linearity (
2.7
) and the conjugation property (
2.8
) of the inner product, there follows for every
s\in\mathbb{R}_{d}

\langle T^{*}(w)s,v\rangle=\overline{s}\langle T^{*}w,v\rangle=\overline{s}\langle w,Tv\rangle=\overline{\langle Tv,w\rangle s}=\overline{\langle Tv,ws\rangle}=\langle ws,Tv\rangle,\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(T).

Hence there is
ws\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})
and
T^{*}(ws)=T^{*}(w)s
.
∎

The condition that the adjoint operator in Definition
3.1
is only defined for operators
T
with dense domain, is crucial in the sense that otherwise the element
v_{w}
in (
3.2
) is not unique, and
T^{*}w
not well defined. However, especially in Section
4.4
, this assumption will often be not satisfied for the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus. To circumvent this problem, we need a generalization of the concept of operators.

Definition 3.3

(Multivalued operators)
.

A right-linear subspace
T\subseteq V\times V
of the product space, is called a
multivalued operator
. A right-linear operator
T:\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\rightarrow V
is considered as a multivalued operator in the sense that
T
is identified with
\operatorname{graph}(T)
.

Let now
T,S\subseteq V\times V
be two right-linear multivalued operators, and
s\in\mathbb{R}_{d}
. Then the sum and product of multivalued operators is defined as

\displaystyle T+S:=

\displaystyle\big\{(v,w_{1}+w_{2})\;\big|\;(v,w_{1})\in T,\,(v,w_{2})\in S\big\},

\displaystyle sT:=

\displaystyle\big\{(v,sw)\;\big|\;(v,w)\in T\big\},

\displaystyle T\circ S:=

\displaystyle\big\{(v,u)\in V\times V\;\big|\;\exists w\in V\text{ such that }(v,w)\in S\text{ and }(w,u)\in T\big\}.

The great advantage of treating multivalued operators is that for every right-linear multivalued operator
T\subseteq V\times V
there exists the corresponding closure

(3.6)

\overline{T}:=\big\{(v,w)\in V\times V\;\big|\;\exists(v_{n},w_{n})_{n}\in T\text{ such that }(v,w)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(v_{n},w_{n})\big\},

as well as the corresponding adjoint multivalued operator

(3.7)

T^{*}:=\big\{(w,v)\in V\times V\;\big|\;\langle v,\widetilde{v}\rangle=\langle w,\widetilde{w}\rangle,\text{ for all }(\widetilde{v},\widetilde{w})\in T\big\}.

Note, that in the case that
T
is a densely defined right-linear operator, the definition (
3.7
) of the adjoint multivalued operator coincides with the definition (
3.1
) of the classical adjoint operator, if one identifies
T
and
T^{*}
with their respective graphs of course.

The next lemma states some more basic properties of the adjoint operator. The proofs follow the same arguments as in the complex case.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
T,S\subseteq V\times V
be right-linear multivalued operators. Then there holds the following properties:

i)

If
T
is closable, then
\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T^{*})=V
, and
\overline{T}=(T^{*})^{*}
and
(\overline{T})^{*}=T^{*}
;

ii)

If
T\in\mathcal{B}(V)
, then also
T^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
with
\|T^{*}\|=\|T\|
;

iii)

If
T\subseteq S
, then
S^{*}\subseteq T^{*}
;

iv)

(ST)^{*}\supseteq T^{*}S^{*}
,

and if
S\in\mathcal{B}(V)
then even
(ST)^{*}=T^{*}S^{*}
,

and if
S\in\mathcal{K}(V)
,
T\in\mathcal{B}(V)
then
(ST)^{*}=\overline{T^{*}S^{*}}
;

v)

(T+S)^{*}\supseteq T^{*}+S^{*}
, and if either
T\in\mathcal{B}(V)
or
S\in\mathcal{B}(V)
then
(T+S)^{*}=T^{*}+S^{*}
;

vi)

For closed operators
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
, we have that
T
bijective if and only if
T^{*}
is bijective. In this case there is
(T^{*})^{-1}=(T^{-1})^{*}
.

A more detailed introduction into multivalued operators
can be found in
[
8
, Section 1]
.

Let us now come back to the
S
-spectrum, and start by proving that the one of
T
and
T^{*}
coincide. The following theorem is the
S
-spectrum analog of the well known connection between spectrum of an operator and its adjoint.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be densely defined. Then there is

(3.8)

\sigma_{S}(T^{*})=\sigma_{S}(T).

Moreover, for every
s\in\rho_{S}(T^{*})
, the
S
-resolvent operators of
T^{*}
are given by

(3.9)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T^{*})=S_{R}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)^{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,T^{*})=S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)^{*}.

Proof.

Instead of (
3.8
), it is equivalent to prove
\rho_{S}(T^{*})=\rho_{S}(T)
. For the first inclusion
\text{“}\supseteq\text{“}
, let
s\in\rho_{S}(T)
. From Theorem
2.4
we know that
(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
. Since
\mathcal{I}^{R}s
is a bounded operator with adjoint given by
(\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{*}=\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s}
, we have
(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{*}=T^{*}-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s}
. From Lemma
3.4
vi) we know that
(T-\mathcal{I}^{R}s)^{*}
is bijective, and hence there is also
T^{*}-\mathcal{I}^{R}\overline{s}
bijective. It then follows again from Theorem
2.4
that
\overline{s}\in\rho_{S}(T^{*})
. Finally, since
\rho_{S}(T^{*})
is axially symmetric, we also get
s\in\rho_{S}(T^{*})
.

The inverse inclusion
\text{“}\subseteq\text{“}
follows from the first one applied to the operator
T^{*}
, and using that
(T^{*})^{*}=T
, see Lemma
3.4
i).

Finally, we want to verify the relations (
3.9
) of the
S
-resolvent operators. It follows immediately from Lemma
3.4
iv) and v) that
Q_{s}[T]^{*}\supseteq Q_{s}[T^{*}]
. However, since
s\in\rho_{S}(T^{*})
, there is
\operatorname{ran}(Q_{s}[T^{*}])=V
, and
Q_{s}[T]^{*}=Q_{s}[T^{*}]
is satisfied with equality. Consequently, there is also

Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}=(Q_{S}[T]^{*})^{-1}=(Q_{s}[T]^{-1})^{*}

Moreover, for the operator
TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}
this also implies

(TQ_{s}[T]^{-1})^{*}\supseteq(Q_{s}[T]^{-1})^{*}T^{*}=Q_{S}[T^{*}]^{-1}T^{*}.

Since the left hand side is a closed operator, there also is

(3.10)

(TQ_{s}[T]^{-1})^{*}\supseteq\overline{Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}T^{*}}.

In order to show that the right hand side is everywhere defined, let
v\in V
. Since
T^{*}
is densely defined by Lemma
3.4
i), there exists a sequence
(v_{n})_{n}\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})
with
v=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{n}
. Since moreover
T^{*}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
there also converges

T^{*}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}v=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}T^{*}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}v_{n}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}T^{*}v_{n}.

Hence there is
v\in\operatorname{dom}(\overline{Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}T^{*}})
and

\overline{Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}T^{*}}v=T^{*}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}v.

This in particular means that the closure
\overline{Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}T^{*}}
is everywhere defined, and the inclusion (
3.10
) turns into the equality

(TQ_{s}[T]^{-1})^{*}=T^{*}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}.

With this identity, we then conclude the forms (
3.9
) of the
S
-resolvent operators, namely

\displaystyle S_{R}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)^{*}

\displaystyle=\big((s-T)Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\big)^{*}=\big(sQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\big)^{*}-\big(TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\big)^{*}

\displaystyle=Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}\overline{s}-T^{*}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}=S_{L}^{-1}(s,T^{*}),

as well as

\displaystyle S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)^{*}

\displaystyle=\big(Q_{s}[T]^{-1}s-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\big)^{*}=\big(Q_{s}[T]^{-1}s\big)^{*}-\big(TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\big)^{*}

\displaystyle=\overline{s}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}-T^{*}Q_{s}[T^{*}]^{-1}=S_{R}^{-1}(s,T^{*}).\qed

We notice that some of the results of Theorem
3.5
have been explored in the quaternionic setting by other authors. For more details, see the various comments in
[
18
, Section 9.2]
, the recent paper
[
11
]
and the references therein.

4.
The
S
-functional calculus of the adjoint operator

In this section let
V
be a Hilbert module over the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{d}
. Since Theorem
3.5
proves that the
S
-spectra of
T
and
T^{*}
coincide, it is reasonable to also find a connection between the various
S
-functional calculi of
T
and
T^{*}
. We will do this for the bounded
S
-functional calculus in Theorem
4.4
, for the unbounded
S
-functional calculus in Theorem
4.6
, for the
\omega
-functional calculus in Theorem
4.12
and for the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus in Theorem
4.15
. To do so, we introduce for functions
f
, the corresponding function
f^{\#}
as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be open and axially symmetric. Then for every
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
, we define the function

(4.1)

f^{\#}(s):=\overline{f(\overline{s})},\qquad s\in U.

The function
f^{\#}
now admits the following basic holomorphicity properties.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be open and axially symmetric.

i)

For every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U)
, there is
f^{\#}\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
;

ii)

For every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
, there is
f^{\#}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U)
;

iii)

For every
f\in\mathcal{N}(U)
, there is
f^{\#}=f
.

Proof.

i)  Let
f_{0},f_{1}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
be the two components of the function
f
according to the decomposition (
2.2
). Then we can write the function
f^{\#}
for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{U}
,
J\in\mathbb{S}
as

f^{\#}(x+Jy)=\overline{f(x-Jy)}=\overline{f_{0}(x,y)-Jf_{1}(x,y)}=\overline{f_{0}(x,y)}+\overline{f_{1}(x,y)}J.

Hence the components of
f^{\#}
are given by

f_{0}^{\#}(u,v):=\overline{f_{0}(u,v)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{1}^{\#}(u,v):=\overline{f_{1}(u,v)},\qquad(u,v)\in\mathcal{U}.

Since
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the symmetry conditions (
2.3
) as well as the Cauchy-Riemann equations (
2.4
), the same is true also for
f_{0}^{\#},f_{1}^{\#}
. This means we have proven
f^{\#}\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
.

ii)  Analogously to i).

iii)  If
f
is intrinsic, there clearly is

f^{\#}(s)=\overline{f(\overline{s})}=\overline{\overline{f(s)}}=f(s),\qquad s\in U.\qed

4.1.
The bounded
S
-functional calculus

In this subsection we derive the connection between the
S
-functional calculus of a bounded operator
T
and its adjoint
T^{*}
. Let us start with the definition of the
S
-functional calculus of a bounded operator.

Definition 4.3

(Bounded
S
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(V)
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U^{\prime})
(resp.
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U^{\prime})
), defined on some axially symmetric, open set
U^{\prime}\supseteq\sigma_{S}(T)
, the
bounded
S
-functional calculus
is defined as

U^{\prime}
\mathbb{C}_{J}
\sigma_{S}(T)
U

(4.2a)

\displaystyle f(T):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s),

(4.2b)

\displaystyle\bigg(\text{resp. }f(T):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}f(s)ds_{J}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\bigg).

Here
J\in\mathbb{S}
is arbitrary and
U
is any axially symmetric, open set, with
\sigma_{S}(T)\subseteq U\subseteq\overline{U}\subseteq U^{\prime}
, and regular enough boundary.

For more information on the bounded
S
-functional calculus we refer to
[
18
, Section 3.2]
or
[
26
, Chapter 3.3]
.

Since we know from Lemma
3.4
ii) that there is
T^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
whenever
T\in\mathcal{B}(V)
, the following theorem gives a connection between the bounded
S
-functional calculus of
T
and
T^{*}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(V)
and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U^{\prime})
or
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U^{\prime})
for some axially symmetric, open set
U^{\prime}\supseteq\sigma_{S}(T)
. Then the bounded
S
-functional calculi of
T^{*}
and
T
are connected via

(4.3)

f(T^{*})=f^{\#}(T)^{*}.

If moreover
f\in\mathcal{N}(U^{\prime})
is intrinsic, then there is even
f(T^{*})=f(T)^{*}
.

Note that if
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U^{\prime})
, then
f(T^{*})
is understood as the left bounded
S
-functional calculus (
4.2a
), and
f^{\#}(T)
as the right bounded
S
-functional calculus (
4.2b
). If on the other hand
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U^{\prime})
, then
f^{\#}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U^{\prime})
, and the two sides of (
4.3
) are understood accordingly.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Without loss of generality let us only consider
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U^{\prime})
. The case
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U^{\prime})
is analog. Using the definition (
4.2b
), we write
f^{\#}(T)^{*}
as

(4.4)

f^{\#}(T)^{*}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\bigg(\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}f^{\#}(s)ds_{J}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\bigg)^{*}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)^{*}d\overline{s_{J}}\,\overline{f^{\#}(s)}.

By (
3.9
) and (
4.1
), the individual terms of this integrand can now be written as

S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)^{*}=S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T^{*}),\qquad d\overline{s_{J}}=d\overline{s}_{-J},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{f^{\#}(s)}=f(\overline{s}).

This reduces (
4.4
) to

(4.5)

f^{\#}(T)^{*}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T^{*})d\overline{s}_{-J}f(\overline{s})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{-J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T^{*})ds_{-J}f(s)=f(T^{*}),

where in the second equation we used that the integral over
\overline{s}
coincides with the one over
s
since the integration path
\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}
is symmetric with respect to the real line. In the last line we used that the
S
-functional calculus is independent of the chosen imaginary unit
J
or
-J
.

Finally, for
f\in\mathcal{N}(U^{\prime})
, there is
f=f^{\#}
by Lemma
4.2
iii), and
f(T^{*})=f(T)^{*}
is a consequence of (
4.3
).
∎

4.2.
The unbounded
S

In comparison to the Section

.

4.3.
The

Since for unbounded operators

in Section

Definition 4.7

(Bisectorial operators)
.

An operator

Remark 4.8
.

The instance that for bisectorial operators only an upper bound on the left

is assumed, is not a restriction to left holomorphic functions of the corresponding functional calculus. Indeed, it is proven in
[
44

, that it already follows from (

) that also the right
S
-resolvent operator

is bounded by

(4.10)

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{2C_{\varphi}}{|s|},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\}).

In order to make the integrals in Definition
4.10
of the
\omega
-functional calculus converge, we also have to assume certain integrability assumptions on the function

Definition 4.9
.

For every

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;f\text{ is bounded},\;\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,\;J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\theta}\bigg\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{0}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;f\text{ is bounded},\;\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,\;J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\theta}\bigg\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;f\text{ is bounded},\;\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,\;J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\theta}\bigg\}.

The idea of the upcoming
\omega

[

.

4.4.
The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, which we consider in this subsection, is a regularization procedure that extends the
\omega
-functional calculus of Definition
4.10
. In particular, it avoids the very restrictive integrability assumptions on
f
of Definition
4.9
, and rather treats the polynomially growing functions from Definition
4.13
. The main result will then be formally the same identity as in (
4.12
), but interpreted as unbounded or even multivalued operators.

Definition 4.13
.

For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
let
D_{\theta}
be the double sector from (
4.8
). Then we define the function spaces

i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\exists C,\alpha\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq C\Big(|s|^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{|s|^{\alpha}}\Big),\text{ for every }s\in D_{\theta}\bigg\};

ii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\exists C,\alpha\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq C\Big(|s|^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{|s|^{\alpha}}\Big),\text{ for every }s\in D_{\theta}\bigg\};

iii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\exists C,\alpha\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq C\Big(|s|^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{|s|^{\alpha}}\Big),\text{ for every }s\in D_{\theta}\bigg\}.

In contrast to the previous sections on the bounded, the unbounded and the
\omega
-functional calculus, the following definition of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus is different for left and for right slice hyperholomorphic functions.

Definition 4.14

(
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be an injective bisectorial operator of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
, for some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, we define the
left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus
as

(4.13)

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T),

and for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
, for some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, the
right
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus
as

(4.14)

f(T):=\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}},

where all the terms
e(T)
,
(ef)(T)
and
(fe)(T)
are understood as the
\omega
-functional calculus (
4.11
). In both cases, the regularizer function
e\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
is given by
e(s)=\frac{s^{m}}{(1+s^{2})^{m}}
, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m>\alpha
, and
\alpha
from Definition
4.13
. Also, since
T
is injective, the operator
e(T)=T^{m}(1+T^{2})^{-m}
is injective as well, i.e.
e(T)^{-1}
is an unbounded closed operator. Hence the left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
4.13
) is a closed operator (as the product of a closed and a bounded operator), while the right
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
4.14
) is a multivalued operator (since
(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}
is in general not closable and the closure is understood in the sense of multivalued operators (
3.6
)). For more details on the
H^{\infty}
-functional on the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus we refer to
[
22
,

]
.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be injective and bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
or

, for some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, the

-functional calculi of

and

are connected via

Note, that if
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
, then

is understood as the left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
4.13
) and

as the right
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
4.14
). Moreover, since

is densely defined by
[

, Proposition 4.10]
, the adjoint

is considered as the adjoint (

) of a closed operator. Conversely, if

, then

is a multivalued operator as in (

), and since

for the left holomorphic function

is not densely defined in general, its adjoint

has to be considered in terms of multivalued operators (

).

Proof of Theorem
4.15
.

In order for
f(T^{*})
to be well defined, we have to check if
T^{*}
satisfies the assumptions of Definition
4.14
. The fact that
T^{*}
is bisectorial of angle
\omega
was already proven in Lemma

, and the injectivity follows from

where the range of a injective, bisectorial operator is dense by
[

, Theorem 3.3 ii)]
.

In the first part of the proof we will treat the case

. Let

be the regularizer of the function

in Definition
4.14
. Then the same function

is also a regularizer of

. From the definition of the

-functional calculus there then follows

Using now that

e(T^{*})=e(T)^{*},\qquad\text{as well as}\qquad(ef)(T^{*})=(f^{\#}e)(T)^{*},

by (

), and also using the properties of Lemma

Finally, using the property Lemma

-functional calculus of

, namely

In the second part of the proof we will treat the case

. Then the definition (

) of the left

-functional for the function

Now using the Lemma

Then similarly to (

), we can then carry the adjoint on the operator, which leaves us with exactly the definition of the right

, namely

\big(f^{\#}(T)\big)^{*}=\overline{(fe)(T^{*})e(T^{*})^{-1}}=f(T^{*}).\qed

5.
The multiplication operator in the Clifford setting

Now that we have developed all the

-functional calculi of the adjoint operator in Section

Definition 5.1
.

Let
X
be a measure space, and
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
measurable. Then define the

(5.1)

(M_{h}f)(x):=h(x)f(x)\qquad\text{with}\qquad\operatorname{dom}(M_{h}):=\big\{f\in L^{2}(X)\;\big|\;hf\in L^{2}(X)\big\}.

The following lemma provides some basic properties of the multiplication operator (
5.1
). Since the proofs are mainly the same as in the complex case, they will be omitted.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X
be a measure space, and
g,h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
be measurable. Then there is:

i)

M_{h}
is a densely defined, closed operator;

ii)

If
X
is
\sigma
-finite, then
M_{h}\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(X))
if and only if
h\in L^{\infty}(X)
.

In this case there is
\|h\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|M_{h}\|\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}\|h\|_{L^{\infty}}
;

iii)

M_{g}M_{h}\subseteq M_{gh}
, with
\operatorname{dom}(M_{g}M_{h})=\operatorname{dom}(M_{gh})\cap\operatorname{dom}(M_{h})
.

An, in particular for this paper, important observation is now that the adjoint of the multiplication operator is again a multiplication operator with the conjugate function.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
X
be a measure space, and
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
be measurable. Then there is

(M_{h})^{*}=M_{\overline{h}}.

Proof.

For the inclusion
\text{“}\subseteq\text{“}
let
g\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h}^{*})
, i.e., by Lemma
3.2
, there is

\langle M_{h}^{*}g,f\rangle=\langle g,M_{h}f\rangle,\qquad f\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h}).

Plugging in the definition of
M_{h}
and using the properties (
2.8
) of the inner product, we can rewrite the right hand side as

\langle M_{h}^{*}g,f\rangle=\langle g,hf\rangle=\langle\overline{h}g,f\rangle,\qquad f\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h}).

Since this is true for every
f
in the dense subset
\operatorname{dom}(M_{h})
, it follows that there coincides

M_{h}^{*}g=\overline{h}g.

This equality now proves that
\overline{h}g\in L^{2}(X)
, i.e.
g\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{\overline{h}})
, as well as

M_{h}^{*}g=M_{\overline{h}}g.

For the inverse inclusion
\text{“}\supseteq\text{“}
let
g\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{\overline{h}})
, i.e
g\in L^{2}(X)
and
\overline{h}g\in L^{2}(X)
. Then there holds the identity

\langle M_{\overline{h}}g,f\rangle=\langle\overline{h}g,f\rangle=\langle g,hf\rangle=\langle g,M_{h}f\rangle,\qquad f\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h}).

By the definition (
3.1
) of the adjoint operator, this shows
g\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h}^{*})
and
M_{h}^{*}g=M_{\overline{h}}g
.
∎

Next we define the following subset of the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{d}

(5.2)

\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}_{d}):=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}_{d}\;\big|\;|s|^{2}=s\overline{s}=s\overline{s}\big\}.

Note that every element
0\neq s\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}_{d})
is invertible, with inverse given by
s^{-1}=\frac{\overline{s}}{|s|^{2}}
.

If we now reduce ourselves to functions
h:X\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which map into this space (
5.2
), the following theorem transfers from the complex into the Clifford setting with the same proof.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
X
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space, and
h:X\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}_{d})
be measurable.

i)

M_{h}
is injective if and only of
h(x)\neq 0
for a.e.
x\in X
.

In this case the inverse operator is given by
M_{h}^{-1}=M_{h^{-1}}
.

ii)

M_{h}
is surjective if and only if there exists
\varepsilon>0
, such that
|h(x)|\geq\varepsilon
, for a.e.
x\in X
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
X
be a measure space,
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be measurable. Then for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, the operator
Q_{s}[M_{h}]
from (
1.1
) can be written as

(5.3)

Q_{s}[M_{h}]=M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}}.

Proof.

First, since
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
maps into set of paravectors, the real polynomial combination
h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}:X\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}_{d})
maps into the set
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
from (
5.2
).

For the operator inclusion
M_{h}^{2}-2s_{0}M_{h}+|s|^{2}\subseteq M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}}
, let
f\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h}^{2})
. This means that
h^{2}f,hf\in L^{2}(X)
, and consequently also
(h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2})f\in L^{2}(X)
. This proves that
f\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}})
, and moreover, the equality of the operation actions
(M_{h}^{2}-2s_{0}M_{h}+|s|^{2})f=M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}}f
is then trivial.

For the inverse inclusion
M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}}\subseteq M_{h}^{2}-2s_{0}M_{h}+|s|^{2}
, let
f\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}})
, i.e.
f\in L^{2}(X)
and
(h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2})f\in L^{2}(X)
. Let us now choose the constants

M:=\sup\limits_{q\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\frac{|q|}{1+|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}|}<\infty,

with which we can now estimate

|h(x)f(x)|=|h(x)||f(x)|\leq M\big(1+|h(x)^{2}-2s_{0}h(x)+|s|^{2}|\big)|f(x)|,\qquad x\in X.

Since
f\in L^{2}(X)
and
(h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2})f\in L^{2}(X)
, it follows that also
hf\in L^{2}(X)
. Consequently, there follows also
h^{2}f\in L^{2}
, and in particular
f\in\operatorname{dom}(M_{h}^{2})
. ∎

Next, we want to describe the
S
-spectrum of the multiplication operator
M_{h}
. The key object in this description will be the essential range of a function.

Definition 5.6

(Essential range)
.

Let
X
be a measure space. Then for every measurable function
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we define the
essential range

(5.4)

\operatorname{essran}(h):=\Big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\Big|\;\forall\varepsilon>0:\mu\big\{x\in X\;\big|\;|h(x)-s|<\varepsilon\big\}>0\Big\}.

In the same way as in the complex case one now proves the following basic properties of the essential range.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
X
be a measure space,
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
measurable. Then there is

i)

\operatorname{essran}(h)
is closed, with
\operatorname{essran}(h)\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(h)
;

ii)

\big\{x\in X\;\big|\;h(x)\notin\operatorname{essran}(h)\big\}
is a set of measure zero;

iii)

\|h\|_{L^{\infty}}=\sup(\operatorname{essran}(|h|))
;

iv)

For every continuous
f:\operatorname{essran}(h)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{d}
, there is
\operatorname{essran}(f\circ h)=\overline{f(\operatorname{essran}(h))}
.

Before we now state the actual characterization of the
S
-spectrum in terms of the essential range, we need the following preparatory lemma.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Then there exists some
\varepsilon_{s}>0
, such that there holds

(5.5)

[s]+U_{\varepsilon_{s}}(0)\subseteq\big\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}|<\varepsilon^{2}\big\}\subseteq[s]+U_{\varepsilon}(0).

Proof.

For the first inclusion let
q\in[s]+U_{\varepsilon_{s}}(0)
, for a not yet specified
\varepsilon_{s}>0
. I.e., there exists some
t\in[s]
with
|q-t|<\varepsilon_{s}
. Clearly it is possible to choose
t
in the same complex plane as
q
. Hence we can estimate

\displaystyle\big|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|q^{2}-2t_{0}q+|t|^{2}\big|=|q-t||q-\overline{t}|\leq|q-t|\big(|q-t|+|t-\overline{t}|\big)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{s}\big(\varepsilon_{s}+2|\operatorname{Im}(t)|\big)=\varepsilon_{s}\big(\varepsilon_{s}+2|\operatorname{Im}(s)|\big).

It is now possible to choose
\varepsilon_{s}>0
small enough, such that the right hand side of this inequality is smaller than
\varepsilon^{2}
. This then proves
|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}|<\varepsilon^{2}
.

For the second inclusion in (
5.5
), let
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with
|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}<\varepsilon^{2}
. Choose now
t\in[s]
such that
t
is in the same complex plane as
q
. Then there is also

|q-t||q-\overline{t}|=|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}|<\varepsilon^{2}.

From this inequality it the follows immediately that

\displaystyle|q-t|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|q-t||q-\overline{t}|<\varepsilon^{2},\qquad\text{if }|q-t|\leq|q-\overline{t}|,

\displaystyle|q-\overline{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|q-t||q-\overline{t}|<\varepsilon^{2},\qquad\text{if }|q-t|\geq|q-\overline{t}|,

which means that
q\in U_{\varepsilon}(t)\cup U_{\varepsilon}(\overline{t})\subseteq[s]+U_{\varepsilon}(0)
.
∎

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
X
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space, and
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
measurable. Then the
S
-spectrum of the multiplication operator
M_{h}
is given by

\sigma_{S}(M_{h})=[\operatorname{essran}(h)],

where
[\operatorname{essran}(h)]=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;[s]\cap\operatorname{essran}(h)\neq\emptyset\big\}
is the rotation of
\operatorname{essran}(h)
around the real axis. Moreover, for every
s\in\rho_{S}(M_{h})
in the
S
-resolvent set, the left
S
-resolvent operator acts as the multiplication operator

(5.6)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,M_{h})=M_{S_{L}^{-1}(s,h(\cdot))}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,M_{h})=M_{S_{R}^{-1}(s,h(\cdot))}.

Proof.

If we use the representation
Q_{s}[M_{h}]=M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}}
from Lemma
5.5
, then Theorem
5.4
shows that
Q_{s}[M_{h}]
is not bijective if and only if

(5.7)

\mu\big\{x\in X\;\big|\;|h(x)^{2}-2s_{0}h(x)+|s|^{2}|<\varepsilon^{2}\big\}>0,\qquad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.

Here
\mu
is the measure, corresponding to the space
X
. We now want to show that (
5.7
) is equivalent to
s\in[\operatorname{essran}(h)]
.

For the first implication let
s\in[\operatorname{essran}(h)]
and
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary. Then there exists some
t\in\operatorname{essran}(h)
, such that
t\in[s]
. By Lemma
5.8
there then exists some
\varepsilon_{s}>0
, with

U_{\varepsilon_{s}}(t)\subseteq[s]+U_{\varepsilon_{s}}(0)\subseteq\big\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}|<\varepsilon^{2}\big\}.

Hence, we also get

\mu\big\{x\in X\;\big|\;|h(x)^{2}-2s_{0}h(x)+|s|^{2}|<\varepsilon^{2}\big\}\geq\mu\big\{x\in X\;\big|\;|h(x)-t|<\varepsilon_{s}\big\}>0,

where the measure of the set on the right hand side is positive due to
t\in\operatorname{essran}(h)
and the definition (
5.4
) of the essential range.

For the inverse implication, let (
5.7
) be satisfied and assume that
s\notin[\operatorname{essran}(h)]
. This means that
[s]\cap\operatorname{essran}(h)=\emptyset
. Since
[s]
is compact and
\operatorname{essran}(h)
is closed, there exists some
\varepsilon>0
, such that

[s]+U_{\varepsilon}(0)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\operatorname{essran}(h).

By Lemma
5.8
there then also is

\big\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\;\big|\;|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}|<\varepsilon^{2}\big\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\operatorname{essran}(h).

Since the set in (
5.7
) has positive measure by assumption, we also get

\mu\big\{x\in X\;\big|\;h(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\operatorname{essran}(h)\big\}\geq\mu\big\{x\in X\;\big|\;|h(x)^{2}-2s_{0}h(x)+|s|^{2}|<\varepsilon^{2}\big\}>0.

However, since the left hand side has measure zero by Lemma
5.7
ii), this is a contradiction. Hence we have proven
s\in[\operatorname{essran}(h)]
.

In order to verify the explicit form of the left
S
-resolvent operator (
5.6
), we first note that by its definition in (
1.3
), there is

S_{L}^{-1}(s,M_{h})=Q_{s}[M_{h}]^{-1}\overline{s}-M_{h}Q_{s}[M_{h}]^{-1}.

Using now (
5.3
), the form of the inverse multiplication operator in Theorem
5.4
, as well as the product formula for the multiplication operator in Lemma
5.2
iii), we can further simplify

\displaystyle S_{L}^{-1}(s,M_{h})

\displaystyle=M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}}^{-1}\overline{s}-M_{h}M_{h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=M_{(h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2})^{-1}}\overline{s}-M_{h}M_{(h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2})^{-1}}

\displaystyle=M_{(h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2})^{-1}\overline{s}-h(h^{2}-2s_{0}h+|s|^{2})^{-1}}=M_{S_{L}^{-1}(s,h(\cdot))}.

The representation of the right
S
-resolvent operator is analog.
∎

Now, since Theorem
5.9
tells us how the
S
-spectrum of the multiplication operator looks like, we turn our attention to the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of Definition
4.14
. To do so, we start with the bisectoriality, according to Definition
4.7
, of the operator
M_{h}
.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
X
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space, and
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
measurable, with

(5.8)

\operatorname{essran}(h)\subseteq\overline{D_{\omega}},

for some
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then the multiplication operator
M_{h}
is bisectorial of angle
\omega
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{D_{\omega}}
is axially symmetric, it follows from Theorem
5.9
together with (
5.8
), that

\sigma_{S}(M_{h})=[\operatorname{essran}(h)]\subseteq[\overline{D_{\omega}}]=\overline{D_{\omega}}.

In order to prove the
S
-resolvent estimate (
4.9
), let
\varphi\in(\omega,\pi)
and
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
. Using the explicit form of the
S
-resolvent operator (
5.6
) as well as the operator norm of the multiplication operator in Lemma
5.2
ii), we get

\displaystyle\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,M_{h})\|

\displaystyle=\|M_{S_{L}^{-1}(s,h(\cdot))}\|\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,h(\cdot))\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{d}{2}}\operatorname{esssup}_{x\in X}\big|Q_{s}(h(x))^{-1}\overline{s}-h(x)Q_{s}(h(x))^{-1}\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}\operatorname{esssup}_{x\in X}\frac{|s|+|h(x)|}{|h(x)^{2}-2s_{0}h(x)+|s|^{2}|}\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}\sup\limits_{q\in\overline{D_{\omega}}}\frac{|s|+|q|}{|q^{2}-2s_{0}q+|s|^{2}|}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{d}{2}}\sup\limits_{q\in\overline{D_{\omega}}}\frac{|s|+|q|}{|q-s|^{2}}\leq\frac{2^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}{|s|\sqrt{1+\cos(\varphi-\omega)}\big(\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{1+\cos(\varphi-\omega)}\big)}.\qed

As the final part of this application section, we want to derive how the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of Definition
4.14
acts on the multiplication operator.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
X
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space, and
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
measurable, with
\operatorname{essran}(h)\subseteq\overline{D_{\omega}}
, for some
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
or
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, there holds

(5.9)

f(M_{h})=M_{f\circ h}.

Proof.

Let us start with the
\omega
-functional calculus (
4.11a
) for functions
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. Evaluated for
u\in L^{2}(X)
, and
x\in X
, we have

\displaystyle f(M_{h})u(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,M_{h})ds_{J}f(s)u(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,h(x))ds_{J}f(s)u(x),

where in the second line we plugged in the action (
5.6
) of the
S
-resolvent operator. Using now the Cauchy integral formula

f(q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,q)ds_{J}f(s),\qquad q\in D_{\varphi},

with
q=h(x)
, gives

\displaystyle f(M_{h})u(x)=f(h(x))u(x),\qquad u\in L^{2}(X),\text{ for a.e. }x\in X.

At least this is true for every
x\in X
such that
h(x)\in\operatorname{essran}(h)
, which is almost everywhere due to Lemma
5.7
iii). Since this is true for every
u\in L^{2}(X)
and a.e.
x\in X
, we see that
f(M_{h})
acts as the multiplication operator with the function
f\circ h
, i.e. we have proven (
5.9
) for functions
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. The same formula for
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{0}(D_{\theta})
is analog.

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
5.9
) for functions
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
, is defined as

f(M_{h})=e(M_{h})^{-1}(ef)(M_{h}).

With the already derived identity (
5.9
) for functions in
\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, in particular for
e
and
ef
, we can rewrite this definition as

f(M_{h})=M_{e\circ h}^{-1}M_{(ef)\circ h}=M_{(e\circ h)^{-1}}M_{(ef)\circ h}=M_{(e\circ h)^{-1}((ef)\circ h)}=M_{f\circ h}.

On the other hand, for
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
, the definition (
4.14
) of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for right holommorphic functions tells us that

f(M_{h})=\overline{(fe)(M_{h})e(M_{h})^{-1}}=\overline{M_{(ef)\circ h)(e\circ h)^{-1}}}=M_{((ef)\circ h)(e\circ h)^{-1}}=M_{f\circ h},

where, additionally to the left case, we used that the multiplication operator is closed.
∎

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
X
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space, and
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
measurable, with
\operatorname{essran}(h)\subseteq\overline{D_{\omega}}
, for some
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
or
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, there is

f(M_{h})^{*}=M_{\overline{f}\circ h}.

Proof.

Consecutively applying the results of Theorem
4.15
, Theorem
5.3
, and Theorem
5.11
, as well as the definition of
f^{\#}
in (
4.1
), gives

f(M_{h})^{*}=f^{\#}(M_{h}^{*})=f^{\#}(M_{\overline{h}})=M_{f^{\#}\circ\overline{h}}=M_{\overline{f}\circ h}.\qed

6.
Concluding remarks on applications and research directions

The spectral theory based on the
S
-spectrum was inspired by quaternionic quantum mechanics and began its development in 2006. A comprehensive introduction can be found in
[
18
]
, with further explorations done in
[
3
,
4
,
26
]
. Applications on fractional powers of operators are investigated in
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
36
,
37
]
and some results from classical interpolation theory have been recently extended into this setting
[
28
]
. The spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum extends complex spectral theory to noncommuting operators, broadening its applicability to various research fields. Without claiming completeness we mention:

Quaternionic formulation of quantum mechanics.
The interest in spectral theory for quaternionic operators is motivated by the 1936 paper
[
10
]
on the logic of quantum mechanics by G. Birkhoff, J. von Neumann. There, the authors showed that Schrödinger equation can be written basically in the complex or in the quaternionic setting, see also the book of Adler
[
1
]
.

Vector analysis.
It is the natural spectral theory for operaotrs appearing in vector analysis such as the gradient operator with nonconstant coefficients in
n
dimensions, represents various physical laws, such as Fourier’s law for heat propagation and Fick’s law for mass transfer diffusion. This can be expressed as

T=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x)\partial_{x_{i}},\qquad x\in\Omega,

and is associated with various boundary conditions, as discussed in
[
21
]
.

Differential geometry.
This theory applies to Dirac operators on manifold. For more details study the reader can refer to the book
[
38
]
. As a special case the paper
[
9
]
considers the Dirac operator in hyperbolic and spherical spaces, that takes the explicit forms

\mathcal{D}_{H}=x_{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}-\frac{n-1}{2}e_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{D}_{S}=(1+|x|^{2})\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}-nx,

where for
\mathcal{D}_{H}
we pick
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
\mathcal{D}_{S}
.

Hypercomplex analysis.
The well-known Dirac operator and its conjugate

D=\partial_{x_{0}}+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{D}=\partial_{x_{0}}-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}},

are widely investigated in
[
29
]
as well as in
[
38
]
. For integers
\alpha,\beta,m
, the operators of the Dirac fine structure on the
S
-spectrum are defined as

T_{\alpha,m}=D^{\alpha}(D\overline{D})^{m}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{T}_{\beta,m}=\overline{D}^{\beta}(D\overline{D})^{m}.

The fine structures on the
S
-spectrum constitute a set of function spaces and their related functional calculi. These structures have been introduced and studied in recent works
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
30
,
31
]
, while their respective
H^{\infty}
-versions are investigated in
[
24
,
32
]
.

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculi also have a broader context in the recently introduced fine structures on the
S
-spectrum. These are function spaces of nonholomorphic functions derived from the Fueter-Sce extension theorem, which connects slice hyperholomorphic and axially monogenic functions, the two main notions of holomorphicity in the Clifford setting. The connection is established through powers of the Laplace operator in a higher dimension, see
[
35
,
47
,
48
]
and also the translation
[
25
]
.

It is important to highlight that in the hypercomplex setting there exists another spectral theory based on the monogenic spectrum, which was initiated by Jefferies, McIntosh and J. Picton-Warlow in
[
43
]
. This theory is founded on monogenic functions, which are functions in the kernel of the Dirac operator, and their associated Cauchy formula (see
[
29
]
for a comprehensive treatment). The monogenic spectral theory, based on monogenic functions
[
29
]
, has been extensively developed and is well described in the seminal books
[
39
]
and
[
45
]
. These texts provide an in-depth exploration of the theory, with particular emphasis on the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus in the monogenic setting.
