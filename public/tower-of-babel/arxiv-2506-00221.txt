Integrating Expert Knowledge and Recursive Bayesian Inference: A Framework for Spatial and Spatio-Temporal Data Challenges
Mario Figueira, David Conesa, Antonio López-Quílez, Håvard Rue

Abstract
Expert elicitation is a critical approach for addressing data scarcity across various disciplines. But moreover, it can also complement big data analytics by mitigating the limitations of observational data, such as incompleteness and reliability issues, thereby enhancing model estimates through the integration of disparate or conflicting data sources. The paper also outlines various strategies for integrating prior information within the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation method and proposes a recursive approach that allows for the analysis of new data as it arrives. This paper presents a comprehensive approach to expert elicitation, with a particular emphasis on spatial and spatio-temporal contexts. Specifically, it introduces a typology of expert-based model implementations that addresses different change of support scenarios between observational and expert data. Detailed examples illustrating clear and replicable procedures for implementing expert elicitation and recursive inference are also presented.

1
Introduction

Expert elicitation is a fundamental approach to address data scarcity in various applied fields, including ecology, environmental sciences, public policy, and risk management
(
Kynn 2008
;
Kuhnert et al. 2010
;
Morgan 2014
;
Zhang and Thai 2016
)
. Additionally, it complements “big data” analytics by filling gaps where observational data is incomplete or unreliable. Data combination is particularly valuable in integrated models
(
Ovaskainen and Soininen 2011
;
Taylor-Rodríguez et al. 2017
;
Paradinas et al. 2023
)
and in fusion models
(
Wang et al. 2018
;
Chacón-Montalván et al. 2024
)
, where expert knowledge can help reconcile conflicting or sparse data sources. It can involve incorporating new data, strengthening weakly informed areas, or leveraging expert judgment to address inconsistencies and uncertainties, as seen in expert elicitation methods and computer models
(
Kennedy and
O’Hagan 2001
;
O’Hagan 2006
;
Dave Higdon and
Rightley 2008
;
Morgan 2014
;
Zhang and Thai 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
;
Cadham et al. 2021
)
.

The information provided through expert elicitation can be categorized into two types: (1) prior elicitation, which involves defining the prior distribution for some unknown parameters of the model
(
O’Hagan 2006
)
, and (2) the Supra-Bayesian approach, where experts provide information on more intuitive observable variables
(
Albert et al. 2012
)
, such as the probability of the presence of a species in a given area, along with an uncertainty quantification for that estimate
(
Pearman-Gillman et al. 2020
)
.

In this paper, we present a comprehensive approach to perform expert elicitation, with a particular focus on spatial and spatio-temporal scenarios, although not exclusively. Our proposal accounts for both types of expert elicitation previously described, and it is implemented within the Bayesian inference framework, specifically leveraging the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA,
Rue et al. 2009
) approach for high computational performance. Furthermore, implementing our approach within the Bayesian framework allows us to incorporate it into a recursive Bayesian inference process, enabling a sequential model fitting as new data sources arrive. This is particularly advantageous due to the sequential nature of Bayes’ theorem, as it allows new information to be incorporated into the inference sequence without requiring a complete model recomputation, as in online updating
(
Schifano et al. 2016
)
and Bayesian filtering
(
Särkkä 2013
)
.

In prior elicitation, experts define the specific shape of the distribution or characterize it by specifying certain moments
(
Cadham et al. 2021
)
, such as the mean or variance. Additionally, prior information can also be obtained from another model, where the estimation of certain effects is assumed to be very similar to those in the target hierarchical Bayesian model. To incorporate this information into the target model, we can use alternative data sources (e.g., data from citizen science, institutions, or previous research) and fit auxiliary models, which yield estimates of the effects that can be incorporated as initial conditions in the target model. That is, posterior distributions from these auxiliary models can be used to inform prior distributions of the parameters and hyperparameters in the target model. In this paper, our proposal for prior elicitation encompasses different strategies exploiting the INLA approach to implement a sequential consensus strategy and a recursive framework for updating prior information as new data arrive.

The integration of expert data along with observational data can be achieved through the Supra-Bayesian approach
(
Albert et al. 2012
;
Billari et al. 2014
)
, analyzed within the framework of Bayesian Hierarchical Joint Models
(
Albert et al. 2012
;
Perälä et al. 2020
)
. In this context, experts may provide information at the same scale as the observational data or at a different scale, often due to spatial or temporal change of support or misalignment; this is the case in several real-world examples where both sources are misaligned
(
Gobbi et al. 2012
;
Aizpurua et al. 2015
;
Pearman-Gillman et al. 2020
;
Kaurila et al. 2022
)
. Our proposal for performing expert elicitation within the Supra-Bayesian approach addresses the challenge of change of support between observational and expert data. It enhances model estimates within a joint modeling structure that simultaneously integrates expert knowledge calibration and uncertainty analysis alongside observational data in change of support scenarios. Additionally, this information is not limited to expert judgment but can also come from simulators or computer models. These deterministic models emulate the reference process and can be incorporated in the same way as expert-provided data. Accordingly, we also present a typology of expert-based model implementations, addressing different change of support scenarios between observational and expert data.

In summary, Section 2 provides a brief overview of the general framework for conducting expert elicitation, either through joint models or prior elicitation, along with the inferential approach used. Section 3 outlines the elicitation of model parameter distributions and the various ways it can be implemented, including the recursive Bayesian inference approach. Section 4 details expert elicitation via expert integration through joint models, discussing the different types of integration and the necessary methodological considerations. Section 5 presents concrete examples that illustrate the procedure in a clear, concise, and replicable manner. Finally, the conclusions discuss the scope of the proposed procedures and the potential limitations encountered in their implementation.

2
Expert elicitation framework

Elicitation models integrate expert knowledge from individuals or structured expert-based frameworks, including technicians, policymakers, and citizen science participants
(
O’Hagan 2006
)
. They can also incorporate information from deterministic or computer models developed in specific scientific fields, which mathematically encode the dynamics of a phenomenon under given conditions
(
Kennedy and
O’Hagan 2001
)
. Since observational statistical models fundamentally differ from deterministic models, the latter often serve as a more effective source of expert information when developing new experimental models. In this context, leveraging deterministic models as “expert models” may be more efficient than relying solely on observational statistical models based on prior experiments. Another valuable source of information comes from previous experiments related to the same phenomenon under study.

A prior elicitation approach can be applied in such cases, allowing information from existing statistical models to inform the prior distributions of parameters and hyperparameters within a Bayesian framework. The approach proposed in this paper for integrating expert knowledge and the implementation of recursive inference to allow an updatable procedure of the model, has been implemented within the framework of the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation
(
Rue et al. 2009
;
van Niekerk et al. 2023
)
in the
R-INLA
software
(
Martins et al. 2013
)
. INLA is a deterministic approximation approach deeply rooted in GMRF theory
(
Rue and Held 2005
)
for Bayesian inference
(
Rue et al. 2009
;
van Niekerk et al. 2023
)
and Latent Gaussian Models (LGM). INLA focuses on estimating the marginal posterior distributions of the model parameters, allowing the computation of marginal likelihoods and standard goodness-of-fit metrics such as Deviance Information Criterion (DIC)
(
Spiegelhalter et al. 2002
)
, Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC)
(
Watanabe 2013
)
, Conditional Predictive Ordinates (CPO)
(
Pettit 1990
)
, and leave-group-out cross validation
(
Liu and Rue 2024
)
, all of which are essential for model evaluation and comparison. However, it could also be implemented with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods.

3
Prior elicitation and recursive inference

Prior elicitation, as previously stated, can be performed by leveraging different information sources, including experts, citizen science, deterministic models, computational models, or data from previous experiments and surveys that inform new studies on the same phenomenon. However, this information may not always be available simultaneously with the data being analyzed or may become available during new surveys. In such cases, it is preferable to incorporate expert information into models for new data without needing to reprocess all previous information. It is possible to achieve this through recursive or sequential inference methods, such as online updating using a frequentist inferential approach
(
Schifano et al. 2016
)
, sequential Bayesian inference
(
Scott et al. 2016
;
Hooten et al. 2021
;
Kessler et al. 2023
)
, or a continual learning approach
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
.

Sequential inference is approached from a wide range of perspectives and applied to various fields, such as online updating
(
Schifano et al. 2016
)
, spatial statistics
(
Hooten et al. 2021
)
, or distributed computing
(
Minsker et al. 2017
;
Guhaniyogi et al. 2022
)
. In Bayesian statistics, many sequential inference methods rely on MCMC
(
Scott 2017
;
Rendell et al. 2021
)
or in the properties of samplers for the MCMC, taking advantage of features from the Metropolis-Hastings sampler
(
Lunn et al. 2013
;
Hooten et al. 2021
)
, the Weierstrass sampler
(
Wang and Dunson 2014
)
, and others. In this case, we build on the methodological foundations of INLA to implement a recursive inference procedure.

In this section, we introduce different strategies for integrating prior information within Bayesian inference, with a particular focus on implementation using the INLA approach. These strategies can be applied using the INLA methodology through the
R-INLA
software
(
Martins et al. 2013
)
, combined with other methods such as Monte Carlo (MC,
Berild et al. 2022
) and MCMC
(
Gómez-Rubio and Rue 2018
)
, or implemented entirely using MCMC approaches. The section is divided into two parts. The first part discusses the updating of prior information and how, when combined with a consensus approach
(
Scott et al. 2016
)
, it enables sequential consensus inference
(
Figueira et al. 2025
)
. The second part introduces our proposed approach for performing recursive inference within the INLA framework, without relying on the assumptions and approximations of the previously discussed sequential consensus approach.

3.1
Prior updating

In a general sense, prior updating refers to the process of refining a prior distribution based on new knowledge or relevant information. This is a fundamental concept in Bayesian statistics, as Bayes’ theorem itself is built upon the principle of updating prior beliefs to obtain a posterior distribution. In this section, we present different strategies for updating model information: direct prior updating (
prior updating by moments
and
full prior updating
) as well as an algorithm that integrates sequential prior updating with consensus to update the entire model’s information.

Prior updating by moments

In this strategy, we focus on updating the moments that define the kernel of the prior distribution, such as the mean and variance in a Gaussian kernel. This approach can be easily implemented in both MCMC and INLA for univariate distributions. Since INLA relies on latent Gaussian models (LGM) and the fixed parameters (the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
coefficients) are expected to follow a Gaussian distribution, we can update their Gaussian prior by modifying its characteristic moments, as discussed in
Figueira et al. 2024
. For hyperparameters in INLA, we can either update the existing kernel of the prior distribution or introduce a new one, refining it to better align with the posterior distribution from a previous model. However, for the linear coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, we remain constrained to Gaussian priors in the INLA framework.

This can be achieved by determining the parameters
\boldsymbol{\xi}
of
\pi(x\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})
based on the moments (e.g., mean and variance) of the marginal posterior distribution
\pi(x\mid\mathbf{y})
. Alternatively,
\boldsymbol{\xi}
can be estimated by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) between the two distributions, i.e., solving
\min_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\text{KLD}(\pi(x\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})\lVert\pi(x\mid\mathbf{y}))
.

Full prior updating

Full prior updating enables the definition of arbitrary continuous distributions, including joint prior distributions for both parameters and hyperparameters. The goal is to define joint prior distributions
\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})
based on the posterior distributions
\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
. Within the R-INLA framework, three key features facilitate hyperparameter updating: the table and expression functionalities
(
Gómez-Rubio 2020
)
, along with the rprior function, which streamlines the creation of custom priors. This functionality allows us to define a distribution by specifying a set of values for the random variable along with their corresponding density values,
\{x_{i},\pi(x_{i})\}
. It is important to note that these density values do not always directly correspond to the parameter itself but may instead relate to a transformation of the parameter. Therefore, careful consideration of the appropriate transformation, accounting for internal parameterization, is essential. However, constructing joint priors and assigning non-Gaussian priors to latent field parameters presents certain challenges.

To address these challenges, integrating MCMC methods with INLA provides a viable solution
(
Gómez-Rubio and Rue 2018
)
. For instance, a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm can be implemented to jointly update a set
\boldsymbol{\nu}
of latent field parameters (
\mathbf{x}
) or hyperparameters (
\boldsymbol{\theta}
), where
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\{\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}\}
. This is achieved by defining a suitable proposal distribution
q(\cdot\lvert\cdot)
and computing the acceptance probability:

\alpha=\min\left\{\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*})\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*})q(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*})}{\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}\mid\mathbf{y})\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)})q(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)})}\right\},

where
\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)})
and
\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*})
are the conditional marginal likelihoods for
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}
, respectively. Alternatively, Importance Sampling or Adaptive Multiple Importance Sampling
(
Berild et al. 2022
)
can be used to perform full prior updating.

Sequential Consensus inference

The combination of prior updating procedures, particularly prior updating by moments, along with a consensus approach, allows us to define sequential algorithms for conducting Bayesian inference with INLA. The sequential consensus inference approach
(
Figueira et al. 2025
)
focuses on sequentially updating the marginal distributions of the fixed effects of the latent field
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
according to prior updating by moments, and reaching a consensus on the latent field related to the random effects, either marginal or multivariate. Specifically, the following approximation is assumed for the marginal posterior distribution of the fixed effects:

\pi(\beta_{i}\mid\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{1})\approx\pi(\mathbf{y_{2}}\mid\beta_{i})\pi(\beta_{i}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1}),

(1)

and a similar approximation is assumed for the hyperparameters. Once the sequence is completed, a marginal consensus of the random effects is proposed, either by the weighted sum of the
n
parts of the sequence for the random effects of the latent field:

x_{i}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{ij}\cdot x_{ij},

(2)

where
w_{ij}=\tau_{ij}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{ij}
represents the optimal weights. Alternatively, a multivariate consensus of the nodes of the latent field can be employed:

\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})\approx\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1})\cdot\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}_{2}),

(3)

where, assuming both
\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1})
and
\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}_{2})
are multivariate Gaussian distributions.

The precision and mean of the consensus distribution of the posteriors are:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{Q}&=&\mathbf{Q}_{1}+\mathbf{Q}_{2},\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}&=&(\mathbf{Q}_{1}+\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{-1}(\mathbf{Q}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\mathbf{Q}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}).\end{array}

(4)

The precision matrices
\mathbf{Q}
and vector means
\boldsymbol{\mu}
for performing the multivariate consensus would be those obtained in the modal configuration for the hyperparameters at the different steps of the sequence. For the marginal consensus, the random effects are distributed according to the marginal distributions of the nodes of the latent field related to those random effects.

3.2
Recursive Bayesian inference

INLA focuses in the posterior marginal distributions for the nodes of the latent field (
\mathbf{x}
) and for the hyperparameters (
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\}
). However, to achieve this it needs to approximate the posterior distribution of the hyperparameters and the posterior distribution of the latent field given a set of support points
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}
in the hyperparameter space
(
Rue et al. 2009
)
. The approximation used to compute the posterior of the hyperparameters is
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
, defined as:

\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})=\left.\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\pi_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right|_{\mathbf{x=\mathbf{x}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}},

(5)

where
\tilde{\pi}_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the Gaussian approximation for the posterior of the latent field, and the whole expression is analyzed in the modal configuration of posterior of the latent field under the Gaussian approximation.

To calculate the posterior distribution of the latent field, a Gaussian approximation is used, evaluated at the support points, and a low-rank correction is applied to the joint mean of the posterior distribution of the latent field under the Gaussian approximation
(
van Niekerk et al. 2023
;
van Niekerk and Rue 2024
)
. This correction can also be used to correct the marginal variance. The Gaussian approximation to the true
\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
is done by matching the mode and the curvature around the mode of
\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
using a second-order Taylor series expansion around the mode of
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

(
Rue et al. 2009
;
van Niekerk et al. 2023
)
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\pi_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})&\approx&\exp(\log\pi(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})+\nabla\left.\log\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right|_{\mathbf{x}=\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mathbf{x}\\[4.2679pt]
&&+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{T}\nabla^{2}\left.\log\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right|_{\mathbf{x}=\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mathbf{x}.\end{array}

(6)

This posterior approximation is obtained for each support point
{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}}
, and the mean is corrected through an iterative procedure of low-rank variational Bayes correction
(
van Niekerk and Rue 2024
)
. This yields a GMRF (Gaussian Markov Random Field) with a corrected mean for each support point
{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}}
:

\pi_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})=\text{GMRF}(\mu^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}),\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})),

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the corrected mean and
\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the precision matrix. The GMRF of the latent field for each
{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}}
is one of the outputs returned by the
inla
call in the
R-INLA
package, and it is be one of the key elements for implementing a recursive inference procedure with INLA.

Finally, two distinct strategies are used to obtain the marginals of the hyperparameters and the latent field. For the hyperparameters, interpolation is performed over the values obtained at the support points
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\}
to yield a marginal distribution for each hyperparameter. Meanwhile, to obtain the marginal of the latent field, the following approximation is defined as a mixture of the marginal distributions at each support point:

\tilde{\pi}(x_{i}\mid\mathbf{y})\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{G}(x_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k},\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\mid\mathbf{y})\Delta_{k},

(8)

where
\pi_{G}(x_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k},\mathbf{y})
is the marginal distribution given the GMRF with the corrected mean, and
\Delta_{k}
are weights associated with each support point, related to their distribution.

Therefore, based on what we have explained about the INLA methodology, and in the fact that INLA also relies in the conditional independence of the data process given the latent field and hyperparameters
\pi(y_{i},y_{j}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(y_{i}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\cdot\pi(y_{j}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we can implement a recursive procedure to perform inference.

The recursive procedure assumes that we have a dataset that can be split —or is naturally partitioned— into
N
disjoint subsets
\mathbf{y}=\{\mathbf{y}_{1},...,\mathbf{y}_{N}\}
. The core structure for performing recursive inference involves analyzing the first partition,
\mathbf{y}_{1}
, as usual. From this we obtain the support points
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, the joint posterior density of the hyperparameters evaluated at these points,
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1})
, and the posterior GMRF of the latent field,
\pi_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})
, for each support point. Using these outputs, we can reuse the support points
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\}
and the posterior GMRF of the latent field for each support point. This allows us to perform an analysis of the next subset by fixing the posterior GMRF and running parallel calls for each support point, resembling the following expression:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k},\mathbf{y})&\propto&\prod_{i=1}^{N}\pi(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}),\\[4.2679pt]
&=&\prod_{i=2}^{N}\pi(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y_{1}},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}),\end{array}

(9)

where for the
i-th
step we use as prior distribution for the latent Gaussian field the posterior from the previous one (
i-1
). This allow us to compute the joint posterior distribution of the latent field.

The next step is to compute the posterior distribution for the hyperparameters throughout the sequence. In the first step, we have the support points and the density of the joint posterior distribution at these points. To update the density at the support points in subsequent steps, we can rely on the marginal likelihood of the model. Since we are fixing the computations at these support points, the marginal likelihood in steps beyond the first is conditioned on them:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\mid\mathbf{y})&\propto&\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}),\\
&=&\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{N}\pi(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}),\end{array}

(10)

where
\pi(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})
is the marginal likelihood conditioned on the set of support points
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.

Therefore, the posterior distribution
\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\mid\mathbf{y})
computed through the recursive algorithm is:

\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\mid\mathbf{y})\propto\left[\left.\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}_{1}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}{\pi_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\right|_{\mathbf{x=\mathbf{x}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}}\right]\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{N}\pi(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}),

(11)

and from this expression, the marginal likelihood can be approximated using a numerical integration scheme (e.g., central composite design, grid exploration, etc.):

\tilde{\pi}(\mathbf{y})=\displaystyle\int\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\pi_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}d\boldsymbol{\theta}\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left.\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}{\pi_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\right|_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\;.

(12)

The final expression for the joint distribution of the hyperparameters when performing the recursive INLA procedure is:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\mid\mathbf{y})&\propto&\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{N}\left[\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\cup_{l=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{y}_{l},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}{\pi^{*}_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\cup_{l=1}^{i}\mathbf{y}_{l},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\right]_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\\
&&\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}_{1}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}{\pi_{G}^{*}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\right]_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\\
&=&\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\cup_{l=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{y}_{l},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}{\pi^{*}_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\cup_{l=1}^{i}\mathbf{y}_{l},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\right]_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\end{array}.

(13)

Once the joint posterior distributions of the hyperparameters and the GMRF of the latent field are obtained, with the mean rectified by the low-rank correction, the marginal distributions can be calculated. For the hyperparameters, this is done through interpolation and integration, while for the latent field, we use the same approximation employed by INLA, as described in Eq.
8
, where we reuse the weights
\Delta_{k}
obtained in the first step of the sequence.

In the proposed procedure for performing recursive inference, the first discrepancy that may arise, compared to simultaneous inference on all the data, comes from the identification of the support points
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. The main issue stems from the potential shift in the mode of
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathbf{y})
throughout the sequence, leading to a possible inadequate exploration of the posterior distribution. This could be corrected by assessing the shift in the mode. However, the non-rectifiable discrepancy lies in the fact that the computed posterior distribution density is not exactly the same as that obtained through the simultaneous analysis of all the data:

\tilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}\mid\mathbf{y})\propto\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\left.\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}{\pi_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right|_{\mathbf{x=\mathbf{x}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}}\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}),\quad\text{(INLA)}\\
\displaystyle\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\pi(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\cup_{l=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{y}_{l},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}{\pi^{*}_{G}(\mathbf{x}\mid\cup_{l=1}^{i}\mathbf{y}_{l},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\right]_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})}\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k}).\end{array}\right.

(14)

This discrepancy would transfer to the computation of the marginal distributions of the nodes of the GMRF in the latent field, being the only non-rectifiable discrepancy. However, it is estimable and, therefore, analysable.

4
Dealing with change of support in elicitation models

The fundamental distinction between expert data with and without a change of support lies in whether the scale at which expert data integrate with observational data remains consistent. This aspect directly impacts the Supra-Bayesian approach, as expert information in this context pertains to observable variables provided by the expert. Scenarios where data are available at different scales are commonly referred to as misalignment or change of support, sometimes described as cross-referencing information scales.

In many cases, expert information—regardless of its source—is provided on a scale that differs from that of the observational data. It is common for expert knowledge to be aggregated or presented at a different spatial, temporal, or categorical scale, and in many instances, combinations of these differences occur. For example, in ecology, numerous studies highlight cases where experts provide information at spatial and/or temporal scales distinct from those of field-collected data
(
Yamada et al. 2003
;
Gobbi et al. 2012
;
Aizpurua et al. 2015
;
Di Febbraro et al. 2018
;
Fitzgerald et al. 2021
;
Kaurila et al. 2022
)
. Consequently, expert information may be available at a lower (or higher) resolution than observational data, requiring an integration approach that accounts for the specific type of change of support. The classification below provides a framework for assessing and integrating expert information based on its relationship to observational data: (i) spatial change of support, (ii) temporal change of support, (iii) spatio-temporal change of support, and (iv) categorical change of support.

The first three cases of expert information can be effectively integrated through a downscaling process using a Gaussian field with a Matérn structure
(
Moraga et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
. This can be applied in one dimension for temporal downscaling, in two dimensions for spatial downscaling, or as a combination of both for spatio-temporal downscaling. In the case of categorical change of support, where categorical levels are grouped or intersected, expert information should influence the aggregated category, either by informing the new grouping or by adjusting the parametric structure assessing the categorical level being aggregated.

An additional challenge arises when both observational and expert data are provided at an areal level but do not match. In such cases, downscaling can be applied to both datasets to facilitate their joint evaluation, or appropriate aggregation methods can be used when feasible. Furthermore, following the recommendations of
O’Hagan 2006
, it is crucial to integrate uncertainty quantification within the modeling framework to account for the experts’ confidence in their own knowledge.

In the following subsections, we examine the different cases and propose how to deal with change of support within the Supra-Bayesian approach: (i) no change of support, (ii) spatial change of support, (iii) temporal change of support, (iv) spatio-temporal change of support, and (v) categorical change of support.

4.1
No change of support

In the case with no change of support, both observational and expert data exist at the same scale, allowing for a integration as illustrated in Figure
2
. To integrate a single expert information source with observational data—or multiple expert-based sources—we propose the following mechanisms for constructing an integrated model that calibrates expert auxiliary models and assesses their associated uncertainty.

An integrated model with no change of support can be defined by a likelihood function
\ell
for the observational data
y_{i,obs}
, within a given model structure
\mathcal{M}\equiv\{\ell,\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}\}
. Here,
\boldsymbol{\eta}
represents the linear predictor,
\mathbf{A}
is the design matrix of covariates,
\mathbf{x}
denotes the latent Gaussian field, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
consists of the hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}
corresponds to the likelihood parameters and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
to the latent field parameters.

This observational data model is combined with expert data using the Supra-Bayesian approach
(
Albert et al. 2012
;
Billari et al. 2014
;
Perälä et al. 2020
)
. In this framework, the expert data
y_{i,exp}
is treated as additional observations. Since expert data is at the same scale as the observational data, it follows the same structure for the latent effects. Consequently, the integrated model can be expressed as:

\begin{array}[]{c}y_{i,obs}\sim\ell(\mu_{i,obs},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\\
g(\mu_{i,obs})=\eta_{i,obs}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}(z_{ik}),\\
y_{i,exp}\sim\ell_{e}(\mu_{i,exp},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{(e)}),\\
g_{e}(\mu_{i,exp})=\alpha\cdot\eta_{i,obs}+u_{i}=\alpha\left[\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}(z_{ik})\right]+u_{i},\\
\end{array}

(15)

where
\mathbf{x}=\{\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{f}\}
is the latent field composed by the fixed
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and random effects
\mathbf{f}
. In this integrated model, the linear predictor of the observational model,
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{obs}
, is incorporated into the expert auxiliary model and scaled by a parameter
\alpha
.

The scaling parameter
\alpha
is expected to be close to
1
if the expert data is consistent with the observational data. However,
\alpha
can also be defined as a spline function, allowing for an assessment of its deviation from
1
across different predictor values. The term
u_{i}
represents a random effect that captures the residual uncertainty in the expert model that is not explained by the scaled predictor from the observational model. Together, these components define a joint model that integrates an observational model with a calibrated auxiliary expert model.

In Eq.
15
, it is crucial to analyze the hyperparameter associated with the variance (or precision) of the expert auxiliary model, as recommended by
O’Hagan 2006
. This analysis helps quantify the uncertainty introduced by the expert. If we define the precision of the likelihood for the expert data by
\tau_{i,exp}
, the expert may provide an estimate of the deviation or precision for each data point,
\{y_{i,exp},\phi_{i,exp}\}
. The corresponding uncertainty can be represented as:

\tau_{i,exp}=\psi\cdot\phi_{i,exp},

where
\phi_{i,exp}
is the expert-provided deviation or precision for each data point
y_{i,exp}
, and
\psi
is a parameter to be estimated.

The elicitation process can incorporate information from multiple expert sources, including expert-informed models, computational models, or specialized professionals. In such cases, it is beneficial to explore potential correlations among these experts, as highlighted by
Albert et al. 2012
. Within the INLA framework, these correlations can be conceptualized as a multivariate Gaussian error term.

Extending the previous model, if we have multiple experts
m\in\{1,...,M\}
, each with their own scaling parameter
\alpha_{m}
, the model can be expressed as:

\begin{array}[]{c}Y_{i,obs}\sim\ell(\mu_{i,obs},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\\
g(\mu_{i,obs})=\eta_{i,obs}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}(z_{ik}),\\
Y_{i,exp1}\sim\ell_{e}(\mu_{i,exp1},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(e_{1})}_{1}),\\
g_{e}(\mu_{i,exp1})=\alpha_{1}\cdot\eta_{i,obs}+u_{i1}+u_{iC}=\alpha_{1}\left[\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}(z_{ik})\right]+u_{i1}+u_{iC},\\
\vdots\\
Y_{i,expM}\sim\ell_{e}(\mu_{i,expM},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(e_{M})}_{1}),\\
g_{e}(\mu_{i,expM})=\alpha_{M}\cdot\eta_{i,obs}+u_{iM}+u_{iC}=\alpha_{M}\left[\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}(z_{ik})\right]+u_{iM}+u_{iC},\\
\end{array}

(16)

where each expert
m
has its own scaling parameter
\alpha_{m}
. As above mentioned, the scaling structure can be made more flexible by defining it as a spline, allowing the scaling value to vary depending on the linear predictor. The term
u_{iC}
captures the correlation among expert linear predictors, assuming a Multivariate Normal (MVN) distribution for the experts:

\mathbf{u}_{C}\sim\mathbf{MVN}(\mathbf{0},\Sigma_{C}),

where the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix are defined as
\Sigma_{ii}=1/\tau_{i}
, and the off-diagonal elements
(i\neq j)
are
\Sigma_{ij}=\rho_{ij}/\sqrt{\tau_{i}\tau_{j}}
.

It is important to note that real case studies often involve more complex models, where expert assessments (or expert data) may follow a different likelihood function than the one used for observational data. A common example in ecological studies—such as habitat suitability or fisheries management—occurs when observational data are presence/absence-based (Bernoulli distribution), while expert information relates to the probability of presence (Beta distribution). In such cases, when data distributions differ, the modeled quantity must remain compatible across likelihoods. For instance, the probability in a Bernoulli model must align with the probability in a Beta model. In some scenarios, linear predictors within the likelihood functions must be transformed to ensure that the shared information between them is correctly defined.

4.2
Spatial change of support

Spatial change of support implies that expert information does not match the spatial structure of the observational data. This can be assessed based on whether the observational data is continuously structured or organized into areas (or networks).

The different scenarios that we may encounter for spatial change of support are: (i) geostatistical-area change of support, where observations are continuous geostatistical data,
s_{i}\in\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and expert information is structured into areas,
C_{i}\subset\mathcal{D}
, and (ii) area-area change of support, where both observational and expert data is structured in areas,
B_{i}\in\mathbf{B}
and
C_{i}\in\mathbf{C}
respectively, but these spatial partitions are different (
\mathbf{B}\neq\mathbf{C}
).

In the case of geostatistical-area change of support we propose to implement the development around the fusion or melding models framework
(
Gelfand 2001
;
Berrocal et al. 2012
;
Moraga et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2018
;
Wang and
Furrer 2021
;
Villejo et al. 2023
;
Chacón-Montalván et al. 2024
;
Zhong et al. 2025
)
. The core idea is to use a continuous Gaussian field
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{s})\sim\text{GF}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{Q})
in the study area
\mathcal{D}
, similar to a standard geostatistical model. However, in the model for expert information, we integrate the Gaussian field within each corresponding area to obtain the mean aggregated effect or the total aggregated effect. This involves setting a set of integration points (
s_{k}
) to approximate the integral via numerical integration. Moreover, we can compute the mean aggregated effect as
\iint_{s\in C_{i}}u(s)/|C_{i}|ds\approx\sum_{s_{k}\in C_{i}}u(s_{k})/n\;:\;(s\in C_{i})
, where we consider that each integration point has associated the same area, or we can compute the total aggregation
\iint_{s\in C_{i}}u(s)
.

A model within the Supra-Bayesian approach to deal with this change of support in expert elicitation can be defined as in the previous section. This integrated model consists of a likelihood
\ell
for the observations
\mathbf{y}_{obs}
in a continuous study region
\mathcal{D}
, and a set of expert data
\mathbf{y}_{expert}
structured in a (non-intersecting) partition of the study region
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{k\in K}C_{k}
. For this case, the integration model we propose, assuming that the observational and expert data share the same core underlying process
\eta_{i,obs}
, while incorporating an additional component
u_{j}
to account for the uncertainty in expert assessments, can be expressed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}Y_{i,obs}&\sim&\ell(\mu_{i,obs},\tau_{obs}),\\
g(\mu_{i,obs})&=&\eta_{i,obs}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{A}_{u}(s)\mathbf{u}(s|\rho,\sigma),\\
Y_{j,exp}&\sim&\ell_{e}(\mu_{j,exp},\tau_{exp}),\\
g_{e}(\mu_{j,exp})&=&\alpha\cdot\iint_{s\in C_{j}}\frac{\eta_{j,obs}(s)}{|C_{j}|}ds+u_{j},\\
&=&\alpha\left[\sum_{s_{k}\in C_{j}}\frac{\mathbf{A}(s_{k})\boldsymbol{\beta}}{n(s_{k}\in C_{j})}+\sum_{s_{k}\in C_{j}}\frac{\mathbf{A}_{u}(s_{k})\mathbf{u}(s_{k}|\rho,\sigma)}{n(s_{k}\in C_{j})}\right]+u_{j},\\
\end{array}

(17)

where we are integrating the effect associated with the observational predictor in each region where expert information is available. If the values of the covariates are not available at the required integration points, sub-models can be constructed for each covariate and incorporated into the observational predictor, similar to an Error model
(
Muff et al. 2015
)
, or in a two-stage process
(
Villejo et al. 2023
)
. Generalisation to non-Gaussian data and multiple experts with different aggregations is trivial given the previous formulation.

In the case of area-area change of support, we propose the same approach but perform the integration of the continuous field in both the observation predictor and when it is shared in the model associated with expert information. Following the structure of the previous example, we can present it as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}Y_{obs}(B_{i})&\sim&\ell(\mu_{obs}(B_{i}),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\\
g(\mu_{obs}(B_{i}))&=&\sum_{s_{k}\in B_{i}}\frac{\mathbf{A}(s_{k})\boldsymbol{\beta}}{n(s_{k}\in B_{i})}+\sum_{s_{k}\in B_{i}}\frac{\mathbf{A}_{u}(s_{k})\mathbf{u}(s_{k}|\rho,\sigma)}{n(s_{k}\in B_{i})},\\
Y_{exp}(C_{j})&\sim&\ell_{e}(\mu_{exp}(C_{j}),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{(e)}),\\
g_{e}(\mu_{exp}(C_{j}))&=&\alpha\cdot\sum_{s_{k}\in C_{j}}\frac{\eta_{j,obs}(s)}{|C_{j}|}+u_{j},\\
&=&\alpha\left[\sum_{s_{k}\in C_{j}}\frac{\mathbf{A}(s_{k})\boldsymbol{\beta}}{n(s_{k}\in C_{j})}ds+\sum_{s_{k}\in C_{j}}\frac{\mathbf{A}_{u}(s_{k})u(s_{k}|\rho,\sigma)}{n(s_{k}\in C_{j})}\right]+u_{j},\\
\end{array}

(18)

where the observational and expert data are related to
B_{i}
and
C_{j}
areas respectively, and the continuous Gaussian field allow us to combine both datasets, which can also be applied to covariates in case we lack their information.

However, in the case of area-area change of support, it is also possible to explore alternative processes beyond defining a continuous Gaussian field. Specifically, when expert information is available at a coarser spatial scale than the observational data, an alternative downscaling approach can be considered without explicitly defining a continuous Gaussian field. This approach is particularly useful when the areas associated with observational data are entirely nested within a group of larger areas corresponding to expert information, such that
C_{j}=\bigcup_{k}B_{k}
. In this scenario, the predictor associated with each polygon
C_{j}
can be expressed as:

\eta_{j}\propto\sum_{k:B_{k}\subset C_{j}}\frac{|B_{k}|}{|C_{j}|}\eta_{k},

(19)

where the predictor is progressively constructed from finer-scale observational data and aggregated upwards. In the limit, this structure aligns with cases where information is not directly cross-referenced between scales, i.e. without change of support or misalignment. This component represents the shared predictor, and when expert data is available at multiple scales, additional calibration mechanisms can be introduced. This approach serves as an alternative to continuous Gaussian field-based downscaling while preserving the ability to align expert-based models with observational data and to quantify the uncertainty in expert assessments that is not explained by the observational model.

4.3
Temporal change of support

Temporal change of support implies that the expert information does not align in its temporal structure with the observational data. This discrepancy can be evaluated by determining whether the data are continuously structured or organised into intervals. A similar issue arises in spatial analysis when space is defined in only one dimension, such as in distance sampling models
(
Yuan et al. 2017
)
or metric graphs
(
Bolin et al. 2024
)
.

Therefore, the procedures we have proposed for the two-dimensional spatial case can also be applied in this setting. In a joint model for a continuous process over time, where we have different observations over a period
\mathcal{T}
and an expert provides information at intervals
T_{i}=(t_{i1},t_{i2})
that may or may not completely cover the period
\mathcal{T}
, a structure that integrates both sources of information can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}Y_{i,obs}&\sim&\ell(\mu_{i,obs},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\\
g(\mu_{i,obs})&=&\eta_{i,obs}=\mathbf{A}(s)\boldsymbol{\beta}+u(s|\rho,\sigma),\\
Y_{j,exp}&\sim&\ell_{e}(\mu_{j,exp},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{(e)}),\\
g_{e}(\mu_{j,exp})&=&\alpha\cdot\int_{s\in T_{j}}\frac{\eta_{j,obs}(s)}{|T_{j}|}ds+u_{j},\\
&=&\alpha\left[\sum_{s_{k}\in T_{j}}\frac{\mathbf{A}(s_{k})\boldsymbol{\beta}}{n(s_{k}\in T_{j})}+\sum_{s_{k}\in T_{j}}\frac{\mathbf{A}_{u}(s_{k})u(s_{k}|\rho,\sigma)}{n(s_{k}\in T_{j})}\right]+u_{j},\\
\end{array}

(20)

where
s
and
s_{k}
stand for temporal or one-dimension spatial locations and
u_{j}
is an additional component to account for the uncertainty in expert assessments. This equation is a reformulation of Equation (
17
) for the one-dimensional case, which is commonly encountered in temporal analysis. Moreover, it does not impose any regularity on the expert intervals—they can be irregular and do not necessarily represent an average over the interval. Instead, by setting the normalization factor to
1
, we obtain the cumulative quantity.

4.4
Spatio-temporal change of support

An extension to the spatio-temporal case can be achieved by combining our previous two proposals (spatial and temporal). For spatio-temporal change of support, we propose the same procedures as for spatial and temporal change of support. The key difference is that, in the spatio-temporal case, the integration occurs in three dimensions, requiring the approximation of volume integration. In this setting, the response variable is continuous in both space and time, and an expert provides information within specific volumes of the spatio-temporal region. Consequently, a possible model that combines observational data
Y_{i,obs}
and expert data
Y_{j,exp}
given a spatio-temporal aggregation into voxels (
V_{j}
) of expert information, can be written as the following one:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}Y_{i,obs}&\sim&\ell(\mu_{i,obs},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\\
g(\mu_{i,obs})&=&\eta_{i,obs}=\mathbf{A}(s)\boldsymbol{\beta}+u(s),\\
Y_{j,exp}&\sim&\ell_{e}(\mu_{j,exp},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{(e)}),\\
g_{e}(\mu_{j,exp})&=&\alpha\cdot\iiint_{s\in V_{j}}\frac{\eta_{j,obs}(s)}{|V_{j}|}ds+u_{j},\\
&=&\alpha\left[\sum_{s_{k}\in V_{j}}\frac{\mathbf{A}(s_{k})\boldsymbol{\beta}}{n(s_{k}\in V_{j})}+\sum_{s_{k}\in V_{j}}\frac{u(s_{k})}{n(s_{k}\in V_{j})}\right]+u_{j},\\
\end{array}

(21)

where
s
and
s_{k}
represent spatio-temporal locations and
u_{j}
is an additional component to account for the uncertainty in expert assessments. This equation represents the extension of the previous two cases for spatial and temporal change of supports, but now accounting for the three dimensional nature of the data.

4.5
Categorical change of support

The latter situation arises when dealing with a categorical change of support. In this case, categorical change of support occurs when expert information is provided using a smaller set of categories derived from an aggregation of a larger set in the observational data model; the reverse scenario is also possible. This means that the effects of categories can be combined if there is a clear correspondence between groups. For instance, consider an observational categorical variable with
5
categories,
\mathbf{u}={u_{1},\dots,u_{5}}
, and expert information consisting of
3
categories,
\mathbf{u}^{*}=\{u^{*}_{1},\dots,u^{*}_{3}\}
, such that
u^{*}_{2}=\{u_{2},u_{3}\}
and
u^{*}_{3}=\{u_{4},u_{5}\}
. In this case, the aggregation in the joint model assumes that in the expert data, the estimate for
u^{*}_{2}
and
u^{*}_{3}
is equivalent to the sum of the effects of the related categories:
u^{*}_{2}=u_{2}+u_{3}
and
u^{*}_{3}=u_{4}+u_{5}
. In general, it can be stated as follows: if we have a categorical variable with
K
categories and expert information that provides information for the same variable in
K^{*}
categories, where
K>K^{*}
, such that there is a correspondence between disjoint groups
g_{k}=\{\mathbf{u}_{k}\}
of levels of the observational variable with respect to the expert information, where
g_{k}\in\{u_{1},\dots,u_{K}\}
and
g_{i}\cap g_{j}=\emptyset\iff i\neq j
. Then, the aggregation can be written as follows:

u^{*}_{k}=\sum_{u_{i}\in g_{k}}u_{i}.

(22)

This approach relies on the underlying assumption that, across the grouped factor levels in the higher structure—i.e., at the lower resolution scale—the grouping from the higher resolution scale is balanced. However, a more general and flexible formulation accounts for unbalanced aggregation. In this case, Equation (
22
) is modified as follows:

u^{*}_{k}=\sum_{u_{i}\in g_{k}}w_{ik}u_{i}.

(23)

where
w_{i}
represents the weight associated with each factor level
u_{i}
in the aggregation
u_{i}\in g_{k}
, reflecting its actual proportion in the grouping, given by
n(u_{i}\in g_{k})/\sum_{i=1}^{K}n(u_{i}\in g_{k})
. The weights satisfy the constraints
w_{ik}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_{ik}=1
.

Therefore, its implementation, following the model structures previously used, can be expressed as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}Y_{i,obs}&\sim&\ell(\mu_{i,obs},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\\
g(\mu_{i,obs})&=&\eta_{i,obs}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}+u(z_{i}),\\
Y_{j,exp}&\sim&\ell(\mu_{j,exp},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{(e)}),\\
g_{e}(\mu_{j,exp})&=&\alpha\cdot(\mathbf{A}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}+u^{*}(g_{j}))+u_{j},\\
&=&\alpha\cdot(\mathbf{A}_{e}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{z_{i}\in g_{j}}u(z_{i}))+u_{j},\\
\end{array}

(24)

where
\{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}_{e}\}
are the design matrices for the observed and expert data, respectively,
u^{*}(g_{j})=\sum_{z_{i}\in g_{k}}u(z_{i})
represents the change of support across different category levels, following Equation (
22
), and
u_{j}
is an additional component to account for the uncertainty in expert assessments.

A limiting case of this aggregation occurs when it is applied to the entire categorical variable. In this scenario, the effects of categories not considered in the expert information should not be shared between the observational model and the expert model. This is because categorical variables often assume a sum-to-zero constraint,
\sum^{K}_{k}=u_{k}=0
, where
K
is the total number of categories. Consequently, the aggregation of this effect in the auxiliary expert model is also equal to zero. Therefore, only the aggregated information across the category levels is considered. Since we have
\sum_{k}u_{k}=0
, the integration over the entire category results in zero in the auxiliary expert model.

5
Examples

In this section, three examples are presented to demonstrate the implementation of expert elicitation and sequential inference using INLA. The first two examples focus on the implementation of expert elicitation: the first deals with spatial change of support, while the second addresses categorical change of support. The third example presents a case of sequential inference with a spatial-temporal structure, comparing the results of performing the procedure sequentially against fitting the model to all the data simultaneously.

5.1
Expert elicitation with spatial change of support

In this first example, we will consider a case where limited data is combined with expert knowledge, following the Supra-Bayesian approach
(
Albert et al. 2012
)
. This simulated data example aims to replicate a structure for incorporating expert information similar to that presented in
(
Pearman-Gillman et al. 2020
)
, where the expert could provide information related to the probability of presence in survey areas. It also refers to cases where expert elicitation may be based on the expert’s knowledge of the species’ spatial distribution. As such, this knowledge could be integrated using a strategy of this kind
(
Yamada et al. 2003
;
Gobbi et al. 2012
;
Aizpurua et al. 2015
)
.

Figure 3:
Observational data and information from two experts according to two different spatial structures.

In this specific case, we assume that there are two experts providing information according to a certain spatial structure. This can occur either by providing a predetermined structure that covers the study area (
\text{S}_{1}
), as in
(
Kaurila et al. 2022
)
, or by allowing the expert to design specific areas (
\text{S}_{2}
) where they can provide information. In Fig.
3
, we present the two ways in which experts could provide information (
\text{S}_{1}
or
\text{S}_{2}
) along with the available observations in a scenario with limited spatial data. Therefore, the model used to simulate and infer the data is one that incorporates the spatial change of support between the expert and observational information, along with an assessment of the correlation between the experts:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}y_{i,obs}&\sim&\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\tau),\\
\mu_{i}&=&\beta_{0}+u_{s}(s_{i}),\\
\mathbf{y}_{j,exp}&\sim&\mathcal{MVN}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}&=&\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0,exp}+\int_{C_{j}}\frac{\beta_{0}+u_{s}(s)}{|C_{j}|}\text{d}s,\end{array}

(25)

where we share the entire latent field between the observational model and the auxiliary expert model. The matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
represents the variance-covariance between the experts, capturing both the marginal uncertainty of the experts and the correlation between them. In Fig.
4
, the results of the spatial field are presented, either modeled using only the observational data or integrating expert information using the aforementioned model, according to the two spatial structures in which the experts provided their information.

Figure 4:
Simulated spatial effect, along with the mean and standard deviation of the posterior distribution of the spatial effect, analyzed either using only the observational data or by integrating expert information according to the two structures (
\text{S}_{1}
or
\text{S}_{2}
) for expert information.

5.2
Expert elicitation with categorical change of support

In this example, it was assumed that the observational data is given with a categorical variable of 5 levels, while an expert would provide information structured by aggregating the first three levels of the categorical variable in the observational model, with the other two levels remaining unchanged. That is, the categorical variable in the observational model would be
\mathbf{u}_{obs}=\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4},u_{5}\}
, and for the expert, it would be
\mathbf{u}_{exp}=\{u^{*}_{1},u^{*}_{2},u^{*}_{3}\}
, such that
u^{*}_{1}=u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3}
. Therefore, the model used is the following:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}y_{i,obs}&\sim&\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\tau_{obs}),\\
\mu_{i,obs}&=&\beta_{0}+u_{i},\\
y_{j,exp}&\sim&\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j},\tau_{exp}),\\
\mu_{j,exp}&=&\beta_{0}+u^{*}_{i},\end{array}

(26)

where the information about the intercept and the effect of category
u_{i}
would be shared, considering the previously mentioned aggregation in the expert model. In Fig.
5
, the results for the intercept and the posterior distributions for each category level are presented.

Figure 5:
Posterior distributions for the intercept and the levels of the categorical variable. The vertical black line indicates the true value, the red line corresponds to the model that integrates both expert and observational information, the blue line is associated exclusively with the observational part, and the green line represents the expert’s contribution (which is why it is not present for the first three categories).

5.3
Recursive inference for spatio-temporal data

In this last example, a recursive inference procedure is performed for a spatio-temporal model. The structure of the model to simulate and infer is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\tau\mathbf{I}),\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\beta_{0}+\mathbf{u}_{s}+\mathbf{u}_{t},\end{array}

(27)

where
\beta_{0}
is the intercept,
\mathbf{u}_{s}
stands for the spatial effect, and
\mathbf{u}_{t}
represents the temporal effect. The data have been simulated with
60
temporal nodes and
300
spatial points for each temporal node.

In the recursive inference procedure, we proposed splitting the data temporally, as if they were arriving at different moments for analysis. The data were divided into groups of
20
consecutive temporal nodes. Therefore, there are three subsets
\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3}\}
for recursive analysis, corresponding to the temporal division of the data:
u^{(1)}_{t}=\{u_{1t},u_{20t}\}
,
u^{(2)}_{t}=\{u_{21t},u_{40t}\}
and
u^{(3)}_{t}=\{u_{41t},u_{60t}\}
. In Fig.
6
, the results of the inferential process for the temporal structure are presented. Since we have split the data according to the spatial structure, evaluating this allows us to see more clearly the effect of the inferential procedure and the updates with the incorporation of each subset into which the original data have been split. Finally, in Fig.
7
, the comparison of the mean and standard deviation of the posterior distribution of the spatial effect is shown between analyzing all the data simultaneously (standard INLA approach) or analyzing them recursively (recursive INLA), as previously explained.

Mean values along with the
0.025
and
0.975
quantiles of the posterior distribution associated with the temporal effect as additional partitions are incorporated into the analysis. In plots A, B, and C, the black line represents the true temporal effect, while the red line shows the update introduced by the partition being added at each step. The blue line represents the posterior mean and quantiles of the temporal effect as the sequential updates progress (except at the first step, where it coincides with the red line and is thus omitted). 6
Conclusions

In this work, several ways to incorporate prior information have been synthesised, either through prior elicitation or the Supra-Bayesian approach, focusing on its implementation in
R-INLA
. Regarding the Supra-Bayesian approach, it has been indicated that there may be a change in the support between the observation model and the auxiliary expert model, with respect to the support associated with the observational data. It has also been explained how to conduct prior elicitation, presenting various strategies to incorporate information into prior distributions in INLA or by combining INLA with other approaches, along with a proposal for conducting a recursive inferential procedure leveraging the methodology and underlying assumptions of the INLA framework.

In the recursive inferential approach, leveraging the underlying assumptions of INLA, we have highlighted that the determination of support points for the hyperparameters is likely to be different from those determined using the entire dataset. Another key difference is that the computation of the posterior distribution of the hyperparameters in the recursive approach differed from the one obtained when using the whole dataset at once. However, the results shown in the spatio-temporal example indicate that these differences were negligible.

In the various examples, the improvement in the posterior distributions of the spatial structure has been demonstrated in the presence of spatial change of support between the observations and the expert information, as well as in the case of categorical change of support. Finally, an example implementing the sequential inference procedure with INLA has been shown, where the data were temporally divided, as if accessed at different moments, and the result was compared with the model analyzing all data simultaneously. In this case, the effect of the updates as the sequence progressed was shown, with results that were nearly identical between the sequential procedure and the simultaneous analysis of all data.
