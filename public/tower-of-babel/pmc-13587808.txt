Management of a large angiomyofibroblastoma of the vulva: a case report describing radical excision and reconstruction
Authors: Antoni Sierant, Agnieszka Gajda-Czerwinska, Anna Pekalak-Czernek, Ewelina Szyszkowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAngiomyofibroblastoma is a rare, benign mesenchymal tumor of the vulvovaginal region, often misdiagnosed due to its nonspecific clinical presentation. Accurate diagnosis and appropriate surgical management are essential to achieve optimal oncological and aesthetic outcomes. This reports presents the case of a 28-year-old woman with a two-year history of a slowly enlarging, painless mass of the left labium majus. Clinical and ultrasonographic evaluation suggested a benign lesion. The patient underwent surgical excision via an interlabial sulcus approach, selected to ensure adequate exposure and optimal cosmetic outcome. Histopathological examination revealed a well-circumscribed mesenchymal tumor composed of spindle and oval stromal cells arranged around numerous thin-walled vessels, without atypia or mitotic activity. Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for desmin, estrogen receptors, and progesterone receptors, with negative staining for CD34 and S100 protein, confirming the diagnosis of angiomyofibroblastoma. The postoperative course was complicated by hematoma and edema, which resolved with conservative management. At one month, a favorable cosmetic outcome had been achieved, and no evidence of recurrence was observed at five-month follow-up. Angiomyofibroblastoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of vulvar masses. Complete local excision is curative in most cases. In large tumors, careful surgical planning with reconstructive techniques is essential to achieve both oncological safety and satisfactory cosmetic results. Long-term follow-up is recommended despite the benign nature of the disease.Keywords: Angiomyofibroblastoma, Vulvar tumor, Case report, Surgical excision, Reconstruction

Introduction

Angiomyofibroblastoma (AMFB) is a rare, benign mesenchymal tumor of the lower female genital tract, with a marked predilection for the vulva and vagina. It was first described by Fletcher et al. in 1992 as a distinct clinicopathological entity, clearly differentiated from aggressive angiomyxoma [1]. To date, approximately 70–90 cases of vulvar AMFB have been reported, underscoring its rarity and the limited clinical experience available [2], [3], [4].

AMFB predominantly affects women of reproductive and middle age, with a median age of approximately 45 years (range 16–86 years) [2], [3], [4], [5]. Clinically, it typically presents as a slowly enlarging, painless vulvar mass that may remain unnoticed for months or even years prior to diagnosis (mean duration 21–29 months) [2], [6]. On physical examination, lesions are characteristically well circumscribed, soft, and mobile, without evidence of infiltration into adjacent structures [2], [3]. Tumor size is highly variable; although most lesions measure less than 5–7 cm, larger tumors—including rare giant forms up to 37 cm—have been documented [2], [5].

Due to its nonspecific clinical presentation, AMFB is frequently misdiagnosed as a Bartholin gland cyst, lipoma, or other benign vulvar lesions [2], [5], [7]. Ultrasound is typically the first-line imaging modality and often demonstrates a well-defined solid lesion; however, these findings are not diagnostic [2], [8]. Magnetic resonance imaging may be useful in selected cases, particularly when aggressive angiomyxoma is suspected, as AMFB typically exhibits well-circumscribed, non-infiltrative margins [5], [8].

Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological evaluation. Macroscopically, AMFB appears as a well-demarcated, soft, gray-white to yellow tumor, sometimes resembling adipose tissue [1], [9]. Microscopically, it is characterized by alternating hypercellular and hypocellular edematous areas, numerous thin-walled vessels, and bland spindle to oval stromal cells arranged in a perivascular pattern, with no significant atypia or mitotic activity [1], [10], [11].

Immunohistochemically, AMFB typically demonstrates positivity for desmin, vimentin, estrogen receptors (ER), and progesterone receptors (PR), with negative staining for S100 protein and usually CD34 [6], [7]. The consistent expression of ER and PR supports the hypothesis of hormonal responsiveness and may explain the tumor's predilection for women of reproductive age [6].

The principal differential diagnoses include aggressive angiomyxoma, cellular angiofibroma, superficial angiomyxoma, and vulvar smooth muscle tumors [10], [12], [13]. Distinguishing AMFB from aggressive angiomyxoma is critical, as the latter demonstrates infiltrative growth and a high recurrence rate, necessitating wide surgical excision [1], [5], [8]. In contrast, AMFB is a well-circumscribed lesion with a benign clinical course.

AMFB is associated with an excellent prognosis. Recurrence is exceedingly rare and is typically related to incomplete excision or, in exceptional cases, malignant (sarcomatous) transformation [2], [3], [6], [14]. Importantly, no cases of distant metastases have been reliably documented [2], [3].

Case Presentation

A 28-year-old woman was referred to the gynecology department with a slowly enlarging, painless mass of the left labium majus, first noticed approximately two years prior to presentation. The patient's general condition was good. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no chronic diseases, prior surgeries, or regular medications. She reported nicotine use but denied any known allergies. Family history was notable only for colon cancer in a grandfather diagnosed after the age of 70.

Gynecological examination revealed a soft, subcutaneous mass extending from the pubic symphysis to the lower pole of the left labium majus (Fig. 1). The lesion did not involve the urethra or the rectum. The remainder of the genital tract appeared normal.

Pelvic ultrasonography demonstrated a well-circumscribed, hyperechoic lesion measuring approximately 80 × 40 mm, located within the left labium majus. The mass showed no internal vascularization and appeared to separate from adjacent tissues under pressure, suggesting a benign process such as a lipoma [8]. The uterus and adnexa were normal, and no free fluid was seen in the pelvis.

Surgical Approach and Rationale

The patient was indicated for surgical intervention of the vulvar mass, with a management strategy designed to achieve both oncological/functional clearance and aesthetic restoration of the labium majus (Fig. 1). The selection of the interlabial sulcus as the primary incision site was predicated upon the following clinical considerations:

•Optimization of cicatrization: Positioning the incision within the natural anatomical fold facilitates superior scar concealment, thereby enhancing the long-term aesthetic outcome.

The procedure was performed and the specimen was sent for histopathological examination.

Gross pathological examination revealed an encapsulated, multinodular, gray-yellow tumor measuring 11.0 × 7.0 × 4.0 cm, with a solid cut surface resembling fatty tissue (Fig. 2). Microscopic analysis demonstrated a well-demarcated mesenchymal tumor composed of spindle and oval stromal cells arranged around numerous thin-walled blood vessels, within an edematous and fibromyxoid stroma. No nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, or necrosis was observed. The lesion was sharply demarcated from surrounding tissue, forming a pseudocapsule corresponding to the surgical margin and indicating complete excision. Based on these findings, the diagnosis of angiomyofibroblastoma of the left labium majus was established (ICD-10: D21.9 - benign neoplasm of connective and other soft tissue, unspecified; WHO classification of soft tissue tumors 8826/0).

The postoperative course was complicated by the development of a hematoma and edema at the surgical site (Fig. 3), despite adequate intraoperative hemostasis. The patient was managed conservatively, and the symptoms resolved spontaneously within several days. She was subsequently discharged in good general condition. At one month postoperatively, a favorable cosmetic outcome had been achieved (Fig. 4). At five-month follow-up, there was no clinical evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

The present case of a 28-year-old woman with a slowly enlarging, painless mass of the left labium majus over a two-year period is fully consistent with the typical clinical presentation described in the literature [2], [6]. Notably, the tumor size exceeding 10 cm places it within the spectrum of rare large or “giant” AMFBs, which remain uncommon but well documented [5]. Unlike the case reported by Sassi et al. [18], the present tumor exceeded 10 cm in diameter and required a reconstructive interlabial sulcus approach to restore vulvar anatomy, highlighting the surgical challenges specific to giant AMFB presentations.

Preoperative ultrasound suggested a benign lesion, such as lipoma, reflecting the well-recognized diagnostic limitations of imaging in AMFB [2], [7]. This reinforces the critical role of histopathological examination in establishing the final diagnosis.

Histologically, the tumor demonstrated classic features of AMFB, including well-defined borders, spindle and oval stromal cells, and prominent thin-walled vascular structures, without atypia or mitotic activity [1], [10]. The immunohistochemical profile (desmin+, ER+, PR+, CD34−, S100−) further confirmed the diagnosis and was entirely consistent with previously reported patterns [3], [6].

Of note, recent molecular studies have identified recurrent MTG1-CYP2E1 gene fusions as a defining molecular feature of AMFB, detectable by RNA sequencing or RT-PCR [15]. While this finding was not applied in the present case, molecular testing may prove valuable in diagnostically challenging cases where histopathological and immunohistochemical features alone are insufficient to distinguish AMFB from other vulvovaginal mesenchymal tumors.

Complete surgical excision was achieved, in accordance with current recommendations. Importantly, the use of an interlabial sulcus approach allowed not only oncological radicality but also optimal aesthetic reconstruction, which is particularly relevant in young patients.

The postoperative course was complicated by hematoma and edema, both of which resolved with conservative management, consistent with previously reported postoperative outcomes [6].

No recurrence had been observed at the time of writing, supporting the well-established favorable prognosis of AMFB following complete excision [2], [6].

Conclusion

Complete local excision with narrow margins is curative in the vast majority of vulvar AMFB cases; radical surgery is reserved for cases suspicious for malignancy or an alternative diagnosis such as aggressive angiomyxoma. In large tumors or significant anatomical distortion, individualized surgical planning with reconstructive techniques is essential to achieve both oncological safety and satisfactory cosmetic outcomes, particularly in young patients.

Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation, with intraoperative frozen section reserved for uncertain cases. Postoperative complications are uncommon and typically managed conservatively, and there is no evidence supporting adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Despite its excellent prognosis, long-term follow-up remains advisable given rare reports of recurrence and malignant transformation.
