Effects of sympathomimetic agents on heart rate variability during general anesthesia in Thoroughbred horses
Authors: Hajime OHMURA, Pedro BOSCAN, James H. JONES, Eugene P. STEFFEY
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHeart rate variability (HRV) is widely used as a noninvasive index of autonomic nervous system activity. The objective of this study was to determine whether the administration of sympathomimetic agents alters HRV indices during general anesthesia in Thoroughbred horses. Five healthy Thoroughbred horses were anesthetized with sevoflurane in oxygen. During 2–5 hr of general anesthesia, five treatments were administered to each horse in a randomized order. Two doses (µg·kg−1·min−1) of epinephrine (0.1 and 0.5), norepinephrine (0.5 and 1), dobutamine (1 and 5), dopamine (5 and 15), and saline (as a control) were infused for 7 min each. Electrocardiograms were recorded to assess HRV, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) was measured to evaluate hemodynamic responses. Data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. During general anesthesia, epinephrine at 0.5 µg·kg−1·min−1 significantly increased low-frequency (LF) power, the LF/high-frequency (HF) ratio, and MAP. Norepinephrine at both doses significantly decreased heart rate and increased HF power and MAP. Dobutamine at 5 µg·kg−1·min−1 significantly increased heart rate and MAP, without changing HRV indices, whereas dopamine did not induce significant changes in heart rate, MAP, or HRV indices. In conclusion, administration of sympathomimetic agents during general anesthesia induces distinct patterns of autonomic modulation in horses. Increases in LF power and the LF/HF ratio may reflect enhanced sympathetic modulation; however, these indices should be interpreted in the context of baroreflex-mediated autonomic regulation. Understanding the differential autonomic effects of sympathomimetic agents may aid in optimizing cardiovascular support during equine anesthesia.

Heart rate variability (HRV) has been widely used as a noninvasive index of autonomic nervous system activity [2, 15]. Power spectral analysis of HRV separates the signal into distinct frequency components, of which the high-frequency (HF) component is primarily associated with parasympathetic modulation, whereas the low-frequency (LF) component reflects a combination of sympathetic and parasympathetic influences as well as baroreflex-mediated regulation [10, 14].

Traditionally, the LF/HF ratio has been used as an index of cardiac sympatho-vagal balance. However, more recent studies have emphasized that LF power and the LF/HF ratio should be interpreted with caution, as they do not represent direct or exclusive measures of sympathetic activity and are influenced by multiple physiological mechanisms, including baroreflex function [3, 17, 19].

General anesthesia induces a reversible state of unconsciousness, amnesia, analgesia, and skeletal muscle relaxation and is known to suppress cardiovascular function and autonomic nervous activity. During anesthesia, hypotension frequently occurs, and sympathomimetic agents are commonly administered to support arterial blood pressure. These agents activate adrenergic receptors and may alter heart rate, vascular tone, and autonomic modulation. Previous studies have demonstrated that administration of adrenergic agents does not necessarily result in an increase in the LF power of HRV and that such responses may be confounded by baroreflex-mediated autonomic adjustments [1, 24].

In horses, autonomic regulation differs from that in other species, with a particularly strong parasympathetic tone at rest and distinct HRV characteristics [6, 8]. Despite the clinical relevance of sympathomimetic agents during equine anesthesia, there are limited data describing their effects on autonomic nervous activity, as assessed by HRV.

We hypothesized that administration of sympathomimetic agents during general anesthesia would be associated with alterations in HRV indices consistent with enhanced sympathetic modulation and baroreflex-mediated autonomic responses in Thoroughbred horses. The objective of this study was to evaluate changes in heart rate, LF power, HF power, and the LF/HF ratio following administration of epinephrine, norepinephrine, dobutamine, and dopamine during sevoflurane anesthesia.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Five healthy unmedicated university-owned horses (2 females and 3 geldings) that ranged in age from 6 to 11 (8.8 ± 1.9 years; mean ± SD) and weighed 533 ± 31 kg were studied. The University of California, Davis, Animal Use and Care Committee approved the study (#10893).

General anesthesia

General anesthesia was induced in the otherwise unmedicated horses with only sevoflurane in O2 in the morning following an 8–10 hr fast; water was always available to the horses ad libitum during this interval. Techniques of inhalation anesthetic induction (via face mask) and maintenance (via orotracheal intubation) were similar to previously reported equine studies from this laboratory [7, 20,21,22]. In brief, following inhalation anesthetic induction, an orotracheal tube (30 mm, outer diameter) was inserted, and the tracheal tube was connected to a standard large-animal anesthetic circle rebreathing circuit. The circuit’s 30-l rebreathing bag was housed within a sealed metal drum. This arrangement permitted both spontaneous and controlled intermittent positive-pressure ventilation (IPPV). When study conditions required, IPPV was instituted and maintained (both without the use of neuromuscular blocking drugs) by pressurizing the bag-in-barrel arrangement via a modified Bird® ventilator using a ventilator driving pressure of 90–100 psi O2 [20]. Peak inspiratory pressure during IPPV was set to 20–22 cmH2O as measured by a manometer sensing pressure in the proximal portion of the inspiratory limb of the breathing circuit. All horses were positioned in left-lateral recumbency during anesthetic induction and maintenance. During the first 40–45 min of anesthesia, the horses were instrumented for the study, and during this period, they breathed spontaneously with the end-tidal anesthetic concentration maintained at about 3.4% sevoflurane.

Systolic and diastolic arterial blood pressures were recorded from a 20-gauge, 44-mm-long catheter inserted aseptically and positioned percutaneously in the right facial artery. The catheter was connected to a strain-gauge transducer, and the pressure data were recorded with data acquisition software (Windaq® Pro+, Dataq Instruments, Akron, OH, USA). The transducer and software were calibrated at the start and end of each study day with an Hg-manometer.

The urinary bladder was aseptically catheterized during the instrumentation period and allowed to drain throughout the study period so as to minimize urinary bladder volume changes and the possibility for any potential associated hemodynamic-modifying sensation. Insensible and urinary fluid losses during anesthesia were replaced with Lactated Ringer’s solution (2–3 ml·kg−1·hr−1) via a 14-gauge, 13-cm-long catheter placed aseptically and percutaneously into the right external jugular vein following anesthetic induction.

At approximately 40–45 min from the horse’s first breath of sevoflurane and the completion of instrumentation, IPPV was initiated with brief mild hyperventilation, and the inspired sevoflurane concentration was reduced to establish by 1-hr of anesthesia time an end-tidal sevoflurane concentration of 3.4% (1.2 MAC). During controlled IPPV, the respiratory frequency was adjusted to maintain PaCO2 45 ± 5 mmHg.

Administered sympathomimetic agents

During periods of 2–5 hr of general anesthesia (taken from the time of the first breath of sevoflurane), 5 randomly assigned (a priori) treatments were given to each horse. Two doses of epinephrine (EPI 0.1 and 0.5 µg·kg−1·min−1), norepinephrine (NOR 0.5 and 1 µg·kg−1·min−1), dopamine (DOP 5 and 15 µg·kg−1·min−1), dobutamine (DOB 1 and 5 µg·kg−1·min−1), and saline (as a control) were infused for 7 min. The infusions were given at least 10 min apart to allow a return to baseline.

Electrocardiograms (ECGs) were recorded by base-apex lead using a Holter-type electrocardiograph to identify the effect of infusion on autonomic function for all the drugs.

Analyzing HR variability

The recorded ECGs were analyzed with an ECG processor analyzing system (Softron Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) that has been previously described [8]. The program first detected R-waves and calculated the R-R interval tachogram as the raw HR variability in sequence order. Noise that the software identified as R waves was eliminated manually by use of visual inspection and examination of values that were outside the interval of 75% to 125% of the mean value. A spline curve was fit to the tachogram, from which data sets of 512 points were resampled at 200 msec intervals with the sampling window advanced 20 sec for each data set. These values were selected as a compromise to balance the need for a large time series for accuracy versus the need for reasonably short recording periods. We applied each set of data to a Hamming window and a fast Fourier transform to obtain the power spectrum of the fluctuation. We set the LF power at 0.01–0.07 Hz and HF power at 0.07–0.6 Hz as previously determined for the horse by Kuwahara et al. [8]. Heart rate, LF power, HF power, and LF/HF ratio were obtained from each 5 min of ECG recordings.

Data analysis

Values are expressed as the mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons for HR, HRV indices, and MAP were made with repeated-measures ANOVA and post-hoc pairwise comparisons with the Holm-Sidak multiple-comparison test. Statistical analyses were performed using the SigmaStat 3.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Discussion

We conducted this study to determine whether administration of different sympathomimetic agents alters the LF power component of HRV during general anesthesia in Thoroughbred horses. Epinephrine administration significantly increased LF power, the LF/HF ratio, and mean arterial pressure (MAP). These findings suggest enhanced sympathetic modulation of cardiac autonomic activity, as reflected by the HRV indices. In contrast, norepinephrine administration significantly increased high-frequency (HF) power and MAP, while decreasing heart rate. The increase in MAP induced by norepinephrine likely activated the baroreflex arc, resulting in enhanced parasympathetic nervous activity. Although some sympathomimetic agents did not increase LF power, HRV proved to be a useful index for evaluating autonomic nervous system activity. We conclude that enhancement of sympathetic nervous activity may be associated with increases in LF power and the LF/HF ratio in horses, as has been reported in other species.

Horses may exhibit a pattern of HRV regulation that differs from that observed in other species. Studies using autonomic blockade with atropine, propranolol, or both have shown that atropine alone increases heart rate while decreasing both HF and LF power, whereas propranolol alone increases LF power without altering heart rate. Furthermore, atropine administration following propranolol results in dose-dependent decreases in HF and LF power, with heart rate increasing at higher doses [8, 13]. These responses have been interpreted as indicating that horses possess a particularly strong parasympathetic tone at rest, which is consistent with the frequent occurrence of benign arrhythmias, such as second-degree atrioventricular block, in clinically normal resting horses. Notably, propranolol administration has been reported to increase LF power, contrary to what would be expected with sympathetic blockade, and to do so without affecting heart rate [8]. This finding suggests that sympathetic tone plays a limited role in determining resting heart rate in horses and raises questions regarding the extent to which LF power reflects sympathetic modulation in this species under resting conditions. In support of this concept, Hamlin et al. demonstrated that parasympathetic activity alone was primarily responsible for regulating heart rate up to 110 beats·min−1 in horses, based on heart rate responses to experimentally induced changes in systemic arterial pressure under conditions of selective autonomic blockade [6].

During general anesthesia with saline infusion as a control, HR was 35.2 ± 1.4 beats·min−1, LF power was 8 ± 5 msec2, and HF power was 8 ± 2 msec2. Both LF and HF power appeared to be markedly reduced, although HR was not substantially different from that observed under resting conditions. If autonomic nervous system activity had been completely blocked during general anesthesia, HR would have been expected to increase. This expectation is based on the fact that, in Thoroughbred horses, intrinsic HR—determined following combined administration of atropine and propranolol—is known to be at least twofold higher than resting HR, in contrast to other species [11]. Therefore, autonomic nervous system activity was not completely suppressed during general anesthesia in the present study. These observations may be specific to horses, as the difference between intrinsic and resting heart rates is less pronounced in other species.

To minimize potential time-dependent effects during prolonged anesthesia, treatments were administered in a randomized order over the 2–5 hr anesthetic period. Blood pressure and HR remained stable throughout anesthesia, as indicated by the low standard deviation observed in the saline control, suggesting that sevoflurane anesthesia provided a physiologically stable cardiovascular condition. This study was conducted in healthy horses; therefore, in clinical surgical cases, the autonomic nervous system may already be altered. In addition, under conditions involving highly invasive stimuli, such as abdominal surgery, autonomic responses may differ from those observed in the present study. Consequently, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution when extrapolated to clinical cases involving substantial surgical stress.

The responses of HR, HRV, and MAP to administration of sympathomimetic agents differed among the agents examined. Although these responses depend on the dose and the sensitivity of adrenergic receptors to each agent, it is noteworthy that agents within the same pharmacological class induced distinct cardiovascular and autonomic responses. Epinephrine, which exerts the strongest effects on adrenergic receptors among endogenous catecholamines, has the potential to increase HR through direct activation of myocardial and pacemaker cells.

In the present study, administration of epinephrine was associated with a marked increase in LF power and the LF/HF ratio, together with an increase in mean arterial pressure. However, HR remained unchanged, giving the impression that epinephrine stimulated either cardiac contractility or peripheral vascular resistance to increase MAP. Previous studies have demonstrated that HRV can reflect drug-induced modulation of autonomic nervous activity even in the absence of changes in HR [12, 13]. Therefore, the observed changes in HRV likely reflect the cardiac autonomic effects of epinephrine. The dose of epinephrine used in this study may have been sufficient to elicit a positive inotropic effect without inducing a chronotropic response.

These findings suggest enhanced sympathetic modulation during epinephrine infusion. However, based on recent evidence indicating that LF-related indices are influenced by multiple regulatory mechanisms, including baroreflex activity, these increases should not be interpreted as a direct quantitative measure of sympathetic tone but rather as a shift toward sympathetic predominance in autonomic modulation [3, 19].

Norepinephrine is a potent adrenergic agent with cardiovascular effects similar to those of epinephrine, although its peripheral vascular actions differ. In the present study, norepinephrine administration resulted in a significant increase in HF power and mean arterial blood pressure, together with a decrease in heart rate. The marked elevation in arterial pressure induced by norepinephrine is attributable to its strong vasoconstrictive effect and relatively limited vasodilatory action compared with epinephrine.

These cardiovascular responses are consistent with baroreflex-mediated enhancement of parasympathetic activity secondary to increased arterial pressure. Activation of the baroreflex arc likely accounted for the observed increase in HF power and concomitant reduction in heart rate. In contrast to epinephrine, which increased LF-related indices without altering heart rate, norepinephrine elicited a pattern of HRV changes dominated by parasympathetic modulation. Such findings support recent interpretations that alterations in HRV during pharmacological interventions frequently reflect reflex-mediated autonomic adjustments rather than direct drug effects on cardiac autonomic outflow [17].

Dopamine is an endogenous biogenic amine that exerts diverse cardiovascular effects depending on the dose and receptor activation. Previous studies have shown that dopamine reduces arterial blood pressure, total peripheral vascular resistance, and renal vascular resistance in anesthetized dogs, while increasing cardiac output and renal blood flow. These effects are attributed to activation of dopamine-responsive receptors [18]. In the present study, administration of dopamine at doses of 5 and 15 µg·kg−1·min−1 did not result in significant changes in HR, HRV indices, or MAP during general anesthesia. These findings suggest that, under anesthetic conditions and the dose range used, dopamine exerted minimal influence on cardiac autonomic modulation in horses. This was different from what is observed in other species, such as dogs.

Dobutamine is a synthetic sympathomimetic agent known to elicit positive inotropic and chronotropic effects on the myocardium [25]. Previous studies have reported that dobutamine administered at 15 µg·kg−1·min−1 induces a chronotropic response in halothane-anesthetized horses [9]. In the present study, HR was significantly increased following dobutamine administration, despite the use of 5 µg·kg−1·min−1 as the highest dose. The increase in MAP observed at this dose was likely secondary to both inotropic and chronotropic effects. However, despite the elevation in arterial pressure, HF power did not increase, indicating that baroreflex-mediated parasympathetic activation was not apparent under these conditions. On the contrary, the lower dose of dobutamine at 1 µg·kg−1·min−1 did not have significant effects on any parameter recorded. This is interesting because this lower dobutamine dose is within the clinically used dose range described to improve MAP in anesthetized horses [4, 5]. It highlights the variable effect of low dobutamine doses on cardiac inotropic and chronotropic effects as previously reported [16, 23, 25].

Premedication and sedative agents are known to influence autonomic nervous activity during general anesthesia. For example, continuous rate infusion of dobutamine has been reported not to counteract alterations in ventricular contraction and function induced by romifidine sedation [23]. To avoid such confounding effects, general anesthesia was induced in the present study in otherwise unmedicated horses using sevoflurane alone. This experimental design allowed for clearer evaluation of the autonomic effects of sympathomimetic agents during anesthesia.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that sympathomimetic agents induce distinct patterns of autonomic modulation during general anesthesia in horses. Although increases in LF power and the LF/HF ratio were observed following epinephrine administration, interpretation of these indices requires careful consideration of baroreflex influences and species-specific autonomic characteristics. HRV analysis can be a useful tool to assess autonomic modulation during anesthesia; however, its indices should be interpreted within the context of contemporary understanding of autonomic physiology.
