A Bigger Big Picture: Insights on Environmental Chemicals and Dyslipidemia through Combined “Omic” Analyses
Authors: Charles Schmidt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Study’s stages proceed from exposome–health associations, to integrated analyses, then biological interpretation and clinical validation.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) kills millions of people globally every year.1 Environmental pollutants are increasingly recognized as significant risk factors for these conditions,2,3 but the underlying biological mechanisms are poorly understood.4 New research reported in Environmental Health Perspectives may provide needed insights.5

The findings come from the ongoing Biomarkers of Air Pollutant Exposure (BAPE) study, conducted by scientists at the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in Beijing.6 The overall BAPE cohort comprises 76 healthy adults 60–69 years of age living in Jinan, a large city in Shandong Province. The study aims to identify and prioritize airborne environmental pollutants that may pose CVD risks, as well as to uncover mechanisms underlying pollutant responses in people in this age group.

The new research,5 depicted here, is part of the larger China BAPE study, which was conceived to systematically explore associations between exposures to air pollutants and adverse health outcomes. Image: Ding et al. (2024).5

For this study, the investigators focused on connections between exposures to 102 environmental pollutants and dyslipidemia. The chemicals—and biomarkers of exposure to them—were chosen based on previous associations with air pollution exposure. Dyslipidemia, a metabolic disorder characterized by abnormal levels of fats in the blood,7 is a risk factor for heart attack and stroke.8 The researchers wanted to know how exposures to environmental chemicals might relate to lipid homeostasis.

To find out, they devised an innovative approach for combining exposome research methods with additional -omic analyses of metabolites, blood lipids, and other biomolecules in the body. The exposome is a term that refers to all the environmental exposures a person experiences over a lifetime.9 With this novel assessment, the team was attempting to achieve a “comprehensive understanding of how environmental contaminants influence cardiovascular health at a biomolecular level,” says coauthor Song Tang, a BAPE principal investigator and director of the Chinese CDC’s Department of Environmental Toxicology.

Over the course of 5 months in 2018 and early 2019, Tang and his colleagues met five times with BAPE participants to collect serum, whole blood, and urine samples. Those samples were screened for chemicals of interest spanning multiple chemical classes such as per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), organophosphate esters (OPEs), and metals, among others. The team also screened for dyslipidemia markers, including but not limited to cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and apolipoproteins. Metabolomic, transcriptomic, and lipidomic analyses were performed on the samples, and the examination of all these data provided what Tang calls a “panoramic view” of the associations between the chemicals and dyslipidemia markers.

Among other findings, the researchers observed associations between exposures to several chemicals—including phthalates, triphenyl phosphate, and dibutyl phosphate (DBP)—and increased levels of lipid markers. DBP, an industrial chemical10 that is also a metabolite of the plasticizer tributyl phosphate,11 was strongly associated with elevations in LDL, which can lead to a buildup of cholesterol deposits in the arteries.12 The relationship between DBP and dyslipidemia “is particularly significant and novel in the scientific literature,” Tang says. Strategies are needed to limit environmental exposures, including those in the workplace, he says.

According to Tang, the chemicals noted above were associated with changes in two lipid classes in particular. The first, sphingolipids, participate in tissue development, cell-to-cell interactions, and many other aspects of cell biology.13 Sphingolipids are also implicated in cancer, as well as metabolic and neurodegenerative diseases.14 The second group, sterol lipids, includes cholesterol—important in the structure and function of cell membranes, among other roles.15 Both of these lipid classes, Tang says, “are robust and specific biomarkers for CVD.”

Donghai Liang, an associate professor of environmental health and epidemiology at the Emory University Rollins School of Public Health, says the findings showcase an approach for solving challenging questions about how molecules in the body change in response to many simultaneous chemical exposures. “This investigation was pioneering in that it was the first exposome-wide association study focusing specifically on dyslipidemia,” says Liang, who was not involved in the study. All that's needed now, he says, are large follow-up studies to “validate the findings in different independent research cohorts and further test these hypothesized molecular signatures using in vivo and in vitro models.”

Gary Miller, a professor of environmental health sciences at the Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health, who also was not involved in the study, agrees. “The research identified associations with lipid outcomes and various chemicals, and it provides many avenues for future study,” he says. “Moreover, the authors demonstrated that incorporating chemical profiling—meaning exposomics—into the multi-omic framework is feasible and valuable.”

Tang cites several unknowns the team plans to address, including whether the results are generalizable to different populations around the world. He and his colleagues also plan to analyze how the chemicals associated with dyslipidemia in the current study interact with other CVD risk factors, such as lifestyle, dietary patterns, and genetic susceptibility—a juxtaposition they believe may help to explain the emerging epidemic5 of chronic CVD. In addition, they aim to validate the cardiotoxicity of certain environmental contaminants (e.g., OPEs) using a zebrafish model.

“There’s a critical need to validate if sterol lipids, sphingolipids, and other molecules [that we studied] can serve as sensitive and specific biomarkers of lipid homeostasis disruption,” says Tang. Establishing the reliability of such biomarkers is crucial, he continues, to help us detect and intervene in CVD during its early stages.
