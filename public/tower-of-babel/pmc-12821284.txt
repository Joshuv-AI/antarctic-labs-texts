How does the nasolabial change in patients with unilateral cleft lip and/or palate following primary repair using a modified rotation-advancement technique: a 5-year anthropometry study
Authors: Chao Yang, Yulang Xu, Zhiqiang Liu, Bing Shi, Ni Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective study analyzed nasolabial changes in 69 patients who underwent primary cleft repair using a modified rotation-advancement technique. Photogrammetric measurements were taken preoperatively (T0), immediately postoperatively (T1), one year postoperatively (T2), and five years postoperatively (T3). The ratio of labial width decreased from T2 to T3. The medial-oblique labial height on the affected side showed growth from T1 to T2 but slowed by T3. The ratio of lateral-oblique labial height remained stable and the crista philtra height symmetry improved. However, the ratios of nasal sill, heminasal, and nostril width decreased c onsistently from T1 to T3. The nostril height grew near-symmetrically from T1 to T2, with acceleration thereafter, yet still smaller than the unaffected side at T3. The nostril area on the affected side grew more slowly. The nasal tip deviation angle changed minimally at T2 but showed medial displacement by T3. The columellar angle remained stable, while the affected columellar height showed delayed growth and remained shorter than the unaffected side at T3. There were no significant differences in nasolabial growth between patients with or without cleft palate/alveolar, and complete cleft lips displayed similar growth patterns to those of incomplete forms. In conclusion, to achieve symmetry of the nasal sill and nostril, an overcorrection of the alar base and alar contour on the cleft side was necessary during primary repair. Introduction

Achieving optimal nasolabial symmetry is the ultimate goal of unilateral cleft lip primary repair; however, the prevalence of secondary nasolabial deformities remains significant. Over the years, numerous methods for treating cleft lip have been researched and developed. Each method has its own advantages and limitations, making it difficult to determine a single best approach [1, 2]. Take Millard’s rotation-advancement (RA) technique for example, which has widely adopted for cleft lip repair on a global scale [3]. It places the scar along the philtral column while preserving the philtral dimple and Cupid’s bow [4]; however, it compromises the transverse width of the lateral lip in order to enhance vertical lip height, resulting in a reduced nostril size [5]. Despite the proposal of numerous modifications, there remained a significant amount of dissatisfaction [6].

In light of this challenge, our team has developed a modified rotation-advancement technique (MRAT) to effectively achieve optimal nasolabial symmetry. This technique has been proved to improve the symmetrical leveling of the Cupid's bow and significantly aided in correcting lip height without compromising lip width. Although a short-term study has highlighted the advantages, surgical outcomes in the nasolabial region would be influenced by fourth-dimension changes [7, 8]. Therefore, a long-term study can better evaluate this method, while understanding the temporal changes of different types of cleft lip and palate can provide a basis for further improving surgical techniques and implementing personalized surgery.

To address these, a retrospective investigation was conducted to examine the growth and remodeling pattern in the nasolabial region of patients who underwent cleft lip repair using a modified rotation-advancement technique. Additionally, different types of clefts were evaluated to understand their impact on postoperative nasolabial morphology.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from West China Stomatology Hospital of Sichuan University (WCHSIRB-D-2023–287). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants enrolled in this study.

Subjects and image acquisition

This study involved the analysis of front and basal view measurements from digital photographs for individuals who underwent modified rotation-advancement repair between September 2015 and December 2017. In this technique, a C-flap (C representing the cleft side) is created to form the nasal sill without extending the incision around the alar base on the cleft side. Additionally, the back-cut incision ends at the bisecting line of Cupid's bow angle, which represents the midline in cleft lip patients. The C-flap is then rotated laterally inside the nose to lengthen the columella, while a superiorly based advanced flap on the cleft side is utilized to increase labial height on the cleft side [9] (Fig. 1). The MRAT and RAT diverge significantly in their utilization of the C-flap. In the RAT procedure, the C-flap has a singular role in constructing the nasal sill, with its design deliberately avoiding an incision around the alar base. Conversely, the MRAT technique employs the C-flap for a dual purpose. It is rotated medially to form the nasal sill, while a portion is specifically sutured to cleft side of the columella, thereby actively contributing to its lengthening and structural support.

The original cohort consisted of 82 patients with unilateral cleft lip (complete or incomplete), with or without cleft palate. However, only 69 consecutive patients met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 2). Standard digital photographs were shot preoperatively (T0), immediate-postoperatively (T1), one-year postoperatively (T2) and five-year postoperatively (T3). The inclusion criteria included: non-syndromic unilateral cleft lip patients with high-resolution quality photographs, no pre-surgical nasoalveolar molding (NAM), no nasolabial adhesion, no alveolar gingivoperiosteoplasty, no revisions in the lip and nose area during follow-ups, and a minimum follow-up period of five years. Microform cleft lips without obvious cracks, such as only skin depressions and red lip incisions, were excluded. Patients with corresponding alveolar clefts or depressions on the cleft side are diagnosed with alveolar cleft.

Anthropometric measurements

The nasolabial anatomical landmarks were defined according to Farkas principle [10, 11]. Reference planes and measurements (including 11 linear, 2 angular, and 1 surface area parameter; Figs. 3 and 4) were standardized based on previous nasolabial photogrammetric studies using Adobe Photoshop CS6 software ([7, 8, 12]). All nasal measurements were obtained from basal views, except for nasal base angles, which were obtained from the patients' frontal views, while all labial measurements were obtained from frontal views of the patients. All photographs were cropped and rotated to the horizontal plane using the intercanthal line as a reference line. Photogrammetric analysis was performed on each of the 552 images to gain objective measures. To control inter-individual differences and focus on morphological biases, anthropometric distances were converted into ratios of cleft to noncleft sides for further analysis. All photogrammetric measurements were collected in duplicate with a one-week interval between each measurement session, and the mean value was used for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

Means of the symmetry and relapse of each variable from T0 to T1 were objectively assessed using the paired t-test. Independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to compare the severity of preoperative deformities between different groups at T0. Repeated-measures ANOVA were employed to assess alterations in anthropometric measures from T1 to T2 and from T2 to T3 and to compare the growth changes between the two time periods. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

The mean age at the time of surgery was 163 days (n = 82, SD = 51.7); while the average age at subsequent follow-up evaluation was 1.14 (n = 69, SD = 0.27) and 5.41 years old (n = 69, SD = 1.62), respectively. The mean intervals between surgery and follow-up anthropometric measurements were 0.69 year, 4.96 years, respectively (Table 1).

Immediate postoperative changes

The medial-oblique labial height was significantly improved (P < 0.001), with the ratio increased from 0.60 before surgery to 0.95 after surgery. The labial width and lateral-oblique labial height were both preserved. There was an enhancement in crista philtri symmetry (P < 0.001), resulted from the crista philtri height ratio improved from 0.87 to 0.95. There were also substantial improvements in the nasal sill width and heminasal width. Specifically, the nasal sill width ratio decreased from 2.02 to 1.21 by 0.81, remaining 21% greater than the noncleft side, while the heminasal width ratio significantly reduced from 1.57 to 1.05, representing mere 5% wider than the cleft side. At T0, nasal tip deviation towards the noncleft side was observed about 10.11°; however its severity decreased to 5.10° at T1. Additionally, significant corrections were achieved for nostril width (from 1.86 to 1.26), height (from 0.67 to 0.83) and area (from 2.10 to 1.16) on the cleft side (P < 0.001), accompanied by increased columellar angle (from 62.18° to 77.51°) and elevated columellar height (from 0.62 to 0.84) (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

The postoperative photogrammetric analysis of three time points

The medial-oblique labial height ratio increased 9% from T1 to T2, resulting in an elongation of medial-oblique labial height by 4% compared to the noncleft side at T2, and then reverted back to 5% shorter at T3 (Table 3). It suggested during the first follow-up period, there was observed catch-up growth on the cleft side; however, in contrast, during the second follow-up period, growth lagged on the cleft side. Regarding the lateral-oblique height of the repaired side, it consistently exhibited 10% shorter at T1 and T2 and remained 8% shorter at T3, which suggested there were no significant differences in growth rates between the two sides (Table 3).

At first, the crista philtri height on the cleft side was 5% shorter than the noncleft side; at T2, it decreased to 3%; and at T3, it further reduced to 2% (Table 3). In both following-up periods, there was a noticeable trend of catch-up growth observed in the cleft side from T1-T2, which is consistent with the observation regarding labial width.

The nasal sill width was 21% wider on the cleft side immediately after surgery, and this difference remained statistically significant with a 19% wider at T2 and a 13% wider at T3 (Table 3). These findings suggested that there was a growth delay in nasal sill width on the cleft side in the two following-ups. The heminasal width on the cleft side was initially 5% wider than that of the noncleft side, decreasing to 4% at T2, and further reducing to 2% at T3 respectively (Table 3), demonstrating an asymptotical symmetry in alare development on the cleft side.

The nostril width on the cleft side exhibited a mean difference of 26%, 25% and 13% at T1, T2, T3, respectively, indicating there was no notable disparity in growth rates between the cleft and noncleft sides in the first follow-up, but subsequently, there was a significantly lower growth rate observed on the cleft side in the second follow-up. Nostril height on the cleft side was initially 17% lower compared to that of the noncleft side, which further deteriorated to 21% at T2 and improved to 13% lower at T3. There was no significant difference in the growth rates between both sides in the first follow-up, whereas the nostril height tended to grow more on the cleft side in the second follow-up. Nostril area on the cleft side gradually enlarged with growth, as it was 16% larger on the cleft side at T1, 12% larger at T2, and ultimately 25% larger at T3 (Table 3).

Nasal tip deviation to the noncleft side was observed about 5.10°at T1, with no significant decrease in severity from midline, to around 5.54°at T2, and a minor residual nasal deviation of 3.80° at T3 (Table 3).

The columellar angle was initially corrected to about 77.51°at baseline, which decrease slightly to 75.82° at T2 and then increased marginally to 76.03°. In both follow-ups, no significant difference was presented between the cleft and noncleft side. However, there was a noticeable discrepancy in columellar height between cleft side and noncleft side: at T1, the cleft side exhibited a shorter height by 16%, which remained statistically significantly shorter by 39% at T2 and by 32% at T3. It suggested a significant growth lag in the columella on the cleft side in the first follow-up followed by a catch-up growth in the second follow-up (Table 3).

The growth differences among various types of clefts

The groups were categorized as I to III, representing varying degrees of asymmetrical severity. Specifically, group I (n = 27 patients) consisted of individuals with unilateral cleft lip; group II (n = 19 patients) included those with unilateral cleft lip and alveolus; group III (n = 23 patients) comprised individuals with unilateral cleft lip and palate. Notably, almost all the nasolabial region deformities were more pronounced in patients with cleft lip and palate in comparison with those with cleft lip only or cleft lip and alveolus, except for labial width and heminasal width (Table 4).

Comparison of pre-operative nasolabial deformities between complete and incomplete clefts

From a historical perspective, the reconstruction of complete cleft lip has traditionally been considered more challenging to achieve optimal correction, with noticeable abnormalities in the morphology of the nasolabial region. However, contrary to this hypothesis, the data did not support such findings. It was only observed a significant difference in the lateral-oblique labial height ratio between patients with complete cleft lip and those with incomplete forms at T2 (with a 7% difference) and nasal tip deviation angle at T1&T3 (with a 1.77 and 1.26 difference, respectively), while none of the other measurements exhibited any significant differences (Table 7). During the first follow-up, incomplete forms demonstrated rapid growth. However, during the second follow-up, complete forms exhibited a faster growth rate. Ultimately, there were no significant disparities identified in nasolabial dimensions.

Discussion

The cleft nasolabial deformity in patients with unilateral cleft lip with or without cleft palate (UCL ± P) is characterized by lateral and superior drift of crista philtri, malpositioned alar rim, asymmetrical alar base, inadequate support to the nasal tip, and deviation of the columella towards the cleft side. It was previously founded by Mulliken et al. in 2012 that the alar base of the cleft side drifted laterally, while the labial height growth lagged and transverse labial width grew more prominently on the cleft side in UCL ± P patients. Then, in 2016, Knight et al. further confirmed that by demonstrating a gradual lateral drift of the alar base. Moreover, it was observed that the labial height on the cleft side tended to grow slower during early childhood but proportionately lengthened compared to the noncleft side in late childhood. And labial width on the cleft side would eventually approach that of the noncleft side. Furthermore, during growth and remodeling process, the initial inclination of the philtrum to the cleft side gradually rotated toward the midline [7, 8], potentially leading to a corrected crista philtri on the cleft side [1, 13]. Lacking of comprehensive measurements regarding the relative positioning of the crista philtra, in order to accurately depict its precise location, we illustrated it by drawing a perpendicular line from the crista philtri to the inner canthus line. The obtained data indeed confirmed that MRAT could reestablish the symmetry of the crista philtri height after primary repair, with an increase of 0.08 from T0-T1. Although significant changes in the crista philtri height ratio during T1-T2 due to an increase in the labial width on the cleft side were observed, the ratio remained stable from T2-T3 (Table 3) (Fig. 5).

Regarding the relative insufficiency of the lateral-oblique labial height on the cleft side (10% shorter), compared to previously observed by Mulliken and Knight (3.59% and 3.54% shorter, respectively) [7, 8], it is worth noting that in order to preserve the integrity of alar base during primary repair, we refrained from making an incision here to augment the lateral-oblique labial height. Instead, we employed a superiorly based advanced flap on the cleft side which was laterally rotated to the interior of the nose to form the nasal sill. Additionally, a portion of this flap was sutured to the columella on the cleft side to increase support and elevate columella height [9] (increasing by 22%).

A more comprehensive quantitative evaluation of the nose, compared to previous studies, was employed in this study. This included measurements of structural aspects such as area and volume of the nose, as well as measurements of linear, angular, and proportional dimensions within and between nasal subunits (alare, tip, columella) [1, 14, 15]. Various variables (e.g., nasal tip deviation angle, columellar angle, columellar height, heminasal width and nasal sill width) had been defined as relevant in assessing nasal growth in UCL ± P patients [9, 15, 16].

It had been previously revealed that in patients with UCL ± P, both sides of the alar base and nose base width increased with growth which resulted manifested wider, larger, or flatter noses [17, 18]. However, Tse et al., focusing on the cleft nasal deformity in sagittal direction, reported that the cleft alar base maintained a normal position but was retruded relative to normal control, whereas the non-cleft alar base displayed lateral drift [19]. It was also observed by us that the nasal sill width, the heminasal width, and the nostril width on the cleft side gradually lagged compared to the normal side from T1-T3 (Mean ratio cleft/non-cleft declined from about 20%−10%), albeit slightly less in the heminasal width (5%). Therefore, as an attempt to compensate for lateral drift of alar base on non-cleft side, an 20% overcorrection in alar base and alar contour was deemed necessary during primary repair to obtain the symmetry of the nasal sill and nostril [8, 20].

Concurrently, a reduction in nasal tip deviation angle was observed, signifying the progressive rotation of the nasal tip towards the midline. Considering that the nasal tip is originally oriented to the noncleft side, this suggests a substantially lower rate of columellar growth on the cleft side during the follow-up periods.

Despite one of the primary goals of MRAT being to improve the columellar height, it was indeed extended significantly from T0-T1 (an increasing of 0.22). However, it was then observed a notable decrease from 0.84 at T1 to 0.61 at T2, suggesting growth lags in the columellar height on the cleft side. Nevertheless, compensatory growth in the cleft side columella from 0.61 at T2 to 0.68 at T3 indicated that this parameter requires longer follow-up to determine its final effect. It is noteworthy that extending the columellar height as much as possible during primary repair might facilitate easier correction through intermediate cheilorhinoplasty due to sufficient soft tissue volume.

Traditionally, it has been believed that secondary nasolabial deformities are correlated with the severity of the primary cleft deformity [18, 20–24]. Contrarily, the results were counterintuitive yet reasonable as they indicated that patients with a more severe level of UCL ± P condition did not exhibit a substantial discrepancy when compared to those in the cleft lip only group. Moreover, there were minimal significant differences observed in growth patterns between the complete clefts group and incomplete clefts group, suggesting that the initial degree of severity may have limited or no effect on long-term nasolabial growth outcomes. Nevertheless, further investigation over an extended follow-up period such as ten years is necessary to come to a definitive conclusion.

The limitation of this study is that the follow-up period was 5 years in average, which might not sufficiently capture growth changes, especially during adolescence when rapid growth spurts occur. To address this constraint, it is crucial to conduct a longer-term follow-up until adulthood. Although conventional two-dimensional photographs can provide adequate reliability and are readily available for most cleft lip and palate centers [25], the three-dimensional facial analysis may enhance the generalizability of findings and can be adopted in the future.

Conclusion

According to the findings of the present study, labial variables were effectively corrected and presented no significant differences compared to the noncleft side during the following-ups. However, as growth and remodeling occurred, deformities in columella, alar base and nostril gradually manifested significant rebounds, as an attempt to compensate for lateral drift of alar base on non-cleft side, an overcorrection in alar base and alar contour on the cleft side was deemed necessary during primary repair using a modified rotation-advancement technique to obtain the symmetry of the nasal sill and nostril. It is worth noting that the initial severity of the deformity had limited impact on long-term outcomes.
