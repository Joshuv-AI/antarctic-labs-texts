Biological contributions to manganese oxides in rock varnish at Murujuga (Western Australia)
Authors: Ying-Li Wu, John Hugh Fairweather, Matthew Campbell, Janet Hergt, Emielda Yusiharni, Michael Smirk, Aaron Dodd, Xiao Sun, Peta Clode, Alysia Hubbard, Morten Erik Allentoft, Jo McDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rock varnish is a ubiquitous Mn-rich coating on exposed rock surfaces in arid environments, yet the mechanisms underlying its formation remain debated. Here, we investigate rock varnish from Murujuga, Western Australia, to assess the role of microbial processes in manganese (Mn) accumulation. Bulk compositional and mineralogical analyses confirm high concentrations of Mn, Fe, Al, and Si; however, the Mn matrix is predominantly composed of amorphous to poorly crystalline phases that fall below the indexing or detection thresholds of X-ray diffraction (XRD) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD). Nanoscale characterization reveals a Mn-rich matrix encasing discrete Fe and Al-Si grains, featuring nanometre scale laminations and particle size distribution characteristic of biogenic Mn oxides. High-quality metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) reveal a pronounced dominance of Chroococcidiopsidaceae and Rubrobacter_F, pioneer taxa known to accumulate intracellular Mn for defence mechanisms. Furthermore, targeted functional annotation using Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) confirms a widespread, community-level genomic potential for biologically influenced Mn accumulation and utilization. Because this biomineralisation is an ongoing process governed by local environmental stressors, these rock coatings have high potential as long-term paleoenvironmental and climate proxies. This is the first microbiomic characterisation of the rock varnish from the Murujuga Cultural Landscape, and an important step in unlocking the potential of this deposit as a chronological marker for this region’s petroglyphs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-61982-w.

Introduction and background

Rock varnish, also known as desert varnish, occurs in arid to semi-arid environments across both hot and cold climates. At Murujuga (coastal Pilbara, Western Australia), these coatings are sometimes found superimposed on petroglyphs (rock engravings), as well as found on natural surfaces where the dark, smooth varnish provides colour contrast, enhancing the visual impact of pecked motifs. Varnish that accumulates within petroglyphs has been identified as a potentially powerful tool for constraining their age, but the chronometric goal of varnish dating remains out of reach until the mechanisms of varnish formation are better understood.

Several hypotheses exist for varnish formation, spanning abiotic1,2 and biotic models3,4. A polygenic model involving both abiotic and biotic Mn oxide accumulation is gaining acceptance5 and references therein). Microbiome studies across a range of environments, from cold humid settings such as New York3,6 and the Italian Alps7,8 to extreme and hot arid deserts in the Americas9–16, Turpin Basin17 and the Negev18,19, vary widely in interpretation. Krinsley3,6,20 identified Mn-Fe-enhancing bacteria within varnish and proposed that microbial Mn enrichment drives initial varnish formation, followed by cementation of clay minerals by remobilised Mn after microbial death. Although metagenomic analyses by Lang-Yona et al.11 and Esposito et al.8 both resulted in the absence of a specific Mn-oxidising enzyme, Lang-Yona et al. proposed an indirect bacterial involvement in varnish formation, whereas Esposito et al. recovered oxalate- and siderophore-encoding genes consistent with Mn oxide catalysis by distinct microbial communities. Abiotic models attribute varnish to atmospheric deposition of dissolved constituents1 or photo-oxidation2.

The Murujuga Cultural Landscape (Dampier Archipelago, north-west Australia) is a key setting for studying varnish development (Fig. 1). The landscape is underlain by Archean to Proterozoic mafic, felsic, and sedimentary rocks of the Pilbara Craton, ranging from ~ 2.7 to 3.0 Ga21–24. These slow weathering lithologies25 support stable rock surfaces with extensive varnish development. Arid-to-semi-arid conditions persisting since the Pleistocene26, combined with coastal influences from eustatic sea-level stabilisation at ~ 10 ka27,28 and seasonal inputs from the Intertropical Convergence Zone, have collectively favoured long-term varnish accretion and microbial activity.

Murujuga preserves one of the world’s most extensive petroglyph traditions, with conservative estimates exceeding one million motifs29–32. Continuing cultural occupation of the Pilbara coast extends beyond 50 ka33, spanning major environmental transitions including the Last Glacial Maximum and Holocene islandisation. The durability of these rock surfaces and their weathering rinds has contributed to the exceptional preservation of the petroglyph record25, making Murujuga a critical site for linking surface processes, varnish mineralogy, and long-term human activity.

Our previous work34 identified three varnish morphologies at Murujuga: stromatolitic fabrics dominating the older, lower deposits; laminar layers in the younger, upper accumulations; and detrital material throughout. Mn content varies more in the stromatolitic layers than in laminar domains, suggesting a fluctuating environment in which microbes may have played a significant role. This study builds on that work through a multidisciplinary investigation of the microbe-varnish interface, combining varnish morphology, geochemistry, and mineralogy with biofilm characterisation and metagenomic analyses; the aim is to investigate whether microorganisms play a role in varnish formation, and if that may lead to environmental changes being archived in this rock coating.

Results

The results presented here focus on the microbe-mineral phase interface, with detailed investigation of samples DGS017, DGS028, DGS031, and KHS3 (Table 1, Supplementary Figure S1). These four samples were selected because they yielded the maximum volume of physically recoverable varnish from four underlying substrate lithologies (DGS017/microgabbro, DGS028/gabbro, DGS031/granitoid, KHS3/microgranite). This provided the necessary mass threshold required for X-ray Diffraction (XRD) and Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) analyses. Because a core objective of this study is to unravel in situ microbe-mineral interactions, based on Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM) and cryo-Scanning Electron Microscope (cryo-SEM) results, samples DGS028 and DGS031 were identified with abundant microbes and chosen for further analyses using Tescan Integrated Mineral Analyser (TIMA) and Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD), while microbial-like micromounds in DGS031 were investigated using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). These investigations used state-of-the-art phase identification techniques to determine, at the highest resolutions, the composition of rock varnish and its distribution. The data presented provide an in-depth in situ analysis of microbe-mineral relationships, focusing on strategic examples that exhibit prominent microstructural and biological features. Furthermore, metagenomic analysis of 32 varnish samples (DGS001–DGS032) was performed to provide a detailed view of microbial community composition and tie potential functional capabilities across varnish sites and lithologies.

Physical and chemical properties of the rock varnish

Microscale identification

ICP-OES analysis shows high concentrations of Al, Fe, and Mn in all varnish samples. DGS031 has the highest Mn abundance (137,496 ppm; Supplementary Table S2), indicating minimal substrate contamination. All samples contain minor and trace elements, including Ca, Co, K, Mg, and P. Trace Pb is present in all samples, with DGS031 the highest at 246 ppm. Pb in varnish could indicate modern anthropogenic dust deposition35,36 or recent environmental surface exposure.

XRD analysis shows that all sample powders contain both varnish and substrate minerals (Supplementary Table S3). Mafic substrate minerals dominate the mafic-hosted samples: DGS017 [epidote, quartz, kaolinite, augite, labradorite ± biotite, gibbsite] and DGS028 [epidote, kaolinite, quartz, chlorite, anorthite ± biotite, magnetite]. Felsic substrate minerals dominate the felsic-hosted samples: DGS031 [kaolinite, albite, orthoclase, quartz ± biotite, hematite] and KHS3 [kaolinite, quartz, hematite ± biotite, birnessite]. However, not all identified minerals reflect host-rock entrainment by rock varnish. Quartz is present in all four samples yet is absent from the Rosemary Island microgabbro, indicating aeolian or alluvial transport onto the surface. Regarding sample KHS3, the detection of a minor birnessite signature (0.3 wt%) via XRD highlights the possibility of local crystalline enrichment or better crystallinity allowing these phases to be detected by the XRD, whereas in other samples they remain below the detectable threshold. All samples also contain a significant amorphous fraction, ranging from 14.1% (DGS017) to 29.7% (DGS031). The elevated Mn detected by ICP-OES cannot be accounted for by the crystalline phases, suggesting that Mn is hosted within this amorphous fraction.

XRD results were cross-examined using TIMA and EBSD, two SEM-based techniques that identify mineral phases at ~ 0.5 µm/pixel and provide spatial context within the sample. Both techniques yielded the same result for DGS028 and DGS031: no mineral phases could be identified within the varnish. TIMA resolved the mineralogy of the rock substrate in detail (Fig. 2a and b), but the varnish itself could not be indexed to any mineral phase and was assigned as ‘unindexed’ (black). EBSD was applied to a small varnish region of DGS028 (red boxes in Fig. 2a and b). Mn distribution was first mapped using the Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) detector (Fig. 2c). No diffraction patterns could be obtained from the varnish (Fig. 2d), indicating that the Mn-bearing material lacks a well-ordered crystalline lattice. Diffraction patterns and grain orientations were obtained only from entrained quartz grains of 5–30 μm in size (Fig. 2e).

Varnish structure and composition at the nanoscale with TEM

TEM-EDS elemental mapping of sample DGS031 (Fig. 3) shows that the rock varnish is heterogeneously enriched in Mn, Fe, Al, and Si, wherein it is possible to subdivide Mn-Fe-rich and Al-Si-rich domains. The Mn-Fe-rich domain can be further subdivided into Mn-rich and Fe-rich regions, which together define the diagnostic chemistry of rock varnish.

Mn-rich regions form the main layered body of the varnish and are here referred to as the layered matrix. Individual layers are extremely thin, with thicknesses of approximately 10–100 nm, and are defined by changes in relative Mn concentration. Groups of layers with similar Mn concentration form broader bands, ranging from approximately 60–400 nm in thickness. These bands correspond to the diagnostic varnish layering observed at the micrometre-scale (e.g., SEM-BSE). Notably, Mn is observed only within the layered matrix, wherein no crystal boundaries defining Mn are evident. Mn/Fe ratios within the layered matrix range from approximately 1–10. These observations indicate that subtle variations in the Mn/Fe ratio define the banding within the layered matrix (Fig. 3a).Fe-rich regions occur mainly as subrounded domains approximately 0.2–1 μm in size, with clearly defined boundaries. These domains are consistent with detritus-rich material described by Fairweather et al.34. The Fe-rich grains are embedded within the Mn-rich layers, as shown by the local distortion of the Mn layering. The grains do not crosscut the Mn-rich layers, indicating that this material was incorporated into the varnish rather than grown in situ (Fig. 3b). Their rounded morphology further suggests transport prior to incorporation. In the high Mn micromounds (Fig. 3b), there are Fe-rich grains. However, trace amounts of Fe are also present within the Mn-dominated matrix, indicating that Fe is a consistent secondary component of these micromounds.

Al-Si-rich regions are dominated by aggregations of sub-rounded material and detritus-rich layers, with particle sizes ranging from approximately 0.2–1.5 μm. Most of this material is enriched in Al and Si and is consistent with clay-rich phases. Based on their elemental composition, these phases are interpreted as Al-rich phyllosilicates, such as kaolinite. Within the Al-Si-rich regions, localised domains enriched in K are also observed, where K is a significant secondary element. These occur as sub-rounded particles approximately 0.5 μm in size and display clear internal striations. Their composition and morphology are consistent with K-bearing phyllosilicates, such as illite and/or muscovite.

Microbiome characterisation at the microbe-varnish interface

In situ visualisation of varnish biofilms

The surfaces of varnish samples from three different rock types on the Burrup Peninsula, lifted with forceps, revealed a flourishing microbial community, including cyanobacteria, identified by their morphology and near-IR emission wavelengths corresponding to chlorophyll and photosynthetic pigments. Microbes are found on the surface of the varnish and often aggregated in the cracks and crevices, exhibiting both epilithic and endolithic (chasmoendolithic) behaviours. Rocks from arid and semi-arid climates often harbour endolithic microbes, including cryptoendolithic and chasmoendolithic microbes that seek physical protection beneath the rock surface37,38.

Chasmoendolithic cyanobacteria were observed clustering in crevices on both DGS028 and DGS031 (Fig. 4a), with sarcinoid morphologies characteristic of the cyanobacterial genus Chroococcidiopsis (Fig. 4c, d). Epilithic cyanobacteria were observed on the stromatolitic surface of the varnish in DGS034, embedded in self-excreted extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) that appeared as a yellowish biofilm (Fig. 4b). In the same image, bacteria also form clusters, with new cells protected by a semi-fortress of old cells that can also provide compounds for recycling. This protective colony form is observed in lithic-inhabiting actinobacteria (Geodermatophilus) and cyanobacteria (Chroococcidiopsis, Gloeocapsa)39, also present in our samples. This cluster of bacteria is also visible under the SEM (Fig. 6e). In DGS016, a microgabbro from Rosemary Island, cracks running through the stromatilitic layers emit yellow and orange fluorescence, indicative of pigments and organic molecules from microbial communities (Fig. 4e). BP-GS10, a gabbro with extensive cyanobacteria on the varnish surface reported in34 shows even more intense fluorescence in the stromatolitic layers, with cyanobacteria colonies visible on the varnish surface (Fig. 4f).

Cryo-SEM of the varnish sample DGS028 reveals that the varnish is inhabited by microorganisms other than cyanobacteria. Diplobacilli bacteria cover the varnish surface, 1–2 μm in length, embedded in a thin film of EPS (Fig. 5a). Among the diplobacilli, there are larger diplococci around 3 μm in fewer numbers, which appear to be covered in EPS (Fig. 5b). Where there are fractures, clusters of rod-shaped bacteria are spotted in the cracks (Fig. 5). These rod-shaped bacteria, up to 5 μm long, are clustered on the walls of cracks (Fig. 5d).

Microscopic and elemental profiling of the microbe-varnish stratigraphy

SEM-EDS analysis was performed on samples with extensive microbiomes, as determined by CLSM and cryo-SEM. Specifically, samples DGS028 and DGS031 were cut and polished to expose the chasmoendolithic microbes, and DGS034 was cut and polished perpendicular to the main layering. These samples are described below. For the EDS point spectra, refer to Supplementary Table S4.

DGS028 retained minimal varnish after polishing, but preserved a clear stromatolitic fabric with bright Mn/Fe-rich bands (Fig. 6a, spectrum 4) and darker, lower Mn/Fe bands (Fig. 6a, spectrum 5). Spectra 1, 2, 3, and 7 reflect detrital-rich material with lower Mn, and embedded quartz particles are also present (Fig. 6a, spectrum 6). A second area shows laminar varnish with a low Mn/Fe ratio (Fig. 6b, spectra 20, 21) and amorphous Si-rich material (spectra 22, 25). DGS031 contains abundant chasmoendolithic-like structures consistent with microbial activity (Fig. 6c), associated with elevated Mn (spectra 1–4). Cell-like morphologies and concentric Mn-rich micromounds [40] occur in crevices near the varnish surface (Fig. 6d, spectra 30–36). Mn-rich material also coats the rims of quartz particles deeper in the substrate (Fig. 6c, spectra 6–8), suggesting Mn remobilisation away from the main varnish. A thin (~ 5 μm) Si-P-Fe-rich surface layer is also present (Fig. 6d, spectrum 35); this material is mottled, lacks internal layering, and may represent exogenic detrital input. DGS034 shows loosely stromatolitic varnish layers (Fig. 6e), more porous and fragmented than in DGS028. EDS mapping reveals higher Fe near the surface, transitioning to higher Mn at depth, with quartz and Fe particles dispersed throughout (Fig. 6f). Vaguely concentric patterns at the base of the varnish define the stromatolitic structure. This image corresponds to CLSM Fig. 4b (mirrored due to lens orientation), where the same microbial cluster is visible in the lower-right corner.

Taxonomic composition of the varnish microbiome

The VEBA workflow recovered 168 prokaryotic metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from 32 rock-surface metagenomes. CheckM2 estimates ranged from 50.2 to 100% completeness and 0.02–9.71% contamination. Of these, 28 MAGs met high-quality criteria, defined as ≥ 90% completeness and ≤ 5% contamination, while the remaining 140 met medium-quality criteria, defined as ≥ 50% completeness and ≤ 10% contamination. Across all MAGs, mean completeness was 73.7%, mean contamination was 3.1%, median genome size was 3.43 Mbp, and median N50 was 4,726 bp. A total of 261.3 million reads mapped to the recovered MAGs, with a median of 8.49 million MAG-mapped reads per sample. MAG-associated reads were dominated by Actinomycetota, which accounted for 66.8% of mapped reads, followed by Cyanobacteriota at 17.4%, unclassified MAGs at 6.9%, Pseudomonadota at 5.3%, and Chloroflexota at 1.3%. Dominant families included Rubrobacteraceae, Chroococcidiopsidaceae, Geodermatophilaceae, Solirubrobacteraceae, and Acetobacteraceae. Community composition differed significantly between Burrup and Rosemary samples at both MAG level (PERMANOVA: R² = 0.098, p = 0.0054) and phylum level (R² = 0.316, p = 0.0002). Burrup samples had a stronger Cyanobacteriota signal, whereas Rosemary samples were more strongly dominated by Actinomycetota. Rock type was also associated with significant compositional differences at MAG level (R² = 0.249, p = 0.0006) and phylum level (R² = 0.401, p = 0.0008). These differences were driven primarily by Actinomycetota dominance in microgabbro samples and greater Cyanobacteriota representation in gabbro and microgranite samples. These patterns were consistent with the MAG abundance heatmap, which showed clustering by location and additional structuring by rock type, particularly the separation of Cyanobacteriota-enriched Burrup samples from more Actinomycetota-dominated Rosemary samples (Fig. 7).

Functional potential and manganese oxidation capacity

MAGs filtered to include bins with > 70% completeness and < 10% contamination, 92 in total, were analysed for broader lithotrophic potential, revealing that genes associated with oxygen metabolism are ubiquitously dominant, indicative of a primarily aerobic lifestyle (Fig. 8a). More notably, the communities demonstrated a strong genomic potential for trace gas scavenging. Genes encoding for the metabolism of carbon monoxide, hydrogen, and C1 Compounds are highly prevalent and maintained moderate-to-high copy numbers across the entire dataset. Furthermore, the genomic potential for metal cycling is distinctly partitioned. Manganese metabolism was identified as a significant pathway, with gene counts peaking significantly in specific sample subsets (DGS025, DGS029, DGS030). In contrast, pathways for iron, arsenic, and selenium are detectable but generally maintained at much lower gene counts. Additional metabolic traits, including the metabolism of halogenated compounds and urea, were broadly distributed across lithologies. In contrast, genes associated with anaerobic methane metabolism were largely absent, suggesting that methanogenesis and methanotrophy are not major contributors to carbon cycling in this system.

Detailed profiling of manganese-associated genes (Fig. 8b) reveals distinct functional categories within the varnish microbiome. Genes encoding Mn transport systems (e.g., MntA, MntA1, MntB and Mntp) were ubiquitously detected across samples and maintained consistently high gene counts. This widespread distribution suggests a community-wide strategy for scavenging trace soluble Mn(II). Analysis of the Mn transformation pathways revealed a distinct, unidirectional genomic potential for biomineralisation involving two distinct enzymatic pathways. Genes annotated as driving Mn oxidation, primarily multicopper oxidases (MCO) McoA and animal heme-dependent peroxidases (An_peroxidase), were highly prevalent and abundant across the dataset. Another MCO, MnxG, is present in four samples. In contrast, genetic markers associated with dissimilatory Mn or Fe reduction, such as MtrA, MtrB, MtrC and CymA [41] are absent. This functional asymmetry indicates that the community has a strong genetic potential to actively precipitate insoluble Mn(III/IV) oxides but lacks the canonical reductive pathways to dissolve them. The Mn-binding category highlighted a massive enrichment of Mn-binding enzymes associated with cellular defence mechanisms. Genes encoding Mn-catalase and Fe/Mn superoxide dismutase (SOD_FeMn_Cterminal) exhibited some of the highest overall gene counts in the MnGenie profile, alongside high counts for PEPCK_ATP. It is important to note that these functional profiles are derived from metagenomic (DNA) annotations, which demonstrate the genetic capacity of the microbiome; future metatranscriptomic or culture-based studies will be required to confirm the in situ expression and active enzymatic turnover of these manganese cycling pathways.

Taxonomy and distribution of manganese oxidation genes

Targeted functional profiling of the reconstructed MAGs (> 70% completeness and < 10% contamination, 92 in total) reveals that the genetic potential for Mn oxidation is concentrated within the phylum Actinomycetota, followed by Cyanobacteriota. Minor contributions were also identified within the Chloroflexota, Pseudomonadota, and Acidobacteriota (Fig. 9). At the family level, MAGs carrying putative Mn oxidation genes are predominantly represented by Rubrobacteraceae (Actinomycetota), followed by Chroococcidiopsidaceae (Cyanobacteriota), then Geodermatophilaceae (Actinomycetota) and Thermoleophilaceae (Actinomycetota), with lesser representation from families such as Acetobacteraceae (Pseudomonadota). High-resolution taxonomic classification identified a large proportion of these MAGs as unclassified genera (19 MAGs). Among the resolved genera, Rubrobacter_F dominates the dataset (24 MAGs).

Discussion

The aim of this study is to investigate the feasibility of residing microbes to concentrate Mn oxides in rock varnish, enhancing its potential as a proxy for preserving paleoenvironmental and climate records. Dorn [35] described rock varnish formation starting with Mn and Fe concentrated by budding bacteria, followed by geochemical dissolution and nanoscale reprecipitation of Mn-Fe oxyhydroxides that do not disturb their presence in laminations at the micron scale. Our morphological and chemical analyses largely confirm this, with nanoscale observations revealing a classical laminated varnish with submicron laminations.

At the micrometre scale, stromatolitic and laminated textures indicate organised biological growth rather than purely stochastic particle accretion [34]. In geoscience, stromatolites strongly imply biologically influenced growth (cf [42]), even where direct microbial remains are absent [43]. Many studies at the onset of their analyses quote that rock varnish is approximately “70% clay minerals” and “30% Mn/Fe oxy-hydroxides” [5,44–47]. While true at the micrometre scale, this is not the case at the nanometre scale. The results of this study clearly show that the layered matrix, which comprises most of the Mn-rich varnish material, is composed entirely of Mn-Fe oxides, while “clay mineral” aspect is entrained Al-Si-rich sand and clay dust particles (Fig. 3). While this material is important as it increases the volume and mass of the total rock varnish deposit, it is not produced during the microbe-driven in situ formation of Mn-rich rock varnish, but more likely incorporated as external material. The documentation of transported quartz, detrital inputs, and substrate contaminations are consistent with mixed formation processes (e.g. aeolian deposition, wetting/drying cycles, photo-oxidation).

Analyses of the varnish material through XRD and TIMA mineral databases showed rock varnish was not made of a stoichiometric mineral; rather, it is a complex nanoscale mixture of intergrown phases and amorphous matrices (Fig. 2). To determine what makes up the Mn-Fe-rich components of our rock varnish samples, we observed them at the highest possible resolution using HR-TEM. At this resolution, there were angstrom(Å)-scale Mn-rich laminations within the varnish matrix (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figure S3), suggesting some degree of internal structure at scales below those resolvable by micrometre-scale techniques (e.g., SEM-BSE, EBSD, and TIMA). The measured interlayer spacings of approximately 6–8 Å fall within the range reported for common sheet-layered, poorly crystalline mineral structures [48–51]. The additional observation of the rounded nanoscale ‘masses’, either embedded or formed, within the varnish matrix provides added information not previously reported in the literature. The size-frequency distribution (Supplementary Figure S3) indicates that Mn-Fe-rich ‘masses’ of this size could originate by biogenic processes [52]. Moreover, using HR-TEM, we measured internal spacings of approximately 2.1–2.4 Å within some of the ‘masses’, features that overlap with the lattice spacings reported for Mn-Fe oxide octahedral sheets [53,54]. Therefore, these nanoscale ‘masses’ may represent in situ formed nanoparticulate Mn-Fe oxides. The incorporation of Fe within the varnish matrix is also consistent with adsorption onto Mn-oxide surfaces and/or coprecipitation during microbial oxidation, as layered Mn-oxides produced in natural systems readily bind and incorporate transition metal cations during formation via surface complexation and coprecipitation mechanisms with Fe and Mn in microbial Fe-Mn oxide assemblages [55].

By contrast, better-crystallised abiotic Mn oxides produced under controlled or hydrothermal conditions typically exhibit more regular layer stacking, more consistent cation chemistry, and sharper diffraction features than the highly variable, mixed-cation biogenic phases observed in varnish samples [56]. Potential trace-element inclusions in our samples also support a biologically influenced Mn accumulation. Phosphorus concentrations measured by EPMA average approximately 0.7 ± 0.4 wt% within the varnish layers [34] and, by ICP-OES in this study (Supplementary Table S2), exceed typical values reported for hydrothermal Mn oxides, which are generally below 0.3 wt% [57]. The positive association between P, Fe, and trace Cu is characteristic of Mn oxides formed in the presence of organic matter, where phosphate is readily adsorbed or structurally incorporated during hydrogenous or biogenic growth [58,59]. Although abiotic varnish precipitation cannot be entirely ruled out in the polygenic formation model, the evidence in support of varnish production driven by biological processes at Murujuga is strong.

The presence of Cyanobacteriota, Rubrobacteraceae, Geodermatophilus-like taxa, and EPS/biofilms clearly shows that varnish is a habitable microbial substrate. CLSM, Cryo-SEM, and SEM data show colonisation and spatial association of the microbiome and Mn rich varnish (Figs. 4, 5 and 6). The consistent presence of Cyanobacteriota supports their role as foundational primary producers in the varnish ecosystem (Supplementary Figure S5). Chroococcidiopsis is well documented as a pioneer coloniser of extreme arid environments, capable of maintaining photosynthetic activity under severe desiccation and of resisting ionising radiation [60,61]. Their dominance in the autotrophic niche likely provides the organic carbon necessary to support the surrounding heterotrophic community. It is worth noticing that Deinococcota is also present across Murujuga samples (Fig. 7). Deinococcus radiodurans uses well-documented non-enzymatic oxidation as the first line cellular defence against ionising radiation. By maintaining an exceptionally high intracellular Mn²⁺/Fe²⁺ ratio, these bacteria prevent the formation of toxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH) via the Fenton reaction. Intracellular Mn²⁺ forms complexes with metabolites (e.g., orthophosphates, nucleosides, amino acids) that catalytically neutralise reactive oxygen species (ROS), producing Mn(III) or Mn(IV) compounds [62,63]. This protects DNA repair and replication enzymes, allowing rapid post-irradiation genome reassembly [64]. Spatially, the Rosemary Island site (sampled from microgabbro) yielded MAGs across both Cyanobacteriota and Actinomycetota encompassing genes for carbon fixation and nitrate reduction. In contrast, the Burrup peninsula MAGs (microgranite and gabbro) were dominated by Cyanobacteriota (Supplementary Figure S5). This variation may reflect differences in sample size (Burrup: 9; Rosemary: 23) or distinct physicochemical rock properties and localised environmental conditions.

Functional profiling of MAGs demonstrated a widespread genomic potential on physiological usage of Mn across all sampled lithologies (Fig. 8b). Mn is primarily transported via ATP-binding cassette (ABC-type) permeases (MntA, MntA1, MntB) and the putative efflux pump Mntp involved in cell Mn homeostasis [65–69]. Cellular Mn is utilised for metabolic, photosynthetic, and defensive pathways. In autotrophs, Mn is essential for oxygen production via the photosystem II manganese-stabilizing protein (MSP) and the Mn-binding peptide PsbY [70,71]. Across the broader community, Mn acts as a requisite divalent cation (Mn²⁺) for carbon metabolism via PEPCK ATP, an ATP-dependent phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase [72,73], and a bacterial metallocofactor for ribonucleotide reductase (Ribonuc_red_sm represents the small chain of ribonucleotide reductase) to produce precursors for DNA synthesis [74]. The widespread occurrence of genes encoding Mn-dependent antioxidant enzymes—such as non-heme Mn catalase, glutathione peroxidase, and superoxide dismutases (SODs)—highlights the community’s capacity to catalytically neutralise ROS like superoxide (O₂⁻) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [75–77].

When examining the genetic pathways for Mn oxidation, MnGenie identified three distinct Mn oxidases: MCOs MnxG and McoA, and the AHP ‘An_peroxidase’. MCO-type enzymes, which utilize copper to catalyse the oxidation of Mn(II) to insoluble MnO₂, are found in diverse taxa including Bacillus sp. SG-1 and Pseudomonas putida GB-1 [78,79]. P. putida GB-1 also utilizes AHP, an enzyme also responsible for Mn oxidation in Alphaproteobacteria such as Aurantimonas manganoxydans SI85-9A1 and Erythrobacter sp. SD-21. Because these oxidases are typically active on the outer cell surface, their expression results in cells coated with Mn oxides [80,81]. While Mn oxidation may provide energy for chemolithoautotrophic growth, its primary benefits are likely protection against ROS, UV radiation, heavy metal toxicity, and predation or viruses [79,82]. The presence of multiple oxidase genes across our samples indicates the potential of these bacteria to utilise and coordinate distinct enzymatic strategies to optimise Mn oxidation in fluctuating local microenvironments, as demonstrated by [83] for P. putida GB-1.

The major phyla identified in the Murujuga samples align closely with the varnish microbiome profiles described by Lingappa et al. [15]. While they emphasized the role of Chroococcidiopsis in accumulating Mn as a non-enzymatic antioxidant, our functional analysis further detected these Cyanobacteriota MAGs with a wide array of Mn oxidases (MCO and AHP), suggesting their enzymatic potential to precipitate Mn oxides (Fig. 9). Besides unclassified genera, Rubrobacter_F is the most abundant genus identified in our MAGs and harbours the highest diversity of Mn oxidase genes, indicating a robust genetic capacity to coordinate oxidation via MCO, AHP, or combined enzymes. The profound ionising radiation resistance of thermophiles like Rubrobacter xylanophilus and R. radiotolerans is known to depend on the accumulation of intracellular Mn and trehalose, further supporting the Mn²⁺-dependent ROS scavenging model in aerobic organisms [84,85]. Actinomycetota MAGs in Murujuga samples exhibit diverse oxidase profiles, while Cyanobacteriota MAGs display a higher proportion of MCO enzymes. Crucially, while significant co-occurrences exist, many Murujuga genomes contain only a single Mn oxidase. This aligns with the genomic survey by Kurdi et al. [86], indicating that a single enzyme may be sufficient to oxidise Mn(II) to Mn(IV). In contrast, organisms equipped with multiple distinct oxidases likely require them to maintain oxidative capacity across broader gradients of temperature, nutrients, and oxygen level. Taken together, these results indicate that manganese plays a central role in both microbial metabolism and stress physiology within the varnish microbiome. However, the metagenomic data should be interpreted as evidence of functional potential rather than direct evidence of in situ Mn oxidation activity. Future work will be required to determine whether the identified bacteria are actively involved in microbial Mn precipitation. This will require complementary transcriptomic, proteomic, cultivation, or incubation-based approaches that directly link gene expression or enzymatic activity to Mn oxide formation under varnish-relevant conditions.

Conclusions

Multiscale characterisation of Murujuga rock varnish reveals a complex matrix of poorly crystalline, amorphous Mn-Fe oxides interwoven with clay and detrital material. Crucially, the amorphous state and nanoscale particle size distribution of these Mn oxides are strongly indicative of a biogenic origin. In situ optical and electron microscopy clearly identified Cyanobacteria and heterotrophs at Mn-enrichment zones identified via EDS. This physical evidence is supported by our taxonomic and metagenomic profiling, which demonstrates that the varnish microbiome harbours the genetic repertoire to oxidise Mn via multiple enzymatic pathways, alongside a capacity to utilise Mn for non-enzymatic antioxidant defence. The combined mineralogical, microscopic, and metagenomic evidence supports a significant biologically influenced contribution to Mn accumulation in Murujuga rock varnish, followed by remobilisation at the micro- and nanoscales, with a subordinate but important role for abiotic varnish contributions that is evident in the trapped aeolian detritus. Furthermore, the identification of anthropogenic lead (Pb) within the varnish may be a potential indicator of modern anthropogenic input. Because biological precipitation is inherently governed by local environmental stressors, the resulting varnish stratigraphy holds significant potential as an archive of past climate shifts. By demonstrating the genomic potential of Mn oxidation in the residing microbiome, we open exciting new possibilities for interpreting the deep history of Murujuga through rock varnish.

Materials and methods

This study is complementary to the analytical work conducted by Fairweather et al. [34]. The procedures applied here follow those reported previously, with only minor adjustments specific to the present investigation. For clarity, a concise summary of the key methods in this paper is provided below; further details regarding Electron Probe Microanalysis, as well as complementary Scanning Electron Microscopy, Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy, and Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy, are available in Fairweather et al. [34]

Sampling

Across Murujuga, 35 samples were collected in this study, including 32 microbial samples (DGS001-DGS032) for microbiome analysis and three rock/varnish samples (BP-GS10, DGS034, KHS3) with particularly thick varnish deposits to examine varnish and biofilm morphologies. All samples were collected from the Burrup Peninsula and Rosemary Island (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1). These samples include varnish formed on five different lithologies: microgranite (previously referred to in the literature as ‘granophyre’), gabbro, a fine-grained felsic igneous rock containing granitic enclaves (perhaps representing a partially re-melted granite), microgabbro and volcaniclastic siltstone. Sample types include rock fragments that retain a varnish coating, varnish fragments removed from rock surfaces, and swabs of varnish slurry collected from surfaces for microbiome analysis (Supplementary Table S1).

The microbiome samples were collected in situ by applying distilled water to the rock surface, blending it into a slurry with a handheld Dremel drill, and collecting the slurry with sterile swabs. This material was then stored in a DNA/RNA shield (Zymo Research). The Dremel was cleaned between samples using pure water wipes followed by 70% v/v isopropyl alcohol wipes. Drilled samples were kept frozen and transferred to a -80 °C freezer in the laboratory upon returning from the field.

The samples used to characterise the varnish include ~ 10-20cm3 pieces either collected or chipped off rock outcrops with visible surface patina. Typically, the rock varnish is a thin, black layer that forms on the rock surface, specifically on the red-coloured weathering rind. The varnish usually varies from tens to hundreds of micrometres in thickness. It can accumulate in microbasins on the rock surface, as shown in the profiles of DGS017 and KHS3 (Supplementary Figure S1). In some cases, where the rock varnish was unusually thick (> 1 mm), it was directly removed from the underlying weathering rind with alcohol-wiped forceps while wearing disposable latex gloves, and the sample was kept in sterilised Whirl-Pak sample bags. Three samples were collected from the Burrup Peninsula in this way: a microgranite (DGS034), a gabbro (DGS028), and a fine-grained felsic igneous rock (DGS031). In order to examine the varnish both across the surface and in cross-section, DGS034 was embedded perpendicular to the epoxy resin and polished to expose continuous profiles through the varnish and weathering rind, whereas DGS028 and DGS031 were embedded horizontally and lightly polished to remove areas of the surface varnish and reveal the internal structure. This resulted in a patchy distribution on DGS028 but retained a more continuous cauliflower-like varnish on DGS031 (Supplementary Figure S1).

Analytical methods and approach

The analyses here investigate the correlation between inorganic rock varnish components and the inhabiting microbiome. Building on varnish characterisation in Fairweather et al. [34], the current analysis extended the resolution of varnish morphology from micrometre-scale under the scanning electron microscope to nanometre-scale using the transmission electron microscope. It included bulk chemical analysis (ICP-OES) of the inorganic material to complement phase identification (TIMA and EBSD). The organic biofilm was visualised by in situ imaging of varnish-inhabiting microbes (CLSM and Cryo-SEM), followed by microbial community and metagenomic functional analyses of 32 samples. Although microbial analyses provide a snapshot of the current microbiome, rock varnish continues to form, as indicated by infilling within petroglyphs and by modern anthropogenic influence (see ICP-OES). This investigation of modern microbes yields valuable insights into varnish formation.

Rock varnish characterisation: morphology, geochemistry and mineralogy

Most of our samples from Murujuga are rock chips with varnish, collected after microbiome sampling. In some cases, the rock outcrop was unsuitable for breaking, and only microbial swabs were collected. The thickness of varnish on these rocks is unknown until processed in the lab or observed under a microscope, and those that retained observable varnish after sample preparation (e.g. thin sections, mounts) were investigated extensively with SEM-EDS in Fairweather et al. [34]; therefore, this paper presents SEM imaging, point analyses and EDS mapping on varnish samples identified with extensive microbes by CLSM (DGS028, DGS031 and DGS034). High Mn varnish with microbial-like forms was observed in DGS031 using SEM, prompting further analysis by FIB/TEM. Because samples DGS028, DGS031 and DGS034 were thick enough to be removed from the rock with forceps with only minor host-rock entrainment, they were considered ideal to be powdered for bulk XRD and ICP-OES analysis. DGS034 produced insufficient powder and was replaced by varnish from another microgranite, KHS3, while varnish from DGS017 was used to represent microgabbro. TIMA and EBSD were employed to reveal the mineralogy and mineral proportions of phases preserved in the rock varnish.

Scanning electron microscopy combined with energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)

Samples were cut perpendicular to the rock surface to expose the varnish layers, weathering rind, and underlying rock substrate. All samples were prepared as polished thin sections, with some additional fragments mounted in epoxy resin and polished (e.g., Supplementary Figure S1). The polished samples were carbon-coated (~ 5 nm thick) prior to analysis. Backscattered electron imaging and elemental analysis were conducted using a TESCAN VEGA 3 scanning electron microscope operated in high-vacuum mode at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a beam current of 16 nA. The instrument is equipped with an Oxford Instruments X-Max 50 silicon drift EDS detector and operated with AZtec software, enabling elemental mapping and point analysis.

Focused ion beam (FIB) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Electron-transparent foils were prepared using focused ion beam (FIB) milling on a TESCAN LYRA3 dual-beam field-emission scanning electron microscope (TESCAN) to enable nanoscale characterisation of the rock varnish. The instrument is equipped with a Ga ion source for site-specific milling and an electron column for high-resolution imaging and navigation. Two site-specific foils (named ROI1 and ROI2) were extracted from DGS031, these two ROI’s were identified using electron microscopy (Supplementary Figure S2). A protective platinum cap was deposited before milling to preserve surface textures and reduce ion-beam damage prior to FIB milling. Trenches were milled on either side of the target area using progressively higher ion currents to isolate a lamella, which was then undercut and lifted out using a micromanipulator. The lamella was attached to a TEM grid and progressively thinned by decreasing ion beam current to achieve electron transparency, reaching thicknesses of < 100 nm. Final low-current polishing at a reduced accelerating voltage was performed to minimise curtaining, surface amorphisation, and gallium implantation, and to improve foil quality for diffraction and chemical analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to resolve the nanostructure, mineralogy, and chemistry of the varnish matrix. Crystalline phases were identified using selected area electron diffraction, with ring diffraction patterns interpreted to distinguish nanocrystalline from poorly ordered components. Elemental compositions were determined using Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS), allowing assessment of the distribution of major and minor elements within the varnish. Data acquisition and processing were performed using Thermo Scientific Velox software.

Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES)

Varnish material was mechanically separated from the underlying rock substrate using fine forceps, yielding approximately 0.5 g per sample. During the separation process, small, unquantified amounts of rock substrate remained unavoidably attached to the varnish samples. The material was manually ground in an agate mortar and mounted on low-background sample holders. For high-precision elemental analysis, approximately 50 mg of the powdered sample was weighed into 10 mL polypropylene tubes. One millilitre of 6 M hydrochloric acid and two drops of 30 per cent hydrogen peroxide were added, and the samples were heated to approximately 90 degrees Celsius for 30 min. After cooling, samples were diluted to 10 mL with Milli-Q water, homogenised, and further diluted to approximately 10% by volume before analysis. Element concentrations of Al, Ba, Ca, Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, K, Mg, Mn, Na, Ni, P, Pb, Rb, Sr, V, and Zn were measured using a PerkinElmer Optima 5300 DV ICP OES calibrated with matrix-matched standards.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) of powdered samples

For XRD analysis, the same powdered samples for ICP-OES were prepared using a low-background sample holder. A small amount of powder was placed onto the holder, and pure ethanol was added dropwise to aid in evenly dispersing the material across the surface. The sample was then gently spread to form a thin, uniform layer and left to dry completely, ensuring good particle packing and minimizing preferred orientation effects. X-ray diffraction measurements were performed using a PANalytical Aeris benchtop diffractometer operated at 40 kV and 15 mA, employing Co Kα radiation with an Fe filter. Data were collected over an angular range of 3° to 80° (2θ) with a step size of 0.0109° (2θ) and a total scan time of 45 min per sample. Diffraction patterns were processed using HighScore Plus software. Phase identification was carried out using standard reference databases, and quantitative phase analysis was performed using the Rietveld refinement method. The practical detection limit for crystalline phases is typically around 1–3 wt%, depending on the crystallinity of the phase, sample preparation, and peak overlap. The uncertainty associated with quantitative phase analysis is generally within ± 1–2 wt% for major phases and increases for minor phases present near the detection limit.

Regarding the quantification of amorphous material, an external standard with a known degree of crystallinity was analysed under identical instrumental conditions to establish the instrument constant. The total crystalline content of each sample was then determined by Rietveld refinement, and the amorphous content was calculated as the difference between the measured crystalline fraction and the total sample mass. This approach enables the estimation of amorphous content without the addition of an internal standard and is a well-established method for quantitative XRD analysis.

Tescan integrated mineral analyzer (TIMA) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)

Modal mineralogy maps were generated using a TESCAN Integrated Mineral Analyser. Analyses were performed in dot mapping mode to quantify modal abundance, mineral liberation, and phase associations across different size fractions. The system is equipped with an ultra-fast YAG scintillator backscattered-electron detector and four silicon-drift EDS detectors, positioned to capture the full solid angle, enabling high-throughput X-ray collection. Energy-dispersive spectra acquisition was synchronised with stage movement and the backscattered electron signal, enabling phase identification using a defined spectral library. Analyses were conducted using a spot size of 0.1 micrometres and a pixel size of 1 micrometre at a working distance of 15 mm. The electron beam was operated at 25 keV with an intensity setting of 18 under vacuum conditions (~ 10 –4 Pa). X-ray detection was performed using a PulseTor 30 detector. Data processing and mineral classification were completed using TESCAN TIMA software version 2.12.1.

Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was used to assess whether the rock varnish matrix exhibits resolvable diffraction patterns and to characterise any crystalline phases present. Only one sample, DGS028, was analysed via EBSD owing to the additional sample preparation steps required for EBSD analysis, which require a much finer polish and a thinner carbon coat.

Analyses were conducted on a TESCAN MIRA 3 scanning electron microscope equipped with a variable-pressure field-emission source (VP FE) (TESCAN). Working distances of ~ 20 mm and step sizes of ~ 1–2 μm were employed to optimise spatial resolution. The instrument was operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, a beam intensity of 18.0, and a 70° stage tilt, with diffraction patterns acquired at exposure times of 4–8 ms. Data was processed using software from Oxford Instruments, including AZtec v5.1 and AZtec Crystal v2.1. Post-processing included iterative zero-solution neighbour filtering to remove isolated non-indexed pixels adjacent to indexed regions, with up to 10 iterations, and the removal of random isolated indexed pixels (i.e., wild spikes). Indexing quality was evaluated using band contrast. Phases with average Mean Angular Deviation values greater than 1 were excluded from the analysis.

Rock varnish microbiome visualisation

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) was employed primarily to study in situ biofilm within the varnish. While CLSM visualised cyanobacteria and biofilms, Cryo-SEM captured microbes of different types on the varnish surface. Results from CLSM led to the decision to choose DGS028 for cryo-SEM analysis. Although both microscopic techniques can visualise microbes within the varnish, confirmation of microbial community identities still relies on metagenomic sequencing.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

Imaging of microbes and biofilms at the micrometre scale using CLSM was performed using a Nikon A1R FLIM laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with 10 × (0.45NA Plan Apo λ), 20 × (0.75NA Plan Apo VC) and 40 × (1.15NA Plan Apo LWD) objectives (Nikon, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Image acquisition and processing were performed using NIS-Elements software (Nikon, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Autofluorescence was detected using 405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm, and 640 nm lasers with bandpass filters of 450/50 nm, 525/50 nm, 595/50 nm, and 700/75 nm, all operating in simultaneous mode. For reflection imaging, the same region was captured with 405 nm illumination (passed through an 80/20 filter) and all light detected to provide topographical context for the varnish. Photosynthetic pigments and other unknown biomolecules can be excited by lasers and emit autofluorescence in the range from 495 nm to 780 nm. Autofluorescence from phototrophic pigments collected at 700 nm was assigned to the cyan channel. Emission signals were also collected at 450 nm, 525 nm, and 595 nm, corresponding to the blue, green, and red channels, respectively.

Cryo-SEM

Sample DGS028 was directly mounted onto a cryo-stage using OCT and frozen in a liquid nitrogen-cooled loading station (Leica EM VCM) to prevent any removal or loss of microbes from the sample surface. Frozen samples were transferred under vacuum to the preparation chamber (Leica EM ACE600) before being sublimated at -90 °C for 8 min and then coated with 4 nm Pt at 0° tilt and 4 nm Pt at + 25° tilt. Coated samples were transferred under vacuum to the cryoSEM (JEOL JSM-IT800 FESEM fitted with a Leica EM VCT500 cryo stage) and imaged at -150 °C using the in-chamber secondary electron detector at 3 kV.

Microbial community and metagenomic analyses

Metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatic processing

Microbial DNA was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS™ DNA/RNA Miniprep Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Total genomic DNA was quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with yields ranging from 1 to 10 ng. Shotgun metagenomic libraries were prepared using the QIAseq® FX DNA Library Kit (QIAGEN) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, incorporating specific modifications optimised for low-biomass inputs. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform using a P4 flow cell with a 2 × 151 bp paired-end read configuration. Raw sequencing data was processed using the VEBA (Viral, Eukaryotic, Bacterial, and Archaeal) modular pipeline [87]. MAG quality, taxonomic composition, and community structure were analysed in R using outputs from the VEBA workflow and CheckM2 [88]. VEBA taxonomy and abundance tables were parsed to extract sample identifiers, mapped read counts, and taxonomic assignments, with taxonomic strings separated into phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species ranks. MAG-mapped read depth was summarised per sample, and relative abundances were calculated at phylum and family levels overall and within each location and rock type. Differences in total MAG recovery, high-quality MAG recovery, and MAG-mapped read depth among locations and rock types were tested using Kruskal–Wallis tests, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied for multiple comparisons. Community composition was assessed using MAG-level and phylum-level abundance matrices. Read-count matrices were Hellinger-transformed, and Bray–Curtis dissimilarities were calculated using the vegan package (ref- https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/vegan/index.html). PERMANOVA was used to test for compositional differences by location and rock type with 9,999 permutations. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion was assessed using PERMDISP to determine whether significant PERMANOVA results reflected differences in group centroids, within-group dispersion, or both. For heatmap visualisation, MAGs were filtered to retain bins with > 70% completeness and < 10% contamination based on CheckM2 estimates. Reads were summed by sample and combined phylum–family group, and the 30 most abundant families were retained. Count matrices were converted to sample-wise relative abundance, log10-transformed, and row-standardised as z-scores to highlight relative enrichment and depletion across samples. Heatmaps were generated using hierarchical clustering of samples and taxa, with column annotations for location and rock type and row annotations for phylum.

Functional profiling of lithic biogeochemistry and manganese metabolism

Predicted protein sequences derived from the high-quality MAGs (> 70% completeness < 10% contamination) were screened against curated Hidden Markov Model (HMM) databases using the MagicLamp [89] toolkit with two specific modules, LithoGenie and MnGenie. The LithoGenie was used to characterise lithotrophic and alternative energy metabolism of the community. This included screening for genetic markers associated with trace-gas scavenging (e.g., carbon monoxide, hydrogen, and C1 compounds), aerobic, and the cycling of transition metals and metalloids (e.g., iron, arsenic). MnGenie was employed to identify genes associated with manganese cycling, including high-affinity Mn transport, putative Mn oxidation, dissimilatory Mn/Fe reduction, and Mn-dependent antioxidant defence systems. Gene counts for each functional category were aggregated at the sample level and visualised using the ggplot2 package in R.

Core metabolic potential was further assessed by screening high quality MAGs with a curated panel of KEGG Orthology (KO) identifiers (Supplementary Figure S5). This included markers for Rubisco (Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase), the essential enzyme in photosynthesis that catalyzes the first step of the Calvin-Benson-Bassham cycle (RuBisCO subunits cbbL/K01601, cbbS/K01602), nitrogen fixation (nifH/K02588, nifD/K02586), anaerobic nitrate respiration (narG/K00370, narH/K00371), and sulfur cycling (aprA/K00394, aprB/K00395).
