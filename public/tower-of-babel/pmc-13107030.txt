Response to “Evaluation of severe male infertility”. Perspectives of exome sequencing as the first-line genetic test in severe spermatogenic failure
Authors: Stanislav Tjagur, Margus Punab, Avirup Dutta, Maris Laan
Published: 2026

The recent Editorial (1) highlighting our study (2) has concluded “…the published case report suggests that all men with nonobstructive azoospermia need whole exome sequencing.” [WES]. Herewith, we would like to correct this somewhat misleading conclusion to the readership of F&S Reports. In the Discussion section of the paper (2), we have suggested “There are several advantages to opt for WES as the first-line molecular test in severe spermatogenic failure with a suspected genetic cause.”

Nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA) represents an extreme form of male infertility stemming from impaired sperm production because of acquired or congenital conditions, including a diverse set of genetic causes. Before any genetic testing of NOA patients, routine clinical workup should exclude the causal nongenetic factors. Current standard genetic testing for NOA recommends karyotyping and analysis of Y-chromosome microdeletions in the azoospermia factor (AZF) regions, providing a molecular diagnosis in 15%–20% of cases (3, 4).

Extensive research during the past 10–15 years has revealed that the genetic architecture and etiology of NOA, both isolated and syndromic, is far more complex than previously suspected. It is likely that there are hundreds of genes implicated in diverse forms of monogenic male infertility (3, 4, 5). In our center, WES reached molecular diagnosis in over 10% of cases with idiopathic severe spermatogenic failure and a suspected monogenetic cause (5).

Whole exome sequencing appears to be superior as a first-tier method in genetic testing compared with other approaches. It does not need a prior hypothesis about the nature of a genetic condition and is suitable for simultaneous screening of monogenic defects and gross chromosomal abnormalities, and possibly also AZF deletions when implementing advanced analytical tools (3, 4). A recent study by Oud et al. (3) has successfully experimentally validated the proposed “all-in-one exome sequencing strategy” for diagnostic genetic testing in male infertility. Whole exome sequencing represents a standardized, rapid, and largely automated approach that requires only a small amount of genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) as an input. We and other investigators have shown that WES data also have sufficient sensitivity for accurate detection of cellular mosaicism (2, 3, references therein). In contrast, conventional karyotyping requires fresh biological material, involves labor-intensive manual procedures, and is susceptible to intrinsic limitations, such as lower resolution because of analyzing only a subset of cells, and subjective human factors, even in experienced laboratories. Further advantage of WES is an option for data re-analyses at any future time point in the light of advanced knowledge.

It is important to note that in quality molecular diagnostics practice, WES-based findings are validated with an independent targeted experimental approach. In the study by Tjagur et al. (2), we were interested in assessing the accuracy of WES-based prediction of mosaicism, and tissue distribution of mosaic cell lines. Therefore, a broader range of validation methods was used than in a clinical setting.

The aim of any diagnostic workup is to find the primary causal factor that will guide more individualized clinical decision-making, both for reproductive and general health risks and conditions. We completely agree with the Editorial (1) that genetic testing in NOA is expected to contribute to predicting the likelihood of successful testicular sperm retrieval and biological fatherhood. There is growing evidence that not only AZF microdeletions, but also monogenic causes of NOA, typically identified using WES data, are informative in this aspect (3, 4, 5). Importantly, identifying the genetic etiology of infertility is clinically relevant not only for reproductive counseling but also for long-term health surveillance. The diagnosis of 45,X/46,XY karyotype for our NOA case (2) was crucial in developing his management strategy and follow-up clinical assessments.

Critical aspects to consider for any laboratory tests are turnover time, robustness and cost, including resources for the setup and running of a test. In advanced molecular diagnostic centers, the turnaround time from tissue sampling to WES report can be less than a week. It has been estimated that the cost of using WES as the first-line test does not exceed the combined price of current genetic testing in NOA (3). Ultimately, it is more cost effective as a broad spectrum of genetic defects is tested and analyzed simultaneously. Current accessibility to WES varies greatly between countries and clinical centers. This has been the major limiting factor in introducing this approach more broadly in the workup of idiopathic NOA with a suspected genetic cause. However, given the dropping prices and overall rapid spread of WES-based molecular diagnostics in medical genetics laboratories worldwide, the time to update the guidelines with recommendations for using WES in the management of infertile men may come sooner than thought.
