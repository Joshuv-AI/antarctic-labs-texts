Real-World Outcomes of Ultrasound-Guided Carpal Tunnel Release in 1,082 Hands: 6-Month Results From the MISSION Registry
Authors: Victor M Marwin, Stuart M Hilliard, James F Watt, John J Callahan, Paul E Perry, Alan J Micev, Larry E Miller, Johnny T Nelson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report 6-month outcomes from the MISSION registry in patients treated with ultrasound-guided carpal tunnel release (UGCTR) in routine clinical practice.MethodsMISSION is a prospective multicenter observational registry of patients with carpal tunnel syndrome treated with UGCTR after failed nonsurgical management. Outcomes included time to return to normal activities and work, Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire Symptom Severity Scale and Functional Status Scale, pain severity (0–10 scale), health-related quality of life measured with the EuroQoL 5-Dimension 5-Level, patient satisfaction, adverse events, and revision procedures through 6 months of follow-up.ResultsA total of 887 patients (1,082 hands) underwent UGCTR at 22 sites in the United States. Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet was used in 82.1% of procedures, 22.0% of patients were treated with simultaneous bilateral release, and the mean incision length was 5 ± 1 mm. The median time to resume normal activities was 3 days (interquartile range 1–5 days), and the time to return to work was 4 days (interquartile range 2–7 days). Follow-up was available on over 98% of patients at 6 months. All patient-reported outcomes significantly improved over this period, with mean changes of −1.66 points (95% confidence interval [95% CI], −1.69 to −1.62) on the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire-Symptom Severity Scale, −1.06 points (95% CI, −1.10 to −1.03) on the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire-Functional Status Scale , −3.9 points (95% CI, −4.1 to −3.8) for pain severity, and 0.15 points (95% CI, 0.14–0.16) on the EuroQoL 5-Dimension 5-Level. Among all treated hands, patient satisfaction was 89.9% at 6 months. No serious adverse events occurred and the reoperation rate was 0.1%. Clinical outcomes were similar for unilateral and simultaneous bilateral procedures.ConclusionsIn this real-world cohort, UGCTR was associated with short recovery times, substantial improvement in symptoms and function, high patient satisfaction, low complication rates, and a reoperation rate of 0.1% through 6 months.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic III.Key words: Carpal tunnel syndrom, Carpal tunnel release, MISSION, UGCTR, Ultrasound

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common entrapment neuropathy, affecting an estimated 14% of individuals worldwide1 and accounting for 11% of hand specialist consultations.2 Persistent symptoms including pain, numbness, and paresthesias in the median nerve distribution may lead to functional limitations that negatively impact health-related quality of life3 and workplace productivity.4 Although nonsurgical management such as splinting, activity modification, and corticosteroid injections is often attempted, its effectiveness is inconsistent and symptom relief is generally transient.5

Carpal tunnel release (CTR) remains the definitive treatment for CTS because it directly decompresses the median nerve by dividing the transverse carpal ligament. Carpal tunnel release is safe and effective, with superior outcomes compared with nonsurgical options in most comparative studies.6 Although there is no consensus regarding the optimal CTR technique,7 less invasive approaches that minimize soft tissue disruption and facilitate faster recovery are more closely aligned with patient preferences.8

Ultrasound-guided carpal tunnel release (UGCTR) is performed through a small wrist incision and uses continuous visualization of the transverse carpal ligament and adjacent neurovascular structures to ensure procedural safety. Previous studies have demonstrated low complication rates, rapid symptomatic and functional improvement, and high patient satisfaction after UGCTR, with recovery times shorter than those commonly observed with traditional CTR approaches.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 The MISSION registry was developed to evaluate the generalizability and reproducibility of these findings in a large real-world cohort. This report from the MISSION registry presents the 6-month results from 882 patients representing 1,082 hands treated with UGCTR in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Ethics and study design

MISSION is a prospective, multicenter, observational registry established to collect real-world outcomes of patients undergoing UGCTR, open CTR, or endoscopic CTR in routine clinical practice in the United States. An independent central institutional review board approved the study, which was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06071468). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. This report presents the 6-month outcomes of patients treated with UGCTR in the MISSION registry. The reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.17

Participants

Adults aged 18 years or older were eligible for enrollment if they met the following three criteria supporting a CTS diagnosis. Treating physicians first established a clinical diagnosis of unilateral or bilateral idiopathic CTS based on medical history and physical examination. Patients were also required to have a CTS-6 score of ≥12 points in the target hand(s), which is a threshold associated with high diagnostic accuracy for CTS.18 Finally, diagnostic ultrasound had to demonstrate a median nerve cross-sectional area of at least 10 mm2 at the proximal carpal tunnel, which is a validated threshold that further supports the CTS diagnosis.18,19 Severe CTS was defined as a median nerve cross-sectional area of ≥15 mm2.20 Patients with bilateral CTS were eligible for simultaneous bilateral release if both hands satisfied all diagnostic requirements. All patients had previously attempted nonsurgical management, including splinting, corticosteroid injection, or activity modification. No additional inclusion or exclusion criteria were specified to maximize generalizability to real-world practice.

UGCTR procedure

All participating investigators were surgeons who completed a standardized training program consisting of didactic education and cadaver-based instruction. At study initiation, 77% (17 of 23 investigators) had performed fewer than 30 UGCTR procedures. Procedural setting (ie, outpatient office, ambulatory surgery center, or hospital) and anesthetic management were determined by surgeon preference and institutional practice. Procedures were performed under continuous real-time ultrasound guidance with a commercially available device (UltraGuideCTR system, Sonex Health) (Fig. 1). Procedural steps have been described elsewhere.14,21 Briefly, a single incision was made near the proximal wrist crease, and a blunt elevator was used to help create a working space. The device was then advanced, and integrated balloons were inflated to create additional space within the carpal tunnel and protect adjacent neurovascular structures. A recessed, centrally located cutting blade was deployed to divide the transverse carpal ligament from distal to proximal under sonographic visualization. Complete ligament release was confirmed under real-time ultrasound guidance and by probing the divided ligament. Wound management and postoperative care were determined by individual surgeon practice patterns.

Outcomes

Outcomes included time to return to normal activities and work, clinical outcome measures (symptoms, function, and pain), health-related quality of life, patient satisfaction, adverse events, and reoperations. Return to normal activities, return to work, and pain severity were recorded daily for the first 7 days to reduce recall bias during the early postoperative period. For patients receiving local anesthesia, procedural pain was measured for each treated hand on a 0–10 scale.22 Follow-up assessments were performed at 2 weeks and 1, 3, and 6 months. Symptom severity was assessed using the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire Symptom Severity Scale (BCTQ-SSS) on a per-hand basis. Functional status was assessed on a per-patient basis using the BCTQ Functional Status Scale (BCTQ-FSS). Both scales ranged from one to five, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity and functional impairment, respectively.23 Postoperative pain severity was measured for each treated hand on a 0–10 scale.22 Health-related quality of life was assessed for each patient using the EuroQoL 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire, which generates index scores that generally range from zero to one, although negative values are possible in severe health states.24 Patient satisfaction with the procedure was evaluated for each treated hand using a five-point Likert scale ranging from very dissatisfied (one) to very satisfied (five), with scores of four or five categorized as satisfied. Adverse events were classified according to their seriousness and assessed for their relationship with the procedure or device. Revisions included any subsequent procedure performed for treatment of iatrogenic injury, infection, or persistent/recurrent symptoms. An independent medical reviewer adjudicated adverse events using criteria specified by the US Food and Drug Administration.25 An independent Data Safety and Monitoring Board provided trial oversight.

Data analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages. Longitudinal changes in patient-reported outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed models that accounted for repeated measures over time and the correlation between hands in patients undergoing simultaneous bilateral release. Results were reported as baseline-adjusted least squares means with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). A prespecified subgroup analysis descriptively compared patients treated with unilateral versus simultaneous bilateral release. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute). Changes from baseline were considered statistically significant if the 2-sided P value was less than .05.7

Results

Enrollment

A total of 887 patients, representing 1,082 treated hands, were treated with UGCTR at 22 sites by 23 investigators in the United States between February 2024 and May 2025. Follow-up compliance was high, with 98.1% completing 6-month assessments. Additional follow-up is planned at 1 and 2 years.

Baseline patient characteristics

The cohort had a mean age of 58 ± 13 years, a mean body mass index of 31 ± 7 kg/m2, and 62.8% were women. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (46.2%), hyperlipidemia (39.9%), depression/anxiety (32.8%), and diabetes mellitus (16.5%). Among the 64.9% of patients who were employed, 58.2% worked in desk-based occupations, 21.9% in repetitive light manual work, and 20.0% in heavy manual work. Bilateral CTS was present in 62.1% of patients. Carpal tunnel syndrome symptom duration exceeded 6 months in 85.3% of the cohort and 2 years in 48.1%. The mean CTS-6 score was 19.0 ± 4.1, the mean cross-sectional area of the median nerve was 16.3 ± 4.7 mm2, with 57.2% of patients presenting with severe CTS (median nerve cross-sectional area ≥15 mm2 in at least one hand) (Table 1).

Procedural details

Procedures were performed in office settings (29.8%), ambulatory surgery centers (31.8%), or hospitals (38.4%). Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet anesthesia was used in 82.1% of procedures. The mean incision length was 5 ± 1 mm. The dominant hand was treated in 66.7% of patients, and 22.0% underwent simultaneous bilateral release. The mean procedural pain severity was 1.5 ± 2.2. Suture-free closure was performed in 90.0% of cases. All procedures were completed as intended, and no intraoperative complications were reported. Concomitant procedures were performed in 7.4% of patients, most commonly trigger finger/thumb release, cubital tunnel release, and cyst removal.

Postoperative recovery

Opioids were prescribed to 34.1% of patients for postoperative pain. Postoperative opioid use was reported in 17.4% of patients overall, with a median duration of use of 1 day. The median time to resume normal activities was 3 days (IQR, 1–5 days). Among employed patients, the median time to return to work was 4 days (IQR, 2–7 days). Desk-based workers returned in a median of 3 days, whereas those in repetitive light manual and heavy manual occupations returned in a median of 5 days. By 2 weeks, 92.3% of patients had resumed normal daily activities and 80.0% had returned to work (Fig. 2).

Patient-reported outcome measures

Symptom severity significantly improved following UGCTR, with BCTQ-SSS scores decreasing from 3.06 ± 0.72 at baseline to 1.75 ± 0.58 at 2 weeks and 1.41 ± 0.51 at 6 months, corresponding to a mean change of −1.66 (95% CI, −1.69 to −1.62; P < .001). Functional status also improved, with BCTQ-FSS scores decreasing from 2.40 ± 0.78 at baseline to 1.84 ± 0.65 at 2 weeks and 1.33 ± 0.46 at 6 months, representing a mean change of −1.06 (95% CI, −1.10 to −1.03; P < .001) (Fig. 3). Pain severity rapidly declined after UGCTR, decreasing from 4.8 ± 2.9 at baseline to 1.3 ± 1.7 at 2 weeks and 0.85 ± 1.63 at 6 months (mean change, −3.9; 95% CI, −4.1 to −3.8; P < .001) (Fig. 4). Health-related quality of life as assessed by the EQ-5D-5L increased from 0.73 ± 0.22 at baseline to 0.84 ± 0.16 at 2 weeks and 0.89 ± 0.16 at 6 months (mean change, 0.15; 95% CI, 0.14–0.16; P < .001) (Fig. 5). Among all treated hands, patient satisfaction was 89.9% at 6 months.

Adverse events

No serious adverse events were reported. Seven nonserious adverse events occurred (0.8% per patient; 0.6% per hand). These included one superficial infection, one wound dehiscence in a nonsutured patient managed with observation, one case of postoperative bleeding in a nonsutured patient who returned the same day for a dressing change, one superficial hematoma managed with observation, one case of pillar pain managed with corticosteroid injection and physical therapy, one patient with postprocedural pain that resolved with conservative care, and one patient who underwent revision with open CTR approximately 6 months after treatment, at which time scar tissue bridging between the transverse carpal ligament leaflets was observed to be compressing the median nerve. This was the only revision procedure in the study (0.1% through 6 months). No nerve, tendon, muscle, or vascular injuries, anesthetic reactions, or deep infections were observed.

Subgroup analysis of unilateral versus simultaneous bilateral releases

The median time to resume normal activity was 2 days after unilateral release and 3 days after simultaneous bilateral release, with return to work of 4 days and 5 days, respectively. Changes in patient-reported outcomes through 6 months were similar between the subgroups for BCTQ-SSS (−1.7 vs −1.6), BCTQ-FSS (−1.1 vs −1.0), pain severity (−4.0 vs −3.6), and EQ-5D-5L (0.15 in each). Patient satisfaction was 91.0% and 87.9%, respectively, and adverse events and revisions were rare in both subgroups (Table 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this article reports the largest prospective evaluation of UGCTR to date, with 887 patients and 1,082 treated hands across 22 sites in the United States. In this real-world cohort, the procedure was associated with short recovery times, substantial improvements in symptoms and function, high patient satisfaction, low complication rates, and a reoperation rate of 0.1% through 6 months. Patients treated with unilateral or simultaneous bilateral release experienced similar treatment benefits. These findings support the safety and effectiveness of UGCTR for the treatment of CTS in real-world clinical settings.

Several features of the UGCTR technique may help explain the favorable outcomes observed in this registry. The small wrist incision, which averaged 5 mm, limits soft tissue disruption and may reduce postoperative discomfort compared with open approaches that require larger incisions, which have been associated with higher rates of wound-related complications.26 The widespread use of wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet is associated with lower anesthetic risk and may broaden suitability for patients with medical comorbidities. Continuous ultrasound guidance provides direct visualization of the transverse carpal ligament and adjacent neurovascular structures throughout the procedure, which enhances procedural precision and may contribute to the low incidence of complications. Taken together, these technical attributes likely contributed to the low rate of complications, rapid recovery, and significant symptom relief observed in the registry.

Patients with CTS commonly present with bilateral symptoms.27 In this study, 22.0% of the cohort were treated with simultaneous bilateral release. For individuals with bilateral disease, simultaneous bilateral release may reduce the overall treatment burden by consolidating perioperative recovery into a single care episode. Prior economic analyses have shown that simultaneous bilateral CTR is associated with lower total costs, greater overall effectiveness,28 and fewer postoperative complications29 compared with staged procedures. In the current study, simultaneous bilateral UGCTR procedures were well tolerated, with postoperative recovery and complication rates similar to those of patients treated with unilateral release. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting comparable outcomes between unilateral and simultaneous bilateral UGCTR.10,13,21 Collectively, the evidence supports the use of simultaneous bilateral UGCTR for treatment of bilateral CTS in routine clinical practice.

The findings of this study have practical implications for patients, providers, and health care systems. For patients, the short recovery period and early return to work observed after UGCTR may lessen the financial impact of time away from employment, which is particularly relevant given that nearly two-thirds of the cohort were employed at the time of treatment. The improvements in quality of life, high satisfaction rates, and low risk of complications further indicate that the procedure provided patient-centered benefits. For providers, these results offer practical guidance for counseling patients about expected recovery, with a median return to activities in 3 days and return to work between 3 and 5 days, depending on occupational demands. The ability to perform UGCTR under wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet in most cases reduces reliance on anesthesia services and may improve procedural efficiency and reduce resource utilization. From a health care system perspective, techniques that enable rapid functional recovery without compromising safety may reduce overall health care expenditures, given the CTS-related health care costs of over $2 billion annually in the United States.30, 31, 32, 33 The minimal incision approach and early recovery associated with UGCTR align with value-based care initiatives and patient preferences for smaller incisions that allow faster return to normal function.8

This study was associated with several strengths. Notably, this study included the largest known prospective cohort of patients treated with UGCTR reported to date, with high follow-up compliance at 6 months. Because patients were treated in routine clinical settings without restrictive eligibility criteria, patient characteristics were typical of individuals with CTS in contemporary practice; thus, the study findings are generalizable to the CTS population. The improvements in recovery metrics, symptom severity, functional status, quality of life, and patient satisfaction after UGCTR are consistent with previous studies9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 and indicate clinical benefit across multiple domains. This study also had several important limitations. First, although the low complication rate and magnitude of clinical improvement were similar to those of previously published studies of open and endoscopic CTR,34,35 this analysis did not directly compare UGCTR with other CTR techniques. However, propensity-matched analyses comparing UGCTR with open and endoscopic CTR have reported similar patient-reported outcomes and complication rates, with lower rates of wound-related symptoms after UGCTR.36,37 Second, the present report included outcomes collected through 6 months of follow-up. Although this period reflects the interval during which most recovery after CTR typically occurs,38 longer-term follow-up will be informative to determine the durability of symptom relief. Previous studies of UGCTR have demonstrated long-term effectiveness over mean follow-up periods ranging from 1 to 4 years.9, 10, 11,14 Patient follow-up in the MISSION registry is ongoing through 2 years to determine treatment durability. Third, because neither direct medical costs nor indirect economic measures were collected, the potential financial impact of UGCTR could not be evaluated. Fourth, although investigators completed standardized training in UGCTR before enrolling patients, differences in technique, experience, and perioperative management may have influenced outcomes. Finally, UGCTR in this study was performed using a specific technique that creates additional space within the carpal tunnel and protects adjacent neurovascular structures. Therefore, the results of this trial may not be generalizable to other UGCTR techniques.

Overall, the results of this study demonstrate the safety and effectiveness of UGCTR in real-world practice, as well as the comparability of outcomes between unilateral and simultaneous bilateral procedures.
