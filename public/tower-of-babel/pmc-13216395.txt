Mapping the research landscape on diabetes mellitus and periodontal disease: a scientometric study
Authors: Angela Repanovici, Mihaela Emanuela Vîlcu, Ileana Pantea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To map the scientific landscape linking diabetes mellitus and periodontal disease using bibliometric methods, identify major thematic clusters, and examine how research supports clinical and educational perspectives relevant to integrated diabetes–oral health research. The central research question was: What are the dominant knowledge structures and interdisciplinary connections in the literature on diabetes mellitus and oral health? A bibliometric analysis was conducted using the Web of Science Core Collection (1995–2024). The search strategy combined diabetes related and oral health–related terms. After applying the eligibility criteria, 1,058 publications were included. Keyword preprocessing involved synonym unification, abbreviation harmonization, and manual validation. VOSviewer v1.6.18 was used for keyword co occurrence mapping. Potential sources of bias related to database selection and keyword variability were acknowledged. Eight thematic clusters were identified, covering periodontal disease, prevention, epidemiology, systemic comorbidities, microbiome research, quality of life, clinical interventions, and biomarkers. Cluster structure remained stable across sensitivity thresholds. The literature on diabetes and oral health exhibits recurring thematic patterns and interconnected research domains. While bibliometric clustering reflects co occurrence structures rather than conceptual maturity, the findings provide an analytical overview of how research areas relate to one another. The findings indicate recurring thematic patterns and interconnected research domains. The identified clusters outline priority domains relevant to risk assessment, prevention, and interdisciplinary care. Although bibliometric findings do not directly inform clinical decision making, they highlight areas where integrated diabetes–oral health strategies may be strengthened.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus affects over 500 million adults worldwide and represents a major global public health challenge contributing to substantial morbidity through its metabolic and systemic complications. Among these, periodontal disease, the most clinically significant oral complication—particularly periodontal disease—has received increasing attention due to its bidirectional relationship with diabetes (Dodds et al. 2005; Ferizi et al. 2018; Lamster 2017). Persistent hyperglycemia disrupts immune regulation, shifts the composition of the oral microbiota, and intensifies inflammatory activity (Lalla and Papapanou 2011; Mealey and Rethman 2003; Mealey and Oates 2006). Conversely, periodontal inflammation may exacerbate systemic inflammatory burden and negatively influence glycemic control, reinforcing the concept of a two-way interaction between the two diseases (Chapple et al. 2013; Giannobile 2013; Preshaw et al. 2012).

Over the past decades, research exploring the diabetes–oral health relationship has expanded considerably, encompassing epidemiological studies, mechanistic investigations, microbiome analyses, salivary biomarker research, and interventional trials (Verhulst et al. 2019; Subramaniam et al. 2015). Evidence suggests that periodontal disease is associated not only with diabetes progression but also with cardiovascular, renal, and respiratory comorbidities, highlighting its relevance within chronic disease management frameworks (Hujoel 2009; Petersen and Ogawa 2012; Bahekar et al. 2007; Scannapieco and Mylotte 2001). At the same time, studies on patient awareness and preventive behaviours indicate persistent gaps in oral health literacy among individuals with diabetes, underscoring the need for integrated educational strategies (Babu et al. 2018; Javed et al. 2012; Javed et al. 2013; Lockhart et al. 2012).

Despite the clinical and public health importance of this topic, the rapidly growing and interdisciplinary nature of the literature makes it difficult to identify dominant themes, conceptual gaps, and emerging research directions. Bibliometric analysis provides a rigorous and reproducible method for mapping scientific landscapes, quantifying research output, and visualizing thematic structures through co-occurrence networks and cluster analysis.

This study aims to perform a bibliometric and thematic analysis of the international literature on diabetes mellitus and periodontal disease (1995–2024), identifying major research clusters, central concepts, and emerging directions to support clinical, translational, and educational initiatives. Although numerous narrative and systematic reviews have examined specific clinical or mechanistic aspects of the diabetes–oral health relationship, these approaches are inherently limited in scope. They synthesize evidence within predefined questions but cannot capture how research topics are structurally organized, how conceptual domains interact, or how interdisciplinary themes evolve. No existing review provides a field-level mapping of the literature or identifies how established areas (e.g., periodontal inflammation) connect with emerging domains such as microbiome science, salivary diagnostics, or patient-reported outcomes. This absence represents a methodological gap. A bibliometric analysis addresses this gap by offering a data-driven visualization of thematic clusters, co-occurrence patterns, and structural linkages across nearly three decades of research. By doing so, it complements traditional evidence-synthesis methods and provides a broader analytical perspective on how the field is organized and where future research opportunities may lie.

Materials and methods

The central hypothesis was that literature exhibits identifiable thematic clusters and structural relationships that can be mapped using bibliometric methods.

Data source

We used the Web of Science Core Collection because it offers high-quality indexing, standardized citation structures, and broad coverage of peer-reviewed literature relevant to this topic. All records were retrieved on 20 October 2025, ensuring reproducibility of the dataset.

The Web of Science (WoS) Core Collection was selected as the exclusive data source because of its rigorous indexing standards, structured citation metadata, and broad coverage of peer‑reviewed journals in medicine, dentistry, and public health. WoS is widely used in bibliometric studies due to its consistency in author keywords, controlled vocabulary, and citation linkages, which support reliable co‑occurrence and network analyses.

We acknowledge that relying on a single database may influence the thematic structure of the results, as database-specific coverage can affect the visibility of regional journals, emerging research areas, or non‑English publications. This potential indexing bias is addressed in the limitations section. The Web of Science Core Collection was selected due to its standardized citation indexing and compatibility with bibliometric software. However, we acknowledge that relying on a single database may exclude relevant studies indexed in Scopus or PubMed. This choice may introduce coverage bias, particularly for regional journals or emerging research areas. The implications of this limitation are addressed in the Discussion.

The research was conducted at Transilvania University of Brașov, Faculty of Product Design and Environment and Faculty of Medicine.

Data extraction and preprocessing

Keyword normalization followed a structured protocol. Synonyms were merged when terms referred to the same clinical or conceptual entity (e.g., periodontitis and ‘periodontal disease’), based on established terminology in periodontology and diabetology. Keyword preprocessing followed a structured protocol. Manual validation was performed independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through predefined criteria (semantic equivalence, clinical relevance, and consistency with established terminology). A 5% random sample was selected to balance feasibility with adequate representation of the dataset; this proportion aligns with previous bibliometric validation practices.

Terms were excluded if they were non‑informative (e.g., ‘study, analysis’), overly broad (e.g., ‘health’), or methodological descriptors not contributing to thematic interpretation.

Abbreviations were harmonized by mapping each abbreviation to its full form (e.g., HbA1c’ ↔ ‘glycated hemoglobin’) to avoid duplicate representations.

All normalization steps were manually reviewed by two independent researchers, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Keyword selection

The analysis used all author keywords and Keywords Plus. Terms with fewer than 5 occurrences were excluded to improve network clarity. The final dataset included 398 unique terms.

Both Author Keywords and Keywords Plus were included to ensure comprehensive coverage of thematic concepts. Author Keywords represent the authors’ intended focus, while Keywords Plus are algorithmically generated from cited references and may capture broader or emerging topics.

Because Keywords Plus may introduce terms with indirect thematic relevance, we acknowledge their potential influence on cluster formation. To mitigate this, all terms underwent preprocessing, synonym unification, and manual validation. Cluster labels were subsequently reviewed by two domain experts to ensure conceptual coherence.

A minimum occurrence threshold of 5 was selected to balance network interpretability with adequate thematic coverage. This threshold is consistent with established bibliometric practices in oral‑health and diabetes research, where thresholds between 3 and 10 are commonly applied to reduce noise while preserving conceptual structure.

To ensure that the chosen threshold did not distort cluster formation, sensitivity analyses were conducted at thresholds of 3 and 10. The stability of cluster membership across these thresholds supports the robustness of the selected cutoff.

Full counting was applied in the co‑occurrence analysis, meaning that each keyword occurrence contributed equally to the network. Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of cluster structure, alternative minimum occurrence thresholds (3 and 10) were tested. Cluster membership, density, and centrality patterns were compared across thresholds.

Expert validation

Cluster labels were first generated algorithmically and then reviewed independently by two domain experts in periodontology and diabetology. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus, following established practices for thematic validation in bibliometric research (Chapple et al. 2013; Lalla and Papapanou 2011).

Preliminary cluster labels were generated based on the dominant terms within each cluster and their semantic coherence. Two domain experts independently reviewed the full list of terms and proposed labels using predefined criteria: conceptual dominance, clinical or mechanistic relevance, and consistency with established terminology. Discrepancies were resolved through a consensus discussion, ensuring that final labels reflected both data‑driven patterns and expert interpretation.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the corpus

The search strategy identified 1,058 eligible publications on the relationship between diabetes mellitus and oral health (1995–2024). The mean number of citations per article was 34, with a median of 18. The most cited publication was the WHO/FAO - WHO – World Health Organization, FAO – Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, technical report on diet and chronic disease prevention, with 1,240 citations (World Health Organization; Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations 2003).

The reported mean citation count (34 citations per article) and median value (18 citations) refer to the entire set of 1,058 publications included in the analysis after applying eligibility criteria and removing duplicates. These metrics provide an overall indication of the bibliometric impact of the corpus. Reviews were included because bibliometric mapping aims to capture the conceptual structure of a field, and review articles contribute meaningfully to thematic co-occurrence patterns.

The annual number of publications increased steadily after 2005, reflecting growing interdisciplinary interest in the diabetes–oral health nexus.

Keyword co-occurrence analysis

After preprocessing and applying a minimum occurrence threshold of 5, a total of 398 unique terms were included in the co-occurrence map. VOSviewer- Visualization of Similarities Viewer (software for scientometric mapping) identified eight thematic clusters, each representing a distinct research direction within the field (Fig. 1).

Cluster 1: Diabetes and periodontal disease (72 terms; 436 articles)

This thematic group encompassed key concepts including diabetes mellitus, periodontal disease, inflammatory mechanisms, and metabolic regulation. These terms reflect the well-established bidirectional relationship between diabetes and periodontal disease, supported by numerous clinical and mechanistic studies (Chapple et al. 2013, Giannobile 2013, Lalla and Papapanou 2011, Mealey and Rethman 2003, Preshaw et al. 2012).

Cluster 2: Oral hygiene and prevention (50 terms; 278 articles)

Key terms included oral hygiene, tooth brushing, patient education, and preventive care. Studies in this cluster emphasize behavioral determinants and the need for targeted educational interventions in diabetic populations (Babu et al. 2018, Javed et al. 2012, Javed et al. 2013, Lockhart et al. 2012).

Cluster 3: Epidemiology and population studies (60 terms; 312 articles)

Representative terms such as prevalence, risk factors, cross-sectional study, and case-control study highlight the epidemiological burden of oral complications in diabetes and the global variability of disease patterns (Hujoel 2009, Petersen and Ogawa 2012).

Cluster 4: Systemic complications and comorbidities (45 terms; 241 articles)

Terms including cardiovascular disease, obesity, systemic inflammation, and renal disease reflect the interconnected nature of chronic conditions and the systemic impact of periodontal inflammation (Friedewald et al. 2009, Bahekar et al. 2007, Scannapieco and Mylotte 2001).

Cluster 5: Microbiome and pathogenesis (40 terms; 206 articles)

This cluster contained terms such as oral microbiota, Porphyromonas gingivalis, biofilm, and pathogenesis. These studies explore microbial dysbiosis and inflammatory pathways linking diabetes and periodontal disease.

Cluster 6: Quality of life and patient perception (30 terms; 159 articles)

Terms such as quality of life, self-care, health literacy, and patient perception indicate a growing interest in patient-reported outcomes and personalized care strategies [13, 24, 30].

Cluster 7: Dental treatment and clinical interventions (40 terms; 223 articles)

Representative terms included periodontal therapy, clinical outcomes, scaling and root planing, and follow-up. Evidence from this cluster supports the beneficial effects of periodontal treatment on systemic inflammation and metabolic control (Giannobile 2013, Mealey and Oates 2006).

Cluster 8: Biomarkers and laboratory research (61 terms; 294 articles)

Key terms such as HbA1c, CRP, oxidative stress, and salivary biomarkers reflect the increasing use of laboratory indicators and saliva-based diagnostics in monitoring diabetic patients (Coelho et al. 2020, Guggenheimer et al. 2003, Lamster et al. 2008, Siudikiene et al. 2008). Across the eight clusters, total link strength ranged from 2,145 to 6,982, with Cluster 1 (diabetes–periodontitis) exhibiting the highest structural centrality. Density values ranged from 0.21 to 0.47, indicating varying degrees of thematic cohesion. Cluster 5 (microbiome and pathogenesis) showed the highest internal density, suggesting a tightly interconnected research domain, whereas Cluster 6 (quality of life) appeared more peripheral.

Discussion

This bibliometric analysis provides a comprehensive overview of the interdisciplinary research landscape linking diabetes mellitus and oral health over 30 years. Interpretation of citation statistics requires caution, as citation counts are time‑dependent and tend to favor older publications with longer visibility. Consequently, highly cited works may not fully represent contemporary research influence or emerging scientific directions. To address this limitation, citation metrics were interpreted in conjunction with co‑occurrence patterns and cluster structures, providing a more balanced assessment of the field’s development. The eight thematic clusters identified reflect the conceptual, clinical, and translational evolution of the field and highlight areas of convergence between dentistry, endocrinology, microbiology, and public health.

The centrality of the terms diabetes mellitus, periodontitis, and oral health confirms the well-established bidirectional relationship between diabetes and periodontal disease, extensively documented in mechanistic and clinical studies (Chapple et al. 2013, Giannobile 2013, Lalla and Papapanou 2011, Mealey and Rethman 2003, Preshaw et al. 2012). Chronic hyperglycemia promotes impaired immune responses, dysbiosis, and exaggerated inflammatory pathways, while periodontal inflammation contributes to systemic inflammatory load and may negatively influence glycemic control. The prominence of this cluster underscores its foundational role in the field.

The strong representation of prevention-related terms (Cluster 2) indicates persistent gaps in oral health literacy among diabetic patients, consistent with previous studies showing limited awareness of oral complications and suboptimal hygiene behaviors (Babu et al. 2018, Javed et al. 2012, Javed et al. 2013, Lockhart et al. 2012). This highlights the need for integrated educational strategies within both dental and medical care pathways.

Epidemiological and population-based research (Cluster 3) remains a major component of the field, providing essential data on prevalence, risk factors, and demographic patterns. These findings align with global assessments of periodontal disease burden and its association with chronic conditions (Hujoel 2009, Petersen and Ogawa 2012).

The cluster on systemic comorbidities (Cluster 4) reinforces the interconnected nature of chronic diseases. Evidence linking periodontal disease with cardiovascular, renal, and respiratory conditions (Friedewald et al. 2009, Bahekar et al. 2007, Scannapieco and Mylotte 2001, Rusu et al. 2013) supports the integration of oral health into broader chronic disease management frameworks.

Emerging research on the oral microbiome and pathogenesis (Cluster 5) reflects advances in molecular diagnostics and microbial profiling. Dysbiosis involving pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis has been implicated in both periodontal destruction and systemic inflammation, offering new avenues for therapeutic intervention.

The presence of clusters focused on quality of life (Cluster 6) and clinical interventions (Cluster 7) indicates a shift toward patient-centered care and evidence-based periodontal therapy. Studies have shown that periodontal treatment may improve metabolic control and reduce systemic inflammatory markers in diabetic patients (Giannobile 2013, Mealey and Oates 2006).

Finally, the growing interest in biomarkers and laboratory research (Cluster 8) highlights the potential of salivary diagnostics, oxidative stress markers, and inflammatory mediators for monitoring disease progression and treatment response (Coelho et al. 2020, Guggenheimer et al. 2003, Lamster et al. 2008, Siudikiene et al. 2008).

The thematic structure identified in this study aligns with previous narrative and systematic reviews emphasizing the bidirectional diabetes–periodontitis relationship (Chapple et al. 2013, Giannobile 2013, Lalla and Papapanou 2011, Preshaw et al. 2012, Repanovici et al. 2024, Stefan et al. 2017). However, the bibliometric approach provides a broader and more objective mapping of research evolution, revealing the emergence of new domains such as microbiome science, salivary biomarkers, and patient-reported outcomes.

The stability of cluster structure across sensitivity thresholds confirms the robustness of the thematic landscape and supports the validity of the eight-cluster model.

The bibliometric structure highlights several conceptual shifts in the field. While early research focused primarily on mechanistic and epidemiological aspects, more recent clusters emphasize emerging paradigms such as microbiome‑driven approaches, salivary biomarker development, and patient‑reported outcomes. The growing prominence of prevention‑ and quality‑of‑life‑related terms reflects a transition toward patient‑centered and interdisciplinary care models. These trends demonstrate an evolution from traditional clinical frameworks toward more holistic and translational perspectives.

Inter‑cluster analysis showed that the thematic areas are interconnected rather than isolated. Terms such as inflammation, glycemic control, and microbiome exhibited high cross‑cluster co‑occurrence, linking periodontal disease with systemic complications and mechanistic research. Cluster 1 and Cluster 4 displayed the strongest inter‑cluster connectivity, underscoring the central role of periodontal inflammation in systemic metabolic pathways. The microbiome cluster also acted as a bridge between mechanistic and clinical domains, reflecting its emerging integrative role in the field.

The thematic clusters identified in this study map directly onto established clinical decision‑making frameworks. Clusters related to periodontal disease and systemic complications correspond to risk assessment and comorbidity management, while prevention‑ and quality‑of‑life‑focused clusters highlight the importance of patient‑centered education and behavioral interventions. Emerging clusters on microbiome research and salivary biomarkers point toward translational opportunities in precision diagnostics and personalized monitoring. Together, these interlinked clusters provide a structured framework that can support interdisciplinary clinical pathways and inform curriculum development. While the clusters identified in this study align with well-established clinical themes, bibliometric mapping reflects patterns of co-occurrence rather than causal or clinical relationships. Therefore, interpretations must remain descriptive. The prominence of periodontal disease and systemic inflammation within the network indicates their centrality in the literature, but this does not imply clinical primacy. Similarly, emerging clusters such as microbiome research and salivary biomarkers suggest growing scientific interest, yet bibliometric data alone cannot determine their translational readiness.

The exclusive use of the Web of Science Core Collection was motivated by its standardized metadata structure and compatibility with bibliometric software. However, this choice may introduce indexing bias, as certain regional journals or emerging research outlets are indexed only in Scopus or PubMed. As a result, the thematic structure identified in this study primarily reflects literature visible within WoS, and the findings should be interpreted in light of this methodological limitation.

Although the Web of Science Core Collection provides standardized metadata and strong compatibility with bibliometric tools, relying on a single database may limit the comprehensiveness of the dataset. Relevant studies indexed exclusively in Scopus, PubMed, or regional databases may not be captured, potentially influencing the visibility of emerging research areas or non-English publications. This constraint introduces an inherent indexing bias, and the thematic structure identified in this study should therefore be interpreted within the context of WoS coverage.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of a rigorous bibliometric methodology, including preprocessing, cluster-level metrics, sensitivity analysis, and expert validation. The 30-year time window and large dataset (1,058 articles) enhance the comprehensiveness of the analysis.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The use of a single database (Web of Science) may introduce indexing bias, potentially underrepresenting regional or non-English literature. Analyses relying on keyword frequency may still be affected by inconsistencies in terminology, even after extensive harmonization Citation metrics may favor older publications, introducing survivorship bias. Finally, bibliometric analysis maps research structure but cannot assess causality or clinical effectiveness. In addition to database selection and citation bias, several methodological constraints are inherent to bibliometric research. Author-assigned keywords may vary in specificity, and terminology inconsistencies may persist despite normalization. Algorithm-driven clustering may group terms based on statistical proximity rather than conceptual meaning. These factors should be considered when interpreting thematic structures. In addition to database coverage constraints, bibliometric analyses are limited by variability in author-assigned keywords, inconsistencies in terminology despite normalization, and the algorithmic nature of cluster formation. These factors may influence cluster boundaries and thematic interpretation. Furthermore, bibliometric methods provide structural insights but cannot assess causality, clinical effectiveness, or the quality of individual studies.

Conclusions

This bibliometric analysis provides a comprehensive and reproducible mapping of scientific literature addressing the relationship between diabetes mellitus and oral health over 30 years. The eight thematic clusters identified—ranging from the bidirectional diabetes–periodontitis relationship to prevention, systemic comorbidities, microbiome research, patient-reported outcomes, clinical interventions, and biomarker development— indicate the interdisciplinary maturity of this field.

The centrality of terms such as diabetes mellitus, periodontitis, and oral health suggests the conceptual core of the domain, while the emergence of clusters related to salivary biomarkers, microbiome dysbiosis, and quality of life reflects evolving research priorities. The stability of cluster structure across sensitivity thresholds further supports the robustness of the thematic landscape.

The thematic clusters derived from this analysis represent patterns of term co-occurrence rather than fixed conceptual divisions within the field. Bibliometric mapping provides an analytical representation of thematic organization, but the field remains dynamic, with emerging areas such as microbiome research and salivary diagnostics continuing to reshape its structure. Therefore, statements regarding field maturity or stability must be understood within the exploratory nature of bibliometric analysis. This bibliometric analysis provides an exploratory mapping of research linking diabetes mellitus and oral health. The eight identified clusters indicate recurring thematic patterns rather than definitive conceptual boundaries. The findings suggest areas of concentrated research activity and highlight emerging domains such as microbiome science and salivary diagnostics. As bibliometric analyses are descriptive, conclusions regarding field maturity should be interpreted cautiously.

Overall, the findings highlight the need for integrated clinical pathways, improved oral health education for diabetic patients, and continued development of non-invasive diagnostic tools. This study offers a solid foundation for future systematic reviews, interdisciplinary curricula, and translational initiatives aimed at strengthening the integration of oral health within chronic disease management.
