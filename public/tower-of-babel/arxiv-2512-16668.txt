Obstacle Mean Curvature Flow: Efficient Approximation and Convergence Analysis
Fabius Krämer, Tim Laux

Abstract
. We introduce a simple and efficient numerical method to compute mean curvature flow with obstacles. The method augments the Merrimam-Bence-Osher scheme with a pointwise update that enforces the constraint and therefore retains the computational complexity of the original scheme. Remarkably, this naive scheme inherits both crucial structural properties of obstacle mean curvature flow: a geometric comparison principle and a minimizing movements interpretation. The latter immediately implies the unconditional stability of the scheme. Based on the comparison principle we prove the convergence of the scheme to the viscosity solution of obstacle mean curvature flow. Moreover, using the minimizing movements interpretation, we show convergence of a spatially discrete model. Finally, we present numerical experiments for a physical model that inspired this work. Keywords: Mean Curvature Flow, Obstacle Problem, MBO, Diffusion Generated Motion, Thresholding, Viscosity Solution, Gradient Flow Mathematical Subject Classification (MSC2020) : 53E10; 74H15 (Primary); 35D40; 35R35; 49Q05 (Secondary)



1.
Introduction

Motivated by recent physical studies
[
26
]
we introduce a novel approximation to mean curvature flow with obstacles. To that aim we adapt the famous Merriman-Bence-Osher (MBO) scheme
[
28
]
that is well known to discretize mean curvature flow. The new scheme, although computationally cheap, inherits the properties of unconditional stability, monotonicity, has a minimizing movement interpretation and satisfies the obstacle constraints exactly. We prove convergence of our method to obstacle mean curvature flow in the viscosity sense. Furthermore, we show possibilities of space discretizations of the scheme and their convergence.

The obstacle mean curvature flow describes the geometric evolution of a hypersurface whose normal velocity
V
is given by its scalar mean curvature
H
, except at points where this motion would cause the surface to penetrate the obstacle. In such regions, the evolution is constrained so that the hypersurface may remain in contact with, but does not enter, the obstacle. Formally, this equations reads

V=\begin{cases}\max\{0,-H\}&\text{on }\partial\Phi,\\
-H&\text{in }(\Phi\cup\Psi)^{c},\\
\min\{0,-H\}&\text{on }\partial\Psi,\end{cases}

where
\Phi
is the inner and
\Psi
the outer obstacle (see Fig.
1
).

Obstacle mean curvature flow is a model flow related to phenomena in biology, for example cell motility, see
[
10
]
. Another very common flow in biology is positive mean curvature flow
V=\max\{0,-H\}
which only evolves the surface in positive curved parts and is for example a model for wound healing and tissue repairing
[
2
,
1
]
. This is a special case of obstacle mean curvature flow. Indeed, one can reconstruct any given positive mean curvature flow by placing obstacles precisely at the negatively curved regions
[
3
]
.

From a mathematical perspective there are several works regarding the existence, uniqueness and regularity of mean curvature flow with obstacles, see for example
[
27
,
3
,
15
]
.

Our main motivation for introducing an efficient scheme for the approximation of obstacle mean curvature flow is the domain convexification model for invasion processes of Martin–Calle and Pierre–Louis
[
26
]
. Their model describes for example spreading of species in ecological models, phase transition in magnetization or de-adhesion transition, which have in common that they are all to some extent curvature driven motions. In their work
[
26
]
they are mainly interested in finding the density ratio of the initial conditions such that the flow floods the whole domain.
Their initial condition is a union of disks that are randomly seeded in the plane.
To roughly approximate the positive curvature flow from the union of disks, they use that the flow converges to the (mean-)convex hull. Hence, they only compute the convex hull of clusters in every iteration of the scheme but not the dynamics that lead to it. We are interested in filling this gap by providing a simple scheme to efficiently compute the precise dynamics.

For given obstacles
\Phi,\Psi\subset\Omega
(where
\Omega:=\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\Omega:=\mathbb{T}^{d}
) and an initial phase described by
u^{0}:\Omega\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
we investigate the following iterative scheme.

Step 1

Diffusion: Compute for fixed diffusion time
h

u_{h}^{\ell+1/3}=e^{-h\Delta}u^{\ell}

where we denote by
e^{-h\Delta}
the heat semi-group (with the sign convention such that the Laplacian
\Delta
has non-negative eigenvalues).

Step 2

Threshold: Reconstruct a
\pm 1
-valued function by setting

u_{h}^{\ell+2/3}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }e^{-h\Delta}u_{h}^{\ell+1/3}>0,\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Step 3

Update on obstacles: Ensure that the obstacle constraint is satisfied by setting

u_{h}^{\ell+1}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in\Phi,\\
u_{h}^{\ell+2/3}(x)&\text{if }x\in\Omega\setminus(\Phi\cup\Psi),\\
-1&\text{if }x\in\Psi.\end{cases}

Note that Step 3 of the scheme can also be written like

u_{h}^{\ell+1}=\chi_{\Phi}\vee-\chi_{\Psi}\wedge u^{\ell+2/3},

where we denote

\chi_{\Phi}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in\Phi,\\
-1&\text{if }x\notin\Phi,\end{cases},\quad\chi_{\Psi}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in\Psi,\\
-1&\text{if }x\notin\Psi,\end{cases}

as well as
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
and
a\vee b=\max\{a,b\}
.
If the obstacles are non-trivial, i.e.
\Phi\cup\Psi\neq\emptyset
, one can use the fact that
u^{\ell+1/3}\in(-1,1)
and denote
\mathbbold{1}_{S}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in S,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
for any
S\subset\Omega
and
x\in\Omega
to shortly write the above scheme as

(1)

u^{\ell+1}=\mathrm{sign}(e^{-h\Delta}u^{\ell}+\mathbbold{1}_{\Phi}-\mathbbold{1}_{\Psi}).

One recognizes that the scheme without Step 3 is exactly the thresholding scheme of Merriman, Bence and Osher
[
28
]
. The convergence of the thresholding scheme to mean curvature flow (as
h\downarrow 0
) is a well studied subject
[
13
,
4
,
14
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
8
,
21
,
18
]
. Hence, heuristically, the above scheme (
1
) moves with a discrete mean curvature motion away from the obstacles while Step 3 guarantees the constraints imposed by the obstacles. So it should be of no surprise that the above scheme indeed converges to obstacle mean curvature flow. This will be proven later on in the viscosity solution setting, see Theorem
2
. One can view the scheme also from a different viewpoint to underline that our treatment of the obstacle is meaningful. In Lemma
2
we show that adapting the minimizing movement interpretation of
[
12
]
to our problem yields that the scheme (
1
) is equivalent to

u^{\ell+1}\in\argmin_{u:\Omega\rightarrow\{-1,1\}}E_{h}(u)+\frac{1}{2h}d_{h}^{2}(u,u^{\ell})\quad\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\quad u\equiv 1\text{ on }\Phi\text{ and }u\equiv-1\text{ on }\Psi,

with the energy and distance given by

\displaystyle E_{h}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Omega}(1-u)e^{-h\Delta}(1+u)\,dx,

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2h}d_{h}^{2}(u,\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Omega}(u-\tilde{u})e^{-h\Delta}(u-\tilde{u})\,dx.

Remember, mean curvature flow is the gradient flow of the perimeter and it is well known that
E_{h}
converges to the perimeter functional. So, the minimizing movement interpretation reflects on the discrete level the gradient flow structure of obstacle mean curvature flow by simply adding the obstacles as constraints in each step.
Using the minimizing movement interpretation to introduce constraints in the discrete flow has shown already to be sucessful in the case of volume constraints
[
16
,
19
]
.

Furthermore, the minimizing movement interpretation (
15
) immediately implies unconditional stability of the scheme. Another important property of the scheme is that because Step 1, Step 2 and Step 3 above are monotone, the whole scheme is monotone. This means, if
u_{h}^{\ell}
are the iterates with initial condition
u^{0}
, if
\tilde{u}_{h}^{\ell}
the ones with
\tilde{u}^{0}
and if
u^{0}\leq\tilde{u}^{0}
then it holds
u^{\ell}_{h}\leq\tilde{u}^{\ell}_{h}
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. We will use both of these structural properties to understand the asymptotic behavior of the scheme.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we prove convergence of the scheme (
1
) to the viscosity solution of obstacle mean curvature flow. There, the main results are given by Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
. They state, respectively, that the limit of (
1
) is correct away from the possible fattened interface and convergence of the fronts in case of no fattening. Section
3
shows for a big class of space discretizations the convergence to the space-continuous scheme. Finally, we apply in Section
4
our space- and time-discretized scheme to simulate the invasion processes from
[
26
]
.

Convergence to Obstacle Mean Curvature Flow

In the following we write
\Omega_{0}:=\{u^{0}=1\}
,
\Gamma_{0}:=\partial\Omega_{0}

\Omega_{0},\Phi\subset\Omega_{0},\Psi\subset\Omega_{0}^{c}

Our aim is to prove the convergence of the obstacle MBO scheme (
1

[
6

]
for general properties of viscosity solutions of Obstacle Mean Curvature Flow. With these properties at hand, the remainder of the convergence proof is almost identical to the very elegant proof of

Let us define
F:(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
F(p,X):=-\textup{tr}(X-\frac{p\otimes p}{|p|^{2}}X)
which is geometric, i.e.,

(3)

F(\lambda p,\lambda X+\mu(p\otimes p))=\lambda F(p,x)\quad\quad\text{ for any }\lambda>0,\mu\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that if the levelsets of a function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are smooth and evolve by mean curvature flow, it holds

(4)

\partial_{t}u+F(Du,D^{2}u)=0\quad\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,\infty).

In the following we will denote for any function
f
the upper and lower semi-continuous envelope by

f^{*}(x):=\limsup_{y\rightarrow x}f(y)\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad f_{*}(x):=\liminf_{y\rightarrow x}f(y),

respectively.
For any symmetric matrix
X
there is no possibility to define
F(0,X)
such that
F
is continuous at this point. But independent by of the choice at
F(0,X)
the lower semi-continuous envelope
F_{*}
is given by

(5)

F_{*}(p,X)=\liminf_{\tilde{p}\rightarrow p}F(\tilde{p},X)=\begin{cases}F(p,X)&\text{if }p\neq 0,\\
-\textup{tr}(X)+\lambda_{\text{min}}(X)&\text{if }p=0.\end{cases}

The upper semi-continuous envelope is similarly

F^{*}(p,X)=\limsup_{\tilde{p}\rightarrow p}F(\tilde{p},X)=\begin{cases}F(p,X)&\text{if }p\neq 0,\\
-\textup{tr}(X)+\lambda_{\text{max}}(X)&\text{if }p=0.\end{cases}

Now we can define viscosity solutions for discontinuous Hamiltonians with obstacles
\phi,\psi
, see
[
15
]

Definition 1
.

We call a locally bounded function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a viscosity subsolution of (
4
) with obstacles
\phi,\psi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
if for every
\eta\in C^{2}(\Omega)
with local maximum at
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\Omega\times[0,T)
of
u^{*}-\eta
it holds

(6)

\max\{\min\{\partial_{t}\eta+F_{*}(D\eta,D^{2}\eta),u^{*}-\phi^{*}\},u^{*}-\psi^{*}\}\leq 0.

Similarly, a supersolution is defined by satisfying
for every
\eta\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, if
u_{*}-\eta
has a local minimum at
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\Omega\times[0,T)
then

(7)

\max\{\min\{\partial_{t}\eta+F^{*}(D\eta,D^{2}\eta),u_{*}-\phi_{*}\},u_{*}-\psi_{*}\}\geq 0.

Definition 2
.

A locally bounded function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a viscosity solution of (
4
) with obstacles
\phi,\psi
if it is a viscosity subsolution and a viscosity supersolution with respect to
\phi,\psi
.

Remark 1
.

Let us abbreviate
F=\partial_{t}u+F(D\phi,D^{2}\phi)
for this remark. Although the expression
\max\{\min\{F,u-\phi\},u-\psi\}
in (
6
), (
7
) looks asymmetric in
\phi,\psi
at first glance, this is not the case. One checks, using
\phi\leq\psi
, the facts

\max\{\min\{F,u-\phi\},u-\psi\}\leq 0\Leftrightarrow(F\leq 0\vee u\leq\phi)\wedge u\leq\psi,

and

\max\{\min\{F,u-\phi\},u-\psi\}\geq 0\Leftrightarrow(F\geq 0\vee u\geq\psi)\wedge u\geq\phi,

which show the symmetry in
\phi,\psi
. Here, one can also see nicely that every function
u
with
u\leq\phi
is a viscosity subsolution although the obstacle constraint
u\geq\phi
might be violated. This flexibility of subsolutions will later be crucial for constructing test functions for the comparison principle.

(Maximal subsolution, minimal supersolution)
.

Let

\partial_{t}\eta(x,t)+F_{*}(D\eta(x,t),D^{2}\eta(x,t))=\partial_{t}\eta(x,t)-\Delta\eta(x,t)+\lambda_{\text{min}}(D^{2}\eta(x,t))\leq 0.

h
small enough and at the maximum point one thus has

u_{h}^{*}(x,\ell h)\leq 1-\eta(x_{h},\ell_{h}h)+\eta(x,\ell h)\quad\quad\text{ for any }(x,\ell)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{N}.

Checking both cases
u_{h}^{*}(x,\ell h)=\pm 1
one justifies that this implies

u_{h}^{*}(x,\ell h)\leq\mathrm{sign}^{*}(\eta(x,\ell h)-\eta(x_{h},\ell_{h}h))\quad\quad\text{ for any }(x,\ell)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{N}.

Because
\Omega\setminus(\bar{\Phi}\cup\bar{\Psi})
is open it follows from
x\in\Omega\setminus(\bar{\Phi}\cup\bar{\Psi})
that we can assume w.l.o.g.
x_{h}\in\Omega\setminus(\bar{\Phi}\cup\bar{\Psi})
. The update by the scheme (
1
) for any
x\in\Omega\setminus(\bar{\Phi}\cup\bar{\Psi})
is

u_{h}(x,\ell_{h}h)=\mathrm{sign}_{*}(e^{-h\Delta}u_{h}(x,(\ell_{h}-1)h)),

which immediately implies

u_{h}^{*}(x,\ell_{h}h)\leq\mathrm{sign}^{*}(e^{-h\Delta}u_{h}^{*}(x,(\ell_{h}-1)h)).

Assembling the previous steps for
x=x_{h}
yields

\displaystyle 1=u_{h}^{*}(x_{h},\ell_{h}h)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{sign}^{*}(e^{-h\Delta}u_{h}^{*}(x_{h},(\ell_{h}-1)h))

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{sign}^{*}\big(e^{-h\Delta}\big[\mathrm{sign}^{*}(\eta(\cdot,(\ell_{h}-1)h)-\eta(x_{h},\ell_{h}h))\big](x_{h})\big)

which is equivalent to

e^{-h\Delta}\big[\mathrm{sign}^{*}(\eta(\cdot,(\ell_{h}-1)h)-\eta(x_{h},\ell_{h}h))\big](x_{h})\geq 0.

As
e^{-h\Delta}1=1
this can again be reformulated as

\frac{1}{(4\pi h)^{d/2}}\int_{\{\eta(y,(\ell-1)h)-\eta(x_{h},\ell_{h}h)\geq 0\}}e^{-\frac{|x_{h}-y|^{2}}{4h}}\,dy\geq\frac{1}{2}.

For the case
D\eta(x,t)\neq 0
one applies the consistency result
[
4
, Proposition 4.1]
with
\eta_{h}(x,t)=\eta(x,t)-\eta(x_{h},\ell_{h}h)
to conclude with (
5
) that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq 2\sqrt{\pi}|D\eta(x,t)|\liminf_{h\downarrow 0}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{(4\pi h)^{d/2}}\int_{\eta_{h}(y,(\ell-1)h)\geq 0}e^{-\frac{|x_{h}-y|^{2}}{4h}}\,dy\right)

\displaystyle\geq\partial_{t}\eta(x,t)+F_{*}(D\eta(x,t),D^{2}\eta(x,t)).

Furthermore, if
D\eta(x,t)=0
, by
[
4
, Proposition 2.2]
it is enough to check the case
D^{2}\eta(x,t)=0
. By
[
4
, Proposition 4.1]
it holds
\partial_{t}\eta(x,t)\leq 0
, so that indeed

0\geq\partial_{t}\eta(x,t)=\partial_{t}\eta(x,t)+F_{*}(D\eta(x,t),D^{2}\eta(x,t)).

As the obstacles constraints are satisfied this finishes the proof for the subsolution.
∎

Having done the main work in the two previous propositions the main result of this section follows immediately.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Omega_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega:u(x,t)>0\}
and
\Gamma_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega:u(x,t)=0\}
, then, for any
t>0
it holds

{\liminf}_{*}u_{h}(x,t)=1\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{t},\quad\text{ and }\quad{\limsup}^{*}u_{h}(x,t)=-1\quad\text{ in }(\Omega_{t}\cup\Gamma_{t})^{c}.

Proof.

Combing Proposition
1
with Proposition
2
immediately yields

{\limsup}^{*}u_{h}\leq\mathrm{sign}^{*}(u)\quad\text{ and }\quad{\liminf}_{*}u_{h}\geq\mathrm{sign}_{*}(u)\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,+\infty).

This implies the result as
u_{h}
has values in
\{-1,1\}
.
∎

The next result is basically exactly as in
[
4
, Corollary 1.3]
. One only needs to care about compactness in time, as the flow might not converge to a steady state in final time.

Corollary 1
.

For any finite time horizon
T>0
, if
\Omega_{0}
is bounded and
\cup_{t=0}^{T}\Gamma_{t}\times\{t\}=\partial\{(x,t):u(x,t)>0\}=\partial\{(x,t):u(x,t)<0\}
, then
F^{h}:=\cup_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor\frac{T}{h}\rfloor}\partial\{x\in\Omega:u_{h}(x,\ell h)=1\}\times\{\ell h\}
converges to
F:=\cup_{t=0}^{T}\Gamma_{t}\times\{t\}
in the Hausdorff distance.

To prove Corollary
1
we will use the following lemma about boundedness of the iterates of the scheme.

Lemma 1
.

If
\Omega_{0}\subset\bar{B}_{R}
for any given radius
R>0
then
\Omega_{\ell h}^{h}:=\{x\in\Omega:u_{h}(x,\ell h)=1\}\subset\bar{B}_{R}
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

We first notice that
\Omega_{0}\subset\bar{B}_{R}
implies
\Phi\subset\bar{B}_{R}
. If one initializes the scheme with a half space
H_{e}:=\{x\in\Omega:e\cdot x<R\}
for some
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
, i.e.,

u_{h}^{e}(x,0)=-\mathbbold{1}_{\Psi}\wedge(\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}}-\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}^{c}}),

then, using
e^{-h\Delta}(-\mathbbold{1}_{\Psi}\wedge(\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}}-\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}^{c}}))\leq e^{-h\Delta}(\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}}-\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}^{c}})
, one inductively checks that

(11)

u_{h}^{e}(\cdot,\ell h)\leq-\mathbbold{1}_{\Psi}\wedge(\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}}-\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}^{c}})\quad\quad\text{for all }\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

Furthermore, as the scheme is order preserving and
\Omega_{0}
respects the obstacles it follows from

\mathbbold{1}_{\Omega_{0}}-\mathbbold{1}_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}\leq-\mathbbold{1}_{\Psi}\wedge(\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}}-\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}^{c}}),

together with (
11
) that the inequality is preserved for all later times

, i.e.,

u_{h}(\cdot,\ell h)\leq u_{h}^{e}(\cdot,\ell h)\leq-\mathbbold{1}_{\Psi}\wedge(\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}}-\mathbbold{1}_{H_{e}^{c}}).

As

was arbitrary, one concludes the lemma.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1
.

By the definition of the Hausdorff distance we need to prove that

0\xleftarrow{h\downarrow 0}\textup{dist}(F,F^{h}):=\max\{\max_{(x,t)\in F^{h}}\textup{dist}((x,t),F),\max_{(x,t)\in F}\textup{dist}((x,t),F^{h})\}.

We start by proving
\max_{(x,t)\in F^{h}}\textup{dist}((x,t),F)\rightarrow 0
. Let
R>0
be such that
\Omega_{0}\subset\bar{B}_{R}
(which exists as
\Omega_{0}
is bounded by assumption). For fixed but arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
define
K_{\varepsilon}:=\{(x,t)\in\bar{B}_{R}\setminus(\Psi\cup\Phi)\times[0,T]:\textup{dist}((x,t),F)\geq\varepsilon\}
. The function
(x,t)\mapsto\mathrm{sign}(u(x,t))
is continuous on
K_{\varepsilon}
since it is locally constant on
K_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, on the compact set
K_{\varepsilon}
(because
\Phi
and
\Psi
are open by assumption) it holds by Proposition
2
and Proposition
1
that

{\liminf}_{*}u_{h}\leq{\limsup}^{*}u_{h}\leq\mathrm{sign}^{*}(u)=\mathrm{sign}_{*}(u)\leq{\liminf}_{*}u_{h},

which implies

(12)

{\liminf}_{*}u_{h}={\limsup}^{*}u_{h}=\mathrm{sign}(u).

Assume that
u_{h}
does not convergence uniformly to
\mathrm{sign}(u)
on
K_{\varepsilon}
. Then there exists a subsequence
(x_{h},t_{h})
and
\delta>0
such that
|\mathrm{sign}(u(x_{h},t_{h}))-u_{h}(x_{h},t_{h})|>\delta
. As
K_{\varepsilon}
is compact it holds
(x_{h},t_{h})\rightarrow(x,t)
for some
(x,t)\in K_{\varepsilon}
up to a further subsequence. But this already implies by (
12
) and the continuity of
u
that
0=|\mathrm{sign}(u(x,t))-\mathrm{sign}(u(x,t))|\geq\delta
, a contradiction. Hence,
u_{h}
has the uniform limit
\mathrm{sign}(u)
on
K_{\varepsilon}
. The uniform convergence implies
u_{h}=\mathrm{sign}(u)
for
h
small enough on
K_{\varepsilon}
as
u_{h}
is
\pm 1
-valued. This means that
u_{h}
is locally constant on
K_{\varepsilon}
for
h
small enough which means that
F_{h}
cannot intersect the interior of
K_{\varepsilon}
. One concludes that
F_{h}\subset\{(x,t)\in\bar{B}_{R}\times[0,T]:\textup{dist}((x,t),F)\leq\varepsilon\}
. As
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, this shows the claimed
\max_{(x,t)\in F^{h}}\textup{dist}((x,t),F)\rightarrow 0
.

Turning towards the second claim
\max_{(x,t)\in F}\textup{dist}((x,t),F^{h})\rightarrow 0
, one notices that the assumed
\cup_{t=0}^{T}\Gamma_{t}\times\{t\}=\partial\{(x,t):u(x,t)>0\}=\partial\{(x,t):u(x,t)<0\}
implies

(\mathrm{sign}^{*}(u))_{*}=\mathrm{sign}_{*}(u)\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad(\mathrm{sign}_{*}(u))^{*}=\mathrm{sign}^{*}(u)\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T).

Furthermore, by Proposition
2
we know that

\bar{u}(x,t)=\limsup_{y\rightarrow x,\ell h\rightarrow t}u_{h}(y,\ell h)\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\underaccent{\bar}{u}(x,t)=\liminf_{y\rightarrow x,\ell h\rightarrow t}u_{h}(y,\ell h),

are viscosity sub- and supersolution, respectively, with initial data
\mathbbold{1}_{\Omega_{0}}-\mathbbold{1}_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}
and obstacles
\psi^{\Psi},\phi^{\Phi}
. Again, Proposition
1
implies now

\mathrm{sign}_{*}(u(x,t))\leq\underaccent{\bar}{u}(x,t)\leq\bar{u}(x,t)\leq\mathrm{sign}^{*}(u(x,t)).

Application of the upper semi-continuous envelope or lower semi-continuous envelope yields

\liminf_{y\rightarrow x,\ \ell h\rightarrow t}u_{h}(y,\ell h)=\mathrm{sign}_{*}(u(x,t))\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\limsup_{y\rightarrow x,\ \ell h\rightarrow t}u_{h}(y,\ell h)=\mathrm{sign}^{*}(u(x,t)).

Assume there is a sequence
h\downarrow 0
such that
\max_{(x,t)\in F}\textup{dist}((x,t),F^{h})\geq\varepsilon>0
. Because
F
is compact in
\Omega\times[0,T]
, there exists a subsequence, again denoted by
h
, and points
(x_{h},t_{h})\in F
converging to some

such that
\textup{dist}((x_{h},t_{h}),F^{h})\geq\varepsilon
. Again up to a subsequence we therefore have either

u_{h}(y,\ell h)=1\quad\quad\text{ for any }h\text{ if }|y-x_{h}|+|\ell h-t_{h}|<\varepsilon

or

u_{h}(y,\ell h)=-1\quad\quad\text{ for any }h\text{ if }|y-x_{h}|+|\ell h-t_{h}|<\varepsilon.

In the first case this implies

\mathrm{sign}_{*}(u(x,t))=\liminf_{y\rightarrow x,\ell h\rightarrow t}u_{h}(y,\ell h)=1.

But this is a contradiction as
(x,t)\in F
which means
\mathrm{sign}_{*}(u(x,t))=-1
. Similarly, one concludes the other case.
∎

3.
Space Discretization

We discretize our domain
\Omega
into finitely many points
X_{N}\subset\Omega
with
\#X_{N}=N
. In this numerical setting
\Omega
has to be finite with some sort of boundary conditions to be computable. So, choosing
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
as the
d
-dimensional torus is a very natural choice. Denote by
\mu_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}\delta_{x}
the empirical measure of
X_{N}
. Furthermore assume there are functions
T_{N}:\Omega\rightarrow X_{N}
such that
T_{N}\rightarrow\textup{id}
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\mu_{N}=(T_{N})_{\#}\mu
for some measure
\mu=\rho\,\mathcal{L}^{d}\big|_{\Omega}
that has density
\rho>0
with respect to the Lebesgue meausure on
\Omega
. Let
\Delta_{N}:\{f:X_{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric discretization of the Laplacian such that

(13)

(e^{-h\Delta_{N}}u_{N})\circ T_{N}\rightarrow e^{-h\Delta}u\quad\quad\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega)\text{ if }u_{N}\circ T_{N}\rightharpoonup u\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(\Omega).

Remark 2
.

The above framework covers the standard discretization scenarios, for example a uniform grid or a random geometric graph.

i)

Under a random geometric graph we understand that
X_{N}
is a collection of i.i.d. samples of
\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d}
and

\Delta_{N}u(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y\in X_{N}}w_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\left(\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

is a graph Laplacian with weights
w_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\eta(\frac{|x-y|}{\varepsilon})
for a suitable kernel
\eta:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a length scale
\varepsilon
. For further information see for example
[
22
]
. One can then define optimal transport maps
T_{N}
as in
[
22
]
to get the convergence (
13
) with probability
1
as done in
[
22
, Theorem 2]
or in the best possible parameter regime in
[
29
, Theorem 4.3]
.

ii)

Also deterministic discretizations fit in the framework. One needs to choose
X_{N}
such that the empirical measure of
X_{N}
converges to a measure
\mu
. This is for example the case if
X_{N}=\mathbb{T}^{d}\cap\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a uniform grid. One could then take

T_{N}:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow X_{N},x\mapsto\varepsilon z\text{ if }\|x-\varepsilon z+Lk\|_{\ell^{1}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{ for some }k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\text{ and }z\in\mathbb{Z}

together with
\mu=\mathcal{L}^{d}
and for any
u=(u_{i})_{i\in X_{N}}
the graph Laplacian induced by the kernel
\eta=\mathbbold{1}_{[0,1]}
simplifies to a finite difference matrix

(14)

\big(\Delta_{N}u(x)\big)_{i}=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{u_{i+\varepsilon e_{j}}-2u_{i}+u_{i-\varepsilon e_{j}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

One can show with the same techniques as in case (i) that (
13
) holds.

We define the discrete obstacle MBO scheme on
X_{N}
by

u_{N}^{\ell+1}=\mathrm{sign}(e^{-h\Delta_{N}}u_{N}^{\ell}+\mathbbold{1}_{\Phi_{N}}-\mathbbold{1}_{\Psi_{N}})

where
\Phi_{N},\Psi_{N}\subset\Omega
are discrete obstacles. We will later also denote

\phi_{N}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in\Phi_{N}\\
-1&\text{if }x\notin\Phi_{N},\end{cases}\quad\quad\text{ and }\psi_{N}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in\Psi_{N}\\
-1&\text{if }x\notin\Psi_{N}.\end{cases}

We want to prove the convergence of the discrete scheme (
3
) to the continuous scheme (
1
). Our main tool to that aim will be the following minimizing movement interpretation that is a adaptation of
[

]
.

Lemma 2
.

The scheme (
3

(15)

u_{N}^{\ell+1}\in\argmin_{u:X_{N}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}}E_{N}(u)+\frac{1}{2h}d_{N}^{2}(u,u_{N}^{\ell})\quad\quad\text{ s.t. }u\equiv 1\text{ on }\Phi_{N}\text{ and }u\equiv-1\text{ on }\Psi_{N},

or reformulated with a Lagrange multiplier

(16)

u_{N}^{\ell+1}\in\argmin_{u:X_{N}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}}E_{N}(u)+\frac{1}{2h}d_{N}^{2}(u,u_{N}^{\ell})+\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}\Big((\phi_{N}(x)-u(x))_{+}+(\psi_{N}(x)-u(x))_{-}\Big)

where the energy and distance are given by

\displaystyle E_{N}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}(1-u)(x)\left(e^{-h\Delta_{N}}(1+u)\right)(x),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2h}d_{N}^{2}(u,\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}(u-\tilde{u})(x)\left(e^{-h\Delta_{N}}(u-\tilde{u})\right)(x).

Proof.

We start with the equivalence of the scheme (
3

(17)

b(f,g):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}f(x)\left(e^{-h\Delta_{N}}g\right)(x).

Then it holds

\displaystyle E_{h}(u)+\frac{1}{2h}d_{N}^{2}(u,u_{n}^{\ell})

\displaystyle=b(1-u,1+u)+b(u-u_{N}^{\ell},u-u_{N}^{\ell})

\displaystyle=b(1,1)-2b(u,u_{N}^{\ell})+b(u_{N}^{\ell},u_{N}^{\ell})

such that the minimization problem (
15

u_{N}^{\ell+1}\in\argmin_{u:X_{N}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{h}N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}u(x)\left(e^{-h\Delta_{N}}u_{N}^{\ell}\right)(x)\quad\quad\text{ s.t. }u\equiv 1\text{ on }\Phi_{N}\text{ and }u\equiv-1\text{ on }\Psi_{N}.

or equivalently the minimizer is given by (

Proposition 3
.

Let

and

Remark 3
.

The condition

Now we turn to the proof of Proposition

Proof of Proposition

.

We prove the statement by showing that for every

.

We first note that because of (

\displaystyle E_{N}(u_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}(1-u_{N})(x)\left(e^{-h\Delta_{N}}(1+u_{N})\right)(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Omega}(1-u_{N})(T_{N}(x))\left(e^{-h\Delta_{N}}(1+u_{N})\right)(T_{N}(x))\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Omega}(1-u)(x)\left(e^{-h\Delta}(1+u)\right)(x)\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=E_{h}(u).

Similarly it holds

(20)

\frac{1}{2h}d_{N}^{2}(u_{N},u_{N}^{0})\xrightarrow{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2h}d_{h}^{2}(u,u^{0}).

Hence it is enough to prove that

(21)

\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}\Big((\phi_{N}(x)-u_{N}(x))_{+}+(\psi_{N}(x)-u_{N}(x))_{-}\Big)

\Gamma
-convergences to its mean field limit. We concentrate on the first summand; the other one is treated similarly. Starting with the lower bound let a sequence of functions
u_{N}:X_{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be given such that
u_{N}\circ T_{N}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. As
\phi_{N}\circ T_{N}\rightarrow\phi^{\Phi}
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
it is enough to prove that

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(f):=\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Omega}f_{+}(x)\,d\mu(x)

is lower-semicontinuous with respect to the weak
L^{2}
-topology. But this is just a consequence of the continuity of
\tilde{G}
in the strong
L^{2}
-topology together with the convexity of
\tilde{G}
.

Turning towards the recovery sequence take any sequence of Lipschitz-approximations
\{\tilde{u}_{\delta}\}_{\delta}
such that
\tilde{u}_{\delta}\xrightarrow{\delta\downarrow 0}u
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega\setminus(\Phi\cup\Psi))
and
\tilde{u}_{\delta}=1
on
\Phi
and
\tilde{u}_{\delta}=-1
on
\Psi
. Because
G
is continuous in
L^{2}(\Omega)
it is thus enough to construct a recovery sequence for
\tilde{u}_{\delta}
with
\delta
fixed. The recovery sequence for
u
follows by taking a diagonal sequence. We therefore define
u_{N}
as the restriction of
\tilde{u}_{\delta}
to the set
X_{N}
, i.e.,

u_{N}(x):=\tilde{u}_{\delta}\big|_{X_{N}}(x).

By the Lipschitz-continuity of
\tilde{u}_{\delta}
it follows

\int_{\Omega}|u_{N}\circ T_{N}-\tilde{u}_{\delta}|\,d\mu\leq\int_{\Omega}|u_{N}(T_{N}(x))-\tilde{u}_{\delta}(T_{N}(x))|+|\tilde{u}_{\delta}(T_{N}(x))-\tilde{u}_{\delta}(x)|\,d\mu\leq|\Omega|L\|T_{N}-\textup{id}\|_{\infty}.

Hence, using
T_{N}\rightarrow\textup{id}
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
one concludes that
u_{N}\circ T_{N}
converges to
\tilde{u}_{\delta}
as
N
to infinity. In the following we denote
\Phi_{N}:=\{\phi_{N}\circ T_{N}=1\}
. Next we want to show that the symmetric difference
\Phi_{N}\triangle\Phi
converges to zero. To that aim define the auxiliary functions

f_{N}:=\frac{\phi_{N}\circ T_{N}+1}{2}\in\{0,1\},\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad f:=\frac{\phi^{\Phi}+1}{2}\in\{0,1\}.

As
\phi_{N}\circ T_{N}\rightharpoonup\phi^{\Phi}
it holds
f_{N}\rightharpoonup f
weak in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, it follows

\displaystyle|\Phi_{N}\triangle\Phi|=\int_{\Omega}f(1-f_{N})+f_{N}(1-f)\,d\mu\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}f(1-f)+f(1-f)\,d\mu=0

where we used that
f_{N},f\in\{0,1\}
and
f_{N}=\mathbbold{1}_{\Phi_{N}},f=\mathbbold{1}_{\Phi}
.
As
u_{N}\in[-1,1]
one has

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}N}\sum_{x\in X_{N}}(\phi_{N}(x)-u_{N}(x))_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Omega}(\phi_{N}(T_{N}(x))-u_{N}(T_{N}(x)))_{+}\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Phi_{N}}(1-u_{N}\circ T_{N})_{+}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Phi}(1-u_{N}\circ T_{N})_{+}\,d\mu+\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Phi_{N}\triangle\Phi}(1-u_{N}\circ T_{N})_{+}\,d\mu.

This yields the convergence of the energy as

\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Phi}(1-u_{N}\circ T_{N})_{+}\,d\mu=\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Phi}1-u_{N}\circ T_{N}\,d\mu\xrightarrow{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Phi}1-\tilde{u}_{\delta}\,d\mu=\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Omega}(\phi^{\Phi}-\tilde{u}_{\delta})_{+}\,d\mu

and

\left|\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}\int_{\Phi_{N}\triangle\Phi}(1-u_{N}\circ T_{N})_{+}\,d\mu\right|\leq\frac{8}{\sqrt{h}}|\Phi_{N}\triangle\Phi|\xrightarrow{N\rightarrow 0}0.\qed

We briefly describe our implementation of the dynamics of the invasion processes of
[
26
]
in two dimensions.
As introduced in the previous section we work on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}=[0,1)^{2}
and discretize with a uniform grid as explained in Remark
2
ii). The two-dimensional Fourier transform of the heat kernel assosciated to (
14
) can be explicitely computed to be

\hat{G}_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}e^{2Nh(\cos(\frac{2\pi i}{\sqrt{N}})+\cos(\frac{2\pi j}{\sqrt{N}})-2)}.

Here,
N=(1/\varepsilon)^{2}
is the number of pixels introduced by a uniform grid with distance
\varepsilon
between the grid points. The scaling
N
is such that the width of the heat kernel is only dependent on
h
but not on
\varepsilon=1/N
.
Hence, solving the heat equation can be done efficiently by pointwise multiplication in Fourier space. Thus, updating
u^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
by the scheme (
3
) is simply given by

(22)

u^{\ell+1}=\chi_{\Phi}\wedge-\chi_{\Psi}\vee\texttt{IFFT}(\hat{G}\odot\texttt{FFT}(u^{\ell})),

where
FFT
is the two-dimensional Fast Fourier Transform,
IFFT
is the two-dimensional Inverse Fast Fourier Transform and
\odot
denotes pointwise multiplication. The overall running time is in
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
where the computational bottle neck is the Fast Fourier Transform. Fig.
3
shows the average running time per iteration dependent on the number of pixels
N
. To measure the running time we used our Python implementation on a single AMD Ryzen 5 PRO 8540U. The code for all our tests can be found at
[
20
]
. The plot shows agreement with the theoretical (up to logarithmic correction) linear relation between
N
and the complexity.

In view of approximating the dynamics of the invasion processes described by
[
26
]
two forms of approximation errors are visible in our experiments; compare with Fig.
4
. First, two components can get connected when they are close to each other although they might not touch yet. This happens due to the non-locality of the kernel. The length scale of this phenomenon is the width of the heat kernel
\sqrt{h}
. That means that the components get connected when they are roughly
\sqrt{h}
apart. For the invasion process of
[
26
]
this means that the front propagates faster than it should. On the other hand, a second effect slows down propagation; the dynamics can get pinned although the convex hull is not yet reached. For a steady state of obstacle mean curvature flow the interface should be straight away from the obstacle. But in our discrete dynamics with discrete normal velocity
V
it has to hold
Vh\gtrsim\varepsilon
such that the interface can jump to the next grid point and not get pinned. As
V\approx-H
this means that the discrete curvature
-H
can be of the order
\frac{\varepsilon}{h}
for a discrete steady state. This observation is in line with
[
30
]
where they prove convergence of the classical MBO scheme on grids when
\varepsilon\ll h
.

We want to simulate the dynamics of the invasion process of
[
9
]
. This is done by sampling disks which serve as obstacles but also initial condition for the dynamics generated by (
22
). To be more precise, we use a uniform grid, as in Remark
2
ii), on the two-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{5000}
wich leads to
N=5000^{2}
. We choose the disk radius
r_{d}=0.005642
such that the dimensionless system size
\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|}{\pi r_{d}^{2}}=:\bar{A}_{syst}=10000
. Furthermore, we choose the number of disks for the initialization to be
N_{d}=2999
such that the initial concentration is
N_{d}\pi r_{d}^{2}=:C=0.3
. The centers of the disks are uniformly randomly distributed over the domain. The characteristic function of the disks
\chi_{d}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\text{ is in a disk},\\
-1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
is used as initial condition
\chi^{0}
and as inner obstacle
\chi_{\Phi}
. Note, we also add a zero padding to avoid changes in the dynamics due to the periodic boundary conditions. The size of the padding depends on the diffusion time. The right choice of diffusion time depends on
N
and the desired approximation error as previously discussed. In our tests
h=r_{\text{disk}}^{2}/16
seemed to be a fair trade between approximation accuracy and number of iterations needed. Note, the choice for
h
is such that the width of the kernel is a quarter of the width of the obstacles. Some snapshots of the simulation can be seen in Fig.

5.
Outlook

Our convergence proofs show that combining the MBO scheme with a pointwise update by the obstacle constraint yield an efficient algorithm for the approximation of mean curvature flow with obstacles. We believe that also other variants of the MBO scheme can be similarly adapted to incoperate an obstacle constraint. This is relevant for applicatations. For example the model of
[
10
]
incorporates, among other effects, obstacles but also volume constraints on the cell. This can be approximated by adapting the volume-constrained MBO scheme
[
16
,
19
,
18
]
. The scheme for two phases then reads:

Step 1

Diffusion:
u^{\ell+1/3}=e^{-h\Delta}

Step 2

Update on obstacle:
u^{\ell+2/3}=\chi_{\Phi}\wedge-\chi_{\Psi}\vee u^{\ell+1/3}

Step 3

Threshold with volume constraint:
u^{\ell+1}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }u^{\ell+2/3}>\lambda,\\
-1&\text{if }u^{\ell+2/3}<\lambda,\end{cases}
where
\lambda
is a thresholding value such that
|\{u^{\ell+2/3}>\lambda\}|=V
for a given volume
V
.

The Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
can be found in
\mathcal{O}(N)
with standard weighted median algorithms
[
17
, Theorem 17.3]
.
The above scheme is so powerful because it keeps the minimizing movement interpretation

(23)

\displaystyle u^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{u:\Omega\rightarrow\{-1,1\}}E_{h}(u)+\frac{1}{2h}d_{h}^{2}(u,u^{\ell})+\lambda\int u\quad\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\quad\chi_{\Phi}\leq u\leq\chi_{\Psi},

or equivalently

\displaystyle u^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{u:\Omega\rightarrow\{-1,1\}}E_{h}(u)+\frac{1}{2h}d_{h}^{2}(u,u^{\ell})\quad\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\quad\chi_{\Phi}\leq u\leq\chi_{\Psi}\quad\text{ and }\quad|\{u=1\}|=V,

for the unconditional stability and the relation to the gradient flow structure of mean curvature flow. Although, adapting the algorithms seems to be straight forward, the analytical side for volume-preserving mean curvature flow with obstacles is not studied yet; so, there is still more work to do to understand the volume preserving, obstacle MBO.

Furthermore, a multiphase version of the here presented obstacle MBO scheme would be a natural extension. The obstacles
\Phi_{i}
are then inner obstacles for the phase
i
and outer obstacles for any other phase
j\neq i
. Again, this can be done with volume constraints, such that the minimizing movement interpretation (
23
) is kept. The Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
and consequently a minimizer
u^{\ell+1}
can be computed by using one of the algorithms
[
16
,
18
]
.

The concept of viscosity solutions is not available for multiphase, obstacle mean curvature flow, as this flow does not satisfy a comparison principle. Instead one should tackle the multiphase problem by its gradient flow structure and show convergence to Brakke
[
24
]
, De Giorgi
[
25
,
19
]
or distributional
[
23
]
solutions. The main challenge to that aim is to understand the convergence of the Lagrange multiplier
\frac{4}{\sqrt{h}}
in the minimizing movement interpretation (
18
).

A second meaningful adaptation would be anisotropic motions. Mean curvature flow origins from material science where it models the motion of grain boundaries in polycristals. Here, obstacles are important to model impurities in the material. But due to microscopic crystal structure the motion is actually anisotropic. Therefore, it is of interest to adapt the MBO scheme for anisotropic mean curvature flow with obstacles. The natural approach to adapt the scheme (
1
) is to replace the heat kernel with an anisotropic kernel. For the anisotropic MBO scheme without obstacles there are already several papers answering the question on how to choose the kernel. First, Ishii-Pires-Souganidis
[
14
]
consider a broad class of positive kernels. But their kernels may not be positive in Fourier space such that there is no guarantee of energy dissipation in (
18
). Bonnetier-Bretin-Chambolle
[
7
]
study kernels with positive Fourier transform but the kernels may not be positive in physical space such that the monotonicity of the scheme may break down. Furthermore, Elsey-Esedoğlu
[
11
]
characterize which anisotropies can be achieved as limits of the MBO scheme with positive kernels; they also showed that there exists in physical and in Fourier space positive kernels for those anisotropies. Nevertheless, the analysis for those kernels with an obstacle constraint is still an exciting, completely open problem.
