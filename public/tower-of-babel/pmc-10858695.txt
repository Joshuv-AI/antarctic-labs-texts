A systematic review and quality analysis of cancer pain guidelines
Authors: Raghu S. Thota, Seshadri Ramkiran, Sarita Singh, Anuja Damani, Anjana S. Wajekar, Lakshmi Koyyalagunta
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Cancer pain guidelines remain confined due to implementation barriers, preventing them from attaining a global perspective. The guidelines must be robust in development and inculcate high-quality content to achieve practical utility. Quality indicators related to safe opioid practice empower effective guideline implementation.Methods:The protocol was registered prospectively in PROSPERO (CRD42021244823). Guidelines published over the last decade providing insights into cancer pain management and incorporating safe opioid practice were evaluated. The review's primary outcome was to evaluate the quality of cancer pain guidelines. Appraisal of guidelines for research and evaluation II (AGREE II) instrument was used to assess a guideline's quality. The ADAPTE collaboration-guideline adaptation resource tool kit (ADAPTE) provided insights into its adaptation based on specific questions within the guideline.Results:Fourteen cancer pain guidelines met the eligibility criteria and were included for quality evaluation. Eight guidelines were evaluated with combined AGREE II and ADAPTE process, attaining >66.7% in the rigour of development domain score, summated scaled domain score, and specific ADAPTE tools to evaluate the quality of each guideline. The intra-class correlation coefficient was utilised for resolving inter-rater agreement. ‘Safe opioid practice’ within a guideline was assessed for quality content implementation.Conclusion:Combined AGREE II and ADAPTE identified four cancer pain guidelines, namely Ministry of Health Malaysia, National Comprehensive Cancer Network, NCEC-National Clinical Guideline, and World Health Organization, which were of the highest quality and incorporated safe opioid practice effectively.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer pain is the most distressing symptom in a patient's life, with fear of unrelieved pain sometimes exceeding the patient's fear of death.[1] The concept of ‘total pain management’ in cancer utilising a bio–psycho–social multi-modal interdisciplinary approach thrives to achieve a positive psychological ‘affect effect’ and improved functional quality of life.[234]Guidelines providing recommendations on various modalities of cancer pain management addressing the availability of opioids and safe opioid practice have emerged. The quality content of a guideline needs to be robust, methodologically validated, and rigorous in its development to attain global outreach.[2]

It is reported that 66% of the world population has no access to opioids, and an additional 10% have limited access to cancer pain management.[5] The opioid crisis, at the same time, is looming large, wherein the discharge of opioid prescriptions unscrupulously has resulted in opioid abuse and misuse. This has led to the creation of awareness within cancer pain guideline formulation to include ‘safe opioid practice’, attributing a quality measure.[6] In addition, individualised protocols for neuropathic pain, breakthrough pain, pediatric cancer pain, refractory cancer pain, metastatic bone pain, and terminal cancer pain must be comprehensively included within cancer pain guidelines. The triumph of a practical cancer pain guideline would be how end-of-life care pain management has been addressed.[7]

This systematic review's primary objective was to evaluate the quality of cancer pain guidelines with a secondary objective of quality analysis of guidelines in cancer pain management based on an inclusive, safe opioid practice.

METHODS

Study design

Methodological quality was evaluated by appraisal of guidelines for research and evaluation II (AGREE II) tool by two independent reviewers.[89] The AGREE II instrument and the ADAPTE collaboration guideline adaptation resource tool kit (ADAPTE) process were utilised to assess the guideline's quality.[10] The review's primary outcome was to evaluate the quality of cancer pain guidelines. Policies providing recommendations on various modalities of cancer pain treatment incorporating safe opioid practice and patient feedback were included for evaluation.

Setting

Systematically developed guidelines involved in cancer pain management were subjected to quality evaluation by using the statistical intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) by the mixed-effect model.

The review included guidelines based on PICOS [Population (cancer), Intervention (pain interventions and multi-modal pharmacological and non-pharmacological methods or techniques), Comparison (non-protocol modalities), Outcome (all quantitative guidelines related to cancer pain), and Search (qualitative guideline search for compliance, execution, development, and implementation of safe opioid practice)] with study population involving all adult cancer patients experiencing pain and pharmacological or non-pharmacological, interventional pain modalities after an initial systematic pain assessment by pain care providers ranging across diverse clinical specialities, targeting multi-modal pain management, as against not involving inter-disciplinary multi-modal pain management as comparator where applicable. All the guidelines were evaluated in their fully available version. Emphasis was laid upon safe opioid practice, bringing effective pain management as a primordial outcome measure.

Registry

Protocol for the review was registered prospectively in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (vide registration number: CRD42021244823) and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. Documents that would be categorised into systematically developed statements on decisions and recommendations about pain management appropriate in a cancer setting were qualified to be included as a cancer pain guideline. A pre-question subset was developed to determine whether a guideline would address cancer pain. A specific research question on safe opioid practice was included for content analysis to validate the quality within a guideline further.

Search strategy and data collection

A systematic literature search was performed involving a database search in PubMed, EMBASE, The Cochrane Library, National Guideline Clearinghouse (www.guideline.gov), Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (ahrq.gov), Guidelines International Network (https://g-i-n.net/library and https://guidelines.ebmportal.com), Emergency Care Research Institute (ECRI) Guideline Trust (guidelines.ecri.org), Turning Research Into Practice (TRIP) (https://www.tripdatabase.com), and pages of selected scientific societies guideline database as well as non-indexed guidelines that were PRISMA compliant.

The search strategies were defined to include cancer pain guidelines published in English between 1 January 2009 and 27 May 2021 about ‘cancer pain guidelines’, providing abstracts mentioning clear cancer pain treatment policy, guidelines, and recommendations with full text available for retrieval. The most comprehensive recent guideline version was included without dwelling upon past revisions or an incomplete newer version.

Literature search

Keywords in Mesh terminology included population-based (cancer), ‘cancer pain guidelines’, ‘cancer pain intervention’, ‘cancer pain recommendations’, ‘cancer society pain guides’, and publication-based (guideline, practice guideline, clinical practice guideline, and recommendation). Additional sources were obtained by searching the reference list of included guidelines.

Methodological tools for quality assessment

AGREE II instrument and ADAPTE process were utilised for quality assessment of a selected guideline.[8910] All eight guidelines were subjected further to the ADAPTE process for determining the robustness in the guideline quality development, adaptation, implementation, and reporting. Each guideline was analysed by extracting the recommendation upon specific research question ‘safe opioid practice’ within the guideline.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA) and Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) statistical software version 25.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Descriptive analysis values included median and interquartile range (IQR). An intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated by statistical computing to assess the consistency of evaluations by each appraiser within each domain. The inter-rater agreement was computed using the ICC with a two-way random effects model computed for each domain and the total score. The ICC was determined using a mixed-effect model assuming a random effect of ratings and a fixed effect of the raters. The degree of agreement between reviewers was determined by the measurement of ICC and its 95% confidence interval (CI). An ICC of >0.9 was considered ‘very good’, between 0.71 and 0.9 ‘good’, between 0.51 and 0.7 ‘moderate’, between 0.31 and 0.5 ‘fair’, and <0.31 ‘poor’ or ‘non-existent’. An ICC of 0.6 was used for acceptable inter-rater reliability.

Characteristics of cancer pain guidelines and their development methods

The criteria used to grade evidence quality were heterogeneous, and each cancer pain guideline reported a different method for quality adaptation [AGREE-II; ADAPTE; Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (CEBM OXFORD); GuideLines Into Decision Support (GLIDES); and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE)][911121314] [Table 1]. Though AGREE II and ADAPTE were commonly used as quality indicators, GRADE was the most sought and popular among guideline developers [European Association for Palliative Care (EAPC), Spanish Society of Medical Oncology (SEOM), World Health Organisation (WHO) guidelines].[15] Some assessment tools were not explicitly meant for guideline quality assessment [Guide Line Implementability Appraisal (GLIA), Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR), and International Network of Agencies for Health Technology Assessment (INAHTA)].[161718] Some tools were meant for guideline adoption and adaptation in a clinical setting rather than quality assessment [ADAPTED ADAPTE; Rapid Guideline Development (RAPADAPTE); and Adoption, Adaptation, and De novo Development (GRADE ADOLOPMENT)].[19202122] Few established guideline groups had their innate quality evaluation assessment system [National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN guideline-based tool)] and did not incorporate dedicated external quality methods[23][Table 1].

Quality evaluation of guidelines

AGREE II scores a guideline individually under the quality domain. Though AGREE II does not explicitly differentiate a high-quality guideline from a low-quality one by providing a cut-off value, the most common method to distinguish is to rank the guideline by using the rigour of the development domain [Figure 2].

Data analysis

The scaled domain scores in the AGREE II evaluation revealed good rigour of domain scores among eight guidelines, reaching beyond 66%, and six guidelines fared poorly, needing to be subsequently excluded from the analysis [Table 1, Figures 2 and 3]. Australia Wiki, American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), EAPC, and Indian guidelines fared well in the rigour of development domain assessment. WHO, National Clinical Guidelines (NCEC-NCG), National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), and Ministry of Health Malaysia (MOHM) guidelines attained excellent scores among rigour of domain and had incorporated methodological rigour at the level of their inception. The rigour of the development domain clearly distinguishes methodologically robust guidelines from those that could not incorporate it at the time of conception and development phases of a guideline itself. Although the clarity of the presentation and scope and purpose domains were excellent in most guidelines, the real distinction was the domain of ‘rigour of development’. The domain of applicability provided practical insights into implementing a guideline for global clinical practice and was the highest in the WHO and NCEC-NCG guidelines. The guideline with the highest editorial independence was among WHO, MOHM, and Indian guidelines [Table 1 and Figure 3].

Data extraction and synthesis of recommendation

AGREE-II instrument integrates all the domains into two global rating items (‘overall assessment’) to interpret a guideline overall quality as ‘Yes, Recommended’, ‘Recommended with modification’, and ‘No, not recommended’.

Four guidelines, namely WHO, NCCN, NCEC-NCG, and MOHM, were unanimously accepted with an emphatic ‘Yes, Recommended’. These were based on scaled domain scores as well as two global rating overall assessment scores in the AGREE II tool. Two guidelines, SEOM and LAG, were interpreted as ‘No, Not recommended’. Five guidelines, namely BPS, EAPC, ESMO, NHS-QIS, and Polish, were ‘Recommended with modifications’ by both assessors, and three guidelines were suggested ‘Recommended with modifications’ by either assessor or ‘Yes, Recommended’ by the other assessor [Table 1].

Guideline evaluation and level of agreement

Methodological quality was evaluated using the AGREE II tool by two independent reviewers. Discrepancies in the scoring were discussed involving a neutral reviewer to eliminate any bias or conflict and reinterpreted. If the scoring discrepancy was unresolved, it was subjected to the statistical ICC, which would determine the level of agreement among observers. Inter-rater reliability was good for all domains, and overall ICC scores achieved 0.742, which is considered a good level of agreement [Table 2].

Final combined assessment of guideline quality

Although a cut-off of 60% in the rigour domain overall assessment of AGREE II was recommended by Centro Nacional De Excelencia Tecnologica En Salud CENETEC [http://www.cenetec.salud.gob.mx/gpc.pdf] for guideline quality evaluation, most systematic reviews have included a cut-off of >66.7% in the rigour of development domain score. This was based on consensus amongst the reviewers as per AGREE II manual instructions for achieving the highest quality evaluation. Nevertheless, none of the guidelines scored between 60% and 66.7% in our review's rigour of development domain. Guidelines achieving beyond the selected cut-off of 66.7% in the rigour of the development domain were subjected to further quality evaluation by ADAPTE [Figure 4]. Similarly, an overall domain score of <50% in the rigour of development would render a guideline to be poor quality, and these guidelines (LAG, BPS, POLISH, NHS-QIS, SEOM, and ESMO) were excluded from further ADAPTE quality evaluation [Figure 4].

Eight guidelines attained >66.67% on the rigour of development domain, out of which four guidelines attained the highest quality on overall global quality assessment, attaining a score of seven on seven over the Likert scale and were unanimously recommended for use by both reviewers after resolving inter-observer variations over an ICC [Table 2 and Figure 4].

Finally, each guideline was assessed by a selected tool kit from the adaptation phase of ADAPTE concerning the specific research question ‘safe opioid practice’ and scored for an overall total of seven score subsets on a Likert scale based on yes/no/unsure as a quality measure [Table 3]. All eight guidelines achieved the highest ADAPTE quality rating score in all seven items rated as ‘ yes’. The most critical measure was to assess the actual applicability and implementation of guidelines [Tool 15], which could not be carried out rigorously utilising ADAPTE alone. Adding a guideline implementation assessment tool (GLIA) would have further strengthened the evaluation, but this was beyond our study objective. A combination of AGREE II and ADAPTE tools was analysed for final guideline quality interpretation.

DISCUSSION

Most of the fourteen cancer pain guidelines were comprehensive, involving multi-modal, inter-disciplinary pain management protocols. Out of the selected eight guidelines subjected to combined AGREE II and ADAPTE tools, four guidelines, namely MOHM, NCCN, NCEC-NCG, and WHO, emerged to be recommended by both evaluators to be well implemented globally. Six out of the fourteen guidelines did not consider feedback from its target population and financial resource implications, which were quality determinants. Four guidelines were not validated by an external reviewer and failed to present audit introspection. In addition, guideline development groups have not kept up with timelines to update their recommendations.

This systematic review evaluated fourteen cancer pain guidelines from various governmental and international scientific societies for quality assessment.[24252627282930313233343536373839] Although the clarity of the presentation and scope and purpose domains was excellent in most guidelines, the real distinction was the domain of ‘rigour of development’.[4041] The domain of applicability provided a practical insight into implementing a guideline to global clinical practice and was the highest in the WHO and NCEC-NCG guidelines.

The combined process of AGREE II and ADAPTE was carried out as AGREE II assessment alone would not consider the quality content of a guideline.[42] The ADAPTE quality evaluation process was done using specific tools to evaluate the content quality of the guideline and its implementability. We utilised select ADAPTE tools (tools 9–10 and 13–15) specific for guideline quality evaluation and implementation.[43]

MOHM, though robust, has not updated its guidelines for nearly a decade. NCEC-NCG has provided an excellent framework for cancer pain guidelines but without subsequent revisions. NCCN has regularly updated the cancer pain guidelines and recommendations, rendering them more credible. WHO has been in the guideline framework for pain for a long time and has been updating its cancer pain guidelines in the last decade. Regular follow-up updates and revision of guidelines have emerged only for NCCN and WHO, which have kept up their updated time lines. Recent ASCO guideline publication on opioids in cancer-related pain is very promising, although not a part of this review.[44]

Strengths and Limitations of the study

AGREE II and ADAPTE tools contribute to the guideline development process, methodology evaluation, and validation. Though the AGREE II tool does not evaluate the quality content of the guideline, ADAPTE evaluates its subjective interpretation. The combination provides comprehensive assessment, reliability, and validity in a guideline, which became the strength of our study. This review was not intended to rank the guidelines based on quantitative scores but to reflect upon their adapted implementation quality and inclusion of safe opioid practice.

CONCLUSION

Combined AGREE II and ADAPTE identified eight quality guidelines for quality evaluation, of which four cancer pain guidelines (MOHM, NCCN, NCEC-NCG, and WHO) were evaluated to be of the highest quality. All the evaluated guidelines inherently incorporated safe opioid practice and patient feedback systems, which are quality attributes.
