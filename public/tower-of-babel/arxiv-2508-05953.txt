SCALEFeedback: A Large-Scale Dataset of Synthetic Computer Science Assignments for LLM-generated Educational Feedback Research
Keyang Qian, Kaixun Yang, Wei Dai, Flora Jin, Yixin Cheng, Rui Guan, Sadia Nawaz, Zachari Swiecki, Guanliang Chen, Lixiang Yan, Dragan Gašević

Abstract
Using Large Language Models (LLMs) to give educational feedback to students for their assignments has attracted much attention in the AI in Education (AIED) field. Yet, there is currently no large-scale open-source dataset of student assignments that includes detailed assignment descriptions, rubrics, and student submissions across various courses. As a result, research on generalisable methodology for automatic generation of effective and responsible educational feedback remains limited. In the current study, we constructed a large-scale dataset of S ynthetic C omputer science A ssignments for L LM-generated E ducational Feedback research ( SCALEFeedback ). We proposed a Sophisticated Assignment Mimicry ( SAM ) framework to generate the synthetic dataset by one-to-one LLM-based imitation from real assignment descriptions, rubrics, and student submissions to produce their synthetic versions. Our open-source dataset contains 10,000 synthetic student submissions spanning 155 assignments across 59 university-level computer science courses. We evaluated the dataset’s resemblance to real data in terms of assignment marks, semantic similarity, length, and privacy protection. Using the dataset, we employed 10 common LLMs to generate feedback on both real and synthetic assignment submissions and compared effectiveness of their feedback. Our findings showed that our synthetic assignment descriptions achieved a BERTScore F1 of 0.859 for semantic similarity, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) values of 0.931 for length, while our synthetic student submissions achieved a BERTScore F1 of 0.840 for semantic similarity, PCC values of 0.624 for assignment marks and 0.852 for length, compared to the corresponding real-world assignment dataset, while ensuring perfect protection of student private information. All these results of our SAM framework outperformed results of a naïve mimicry method baseline. Moreover, the LLM-generated feedback for our synthetic assignments demonstrated the same level of effectiveness compared to that of feedback generated for the real-world assignment dataset. Our research showed that one-to-one LLM imitation is a promising method for generating open-source synthetic educational textual datasets that preserve the original dataset’s semantic meaning and student data distribution, while protecting student privacy and institutional copyright. SCALEFeedback enhances our ability to develop LLM-based generalisable methods for offering high-quality, automated educational feedback in a scalable way.

(
Liffiton et al. 2023

Research on generalisable methodologies for LLM-based automated feedback systems – tested across different types of computer science assignments for effectiveness and responsibility – remains largely unexplored. A critical barrier limiting such research is the lack of open educational datasets

;

. To the best of our knowledge, there is currently no open dataset that comprehensively includes assignment descriptions and diverse student submissions across various assignments and courses. The creation of such a dataset is likely to conflict with university intellectual property rights and student privacy.

To address this pressing need, in the current study, we constructed a large-scale dataset of Synthetic Computer science Assignments for LLM-generated Educational Feedback research,
SCALEFeedback
. To construct SCALEFeedback, we developed and used a novel Sophisticated Assignment Mimicry (
SAM
) framework that leverages LLMs to generate synthetic versions by imitating real assignment descriptions, rubrics, and student submissions. By generating one-to-one synthetic assignments from real ones using LLMs, we maintained strong similarity to the original assignment descriptions and preserved the distribution of both correctness levels and student writing styles.. We evaluated SCALEFeedback through (i) direct data resemblance including semantic similarity, length and student marks; and (ii) utility evaluation of by comparing the effectiveness of LLM-generated feedback for real student assignment submissions versus our synthetic ones. We compared our results against synthetic data generated by naïve mimicry and examined how the abstraction-based privacy protection mechanisms in the SAM framework influenced performance. Moreover, we analysed the effects of choosing different LLMs in generation of synthetic assignment descriptions and synthetic student submissions.

Our contributions can be summarised as follows:

·

We constructed an open assignment dataset with 10,000 synthetic student submissions spanning 155 assignments across 59 university-level computer science courses, facilitating development of generalisable methods for offering high-quality, automated educational feedback in a scalable way. Since the AAAI26 anonymous submission does not allow insertion of hyperlinks, the dataset and codes are put in supplementary materials, and will be made publicly available upon acceptance.

·

We proposed the SAM framework that can transform sensitive real-world dataset into synthetic, open-sourcable dataset. We found that this new method of LLM-based one-to-one data synthesis can highly preserve the original sensitive dataset’s semantic meaning and distribution, while protecting private information and institutional copyright.

·

Our results highlighted the importance of privacy protection methods in LLM-based one-to-one data synthesis.

·

The effects of choosing different LLMs in one-to-one data synthesis are compared and discussed in the current work.

Dataset Construction

Figure 1:
Overview of SCALEFeedback dataset generation and evaluation experiments.

Real-world Dataset

We retrieved the assignment dataset of all university courses delivered during the first semester of 2021 from the computer science school in a [country blinded] university. Within these courses, all assignment descriptions (including rubrics, reading materials and other assignment supplementary materials) and student submissions, where the majority of assignment files were in the .pdf, .txt, .sql, .py, .docx, .ipynb and .rmd file formats, were collected. We converted them into .txt files, where tables in these files were transformed carefully to structured words that LLMs can understand. This ensured that all assignment files were understandable for LLMs developed since the release of GPT-3.5.

We collected 45,126 student submissions in total, where some of them were too long for some LLMs to take as inputs. We excluded assignments with more than 7,500 words (10,000 tokens for LLMs) in assignment descriptions and corresponding student submissions in total, so that even old and affordable LLMs like GPT-3.5 (with 16k context window) can take assignments in our dataset as input to generate educational feedback to students. We then randomly selected 10,000 student submissions and their corresponding assignment descriptions based, proportionally weighted the selection by the number of submissions per assignment, to reduce cost of LLM-based data synthesis. These 10,000 student submissions spanned 155 assignments across 59 university-level computer science courses.

Data Synthesis

SAM.
We developed a
S
ophisticated
A
ssignment
M
imicry (
SAM
) framework to generate the synthetic assignment descriptions and synthetic student submissions in SCALEFeedback dataset, to preserve the original dataset’s semantic meaning and student data distribution, while protecting student privacy and institutional copyright. This was done by one-to-one LLM-based imitation. A real university assignment description was included in the prompt for LLMs to generate a synthetic one. After that, both the real assignment description and the corresponding synthetic one, and a real student submission for the real assignment, were included in the prompt for LLMs to generate a synthetic student submission in response to the synthetic assignment description.

After inclusion of real assignments, the SAM framework adopted a 4-step mimicry instruction in the prompt:

1).

Evaluate the original one.
To generate synthetic assignment descriptions, this included theme, objectives, writing style, length and rubric details of the original one. To generate synthetic student assignment, this included i) to what extent, ii) how detailed, and iii) how correctly the real student submission answered the assignment description, where the LLM was instructed to assess the mark of the real submission according to its rubric. The number of words of the real submission was directly added to the prompt.

2).

Generate a synthetic one.
The LLM was instructed to imitate the evaluated dimensions in step 1 to create a synthetic assignment description / submission in this step.

3).

Evaluate the synthetic one.
The LLM needed to evaluate the generation of step 2 along the same evaluation dimensions as it evaluated the original one.

4).

Compare, then output or loop to step 2.
The LLM compared evaluation results of the synthetic one produced in step 3 with the real one produced in step 1. If the LLM thought the results reflecting a successful imitation, then output the synthetic one; else loop to step 2. For example, when generating a synthetic student submission, the LLM needed to compare the mark of the original one concluded in step 1 with the mark of the synthetic one concluded in step 3, together with evaluation results of other dimensions, to make a decision.

Despite the LLM’s internal evaluation within prompt instructions in generation of synthetic assignments, we set up an external LLM-based privacy gate to further ensure privacy protection of real students. The complete synthetic student submission generation process was as follows:

1).

Generate.
Use the above 4-step mimicry instruction prompt to generate a synthetic student submission of a synthetic assignment.

2).

Judge.
Use o4-mini (high reasoning effort) to check whether real students’ private information was included in the generated synthetic student submission or not. This was done by a 3-step instruction prompt: i). Output the student name and id found in the real student submission in the first line; ii). Output the student name and id found in the synthetic one in the second line; iii). If the first line equaled to the second line, output “YES” in the third line.

3).

Check, then add to the dataset or loop to step 1.
If “YES” was included in step 2 output, then loop to step 1. Else, add step 1 generation results to the SCALEFeedback dataset.

Naïve mimicry baseline.
As discussed in Related Work section, there is currently no algorithm in the literature that is designed to generate a synthetic dataset by one-to-one imitation from a real-world sensitive dataset. Therefore, we designed a
Naïve mimicry
method to serve as the baseline, which simply replaced the above 4-step instructions in the mimicry prompt by a plain instruction: “
Please try to generate a university assignment description (submission) by imitating the following assignment description (submission)
”, and removed the privacy gate schema. The choice of LLMs in synthetic data generation was the same as in the SAM generation.

Resemblance Evaluation

From 155 assignments, we proportionally sampled 200 synthetic student submissions and their corresponding assignment descriptions, weighted by the number of submissions per assignment, for use in the resemblance evaluation. We compared the evaluation results with assignments generated using the naïve mimicry method for the same original real assignments. We held the believe that the synthetic data should be as similar to the original real data as possible to preserve meaningful real-world data distribution and can be used like the original dataset in research. The evaluation dimensions included:

·

BERTScore F1 and Std.
We used this common metrics to evaluate semantic similarity
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
between synthetic assignments and original ones.

·

PCC and MAE for Length.
We used Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of the number of words in synthetic assignments compared with corresponding original ones to measure how well synthetic assignments maintained the same length of the original assignments.

·

PCC and MAE for Assignment Marks.
We used LLMs as assignment graders
Yeung et al. 2025
;
Zeng et al. 2024
of real and synthetic student assignment submissions and then used PCC and MAE of submission marks of synthetic student submissions compared with original ones to evaluate how well synthetic student submissions maintain the same level of correctness as the corresponding original real submissions. We observed that different LLMs could give substantially different marks to the same student submission, despite following the same assignment description and rubric. To address this variability, we graded each submission with three LLMs (o3-high, o4-mini-high and GPT-4.1) and computd the mean value of their scores, on a 0–100 scale, as the final mark.

The violin plots in Figure

Regarding assignment submission marks, the SAM submissions had a moderate positive correlation with original ones (PCC=0.624), which was much stronger than that of submissions generated by the naïve mimicry method (PCC=0.624). While SAM achieved lower mean absolute error (MAE) than naïve mimicry (19.98 vs. 26.09), both methods exhibited a notable level of error in assignment marks, reflecting significant discrepancies from the real submissions and underscoring the need for further refinement. The violin reinforce these findings. While the distribution of SAM-generated marks more closely matched that of the original assignment marks compared to the naïve mimicry, the average mark remained substantially higher than the original average. However, the violin plots of synthetic submissions generated by both methods exhibited a more centred distribution than the real submissions This suggests that LLM-based data synthesis is less effective at imitating long-tailed patterns of assignment submissions, particularly those with very lengthy responses and higher-than-average marks.

Utility Evaluation

For the utility evaluation, we employed 10 common commercial LLMs – OpenAI (GPT-4.1 nano, GPT-4.1, o4-mini-high and o3-high), Google (Gemini 2.0 Flate-Lite, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash and Gemini 2.5 Pro) and Deepseek (Deepseek R1 0528 and Deepseek V3 0324). Each model generated 100 feedback instances for the assignments in SCALEFeedback and also generated 100 feedback instances for their corresponding original real assignments, resulting in 2,000 feedback samples - i.e, 1,000 for SCALEFeedback and and 1,000 for the real ones. We chose these LLMs to represent a wide variety of current common LLMs, spanning from old models to the newest models, from weak models to state-of-the-art models, from non-reasoning models to reasoning models. We then evaluated and compared the feedback quality between the synthetic and real datasets. The 1,000 assignment submissions were proportionally selected based on the number of submissions for each assignment in the dataset. To assess the general quality of LLM‑generated feedback, we selected a diverse range of LLMs that differed in size, capability, release recency, and reasoning capacity.

We comprehensively evaluated the generated feedback with the following dimensions that are widely recognised evaluation dimensions in the feedback and LLM literature. The supporting literature are included for each dimension as follows.

Discussion

The availability of SCALEFeedback is poised to catalyse a new generation of research into automated educational feedback. By providing a diverse, multi-assignment, multi-course benchmark, it creates opportunities for the field to move from bespoke, context-specific solutions to the development of generalisable feedback systems. Researchers can now investigate more sophisticated questions: How can feedback models adapt to different pedagogical goals as expressed in varying rubrics? How can we generate feedback that is not only correct but also motivationally attuned and aligned with principles of effective pedagogy? Furthermore, the SAM framework itself is a powerful contribution with broad implications. It provides a clear methodology for other domains and education institutions to unlock their own vast repositories of open data. This could lead to a proliferation of similar large-scale, privacy-safe datasets across numerous academic disciplines, fostering a more collaborative, open, and accelerated research ecosystem.

Despite the advancements, the current work also has some limitation. The violin plots in Figure
2
and Feedback quality Std in Figure
3
shows that our LLM-based data synthesis method did not fully capture the behavioural diversity in imitating long-tailed data of assignment submissions with too many words and higher-than-average marks. This aligns with results in the literature showing that LLM’s responses often lack diversity which exhibit significant biases
(
Tseng et al. 2024
;
Santurkar et al. 2023
)
. To improve, one avenue of future research is the integration of more advanced persona-prompting techniques into our framework, such as the Mixture-of-Personas framework proposed by
Bui et al. 2025
.

The results in Table
3
and Table
1
show that all currently common OpenAI LLMs and the two one-to-one synthetic data generation share similar values of BERTScore F1. This may indicate that BERTScore F1 is not a distinctive enough metrics in assessing to what extent LLMs can generate synthetic assignments with the same theme and writing styles of the original real assignments. Therefore, more advanced metrics, like comparative evaluation of generated synthetic assignments through the LLM-as-a-Judge method
(
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
)
is an important direction for future research.

As shown in Table
3
, several OpenAI models – including both reasoning and non-reasoning models – exhibited similar performance on the assignment imitation task. This finding is consistent with prior research indicating that chain-of-thought reasoning primarily improves performance in mathematical and symbolic reasoning tasks
(
Sprague et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Lobo, Agarwal, and Lakkaraju 2024
)
. Because o3-pro (high reasoning effort) achieved the highest PCC and lowest MAE values for lengths of synthetic assignment descriptions, and the original dataset contained relatively few number of assignment descriptions, this model was the most suitable choice for generating synthetic assignment descriptions. Regarding the generation of synthetic student submissions, the results showed that multiple models achieved comparable performance. Considering the large volume of submissions, o4-mini offers the most practical and cost-effective option.

Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we introduced SCALEFeedback, a novel, large-scale dataset of synthetic computer science assignments designed to accelerate research in LLM-generated educational feedback. We proposed the Sophisticated Assignment Mimicry (SAM) framework, a one-to-one LLM-based imitation method, to transform a real-world, sensitive educational dataset into a publicly accessible resource. Our evaluations demonstrated that SAM successfully generated synthetic assignments and student submissions that maintained high fidelity as compared to the original data in terms of semantic content, length distribution, and student performance distribution. Crucially, our framework achieved this while ensuring perfect protection of student privacy, significantly outperforming a naïve baseline. Furthermore, a utility evaluation confirmed that the quality of LLM-generated feedback for our synthetic dataset was statistically indistinguishable from that generated for the original real-world assignments. The SCALEFeedback dataset and SAM framework overcome a critical barrier in research on LLM-based automated feedback for university assignments, facilitating the development and rigorous benchmarking of scalable, generalisable, effective, and responsible automated feedback systems.

Looking ahead, several promising avenues for future research emerge from this work. A primary direction is to enhance the mimetic capabilities of the SAM framework to better capture the full spectrum of student diversity. Our findings indicate that LLM-generated submissions tend to cluster around the mean, failing to replicate the long-tail distributions of exceptional or struggling students. Future iterations could integrate more sophisticated persona-prompting techniques, such as a mixture-of-personas approach
(
