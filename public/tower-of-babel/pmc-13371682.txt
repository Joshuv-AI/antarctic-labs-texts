Conversational artificial intelligence in the operating room: an exploratory cross-sectional single-center survey among anesthesiology residents
Authors: Dario Gaetano, Viola Lomonaco, Salvatore Ferraioli, Vincenzo Pota, Umberto Colella, Maria Caterina Pace, Pasquale Sansone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConversational artificial intelligence (AI) tools, such as large language models and chat-based systems, are increasingly used by clinicians and trainees. However, empirical evidence on how anesthesiology residents integrate conversational AI into perioperative workflows, especially within the operating room, is lacking. This exploratory single-center observational study aimed to characterize prevalence, patterns, and determinants of conversational AI use in the operating room.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, anonymous online survey of anesthesiology and intensive care medicine residents in a single training program. The instrument was developed through iterative refinement and addressed knowledge, training, general AI usage habits, perioperative use, supervision, attitudes, safety perceptions, and data protection behaviors. Reporting followed STROBE and CHERRIES guidelines. The primary outcome was self-reported use of conversational AI in the operating room within the previous 3 months. Bivariate analyses and logistic regression examined associations with demographic, behavioral, and attitudinal factors.ResultsAmong 101 responses, 86 residents met inclusion criteria. The mean age was 31.8 (SD 3.9) years; 58.1% were female. Conversational AI use in the operating room was reported by 58.1% (95% CI 47.6–68.0%). Higher general frequency of AI use was independently associated with operating room use (adjusted OR 3.10; 95% CI 1.83–5.92). Training seniority was also associated with operating room use (adjusted OR 1.97 per additional residency year; 95% CI 1.27–3.33). Female gender was associated with higher odds of operating room use (adjusted OR 3.97; 95% CI 1.17–15.28). Attitudinal variables showed strong univariable associations with operating room conversational AI use, but these associations were attenuated after multivariable adjustment. Cronbach’s alpha for the attitudinal scale was 0.79 (95% CI 0.71–0.85).ConclusionsIn this exploratory single-center study, conversational AI use in the operating room was reported by a substantial proportion of anesthesiology residents (~ 60%) and was associated with general AI usage patterns and residency seniority. Formal training, supervision, and institutional policies appeared limited. These findings highlight the need for competency-based educational initiatives and clear governance to support safe and effective integration of conversational AI into perioperative care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03937-2.Keywords: Conversational artificial intelligence, Operating room, Anesthesiology residents, Digital health, Perioperative care, Clinical survey, Medical education

Background

Conversational artificial intelligence (AI) tools—most notably large language models and chat-based assistants—have rapidly transitioned from experimental prototypes to readily available applications on personal smartphones, institutional computers, and web browsers. Clinicians and trainees increasingly interact with these systems for a broad spectrum of tasks, including rapid information retrieval, assistance with clinical reasoning, drafting of clinical documentation, translation and rewriting of text, and support for teaching and learning activities [1]. Recent advances in large language models have shown that these systems encode substantial amounts of clinical knowledge and can generate medically coherent responses across a wide range of clinical tasks, including diagnosis, management reasoning, and medical question answering [2, 3]. In anesthesiology, this evolution is occurring in parallel with a longer trajectory of AI adoption in areas such as predictive analytics, closed-loop drug delivery, automated monitoring, and scheduling optimization.

However, most of the existing literature on AI in anesthesiology has focused on non-conversational systems: predictive models for perioperative complications, algorithms for hemodynamic optimization, or closed-loop anesthesia controllers embedded in monitoring platforms and anesthesia information management systems [4]. These studies have primarily evaluated performance metrics such as discrimination, calibration, or impact on predefined clinical endpoints [5]. In contrast, relatively little is known about how conversational AI tools—general-purpose systems like ChatGPT and similar models—are actually used by anesthesiologists and, critically, by anesthesiology residents in routine clinical practice [4].

Cross-sectional surveys of physician anesthesiologists have begun to describe knowledge, exposure, and attitudes toward AI more broadly, reporting considerable openness to future AI integration but also concerns related to malpractice, algorithmic opacity, and professional identity [6, 7]. These studies typically address “AI in anesthesiology” at a high level and focus on physicians’ expectations or perceived impact on workflow and workforce. Yet they seldom capture the granular, context-specific behaviors that characterize the real-world adoption of conversational AI tools in high-stakes environments such as the operating room [4]. Moreover, the small body of survey literature that does exist rarely isolates anesthesiology residents as the primary population of interest. To our knowledge, there is limited evidence focused exclusively on anesthesiology residents’ use of conversational AI within the operating room environment, where supervision, accountability, and patient-safety considerations are particularly salient [8, 9].

Anesthesiology residents represent a particularly important group in this respect. As digital-native clinicians and frequent early adopters of new technologies, they may introduce conversational AI into perioperative care before institutional policies, educational programs, or explicit supervision models have been fully developed [10]. Residents routinely rotate across different surgical specialties and operating room teams, face high cognitive load and time pressure, and actively seek tools that promise efficiency, support for decision-making, or educational value [7, 8]. It is therefore plausible that conversational AI tools are already being used in ways that may affect information flow, clinical reasoning, and communication in the operating room —well ahead of formal governance structures [11, 12].

Despite this, there is a striking paucity of empirical data on how often anesthesiology residents use conversational AI in the operating room, for which purposes, under which conditions, and which individual factors are associated with adoption [4]. In particular, little is known about the relationship between general AI usage patterns and perioperative use, about attitudinal profiles (perceived efficiency, safety, educational benefit, and risk), or about the role of training seniority and supervision [9, 10, 13]. In the absence of such data, efforts to design targeted educational interventions, supervisory frameworks, or institutional policies risk being either misaligned with current practice or excessively reactive to anecdotal concerns [14].

To address this gap, we conducted a single-center, exploratory, observational study based on an anonymous online survey of anesthesiology and intensive care medicine residents in a single residency program. This cross-sectional survey was specifically designed to capture prevalence, patterns, and determinants of conversational AI use in the operating room, with a focus on real-world behaviors and attitudes. The study is explicitly exploratory and single-center, with the dual aim of generating early empirical evidence and informing subsequent multicenter and longitudinal work.

The primary objective was to estimate the prevalence of conversational AI use in the operating room among anesthesiology residents over the preceding 3 months. Secondary objectives were to:

(i)describe general and perioperative AI use patterns;

describe general and perioperative AI use patterns;

(ii)characterize attitudes, perceived risks, and perceived benefits of conversational AI; and.

(iii)identify factors independently associated with operating room use, including general frequency of AI use, training level, and attitudinal variables.

The study follows the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) recommendations for cross-sectional observational studies and the CHERRIES (Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys) guidelines for online surveys, with the intention of ensuring transparency, reproducibility, and adequate reporting of an exploratory, single-center design [15, 16].

Methods

Study design and setting

We performed a cross-sectional, anonymous, questionnaire-based survey among residents enrolled in a single anesthesiology and intensive care medicine residency program. The study design is observational, exploratory, and single-center, reflecting the intent to generate hypothesis-generating data from one institutional context rather than to provide definitive estimates of national or international prevalence.

The survey was open during a predefined time window. No changes were made to the questionnaire once the survey was launched. Data collection and analysis were conducted independently of the developers of the AI tools under study.

Survey administration and CHERRIES items

The survey was administered using Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) and distributed via institutional email to residents enrolled in the anesthesiology and intensive care medicine training program. The survey was distributed through an invitation-only institutional mailing list (closed survey) and was accessible exclusively to invited participants. Data collection took place from November 2025 to December 2025.

To preserve full participant anonymity, the survey did not require login with a Google or institutional account and did not collect email addresses, IP addresses, or other identifiers. Technical restrictions such as the “limit to one response” option were not applied in order to maintain full anonymity. Participants were explicitly instructed to complete the survey only once. Given the controlled distribution and the defined target population, the likelihood of multiple submissions from the same participant was considered low. All questionnaire items were mandatory except the item on the prevalent rotation site/presidio, which was optional and was excluded a priori from inferential analyses due to missingness and heterogeneity of responses. Conditional branching logic was implemented: follow-up questions regarding clinical decision support (e.g., drug or dosage suggestions, verification practices, supervision, context and account used) were displayed only to participants who reported using conversational AI for clinical decision support. The questionnaire was administered as a structured multi-section survey using Google Forms, with items distributed across sequential sections. No dedicated review step was implemented; participants submitted responses directly after completing the questionnaire. No changes were made to the questionnaire after survey launch. Reporting followed the STROBE statement for cross-sectional observational studies and the CHERRIES checklist for internet e-surveys (15,16). The completed CHERRIES checklist is provided as Additional file 3, and the STROBE checklist as Additional file 4.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), version 4.5.2. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed due to the exploratory design. Model complexity was limited a priori using a parsimonious, theory-driven approach to reduce overfitting given the expected number of events. Continuous variables were summarized as mean (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as absolute counts and percentages. The prevalence of conversational AI use in the operating room (D0) was estimated with a 95% confidence interval (CI) computed using the Wilson method for binomial proportions [18].

The internal consistency of the attitudinal scale F1–F6 was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha [17]. A value around 0.7 was considered acceptable for an exploratory attitudinal measure.

For bivariate analyses, residents who reported using conversational AI in the operating room (D0 = “Yes”) were compared against those who did not (D0 = “No”). Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests, and continuous or ordinal variables—including Likert scores—were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Fisher’s exact test was used instead of the chi-square test when expected cell counts were less than 5.

To explore factors associated with the primary outcome, we first fitted univariable logistic regression models with D0 as the dependent variable and each candidate predictor entered separately. We then constructed a multivariable logistic regression model including a parsimonious, theory-driven set of predictors, in line with events-per-variable considerations for a relatively small, exploratory dataset. The final model included:

general frequency of AI use (D1, 0–4);

general frequency of AI use (D1, 0–4);

comfort with AI (F4, 1–5);

comfort with AI (F4, 1–5);

year of residency (B1, 1–5);

year of residency (B1, 1–5);

gender (B3; female vs. male);

gender (B3; female vs. male);

age (B4, years).

age (B4, years).

The general frequency of conversational AI use (D1) was modeled as an ordinal predictor assuming an approximately linear effect across ordered categories (0–4), in line with a parsimonious approach suitable for an exploratory analysis. Under this specification, the corresponding odds ratio should be interpreted as an approximate average effect associated with a one-category increase in conversational AI use frequency.

For each predictor, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs and p-values were reported. The McFadden pseudo-R² was used to summarize overall model performance. This metric does not represent explained variance in the linear-regression sense and should be interpreted as a relative measure of model fit rather than a proportion of variance explained. All tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the number of bivariate comparisons performed, no formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied and findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously because of the increased risk of type I error. Item non-response was minimized by mandatory fields except for B2. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and sensitivity analyses and penalized regression models were performed to assess the robustness of findings (Additional file 2).

Ethics

The study protocol (CAI-OR-25-11) was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of University of Campania “Luigi Vanvitelli” (approval registration n° 0010196/I). The anonymous survey involved healthcare professionals only and did not collect identifiable personal data. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained through a mandatory initial consent question before participants could proceed with the questionnaire. The study was conducted in accordance with applicable data protection regulations [18] and with the internationally accepted ethical principles for medical research involving human participants, Declaration of Helsinki [19].

A total of 101 survey responses were received during the study period, corresponding to 101 responses from 330 invited residents (Fig. 1). Of these, one participant was excluded for declining consent (A1 = “No”), and 14 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria (A2 = “No”), leaving 86 residents in the final analytic sample (inclusion rate: 86/101). Because the exact number of residents who had worked in the operating room during the preceding 3 months was not known at the time of the survey, the overall response rate among eligible residents could not be precisely calculated and is therefore not reported as a percentage.

Survey flow

A total of 101 participants accessed and submitted the questionnaire. One response was excluded due to declined electronic informed consent, and 14 responses were excluded because inclusion criteria were not met. The final analytic sample therefore consisted of 86 eligible participants who completed the survey.

The mean age of respondents was 31.8 (SD 3.9) years (range 26–51). Baseline demographic and training characteristics of the final analytic sample are summarized in Table 1. Formal training in AI or digital health (C1) was reported by a minority of residents, whereas the vast majority reported at least some familiarity with conversational AI tools (C2). Awareness of institutional policies (C3) and specific data protection issues (C4–C5) was variable, with a substantial proportion of “I do not know” responses, suggesting uncertainty about formal regulatory frameworks.

Prevalence and patterns of conversational AI use

Overall, 50 of 86 residents (58.1%) reported using conversational AI in the operating room during the previous 3 months (D0 = “Yes”). The 95% CI, calculated using the Wilson method, was 47.6–68.0%, indicating that conversational AI use in the operating room was reported by a substantial proportion of residents even within this single-center exploratory sample (Table 2).

Clinical decision-support–related conversational AI use was reported by 51 of 86 residents (59.3%). Among these residents, 48 of 51 (94.1%) also reported using conversational AI for approximate drug or dosage suggestions (D4). Verification practices among residents reporting clinical decision-support–related conversational AI use (D5–D6) were variable. Of the 51 residents who reported such use, 29 (56.9%) reported always verifying conversational AI outputs, 16 (31.4%) reported frequent verification, 4 (7.8%) reported occasional verification, and 2 (3.9%) reported never verifying AI-generated information. Reported verification strategies included cross-checking against guidelines, textbooks, or discussion with attending anesthesiologists. Residents reported heterogeneous patterns of conversational AI use across devices, accounts, and clinical contexts (D7-D8). Residents described using conversational AI on personal smartphones, personal laptops, and less frequently on institutional computers. Personal, non-institutional accounts were more commonly reported than institutional or dedicated professional accounts. Missing responses were limited for most variables included in descriptive and inferential analyses because questionnaire items were primarily mandatory. As expected, higher missingness was observed for the optional item on rotation site (B2) and for selected contextual variables (D7–D8), which were not included in inferential models.

Attitudes and psychometric properties

Residents’ attitudes toward conversational AI were assessed using six Likert-scale items (F1–F6) addressing perceived efficiency, perceived error reduction, educational value, comfort with AI, desire for formal training, and perceived balance between risks and benefits.

Overall, attitudes were moderately to strongly positive. Median scores were highest for perceived efficiency and educational value, whereas perceptions related to error reduction and overall safety were more heterogeneous. A large proportion of residents expressed a strong desire for formal training on the appropriate use of conversational AI in clinical settings, regardless of current operating room use.

When stratified by operating room use status, residents who reported using conversational AI in the operating room showed consistently higher median scores across most attitudinal items compared with non-users, particularly for perceived efficiency, comfort with AI, and desire for training. These differences were explored further in bivariate and multivariable analyses (Table 3).

Discussion

In this exploratory, single-center observational study, we found that a more than half of residents of anesthesiology residents (58,1%) reported recent conversational AI use in the operating room. General AI usage habits, training seniority, and gender were independently associated with this behavior. These findings provide an early empirical description of conversational AI use patterns among anesthesiology trainees within perioperative environments, in a context where formal policies, training curricula, and supervision frameworks are still evolving [20, 21]. Given the cross-sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. By focusing on anesthesiology residents in the operating room, an environment encompassing clinical phases with heterogeneous levels of acuity and decision urgency, our results have relevance beyond the local context, informing how training programs may anticipate and govern conversational AI use before it becomes formally embedded in perioperative workflows [22, 23].

Principal findings and interpretation

One of the main findings was the relatively high prevalence of self-reported conversational AI use in the operating room (58.1%, 95% CI 47.6–68.0%), despite the lack of structured institutional strategies for AI integration. These findings are consistent with the possibility that conversational AI use within high-stakes environments may currently occur in a predominantly resident-driven manner, reflecting broader patterns of conversational AI use and digital tool engagement rather than deliberate organizational planning [10].

General frequency of conversational AI use showed the strongest association with operating room use in the primary multivariable model, although supplementary analyses suggested that this relationship may not be strictly linear across increasing frequency categories. Residents who engaged more frequently with conversational AI for any purpose were substantially more likely to use it in the operating room, even after adjusting for age, gender, and training level. This association may reflect broader patterns of digital engagement and familiarity with conversational AI tools in everyday tasks. Formal AI training was infrequently reported in our sample, whereas self-reported conversational AI use was common across varying levels of prior training exposure [24].

Training seniority emerged as another key predictor. More advanced residents, those in later years of the program, were more likely to use conversational AI in the operating room, independent of general AI usage frequency. Senior residents typically have greater autonomy in managing perioperative care, more responsibility for supervising juniors, and a broader understanding of workflows and information needs. One possible interpretation is that conversational AI may be perceived as supportive of both clinical and educational activities among more senior trainees, including structuring explanations for junior residents and rapidly cross-checking information at the point of care [1, 7].

An association between female gender and operating room conversational AI use was observed in the multivariable model. In contrast to previous surveys of physician anesthesiologists, where male gender was associated with more positive attitudes toward AI, our exploratory findings showed a different pattern within this resident cohort [6]. However, this estimate was characterized by a wide confidence interval and should be interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size and single-center design. These findings should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of gender-related differences in conversational AI adoption.

Attitudinal variables such as perceived efficiency, error reduction, comfort with AI, and desire for training were strongly associated with operating room use in univariable analyses, but their effects were attenuated in the multivariable model once general AI use and seniority were included. This pattern may indicate overlap between attitudinal measures and broader conversational AI usage behaviors, although the directionality of these associations cannot be determined in a cross-sectional design. These findings may nevertheless be relevant in the context of prior discussions on experiential reinforcement and downstream cognitive biases in medical education literature [25]. Nevertheless, the consistently high desire for formal training highlights that residents recognize both the potential and the risks of conversational AI and are seeking guidance on how to use these tools appropriately [8, 10].

Comparison with existing literature

Most prior work on AI in anesthesiology has focused on non-conversational applications and on staff anesthesiologists, emphasizing predictive performance, technical feasibility, and high-level attitudes [4–6]. Our study differs in several respects:

It focuses on conversational AI tools rather than on predictive algorithms or monitoring systems [4, 5].

It targets residents rather than attending physicians, capturing a population that is likely to be at the forefront of early adoption in everyday practice [7, 10].

Our findings align with previous surveys in showing that many anesthesiologists—and, in our case, residents—are open to AI and perceive potential benefits in terms of efficiency and effectiveness [6, 8]. They also echo prior concerns about safety, legal liability, and algorithmic opacity [23], even though these were not the primary focus of our questionnaire. However, by documenting concrete patterns of conversational AI use in the operating room, this study moves beyond abstract attitudes and provides early empirical evidence that conversational AI tools are being used by some trainees within day-to-day perioperative practice, often in the absence of formal oversight [7, 9].

Taken together, these findings support the development of institution-level, privacy-preserving policies that define acceptable use cases, verification expectations, and supervision responsibilities for conversational AI in perioperative care [20, 22]. Establishing replicable policy templates and competency-based curricula could facilitate safer, more consistent implementation across centers as conversational AI tools and regulatory frameworks evolve [23, 28].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It is, to our knowledge, among the first to focus specifically on conversational AI use in the operating room by anesthesiology residents, complementing the limited existing survey literature that has often combined attending anesthesiologists and trainees [8]. The questionnaire was systematically developed and covered a wide range of domains, from knowledge and behaviors to attitudes, supervision, and data protection, allowing for a nuanced description of emerging patterns. The analytic strategy combined descriptive, psychometric, and multivariable approaches, and reporting adheres to STROBE and CHERRIES recommendations, enhancing transparency and reproducibility [15, 16].

However, the limitations, closely linked to the exploratory and single-center design, are equally important:

The sample is not representative of all residency years, with an overrepresentation of senior residents, which may limit generalizability and bias prevalence estimates toward higher adoption. Although residency year was included in the multivariable model and independently associated with operating room AI use, residual confounding and limited representativeness of earlier training stages cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In this exploratory, single-center observational study, conversational AI use in the operating room was reported by a substantial proportion of anesthesiology residents, with approximately 60% reporting recent use. General frequency of AI use and training seniority were independently associated with operating room conversational AI use, whereas the observed gender association should be interpreted cautiously given the imprecision of the estimate. Overall, the findings highlight associations between operating room conversational AI use and broader digital usage patterns, residency seniority, and demographic characteristics.

Residents generally perceived conversational AI as potentially beneficial for efficiency and education, but expressed uncertainty regarding safety, medico-legal implications, and institutional policies. Formal AI training, supervision, and governance structures appeared limited or absent.

These findings underscore the need for structured, competency-based educational programs and clear institutional guidance to support safe, ethical, and effective use of conversational AI in anesthetic practice. As AI tools and regulatory frameworks evolve, ongoing monitoring of adoption patterns among trainees will be essential to ensure that conversational AI enhances, rather than undermines, the quality and safety of perioperative care.
