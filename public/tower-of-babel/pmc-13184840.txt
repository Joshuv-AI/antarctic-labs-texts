Delayed Diagnosis of Congenital Aortopathies
Authors: Mourad Boukheloua, Yehya Khlidj, Aziza Baali, Selma Aroua, Mohamed Berrehal
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent case report of Lin et al1 describing a double aortic arch (DAA) incidentally identified during pre–transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) evaluation in a comorbid elderly woman. Despite having a marked right-sided arch angulation with extreme tortuosity, the patient remained asymptomatic until her seventh decade, when she started to experience dyspnea and chest tightness. This led to the identification of severe aortic stenosis by transthoracic echocardiography. Subsequently, the DAA was revealed by a computed tomography (CT) scan.

Such a scenario is exceptional, as DAA typically manifests in childhood (median age: 11 years), often due to tracheal or esophageal compression.2 The complexity of the aortic arch anatomy, alongside bilaterally stenotic femoral arteries, prevented proper delivery and positioning of the TAVR device despite multiple attempts and the use of snare-assisted navigation. Postoperatively, the patient developed an embolic stroke, likely due to dislodged calcific debris from the aortic valve or root. Yet, follow-up demonstrated considerable improvement in cardiac symptoms.

This case reminded us of 2 young adults who recently presented to our department with progressive dyspnea, chest discomfort, and diaphoresis. Physical examination revealed pressure gradient between the upper and lower limbs alongside absent femoral pulse. Ultimately, they were found to have coexistent aortic coarctation (CoA) and aberrant right subclavian artery (ARSA) (Figure 1). As in the case described by Lin et al,1 diagnosis was achieved only through combined echocardiography and CT scan, which demonstrated mild to moderate postductal aortic narrowing and a right subclavian artery originating from the aortic isthmus. The aberrant artery crossed posterior to the trachea and esophagus in the left-to-right direction without any signs of compression. The absence of severe hemodynamic burden or compression of local structures would have contributed to the subtle symptomatology, thereby delaying diagnosis into adulthood. Congenital heart diseases are characterized by a wide spectrum of heterogeneous clinical presentations, with possible asymptomatic forms until adulthood or even geriatric age.3 Early recognition of these complex entities is challenging and requires special vigilance from cardiologists. Accordingly, reporting unexpected or atypical scenarios remains essential to expand clinical knowledge, improve diagnostic strategies, and promote patient-tailored management. Current guidelines recommend echocardiography as the first-line imaging modality for suspected aortic disease, particularly for evaluation of the aortic root and ascending aorta.4 However, in patients with persistent unexplained cardiopulmonary symptoms, even in the setting of a normal echocardiogram, CT imaging is warranted to unmask a potential congenital aortopathy.
