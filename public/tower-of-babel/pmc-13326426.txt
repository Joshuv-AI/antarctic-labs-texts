Urban park use and children’s physical activity in Minneapolis-Saint Paul Minnesota: Parks and Recreation for Kids Study (PARKS)
Authors: Simone A French, Daheia J Barr-Anderson, Susan M Mason, Natalie Scholz, Katherine R Hendel, Nancy E Sherwood, Dawn Nederhoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examined urban park use and physical activity (PA) among diverse children to better understand the potential for park use to increase child PA and reduce early childhood cardiometabolic risk factors.MethodsCross-sectional data were collected (2022—2023) from 430 parent–child dyads who lived within a two-mile radius of one of 20 urban parks. Children ages 6—12 years wore an accelerometer for a minimum of 4 days (one weekend day and three weekdays) for ten hours each day to capture moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), light and sedentary activity. Parents reported the child’s past week PA and past year frequency of any park use. General linear models examined differences in child PA by any park use frequency.ResultsObjectively-measured child PA (MVPA, light and sedentary) was not significantly associated with any park use frequency during the past year. Parent-reported child PA was significantly higher among children who most frequently used parks during the past year (12 + park visits/month; active on an average 6.5 days per week (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 6.1, 6.9) compared with children whose park use was less frequent (0—4 park visits / month; active on average of 5.6 days per week (95% CI: 5.3, 6.0; p < 0.01).ConclusionsChild park use frequency was not associated with objectively-measured child PA but was significantly positively associated with parent report of child PA. Objective measures of individual child total daily PA paired with location data are needed to understand the incremental contribution of park use to child PA.Trial registrationNCT05231827.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06978-1.Keywords: Physical activity, Parks, Recreation, Urban, Cardiometabolic health, Children

Background

Physical activity (PA) in children can promote a healthy body weight and reduce cardiometabolic risk factors [1–5]. Children from low-income and marginalized racial/ethnic minority groups are at greatest risk for low PA [5–8]. Parks offer an attractive and affordable opportunity for both unstructured play and structured sports programs [6–9]. However, 30% of children from low-income households do not participate in organized sport activity through school, park and recreation, or other community settings [5–8]. For example, a recent study of 534 diverse 3—5 year-old children in lower-income households found that 50% did not use any of their local parks within 0.5 miles of their home during the past month [10]. Another study of inner city youth found that only 59% ever used a park for PA [11].

Studies of children’s frequency of park use in relationship to their physical activity levels are few and vary in study designs and measures. Child PA has been measured at the park-level [12–16] and the individual child-level [17–21, 21]. Few studies included both park-based and individual child PA measures [10, 22, 23]. For example, at the individual level, a study of high school students reported marginally significant associations between reported park use and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) using individual-level accelerometry [24]. Another study did not measure park use frequency, but observed a positive association between objectively measured number of parks near middle-school-aged girls’ homes and their outside-of-school MVPA measured using accelerometry [21]. A study that measured PA at the park-level [16] observed 2712 children during the summer months as they played in 20 parks. Children were more physically active in court areas, playgrounds and open spaces than in picnic areas. Younger children were more active than older children. PA level did not vary by neighborhood. However, no individual measure of park use frequency was collected. Studies of park renovations and child PA typically focus on children’s PA in the park, using park-level observations of children’s PA [13–15, 22]. Studies that use park-level measurement leave unanswered questions about whether park renovations increase the activity of individual children who are already park users or has any impact on children who are not currently using the park. Results of park renovation studies have shown varied associations with child PA, including no effects [14], decreases in PA among girls [15], and increases among boys [13]. Inconsistencies in the level of measurement of PA (individual-level accelerometry or park-observation) and the child park-user populations (current users or both nonusers and users) could explain some between-study inconsistencies in results.

Another measurement variation between studies that may contribute to the inconsistent findings in the available literature is whether child PA is parent-reported or objectively measured. For example, a park-based intervention found that parents reported that their children increased their physical activity during park visits during an intervention, but objective researcher-observed MVPA was higher among children in control parks [11]. Few studies have measured PA using both methods and rarely is a conceptual rationale provided for the selection of individual- or park-level PA measures.

Measures of park use (or “exposure” to parks) also lack rigor and may in part be responsible for the inconsistent findings in the current literature [10, 16, 18, 20, 22, 24]. Most youth park use measures do not assess season-specific park use or specific activities engaged in during park visits. Parks may be visited more frequently during spring and summer seasons, and for different activities during each season. Park use frequency reported in response to a single general question may include sedentary activities participated in during park visits, such as picnics or arts and crafts activities, and thus would not be expected to be associated with PA. Season- and activity-specific questions about park use are needed to understand how park use may be supportive of higher levels of child PA or why it is not related to child PA.

This study examined park use in relation to youth PA levels among 430 parent–child dyads using the baseline data from the PARKS study (Parks and Recreation for Kids Study). It aimed to expand and clarify research questions about youth park use and PA by using detailed measures of youth park use, including in different seasons and for specific activities (physically active and not physically active). Total daily child PA was captured at the individual level with both objective and parent-reported measures. Two research questions were examined. First, how frequently do children use local parks and recreation centers? Second, is child park use associated with higher levels or certain types of PA (MVPA, light PA)? It was hypothesized that frequent park visits would be associated with higher levels of child MVPA.

Methods

Study design and population

The current paper is a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from the PARKS study. The PARKS study was implemented over a three-year period in partnership with two city park systems: the Saint Paul Parks Department and the Minneapolis Park and Recreation Board, both in Minnesota, USA. (NIH: NCT05231827). Twenty parks (referred to as “study parks” hereafter) located in lower income, racial/ethnically diverse neighborhoods were chosen by the collaborating park system administrators to participate in the study (12 in Saint Paul and 8 in Minneapolis). Every study park included a recreation center building with a gymnasium. The term “park” is used hereafter to refer to both the park and recreation center as a unit.

The evaluation cohort consisted of 430 parent–child dyads who lived within a two-mile radius of one of the 20 study parks. A two-mile radius was selected after the pilot study revealed that the use of a smaller buffer would not achieve recruitment goals within the study time frame and funding constraints. Recruitment strategies included in-person recruitment at parks, community events, schools and libraries, and fliers posted on social media sites. Recruitment was conducted in both Spanish and English and took place over an 18-month period (2022—2023).

Eligibility criteria included: 1) reside within a two-mile radius of one of the 20 study parks; 2) parent of a child ages 6—12 years; 3) child has no medical conditions that prevent usual PA; and 4) child has not enrolled in ≥ 3 youth sports teams during the past year [5, 6]. Adults consented and children assented to participate in this study. This study was reviewed and approved by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Measures were collected on a rolling basis at the park’s recreation center by trained and certified English–Spanish bilingual research staff using a standardized protocol. Parents and children were provided with accelerometers to wear for one week and instructed on how to wear them. Follow-up phone calls and texts were used to achieve high compliance for the accelerometry wear time minimum requirements. A parent Zoom interview survey was conducted during the week of the accelerometry wear. Parents received $20 for completing the survey and $50 for completing the child and their own accelerometry wear (a total of $70). Parent–child dyads were enrolled in the study only if the child met the minimum wear time criteria for accelerometry and the parent completed the Zoom-based interview.

Child physical activity

MVPA, light and sedentary behavior

PA was measured at baseline for the enrolled child using a commercially available ActiGraph GT3X + accelerometer. The validity of the ActiGraph has been examined in several studies involving children aged 2 to 18 years [25–31]. The enrolled child in the study wore the GT3X + monitor on the right hip for seven days for a minimum of ten hours per day. The valid minimum wear time criteria were four days (three weekdays and one weekend day) of at least ten hours of awake time per day with 33% non-zero 15-s epochs per hour [31]. Accelerometry data from each individual child was averaged over the number of days the accelerometer was worn for the minimum wear time. Minutes per day in sedentary, light, moderate and vigorous PA were computed using Evenson’s child-specific cutpoints [31]. Cutpoints are as follows: Sedentary: 0—100 counts; Light: 101—2295 counts; Moderate: 2296—4011 counts; Vigorous: ≥ 4012 counts).

Parent-reported child PA

Parents reported the frequency of their child’s PA using the following question from the Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBSS) [32]: “During the past 7 days, on how many days was [child’s name] physically active for a total of 60 min per day?” Response options were 0 days, 1 day, 2 days, 3 days, 4 days, 5 days, 6 days or 7 days.

Park use

Parents reported their child’s frequency of use of any park (not limited to specific study parks) for any reason during each of the seasons: winter, spring, summer and fall. Seasonal use frequency was summed across seasons to create a cumulative past year park use metric which was then recoded to the month level. The resulting frequency for past year any park use (for any reason) was recoded as follows: “0—4 times/month”, “5—7 times/month”, “8—11 times/month”, and “12 + times/month”. Separately within each season, parents reported the frequency of use of any park for specific reasons, including sports team practice, PA programs, play, and reasons other than PA. Few parents reported using parks 8 or more times a month for any one reason (e.g., sports practice), and thus specific reasons variables were recoded to “0—4 times/month” or “5 + times/month” to avoid sparse cell sizes. Parents reported the number of park and recreation youth sports teams that their child participated in during the past year (responses: zero, one, two, three or more).

Child body mass index

Child body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using research precision grade, calibrated, digital scales and height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a free-standing stadiometer (Seca Corp., Hanover, MD). Child age- and sex-specific body mass index (BMI) percentiles were calculated for children [33, 34].

Household and demographic variables

Parents reported household demographics including annual household income, their own highest level of educational attainment, number of children living in the household, marital status, employment status and child age, gender, race and ethnicity.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS (version 9.4; Cary NC). Descriptive statistics were computed using frequencies and means. The primary analysis examined frequency of any park use for any reason during the past year as a predictor of accelerometry-based minutes of child PA. Categories of any park use frequency (“0—4 times/month”, “5—7 times/month”, “8—11 times/month”, and “12 + times/month”) were used in this analysis. Mixed linear models were used adjusted for seasonality of enrollment, parent education, child age, child gender, and child BMI percentile with a random intercept for assigned study park. In separate equations, park use for specific reasons were examined in relation to child PA, adjusting for the same covariates. Response categories for the specific reasons for park use variables were dichotomized as “0—4 times/month” and “5 + times/month” for analysis due to small cell sizes for the highest frequency of use response categories. Covariates were selected based on the youth PA research literature that shows differences in PA by season, child age, child gender, and child BMI. Parent education was included to adjust for individual-household level socioeconomic status. Household income was not adjusted for because all 20 parks were located in lower-income neighborhoods (determined by census block data). Models were run separately for each of the levels of PA intensity (MVPA, light and sedentary minutes/day). There were no missing child accelerometry data because complete accelerometry data were required for study enrollment. Parent-reported child PA and number of park youth sports programs enrolled in during the past year was analyzed using the same mixed models used for the accelerometry PA outcomes. The parent-reported child PA ranged from zero to seven days (with 95% confidence intervals) and program enrollment used categories “None” and “1 or more”. A priori hypotheses about subgroup differences in the park use–PA association were not formulated. However, to better inform the literature in this area, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to examine potential effect modifiers of the association between child park use frequency and PA. Separate stratified mixed linear models were run for the following variables using the same model described above minus the covariate used to stratify: child sex (male, female), child age group (5—7 yrs; 8—9 yrs; 10—12 yrs), child ethnicity(Hispanic, not Hispanic), child race (White, Black, Other), household income (< $65,000/year, > = $65,000/year), season of data collection (Winter, Spring, Summer, Fall) and park youth sports program enrollment (none, any). Cell sizes were small and therefore many results had wide confidence intervals.

Results

Demographic variables associated with park use frequency

PARKS participant demographic variables and their association with any park use frequency are shown in Table 1. Seventy-one percent of parents were married or living with their partner. Forty-five percent reported an annual household income of < $65,000/year. Forty-four percent of parents had less than a college degree. Fifty-six percent of the children were a race other than White and 27% were Hispanic ethnicity. Children’s ages were evenly distributed between 6—12 years, and 52.1% were female. Thirty-nine percent were in the overweight/obese body weight category [33, 34]. Any park use was significantly more frequent among younger children (5—7 yrs compared with 10—12 yrs) and girls.

Although not a representative sample, the children in the attained sample were racially/ethnically diverse, not already highly physically active and not currently participating at high levels in park and recreation youth sports teams. Compared to census data for these 20 neighborhoods, the enrolled PARKS sample included a larger proportion of Hispanics (20.4% study vs. 9.8% census); Native Americans (7.6% vs. 1.0%); and Other race (13.0% vs. 4.0%). Black and White enrolled participants closely matched census data (19.4% vs. 20.0%, and 61.3% vs. 52.8%, respectively). Fewer Asians enrolled in the study compared to census data for these neighborhoods (6.5% vs. 13.9%).

The attained PARKS sample was diverse in terms of its current park use. Use of any park was distributed evenly among frequency categories (never or less than once a month 27%; about once a week 23%; about twice a week 23%; more than twice a week 26%). Half the sample reported no youth recreation sports program enrollment during the past year. Seventy-three percent of the enrolled children did not meet CDC guidelines for PA (as measured using baseline accelerometry data) [5].

Is park use associated with youth PA?

Objectively-measured PA (MVPA, light or sedentary) was not significantly associated with frequency of child use of any park for any reason, nor for specific reasons (see Table 2). Children who visited any park for any reason 12 + times per month during the past year had an average daily MVPA of 48.9 min/day (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 45.0, 52.8), similar to those who visited any park for any reason zero to four times per month (49.6 min/day; 95% CI: 45.9, 53.3). Minutes of light PA per day were higher among children who visited the park zero to 4 times per month compared with those who visited the parks 12 + times per month (274.7 min/day (95% CI: 265.5, 283.8) versus 272.5 min/day (95% CI: (262.9, 282.2), respectively). Sedentary minutes per day were lower among infrequent park users (0—4 times/month) compared with more frequent park users (12 + times/month; see Table 2). None of these associations were significantly different.

By contrast, parent-report of their child’s PA was significantly associated with child any park use. Parents of children who visited parks for any reason 12 + times per month reported their children were active for 60 min or more on average of 6.5 days per week (95% CI = 6.1, 6.9) compared with an average of 5.6 days per week (95% CI = 5.3, 6.0) among children who used parks 0—4 times per month (p < 0.01). Children who visited parks for play 5 + times per month were reported to be active on average 6.1 days per week (95% CI = 5.9, 6.4) compared with 5.6 days per week (95% CI = 5.3, 5.9) among those who used parks for play 0—4 times per month. Park use for other reasons also was significantly positively associated with higher parent-reported child PA (6.4 days per week, 95% CI = 6.0, 6.9 among those who used parks for other reasons 5 + times per month compared with 5.8 days per week, 95% CI = 5.6, 6.1 among those who used parks 0—4 times per month). Park recreational youth sports program enrollment was not significantly associated with either objectively measured or parent-reported child PA, although the direction favored greater MVPA among those who participated in one or more organized sports programs compared to those who did not participate in any organized sports programs.

Is park use associated with youth PA among certain subgroups?

Table 3 shows associations between park use frequency (any park, any reason) and child PA by sex, age group, race and ethnic group, household income and season of park use. Accelerometry measures of PA were not significantly associated park use frequency or youth sports program enrollment among any subgroups. Parent-reported child PA was significantly associated with park use frequency among younger children but not older children. Among 5—7 yr old children, those who visited parks for any reason 8 + times per month were reported to be physically active on 6.8 days per week (95% CI: 6.3, 7.2), compared to those who visited parks for any reason 0—4 times per month (5.7 days/week; 95% CI: 5.0,6.4; p-value for linear trend p < 0.04). Among children identified by parents as non-Hispanic ethnicity, park use frequency was significantly positively associated with parent-reported physically active days among children who visited the park 8 + times per month (6.5 days per week (95% CI: 6.2, 6.8), compared with 5.8 days per week (95% CI: 5.4, 6.2) among those who visited 0—4 times per month (p-value for linear trend: p < 0.02).

Discussion

This research examined children’s park use frequency and PA among a diverse sample of urban youth ages 6—12 years who lived within a two-mile radius of a local park with a recreation center. In this study, objectively-measured child PA was not significantly associated with parent report of their child’s frequency of use of any park. However, parent-reported child PA was significantly associated with frequency of their child’s use of any park for any reason, for play, and for other reasons.

The discrepant results for the association between child park use and parent-reported versus objectively-measured child PA raises the question of whether park use frequency should be expected to significantly contribute to children’s total daily PA. Specific answers require the use of both an individual-level PA measure that can quantify the incremental contribution of park-based PA to a child’s total PA and PA of different intensities paired with child PA location data. Although originally planned, the present study found that the collection of child location data over an extended period raised privacy and ethical concerns among the parents of children in this community-based study. Therefore, GPS data on PA location were not collected while children in the present study wore accelerometer devices. Neither accelerometry-based PA measures without paired location data nor park-level observations of the PA of self-selected child park users are adequate to determine whether park use contributes incrementally in a significant way to child total daily PA or PA of specific intensities.

The use of parent-proxy report of child PA yielded different results in the present study compared to the use of objective accelerometry measures, although the two measures were modestly correlated (r = 0.29; p < 0.0001 for parent number of days child is physically active and accelerometry-measured MVPA mins/day). This finding raises questions about the validity of parent report of their child’s PA. Could the parent report be a more valid or representative measure of the child’s usual PA patterns than is a single seven-day period of objectively measured PA? Parents have the unique opportunity to observe their child’s PA in many different settings and over longer time periods than typically captured with accelerometry. By contrast, it is possible that parents who report that their child is highly physically active also overreport the frequency of park use by their child.

This study had many strengths, including its diverse urban sample of children and their parents, the wide range of neighborhoods captured, detailed measures of park use across seasons and for a range of reasons, and objective measures of child PA. Limitations included a mismatch between the time frame covered by the objective child PA measures and the reference period for parent-reports of child park use. The seven-day period in which the accelerometer was worn may not have been representative of the child’s PA throughout the entire year, the period for which the parent reported park use frequency by the child. This may have attenuated associations observed between objectively-measured PA and parent-reported park use. Although the parent reported child PA measure has been used widely in population-level research surveys [32], its validity may be compromised by social desirability (inflated reports) and recall bias (inflated or deflated reports). The validity of the parent-report of child park use has not been established [10, 11, 17, 19–22, 22, 24]. Truncation of the distributions for analysis of the specific reasons for park use may have decreased the power to detect significant associations with child PA. Future studies can provide greater clarity by separately measuring youth park-specific PA in addition to total daily PA. It is important to continue to use both objective and parent-reported child PA measures to develop a clear understanding of overlapping and unique aspects captured by each, and in which situations each may be preferred. The sample for the present study, although diverse with respect to levels of park use, current physical activity, and demographics, was not a representative sample and therefore results may not generalize to the broader population. The use of a smaller buffer size around each study park may have produced different results than those observed using a 2-mile buffer as in the present study. Lack of sensitivity analyses using varied park buffer sizes is a study limitation. Finally, these results may not be generalizable to or comparable with previous research involving parks that do not have rec centers on site.

Conclusions

Child park use frequency was not associated with objectively-measured child PA but was significantly positively associated with parent report of child PA. Objective measures of individual child total daily PA paired with location data are needed to understand the incremental contribution of park use to child PA.
