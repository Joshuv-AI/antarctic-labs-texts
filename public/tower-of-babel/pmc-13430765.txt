Factors associated with time to first marriage among women in Somalia: a Weibull survival analysis
Authors: Ahmed Abdinasir Abdulle, Mohamed Abdirahim Omar, Hana Mohamed Shidane, Abdisalam Hassan Muse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe timing of a woman’s first marriage is a critical demographic event with profound implications for health, fertility, and socio-economic development. In Somalia, where early marriage is prevalent, a nuanced understanding of its associated factors is essential for developing effective policies. This study aims to identify the factors associated with the time to first marriage among Somali women using parametric Weibull survival analysis.MethodsThis study utilized data from the 2020 Somali Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) for 16,428 women aged 15–49. Survival analysis was employed to model time from birth to first marriage. After evidence of non-proportionality in the Cox proportional hazards model, parametric survival models were compared, and the Weibull proportional hazards model was retained as the best-fitting hazard-based model according to the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).ResultsThe median age at first marriage was 20 years (Interquartile Range [IQR]: 15–24). The multivariable Weibull analysis revealed that secondary (adjusted Hazard Ratio [aHR] = 0.75; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.67–0.84) and higher education (aHR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.46–0.64) and belonging to the rich wealth category (aHR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.82–0.93) were significantly associated with a lower hazard of early marriage. Conversely, illiteracy (aHR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.13–1.29), lack of radio exposure (aHR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.07–1.25), and living in a nomadic community (aHR = 1.10; 95% CI: 1.03–1.18) were associated with a higher hazard. Regional heterogeneity was observed, with women in the Bay region facing a more than three-fold higher hazard of early marriage compared to those in Awdal (aHR = 3.23; 95% CI: 2.76–3.78).ConclusionEducation and household wealth are critical protective factors against early marriage in Somalia. However, the strong association with illiteracy, nomadic lifestyles, lack of media exposure, and extreme regional disparities highlights the need for tailored, context-specific interventions beyond uniform national policies. Programs should prioritize increasing girls’ access to education while addressing the unique circumstances of nomadic populations and high-prevalence regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02373-2.Keywords: Early Marriage, Survival Analysis, Weibull Model, Somalia, Demographic and Health Survey (DHS)

Introduction

The time to a woman’s first marriage is a critical demographic event, marking a significant transition to adulthood and the beginning of a socially sanctioned period for sexual activity and childbearing in many societies [1]. It is widely used as a proxy indicator for the age at which women are first exposed to the risks of pregnancy and childbirth [1, 2]. The timing of this event is a primary factor of fertility patterns; an earlier age at marriage typically lengthens a woman’s reproductive window, often leading to higher lifetime fertility, especially in contexts with low contraceptive use [3]. Globally, early and child marriage remains a pressing issue, with the highest prevalence rates concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [4]. Within sub-Saharan Africa, where women’s sexual autonomy is often limited, the challenge of early marriage is particularly acute and has profound implications for health, education, and economic development [5, 6].

The consequences of early marriage are severe and multifaceted. Girls who marry before the age of 18 are disproportionately affected by complications from pregnancy and childbirth, which are the leading causes of death for adolescent girls in low- and middle-income countries [7]. Young mothers face elevated risks of poor prenatal outcomes, including obstetric fistula, eclampsia, and gestational hypertension, while their children experience higher rates of morbidity and mortality [8, 9]. Beyond the immediate health risks, early marriage curtails educational attainment, increases the likelihood of intimate partner violence, and can lead to marital instability and divorce [5, 10, 11]. These adverse outcomes perpetuate cycles of poverty and gender inequality, presenting barriers to achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) 5.3 [12].

A substantial body of research has identified a consistent set of factors associated with the timing of marriage. Education is a powerful predictor, likely because schooling increases the opportunity cost of marriage and empowers women with autonomy [13, 14]. Similarly, higher socio-economic status is associated with later marriage, as poverty often drives families to marry off daughters early to reduce economic burdens [15, 16]. Furthermore, context-specific factors, including place of residence (urban, rural, or nomadic), religion, and prevailing socio-cultural norms, play a significant role in shaping marriage patterns [1]. The considerable regional variation observed in countries like Bangladesh and Ethiopia highlights the influence of community-level norms and localized administrative effectiveness, underscoring the need for geographically targeted interventions [14].

Despite the wealth of research on this topic, many studies have relied on statistical methods such as logistic or Poisson regression. However, these approaches have methodological limitations. Logistic regression typically dichotomizes age at marriage into ‘early’ versus ‘not early,’ thereby losing granular information about the exact timing of the event. Similarly, standard Poisson regression often assumes a constant hazard rate, which may not reflect the changing risk of marriage over a woman’s life course. While age is recorded in discrete years, survival analysis is methodologically superior because it accounts for right-censored observations—women who have not yet married by the time of the survey—utilizing all the information available in the data [14, 17]. However, the standard Cox proportional hazards (PH) model relies on the critical assumption that hazard ratios are constant over time, an assumption that is often violated in complex social data. This necessitates the use of more flexible parametric models.

By applying parametric survival modeling to the context of Somalia, this study addresses previous methodological limitations. It seeks to provide a more nuanced understanding of the factors associated with time to first marriage, quantifying the independent effects of various factors while accounting for regional heterogeneity. This study provides the first nationally representative survival analysis of time to first marriage in Somalia. By explicitly including nomadic populations, it offers critical, country-specific evidence on how traditional lifestyles and geographic isolation shape marriage timing, thereby addressing a significant gap in the literature.

Methods

Study design, setting, and data

This study used a population-based cross-sectional dataset from the 2020 Somali Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS). The SDHS is a nationally representative survey designed to provide demographic and health indicators for women of reproductive age across Somalia. The survey collects retrospective information on key life events, including age at first marriage. Although the SDHS is cross-sectional, the present analysis reconstructed retrospective time-to-event histories using self-reported age at first marriage and age at interview. Therefore, the study should not be interpreted as prospective follow-up; rather, it uses retrospectively reported marriage timing to estimate survival time and right-censoring among never-married women. The initial dataset included 16,486 women aged 15–49 years. After excluding observations with missing values on the outcome or selected covariates, the final analytical sample consisted of 16,428 women. Among these, 11,818 had experienced first marriage and 4,610 were never married and right censored at age at interview. To assess potential recall bias and age heaping, the distribution of reported age at first marriage was evaluated (Supplementary Figure S1).

Variables in the study

Dependent variable

The primary outcome was time to first marriage, defined as age in completed years from birth until first marriage or censoring. Women who were currently married, divorced, or widowed were coded as having experienced the event because all had entered a first marriage or union. Never-married women were coded as right-censored, with survival time defined as current age at interview. The earliest reported age at first marriage was 10 years. Therefore, marriage events were included from age 10 onward. Because age at first marriage was retrospectively reported in completed years, the analysis also assessed digit preference and very early reported marriage ages as part of sensitivity and limitation assessment.

Independent variables

The independent variables (covariates) considered in this study were selected based on their theoretical relevance and previous studies [18, 19]. These included the respondent’s region of residence (categorized into the 16 administrative regions of Somalia), type of residence (urban, rural, nomadic), education level (no education, Primary, Secondary, Higher), literacy (can read, cannot read), Wealth quantile was derived from the DHS wealth index based on asset ownership; for this analysis, the original five quintiles were aggregated into three distinct categories: poor (combining the lowest and second quintiles), Middle, and Rich (combining the fourth and highest quintiles), household size (≤ 5members, >5members), and media exposure (frequency of listening to radio and watching TV). Household size was dichotomized at > 5 members to reflect the national average household size in Somalia. While media exposure may change over the life course, it was treated as a time-invariant covariate due to the limitations of cross-sectional DHS data, serving as a proxy for general household media access. To address potential conceptual overlap between education and literacy, multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF); the absence of high VIF values justified their simultaneous inclusion. Furthermore, supplementary analysis confirmed that an interaction term between education and literacy was not statistically significant, and the model including both variables independently provided the best fit compared to models with either variable alone (Supplementary Table S2).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata 16. The analysis consisted of three stages: descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, and multivariable survival modeling. The primary survival analysis was conducted using the unweighted analytical sample because the objective was to estimate model-based associations and compare candidate parametric survival models using likelihood-based fit statistics, including AIC and BIC, rather than to generate population-level prevalence estimates.

Descriptive and bivariate analysis

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. The overall time to first marriage was visualized using a Kaplan–Meier survival curve. In the bivariate analysis, the log-rank test was used to compare survival curves for time to first marriage across categories of each independent variable.

Multivariable survival modeling

To model the influence of covariates on time to first marriage, several survival models were fitted. First, a Cox proportional hazards model was estimated. The Cox model is a semi-parametric model that estimates the association between covariates and the hazard of experiencing an event. The proportional hazards assumption, which requires hazard ratios to remain constant over time, was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. The global test indicated evidence of non-proportionality (p < 0.0001), suggesting that the standard Cox model was not adequate as the primary model for these data. After evidence of non-proportionality in the Cox proportional hazards model, parametric survival models were additionally evaluated rather than relying on the Cox model alone. Specifically, Weibull and Gompertz proportional hazards models were fitted and compared using AIC and BIC. The Weibull PH model was retained as the primary model because it provided the best fit among the candidate hazard-based models and allowed adjusted hazard ratios to be reported for interpretation.

Survey weights and PSU-level clustering were not applied in the primary Weibull PH model because the study focused on model-based association estimation and likelihood-based parametric model comparison rather than design-weighted national prevalence estimation. Complex survey corrections can complicate likelihood-based model comparison using AIC and BIC. Region was included as a covariate to capture geographic heterogeneity. Therefore, the estimates should be interpreted as association measures rather than weighted national population estimates. Because age at first marriage was recorded in completed years, event time was treated as a continuous approximation in the primary Weibull model. To assess whether this assumption affected the findings, two sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, the model was re-estimated using time since age 10 as the analysis time, reflecting the earliest reported age at first marriage in the dataset. Second, a complementary discrete-time survival model was fitted using person-year data and a complementary log-log link. In this model, age was specified using linear and quadratic terms to avoid sparse-cell instability at older ages. The discrete-time sensitivity analysis is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Model comparison and selection

Model selection was based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [20]. The model with the lowest AIC value was considered the best-fitted model for the dataset. Based on this criterion, the Weibull parametric model was selected for the final interpretation of the results. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. To ensure model reliability and address potential collinearity between predictors such as education and literacy, multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF).

Results

Descriptive statistics

This analysis included 16,428 women aged 15–49. Of these, 11,818 (71.94%) were or had been married previously. The overall median age at first marriage for the cohort was 20 years, with 25% of women marrying by age 15 and 75% by the age of 24. The majority of the respondents had no formal education (74.32%), while 15.08% had primary, 7.68% had secondary, and 2.92% had higher education. Media exposure: 88.64% of respondents reported not listening to the radio and 83.89% reported not watching television. Regarding residence, 47.74% of women lived in urban areas, 25.72% in rural areas, and 26.55% in nomadic settings (Table 1).

Bivariate analysis

The log-rank test was used to assess the unadjusted association between each predictor variable and the time to first marriage. The results, summarized in Table 1, indicated that all the examined variables, residence, education level, wealth quintile, literacy, media exposure (radio and TV), and household size—showed a statistically significant difference in the survival curves (p < 0.0001). This suggests that when considered individually, each of these factors is significantly associated with the timing of a woman’s first marriage. These variables were therefore considered for inclusion in the multivariable model to determine their independent effects while controlling for other factors.

The distribution of reported age at first marriage showed evidence of digit preference: 3,021 reported ages (25.56%) ended in 0 or 5. Among ever-married women, 3,332 reported first marriage before age 15, representing 28.19% of ever-married respondents. These findings were considered when interpreting the results and were explicitly addressed in the sensitivity analyses and limitations. See Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1.

A sensitivity analysis excluding women who reported first marriage before age 15 produced broadly consistent findings, particularly for secondary and higher education, literacy, radio exposure, nomadic residence, and regional heterogeneity. To address the completed-year nature of the age-at-first-marriage variable, a complementary discrete-time survival model was fitted using person-year data. The model included 154,149 person-year observations from 16,428 women and used cluster-robust standard errors at the respondent level. The findings were broadly consistent with the primary Weibull model: secondary and higher education and richer household wealth remained associated with delayed first marriage, while illiteracy, lack of radio exposure, and regional heterogeneity remained associated with earlier first marriage (Supplementary Table S3).

Model selection

To identify the most appropriate model for analyzing the factors associated with the time to age at first marriage, we compared the performance of a semi-parametric model (Cox Proportional Hazards) with two fully parametric survival models (Weibull and Gompertz). The selection was based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Both criteria assess the goodness of fit of a model while penalizing the number of parameters, with lower values indicating a better balance of fit and parsimony.

As shown in Table 2, the Weibull model yielded substantially lower AIC (10,308.7) and BIC (10,539.9) values compared to both the Gompertz and the Cox Proportional Hazards models. This indicates that the Weibull distribution provides a superior fit to survival data on the age at first marriage in this context. Consequently, the Weibull model was selected for the final multivariate analysis to report the factors associated with the time to first marriage. To further validate the reliability of the model, multicollinearity was tested (Table 3). The mean VIF was 1.76, with the highest individual VIF being 2.19 (Wealth Quintile), and Education/Literacy both falling around 2.0. These results indicate that the model estimates are stable and not biased by multicollinearity.

Multivariable analysis using the Weibull proportional hazard (PH) model for factors associated with time at first marriage in Somalia

Educational attainment is a complex predictor of the timing of marriage. Women with secondary (Adjusted Hazard Ratio [aHR] = 0.75; 95% CI: 0.67–0.84) and higher education (aHR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.46–0.64) had a significantly lower hazard of early marriage than women with no formal education. Conversely, women with only primary education exhibited a higher hazard of marrying earlier (aHR = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06–1.23) than those with no education. Household wealth and literacy are also significant protective factors. Women from the rich wealth quintile had a 12% lower hazard of early marriage than those from the poor quintile (aHR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.82–0.93). Similarly, literacy was strongly associated with delayed marriage; women who could not read had a 21% higher hazard of marrying earlier than those who could read (aHR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.13–1.29). Media Exposure variables revealed that radio access was significantly associated with marriage timing. Women who did not listen to the radio at all had a 16% higher hazard of early marriage (aHR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.07–1.25) than those who listened at least once a week. However, television viewership was not statistically significant in the multivariate model (p > 0.05). The type of residence played a significant role, particularly in nomadic populations. Women residing in nomadic settings had a 10% higher hazard of marrying at a younger age (aHR = 1.10; 95% CI: 1.03–1.18) than their urban counterparts. Rural residence was not significantly different from urban residence (p = 0.604). The model confirmed profound regional heterogeneity. Women in the Bay region faced the highest risk, with a more than three-fold higher hazard of early marriage than women in the Awdal region (aHR = 3.23; 95% CI: 2.76–3.78). Other regions with markedly elevated hazards included Middle Shabelle (aHR = 2.98), Hiraan (aHR = 2.93), and Galgaduud (aHR = 2.61).

Discussion

This study utilized multivariable survival analysis to investigate the factors associated with time to first marriage among women in Somalia, drawing on data from the Demographic and Health Survey. By employing a parametric Weibull model, which provided a superior fit to the data compared to the semi-parametric Cox model, our analysis identified education, wealth, literacy, residence type, media exposure, and region as important independent factors associated with marriage timing. The findings reveal a median age at first marriage of 20 years, with significant heterogeneity across different socio-demographic and geographic strata.

A central finding of this study is the strong protective association of education against early marriages. Women who attained secondary or higher education had a significantly lower hazard of marrying early, a finding that is remarkably consistent with a large body of literature from diverse contexts, such as Ethiopia, Bangladesh, and Nigeria [9, 14, 21]. This relationship is likely driven by multiple mechanisms: education empowers women with knowledge and skills, expands their economic opportunities outside of marriage, and may shift their aspirations and decision-making autonomy regarding their own lives [13]. The strong and independent effect of literacy further underscores this point. The ability to read provides access to information and resources, which can foster critical thinking and awareness of the health and social consequences of early marriage, thereby serving as a crucial tool for empowerment [16].

Interestingly, women with only primary education showed a slightly higher hazard of early marriage (aHR = 1.14) compared to those with no education. This ‘primary education paradox’ has been observed in similar contexts, where girls who drop out of primary school may immediately enter the marriage market, whereas the ‘no education’ reference group may include women in extremely marginalized or isolated settings with different marriage dynamics. However, this protective effect was clearly established at the secondary and higher-education levels [22].

Socio-economic status also emerged as a critical factor. The finding that women from the rich wealth quintile marry later than their poorest counterparts aligns with research that identifies poverty as a primary driver of early marriage [23]. For families facing economic hardship, marrying a daughter early may be perceived as a strategy to reduce economic burdens or gain financial security through bride price. Conversely, wealthier families may have the resources to invest in their daughters’ education for a longer period, thus delaying their marriage. This highlights the intersectionality of poverty and education in shaping the life outcomes of young women.

The study revealed crucial insights into the influence of contextual factors unique to Somalia. The finding that women from nomadic populations have a higher hazard of marrying early compared to their urban peers points to the enduring influence of traditional lifestyles. In nomadic communities, marriage often serves as a vital social and economic alliance between families, and there may be less access to formal schooling and other institutional structures that tend to delay marriage in settled urban areas [24]. This finding represents a unique, country-specific contribution, highlighting how geographic isolation and traditional pastoralist structures in Somalia shape demographic outcomes. Additionally, the study found that a lack of exposure to mass media, specifically radio, was associated with an increased hazard of early marriage. Radio remains a primary source of information in Somalia; thus, women with limited exposure may miss critical public health messaging regarding the benefits of delaying marriage and family planning. Furthermore, radio ownership itself may serve as a proxy for broader socio-economic status or geographic access, compounding its association with marriage timing.

Most strikingly, the analysis uncovered substantial regional heterogeneity in marriage timing. The hazard of early marriage in regions such as Bay, Hiraan, and Galgaduud was more than double or triple that in Awdal. These regional differences may reflect unmeasured contextual factors, including variation in social norms, education access, economic insecurity, displacement, service availability, and local governance conditions. Because these mechanisms were not directly measured, regional interpretations should be considered exploratory [25]. A one-size-fits-all national policy is unlikely to be effective in a country with such deep regional disparities.

Policy implications

The findings of this study have direct implications for policy and program design in Somalia. First, the strong protective effect of education underscores the need for continued investment in girls’ schooling, particularly at the secondary level, as a primary strategy to combat early marriage. Second, the significant impact of wealth suggests that economic empowerment programs, such as cash transfers or vocational training for adolescent girls and their families, could help alleviate the economic pressures that drive early marriage. Third, given the association between radio exposure and delayed marriage, mass media campaigns utilizing radio should be scaled up to disseminate information on the risks of early marriage, particularly targeting remote and nomadic communities. Finally, the stark regional and residential (nomadic) disparities demand a shift away from uniform national policies towards targeted, context-specific interventions. Programs should be developed in collaboration with local community leaders to address the unique cultural norms and socio-economic conditions in high-prevalence regions, such as Bay and Hiraan, and within nomadic communities. These targeted strategies are essential for accelerating progress toward Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 5.3, which aims to eliminate all harmful practices, including child, early, and forced marriage.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this study is its use of a large, nationally representative dataset (n = 16,428) and an appropriate survival analysis framework that accounts for right-censoring among never-married women. The study also formally assessed the proportional hazards assumption, compared candidate survival models using information criteria, and selected the Weibull PH model because it provided the best fit among the hazard-based models. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess digit preference, very early reported marriage ages, and the completed-year nature of the age-at-first-marriage variable.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of the DHS data precludes establishing causality. While we observe a strong association between education and later marriage, we cannot definitively conclude that one causes the other. Second, the data rely on self-reported age at first marriage, which may be subject to recall bias and digit preference, particularly among older respondents who recall events from decades ago. Third, the household wealth index is measured at the time of the survey and may not accurately reflect the socio-economic status of the woman’s household at the time she married. Similarly, media exposure, literacy, and household size were measured at the time of the survey and may not fully represent conditions at the time of first marriage. Therefore, these variables should be interpreted as associated markers rather than strictly time-varying causal exposures. Finally, the primary parametric survival model was estimated using the unweighted analytical sample without PSU-level clustered standard errors. This decision was made to preserve likelihood-based comparison across candidate parametric survival models using AIC and BIC and to focus on model-based associations rather than nationally weighted prevalence estimates. However, the absence of full complex survey adjustment may affect the precision and population generalizability of the estimates. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as model-based associations rather than design-weighted national estimates.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the median age at first marriage for women aged 15–49 in Somalia was 20 years. The key factors that have a direct association with delaying marriage are higher educational attainment and greater household wealth. Conversely, factors that contribute to a higher risk of early marriage include illiteracy, residing in a nomadic community, lack of exposure to radio, and living in specific geographic regions of the country. The study provides strong evidence that a woman’s education is an important factor in determining her age at marriage. Women who progress to secondary and higher education are significantly more likely to delay marriage compared to those with no education. The findings also show significant heterogeneity in marriage age across Somalia’s regions, indicating that cultural and contextual factors may play an important role. Therefore, the Somali government and its partners should prioritize policies and programs aimed at delaying early marriage. These interventions should focus on increasing girls’ access to and completion of secondary education and utilizing mass media for behavioral change communication. Furthermore, programs should be tailored to address the unique circumstances of nomadic populations and the specific drivers of early marriage in high-prevalence regions such as Bay, Hiraan, and Middle Shabelle to reduce its adverse consequences for women and children in Somalia.

This study utilized secondary data from the 2020 Somali Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS) and was conducted in accordance with established ethical guidelines. This study complied with ethical principles by obtaining the necessary approvals from the Somalia National Health Research Ethics Committee and the ICF Institutional Review Board. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection, ensuring that their rights and confidentiality were upheld throughout the research process.
