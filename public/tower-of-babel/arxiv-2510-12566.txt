Abstract
. Sustainability reporting in web-based services increasingly relies on simplified energy and carbon models such as the Danish Agency of Digital Government’s Digst framework and the United Kingdom-based DIMPACT model. Although these models are widely adopted, their accuracy and precision remain underexplored. This paper presents an empirical study evaluating how well such models reflect actual energy consumption during realistic user interactions with common website categories. Energy use was measured across shopping, booking, navigation, and news services using predefined user flows executed on four laptop platforms. The results show that the commonly applied constant-power approximation ( P × t P\times t ) can diverge substantially from measured energy, depending on website category, device type, and task characteristics. The findings demonstrate that model deviations are systematic rather than random and highlight the need for category-aware and device-reflective power parameters in reproducible sustainability reporting frameworks.



1.
Introduction

Digital sustainability reporting has become central to the evaluation of web-based services. Governments and organizations increasingly depend on simplified models to estimate energy use and greenhouse gas emissions. Two such models are the Danish Agency of Digital Government’s Digst framework
Digitaliseringsstyrelsen 2025
and the UK-based DIMPACT framework
DIMPACT 2022
;
Schien et al. 2022
based on
Preist et al. 2019
. However, despite their growing role, the empirical validation of these models is limited. Without systematic assessment, it remains unclear how accurately they capture the energy demands of real-world web interactions.

This paper addresses this gap by presenting an experiment that directly compares measured energy consumption against model-based estimates.
Following the recommendation by Guldner et al.
Guldner et al. 2024
to evaluate software energy efficiency under representative user scenarios, and inspired by the online-media categories proposed by Schien et al.
Schien et al. 2013
—who modeled energy use across text-based, interactive, and streaming web content—we focus on non-streaming web services (shopping, booking, navigation, and news). In doing so, we complement prior work that has predominantly investigated streaming services. While categories such as streaming and generative AI are highly relevant to digital sustainability, their energy use depends on complex scaling parameters (e.g., video quality
Suski et al. 2020
or token length) that require separate, parameter-specific studies. We therefore delimit our study to web services with more stable and comparable interaction patterns.

Existing sustainability reporting frameworks for web-based services, such as
Digst and DIMPACT, estimate end-user device energy use through constant-power
assumptions.
While these models enable comparability across services, their empirical
accuracy and the factors influencing their validity remain largely unexplored.
To address this gap, we investigate two research questions:

RQ1:

How accurately do current reporting models estimate
the energy consumption of end-user devices during realistic web-service
interactions?

RQ2:

Which factors—such as device type, website category,
or interaction duration—explain systematic deviations between modeled and
measured energy use?

Our contributions are threefold:
(1) a reproducible experimental methodology for measuring the energy use of
web-service interactions,
(2) empirical evidence on the accuracy and precision of Digst- and
DIMPACT-style energy estimates, and
(3) insights for improving fairness and transparency in digital
sustainability reporting.

2.
Background and Related Work

This section situates the study within existing research on the estimation
and reporting of ICT-related energy use.
We first review sector-level and national models that underpin current
digital sustainability frameworks, focusing on their assumptions about
end-user devices.
We then outline software- and measurement-level approaches that aim to
quantify energy use more precisely at the program or interaction level.
Together, these strands of work frame the methodological gap that this
paper addresses: linking reproducible, software-level energy measurements
to the simplified models used in large-scale sustainability reporting.

2.1.
Sector-Level Models

Global and sector-level ICT footprint models establish the broader context for digital sustainability research. Early studies, e.g.,
Andrae & Edler 2015
;
Malmodin & Lundén 2018
;
Belkhir & Elmeligi 2018
, raised awareness of the growing energy challenges, while Freitag et al.
Freitag et al. 2021
analyzed the methodological assumptions that explain divergent estimates of Internet energy intensity. Later studies, such as Malmodin et al.
Malmodin et al. 2024
, provide comprehensive assessments of the ICT sector’s total energy use and greenhouse gas emissions, emphasizing harmonized system boundaries across data centers, networks, and end-user devices.

These models typically employ top-down allocation and averaged intensity factors to distribute total sector energy across subdomains. As a result, they inherently contain uncertainties, and Malmodin et al. specifically acknowledge that “the number of user devices in operation and their average electricity consumption remain a more uncertain part in line with previous studies”
Malmodin et al. 2024
. This uncertainty is echoed by Furberg et al.
Furberg et al. 2026
, who note that “to estimate the electricity use of devices, data on actual device usage patterns (e.g., operational service time per year) are required. In practice, studies on the ICT sector’s direct climate impact usually do not collect device usage data but rely on secondary data from various sources.”e.g., averages over multiple sources
Malmodin et al. 2024
.

Although these studies establish the overall scale of ICT’s environmental impact, their level of aggregation prevents validation of individual services or user interactions. Consequently, sector models cannot determine whether specific web services, user actions, or design choices meaningfully reduce energy demand. These limitations motivate the development of service-level models for digital sustainability reporting, which attempt to translate aggregate insights into interpretable metrics for individual services.

2.2.
Service-Level Models

Service-level models aim to make the environmental assessment of digital services practical and comparable. Frameworks such as the Danish Agency of Digital Government

Although these frameworks facilitate transparency and comparability, they rely on several simplifying assumptions: constant device power, uniform user behavior, and linear relationships between time, data volume, and energy use.

The precision and accuracy of these models have not yet been evaluated against measured data from real user interactions. Our study directly addresses this gap by empirically testing the validity of the
P\times t
assumption across realistic user flows and hardware configurations. By comparing measured energy data with estimates derived from Digst- and DIMPACT-style models, we provide an evidence-based assessment of their accuracy and identify conditions under which these models deviate from actual energy consumption.

While these models target sustainability reporting, a parallel line of work addresses developer-facing concerns by improving software energy efficiency and promoting reproducible energy-measurement practices.

2.3.
Software-Level Efficiency and Measurement Frameworks

Research on software-level energy efficiency has produced a range of tools and methodologies for analyzing, predicting, and improving the energy behavior of programs. Early frameworks such as EACOF
Field et al. 2014
enabled applications to access power data at runtime, fostering awareness of energy use during development. Static and data-dependent analyses by Grech et al.
Grech et al. 2015
and Pallister et al.
Pallister et al. 2017
estimate energy consumption directly from program structure or input characteristics, while comparative studies such as Georgiou et al.
Georgiou et al. 2017
and Pereira et al.
Pereira et al. 2021
quantify how programming language choice influences energy efficiency. Complementary approaches examine reusable solutions for efficient design—such as energy-related software patterns
Rani et al. 2024
;
Feitosa et al. 2021
—and energy-aware debugging and testing frameworks for mobile and desktop applications.

More recently, Guldner et al.
Guldner et al. 2024
proposed the Green Software Measurement Model, a standardized process for measuring software resource efficiency under representative user scenarios. Their rationale is that meaningful and reproducible results require measurements grounded in realistic user interactions rather than synthetic benchmarks. This principle directly aligns with our methodological choice to evaluate energy consumption through end-to-end user flows across web services.

While these works provide methodological foundations for measuring and improving software efficiency, their focus remains on optimizing or characterizing software behavior in isolation. Our study builds upon their measurement philosophy but applies it to a different objective: validating the accuracy of service-level reporting models on real-world services.

2.4.
Web-Specific Energy Studies

Energy consumption in web-based services has been investigated from multiple perspectives, ranging from modeling of user-device interactions to experimental measurement of browser and video workloads. Schien et al.
Schien et al. 2013
provided one of the earliest models of online media consumption, distinguishing between text-based, interactive, and streaming content across devices such as desktops, laptops, and mobile phones. Their results revealed that end-user devices, particularly laptops, dominate overall energy consumption, and that usage patterns substantially influence total impact. This modeling work established a foundation for categorizing web services by data intensity and interaction type—a principle we adopt when structuring our own experimental categories.

Subsequent studies examined specific behavioral and technical factors that shape web energy use. Suski et al.
Suski et al. 2020
demonstrated that user decisions, such as video resolution and device choice, strongly affect streaming emissions. Rani et al.
Rani et al. 2024
identified recurring energy-related design patterns in web applications, emphasizing that front-end implementation choices can meaningfully alter client-side power demand. Macedo et al.
De et al. 2022
compared WebAssembly and JavaScript, showing that runtime technology influences both execution time and energy efficiency. Together, these studies highlight the diversity of determinants—behavioral, architectural, and technological—that drive energy use in web contexts.

Recent work has advanced the methodological basis for measuring energy in multimedia and browser environments. Katsenou et al.
Katsenou et al. 2024
compared hardware- and software-based power measurements in video compression, while Herglotz et al.
Herglotz et al. 2025
formalized procedures for synchronized power and time measurements in video communication systems. Their controlled setups provide technical precision that complements our empirical approach. In our study, news-site user flows occasionally included embedded video playback, allowing us to relate these real-world observations to the controlled results reported by Herglotz et al. Similarly, our measured laptop data provide empirical confirmation of Schien et al.’s modeled dominance of device energy use.

Collectively, this literature demonstrates that web-service energy use is heterogeneous and context dependent. However, few of these studies examine whether the simplified assumptions used in service-level reporting models can capture such variability. Our work addresses this gap by providing measured, category-based data to test the validity of those assumptions under realistic web interactions.

2.5.
Positioning of This Work

Existing literature thus ranges from sector-wide modeling to code-level analysis, with few studies bridging measured data and reporting models. Our study occupies this intermediate layer: it empirically tests the accuracy and precision of service-level energy models (Digst and DIMPACT) under realistic user flows. Following the recommendation of Guldner et al.
Guldner et al. 2024
and building on the content categories proposed by Schien et al.
Schien et al. 2013
, we structure our experiment around four non-streaming web-service categories—shopping, booking, navigation, and news—and later relate our results to prior findings on device-level energy variation and video playback effects.
The following section details our experimental design, which applies these principles in a controlled setting to test model validity across four representative web-service categories.

3.
Methodology

This section describes the experimental design, data collection, and model
evaluation procedures used to address the research questions.
We outline the selection of representative web services and user flows,
the hardware and participant setup, and the measurement process for
recording power and duration.
Finally, we detail how the measured data were compared with the simplified
energy models used in existing reporting frameworks to assess their
accuracy and structural bias.

3.1.
Websites and User Flows

We selected eight popular websites grouped into four categories:

(1)

Shopping: ebay.fr, amazon.fr;

(2)

Booking: hotels.com, booking.com;

(3)

Directions: google.com/maps, bing.com/maps; and

(4)

News: dr.dk, tv2.dk

For each category, we defined realistic user flows that mimic everyday
usage—searching for a product, reserving a hotel room, generating a
route, or reading a news article—so that equivalent functionality was
tested across services. Streaming and other parameter-dependent
services were deliberately excluded to ensure comparability of
interaction length and structure.

Devices

Experiments were performed on four laptops representing different
hardware generations and OEM configurations:

Laptop 1:

Laptop 2:

Laptop 3:

Dell Latitude E5250 (Intel 5th-gen, compact chassis),

Laptop 4:

Dell Latitude E5550 (Intel 5th-gen, larger chassis).

This selection captures both processor-generation and design-level
power variation.

3.3.
Participants and Experimental Design

Ten participants completed the experiment; one participant (P10)
contributed only two runs and was excluded from the mixed-effects
analyses to ensure stable estimation of random effects.
The remaining nine participants provided between eight and twenty-four
valid runs each.
The design crossed eight user flows with four laptops, and every
flow–computer combination was executed by at least four and up to seven
distinct participants, ensuring balanced coverage.
To counter potential learning or fatigue effects while maintaining full
coverage, the order of user flows and the allocation of laptops were
randomized under constraints that ensured each flow–device combination
was represented across participants.
Each flow was repeated three times per device, yielding
a total of
164
valid observations.

3.4.
Experimental Setup and Measurements

All laptops ran identical operating-system images (Ubuntu Desktop 24.04.3 LTS) with default power
settings and brightness. The same Chromium browser version was used in incognito mode, and caches were cleared between runs.
Each measurement recorded device power (W) via a Siglent SPD3303X-E programmable power supply and task
duration (s), allowing energy to be computed as integrated power
over time. The external power-supply measurements was
user-synchronized via task start and stop events to ensure
precise alignment between user interaction and power logging. The setup can be seen in Figure

.
Key sources of potential variance included machine architecture,
user execution behavior, and learning effects, which were addressed
through randomization and replication. Potential influences of these factors and the sample size are discussed in Section

6.
Discussion

The results show that deviations between modeled and measured energy are not random but scale systematically with interaction length, revealing a structural bias in the constant-power assumption of existing reporting models. The nearly perfect linear relation between Digst error and task duration confirms that this bias arises from the model formulation rather than from random measurement noise.

6.1.
Improving Accuracy of Reporting Frameworks

The analysis in Section
5.2
showed that model deviations are systematic rather than random, driven primarily by reproducible differences in device power and service category. While incorporating hardware parameters can explain nearly all variance (
R^{2}=0.994
), this level of granularity is impractical for sustainability reporting. A more feasible improvement lies in introducing a limited set of representative service categories —such as shopping, booking, navigation, and news— which capture much of the same systematic variation (
R^{2}=0.973
). This categorical differentiation could substantially increase model precision while preserving the simplicity and comparability that reporting frameworks require. Such category grouping could therefore enhance the transparency and fairness of sustainability reporting without introducing additional measurement burden or model complexity.

6.2.
Toward Reproducible and Transparent Sustainability Assessment

Digital sustainability models should incorporate reproducibility standards and sensitivity analyses that explicitly address hardware and user variance. Transparent reporting of assumptions, parameter sources, and version-controlled model dependencies would enable consistent comparisons across services and over time. The open repository accompanying this paper provides all datasets and scripts necessary to reproduce the results, including detailed software version information to ensure full experimental repeatability.

While our measured laptop powers (9–13 W) correspond well to these empirically grounded estimates, they are markedly lower than the fixed constants used in current sustainability reporting frameworks. For instance, the Danish Agency of Digital Government’s Digst model assumes 22 W, and the DIMPACT framework applies 15 W for browser-based scenarios. These simplified constants serve comparability purposes but appear conservative relative to modern laptop behavior, leading to systematic overestimation of end-user energy use in typical web interactions. Because both under- and overestimation of average power compound proportionally with interaction duration, any mismatch between assumed and actual device power introduces systematic rather than random error in total energy estimates.
This observation suggests a need to explore alternative formulations that decouple energy estimation from pure duration, for instance by distinguishing a time-dependent baseline component from an activity-dependent component that captures variations in user interaction intensity.

6.4.
Broader Context

Beyond laptop-based web interactions, similar mechanisms shape the energy
profiles of other end-user workloads such as streaming and video communication.
While this study focused on browser-based interactions, similar principles
apply to other classes of end-user workloads, including streaming and video
communication. Herglotz et al.
Herglotz et al. 2025
demonstrated that energy
measurement of video workloads requires precise synchronization between
playback and power sampling.
Our observations for the
TV2
flow, which contained embedded
autoplay videos, show markedly higher average power and total energy
consumption than non-video pages, consistent with the controlled findings
of Herglotz et al.
Herglotz et al. 2025
. Whereas their work isolated and
synchronized video playback under laboratory conditions, our setup captured
realistic end-user browsing sessions in which short videos appeared as part
of ordinary news-site activity. Together, these perspectives confirm that
even brief embedded videos can dominate device-level energy use, highlighting
the need for category-specific modeling in sustainability reporting.

7.1.
Model Structure and Bias

The proportional
P\times t
formulation used in simplified reporting
models inherently amplifies any misestimation of average device power.
If the assumed power value is too high or too low, the resulting energy
estimates accumulate a systematic bias proportional to task duration, as
observed in Section
5.1
.
Consequently, even small parameter errors can produce large deviations for
long-running interactions, limiting the external validity of constant-power
approximations.

Participant Learning and Fatigue

Repeated participation may influence execution efficiency and thereby
measured energy use.
To mitigate such order effects, both the sequence of user flows and the
allocation of laptops were randomized under constraints that ensured full
coverage.
Average energy use across all userflows declined from the first run (1453 J) to the second run

7.3.
Coverage and Inter-User Variance.

The experiment crossed eight user flows with four laptop platforms,
resulting in a balanced dataset in which each flow–computer combination
was represented by at least four and up to seven distinct participants
(Table
3
).
This design ensured that device- and category-level effects were not driven
by single-user behavior.
Analysis of participant-level random effects in the mixed model
(Section
5.2
) yielded an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)
of 0.012, indicating that only 1.2 % of total variance originated from
between-participant differences.
Thus, inter-user variability was negligible, and statistical power was
sufficient for stable estimation of category- and device-level effects.

7.4.
External Validity

The study focused on browser-based, non-streaming web services executed on four laptop platforms representative of common Intel generations and OEM designs. This scope ensured comparability across devices and categories but limits the generality of the findings in three respects.
First, the tested service categories—shopping, booking, navigation, and news—represent only a subset of web interactions; extending the analysis to other categories such as streaming, productivity, or AI-assisted services would clarify the broader applicability of the results.
Second, the dataset covers four laptop models, providing internal consistency but limited diversity across architectures. Expanding the hardware base to include a wider range of contemporary Energy Star–rated laptops would strengthen the trustworthiness of the derived power parameters.
Third, the focus on laptops excludes other end-user devices such as mobile phones, tablets, and high-performance desktops, which may differ in baseline power and dynamic scaling behavior.
All interactions were performed on live websites under real network conditions, ensuring ecological validity for laptop-based browsing. Consequently, the results are representative of realistic end-user conditions within this scope, while broader generalization to additional service types, hardware generations, and device classes should be examined in future studies.

8.
Conclusion & Future Work

This study show that the constant-power assumptions used in digital sustainability reporting frameworks such as Digst and DIMPACT may systematically overestimate end-user device energy use. Empirical measurements of realistic browser-based interactions revealed typical laptop powers between 9 W and 13 W—substantially lower than the 22 W and 15 W values assumed in these models. As a result, model bias increases proportionally with task duration, producing structural rather than random error.

Assuming a near-perfect constant-power exists, we found that intorducing hardware and service-category parameters increases model precision markedly. This showed that most variance originates from reproducible, context-dependent differences in devices and workloads. These findings suggest that sustainability reporting can remain simple yet become substantially more accurate by adopting, e.g., category-specific power parameters.

All data, analysis scripts, and software versions are openly provided, enabling full reproducibility and future extensions of this work. Together, these results establish a methodological foundation for transparent, empirically grounded models of end-user device energy use, supporting more credible and comparable digital sustainability reporting.

Future work should aim to enhance the accuracy, transparency, and representativeness of end-user device energy models. First, expanding the hardware base to include a broader selection of contemporary laptops would improve the trustworthiness of derived parameters. Second, building on the evidence that constant-power assumptions systematically overestimate device energy, new models should explore ways to reduce systematic error by relating energy use to task context, interaction type, and hardware characteristics.

Although this study focused on browser-based interactions, it may be beneficial to explore whether the same principles could be extended to other software categories—such as streaming, productivity, and AI-assisted applications—to examine whether similar proportional relationships hold. Further research is required to refine category definitions and develop standardized, fair user flows to support reproducible end-user device energy assessments.
