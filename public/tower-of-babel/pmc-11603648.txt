The effect of incentive factors on turnover intention of PHC workforce in rural China—from the perspective of two-factor theory
Authors: Lingzhi Sang, Xin Zheng, Guimei Chen, Zhongliang Bai, Ren Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe working situation and turnover intention of rural primary healthcare(PHC) workforce have always been of concern. This study aims to explore the effect of different incentive factors on turnover intention of PHC Workforce, with the goal of optimizing health human resources in China.MethodsStructured questionnaires were used for data collection, including incentive factors questionnaire based on two-factor theory, turnover intention, perceived social support and job burnout. Multiple linear regression was used to analyze the influence of different incentive factors on turnover intention. Structural equation model was used to explore the effect of motivation factors on turnover intention through different paths of job burnout and social support.ResultsThe total number of participants was 1112. PHC Workforce scored higher on hygienic factors than on motivation factors, both of which showed moderate satisfaction levels and the turnover intention level was below average. In regression analysis, the factors related to turnover intention included individual status (B =-0.854), salary (B =-0.610), individual progress (B =-0.628), education level (B = 0.971), monthly income (B =-0.161) and marital status (B = 1.664). The indirect effects of different types of incentive factors through social support were 0.097 (P < 0.001), 0.045 (P < 0.001), and 0.136 (P < 0.001).ConclusionDifferent incentive factors may decrease turnover intention of PHC Workforces, which is reflected in three key factors: individual status, salary, and individual progress. The mediating effect of social support is significant, suggesting that managers should prioritize staff mental health and family relationships. Also, implementing diverse incentive strategies and addressing individual needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11790-7.Keywords: PHC workforce, Turnover intention, Incentive factors, Two-factor theory, Health human resources (HHR)

Introduction

With the development of global primary health care, rural PHC workforce’s turnover intention gradually attracts worldwide attention. WHO’s global strategy for human resources for health [1] predicted a global shortage of 10 million health workers by 2030. About 50% of PHC workforce who already felt overworked and underpaid before the pandemic felt burned out during the outbreak, increasing their turnover intention. Boniol et al. [2] reported that primary health workforces’ shortage in Africa and the Eastern Mediterranean region would decrease by 9%and 7% respectively by 2030, which is likely to continue to widen the gap between them and middle-and high-income countries. But developed countries are not immune. A study in European countries [3] found there might be thousands of PHC workforce shortage in the coming years, dissatisfaction with training and pay has led to rise in turnover intention. In China, the stability of PHC workforce remains a serious problem despite government efforts to optimize health workforce. Qin et al. [4] found that the rural health workforce after the New Health-Care Reform did not meet the needs of primary health care and that there was a tendency for staff turnover in some underdeveloped areas. Lin et al. [5] found that PHC workforce in China face the risk of turnover intention, and the high-risk population was mainly the bachelor’s degrees. Liu et al. [6] found that job satisfaction paly an effect of work stress on turnover intentions of PHC workforces, and the salary was an obvious mediator. A cross-sectional study in China [7] reported job satisfaction etc. were predicators of village doctors’ turnover intention, which could be reduced through improving job satisfaction, resilience and work engagement. An empirical study [8] found the significant turnover intention in western China, suggested by improving salary, promotion and job safety to reduce turnover intention among primary care doctors. Meanwhile, Chen et al. [9] found that various incentive factors influencing job satisfaction may also play an important role in reducing turnover intention.

According to the two-factor theory, hygiene-motivation factors contribute to improving job satisfaction and enthusiasm, which is indispensable to reduce the turnover intention of rural PHC workforce. As a classical management theory, two-factor theory has been applied to management practice, including health workforces. Alrawahi et al. [10] used focus group discussions to identify job dissatisfaction factors for clinicians: health and safety, pay and organizational policies; satisfaction factors: relationships with colleagues and leaders, career development. There is heterogeneity in the effect of hygienic and motivational factors on turnover intention. Marc Bonenberger et al. [11] found that job satisfaction (workload, management, salary, etc.) were the main factors influencing the turnover intention of Ghanaian health workers, but motivation (training, career planning, etc.) may plays a more sustained role. In China, a meta-analysis of He et al. [12] summarized various factors were significantly associated with turnover intention, including demographic factors (education, marital status etc.), job characteristic factors (title, social status etc.) and work satisfaction (training, relationship etc.). Li et al. [13] measured rural PHC Workforce on the work motivation scale and found that they could be classified into four motivation levels. However, previous studies have not sorted out the categories of incentive factors in detail, nor have they developed relatively comprehensive hygiene-motivation factors questionnaires. Meanwhile, some studies have shown that job burnout and social support may be mediating factors influencing turnover intention of rural PHC Workforce.

Job burnout and social support have distinct effects on turnover intention, but how incentive factors are related to turnover intention through their mediation has not been well explored. A study in Saudi Arabia [14] found that social support mediates stress and turnover intention, and it alleviate rural PHC Workforce’ dissatisfaction with current affairs. Chen et al. [15] reported severe burnout and high turnover among rural Chinese doctors in a systematic review and suggested the importance of incentives. Wang et al. [16] found that the primary care providers in rural China may have high turnover intentions and job satisfaction had negative direct effects on burnout and turnover intention. A Chinese study [17] reported that emotional exhaustion partially mediates the relationship between surface acting and turnover intention among family doctors in rural China. Qu et al. [18] found the turnover intention of GPs in China has improved and job burnout would play an intermediary role between professional identity and turnover intention. Meanwhile, Duan et al. [19] found high prevalence of workplace violence in Chinese tertiary hospitals, and the social support was a partial mediator, as well as burnout and turnover intention. Also, Chen et al. [20] reported the importance of social support and psychological capital for diminishing burnout for lowering turnover intention in PHC Workforce. However, as important factors influencing turnover intention, the mediating role of job burnout and social support has not been fully explored.

To sum up, current researches on rural PHC workforce still lacks detailed and comprehensive questionnaires on hygienic-motivational factors, and studies on how these factors mediate turnover intention through job burnout and social support are relatively rare. Unlike developed countries and developing countries with less population pressure, China’s primary health care needs cannot be met by existing health resources, including the rural health human resource. As the health gatekeeper of Chinese rural residents [21], the stability of rural PHC workforce is undoubtedly crucial, and they need to be optimized through different types of incentives and psychological and support factors. Therefore, this study developed a questionnaire on incentive factors, combining existing scales of turnover intention, job burnout and social support, aiming to: (1) describe the incentive level and turnover intention of PHC workforce in rural China; (2) explore the mediating role of job burnout and social support in incentive factors on turnover intention paths. Thus, our study hypothesizes:

H1a: Hygiene-motivation factors are positively related to Perceived social support;

H1a: Hygiene-motivation factors are positively related to Perceived social support;

H1b: Hygiene-motivation factors are negatively related to Job burnout;

H1b: Hygiene-motivation factors are negatively related to Job burnout;

H2a: Perceived social support is negatively related to turnover intention;

H2a: Perceived social support is negatively related to turnover intention;

H2b: Job burnout is positively related to turnover intention;

H2b: Job burnout is positively related to turnover intention;

H3a: Perceived social support mediates between hygiene-motivation factors and turnover intention;

H3a: Perceived social support mediates between hygiene-motivation factors and turnover intention;

H3b: Job burnout mediates between hygiene-motivation factors and turnover intention.

H3b: Job burnout mediates between hygiene-motivation factors and turnover intention.

Methods

Sample selection

The survey was conducted in July to August in 2022, we chose Anhui Province in China as the survey site for the following reasons: (1) it boasts a supportive environment for healthcare reforms, being a pioneer in China’s medical reforms; (2) it has a large population of rural medical personnel, yet research on this group remains limited; (3) our team has prior experience conducting surveys of health workers here before the pandemic. We employed a multi-stage cluster sampling method, dividing Anhui into northern, central, and southern regions. From each region, one city was randomly selected. Figure 1 shows the selection of survey sites.

Selection of survey sites

Participants in this survey included doctors, nurses and administrators working in rural township hospitals and village clinics. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for the questionnaire include, but are not limited to: (1) age ≥ 22 years old; (2) working age ≥ 2 years.

Data collection

Before conducting the survey, all team members were divided into interview group and quality control group, and all received training. The interview group is responsible for handing out the questionnaires to the interviewees. The quality control group is responsible for sorting out the completed questionnaires and initially checking the reliability of the respondents ‘answers. If significant bias is found, such as regular responses, respondents are informed that they need to re-answer. We used Epidata software(version3.1) to enter data and screen out sub-standard questionnaires. Finally, we actually collected 1112 questionnaires, of which 1003 were valid, and the response rate was 90.20%.

Instruments

The survey tool is attached as an additional document in Supplement 1, including:

Hygienic factors (HF) and motivational factors (MF)

We compiled relevant contents from the two-factor theory and adapted it to the context of PHC Workforce in rural China. The two factors were designed differently: hygienic factors used interrogative sentences (“Are you satisfied with this item?“), while motivational factors used declarative statements, such as “I think…”, followed by agreement/disagreement options. This reflects the theoretical characteristics and practical applications of each factor. Each incentive factor was assessed by 2–3 questions, and the scoring scale is a 5-point Likert scale with the following anchors: “very dissatisfied (agree)”, “dissatisfied (agree)”, “average”, “satisfied (agree)”, “very satisfied (agree)”. The reliability and validity analyses of this questionnaire are presented in the Results section.

Turnover Intention (TI)

We adopted Michael et al.‘s [22] turnover intention scale, which comprises 6 items and measures 3 dimensions. The scale used a reverse-scored Likert scale. The turnover intention was categorized into four levels: “very low”, “relatively low”, “relatively high”, and “very high” based on average score thresholds: less than 2, 2~4, 4~6, and greater than 6, respectively.

Job Burnout (JB) and Perceived Social Support (PSS)

We employed the Chinese Maslach Burnout Inventory (CMBI) [23, 24] to assess three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (items 1, 4, 7, 10, 13), depersonalization (items 2, 5, 8, 11, 14), and reduced sense of achievement (items 3, 6, 9, 12, 15). The latter dimension was scored inversely, while the entire scale used a seven-point scoring system. The Perceived Social Support scale [25, 26] comprises 12 items measuring family support (items 3, 4, 8, 11), friend support (items 6, 7, 9, 12), and other support (items 1, 2, 5, 10). Among them, JB is a Likert five-point scale, and PSS is a Likert seven-point scale.

Statistical analyses

First, we assessed the reliability and validity of this questionnaire: the results of the reliability test were shown by the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient; the validity test was exploratory factor analysis.

Second, descriptive method(mean and standard deviation) was used to analyze the demographic characteristics, scores of hygienic and motivational factors, TI, JB and PSS of the respondents. Multiple-linear regression model was conducted to identify associated factors of turnover intention, the significance level α = 0.05.

Third, the influencing factors identified in the regression analysis (P < 0.05) were then included in the structural equation model(SEM) to explored the relationships between different incentive factors and turnover intention and tested the parallel mediating effects of JB and PSS.

We used the SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, version26) for analysis of reliability and validity, descriptive method and multiple linear regression. The Mplus(Version 8.10) was used to construct SEM and perform mediation analysis.

Discussion

Our study found that HF and MF levels were above middle, but the scores of each factor were significantly different. Furthermore, HF scored slightly higher than MF, but the difference was not significant. TI increased compared to pre-pandemic studies of rural PHC workforce in China [28, 29]. Although our results indicate that PSS is at a high level, job burnout is still above average. The linear regression results showed that the correlation between TI and individual status, salary in HF and individual progress in MF was statistically significant. Demographic characteristics and job factors such as age, education level and marital status were also analyzed to correlate with TI. It is worth noting that PSS and JB are heterogeneous in the mediating effect of incentive factors on TI. When considering from different incentive factors, it is necessary to select the reinforcement of JB and PSS according to different incentive backgrounds. The possible reasons for these results could be explained by the driving forces of different incentive strategies, the impact of pandemic on China’s rural health care system, and the guiding role of job burnout and social support.

Poor HF support and low MF level may lead to insufficient endogenous motivation of rural PHC workforce, which may lead to an increase in TI. We found that “Salary” scored the lowest, reflecting rural PHC workforce dissatisfaction with their job treatment. The reason may lie in the economic development level of Anhui Province. As a central province of China, although its economy and tax revenue are ahead of the underdeveloped western regions, there is still a big gap with developed regions such as Shanghai and Zhejiang. A study in Shanghai suggested job satisfaction and team stability among rural doctors were relatively high, potentially due to the city’s high economic development level and its leading diagnosis and treatment facilities in China [30]. However, a UK study by Staples et al. [31] indicated that rural doctors’ TI might not change despite incentive design alterations. This could be due to insufficient monetary value, lack of timeliness between incentives and behavior, or favoring compensation incentive policies for personnel stability. However, according to our results, we speculate that the motivation of rural PHC workforce in Anhui Province can still be improved by salary incentive, which is conducive to maintaining stability.

The highest score for “Personal life” indicates the harmonious relationship between rural PHC workforces and the importance of humane management and supervision in this province. Teamwork is important for the implementation of rural healthcare, such as the current family doctor service in China, which integrates the human resources of rural doctors and township hospitals, making the chronic disease management of rural residents more accurate and efficient [32, 33]. However, our study reported low scores for “team culture,” which may be where government staff need to improve. Generally speaking, economic factors such as working conditions and salary are important, but it is still necessary focus cultivating the work identity, cooperation consciousness and personal career development of rural PHC workforces.

While the pandemic has promoted unity of health system in rural China, COVID-19 has increased the workload of rural health care workers, which in turn has caused them to feel tired, leading to emotional exhaustion. Due to its large population in rural area, PHC workforce is overloaded with prevention and control work, and front-line workers often become the first responsible persons. The huge workload makes them bear a lot of mental pressure, which is easy to produce burnout and self-efficacy reduction [34, 35]. Despite the effective blockade and control policies of New Zealand, Viet Nam and other countries, medical personnel in their own countries still bear a psychological burden [36, 37], which may lead to TI. In addition, European countries face a shortage of general practitioners [38], especially during the pandemics when the deficit is likely to increase. It is worth acknowledging that the support given by the Chinese government and the public to rural PHC workforces during the pandemic was crucial. Besides, family members of health workers are supportive of their work, which is positive for the psychological capital of rural PHC workforces and essential for pandemic prevention and control [39, 40]. Nevertheless, the burnout problem of rural PHC workforces can not be ignored and should be highlighted.

We found that Individual Progress, Status and Salary affect TI through PSS, which may be the related factors the health department need to consider. Individual development of rural PHC workforces would increase their own salary, and their social status may also be improved, which is more conducive to the stability of the medical team. Just as Wang [41] calls training important for retaining rural doctors, it increases doctors ‘professional identity and makes it improve the individual Progress. Coppens et al. [42]. found that training had a positive effect on improving the psychological suggestion ability of general practitioners, which was conducive to enhancing their individual Status. In addition, promotions in positions and titles also increase their confidence. As they rise in social status, they are less likely to quit. The salary, of course, is the basic factor of individuals. Hu et al. [43] found that salary were significant factors of social support, and the enhancement of social support could continuously enhance self-efficacy, and may affect the occurrence of turnover among nurse practitioners. Doctors ‘salary is a crucial correlation, mediated by social support: both Shi et al. [44] and Deng et al. [45] suggest the need to strengthen family and personal support for doctors and implement pay reforms to increase their professional identity and reduce their willingness to leave, and our findings are also similar. Although JB’s mediating effect is relatively weak, health department still needs to strengthen psychological assistance to rural PHC workforces. Li et al. [46] have reported that work intensity and input increase JB and probably TI as well. After all, PSS needs to be enforced by gradually lowering JB.

For the human resource management of PHC workplaces, our study would also put forward some implementation suggestions. For Individual Progress and Status, we suggest that conditions need to be created for PHC workforces to upgrade their academic qualifications and participate in skill-enhancing training. After all, the knowledge of medical workers needs to be constantly updated. For salary, we believe that the government needs to implement salary reform and strengthen policy support, not just social support. Of course, the social level also needs to pay attention to psychological assistance to rural PHC workforces to help them enhance their self-efficacy to improve professional identity and self-confidence, which may reduce the mobility of PHC workforces.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, this study cannot establish a causal relationship between factors influencing TI and actual turnover behavior among rural PHC workforce. Secondly, due to its cross-sectional design, the limited sample size may not represent the universal experiences of rural PHC workforce. Thirdly, potential confounding factors such as regional economic disparities, cultural differences, and individual personality traits may have introduced bias into the survey analysis. Nevertheless, the results of this study could still help us better understand the association between incentive factors and TI of PHC workforce in rural China.

Conclusion

This study revealed the incentive level and turnover intention of PHC workforce in rural China. Also, the mediating role of job burnout and social support in incentive factors on turnover intention paths were explored. Therefore, the evidence from our study could be used to optimize motivation of medical staff and stabilize their teams. First, health department needs to provide regular and effective training and promotion opportunities for rural PHC workforces, and increase their allowances and benefits. Second, social support for rural PHC workforces needs to be strengthened. And implement humanistic management, cultivate team culture, enhance their sense of professional belonging. Third, it is necessary to rationalize the work pressure of rural PHC workforce, reduce their job burnout, and carry out psychological assistance for medical staff and their families from the institutional level to improve the level of the family support.
