Antibiotic resistance: a case of a chronic maxillofacial osteomyelitis complicating an odontogenic cellulitis

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:The post-antibiotic era is here. Oral diseases affect about half of the world’s population, and hence, antibiotic resistance can have serious consequences given that antibiotics are the cornerstone of modern medicine. Nowadays, with the massive, abusive use of antibiotics, we are forcing bacteria to mutate genetically in order to survive. This antibiotic Darwinism creates resistant bacterial strains that encroach on practitioners’ therapeutics. The European Centre for Disease Control estimates that 25 000 people die every year as a result of antibiotic resistance.Case presentation:We describe a case of antibiotic resistance in dentistry. This phenomenon, known as antibiotic resistance, is a matter of public health concern. Introduction

Recognition of issues related to the use of antibiotics has been present since their early clinical introduction in the 1940s[1]. Since then, the use of antimicrobials and often inappropriate use of these have been increasing. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the top global public health and development threats. It is estimated that bacterial AMR was directly responsible for 1.27 million global deaths in 2019 and contributed to 4.95 million deaths[2]. Antibiotic resistance in the United States kills approximately 23 000 patients a year and incurs over $20 billion in additional medical expenses. The European Centre for Disease Control estimates that 35 000 people die every year as a result of antibiotic resistance[3]. In addition to death and disability, AMR has significant economic costs. The World Bank estimates that AMR could result in US$ 1 trillion additional healthcare costs by 2050.

AMR happens when germs like bacteria and fungi develop the ability to defeat the drugs designed to kill them, which means that the germs are not killed and continue to grow. Specifically antibiotic resistance occurs when bacteria evolve or change to evade the effect of antibiotics through multiple different mechanisms[1].

Dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes is an ecological and public health concern[1]. Bacteria can be resistant to one or more antibiotics, in which case they are known as multi-resistant bacteria. In extreme cases, fortunately still very rare, a bacterium can be resistant to all antibiotics usable in humans. It is then said to be pan-resistant and can lead to therapeutic failure[3].

The mechanism of antibiotic resistance can be categorized into four groups: intrinsic resistance, acquired resistance, genetic change, and DNA transfer (through horizontal gene transfer)[1]. Bacteria such as Actinobacter, Pseudomonas, and various enterobacteria (Klebsiella, Escherichia coli, Serratia, and Proteus) can cause severe, often fatal, infections due to their antibiotic resistance[4]. Several factors are associated with the emergence of microbial resistance, such as widespread and inappropriate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, for example, the treatment of viral diseases with antibiotics.

This article deals with a case of antibiotic resistance observed at Yaoundé University Hospital.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old female student, mother of one, was referred to our hospital facility for management of a facial mass swelling.

The onset of symptoms would date back 3 months marked by intense, insomniating, radiating pain in the left ear with an oral-sinusal discharge leading to discomfort when eating. The pain was aggravated by local massage. The patient’s general condition was altered by asthenia and anorexia. On extraoral examination, we noted facial asymmetry due to a shiny swelling of the left hemiface obliterating the nasolabial fold and an erythematous bulbar conjunctiva (Fig. 1a). Palpation revealed palpable lymphadenopathies under the left mandible, mobile, soft, and infracentimetric, and an indurated cord facing the nasolabial fold; painful palpation of the bony bases opposite the left temporal region; and hypoesthesia of the left suborbital region. On intraoral examination, we noted the presence of a trismus, which limited our examination; the presence of a fistula opposite vestibular and palatal 27 and 28; foul breath; and impaired mastication and swallowing by the presence of bucco-sinusal communication. We had as provisional diagnosis, a thrombophlebitis of the facio-ophthalmic vein complicating a chronic diffuse odontogenic cellulitis with bucco-sinusal communication.

Our differential diagnosis was a thrombophlebitis of the cavernous sinus. We also carried out additional investigations: maxillofacial CT scan, lymphadenopathy-associated virus (LAV) serology, CBC, TCK/TP, and ESR.

The LAV serology showed that the patient was HIV negative, and other blood tests were without particularity. The CT scan revealed an important left maxillary osteolysis (Fig. 1b)

The severity of the patient’s presentation coupled with the important asthenia warranted her hospitalization after admission.

We administered probabilistic antibiotics, which consisted of a large spectrum of antibiotics, all by the intravenous route (an association of amoxicillin and clavulanic acid, metronidazole, and paracetamol in the advent of pain) while awaiting the results of the antibiogram which took 2 weeks to arrive. Microscopic examination of the antibiogram showed the presence of numerous Gram-positive cocci in clusters and tetrads and a few Gram-negative bacilli.

Culture on enriched media isolated three germs: Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae and a non-fermentative Gram-negative bacillus.

These three germs were resistant to many antibiotics but sensitive to some:

The non-fermentative Gram-negative bacillus was sensitive to imipenem and Gentamycin.

Klebsiella pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae was sensitive to amikacin, Gentamycin, and Fosfomycin.

Staphylococcus aureus was sensitive to Gentamycin, Clindamycin, Pristinamycin, Minocycline, Rifampicin, and Fusidic acid.

Once we had the results, we changed the antibiotic therapy to one of certainty as per the antibiogram, with no real change in the course of the infection.

We eliminated the deep bone sequestration during surgery under general anesthesia to improve prognosis when the financial means became available.

Nevertheless, we continued with our daily local debridement using sterile gauzes and with local antiseptics. The patient later passed away unfortunately after close to 2 months in the hospital.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first case of antibiotic resistance in dentistry reported in Cameroon.

Regarding antibiotic resistance in Cameroon, a review reported 45 human studies from different sites including urinary tract infections, gastro-intestinal infections and respiratory infections and none from dentistry[5]. The most incriminated resistant pathogens were E. coli (51.1%), K. pneumoniae (42.2%), and Staphylococcus spp. (40%), respectively.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has highlighted that AMR is one of the three greatest threats to healthcare systems globally[2].

In addition, drug-resistant infections impact the health of animals and plants, reduce productivity in farms, and threaten food security. The WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) 2022 report highlights alarming resistance rates among prevalent bacterial pathogens. Median reported rates in 76 countries of 42% for third-generation cephalosporin-resistant E. coli and 35% for methicillin-resistant S. aureus are a major concern.

With respect to self-medication, we have carried out a study showing that 88% of patients attending a dental institute carried out self-medication[6], with 34% self-medication with antibiotics. With respect to antibiotics, 47% had bought an antibiotic without prescription and 88.3% reported that all microbes, including viruses, can be treated with antibiotics[6].

Also, the fourth decade was the most represented, followed by the third decade (21–30). In the literature, it appears that infectious accidents are more frequent in the age bracket between 20 and 30 years, which was the case in our work, as our patient was 29 years old when she was admitted to hospital and 30 years old when she died after she spent close to 2 months in the hospital.

Pertaining to the clinical evolution, we observed an orbital involvement that got more important as time progressed. This can be explained by an initial dental an oral infection located in the posterior maxilla or upper arch (Figs 1c and 2d) that could spread through the inferior orbital fossa to the orbital space and in the absence of adequate treatment and cause this orbital involvement further demonstrating one of the several craniofacial osseus tracts of dental or jaw infection spread.

Given that our patient was on antibiotic therapy, in part self-medicated, prior to our consultation, we may wonder whether the initial antibiotic dosage was inappropriate; whether the chosen molecule was inappropriate; or whether there was poor compliance on the part of the patient. These are all factors that favor antibiotic resistance.

Some authors believe that the toxicity and spread of infection may be associated with symbiosis between anaerobic and aerobic Gram-negative bacteria.

Polymicrobial infections must be treated rapidly, without waiting for the results of bacterial culture and sensitivity tests. Delayed treatment and antecedents are always cited as risk factors for infectious processes. Delayed diagnosis and delayed and/or inadequate surgery are the main factors behind the many failures in management. In the same light, we had to wait 2 weeks for the antibiogram results to be able to start the antibiotic therapy of certainty.

Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is a constant source of concern due to its potential resistance to broad-spectrum antibiotics, its high-risk prevalence in healthcare establishments, and its ability to cause serious and fatal infections. This nosocomial pathogen has become a major threat to hospitals worldwide. Up to 40% of the normal population carries S. aureus in the anterior nares, and this rate is often higher in hospitalized patients and their caregivers. In recent years, community-acquired MRSA has been accepted as an important cause of skin and soft tissue infections, bacteremia, and pneumonia.

As observed in patients during antibiotic therapy, the progressive growth of pathogens due to persistent sub-mimimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) antibiotic levels is a key factor in the current antibiotic resistance crisis[7–9]. However, the principle of antibiotic dosing is to have a concentration of antimicrobials that is just higher than the MIC for the period needed to eliminate the infection, and this dosing varies with respect to the class of medications[10,11].

Up till the last decades, due to a combination of the presence of diverse effective antibiotics and unlikelihood of clinical resistant infections, therapeutic success was very high despite the presence of resistant microbial mutants. As clinical resistance was on the rise, emphasis was placed on the mechanisms of multi-resistant strains and how they were selected[11–13]. This primordial emphasis on antibiotic doses greater than the MIC led to an erroneous disregard for sub-MIC doses as a cause of resistance[11].

To further understand the sub-MIC doses exposure, it is important to state that antibiotics are not new, having been produced for millions of years by microbes such as fungi and bacteria naturally. However, in the last century approximately, humans have manufactured important amounts of antimicrobial agents for drugs as well as for agricultural objectives. This leads to a variation in antibiotic concentrations termed an antibiotic gradient in the human body as well as the general environment. The result of this gradient is bacterial exposition to both sub-MIC doses and doses greater than the MIC[11].

Antibiotic resistance is a subgroup of antibiotic failure; hence, there are more cases of antibiotic failure than antibiotic resistance. This antibiotic failure can be defined as any situation in which an antibiotic therapy is not successful in eradicating a bacterial infection, paving the way for a stagnation or degradation of the prognosis. Factors such as immune dysfunction and pathogen expansion through biofilm formation could lead to antibiotic failure[14].

There exists a close interchangeable relation resulting in the exchange of antibiotics and microbes, including resistant microbes, between two groups of antibiotic reservoirs: on one hand, treated humans, animals, aquaculture, and plants, and on the other hand, the general environment[11]. Taking this into consideration, there is a pressing need for a precise and comprehensive international surveillance effort to comprehend the bacterial zoonotic diseases which are infectious diseases of humans from a non-human source and the resistant genes associated.

Our study was carried out in accordance with the SCARE criteria[15].

Conclusion

Optimizing antibiotic treatment with appropriate management for each hospital patient can reduce the chances of acquiring antibiotic resistance and multi-drug-resistant organisms, and the time to act is now to prevent complications like death observed in our case.
