Real-world trends in diagnosis, treatment, and survival of non–small cell lung cancer in Vietnam
Authors: Vinh Hung Pham, Dinh Chinh Duong, Viet Binh Nguyen, Huyen Nga Do, Khanh Toan Nguyen, Thi Thuy My Nguyen, Thi Huong Pham, Van Lam Ngo, Van Tuan Nguyen, Muoi Dang Ho, Khanh Hung Truong, Ba Duc Ho, Van Nhat Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence describing temporal changes in disease stage, molecular testing, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes in non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) from low- and middle-income countries remains scarce. We assessed longitudinal real-world trends in NSCLC management and outcomes at a provincial oncology center in Vietnam over more than six years.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included consecutive adults with histologically confirmed NSCLC treated at Nghe An Oncology Hospital, Vietnam between January 2018 and August 2024 (n = 3,087). Trends in stage at diagnosis (AJCC 8th edition), biomarker testing uptake (primarily epidermal growth factor receptor [EGFR]; limited anaplastic lymphoma kinase [ALK]), and first-line treatment modalities were evaluated. Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared across diagnosis periods using log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify independent prognostic factors.ResultsThe median age was 64 years (range, 24–97), 73.5% were male, 71.6% had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1, and adenocarcinoma accounted for 77.0% of cases. The proportion of early-stage disease (stage I–II) increased from 3.1% in 2018 to 9.8% in 2023–2024, while stage III disease declined from 33.3% to 20.4%. Molecular testing uptake increased from 28.7% to a peak of 65.1% in 2022 and remained approximately 60% thereafter. Use of first-line targeted therapy rose from 7.7% to 20.8%, and immunotherapy from 0% to 2.5%. Median OS improved from 12.0 months in 2018 to 21.7 months in 2023–2024 (log-rank p < 0.001). Diagnosis during 2021–2024 was independently associated with lower mortality compared with 2018–2020 (adjusted HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.73–0.87).ConclusionsSubstantial improvements in diagnosis, molecular testing, and treatment adoption were observed over time and were associated with significantly improved survival in a large real-world Vietnamese NSCLC cohort.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15982-8.Keywords: Non–small cell lung cancer, Real-world data, Temporal trends, Molecular testing, Survival outcomes, Vietnam

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most pressing public health challenges worldwide, including in Vietnam, owing to its high mortality and sustained disease burden. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, nearly 2.5 million new lung cancer cases and more than 1.8 million deaths occur globally each year, accounting for over 12% of all newly diagnosed cancers and approximately 19% of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. In Vietnam, lung cancer ranks third in incidence, with an estimated 24,426 new cases annually, and second in cancer-related mortality, with more than 22,597 deaths each year [2]. Non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes approximately 80–85% of all lung cancers, and the majority of patients continue to be diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in persistently poor survival outcomes at the population level despite substantial therapeutic advances in recent years [1, 3].

Over the past two decades, the management of NSCLC has undergone profound transformation driven by advances in precision oncology. The identification of oncogenic driver alterations—most notably activating mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene and rearrangements involving anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), ROS1, and other molecular targets—has fundamentally reshaped first-line treatment strategies. Landmark randomized clinical trials, including IPASS and EURTAC, demonstrated that EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) significantly improve progression-free survival and quality of life compared with platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC [4, 5]. Subsequently, the FLAURA trial established osimertinib as the preferred first-line EGFR TKI by demonstrating superior progression-free and overall survival compared with earlier-generation TKIs [6]. Similarly, in ALK-rearranged NSCLC, the ALEX trial showed that alectinib provides superior efficacy and a more favorable safety profile compared with crizotinib, further reinforcing the role of molecularly guided therapy [7].

In parallel, immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis have introduced a new therapeutic paradigm for patients with NSCLC lacking actionable driver mutations. Phase III trials have demonstrated that pembrolizumab, either as monotherapy in patients with high PD-L1 expression or in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy, significantly improves overall survival compared with chemotherapy alone [8, 9]. In unresectable stage III NSCLC, durvalumab consolidation following concurrent chemoradiotherapy has become a standard of care, while more recent evidence has shown that osimertinib consolidation improves disease-free survival in patients with EGFR-mutated disease [10, 11]. Collectively, these advances have translated into meaningful survival improvements, particularly in high-income countries with broad access to molecular diagnostics and novel systemic therapies [12].

Beyond randomized clinical trials, real-world data (RWD) play an increasingly important role in evaluating how therapeutic innovations are implemented in routine clinical practice. Large population-based and registry studies from North America, Europe, and East Asia have reported increased uptake of biomarker testing, shifts toward earlier-stage diagnosis in selected settings, and expanded use of targeted therapy and immunotherapy over time, accompanied by improvements in survival outcomes [13–15]. However, these trends are heterogeneous and may differ substantially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where healthcare infrastructure, reimbursement policies, and access to molecular diagnostics and novel treatments remain variable and often limited [16].

In Vietnam and other Southeast Asian countries with comparable healthcare systems, access to molecular testing—particularly for EGFR and ALK alterations—and modern systemic therapies has gradually improved over recent years. Nevertheless, existing RWD studies from the region have largely focused on selected patient subgroups, such as unresectable stage III NSCLC, and have not provided a comprehensive assessment of temporal changes in stage distribution, molecular testing uptake, first-line treatment patterns, and survival outcomes across the broader NSCLC population [17].

Therefore, we conducted a retrospective real-world study of patients with NSCLC diagnosed and treated at a large provincial oncology center in Vietnam over more than six years, from January 2018 to August 2024. The primary objectives were to describe temporal trends in stage at diagnosis, molecular testing uptake, and first-line treatment patterns, and to evaluate changes in overall survival over time. Secondary objectives included identifying clinical and pathological prognostic factors associated with survival in a large real-world Vietnamese NSCLC cohort.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective, observational real-world study included consecutive adult patients with non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), staged according to the AJCC 8th edition, who were diagnosed and/or treated at Nghe An Oncology Hospital between January 2018 and August 2024 [18]. Nghe An Oncology Hospital is a provincial tertiary cancer center serving the North Central region of Vietnam. Clinical outcomes and vital status were updated through November 1, 2024, which served as the data cut-off for follow-up and survival analyses.

Study population

Eligible patients were adults aged ≥ 18 years with histologically confirmed NSCLC. Patients with small cell lung cancer, mixed small cell histology, or missing histological confirmation were excluded. For patients with multiple lung cancer diagnoses, only the first NSCLC diagnosis during the study period was included. There were no restrictions on disease stage or treatment modality, provided that baseline clinical and pathological data were available.

Data collection and study variables

Clinical and pathological data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital’s electronic and paper-based medical records. Collected variables included age at diagnosis, sex, smoking status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, histological subtype, and tumor stage at diagnosis according to the AJCC 8th edition. For analytical purposes, tumor stage was categorized as stage I–II, stage III, stage IV, or unknown.

Molecular testing variables included epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) testing status, testing methodology, and the presence of actionable mutations or rearrangements. EGFR and ALK testing were performed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–based assays or next-generation sequencing (NGS), depending on test availability and institutional practice. In Vietnam, EGFR testing is reimbursed by the national health insurance system, whereas broader NGS panels were selectively performed. Testing for additional oncogenic drivers was recorded when available. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) testing was performed in a limited subset of patients and was therefore not included in the primary molecular analyses.

Initial treatment modality was defined as the first anticancer treatment administered after diagnosis and was categorized as surgery, radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy (RT ± CT), chemotherapy alone, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, radiofrequency ablation (RFA), or best supportive care (BSC).

For temporal analyses, patients were grouped by year of diagnosis from 2018 to 2022, with 2023–2024 combined to ensure adequate sample size and analytical stability.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Patients who were alive at the data cut-off date or lost to follow-up were censored at the date of last known contact. Secondary outcomes included temporal trends in OS and identification of independent prognostic factors associated with survival.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics, disease stage distribution, molecular testing uptake, and treatment patterns were summarized descriptively according to the year of diagnosis. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between diagnosis periods using log-rank tests.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify independent prognostic factors for OS. Covariates included age, sex, smoking status, ECOG performance status, histological subtype, stage group, and diagnosis period. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nghe An Oncology Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived because the study involved retrospective analysis of de-identified routinely collected clinical data.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between January 2018 and August 2024, a total of 3,087 patients with histologically confirmed non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) were included in the analysis (Table 1). The median age at diagnosis was 64 years (range, 24–97); 41.0% of patients were aged 60–69 years and 27.8% were aged ≥ 70 years. Most patients were male (73.5%), and 64.5% had a history of smoking.

Overall performance status was relatively well preserved, with 71.6% of patients having an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1. Adenocarcinoma was the most common histological subtype (77.0%), followed by squamous cell carcinoma (15.4%). At the time of diagnosis, only 8.4% of patients presented with early-stage disease (stage I–II), whereas 23.7% had stage III disease and 65.3% had stage IV disease, indicating that the majority of patients were diagnosed with advanced-stage NSCLC.

Temporal trends in stage, molecular testing, and treatment patterns

Stage distribution

Over the more than six-year study period, a modest but statistically significant shift toward earlier-stage diagnosis was observed (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1). The proportion of patients diagnosed with stage I–II disease increased from 3.1% in 2018 to 9.8% in 2023–2024, whereas the proportion of stage III disease declined from 33.3% to 20.4%. Stage IV disease remained predominant throughout the study period but was relatively stable over time, ranging from approximately 59.4% to 67.3%. The proportion of patients with unknown stage remained consistently low, between 2.1% and 4.2%.

Molecular testing

Uptake of molecular testing increased substantially over time (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2). The proportion of patients undergoing any molecular testing—predominantly epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), with a smaller contribution from anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)—rose from 28.7% in 2018 to 54.2% in 2020 and peaked at 65.1% in 2022, before stabilizing at approximately 60.1% in 2023–2024. Correspondingly, the proportion of patients without molecular testing declined from more than 70% in 2018–2019 to below 40% after 2020.

The absolute number of detected actionable EGFR mutations increased over time, largely in parallel with expanded testing uptake, rather than indicating a true increase in mutation prevalence. ALK testing remained limited and was selectively performed; therefore, ALK results are presented descriptively in Supplementary Table S2.

First-line treatment patterns

First-line treatment patterns changed markedly during the study period (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3). The use of first-line chemotherapy alone declined from 69.0% in 2018 to approximately 40–41% from 2020 onward. In contrast, the proportion of patients receiving first-line targeted therapy increased from 7.7% in 2018 to approximately 20–22% after 2021.

Use of curative-intent treatment modalities, including surgical resection and concurrent chemoradiotherapy, increased modestly over time. Surgical resection rose from 5.4% in 2018 to approximately 9.0–10.5% in later years, and the use of concurrent chemoradiotherapy also increased from 0.4% in 2018 to approximately 13–14% in subsequent years. First-line immunotherapy remained infrequent (≤ 5%), reflecting limited access during much of the study period, but showed a gradual increase in more recent years (Supplementary Table S3).

Overall, temporal changes in stage distribution, molecular testing uptake, and first-line treatment patterns were statistically significant over time (all p < 0.001).

Survival outcomes

Overall survival (OS) for the entire cohort is shown in Fig. 1. Marked differences in survival were observed according to disease stage (Table 3; Fig. 2). Median OS was 74.6 months for patients with stage I–II disease, compared with 20.5 months for stage III and 12.9 months for stage IV disease. One-, three-, and five-year OS rates declined progressively with advancing stage, from 89.7%, 69.2%, and 52.5% for stage I–II disease to 68.8%, 31.2%, and 20.2% for stage III disease, and 51.3%, 18.1%, and nearly 0% for stage IV disease. Patients with unknown stage at diagnosis demonstrated intermediate survival outcomes.

After adjustment for age, sex, ECOG performance status, disease stage, histology, and smoking status, patients diagnosed during 2021–2024 had a significantly lower risk of death compared with those diagnosed during 2018–2020 (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 0.80, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.73–0.87; p < 0.001). Poor performance status was a strong adverse prognostic factor, with patients with ECOG performance status ≥ 2 exhibiting nearly a twofold increased risk of death compared with those with ECOG 0–1 (adjusted HR 1.98, 95% CI 1.79–2.18; p < 0.001).

Disease stage remained a key determinant of prognosis. Relative to stage I–II disease, both stage III and stage IV disease were associated with significantly increased mortality risks, with adjusted HRs of 2.49 (95% CI 1.95–3.19) and 4.26 (95% CI 3.37–5.37), respectively (both p < 0.001). Patients with unknown stage also had a higher risk of death compared with those with stage I–II disease (adjusted HR 2.94, 95% CI 2.04–4.24; p < 0.001).

Squamous cell carcinoma was associated with worse survival compared with non-squamous histologies (adjusted HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.21–1.55; p < 0.001). Male sex (adjusted HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.07–1.46; p = 0.005) and a history of smoking (adjusted HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.12–1.48; p < 0.001) were also independently associated with increased mortality. In contrast, age ≥ 65 years was not significantly associated with overall survival after multivariable adjustment (adjusted HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.89–1.07; p = 0.65).

Discussion

This large real-world analysis of 3,087 patients with non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated at a provincial oncology hospital in Vietnam between January 2018 and August 2024 provides comprehensive insights into evolving patterns of diagnosis, molecular testing, treatment, and survival in routine clinical practice.

First, we observed a modest but statistically significant shift toward earlier-stage diagnosis over time, with the proportion of stage I–II disease increasing from 3.1% in 2018 to nearly 10% in 2023–2024. Although advanced-stage disease remained predominant, this trend likely reflects improvements in referral pathways, increased disease awareness, and the gradual adoption of opportunistic early detection rather than organized population-based screening. Similar stage migration has been reported in real-world studies from other Asian countries; however, early-stage detection rates in Vietnam remain substantially lower than those reported in high-income settings [13, 19]. This persistent gap may be attributable, at least in part, to the absence of a national LDCT-based lung cancer screening program and the limited implementation of systematic early detection strategies in Vietnam. While LDCT screening has been introduced in selected centers and pilot programs, its implementation remains fragmented and is not yet organized at a population level [20, 21].

Second, molecular testing uptake increased markedly during the study period. EGFR testing rose from less than 30% in 2018 to over 60% after 2021, paralleling improvements in diagnostic infrastructure and broader implementation of molecular diagnostics at the provincial level. This expansion was accompanied by a substantial increase in first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) use, from 7.7% to more than 20%, underscoring the growing integration of precision oncology into routine clinical practice. Identification of actionable oncogenic driver alterations enables the selection of highly effective targeted therapies and has fundamentally transformed the management of NSCLC [22]. In our cohort, the prevalence of EGFR mutations was 43.7%, consistent with prior reports from East Asian populations, where EGFR mutation rates typically range from approximately 40% to 60% [23, 24].

Comparable expansions of guideline-recommended molecular diagnostics and biomarker-guided treatment have been reported across several Asian settings. In China, real-world studies in molecularly defined subgroups have demonstrated favorable survival outcomes in the era of targeted therapy, while registry-based analyses from Taiwan have described evolving survival patterns alongside recent initiatives to scale comprehensive genomic profiling in routine practice [25–27]. Notably, molecular testing in the present cohort primarily reflected EGFR mutation testing, which mirrors real-world clinical practice in Vietnam during much of the study period, whereas broader next-generation sequencing panels were not routinely available.

Third, treatment patterns evolved toward more contemporary and guideline-concordant approaches. The use of first-line chemotherapy alone declined substantially, while rates of surgical resection and concurrent chemoradiotherapy modestly increased, reflecting improvements in multidisciplinary care and access to potentially curative-intent treatments. Although first-line immunotherapy remained infrequent for much of the study period, increasing from 0% in 2018 to 2.5% in 2023–2024, it represents a major paradigm shift in NSCLC treatment. A clear upward trend was observed after 2021, consistent with gradual improvements in drug availability and reimbursement. Limited availability and inconsistent implementation of PD-L1 testing may have further constrained the use of immunotherapy in routine clinical practice. Given that PD-L1 expression is a key determinant of treatment selection, the absence of systematic PD-L1 assessment likely contributed substantially to the underutilization of immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly in patients who may have been eligible for biomarker-driven treatment strategies. These changes parallel global trends in NSCLC management, in which conventional chemotherapy is increasingly supplanted by biomarker-driven systemic therapies and multimodal treatment strategies [10, 28, 29].

Importantly, these temporal changes in diagnosis and treatment were accompanied by clinically meaningful improvements in survival outcomes. Median overall survival increased from 12.0 months in 2018 to 21.7 months in 2023–2024, and patients diagnosed during 2021–2024 experienced an approximately 20% reduction in mortality risk compared with those diagnosed during 2018–2020 after multivariable adjustment. These improvements likely reflect a combination of earlier-stage diagnosis, expanded access to molecular testing, and increased use of targeted therapies over time, and are consistent with population-based and real-world evidence from other regions demonstrating survival gains in the era of precision oncology [15]. Nevertheless, residual confounding related to comorbidities, subsequent lines of therapy, and unmeasured socioeconomic factors cannot be excluded.

Consistent with prior literature, disease stage and performance status remained the strongest prognostic factors in this cohort. Advanced stage and poor ECOG performance status were associated with markedly increased mortality, while squamous histology, male sex, and smoking history were also independently associated with worse outcomes. The observed association between male sex and worse survival may be multifactorial. Higher cumulative smoking exposure and a greater burden of smoking-related comorbidities among men may partly contribute to poorer outcomes. In addition, sex-related biological differences, including a higher prevalence of EGFR mutations among female patients, may influence treatment responsiveness and survival. Residual confounding related to differences in occupational exposures, health-seeking behavior, and access to care may also play a role. In contrast, chronological age was not independently associated with survival after multivariable adjustment, highlighting the greater prognostic importance of functional status and disease burden over age alone [30–33].

Overall survival differed significantly according to EGFR mutation status. Patients with EGFR-mutated tumors had the longest median survival (24.3 months), whereas those with EGFR wild-type tumors and patients without EGFR testing had comparably shorter median survival times (13.8 and 13.9 months, respectively). These findings are consistent with prior real-world evidence demonstrating superior outcomes in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC treated in the era of targeted therapy. Notably, patients without molecular testing exhibited survival outcomes similar to those with EGFR wild-type disease, likely reflecting more advanced disease, poorer performance status, or limited access to biomarker testing in routine practice. Retaining patients without molecular testing as a separate analytical group allowed a more accurate representation of real-world practice and helped mitigate selection bias inherent in biomarker-stratified analyses.

Advanced radiotherapy techniques such as stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) are increasingly used in selected patients with early-stage and oligometastatic NSCLC. However, SBRT was not available at our institution during the study period, and radiotherapy was delivered using conventional techniques. Therefore, SBRT-specific trends and outcomes could not be evaluated. This also reflects real-world constraints in resource-limited settings and highlights an important area for future healthcare system development.

Taken together, this large longitudinal real-world cohort provides robust evidence of improving NSCLC outcomes in Vietnam and offers an important benchmark for evaluating ongoing national efforts to expand access to molecular diagnostics, precision oncology, and modern multimodality cancer care in resource-limited settings. When interpreted alongside other real-world data from Vietnam and the broader Asian region, these findings underscore both the substantial progress achieved and the persistent gaps in access to contemporary lung cancer care across low- and middle-income countries [17, 34].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis conducted at a provincial oncology hospital, the findings may not fully reflect national practice patterns or regional disparities in access to lung cancer care across Vietnam. Second, several potentially important variables—including programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, comorbidities, cumulative smoking exposure, and socioeconomic factors—were unavailable or incompletely captured in routine medical records.

In addition, follow-up duration differed by year of diagnosis; therefore, overall survival estimates for patients diagnosed in 2023–2024 are less mature. Temporal survival improvements should be interpreted with caution, taking into account potential lead-time bias, stage migration, and evolving post-progression treatment strategies over time. Third, molecular testing was not uniformly performed, particularly during the early years of the study, introducing potential selection bias in estimates of biomarker prevalence and targeted therapy utilization.

Fourth, treatment decisions were influenced by drug availability and reimbursement constraints, which may differ substantially from practice patterns in high-income settings. Fifth, survival data were derived from hospital-based records and may not have fully captured deaths occurring outside the healthcare system. Finally, as an observational study, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and causal inferences cannot be definitively established. Multicenter prospective studies and linkage with national cancer registries would be valuable to validate and extend these findings. Radiotherapy technique-level data were limited, and SBRT was not available at our institution during the study period.

Clinical and policy implications

These findings highlight the growing impact of precision oncology in routine clinical practice in Vietnam and underscore the importance of continued expansion of molecular testing, particularly PD-L1 assessment and broader next-generation sequencing panels. Improved access to epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immunotherapy may further enhance survival outcomes for patients with non–small cell lung cancer. Importantly, given the persistently high proportion of advanced-stage disease at diagnosis, strengthening early detection strategies should also be prioritized. The development of structured lung cancer screening programs, including the potential implementation of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT)-based screening, may play a critical role in shifting stage distribution and improving long-term outcomes.

From a health system perspective, greater integration of molecular diagnostics into national cancer control strategies and expanded reimbursement coverage for biomarker-driven therapies could help reduce disparities in access to contemporary lung cancer care. Real-world evidence such as that generated by this study may inform ongoing efforts to optimize resource allocation and guideline implementation in resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion

In this large real-world cohort, substantial improvements were observed over time in early-stage detection, molecular testing uptake, treatment patterns, and overall survival among patients with non–small cell lung cancer in Vietnam. Expansion of EGFR testing and increased use of targeted therapies, together with modest gains in earlier diagnosis, were accompanied by clinically meaningful improvements in survival. Disease stage, ECOG performance status, and histological subtype remained the most important prognostic factors. Continued investment in molecular diagnostics, early detection strategies, and equitable access to targeted and immune-based therapies will be critical to further improving lung cancer outcomes in Vietnam.
