Plasma Microbial Cell-Free DNA Sequencing for the Diagnosis of Chronic Disseminated Candidiasis in Patients with Hematologic Malignancies
Authors: Alejandro De La Hoz, Victor Kovac, Ann E Woolley, Nicolas C Issa, Alyssa Letourneau, Lindsey R Baden, Sarah P Hammond, Jessica S Little
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnosis of chronic disseminated candidiasis (CDC) is frequently delayed because clinical manifestations are nonspecific and conventional diagnostic tests have limited sensitivity. We describe a series of 19 CDC cases in which plasma microbial cell-free DNA sequencing was performed due to diagnostic uncertainty. Results influenced antifungal management in most cases.Keywords: candidiasis, invasive fungal infections, leukemia, metagenomics, cell-free nucleic acids

BACKGROUND

Chronic disseminated candidiasis (CDC) is a fungal disease most commonly observed in patients with hematologic malignancies (HM) after chemotherapy or allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HCT) [1]. Diagnosis of CDC is challenging, as patients may be asymptomatic or present with nonspecific symptoms. Current diagnostic methods, including blood cultures, serum (1→3)-β-D-glucan assay (BDG), and histopathology, have limitations: blood cultures have low sensitivity, BDG is nonspecific, and biopsies can present risk and are often nondiagnostic [2].

Plasma microbial cell-free DNA sequencing (mcfDNA-seq), specifically Spectrum-Karius (Redwood City, CA, USA), can detect DNA from >1000 microbes [3]. Recent studies have evaluated its use in syndromes for which current diagnostic methods have a low sensitivity. In this study, we describe a series of CDC cases in patients with hematologic malignancies in which plasma mcfDNA-seq was utilized for diagnosis, and we describe the motivations for its use as well as its impact on patient care.

METHODS

We reviewed all cases in which mcfDNA-seq was used in our institutions from January 2020 to June 2025 and performed detailed chart review on all of those in which Candida sp. was detected to identify cases of CDC. CDC cases were adjudicated by 3 oncology infectious disease physicians and classified according to the 2020 European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer and the Mycoses Study Group Education and Research Consortium (EORTC/MSGERC) criteria as proven, probable, or possible [4]. Possible cases were included if plasma mcfDNA-seq identified Candida in the presence of a host with HM and compatible clinical syndrome and imaging findings as defined by the EORTC/MSGERC classification. HM was defined using the National Cancer Institute definition. Time of syndrome onset was defined as the date on which symptoms first appeared, including fever and abdominal pain, or the date on which a diagnostic test (imaging or BDG) raised suspicion during the index admission in asymptomatic patients. The time of suspected diagnosis was defined as the first time that CDC was mentioned in the clinical chart as the probable diagnosis.

We collected demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, race, underlying clinical condition and treatment for the underlying malignancy. Microbiological data included cultures and serum BDG testing 4 weeks before or after the diagnosis. The BDG test was performed using the Fungitell assay (Associates of Cape Cod, Inc., MA, USA). The upper limit of normal was 80 pg/mL. Imaging results and antifungal treatments were collected. We reviewed each clinical chart to identify the motivation for plasma mcfDNA-seq and whether the treatment was adjusted based on the results. Plasma mcfDNA-seq was performed using Spectrum-Karius, Inc. (Redwood City, CA, USA). We used descriptive statistics as appropriate. We calculated the time from syndrome onset to suspected diagnosis, mcfDNA-seq, imaging and first positive BDG. This study was approved with a waiver for informed consent by the Ethics Committee of Mass General Brigham (IRB protocol number: 2022P003176).

RESULTS

A total of 287 mcfDNA-seq tests were performed during the study period of which 22 detected Candida species. Of those, 19 out of 22 (86%) had a diagnosis of CDC, which were the cases described here. Of the 3 other cases, 2 were considered to represent gut translocation and were not treated with directed antifungals, 1 case involved a patient with infected pseudoaneurysm who subsequently developed candidemia and did receive antifungal therapy. Ten (52.6%) patients were female, and the median age was 59 (interquartile range 48–64) (Table 1). Underlying conditions included acute myeloid leukemia (n = 12, 63.1%), myelodysplastic syndrome (n = 2, 10.5%), myelofibrosis (n = 1, 5.3%), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (n = 1, 5.3%), acute lymphocytic leukemia (n = 1, 5.3%), and diffuse large B cell lymphoma (n = 2, 10.5%). All patients had received chemotherapy (n = 19, 100%), and 7 were recipients of allogeneic HCT (36.8%). Four cases of CDC were proven (21%), 1 was probable (5.3%), and 14 were possible (73.7%). Microbial cell-free DNA sequencing identified Candida sp. in all 19 cases, C. albicans was the most common species (n = 15, 78.9%) followed by C. dubliniensis (n = 3, 15.8%), and C. tropicalis (n = 1, 5.3%). In 7 (36.8%) cases other microorganisms were identified by mcfDNA-seq.

Most patients (73.7%) presented with fever; other symptoms included abdominal pain, nausea, weakness, headache, and diarrhea. Serum BDG was below the upper limit of normal in 2 cases throughout follow up. In 10 cases it was <31 pg/mL at time of syndrome onset. The median peak value of the serum BDG in those with a positive result was 369 pg/mL. The median time from syndrome onset to a positive BDG was 1 day (range: −40 to 103 days), to imaging suggestive of CDC was 3 days (range: −12 to 39 days), to mcfDNA-seq was 17 days (range: 5–77 days), and to confirmed diagnosis was 15 days (range: 0–77 days). All patients had blood cultures drawn during their index admission. Candida sp. was isolated from sterile cultures in only 3 (15.8%) patients. Biopsy was performed in 6 patients, 3 of these demonstrated histopathologic signs of invasive yeast infection, and 1 grew Candida sp. on culture. Liver (n = 17, 89.5%), lung (n = 13, 68.4%), and spleen (n = 12, 63.1%) were the most frequently involved organs. There were 6 cases of brain infection (microabscess) and 3 of endophthalmitis.

The most common indication for plasma mcfDNA-seq was diagnostic uncertainty. Some cases cited compatible imaging but negative BDG and cultures. In other instances, pulmonary nodules were the primary radiographic findings and the differential was considered broad. Other cases mentioned persistent signs of infection despite appropriate therapy, such as persistent fever, respiratory failure, and increasing size of pulmonary or brain lesions on imaging (Table 2). Microbial cell-free DNA sequencing changed antimicrobial management in 11 cases (57.9%), including only switching from intravenous to oral therapy (n = 4), only stopping anti-mold therapy (n = 4), stopping anti-mold therapy and switching IV to oral therapy (n = 2), and starting antifungals (n = 1). Fluconazole was the definitive treatment in most cases (n = 11, 57.9%). Median treatment duration was 219 days (range: 23–826 days). Imaging findings improved in 10 cases (52.6%); BDG fell to below the upper limit of normal in 9 (47.4%) cases; and 7 (36.8%) patients completed therapy and were considered cured. Six- and 12-week all-cause mortality rates were 21% and 31.6%, respectively. Only 1 patient presumably died from CDC despite recovery from neutropenia, 12-week mortality not attributable to CDC was 26.3%, mostly due to other infections or the underlying disease. Five patients underwent HCT during treatment for CDC without any CDC-related complications.

DISCUSSION

We characterized 19 cases of CDC in which mcfDNA-seq provided molecular diagnostic support. Plasma mcfDNA-seq was used in cases of diagnostic uncertainty when clinical symptoms persisted despite appropriate therapy or fungal markers were unrevealing but the remaining clinical picture was suggestive of CDC. Results were used to adjust treatment in more than 50% of the patients.

When CDC is suspected, imaging, blood cultures, histopathology, BDG, and tissue sequencing are recommended [5]. Diagnostic criteria for proven CDC include positive blood or tissue cultures or histopathology, and a compatible clinical syndrome [4, 5]. However, cultures have low sensitivity and biopsy with histopathology is often nondiagnostic [5]. In addition, BDG can be negative initially, with a sensitivity as low as 50% even later in the disease, and it is nonspecific for Candida sp. [2] Imaging can be more sensitive but less specific, particularly when organs other than the liver or spleen are involved. Studies have analyzed other imaging modalities, including PET-CT, which does not replace conventional imaging for diagnosis.

McfDNA-seq has emerged as a new diagnostic tool for multiple infectious syndromes, given the limited sensitivity and specificity of culture-based methods and serologic tests.[3] Recent studies have evaluated its diagnostic performance for neutropenic fever [6, 7], fever of unknown origin [8], and invasive fungal infections [9–11]. However, there are few reports of CDC diagnosed by plasma mcfDNA-seq. Only 1 study of 3 patients from China described a case of CDC diagnosed by plasma mcfDNA-seq, which identified C. glabrata [12]. McfDNA-seq has been used in other studies to address diagnostic uncertainty or in the absence of clinical improvement with antifungal medications, yielding findings similar to ours. In our series, it helped confirming CDC as the main diagnosis. McfDNA-seq may also help guide appropriate antifungal therapy when fluconazole-resistant species, such as C. krusei, are identified. We also observed potential for antimicrobial stewardship, as mcfDNA-seq results frequently led to targeted antifungal therapy and an IV-to-oral switch, potentially reducing treatment costs and toxicities.

The frequency of lung and brain involvement was significant in our study. Many series have described brain involvement as 10% or lower [13–15]. However, in our series, brain involvement was observed in 31.6% of patients. Ophthalmologic evaluation was performed in 7 cases, of whom 3 (15.8%) had signs of ocular candidiasis. We also found multiple cases of C. dubliniensis, which has rarely been reported causing CDC in the past [13–15].

This report has a few limitations. First, CDC cases were retrospectively described and adjudicated, which may lead to bias. Second, not all clinical CDC cases at our institution were included and mcfDNA-seq is not used systematically for this indication; therefore, specificity or sensitivity cannot be calculated accurately. Third, since possible CDC cases were included when mcfDNA-seq was positive this may have led to incorporation bias. However, we present a robust description of all cases and the circumstances in which mcfDNA-seq was used, adding to the literature on CDC and mcfDNA-seq.

In summary, our findings support the use of plasma mcfDNA-seq in cases with clinical suspicion for CDC and persistent diagnostic uncertainty after a standard work-up has been obtained. A subject of interest regarding plasma mcfDNA-seq testing is diagnostic stewardship, given the relatively high costs and limited availability. Further investigation of the application and cost-effectiveness of this novel testing modality to this unique population and clinical syndrome is warranted.
