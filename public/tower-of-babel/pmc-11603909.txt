Etiological analysis of acute respiratory infections in hospitalized children after the relaxation of COVID-19 non-pharmacological interventions in Quzhou, China
Authors: Canya Fu, Chunting Zhou, Canjie Zheng, Sheng Li, Wei Song, Jiaoxian Yao, Chuanxi Fu, Zhiying Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute respiratory infections (ARIs) can cause morbidity and mortality in children. This study was to determine the characteristics of pathogens in hospitalized children with ARIs after the relaxation of COVID-19 non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) in Quzhou, China.MethodsHospitalized children with ARIs were enrolled between May and October 2023, and thirteen common respiratory pathogens were tested by fluorescent quantitative polymerase chain reaction. Mono- and co-infections were assessed, and the association between pathogens and age was explored using restricted cubic spline analysis.ResultsA total of 1225 children were included, 820 of them detected one pathogen and 238 of them detected two or more pathogens. The dominant pathogen varies monthly. Mycoplasma pneumoniae (Mp) was the most common pathogen in monoinfection, followed by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and human rhinovirus (HRV), while influenza virus was detected at a lower rate. Mp + HRV was the most common combination of coinfections. The detection rates of Mp and HRV were higher in coinfections than in monoinfection, but there was no difference in the detection rate of RSV. Children aged 1–3 years had the highest positive detection rates and were more likely to be infected with multiple pathogens, with 40% of respiratory pathogen monoinfection and 47.48% of coinfections (χ2 = 4.245, P = 0.039). In the restricted cubic spline models, a J-shaped association was consistently observed between age and Mp infection, the risk of HRV first increased and then decreased, the risk of RSV was relatively flat until 1.5 years and then decreased rapidly.ConclusionOur study revealed the epidemiological characteristics of ARIs pathogens after the relaxation of NPIs. The positivity rates of Mp, RSV, and HRV are the highest, while those of influenza virus are still low. Additionally, age and season affect the distribution of respiratory pathogens. These findings underscore the importance of ongoing regional pathogen surveillance to guide local public health responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10257-8.Keywords: Acute respiratory infections, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Monoinfection, Coinfections, Epidemiology

Introduction

Acute respiratory infections (ARIs) are acute illnesses, typically lasting no longer than 21 days, caused by various pathogens that produce symptoms such as cough, sputum, shortness of breath, sore throat, and runny nose [1]. ARIs represent a formidable and pervasive challenge to global public health [2, 3], contributing to morbidity and mortality across all age groups, especially among children [4–7]. This class of infections, which primarily affects the respiratory tract, encompasses a spectrum of diseases ranging from mild upper respiratory tract infections to severe lower respiratory tract infections, with considerable variations in clinical manifestations and severity [8]. Viruses are responsible for a large proportion of ARIs [9–11], with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human rhinovirus (HRV), human metapneumovirus (HMPV), human parainfluenza virus (HPIV), influenza virus (IFV), human coronavirus (HCoV), adenovirus (ADV), and human bocavirus (HBoV) accounting for approximately 70% of cases [12]. Among hospitalized children, mixed respiratory viral infections are detected at rates between 10% and 30% [13].

In addition to viruses, infections with bacteria, mycoplasma, and chlamydia can also lead to pneumonia. Recent reports of children hospitalized with pneumonia due to atypical symptoms and signs have raised concerns about Mycoplasma pneumoniae (Mp) as the cause of the outbreaks [14]. Mp is a common cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), with outbreaks occurring annually or every few years. Since the beginning of 2020, the introduction of non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) (maintenance of social distancing and isolation) during the COVID-19 pandemic has kept the prevalence of Mp pneumonia at extremely low levels, which would continue until 2021/22 [15]. In the six months between April 1 and September 30, 2023 (a long time after the relaxation of NPIs), prospective surveillance data from 45 locations in 24 countries in Europe, Asia, Oceania, and the Americas showed that the average incidence rate of Mp had risen to 4.12% [15]. The delayed re-emergence of Mp after COVID-19 pandemic restrictions has attracted public attention.

In September and October 2023, Quzhou, located in the western Zhejiang Province, China, experienced a surge in hospital reported cases of childhood pneumonia. After the relaxation of the NPIs during the COVID-19 pandemic, the pattern of respiratory infections showed changes in pathogen species, temporality, and frequency [16]. At present, the changes of ARIs pathogens in hospitalized children after the relaxation of COVID-19 NPIs in Quzhou is still unclear. The aim of this study was to evaluate the frequency of respiratory pathogens and coinfections, especially Mp, RSV and IFV, in children under 18 years of age with ARIs treated in a hospital in Quzhou. We collected the nasal and pharyngeal swabs from 1225 children hospitalized with ARIs from May to October 2023 in Quzhou, tested 13 common respiratory pathogens, and assessed the pathogen prevalence, age distribution, and variability of coinfections. The data obtained may be important for predicting disease progression, prognosis, and treatment strategies, especially in high-risk populations such as young children.

Methods

Study participants

Children with signs or symptoms of respiratory tract infection (such as fever, coughing, nasal obstruction, sneeze), or signs of lower respiratory tract infection (tachypnea, dyspnea, or wheezing/rales on auscultation, etc.) were defined as ARIs [17]. A total of 1967 children (< 18 years) were admitted to the pediatric department of Quzhou Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital from May to October 2023. Finally, 1225 ARIs children with pathogenic test results were retrospectively enrolled in this study. Data including sex, age, birth date, admission date, discharge date, hospitalization days, discharge diagnosis, hospitalization cost, transfer, test number and pathogen test results were obtained from the hospital database, and personal information such as name, address, and telephone number were deleted during data analysis. The participants were divided into four age groups: <1 years (infants), 1–3 years (toddlers), 4–6 years (preschoolers), ≥ 7 years (schoolchildren).

Specimen collection and testing

Nasopharyngeal swabs were taken by trained personnel within 24 h of enrollment according to standard operating procedures. The specimen should be kept in the shipping container and stored at − 80 °C prior to shipment to a third-party testing facility. All specimens were tested for 13 pathogens including Influenza A (FluA), Influenza A H1N1 pdm09 (FluA-H1N1), Influenza A H3N2 (FluA-H3N2), Influenza B (FluB), RSV, HRV, HMPV, HPIV, HCoV, ADV, HBoV, Mp and Chlamydia (Ch) using fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction by the laboratory of Hangzhou Jinnuo Medical Laboratory Co., LTD, Zhejiang Province, China [18].

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed measurement data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (‾x ± s), and non-normally distributed data are presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR). The differences between the groups were examined by ANOVA, or the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical data are presented as the number of cases and percentage, and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. We also used restricted cubic spline model fitted for logistic regression with 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of infected pathogens with age, adjusted for sex. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 16.0; SPSS Inc., USA). Data were visualized in R (v4.3.2), using packages “ggplot2” and “rms”. A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Quzhou Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Approval No.: 2024-P-NO.05). Written informed consent was obtained from the children’s legal guardians before collecting nasopharyngeal swab specimens.

Results

Basic characteristics and detection of ARIs

A total of 1967 children were admitted to the pediatric department between May 1 and October 31, 2023. Finally, 1225 children with ARIs were tested for 13 common pathogens and enrolled in this study. Among them, 13.63% (167/1225) were negative for 13 respiratory pathogens tested, 66.94% (820/1225) detected one pathogen named monoinfection and 19.43% (238/1225) detected two or more pathogens named coinfections. There were 668 males (54.53%) and 557 females (45.47%), and the age ranged from 28 days to 16 years old, with a median age of 3 years. Pathogen-uninfected, monoinfection and coinfections did not differ between gender (χ2 = 1.868, P = 0.393), whereas they differed between age groups (χ2 = 46.024, P < 0.001). The highest positive detection rates were in children aged 1–3 years [36%, (328 + 113)/1225] and in October [20.57%, (174 + 78)/1225]. Mean hospitalization days were highest in coinfections at 5.42 days, followed by monoinfection (5.11 days), undetected group (4.54 days) with significant differences between the different pathogen detection profiles (F = 9.046, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Further Bonferroni multiple comparisons revealed a significant difference in the length of hospitalization between the undetected and monoinfection group (P = 0.003), as well as between the undetected and coinfections group (P < 0.001). However, no significant difference was observed between monoinfection and coinfections group (P = 0.127).

Epidemiological characteristics of 13 pathogens

The most common pathogen of total samples was Mp at 34.53% (423/1225), followed by HRV at 25.14% (308/1225) and RSV at 19.67% (241/1225). Other pathogens, including ADV, HBoV, HCoV, FluA, FluB, FluA-H3N2, and Ch, were detected in a 0.16–2.37% samples. FluA-H1N1 was not detected (Supplementary Table 1). Mp (36.10%, 296/820) was the most common pathogen in monoinfection, followed by RSV (22.80%, 187/820) and HRV (17.32%, 142/820). Of the coinfected children, 203 were infected with two pathogens, 32 were infected with three pathogens, and 3 were infected with four pathogens. Monoinfection or coinfections with Mp would produce longer average hospitalization days and costs (Table 2). The different combinations of pathogens are shown in Supplementary Table 2, with a total of 27 combinations of two pathogens, 16 combinations of three pathogens, and 3 combinations of four pathogens. Mp + HRV (35.7%, 85/238) was the most common combination of coinfections, followed by HRV + RSV (13.0%, 31/238) and HRV + HPIV (12.2%, 29/238) (Fig. 1). The detection rate of HRV, Mp, HPIV and HMPV coinfections were significantly higher than that of monoinfection (P < 0.001), whereas there was no significant difference between the detection rates of RSV monoinfection and coinfections (χ2 = 0.001, P = 0.970) (Table 3). Figure 2 displays the monthly distribution of tested pathogens between May and October 2023. The high detection of Mp was in October and August, while HRV was in June and October, RSV was in June and September. During the six-month period, the detection rates for ADV, HCoV, HBoV, IFV, and Ch remained consistently below 5%.

The risk of pathogens infection with age

The age group distribution of detected pathogens is shown in Fig. 3. In the infant group, RSV (37.60%) was the most prevalent pathogen followed by HRV (18.60%) and HPIV (17.83%), while FluA, FluA-H3N2, FluA-H1N1 and FluB were not detected. In the toddler group, HRV (33.07%) was the most prevalent pathogen, followed by RSV (25.90%) and Mp (20.72%), the detection rates of HBoV, ADV, HCoV, FluA and FluB were less than 5%. In the preschool group, Mp (57.48%) was the most prevalent pathogens, HRV (22.45%) was the second-most prevalent pathogens, followed by HMPV (15.99%), the detection rates of FluA, FluB and Ch were less than 0.5%. In the schoolchildren group, Mp (79.53%) was the most prevalent pathogens, followed by HRV (16.37%), other pathogens were rarely detected. Table 3 presents respiratory pathogen monoinfection and coinfection in children of different age groups. The infant group was more likely to be infected with a single pathogen, with 19.63% of respiratory pathogen monoinfection and 13.45% of coinfections (χ2 = 4.737, P = 0.030). In contrast, the toddler group was more likely to be infected with multiple pathogens, with 40% of respiratory pathogen monoinfection and 47.48% of coinfections (χ2 = 4.245, P = 0.039). There were no significant differences between respiratory pathogen monoinfection and coinfections infections in preschoolers and schoolchildren. In the restricted cubic spline models, the infection risk of Mp, HRV, and RSV with age showed a nonlinear association (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4A-Fig. 4C). A J-shaped association was consistently observed between Mp infection and age, with the risk rapidly increasing after 3 years of age (Fig. 4A). In contrast, a reverse U-shaped association was found between HRV infection and age, with the risk showing an increase followed by a decrease (Fig. 4B). The risk of RSV was relatively flat up to 1.5 years and then declined rapidly (Fig. 4C), while the risk of HPIV was decreased with age increasing (Fig. 4D).

Discussions

This study described and analyzed the epidemiology of respiratory pathogens in children under 18 years of age who were hospitalized for ARIs in Quzhou City, China, more than 4 years after the implementation of COVID-19 pandemic restrictions. Of all children hospitalized with ARIs, nearly two-thirds were under three years old, with the highest proportion in the 1–3 year age group (40.98%), which was inconsistent with Julia Tabatabai et al. [19], who had the highest proportion in the < 6 month age group (39.0%).

Regarding the profile of respiratory pathogen detected in the present study, the most common pathogen was Mp, followed by HRV, RSV, HPIV and HMPV, with a low rate of influenza virus detection. At least one viral pathogen was detected in 62.12% of the hospitalized children. It was worth noting that in our study, the most frequently detected respiratory pathogen was Mp, rather than RSV or HRV, as found in some other studies [10, 19–21]. Cheng Gong et al. [22] found an increase in the incidence of respiratory diseases among children in Beijing during the fall and winter seasons in 2023, with the highest detection rate of Mp, which was consistent with our results. Mp is a non-cellular prokaryotic microorganism that is mainly transmitted by air droplets, coughing and close contact [23]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Mp infections declined in children of all ages due to a series of strict measures, including reduced contact, increased awareness of mask use, and attention to hand hygiene [24]. The immunity of the population to Mp decreased after the COVID-19 pandemic, which led to the re-emergence of Mp. The delayed re-emergence of Mp after the containment of the COVID-19 pandemic may be related to the attenuation of cell-mediated herd immunity [15]. In addition, the atypical characteristics of Mp compared to other pathogens, including a slow generation time (6 h), a relatively low transmission rate, and an incubation period of up to 1–3 weeks, may be contributing factors to the long intervals required for Mp to re-establish infection in the population [15].

In our study, the detection of RSV (19.67%) increased but was lower than HRV (25.14%), while the detection of influenza viruses remained low. Research suggests that during the COVID-19 pandemic, non-pharmacological prophylaxis, intra-host competition, and population-level interactions reduced the transmission of common respiratory viruses, including influenza, RSV, HMPV, and PIV [25–28]. In contrast, rhinovirus and adenovirus circulation was less affected [28, 29]. This may be due to the fact that HRV is a non-enveloped virus. Compared with enveloped viruses such as influenza virus and RSV, HRV is highly resistant to ethanol-based disinfectants, and a single application of ethanol-based disinfectant does not reduce the amount left on the skin, increasing the risk of surface contamination [30, 31]. In addition, wearing masks during NPIs prevented the transmission of human coronaviruses and influenza viruses, but did not reduce the transmission of rhinovirus-containing droplets and aerosols, increasing the risk of HRV infection [32]. It has been suggested that HRV is not affected by the presence of SARS-CoV-2 [33]. Consequently, rhinovirus circulation was not impacted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which partially contributes to the sustained high detection rates of rhinovirus following the relaxation of NPIs. However, RSV was effectively contained by NPIs during the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in a longer period of recirculation, which, to some extent, explains the lower detection rate of RSV compared to HRV observed in this study. The decrease in influenza virus activity after relaxation from NPIs may be due to viral interference, usually caused by another respiratory virus that can compete with influenza viruses in the respiratory tract [34]. Wu et al. [35] found a negative interaction between HRV and influenza. In addition, widespread local influenza vaccination in Quzhou may have contributed to the low influenza virus detection rate in our study.

In monoinfection and coinfections, different pathogens were detected at different rates. Among them, HRV, Mp, HPIV and HMPV were more inclined to be detected in coinfections. In this study, 30.02% (127/423) of the Mp-positive patients had one or more other respiratory pathogens detected in their respiratory specimens at the same time, which was closer to the results of Wang Xue et al. [36]. The highest percentage of Mp + virus co-detection was for Mp + HRV (35.71%), while the highest percentage of virus + virus co-detection was for HRV + RSV (13.03%), the latter expected because HRV and RSV are the most common causes of upper and lower respiratory tract infections in children, respectively, and are the two most common respiratory viruses in viral surveillance studies [37–40].

Age is an important factor affecting respiratory pathogen infections. Our study suggested that in children younger than 18 years, the detection rate of Mp tended to increase with increasing age, while the detection rate of RSV was the opposite, which was consistent with relevant research [10, 13, 24, 36, 41]. Earlier studies relying upon serology and culture reported that M. pneumoniae pneumonia to be somewhat uncommon in children under 5 years old, and greatest among school-aged children 5 to 15 years of age, with a decline after adolescence and on into adulthood [42, 43]. To be noted, RSV was reported to be one of the leading causes of hospitalization and mortality among children under 5 years old, particularly those less than 1 year of age [44, 45]. Pathogen characteristics and host immunity may lead to age-related differences in respiratory pathogen infections. Furthermore, our study found that the infant group was more susceptible to infection with respiratory mono-pathogens, which may be attributed to the restricted capacity for independent activities among them. The coinfections rate was higher in children aged 1–3 years than in other age groups. This finding was consistent with that reported by Song et al. in a previous study [46]. As age increases, the exposure of 1-3-year-old children to pathogens increases, and at this time their immunity is not yet mature, which may make them more susceptible to multiple respiratory pathogen infections [47]. For preschoolers and schoolchildren, they may be protected by pathogen cross immunity in collective environments, so there is no significant difference in single pathogen or multi pathogen infections.

It is well known that pathogen distribution can be affected by seasonality, and climate as an important factor can alter the duration of the pathogen in the environment [48]. In our study, we found that there were differences in the distribution of pathogens in different months. Mp had the highest detection rate in October and showed an opposite trend to RSV in June-October. This may be related to the different epidemiological seasons of Mp and RSV, with Mp being detected in all seasons, with a slightly higher rate in spring and autumn, and RSV being detected at a higher rate in winter and spring [49]. In order to comprehensively understand the prevalence of these pathogens, a monitoring plan covering multiple seasonal cycles must be implemented in the future.

Our study has several limitations that should be noted. First, as a single-center study conducted over a short period of time (six-month) in Quzhou, our results may not be generalizable to other regions or seasons, particularly given the influence of local epidemiology and temporal variations in respiratory pathogen prevalence. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted in the context of the limited region and time period of our study and future studies should cover multiple seasons. Second, we did not perform further typing for pathogens, nor did we include tests for other potential pathogens, such as respiratory bacteria or fungi. This restriction may have led to an incomplete understanding of the pathogenic spectrum and underestimation of viral and/or bacterial coinfections in children with ARIs. Finally, due to the lack of clinical symptom data, we were unable to analyze the relationship between specific pathogen coinfections and the severity of respiratory symptoms.

Conclusion

Our single-center study provides insight into the epidemiologic characteristics of 13 common pathogens in hospitalized children with ARIs after the relaxation of COVID-19 NPIs in Quzhou, China from May 1 to October 31, 2023. In this specific setting, Mp, RSV and HRV were identified as the top three monoinfection pathogens, with Mp + HRV being the most common coinfection combination. Notably, the risk of Mp infection was observed to increase with age, highlighting a potential regional public health concern. These findings underscore the importance of ongoing regional pathogen surveillance, particularly for Mp, to guide local public health responses.
