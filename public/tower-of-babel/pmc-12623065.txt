Capturing surgical site infection after hip fracture surgery
Authors: James Masters, David Metcalfe, Matthew L Costa, Andrew Judge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo evaluate the incidence, timing, and capture of surgical site infections (SSIs) following hip fracture surgery using routinely collected primary and secondary care datasets, and to assess the limitations of using such resources to identify SSIs.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using linked Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) and Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) data from 1999 to 2013. Patients with a hip fracture were identified and compared with age- and sex-matched controls. SSIs were defined using diagnostic codes and categorized as ‘definite’ or ‘possible’. Timing of diagnosis was evaluated, and risk factors for developing infection assessed, using Cox regression.ResultsAmong 13,920 hip fracture patients, 192 developed definite a SSI (n = 192, 2.4%) or possible SSIs (n = 15, 0.1%). Most infections were recorded in CPRD, with limited overlap between CPRD and HES. Median time to diagnosis was 130 days, with 43% of cases identified more than 90 days postoperatively. Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥ 3 was significantly associated with increased risk. Reoperation for infection occurred in 1.2% of cases (n = 37).ConclusionRoutinely coded data sources likely under-capture SSIs after hip fracture surgery. Poor agreement between primary and secondary care records, combined with delayed identification, limits the utility of routinely collected administrative datasets to accurately identify postoperative infection.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2025;6(11):1479–1486.Keywords: Trauma, Hip fracture, Infection, Epidemiology, hip fracture surgery, surgical site infections (SSIs), infections, revision surgery, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), postoperative infection, retrospective cohort study, Cox regression, arthroplasty

Introduction

Hip fractures represent a significant public health concern among older adults due to the associated morbidity, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare costs.1-3 The incidence of hip fractures is expected to rise globally due to an ageing population, with projections estimating a dramatic increase over the coming decades.4 Hip fractures almost always require surgical intervention, which is effective at restoring mobility, but carries the risk of postoperative complications, such as surgical site infections (SSIs).5

SSIs represent a substantial and underappreciated burden among patients undergoing hip fracture surgery.5 The hip fracture population is typically older, frail, and living with multiple comorbidities, which increases the risk of postoperative complications such as infection.6 SSIs can be devastating in this context, contributing to poor functional recovery, prolonged hospitalization, and increased likelihood of readmission or long-term care placement.7-11 These infections are also associated with higher short- and long-term mortality rates, particularly in the case of deep or periprosthetic joint infections.12 From a health system perspective, SSIs drive substantial costs through extended inpatient care, reoperations, complex wound management, and the need for prolonged rehabilitation and social support.13 Moreover, treatment often requires extended courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics, which may increase the risk of antimicrobial resistance and Clostridioides difficile infection, raising further concerns around antimicrobial stewardship.14

Despite their importance, SSIs in hip fracture patients are inconsistently captured and likely under-reported,5 limiting opportunities for surveillance and quality improvement. Enhanced understanding of the incidence, risk factors, and outcomes associated with SSIs in this vulnerable group is essential to inform prevention strategies, optimize antibiotic use, and reduce both patient harm and healthcare burden.

Detecting infections following surgery for fragility fracture is particularly difficult due to the diverse clinical presentations and the potential for delayed diagnoses.5 Many SSIs may not be identified during the initial hospital admission, instead manifesting weeks or months later. Traditional surveillance methods, which rely on microbiological confirmation or clinician-reported diagnoses, may miss cases that are diagnosed and managed in outpatient or community settings.6,15 Administrative datasets provide a potential solution by enabling large-scale observation of infection trends, treatment patterns, and patient outcomes across different healthcare settings.16 However, it is unknown whether such datasets are suitable for identifying SSIs in the hip fracture population.17,18

This study aimed to evaluate the incidence of SSIs using linked national primary and secondary care datasets, and to assess their utility as a tool for SSI surveillance.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study using linked primary and secondary care datasets: Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) GOLD and Hospital Episode Statistics (HES).

Data sources

CPRD GOLD contains anonymized primary care records from approximately 7% of general practices across the UK.19 Practices contributing to the database are broadly representative of the national population. Patient records include diagnoses, prescriptions, investigations, and other healthcare events recorded in primary care. Diagnoses are coded using the Read code system.20

Although CPRD primarily captures primary care data, information from hospital care—such as discharge diagnoses and procedures—may also be incorporated into patient records following communication between secondary and primary care. Prescription data are coded within CPRD GOLD using the British National Formulary (BNF) classification system.21

HES admitted patient care data provide detailed administrative data on secondary care episodes in England, including inpatient admissions. Diagnoses are recorded using the International Classification of Diseases of the World Health Organization, 10th revision (ICD-10),22 and procedures using the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Surgical Operations and Procedures, fourth revision (OPCS-4).23 Each entry includes a date stamp allowing temporal analysis of care episodes.

Cohort definition

Patients with hip fracture were identified in CPRD GOLD using diagnostic Read codes as has been previously described (Supplementary Material).20 Each patient was required to have at least one hip fracture diagnosis recorded. Controls were selected from CPRD and matched on age and sex from among patients with no coded history of hip fracture in CPRD.

A subset of the cases identified in CPRD had a corresponding hip fracture episode captured in HES. Linkage was possible only for patients who were treated in England and registered with practices that had opted into data linkage. Some individuals with available HES data did not have a hip fracture episode recorded in HES, despite being identified in CPRD. Consequently, analyses using the combined datasets were restricted to CPRD cases with a confirmed hip fracture episode recorded in HES, or where linked HES data were available.

Cohort characteristics

A total of 13,920 hip fracture cases and 26,729 matched controls were included in the study. The mean age of hip fracture patients was 81.3 years (SD 10.2), and the majority were female (75.2%), comparable to the control group (mean age 80.8 years, 75.3% female). A CCI score of ≥ 3 was observed in 4.6% of cases, compared with 3.4% among controls (Table I).

Diagnostic coding

Diagnosis and procedure codes were identified using curated code lists specific to each data source. These were either adopted from existing studies or developed specifically for this analysis. Where new code lists were created—primarily for SSIs and related wound complications—two clinicians (DM, JM) independently generated lists based on clinical relevance and coding consistency. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion, with arbitration by a senior clinician (MLC).

In CPRD, dates reflect the time of entry into the primary care record, which may lag behind the clinical diagnosis, especially for postoperative complications that require hospital follow-up and transcription of discharge summaries or letters.

Surgical site infection

SSI was defined using two sets of code lists in both CPRD (Read codes) and HES (ICD-10 codes): a narrow list containing specific infection codes, and a broader list incorporating wound complications such as dehiscence, haematoma, and wound breakdown (Supplementary Material). A combined list was used to estimate the upper bound (‘worst-case scenario’) incidence of SSI. The ICD-based list was informed by a prior published study using HES data.16

SSI case definitions

SSI cases were identified based on diagnostic codes recorded in CPRD and HES. A 120-day postoperative window was applied to CPRD diagnoses, and a 365-day window to HES, due to the greater specificity of ICD-10 codes for prosthetic-related infection. Based on the coding detail, SSI cases were classified as ‘definite SSI’ (patients with a specific diagnostic code indicating SSI) and ‘possible SSI’ (patients with codes suggestive of wound-related complications, such as wound dehiscence or haematoma, but without an explicity SSI diagnosis).

Identification of reoperation using HES data

Surgical intervention, alongside systemic antibiotic therapy, is central to the management of orthopaedic infection. This may involve limited procedures, such as washout and debridement, or more extensive revision surgery involving removal or replacement of implants. Reoperation events were identified within the HES dataset using OPCS-4 procedure codes, with three code lists developed to capture revisions following internal fixation or arthroplasty, as well as infection-related procedures such as debridement. The arthroplasty revision codes were derived from the National Joint Registry OPCS-4 list, while fixation and infection-related codes were compiled de novo. Given the potential for crossover between surgical strategies—such as conversion from fixation to arthroplasty—revision was defined broadly. Procedural data were extracted from HES only. Each patient was included only once, regardless of the number of reoperations. Laterality was confirmed for a subset by linking with the index procedure code.

Application of diagnostic codes

To improve attribution of infection to the hip fracture event, all codes were restricted to the timeframes defined above. In analyses using combined datasets, the denominator was limited to patients with a confirmed hip fracture episode in HES. The numerator included all SSI events identified in HES, as well as those from CPRD among patients with HES linkage eligibility.

Governance and legal framework

No information that can identify a patient is ever sent to CPRD from the contributing GP practices. Because a patient cannot be identified from data a GP practice sends to CPRD, the GP practice does not need to seek a patient’s consent to share data with CPRD.24 CPRD has obtained ethical approval from a National Research Ethics Service Committee (NRES) for all purely observational research using anonymized CPRD data; namely, studies which do not include patient involvement. The study has been approved by ISAC (Independent Scientific Advisory Committee) for MHRA Database Research) (protocol number 13_069RA2). All data were fully anonymized before being accessed for this study by the authors. The authors had no access to information that could identify individual participants.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline patient characteristics and SSI incidence. Proportions are reported with 95% CIs.

Associations with SSI were evaluated by fitting survival models. Time to first SSI was the primary outcome, with Cox proportional hazards regression used to estimate hazard ratios. SSI events were defined as above. The index date was the date of first recorded hip fracture. Follow-up continued until the earliest event of: death, loss to follow-up, or 365 days post-fracture. Death was treated as a censoring event under the assumption that it was non-informative.

Candidate covariates were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature, and biological plausibility. To minimize the risk of overfitting, variables were limited according to the ‘ten events per variable’ rule. The final model included age, sex, smoking status, BMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI),25 and antibiotic use prior to admission. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. All analyses were conducted using Stata 15 (StataCorp, USA).

Surgical site infection rates

Across both CPRD and HES datasets, 192 cases (2.4%) satisfied the prespecified criteria for a definite SSI following hip fracture surgery (Table III). Of these, 152 cases (1.1%) were identified in CPRD, and 48 cases (0.8%) in HES. When including possible SSIs – defined as wound complications or related proxy indicators – an additional 15 new cases were identified, bringing the upper bound of the estimated total SSI incidence to 2.6% (n = 207). Most SSIs were captured in CPRD, although HES contributed additional diagnoses. These outcomes could be combined in two ways. First, SSI cases identified in HES could be added to those identified in CPRD for the CPRD-defined hip fracture cohort. Alternatively, SSI cases identified in CPRD could be added to those identified in HES for the HES-defined hip fracture cohort, provided the original case definition from HES still applied. In both approaches, overlapping cases were removed, and the original time window for defining SSIs was maintained.

Timing of infection diagnosis

The median time to SSI diagnosis among definite cases was 130 days (IQR 100 to 180) across both data sources (Table IV). However, CPRD diagnoses occurred earlier (median 119 days (IQR 90 to 140)) than those recorded in HES (median 186 days (IQR 150 to 220)). One-quarter of SSIs were diagnosed within 30 days postoperatively (25.0%, n = 48), with a further 35.9% (n = 69) diagnosed between 31 and 90 days. Notably, 43.2% (n = 83) of definite SSIs were diagnosed more than 90 days after surgery, which highlights the importance of extended follow-up when using routine data to ascertain postoperative infection outcomes.

SSI risk factors

Within multivariable Cox regression analysis, patients with a CCI of > 3 had a significantly increased risk of SSI compared to those with no comorbidities (hazard ratio (HR) 2.37; 95% CI 1.14 to 4.93; p = 0.021) (Table V). Other factors, including prior chest or urinary tract infection, sex, age, antibiotic use before admission, smoking status, and BMI, were not significantly associated with SSI risk in this model. While smoking and increasing age showed elevated point estimates, the CIs were wide and compatible with there being no significant difference.

Reoperation following hip fracture surgery

Among the 6,239 patients in the cohort, 249 unique cases (4.0%) underwent at least one further surgical procedure recorded in HES, comprising revision arthroplasty (n = 151, 2.4%), revision fixation (n = 100, 1.6%), and non-revision infection-related procedures (n = 20, 0.3%) (Table VI). Of these reoperations, 24 (9.6%) were coded with a SSI diagnosis in HES, and 15 (6.0%) in CPRD. There was limited overlap in infection recording between datasets, with only two patients identified in both. When combining sources, 35 reoperations (14.9%) were associated with an infection diagnosis in either HES or CPRD. Infection was most frequently recorded among those undergoing non-revision further surgery (n = 9, 45.0%), followed by revision fixation (n = 17, 17.0%) and revision arthroplasty (n = 22, 14.6%). These findings highlight both the heterogeneity of reoperation procedures and the variability in how infection is captured across routine data sources.

Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort study using linked primary (CPRD) and secondary (HES) care data, we found that 2.4% (n = 192) of patients who underwent hip fracture surgery were diagnosed with a SSI. The majority were recorded in CPRD, with surprisingly little overlap between the two datasets. Notably, 43% of infections were diagnosed more than 90 days postoperatively, and higher comorbidity burden (CCI ≥ 3) was associated with a significantly increased risk of SSI. Despite concerns that administrative data may underestimate complications, our findings are consistent with prior literature and provide insight into real-world infection patterns.

The headline SSI rate of 2.4% aligns with prior UK estimates, though it may still represent an underestimation.5,6,15 Studies assessing the accuracy of ICD-10 coding in HES have reported a median diagnostic accuracy of 83.2%, with considerable variation (IQR 67.3% to 92.1%).26 However, most of these studies focus on primary diagnoses rather than postoperative complications. The limited overlap between CPRD and HES diagnoses in our study—only 11 patients were identified in both datasets—suggests poor reciprocal coding between care settings. While reciprocal documentation between primary and secondary care is expected, this was observed inconsistently, likely reflecting incomplete outpatient coding or limitations in data linkage. In the case of SSI, diagnostic confirmation and definitive management are expected to be undertaken in secondary care. It is therefore reasonable to expect that any SSI recorded in primary care would also be reflected in hospital records. Discrepancies may indicate under-recording in routine secondary care datasets. The increased recording of SSIs in CPRD compared with HES has a number of possible explanations. First, hospital diagnoses are usually communicated to general practitioners (GPs) by way of a discharge summary or clinic letter and should then be coded into the primary care record. However, a diagnosis made in primary care will not always be communicated to hospital and – even if it is – would not be coded as part of a hospital admission episode while the patient is still in the community. Second, this could reflect overdiagnosis of SSIs in primary care as GPs will not have equivalent access to diagnostic adjuncts, such as inflammatory markers. Third, this discrepancy may identify a large burden of postoperative work falling on GP services that goes unnoticed by those working in secondary care. This should be taken into consideration by future work trying to understand the true health and social care burden of SSIs.

Our SSI incidence findings are broadly consistent with prior studies using administrative data.16 For instance, Jen et al16 reported a SSI rate of 3.3% using HES data, though their case definition included only hemiarthroplasties and applied no time restrictions. In contrast, our study considered multiple surgical procedures and imposed a defined follow-up window. Jettoo and James27 also analyzed HES data for intracapsular fractures treated with sliding hip screw or cannulated screws, and reported a SSI rate of 0.41%, relatively comparable to our HES-derived estimate of 0.85%. Together, these comparisons illustrate the variability in SSI estimates depending on definitions, procedures studied, and surveillance periods.

The WHiTE cohort study, which studied 24,523 consecutive patients with hip fractures, reported a 3.4% rate of wound infection within 120 days—somewhat higher than our 2.4% estimate.28 This difference may reflect the WHiTE study’s prospective design and clinical follow-up, allowing for more complete capture of postoperative complications, particularly those diagnosed or managed in outpatient settings. Our findings, which rely on routine administrative coding, likely reflect a more conservative estimate and underscore the known underestimation challenges associated with secondary data use.

In our cohort of 6,239 patients, 249 (4.0%) underwent at least one further surgical procedure within 365 days as recorded in HES. This reoperation rate is lower than that reported in other relevant cohort studies. For example, Wolf et al29 described a 12-month reoperation rate of 7% in patients with femoral neck fractures, varying by primary surgical fracture type and treatment (internal fixation or arthroplasty). Similar work on neck fractures by Labmayr et al30 described reoperation rates between 5% and 9% again depending on initial fracture pattern and procedure. Similar reoperation rates in trochanteric fracture groups of 6% have also been reported.31

The relatively low rate of reoperation observed in this study may partly reflect the limitations of relying solely on procedural codes which are explicitly linked to revision surgery, but again likely reflects an underestimation, particularly as the case definition of reoperation here was very broad and included non-revision codes relevant to infection. In clinical practice, especially in frail older adults, treatment for suspected infection may not always involve formal revision. Instead, limited interventions such as washout or debridement may be preferred, particularly when the risks of major surgery outweigh the benefits. These procedures are often inconsistently captured in administrative datasets, or may not be coded as ‘revision’ despite representing meaningful reoperation events for patients.

Key strengths of this study include its large sample size, national scope, and use of linked primary and secondary care datasets, which allowed for extended follow-up and identification of SSIs occurring outside the inpatient setting. Our approach also leveraged a broad case definition, capturing both definite and possible SSIs.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively low proportion of CPRD cases with linked HES data (57.4%) and confirmed hip fracture codes in HES (44.8%) is consistent with CPRD documentation indicating that around 60% of CPRD GOLD patients had HES linkage at the time. The patients reflected in these practices are recognized to be representative of the wider UK population.32 In addition, the use of CPRD-HES linkage—rather than Office for National Statistics or other national-level datasets—may also contribute to differential levels of linkage seen in other study datasets.33,34

Case estimation may be incomplete due to inaccuracies in coding, especially in outpatient settings where coders rely on unstructured clinical notes. The diagnosis of SSI is not always clear, and it is possible that a provisional diagnosis of SSI could be coded into the health record before it subsequently becomes clear that there was no infection. The low number of events in our cohort limited the precision of our multivariable model, with only comorbidity score showing a statistically significant association. Although other covariates such as smoking and obesity are recognized risk factors, our study may have lacked sufficient power to detect these effects.

Additionally, data from CPRD and HES do not consistently capture outpatient diagnoses or episodes managed entirely in the community.35 While some infections may be managed in primary care without hospital re-presentation, our findings cannot confirm or quantify this pattern. Furthermore, financial incentives introduced part-way through the study period and national surveillance initiatives may have influenced coding behaviour over time in ways that are difficult to disentangle.

Our findings have potential implications for both surveillance and clinical practice. Relying solely on inpatient diagnoses and readmissions – risks underestimating the true burden of SSI following hip fracture surgery – particularly given the delayed timing of many diagnoses.

From a research perspective, the results reinforce the value of linking datasets to better capture postoperative complications. Routine data can support large-scale studies and registry-based trial designs, but the limitations of administrative coding—especially for conditions like SSI with variable diagnostic certainty—must be recognized. Prospectively collected data often capture higher rates of wound-related complications, suggesting that real-world infection rates may be under-reported when relying on routinely collected health data.

The differences between prospective and administrative data point to the strengths of each approach. A combined strategy, integrating clinical follow-up with routine sources, is likely to offer the most accurate view of postoperative outcomes. As healthcare delivery shifts towards shorter stays and more community-based care, it is increasingly important that surveillance systems evolve to avoid missing complications due to incomplete or siloed coding across care settings.

In conclusion, SSI following hip fracture surgery is a relatively uncommon but very serious complication. The true incidence of SSI may be underestimated in current surveillance systems. The use of linked CPRD and HES data improves detection compared to single data source strategies, but reveals discrepancies in recording across care settings. High comorbidity is a significant risk factor, and the timing of diagnosis suggests that extended follow-up is necessary to capture the full burden. Although administrative data offer opportunities for scalable infection surveillance, they must be interpreted with awareness of its limitations. Closer integration of primary and secondary care records, along with efforts to improve coding accuracy, will be essential for advancing the use of routine data in surgical outcomes research.

Future research should focus on integrating administrative data with clinical registries, microbiological results, and standardized reporting frameworks. Enhancing surveillance accuracy will help refine risk stratification, improve early detection, and guide interventions to reduce SSIs and associated complications in hip fracture patients. Strengthening data quality and validation efforts will ensure that administrative datasets can be leveraged more effectively for postoperative infection surveillance and quality improvement in orthopaedic care.

Take home message

- Surgical site infection (SSI) after hip fracture surgery is challenging to identify due to dispersed care across hospital and community settings.

- Linked hospital and primary care administrative data can detect many SSI cases and offer insight into the timing of diagnosis, often occurring weeks after surgery.

- While this approach shows promise for large-scale surveillance, further validation is needed before routine data can reliably support outcome assessment in clinical trials.
