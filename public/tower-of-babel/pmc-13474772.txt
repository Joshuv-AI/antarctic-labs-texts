Perception of physiotherapy researchers about patient and public involvement in health research design and planning in Nigeria
Authors: Henrietta O Fawole, Perpetua O Osuji, Opeyemi A Idowu, Sochima J Obiekwe, Francis O Kolawole, Kadiree E Fatai, Anita C Okafor, Grace A Aku-Dania, Chukwuekwu D Bolu, Tolulope Adeniji, Olayinka Akinrolie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient and public involvement (PPI) enhances the efficiency, reliability, and relevance of research design. Although evidence supports its value, PPI remains underdeveloped in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly Nigeria. This study explored physiotherapy researchers’ perceptions of PPI and identified potential training needs for PPI implementation in health research in Nigeria.MethodsThis study included a convenience sample of physiotherapy researchers in Nigeria who met the eligibility criteria. This cross-sectional descriptive study was mapped using the INVOLVE framework and data on socio-demographics, perception of PPI and PPI training needs were collected using an online survey.ResultsOut of 58 participants invited to this study, 50 (mean age = 40.85 ± 8.86 years; 64% male and 82% full-time physiotherapy educators) completed the online survey. The result showed participants favoured both collaborative and consultative approaches to PPI. Majority of the study participants agreed that PPI concepts were important in health research, particularly in identifying research gaps and setting priorities (84%), as well as helping to identify appropriate outcomes (94%) and recognising PPI as integral to health system improvement (100%). However, participants disagreed that PPI should be involved in data analysis (64%), report writing (60%) and disseminating results (44%). Eight-two percent of the participants expressed the need for training support in implementing PPI while the majority (98%) indicated a willingness to participate in relevant training programmes.ConclusionsPhysiotherapy researchers in Nigeria demonstrated promising baseline information on the perception on PPI particularly in research priority setting, with unanimous agreement for both consultative and collaborative approaches to PPI in health research. However, uncertainty remains regarding its role in analysis, reporting, and methodological specifics. The findings underscore the need for structured training and accessible support resources to enhance the effective implementation of PPI in health research within Nigeria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14925-0.

Background

Patient and public involvement (PPI) is described as research being carried out ‘with’ or ‘by’ patients or public rather than ‘to’ ‘about’ or ‘for’ them [1]. PPI is an evolving valuable tool in health research with the aim of improving the quality and relevance of research for the patients and wider public [1]. It represents a relatively new skill for many researchers, including both early-career and established professionals. Over the last decade, PPI has demonstrated growth in health research leading to great improvement of research projects [2]. The concept of PPI in the conduct of research is common and recommended in developed countries such as Canada, United States, Australia, and United Kingdom as a requirement for grants and research funding [3].

Several frameworks and conceptual models have been developed to support the use of PPI in health research, including approaches for assessing its quality and impact [4–7]. However, these models have also been criticised for failing to fully capture the diverse and complex nature of PPI [8]. Conversely, scholars have argued that the experiential knowledge of patients and the public is itself a critical form of expertise, offering insights into complex health issues and helping to create more inclusive and practical solutions that strengthen both health practice and research [9]. Nonetheless, partnership-focused frameworks, such as the INVOLVE approach, were explicitly developed to support collaborative and consultative research by strengthening partnerships between researchers and members of the public, enabling the assessment of key dimensions of partnership success while also recognising experiential knowledge as an important source of insight in research [1, 6, 7].

PPI involves patients and the public at every stage of research cycle, from the identification and prioritization of research problems to research commissioning, design and management, and the dissemination of research and services that affect patients and members of the public [10]. The impact of such involvement in making clinical trials more patient-centred has been documented in a systematic review [11]. This approach helps evaluate health conditions, interventions, medications and treatment methods from the patient and public perspectives [10]. PPI encompasses various levels of engagement, including informing, involving, consulting and empowering patients and public [12]. Implementation of PPI enhances the efficiency, reliability and relevance of research design to and for the public [13] and has been shown to significantly impact health practices [14]. This includes the development of accessible outputs, such as plain-language summaries, which are crucial for dissemination [15].

Globally, PPI is gaining recognition, but remains relatively underdeveloped in Africa, especially sub-Saharan Africa [2]. Bedwell and Lavender (2020) highlight the need to strengthen research through PPI in this region [2], yet its use in research design across Africa, including Nigeria, is minimal. The lack of implementation of PPI in Africa may stem from insufficient awareness and knowledge, lack of documentation, and perceived lack of relevance of PPI studies making it difficult to enforce research findings in terms of best health practice.

PPI guides researchers in exploring what is relevant to patients and population [16], for instance, in ensuring that the outcomes measured in research are truly important to those living with a health condition [17, 18] given that researchers can sometimes make assumptions that are biased due to lack of experiential knowledge, resulting in findings being missed or considered as unimportant [16]. Some researchers perceive PPI as a time-consuming approach that increases workload whilst others consider PPI, as a threat to the scientific potency of their research works [10]. A study in Canada highlighted the lack of training of researchers, insufficient report of evidence to inform training, funding, time, compensation and logistics, recruitment, and motivation at both the patient and researcher level, and the necessary skills needed for researchers to engage in PPI as barriers hindering PPI [12]. To be at par with developed countries on good research implementation, it is essential to adhere to good principles in research conduct and to be aware of these principles [19]. To the best of the researchers’ knowledge, there is no report on the perception of PPI in research design planning among physiotherapy researchers in Nigeria. Physiotherapy is a field of allied health care in which evidence based patient centred practices are strongly emphasised. Guided by feasibility considerations and the need to generate baseline descriptive PPI evidence within physiotherapy, this study aims to investigate the perception of PPI among physiotherapy researchers in Nigeria and may serve as a starting point to understanding perception of PPI amongst other similar health care professionals in Nigeria.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional descriptive study conducted between March and October 2022 in two waves (i.e., March and October waves). We obtained ethics approval from Ethics Research Committee of College of Medical Sciences, University of Benin (CMS/REC/2022/316).

Participants and eligibility criteria

Participants were included in this study if they were physiotherapists: licensed by the Medical Rehabilitation Therapists Board (MRTB) of Nigeria, worked either as a university educator and/or clinical educator, and spent at least 10% of their working time conducting health research in Nigeria. Physiotherapy researchers who were graduate assistants with less than three years of research experience, who were non-faculty members, and clinicians without affiliation to a private or public training institution were excluded from this study.

A convenience sampling technique was employed to target physiotherapy researchers with affiliations to university institutions offering physiotherapy training and data was collected from physiotherapy researchers who were willing to participate in the study.

Sample size calculation

The estimated population size of educators in Nigeria was 110 as at March 2022 (WhatsApp© Physiotherapy Educators’ Group). With 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error and 50% response distribution, a sample size of 86 was required for this study. The sample size calculator can be found at: http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html. However, the estimated sample size was not met due to prolonged strike in Nigerian universities at the time of data collection. Thus, a total of 58 participants were recruited for this study.

Instruments

The research instrument used in this study was adapted from two studies conducted earlier in Canada [12] and Ireland [20]. This adapted questionnaire assessed perceptions of PPI and training needs among physiotherapy researchers (Appendix 1). The INVOLVE framework was used in the development of questionnaire and interpretation [7, 21], drawing on collaborative (i.e., questions 4, 5, 7, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18), consultative (i.e., questions 8, 10, 11, 12, 17) and combined collaborative and consultative (i.e., questions 1, 2, 3, 6, 9) approaches. The process of adaptation comprised review of the research instrument to suit the aim of the study, consultation with experts, and modifications of questions [22]. Face and content validity were established by three independent physiotherapists who reviewed the questionnaire for adequate coverage of the constructs being assessed whilst the modified questionnaire items and feedback were integrated into the final version of the instrument (Appendix 2). For test-retest reliability, the questionnaire was self-administered on five physiotherapy researchers who were not part of the current study on two occasions, seven days apart. The responses during the first administration of the questionnaire were analysed to determine its internal consistency. Test–retest reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement [23]. The instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 [95% confidence interval: 0.49 to 0.98], and good test–retest reliability, with an ICC of 0.91 [95% confidence interval: 0.74 to 0.99]. The instrument contains 33 items divided into three sections: section A includes 12 questions on demographic characteristics; section B contains 18 questions on perception of PPI; section C contains 3 questions on training and support needs for PPI. The responses for perception of PPI were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree (5) to strongly disagree (1). Strongly agree, agree and somewhat agree were considered as agree while disagree and strongly disagree were deemed as disagree. This recategorisation was undertaken to improve interpretability and to address the small sample size and sparse distribution of responses across some categories [24].

Data collection

The data for this study was collected using the online survey. The link for the online questionnaire was sent to the Nigeria physiotherapists’ educators WhatsApp© platform and Heads of Department WhatsApp© platforms. The permissions of Nigeria Society of Physiotherapists (NSP) and Association of Clinical and Academic Physiotherapists of Nigeria (ACAPN) were sought prior to posting the online survey link on WhatsApp© platform for physiotherapy researchers in Nigeria. The responses were sent to the researcher’s email immediately after the participants completed the online questionnaire.

Data analysis

All data survey results were downloaded as raw data from Google Documents to Microsoft Excel 2010 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) was used to analyse data. Descriptive statistics of frequency and percentage were used to summarize the quantitative data. Open-ended questions were manually entered into a Microsoft Word document. The open-ended comments were analysed using counts and aggregated into themes.

Engagement of patient and public in interpretation of data and writing of lay summary

To facilitate meaningful interpretation of the study’s findings, we held a professional stakeholder engagement during the result interpretation phase. This stakeholder committee consisted of three physiotherapy educators representing the public, private, and clinical institutions, ensuring diverse perspectives. Their responsibilities included the evaluation of the methodology, the research findings, as well as the drafting of a lay summary for the study. To facilitate their involvement, a comprehensive document delineating the objectives of the stakeholder group, project guidelines, and the manuscript was sent via email. The professional stakeholder committee members were requested to communicate their feedback through email within a span of three days. Subsequently, a Google Meet session was arranged to engage in an in-depth discussion of their comments with the research authors. The stakeholder committee rendered constructive critiques on various dimensions.

The stakeholder committee suggested that a qualitative approach, as opposed to the cross-sectional design employed, could provide more profound insights into the perception of PPI among physiotherapy researchers in future studies. A stratified sampling approach was advocated as a more appropriate alternative to the convenience sampling technique utilized. Moreover, the committee also underscored the necessity of presenting PPI in accessible language prior to the introduction of the standard definition to enhance clarity and accessibility for a wider audience. The lay summary provided by the professional stakeholder committee was merged and revised by the research authors and re-sent to the committee members for final review. The feedback received was instrumental in refining the final lay summary.

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of 58 participants invited to this study, 50 completed the online survey. The mean age of participants was 40.85 ± 8.86 years. The majority of participants were male (64%), had Master’s degree (52%), were early-career researchers (48%), and were mostly full-time educators (82.0%) (see Table 1).

Perception of patient and public involvement

The majority of study participants agreed that PPI concepts are important part of health research, with 13 of the 18 questions (72%) reflecting this view (see Table 2). These concepts ranged from using PPI to identify research gaps, questions, and setting research priority (84%, n = 42) to PPI being an integral process in health research and for improvement of healthcare system (100%, n = 50). Furthermore, 70% (n = 35) of participants agreed that patients and the public should be involved in selecting outcome assessment instruments and reviewing research reports. More than half of the participants (66%, n = 33) agreed that funding agencies provide sufficient financial reimbursement for the involvement of patients and/or the public in health research. However, 64% (n = 32) and 60% (n = 30) of participants disagreed that patient and the public should be involved in data analysis and in writing research reports, respectively. Half of the study participants agreed that PPI should help with disseminating results (54%, n = 27) and producing lay summaries of scientific reports (56%, n = 28) (Table 2). The mapping of the results based on INVOLVE framework showed that participants favoured both collaborative and consultative approaches to PPI as presented in Appendix 3.

Perception of PPI among physiotherapy researchers

Needs for training and types of support for PPI among physiotherapy researchers

Most participants reported the need for support in involving patients and the public in health research (82.0%, n = 41). Participants indicated their interest to participate in training if made available (98%, n = 49). Researchers highlighted the need for a training workshop (72%, n = 36), access to a resource such as website (58%, n = 29) and online module (48%, n = 24). The least reported types of support comprised support to help find people to engage in the research process (34%, n = 17) and manual/guidebook (30%, n = 15).

Discussion

This study provides the first baseline evidence of physiotherapy researchers’ perception of patient and public involvement (PPI) in health research within Nigeria. While PPI is increasingly recognised and embedded in research practice in high-income countries, such as Canada, the United Kingdom and Ireland [2, 12, 20], it remains at an emergent stage in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Our findings therefore represent an important step in understanding the perception and training needs of Nigerian physiotherapy researchers, offering a foundation for future implementation and capacity-building initiatives [25].

Overall, participants demonstrated a strong appreciation for the value of PPI in identifying research priorities and improving the health system, with unanimous agreement that PPI is both an integral process in health research and essential for healthcare system improvement. This aligns with international findings, where researchers often recognise the conceptual importance of PPI even in contexts where it is not yet systematically implemented [12]. Notably, the substantial proportion of respondents supporting PPI in research priority setting aligns with the results reported by O’Shea et al. [20], who observed comparable trends of acknowledgement among health economists in Ireland. Such findings suggest that, while structural enablers differ across contexts, there may be a consistent baseline understanding of PPI’s potential role across disciplines and regions.

Despite this encouraging recognition, important gaps were identified in the perceived role of patients and the public in data analysis and report writing. More than 60% of participants disagreed that PPI should extend into data analysis and report writing phases, highlighting persistent scepticism about the capacity of non-academic stakeholders to contribute meaningfully to technical aspects of research. This perception has also been reported in high-income settings, where concerns about scientific rigour and time burden often limit deeper involvement [26]. Such attitudes are reflected in international surveys of researchers’ experiences with PPI [25]. In the present study, this finding may partly reflect the predominance of quantitative research traditions among physiotherapy researchers in Nigeria, where patients and the public are less commonly involved in interpretation stages of research. Although qualitative approaches may sometimes incorporate participant feedback through techniques such as member checking, these processes do not necessarily constitute collaborative patient and public involvement in data analysis. However, emerging literature shows that PPI partners can provide unique experiential insights that strengthen interpretation, validate findings, and enhance the relevance of outputs [27]. This has been demonstrated even in complex methodological areas such as systematic reviews and other forms of evidence synthesis [28]. Interestingly, a more moderate gap was also evident in relation to outcome measurement, with only 70% agreeing that patients and the public should comment on instrumentation used to measure outcomes. This suggests a broader hesitation regarding the involvement of lay stakeholders in methodological specifics, not only in the final stages of research.

Using the INVOLVE framework as an interpretive lens [1], this study’s findings show that majority of physiotherapy researchers predominantly support consultative and collaborative approaches to PPI in health research. Most participants agreed to involving patients and public in setting research priorities, identifying research questions and outcomes, participant recruitment and data collection. However, respondents were less supportive of involving patients and the public in more technical and critical stages of the research process such as in data analysis and report writing. In addition, about 70% of the physiotherapy researchers supported public involvement in reviewing research reports rather than collaborating in writing them (38%), while only 56% agreed that patients and the public should be involved in producing lay summaries of scientific reports. These findings suggest that PPI was more readily acceptable within consultative and collaborative approaches; however, support for collaboration appeared to be stronger in the earlier and less technical stages of the research process than in its more technical phases. This pattern is consistent with previous studies showing that public involvement in analytically complex phases of research is less well established and may require additional support, guidance, and role clarification for both researchers and public contributors [29, 30]. In the Nigerian context, this pattern may likely reflect both limited exposures to established PPI frameworks and the contextual hierarchical dynamics between researchers and participants which will require both cultural and educational shifts to overcome [31].

Furthermore, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the broader Nigerian research context. In comparison with many high-income countries, where PPI is supported by established frameworks, funding structures, and institutional expectations, PPI in low-income settings (e.g., Nigeria) is still emerging. As a result, several factors such as limited resource allocation, lack of formal structures or policies for participatory research, and hierarchical researcher–participant relationships may shape how public involvement is viewed within the research process [2, 31–33]. This may help explain why physiotherapy researchers showed stronger support for PPI in principle than for involvement in more technical stages of research.

Another notable finding relates to perceptions of funding. A majority of participants (66%) believed that funding agencies provide sufficient financial reimbursement for PPI. However, in LMICs such as Nigeria, this perception does not reflect on how research is funded [34]. Limited research funding across many African settings may pose a significant challenge to the implementation of PPI [28]. Effective PPI implementation often require adequate reimbursement of PPI participants and providing training and support for the public [35]. However, barriers including a lack of trained PPI personnel, and entrenched paternalistic healthcare systems which marginalises patient voices may outweigh the funding realities [36–38]. Thus, addressing these barriers should be prioritised. Future research should explore this perception in more depth, as funding and resource availability are frequently identified as barriers to effective PPI in LMICs.

The strong willingness of participants (98%) to engage in training is another important finding. Similar needs were documented among Manitoba researchers in Canada [12], who expressed uncertainty about how to operationalise PPI despite positive attitudes. In our study, physiotherapy researchers highlighted workshops, online modules, and access to digital resources as preferred supports. The high demand for workshops (72%) and access to online resources (58%), combined with the call for advisory guidance (36%), suggests that researchers are seeking not only foundational knowledge but also ongoing practical support to sustain PPI activities. Training in specific skills, like co-creating plain-language summaries, can also advance goals of equity and accessibility in research [39]. This demonstrates an appetite for structured learning opportunities and points to feasible strategies for professional associations, universities, and regulatory bodies to incorporate PPI into continuing professional development. Embedding these training opportunities into existing physiotherapy curricula would also help build capacity among early-career researchers, reducing future knowledge gaps.

A key strength of this study is that it provides the first discipline-specific evidence on physiotherapy researchers’ perceptions of PPI in Nigeria and most likely in sub-Saharan Africa where comparable literature remains limited and where most existing works have focused on broader conceptual or community engagement issues rather than patients and public perspectives involvement in health research. In addition, our study heeds to the call of Bedwell & Lavender [2], which highlighted the importance of implementing PPI in Sub-Saharan Africa. However, these findings should be considered in light of the limitations of our study design. First, the sample size for the reliability testing and the study was smaller than anticipated due to a prolonged strike in Nigerian universities, and the use of convenience sampling may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Participants were recruited through professional WhatsApp groups, particularly those involving physiotherapy educators, which may have increased the likelihood of recruiting academics while excluding clinicians without academic affiliations. As a result, the findings presented in this study may reflect the views of academically inclined physiotherapists and should be interpreted with caution. Second, because the data were based on self-reported measures, responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias, with participants potentially expressing more favourable views towards PPI than they may hold or apply in practice. Third, based on the recategorization of the 5-point Likert scale into agree/disagree categories, fine nuances from patient’s data may oversimplified and meaningful variation may have been missed. Also, our analysis is limited to descriptive statistics; we could not perform other inferential statistics due to the small sample size. Nonetheless, as a descriptive baseline study, the purpose was not to establish correlational relationships but to provide a preliminary exploratory finding on how Nigerian physiotherapy researchers currently perceive PPI. A further limitation of this study is that the stakeholder engagement component involved only professionals and did not include patients, carers, or members of the public. Therefore, the input obtained may reflect professional perspectives, in line with the study’s focus. However, the results highlight both opportunities and barriers that future qualitative studies, such as semi-structured interviews and focus groups, can explore in more depth. Such work will be crucial for unpacking the cultural, institutional, and policy factors that shape PPI in Nigeria and informing strategies for sustainable integration. The findings of this study have implications for institutional PPI policy. In resource-limited settings where PPI is still emerging, universities, ethics committees, and funding bodies may need to set clearer expectations for PPI within research training, grant development, and research governance processes. In this regard, an African contextualised guideline similar to United Kingdom National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) guideline [40], may provide a useful model that can be adopted in the African settings. In the Nigerian context, adapting such guidance within physiotherapy curricula and continuing professional development may help strengthen institutional readiness for meaningful PPI implementation. Equally, offering training to study participants based on PPI methodology prior to engaging in PPI could enhance confidence in some technical aspects.

In summary, physiotherapy researchers in Nigeria demonstrated promising baseline information on the perception of PPI, particularly in research priority setting, with unanimous agreement for both consultative and collaborative approaches to PPI in health research. However, uncertainty remains regarding the roles of patients and public in analysing, reporting, and methodological specifics. By addressing training needs and embedding PPI concepts into educational and professional frameworks, Nigeria has the opportunity to build capacity that not only strengthens health research but also ensures its relevance to patients and the public. These findings, when taken alongside comparable baseline audits from other contexts reinforce the global relevance of documenting and responding to researchers’ experiences of PPI at early stages of adoption.
