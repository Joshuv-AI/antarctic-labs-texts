Emergency triage in coastal Kenya: a survey of public Emergency Departments
Authors: Gideon Loevinsohn, Wambui Muiruri, Elwyn Chondo, Arthur Nderitu, Mercy Bruba, Ahmed Adam, Justin Machu, Josefridah Nyavula Ngowa, Samuel Mwakai, Martha Oguna, Dickson Okello, Zachary Gross, Adam R. Aluisio, Benjamin Wachira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTriage is a cornerstone of emergency care, with profound impacts on care prioritization and mortality. However, implementation of structured triage systems across sub-Saharan Africa has been inconsistent, hindered by context-specific challenges. Little is known about the landscape of triage in public emergency departments (EDs) in Kenya. We conducted a cross-sectional survey to characterize triage infrastructure, practices, and staff perceptions across public EDs in coastal Kenya.MethodsIn February 2026, trained study staff administered a standardized questionnaire to clinical leaders at all ten public EDs in Kilifi, Kwale, and Mombasa counties. The survey included items on triage infrastructure, staffing, equipment, procedures, and staff perceptions. Data were analyzed descriptively.ResultsNine of ten public EDs (90%) reported performing triage, yet only two (22%) used standardized tools or written protocols, with the remainder relying on clinical judgment. Among the nine EDs performing triage, seven (78%) had a dedicated triage area and seven or more (≥78%) reported adequate availability of equipment for vital sign measurement. Staffing was the most consistently identified barrier to effective triage, cited at eight of nine EDs (89%). Satisfaction with local triage functioning was mixed, with respondents at 67% of EDs (6/9) reporting dissatisfaction. Despite this, all respondents reported that triage as performed locally improves patient care and outcomes.ConclusionsTriage is commonly performed across public EDs in three coastal Kenyan counties but formalized in only a minority of facilities. Consistent clinical leader endorsement of the value of local triage, alongside existing infrastructure, suggests that the foundational elements for effective triage strengthening are present. Reported gaps in standardized tools, training, and nursing staffing represent an actionable agenda for local, county, and national stakeholders. Targeted and sustained efforts to address these gaps, supported by partners operating across and within Kenya's decentralized health care system, offer a significant opportunity to strengthen emergency triage systems and reduce preventable mortality.

Introduction

Conditions requiring emergency care are disproportionately prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa[1], accounting for over five million deaths annually from emergent traumatic, communicable, and non-communicable conditions [2] While recent years have seen significant investment in emergency care and rapid development of regional emergency care systems[3], key gaps remain including in access to hospitals[4], timely provision of emergency interventions within facilities [5,6], and allocation of often scarce clinical resources [7,8]

Emergency triage systems are a cornerstone of emergency care and serve to identify patients requiring immediate attention as well as those at high risk of clinical decompensation. Triage systems are key tools in allocating emergency clinical resources [9,10] Studies from across sub-Saharan Africa have reported reductions in mortality, improved identification of patients requiring advanced care, and reductions in waiting times for priority conditions with the introduction of systematic triage systems [9,11,12] However, implementation of triage processes has been inconsistent[8,13], hindered by gaps in staff training, absence of dedicated triage areas, and limited alignment of validated triage tools with local contexts [12,[14], [15], [16]]

Kenya faces a complex set of intertwined challenges in emergency care delivery, including shortages of trained personnel, essential supplies, technologies, and infrastructure alongside high rates of traumatic injuries and advanced or decompensated medical conditions [[17], [18], [19]] Additionally, devolution of the national health system into 47 counties has meant that approaches to emergency care challenges are developed and implemented at the county level [20] Amid these challenges, little is known about the current landscape of triage across public emergency departments (EDs) in Kenya, outside of pediatric pilot studies conducted in large national referral centers in Nairobi [21,22] While the impact of absent triage systems is difficult to assess, early evidence from an urban Kenyan ED raises concern for prolonged time to assessment for severely injured trauma patients [23] Understanding triage across public EDs in Kenya is vital to identifying opportunities for systems-level interventions to strengthen emergency care and reduce preventable deaths. This study presents findings from a cross-sectional survey of triage across public EDs in three counties in Kenya, intended to address existing gaps and inform programmatic approaches.

Methods

Setting: The study was conducted in three counties in Kenya: Kilifi, Kwale, and Mombasa. The counties are located in the country’s southeast with a combined population of over 3.5 million [24] The three counties span a substantial range of population density and urbanicity: Mombasa is the second most densely populated county in Kenya, while Kilifi and Kwale are predominantly rural [25]

In Kenya, a public ED is defined as an emergency care unit (including emergency department, accident and emergency department, casualty department, and/or outpatient department [OPD]) in a public hospital that receives unscheduled patients 24 hours a day and maintains a functioning operating theatre [26] Clinical care in public EDs is provided by both physicians and clinical officers (non-physician mid-level healthcare providers). Specialty-trained emergency medicine practitioners are rare, as there is a single emergency medicine residency program nationally and few training opportunities [20] Public hospitals in Kenya are classified by referral function. Sub-county hospitals serve a defined sub-county population, while county referral hospitals serve as the referral center for an entire county and provide a broader range of specialist services.

This study was conducted in collaboration with local county governments and the Emergency Medicine Kenya Foundation (EMKF), a national NGO that supports the strengthening of emergency healthcare systems across Kenya through capacity building, knowledge development, advocacy, and research [27,28] EMKF provides training courses on triage to frontline clinical staff working in public EDs across Kenya.

Study Design: This study was nested within a broader NIH-funded cluster-randomized trial (R01AI189326) focused on strengthening ED-based HIV care and prevention services in public EDs in the Coast region of Kenya [29] As part of the study, a cross-sectional survey of all public EDs in the three study counties was conducted between February 3 and February 10, 2026 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1The Surveyed Facilities in Kenya’s Coast Region.Fig 1 dummy alt text

At each ED, trained study staff administered a standardized questionnaire to a clinical leader in the ED familiar with patient flow and triage processes. Clinical leaders were identified by the facility’s administrative leader and had a formal, permanent clinical role within the ED and direct responsibility for performing triage. Key quantitative data, including monthly patient volume and catchment population, were verified by each hospital’s health records personnel. Where accessible, study staff directly observed the areas in which triage was performed, including the presence and configuration of dedicated triage spaces and the availability of key equipment. Data were collected using paper forms, and entered into and managed using the REDCap electronic data capture platform [30,31] In hospitals with multiple triage points (such as with separate triage areas for patients presenting to OPD and casualty department), questionnaires were administered separately to clinical leaders in each of the units to capture the breadth of local triage practice. At one facility with two triage points, a single clinical leader held responsibility for both units and completed one questionnaire covering both triage points.

The survey tool was developed collaboratively by the research team and EMKF using items from the WHO Hospital Emergency Unit Assessment Tool (HEAT) [32], together with context-specific items. The survey instrument included information about ED and hospital infrastructure and resources, staffing, and triage procedures and resources. Hospital-level characteristics including patient volume, referral designation, and clinical staffing composition were collected to describe the settings in which triage and emergency care were performed. Consistent with the HEAT survey process and prior studies of emergency care capacity, the survey instrument included five question types including numerical, open-ended, discrete, Likert-scale, and availability rating questions [33,34] The availability ratings were used to assess the capacity of the facility to perform key triage functions and included a three-tiered response option of 1) adequately available (available for all or almost all patients); 2) somewhat available (available for only some patients); 3) generally unavailable. For a rating less than adequately available, follow-on survey items probed the respondent to identify barriers to availability.

The instrument was reviewed prior to use by Kenyan emergency medicine specialists familiar with public sector emergency care, and was pretested to assess item clarity, response options, and administration time. Surveys were administered in English, the language of clinical practice and documentation in Kenyan public hospitals; all study staff were fluent in Kiswahili and provided clarification in Kiswahili where requested. Study staff received training on the survey instrument, delivered by the study investigators, prior to data collection.

Data analysis

Hospitals and EDs were characterized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges, and distributions of categorical characteristics among surveyed facilities were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Analysis of triage procedures and resources was restricted to facilities that reported performing triage. Infrastructure and equipment were considered adequately available if they were reported to be available for all or nearly all patients who required them. For facilities with multiple triage points, where more than one questionnaire was administered, the availability of infrastructure and equipment for triage and responses to Likert-scale questions were summarized by using the response indicating lower availability or satisfaction, ensuring that unmet resource needs or concerns at any triage point are captured. Survey responses of “don’t know” with respect to service availability were considered equivalent to unavailability, as it was considered unlikely that the service would be used if clinical leaders were unaware of them. Analyses were performed using Stata statistical software (Version 17, StataCorp, College Station Tx, USA), R (version 4.5.3), and Python (version 3.13). Locations of study facilities were mapped using openly licensed cartographic data (OpenStreetMap) [35] Likert-scale responses of respondents’ perceptions of triage were displayed using diverging horizontal bar charts and reported facilitators and barriers of triage were summarized using waffle plots.

Study ethics

The parent cluster-randomized trial, including the cross-sectional assessment of ED triage reported here, was reviewed and approved by the Brown University Health Institutional Review Board (2232,153–3), the joint Kenyatta National Hospital and University of Nairobi Ethics Research Committee (R/161), and the Coast General Teaching and Referral Hospital Ethics Review Committee (004/01/26). Approval was also obtained from each of the county Departments of Health.

Results

Facility and respondent characteristics

All 10 public EDs in the study counties were surveyed (100% facility response rate). Questionnaires were completed by 13 clinical leaders across the 10 facilities. Respondents comprised nursing cadres (8/13, 62%), clinical officers (4/13, 31%), and one HIV testing services counsellor (1/13, 8%). Two questionnaires were completed at each of three facilities with separate triage points in the outpatient and casualty departments, and one questionnaire at each of the remaining seven facilities. Characteristics of the facilities are presented in Table 1. Three of the facilities were designated as county referral hospitals and seven were sub-county hospitals. Monthly ED patient volume ranged from 1172 to 17,142 with a median of 2600 encounters (IQR 1677–5838). Emergency department triage practices

Across the 10 public EDs, nine (90%) reported performing triage on patients presenting for unscheduled care. Of these nine, seven (78%) reported performing triage on all patients presenting for care, while two (22%) reported gaps in consistent triage based on time of day (Table 1). Five of the EDs (56%) triaged patients at a single triage point, while four (44%) triaged separately for patients presenting to the OPD and casualty department. Only two EDs (22%) reported using standardized triage tools for assessment of patients; one ED reported using a version of the WHO Interagency Integrated Triage Tool (IITT), while another used an adaptation of the South African Triage Scale (SATS) [36,37] The IITT uses rapid assessment for emergency and priority signs to assign patients to one of three triage categories while the SATS combines an early warning score with clinical discriminators to assign patients to one of four triage levels. Both facilities with standardized triage tools were county referral hospitals. None of the facilities reported using dedicated pediatric triage tools. Among facilities not using standardized tools, respondents reported using vital signs, visual assessment of the patient, and presenting complaints to prioritize patients requiring urgent or emergent care. None of the facilities without standardized triage tools reported the presence of written triage protocols or standard operating procedures. Seven of the nine EDs actively performing triage (78%) had dedicated triage areas while one ED performed triage of patients at the point of care. Nurses were the most commonly reported triage staff (8/9 EDs; 89%) while community health workers were the principal triage staff at one ED (11%).

With regard to equipment for triage, among the nine EDs performing triage, respondents reported generally high availability of many key triage equipment items including blood pressure measurement devices (8/9; 89% of facilities with ongoing triage), thermometers (7/9; 78%), and pulse-oximeters (7/9; 78%), though the availability of glucometers and testing strips was notably lower (5/9; 56%; Supplementary Table 1).

Perceptions of triage

Overall, respondents across the nine EDs performing triage reported mixed satisfaction with how triage functions at their EDs. Respondents from two of the nine facilities performing triage (22%) reported being satisfied with triage as performed at their facilities, one (11%) reported neither satisfaction nor dissatisfaction, while two-thirds (6/9; 67%) reported being dissatisfied or very dissatisfied (Fig. 2). Despite these mixed perceptions, respondents from all facilities reported that triage improves overall care and patient outcomes in their EDs (9/9; 100% agree or strongly agree) and nearly all reported that it enables the timely identification of critically ill patients (8/9; 89% agree or strongly agree). Respondents from two-thirds of facilities (6/9; 67%) believed that their ED’s triage practice was effective in identifying patients requiring urgent but not emergent care and had the necessary equipment for triage assessment. Reported facilitators and barriers to triage

The most frequently cited facilitators of effective triage across EDs were staff teamwork, staff dedication and skills, and the availability of both essential clinical equipment and a diverse group of clinical staff to perform triage. Regarding barriers to effective triage, limited staffing (in particular of nursing staff) was reported by eight of the nine facilities with ongoing triage (89%). Triage training deficiencies and equipment or supply shortages were each identified as barriers at four EDs (44%). Deficiencies in standardized triage tools and protocols were reported as barriers at three EDs (33%).

Discussion

This study provides one of the first systematic assessments of triage in public EDs in Kenya. Triage is commonly performed across the surveyed EDs in Kenya’s Coast region, yet implementation is variable and occurs largely without standardized tools or protocols. At the same time, the physical infrastructure to support triage was reported to be in place at most facilities, and clinical leaders consistently endorsed the value of triage for patient care. These findings characterize systems in which the foundations for effective triage exist, but which would be enhanced by deliberate investment in standardization, training, and workforce development to translate into reliable patient prioritization and the efficient allocation of scarce clinical resources.

Among the EDs surveyed, triage models varied in structure: five facilities triaged patients at a single triage point, four conducted triage separately for patients presenting to OPDs and casualty department. The differing triage models across study facilities correspond to distinct functional roles for triage. In casualty departments, which are designed to evaluate and manage acutely ill patients, triage serves primarily as a tool for acuity-based prioritization, enabling the allocation of often limited clinical resources and minimizing time to treatment for critical health needs. In OPDs, by contrast, where most patients present with less acute illness, triage serves principally as a means of risk identification and escalation, identifying occult serious illness among a predominantly lower-acuity population and recognizing patients who may require a higher level of care. Where triage is conducted at a single unified point, these functions necessarily coexist, requiring triage staff to simultaneously prioritize the critically ill and identify those with less obvious yet potentially critical acute care needs. Despite these differences in purpose, systematic triage is vital in all settings where patients present for unscheduled care, where they may be acutely ill, and where failure to identify and prioritize them can contribute to preventable morbidity and mortality [9]

Although nine of ten EDs reported performing triage, the practice was formalized in only two, with the remainder reporting reliance on clinical judgment without standardized triage tools or written protocols. The low prevalence of structured, protocol-driven triage (20%) is broadly consistent with findings from a survey in Northern Uganda (17%) [13] However, these findings contrast with those from a cross-sectional survey of ED capacity across 11 facilities in Northern Tanzania, in which 82% reported having a protocol for systematic triage [16] These comparisons suggest substantial variability in implementation of formal triage systems across East African settings. Despite the limited use of formal triage tools or procedures, the physical infrastructure to support triage was reported to be largely in place across study EDs. Dedicated triage areas were present at seven of nine facilities performing triage, and respondents reported high availability of equipment for vital sign assessment. These findings, coupled with respondents' identified barriers, suggest that the primary obstacles to structured triage in this setting are not infrastructural but rather reflect gaps in the training and operational frameworks to use existing infrastructure consistently and effectively.

Staffing insufficiency was the most consistently reported barrier to effective triage across study facilities, cited at eight of nine facilities performing triage. This aligns with broader literature on emergency care capacity in sub-Saharan Africa, where health workforce shortages are among the most pervasive constraints on care delivery [3,38] In Kenya specifically, a national training needs assessment found significant nursing shortages, with accident and emergency nurses among the most scarce specialist cadres nationally [39] The burden placed on nursing staff is notable in the triage context: nurses were the principal triage workforce at eight of nine facilities, and yet insufficient nursing staffing was simultaneously the most commonly cited barrier to consistent triage completion. This discordance between reliance on nursing staff as the backbone of triage systems and persistent shortfalls in their numbers is not unique to this setting and has been identified as a central challenge to triage implementation in other regional settings [13] Progress on this front will likely depend on county-specific initiatives responsive to local healthcare priorities coupled with sustained prioritization at the national level, as affirmed by the Kenya Emergency Care Policy [40]

Gaps in triage-specific training and the absence of standardized tools and protocols were identified as barriers at nearly half of facilities and represent targets for triage programming and improvement. The introduction of standardized triage instruments is a natural first step, with tools validated in comparable East African settings including the South African Triage Scale and the WHO Interagency Integrated Triage Tool [9,41,42] Introduction of standardized tools has been associated with reductions in both mortality and patient waiting times across LMIC emergency settings [9]

Alongside standardized clinical tools, formalization of triage documentation represents an important and underemphasized component of triage strengthening. Standardized triage registers have the potential to support consistent triage performance, enable rapid characterization of the spectrum of patients and health needs across emergency care clinical settings, and generate the longitudinal data to evaluate the impact of systems and interventions [[43], [44], [45]] Deployment of these registers is among the most immediately actionable steps toward formalizing triage practice, requiring minimal additional infrastructure beyond what is in place at most facilities.

Addressing the range of reported gaps across staffing, training, standardized tools, and triage documentation will require coordinated, sustained effort across facilities and counties. Under Kenya's devolved health system, county departments of health hold primary responsibility for service delivery, while national bodies including the Ministry of Health and emergency care professional and training organizations shape policy and standards. Sustained progress will likely require engagement across both levels. The findings of this study are intended to inform and support these efforts.

This study's findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design characterizes triage practice at a single timepoint and cannot capture variation in performance over time, nor the full impact of any ongoing local improvement programs. Facility characteristics are reported descriptively, as the small number of facilities precluded assessment of their association with triage practices or perceptions. Second, data were collected from a single respondent per triage area, which may not fully reflect the range of practice or perceptions across staff within a facility. Respondents were, however, purposively sampled to identify individuals with broad knowledge of local triage processes and operational realities, helping to ensure that key facility-level challenges were captured. Third, many measures of triage practice were derived from respondent report rather than from independent verification. Although study staff observed triage areas and equipment where accessible, these findings describe what clinical leaders reported about their units rather than verified triage performance. Responses were subject to social desirability bias, with risk of overreporting the consistency or quality of local triage. Prior staff exposure to triage training was likewise not captured, and its relationship to the practices reported could not be assessed. Finally, the study was focused on public EDs that serve most patients and represent the principal target for county and national health system strengthening efforts. Findings cannot be assumed to apply to private, NGO-led, or faith-based facilities.

Conclusion

This study offers one of the first characterizations of triage across public EDs in coastal Kenya, revealing a system where the foundations for effective triage are present but require deliberate investment to be developed into reliable practice. Identified gaps in standardized tools, triage training, and nursing staffing point to a clear and actionable agenda for local, county, and national stakeholders to strengthen triage, with the potential to reduce morbidity and mortality.

Dissemination

Findings from this study were shared with the Departments of Health of Kilifi, Kwale, and Mombasa counties. Results will further inform ongoing triage strengthening efforts under the parent NIH-funded trial (R01AI189326) operating across the same facilities.
