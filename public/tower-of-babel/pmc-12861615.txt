Breaking barriers in the education of persistent physical symptoms
Authors: Damir Rafi, Imran Rafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent physical symptoms (PPS) account for around 45% of all primary care consultations, and are associated with significant disability and costs. While not always the case, co-existent mental health difficulties can often present alongside PPS. Individuals suffering from PPS may present with psychological symptoms such as rumination and catastrophising, as well as early adverse childhood events. Research has shown that adequately understanding and managing psychological factors associated with PPS can prevent symptoms from being more chronic and disabling.Adequate education therefore is vital for medical students and doctors, particularly GPs, such that they are able to manage PPS in a professional, knowledgeable and patient-centred manner. At present there exist several barriers to such education – including curriculum overload and a lack of alignment with the current structure of medical school, whereby physical and mental health are often taught in separate blocks.We suggest several ways in which barriers can be broken, such that GPs feel more empowered to manage patients with PPS. These include a greater emphasis on the psychological dimensions of illness from an early stage in medical school, an improved framework for taking a history from patients with PPS, more exposure to case studies and real-life patient experiences of PPS, narrowing the gap between theoretical teaching and the realities of the workplace, including importantly highlighting multidisciplinary working and improved education around communicating effectively with patients with PPS. We hope that by breaking these barriers, doctors can feel more able to enquire about, understand and manage PPS, ultimately leading to better outcomes for patients.Keywords: MUS, PPS, Persistent physical symptoms, Education, Medicine

Introduction

Medically unexplained symptoms (MUS), also known as persistent physical symptoms (PPS), refer to distressing physical complaints which are not explainable by medical examination or investigations.1 It is estimated that around 45% of all primary care consultations involve such symptoms, and around 20% of primary care patients have diagnosable somatoform disorders.2 It has been estimated that PPS is associated with significant disability, healthcare utilisation, and costs of billions of pounds per year.3

Despite the prevalence and impact upon individuals and society, teaching on PPS at both an undergraduate and postgraduate level in the UK remains markedly limited.4 There remains a heavy focus on diagnosable disorders with clear biological causes within medical schools, which often contrasts with the realities of working life, in which patients’ symptoms often do not fit so neatly into specific known categories.5

This commentary will highlight some of the current challenges and suggest ways in which the individual and society can be better served by healthcare professionals – specifically focusing on training interventions within primary care.

Interplay between physical and mental health

As part of the postgraduate GP curriculum for Membership of the Royal College of General Practitioners examinations, there is an emphasis on ‘the interactions between physical and mental health and the need for holistic care’.6 It has been widely recognised over recent years that this overlap between physical and mental health is a crucial one in the case of best managing short- and long-term conditions. With regards to PPS, recent evidence has highlighted the importance of understanding the psychological factors that may underpin such symptoms.7 While the impact of psychological factors will vary from patient to patient, it has been shown that adequately ascertaining these can play a large role in preventing PPS from developing into more chronic and disabling somatoform disorders.

Psychological factors that may be present in PPS sufferers include early adverse childhood events and insecure attachment, but also specific current negative thought patterns, such as rumination, avoidance and catastrophising. Evidence has shown that those with a higher intensity and degree of negative patterns of thinking are more likely to view MUS as more threatening, and be more acutely aware of their symptoms, which can result in a higher cognitive and emotional load.8

From a primary care perspective, patients who require assessment of both physical and mental health in combination, and in whom a clear diagnostic label cannot be attached, have often been described as ‘heartsink’.9 Such a description reflects not only the time-consuming nature of such consultations, but also the lack of confidence that clinicians feel in adequately explaining the problem to the patient. Research has shown that patients often invalidated by clinicians’ explanations of PPS, and feel as though they are being accused of ‘making up’ their symptoms, or imagining them.10

In order to assuage such feelings of discomfort, or for fear of missing something, clinicians often send their patients for further tests, often involving multiple specialties. This can create a vicious cycle that can be challenging to break,11 as well as further reinforce the patient’s opinion that their ‘true’ diagnosis is within reach. Evidently, such an approach on a societal level carries significant financial strain to the healthcare service.

PPS in medical education

Previous research has highlighted the barriers to educating undergraduate medical students in the topic of PPS.4 Particular prominent issues include curriculum overload and a lack of agreement as to at what stage, and where in the curriculum, this topic should be taught. While medical schools typically teach mental health in a separate distinct module, there is a lack of clarity as to whether PPS fits into this section of the curriculum, or more into physical health blocks. Indeed, while psychological factors were discussed earlier in this commentary, it is also important to avoid the assumption that all PPS is psychological in origin, and ignore possible physiological explanations. The fact therefore that this topic transcends one particular specialty means that it struggles to align with the current structure of medical school teaching.

Breaking barriers

The feelings of helplessness and frustration felt by GPs when dealing with patients with PPS has been well documented across several decades.12 In spite of an increasing prevalence of PPS and somatoform disorders, such sentiments continue to persist. This is unsurprising, given the continued lack of focus and attention that is given to the topic within medical education. In order to tackle this issue, and empower future clinicians and improve PPS outcomes for patients, a multipronged approach is needed.

First, teaching on the psychological dimensions of illness could be prominent throughout medical school clinical placements. Such training could include, but not be limited to, the overlap between long-term physical health conditions and mental health problems, the impact of adverse childhood experiences on future physical and mental health, and the potential role of psychosocial stressors on health. The King's College ‘Mind and Body’ programme, for example, is a step forward in promoting holistic care with a consideration of both physical and mental wellbeing.13

Second, an improved model or framework should be created with regards history-taking in patients with suspected PPS. While medical students learn how to take various medical, surgical and mental health histories, all separately, it can become confusing when having to integrate various components of each type of history during a single short consultation. Knowing which questions to prioritise, and how much focus to place on physical symptoms versus mental health, can be challenging. Specific training should take place to better educate undergraduate students and doctors in this regard.

Third, specific training on PPS could be embedded into the medical curriculum – in the form of lecture-based teaching, case studies, and encountering real patients with PPS within inpatient and outpatient settings during medical school rotations. One survey among GP trainees found that over three-quarters had had no experience of identifying or managing PPS patients until after qualification.14 Such training and education should also apply on a postgraduate level, within all specialties of medicine, given the widespread prevalence of PPS. Expert patients should be consulted when designing such training, with opportunities for co-production.

Fourth, more broadly, there needs to be a discussion around a more widespread culture change in the way that medical schools deliver education. In the same survey as mentioned above, one GP remarked that: ‘In medical school you’re taught that patients have things wrong with them and there’s always a medical cause for it and you’ve got to try and find it’.14 The disconnect between the kinds of conditions taught about in medical school and the realities of the workplace – as evidenced by the high proportion of PPS, somatoform and functional disorders within primary care – can lead to feelings of powerlessness among newly qualified doctors.

Fifth, specific communication training conducted at all levels of medical education in helping clinicians could better explain PPS to patients. Common phrases to use or avoid should be highlighted, with the ultimate aim of enabling patients to feel heard and validated. As yet, research into specific communication primary care interventions for PPS-related conditions has not yielded conclusively effective strategies.15 However, with ongoing research, and broad communication models taught at a medical school level, clinicians will ultimately feel more empowered to successfully explain PPS and related disorders to their patients. Joint working between professional bodies, as well as with those patients affected, sends a strong message that recognises that a collective response around education and training remains important.16

Conclusion

Most people with PPS will initially present to primary care. Despite this, current experience and research demonstrates that most clinicians feel unable to effectively investigate or manage such patients. With the individual, societal and financial impacts of PPS growing with every decade, medical schools require a coordinated strategy to better educate students on this topic, to integrate physical and mental health within curricula, as well as incorporating teaching around PPS at all stages of education.
