Speaker Disentanglement of Speech Pre-trained Model Based on Interpretability
Xiaoxu Zhu, Junhua Li

Abstract
In recent years, Large Language Models have made significant progress in processing text, images, and audio information. In the field of speech, the introduction of large-scale, self-supervised pretraining techniques has greatly enhanced the speech generation quality and task generalization ability of speech models. Different layers of speech pretrained models contain information useful for different tasks (such as content, timbre, and paralinguistic information), demonstrating vast potential in full-stack speech tasks. However, decoupling of content and timbre information poses significant challenges, when employing weighted embeddings from different layers for various downstream tasks. As removing speaker-specific information can easily lead to content loss, and the detriment to the latter often outweighs the benefits of the former. Additionally, research indicates that successful speaker disentanglement aids in a wide range of content-related voice processing tasks. In current research on voice pretraining models, different models use various metrics to indirectly reflect the effectiveness of their encoding in disentangling speaker. However, there are no direct metrics to represent the residual timbre; and it has found in practice that residual timbre information can significantly impair the performance of models in executing content-related downstream tasks. Moreover, interpretability methods are less explored in the domain of voice, and employing such methods for deeper understanding and application of voice models is crucial. Addressing these issues, this paper conducts an in-depth study on the speaker disentanglement of speech pretraining models. It quantitatively evaluates the degree of timbre residual in the embeddings of different layers of various speech pretraining models using interpretability methods and improves speaker disentanglement using interpretive representations. The main contributions are as follows: 1. Introduction of a Timbre Residual Recognition and Quantitative Evaluation Benchmark of Speech pre-training Models Encoding via Interpretability (InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark). The benchmark utilizes the concatenation of content embeddings from speech pretraining models and extracted timbre embeddings to perform speaker classification tasks. Then it utilizes the Gradient SHAP Explainer to interpret the speaker classification models, obtaining quantified representations of residual timbre in the content encoding of the speech pretraining models, providing researchers with an objective and reusable assessment framework. This benchmark is used to evaluate the residual timbre in five types of voice pretraining models, with a total of seven variations. The assessment results are generally consistent with the models’ indirect representational information, validating the effectiveness of the benchmark. 2. Introduction of a Timbre Filter of Speech pre-training Models Encoding via Interpretability (InterpTF-SptME). The method utilizes the quantified representations of residual timbre obtained from InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark to transform the intermediate layer encoding (content embeddings) of the speech pretraining models, removing the timbre information. It is model-agnostic, optimization-target-layer-agnostic, and independent of whether the data is labeled, thus highly versatile. Experiments conducted on the VCTK dataset and HuBERT LARGE model show the desired content preservation and significant speaker disentanglement optimization. Through these efforts, this paper successfully achieves the goal of maintaining complete content information in the encoding of speech pretraining models while improving speaker disentanglement, thus contributing to enhanced performance in content-related voice processing tasks and avoiding privacy leaks related to voice timbre.

As noted in OpenAI’s GPT-4V system introduction, "integrating additional modalities into large language models is considered a key frontier in AI research and development." According to the Speech processing Universal PERformance Benchmark (SUPERB)

Complete contextual understanding further requires structured noise including laughter, coughs, background vehicle engines, bird songs, or sizzling food sounds. Therefore, speech signals can be broadly categorized into three components: content information, timbre information, and paralinguistic information including style, prosody, and emotion.

Traditional speech generation models were primarily task-specific, trained on different datasets customized for particular objectives

This limitation arose because different tasks focus on different aspects of speech signals—speaker verification requires models to learn speaker characteristics while disregarding content, whereas speech recognition demands content focus while discarding speaker features. 1.1
Research Problem and Motivation

Content and timbre information decoupling represents a critical challenge in speech processing. For instance, extracting content information from Speaker A and incorporating timbre information from Speaker B achieves voice conversion. In architectures like SPEAR-TTS, the quality of content-related intermediate layer results from speech pre-training models significantly impacts overall model performance. However, speaker disentanglement is highly challenging because removing speaker-specific information can easily lead to content loss, where the detriment often outweighs the benefits.

Moreover, neural network models are notorious "black box" systems whose internal mechanisms remain opaque, making it difficult to directly reason about and quantitatively evaluate the degree of timbre residual in content embeddings. Current research typically relies on indirect evaluation through downstream task performance. In current voice pre-training model research, different models use various metrics to indirectly reflect the effectiveness of their speaker disentanglement encoding, but there are no direct metrics to represent residual timbre information. Additionally, the lack of unified quantitative dimensions leads to inconsistent evaluation data across different literature.

1.2
Research Significance and Contributions

This paper addresses these challenges by conducting an in-depth study on speaker disentanglement in speech pre-training models using interpretability methods. We quantitatively evaluate the degree of timbre residual in embeddings from different layers of various speech pre-training models and improve speaker disentanglement using interpretive representations.

Our main contributions include:

Theoretical contributions:
First, interpretability research in speech is limited, with existing cases primarily focusing on phoneme recognition and reinforcement learning applications. This work introduces interpretability methods to speech pre-training models, achieving methodological transfer and laying foundations for future research. Second, we propose a novel interpretability-based benchmark for timbre residual recognition and quantitative evaluation, providing researchers with an objective and reusable assessment framework. Third, we innovatively introduce the concepts of "SHAP Noise" and "SHAP Cropping," designing interpretability-based timbre filtering methods that maintain content information integrity while achieving significant speaker disentanglement effects.

Practical contributions:
Successful speaker disentanglement in speech pre-training models can prevent timbre privacy leakage and enable timbre-independent task execution. Research indicates that successful speaker disentanglement benefits a wide range of content-related voice processing tasks, making our filtering methods potentially valuable for improving model performance on content-related speech processing tasks.

2
Related Work

2.1
Speech Pre-training Models

The development of speech models can be traced through several distinct phases. Traditional speech recognition systems evolved from early exploration in the 1950s through statistical models (GMM-HMM), deep learning approaches (DNN-HMM), to end-to-end models (CTC, Attention-based). However, these approaches suffered from complex training pipelines, limited generalization, and heavy dependence on labeled data.

The breakthrough came with large-scale self-supervised pre-training models. Inspired by the success of pre-training paradigms in NLP, speech models began exploring self-supervised learning techniques. Early attempts including APC, MPC, CPC, and wav2vec showed promise but achieved limited success. The landscape changed dramatically with the introduction of wav2vec 2.0 by Facebook (Meta) in June 2020, which combined contrastive learning and masking techniques using a CNN+Transformer architecture. This model demonstrated the feasibility of multilingual speech recognition with limited labeled data, significantly reducing dependence on annotated datasets.

Building upon wav2vec 2.0’s success, HuBERT (Hidden-Unit BERT) was proposed by Facebook in June 2021 to address the challenges of applying contrastive learning to speech data. HuBERT uses k-means clustering on MFCC features to generate training targets and employs iterative training, where clustering quality improves across iterations. The model adopts masked prediction similar to BERT but operates on clustered acoustic units rather than discrete tokens.

Microsoft’s WavLM, introduced in October 2021, extended HuBERT’s capabilities to handle multi-speaker scenarios. WavLM incorporated three key improvements: gated relative position bias in Transformers, denoising pre-training with overlapping speech simulation, and training on larger datasets (94k hours). These enhancements significantly improved performance on speaker-related tasks such as speaker verification and speech separation.

Recent developments have moved toward weakly supervised and semi-supervised approaches. OpenAI’s Whisper (October 2022) applied weak supervision on 680k hours of multilingual data, achieving high-quality results without fine-tuning on specific datasets. Google’s USM (Universal Speech Model, September 2023) combined self-supervised and semi-supervised learning using separate text data for cross-modal training, extending ASR capabilities to over 100 languages with enhanced robustness.

The evolution toward multi-modal universal representation learning is exemplified by Meta’s data2vec series. Data2vec (January 2022) introduced a unified framework for self-supervised learning across speech, vision, and language modalities using the same architecture and training principles. Data2vec 2.0 (December 2022) improved training efficiency by 10.6 times while maintaining comparable performance through asymmetric encoder-decoder architectures and efficient masking strategies.

Speaker Disentanglement

Speaker disentanglement, a key aspect of disentangled representation learning
[
5
]
, aims to separate speaker-specific information (timbre) from content information in speech signals. This challenge is crucial for various applications including voice conversion, speaker anonymization, and privacy protection
[
35
]
, especially following regulations like GDPR that emphasize personal data protection.

Traditional approaches to feature disentanglement employ Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs). GAN-based systems like StarGAN-VC
[
12
]
and CycleGAN-VC
[
14
]
use discriminators to distinguish different speakers, encouraging generators to produce speaker-agnostic representations. VAE-based approaches such as VAE-VC
[
10
]
, ACVAE-VC
[
13
]
, AutoVC
[
26
]
, and VoiceMixer
[
20
]
leverage bottleneck architectures and similarity-based downsampling to achieve speaker-content separation.

Recent advances focus on improving representation quality while maintaining content integrity. Research has shown that discrete speech units effectively remove speaker information but may discard linguistic content, leading to pronunciation errors
[
36
]
. Soft speech units, which model uncertainty in discrete unit assignments, have been proposed to preserve more content information
[
7
]
. Vector quantization techniques have also been applied to ASR bottleneck features to enhance speaker anonymization while maintaining speech recognition utility
[
3
]
.

Self-supervised speech representation learning models like wav2vec 2.0
[
4
]
, HuBERT
[
11
]
, and their variants primarily use discriminative tasks to learn feature representations
[
39
]
. ContentVec
[
27
]
, proposed by MIT in April 2022, specifically addresses speaker disentanglement in the HuBERT framework through three key components: teacher disentanglement using voice conversion systems, student disentanglement through contrastive loss on augmented versions, and speaker conditioning to provide necessary speaker information separately. This approach demonstrated improved content modeling while significantly reducing speaker information in learned representations.

2.3
Explainability Methods

As deep learning models, particularly large-scale models, are often characterized as "black box" systems, explainability research has become crucial for understanding model behavior and ensuring trustworthy deployment
[
43
]
. Explainability methods can be categorized along several dimensions: intrinsic vs. post-hoc, model-specific vs. model-agnostic, and local vs. global explanations.

Post-hoc model-agnostic methods are particularly relevant for pre-trained models. Global explanation methods include Partial Dependence Plots (PDP), Accumulated Local Effects (ALE), permutation feature importance, and global surrogate models. Local explanation methods encompass Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE), Local Surrogate (LIME)
[
30
]
, counterfactual explanations, Shapley values, and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)
[
22
]
.

SHAP, based on game-theoretic Shapley values, provides both local and global explanations by computing each feature’s contribution to individual predictions. It offers a unified framework connecting various explanation methods and provides theoretically grounded feature attributions with additive properties.

For neural networks, specific interpretation techniques include feature visualization, pixel attribution (saliency maps)
[
32
]
, concept detection methods like TCAV
[
16
]
, adversarial examples, and influential instance analysis
[
17
]
. Large language models have driven additional research into attention-based explanations and probe-based analysis of learned representations
[
1
,
34
]
.

2.4
Explainability-Driven Model Optimization

While most explainability methods focus on visualization and understanding, recent research has explored using explanations as feedback to improve model performance
[
33
]
. In language models, explanations closely aligned with examples significantly improve few-shot learning accuracy
[
19
,
42
]
. The AMPLIFY framework leverages post-hoc explanations to provide corrective signals for large language models, achieving 10-25% improvement in prediction accuracy
[
18
]
.

Applications extend beyond NLP to computer vision and other domains. LIME has been used for trust-based model selection and classifier improvement. SHAP values have been applied as weighted parameters in loss functions for radar-based people counting, improving accuracy by 3-4%. The OptDIA framework uses Grad-CAM explanations to guide data augmentation strategies, achieving 0.39-2.15% accuracy improvements on image datasets.

In speech processing, explainability research is limited but growing
[
40
]
. LIME has been applied to phoneme recognition in Kaldi/TIMIT systems, with variants LIME-WS and LIME-TS showing improved accuracy through window-based and time-segment-based perturbations
[
23
]
. LRP (Layer-wise Relevance Propagation)
[
2
]
has been integrated into GRU-based speech recognition models to enhance interpretability without sacrificing accuracy
[
6
]
. LIME has also been used in keyword spotting tasks for incremental learning, where explanation distances serve as weighted loss function parameters.

2.5
Technical Foundations

This work evaluates five open-source speech pre-training models: HuBERT (BASE & LARGE), DPHuBERT, ContentVec, WavLM Base+, and Whisper-ppg. All models employ self-supervised learning with CNN feature extractors and Transformer
[
37
]
encoders, but differ in architecture scale, training data, and optimization objectives.

For explainability analysis, we employ SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) as our primary interpretation method. SHAP provides theoretically grounded feature attributions based on cooperative game theory, computing each feature’s contribution to model predictions through the Shapley value formulation:

\phi_{j}=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\}\setminus\{j\}}\frac{|S|!(p-|S|-1)!}{p!}[f(S\cup\{j\})-f(S)]

(1)

where
\phi_{j}
represents the Shapley value for feature
j
,
S
denotes feature subsets excluding feature
j
,
p
is the total number of features, and
f(\cdot)
is the value function mapping feature sets to model outputs. The additive property ensures that explanations sum to the difference between the prediction and the expected baseline value.

3
Methodology

3.1
Timbre Residual Quantification in Speech Pre-training Models

Speech pre-training models are widely applied to various speech processing tasks including Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), Voice Conversion (VC), Text-to-Speech (TTS), speech translation, multi-modal tasks, and human-computer interaction. Research on ContentVec demonstrates that effective speaker disentanglement can improve performance across a broad range of content-related speech processing tasks.

As analyzed in Section
2
, the widely-used HuBERT BASE model shows that its high-performing 9th Transformer layer from the second iteration achieves approximately 70% phone purity and 10% cluster purity, indicating substantial timbre information residual. While HuBERT LARGE reduces Word Error Rate (WER) from 8.1% to 3.9% through training on richer datasets, and ContentVec significantly improves speaker disentanglement by reducing Speaker Identification (SID) accuracy from 73.7% to 37.7%, these models lack direct metrics for quantifying timbre residual.

Current speech pre-training models use various indirect metrics such as ASR accuracy, SID accuracy, speaker-related task performance, phone purity, and cluster purity to reflect speaker disentanglement effectiveness. However, the absence of direct timbre residual metrics and unified quantification dimensions leads to inconsistent evaluation across different studies.

To address this limitation, this chapter proposes the
InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark
(Timbre Residual Recognition and Quantitative Evaluation Benchmark of Speech pre-training Models Encoding via Interpretability), which introduces interpretability methods to speech pre-training model performance characterization, providing quantitative evaluation criteria for speaker disentanglement effectiveness.

3.2
InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark

3.2.1
Problem Definition

We evaluate seven speech pre-training models on the VCTK dataset: five open-source models (HuBERT BASE & LARGE
[
11
]
, DPHuBERT
[
25
]
, ContentVec
[
27
]
, WavLM Base+
[
8
]
, Whisper-ppg
[
28
]
) and one Chinese-specific model (HuBERT-CH) fine-tuned on Chinese scenarios
[
44
]
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{k}\}
denote the set of selected speech pre-training models,
\mathcal{W}=\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\}
represent the wav samples from the chosen dataset, and
f_{i}:\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
be the wav-to-embedding mapping for model
M_{i}
. For model
M_{i}
and sample
w_{j}
, the content embedding is
\mathbf{c}_{ij}=f_{i}(w_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
, where
d_{c}
represents the content embedding dimension (1024 for HuBERT LARGE and Whisper-ppg, 768 for others).

Using a pre-trained speaker model, we extract speaker embeddings
\mathbf{s}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
for each sample, where
d_{s}=192
. The fused feature
\mathbf{x}_{ij}=[\mathbf{c}_{ij};\mathbf{s}_{j}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}+d_{s}}
is input to a speaker classification model. Since the input explicitly contains speaker embeddings, classification accuracy should approach 1.0.

Using Gradient SHAP Explainer
[
22
]
to interpret each input dimension’s contribution to speaker classification, the timbre residual quantification for the
i
-th speech pre-training model is defined as:

\text{TRQ}_{i}=\frac{\frac{1}{d_{c}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{c}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\phi_{c,jk}|}{\frac{1}{d_{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{s}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\phi_{s,jk}|}

(2)

where
\phi_{c,jk}
represents the SHAP contribution of the
j
-th element in content embedding for sample
k
,
\phi_{s,jk}
represents the SHAP contribution of the
j
-th element in speaker embedding for sample
k
,
n
is the total number of samples, and
d_{c},d_{s}
are the dimensions of content and speaker embeddings respectively.

Using speaker embeddings as a unified comparison baseline, the timbre residual quantification across different speech pre-training models maintains objectivity.

3.2.2
Benchmark Framework

The InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark framework comprises the following processes, as illustrated in Figure
1
:

1. Content Embedding Extraction:
Using prepared speech pre-training models, extract content embedding representations from wav samples in the selected dataset.

2. Speaker Embedding Extraction:
Using a pre-trained speaker model, extract speaker embedding representations from the same wav samples.

3. Feature Fusion:
Concatenate content and speaker embeddings to form the input dataset for speaker classification. Based on whether frame dimensions are preserved, choose either "frame-wise concatenation" or "embedding vector sequence concatenation."

4. Speaker Classification:
Perform speaker classification using the fused feature dataset. Since speaker classification model inputs contain explicit speaker embeddings, expected classification accuracy should approach 1.0.

5. Interpretability Analysis:
Use Gradient SHAP Explainer to interpret each input dimension’s contribution to speaker classification. Leveraging SHAP’s additive properties and consistency between local and global explanations, treat content and speaker embedding portions as separate wholes for contribution characterization.

Figure 1
:
InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark framework overview. The framework processes speech data through speaker and speech pre-training models to extract embeddings, fuses them for speaker classification, and uses gradient explainer to quantify timbre residual in content embeddings.

3.2.3
Benchmark Construction

Dataset and Preprocessing:
We select the VCTK (Voice Cloning Toolkit Corpus) dataset, which contains speech data from approximately 110 different English native speakers with various accents. For computational efficiency, we use data from the first 20 speakers (p225-p246, excluding missing p235 and p242), totaling 7,758 audio samples.

All audio files are standardized to 16kHz sampling rate, 16-bit depth, mono WAV format using SOX audio processing tools. Energy normalization adjusts peak energy to -3dB level, reducing volume fluctuation impacts on model performance.

Feature Extraction:
Speaker embeddings are extracted using SpeechBrain’s
[
29
]
spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb system, which employs a pre-trained ECAPA-TDNN model on VoxCeleb dataset for speaker verification tasks
[
38
]
.

Content embeddings are extracted by registering forward hooks on specific layers of speech pre-training models. Layer selection is based on optimal content-related task performance: HuBERT BASE and HuBERT-CH use 9th layer embeddings, HuBERT LARGE uses 21st layer embeddings, DPHuBERT, ContentVec, and WavLM Base+ use complete 12th layer outputs, and Whisper-ppg uses the complete Whisper Encoder output.

Embedding Concatenation:
Two concatenation methods are available:
1. Frame-wise concatenation: Preserves detailed frame information
2. Embedding vector sequence concatenation: Simplifies feature representation by averaging content vectors along frame dimension before concatenation with speaker vectors

We adopt the second method for speaker classification tasks, resulting in features of shape
[d_{s}+d_{c}]
.

Speaker Classification:
A simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with four fully connected layers and ReLU activation functions performs speaker classification using cross-entropy loss.

Interpretability Analysis:
Gradient SHAP Explainer provides explanations for speaker classification models. To ensure explanation stability and reliability:
- Overfit classifier to 1.0 accuracy
- Use entire dataset as explanation samples
- Maintain consistent representative baseline dataset (256 samples)
- Use consistent batch sizes (256)
- Apply local smoothing (0.1) to reduce individual data point influence

4
InterpTF-SptME: Interpretability-based Timbre Filtering Method

4.1
Interpretability-Driven Filtering Approach

From the analysis in Section
2
, interpretability information can serve as feedback to improve model performance. Existing approaches utilizing post-hoc explanations typically provide corrective signals for model misclassification samples, such as constructing few-shot prompts using samples and corresponding explanations, using contributions as weighted parameters in loss functions during incremental learning, or employing explanation information for data transformation in neural network retraining.

In the speech domain, interpretability methods are primarily applied to phoneme recognition tasks, such as using highest-weighted phonemes as prediction results to improve phoneme recognition accuracy, or utilizing deep neural network interpretation techniques like LRP to obtain sample-point relevance scores while enhancing interpretability without compromising model accuracy.

Our objective is to leverage the explanation information from the proposed InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark to improve speaker disentanglement in speech pre-training models—specifically, to reduce timbre residual in speech pre-training encodings. However, since the Benchmark framework introduces and trains a speaker classification model with 1.0 accuracy, we cannot directly obtain explanation scores to adjust model loss functions or integrate into model training processes. Therefore, we choose to utilize explanation information for data transformation and, combined with speech encoding characteristics, propose "SHAP Noise" and "SHAP Cropping" as two methods for loading explanations. We collectively term this the
InterpTF-SptME
(Timbre Filter of Speech pre-training Models Encoding via Interpretability) method.

4.2
InterpTF-SptME Framework

As discussed in Section
3
, the quality of intermediate layer encodings highly correlated with content information in speech pre-training models affects all downstream tasks focusing on semantic content information. Complete speaker disentanglement expects content embeddings extracted from speech pre-training models to be pure semantic content without timbre information.

To improve speaker disentanglement in speech pre-training models—removing timbre information from content embeddings—and considering the characteristics of our proposed method, we do not adjust the model before content embedding generation. Instead, we utilize timbre residual explanation information obtained from InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark to perform data transformation on speech pre-training model intermediate layer encodings (content embeddings) to remove timbre information. We term this process "Timbre Filtering."

Figure
2
illustrates the overall framework of the interpretability-based speech pre-training encoding timbre filtering method (InterpTF-SptME). The framework leverages explanation information from the InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark to guide the timbre filtering process, enabling effective removal of speaker-specific information while preserving content integrity.

Figure 2
:
InterpTF-SptME overall framework. The system utilizes explanation information from InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark to guide data transformation of speech pre-training model encodings, effectively filtering timbre information while maintaining content semantics through SHAP-based methods.

4.2.1
SHAP Noise Method

The SHAP Noise method treats SHAP explanations as beneficial noise added to content embeddings.

First, we standardize the content embedding representation portion of SHAP values as Gaussian noise:

\mathcal{N}_{\text{SHAP}}=\frac{\text{SHAP}_{\text{content}}-\mu(\text{SHAP}_{\text{content}})}{\sigma(\text{SHAP}_{\text{content}})}

(3)

For original content embeddings with shape
[n_{\text{frames}},d_{\text{content}}]
, we replicate and expand the Gaussian noise to frame dimensions, adding noise scale and offset to generate the loaded noise
\mathcal{N}_{\text{loaded}}
:

\mathcal{N}_{\text{loaded}}=\sigma_{\text{scale}}\cdot\mathcal{N}_{\text{SHAP}}+\mu_{\text{offset}}

(4)

Finally, we directly add
\mathcal{N}_{\text{loaded}}
to the original content embeddings to generate filtered content embeddings:

\mathbf{C}_{\text{filtered}}=\mathbf{C}_{\text{original}}+\mathcal{N}_{\text{loaded}}

(5)

4.2.2
SHAP Cropping Method

The SHAP Cropping method assumes that portions with higher SHAP values encode more timbre information, thus selecting dimensions with higher SHAP values for cropping, with SHAP values representing cropping scale.

First, using the content embedding representation portion of SHAP values, we determine cropping positions according to a set cropping ratio
r
and set SHAP values below the cropping position to 0:

\text{threshold}=\text{percentile}(\text{SHAP}_{\text{content}},(1-r)\times 100)

(6)

\text{SHAP}_{\text{cropped}}=\begin{cases}\text{SHAP}_{\text{content}}&\text{if }\text{SHAP}_{\text{content}}>\text{threshold}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(7)

Then we normalize it to
[0,w_{\text{cut}}]
, where
w_{\text{cut}}
represents cropping intensity:

\mathcal{W}_{\text{crop}}=w_{\text{cut}}\cdot\frac{\text{SHAP}_{\text{cropped}}}{\max(\text{SHAP}_{\text{cropped}})}

(8)

Finally, we use
\mathcal{W}_{\text{crop}}
to crop the original content embeddings:

\mathbf{C}_{\text{filtered}}=\mathbf{C}_{\text{original}}\odot(1-\mathcal{W}_{\text{crop}})

(9)

Key Findings:

ContentVec demonstrates superior disentanglement:
With only 5.20% timbre residual, ContentVec achieves the best speaker disentanglement performance, removing approximately 62% of timbre residual compared to HuBERT BASE. This validates the effectiveness of its three-component disentanglement mechanism.

Model compression maintains disentanglement:
DPHuBERT, despite having only 25% of HuBERT BASE’s parameters, achieves 7.73% timbre residual, effectively removing approximately 44% of timbre information through structured pruning.

Enhanced training improves disentanglement:
WavLM Base+ reduces timbre residual to 9.02% (34% improvement over HuBERT BASE) through denoising mechanisms and large-scale diverse data training, while maintaining comparable parameter count.

Parameter scaling affects timbre residual:
HuBERT LARGE shows 18.65% timbre residual (36% increase over BASE), indicating that larger models with finer-grained content encoding may capture more subtle timbre information alongside enhanced content representation.

Cross-lingual considerations:
HuBERT-CH maintains similar English timbre residual (13.93%) to HuBERT BASE, while Whisper-ppg demonstrates excellent disentanglement (7.46%) for English speech despite its multilingual training.

5.3
Performance Analysis

SHAP Noise significantly outperforms SHAP Cropping:
This superior performance suggests that negative SHAP values also contribute positively to timbre residual removal. This finding aligns with timbre perception principles—timbre relates to relative intensity and phase differences across frequency components, regardless of positive or negative contributions.

The experimental results demonstrate that both methods can effectively improve speaker disentanglement in speech pre-training encodings while maintaining content information integrity. The tolerance for accuracy loss can be adjusted within controllable ranges, enabling flexible trade-offs between content preservation and speaker disentanglement.

Our InterpTF-SptME method exhibits several important characteristics:

Model-agnostic:
The method works with any speech pre-training model without architectural constraints.

Layer-agnostic:
Not limited to specific layers, though layer selection affects performance.

Label-independent:
Both interpretability characterization and timbre filtering modules operate without labeled data.

Practical applications:
Beyond privacy protection, filtered embeddings can be used for improved content-related speech processing tasks and enhanced pre-training model development.

5.4
Layer Sensitivity Analysis

5.4.1
Experimental Setup

To test the quality of generated embeddings, we connect an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) model to detect recognition accuracy representing content information integrity. This requires the ASR model to accept intermediate layer embeddings as input. Therefore, we selected Meta’s officially released speech recognition model Hubert-Large-Finetuned, which is fine-tuned from HuBERT LARGE on the 960h LibriSpeech dataset. Accordingly, the speech pre-training model to be optimized is HuBERT LARGE.

Based on research conclusions by Zhao et al., Transformer layers 18-21 of speech pre-training models are relatively more advantageous for ASR tasks. We conducted interpretability analysis on HuBERT LARGE layers 18 and 21 and performed tests on speech recognition accuracy across different model layers.

We evaluate using CTC loss (Connectionist Temporal Classification loss) as the speech recognition metric, which considers all possible alignment methods for mapping input sequences to given output sequences without requiring strict temporal alignment between input and output sequences.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

6.1
Conclusion

The main research content of this paper focuses on using interpretability methods to quantitatively evaluate the timbre information residue in intermediate layer embeddings of different speech pre-training models, and utilizing interpretation information to improve the speaker disentanglement effect of models, avoiding timbre privacy leakage while helping to improve the performance of models when executing content-related speech processing tasks.

In current speech pre-training model research, different models use different metrics to indirectly reflect the speaker disentanglement effect of model encoding, but there are no direct indicators to characterize timbre residue. Due to the lack of unified quantitative dimensions, different literature highlighting respective model advantages from different perspectives can also lead to inconsistent evaluation data. Additionally, practical findings show that timbre information residue has a significant negative impact on model performance when executing content-related downstream tasks. Optimizing model performance with higher parameter counts and larger-scale training data also places higher demands on training computational resources. Finally, interpretability method research in speech is limited, with the few existing cases mainly focused on phoneme recognition and reinforcement learning applications. Utilizing interpretation to bring deeper understanding and applications to speech models is crucial.

Addressing the above issues, the main research work and innovations of this paper include the following aspects:

1.

We propose an interpretability-based speech pre-training encoding timbre residue identification and quantitative evaluation benchmark (InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark). This benchmark uses content embeddings from speech pre-training models concatenated with extracted timbre embeddings to perform speaker classification tasks, uses Gradient SHAP Explainer to interpret the speaker classification model, and obtains quantitative characterization of timbre residue in speech pre-training model content encoding, providing researchers with an objective, reusable evaluation benchmark. Using this benchmark, we tested the timbre residue amounts in 7 speech pre-training models from 5 types, validating the effectiveness of the benchmark.

2.

We propose an interpretability-based speech pre-training encoding timbre filtering method (InterpTF-SptME), providing two filtering approaches: "SHAP Noise" and "SHAP Cropping". By utilizing the timbre residue quantitative characterization obtained from the InterpTRQE-SptME Benchmark, we perform data transformations on intermediate layer encodings (content embeddings) of speech pre-training models to remove timbre information while maintaining content information integrity, achieving significant speaker disentanglement effect optimization. This is a model-agnostic, layer-agnostic, and data annotation-independent method with broad applicability. Experimental results on the VCTK dataset show that for HuBERT LARGE layer 21 embedding filtering, the SHAP Noise filtering method can reduce timbre residue from 18.05% to 0 at maximum, with some loss in speech recognition accuracy; tolerance for this accuracy loss can be adjusted within the range of 0-5.08

3.

During the experimental process, we discovered that the Hubert-Large-Finetuned model uses layer 21 output to extract representations for speech recognition tasks, but HuBERT LARGE layer 21 encodes a higher proportion of timbre information compared to layer 18. Therefore, from the perspective of speaker disentanglement effect, we believe that HuBERT LARGE layer 18 is more suitable than layer 21 for executing content-related speech processing tasks. The SHAP Noise method significantly outperforms SHAP Cropping, indicating that parts with negative SHAP values in timbre residue representations also have positive effects on removing timbre residue, which may stem from timbre’s attention to relative intensity and phase differences of different frequency components.

6.2
Future Work

This paper focuses on research around using interpretability methods to improve speaker disentanglement effects of speech pre-training models, achieving certain results and completing experimental validation and analysis. For possible improvement directions and further work in the future, we propose the following thoughts and summaries:

1.

The voiceprint extraction model and speaker classification model in the interpretability representation framework of this paper are implemented in relatively simple ways. We recommend further exploration of replacing them with domain SOTA (State of the Art) models, or training them separately to be more suitable for interpretability analysis. Better voiceprint extraction models can make interpretation information represent more timbre residue information, which will benefit further improvement of model speaker disentanglement effects using interpretation information. Speaker classification models focus on adapting to the interpretation capability of interpretability methods. Overly complex models will lead to insufficient interpretation capability of interpretability methods, while overly simple methods may not effectively judge the essence of timbre information, so a balance needs to be achieved.

2.

This paper uses the English speech dataset VCTK to conduct interpretability representation experiments, which only quantifies the English speech encoding timbre residue proportion of models. For multilingual models or Chinese models, we recommend exploring the optimization of evaluation dataset design by referencing model vocabularies in certain ways.

3.

The timbre filtering parameters in this paper, including noise scale sigma and cropping intensity cut_weight, are selected through parameter enumeration experiments. Consider designing a complete toolkit to select appropriate filtering parameters for different speech pre-training models.

4.

Limited by computational resources, the experimental dataset in this paper consists of only 7,758 samples from the VCTK dataset, approximately 8 hours of speech data, belonging to small-sample experimental testing. Consider conducting validation testing on larger-scale annotated datasets to prevent situations not considered in this paper and to apply more confidently to extensive unlabeled data.

5.

Consider using filtered content embeddings to improve model performance when executing content-related speech processing tasks. For example, using filtered content embeddings as training samples to fine-tune speech pre-training models for ASR tasks, expecting to achieve better speech recognition accuracy; using filtered clustering-generated labels from HuBERT BASE layer 9 embeddings to replace HuBERT LARGE’s original training objectives, improving the model’s speaker disentanglement effect and performance on content-related downstream tasks. Of course, this also needs to consider the computational cost brought by the SHAP interpretation generation process.

6.

During the experimental process, we discovered that the Hubert-Large-Finetuned model uses layer 21 output to extract representations for speech recognition tasks, but HuBERT LARGE layer 21 encodes a higher proportion of timbre information compared to layer 18. Therefore, from the perspective of speaker disentanglement effect, we believe that HuBERT LARGE layer 18 is more suitable than layer 21 for executing content-related speech processing tasks. Of course, this needs further verification.

7.

Due to the introduction and training of a speaker classification model in the interpretability framework proposed in this paper, interpretation scores cannot be directly obtained and incorporated into the model training process, so we chose data transformation methods to utilize interpretation information. Consider using LRP and other interpretation methods directly related to neural network models, rather than post-hoc interpretation methods, to obtain interpretation information that directly acts on model training, optimization, and fine-tuning processes.
