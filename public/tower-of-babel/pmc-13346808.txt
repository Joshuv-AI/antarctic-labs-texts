Serum electrolyte disturbances and treatment outcomes in hospitalized children with Severe Acute Malnutrition in Ethiopia
Authors: Molla Hailu, Woldeteklehaymanote Kassahun, Hassen Ahmed Yesuf, Animaw Lingerew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere Acute Malnutrition [SAM] is a leading cause of pediatric morbidity and mortality, particularly in low-resource settings. Electrolyte disturbances significantly influence treatment outcomes but are often underemphasized in clinical protocols. Understanding these disturbances and their impact on recovery is critical to improving management strategies and survival rates among children with SAM. To assess serum electrolyte and their association with treatment outcomes among children with SAM. A prospective cohort study was conducted among 110 under five children who were admitted with SAM in Woldia comprehensive specialized Hospital, Ethiopia. Serum electrolytes were measured at admission using standard laboratory procedures. multinomial logistic regression was employed to evaluate predictors of treatment outcomes, categorized as recovery, default, transferred out, and died. Of the 110 participants, 85.5% were transferred out after improvement, 8.2% died, and 6.4% defaulted. Hyponatremia, observed in 55.5% of study participants, was the strongest predictor of mortality with 77.8% of children who died having sodium levels below 135 mmol/L. Serum electrolyte disturbances significantly influence treatment outcomes in children with SAM, with hyponatremia being the most critical predictors of mortality. Integrating electrolyte management into SAM protocols and addressing comorbidities can enhance recovery rates and reduce mortality.Keywords: Severe Acute Malnutrition, Under-five Children, Serum Electrolytes, Treatment OutcomeSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Background

Proper nutrition during the initial five years of life has significant public health consequences, playing a key role in the morbidity and mortality of neonates, infants, and young children, and influencing long-term outcomes in physical growth, cognitive development, academic performance, and workforce productivity1.

Malnutrition refers to a condition where an individual’s nutritional status is not optimal, leading to potential impairments in health and growth. This condition can arise from illnesses that affect nutritional intake and metabolism, or from insufficient intake of macronutrients and micronutrients.Acute malnutrition, also known as wasting, is a type of undernutrition characterized by a low weight-for-height ratio or the occurrence of nutritional edema2. Acute malnutrition can be classified into moderate acute malnutrition or severe acute malnutrition (SAM) depending on its level of severity. Marasmus and kwashiorkor (edematous) are the two classical clinical forms of severe protein energy undernutrition3,4. Marasmus reflects a generalized deficiency of all macronutrients, producing extreme wasting of fat and muscle and an emaciated appearance, often with profound impacts on growth and organ function. Kwashiorkor, in contrast, is marked by severe protein deficiency frequently occurring in contexts where caloric intake may still include carbohydrates and is distinguished clinically by fluid retention and swelling with a deceptively distended abdomen5,6. In general, distinguishing between marasmus and kwashiorkor offers little practical benefit, as modern WHO guidelines classify both under severe acute malnutrition and apply the same clinical criteria and treatment approach7. The combined forms of severe acute malnutrition is an urgent nutritional crisis marked by a weight-for-height Z score of less than − 3, a mid-upper arm circumference of less than 11.5 cm, and the presence of bilateral nutritional edema8.

Outcomes in severe acute malnutrition depend largely on early detection, prompt therapeutic feeding, and effective management of complications9,10. Current studies also indicate that disturbances in serum electrolytes including sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, chloride, and bicarbonate are major contributors to poor outcomes in children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM)11. These electrolytes are essential for maintaining cellular electrical neutrality, supporting nerve impulse transmission, regulating muscle contraction, and preserving fluid balance and acid–base homeostasis12–14. Electrolyte homeostasis is normally maintained through dietary intake, gastrointestinal absorption, renal regulation, and intracellular–extracellular shifts15,16. Both forms of SAM: Marasmus and kwashiorkor significantly alter serum electrolyte levels due to impaired nutrient intake, disrupted gastrointestinal absorption, and altered renal and cellular handling17. In marasmus, total body deficits of sodium, potassium, magnesium, and phosphate are pronounced because of severe wasting and muscle loss, even when serum levels appear normal18,19. On the other hand, In kwashiorkor, edema, oxidative stress, and protein deficiency further disturb electrolyte distribution, often leading to hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and acid–base imbalances. These abnormalities contribute to the high risk of metabolic complications, infections, and mortality observed in severe acute malnutrition20,21. As a result, both hypo- and hyper-electrolyte abnormalities are common in children with SAM and are strongly associated with increased morbidity and mortality22.

Despite the recognized importance of electrolytes in SAM pathophysiology, their specific roles in predicting treatment outcome remains under explored in many clinical contexts, including low-income countries such as Ethiopia. Understanding the prevalence and impact of serum electrolyte disturbances during hospitalization for SAM is critical for guiding clinical management, optimizing treatment protocols, and ultimately improving survival rates23.

Statement of the problem

One of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) is to reduce under-five mortality to no more than 25 deaths per 1,000 live births by 2030, with a focus on eliminating preventable child deaths24. How ever, acute malnutrition affects over 17 million children worldwide, resulting in approximately 2.9 million hospitalizations. Of these hospitalized cases, around 17% require immediate intervention8. In Africa, 14 million children under five are suffering from acute malnutrition, with SAM representing around 4.1 million of those cases25. The likelihood of marasmus among children under five varies considerably across regions, affecting roughly 22% in West Africa, 43% in East Africa, and 6% throughout sub-Saharan Africa, underscoring pronounced regional disparities in severe acute malnutrition26. On the other hand, the prevalence of kwashiorker among the Congo is estimated upto 14.9%27 and 1.6% in Bengoa6. In Ethiopia, more than 25,000 children suffering from severe acute malnutrition are admitted to hospitals each month28.

In low- and middle-income countries, inpatient mortality for severe acute malnutrition ranges from 3.5% to 35%. However, adherence to WHO clinical guidelines can reduce these rates to between 1% and 5%29. In Ethiopia, the mortality rate among children under five admitted with severe acute malnutrition is notably high, estimated at around 11.3%30.

Among the factors that increase the mortality of children with SAM, electrolyte disturbance and metabolic acidosis contribute to the major role which accounts for about 80% of death31,32. Severe acute malnutrition is highly associated with disturbance of the different forms of electrolytes, such as hypokalemia, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, hypernatremia, and hypocalcemia33. The mortality of under-five children admitted with SAM that have disturbed serum electrolytes might increase to 50% as compared to those with normal serum electrolytes34,35.

Reducing mortality among children under five requires evidence-based strategies, including updating medication guidelines for malnourished children, establishing clear criteria for admission and discharge in nutrition programs, and integrating the management of moderate acute malnutrition into routine health services. Identifying children at the highest risk as early as possible is crucial to ensure they receive the care and attention they urgently need. Reliable biomarkers that can signal potential complications, both before and during treatment, are therefore essential. Serum electrolyte profiling could provide a simple and cost-effective way to anticipate adverse outcomes. Children with severe acute malnutrition often present as either marasmus or kwashiorkor, and these different forms can affect electrolyte balance and overall prognosis. Yet, there is still a shortage of research exploring how serum electrolyte levels relate to treatment outcomes in these vulnerable children. Therefore, This study aims to address this knowledge gap by analyzing serum electrolyte profiles in hospitalized children with SAM at Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia, and examining how electrolyte disturbances relate to treatment outcomes. The findings are expected to provide actionable evidence to improve clinical care for this vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

Study setting and design

An institution-based prospective observational cohort was conducted from February to August 2024 at Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, a major referral facility located in Woldia town, North Wollo Zone of the Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia. The hospital provides pediatric and general medical services to an estimated catchment population of over one million people drawn from Woldia town and neighboring districts, as well as nearby areas of the Afar and Tigray regions. The catchment area also hosts several refugee camps that accommodate displaced populations affected by conflict and recurrent drought, which contribute to high rates of food insecurity and malnutrition. The region’s economy is largely agrarian, and seasonal fluctuations in food availability often result in peaks of child undernutrition during the pre-harvest period. Woldia Hospital operates a dedicated inpatient therapeutic feeding unit (TFU) within its pediatric ward, staffed by trained pediatric nurses and physicians who follow national and WHO guidelines for the management of severe acute malnutrition. The hospital provides comprehensive care, including nutritional rehabilitation, management of comorbid infections, and laboratory support for biochemical and hematologic investigations.

Study population

All under-five children diagnosed with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and admitted consecutively to the inpatient therapeutic feeding unit of Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital during the study period were considered for inclusion.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Children aged 6 months to 5 years with SAM, defined according to WHO criteria (weight-for-height Z-score < − 3 SD, mid-upper arm circumference < 11.5 cm, or bilateral pitting edema).

Exclusion criteria yyy

Children with chronic illnesses such as chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, or other persistent metabolic disorders, as well as critically ill children whose conditions could confound the relationship between electrolyte disturbances and treatment outcomes.

The exclusion criteria aimed to ensure a homogenous study population in which observed treatment outcomes primarily reflected the influence of electrolyte status rather than the effects of unrelated critical conditions.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Treatment outcome (children who transferred out, defaulted, died or cured at the end of the treatment).

Independent variables

Serum electrolytes, socio-demographic variables, co-morbidities, type of malnutrition, and length of stay in hospital.

Operational definitions

Severe acute malnutrition is defined by the presence of bilateral pitting edema or severe wasting [i.e., MUAC < 11.50 cm or WFH < − 3 Z score]36.

Transfer out:

A child with SAM transferred from Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital to other nearby health centers after achieving clinical improvement, where they continue receiving nutritional care and monitoring.

Defaulter:

A child who discontinued inpatient SAM treatment before reaching discharge criteria and whose outcome status could not be determined.

Disturbed serum electrolytes:

Refers to any abnormality characterized by either an increase or a decrease in the serum levels of one or more key electrolytes, including sodium (Na), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and chloride (Cl), outside their normal reference ranges.

Undisturbed serum electrolytes:

Indicates that all measured serum electrolytes (Na, K, Ca, Cl) are within their respective normal reference ranges.

Serum electrolytes: sodium, potassium, calcium, and chloride.

Sample size determination and sampling procedures

Since the study attempts to compare the treatment outcome of SAM among children with disturbed and undisturbed serum electrolytes, the sample size was calculated using the formula of hypothesis testing for double population proportion.Therefore, the total sample size was 110 together with 10% lost to follow-up.

Sampling techniques

All under-five children with SAM who fulfill the inclusion criteria and their guardians/caregivers are volunteered to take part in the study were selected consecutively until the sample size was saturated.

Data collection tool and process

The data for this study were collected using structured data collection tools designed to capture a comprehensive set of variables related to socio-demographic information, nutritional status, clinical assessments, laboratory tests, and medical conditions beginning at admission and extending through follow-up periods. The process of data collection was organized into the following key components.

Socio-demographic and clinical data collection

The interviewer administered questionnaire which was adapted from literature was used for data collection. The questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% of participants in Kobo Primary Hospital to assess clarity and completeness before being used in the actual study. Socio-demographic data such as the child’s age, sex, and place of residence, as well as socio-economic factors, including the parent’s or caregiver’s level of education, household income, and employment status data, was collected via face-to-face interviews with the child’s primary caregiver. Additionally, the study collected detailed nutritional history, focusing on the child’s feeding practices before admission, including breastfeeding duration, complementary feeding, and any significant dietary changes; all might have influenced the treatment outcomes of the study participants.

Information on recent illnesses such as diarrhea, vomiting, cough, and fever was obtained from caregivers using a structured questionnaire at admission. Caregivers were asked whether the child had experienced each symptom and, if yes, for how long (hours, days, or weeks) prior to admission. Because the reported durations varied substantially across participants, symptoms were coded dichotomously as present or absent regardless of duration for statistical analysis. This approach was chosen to minimize recall bias and to capture the general presence of acute or subacute illness that could influence treatment outcomes.

In parallel, the clinical assessment was done by two experienced pediatric nurses who work in the pediatrics ward and attending physicians. The data collectors have assessed all the newly admitted children’s eligibility for inclusion into the study based on the predefined criteria. The clinical assessment began with gathering the child’s history and symptoms reported by the caregiver, including feeding difficulties, previous illnesses, and any recent changes in the child’s condition. Then, a physical examination was followed, which included recording of vital signs, anthropometric measurements, assessment of hydration status through signs of dehydration [such as reduced skin turgor, dry mucous membranes, and sunken eyes], the presence of edema, particularly bilateral edema or pitting edema.

Anthropometric measures, including MUAC and WHZ, were recorded as continuous variables at admission. Edema status was recorded dichotomously (present or absent), without grading by severity. We did not prospectively classify MUAC and WHZ into categorical groups at enrollment. Summary statistics (mean, median, range) are reported for these variables in the results. Following the history and physical examination, laboratory tests such as levels of hemoglobin, blood glucose concentration, white blood cell count, and serum electrolytes [sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride] were performed.

After the initial assessment, children were classified based on their serum electrolyte levels as children with disturbed and undisturbed serum electrolytes. Then, each child was closely followed up throughout their hospitalization to monitor treatment progress, and the treatment outcomes were divided as transferred out with improvement, died, and defaulter. The treatment outcome data were systematically recorded from the participant’s medical record sheet, which had been prepared at the time of admission. Key variables, including nutritional status [measured through weight, MUAC, and edema], hydration status, and vital signs, were continuously monitored during the follow-up period. In cases whose adverse clinical implications were identified, the information was promptly communicated to the attending physician, ensuring that timely interventions were provided. The recorded data were cross-checked by the study supervisor for accuracy and completeness, ensuring the integrity of the data while upholding ethical standards by prioritizing the health and well-being of the children.

Sample collection procedure and laboratory analysis

Approximately five milliliters of the venous blood sample was taken from each study participant by trained laboratory technicians using a clean syringe with a needle under possible aseptic techniques. The sample was poured into a sterile serum separator tube [SST] and immediately transported to the laboratory for serum electrolyte analysis. Serum electrolytes were determined for the concentration of Sodium, Potassium, magnesium, calcium, hydrogen, chloride, and Bicarbonate. Serum electrolyte analysis was done in Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital’s laboratory by strictly following laboratory standard operation procedures [SOP]. Individual study participants with abnormal test findings were linked to the treating health professional and necessary health education was given to the caregivers.

All study participants received standard inpatient care for severe acute malnutrition according to the Ethiopian national protocol and WHO guidelines. Management included nutritional rehabilitation, cautious rehydration, infection treatment, and correction of electrolyte and micronutrient imbalances. Serum electrolyte measurements were performed as part of routine clinical monitoring at admission to guide treatment decisions. The study did not alter or interfere with the standard clinical management process. Abnormal electrolyte results identified during analysis were promptly communicated to the attending pediatrician. Corrective management—such as cautious sodium correction, potassium supplementation, or fluid therapy—was implemented in line with the hospital’s standard treatment protocols. These actions were part of routine clinical care and not introduced specifically for research purposes.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data was cleaned, coded, and entered into SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were computed for socio-demographic characteristics, clinical variables, and serum electrolytes of the study participants.

To identify the predictors of the treatment outcomes, multinomial logistic regression was used with “transferred out” as the reference category. To determine the strength and significance of associations, adjusted relative risk ratios [aRRR] with 95% confidence intervals [CI] and p-values were reported. The model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using pseudo-R-squared values and likelihood ratio tests, while the independence of irrelevant alternatives [IIA] assumption was evaluated with the Hausman-McFadden test indirectly by comparing the full model [including all three outcome categories] with a restricted model [excluding the “defaulter” category]. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

A formal institutional collaboration letter was obtained from the Woldia University Research Publication, System, and Ethics Directorate office on December 20/2023 [ethical approval number WU/256/23] and was submitted to the Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. After a brief description of the study objectives, purpose, confidentiality, and benefits of the study, permission was sought from the study institution to conduct this study. Written informed consent was taken from the children’s parents or primary caregivers during the data collection. It was also explained to the participant’s parents/caregivers that there was no intention to collect additional information from the study participants other than the stated objectives. It was also explained that the results will be kept confidential.

Discussion

Severe Acute Malnutrition [SAM] remains one of the most pressing global public health challenges, with devastating effects on child mortality, particularly in low-resource settings such as sub-Saharan Africa37. This study aimed to explore the prevalence of serum electrolyte disturbances and their associations with the treatment outcomes in children with SAM admitted at Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. Our findings reveal the complex interaction of demographic factors, comorbidities, dehydration, and electrolyte imbalances in determining the clinical outcomes of SAM, with a critical focus on the importance of timely management of these factors in improving recovery and survival rates.

Electrolyte disturbances are one of the hallmark features of SAM and highlight their critical role in influencing clinical outcomes. This study found that sodium disturbances were the most common electrolyte abnormality, affecting 55.5% of participants. This result is consistent with finding from a study conducted in Pakistan, which found electrolyte disturbances in 93.5% of children with SAM, including high rates hyponatremia (48.9%)38. A plausible explanation for electrolyte disturbances in children with SAM is the combined effect of poor dietary intake of specific minerals, increased losses from vomiting or diarrhea, impaired kidney function, and altered hormonal regulation39. On the other hand, this finding is higher than the finding of the study conducted in pakistan which revealed (32.60% of the participants had hyponatremia33. One possible explanation is the difference in study settings. The reference study was conducted among children admitted to a nutritional stabilization center, where structured and comprehensive care is routinely provided, which may have influenced the reported outcomes.In addition, the reference study included patients with diarrhea, who were likely treated with ReSoMal. Because ReSoMal contains a low concentration of sodium, its use is frequently associated with hyponatremia40. The effect of sodium level disturbance on the treatment outcome of SAM patient was assessed. Hyponatremia was strongly associated with increased mortality, with 12.7% of affected children dying.These findings are consistent with previous studies by Kumar et al., [2019] which identified sodium imbalances as a major predictor of mortality in SAM children41. A plausible explanation is that sodium is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis, osmotic balance, and vascular volume. Sodium depletion can lead to life-threatening complications, including edema and impaired cardiac function, which may exacerbate malnutrition through fluid shifts, tissue hypoxia, and organ dysfunction42. On the other hand, hypernatremia was less common affecting only 5.5% of the children. This finding aligns with another study reporting a hypernatremia prevalence of approximately 6%. The lower prevalence may be explained by physiological compensatory mechanisms, including increased thirst and reduced renal sodium excretion, which help maintain sodium balance43. Hypernatremia in children with Severe Acute Malnutrition [SAM] is often associated with severe dehydration, which results from insufficient fluid intake or excessive fluid loss. This condition is exacerbated by poor access to rehydration therapy, especially in resource-limited settings where timely and adequate fluid management may not be available44.

Hypokalemia also became the major electrolyte problem affecting 13.6% of the study participants. This finding is inligh with the study findings from Ethiopia43,45.A plausible explanation for this association could be the shift of potassium from the extracellular to the intracellular compartment, which is commonly observed in malnourished children due to metabolic adaptations and insulin-mediated potassium uptake during nutritional rehabilitation46. How ever, This finding is lower than the study conducted in Pakistan which reported 70% of the participants are hypokalemic33.The plausible justification could be the difference in the study design, sample size and the eligibility criteria among the studies. Low potassium levels are associated with severe clinical outcomes, including cardiac arrhythmias, muscle weakness, and respiratory failure, all of which contribute to increased mortality in children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM). In this study, 33.3% of children with low potassium (< 3.5 mmol/L) died. These findings are consistent with previous research, such as Alasad et al. (2023), which highlighted hypokalemia as a common and potentially fatal electrolyte disturbance in malnourished children. This may be explained by the critical role of potassium in maintaining cardiac and neuromuscular function, where deficiency can rapidly precipitate life-threatening complications47,48.

Hyperkalemia was less common in our study, affecting only 1.8% of participants. However, it is still a notable electrolyte disturbance that warrants attention. This finding is contradicted with the finding from the previous study by Ngwenya N, have shown that hyperkalemia is a common complication in children with SAM49. This discrepancy may be due to the inclusion of study participants receiving treatment, resulting in closer follow-up by the physician.In patients with SAM, hyperkalemia may result from kidney dysfunction, excessive potassium intake, or the body’s inability to excrete potassium properly due to dehydration or acute renal failure50. An elevated potassium level can cause serious complications, including cardiac arrhythmias, muscle weakness, and paralysis51.

In our study, chloride disturbances were found in 22.7% of the children with SAM. Among these, 21.4% of those with low chloride levels [< 96 mmol/L] experienced mortality, while only 9.1% of those with high chloride levels [> 108 mmol/L] died. This suggests that low chloride levels were more strongly associated with an increased risk of mortality. The possible justification could be chloride is essential for maintaining fluid balance, acid-base homeostasis, and cellular function. Disturbances in chloride levels can affect cellular function and respiratory status, as well as contribute to complications like metabolic acidosis or alkalosis, which in turn may exacerbate the clinical course of SAM, especially in the context of other electrolyte abnormalities. The mortality risk observed in our study aligns with findings from other research, such as Eisenhut M, which highlighted the potential dangers of electrolyte imbalances in SAM patients52.

Similarly, calcium disturbances were observed in 23.6% of participants, with hypocalcemia strongly linked to increased mortality. Specifically, 9.1% of children with low calcium levels died, underscoring the critical role of calcium imbalance in worsening the clinical outcomes of SAM. This finding is consistent with research conducted by Chisti et al., [2023], which highlights that hypocalcemia significantly exacerbates mortality risk in malnourished children, impairing cardiovascular and neuromuscular functions. The study emphasizes that calcium deficiency, particularly when combined with other electrolyte disturbances, contributes to a higher risk of mortality due to complications such as arrhythmias, seizures, and respiratory failure53. This reinforces the necessity for prompt detection and management of calcium disturbances in malnourished children to mitigate the risk of fatal outcomes, as this imbalance plays a pivotal role in influencing overall survival.

The demographic characteristics of children, specifically age and gender were found to significantly influence SAM outcomes. Age is significantly associated with poor outcomes in our study. Of the total deaths recorded, 88.9% occurred in children under two years of age. This finding aligns with a study by Kerac et al.,[ 2014] which reported significantly worse outcomes in younger children with SAM54. The proposed mechanism behind this heightened risk involves the immaturity of their immune systems, greater nutritional demands, and reduced physiological reserves, making them less capable of withstanding the complications of severe malnutrition55,56. In addition, males have higher mortality rate than females, which aligns with findings from other studies56. This may be attributed to the fact that the male immune system is often less responsive than the female immune system, due to the immunosuppressive effects of testosterone, resulting in increased susceptibility to infections and delayed recovery from malnutrition57,58. These findings underscore the importance of considering gender-specific interventions when addressing SAM in resource-constrained settings.

The effect of clinical parameters on the treatment outcome of the patient with SAM were also assessed. In this study, the duration of hospitalization was a significant predictor of mortality, with each extra day in the hospital associated with a 19% higher likelihood of death.This is consistent with a study by Ashine et al. [2020], which found that longer Hospital stays in SAM patients increased mortality due to cumulative complications59. The possible justification could be prolonged hospitalization in children with SAM reflects greater disease severity, complications, and a higher risk of hospital-acquired infections [HAIs], which contribute to adverse outcomes60,61. This study also underscores diarrhea as a predictor of mortality among SAM patients.It accounts about 8.2% of the total study population. This finding is in line with a study conducted by Grenov et al., [2019], which highlighted diarrhea as a leading cause of mortality in children with SAM62. Diarrhea may exacerbate malnutrition by causing substantial fluid and nutrient losses, leading to dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, and multi-organ dysfunction in vulnerable children. These findings highlight the importance of timely and effective management of diarrhea in children with SAM to reduce mortality and improve outcomes63,64. In addition, anemia was a prevalent comorbidity in this study, affecting 60.9% of children. These findings align with other studies that observed a strong link between anemia and malnutrition in children under five65. The possible justification could be the reduced oxygen-carrying capacity associated with anemia can hinder recovery, impair immune responses, and worsen the severity of malnutrition66.Therefore, this study demonstrates that clinical factors such as extended hospital stay, diarrhea, and anemia substantially influence mortality among children with SAM, underscoring the importance of vigilant monitoring, prompt management of complications, and integrated care strategies to enhance survival and recovery.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strength of the study

This study provides valuable insights into the effect of serum electrolyte disturbances on the treatment outcomes of children with SAM. By utilizing a prospective observational cohort study design, the research captures dynamic changes in electrolyte levels and their direct impact on patient recovery, defaulting, and mortality. The study’s inclusion of multiple electrolytes, sodium, potassium, calcium, and chloride offers a comprehensive understanding of their roles in malnutrition-related complications. Additionally, the use of standard laboratory procedures for electrolyte measurement enhances the reliability of findings, making them applicable for clinical decision-making. Importantly, the study accounts for key confounders such as socio-demographic factors, comorbidities, and length of hospital stay, ensuring that the observed associations between electrolyte disturbances and treatment outcomes are more robust and adjusted for potential biases.

Limitations of the study

Despite its strengths, this study has some limitations. First, it was conducted in a single hospital setting, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare systems with different resources and practices. Second, although children who showed initial improvement were transferred to nearby health centers, their outcomes were not tracked. As a result, we could not assess their long-term recovery or potential relapse. Third, the study design was observational, which restricts causal inference between electrolyte disturbances and treatment outcomes. Forth, MUAC and WHZ values and edema status were recorded using continuous values and binary presence/absence, respectively, rather than prespecified categories. This restricts our ability to present baseline characteristics in clinically relevant groupings. Finally, some potentially relevant factors, such as micronutrient deficiencies, socioeconomic status, and underlying comorbidities beyond those recorded, were not fully assessed. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusions

Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) remains a critical challenge in pediatric healthcare, with serum electrolyte disturbances strongly associated with adverse treatment outcomes. This study highlights hyponatremia as the strongest predictor of mortality, followed by hypokalemia and hypocalcemia, particularly among children under two years of age. Prolonged hospital stays, likely reflecting greater disease severity and the risk of hospital-acquired complications, were also linked with higher mortality.

These findings support the importance of routine monitoring and timely correction of electrolyte imbalances in the management of SAM, alongside the management of common comorbidities such as diarrhea and anemia. While further multicenter and longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these results and explore causal mechanisms, our findings contribute practical evidence to improve SAM care strategies, especially in resource-constrained settings.
