Early Outer Hair Cell Dysfunction Associated With Educational Noise Exposure in Dental Students: Implications for Hearing Conservation in Dental Education
Authors: Merve Yelken Kendirci, Mehtap Ungor, Handan Turan Dizdar, Ahmet Faruk Erturk, Ilknur Ozcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDental education involves continuous exposure to high‐frequency noise from clinical instruments, which may cause early outer hair cell damage even before professional practice begins. While noise‐induced hearing loss (NIHL) is a recognised occupational hazard among dentists, evidence regarding subclinical outer hair cell changes during undergraduate training remains limited. As this study did not include a non‐dental, age‐matched control group, the observed differences are reported as an association with year of dental education rather than as proof of a causal effect of educational noise exposure.Materials and MethodsThis comparative cross‐sectional study evaluated 140 dental students: 88 first‐year and 52 fifth‐year at Biruni University. Pure‐tone and high‐frequency audiometry, transient‐evoked otoacoustic emissions (TEOAE) and distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAE) were measured to assess auditory function. Environmental noise levels in preclinical and clinical settings were recorded over an 8‐h period using calibrated sound level meters.ResultsAverage noise exposure was 77.1 dB in preclinical and 81.2 dB in clinical settings. While no significant differences were observed in conventional audiometric frequencies, fifth‐year students showed significantly elevated hearing thresholds at 9–20 kHz (p < 0.05). TEOAE and DPOAE signal‐to‐noise ratios were markedly reduced in fifth‐year students, indicating early outer hair cell dysfunction (reduced cochlear amplifier function).DiscussionThese findings reveal subtle yet measurable outer hair cell changes associated with advancing dental education. Such early alterations, undetectable by standard audiometry, suggest that dental training environments may pose a latent risk for NIHL.ConclusionsThis study provides early evidence of an association between advancing dental education and subclinical outer hair cell dysfunction in dental students, emphasising the need to integrate hearing conservation and noise‐awareness education into dental curricula to protect future professionals' auditory health.Keywords: dental education, noise‐induced hearing loss, occupational exposure, otoacoustic emissions, outer hair cell dysfunction

Introduction

Noise pollution has evolved into a daily challenge affecting all demographic segments, attributable to environmental factors, street commotion, railway and aviation noise, in addition to industrial sources, and the consumption of diverse media and music [1]. In a study by Lewis et al. [2], the risk of permanent noise‐induced hearing loss was estimated for a large urban setting. They found that, in addition to noise pollution from occupational activities, exposure to noise from ‘non‐occupational activities’ (e.g., listening to music, use of mass transit) added substantially to the risk of hearing impairment.

Prolonged exposure to high noise levels among healthcare professionals may have a negative effect on hearing [3]. Physical consequences can include tinnitus, hearing impairment, hypertension, annoyance and sleep disturbance [4]. It is well known that generally high sound levels have a negative effect on extra‐auditory systems. These effects occur especially at noise levels above 85 dB(A) [5]. Noise‐induced hearing loss (NIHL) is the most common type of acquired hearing impairment; importantly, it is largely preventable through early awareness and intervention. The extent of noise‐induced hearing loss depends on the intensity, duration and frequency of the noise. One such occupation at risk for noise‐induced hearing loss consists of the individuals in the field of dentistry. Occupational noise exposure in dentistry is a result of the equipment and machinery used in clinics. Clinical handpieces, turbines, drills, suction devices and other equipment such as laboratory motors are all responsible for emitting loud noises. Studies have found that dental instruments and dental environments exceed the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) standards for daily permissible noise exposure, and hearing loss among dental students and professionals is most likely correlated with the noise levels present in the clinic.

Presently, a paucity of comprehensive studies exists to ascertain the awareness level among dentists and dental students concerning their routine exposure to potentially hazardous noise levels. Despite recurring advisories from the American Dental Association emphasising the use of personal hearing protection by dentists as a preventive measure, the significance of hearing protection remains somewhat understated. Augmenting knowledge about noise exposure and the necessity of hearing protection could be achieved through educational regulations aimed at raising awareness [6]. The curriculum of dental schools encompasses both theoretical and practical classes, inherently necessitating the use of noise‐emitting apparatus such as clinical handpieces, turbines and laboratory engines [5]. The acoustic environment within dental practical classrooms is characterised by elevated noise levels compared to other educational spaces, primarily due to the simultaneous operation of noise‐producing dental equipment by multiple individuals. This situation is exacerbated when classroom surfaces are composed of hard materials, acting as sound reflectors. Messano and Petti [7] noted that even a single day spent in the clinic resulted in hearing impairment among dental students. Consequently, it is reasonable to deduce that prolonged exposure to high noise levels by both students and instructors will inherently yield discernible adverse effects. However, the precise impact of noise exposure stemming from dental activities on the hearing thresholds of dental students throughout their undergraduate training remains inadequately explored. In dental education, students' exposure to occupational noise varies significantly across different years of study. First‐year students participate in practical sessions only two half‐days per week, and since these sessions do not always involve the use of high‐frequency dental instruments, their exposure to occupational noise remains minimal. In contrast, fifth‐year students actively treat patients in the clinic for five half‐days per week, following a similar schedule in their fourth year. Consequently, as students progress through their education, their cumulative exposure to dental workplace noise increases substantially, potentially impacting their hearing health over time. Despite numerous studies on occupational hearing loss in practicing dentists, limited evidence exists regarding early cochlear changes during dental education—a critical period when preventive strategies can be most effective. Although previous research has investigated occupational noise exposure among practicing dentists, few studies have explored early cochlear changes in dental students. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate subclinical cochlear function in this group using a combination of high‐frequency audiometry and otoacoustic emission testing. Thus, the objective of this study is to ascertain and compare the hearing thresholds of first‐year undergraduate dental students with those in their fifth year at Biruni University Faculty of Dentistry, with the ultimate aim of implementing preventive interventions to avoid the onset of noise‐induced hearing loss before it occurs.

Material and Methods

This comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted between March 2022 and June 2022 and included 140 dental students, comprising 88 first‐year and 52 fifth‐year students. The inclusion criteria were no previous ear surgery, no congenital or acquired hearing impairment, and no history of chronic ear disease, ear trauma, ototoxic drug use or habitual loud‐music listening with earbuds. The ethics committee of Biruni University, Istanbul (Approval No: 2019/27‐15) and all participants provided informed consent. All participants underwent otoscopic examination, and all tests were performed by the same audiologist. Dental students were enrolled in this study, and after otoscopic examinations, their Pure‐Tone Thresholds (PTT), High‐Frequency Audiometry, Speech Audiometry, Transient Otoacoustic Emissions (TEOAE), Distortion Product Otoacoustic Emissions (DPOAE) and Tympanometry were assessed. Pure‐tone and high‐frequency audiometry tests were performed using an Interacoustics AC40 Hybrid Audiometer (Clinical Audiometer, Denmark), while TEOAE and DPOAE tests were conducted using a Madsen Capella 2 otoacoustic emission device (GN Otometrics, Taastrup, Denmark). Tympanometric assessments were carried out using a Madsen Otoflex 100 tympanometer (Otometrics) with Otosuite software.

The Pure‐Tone Audiometric Thresholds (PTA) between 125 Hz and 20 kHz were analysed, along with Speech Recognition Thresholds (SRT), Speech Discrimination [8] and Uncomfortable Loudness Level (UCL) tests. TEOAEs were conducted using an 85 dB SPL click stimulus. TEOAEs were recorded at 1000, 1500, 2000, 3000 and 4000 Hz. DPOAEs were measured at frequencies of 996, 1416, 2002, 2832, 4004, 5654 and 7998 Hz. In the DPOAE test, L1 was set as 65 dB and L2 55 dB. Emission responses at frequencies of 2F1‐F2 were recorded. The f2/f1 ratio was 1.22.

The mean hearing threshold values for each test and frequency were compared between fifth‐year dental students and first‐year students, who served as the control group. The results of the pure‐tone threshold test, acoustic reflex threshold and otoacoustic emission test were evaluated to assess auditory function in the participants. The noise levels in dental pre‐clinic and clinical settings, identified as potential sources of hazardous noise exposure, were measured using the NIOSH (US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) sound level meter application for iOS devices. To ensure accuracy, this application was calibrated daily using a Brüel & Kjær Type 4231 acoustic calibrator (Brüel & Kjær Sound & Vibration Measurement A/S, Denmark) and cross‐validated with a Brüel & Kjær 2250 Sound Level Meter before each measurement session. Noise exposure measurements were conducted at identical locations in first‐year pre‐clinics and fifth‐year student clinics over an 8‐h period, with readings taken hourly. In the fifth‐year student clinic, eight hourly measurements ranged from 74.3 to 83 dB, with an average noise level of 81.2 dB. Similarly, in the first‐year pre‐clinic, hourly noise levels ranged from 67.9 to 82 dB. with an average exposure of 77.1 dB Only equivalent continuous A‐weighted sound levels (LAeq) were recorded in this study; a detailed spectral characterisation (octave‐band or spectrogram analysis) of the dominant frequency content generated by dental handpieces, turbines and suction units was not performed. Previous instrument‐level acoustic analyses have reported that high‐speed turbines and ultrasonic scalers generate substantial acoustic energy above 4 kHz, extending into the ultrasonic range [9, 10]; while this is broadly consistent with the high‐frequency pattern of threshold and otoacoustic emission changes observed in the present study, the absence of an octave‐band or spectrogram analysis of our own noise recordings limits our ability to directly link specific frequency bands of clinical noise to the audiometric findings, and this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Statistical Analysis (Data Analysis)

All test results of first‐year and fifth‐year dental students were statistically compared. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS v23. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The Independent Samples t‐test was used to compare normally distributed data between groups, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non‐normally distributed data. Within‐group comparisons between the right and left ears were conducted using the Paired Samples t‐test for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon test for non‐normally distributed data. Analysis results were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum) for quantitative variables. To minimise Type I error due to multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied separately within each family of frequency‐wise comparisons for a given test modality and ear (air‐conduction pure‐tone thresholds: 16 frequencies; TEOAE: 5 frequencies; DPOAE: 7 frequencies), yielding family‐wise adjusted significance thresholds of approximately p < 0.003 (0.05/16), p < 0.01 (0.05/5) and p < 0.007 (0.05/7), respectively. The p values reported in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 are raw (unadjusted) values; findings marked as significant at the conventional p < 0.05 level that do not remain significant after this stricter Bonferroni‐adjusted threshold should therefore be interpreted with caution, and are noted as such in the Discussion. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the primary, unadjusted analysis.

Results

In our study, a total of 140 dental students were evaluated, including 88 first‐year students (57 females, 31 males) and 52 fifth‐year students (30 females, 22 males). The mean age of first‐year students was 19 years (min: 18, max: 20), while the mean age of fifth‐year students was 24 years (min: 23, max: 25). No statistically significant difference was found in gender distribution within or between groups. Following otoscopic examinations, further audiological assessments were conducted. Air conduction thresholds (125–20 000 Hz) and bone conduction thresholds (500–4000 Hz) for both the right and left ears were analysed. The mean, minimum and maximum values, along with p values, are presented in Table 1. When comparing pure‐tone thresholds between first‐year and fifth‐year students, no statistically significant differences were observed in air conduction thresholds from 125 to 8000 Hz or in bone conduction thresholds from 500 to 4000 Hz (p > 0.05) (Figure 1).

Mean air‐conduction hearing thresholds (± SD, dB HL) of first‐year and fifth‐year dental students across test frequencies (125–20 000 Hz) for the right and left ears. Asterisks denote frequencies with a statistically significant between‐group difference (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.05).

However, air conduction thresholds in the right ear of fifth‐year students were significantly higher at 9000, 11 200, 12 000 and 14 000 Hz compared to first‐year students (p < 0.05). Additionally, at 16 000, 18 000 and 20 000 Hz, both right and left ears of fifth‐year students exhibited significantly higher thresholds than those of first‐year students (p < 0.05). In speech audiometry tests, no statistically significant differences were found between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1). Group‐level comparison of mean thresholds (Figure 1) showed a consistent pattern of elevated high‐frequency thresholds in fifth‐year students relative to first‐year students, most pronounced above 9000 Hz.

In the tympanometry test, no statistically significant differences were found between first‐year and fifth‐year students in ipsilateral reflex thresholds (p > 0.05) (Table 2). In the TEOAE test, signal‐to‐noise ratios (SNRs) at 1000, 1500, 3000 and 4000 Hz were significantly lower in fifth‐year students compared to first‐year students in both ears (p < 0.05). Although no statistically significant difference was found at 2000 Hz, the SNR of fifth‐year students at this frequency was still lower than that of first‐year students (p > 0.05) (Table 3). In the DPOAE test, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in SNRs at 2002, 4004 and 7998 Hz (p > 0.05). However, in fifth‐year students, SNRs at 996 and 5654 Hz in the left ear and 1416 Hz in the right ear were significantly lower than those of first‐year students (p < 0.05). Additionally, at 2832 Hz, fifth‐year students exhibited significantly lower SNRs in both ears compared to first‐year students (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

In summary, audiometric and otoacoustic results consistently demonstrated subtle yet measurable outer hair cell changes in fifth‐year students compared to first‐year students.

Discussion

There is considerable interest in the long‐term effects of high noise exposure in dentistry, as dental professionals frequently operate high‐noise equipment such as handpieces and ultrasonic scalers. However, there is no consensus in the literature regarding this issue. Recent guidelines suggest that while dentists are exposed to high levels of noise, the duration of exposure is insufficient to cause permanent auditory damage. During equipment testing in this study, the maximum recorded noise level was 107 dB(A) [2]. To minimise the risk of occupational noise‐induced hearing loss, it is recommended that noise levels be kept as low as possible. Sound insulation and absorption are essential strategies to mitigate noise transmission. Porous materials, such as fibreglass, can effectively absorb sound energy and convert it into heat [2]. Although dentistry is recognised as a prestigious profession, practitioners are frequently exposed to various occupational hazards, including infectious agents, musculoskeletal strain and psychological stress. Among these, exposure to high‐intensity noise is particularly concerning, as it may lead to cochlear damage and sensorineural hearing loss through the destruction of delicate inner ear structures [11, 12]. Noise not only affects auditory function but also induces systemic stress responses, such as elevated heart rate, blood pressure and mental fatigue. Importantly, repetitive noise exposure in the 3.0–6.0 kHz range often goes unnoticed until it affects speech discrimination. Therefore, monitoring early‐stage auditory changes using advanced diagnostic tools is essential to prevent irreversible damage [13]. This study uniquely examines dental students—a rarely investigated population—using a dual‐modality approach that enhances sensitivity for detecting early cochlear alterations associated with educational noise exposure.

A study by Kilpatrick [9] documented the noise levels of various dental office instruments, reporting 70–92 dB for high‐speed turbine handpieces, 86 dB for ultrasonic scalers and 74 dB for low‐speed handpieces. Furthermore, high‐speed dental turbines have been shown to produce noise levels of up to 32 kHz and 110 dB, increasing the risk of auditory complications [10]. In the study by Kilpatrick et al., it was reported that high‐speed dental turbines can produce noise levels of up to 110 dB and generate sounds at frequencies up to 32 kHz, posing a potential risk to auditory health. However, their study did not directly assess hearing loss. In contrast, our study compared the hearing thresholds of first‐year and fifth‐year dental students and found a significant increase in high‐frequency air conduction thresholds (9000–20 000 Hz) among fifth‐year students. Additionally, otoacoustic emission (OAE) tests revealed a marked reduction in signal‐to‐noise ratios among fifth‐year students. These findings suggest that prolonged exposure to dental noise may be associated with early outer hair cell damage and that otoacoustic emission testing can detect early auditory changes that may not be evident in conventional audiometric evaluations.

To our knowledge, no previous study has compared hearing function between first‐ and fifth‐year dental students using both high‐frequency audiometry and otoacoustic emission analysis. This dual‐modality approach enhances early detection sensitivity and provides a novel perspective on subclinical cochlear damage during dental education. As stated in the literature, dental practice environments are inherently noisy, and prolonged exposure to noise at certain intensities can lead to hearing loss [14]. When measuring the noise levels in the environments where students undergo training, first‐year students were exposed to an average of 77.1 dB, while fifth‐year students experienced 81.2 dB. According to NIOSH, these levels pose a risk for hearing loss. Both first‐year and fifth‐year students are exposed to noise during their education; however, in fifth‐year students, the cumulative effect of prolonged exposure makes this impact more noticeable. The literature indicates that oral health practitioners are frequently exposed to excessive noise levels exceeding the 85 dBA limit over an eight‐hour workday, thereby increasing their risk of noise‐induced hearing loss (NIHL). It is imperative that dental professionals recognise this occupational hazard and implement preventive strategies to mitigate the risk of hearing impairment. Effective preventive measures include hearing protection devices (HPDs), educational programs, insulated noise‐absorbing materials and regular monitoring of noise exposure [15].

Earplugs and earmuffs are widely used in industrial sectors where noise exposure is significantly higher for prolonged durations, and their use in dentistry has been recommended [16]. Additionally, dental professionals should maximise their distance from noise‐generating equipment, while maintaining appropriate ergonomic posture. A minimum distance of 35 cm between the operator's eye and the patient's mouth has been suggested [17]. Any unwanted sound produced in the workplace is classified as occupational noise [18], and hearing impairment due to occupational noise can affect not only the individual but also their family members [19] and coworkers [20]. Among various occupational noise sources, dental noise is one of the most frequently studied types [17]. Dental professionals are considered a high‐risk occupational group, as they are exposed to varying noise levels generated by high‐frequency dental equipment such as handpieces, ultrasonic devices, amalgamators, high‐speed suction units and compressors [21, 22, 23].

A self‐reported survey conducted in Thailand identified hearing loss as one of the most commonly reported issuesamong dental professionals [24]. Additionally, a study by McDonald et al. [25] among dentists and dental auxiliaries found that 16.6% of participants reported tinnitus, 30% experienced speech discrimination difficulties and 30.8% struggled with speech discrimination in noisy environments. In our study, we did not specifically assess the presence of tinnitus in participants; however, it should be noted that tinnitus may also occur, particularly in cases of high‐frequency hearing loss. Sensorineural hearing loss results from damage to the cochlear structures responsible for hearing. The most prevalent type is age‐related hearing loss (presbycusis), followed by noise‐induced hearing loss (NIHL). NIHL occurs due to damage to the delicate structures of the inner ear caused by prolonged exposure to excessive noise levels [16, 26, 27]. It can affect one or both ears, resulting from cumulative exposure to moderate or high‐intensity sounds over time. This damage is permanent and irreversible. However, early diagnosis during the initial stages can help limit its progression through preventive interventions [12, 28]. Unlike presbycusis, NIHL is not age‐dependent and can develop at any point based on noise exposure conditions [29].

Another study raised concerns regarding whether dental students are at risk for NIHL, considering the cumulative noise exposure from multiple operators in dental school environments [30]. In addition to investigating the causes of NIHL and the role of hearing protection devices (HPDs) in dental settings, one study outlined various strategies for reducing noise levels in dental clinics, such as incorporating sound‐absorbing materials into walls. In our study, participants did not use protective equipment to shield themselves from noise. Additionally, the curriculum did not include content on the effects and harms of noise. Incorporating courses on noise and noise‐induced hearing loss into the dental school curriculum could raise awareness and help protect the hearing of students and dentists who are required to study or work in noisy environments by adopting simple preventive measures at an early stage.

Lopes et al.'s [31] study evaluated the hearing health of dental professionals and emphasised that high‐frequency audiometry tests are more sensitive in detecting early hearing loss. Their study also included an acoustic impedance test; however, the data obtained did not provide a detailed analysis of differences in acoustic reflex thresholds. In our study, no significant difference was found between first‐ and fifth‐year students in acoustic reflex thresholds. The reason for this is that in the frequency ranges where acoustic reflex thresholds were measured, there was also no significant difference in pure‐tone thresholds between the groups. When compared with Lopes et al.'s study, their findings indicate significant hearing loss at high frequencies, whereas our study shows no significant changes in the conventional frequency range. However, in our study, a significant increase in high‐frequency hearing thresholds was observed in fifth‐year students, which is a finding consistent with Lopes et al.'s results. While Lopes et al.'s study supports the notion that occupational noise exposure may lead to hearing loss in dental professionals, our study suggests that this process may begin early during dental education.

Vaddamanu et al.'s [32] study evaluated the impact of occupational noise exposure on the hearing of dental technicians. Their findings demonstrated that prolonged noise exposure led to significant hearing loss, particularly at 4000–6000 Hz, with higher hearing thresholds observed in the left ear compared to the right, and the degree of hearing loss increasing with years of exposure. In contrast, our study focused on dental students, investigating the effects of noise exposure during dental education. While no significant differences were found in conventional frequency thresholds, a significant increase in high‐frequency hearing thresholds (9000–20 000 Hz) was observed in fifth‐year students. This finding aligns with Vaddamanu et al.'s study, suggesting that occupational noise‐induced hearing loss may begin early during dental training and progress with continued exposure in professional practice. Moreover, our study incorporated otoacoustic emission (OAE) tests to assess early indicators of hearing impairment. In fifth‐year students, transient‐evoked otoacoustic emissions (TEOAE) tests revealed significantly lower signal‐to‐noise ratios (SNRs) at 1000, 1500, 3000 and 4000 Hz. Similarly, significant differences were observed at specific frequencies in the distortion‐product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAE) test. These findings suggest that prolonged exposure to occupational noise in dental students may lead to early auditory changes.

Shetty et al.'s [33] study evaluated the effects of long‐term occupational noise exposure on the hearing of dentists and technicians, identifying significant hearing loss in the 4000–6000 Hz frequency range, with progressive deterioration of hearing thresholds as professional experience increased. In contrast, our study focused on dental students, examining the early effects of noise exposure during education. While no significant differences were observed in conventional frequencies (125–8000 Hz), notable threshold shifts were detected in the 9000–20 000 Hz range among fifth‐year students (p < 0.05), suggesting that hearing loss may begin during the educational phase before entering professional practice. Both studies found that the left ear was more affected than the right, and OAE tests proved to be a valuable tool for detecting early auditory damage. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has directly compared hearing thresholds between first‐year and fifth‐year dental students. If this remains the case, our study highlights a critical gap in the literature regarding the cumulative effects of occupational noise exposure throughout dental education. This finding underscores the necessity for further research in this domain.

A further important limitation is that this study did not include an age‐matched control group of non‐dental students. The significant between‐group differences observed here were concentrated at 9–20 kHz, a frequency range in which hearing thresholds are known to show age‐related changes even within young adult populations [34, 35]. Because first‐year students (mean age 19 years) and fifth‐year students (mean age 24 years) differed in age by approximately five years, part of the observed threshold elevation at these frequencies cannot be fully separated from normal age‐related change using the present design. Reference data from Jilek et al. and Rodríguez Valiente et al. [34, 35] indicate that extended high‐frequency thresholds gradually rise with age even in otologically healthy adults under 30 years old; formal normalisation of our thresholds against these published age‐correction equations was not performed, as the original dataset did not permit this recalculation post hoc. Consequently, we cannot exclude a contribution of normal auditory maturation/aging to the high‐frequency findings, and the observed differences should be interpreted as an association between more advanced dental education (and the cumulative noise exposure it entails) and higher hearing thresholds, rather than as direct proof that educational noise exposure alone caused the changes. Future studies should incorporate an age‐matched, non‐dental control group and apply published age‐correction equations to more rigorously isolate the contribution of occupational noise exposure from that of normal aging.

One of the limitations of our study is that we did not conduct a longitudinal assessment of the same students from their first to fifth year. Instead, we compared two distinct cohorts at different stages of their education. A longitudinal study design tracking the same individuals over the course of their training would provide a more precise evaluation of the progressive impact of noise exposure on hearing thresholds. Therefore, future studies should aim to implement such a methodology to better understand the long‐term consequences of occupational noise exposure in dental students. While high‐frequency hearing thresholds are often not assessed in routine clinical settings, our findings demonstrate their critical importance in detecting subclinical changes caused by occupational noise exposure. The use of OAE testing in conjunction with pure‐tone audiometry allows for a more sensitive evaluation of early cochlear changes. This study not only reinforces the need for enhanced monitoring but also highlights the importance of integrating noise awareness and hearing conservation strategies into the dental curriculum. By identifying auditory changes before symptoms appear, it may be possible to prevent the development of noise‐induced hearing loss in dental students through early intervention and protective measures.

These findings support the implementation of regular hearing screening and mandatory training on occupational noise exposure in dental schools. Integrating such preventive education early in training could substantially reduce the risk of NIHL among future dental professionals.

Conclusion

Ensuring the health and safety of students is a top research priority. The implementation of this study will contribute to raising awareness within dental schools regarding the potential risk of hearing loss associated with high‐speed dental instruments. Consequently, dental students will be better equipped to make informed decisions about their hearing health throughout their careers. By focusing on dental students, this study aims to foster attitudes and beliefs that encourage proactive measures for hearing conservation, ensuring that such practices are maintained throughout their professional lives. This is particularly crucial, as research has demonstrated that dental professionals are indeed exposed to hazardous noise levels [10]. The findings of this study indicate that fifth‐year dental students exhibited significantly higher rates of hearing impairment compared to first‐year students. Occupational exposure to noisy dental devices, along with additional noise sources such as music listening habits or environmental noise pollution, must be taken into consideration. A majority of the students were unaware of the health effects of noise exposure and preventive measures. Although noise levels recorded within the dental school were below the threshold for noise‐induced hearing loss (85 dB(A)), they were still substantially higher compared to other academic settings. To mitigate noise exposure, it is recommended that sound‐absorbing materials be incorporated into the construction of dental clinics and laboratories. Our findings offer novel early evidence of an association between advancing dental education and subclinical outer hair cell dysfunction, emphasising the importance of preventive hearing conservation education in dental curricula.
