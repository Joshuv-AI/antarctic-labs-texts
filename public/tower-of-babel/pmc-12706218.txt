‘This level of racism has always been there’: clinicians’ views on supporting racially minoritised university students – a qualitative study in student support services in North East England
Authors: Mma Felicia Yeebo, Taf Kunorubwe, Lucy Robinson, Sarah Thwaites
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the experiences of clinicians providing pastoral and mental health services to racially and ethnically minoritised students (REMS) at UK universities, aiming to understand the challenges REMS face in accessing support and to identify ways to improve service inclusivity.DesignQualitative study using semi-structured interviews.SettingStudent health and well-being services at five universities in the North East of England, a region with comparatively low racial diversity.ParticipantsTen clinicians (nine female, one male; nine White British, one other ethnic background; mean age 42.8 years) working in therapeutic roles with experience supporting REMS. Participants were recruited via opportunity sampling.MethodsSemi-structured interviews, averaging 44 min, were video-recorded, transcribed verbatim and analysed using thematic analysis to identify key themes.ResultsSix overarching themes were identified: (1) the chokehold of layered systemic challenges, (2) dynamics of power, (3) lack of safety for REMS, (4) “Am I really getting it?”, (5) psychological therapies for white people by white people and (6) the thirst for expertise. Clinicians were enthusiastic about providing culturally responsive care but reported limited access to reflective spaces and training. Contextual factors—including racism, Brexit and the marketisation of higher education—were perceived to affect service delivery and REMS’ engagement with mental health support. Business-model approaches to service provision were sources of frustration.ConclusionsClinicians face structural and systemic challenges in providing culturally sensitive mental health support to REMS. Enhancing staff training, reflective practice and service adaptation may improve access and efficacy. Findings offer practical insights for universities aiming to strengthen equity in student mental health services, and future work could evaluate interventions to increase clinician preparedness and REMS engagement.

Introduction

This research uses the term racially and ethnically minoritised (REM) to refer to groups who experience racialisation and systemic minoritisation. Alternative umbrella terms such as Black, Asian and Minority Ethnic (BAME) were considered; however, REM was chosen because it emphasises minoritisation as an active social and structural process, rather than an inherent demographic characteristic. This distinction is important as it avoids some of the limitations and critiques associated with other terminology.14

In recent years, student mental health has gained significant attention in UK universities, highlighting the importance of creating inclusive and accessible support systems for all students. Demand for student mental health services continues to rise5 6 with a 450% increase in student mental health declarations to UCAS between 2011 and 2021.7 Students with mental health difficulties are known to experience poorer academic achievement.8 Although provision within universities has expanded,9 disparities persist in the provision and accessibility of such support, particularly for racially and ethnically minoritised students (REMS). This issue is significant, as British universities are seeing more racial diversity with individuals from Black African, Indian and Chinese backgrounds more likely to attend university than their white peers.1012 However, REMS are more likely to express dissatisfaction with their student experience than white students,13 and a disproportionate number (1 in 20) withdraw from their studies due to racial harassment.14 15 In 2019, 24% of REMS reported racial harassment on campus, yet two-thirds did not report incidents due to a lack of confidence in reporting structures,16 fear of repercussions or prior negative experiences of reporting.17 Additional challenges include under-representation,18 the attainment gap,19 isolation, ignorance and racial discrimination on campus and in academic environments,20 21 and racialised stereotypes around intelligence which shape how REMS interact with university spaces.22 23 These challenges, combined with higher levels of stress and depression that REMS may experience,24 influence academic progression and earning potential, widening attainment gaps in and reinforcing barriers that reduce the presence of racially minoritised professionals in academia.25 This attrition of REMS progressing into academia is often described as the ‘leaky pipeline’.26 Collectively, these issues demonstrate that racism remains embedded within higher educational institutions. Racism not only exacerbates mental health difficulties but also harms physical health through stress pathways.2730 The North East of England is the least racially diverse region in the UK.31 Research suggests that racism and discrimination can be more pronounced in areas with limited racial diversity.32 Focusing on this region reflects both the remit of the wider project—exploring the mental health needs of racially and ethnically minoritised students—and the demographic context that shapes student experiences and clinicians’ work in university support services.

Research shows that REMS are less likely to access university mental health support33 and often hold less positive attitudes towards seeking help for the mental health challenges they encounter while at university, particularly male students.34 Prior evidence highlights that lower rates of access are not due to individual or cultural deficits but to systemic barriers, including discrimination, lack of culturally competent provision and structural inequalities across healthcare and higher education.3537

Given the increased awareness of mental health difficulties among students, it is crucial to identify groups that are at higher risk of poorer mental health, assess their additional needs and establish effective support pathways. This study forms part of a broader project on the mental health needs of REMS. Specifically, it focuses on clinicians providing mental health support in Student Health and Well-being Services (SHWS) to REMS in the North East universities, aiming to develop a nuanced understanding of the institutional and clinical contexts shaping this issue.

This study aimed to explore the following: how do clinicians in SHWS perceive and respond to the mental health and well-being needs of racially and ethnically minoritised university students in the North East of England?

Methods

Design

This exploratory study used semi-structured interviews to examine the experiences of clinicians working in SHWS at five universities in the North East of England. The study was underpinned by a qualitative, thematic analysis approach38 allowing for identification, analysis and interpretation of patterns in clinicians’ accounts of supporting racially and ethnically minoritised students. The North East was selected because it represents the least racially diverse UK region, making it a salient context to explore challenges faced by REMS. Ethical approval was obtained from Newcastle University’s Faculty of Medical Sciences Research Ethics Committee (2479/27221). This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) 32-item checklist.39

Procedure

Participants were recruited through multiple channels to maximise engagement, including emailed advertisements, direct emails to colleagues and posters displayed in relevant university areas. Interested staff completed a brief online expression of interest form, providing their contact details, demographic information, job role and information regarding length of time in current and previous job roles. They were then contacted directly via email and invited to participate in an online interview. Interviews lasted between 24 and 56 min (mean=44.14 min). The interview schedule was applied flexibly, allowing participants to elaborate on their experiences and reflections. Interviews were video recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Materials/PPI statement

The interview schedule was developed iteratively with input from current postgraduate REMS, clinicians and clinical psychologists, including individuals from racially minoritised backgrounds. It consisted of open-ended questions exploring clinicians’ experiences of supporting REMS, their perceived proficiency and sources of external support, and was applied flexibly with prompts to encourage reflection and elaboration. Written informed consent outlining study aims, confidentiality and voluntary participation was obtained prior to participation. Interviews were video recorded and transcribed verbatim, and participants received a £20 gift voucher.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Participants

Participants were recruited using opportunity sampling via multiple channels, including emails through regional and national staff networks, direct contact with colleagues and posters displayed in relevant university areas. The study targeted clinicians from all five higher education institutions in the North East, ensuring comprehensive regional coverage. Eighteen individuals expressed interest in participating, of whom ten met inclusion criteria—current or previous employment at SHWS with clinical experience supporting REMS—and took part in the study (figure 1). Sample size was guided by qualitative methodology, with the aim of balancing specificity, depth and analytic insight rather than pursuing numerical representativeness. Data saturation was not applied in a prescriptive manner, given critiques of its incompatibility with reflexive thematic analysis.40 Instead, the study was guided by the principle of information power,41 which emphasises the adequacy of data in relation to its richness and relevance to the study aims. Prior work shows that meaningful qualitative insights can be generated from relatively small samples,42 and reflexive TA has been successfully conducted with samples of this size, where adequacy is judged by analytic depth rather than numerical thresholds.43 On this basis, the final sample was considered sufficient to support detailed and in-depth thematic analysis of clinicians’ perspectives. Interviews were scheduled at a mutually convenient time and conducted online, with only the interviewer and participant present. Nine interviews were conducted by a master’s student under the supervision of the lead researcher and one by an experienced qualitative researcher. The interview schedule was applied flexibly, allowing participants to elaborate on their experiences and reflections. Both interviewers made notes during and after the interviews.

Analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis,38 an idiographic approach which centres the experiences of the participants. Two researchers independently familiarised themselves with transcripts, coded data and generated initial themes. Themes were reviewed collaboratively, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Reflexive logs were maintained, and an external researcher cross-checked themes to enhance credibility. Illustrative quotes are pseudonymised and truncated.

Reflexivity

Researcher positionality shaped the study. MFY is a Black African woman and clinical psychologist raised in Britain, with interests in trauma and systemic approaches. TK is a Zimbabwean-born CBT therapist of Zimbabwean and British parentage, raised in the UK. Both maintained reflexive journals and situated findings within broader social and institutional contexts (see online supplemental appendix A).

Results

Six superordinate themes were identified from the ten clinician interviews (table 1), with illustrative quotes provided in online supplemental appendix B. The sample included clinicians with diverse therapeutic roles and varying levels of experience; details are reported in online supplemental tables 3 and 4. The quotations presented here are illustrative exemplars of key themes, while all interpretations remain firmly grounded in the full dataset, reflecting the broader patterns and diversity of participants’ responses.

Overview of themes and subthemes

REMS, racially and ethnically minoritised students.

Theme 1: the chokehold of layered systemic challenges

This theme explored participants’ perspectives of the systemic challenges faced by REMS engaging in therapy.

Subtheme 1.1: university as a business

Participants collectively depicted a landscape where higher education institutions (HEIs) have increasingly seen higher student numbers and transitioned into profit-driven entities, prioritising financial gains over other aspects. This results in a discrepancy between increased tuition fees and the perceived stagnation in the student experience, as well as a reduction in support provision, indicating a shift towards prioritising revenue generation. This may limit diversity and inclusivity within academia, despite educational frameworks and university policies advocating for representation.

Subtheme 1.2: service limitations

Participants emphasised the need for sufficient and impactful mental health support for students. They reported that SHWS were constrained and diminishing due to inadequate resourcing of mental health support services within their HEIs. There was recognition that excessively short-term service delivery models were prevalent to address extensive waiting lists and reduce costs. Despite institutions acknowledging the importance of services, participants described them as underfunded and insufficient to meet demand, posing challenges related to providing comprehensive and meaningful mental health support.

Theme 2: dynamics of power

Each clinician, because of their own racial, ethnic and cultural background, as well as their job role, occupied certain positions of power or the lack thereof. As a result of their respective positioning, they navigate a complex terrain that affords certain luxuries or disadvantages, introduces blind spots and shapes their interactions with REMS.

Subtheme 2.1: privilege

Among clinicians there was an acknowledgement of the existence of privilege due to their white racial identity. They highlighted the need to reflect on their positions of privilege, particularly in the context of being part of the racial majority within the university or society as a whole. This highlighted the typical disparities in life experiences between SHWS staff and REMS due to cultural differences and exposure to racism, discrimination and oppression and was part of their experience of engaging in therapy with REMS.

Subtheme 2.2: perception of topic as controversial

Participants expressed that conversations around race and racism often felt, or were perceived as, controversial, with the potential to cause discomfort. This would provoke a range of different responses – from denial to avoidance to fear and anxiety. Participants explored how some, particularly those in the racial majority within the UK, may attempt to explain away instances of discrimination, thereby dismissing and minimising the experiences of marginalised individuals. Furthermore, a concern was expressed that some staff may lack an understanding of the systemic, structural and individual racism that REMS can experience, potentially leading to ineffective support or engagement of such students in mental health interventions.

Theme 3: lack of safety for REMS

Participants expressed concerns about the lack of physical and psychological safety for REMS within society as a whole, the university environment and even within SHWS. They highlighted systemic issues such as racism, microaggressions and the perpetuation of oppressive structures that contribute to an unsafe atmosphere for REMS, which can negatively impact on their mental health and academic attainment.

Subtheme 3.1: racism is alive and well

Participants emphasised the existence of systemic and structural racism and the perpetuation of oppressive structures and practices. These factors create an unsafe, dangerous and damaging environment for REMS, which may at times be their reason for seeking therapy. Associated with this were perceived feelings of isolation and a lack of representation experienced by REMS, reflecting broader societal dynamics of exclusion and marginalisation. There was some reflection on the escalation of racism post-Brexit, attributed to a normalisation and legitimisation of racist attitudes and leading to more openly expressed discrimination. Together, this painted a stark picture of the pervasive overt and covert presence of racism within society and its detrimental effects on individuals’ well-being. Clinicians shared feelings of powerlessness, as well as of a lack of clarity around their role in addressing these issues.

Subtheme 3.2: REMS searching for psychologically safe spaces to unpack their experiences

Participants reflected that REMS appeared to search for psychologically safe spaces to discuss experiences of racial discrimination and would display this through ‘testing’ their therapist in terms of initially disclosing limited information to explore how this was received. Part of this hesitation was perceived to stem from REMS’s fears of judgement, misunderstanding or invalidation from their clinicians. Participants acknowledged the lack of racial diversity among their workforce, which they felt may deter REMS from seeking support related to these topics, as they may doubt the clinician’s ability to understand and empathise with their experiences. Participants felt there was a need for SHWS to outwardly advertise their acceptance and proficiency in exploring such issues, to help REMS feel able to bring these issues to therapy.

Theme 4: “Am I really getting it?”

This theme relates to clinicians from white racial backgrounds recognising their own positionality when navigating the complexities of understanding the multifaceted experiences of REMS. Some clinicians reflected on the challenge of understanding experiences of individual and systemic racial discrimination and the cultural conflicts and challenges REMS may bring to therapy. Emphasis was placed on the importance of minimising assumptions and recognising any differences in needs between themselves and their clients. They acknowledged their limitations in fully comprehending the experiences of REM individuals due to differences in backgrounds. These instances underscore the clinicians’ recognition of their limitations, despite their efforts to empathise and avoid assumptions.

Theme 5: psychological therapies for white people by white people

Clinicians reflected on how the approaches and therapies they had been trained in were developed and researched within the context of white-majority populations in Western nations, leading to some elements that could conflict with the values and cultural perspectives of individuals from non-Western cultures or marginalised backgrounds.

Subtheme 5.1: dominant Western theoretical positions

This subtheme relates to the inherent Western-centric nature of therapeutic models within the UK, which predisposes them towards prevailing values and perspectives. This predisposition created a potential mismatch when applied to REMS with diverse beliefs and cultural influences. Clinicians reflected on the existence of this gulf but felt ill-equipped to consider ways to bridge this gap therapeutically. These prevailing assumptions within psychological therapies were thought to be perpetuated because of the typical lack of focus on working in a culturally responsive way within core training programmes.

Subtheme 5.2: what adaptations do we have to make to our practice?

This subtheme pertains to clinicians highlighting the need for ongoing nuanced, individualised adaptations to their therapeutic practices in order to better suit REM clients. They felt that delivering therapy in a copy-and-paste manner was ineffective at best. Such changes also entailed acknowledging the importance of addressing differences in identity and creating a safe space for open discussions about race, ethnicity, religion and gender. Clinicians relayed the importance of acknowledging any differences and/or similarities between their backgrounds and those of their clients and addressing any anxieties that may arise from these differences to maximise understanding and empathy. They stressed the importance of being upfront and honest about their own limitations in understanding the experiences of REMS actively in the therapeutic process.

Theme 6: the thirst for expertise

This theme encompasses reflections of needing to seek further knowledge to aid effective working with REMS and actively consolidating this in clinical practice.

Subtheme 6.1: seeking further knowledge

Participants conveyed that their core training hadn't fully equipped them to work in culturally responsive ways or discuss experiences of racism or their impact. They reported searching for sources of knowledge about bringing these issues into therapy, adapting ways of working and often attending trainings and workshops in their spare time or at a cost to them. Despite finding some aspects helpful, they highlighted that these training opportunities were often pitched at the wrong audience or at a level that did not meet their needs. This triggered ongoing searches for more specialised knowledge. There was therefore an identified need for training that is tailored to their specific professional context and that potentially addresses the issues that can arise when working with issues of racial discrimination as a white clinician in a university mental health service within a region with low racial diversity.

Subtheme 6.2: need for continued reflection and consolidation

Participants highlighted a need for continued personal and professional reflection in order to be better able to work with REMS, including deliberate consideration of their relative positionality to challenge any personal biases they may hold and guard against making assumptions about clients’ experiences or their culture. This was considered to be vital, despite the potential challenges and feelings of discomfort this might provoke. They emphasised the need for ongoing reflection and consolidation of knowledge and skills in the practice of therapy. They centred the need for clinicians to remain self-aware and committed to continuous learning in order to effectively address the diverse needs and experiences of their clients. There was a recognised need to approach this work with humility and to be open-minded to being taught/enlightened about relevant cultural/racialised experiences by clients without this becoming an expectation. The need for, and lack of, appropriate clinical supervision to support these processes was raised, as was the need for consolidation and application of these reflections into wider clinical practice, SHWS and other university contexts.

Discussion

Real consideration of whether current student mental health services meet the needs of a diverse student body requires moving beyond generic commitments to diversity, equality and inclusion. Our findings suggest that embedding race equity into service design, staff training and reflective practice may support more culturally responsive services. Universities recruiting increasing numbers of international students and following governmental directives to widen participation for under-represented groups, including REMS,44 could consider ensuring that services adapt to these changes.45

In the North East of England, where only a minority of students identify as racially or ethnically minoritised, the demographic context may intensify systemic challenges, power dynamics and potential lack of psychological safety. The comparatively small numbers of REMS may increase the risks of isolation and invisibility, making it more likely that the unique experiences of specific subgroups are overlooked in policy and practice.46 Our findings suggest that students’ racial, ethnic, religious and political backgrounds may shape their mental health needs, potentially influencing how they engage with services and the kind of support they require. Clinicians need to recognise and respond to this heterogeneity rather than treating REMS as a homogeneous group.

Clinicians in this study expressed enthusiasm for supporting REMS but also feelings of powerlessness in the face of systemic barriers. Limited prior exposure to racially diverse groups, insufficient preparation in training programmes and scarce opportunities for reflective supervision were commonly noted. These findings align with broader research suggesting that low racial diversity in mental health staffing can discourage REMS from accessing services46 and that clinicians are often perceived as struggling to engage with the cultural, religious and political factors shaping clients’ mental health understandings.47 Clinicians described navigating these challenges through informal learning and ongoing self-reflection, indicating that ongoing professional development could support culturally sensitive and contextually appropriate interventions.

Existing research suggests that REMS may face unique stressors in higher education48 49 including stereotyping, heightened visibility, exclusion and experiences of racism, which can exacerbate psychological distress.50 Systemic barriers such as stigma, mistrust of mental health services and cultural insensitivity may contribute to avoidance, limited engagement,51 delayed help-seeking52 53 and higher dropout rates.51 Our findings situate clinicians’ experiences within this broader evidence base, highlighting how workforce demographics, training gaps and service structures may intersect to influence support for REMS.

Policy and practice implications

The demographic context of the North East may magnify these challenges with implications for policy and practice. Universities could demonstrate explicit commitment to race equity within student health and well-being services through targeted staff development, reflective supervision and service adaptations. Standardised, short-term service models may be insufficient; longer-term, individually tailored support may help build trust and facilitate engagement. Institutions should map existing service provision, identify gaps and develop positive practice guides to promote consistent, evidence-informed approaches while allowing for local adaptation.

This research has informed the development of a practical toolkit, which was informed by themes from this study to support clinicians’ professional development and institutional support.

Strengths

This study is the first to explore UK-based student health and well-being clinicians’ experiences working with REMS. Using qualitative methodology allowed us to capture rich, nuanced perspectives, providing insights into systemic challenges and clinician strategies for supporting diverse student populations. These findings suggest actionable recommendations for improving inclusivity, accessibility and effectiveness of mental health services.

Limitations

Opportunity sampling may have introduced selection bias, as clinicians with particular interest in supporting REMS may have been more likely to participate. The study did not include other SHWS staff, including senior leadership, which limits the perspectives captured and the breadth of organisational insight. Similarly, student perspectives were not collected within this phase; these were considered in a separate, concurrently conducted phase of research (manuscript under review), meaning the findings reflect only clinician-reported experiences. The focus on the North East limits generalisability to other regions with different workforce and student demographics. Terminology presents another limitation. The term REMS was deliberately selected over alternatives such as BAME, People of Colour or People of the Global Majority to emphasise experiences of minoritisation; however, no single term can fully capture the ethnic and racial diversity of the sample. Finally, the sample demographics reflect broader workforce patterns, with predominantly White female participants,54 55 which may limit the diversity of clinician perspectives captured.

Take home message

To improve accessibility and effectiveness of psychological interventions, universities could consider creating psychologically safe spaces, providing ongoing staff training and fostering reflective practice to understand and support the needs of REMS. Recognising structural and systemic challenges, alongside providing concrete tools, may enhance equity and well-being for all students. Clinicians and institutions should also consider the diversity within REMS, adapting support where possible to the individual racial, ethnic, religious and political contexts of students.
