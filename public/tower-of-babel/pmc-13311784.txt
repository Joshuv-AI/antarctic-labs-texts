Introduction

Debris flow disasters are complex natural events involving a mixture of rock, soil, and water, typically triggered by phenomena such as torrential mountain rains, melting ice and snow, or seismic activity.1 Due to their rapid onset and immense destructive power, these disasters pose severe threats to life, property, and infrastructure, particularly in mountainous and earthquake-prone regions.2 In recent years, global climate change has exacerbated their frequency and intensity, leading to devastating human and economic losses. Debris flows not only as primary natural disasters but also as catalysts for secondary calamities like floods and landslides, thereby amplifying the complexity of post-disaster rescue efforts.3 Thus, research into medical rescue strategies following debris flow disasters is of critical importance.

Although research output on debris flows has grown rapidly, the knowledge base remains heavily weighted toward environmental prediction and engineering mitigation rather than medical rescue. The efficacy of emergency medical rescue operations following debris flow disasters directly impacts the survival and well-being of affected populations. In recent years, this aspect has gained increasing attention in the fields of medicine and disaster management. Studies affirm that prompt medical intervention significantly enhances survival rates and mitigates the risk of post-disaster complications.4 Nonetheless, numerous challenges persist, including addressing the psychological well-being of rescuers, optimizing resource allocation, and planning for long-term recovery.5 Moreover, the geographical and environmental conditions associated with debris flow disasters impose heightened demands on rescue efforts.6 In terrains marked by complex topography and harsh climatic conditions, communication and coordination during rescue operations become particularly arduous. To support this observation, a preliminary bibliographic screening was conducted across major databases such as PubMed and Web of Science. The majority of debris-flow-related publications were found to focus on environmental prediction, geological mechanisms, and engineering mitigation, whereas only a limited proportion addressed medical response, emergency care, or disaster health management. This imbalance highlights a critical gap in the integration of medical perspectives into debris-flow disaster research.

The review is designed not only to summarize evidence, but also to inform research priorities and operational policy. By analyzing prevailing practices, identifying challenges, and proposing avenues for improvement, we seek to provide a comprehensive reference for advancing research and practical implementation in this domain. Through this exploration, we hope to contribute meaningfully to enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of post-disaster medical rescue operations.

Importantly, the challenges associated with debris-flow disasters differ from those observed in general mass-casualty incidents. Unlike earthquakes or urban disasters, debris flows typically occur in mountainous and remote regions, characterized by difficult terrain, limited accessibility, and high risk of secondary hazards such as recurrent flows and landslides. These unique environmental and logistical constraints impose distinct demands on emergency medical response, including prolonged pre-hospital time, limited evacuation capacity, and the need for extended field-based care. Therefore, conclusions drawn from broader disaster medicine literature may not be fully applicable to debris-flow contexts, underscoring the necessity of a focused review.

This review is intended for emergency medical service (EMS) providers, disaster response planners, and emergency physicians involved in disaster preparedness and response. By tailoring the scope and level of detail to these audiences, the review aims to bridge the gap between research evidence and operational implementation.

Discussion

The evolution of emergency medical response in disaster settings has been marked by several key milestones that have significantly influenced debris-flow rescue practices. The establishment of Disaster Medical Assistance Teams (DMATs) represented an early shift toward organized, rapid-response medical systems capable of deployment in austere environments. Subsequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) Emergency Medical Team (EMT) classification system standardized the capabilities, staffing, and operational requirements of deployable medical teams, improving international coordination and quality assurance in disaster response.

In parallel, the integration of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), particularly Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST), into prehospital and field settings has enhanced early diagnosis of internal injuries, enabling more effective triage and decision-making in resource-limited environments. These milestones collectively reflect a transition from ad hoc rescue efforts toward structured, protocol-driven, and technology-supported medical response systems.

Major health risks in debris flow disasters

Epidemiological studies of landslides and debris-flow disasters indicate substantial mortality and injury burdens. Reported case-fatality rates vary widely depending on disaster scale and response capacity but can exceed 20–30% in severe events. Trauma-related injuries, including crush injuries, fractures, and head trauma, are the leading causes of death, while delayed complications such as infection and renal failure contribute to additional morbidity. These epidemiological patterns underscore the critical importance of rapid and effective medical response. Debris flow disasters not only pose immediate threats to life but also introduce a range of health risks, including physical injuries, infectious diseases arising from environmental contamination, and profound mental health challenges (Figure 1 provides an overview of major health risks in debris flow disasters). These multifaceted health risks directly influence public health needs and shape medical rescue strategies in post-disaster scenarios.7

Major health risks in debris flow disasters.

Direct physical injury

The abrupt and catastrophic nature of debris flow disasters often results in severe physical trauma. High-velocity flows of soil, rocks, and debris obliterate infrastructure, leading to collapses and crush injuries. Studies indicate that common injuries sustained during such events include open wounds, fractures, visceral trauma, and head injuries, often compounded by significant hemorrhage.8 Without timely medical intervention, these conditions can rapidly deteriorate, culminating in fatal outcomes. In high-altitude regions, rescue operations are further impeded by obstructed roads and limited communication, making trauma management even more challenging. Critical early interventions, such as hemorrhage control, wound stabilization, and initial debridement, are vital for victims.9 Crush syndrome is another prevalent condition during debris flow disasters, resulting from prolonged compression of skeletal muscles. This leads to the release of cellular contents into the bloodstream, causing acute kidney failure and multiple organ dysfunction, necessitating prompt medical attention, pre-hospital administration of intravenous fluids, which is key to preventing renal complications.10

Advances in the management of crush syndrome have significantly improved survival outcomes in disaster settings, including debris-flow events. Early and aggressive prehospital fluid resuscitation, particularly with isotonic crystalloids, has been shown to reduce the incidence of acute kidney injury by diluting nephrotoxic substances such as myoglobin. Protocols emphasizing early intravenous access and fluid administration prior to extrication have become increasingly adopted in disaster medicine.

In addition, improvements in renal support strategies, including the timely use of renal replacement therapy (RRT), have further enhanced patient outcomes in severe cases. The extension of survival time through optimized fluid resuscitation and renal protection strategies has allowed more patients to reach definitive care, representing a critical advancement in disaster medical response.

Infectious diseases caused by environmental pollution

Beyond physical harm, debris flow disasters frequently cause environmental pollution, significantly heightening the risk of infectious diseases.11 Contaminated water sources and deteriorating sanitation conditions create ideal conditions for pathogen proliferation, particularly in overcrowded temporary shelters. Waterborne illnesses such as cholera, dysentery, and typhoid fever are common, often linked to compromised drinking water safety.12 Water pollution in disaster-stricken areas is primarily attributed to microorganisms and harmful chemicals carried by debris, disrupting groundwater systems and introducing pathogens like E. coli and Salmonella. These contaminants can lead to gastrointestinal diseases, while airborne debris and dust elevate the risk of respiratory infections such as bronchitis and asthma.13 The dual challenge of addressing water and air quality is critical to preventing secondary health crises in affected regions.

Post-disaster mental health problems

In addition to physical and infectious risks, the psychological aftermath of debris flow disasters has a profound and lasting impact. Survivors often experience post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression.14 The intense fear and helplessness associated with such events disrupt daily life and productivity. PTSD is characterized by symptoms such as avoidance behaviors, intrusive flashbacks, and emotional numbing, which, in severe cases, may escalate to behavioral disorders or suicidality.15 Providing psychological support is a crucial component of post-disaster rescue operations. Structured interventions, including counseling and trauma-focused therapies, are essential to help survivors process their experiences, rebuild resilience, and regain a sense of normalcy in the aftermath of disaster.16

Emergency medical rescue in debris flow disasters.

Preparation of emergency supplies and equipment

The large-scale devastation caused by debris flow often results in severe shortages of essential first aid materials and equipment in affected areas. Prioritizing the preparation of trauma care supplies such as tourniquets, wound dressings, fracture immobilization devices, antibiotics, and pain relievers is critical.17 Establishing pre-positioned emergency material reserve stations in high-risk areas can significantly enhance the efficiency of initial rescue efforts.18

Advances in portable medical devices have greatly benefited disaster rescue operations. Portable ultrasound machines, X-ray devices, and handheld vital sign monitors enable rapid and precise assessment of injuries, such as traumatic bleeding, fractures, and soft tissue damage.19,20 Portable ultrasound, in particular, has proven invaluable due to its ease of use and clarity of imaging. These modern tools not only improve diagnostic accuracy but also reduce rescue times, contributing to better outcomes.

Environmental conditions in debris-flow zones directly shape supply and equipment needs. Intense rainfall, unstable slopes, poor visibility, and the risk of secondary hazards (e.g., additional slides, flooding, and falling rocks) require ruggedized and waterproof packaging, redundant power sources, and protective gear for responders, while also limiting ground access and increasing reliance on modular, rapidly deployable kits. In addition, mud and water contamination can compromise sterile fields and cold-chain integrity, necessitating strict infection-control measures, sealed storage, and temperature monitoring for blood products and critical medications.21 Communication disruption and terrain complexity further justify multi-path communication devices and navigation support to maintain coordination and safe transport.22

On-site dispatch and protective measures for medical teams

The complex and hazardous environments typical of debris flow disaster zones demand a multidisciplinary rescue team composed of emergency physicians, surgeons, nurses, and psychologists to ensure comprehensive medical care.23,24 Effective collaboration among these professionals enhances rescue efficiency and balances workload distribution. Rescue personnel face heightened risks due to unstable terrain and limited communication. Personal protective equipment (PPE) such as helmets, protective boots, and dust masks is essential to safeguard against secondary disasters.25,26 The ``secondary rescue'' approach, emphasizing site assessment before initiating rescue operations, has proven effective in mitigating risks. Tools like drones and remote sensors enable safe reconnaissance of disaster zones, facilitating the identification of optimal rescue routes. Deploying temporary communication systems, including satellite phones and short-wave radios, further enhances coordination and responsiveness.27,28

Optimization of medical resource distribution using geological data

Debris flow disasters predominantly occur in mountainous areas, where conventional medical resource allocation strategies may be inadequate. Integrating geographic information systems (GIS) and remote sensing technology allows for precise risk assessment and optimized resource deployment. By combining GIS with disaster risk models, high-risk areas can be identified, and medical supply depots strategically located to ensure rapid response.29

Real-time geological data, such as seismic activity and meteorological forecasts, further supports preemptive deployment of rescue teams and resources. For example, studies in Sichuan’s Dadu River Basin utilized geological and historical disaster data to identify high-risk zones and refine resource allocation strategies, significantly enhancing rescue efficiency.30

Post-disaster resource distribution and collaborative management

Post-disaster medical resource distribution often faces challenges such as material shortages and disrupted transportation networks. Centralized distribution mechanisms and cross-departmental collaboration are essential to maximize resource utilization. Temporary medical centers can provide immediate healthcare and psychological support, alleviating pressure on disaster-stricken areas.31

Modern supply chain management methods, supported by Internet of Things (IoT) technology, enable real-time monitoring of material consumption and dynamic adjustments to resupply plans.32 Drones have demonstrated exceptional utility in delivering supplies to isolated areas, ensuring timely access to essential resources.33

These advancements span trauma management, on-site diagnostic tools, telemedicine, and data-driven support systems, aiming to enhance rescue efficiency, optimize resource allocation, and safeguard the well-being of both rescuers and casualties can be seen in Figure 3. Advances in emergency medical rescue for debris flow disasters.

Innovations in on-site first aid techniques

In the aftermath of debris flow disasters, casualties often necessitate swift on-site intervention to stabilize vital signs and prevent clinical deterioration. Recent advancements in portable medical equipment have revolutionized disaster scene operations, markedly improving rescue efficiency. Hemostatic innovations have proven indispensable in managing external bleeding in debris flow scenarios. Cutting-edge materials such as rapid hemostatic patches and coagulation sponges significantly mitigate blood loss, reducing the incidence of hemorrhagic shock.34 Furthermore, self-administering emergency drugs, including auto-injectable epinephrine and analgesics, play a crucial role in managing multiple casualties effectively, particularly in scenarios where medical personnel are overwhelmed.35,36

Evolution of disaster emergency medical systems

The application of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in disaster response has evolved from basic aerial imaging to multifunctional operational tools. Early use focused primarily on post-disaster reconnaissance and mapping, while more recent applications include real-time victim detection, route planning, and targeted delivery of medical supplies in inaccessible debris-flow zones. In advanced systems, UAVs are integrated with artificial intelligence (AI)-based image recognition and terrain analysis, enabling rapid hazard mapping and decision support. However, the level of readiness for these technologies varies. UAV-based reconnaissance and supply delivery are increasingly operational and have been implemented in multiple disaster settings, whereas AI-assisted mapping and autonomous decision-making systems remain in pilot or early deployment stages, requiring further validation in real-world debris-flow environments.37

UAVs have gained prominence in disaster response, serving roles such as terrain reconnaissance, survivor location, and medical supply transport. Particularly in mountainous regions, UAVs overcome geographical constraints, delivering emergency supplies to inaccessible areas and enhancing rescue timeliness.38 Quantitative evidence supports these tools as force multipliers: UAV reconnaissance has been associated with 30–50% reductions in early scene-assessment/access-planning time in complex terrain, and portable ultrasound (FAST/POCUS) demonstrates high diagnostic accuracy in austere/prehospital settings.

Telemedicine technology has also made remarkable strides in debris flow rescue efforts. Leveraging satellite communication and internet connectivity, remote medical experts can guide on-site personnel through emergency procedures and diagnoses. This system compensates for skill gaps among on-site responders while alleviating their workload.39 Real-time video monitoring enables remote physicians to provide surgical guidance and pharmacological advice, significantly enhancing medical support in remote locations.40

Advanced wound management techniques

Recent advancements have introduced innovative wound management technologies. For example, 3D printing has revolutionized fracture treatment by enabling the rapid production of customized fixtures tailored to individual injuries, enhancing therapeutic precision.41

Smart bandages represent another breakthrough in wound care, integrating sensors to monitor wound humidity, temperature, and infection status. These devices transmit real-time data to medical staff, facilitating continuous monitoring and accelerating healing processes. Their efficacy in preventing infections and promoting recovery has been well-documented.42

Bioengineered materials, including biodegradable polymers and gels, have further enhanced wound management. These materials provide effective wound coverage, reduce infection risks, and offer hemostatic and anti-inflammatory benefits, simplifying field interventions and improving rescue outcomes.43

Data-driven decision support and intelligent systems

The advent of big data and artificial intelligence (AI) has revolutionized decision-making in debris flow disaster medical rescue. Sophisticated data processing and analysis systems now integrate vast datasets, including topographical maps, meteorological data, and demographic information, to support real-time decision-making. GIS-based tools generate disaster and risk distribution maps, offering precise guidance for rescue operations.44

AI-driven systems dynamically allocate rescue resources, utilizing machine learning algorithms to predict demand patterns and optimize material and personnel distribution. These systems significantly enhance resource utilization efficiency, ensuring timely support for high-priority areas.45

Innovations in psychological intervention and social support

In addition to physical injuries, debris flow disaster survivors often face psychological challenges such as PTSD, anxiety, and depression. Virtual reality (VR) technology has emerged as a novel intervention tool, enabling gradual desensitization to traumatic memories through immersive simulations of disaster scenarios.46 Online psychological counseling platforms provide accessible mental health support, facilitating continuous interaction between survivors and professionals. These platforms play a crucial role in emotional stabilization and psychological rehabilitation.47 Furthermore, robust social support systems, involving families, community volunteers, and mental health experts, foster holistic recovery and reintegration for affected populations.48

Addressing these multifaceted challenges requires the integration of diverse disciplinary expertise, as the resources of any single field often prove insufficient. Multidisciplinary collaboration enables a holistic assessment of disaster risks, optimizes resource allocation, enhances rescue efficiency, ensures public health, and provides robust support for post-disaster recovery.

Multidisciplinary collaboration in debris flow disasters.

Collaborative efforts in disaster risk assessment

The genesis of debris flow disasters is influenced by an interplay of geological, meteorological, and hydrological factors. Accurate prediction and risk evaluation demand the combined efforts of geologists, meteorologists, and hydrologists. Geologists analyze geological structures, soil types, rock compositions, and groundwater dynamics to identify regions vulnerable to debris flows. Meteorologists, leveraging data on rainfall, temperature fluctuations, and wind patterns, forecast weather conditions conducive to debris flow, issuing timely warnings to guide preventive actions.49,50

Hydrologists contribute by examining watershed hydrology, simulating rainfall-runoff relationships, and predicting debris flow velocity, volume, and impact zones. This multidisciplinary synergy ensures a comprehensive understanding of debris flow dynamics, providing critical insights for resource allocation, evacuation planning, and recovery strategies.51

The advent of remote sensing and GIS technologies has further enhanced interdisciplinary collaboration. These tools enable real-time monitoring and risk mapping of disaster-prone areas, facilitating the identification of high-risk zones, tracking disaster progression, optimizing rescue operations, and streamlining resource distribution.52

Multidisciplinary coordination in emergency rescue

Post-disaster rescue operations demand swift, coordinated action across various domains, including medical aid, search and rescue, and infrastructure restoration. Rescue teams comprise medical professionals, emergency responders, engineers, and firefighters, each fulfilling specialized roles. While healthcare providers focus on stabilizing and transporting the patients, engineers and firefighters clear obstructions, locate survivors, and assess structural safety. Engineers employ structural evaluation techniques to determine the stability of damaged buildings and guide rescue personnel in ensuring operational safety.53,54

In scenarios where roads are blocked or communication networks disrupted, multidisciplinary coordination becomes critical. Transportation and logistics experts collaborate to restore access routes and facilitate the delivery of essential supplies. Public health professionals and logistics managers work together to ensure that medical resources are distributed efficiently to areas of greatest need. This integrated approach minimizes delays and maximizes the impact of relief efforts.54

Collaboration in public health management

Public health management following debris flow disasters extends beyond disease control to include environmental restoration, long-term rehabilitation, and healthcare infrastructure reconstruction. A multidisciplinary team comprising public health experts, environmental engineers, and epidemiologists devises comprehensive health management strategies to mitigate health risks. Environmental contamination, particularly of water sources, often accompanies debris flow disasters. Public health specialists assess the risks associated with polluted drinking water, while epidemiologists evaluate the potential for disease outbreaks such as cholera or typhoid. Coordinated efforts ensure the implementation of water purification systems and the provision of safe drinking water. Environmental engineers oversee the remediation of contaminated soils, sewage, and waste, aiding in ecological restoration and improving living conditions.55,56

Public health departments, in collaboration with social workers, organize health education initiatives to teach survivors about wound care, food safety, and sanitation practices. These efforts mitigate the risk of disease transmission and promote community resilience.57

Integrated psychological interventions

Survivors of debris flow and other rapid-onset hazards commonly experience acute stress reactions and longer-term psychological morbidity such as PTSD, anxiety, depression, sleep disturbance, and complicated grief, underscoring the need for an integrated, multidisciplinary mental-health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) response. In practice, support should follow a layered, stepped-care approach consistent with international MHPSS guidance.58 Recent disaster-psychology evidence strengthens the intervention basis beyond descriptive reports: a recent meta-analysis of randomized trials in natural-disaster survivors reported meaningful benefits of psychological interventions particularly trauma-focused CBT-oriented approaches for PTSD and comorbid depression, and a scoping review summarized RCT evidence across earthquakes, floods, and tsunamis supporting scalable delivery formats in resource-constrained settings.59 In addition, a randomized trial in earthquake- and flood-affected communities showed that a brief, culturally adapted community intervention integrating mental health support with disaster preparedness improved preparedness behaviors and mental-health outcomes, supporting the inclusion of mental-health readiness in preparedness planning.60

Challenges and solutions for emergency medical rescue in debris flow disasters

Debris flow disasters, characterized by their sudden onset and destructive nature, present multifaceted challenges for emergency rescue operations. These challenges encompass environmental obstacles, resource allocation issues, communication breakdowns, and the safety of rescue personnel. Effective responses require tailored strategies to address these obstacles (Figure 5 provides an overview of challenges and solutions for emergency medical rescue in debris flow disaster).

Challenges and solutions for emergency medical rescue in debris flow disasters.

Challenges posed by the disaster environment

When debris flow disaster occurs, the disaster area is often in a mountainous area with complex terrain and inconvenient transportation. Road damage and traffic disruption are among the most important challenges in disaster relief. Debris flow not only destroys infrastructure, such as roads, bridges, communication facilities, etc., but also changes river courses and terrain, causing serious secondary disasters. In this case, emergency rescue workers were unable to enter the disaster area in a timely manner, seriously affecting the timely treatment of the patients and the timely supply of materials.61,62

Aiming at the complex terrain of debris flow disaster area, it is necessary to establish effective traffic diversion and restoration plan. Engineers can use drones and satellite images to evaluate disaster areas, help plan rescue routes and evaluate potential safety hazards. In addition, modern technologies such as drones and all-terrain vehicles (ATVs) can carry out preliminary search and rescue operations in remote areas of the disaster area and rapidly deliver emergency materials and emergency personnel to the disaster area. Especially in areas with blocked traffic, drones have proven to be an important complementary rescue tool capable of delivering supplies and collecting information in a short time.63,64

Challenges in healthcare resource allocation

Healthcare resources are often scarce in debris flow-affected areas, particularly in remote regions. The influx of casualties, combined with complex injury profiles, overwhelms available medical staff and equipment. Inefficient distribution of medical supplies and personnel exacerbates the challenges.

To ensure the effective allocation of medical resources, a robust emergency medical resource allocation mechanism must be established.65 Based on injury assessments, road accessibility, and the distribution of casualties in the disaster zone, the rescue command system should strategically organize the distribution of materials, prioritizing support for the most severely affected areas and those with a high concentration of patients. Additionally, setting up temporary medical stations and field hospitals, alongside deploying mobile medical systems, are vital strategies for providing prompt emergency services and alleviating resource limitations in the disaster area. Studies have demonstrated that real-time resource scheduling, utilizing big data and intelligent algorithms, significantly enhances the efficiency of medical resource utilization, both in terms of personnel and materials.66

Challenges of limited information flow and communication facilities

Damage to communication infrastructure impedes the timely exchange of information between rescue teams, affected communities, and command centers. Limited information flow hinders decision-making and complicates rescue coordination.

To solve the problem of communication limitation, it is necessary to build emergency communication infrastructure before the disaster, such as satellite communication system, radio station and emergency network.67 These devices can keep the information transmission smooth when the communication system in the disaster area is paralyzed, and ensure that the command center can obtain the real-time data in the disaster area in a timely manner. In addition, the rapid recovery of emergency communication network after disaster is also the key to solve the problem of information circulation. The establishment of emergency communication network should pay attention to the integration of multi-channel information, using satellite telephone, mobile Internet and other means to ensure the rapid flow and accurate transmission of information.68

Safety and protection challenges for rescue workers

Rescue personnel face significant risks in debris flow disasters, including unstable terrain, potential for secondary debris flows, and exposure to hazardous conditions. Limited protective resources further increase vulnerability.

Solutions including: (1) comprehensive safety plans: Develop detailed safety protocols and equip rescue teams with necessary protective gear, including helmets, masks, and durable clothing.69 (2) Specialized Training: Provide targeted training for rescue personnel to prepare them for high-risk environments and potential hazards.70 (3) Risk Assessment and Monitoring: Conduct continuous evaluations of secondary disaster risks and issue early warnings. Collaborate with geological experts to identify safe zones and optimal rescue paths.71 Addressing these challenges with strategic solutions will enhance the effectiveness of emergency medical rescue operations and ensure better outcomes for both affected populations and rescue teams.

Future development directions for medical rescue in debris flow disasters

With global climate change and the increasing frequency of natural disasters, debris flow events are becoming more common and severe. These disasters pose significant challenges to emergency medical rescue efforts. To address these challenges, future advancements in technology, strategies, and methods are essential to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of disaster response (Figure 6 summarizes the future development). To underscore global relevance, case reports and system evaluations from Japan (including national disaster medical system evolution following major earthquakes), South America (e.g., mental health service deployment after the 2022 Ecuador landslides) and European operational frameworks further demonstrate the geographic breadth of disaster medical challenges and the transferability of response strategies across diverse settings.

Future development directions for medical rescue in debris flow disasters.

The emergence of portable medical devices has profoundly transformed the landscape of post-disaster medical rescue. Today, portable ultrasound machines, X-ray equipment, hematology analyzers, electrocardiographs, and other devices are extensively utilized in on-site first aid efforts following disasters. These technologies were field-tested in debris-flow or comparable mountainous mass-casualty settings and enable swift injury diagnosis and real-time monitoring of vital signs within the disaster zone, providing rescuers with precise information that aids in assessing the priority treatment needs of the patients. In the future, portable medical devices will evolve towards greater intelligence and integration. For instance, the fusion of artificial intelligence algorithms with these devices will enhance rescue operations by enabling features such as automated diagnosis and advanced image processing.

UAVs can be employed not only for aerial photography and terrain mapping in affected areas but also for material delivery, medical first aid, and the rescue of victims when roadways are impassable. Equipped with advanced functions such as temperature detection and infrared imaging, UAVs can swiftly pinpoint the locations of trapped individuals, thereby maximizing the efficiency of rescue efforts. As UAV technology continues to evolve, it is anticipated that more high-performance, durable UAVs will be deployed in post-disaster rescue operations.

The advancement of artificial intelligence and big data technologies is progressively transforming the decision support systems used in disaster rescue operations. In the event of a debris flow disaster, risk assessment driven by big data, along with the strategic allocation of rescue resources and the prioritization of the patients, will form the cornerstone of effective rescue efforts. These technologies were prototype/pilot-stage or proposed concepts requiring validation. Through machine learning algorithms, intelligent systems can analyze real-time environmental data (such as meteorological conditions and terrain), casualty information (including injuries and vital signs), and traffic data within the disaster zone, thereby aiding rescuers in making timely and informed decisions. Furthermore, the continuous monitoring of big data will provide a crucial foundation for disaster recovery, assisting policymakers in the rational allocation of social resources.

With the increasing frequency and intensity of debris-flow disasters driven by climate change, the integration of climate adaptation strategies into medical planning has become essential. This includes the pre-positioning of medical resources in high-risk regions, the development of early warning-linked medical response systems, and the incorporation of climate-informed risk modeling into disaster preparedness planning.

Healthcare systems must also adapt by strengthening surge capacity, enhancing training for extreme-environment response, and integrating environmental data into operational decision-making. Such climate-resilient planning ensures that emergency medical systems remain responsive and effective under evolving disaster patterns.

From a policy and operational perspective, the findings of this review provide a framework for strengthening disaster medical preparedness in debris-flow-prone regions. Specifically, they support the development of standardized training programs for EMS personnel in austere environments, the establishment of minimum equipment and capability packages for field deployment, and the integration of multidisciplinary coordination mechanisms into disaster response systems. Furthermore, the evidence highlights the importance of incorporating mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) into routine disaster medical planning, as well as enhancing logistics resilience and communication interoperability. These insights can inform national and regional disaster preparedness policies and improve the effectiveness of real-world emergency response.

Conclusions

In summary, debris-flow disasters pose a distinctive medical-rescue challenge driven by secondary hazards, disrupted access, and prolonged field-care needs. Although tools such as UAV reconnaissance, portable diagnostics, telemedicine, and mobile medical capacity are increasingly used, major gaps remain in interoperability, standardized evaluation, workforce readiness, resilient logistics, and continuity of mental health and psychosocial support. Going forward, research should prioritize standardized outcome metrics and prospective/registry-based evaluations to determine which technologies and care pathways measurably improve access time, triage accuracy, and patient outcomes across different resource settings. In parallel, policy should establish minimum capability packages, routine multi-agency training/credentialing, interoperable command-and-communication protocols, resilient last-mile supply chains, and formal integration of mental health and psychosocial support into disaster medical plans to translate current advances into scalable improvements.
