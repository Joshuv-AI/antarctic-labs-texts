Abstract
We provide the first non-trivial lower bounds for single-qubit tomography algorithms and show that at least Ω ⁡ ( 10 N N ​ ε 2 ) {\Omega}\left(\frac{10^{N}}{\sqrt{N}\varepsilon^{2}}\right) copies are required to learn an N N -qubit state ρ ∈ ℂ d × d , d = 2 N \rho\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d},d=2^{N} to within ε \varepsilon trace distance. Pauli measurements, the most commonly used single-qubit measurement scheme, have recently been shown to require at most O ⁡ ( 10 N ε 2 ) O\left(\frac{10^{N}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right) copies for this problem. Combining these results, we nearly settle the long-standing question of the complexity of single-qubit tomography.

Our results

2
Why ten?

It may seem surprising that the number
10
appears in the sample complexity of Pauli tomography. A qubit resides in a two-dimensional Hilbert space, with three Pauli operators per qubit. Thus, one might expect the tight sample complexity to be related to powers of two and/or three. In this context, ten is peculiar because it includes 5 as a prime factor—a seemingly unrelated number.

In this paper, we argue that
10
is very natural for Pauli tomography.
It’s just that we should not look at the prime factors. For our problem, the right way to decompose the number is

\mathbf{10=1+3^{2}}.

Thus, when raising it to the power of
N
, using the binomial theorem,

10^{{N}}=(3^{2}+1)^{N}=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\binom{{N}}{m}(3^{2})^{m}.

(1)

In other words,
10^{N}
is a combination of even powers of 3 and binomial coefficients, which now appears more naturally related to our problem:
\mathrm{3}
comes from Pauli measurements.
This simple expression (or at least a partial sum of it) is the magic equation that arises in the proofs of both the upper and lower bounds.

2.1
Review of the algorithm:
1+{3}^{\mathrm{2}}
comes from
\ell_{2}
norm analysis

We now review the algorithm that achieves the
10^{{N}}
sample complexity in
Yu 20
;
dGK 24
;
ADLY 25
. Through the analysis, we not only observe how 10 arises in Pauli tomography, but also gain an important insight on how to close the gap in the lower bound.

Since there are a total of
3^{N}
Pauli measurements, we evenly divide
n
copies into
3^{N}
groups of equal size
m=n/3^{N}
, each group measures the copies with the Pauli measurement associated with
P=\sigma_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{{N}}
where
\sigma_{i}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
. From the binary strings obtained from the measurements, we can obtain empirical estimates for the expectation value of each Pauli observable
Q\in\{X,Y,Z,\mathbb{I}_{2}\}^{\otimes{N}}
,
\Tr[\rho Q]
.

Let
w(Q)
be the weight of
Q
, which is the number of non-identity components in
Q
. We can learn about
\Tr[\rho Q]
for all Pauli measurement
P
that match the non-identity components in
Q
. For example, if
Q=X\otimes X\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}^{\otimes({N}-2)}
, then all
P
with
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}=X
can be used to learn about
\Tr[\rho Q]
by multiplying the
\{-1,1\}
outcome from the first 2 qubits. For
Q
with weight
w
, the number of such useful Pauli measurements is

n(Q)=3^{{N}-w(Q)}.

Thus we get
n(Q)m
i.i.d.
\{-1,1\}
binary samples to estimate
\Tr[\rho Q]
. The empirical average
E(Q)
has a variance of at most
1/(n(Q)m)
,

\mathbb{E}\left[(E(Q)-\Tr[\rho Q])^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n(Q)m}=\frac{3^{w(Q)}}{n}.

(2)

Note that a quantum state can be seen as a linear combination of Pauli observables (including
\mathbb{I}_{d}
),

\rho=\sum_{Q}\frac{\Tr[\rho Q]}{d}Q.

Our estimate
\hat{\rho}
is then
\hat{\rho}=\sum_{Q}\frac{E(Q)}{d}Q
.
Since Pauli observables are orthogonal, we can compute the Hilbert-Schmidt distance in terms of the
\ell_{2}
distance of expectation values,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|\rho-\hat{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{Q}\mathbb{E}\left[(E(Q)-\Tr[\rho Q])^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{Q}\frac{3^{w(Q)}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{w=0}^{{N}}\binom{{N}}{w}3^{w}\cdot\frac{3^{w}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d\cdot n}\sum_{w=0}^{{N}}\binom{N}{w}9^{w}

\displaystyle=\frac{10^{N}}{dn}.

The key is the second step, where we split the summation according to the weight of Pauli observables. Then the combinatorial Equation (
1
) naturally pops up. Using Cauchy-Schwarz and Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|\rho-\hat{\rho}\right\|}_{1}\right]\right)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|\rho-\hat{\rho}\right\|}_{1}^{2}\right]\leq d\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|\rho-\hat{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{10^{N}}{n}.

Setting the right-hand-side to be
\varepsilon^{2}
gives the desired bound for
n
.

From the above analysis, especially (
2
), we can see that Pauli observables with larger weights contribute more to the final error, which grows exponentially with weight.

Less is more: weakness in prior works

ADLY 25

In that lower bound, we explicitly constructed perturbations along roughly

\sigma_{z}={\rho_{\text{mm}}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{d\ell}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}z_{i}V_{i},\quad z\sim\{-1,1\}^{\ell},

where

It seems natural to choose

BCL 20

However, this is not necessarily true for restricted settings such as single-qubit measurements. The algorithm in

To see this, let’s ask a question: does there exist a state that lies in an
o(d^{2})
-dimensional subspace, but the algorithm still incurs roughly the same error? Let us recall the expected Hilbert-Schmidt error and write things slightly differently,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|\rho-\hat{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{dn}\sum_{w=0}^{{N}}\binom{N}{w}9^{w}=\frac{10^{N}}{dn}\sum_{w=0}^{{N}}\binom{N}{w}\left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^{w}\left(\frac{1}{10}\right)^{{N}-w}.

The summation trivially equals 1. However, what if the summation instead starts at
9{N}/10

\sum_{w=9{N}/10}^{{N}}\binom{N}{w}\left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^{w}\left(\frac{1}{10}\right)^{{N}-w}=\Pr\left[\,\operatorname{Bin}\left({N},\frac{9}{10}\right)\geq\frac{9{N}}{10}\,\right]\simeq\frac{1}{2},

which is still at least a constant (since the median of the binomial distribution is roughly the same as its mean). Therefore, even if a state

10^{{N}}/\varepsilon^{2}

0.9{N}

\sum_{w=9{N}/10}^{{N}}\binom{{N}}{w}3^{w}={O\left(d^{1.9}\right)}.

This strongly suggests that we should only choose Pauli observables with

in the construction since they already contribute to nearly half of the error in the algorithm, and thus

. We will later show that this observation is not due to a weak upper bound analysis, but a fundamental constraint present in all

3.2
Measurement information channel for single-qubit measurements

We provide an intuition for why Pauli measurements should be the best among single-qubit measurements.
The Pauli bases,
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\},\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\},\{|+\text{i}\rangle,|-\text{i}\rangle\}
form a 2-design in the space of qubits.
Thus, when we evenly assign all Pauli measurements, it is almost like performing a Haar-random basis measurement on each qubit. However, to rigorously prove the optimality of Pauli measurements requires some work.

The measurement information channel (MIC)
LA24a
is a powerful tool to quantify the distinguishability of measurements.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{x}\}_{x}\in\mathcal{X}
be a measurement. The measurement information channel is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(A):=\sum_{x}M_{x}\frac{\Tr[M_{x}A]}{\Tr[M_{x}]},\quad{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}:=\sum_{x}\frac{|M_{x}\rangle\rangle\langle\langle M_{x}|}{\Tr[M_{x}]}\in\mathbb{C}^{d^{2}\times d^{2}},

(4)

where
|M_{x}\rangle\rangle=\text{vec}(M_{x})
and
\langle\langle M_{x}|=\text{vec}(M_{x})^{\dagger}
.
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}
is its Kraus representation whereas
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}
is the matrix representation that satisfies
|\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(\rho)\rangle\rangle={\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\rangle
.

When
\mathcal{M}
is a projective measurement, MIC has a physical meaning: it is exactly the quantum channel/instrument that describes the post-measurement state transition when the outcome is lost.

As a toy example, consider single qubit states
\rho=\mathbb{I}_{2}/2
and
\phi_{z}=\mathbb{I}_{2}/2+\alpha(z_{X}X+z_{Y}Y+z_{Z}Z)
, where
z_{X},z_{Y},z_{Z}
are drawn uniformly from
\{-1,1\}^{3}
,
\alpha
is chosen sufficiently small to make
\phi
a valid quantum state. We are essentially mimicking the construction (
3
) for single-qubit states.

For simplicity, consider a single-qubit basis measurement
\mathcal{M}=\{|u_{1}\rangle\langle u_{1}|,|u_{2}\rangle\langle u_{2}|\}
. Using MIC, we can compute the distance of post-measurement states of
\rho
and
\phi_{z}
. After measuring
\rho
with
\mathcal{M}
, the post-measurement state is still
\mathbb{I}_{d}/2
. For
\phi
, the post-measurement state is

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(\phi)=\sum_{i=1,2}|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{i}|\langle u_{i}|\phi|u_{i}\rangle=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{2}}{2}+\alpha\sum_{i=1,2}|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{i}|\langle u_{i}|X+Y+Z|u_{i}\rangle.

The Hilbert-Schmidt distance is

{\left\|\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(\rho-\phi_{z})\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{2}=\alpha^{2}\sum_{i=1,2}\langle u_{i}|z_{X}X+z_{Y}Y+z_{Z}Z|u_{i}\rangle^{2},

which is also the
\ell_{2}
distance and chi-square divergence (up to constants) between outcome distributions. Since
d=2
, the total-variantion distance is within a constant factor of
\ell_{2}
distance, so the above quantity characterizes distinguishability between two states. Note that
z_{X},z_{Y},z_{Z}
are chosen independently from
\{-1,1\}
. In expectation,

\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\,{\left\|\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(\rho-\phi_{z})\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{2}\,\right]=\alpha^{2}\sum_{i=1,2}(\langle u_{i}|X|u_{i}\rangle^{2}+\langle u_{i}|Y|u_{i}\rangle^{2}+\langle u_{i}|Z|u_{i}\rangle^{2})=2\alpha^{2}.

The final step is because
\mathbb{I}_{2},\sigma_{X},\sigma_{Y},\sigma_{Z}
form an orthogonal basis for
2\times 2
complex matrices and
\Tr[(|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{i}|)^{2}]=1
, so
\langle u_{i}|X|u_{i}\rangle^{2}+\langle u_{i}|Y|u_{i}\rangle^{2}+\langle u_{i}|Z|u_{i}\rangle^{2}=1
.

Therefore, regardless of the choice of the basis measurement, the expected distance between the post-measurement states of
\rho
and
\phi_{z}
is still close. This serves as a simple but intuitive example of how MIC characterizes the power of any single-qubit measurement.

In
Section

6.2
, we provide a complete argument for arbitrary single-qubit POVMs and how our construction using Pauli observables still suffices to show a tight lower bound for single-qubit measurements.

4
Preliminaries

4.1
Quantum States and Measurement

Quantum states.

A
pure state
is presented by a unit vector
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
\ket{\psi}
is in the span of the orthonormal basis of the Hilbert space,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i},\ket{e_{i}}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\alpha_{i}|^{2}=1.

Measuring the state along the basis results in observing
\ket{e_{i}}
state with probability
|\alpha_{i}|^{2}
.

More generally, one can write a statistical mixture of pure states, known as mixed states.

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}},

where the pure state
\ket{\psi_{i}}
is observed with probability
p_{i}
. Mixed states have the following properties:

\displaystyle\rho\succeq 0,\quad\Tr[\rho]=1.

POVMs.

Measurements are typically represented using
Positive Operator-Valued Measure
(POVM). They are represented by a collection of p.s.d observables that add up to the identity.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\left\{M_{x}\right\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}},\quad\sum_{x\in X}M_{x}=\mathbb{I}_{d}.

Born’s rule states that the probability of observing outcome
x
for state
\rho
is

\displaystyle\Pr[X=x]=\Tr[M_{x}\rho].

Moreso, Born’s rule states that
\rho
collapses to some state after measurement. In some cases, the measurement is repeatable on the same state if
M_{x}
are all orthogonal. However, this is not typically the case. Thus, the copy-wise tomography task considers
measuring each copy once
.

4.2
Problem setup

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\binom{[N]}{m}}\prod_{i\in S}\sum_{\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma}\sum_{o\in O}\alpha_{o}^{i}{\beta_{\sigma_{i}}^{i,o}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\binom{[N]}{m}}\prod_{i\in S}\sum_{o\in O}\alpha_{o}^{i}\sum_{\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma}{\beta_{\sigma_{i}}^{i,o}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\in\binom{[N]}{m}}\prod_{i\in S}1=\binom{N}{m}.

The concluding step is due to the convexity of the coefficients
\left\{\alpha_{o}\right\}_{o\in O}
and the sub-normalization of the factors
\left\{\mid\beta_{\sigma}^{i,o}\mid\right\}_{\sigma\in\Sigma}
. Thus, we get the desired bound by summing over
m=\lceil 9{N}/10\rceil
to
{N}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\langle\langle V_{i}|{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\mathcal{M}}|V_{i}\rangle\rangle\leq\sum_{m=9N/10}^{N}{{N}\choose m}.

∎

6.3
Putting things together

When discussing lower bounds for single-qubit measurements, a combinatorial argument naturally arises to bound the spectral norm of the MIC.
Thus, it suffices to establish the combinatorial bound to guarantee almost-tight copy-complexity bounds for Pauli measurements and general single-qubit measurements. In addition to the single-qubit generalization, we have made a harder instance construction by reducing
