Vδ1 T cells exhibit high lactic acid resistance and antitumor activity in solid tumors

Introduction

The advent of cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized oncology, with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T-cell therapy achieving remarkable success in treating hematological malignancies. However, there are significant challenges in the application of autologous αβ T cells in solid tumors, including on-target/off-tumor toxicity, a suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), inefficient trafficking, and costly, personalized manufacturing [1–3]. Natural killer (NK) cells and γδ T cells represent promising innate immune effector platforms for allogeneic therapy because of their major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-independent tumor recognition ability and low risk of inducing graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) [4].

Among γδ T cells, the Vδ2 subset (often paired with Vγ9) is predominant in peripheral blood and can be robustly expanded using phosphoantigens such as zoledronate [5]. However, clinical responses are variable, possibly because of functional heterogeneity and terminal differentiation upon expansion [6–8].

However, the focus is increasingly shifting to the Vδ1 subset of γδ T cells, which predominantly reside in tissues such as the intestinal epithelium, skin, and lungs [9] and exhibit superior tissue homing, infiltration, and resistance to immunosuppressive factors such as galectin-3 [10]. In contrast to Vδ2 T cells, Vδ1 T cells exhibit reduced alloreactivity [11] and are less prone to inducing GvHD. Notably, Vδ1 T cells exhibit a degree of stemness within the tumor microenvironment that enables them to resist TME-induced exhaustion, sustain their proliferative potential, and continuously replenish effector cell pools, which are key advantages for their application in adoptive cancer immunotherapy [12, 13] and making them ideal candidates for use in allogeneic therapy [5].

Although Vδ1 T cells exhibit superior antitumor capabilities, their clinical application has been hindered by difficulties in acquisition because of their tissue distribution characteristics [5], a lack of safe and efficient expansion methods [14, 15], and insufficient investigation of their underlying mechanisms. Recent advances, including the “Delta One T” (“DOT”) cell expansion protocol, have enabled the generation of Vδ1-enriched products, renewing interest in their therapeutic application [16]. Preclinical studies on genetically engineered Vδ1 T cells have demonstrated promising results [17–22], with anti-CD19 CAR Vδ1 T cell products receiving regulatory approval in China, underscoring the considerable therapeutic potential of this cell type.

Building on this foundation, we developed a highly efficient, feeder-free, scalable expansion protocol for Vδ1 T cells using an TCR Vδ1 agonist antibody. We performed a comprehensive comparative analysis of the phenotypes, omics and functional capacities of expanded Vδ1 T cells and Vδ2 T cells. Furthermore, we engineered Vδ1 T cells with CAR and a secreted IL-15 construct to increase their specificity and persistence, resulting in potent antitumor efficacy in preclinical solid tumor models. This work establishes a robust path for the clinical translation of Vδ1 T-cell-based therapies.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

The human cancer cell lines OVCAR-8, A549, HCC827, MCF7, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, HepG2, Hep3B, Huh-7, EC109, HT29, SW480, LM-MEL-45, LM-MEL-53, A375, HYM-1, were obtained from the National Infrastructure of Cell line Resources BMCR. OVCAR-8, A549, HCC827, and EC109 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). MCF7, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, HT29, SW480, LM-MEL-45, ML-MEL-53, A375, HYM-1, HepG2, Hep3B and Huh-7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) with 10% FBS (Gibco). All cell lines were subcultured and the medium was refreshed every 3–4 days based on confluency and growth characteristics. Mycoplasma negativity and STR authentication were confirmed for each cell line at regular intervals.

Production of γδ T cells

Vδ1 T Cell Expansion: Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donors using Ficoll-Paque™ density gradient centrifugation (TBD Technology). The PBMCs were resuspended in serum-free cryopreservation medium (NCM Biotech) and stored at − 80 °C. After thawing, cells were seeded into culture wells pre-coated with patented anti-TCR Vδ1 agonist antibody and cultured at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL in KBM 581 (Corning) supplemented with 10% serum replacement (SR; Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX™ (Gibco), animal free 100 ng/mL IL-4, 70 ng/mL IFN-γ, 15ng/ml IL-1β and 7 ng/mL IL-21, (all from Sino Biological) for the first 7 days. After 4 days of initial stimulation, cells were transferred to fresh wells without antibody coating and further cultured for 3 days. On day 8, the medium was replaced with KBM 581 (Corning) supplemented with 10% serum replacement (SR; Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX™ (Gibco), animal free 70 ng/mL IL-15, and 30 ng/mL IFN-γ (Sino Biological), with medium changes every 2–3 days until day 21. “DOT” cytokine cocktail [16] are employed and additional IL-2 (100 IU/ml, Four Rings Biopharmaceutical Co LTD, Beijing) are applied after day8 for further enhancing Vδ1 T cells expansion efficacy.

Vδ2 T Cell Expansion: Thawed PBMCs were resuspended in KBM 581 (Corning) supplemented with 10% serum replacement (SR; Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX™ (Gibco), 200 IU/mL IL-2 (Four Rings Biopharmaceutical Co LTD, Beijing), and 5 µM zoledronic acid (Chia Tai Tianqing Pharmceutical Group Co LTD, Jiangsu) and activated for 4 days. The cells were then transferred to fresh medium without zoledronic acid and cultured until day 12.

After expansion, both Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells were isolated using TCRγ/δ + T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) to remove non-γδ T cells, followed by cryopreservation or experiment.

Production of CAR-γδT cells

Lentiviral vector production

A fourth-generation self-inactivating (SIN) lentiviral vector encoding the chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) was constructed. The MSLN-CAR consisted of an anti-mesothelin (MSLN) single-chain variable fragment (scFv), CD8α hinge and transmembrane domain, 4-1BB and CD3ζ signaling domains, and optionally, a secreted IL-15 (sIL-15) linked via a P2A peptide. The NCL-CAR consisted of an anti-NCL single-chain variable fragment (scFv), CD8α hinge and transmembrane domain, CD28 and 4-1BB co-stimulatory domain plus CD3ζ signaling domains.

For lentivirus production, 293T cells were co-transfected using a fourth-generation lentiviral packaging system, which included: (1) CAR transfer plasmid, (2) pMD2.G (VSV-G envelope), (3) pRSV-Rev, (4) pMDLg/pRRE (Gag/Pol). Supernatants were harvested at 48 and 72 h, filtered (0.45 μm), and concentrated by ultracentrifugation. Viral titer was determined via qPCR or functional assay.

CAR-γδT/αβT cells production

PBMCs were isolated from healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells using Ficoll-Paque™ density gradient centrifugation and cryopreserved as mentioned above.

For CAR-Vδ1 T cells, thawed PBMCs were activated with anti-TCR Vδ1 antibody in KBM 581 medium (Corning) supplemented with 10% serum replacement SR (Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX™ (Gibco), 100 ng/mL IL-4, 70 ng/mL IFN-γ, 15ng/ml IL-1β and 7 ng/mL IL-21, (all from Sino Biological). Two days post-activation, cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors at an MOI of 5 using RetroNectin (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, non-tissue culture-treated plates were coated with RetroNectin (10 µg/mL) for 2 h at 37℃. After blocking with 2% BSA, lentiviral supernatant was added to the plates and centrifuged (2000 ×g, 32 °C, 2 h). Activated Vδ1 T cells were then added to the virus-coated plates and centrifuged (800 ×g, 32 °C, 30 min). Transduced cells were expanded in KBM 581 with 10% SR, 1% GlutaMAX™, 100 ng/mL IL-4, 70 ng/mL IFN-γ, 15ng/ml IL-1β and 7 ng/mL IL-21 for another 5 days then transferred to the medium with KBM 581 containing 10% SR, 1% GlutaMAX™, 70 ng/mL IL-15, and 30 ng/mL IFN-γ. To further enhance expansion efficacy, IL-2(100IU/ml) was added. Non γδT cells were depleted using TCRγ/δ + T Cell Isolation Kit, human (Miltenyi Biotec). The purified CAR-Vδ1 T cells were subjected to subsequent detection, functional assays or cryopreservation.

For CAR-αβT cells, thawed PBMCs were activated with T cell transact (Miltenyi Biotec) for two days before lentiviral infection. αβT cells were cultured in KBM 581 supplemented with 10% SR, 1% Glutamax and 200IU/ml IL-2 for 12 days followed by functional detection, experiment or cryopreservation.

Flow cytometry

Cell surface marker expression was analyzed by flow cytometry. Cells were pre-treated with Human TruStain FcX™ (Fc receptor blocking solution; BioLegend) to minimize non-specific binding. The following fluorescently conjugated antibodies were used: anti-CD3 (clone OKT3; BioLegend) anti-TCR Vδ1 (clone TS8.2 Thermo Fisher Scientific), anti-TCR Vδ2 (clone B6; BioLegend), anti-CD45RA (clone HI100; BioLegend), anti-CD62L (clone DREG-56; BioLegend), anti-NKG2D (clone 1D11; BioLegend), anti-NKp30 (clone P30-15; BioLegend), anti-NKp44 (clone p44-8; BioLegend), anti-DNAM-1 (clone 11A8; BioLegend), and anti-PD-1 (clone A17188A; BioLegend).

For detection of apoptotic cells, Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with PI (Biolegend) were applied according to manufactures’ protocol.

For detection of tumor-associated antigens, the following antibodies were employed: anti-mesothelin (MSLN; clone 420411; R&D Systems), anti-NCL (Nucleolin; clone MS-3; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-MICA (clone 159227; R&D Systems), anti-MICB (clone 236511; R&D Systems), anti-ULBP1 (clone 170818; R&D Systems), anti-ULBP2/5/6 (clone 165903; R&D Systems), and anti-ULBP3 (clone 709116; R&D Systems).

Short-term cytotoxicity assay

Cytotoxic activity was assessed by co-culturing Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells with various tumor cell lines at effector-to-target (E: T) ratios of 5:1, 2:1, 1:2 and 1:5. The co-culture was performed in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) in 96-well plates for 4 h. Cell cytotoxicity was evaluated using the CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after co-incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 4 h, the supernatant was collected, and the lactic acid dehydrogenase (LDH) substrate was added for color development. The reaction was stopped using the provided stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm.The percentage of cytotoxicity was calculated using the following formula:

Aexp = absorbance of experimental wells (effectors + targets).

Aeffspon = absorbance of effector cells alone (spontaneous release).

Atarspon = absorbance of target cells alone (spontaneous release).

Atarmax = absorbance of target cells lysed with detergent (maximum release).

Long-term cytotoxicity assay

On day 0, tumor cells were seeded into a 96-well E-plate and loaded into the Real-Time Cell Analyzer (RTCA; Agilent) for baseline measurement. After 24 h, effector cells were added to the co-culture wells at effector-to-target (E: T) ratios of 5:1 or 2:1. The plate was then returned to the RTCA station for continuous monitoring until 48 h post-target addition. Throughout the experiment, cells were maintained at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Cytotoxic activity was calculated using the following formula:

CItar = cell index of target cells alone,

CIₑₓₚ = cell index of co-culture wells (effectors + targets).

Cytokine detection

Vδ1 T cells were co-cultured with OVCAR8 cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 2:1 in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator for 24 h. Following incubation, the supernatant was collected, and the concentrations of IL-2, IL-15, IFN-γ, TNF-α, GM-CSF, and IL-17 A were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (Dakewei Biotech, Shenzhen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transcriptomics, proteomics, untargeted metabolomics analysis, TCGA and GTEx data analysis

Transcriptomics

Total RNA was extracted from Vδ1/Vδ2 T cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) under different treatment conditions. Transcriptomic library construction and high‑throughput RNA sequencing were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). RNA integrity was assessed using the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Subsequently, mRNA was isolated, fragmented and processed for cDNA library construction. Finally, the cDNA library was sequenced and the obtained reads were aligned to reference genome using Hisat2 (v2.0.5). featureCounts was used to count the reads and FPKM values for each gene were calculated. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was implemented by the clusterProfiler package.

Proteomics and untargeted metabolomics

Following treatment with or without 15mM lactic acid for 24, sorted γδ T cells were washed twice with cold PBS, rapidly frozen, and maintained at − 80 °C until subsequent proteomic and metabolomic analyses. Proteomics and metabolomics sequencing were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China)

Survival and infiltration analyses

Gene expression data and corresponding clinical information were retrieved and analyzed via the GEPIA2 online tool (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/).Subsequent data processing, differential expression analysis, immune infiltration-associated visualization, and survival analysis were performed within the GEPIA2 platform according to its built-in analytical pipeline. The analytical procedures, including data filtering, immune deconvolution inference, and Kaplan–Meier survival modeling, were conducted in accordance with the standard protocol documented in the original GEPIA2 study (PMID: 31114875).

ATP detection

ATP production was quantified using Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were harvested and washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual medium. Subsequently, cells were resuspended in the lysis buffer provided with the kit at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL and lysed on ice for 15 min with occasional vortexing. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet cellular debris, and the supernatants were collected immediately for ATP detection. For the assay, 100 µL of the supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of the luciferin-luciferase reaction mixture in a white 96-well microplate. After incubation at room temperature for 5 min, the relative luminescence units (RLU) were measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, USA). A standard curve was generated using serial dilutions of ATP standard solution provided in the kit to quantify the absolute ATP concentration. The ATP content was normalized to the total protein concentration of each sample (determined by BCA protein assay) to account for variations in cell number and lysis efficiency.

In vivo experiments

Female NOD-Prkdcscid IL2rgtm1/Bcgen (B-NDG) mice aged 6 weeks were purchased from Biocytogen (Beijing) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, controlled temperature, and ad libitum access to food and water.

For the subcutaneous tumor model, 5 × 10⁶ HCC827 cells were inoculated into the flank of each mouse. Once the average tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to different groups (PBS, Vδ2, or Vδ1 T cells) based on tumor size. A dose of 1 × 10⁷ γδ T cells per mouse was administered intravenously every five days. The PBS control group only received an equal volume of PBS. Mice receiving γδ T cell therapy were additionally given 10,000 IU of IL-2. Tumor volume was measured every five days using the formula: Volume = length × width² × π/6.

All mice were euthanized when the maximum tumor volume reached 2000 mm³, tumors and organs were collected for further analysis.

For the MSLN CAR in vivo experiment, 5 × 10⁶ OVCAR-8-Luc cells were injected intraperitoneally into 6-week-old female NSG mice. On day 10 post-inoculation, tumor burden was assessed using bioluminescence imaging system (BLT Technology, Guangzhou), and mice were randomized into different (PBS control, sIl15-Vδ1, MSLN CAR- Vδ1, MSLN CAR-sIL15 Vδ1) groups based on luminescence intensity. Treatments consisting of 1 × 10⁷ Vδ1 T cells and 10,000 IU IL-2 were administered intraperitoneally every four days; the control group only received PBS injection. Bioluminescence was monitored throughout the experiment. After four treatment cycles, survival was evaluated.

For the NCL CAR in vivo experiment, 5 × 10⁶ OVCAR-8-Luc cells were injected intraperitoneally into 5–6-week-old female NSG mice. On day 10 post-inoculation, tumor burden was assessed using bioluminescence imaging system, and mice were randomized into different (PBS control, NCL CAR-αβT, Vδ1 T, NCL CAR- Vδ1 T) groups based on luminescence intensity. Treatments consisting of 1 × 10⁷ Vδ1 T (every 4 days) or αβT cells (every 7 days) and 5000 IU IL-2 were administered intraperitoneally. Bioluminescence was monitored using PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III. After 3-week treatment, survival rate was evaluated.

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Institutional Animal Care and Use(Peking Union Medical College). Every effort was made to minimize suffering, including the use of appropriate anesthesia and humane endpoints such as maximum tumor size and overall health condition.

Immunohistochemistry and H&E staining

Heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and tumor tissues were collected from euthanized mice, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h, and processed for paraffin embedding and sectioning. For immunohistochemical staining, tumor sections were incubated with a rabbit anti-human CD3 antibody (clone SP162, Abcam) at a 1:150 dilution. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on sections of major organs to evaluate morphological changes.

To quantify γδ T cell infiltration in tumor tissues, the number of CD3-positive cells was counted in five randomly selected fields (40× magnification) per sample, and the average count per group was used for comparative analysis.

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted from γδT cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio) supplemented with 1% Protease Inhibitor Mixture (Solarbio) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio). The homogenates were incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Equal amounts of protein (10 µg per lane) were mixed with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer (containing β-mercaptoethanol) and denatured by heating at 95 °C for 10 min. The denatured proteins were separated by 12% or 4–15% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) at a constant voltage of 80 V for the stacking gel and 140 V for the resolving gel. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA) using a wet transfer system (Bio-Rad, USA) at a constant current of 300 mA for 60 min at 4 °C.

The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST: 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 h at room temperature to prevent non-specific binding. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD3ζ/PARP1 (1:1000 dilution, Abcam). After three washes with TBST (5 min each), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000 dilution, Zsbio) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then washed three times with TBST again (5 min each).

The protein bands were visualized using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) Detection Kit (NCM) and imaged with a Tanon 4600 Automatic Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System (Tanon Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). For membrane stripping and re-probing, the blots were incubated in stripping buffer (25 mM glycine, pH 2.0, 1% SDS) at room temperature for 30 min with gentle shaking to remove bound primary and secondary antibodies. The membranes were then washed thoroughly with TBST three times (5 min each) to eliminate residual stripping buffer. After stripping, the membranes were re-blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation for 2 h at room temprature with primary antibody against GAPDH (1:2000 dilution, Zsbio) as an alternative internal control. Subsequent steps were identical to those described above: incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution, Zsbio) for 1 h at room temperature, three washes with TBST (5 min each), ECL visualization, and imaging. The relative intensity of target protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), and the expression levels were normalized to that of GAPDH for protein expression calculation.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by an appropriate post hoc test (e.g. Tukey’s test). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Intratumoral Vδ1 T-cell infiltration is associated with improved survival in patients with various solid cancers

To evaluate the anti-tumor potential of distinct subsets of γδ T cells, we assessed and analyzed the relationships between the intratumoral infiltration of Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells and overall survival across multiple solid tumor types using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database. The results demonstrated that a greater abundance of tumor-infiltrating Vδ1 T cells than Vδ2 T cells in various solid malignancies (Fig. 1A). Additionally, although both Vδ1 T and Vδ2 T cells exhibited greater infiltration within tumors than within normal tissues, the degree of this infiltration in Vδ2 T cells was far less pronounced than that of Vδ1 T cells (Fig. 1B). Data from TCGA database further revealed increased levels of Vδ1 T cell infiltration were positively correlated with prolonged survival in patients with various solid cancers, indicating that increased Vδ1 T cells in tumors is associated with prolonged patient survival (Fig. 1C-D). Vδ2 T cells infiltration did not exhibit a comparable trend and was, interestingly, not significantly associated with clinical outcomes in patients with most of the cancer types examined (Fig. 1C-D). These findings are consistent with those of previously published studies [12, 23], supporting the hypothesis that Vδ1 T cells may have greater therapeutic potential than Vδ2 T cells in the context of solid tumors.

Anti-TCR Vδ1 antibody and cytokines robustly expands Vδ1 T cells from PBMCs

Based on the results above, we plan to further investigate immunotherapeutic strategies leveraging Vδ1 T cells and characterize the functional differences in antitumor immunity between Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells. We established a specific and highly efficient expansion system for Vδ1 T cells derived from healthy human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using our humanized anti-T-cell receptor (TCR) Vδ1 antibody, followed by a two-phase cytokine-based expansion protocol based on the “DOT” method [16]. Vδ2 T cells were activated by zoledronic acid (ZOL) followed by expansion in the presence of IL-2 using the conventional method (Fig. 2A). Vδ1 T cells were robustly expanded through stimulation with anti-TCR Vδ1 antibody in combination with DOT cytokine cocktail, resulting in significant increases in the average purity(Fig. 2B-C), fold expansion and total cell yield from multiple healthy donors (Fig. 2B). After 21 days of culture, the purity of the Vδ1 T cells increased from an initial frequency of less than 2% to more than 68%. The Vδ1 T cells expanded approximately 178,000-fold on average with the average yield exceeding 1.2 × 1010. In addition, the conventional ZOL-based protocol also achieves efficient expansion (purity, expansion fold and cell yield) of Vδ2 T cells in vitro. Notably, the expansion efficacy of the Vδ1 T cells expansion system we developed is superior to that of Vδ2 T cells expanded via the conventional ZOL-based protocol in terms of fold change and numbers. (Figure 2D). Using final expansion purity > 80% and total cell expansion fold > 200 as the cutoff criteria, over 40% of donors achieved the desired expansion of Vδ1 T cells via our developed system, while only ~ 24% of donors met the criteria for Vδ2 T cells expanded by zoledronic acid (Fig. 2E).

Next, we investigated whether the initial proportions of Vδ1 T cells were correlated with the final Vδ1 T cells expansion fold change or purity; however, no significant association was observed (Fig. 2F). Besides, initial Vδ2 γδT cells and the Vδ2/Vδ1 ratio were also explored, no significant correlation was found either (Fig. S1A). In contrast, the terminal expansion purity of Vδ2 T cells was positively correlated with the initial frequency of Vδ2 T cells within the PBMC population, but the terminal fold change was negatively correlated with initial Vδ2 T-cells purity (Fig. 2F). These results suggested that the expansion of Vδ2 T cells is partly dependent on the initial purity, whereas the expansion of Vδ1 T cells appears to be influenced by other factors.

Finally, we compared the expansion performance of our anti-TCR Vδ1 antibody (Ab-3) with that of two commercially available anti-TCR Vδ1 antibodies (TS-1 and TS-8.2). The results demonstrated that Ab-3 significantly outperformed both commercial antibodies in terms of Vδ1 T-cells purity, fold change and yields (Fig. 2G and H).

In conclusion, the use of our antibody with the cytokine protocol can efficiently expand Vδ1 T cells from peripheral blood and ultimately increase the purity and cell number to the required values.

Vδ1 T cells exhibit enhanced tumor infiltration and potent cytotoxicity against solid tumors

To investigate the differences between expanded Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells, we conducted transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that Vδ2 T cells expressed genes associated with cytotoxicity (e.g., NKG7, GNLY, GZMH, and GZMK), effectors (e.g., EOMES, KLRG1, and CX3CR1), antigen presentation (e.g., HLA-DP and HLA-DR), and certain inhibitory markers (e.g., LAG3, ADRB2, and TGFBR3) at higher levels. In contrast, Vδ1 T cells were enriched in genes related to stemness (e.g., TCF7, LEF1, CCR7, BACH2 and KIT), activation (e.g., TNFRSF4, TNFRSF18, IL18R1, and NCR2), and tissue retention (e.g., XCL1 and ITGAX) (Fig. 3A-B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further revealed that Vδ1 T cells were predominantly enriched in pathways involved in inflammation, chemotaxis and tissue migration, whereas Vδ2 T cells were more enriched in pathways related to cytotoxicity and antigen presentation (Fig. 3C). Proteomic analysis corroborated these transcriptomic trends in terms of protein expression levels and revealed that compared with Vδ2 T cells, Vδ1 T cells expressed higher levels of proteins involved in oxidative respiration, fatty acid transport and metabolism (Fig. 3D). These characteristics may confer a more durable antitumor response in Vδ1 T cells than in their Vδ2 counterparts when they contact tumor cells, thereby mitigating their susceptibility to rapid functional impairment and exhaustion.

We further examined the expression of surface activation receptors and phenotypic markers on Vδ1 T cells by flow cytometry. Although both Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells expressed substantial amounts of NKG2D and DNAM-1, Vδ2 T cells lacked the expression of natural cytotoxicity receptors NKp30 and NKp44, which are crucial for mediating tumor recognition (Fig. 3E). In terms of phenotypes, more than 80% of the Vδ1 T cells on average were found to belong to the naïve/stem central memory (CD45RA⁺CD62L⁺) and central memory (CD45RA⁻CD62L⁺) T-cell subsets (Fig. 3F), whose members are known for their superior self-renewal capacity, proliferative potential and relatively low state of differentiation. In contrast, zoledronate-expanded Vδ2 T cells were predominantly classified into the effector memory (CD45RA⁻CD62L⁻) and terminally differentiated effector T-cell (CD45RA⁺CD62L⁻) subsets (Fig. 3F).

To evaluate their antitumor activity, expanded Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells were cocultured with various solid tumor cell lines derived from common malignancies. Compared with Vδ2 T cells, Vδ1 T cells presented enhanced (Fig. 3G and H) cytotoxic activity and, notably, significantly stronger killing abilities in lung and breast cancer cell lines (Fig. 3G). In the killing assay of other tumor cell lines, Vδ1 T cells exhibit matched cytotoxicity to Vδ2 T cells (Fig. S2A-B). Moreover, even at a low effector-to-target (E/T) ratio (1:2), Vδ1 T cells retained their cytotoxic capacity, whereas Vδ2 T cells largely lost their killing ability (Fig. 3I). We further examined the expression of stress ligands on tumor cell lines (HCC827 and MDA-MB-231) susceptible to Vδ1 T-cell killing. The results indicated that sensitive tumor cell lines exhibited high surface expression of MHC class I-related molecular stress ligands identified by Vδ1 TCR and natural cytotoxic receptors (NCRs) (Fig. S3).

In subsequent experiments, we subcutaneously inoculated NSG mice with the HCC827 cell line, which is highly sensitive to Vδ1 T cell-mediated killing. After tumor establishment, the mice received intravenous injections of Vδ1 T cells, Vδ2 T cells, or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Fig. 3J). The results demonstrated that tumor growth in mice treated with either Vδ1 or Vδ2 T cells was slower than that in PBS-treated mice, with the Vδ1 T cells group exhibiting more pronounced suppression of tumor progression (Fig. 3K). Tumor tissue was collected and the tumor volume was measured (Fig. 3L-M). Besides, Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells infiltration were assessed and the results displayed significantly greater infiltration of Vδ1 T cells within the tumor (Fig. 3N-O). Additionally, the histopathological examination of major organs revealed little evidence of tissue damage in either the Vδ1- or Vδ2-T cells treated groups (Fig. S4). Taken together, Vδ1 T cells exhibit superior anti-solid tumor activity compared to Vδ2 T cells.

Vδ1 T cells exhibit high levels of lactic acid resistance mediated by high poly (ADP-ribose)polymerase 1 (PARP1) expression

Solid tumors produce abundant metabolic waste products, yet Vδ1 T cells are enriched within the tumor microenvironment (TME). To investigate the effects of lactic acid, a prevalent immunosuppressive metabolite produced by tumor cells, on these two subsets, we treated the Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells with high concentrations of lactic acid (15 mM) to simulate unfavorable acidic conditions within the tumor microenvironment and assessed their cytotoxic activity against tumor cells. Interestingly, Vδ1 T cells appeared to be less sensitive to lactic acid than Vδ2 T cells (Fig. 4A). At 24 h after lactic acid treatment, the cytotoxic activity of Vδ1 T cells was decreased by approximately 25% on average, whereas that of Vδ2 T cells was decreased by more than 40% (Fig. 4B).

We further compared the transcriptional, proteomic, and metabolic alterations (Fig. S5A-B) in Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells before versus after lactic acid treatment. The results revealed that Vδ2 T cells exhibited markedly impaired oxidative phosphorylation and reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, accompanied by suppressed amino acid metabolism and antigen presentation. However, Vδ1 T cells exhibited only limited downregulation of effector function and fewer altered genes linked to effector activation and amino acid metabolic processes (Fig. 4C-D). Consistent with these observations, in vitro ATP quantification confirmed that 15 mM lactic acid treatment had little effect on ATP production in Vδ1 T cells, whereas Vδ2 T cells showed a significant reduction (Fig. 4E). In addition, metabolite profiling revealed that the expression of certain energy-related metabolites in Vδ1 T cells, such as N-formylmethionine (an amino acid involved in mitochondrial biogenesis), citric acid (a substrate in the tricarboxylic acid cycle), and nicotinamide (a precursor of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, NADH), was moderately upregulated even after lactic acid treatment, suggesting that oxidative respiration is adaptively enhanced to meet energy demands under adverse conditions. Additionally, the increased production of uric acid (associated with nucleic acid metabolism) suggested that DNA repair was reinforced and that the proliferative capacity was sustained under cellular stress (Fig. 4F).

Furthermore, proteomic analysis revealed that the expression level of PARP1 (a DNA repair-associated protein) was significantly greater in Vδ1 T cells than in Vδ2 T cells (Fig. 4G). This finding was further validated by Western blot (WB) analysis, showing that under 45 mM lactic acid treatment, the cleaved-PARP1/total PARP1 ratio (a hallmark of caspase-mediated apoptotic activation) was significantly increased in Vδ2 T cells, whereas Vδ1 T cells showed no significant change (Fig. 4H-I). Critically, compared with Vδ2 T cells, Vδ1 T cells constitutively expressed higher basal levels of PARP1 (Fig. 4H), which is a phylogenetically conserved DNA damage sensor and repair enzyme, playing a crucial role in recognizing and repairing DNA damage [24] under stress.

To validate the adaptability of Vδ1 T cells, different concentrations of lactic acid (15, 30, 45, and 60 mM) were added during γδ T-cells culture. Following lactic acid treatment, both the Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells subsets underwent apoptosis in a concentration-dependent manner. However, under the same concentration of lactic acid, Vδ1 T cells displayed less apoptosis than Vδ2 T cells did. Notably, under extremely high concentrations (45 and 60 mM) of lactic acid, Vδ2 T cells exhibited a pronounced apoptotic effect, whereas a group of Vδ1 T cells remained alive, demonstrating that Vδ1 T cells possess stronger tolerance to lactic acid than Vδ2 T cells did (Fig. 4J).

To further investigate whether PARP1 contributes to the survival of Vδ1 cells under lactic acid exposure, we blocked PARP1 activity using PARP inhibitor olaparib and examined apoptosis in Vδ1 T cells. The results demonstrated that olaparib increased apoptosis in Vδ1 T cells, with a marked elevation under lactic acid conditions (Fig. 4K). Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of Vδ1 cells against OVCAR8 cells was also impaired by olaparib treatment. Notably, Vδ1 cells co-treated with olaparib and lactic acid exhibited a substantial reduction in their killing capacity against OVCAR8 cells (Fig. 4L). Consistently, Western blotting demonstrated markedly elevated expression of DNA damage markers γH2AX and the apoptotic marker cleaved-PARP1 in Olaparib- or Olaparib/lactate-exposed Vδ1 cells (Fig. 4M and N), supporting that Olaparib impaired PARP1-mediated DNA repair capacity and consequently promotes apoptosis.

Collectively, Vδ1 T cells highly express the DNA repair protein-PARP1, which enables them to maintain their survival and effector functions in a lactate-rich environment. Notably, Vδ2 T cells do not exhibit this characteristic, highlighting that Vδ1 T cells possess greater advantages in combating solid tumors.

The recombinant lentiviral vectors were transfected into 293T cells. Subsequent analyses confirmed CD3ζ expression in cells transfected with CAR-containing constructs (Fig. 5B). The lentiviral transduction efficiency in Vδ1 T cells was assessed by measuring EGFP fluorescence using flow cytometry (Fig. 5C). All three lentiviral constructs achieved a transduction efficiency of approximately 20% or greater (Fig. 5D). The surface expression of activating and inhibitory receptors was subsequently compared between transduced and nontransduced Vδ1 T cells by flow cytometric analysis. No significant differences in the expression of activating receptors were detected between the two groups (Fig. S6A-B). Similarly, the expression of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), a classic inhibitory receptor, remained consistently low and did not change notably following transduction (Fig. S6A-B). Phenotypically, the majority of lentivirus-transduced Vδ1 T cells were classified into the naïve/stem central memory and central memory subtypes, while only a small proportion exhibited an effector memory phenotype. Terminally differentiated effector cells were rarely detected (Fig. S6C).

Next, we examined MSLN expressions in various tumor cell lines (Fig. S7). We chose MSLN-high-expressing OVCAR8 and MSLN-unexpressing A549 as target cells. Specifically, CAR-Vδ1 T cells effectively lysed MSLN-expressing ovarian cancer cells (OVCAR8), even at low effector-to-target ratios (2:1), whereas unmodified Vδ1 T cells exhibited limited cytotoxic activity under the same conditions. Notably, CAR-Vδ1 T cells engineered to secrete IL-15 (CAR-sIL15 Vδ1) achieved nearly complete tumor elimination within 24 h (Fig. 5E). Even at very low effector-to-target ratios and within short coculture periods, both CAR-Vδ1 and CAR-sIL15-Vδ1 T cells retained some antitumor activity, in contrast to unmodified Vδ1 T cells, which lost most of their inhibitory capacity (Fig. 5F).Furthermore, compared with their non-IL-15-secreting counterparts, IL-15-secreting Vδ1 T cells displayed enhanced cytotoxicity against tumor cells (Fig. 5G), which is consistent with previous reports on the role of IL-15 in enhancing immune cell function [25, 26].

Additionally, genetically modified Vδ1 T cells showed markedly increased secretion of T helper 1 (Th1)-related and antitumor cytokines after they were cocultured with OVCAR8 cells. In contrast, the level of IL-17 A, a cytokine associated with Vδ1-mediated tumor promotion [27–29], remained low and did not differ significantly among the groups (Fig. 5H).

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of MSLN-CAR-Vδ1 T cells in vivo, OVCAR8-Luc cells were intraperitoneally inoculated into NSG mice to establish a disseminated tumor model. Following tumor engraftment, the mice were divided into treatment groups and administered the respective Vδ1 T cell products via intraperitoneal injection (Fig. 5I). Tumor progression was assessed by in vivo bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 5J). Tumor development was significantly suppressed in mice treated with MSLN-CAR- and MSLN-CAR‑sIL15‑modified Vδ1 T cells (Fig. 5J and K), with more pronounced tumor suppression in CAR‑sIL15 Vδ1 T cells-treated groups, indicating a synergistic effect conferred by IL-15 secretion. Moreover, both the CAR and CAR-sIL15 treatment groups exhibited marked prolongation of overall survival relative to mice receiving unmodified Vδ1 T cells and PBS (Fig. 5L). Furthermore, Vδ1 T cells engineered to only express IL-15 exhibited modest increases in antitumor activity and survival (Fig. 5L). Therefore, the co-expression of CAR and IL-15 is more conducive to enhancing the anti-tumor activity of Vδ1 T cells.

To explore other target for CAR modification, we selected Nucleolin (NCL), a ligand previously identified and reported by our group that is ectopically expressed on the tumor surface under stress and recognized by Vδ1 TCRs [30]. Thus, we engineered a NCL-targeting CAR construct (Fig. 6A) and transfected it into 293T cells to verify the expression of the CD3ζ signaling domain via western blot (Fig. 6B). Lentiviruses were subsequently produced and transduced into Vδ1 T cells. The expansion efficiency of NCL CAR-expressing Vδ1 T cells was not significantly impaired (Fig. 6C). The lentiviral infection rate was higher in αβT cells (average 50%) than in Vδ1 T cells (average 20%) (Fig. 6D-E). In addition, NCL-CAR-Vδ1 T cells presented improved cytotoxicity against OVCAR8 cells (NCL+) (Fig. S8), with cytotoxicity equal to that of CAR-αβT cells (Fig. 6F).

For in vivo experiments, all groups (Fig. 6G) that received adoptive therapy in vivo exhibited significant inhibition of tumor growth. Discussion

Recent advances in immunotherapy have highlighted the potential of γδ T cells as promising effector cells for allogeneic adoptive therapies, particularly because of their inherent MHC-independent cytotoxicity and favorable safety profile [5]. The Vδ1 T-cell subset has attracted increasing interest owing to its tissue-homing properties and potent antitumor activity against various malignancies [31, 32]. Compared with other established cell therapeutic platforms, Vδ1 T cells present distinct advantages across multiple functional dimensions. In contrast to αβ T cells, Vδ1 T cells exhibit inherent HLA-independent recognition, thus avoiding GvHD [5], without any need for the genetic ablation of the endogenous TCR [33, 34]. This preserves their metabolic fitness and longevity [35], as they are less prone to exhaustion and are able to sustain oxidative metabolism [12, 36], which is critical for long-term persistence. Compared to NK cells, Vδ1 T cells present the combination of innate cytotoxicity and adaptive-like immunological memory [37], resulting in a superior proliferative capacity and sustained effector functions upon antigen re-encounter. In contrast, Vδ2 T cells are more abundant in peripheral blood but often exhibit terminally differentiated phenotypes and limited expansion. Building on these previous findings, we developed a platform for Vδ1 T-cell-based immunotherapy for cancer and MSLN/NCL-directed CARs engineered within expanded Vδ1 T cells, with the goal of enhancing their efficacy against multiple solid tumors.

Vδ1 T cells can be expanded to clinical scales using our anti TCR Vδ1 antibody and the expansion system we established exhibits significantly superior expansion efficiency compared to the commercial TCR Vδ1 antibody for Vδ1 cell expansion. Notably, although the Vδ1 expansion system in this study enables robust expansion of peripheral blood Vδ1 cells from the majority of donors, a small subset of donors demonstrates suboptimal Vδ1 cell expansion efficiency. Linear correlation analysis indicated no significant association between the expansion efficiency and the initial proportions of Vδ1 and Vδ2 cells in peripheral blood. It is speculated that the expansion efficiency of Vδ1 cells may be regulated by other more complex factors, including the donor’s individual immune status and cellular epigenetic profiles. Further in-depth investigations are therefore required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms governing this phenomenon.

Furthermore, compared with effector memory and terminally differentiated effector T cells observed in expanded Vδ2 T cells, Vδ1 T cells presented a stronger stem-like phenotype, such as naïve/stem central memory (n/scm) and central memory (cm) cells, which have been previously demonstrated to exhibit superior in vivo persistence and enhanced tumor infiltration capacity, thereby contributing to improved antitumor efficacy [38–41]. Besides, Vδ1 T cells exhibit similar or superior cytotoxic capacity against multiple solid tumor types compared to Vδ2 T cells. Given previous reports that Vδ1 TCRs recognize stress ligands, such as nucleolin (NCL) [30] and MHC class I-related molecules [42, 43], and that Vδ1-mediated tumor recognition and killing primarily rely on TCRs and NCRs [44–46], we examined the expression of stress ligands (MICA, MICB, and ULBP1, 2/5/6,3) on tumor cell lines susceptible to Vδ1 T-cell killing. The results indicated that sensitive tumor cell lines exhibited high surface expression of these MHC class I-related molecular stress ligands, which can be identified by Vδ1 T cells. Next, Vδ1 T cells exhibit enhanced tumor inhibition and infiltration capabilities [12], as demonstrated in our research.

The tumor microenvironment is highly complex, with multiple immunosuppressive factors, such as IL-10, TGF-β1, tumor-associated macrophages, hypoxia, and highly acidic conditions, significantly impairing T-cell antitumor activity [47]. Given that Vδ1 T cells are predominantly located in mucosal tissues, which are often nutrient deprived, hypoxic, and metabolic waste enriched (especially lactic acid), and that they represent the major γδ T-cell subset infiltrating solid tumors, we hypothesized that Vδ1 T cells may possess superior adaptability to adverse factors compared with circulating Vδ2 T cells. Thus, we sought to explore whether Vδ1 T cells have enhanced tolerance to lactate. We selected lactate concentrations of 15, 30, 45, and 60 mM to examine the lactate tolerance of Vδ1 T cells, as lactate levels in the majority of clinical tumor microenvironments reach 10–30 mM, and can even reach 40 mM in more glycolytic or hypoxic tumor regions [48, 49]. Results demonstrate that Vδ1 T cells maintained certain functions after 15mM-lactic acid treatment probably because they have a potent oxidative respiration capability, present less functional alterations and express high levels of DNA-repair proteins than Vδ2 T cells. The high expression of PARP1 in Vδ1 T cells likely enables the efficient repair of lactic acid-induced DNA lesions, thereby preserving genomic integrity and preventing the initiation of apoptosis, and such PARP1-mediated DNA repair is impaired upon olaparib intervention, leading to increased DNA damage and cellular apoptosis. However, Vδ2 T cells exhibited markedly impaired oxidative phosphorylation and reduced ATP production, accompanied by suppressed amino acid metabolism and antigen presentation under 15mM-lactate condition, indicating that their effector functions were suppressed. Interestingly, at an extremely high concentration of 60 mM lactate, nearly all Vδ2 T cells underwent apoptosis, while a small population of Vδ1 T cells still survived, indicating their stronger tolerance to lactate. Collectively, these findings suggest that Vδ1 T cells may be better suited than Vδ2 T cells for functioning within the hostile milieu of solid tumors.

We selected the Vδ1 T-cell platform for CAR engineering over conventional αβ T, NK, or Vδ2 T cells for reasons above. As innate-like T cells, γδ T cells exhibit limited proliferative capacity and high susceptibility to cell death, posing a challenge for genetic modification [50]. As shown in our results, the transduction efficiency of Vδ1 T cells was approximately 20%, which was lower than that of αβ T cells. This moderate transduction efficiency may constrain manufacturing yield of CAR + γδT cells and increase large-scale production complexity and cost. Despite relatively moderate transduction efficiency in γδT cells, our functional data confirm potent antitumor activity even with a moderate CAR-positive proportion, indicating transduction efficiency is not an absolute prerequisite for treatment efficacy. Future efforts need to focus on optimizing ex vivo activation, viral vectors, and non-viral delivery to improve gene transfer efficiency and translational feasibility.

Our work shows that MSLN- or NCL-CAR engineering can improve the effects of Vδ1 T cells by redirecting their specificity toward MSLN- or NCL-expressing tumors, resulting in markedly enhanced cytotoxic activity against tumor cells. The CAR-modified effectors not only preserve their original stemness and activation receptor expression but also demonstrate increased production of T helper 1-related cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, GM-CSF, and IL-2, increasing immune activation within the tumor microenvironment. Engineered IL-15 secretion further enhances effector function and in vivo persistence by sustaining metabolic fitness and preventing exhaustion, improving tumor control in xenograft models—consistent with clinical evidence supporting IL-15 as an adjuvant for cell-based immunotherapy. [25, 26]. Despite these benefits, constitutive IL-15 secretion carries theoretical risks of uncontrolled T-cell expansion and persistent immune activation, potentially precipitating toxicities [51, 52]. Stringent safety monitoring, dose optimization and necessary symptomatic treatment are essential for future translational studies to balance therapeutic benefits and risks. Another important safety issue is the potential off-tumor recognition by introduced CAR. The endogenous γδTCR mainly recognizes stress-induced antigens that are upregulated or ectopically expressed in malignant, infected, or damaged cells, rather than self-antigens on normal cells, which largely contributes to the favorable safety profile of γδ T cells [53]. However, most solid tumor CAR targets are tumor-associated antigens, which may be weakly expressed in normal tissues, precluding complete elimination of CAR-mediated on-target/off-tumor recognition [54]. Thus, the specificity of CAR warrants comprehensive evaluation in future preclinical and clinical studies to ensure treatment safety.

Another advantage of Vδ1 T cells is their resilience under cryopreservation, as they retain high viability, proliferative potential, and cytotoxic activity after thawing. This distinguishes them from certain NK [55] or Vδ2 [56] T-cell products that are highly sensitive to freeze‒thaw cycles, thereby facilitating off-the-shelf availability of Vδ1 T cells and simplifying clinical logistics.

Several limitations of this study are the lack of systemic safety profiling such as CRS-related cytokine monitoring, quantitative histopathological scoring and the different dosing intervals for αβ T cells (every 7 days) versus Vδ1 T cells (every 4 days) that may complicate treatment efficacy comparison. This treatment regimen reflects their distinct biological traits: αβ T cells have stronger in vivo proliferation and longer persistence, while γδ T cells have limited survival and require more frequent infusion [57, 58]. Future studies should concentrate on exploring administration schedule and incorporating systematic safety assessments, aiming to further optimize the safety and therapeutic strategy of γδ T cell-based therapy.

In conclusion, Vδ1 T cells represent a novel and potent approach for targeting solid malignancies. The unique combination of γδTCR- and NCR-dependent targeting found in these cells and their superior metabolic and persistence profiles, enhanced tumor homing, and better tolerance to lactic acid support their consideration as promising candidates for future clinical evaluation. Notably, CAR-modified Vδ1 T cells possess inherent tumor surveillance properties and can be engineered to target a broad range of malignancies. The incorporation of IL-15 further augments the antitumor potency and durability of the cells, offering a compelling strategy for overcoming the limitations of current cell therapies. Ongoing studies will focus on optimizing strategies using Vδ1 T cells and evaluating their treatment efficacy to expedite clinical translation.
