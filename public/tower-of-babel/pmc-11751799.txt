Spatial transcriptomics reveals molecular cues underlying the site specificity of the adult mouse oral mucosa and its stem cell niches
Authors: Anna C. Seubert, Marion Krafft, Sarah Bopp, Moutaz Helal, Pranjali Bhandare, Elmar Wolf, Anna Alemany, Angela Riedel, Kai Kretzschmar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe oral cavity is a multifunctional organ composed of structurally heterogeneous mucosal tissues that remain poorly characterized. Oral mucosal tissues are highly stratified and segmented along an epithelial-lamina propria axis. Here, we performed spatial transcriptomics (tomo-seq) on the tongue, cheeks, and palate of the adult mouse to understand the cues that maintain the oral mucosal sites. We define molecular markers of unique and shared cellular niches and differentiation programs across oral sites. Using a comparative approach, we identify fibroblast growth factor (FGF) pathway components as potential stem cell niche factors for oral epithelial stem cells. Using organoid-forming efficiency assays, we validated three FGF ligands (FGF1, FGF7, and FGF10) as site-specific niche factors in the dorsal and ventral tongue. Our dataset of the spatially resolved genes across major oral sites represents a comprehensive resource for unraveling the molecular mechanisms underlying the adult homeostasis of the oral mucosa and its stem cell niches.

Introduction

The mouth or oral cavity contributes to critical body functions such as breathing, eating, tasting, and speaking. It is highly compartmentalized and has multiple sites, including the tongue, cheeks, gums, and palate. These face constant challenges from the microbiome and masticatory forces, making the oral environment unique. The oral sites consist of a stratified epithelium, the oral epithelium, and an underlying connective tissue, the lamina propria, with embedded blood vessels, muscle, fat, and salivary glands (Jones and Klein, 2013). Despite these basic similarities, the oral sites exhibit a high degree of structural heterogeneity. They are, therefore, classified according to their form and function into three subtypes: masticatory mucosa (e.g., hard palate [HP] and gingiva), lining mucosa (e.g., buccal mucosa [BM], ventral tongue [VT]), and specialized mucosa (dorsal tongue [DT]). Accordingly, the epithelial differentiation and stratification pattern differs between the oral mucosal subtypes and sites (Jones and Klein, 2013). In the mouse, the HP, gingiva, and DT are highly keratinized and cornified. In contrast, the BM and VT are keratinized but do not form a highly cornified mantle (Figures 1A and 1B) (Jones and Klein, 2013). Homeostasis and cell turnover in epithelial tissues are governed by adult stem and progenitor cells residing in specialized tissue compartments termed stem cell niches (Szabo et al., 2023). Although distinct stem and progenitor cell populations have been identified in several oral sites (Byrd et al., 2019; Hisha et al., 2013; Sequeira et al., 2018; Tanaka et al., 2013, 2016), the molecular mechanisms underlying their fate decisions to proliferate and differentiate, and whether these are site specific, remain poorly understood. Genome-wide transcriptome analysis of whole tissue or single-cell preparations has provided valuable insights into the molecular characteristics of the human and mouse oral mucosa (Easter et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2020, 2021; Jones et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2023; Ozekin et al., 2023; Tabula Muris et al., 2018; Tabula Sapiens et al., 2022; Williams et al., 2021). However, these molecular analyses were often restricted to one or two oral sites, and particularly studies on mouse tissues lacked unbiased spatial analyses. Spatial transcriptomics methods allow unbiased gene-expression profiling with spatial resolution (Rao et al., 2021). RNA tomography (tomo-seq) is a spatial method combining tissue cryosectioning, RNA barcoding, and next-generation sequencing (Junker et al., 2014). Tomo-seq has been applied to embryos, organs, and tissues with highly segmented gene-expression profiles to spatially resolve the expression of marker genes and molecular cues in development, homeostasis, and disease (Holler and Junker, 2019; Kruse et al., 2016; Lacraz et al., 2017; Moris et al., 2020; van Beijnum et al., 2023; van den Brink et al., 2020). Here, we combined tomo-seq on the HP, BM, VT, and DT with RNA in situ hybridization and immunohistochemistry to identify spatially resolved molecular markers of the apical-basal axis directed from epithelium to lamina propria (termed epithelium-lamina propria axis from here on) within these major oral sites. Comparative analysis revealed differential expression of niche factors in an oral site-specific manner, which we functionally validated using organoid-based assays. Our data provide a comprehensive resource of spatially reproducible gene-expression profiles for comparative tissue analysis and a better understanding of the molecular and cellular cues underlying site specificity within the adult mouse oral mucosa and its epithelial stem cell niches.

Results

Tomo-seq delineates the expression of cell lineage marker genes along the epithelium-lamina propria axis

To spatially resolve the gene-expression profiles along the epithelial-lamina propria axis of major stratified tissues of the adult murine oral mucosa, we applied tomo-seq to the HP, BM, VT, and DT (Figures 1A–1C) (Junker et al., 2014). For each oral site, two mice were dissected, and the tissues cryosectioned from the epithelium toward the lamina propria into twenty slices of 14 μm thickness and further processed for tomo-seq. Following quality control and gene expression averaging across the biological replicates per oral site (Figures S1B–S1F) (van den Brink et al., 2020), we assessed marker gene expression for major cell lineages (Figures 1D and S2). Epithelial markers were enriched in the first ten sections, while genes encoding for markers of hematopoietic cells and stromal cells, including fibroblasts, blood and lymphatic endothelial cells, neuronal cells, and muscle cells, were enriched in the last ten sections as expected for mucosal tissues (Figures 1D and 1E). The asymmetric marker gene expression recapitulates the asymmetric cellular distribution observed within the stratified oral tissues, as validated by staining for the epithelial marker E-cadherin, the stromal marker Vimentin, and the hematopoietic marker CD45 (Figure 1F). These data show that tomo-seq allows to spatially resolve marker gene expression of major cell lineages in adult mouse oral mucosal tissues along their epithelial-lamina propria axis.

Tomo-seq resolves spatially reproducible molecular markers in the different oral sites

To define molecular markers with distinct expression patterns along the epithelium-lamina propria axis, we identified significantly spatially reproducible genes, i.e., genes with variable expression across the tissue in a section-specific manner that was reproducible within each oral site (p < 0.05). We then performed hierarchical clustering (Figures 2 and S4). To deconvolute our data and annotate cell lineages, we performed gene ontology (GO) analysis and used a recently published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset of the mouse gingiva (Easter et al., 2024) (Figure S3). In the HP, we found 924 spatially reproducible genes organized into five clusters (Figures 2A and S4A; Table S1). We identified clusters 0–2 to be annotated to gene signatures of stromal cells (Figures 2A and S4A–S4C; Table S1). Cell-type deconvolution confirmed that clusters 0–2 were enriched for mural, salivary, and muscle cells (Figure S4C). Among the genes identified in cluster 0 was the mural cell marker Pdgfrb (Vishvanath et al., 2016). Cluster 3 enriched for lymphoid and blood endothelial cells (Figure S4C). Consistent with its gene-expression pattern, we found genes in cluster 4 to be associated with epithelial tissue, such as the keratinization gene Krt10 (Maytin et al., 1999), and cell-type deconvolution confirmed an enrichment for oral keratinocytes (Figure 2A; Table S1). A total of 673 genes that separated into five clusters were significantly spatially reproducible in the BM (Figures 2B and S4D–S4F; Table S1). Genes in cluster 0 were annotated to muscle cells (Figure S4F), suggesting that they align with the stromal portion. We found Des, encoding the muscle marker Desmin (Paulin and Li, 2004), as one of the genes enriched in this cluster (Figure 2B, bottom panel). Clusters 1 and 2 enriched for epithelial cells (Figures 2B and S4F; Table S1). Confirming these data, we found the genes Ly6e (enriched in cluster 1) and Hopx (enriched in cluster 2) were highly expressed in the epithelial segment of the BM (Figure 2B, top panel, and S4G; Table S1). Both markers have previously been found to mark stratified epithelia (Christensen et al., 2020; Lemaire et al., 2004), which we confirmed using RNAscope and immunohistochemistry (Figures 2B and S4G). Clusters 3 and 4 contained stromal markers such as Cdh12 (Figure S4F; Table S1), suggesting that they represent different compartments in the lamina propria.

In the VT, we identified 4,302 significantly spatially reproducible genes separated into nine clusters (Figures 2C and S4H–S4J; Table S1). GO term analysis revealed that genes in cluster 0 annotated biological processes associated with actively cycling cells, suggesting this cluster represents the oral epithelial basal layer. In line, we found several basal layer and proliferation marker genes, including Cd44, and Top1 (Kaya et al., 1997; Tricoli et al., 1985) enriched in this cluster (Figure 2C, bottom panel). In cluster 1, we found keratinocyte differentiation markers, including Ppl, and Evpl (Watt, 1983) (Figure S4J; Table S1). Genes in cluster 2 showed a gradual increase in their expression profiles from the middle sections toward the end sections, which aligns with the extent of the lamina propria (Figure 2C). In this cluster, we identified Acta2, which is highly expressed by stromal (myo-)fibroblasts and vascular smooth muscle cells (Ding et al., 2014) (Figure 2C, top panel; Figure S4J). Clusters 6 and 7 were annotated to glial cells (Figures 2C and S4J; Table S1). Clusters 3, 4, 5, and 8 were annotated to cellular compartments embedded in the lamina propria, including salivary glands and lymphatic vessels (Figure S4J). As expected, tomo-seq allows us also to identify the gene-expression profile of the muscle tissue in the center of the tongue.

Segmentation of the DT along the epithelium-lamina propria axis revealed 2,108 significantly spatially reproducible genes that separated into eight clusters (Figures 2D and S4K–S4M; Table S1). In cluster 0, we identified stromal marker genes, including the endothelial marker Pecam1 (Figure 2D, bottom panel). Cluster 4 contained genes typically upregulated during keratinocyte terminal differentiation, including Lor, Ppl, and Krt16 (Table S1) (Bernot et al., 2002; Lessard and Coulombe, 2012). Cell-type deconvolution confirmed enrichment for oral keratinocytes (Figure S4M). Among the genes detected in cluster 6 were different basal layer marker genes and Emb, which encodes the fibronectin receptor Embigin (Figure 2D, top panel), which was recently defined as a marker of basal sebocytes that plays a role in sebocyte differentiation and metabolism in mouse skin (Sipila et al., 2022). Embigin was reported to be absent from the stratified interfollicular epidermis in mouse skin (Sipila et al., 2022). In contrast, immunostainings revealed Embigin to mark oral epithelial basal cells (Figure 2D, top panel). The remaining clusters 1–3, 5, and 7 were annotated to different cell types located in the lamina propria, including muscle and endothelial and immune cells but also salivary gland cells (Figure S4M; Table S1). We conclude that tomo-seq allows tissue segmentation in all four oral sites in our study. Based on this, we were, therefore, able to resolve spatially reproducible genes, identify sections enriched for epithelial and stromal cells, and uncover new molecular markers with spatial expression profiles in the different oral sites.

Comparative analysis reveals site-specific, unique molecular markers of cellular niches in the oral mucosa

In addition to the intra-tissue heterogeneity identified earlier, the oral mucosal sites exhibit a high degree of cellular heterogeneity for which the underlying molecular cues remain unresolved. We therefore assessed the similarity of the gene lists of all the clusters identified earlier. We found the highest similarity between cluster 2 in the VT and cluster 0 in the DT and, to a lesser extent, clusters 0 of the HP and BM (Figure 3A), which all showed a similar spatial profile and were enriched for stromal genes. In addition, some similarities were found between the clusters enriching for oral keratinocytes (HP cl. 4, BM cl. 2, VT cl. 1, and DT cl. 4; Figure 3A). However, most other clusters appeared largely distinct (Figure 3A). We then asked whether genes significantly spatially reproducible in one oral site were differentially expressed in the other sites. We pooled the gene-expression data from each section per oral site and compared it to the data from the other oral sites using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014). We found that the most significantly spatially reproducible genes in the BM were upregulated in this oral site compared to the other oral sites (Figure 3B). Most spatially reproducible genes in the DT were downregulated compared to their expression level in other oral sites (Figure 3B). Next, we extracted the site-specific gene lists to identify spatially reproducible genes unique to each oral site and performed a comparative analysis. Indicating a high degree of oral site specificity at the molecular level, we found that most spatially reproducible genes were either shared with only one oral site or unique to one oral site (Figures 3C and 3D; Table S2). Among the genes spatially reproducible uniquely in the HP, we found the stromal markers Ly6a and Prrx1 with increased expression toward the stromal side (Figure S5A). We confirmed this using RNAscope, which showed a strong expression of Prrx1 in the lower stromal compartment of the HP, while the expression was more diffuse and weaker in the other oral sites (Figure S5B). Ly6a and Prrx1 were recently described to mark two palatal fibroblast subpopulations with distinct roles in homeostasis and wound healing (Ko et al., 2023). In the BM, we identified Krt17 as one of the spatially enriched genes (Figure S5A). KRT17 was described in psoriasis to induce hyperproliferation in humans (Yang et al., 2017). Spatially segmented in the VT, we identified the keratinocyte differentiation-associated gene Pax9 in the epithelial segments (Figure S5A). Spatially reproducible genes in the lining mucosa of the cheeks and tongue suggest a homeostatic ground state in the BM

The BM and the VT are classified as lining mucosa because their epithelia form a thinner keratinized envelope compared to the masticatory epithelia in the oral cavity. We superimposed the gene lists from both oral sites to assess the degree of similarities between these structurally closely related oral sites. We found that 3.8% of spatially reproducible genes from both oral sites were unique to the BM, while most were unique to the VT (Figures 4A and 4B). Genes that were uniquely spatially reproducible in the BM are typically expressed in muscle and adipose tissue, such as Tagln, while the genes uniquely spatially enriched in the VT, such as Sox15 and Sprr2, are genes involved in keratinocyte differentiation (Hohl et al., 1995; Sulahian et al., 2015) (Figure 4B). We found genes such as Fgf1 and Fgfr2, which encode components of signaling cascades, to be spatially reproducible in both oral sites (Figure 4B). We then performed clustering and GO analysis on the gene list to investigate potential oral site-specific molecular mechanisms (Figures 4C and 4D). Several clusters were mainly enriched for genes spatially reproducible uniquely in the VT. For instance, cluster 2 was significantly enriched for genes annotated to the regulation of osteoblast proliferation (Figure 4D). Cluster 4 contained mainly spatially reproducible genes in the VT or both oral sites, annotated to cellular respiration (Figure 4D). Overall, many genes appeared to be enriched in sections of either epithelium or lamina propria of both oral sites. Spatially reproducible genes in the highly cornified oral mucosa of the palate and tongue confirm a high degree of molecular heterogeneity

DT and HP are both characterized by a high degree of cornification (Jones and Klein, 2013). To explore similarities in spatially reproducible genes, we combined the lists of spatially reproducible genes from both oral tissues. We found that 9.3% of all spatially reproducible genes were shared (Figures 4E and 4F). Almost two-thirds of spatially reproducible genes were unique to the DT, while 24% were unique to the HP (Figure 4E). DT-enriched genes were associated with muscle tissue or keratinocyte differentiation (Figure 4G; Table S3). In the HP, we found Reg3g, an antimicrobial peptide (Shin et al., 2023), and the endothelial marker Itga8 significantly higher expressed in the HP compared to the DT (Figure 4F). We performed clustering and found seven clusters (Figure 4G). Several clusters were enriched for genes uniquely spatially reproducible in the DT (e.g., cluster 3), in the HP (e.g., cluster 4), and in both oral sites (e.g., cluster 0). Genes in cluster 0 were associated with epidermal development (Figure 4H, bottom panel), which aligns with the common developmental origin of the epidermis and oral epithelium (Pereira and Sequeira, 2021). In cluster 3, the DT-enriched genes annotated to epithelial cell differentiation (Figure 4H, center panel), suggesting a distinct differentiation program in the DT epithelium. HP-enriched genes in cluster 4 were significantly annotated to GO, such as cell-matrix adhesion (Figure 4H, top panel), which suggests distinct molecular profiles in its stromal niches. Overall, this comparative analysis of two highly cornified oral mucosal sites reveals site specificity in gene expression that drives epithelial differentiation and stromal niche patterning, demonstrating their heterogeneity despite sharing key histological features.

Spatially reproducible genes in the tongue mucosae reveal a high degree of molecular similarities between the cellular niches

The mucosae of DT and VT are part of the same organ but have distinct forms and functions. The VT in the adult mouse has a flat stratified epithelium with a thin cornified envelope providing a lining barrier, while the DT is involved in mastication and taste reception and comprises a highly keratinized stratified epithelium with embedded specialized papillae structures (Jones et al., 2019). To define shared and distinct molecular profiles of the niches in the tongue mucosae, we compared the gene lists from both oral tissues. We found 27.3% of all spatially reproducible genes to be shared (Figure 5A). The majority of the genes were uniquely spatially reproducible in the VT, while 14.5% of genes were specifically spatially reproducible in the DT (Figure 5A). We next performed clustering (Figure 5B), followed by GO analysis. Genes in cluster 2 were predominantly spatially reproducible only in the VT and were associated with gene transcription (Figure 5D). Most genes in cluster 4 were uniquely spatially reproducible in the DT and annotated for GO terms such as epithelial cell differentiation (Figure 5C). Spatial transcriptomics identifies new niche factors underlying oral site specificity of oral epithelial stem cells

Intrinsic and extrinsic cues regulate stemness and proliferation in epithelial tissues (Szabo et al., 2023). While the role of fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling as an extrinsic cue with the stem cell niches of several epithelia is well documented (Santos et al., 2018), little is known about the functions of its components in the oral mucosa. We found that genes encoding for FGF pathway components showed site-specific spatial expression profiles. For example, expression of Fgfr2 and Fgfr3 was highly variable with the VT (Figure 5F), while Fgfr1 was spatially enriched in the lamina propria in both tongue mucosae (Figure 5F). We also found the FGF ligand genes Fgf1, Fgf7, and Fgf10 to be enriched in the stromal sections of both oral sites (Figure 5F), which we confirmed using RNAscope (Figure S5F). Not all FGF ligands have the same affinity for the receptors: FGF1 binds all FGF receptors (FGFRs) , whereas FGF7 and FGF10 primarily bind FGFR2 (Turner and Grose, 2010). We used tongue epithelial organoids to assess whether the site-specific expression of the FGF signaling molecules had significance for oral epithelial cells (Seubert et al., 2021). We confirmed that epithelial organoids from both VT and DT expressed Fgfr1, Fgfr2, and Fgfr3 (Figures S5C–S5E), whereas, in line with our aforementioned data (Figure 5F), no expression of Fgf1, Fgf7, and Fgf10 was detected. Next, we cultured the organoids with a medium supplemented with FGF1, FGF7, or FGF10 and quantified the number of organoids as a measure of organoid-forming efficiency and the size of the organoids as a proxy for the proliferative capacity. While FGF1 or FGF10 did not significantly increase the organoid-forming efficiency of VT organoids, FGF7 positively affected the cultures (Figures 5G and 5H). Organoid-forming efficiency in DT organoid cultures was promoted by the addition of FGF1 (Figures 5G and 5H). FGF10 increased the size of VT organoids compared to FGF7-treated cells (Figure 5I). However, the size of DT organoids was reduced by FGF7 compared to all other conditions (Figure 5I). The data suggest site-specific differences in the response of cultured oral epithelial cells to niche factors such as FGFs.

Discussion

Here, we generated a comprehensive resource of genome-wide spatial gene-expression profiles of four major oral sites of the mouse using tomo-seq. Sequencing of tissue sections revealed high-quality transcriptomes that allowed us to identify cell lineage markers and define spatially reproducible genes along the epithelium-lamina propria axis. We used these datasets for comparative analysis and defined oral site-specific molecular cues within the adult mouse oral mucosa and its epithelial stem cell niches. Based on our data, we identified signaling molecules of the FGF pathway as potential niche factors for the oral epithelium and validated this using organoid technology.

The cellular and molecular composition of the oral mucosa has recently been extensively studied using single-cell and spatial transcriptomics technologies. Studies on the human oral mucosa focused on different oral sites and different conditions such as homeostasis, inflammation, and cancer (Choi et al., 2023; Quah et al., 2023; Easter et al., 2024; Caetano et al., 2021; Williams et al., 2021). These studies included stromal cell populations and, for example, characterized the remodeling of the epithelial and stromal compartments during chronic inflammation (Easter et al., 2024; Caetano et al., 2021). However, in the mouse, most studies have focused either on one oral site (Easter et al., 2024) or mainly on the oral epithelium (Jones et al., 2019; Tabula Muris et al., 2018). In contrast, our dataset provides an overview of gene-expression profiles across the epithelium, lamina propria, and submucosa of four oral sites, representing all major oral mucosal types. To overcome the lack of cell-type resolution, which is a major drawback of spatial transcriptomics tools such as tomo-seq, we also used a recently published scRNA-seq dataset of the adult mouse gingiva (Easter et al., 2024) containing epithelial, stromal and immune, cells for cell-type deconvolution (Figure S4). Nevertheless, a comprehensive single-cell sequencing dataset of the four oral sites analyzed in this study would allow for a more refined oral site-specific cell-type deconvolution, as some cell types, e.g., several cell types associated with the specialized epithelium of the DT (Du et al., 2016; Jones et al., 2019; Tanaka et al., 2013), are missing in the gingiva. However, such a dataset has not been reported for the mouse oral mucosa so far.

The comparative analysis of spatially reproducible genes in the BM and HP revealed only a small overlap of shared genes (Figures 4A–4D). Despite sharing a transition zone, this suggests that the cellular niches of the two oral sites are molecularly distinct, following their different forms and functions (Jones and Klein, 2013; Treuting and Morton, 2012). Conversely, the lining mucosal tissues BM and VT shared many spatially reproducible genes. HP and DT had the least overlap of spatially reproducible genes. The core of spatially reproducible genes in both oral sites was annotated to be involved in epidermal cell differentiation, consistent with the high degree of keratinization in both tissues. As the DT has several distinct programs for the differentiation of the specialized papillae (Ishan et al., 2023), the majority of spatially reproducible genes were unique to it. However, we found that spatially reproducible genes unique to the HP were annotated to integrin-mediated signaling in our GO analysis. The expression of these genes was spatially enriched in the stromal segments of the HP, which may be due to the larger blood vessels present within the palatal lamina propria and submucosal tissue. DT and VT had the highest number of common spatially reproducible genes. These genes were predominantly expressed in the stromal segments and were annotated for metabolic-related processes, which fits well with the fact that the inner part of the tongue contains a muscle that is likely to have been cut in the last sections of both the DT and VT. Clustering revealed several gene clusters enriched in the VT, which were relevant to epithelial tissue homeostasis. In this analysis, we found only one cluster primarily containing spatially resolved genes enriched in the DT epithelial segments. The low number of such genes and gene clusters is likely due to the papillae structures, which are patterned along the anterior–posterior axis (Junker et al., 2014) and cannot be adequately resolved by horizontal segmentation using tomo-seq.

Since tomo-seq allowed us to resolve the gene-expression profiles of both epithelial and stromal compartments within the oral mucosa, we investigated the expression of several signaling molecules within the lamina propria that may function as niche factors for oral epithelial stem cells. Among these molecules, we identified FGF pathway components that showed spatial expression profiles within tongue mucosal tissues. While Fgfr2 and Fgfr3 were enriched only in the VT epithelial sections, they were robustly expressed along all DT sections. Very little is known about the role of FGF signaling in adult homeostasis, but its critical role in tongue development is well documented. For example, loss of FGFR2B leads to the development of a thinned and disorganized tongue epithelium in mice (Rice et al., 2004), while mice with depleted FGF10 have a defect in proper papilla formation with a lack of circumvallate differentiation and increased size of fungiform papillae (Petersen et al., 2011). One study reported that Fgf7 and Fgf10 expression is restricted to the lamina propria during early mouse tongue development, whereas Fgf1 is expressed from both epithelium and mesenchyme (Rice et al., 2004). Our data indicate a different gene-expression pattern within the adult mouse tongue, with Fgf1 expression being most prominent toward the lower parts of the lamina propria. Fgf7 and Fgf10 appear to be most highly expressed just below the VT basal epithelial layer. In the DT, Fgf7 expression increases along the epithelium-lamina propria axis, while Fgf10 expression is generally lower in the DT compared to the VT. The heterogeneous expression of FGF receptors and ligands in the two tongue mucosae suggested an oral site-specific role for these signaling molecules, which we could be functionally validated using organoids generated from both epithelial tissues. FGF7 treatment most strongly improved the organoid-forming capacity of cultured VT epithelial cells, while incubation with FGF1 increased the organoid-forming capacity of DT epithelial cells. Conversely, FGF7 treatment resulted in reduced growth of organoids established from both oral sites, suggesting distinct roles for these signaling molecules in the tongue epithelium. This implies an oral site-specific sensitivity toward FGF signaling, in line with a recent study showing that FGF ligand bias leads to distinct downstream intracellular signaling during tissue development (Karl et al., 2024). Our study provides a comprehensive spatial transcriptomic segmentation of four major oral sites, highlighting the heterogeneity of different sites within the adult oral mucosa. Using our dataset, we were able to identify and functionally validate, using organoid technology, molecular cues that regulate the organoid-forming capacity of epithelial cells in an oral site-specific manner. It will, therefore, serve as a resource for the community to explore the spatial expression pattern of genes and further investigate their role in the adult oral mucosa and its stem cell niches.

In this study, we have shown that tomo-seq provides valuable spatial transcriptomic data. While tomo-seq is a cost-effective, convenient method with a higher sequencing depth, it is a tissue-sectioning and pseudo-bulk approach, which does not provide the single-cell resolution provided by other spatial transcriptomics technologies (Cheng et al., 2023). This particularly affects the intermediate sections analyzed in this study, where the basal layer and lamina propria intermingle and form a transition zone that cannot be fully resolved molecularly. Furthermore, the architecture of the oral mucosa makes clean sectioning difficult, for instance, due to the rigid and uneven structures of the DT and HP. Finally, the number of significantly spatially reproducible genes in the oral sites, as well as the number of shared spatially reproducible genes across oral sites, may not only be dependent on biological features as discussed earlier but also be influenced by technical considerations, such as the number of replicates, the sampling noise, the sequencing depth, or the applied cutoff for the statistical analysis.

Experimental procedures

Tissue collection and preparation

All animal work has been performed in accordance with the recommendations of the European Community Directive (2010/63/EU) and following European Union and German legislation, C57BL/6J mice were killed for organ removal according to §4 of the German Animal Welfare Act. Oral mucosal tissues were harvested from 8- to 12-week-old C57BL/6J mice, as described before (Seubert et al., 2021). Tissues were embedded epithelial-down into an Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) medium and stored at −80°C.

Organoid-forming efficiency assay

Organoids were established and passaged as described before (Seubert et al., 2021). For the FGF experiments, 5,000 cells were seeded per 10 μL hydrogel drop cultured in media without FGF or containing FGF10, FGF7, or FGF1 (10 ng/mL). Data were generated using organoid lines established from 3 biological replicates. The cultures were imaged using a Tecan Spark Cyto system after 7 days and quantified as described in the supplemental experimental procedures.

Tomo-seq

Tomo-seq was performed as described elsewhere (Junker et al., 2014). For each oral site, two mice were dissected, and the tissues cryosectioned from the epithelium toward the lamina propria into 20 slices of 14 μm thickness. Total RNA was extracted from each section and processed for sequencing as detailed in the supplemental experimental procedures.

Histology

Paraffin or frozen sections were prepared and stained using conventional protocols, as previously described (Seubert et al., 2021). Paraffin embedding, cryosectioning, stainings, and in situ hybridizations using RNAscope are described in the supplemental experimental procedures.

Bioinformatics analysis

Sequencing, mapping to the mouse reference genome, and further analysis of the tomo-seq data were performed as described before (van den Brink et al., 2020). Bulk analysis of the tomo-seq datasets was performed using the DESeq2 package (Love et al., 2014). Further details can be found in the supplemental experimental procedures.
