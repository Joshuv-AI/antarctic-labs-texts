Standing strong and tall—improving mobility and balance in older Aboriginal people through the Ironbark program
Authors: Rebecca Ivers, Kathleen Clapham, Courtney Ryder, Rona Macniven, Anne-Marie Hill, Holger Moeller, Bronte Haynes, Margaret Gidgup, Leanne McCarthy, Tamara Mackean, Kim Delbaere, Anne Tiedemann, Catherine Sherrington, Julieann Coombes, IRONBARK INVESTIGATORS GROUP
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevention of falls aids healthy ageing, but there are few Aboriginal-led and designed programs.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of Ironbark, an Aboriginal co-designed fall prevention program, on functional mobility, a key indicator of falls.MethodsThis controlled pre-post study was conducted in organisations delivering services to Aboriginal people aged 45 years and older in New South Wales and Western Australia. A co-designed fall prevention program incorporating exercises and yarning circles targeting fall risk factors (‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’) was compared to a social program (‘Ironbark Healthy Community’). Data were collected at baseline and after 12 months of follow-up.ResultsThe ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program was delivered at 16 sites and the ‘Healthy Community’ program at three. Due to lower recruitment than anticipated, combined analysis was not possible. There were significant improvements in the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ participants for the primary outcome of functional mobility (Short Performance Physical Battery; a change of 0.8 points, 95% CI: 0.4–1.1), reductions in stepping times, improvements in leg stance and increased incidental and planned exercise. There were no significant changes in falls, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) or psychological distress. Participants in the ‘Healthy Community’ sites had no change in functional mobility but saw declines in HRQoL, physical activity and stepping time.ConclusionsA co-designed and delivered program for older Aboriginal people, implemented in community settings aligned with stated priorities of Aboriginal older people, improved functional mobility.Keywords: indigenous health, co-design, community led, healthy ageing, older people

Background

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people are the First Peoples of Australia, comprising hundreds of diverse groups with varied language, cultures and traditions and numbering close to 1 million people [1]. Older Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people (henceforth referred to as Aboriginal) in Australia are knowledge holders and leaders, and maintaining health and wellbeing to remain independent and contribute to the community is a priority for them [2]. However, ongoing impacts of colonisation, transgenerational trauma and racism have resulted in many older Aboriginal people living with poor health [3], a pattern replicated internationally [4].

Falls are a main contributor to the burden of disease for older people [5], causing devastating injuries and psychological distress, and impact independent living. There is a rising burden of falls among Aboriginal people aged 50 years and over [6], driven by risk factors including medical conditions and functional limitations, poor mobility and medications [7–9] and a lack of co-designed fall prevention programs for Aboriginal people [10].

The effectiveness of structured exercise programs for fall prevention is well established [11]. Building on this knowledge base, our team worked with Aboriginal communities to understand program preferences [12] and through a series of workshops and interactive design with Aboriginal health service providers and community members in New South Wales (NSW), co-designed a fall prevention program. The program, grounded in holistic, reciprocal and inclusive Indigenous perspectives and practises, included a group exercise program that challenged strength and balance, drawing on selected exercises from the Otago program. The exercise program was delivered alongside a yarning circle, an Indigenous facilitated group discussion focused on fall risk factors and sharing knowledge [13]. The program was piloted in NSW and Western Australia (WA) and demonstrated improvements in strength and balance, reduced body mass index and strong community acceptance and program engagement [14–16].

To enable sustainable delivery at scale, and to better understand implementation factors and effectiveness in preventing falls, a larger trial was designed. The aim of this trial was to assess the effectiveness of the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program on the rate of falls in Aboriginal people 45 years and older living in the community, compared to controls who received the ‘Ironbark Healthy Community’ program.

Methods

The study was governed by a steering committee chaired by an Aboriginal researcher, alongside Aboriginal and non-Indigenous community and policy representatives.

Planned design

A cluster randomised control trial with embedded process evaluation was designed to recruit 600 participants in three Australian states—NSW, South Australia (SA) and WA [17, 18]. Recruitment of study sites commenced in January 2020 but was halted in February 2020 due to the Coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic. The pandemic and associated restrictions significantly impacted the feasibility of recruitment. After community and stakeholder consultation, the design was amended to evaluate impacts of the program on measures of functional mobility, given the anticipated smaller sample size. Because of the extensive time needed for consultation about randomisation, and as sites indicated a preference to select their own program post-COVID-19, the randomisation process was removed.

The revised study aim was to assess the impact of the Ironbark program on measures of functional mobility via the Short Performance Physical Battery (SPPB).

Revised design

The amended study was a controlled pre-post design, and sites selected either the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ or ‘Ironbark Healthy Community’ program to deliver at their site for 12 months.

Study programs

For both programs, an Aboriginal site manager from the host organisation coordinated the program, maintained regular contact with the participants to encourage attendance, arranged transport if required and provided a light meal. They fostered an environment of inclusivity and cultural safety and worked with program staff to tailor the program to meet the needs of the community while maintaining the core elements.

The ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program is a weekly program comprising 45 minutes of strength and balance exercises with a yarning circle covering fall risk factors. Hard copy manuals were developed for facilitators and participants using culturally appropriate language and images. Exercises were delivered by an exercise professional (physiotherapist, exercise physiologist or fitness instructor) and were tailored to participants, including progression. Written guidance was provided on home exercises, designed to be completed for 10–15 minutes daily. Yarning circles, focused on falls knowledge and awareness, were led by the exercise professional or an Aboriginal staff member.

The ‘Ironbark Healthy Community’ program is a weekly program fostering cultural engagement and community connectedness, including yarning [19]. Participants engaged in various cultural, non-exercise activities including art activities, visits from guest speakers and other social activities. After 12 months, to ensure all sites had the benefit of the fall prevention program, these program sites were offered the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program for an additional 6 months.

Recruitment and participants

Eligible sites were Aboriginal community-controlled organisations, or non-Indigenous services delivering services to Aboriginal people in NSW or WA who could commit to recruiting 10–15 eligible Aboriginal people. Participants were community-dwelling Aboriginal people aged 45 years and older. Exclusions were those unable to go outside without assistance, with degenerative neurological disorders or medical conditions precluding exercise.

Data on primary and secondary outcomes, participant health, current exercise, medication use, concerns about falling (adapted from other single item questions based on community preferences [20, 21]) and other sociodemographic variables were collected via a detailed interviewer-administered questionnaire and physical measurements at baseline and 12 months by Aboriginal research assistants. Home exercise data were recorded weekly by site managers who also contacted non-attendees for this information.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was change in functional mobility (SPPB) [22] in study participants between baseline and 12-months follow-up.

Secondary outcome measures

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was measured using the EuroQoL tool EQ-5D [23]. Physical activity was self-reported via the Incidental and Planned Exercise Questionnaire (IPEQ) [24]; dynamic balance was measured via the alternate step test [25] and standing leg stance [26]. Activities of daily living were measured through an adapted version of the Late-Life Function and Disability (LLFD) Instrument Function component [27]. Psychological distress was measured using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K-5) [28]. Central obesity was measured through waist circumference measurement [29], and participants were asked about falls in the previous 12 months.

Weekly exercise data were grouped into: (i) three-times-weekly exercise or more (either class participation plus two units of home exercise or three units of home exercise) for 6 months or longer or (ii) less than three-times-weekly exercise [11, 30].

Sample size

For the revised study design, 30 clusters with an average of 10 participants (300 in total) with an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient of 0.03 provided 0.9 power to detect a difference of 1.05 points in the primary outcome (SPPB) between arms, assuming an SD of 2.5 based on data from our pilot study [14]. This sample size provided 0.9 power to detect a rate ratio of 0.6 or 1.66 for differences in secondary binary outcomes between the study arms, and a difference of 0.42 SDs in secondary continuous outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Participants’ baseline characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics. The effect of the program on change in primary and secondary outcome measures was estimated through multi-level linear mixed-model repeated measures with a random intercept at participant level. Due to lower up-take of the control program, data were analysed separately for each program group due to unequal sample sizes. Time of measurement was coded to contrast mean baseline scores to mean scores at follow-up. The analysis was adjusted for potential confounders: age, sex, pre-existing health conditions and participation in a previous fall prevention program. The analysis of participants who reported a fall in the previous 12 months was analysed in multilevel logistic regression model adjusted for covariates and self-reported eyesight, with a random intercept at participant level. The impact of differences in program delivery across sites was explored in a two-level mixed model with site at the second level. We found that variation between sites was explained by individual level variables and only included one level in the final analysis. Sensitivity analysis was carried out to consider differences in program duration at each site and exercise program participation.

For ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program participants, changes in strength and balance outcomes were additionally analysed, stratified for those meeting exercise levels of three times a week for 6 months or longer and those who did not meet these levels. Sensitivity analysis was carried out for shorter time durations of 3 and 4 months. All analyses were carried out using Stata/SE V17.0.

Ethics approval was provided by the Aboriginal Health and Medical Research Council of NSW (reference 1401/18), the WA Aboriginal Health Ethics Committee (reference 962), the Aboriginal Health Research Ethics Committee SA (reference 04–18-790) and the University of NSW (reference 4712). Each site signed a Research Agreement which outlined roles and responsibilities of each organisation, including the intellectual and cultural ownership of data. All participants provided informed consent.

This work was supported by the National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia (grant number 1143085). The funder played no role in the design, execution, analysis and interpretation of data or writing of the study.

Results

Nineteen sites (10 metropolitan; 9 regional) [31] in NSW (n = 12) and WA (n = 7) participated in the study (Table 1) between March 2021 and November 2023; 14 sites elected to deliver the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program, and three sites delivered the ‘Ironbark Healthy Community’ program. Two sites were recruited prior to the pandemic and randomised to the Standing Strong program but commencement was delayed until 2021. A total of 224 participants were enrolled in the study; after withdrawals there were 117 participants in 16 ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ sites, and 21 participants in three ‘Ironbark Healthy Communities’ sites with complete baseline and follow-up data. Of the 197 in the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ (Figure 1), 80 participants (40.6%) stopped attending or did not complete outcome data collection due to illness, family commitments and work commitments (Supplementary Table S1). The program was delivered for at least 9 months in all sites, with 14 sites delivering the program for 11 months or longer (Table 1). The pandemic disrupted program delivery in two sites which commenced early in 2021 so program delivery was extended to ensure 12 months of activity.

Adverse events

No adverse events (falls, injuries and cardiovascular events) were reported during group or home sessions throughout the trial.

Program engagement

‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ participants who completed the program spent a mean 44 weeks (SD 13.6) in the program and participated in classes and/or did home exercise for a mean of 29 (SD 14.3) weeks. Participants attended a mean 22 classes (SD 11.2) and recorded at least one home exercise in 23 (SD 14.7) weeks. A total of 39 (33%) participants reported exercising for three times or more per week for 6 months or longer.

For participants who exercised at least three times a week for 6 months or longer, the mean SPPB score improved from baseline to follow-up by 1.02 (95% CI: 0.52–1.52), leg stance time improved for the right leg by 4.4 seconds (95% CI: 1.08–7.75) and left leg by 4.4 seconds (95% CI: 1.68–7.17), and the time to complete the step test decreased by 2.5 seconds (95% CI: –4.04 to –0.98) (Supplementary Table S4).

For those who exercised less frequently or for a shorter period than 6 months, the mean SPPB score improved from baseline to follow-up by 0.64 (95% CI: 0.15–1.12), and no statistically significant changes were observed in leg stance and step test.

The characteristics of the 80 participants who started the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program but did not complete it were similar to those who completed the program in terms of age, gender and health conditions (Supplementary Table S5). However, a higher percentage of those who did not complete the program felt too crowded where they lived (16, 20%) and reported wages and salaries as the main source of income (11, 13.8%) compared with those who completed the program. Those who started but did not complete the Ironbark program had a lower IPEQ planned sport [0.5 (SD 1.2)] and planned activity scores [1.7 (SD 3.3)] at baseline compared with those who completed the Ironbark program [2.2 (SD 4.9) and 3.6 (SD 6.4), respectively] (Supplementary Table S6). Non-completers also scored lower on the LLFD advanced lower extremity functioning test [24.4 (SD 13.2)] compared with those who completed the program [30.1 (SD 12.7)].

Ironbark Healthy Community program

Three sites elected to deliver the ‘Ironbark Healthy Community’ program; 21 participants, all women, and 20 (95%) were 50 years or older (Table 2). All participants reported at least one pre-existing health condition at baseline and four (19%) reported undertaking exercise and other classes. There was no difference in the overall SPPB score between baseline and follow-up but the SPPB gait speed score improved by 0.4. The time to complete the step test increased by 2.6 seconds. HRQoL (EQ 5D) declined in this group between baseline and follow up by 0.1 points (Supplementary Table S7). Physical activity IPEQ score decreased by 7.4 (95% CI: –13.5 to –1.3) with decreases in walking (–3.5; 95% CI: –6.5 to –0.5) and incidental activity (−6.5; 95% CI: –11.9 to –1.2) scores; waist circumference increased by 2.2 cm.

Discussion

The ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program was co-designed by Aboriginal and non-Aboriginal researchers with Aboriginal health services and community members. While COVID precluded delivery of the planned randomised cluster trial and evaluation of the impact of the program on falls, this current study has demonstrated that program participation was associated with improvements in functional mobility, in line with our previous pilot studies [14, 15], and with measures of dynamic balance. Although our final recruitment fell short of what was planned in the revised design, we found an improvement in functional mobility with an increased 0.8 SPPB score in the whole sample and 1.02 in those who exercised three times per week. These changes are clinically important with prior research reporting a 0.71 improvement as substantial [32], and a 0.54 improvement as a meaningful change for community-dwelling older adults [33]. Improved functional mobility and dynamic balance are associated with reduced falls risk in other trials [34], so while we were unable to assess the impact of falls in this trial, it seems likely that the program would have benefits for fall prevention. The benefits of the program were greater for those who reported more exercise, but importantly those reporting less exercise still saw significant improvements in mobility and balance after 6 months, again suggesting likelihood of conferred benefits in terms of fall prevention, and other health outcomes [35]. We also saw increases in planned and incidental exercise, which again has broader health benefits [34, 35].

As anticipated, we did not see improvements in falls, likely due to the change in study design and sample size, and there was no apparent benefit for HRQoL or psychological distress in the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ group. Research assistants noted that at follow-up interviews participants were more comfortable with the research team and frequently disclosed past trauma, which may have impacted the reporting of these measures.

Participants in both groups reported multiple co-morbidities, and the mean SPPB score for both groups at baseline and follow-up was lower than 10, which is predictive of all-cause mortality [36], highlighting the important need for healthy ageing programs for older Aboriginal people. People who dropped out of the program had worse physical function than those who stayed in the program, suggesting that more support may be required to ensure stronger retention for those with co-morbidities. While we were unable to assess between group differences due to small sample size, the ‘Ironbark Healthy Communities Program’ participants showed declines in physical functioning and HRQoL.

Delivering this study through and immediately after the pandemic was extremely challenging. However, ultimately we succeeded in recruiting multiple sites across a range of urban and regional settings, with a wide cross-section of participants including 40% men, suggesting that the program is acceptable for diverse community members. Of participants who enrolled in the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ sites, 59% attended for the length of the program, which is reasonable retention given 57% of participants had four or more health conditions. Retention of Aboriginal people in community-based prevention trials is infrequently reported but other studies have also reported similar challenges in maintaining retention for exercise programs [37, 38]. The program process evaluation demonstrated that the program was delivered with high fidelity for those participants who maintained attendance [39]. However, program non-completers had worse physical function at baseline than completers, indicating a need for more intensive support or an alternative approach for people in poorer health to maximise participation. A detailed analysis of participant and service feedback will be reported separately.

The research was guided by an Indigenous research paradigm by prioritising the principles of self-determination, cultural respect and community engagement in line with ethics guidance [40]. Indigenous governance and leadership were key aspects of the trial and were key to the significant changes that were made to trial design, and ensured the pandemic-related research re-design took account of community needs. It emphasised a strengths-based perspective, recognising the cultural knowledge and practises of the communities involved. Co-design and Aboriginal leadership are key drivers of research translation [41, 42] in Aboriginal health and were important drivers of the design and delivery of the Ironbark program [43].

Our study has some significant limitations due to the challenging circumstances encountered during implementation. Following the trial modifications most community sites requested delivery of the ‘Ironbark Standing Strong and Tall’ program such that a between-group analysis was not possible. This, and lower than ideal retention rates, have implications for the robustness of results, although it is notable that they replicate our previous findings in pilot studies. [14, 15] However, the change in research design reflects our team’s responsiveness to community preferences, including a desire to cease the randomisation process. Not all study tools we used had been validated with Aboriginal populations, and the mental health and quality of life tools used may not have been sufficiently sensitive or culturally specific for use with diverse Aboriginal people. More holistic cultural measures developed since the planned trial may be more appropriate [44].

The Ironbark program is a novel Aboriginal designed and implemented community-based program that incorporates both physical exercises and yarning to address falls risk factors. Although we were unable to assess effectiveness on falls, the benefits of the program likely extend beyond the demonstrated improvements in measures of fall-related physical function and exercise, given the benefits of social activity and engagement. It is possible that adaptations to the program incorporating more activities that address other common health conditions such as diabetes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease will improve program attendance and ensure more opportunities for funding and sustainable delivery models.
