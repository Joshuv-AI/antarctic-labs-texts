Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is a noninflammatory, nonatherosclerotic arteriopathy that most commonly affects the renal, cervical internal carotid, and vertebral arteries.1,2 FMD is diagnosed primarily by characteristic angiographic findings, including unifocal or multifocal arterial stenosis, typically described as a “string-of-beads” appearance.3

Ruptured intracranial dissecting aneurysms carry a high risk of rebleeding during the acute phase, and prompt definitive treatment is usually recommended.4 However, management is challenging because sacrificing the parent artery is often required, either surgically or endovascularly.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of surgically treated ruptured intracranial internal carotid artery dissecting aneurysm (IICDA) associated with FMD. Herein, we describe a case managed with high-flow bypass and trapping and discuss the rationale for this strategy.

Illustrative Case

A 43-year-old woman with a history of hypertension experienced the sudden onset of severe headache and was transferred to our hospital 2 days later after developing impaired consciousness. On admission, her Glasgow Coma Scale score was 6 (E1V1M4) with no hemiparesis or anisocoria, corresponding to World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade IV. Head CT (Fig. 1A and B) revealed diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), with right-sided predominance (Fisher group 3). CT perfusion revealed decreased cerebral blood flow and a prolonged mean transit time in the right hemisphere (Fig. 1C and D). Digital subtraction angiography (Fig. 1E–K) revealed the characteristic string-of-beads appearance of the bilateral cervical internal carotid arteries (ICAs), fusiform aneurysms of the right vertebral artery, and a dissecting aneurysm of the right intracranial ICA. These angiographic findings involving multiple vessels supported the diagnosis of FMD. Based on the distribution of the hemorrhage and the associated ischemic findings, rupture of the right IICDA was diagnosed. A standard right pterional transsylvian approach was used to surgically trap the affected right intracranial ICA, combined with the construction of a high-flow extracranial-intracranial bypass using a radial artery (RA) graft connecting the external carotid artery (ECA) to the middle cerebral artery (MCA) (ECA-RA-MCA) (Fig. 2). Ventricular drainage and decompressive craniectomy were performed. External decompression was performed based on CT perfusion findings showing lateralized decreased cerebral blood flow. Intraoperatively, bypass patency was confirmed using indocyanine green angiography and Doppler ultrasonography. Postoperative MRI demonstrated a small infarct in the right insular cortex (Fig. 3). No neurological deficits were observed secondary to the right insular infarction. No surgical complications or cerebral vasospasm developed. Follow-up 3D CT angiography confirmed persistent bypass patency and no evidence of recurrent dissection. Cranioplasty was performed on postoperative day 25 following treatment for vasospasm, after confirming stabilization of the patient’s condition despite slight residual brain edema. The procedure was performed using the patient’s autologous bone flap. The patient was transferred to a rehabilitation facility. At the 3-month follow-up, she had complete neurological recovery with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0.

Discussion

Observations

Arterial dissection is relatively common among patients with FMD, with a reported prevalence of 25.7% according to the US registry. The carotid artery is the most frequently affected (64%), followed by the vertebral artery (21%), and multiple cervical dissections have been reported in up to 37% of cases.5,6

SAH resulting from arterial dissection in patients with FMD is rare and most predominantly affects the posterior circulation.7 Involvement of the anterior circulation has been reported in only 4 cases, including the present one (Table 1), which is the only reported case involving IICDA.8–10

FMD and IICDA show a strong predilection for middle-aged women.6,11 Importantly, the coexistence of cerebral infarction and SAH following intracranial arterial dissection is uncommon; nevertheless, 3 of the 4 reported cases exhibited both ischemic and hemorrhagic manifestations. The proposed mechanisms include thrombus formation related to arterial wall irregularities, hypoperfusion secondary to luminal narrowing, and vascular occlusion caused by the dissecting aneurysm itself.12

Ruptured IICDA is an uncommon cause of SAH but carries a high risk of early rebleeding. When FMD is associated with multiple cerebral aneurysms, identifying the rupture site can be challenging. In this case, the diagnosis of IICDA rupture was based on the hemorrhagic laterality and concurrent ischemia in the ipsilateral ICA territory. Despite presentation 2 days after symptom onset, no rebleeding occurred prior to definitive surgical treatment, and the clinical outcome was favorable.

The optimal management strategy for ruptured IICDA remains controversial.11,13 Treatment options include proximal clipping, wrapping, endovascular flow diversion, and trapping with bypass.11,13 Flow diverter stents may pose a high hemorrhagic risk in the acute phase because dual antiplatelet therapy is required.14 In the acute phase of SAH, the need for dual antiplatelet therapy renders flow diversion less favorable, particularly in patients requiring ventricular drainage or decompressive procedures. In Japan, the use of flow diverter stents is also not approved during the acute stage of SAH. Prior studies have reported favorable outcomes after trapping, with no documented recurrences.11

High-flow bypass combined with trapping is a well-established strategy for IICDA. A balloon occlusion test of the right ICA is commonly used to evaluate collateral circulation; however, we omitted this test to avoid the risk of rebleeding. Manual right carotid compression revealed insufficient cross-filling collateral circulation. Because preoperative perfusion imaging demonstrated hemispheric hypoperfusion, low-flow bypass was deemed insufficient to ensure adequate cerebral perfusion; therefore, high-flow bypass was performed in addition to trapping. Given the fragile arterial pathology and the high risk of rebleeding in the acute phase, additional endovascular manipulation was considered potentially hazardous.

RA grafts are commonly used for high-flow bypass procedures because of their valveless structure and ease of anastomosis to the intracranial arteries.13 However, the role of high-flow bypass with trapping in patients with FMD remains uncertain. Bypass grafts are not adversely affected by FMD-related histopathological changes unless multifocal stenosis is present.15–17 Long-term follow-up studies have demonstrated durable bypass patency without progression of FMD-related changes.15 Importantly, FMD primarily affects medium-sized arteries and is not known to compromise RA graft integrity in the absence of multifocal stenosis.

In the present case, the concern for early rebleeding and limitations of endovascular treatment favored surgical trapping with high-flow bypass. Despite the relatively short follow-up period, the RA graft remained patent and no progression of FMD was observed. Long-term imaging surveillance is warranted to confirm the durability.

Lessons

Ruptured IICDA cases associated with FMD are rare. This case highlights that high-flow bypass with trapping is a safe and effective treatment option for preventing rebleeding while preserving cerebral perfusion in carefully selected patients with this challenging condition.
