Evaluation of a novel communication workshop for healthcare professionals working with metastatic breast cancer patients
Authors: Lesley J Fallowfield, Ivonne Solis-Trapala, Ruth Habibi, Valerie A Jenkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNumerous reports criticise the communication between patients with metastatic breast cancer (MBC) and their health care providers (HCPs). System constraints may contribute to HCP burnout and compassion-fatigue, adding further to patient dissatisfaction. We developed and evaluated a novel communication skills training workshop based on verbatim quotes from a survey of patients with MBC.MethodsPrior to attendance at 6-h small group, facilitator-led workshops, participants completed: - General Health Questionnaire (GHQ12), Standford Fulfilment Index (SFI) and the navigating professional boundaries (NPB) questionnaire. Self-confidence discussing 9 key MBC issues was assessed pre- and post-workshop.ResultsSixty-six HCPs (20 surgeons, 26 oncologists, and 20 nurses) participated. Overall, 14/66 (21.2%) had GHQ12 scores of ≥4 indicating heightened psychological concerns, 21/66 (31.8%) had burnout and merely (21/66 (31.8%) felt professionally fulfilled by work. Higher anxiety levels (GHQ-12 ≥ 4) were associated with increased odds of burnout (OR = 6.0, 95% CI: 1.7 to 21.0, p = 0.0057) and reduced odds of professional fulfilment (OR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.015 to 1.00, p = 0.052). The primary outcome of self-confidence communicating about 9 key issues improved significantly post-workshop (mean change = 1.5, 95%CI:1.14 to 1.81). Workshops were rated highly by all participants.ConclusionsBespoke small-group workshops using patient verbatims, and which addressed psychological and practice constraints inhibiting effective communication, significantly improved HCPs’ self-confidence discussing challenging MBC consultations.Keywords: Metastatic breast cancer, Communication workshops, Health care professionals, Burnout

Introduction

Many advances have been made in the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of metastatic/advanced or secondary breast cancer, but it is widely acknowledged that patients with metastatic disease are a relatively neglected and underserved group. Numerous reports and surveys reveal criticisms about the communication between themselves, and their care providers and alarming gaps in patients’ knowledge about diagnosis, recurrence, prognosis, disease management and the resulting impact these have on daily life [[1], [2], [3]]. Consequently, patients with Advanced/Metastatic Breast Cancer (ABC/MBC) often feel isolated with inadequate access to treatments, clinical trials, information, occupational, financial, and psycho-social support. Unless more effort is focused on advancing the quality of patient-healthcare professional exchanges, it is unclear how some of these gaps can be addressed or how the increasing calls for more patient-centric approaches to decision-making can be achieved [4].

There is very good evidence that clinicians and nurses can be taught communication [[5], [6], [7]] and that improving this core clinical skill can alter attitudes and behavior in clinics, thus increasing patient satisfaction [8]. Furthermore, if the pedagogical methodology employed is sound, such improvements are enduring [9]. Training programs that focus on knowledge, skills and personal awareness issues that may influence what and how things are said, produce benefits for HCPs themselves as well as their patients. For example, having flexible repertoires that enable empathic and efficient handling of challenging clinical conversations can help HCPs lower their own risks of developing burnout [10]. The communication deficiencies reported by patients are not just the result of time pressures and poor skills but are often due to clinician burnout and compassion-fatigue. Although medicine is considered a well-respected and often rewarding career, clinicians and nurses globally are suffering [11]. The British Medical Association reported high levels of depression, anxiety, stress, burnout and emotional distress in 7000 clinicians [12] and a meta-analysis of 85 studies showed that nurse burnout was associated with medication errors, lower quality of care, patient safety and satisfaction [13].

The improvements in treatment of MBC means that more patients will survive longer and may build up lengthier relationships with their healthcare teams. Societal expectations of a less deferential attitude towards doctors can mean that certain professional and personal boundaries are breached. Behaviors such as accepting patients as friends on social media sites or offering their personal phone number may lead to complications. For example, clinicians who develop a closer relationship with individual patients admit that they are less than honest about prognosis [14].

The HEARSAY workshops reported in this paper were designed to: - increase HCPs’ understanding as to how patients felt about the communication they encounter; provide evidence-based recommendations for appropriate and flexible approaches when handling challenging consultations; and finally introduce participants to ways in which they might take care of their own well-being thus avoiding burnout and compassion-fatigue.

Methods

There were two phases to the development of the contents for the small-group, interactive, facilitator-led workshops.

Phase 1. development of filmed materials

An integral part of the workshop were the facilitated group discussions, with emphasis placed on why certain phrases are more or less acceptable to patients, how to deal flexibly with individuals who may have different preferences for information, balancing reassurance honestly with plausible and realistic goals and addressing patient concerns about treatment management. Content on this utilised patients’ responses to an on-line survey ‘Living with metastatic breast cancer (LIMBER) [2]. They were asked what things HCPs had said that either helped or hindered their understanding. These verbatims were divided into nine categories: - 1) reality of recurrence 2) diagnosis, 3) prognosis, 4) pitching the conversation 5) treatments and management 6) impact of the working environment 7) specialist nurse support 8) informational gaps and 9) family support. The most frequently reported or pertinent quotes deemed able to generate potentially constructive discussions amongst HCPs were chosen for the filmed materials. Six actors, broadly representative of the LIMBER survey population in terms of age, socio-educational grouping, ethnicity, timing of recurrence and stage of disease at diagnosis, were filmed voicing the most appropriate quotes.

Phase 2: development of further materials

Although workshops were primarily interactive with emphasis centered on discussion around the filmed patients’ quotes, three didactic presentations and several relevant exercises highlighting specific issues were also produced. The presentations included results from the LIMBER survey [2], another on evidence-based methods to improve communication about prognosis, and finally techniques to help HCPs to look after themselves and prevent burnout and compassion-fatigue.

The novel exercises used within workshops varied but included one to promote awareness about the impact of making assumptions and the effect of stereotyping on communication. Others were aimed at helping HCPs to recognise how the blurring of professional boundaries and how their own attitudes and emotions, produced by burnout or compassion fatigue might ‘leak’ into their interactions with patients. Recognition of the difficulties experienced when an individual patient does not benefit from a newly approved drug that the clinician has talked about with enthusiasm was illustrated through a poem ‘The other side of the waterfall’ written by a clinician [15]. All delegates were provided with citations and abstracts, for all references made in the PowerPoint presentations, together with links to professional resource services and organisations.

Recruitment and evaluation of workshop efficacy

Participants

A flyer advertising dates and information about HEARSAY workshops was circulated via UK Breast Cancer meetings, word of mouth, and the SHORE-C website newsfeed (https://shore-c.sussex.ac.uk).

Sample size

A sample of at least 48 participants with complete data was estimated to provide adequate precision to detect a mean change in confidence score, with a two-sided 95% confidence interval half-width of approximately 0.30, assuming a conservative standard deviation (SD) of 1. This SD was informed by previous studies using similar Likert-scale confidence scores (TRUSTING, n = 53, SD = 0.52; TARGET, n = 65, SD = 0.32) [16,17]. Informed by evaluations of previous educational workshops run by SHORE-C, we aimed to recruit a realistic target of at least sixty participants. This allowed for a potential 20% of missing data.

Inclusion criteria

Participants had to be actively engaged in discussing ABC/MBC treatments or supportive management with patients and could be from any appropriate discipline and at any level or seniority. Those interested had to provide fully informed consent which included an agreement to attend the entire 6-hr workshop and to complete all assessments. Workshops were accredited with 6 Continuing Professional Development (CPD) points from the Royal College of Physicians. Brighton & Sussex Medical School Research Governance and Ethics Committee approved the study (ref: ER/VAL2).

Assessments

These were completed online pre-workshop and either on paper or online immediately post-workshop. Although the assessments are subjective, the pedagogical methods utilised in the HEARSAY workshop incorporate simultaneously-knowledge, skills development and self-awareness/self-confidence. These features were used in our earlier educational programs, that also measured skills objectively which were shown to improve [16,17] and transfer into a clinical setting [9,18].

Pre-workshop

Self-confidence questionnaire. A study-specific 9-item questionnaire in which attendees rated their self-confidence when discussing different communication issues with ABC/MBC patients on a scale of 1 (not at all) −10 (very confident). Also, attendees gave examples of the types of patient characteristics or situations that they found most challenging to deal with in the clinic.

Anxiety/Depression Screening Tool. The 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ12) measures likely psychological morbidity [19].

Professional Fulfilment Index. The 16-item Stanford Professional Fulfilment Index (SPFI) measures the intrinsic positive reward derived from work and linked to burnout during the past two weeks [20].

Professional Boundaries questionnaire. The 38-item questionnaire highlights where HCPs’ blurring of personal and professional roles may occur, which can have a detrimental impact on patient care [14].

Post-workshop

Participants completed the self-confidence questionnaire again and were asked to evaluate different elements of the workshop.

Hypotheses

Our a-priori hypotheses were that post workshop 1) HCPs would feel more confident communicating in an appropriate and flexible manner about difficult MBC issues, 2) HCPs who demonstrated a high level of anxiety would be more likely to experience burnout, and 3) those who blurred professional boundaries were more likely to report burnout and/or lack of professional fulfilment.

Statistical analyses

The mean pre-, post-, and change-in confidence scores were summarized both overall and for each of the nine individual self-confidence questions related to different communication issues. Summary statistics included range, mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR). A radar plot was used to illustrate the mean pre- and post-workshop scores. Descriptive statistics were also stratified by participant roles (Surgeon, Oncologist, Metastatic Breast Nurse, and Specialist Breast Nurse). Similarly, fulfilment and burnout scores were calculated and summarized descriptively, along with the single-item scores from the Stanford Professional Fulfilment Index and the Professional Boundaries Questionnaire, both overall and by participant role.

The mean confidence score across the 9 self-confidence questions, as well as each individual score, was modelled using linear mixed-effects regression with a random intercept to account for the correlation between repeated observations within participants. The models included a time indicator (pre-vs. post-workshop) to estimate the mean change in confidence. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was reported to quantify the proportion of total variance attributable to differences between participants, thereby reflecting the correlation among repeated observations within individuals. Burnout and professional fulfilment were modelled using logistic regression, with GHQ-12 score as the explanatory variable. A contingency table cross-tabulated by GHQ-12 score and participant role was used to summarize observed levels of anxiety and burnout, stratified by role. To examine the association between engagement in activities that cross patient–doctor personal boundaries and burnout, additional logistic regression models for burnout included binary indicators (“often” or “sometimes” vs. “never”) for items from Part 2 of the PB questionnaire as explanatory variables.

All models were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation. Diagnostic plots, including plots of residuals, and Q-Q plots for continuous outcomes, were used to check the model assumptions. Goodness of fit was assessed through the model’s deviance statistic for binary outcomes. Statistical analyses were undertaken using R and the package lme4 [21,22].

Patient and public involvement (PPI)

Patients contributed to the generation of questions for the LIMBER survey [2], and in subsequent educational materials (https://youtu.be/wPMmPlH1AGk).

Secondary analyses

Higher anxiety levels (GHQ-12 ≥ 4) were associated with increased odds of burnout (OR = 6.0, 95% CI: 1.7 to 21.0, p = 0.0057) and less likelihood of feeling professionally fulfilled (OR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.015 to 1.00, p = 0·052). Table 4 presents the observed levels of anxiety and burnout, stratified by participant role. Approximately 15% (9/66) of participants across roles experienced both burnout and heightened anxiety. The nurses were the least fulfilled in their work (3/20; 15%) compared with their surgical and oncology colleagues (10/20; 50% & 8/26; 30.8% respectively).

Nurses had higher proportions of both burnout and low self-confidence pre -workshop (17% and 29%, respectively) than surgeons and oncologists (5% and 4%, respectively). However, overall, there was weak evidence that higher pre-workshop self-confidence scores were associated with greater odds of burnout (OR = 1.4, 95% CI: 0.97 to 2.0, p = 0.075).

The responses to the Navigating Professional Boundaries Questionnaire (NPB) are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Fifty-two (78.8%) participants reported that they tried to remain emotionally detached from their patients during consultations and 38 (57.6%) said that if they were too empathic, they could not make objective decisions. Participants who reported having cried with a distressed patient had higher odds of burnout than those who had never done so (OR = 2.5, 95%CI: 1.2 to 11, p = 0.021). There was also weak evidence indicating that those who said they had attended patients’ funerals had increased the odds of burnout (OR = 2.5, 95%CI: 0.85 to 7.4, p = 0.097).

Participants evaluated the six different elements of the Hearsay workshops on a scale of 1 ‘poor’ through to 5 ‘excellent’. The majority rated excellent for all aspects (venues 89.4%; film clips 89.4%; PPT presentations 80.3%; group discussions 81.8%; exercises 84.8%) and an overall rating of 87.9%. All 63 participants who responded said they would recommend workshop attendance to colleagues.

Forty-eight delegates also wrote comments in the feedback; an example is shown below.

‘A wonderful opportunity & method of evaluating personal practice & to stop & look at the patient perspective. Really great to be in such a mixed group & to finish with self-care - a reminder we all need!’

Discussion

HEARSAY workshops significantly improved the self-confidence of HCPs when communicating with patients from varied socio-educational backgrounds, of different ages and time of diagnosis with MBC (either de-novo or following breast cancer recurrence). Self-confidence when explaining prognosis honestly whilst remaining hopeful about plausible and achievable outcomes was a key, challenging topic for many participants pre-workshop and which improved significantly post-workshop following the evidence-based, pragmatic examples provided during the day.

All consultations must be delivered empathically and take account of individual circumstances and personal preferences. Helping HCPs gain more self-awareness of their own predispositions towards making assumptions about certain characteristics of patients through the workshop exercises about stereotyping, and which many patients complain about, was especially revealing and impactful. Fewer HCPs than seen in previous studies said that they had crossed professional boundaries such as providing personal mobile phone numbers and accepting patients they were treating on social media platforms. However, there was evidence that some struggled with striking the right balance between emotional detachment and overt empathy and several found honest disclosure of prognosis difficult with patients whom they liked.

Globally there are multiple constraints and pressures on HCPs that have a deleterious impact on their ability to always communicate well and recognition of this by facilitators was highly valued by participants. Increased numbers of patients, long waiting lists, insufficient staff, all contributed to inadequate consultation time leading to dissatisfaction by everyone involved. HCPs are only too aware that even though they are working long hours, institutional time constraints and throughput challenges often compromise the communication and care they are providing. This can contribute to moral distress or injury, concepts describing the inner conflict and profound sense of guilt or shame HCPs’ may experience when recognising that they may be providing patients with sub-optimal care [23]. Moral injury is strongly associated with the more commonly discussed clinician burnout [24]. Burnout was present in 32% of HEARSAY delegates, with just over a fifth (22%) experiencing probable psychological distress and low numbers of HCPs feeling fulfilled with their job.

When HCPs are struggling to look after others that they can often neglect their own self-care. If those with early signs of work-related distress are not identified and supported, HCPs may resort to unhealthy means to combat their problems. Research shows 20% of HCPs have hazardous drinking habits [25], and higher rates of suicide than the general public [26]. Sinsky and colleagues analysed data from 3024 US physicians and found that only 59.6% took 3 weeks’ vacation or less per year, and 70.4% worked when on leave; both findings were associated with higher rates of burnout [27]. An extremely appreciated part of the workshop was the closing session on ‘Looking after yourselves’. Having interests outside of one’s vocation is vital for the maintenance of good mental health. Sir William Osler back in 1900s emphasized the importance of a physician having a well-rounded life by engaging in art, science or literature in order to maintain a broader perspective in life [28]. These sentiments proved accurate in a study of 4457 clinicians [29]. They showed that those clinicians with high avocation (engaged in leisure activities) also had a greater sense of vocation. Helping HCPs to help their patients more and avoid burnout demands interventions on several levels. This includes at a system/organisational level especially reduction of workload [30]. A recently published statement between ASCO- COSA and ECO has outlined the system-level change required for healthier workplace culture, which includes better communication skills training [31].

Limitations

HEARSAY was conducted in a UK setting but facilitator training programs are currently being conducted for modification by colleagues in Australia to run and evaluate. In the US work has begun on collection of survey data from patients on which to base their version of HEARSAY.

Although the study population was relatively small, the workshops were intensive, with all delegates contributing to discussions. Based on our previous educational randomised interventions, the post workshop results suggest that the increase in self-confidence would transfer into the clinic setting (9, 16-18). Ideally funding to conduct a randomised controlled trial of the educational intervention would provide the level 1 evidence required to answer the question with greater certainty. The small group format led by experienced facilitators was especially valued but this is resource intensive, so an eLearning version is currently being prepared.

Conclusion

The HEARSAY workshop program made extensive use of data derived directly from patients with MBC in a novel manner by filming patient simulators voicing the helpful and less helpful things that had been said to them. Bespoke small-group workshops using patient verbatims, and which addressed psychological and practice constraints inhibiting effective communication, significantly improved HCPs’ self-confidence when discussing challenging MBC consultations.
