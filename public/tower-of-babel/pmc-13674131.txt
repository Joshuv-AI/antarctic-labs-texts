How does a national blood operator detect early infectious disease signals affecting blood and cellular therapy product safety and sufficiency?
Authors: Leisa Bruneau, Carmen L Charlton, Sandra Ramirez‐Arcos, Dilini Kumaran, David Allan, Olivia Norman, Matthew Seftel, Evan M Bloch, Chantale Pambrun, Sheila F O'Brien, Cynthia Cranney, Steven J Drews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic access to up‐to‐date information on infectious diseases and outbreaks has grown rapidly with the advent of artificial intelligence platforms. While this information can improve patient care and support research, the sheer volume of available data coupled with the challenge of identifying misinformation and disinformation can be overwhelming. A key challenge is managing large information volumes and identifying early, subtle signals that may indicate risks or opportunities to prevent infection. The aim of this manuscript is to explain how a national blood operator identifies and evaluates subtle infectious disease signals generated by horizon scanning to proactively protect the blood supply, including biotherapy products.MethodsHorizon scanning protocols were developed to detect early signals of change (emerging/re‐emerging pathogens, geopolitical/environmental changes, scientific and technological advances, new therapies/treatments for infectious diseases, and blood operator transfusion medicine changes). Communication strategies were created to alert national and international transfusion medicine decision makers of blood safety‐related events.ResultsSeveral key components of a horizon scanning approach are presented to illustrate how novel information can be identified, processed, and formulated into knowledge translation products. Efficient and cost‐effective approaches are presented to enable horizon scanning activities with limited resources and time commitments.DiscussionHorizon scanning is an efficient and necessary activity to safeguard the blood supply. Here we present a collaborative group‐based approach, which reduces operator bias and improves overall examination of multiple data sources. This approach does not require expensive technology, can focus on open‐source information, and engages new scientists in learning about infectious diseases.Keywords: blood safety, emerging pathogens, horizon scanning, re‐emerging pathogens, transfusion medicine

INTRODUCTION

Since the 1960s, computing power and information technology (IT) have increased rapidly. 1 Concurrently, the volume of medical information has surged, creating information processing challenges for scientists, clinicians, organizational leaders, and planners. This information revolution is occurring while factors including climate change, microbial evolution, and a growing gap in human socio‐economic conditions fuel emerging and re‐emerging infectious diseases. Emerging pathogens are infectious agents (bacterium, fungus, parasite, prion, or virus) that appear in a new host population, while re‐emerging pathogens are agents that have resurfaced, often due to antimicrobial resistance, weakened public health systems, and/or environmental changes. 2

National health systems, including blood operators, continue to face funding challenges after increased spending in the early COVID‐19 pandemic years. 3 In this resource challenged environment, creative solutions can foster epidemic intelligence gathering and emergency preparedness, especially in low and middle income countries (LMICs). 4 In transfusion and stem cell transplant/cellular therapy medicine, horizon scanning can be carried out, which is an active process to detect weak information signals that may represent a risk or an opportunity. 5 The information identified in horizon scanning can support organizational decision making to support emergency preparedness and improve healthcare innovation and delivery. Horizon scanning is carried out by a coalition of groups and individuals at Canadian Blood Services, the largest blood operator in Canada, which collects blood donations from nine provinces (excluding the province of Québec and territories) and provides cellular products for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

This manuscript describes how a group of scientists, physicians, technologists, and epidemiologists working in a national blood operator setting developed and used horizon scanning protocols to identify infectious disease‐focused risks and opportunities impacting the safety and sufficiency of blood (e.g., platelet and red blood cell concentrates and plasma) and cellular therapy (e.g., hematopoietic stem cells, bone marrow, cord cells) products. It also offers guidance on how to communicate these findings to transfusion and stem cell transplant medicine decision‐makers at national and international levels.

ELEMENTS OF A HORIZON SCANNING APPROACH

Building a team

In 2018, Canadian Blood Services undertook several iterations of horizon scanning to support decision making in transfusion and cellular therapy. Since that time, the Medical Microbiology team identified a need for horizon scanning processes that were protocol focused and less reliant on individual staff members. As a result of this decision, team members from a variety of backgrounds (students, research scientists, medical laboratory technologists, clinical microbiologists, transfusion medicine experts, cellular therapy experts, and epidemiologists) were invited to participate in horizon scanning. Individual team members are responsible for horizon scanning activities that range from daily direct observation to more structured planning and information searching activities. The team ensures preparedness by monitoring the following themes: emerging/re‐emerging pathogens, geopolitical and environmental changes (e.g., travel‐related infections, climate change), supply chain vulnerabilities, scientific and technological advances (e.g., blood product processing, pathogen reduction technologies, testing, biovigilance and hemovigilance, vaccinations), new therapy/treatment, and blood operator transfusion medicine change.

Developing and updating protocols

The United Nations (UN) Global Pulse horizon scan user manual was used as a template for the development of the horizon scanning protocol. 6 The user manual is designed for flexible implementation across diverse settings, including in LMICs. The Canadian Blood Services protocol for horizon scanning was developed using a design‐thinking framework comprising four stages: define, ideate, test, refine.

Although this protocol was implemented within Canadian Blood Services, the framework is intended to be scalable and adaptable. In LMICs, implementation could begin with open‐source information, brief scheduled review periods, freely available search or artificial intelligence (AI) tools, and periodic expert discussion, with scope adjusted to local workforce capacity and blood operator and public health priorities.

Identifying search categories

For direct human observation (Figure 1), team members were asked to spend no more than 15 min a day scanning for relevant information within at least one of the following broad categories; emerging or re‐emerging pathogens, geopolitical and environmental changes (e.g., travel‐related infections, climate change), supply chain vulnerabilities, scientific and technological advances (pathogen reduction technologies, testing, biovigilance and hemovigilance, vaccination), new therapies or treatments, changes in transfusion medicine practices among blood operators. To prepare for AI facilitated scanning, team members identified and refined the types of information to input into the AI search (Figure 1). Through brainstorming, several broad thematic categories were established, including; pathogen status (e.g., known, emerging, re‐emerging), pathogen type/biological classification (e.g., virus, bacterium, prion), transmission routes (e.g., respiratory, blood‐borne), biological characteristics, relevance to the blood system (e.g. potential for transfusion‐transmission), affected blood components, signal type (e.g., weak, static, increasing), and geographic distribution. These categories were designed to be flexible, allowing for addition of new themes as needed based on emerging priorities or insights.

An example of process flow during horizon scanning. Horizon scanning involves a combination of human and artificial intelligence (AI) activities. Phases include brainstorming, searching, collating, decision making, knowledge translation product production and review. This process is iterative and input from reviewers may change how horizon scanning is carried out.

Storing information

An internal Canadian Blood Services SharePoint (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) site and Excel file (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) were developed to support data capture and collation by internal team members (Figure 1). The Excel file (designed as a Horizon Scanning Initial Tracking Spreadsheet with the fields outlined in Table 1 and housed within the SharePoint site) was intended to facilitate data organization, reduce the need for meetings, and enable collaborative input. Manual daily scanning of large volumes of information was found to be overwhelming. This approach was most effective when focused on targeted sources, such as personal communications and non‐public information.

To ensure operational feasibility, the team focused scanning on predefined categories and selected information streams rather than employing unrestricted searches. This allowed for team brainstorming on themes of interest, controlled the mass of new information gathered, and helped the team practice how to change and adapt queries or search strings. Targeted sources included public health alerts and outbreak reports, peer‐reviewed and preprint literature when available, regulatory and guidance updates, blood operator communications, professional society updates, third‐party reports, subscription‐supported manuscripts, and expert or professional communications.

Automating searches

It was determined that the search approach would need to be easy to use, automated, require low IT resources, and contain secure handling of both open‐source and restricted data. Another requirement was a system that could be used within our organization's AI regulations. Google (Mountain View, CA), Chat GPT (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA), and Microsoft 365 (M365) were trialed to scan open‐source information only. We also used M365 as a secure method to scan non‐public information, including third party reports or PDF copies of subscription‐supported manuscripts (Figure 1).

Open‐source searches were separated from restricted or non‐public materials to ensure compliance with organizational policies on IT security. Restricted information that was internal to the organization was reviewed only within approved secure systems and was used to support contextual interpretation rather than automated public searching.

Boolean search string strategy

For AI‐facilitated scanning (Figure 1), a search string agent was developed to maximize recall by capturing a high‐sensitivity range of signals, including early indicators, weak signals, and first reports. The agent titled “Scan It All” incorporates a Boolean search string that enables automated scanning of web‐based sources and links embedded within emails forwarded by team members with the title “scan it all”. The outputs are organized into structured tables, categorized by key variables, and used to generate weekly watchlists. Reviewing and processing information

AI‐generated outputs and human‐derived findings are integrated to produce a range of deliverables including summaries, slide decks, images, maps, pivot tables, and text‐based tables (Figure 1). Information tables generated through direct observation are processed using M365 tools to complement AI‐generated outputs. AI outputs are not considered final and require human review to ensure accuracy and completeness. Instead, AI‐generated content guides further analysis and serves as a starting point for additional searching, collation, summarization, and interpretation by team members. At this phase, consensus‐based expert opinion in a team setting plays an important role in identifying and discounting misinformation and disinformation signals that might be promoted in non‐scientific information sources. 7

Ongoing validation is embedded in the workflow through human verification of AI outputs, source corroboration, expert consensus review, and comparison of identified signals with subsequent operational, surveillance, or policy actions. For example, team members are routinely asked to pick a topic from newly collected information, identify other sources of information on the topic, and present it in more depth at the team's emerging pathogens rounds.

Knowledge translation activities generate usable outputs and support validation of the information. During director level review, team members select a topic identified in outputs (e.g., summaries or pivot tables) for presentation in Emerging Pathogens Rounds (Figure 1). Independently, team members also review the collated information and choose topics for presentation in Emerging Infectious Diseases sessions. These activities facilitate team‐based review and may identify areas requiring further horizon scanning and analysis. Emerging signals with potential implications for the safety or sufficiency of the blood supply are chosen for quarterly briefing notes. These undergo further analysis and summarization. The process is initiated through a shared document containing key highlights (captured as bullet points or paragraphs), which team members iteratively expand by adding new topics, deeper analysis, new references, and revisions to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Knowledge sharing (“push vs. pull”)

Information products such as briefing notes, white papers, or proposed study protocols are generated and disseminated (“pushed”) to internal and external stakeholders (Figure 1). In parallel, knowledge users are actively engaged to identify their specific information needs. Data from pivot tables were used to populate a dashboard that categorizes information focused on both blood and stem cell transplant safety, enabling users to independently access (“pull”) information as needed. The frequency of updates to pivot tables was determined both by the rate of new information and the needs of knowledge users. Updates were scheduled to align with team capacity and resource availability. A beta version of the dashboard was provided to users, with functionality designed to be refined based on user feedback and evolving information needs.

COLLABORATION AND FEEDBACK

Direct observation focused on changing stem cell regulatory requirements

Medical and scientific team members from the Canadian Blood Services Stem Cells Program are asked to review information on pathogens generated by horizon scanning and note current and changing regulatory and guidance information focused on blood stem cell products (e.g., peripheral blood stem cells, bone marrow, umbilical cord blood). In Canada, “The Safety of Human Cells, Tissues and Organs for Transplantation Regulations (CTO Regulations)” identify requirements for processing (e.g., donor screening, donor testing, donor suitability assessment) for cells, tissues or organs (CTO) used in transplantation. 8 Further guidance is provided by the document “Guidance document for cell, tissue and organ establishments—Safety of human cells, tissues and organs for transplantation.” 9 Other documents that support infectious diseases decision making include Canadian Standards Association (CSA); CAN/CSA‐Z900.2.5‐17, 10 the Association for the Advancement of Blood & Biotherapies (AABB) Standards for Cellular Therapy Services, 11 the Foundation for the Accreditation of Cellular Therapy (FACT) Standards, 12 and World Marrow Donor Association standards. 13 Current information and potential changes that are identified are included in a dashboard that has a combined focus on the safety and sufficiency of blood and cellular products.

International collaborations

We observed that collaborators from outside the organization were more likely to challenge our assumptions and contribute fresh perspectives to the information we generated. External reviewers may be national or international colleagues who work in transfusion medicine, microbiology, infectious diseases, transplantation, basic science, or administration. External experts can be asked to provide feedback on knowledge translation products and horizon scanning processes, suggest improvements to search strategies (e.g., Boolean logic strings), and even to act as a source of new information to the team. 14 Collaborators can engage to suggest novel categories of information, fresh data streams, or unexplored knowledge domains to be scanned. This is particularly important in the context of emerging infectious diseases and associated risks to the blood supply. Horizon scanning brings innovative tools to bear in detection. However, its success remains contingent on the information being generated. This highlights a major gap where data gathering may be limited in some jurisdictions, including LMICs, leading to underreporting of, or missing linkages between, infectious disease cases. It is therefore important to identify even subtle shifts in infectious disease tracking to identify new risks to the global blood supply. 15 International organizations, such as the Association for the AABB, the International Society of Blood Transfusion (ISBT), the African Society of Blood Transfusion, and the Asian Association of Blood Transfusion (AATM), have the ability to promote collaboration but lack the resources to support large scale intelligence gathering. This falls on regional blood collection and public health agencies, that similarly contend with a host of competing challenges. 16 , 17

Future work should include structured collaboration with international blood operators, public health partners, and professional societies in LMICs and other regions where there is a high burden of emerging infectious disease. Such partnerships may help refine source selection, improve recognition of locally relevant signals, and test whether low‐resource horizon scanning models can be implemented sustainably.

CONCLUSIONS

This work describes the development of a multidisciplinary team of subject matter experts tasked with identifying weak signals representing potential threats or opportunities related to the safety and sufficiency of the blood supply. Our approach combines human expertise with AI‐supported methods to efficiently navigate and synthesize large volumes of emerging information related to blood safety. Outputs generated through horizon scanning activities serve multiple purposes which include: providing trusted information for consultation, supporting briefing notes to guide responses to emerging events, a place to routinely go for archived and trusted information, and a starting point for generating studies and identifying research opportunities. These activities also support gap analyses for innovative technologies and enhance organizational preparedness. Organizational preparedness fosters initiative‐taking tracking of emerging threats and timely integration of new evidence into operational guidance, ensuring implementation‐ready strategies for both laboratory testing and donor deferral are maintained.

Unlike many horizon‐scanning programs that primarily focus on collecting, analyzing, and disseminating information through periodic reports, the Canadian Blood Services approach integrates multidisciplinary expert review, AI‐assisted analysis, and operational decision making within a single framework. This model emphasizes rapid translation of emerging signals into actionable recommendations, preparedness activities, surveillance initiatives, and policy considerations that directly support blood system safety and sufficiency.

Despite these benefits, several limitations of AI‐supported horizon scanning must be acknowledged. AI outputs require human verification and are intended to streamline and enhance (rather than replace) the scanning process. Results may vary across runs, even when using identical Boolean string searches, as AI generates new text dynamically with differences in structure, emphasis, and content. While underlying facts are expected to remain consistent, outputs are not fully reproducible and should be interpreted with caution. 18

At Canadian Blood Services, horizon scanning has been used to assess emerging issues and engage with expert groups, including public health professionals, microbiologists, and infectious diseases specialists. The process has been progressively standardized through the implementation of an initial tracking system and a user‐friendly pathogen dashboard. Reports generated for medical and executive leadership have resulted in actionable recommendations that support the safety and sufficiency of the blood supply. For example, horizon scanning contributed to the development of a successful multi‐year tick‐borne disease surveillance program at Canadian Blood Services. 19 Future evaluations will assess the value of this approach for informed decision‐making beyond tick‐borne disease surveillance.

Information captured is prioritized based on credibility, relevance, novelty, potential impact, and timeliness. AI supports weekly data summaries by processing large volumes of information from defined sources; however, human review remains essential to verify accuracy and interpret findings. AI‐generated outputs provide an additional layer, with cited sources enabling validation.

Although the Canadian Blood Services experience provides proof of concept, generalizability to other blood systems, particularly in LMIC settings, will need to be assessed through collaborative pilots and outcome‐based evaluation
