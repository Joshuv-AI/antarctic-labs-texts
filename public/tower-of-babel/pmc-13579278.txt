Real-world data sources for pediatric cancers: a targeted literature review
Authors: P.J. Kulkarni, T.T. Vo, X. Liu, S.J. Diede, M. Burcu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric cancers cause substantial childhood mortality and evidence gaps remain for rare tumors. Real-world data (RWD) can complement trials by providing long-term follow-up, broader populations, and external controls. We reviewed global pediatric cancer RWD to characterize sources and key data elements.Materials and methodsPubMed was searched (February 2015-February 2025) for studies referencing cancer databases or initiatives collecting RWD on patients aged 0-25 years. After screening 1666 records, 225 articles yielded 134 unique data sources. We extracted database type, geography, population, tumor/treatment coverage, and availability of demographic, clinical, and outcome variables.ResultsOf 134 sources, 34.1% were pediatric-only. Most were in Europe (47.0%) or the Americas (18.7%). About 75% were population-based registries and ∼10% genomic/biobank resources. Age and sex were documented in >60% of sources; tumor site, histology, and stage in >50%; tumor grade in ∼30%; treatment modalities in 36%-40%; and survival in 61.2%. Race, medical history, and clinical outcomes (progression and response) were infrequently reported.ConclusionsPediatric oncology RWD are numerous but concentrated in high-income regions and vary in completeness. Core demographics and major tumor features are commonly recorded yet sociodemographic detail and many clinical outcomes are often missing. Improving data completeness, quality assessments, and representation from low- and middle-income regions will strengthen RWD utility for research and regulatory use.

Introduction

Pediatric cancers are a leading cause of death in children, with an estimated 400 000 new cases worldwide annually, among those aged 0-19 years.1 Despite overall survival approaching 80%, outcomes remain poor for many rare tumors with poorly understood natural history.1,2 Substantial unmet need persists, partly because pediatric patients were historically excluded from clinical trials and treated using evidence extrapolated from adults. To address this, several regulatory initiatives aim to advance pediatric drug development.3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Randomized clinical trials are the gold standard for drug approval but they often have limited follow-up, restricted generalizability, and small samples. These issues are amplified in pediatric oncology, in which the rarity of childhood cancers limits patient availability, slows study start-up, and raises costs.8 Regulatory agencies are increasingly encouraging the use of real-world data (RWD), including registries, electronic health records, and claims, to fill in these clinical gaps and complement trial findings.8,9 RWD can provide external controls for single-arm pediatric trials, capture diverse and rare outcomes, offer long-term follow-up, and supply information not available in trials.10, 11, 12

Existing assessments of pediatric cancer RWD are limited—prior studies neither provided age or cancer specification nor adequately described the databases.13, 14, 15, 16 We conducted a targeted literature review to identify and characterize real-world pediatric cancer data sources by database type, geography, population, tumor, treatment, and clinical characteristics.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and study selection

A literature search was conducted in PubMed using predefined search terms (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100766) for pediatric cancer studies published between February 2015 and February 2025. This 10-year time frame captures more contemporary data yet is long enough to allow for longitudinal outcomes assessment. We included prospective/retrospective studies, reviews, guidelines, and meta-analyses.

We identified and included studies that utilized or mentioned a cancer database or cancer initiative/organization that collects data from pediatric patients, broadly defined as individuals aged 0-25 years, who were diagnosed with any type of cancer in the real-world or routine clinical practice settings. Clinical trials, nonhuman studies, and studies published in languages other than English were excluded.

Data collection

We conducted a two-stage review. Firstly, we screened articles to identify pediatric cancer RWD sources meeting our eligibility criteria. Secondly, we carried out a targeted search for each data source to verify availability of key data elements: data source type, geographic coverage, demographics, clinical and tumor characteristics, treatment details, and survival data. We distinguished the type of data source based on the setting, purpose, and scope of information captured. These categories included population-based cancer registries, hospital-based cancer registries, genomic registries/biobanks, radiation-specific registries, or patient-reported clinical databases. In brief, usually, population-based registries capture incident cancer cases within defined geographic areas and are potentially well suited for surveillance and population-level outcomes research. Hospital-based registries collect detailed clinical data on patients diagnosed or treated within specific institution(s). Genomic registries and biobanks integrate clinical information with biospecimens and molecular/biomarker data to support biomarker and precision oncology research. Radiation-specific registries focus on patients receiving radiotherapy and patient-reported clinical databases capture outcomes reported directly by patients, such as symptoms, quality of life, and/or treatment burden.

We extracted predefined data elements from each source’s case report or data collection forms. If these were not publicly available, we reviewed full-text articles using the source to identify the elements. Literature materials were managed using EndNote 2117 (Clarivate Plc, Philadephia, PA) for title/abstract screening and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) for data analysis and visualization.

Results

A total of 134 unique cancer data sources were identified. Of these, 46 (34.1%) collected data on pediatric populations only, whereas 89 (65.9%) collected data on both pediatric and adult populations. Geographical coverage varied, with most covering Europe (n = 63, 47.0%), followed by the Americas (n = 25, 18.7%), multiregional (n = 15, 11.2%), Western Pacific (n = 13, 9.7%), South-east Asia (n = 7, 5.2%), Eastern Mediterranean (n = 6, 4.5%), and Africa (n = 5, 3.7%), based on World Health Organization Regional Classification. Although 34 (25.4%) data sources did not specify the type of cancer, 6 (4.5%) covered only hematological cancers, 23 (17.2%) covered only solid tumors, and 69 (51.5%) covered both. Supplementary Figure S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100766) provides granular geographic distribution by countries within each region.

Table 1 describes data source characteristics in detail. Patient demographics such as sex and age were the most widely captured data elements (>60%). Race and medical history were seldom documented (∼20% or less). Data on tumor characteristics varied, with tumor morphology/histology, site/topography, and stage documented in >50% of the data sources but grading was captured in ∼30%. About 36%-40% of the data sources reported data on therapeutic characteristics such as radiotherapy, surgery, and pharmacotherapy, whereas survival/mortality data were captured by 82 (61.2%) of the data sources. Tissue samples were collected by 27 data sources (20.1%). Discussion

This targeted review identified and profiled global RWD sources relevant for pediatric cancer research and found most spanned Europe and North America. Although many had national coverage, over one in four captured more granular information at the regional level, indicating global interest in advancing pediatric cancer care. Nine were a part of the National Cancer Institute Childhood Cancer Data Initiative, which aims to enhance data sharing across pediatric cancer subspecialties.18 Multiple cancer types were covered and 75% were population-based cancer registries, providing close-to-comprehensive data capture. Genomic registries or biobanks, capturing molecular and genomic biomarkers, comprised ∼10% of data sources. These RWD resources advance precision medicine, illuminate population-level health care disparities, and inform clinical trial enrollment. Observational studies using RWD can also potentially serve as external (historical) controls to contextualize single-arm trial results of rare pediatric cancer populations and a target trial emulation framework can be used to address some of the methodological challenges and biases inherent in these nonrandomized studies of clinical interventions in pediatric populations.19

The data sources varied in the information captured. Few documented race, medical history, and tumor grade, underscoring gaps in sociodemographic and clinical detail. In contrast, over half of tumor features, such as site/topography, histology/morphology, and stage, were documented, highlighting the value of RWD in cancer research. Cancer treatment was captured by more than one-third of the data sources, providing valuable information on radiotherapy, surgery, and pharmacotherapy. Additionally, survival or mortality statistics, being important indicators of treatment effectiveness and safety in oncological research, were recorded by more than half of the data sources. Clinical outcomes such as cancer progression and treatment response are less accessible in RWD, due to inconsistencies in their measurement and reporting. Most data sources identified were from Europe and North America. As most pediatric cancer cases and deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries,20 data sources from other regions are also urgently warranted.

This was a targeted literature review of global real-world pediatric cancer data sources with detailed data source information to allow investigators to determine which source is most suitable for their research. This literature search in PubMed identified existing RWD on pediatric cancers and captured tumor and treatment data that were not captured in previous literature.13, 14, 15, 16

These pediatric cancer RWD sources may also have important utility for a range of applied research and regulatory use cases. In particular, databases with longitudinal follow-up and treatment information may be valuable for external control arms, especially in rare pediatric malignancies where randomized trials are challenging to conduct or underpowered.11,12 Similarly, the availability of survival and mortality data in many sources supports longitudinal outcomes research, including evaluation of treatment patterns, disease trajectory, and longer-term effectiveness in routine practice. More broadly, RWD may help facilitate and accelerate the design and conduct of more diverse pediatric clinical trials by improving our understanding of patient characteristics across multiple dimensions, including demographic, clinical, treatment environment and region, and other social determinants of health.10 As data ecosystems continue to evolve, federated platforms may further expand the potential of pediatric oncology RWD by enabling multi-institutional analyses while preserving local governance and privacy.21 However, realizing this potential will require continued attention to data completeness, comparability, and validity, along with improved standardization across data sources.

The study was limited to articles published in PubMed only, which may result in missing RWD sources. However, we expect the most relevant literature on pediatric cancer data sources to be indexed in PubMed. The availability of data elements is strictly based on documented evidence in the data source, i.e. the presence of an element confirms that it was captured, but its absence does not. Although we captured information on measures of performance status, tumor progression, relapse, and remission, we did not report these statistics as they were not collected systematically. The risk of bias or study quality was not assessed as the review was only intended to identify and characterize potential cancer data sources. Future research should prioritize the evaluation of completeness, comparability, and validity of the data reported in pediatric cancer databases.

In conclusion, this review provides a global overview of pediatric cancer RWD sources and demonstrates that these resources capture important information on patient, tumor, treatment, and survival characteristics. However, variability in data completeness and limited capture of sociodemographic variables remain important challenges. Pediatric cancer research requires increased funding, collaboration, and integration of genomic data. RWD sources could be prioritized for this purpose as they offer valuable insights into important patient, tumor, and clinical characteristics.
