Novel biomarkers for sarcopenia: a narrative review
Authors: Xiaoxiao Chen, Fei Wu, Suqin Shi, Le Xia, Yurong Wang, Yu Wei, Jing Wang, Jiaqi Li, Min Zhu, Xiaojie Zhang, Jinqiang Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia is an age-related syndrome characterized by the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function. It is associated with an increased risk of falls, disability, and mortality, as well as a significant healthcare burden. Traditional assessment of sarcopenia relies on imaging techniques and physical function tests, which have important limitations, including operational complexity, difficulty in early identification, and inability to reflect underlying molecular mechanisms. The emergence of candidate biomarkers—including muscle-specific factors, inflammation-related proteins, non-coding RNAs, and nutritional metabolites—has enabled a more precise elucidation of the pathophysiological mechanisms of the disease across multiple dimensions, such as protein homeostasis, chronic inflammation, post-transcriptional regulation, and energy metabolism. These advances provide new avenues for early identification, risk stratification, identification of disease subtypes, and the development of personalized intervention strategies. This article reviews the potential applications and current challenges of these candidate biomarkers in both clinical practice and research on sarcopenia.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is an age-related skeletal muscle disorder characterized by the progressive loss of muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance [1, 2]. Global prevalence estimates vary substantially according to the diagnostic framework used, but a recent systematic review and meta-analysis suggested that prevalence in older adults commonly falls within an approximate range of 10–27%, highlighting the importance of standardized case definitions [3]. As highlighted in a seminal overview by Narici and Maffulli, sarcopenia reflects not only a quantitative loss of muscle but also broader deterioration in muscle quality, regenerative potential, and functional capacity across ageing populations [4]. In China, the prevalence of sarcopenia among middle-aged and older adults has been reported to be approximately 11–29% in community settings and 23–30% in hospitalized populations, with an overall prevalence of about 14% and a generally higher risk in men [5]. In orthopaedic settings, sarcopenia and postoperative muscle loss have also been linked to poorer functional recovery after geriatric hip-fracture surgery, underlining the clinical relevance of timely recognition in musculoskeletal practice [6]. The clinical relevance of sarcopenia is substantial: meta-analytic evidence indicates an approximately 1.60-fold higher risk of falls and a 1.84-fold higher risk of fractures, while older adults with sarcopenia may also face roughly twofold higher risks of disability and all-cause mortality [7–9].

The pathogenesis of sarcopenia is complex and involves chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired anabolic signaling, protein homeostasis imbalance, metabolic disturbances, and reduced regenerative capacity [4, 10–18]. For instance, inhibition of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway and overactivation of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) contribute to protein degradation exceeding synthesis; sustained activation of NF-kappaB-related pathways promotes chronic inflammation and muscle wasting; and dysregulated energy metabolism impairs muscle-cell survival and function [14, 16–18]. In addition, reduced regenerative efficiency, including impaired satellite-cell function and altered neuromuscular signaling, may accelerate the decline in muscle quantity and quality [4, 11, 19]. Current diagnosis still relies mainly on muscle mass, grip strength, and physical performance assessments. However, these approaches often require specialized equipment, may miss early subclinical changes, and do not directly reflect underlying molecular or neuromuscular mechanisms [1, 2, 20, 21]. Accordingly, growing attention has turned to blood- and tissue-based biomarkers that may complement conventional assessments. This review summarizes candidate biomarkers across major biological domains and discusses both their mechanistic relevance and the practical barriers that currently limit their translation into routine sarcopenia management [19, 22, 23].

In recent years, research on sarcopenia biomarkers has expanded from conventional assessments of muscle quantity toward molecular and systems-level indicators. For clarity, the current review organizes candidate biomarkers into four broad domains: muscle-related biomarkers, inflammation-related proteins, non-coding RNAs, and nutrition-metabolism markers (Fig. 1). Neural and neuromuscular biomarkers are discussed within the broader muscle-related category because they reflect neuromuscular junction integrity and muscle function, which are closely linked to the pathophysiology of sarcopenia [22, 23].

Muscle-related biomarkers

Myostatin

Myostatin, also known as Growth Differentiation Factor 8 (GDF-8), belongs to the Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-beta) superfamily. As a negative regulator, it primarily inhibits skeletal muscle regeneration. Under critical illness conditions, systemic inflammation, prolonged immobilization, glucocorticoid use, and oxidative stress can upregulate myostatin activity, thereby contributing to muscle wasting through reduced protein synthesis and enhanced catabolic signaling [22, 24].

Human studies indicate that circulating myostatin is associated with muscle mass in a sex-specific manner, and serum myostatin has also been explored as a frailty-related biomarker in community-dwelling older adults [25, 26]. At the same time, preclinical work suggests that myostatin inhibition can increase muscle size while not necessarily improving force generation, highlighting the need to distinguish hypertrophy from functional benefit [27]. Taken together, myostatin remains a biologically plausible candidate biomarker and therapeutic target, but its clinical utility depends on context, assay standardization, and the balance between muscle quantity and muscle function [22, 25–27].

Insulin-like Growth Factor-1 (IGF-1)

Insulin-like Growth Factor-1 (IGF-1) is a key mediator in the growth hormone signaling pathway and plays a central role in maintaining skeletal muscle mass and function. It is synthesized primarily in the liver but is also expressed locally in tissues such as muscle and bone [14, 15]. Age-related decline in the growth hormone/IGF-1 axis may contribute to sarcopenia through reduced protein synthesis, impaired satellite-cell activity, and greater susceptibility to catabolic stimuli [14, 15]. In community-dwelling older adults, lower IGF-1 has been reported in frail women with concomitant low relative appendicular skeletal muscle mass compared with frail women without that phenotype [26]. Acute exercise and resistance training may increase circulating IGF-1 in older adults, but current evidence does not establish that modulation of this pathway definitively prevents sarcopenia onset [28, 29]. Thus, IGF-1 is best regarded as a mechanistically relevant biomarker candidate and a potentially intervention-responsive pathway, rather than a stand-alone diagnostic marker [14, 15, 26, 29].

Irisin

Irisin is a myokine secreted by skeletal muscle in response to exercise and has been linked to energy metabolism and muscle homeostasis [30, 31]. Experimental studies suggest that irisin may support protein synthesis and attenuate proteolysis, whereas observational studies in humans have produced mixed results [31, 32]. Some community-based data showed lower circulating irisin concentrations in sarcopenic individuals, but diagnostic performance was modest in women (AUC 0.68), and other studies found no significant association with muscle mass or function [33, 34]. Accordingly, irisin should be interpreted as a promising but still inconsistent biomarker candidate whose clinical value will depend on better assay standardization and more reproducible human data [30–34].

MuRF1 and Atrogin-1

In addition to circulating protein markers, key regulatory factors within muscle tissue provide mechanistic insight into muscle atrophy. A central pathway is the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), in which MuRF1 and Atrogin-1/MAFbx are two major E3 ubiquitin ligases [16, 35, 36]. MuRF1 promotes degradation of myofibrillar proteins, whereas Atrogin-1 targets pathways involved in protein synthesis and muscle repair [37, 38]. In rodent denervation and other experimental atrophy models, both markers are markedly upregulated and are tightly linked to Akt/FoxO signaling [17, 35, 38, 39]. These observations support their mechanistic importance, but most direct evidence still comes from preclinical or muscle-tissue studies rather than routine clinical blood testing in humans [17, 36–39].

Other muscle-specific markers

Additional muscle-related candidates include N-terminal propeptide of type III procollagen (P3NP), which reflects collagen synthesis and muscle remodeling, and 3-methylhistidine (3MH), which reflects myofibrillar protein breakdown [40, 41]. P3NP has been associated with muscle mass, strength, and physical-function measures in older adults, and extracellular-vesicle P3NP has also been linked to sarcopenia-related performance phenotypes [40–42]. Plasma 3MH, particularly when indexed to creatinine or kidney function, has been associated with frailty and high muscle catabolism, although dietary confounding remains an important limitation [41]. These markers may complement more established pathways, but none currently provides a sufficiently specific and standardized stand-alone measure of sarcopenia [40–42].

The preceding discussion highlights the relevance of muscle-specific factors such as GDF-8, IGF-1, MuRF1, Atrogin-1, P3NP, and 3MH to anabolic-catabolic balance and tissue remodeling. However, sarcopenia is not driven by a single pathway. As shown in Fig. 2, protein metabolic imbalance interacts with chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuromuscular decline, and much of the detailed mechanistic evidence for these interactions still derives from preclinical work rather than direct human validation [11, 17, 18].

Neural and neuromuscular biomarkers

In addition to muscle-intrinsic factors, growing evidence indicates that age-related muscle decline is also influenced by deterioration of the neuromuscular axis. Therefore, biomarkers reflecting neuromuscular junction (NMJ) stability, axonal integrity, and neuroplasticity may be considered part of the broader muscle-related biomarker landscape in sarcopenia. This perspective is particularly relevant because motor-unit remodeling, partial denervation, and impaired reinnervation are increasingly recognized as contributors to reduced strength and impaired muscle regeneration in older adults [19].

C-terminal agrin fragment (CAF) is a circulating marker of NMJ instability and one of the most frequently discussed neuromuscular candidates. In the GenoFit studies, plasma CAF was associated with appendicular lean mass and, in later work, with both grip strength and sarcopenia-related phenotypes across adulthood, with acceptable diagnostic discrimination in adults aged 50 years and older [43, 44]. Neurofilament light chain (NfL), a marker of axonal injury, has also been associated with lower muscle mass and strength, and higher concentrations have been reported in severe sarcopenia [45, 46]. These findings support the concept that neural injury signatures may track clinically meaningful aspects of skeletal-muscle decline [43–46].

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a regulator of neuroplasticity, represents a third candidate. Recent community-based data reported higher plasma BDNF concentrations in adults with sarcopenia, potentially reflecting compensatory neuromuscular remodeling rather than a straightforward protective signal [47]. Taken together, CAF, NfL, and BDNF extend biomarker discussion beyond muscle tissue itself and emphasize the integrated brain-muscle axis. Nevertheless, the current evidence base remains relatively small, cut-offs are not standardized, and confounding by physical activity, comorbidity, and broader neurological status remains a major challenge [19, 43–47].

Inflammation-related protein biomarkers

Interleukin-6 (IL-6)

Among protein markers, elevated serum levels of inflammatory cytokines such as CRP, IL-6, and TNF-alpha indicate a systemic inflammatory state that is frequently linked to sarcopenia. IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine with roles in both inflammation and muscle metabolism; persistent elevation in ageing and chronic disease has been associated with adverse muscle outcomes [18, 48]. Mechanistically, IL-6-related signaling may amplify catabolic pathways through STAT3- and NF-kappaB-related mechanisms, and longitudinal human data have linked rising IL-6 to declining muscle mass and physical function [49, 50].

Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-alpha)

Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) can promote catabolic signaling through NF-kappaB-related pathways and may increase the expression of atrophy-associated ubiquitin ligases such as MuRF1 and MAFbx [18, 49]. Human data, however, are heterogeneous. Earlier observational work linked higher TNF-alpha to lower muscle mass and strength, whereas one systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant difference between sarcopenic and control groups; a more recent umbrella review suggested that higher TNF-alpha may still characterize at least some sarcopenic phenotypes [23, 51, 52]. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in sarcopenia definitions, comorbidity burden, assay platforms, and adjustment for confounding inflammatory conditions [23, 52].

C-reactive protein (CRP)

C-reactive protein (CRP) is an acute-phase reactant that is commonly used as a broad indicator of systemic inflammation. Elevated CRP has been associated with declining muscle strength, and meta-analytic data suggest that CRP concentrations are, on average, higher in sarcopenia than in control populations [52, 53]. Shared inflammatory patterns across frailty and sarcopenia further support the concept that CRP may help characterize a chronic low-grade inflammatory phenotype, although it remains nonspecific [54].

Other inflammatory markers

Other inflammatory candidates include IL-1beta, IL-15, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). IL-1beta may amplify catabolic signaling together with IL-6 and TNF-alpha, although much of the mechanistic evidence remains preclinical [55]. By contrast, lower circulating IL-15 and higher NLR have each been associated with sarcopenia-related phenotypes in specific clinical settings [56, 57]. These markers may provide complementary information about inflammatory status, but their interpretation is highly context dependent [55–57].

Non-coding RNA biomarkers

In recent years, non-coding RNAs have attracted increasing interest in muscle ageing research. MicroRNAs, circular RNAs, and long non-coding RNAs regulate gene expression at post-transcriptional and transcriptional levels and are involved in pathways related to protein turnover, inflammation, fibrosis, and regeneration [58–61]. At present, however, the clinical use of RNA biomarkers in sarcopenia remains exploratory, and most evidence is preclinical or derived from relatively small human cohorts [60, 61].

MicroRNA biomarkers

MicroRNAs are important post-transcriptional regulators that influence pathways involved in muscle protein metabolism, inflammation, and regeneration. In blood, some muscle-enriched microRNAs such as miR-1, miR-133a, and miR-206 may reflect muscle injury or remodeling. Exosomal microRNAs may offer additional stability, but their translational role remains investigational [60]. Mechanistic studies suggest that selected microRNAs may influence muscle regeneration pathways, and circulating miR-144-3p has recently been associated with low muscle strength in older adults [62, 63]. Overall, microRNAs should be regarded as promising exploratory biomarkers rather than validated clinical tools for diagnosis or treatment monitoring [60, 62, 63].

Circular RNA

Circular RNAs are covalently closed RNA molecules with relatively high stability in body fluids, making them attractive exploratory biomarker candidates [64]. Recent studies have linked specific circRNAs to muscle atrophy, adipogenesis, and translational control. These findings broaden the mechanistic landscape of muscle ageing, but most data remain experimental or disease-specific rather than validated across general sarcopenic populations [65–67].

Long non-coding RNA

Long non-coding RNAs also participate in pathways relevant to muscle atrophy, including ribosome biogenesis, protein translation, inflammation, and fibrosis [61, 68]. Several lncRNAs detectable in blood have shown altered expression patterns in sarcopenic cohorts, suggesting potential for future biomarker development. Nonetheless, these signals currently require much broader human validation before they can be considered clinically actionable [69–72].

Nutrition-related biomarkers

Amino acids

In nutrition-metabolism studies of sarcopenia, altered amino-acid profiles, particularly branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs; leucine, isoleucine, and valine), have attracted considerable attention [73–76]. BCAAs are biologically relevant because they are linked to muscle protein synthesis and whole-body energy metabolism; however, their interpretation as biomarkers is complicated by diet, adiposity, insulin resistance, physical activity, and sex differences [75–80]. Accordingly, BCAAs are better viewed as context-dependent metabolism-related indicators rather than stand-alone biomarkers of sarcopenia [75, 76, 78, 80].

Creatine

Creatine is a nitrogen-containing organic compound synthesized mainly in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas and obtained exogenously from foods such as meat and fish [81, 82]. In the body, creatine is stored predominantly in skeletal muscle as phosphocreatine and contributes to rapid ATP buffering through the creatine kinase system [81, 82]. Preclinical and physiological studies suggest that creatine may influence cellular energetics and, in some contexts, mitochondrial resilience, but much of this broader mechanistic literature is not yet specific to human sarcopenia [82, 83]. Creatine-related measures have also been explored as muscle-mass indicators, such as in D3-creatine dilution approaches [84].

As a nutritional supplementation strategy, creatine is often discussed alongside resistance exercise and adequate protein intake. Some intervention studies suggest benefits for muscle mass and selected functional outcomes, whereas others show little or no additional effect beyond exercise alone, indicating meaningful inter-individual variability in response [85–88]. The practical implications of responder versus non-responder phenotypes remain insufficiently defined in sarcopenia, and evidence in immobilization or critical-illness settings is still inconsistent [85–87]. Therefore, creatine should currently be interpreted as a potentially useful adjunct in selected settings, rather than as a validated sarcopenia biomarker in its own right [83, 85, 87, 88].

Beyond creatine, other metabolism-related circulating markers may provide complementary information about anabolic resistance and nutritional status. Leptin and adiponectin are linked to muscle-liver-adipose crosstalk, while low vitamin D status has repeatedly been associated with poorer muscle mass, strength, and physical performance [89–92]. These markers may help characterize metabolic context, but like other biomarker classes they require standardized interpretation and careful adjustment for confounding factors [89–92].

Clinical translation, current challenges, and future directions

Sarcopenia is a biologically heterogeneous syndrome involving interacting alterations in muscle remodeling, inflammation, neuromuscular integrity, RNA regulation, and systemic metabolism. Accordingly, candidate biomarkers for sarcopenia now encompass muscle-related indicators, inflammation-related proteins, non-coding RNAs, and nutrition–metabolism markers. Within the muscle-related domain, emerging neural and neuromuscular biomarkers such as CAF, NfL, and BDNF further broaden current understanding of muscle function decline and neuromuscular integrity in sarcopenia. Collectively, these markers have broadened current understanding of the mechanisms underlying sarcopenia and may provide complementary information beyond conventional assessments of muscle mass, strength, and physical performance [23, 60, 61].

Despite this progress, several barriers continue to limit the clinical translation of biomarker-based approaches for sarcopenia. Considerable heterogeneity remains across diagnostic criteria, study populations, specimen types, and assay platforms, reducing cross-study comparability and hindering the establishment of standardized cut-off values [1, 2, 23]. In addition, many biomarkers are influenced by age, sex, diet, physical activity, inflammation, and comorbidities, which complicates interpretation, particularly in older adults with complex clinical backgrounds [15, 23]. Some candidate markers also rely on costly or technically demanding assays, and a few require invasive sampling, further limiting their feasibility in routine clinical and public health settings [60, 61]. At present, therefore, no single biomarker appears sufficient to independently support screening, diagnosis, or prognostic stratification in sarcopenia.

Future research should place greater emphasis on standardization, external validation, and clearly defined clinical use-cases. Large prospective studies are needed to determine robust reference ranges, harmonized thresholds, and reproducible assay strategies across diverse populations. Rather than relying on single markers, future approaches may benefit more from multimarker panels integrating biologically distinct domains, such as muscle-specific, inflammatory, metabolic, and neuromuscular indicators, together with established functional assessments and imaging-based measures [23, 47, 75]. In conclusion, biomarkers for sarcopenia should currently be regarded as promising adjuncts rather than stand-alone diagnostic tools. With improved assay harmonization, stronger human validation, and context-specific integration into clinical workflows, they may contribute to more precise and biologically informed management of sarcopenia in the future.
