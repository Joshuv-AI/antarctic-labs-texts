Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Regulation of SYNE1 Mediates Structural and Functional Recovery after Traumatic Brain Injury
Authors: Shun Li, Na Qiu, Chao Zhou, Andrew Ni, Ping Sun, Jia‐Jun Liu, Xin‐Lei Huang, Tian‐Qing Xiong, Yu‐Chi Zhang, Hai‐Zhou Liu, Xiao‐Tao Xu, Rui Wang, Shi‐Qing Zhang, Yu‐Xin Zhang, Silvia Liu, C Edward Dixon, Jun Chen, Ke‐Jie Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTraumatic brain injury (TBI) disrupts the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and compromises endothelial function, leading to persistent neurological dysfunction. Although miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion has recently been shown to be neuroprotective in brain injury, the endothelial pathways mediating long‐term structural and functional recovery after TBI remain unknown. Here, we demonstrate endothelial cell‐specific deletion of miR‐15a/16‐1 is sufficient to confer neurorestoration after TBI by reducing acute BBB leakage, preserving white and gray matter integrity, and accelerating sensorimotor and cognitive recovery. Mechanistically, transcriptomic profiling identified SYNE1 as a direct downstream target of miR‐15a/16‐1, validated via in‐silico binding prediction and 3’‐UTR luciferase assays. Crucially, endothelial cell‐targeted AAV‐mediated knockdown of SYNE1 abrogated the neurorestorative effects of miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion, indicating SYNE1 is required for its function. Furthermore, single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis of human TBI tissue demonstrates significant downregulation of SYNE1 in endothelial cells within the contusion core relative to pericontusional endothelium. Together, these findings define a previously unrecognized miR‐15a/16‐1–SYNE1 axis that promotes endothelial repair after TBI, with significant translational potential.Keywords: endothelial signaling, miR‐15a/16‐1, neurovascular recovery, SYNE1, TBI, white and gray matter integrity

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of long‐term neurological disability and remains a serious public health concern worldwide [1, 2]. Beyond the immediate mechanical injury, TBI also triggers a cascade of secondary processes including neurovascular disruption, neuroinflammation, and maladaptive remodeling which can persist for weeks or even years [3, 4]. A central consequence of this process is the breakdown of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and long‐term endothelial dysfunction, leading to white matter and neuronal loss [5, 6]. Yet, despite decades of research, effective pharmacological therapies capable of restoring BBB integrity and promoting long‐term functional recovery remain elusive.

Increasing evidence suggests the brain endothelium is not just a passive barrier but an active orchestrator of neural repair after TBI [7, 8, 9]. Endothelial cells within the neurovascular unit are known to integrate mechanical and chemical cues to regulate angiogenesis, inflammation, and metabolic support [10, 11, 12], while also functioning as signaling hubs to coordinate neurovascular responses to brain injury [13]. Thus, identifying the molecular switches that govern endothelial dysfunction during the chronic phase of TBI is critical for developing restorative therapies.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are potent post‐transcriptional regulators of endothelial function and vascular homeostasis [14, 15, 16]. Among them, the miR‐15a/16‐1 cluster has emerged as a key regulator of cerebrovascular integrity [17]. Our previous work demonstrated that global genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of miR‐15a/16‐1 protects the brain across multiple disease settings, including ischemic stroke, vascular dementia, and TBI, by attenuating neuroinflammation, BBB disruption, neuronal loss, white matter degeneration, and promoting cerebral angiogenesis [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. While these findings have established miR‐15a/16‐1 as a crucial regulator of the brain response to ischemic or traumatic insults, whether its therapeutic efficacy stems from an endothelial‐intrinsic mechanism and its downstream molecular effectors remain unknown.

Given the important role of endothelial cells in maintaining BBB integrity and coordinating vascular and neural interactions [25, 26], we hypothesized endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 signaling directly governs vascular stability and long‐term neurological recovery after TBI by modulating blood–brain barrier integrity and endothelial dysfunction. To test this hypothesis, we generated mice with endothelial cell‐specific deletion of miR‐15a/16‐1 (EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO) and assessed neurobehavioral outcomes, white and gray matter preservation, and BBB integrity following controlled cortical impact (CCI). Transcriptomic profiling of brain endothelial cells identified SYNE1, a nuclear‐cytoskeletal linker protein, as a direct downstream target of miR‐15a/16‐1. AAV‐mediated endothelial specific knockdown of SYNE1 abolished the neurorestorative effects of endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion, demonstrating SYNE1 is a key mediator of this pathway. Finally, by analyzing single‐cell RNA sequencing data from human TBI patients, we show that SYNE1 is downregulated in contusion‐core endothelial cells compared with pericontusional endothelium, highlighting the translational potential of targeting the miR‐15a/16‐1–SYNE1 axis to promote structural and functional repair after TBI.

Results

Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Deletion Improves Long‐Term Functional Recovery after TBI

To isolate the contribution of the endothelium to TBI outcomes, we subjected EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO and wild‐type (WT) mice to CCI, assessing sensorimotor and cognitive function over 35 days (Figure S1a). While TBI elicited robust sensorimotor deficits in both WT and EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice compared with sham controls, as indicated by reduced rota‐rod latency (Figure 1a), delayed adhesive removal (Figure 1b,c), and increased forepaw and hindpaw foot‐fault rates (Figure 1d–f), endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deficiency markedly accelerated functional recovery. Throughout the 35‐day observation period, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice showed improved sensorimotor outcomes, evidenced by longer latencies to fall, faster adhesive removal, and reduced foot‐fault rates compared to WT controls (Figure 1a–f). Baseline performance and body weight were indistinguishable between genotypes under sham conditions (Figure S1b).

To further assess cognitive performance, we conducted the Morris water maze (MWM) (Figure 1g), passive avoidance (Figure 1k), and Y‐maze spontaneous alternation tests (Figure 1m). Under sham conditions, baseline cognitive performance was indistinguishable between genotypes (Figure 1g–o). As expected, TBI elicited profound deficits across all cognitive domains in both genotypes relative to sham controls (Figure 1g–o). However, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice exhibited significantly shorter escape latencies and spent more time in the target quadrant of the MWM, without noticeable changes in swimming speed, indicating enhanced spatial learning and memory retention (Figure 1h–j). Similarly, in the passive avoidance test (Figure 1k), EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice showed significantly longer latencies to enter the dark compartment after TBI, which reflects preserved aversive memory (Figure 1l). Working memory was also preserved in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice, which exhibited higher spontaneous alternation rates in the Y‐maze (Figure 1n) despite comparable exploratory activity (Figure 1o). Collectively, these results demonstrate that endothelial cell‐specific miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion promotes long‐term sensorimotor and cognitive recovery following TBI.

Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Deletion Preserves Myelin Integrity and Attenuates Demyelination after TBI

Given the critical role of white matter (WM) for coordinated sensorimotor and cognitive function, we investigated whether endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion prevents demyelination after TBI. We assessed WM integrity using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) 35 days after injury to map fractional anisotropy (FA), a proxy of white‐matter integrity reflecting axonal organization and myelination (Figure 2a). Representative axial views of FA and direction‐encoded color (DEC) maps are shown in Figure 2a, with blue and red squares indicating enlarged regions of the corpus callosum (CC) and external capsule (EC), respectively. DTI quantitative analyses showed that TBI significantly reduced FA values in both the CC and EC in WT mice, indicating widespread WM injury. Notably, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice exhibited significantly preserved FA values in both the CC and EC compared with WT controls after TBI (Figure 2b,c). These findings indicate that endothelial cell‐specific deletion of miR‐15a/16‐1 broadly protects white‐matter integrity across major fiber tracts following TBI.

To define the ultrastructural basis of this preservation, we examined the EC tissue 35 days post‐injury using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Representative TEM images illustrate the ultrastructural features of myelinated axons in the external capsule across experimental groups (Figure 2d). Under sham conditions, myelin morphology was intact in both genotypes (Figure 2d). However, following TBI, WT mice exhibited severe pathology characterized by pronounced myelin loss, axonal degeneration, and accumulation of myelin debris (Figure 2d). In contrast, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice largely retained myelin ultrastructure, displaying compact myelin lamellae and fewer degenerating axons. Quantitative analyses further confirmed a significant reduction in dystrophic axons, normalization of g‐ratios, and increased myelin sheath thickness in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice compared with WT controls after TBI (Figure 2e–g).

To validate these findings, we performed Luxol fast blue (LFB) histological staining and double immunofluorescence for myelin basic protein (MBP, a myelin marker) and neurofilament heavy polypeptide (SMI32, an injured‐axon marker) in the cortex, EC, and striatum (STR) 35 days after TBI (Figure 2h,i). WT mice exhibited a marked loss of myelin in the cortex, EC, and STR after TBI, whereas endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion significantly ameliorated demyelination (Figure 2h), resulting in higher relative optical‐density (OD) values of LFB staining across all regions examined (Figure 2j–l). Immunofluorescence analyses revealed substantial myelin loss accompanied by axonal damage in WT mice, as indicated by decreased MBP and increased SMI32 expression (Figure 2m). In contrast, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice preserved MBP expression and showed reduced SMI32 in the CTX, EC, and STR (Figure 2m). Quantitative analyses showed that the TBI‐induced elevation of the SMI32/MBP ratio was significantly attenuated in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice compared with WT controls (Figure 2n–p).

Because efficient axonal conduction depends on nodal and paranodal integrity, we next examined the node of Ranvier (NOR) using co‐immunostaining for Caspr (a paranodal marker) and Nav1.6 (a nodal sodium channel) (Figure 2q,r). TBI severely disrupted nodal architecture, resulting in a significant reduction in NOR number and shortened paranodal length, without detectable changes in paranodal gap width (Figure 2s–u). However, endothelial cell‐specific miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion partially restored nodal organization, significantly increasing both NOR number and paranodal length compared with WT mice after TBI (Figure 2s–u), thereby preserving the structural substrates required for efficient saltatory conduction.

Finally, to determine whether WM integrity was linked to cognitive performance, we correlated WM structural metrics with neurobehavioral outcomes. FA values in both the CC and EC were positively correlated with time spent in the target quadrant during the MWM test (Figure S2a,b). Similarly, relative OD values of LFB staining correlated positively with cognitive performance (Figure S2c–e), whereas the SMI32/MBP ratio was negatively correlated with behavioral performance across the CTX, EC, and STR (Figure S2f–h). Analysis of nodal organization further supported these associations: the number of NOR in the EC exhibited a significant positive correlation with MWM performance (Figure S2i), and paranodal length likewise showed a significant positive correlation (Figure S2j). Together, these data indicate that endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion preserves WM structure and nodal integrity, which is associated with improved axonal connectivity and functional recovery after TBI.

Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Deletion Ameliorates Long‐Term Gray Matter Loss after TBI

Considering the beneficial effects of endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion on WM architecture, we next examined whether this deletion also reduces overall brain tissue and neuronal loss following TBI. Volumetric analysis of MAP2 immunoreactivity across serial coronal sections (Bregma +0.98 to ‐1.58 mm) at 35 days post‐injury revealed extensive brain tissue loss in WT mice (Figure 3a,b). In contrast, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion significantly reduced lesion volume, indicating substantial preservation of brain tissue (Figure 3a,b).

To further assess neuronal preservation, we performed Cresyl Violet (CV) staining in pericontusional regions, including the CTX and hippocampal CA1 and CA3 subfields (Figure 3c,d). WT mice displayed pronounced neuronal loss in the CTX and CA1 following TBI, reflected by a substantial reduction in CV‐positive area (Figure 3c–g). However, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice exhibited significantly larger CV‐positive areas in the CTX and CA1 compared with WT controls, indicating preserved neuronal density.

We further validated these findings using NeuN immunolabeling. In WT mice, a significant reduction in NeuN‐positive area was observed in cortical layers II–VI and in the hippocampal CA1 region after TBI. Conversely, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice showed a significantly greater proportion of NeuN‐positive area in both regions, further confirming enhanced neuronal preservation following trauma (Figure 3h–k). Notably, the CA3 subregion remained largely intact across all experimental groups after injury. These results demonstrate that endothelial cell‐specific deletion of miR‐15a/16‐1 not only preserves white matter integrity but also attenuates gray‐matter and neuronal loss, underscoring its broad neurorestorative effect after TBI.

Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Deletion Mitigates Acute BBB Permeability and Subsequent Neuroinflammatory Activation after TBI

TBI triggers a cascade of vascular and inflammatory events, including BBB breakdown and subsequent parenchymal neuroinflammation. To assess BBB permeability dynamics, we monitored the extravasation of the low‐molecular‐weight fluorescent tracer cadaverine (0.95 kDa), which were intravenously injected 1 h before sacrifice at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 72 h after CCI, and endogenous plasma immunoglobulin G (IgG; 150 kDa). In WT mice, cadaverine leakage was detectable as early as 1 h post‐TBI and remained elevated through 72 h, whereas IgG extravasation increased gradually and peaked at 24 h (Figure S3a–d). Based on these temporal profiles, 24 h after TBI was selected to assess the impact of endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 genetic deficiency on BBB integrity.

Endothelial‐selective deletion of miR‐15a/16‐1 significantly reduced both cadaverine and IgG leakage at 24 h post‐injury, indicating improved endothelial barrier integrity (Figure 4a,b,d,e). Importantly, MAP2 immunostaining revealed comparable lesion areas between WT and EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice (Figure 4c,f), confirming that the observed BBB protection was not attributable to differences in primary injury severity.

To further determine whether endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion preserved BBB structural integrity, we examined the endothelial tight junction proteins Claudin‐5 and Occludin at 24 h after TBI. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated marked reductions in both Claudin‐5 and Occludin immunoreactivity in WT mice following injury, whereas EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice retained significantly greater tight junction protein staining within the cerebral microvasculature (Figure S4a–d). These findings indicate that endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion preserves endothelial tight junction protein expression in addition to reducing vascular permeability.

We next investigated whether preservation of BBB integrity was associated with attenuated secondary neuroinflammatory responses at 48 h after TBI by examining astrocytic activation and peripheral immune‐cell infiltration. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) immunofluorescence revealed marked astrocytic activation in the perilesional CTX, EC, and STR following TBI. However, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice exhibited a markedly reduced GFAP‐positive astrocytic area compared with WT controls across all examined regions (Figure S4e–k). Similarly, immunostaining for Ly‐6B (a neutrophil marker) and F4/80 (a macrophage marker) demonstrated robust leukocyte infiltration into pericontusional regions after TBI, which was significantly attenuated in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice (Figure 4g–j).

Collectively, these findings indicate that endothelial deletion of miR‐15a/16‐1 protects against acute BBB disruption, preserves endothelial tight junction integrity, suppresses early astrocytic and immune activation, and thereby mitigates post‐traumatic neuroinflammation. Given that BBB leakage peaked at 24 h and preceded maximal inflammatory activation at 48 h, this time point was selected for subsequent RNA‐seq analyses to capture early transcriptional responses underlying endothelial injury.

Transcriptomic Profiling Reveals Extensive Endothelial Reprogramming after TBI

To characterize the acute transcriptional responses of brain vascular endothelial cells following trauma, we performed bulk RNA‐sequencing on fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS)‐isolated vascular endothelial cells from the ipsilateral cortex of WT and EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice 24 h post‐TBI or sham operation (Figure 5a and Figure S5a). Quality control analysis using canonical lineage markers confirmed high purity and selective enrichment of endothelial signatures across all samples (Figure S5c and Table S3). Notably, while the proportion of endothelial cells among total live cells significantly decreased in WT mice after TBI, this population was preserved in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice (Figure S5b).

Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that while sham WT and cKO samples largely overlapped, indicating comparable baseline transcriptomes, TBI samples separated clearly from shams along PC1, reflecting robust injury‐induced transcriptional remodeling (Figure 5b). Differential expression analysis in WT mice between Sham and TBI identified 1,669 injury‐responsive genes (845 upregulated and 824 downregulated; Figure 5c). Hierarchical clustering of these genes further revealed distinct transcriptional signatures driven by inflammation, endothelial stress, and cytoskeletal remodeling (Figure 5d). Representative induced genes included Timp1, Tnfrsf1a, Cd200, and Scgb3a1, together with endothelial stress‐associated regulators such as Ecscr and Nr1d1, highlighting coordinated inflammatory and vascular remodeling responses. Conversely, genes associated with endothelial homeostasis were comparatively reduced.

Functional enrichment analysis further demonstrated that TBI profoundly affected endothelial signaling and structural pathways. Gene Ontology (GO) network analysis (Figure 5e) revealed upregulated pathways were heavily enriched for actin cytoskeleton organization, endothelial angiogenesis, membrane remodeling, and protein phosphorylation, reflecting a shift toward structural plasticity. Conversely, downregulated networks were dominated by mitochondrial translation and oxidative stress responses. Top‐ranked GO terms included blood vessel morphogenesis, angiogenesis, epithelial migration, and translational regulation, underscoring the global endothelial reprogramming toward inflammatory and structural remodeling phenotypes after TBI (Figure 5f). Collectively, these data define 24 h post‐injury as a critical window of extensive transcriptional reprogramming, characterized by inflammatory activation, vascular remodeling, and metabolic stress adaptation.

Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Deletion Reprograms Injury‐Induced Endothelial Transcriptional Responses After TBI

To define how the deletion of endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 modulates injury‐induced gene expression, we compared transcriptional profiles between TBI‐WT and TBI‐cKO samples. Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes revealed clear segregation of gene expression patterns between genotypes (Figure 5g). Because microRNAs primarily act to suppress the expression of their target genes, we focused on genes upregulated in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice after TBI (TBI‐cKO). Representative upregulated genes were regulators of nuclear organization and cytoskeletal dynamics, including Akap9, Atrx, and Stag1. Differential expression analysis identified 253 significantly upregulated and 33 downregulated genes in TBI‐cKO compared to TBI‐WT (Figure 5h). As expected, 88% (253/286) of the differentially expressed genes were upregulated in TBI‐cKO, consistent with direct or indirect derepression of miR‐15a/16‐1 targets and its canonical inhibitory role.

Functional enrichment analyses indicated endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion preferentially augmented gene networks associated with chromatin organization, RNA metabolism, and cytoskeletal regulation (Figure 5i–k). This enrichment suggests EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO endothelial cells have improved transcriptional adaptability and structural stability under stress. Network clustering (Figure 5j) demonstrated functional convergence of these pathways, linking epigenetic remodeling and RNA splicing to endothelial cytoskeletal organization and stress adaptation. Furthermore, network analysis of the Endothelial Cells and Actin Cytoskeleton cluster (Figure 5k) further highlighted actin‐associated modules containing Rock2, Sorbs1, and Myo6, pointing to structural reinforcement and enhanced endothelial differentiation following miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion. Together, these findings indicate that endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion reprograms the injury‐induced endothelial transcriptome from a proinflammatory and stress‐reactive state toward adaptive and homeostatic gene‐expression programs, thereby promoting vascular stability and resilience after TBI.

Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Deletion Restores Structural and Signaling Gene Networks Suppressed by TBI

Again, because microRNAs predominantly function as post‐transcriptional repressors, loss of miR‐15a/16‐1 is expected to derepress (e.g., upregulate) its direct downstream targets. Thus, to identify gene networks mediating the vascular protection conferred by endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion, we focused on transcripts that were downregulated after TBI but rescued in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO endothelial cells.

Comparative differential‐expression analysis revealed 56 genes exhibiting this rescue phenotype (downregulated in TBI‐WT vs. Sham‐WT; upregulated in TBI‐cKO vs. TBI‐WT) (Figure 6a,b and Figure S5d). Functional interaction mapping revealed a highly interconnected network primarily centered on cytoskeletal organization and junctional regulation (Figure 6c). Enrichment analyses further demonstrated clustering of these genes into cytoskeletal, adherens junction, vascular, cell‐cycle, and metabolic pathways (Figure 6d,e). To distinguish directly from indirect targets of miR‐15a/16‐1, we combined our results with in silico binding predictions from TargetScan and miRDB (Figure 6f). This helped us identify eight candidate targets: Akap12, Srek1, Syne1, Aff4, Crebrf, Phip, Rock2, and Filip1 that were reactivated in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO endothelial cells after TBI (Figure 6g).

Network and pathway enrichment analyses further demonstrated that the rescued genes converge on biological processes essential for endothelial homeostasis, including endothelial junction assembly, cytoskeletal organization, growth factor and hormone‐mediated signaling, and regulation of vascular permeability (Figure 6h–j). Notably, among these genes, Syne1, Akap12, and Rock2 occupied central network positions linking cytoskeletal organization to endothelial junctional signaling, positioning them as candidate downstream mediators coordinating cytoskeletal remodeling with endothelial barrier regulation.

Together, these findings indicate that endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion reactivates a coordinated cytoskeletal and signaling network that counteracts canonical TBI‐induced transcriptional suppression, thereby preserving endothelial integrity and mitigating post‐traumatic BBB disruption.

Normalized RNA‐seq counts confirmed all eight candidates were significantly upregulated in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice relative to WT controls following TBI, consistent with transcriptional derepression (Figure 7a).

To further refine this candidate list, we next sought to identify if the associated proteins are specifically and highly expressed in the mouse brain endothelium. Double immunofluorescence staining of mouse cortex with the endothelial marker CD31 revealed clear co‐localization for SYNE1, whereas AFF4 and FILIP1 showed minimal endothelial overlap (Figure S6). This endothelial specificity led us to prioritize SYNE1, a key component of the LINC complex critical for nuclear‐cytoskeletal coupling, for subsequent mechanistic investigation.

Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that while TBI markedly reduced SYNE1 protein levels within the CD31+ compartment in WT mice, this loss was effectively prevented in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice (Figure 7c). Quantitative analysis confirmed a substantial reduction in the SYNE1+/CD31+ area fraction in TBI‐WT mice that was rescued by endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion (Figure 7d). Collectively, these data identify SYNE1 as an endothelial cell‐enriched and injury‐sensitive target of miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion.

Cross‐Species Validation of Conserved Endothelial Injury‐Response Genes

To determine whether the endothelial injury‐response program identified in mice is conserved in humans, we analyzed scRNA‐seq data from human TBI samples (contusion‐core and pericontusional regions). Cross‐species comparison revealed 654 shared genes between mouse endothelial differential gene expressions (DEGs) (TBI vs. Sham) and human endothelial DEGs, suggesting substantial overlap in endothelial injury‐associated transcriptional responses across species (Figure 6e).

We next evaluated whether the eight miR‐15a/16‐1‐regulated mouse targets also exhibited injury‐associated expression changes in human endothelial cells. Dot–plot visualization indicated that AFF4, FILIP1, PHIP, SREK1, and SYNE1 exhibited differential expression between contusion‐core and pericontusional endothelial cells, supporting the translational relevance of these endothelial injury‐associated genes across species (Figure 6f).

These findings provide supportive translational evidence that components of the miR‐15a/16‐1‐regulated endothelial gene program identified in mice are also represented in human TBI endothelium. Among these candidates, SYNE1 exhibited conserved endothelial injury‐associated expression across species, prompting us to further investigate whether miR‐15a/16‐1 directly regulates Syne1 through post‐transcriptional repression.

miR‐15a/16‐1 Targets the 3′‐UTR of Syne1 mRNA to Repress Its Translation

To determine if Syne1 is truly a direct target of miR‐15a/16‐1, we conducted dual‐luciferase reporter assays. Bioinformatic prediction identified one conserved miR‐15a binding site within the 3’‐untranslated region (3’‐UTR) of mouse Syne1 mRNA (Figure 8a), suggesting a potential post‐transcriptional regulatory interaction. To test this, we constructed Firefly/Renilla luciferase reporter plasmids containing either the wild‐type Syne1 3’‐UTR or a mutant version with disrupted miR‐15a binding sequences (Figure S7). Then, HEK293 cells were transduced with lentiviruses expressing either miRZip‐15a (miR‐15a knockdown) or Lenti‐miR‐15a (miR‐15a overexpression) for 72 h, followed by transfection with the wild‐type or mutant Syne1 luciferase reporter constructs. Notably, lentiviral knockdown of miR‐15a significantly increased luciferase activity in cells expressing the wild‐type Syne1 3’‐UTR reporter, whereas lentiviral overexpression of miR‐15a/16‐1 markedly reduced luciferase activity (Figure 8b,c). Crucially, these regulatory effects were abolished by mutations in the predicted, miR‐15a binding site, confirming the specificity of the interaction. These data demonstrate that miR‐15a/16‐1 directly binds to the 3’‐UTR of Syne1 mRNA to suppress its translation, thereby providing mechanistic evidence that Syne1 is a bona fide endothelial target of miR‐15a/16‐1.

AAV‐shSyne1 Selectively Targets Brain Vascular Endothelial Cells and Downregulates SYNE1 Expression After TBI in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO Mice

To verify that SYNE1 functions as a downstream effector mediating the long‐term neurorestorative effects and functional recovery induced by endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion following TBI, we generated two adeno‐associated viral (AAV) constructs: one expressing a short hairpin RNA against mouse Syne1 (AAV‐U6‐shRNA(mSyne1)‐CAG‐GFP; hereafter AAV‐shSyne1) and a scrambled control (AAV‐U6‐shRNA(SCRM)‐CAG‐GFP; hereafter AAV‐SC) (Figure S8a,b). Both vectors expressed GFP under a CAG promoter, allowing direct visualization of transduced cells. Systemic administration via tail vein one month prior to injury ensured stable integration and expression.

We first validated the cell‐type specificity of the viral vectors. Immunofluorescence staining revealed fluorescence was almost exclusively colocalized with CD31+ cerebral vascular endothelial cells, with no detectable colocalization with neuron (NeuN), astrocyte (GFAP), microglia (Iba1 oligodendrocyte (Olig2), or oligodendrocyte precursor cell (NG2) markers (Figure 8d). This indicates AAV‐shSyne1 selectively transduces brain vascular endothelial cells in vivo. Comparable transduction patterns were also observed in AAV‐SC‐treated mice (Figure S8c–h). In addition, quantitative analysis of GFP‐positive area relative to CD31‐positive area across the CC, EC, STR, and CTX demonstrated comparable endothelial transduction efficiency between AAV‐SC and AAV‐shSyne1‐injected mice (Figure S9), indicating that subsequent phenotypic differences are unlikely to be attributable to variations in viral delivery or infection efficiency.

Next, we assessed the efficacy of AAV‐mediated Syne1 knockdown in the context of TBI. Mice were subjected to CCI one month after AAV injection. Consistent with our prior observations, pericontusional cortical regions of TBI + EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO + AAV‐SC mice showed a robust increase in SYNE1+/CD31+ area compared with WT controls at 24 h post‐injury (Figure 8e,f). Notably, this injury‐induced upregulation was largely abolished by AAV‐shSyne1 treatment, which markedly reduced the proportion of SYNE1+/CD31+ area. Validation using qPCR on FACS‐sorted brain vascular endothelial cells confirmed that AAV‐shSyne1 significantly suppressed Syne1 mRNA levels relative to scrambled controls (Figure 8g). Together, these data demonstrate AAV‐shSyne1 efficiently and specifically suppresses SYNE1 expression in brain vascular endothelial cells in vivo after TBI, providing a reliable tool to interrogate the downstream functional consequences of endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 signaling.

To exclude the possibility that AAV‐mediated Syne1 knockdown affects physiological function under basal conditions, we assessed cerebral blood flow (CBF) and behavioral performance under sham conditions. Consistent with the endothelial cell‐enriched targeting profile of the AAV construct (Figure 8d and Figure S8c–h), CBF measured 30 days after AAV tail vein injection showed no significant differences among WT + AAV‐SC, WT + AAV‐shSyne1, cKO + AAV‐SC, and cKO + AAV‐shSyne1 mice (Figure S10a,b). Likewise, sham‐operated mice exhibited comparable performance in rota‐rod, adhesive tape removal, foot‐fault, passive avoidance, and Y‐maze tests across all groups (Figure S10c–j). These findings suggest that endothelial SYNE1 knockdown alone does not affect baseline cerebral perfusion or behavioral performance, supporting the conclusion that the phenotypes observed after TBI are more likely related to the role of SYNE1 in injury‐induced neurovascular repair processes rather than nonspecific effects of viral transduction or gene silencing.

Endothelial SYNE1 Knockdown Abolishes the Acute Neurovascular Protection Conferred by Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Deletion After TBI

To determine whether SYNE1 mediates the early neurovascular protection induced by endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion after TBI, we first assessed BBB integrity and lesion severity at 24 h post‐injury. Consistent with our previous findings, TBI + EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO + AAV‐SC mice exhibited significantly reduced endogenous IgG extravasation compared with WT controls, indicating improved BBB integrity (Figure S11a,b). Importantly, this protective effect was largely abolished by AAV‐shSyne1 treatment, resulting in a marked increase in IgG leakage. In contrast, MAP2 staining revealed comparable lesion volumes among the experimental groups at 24 h after TBI (Figure S11c,d), indicating that the observed differences in BBB permeability were unlikely to be attributable to variations in the severity of the primary injury.

To further investigate the structural basis of BBB protection, we examined the endothelial tight junction proteins Claudin‐5 and Occludin. Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion significantly increased the vascular coverage of both Claudin‐5 and Occludin compared with WT controls, whereas AAV‐mediated Syne1 knockdown largely reversed these effects (Figure S11e–h). These findings suggest that SYNE1 contributes to the maintenance of endothelial tight junction integrity after TBI.

Because BBB disruption is closely associated with secondary inflammatory responses, we next assessed inflammatory cell infiltration and glial activation. Compared with WT controls, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice exhibited significantly reduced infiltration of Ly‐6B‐positive neutrophils and F4/80‐positive macrophages in the peri‐contusional cortex at 48 h after TBI. Notably, these anti‐inflammatory effects were substantially attenuated by SYNE1 knockdown (Figure S12a–d). Furthermore, GFAP immunostaining demonstrated reduced astrocyte activation in the cortex, external capsule, and striatum of EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice at 35 days after TBI, whereas AAV‐shSyne1 treatment largely restored GFAP immunoreactivity toward WT levels (Figure S12e–j).

Together, these findings indicate that endothelial SYNE1 is required for the preservation of BBB integrity and suppression of neuroinflammatory responses conferred by endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion after TBI, supporting its role as a critical downstream mediator of endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 signaling in acute neurovascular protection.

Endothelial SYNE1 Knockdown Abolishes the Functional Benefits of Endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 Deletion After TBI

To test whether endothelial SYNE1 in required for the neurorestoration in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice, we conducted a series of long‐term neurobehavioral assessments in WT and EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice treated with AAV‐shSyne1 or control AAV‐SC (Figure S13a). All animals maintained comparable body weights (Figure S13b), indicating no overt systemic effects of AAV treatment.

First, we assessed sensorimotor function. Consistent with our previous findings, EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice treated with control AAV‐SC exhibited markedly improved sensorimotor recovery compared with WT controls, as indicated by increased latency on the rota‐rod, shorter adhesive‐tape touch and removal times, and fewer foot‐faults (Figure 9a–e). Strikingly, however, these improvements were nearly abolished by AAV‐shSyne1 knockdown. EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice treated with AAV‐shSyne1 performed indistinguishably from WT controls across all tests, indicating that upregulation of endothelial Syne1 is indispensable for the enhanced motor recovery observed in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice.

To further evaluate whether endothelial SYNE1 also mediates the structural neurorestoration associated with endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 deletion after TBI, we examined both white matter and gray matter integrity.

In terms of WM integrity, LFB staining revealed extensive myelin loss in the pericontusional CTX, EC, and STR of TBI + WT + AAV‐SC mice, whereas TBI + EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO + AAV‐SC mice exhibited markedly preserved myelin density (Figure 10a–d). Notably, this protection was abolished by AAV‐shSyne1 treatment. Consistent with these results, MBP/SMI32 double immunofluorescence demonstrated reduced axonal degeneration and preserved myelin in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice, both of which were reversed by AAV‐shSyne1 (Figure 10e–h). Importantly, these structural findings parallel the reversal of functional recovery (Figure 9), providing convergent evidence that the endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1–SYNE1 signaling axis is a central regulator of long‐term brain structural and functional recovery following TBI.

Discussion

The miR‐15a/16‐1 cluster has emerged as a crucial regulator of cerebrovascular integrity and angiogenic remodeling across various neurological conditions [17, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Although global deletion of miR‐15a/16‐1 is known to confer robust neuroprotection after TBI [23], the specific cell‐type contributions and downstream molecular effectors driving this benefit have remained elusive. Here, we demonstrate endothelial cell‐specific deletion of miR‐15a/16‐1 is sufficient to reproduce long‐term neurorestorative after TBI and identify SYNE1 as a critical downstream effector mediating this response. By employing endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 conditional knockout mice together with endothelial cell‐targeted AAV‐shSyne1 mediated knockdown, we demonstrate that endothelial SYNE1 contributes to preserving white and gray matter integrity and to maintaining neurobehavioral recovery after TBI. Taking together, these data provide mechanistic insight for the durable benefits of miR‐15a/16‐1 genetic deficiency and uncover a previously unrecognized role of endothelial SYNE1 in neurovascular repair and long‐term recovery after TBI.

Endothelial cells are increasingly recognized as active regulators of neural recovery beyond their barrier function [17, 27, 28]. Consistent with this view, these findings support the concept that endothelial signaling plays an active role in coordinating long‐term neurovascular repair after TBI rather than serving solely as a passive barrier component. Importantly, the long‐term recovery phenotype observed here is unlikely to represent an endothelial‐autonomous process, and endothelial SYNE1 signaling may act in concert with astrocytic, oligodendroglial, and neuronal repair programs within the neurovascular unit, consistent with emerging evidence that durable brain repair depends on coordinated multicellular interactions across the neurovascular unit [29]. The dependence of these protective effects on SYNE1 suggests that endothelial structural organization may contribute to vascular stability and long‐term tissue repair following TBI. Given the established role of SYNE1 within the LINC complex, mechanotransduction represents one potential framework for interpreting these observations. However, this possibility was not directly examined in the present study. These findings are consistent with growing evidence that endothelial mechanotransduction and cytoskeletal dynamics critically influence neurovascular recovery [30]. For instance, perturbations of the LINC complex or actin‐nuclear coupling have been shown to impair endothelial alignment, barrier integrity, and angiogenic remodeling under shear stress [31, 32]. In the central nervous system, endothelial loss of key mechanosensitive regulators, such as PECAM‐1, YAP/TAZ, or VE‐cadherin similarly compromises vascular stability and regeneration after ischemic or traumatic injury [33, 34]. Together with new insights into LINC complex dysfunction in vascular disease [35], and recent evidence demonstrating that endothelial‐directed mechanisms actively regulate BBB recovery after injury [36], our finding extends previous observations that endothelial miR‐15a/16‐1 regulates BBB integrity [17], angiogenesis [21], and inflammatory responses [24], and links it to a SYNE1‐dependent vascular repair.

Mechanistically, Syne1 encodes SYNE1, a spectrin‐repeat scaffold protein that links the nuclear envelope to the cytoskeleton as part of the LINC complex [37]. Our results suggest that miR‐15a/16‐1 normally represses SYNE1 in vascular endothelial cells and that restoration of SYNE1 expression contributes to endothelial protection and tissue repair after TBI. Although SYNE1 is known to participate in nuclear‐cytoskeletal coupling, the downstream cellular processes responsible for the observed protective effects remain to be determined. This interpretation agrees with prior reports showing that SYNE1 and other nesprins strengthen nucleo‐cytoskeletal connectivity and improve cellular mechanosensing [35]. Previous studies have demonstrated that SYNE1 contributes to nuclear‐cytoskeletal organization and cellular responses to mechanical stimuli in multiple systems [31, 32]. Whether similar mechanisms operate in cerebrovascular endothelial cells after TBI remain unknown. In our model, increased SYNE1 expression may promote endothelial stability and tissue repair, although the underlying cellular mechanisms require further investigation. Conversely, SYNE1 knockdown likely disrupts this adaptive program, resulting in persistent myelin and axonal pathology and blunted functional recovery. These results highlight endothelial cytoskeletal integrity as an underappreciated determinant of brain repair, directly linking mechanical homeostasis in the vasculature to neuroprotection after TBI.

Underscoring the clinical relevance of these findings, analysis of single‐cell transcriptomes from human TBI patients revealed a similar spatial pattern of vascular vulnerability. The conserved reduction in SYNE1 expression observed in vascular endothelial cells from the contusion core supports the translational relevance of our findings. These findings suggest that reduced SYNE1 expression may represent a shared response to traumatic vascular stress across species and further support the therapeutic potential of targeting SYNE1‐associated vascular responses during chronic neurovascular repair.

The identification of this pathway provides a unifying mechanistic link between vascular gene regulation and long‐term neural repair. In conjunction with prior work across stroke [17, 19, 21] and vascular dementia [24], our findings position miR‐15a/16‐1 as an important regulator of cerebrovascular resilience that executes context‐specific programs encompassing barrier protection, angiogenesis, anti‐inflammatory regulation, and now SYNE1‐dependent vascular repair mechanisms. Notably, studies in ischemic stroke [38], vascular dementia [39], and chronic traumatic encephalopathy [40, 41] have all demonstrated that chronic vascular dysfunction and myelin degeneration are shared pathological signatures across these disorders. Our findings therefore suggest the miR‐15a/16‐1‐SYNE1 axis may represent a convergent mechanism underlying vascular resilience and white matter repair across diverse neurological contexts. Recent studies have further demonstrated that targeting secondary injury mechanisms, including oxidative stress, inflammasome activation, autophagy dysregulation, programmed cell death, and tissue remodeling, can promote structural preservation and functional recovery across a broad spectrum of traumatic and ischemic disorders. Together, these findings support the concept that modulation of injury‐induced pathological cascades represents a promising therapeutic strategy for enhancing tissue repair and functional recovery [42, 43, 44, 45].

Finally, from a translational perspective, the exclusive endothelial localization of the miR‐15a/16‐1‐SYNE1 axis may offer a therapeutic advantage. The BBB remains the primary challenge for drug delivery to the brain, often severely limiting the efficacy of neuroprotective or neurorestorative agents targeting parenchymal neurons or glia [46, 47, 48]. However, the cerebrovascular endothelium is, by definition, luminally accessible to systemic circulation [49]. Because cerebrovascular endothelial cells are directly accessible from the circulation, therapeutic targeting of the miR‐15a/16‐1–SYNE1 axis may be more feasible than approaches directed toward parenchymal cell populations. Thus, this axis represents a pharmacologically accessible target with significant potential for enhancing neurovascular resilience after TBI.

Several limitations should be noted. This study primarily focused on chronic structural and neurobehavioral outcomes. Although AAV‐shSyne1 achieved efficient endothelial suppression, future studies using inducible or temporally controlled models will be helpful to further validate the specificity of this approach. Importantly, although SYNE1 is a recognized component of the LINC complex and has been implicated in mechanotransduction in other systems, the present study did not directly assess endothelial nuclear mechanics, cytoskeletal organization, mechanosensitive signaling, or shear‐stress responses. Therefore, the precise mechanisms through which endothelial SYNE1 promotes vascular stability and tissue repair after TBI remain to be established. Future studies will be needed to determine whether mechanotransduction contributes to the protective effects observed here, to further define the downstream molecular programs regulated by endothelial SYNE1, and to elucidate how endothelial SYNE1 signaling interacts with glial and neuronal repair processes during chronic neurovascular recovery.

In summary, this study identifies endothelial SYNE1 as a key downstream effector of miR‐15a/16‐1 signaling that governs long‐term neurovascular and neurostructural recovery after TBI. By linking microRNA regulation to endothelial SYNE1‐dependent vascular repair pathways, our findings provide new insights into how cerebrovascular cells drive chronic brain repair and highlight the miR‐15a/16‐1–SYNE1 axis as a promising therapeutic target after TBI.

Experimental Section

Animals and Experimental Design

All animal experiments received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pittsburgh (IACUC) (protocol no. 24065216) and were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male mice (four per cage) and female mice (five per cage) were group‐housed in a temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled facility under a 12 h light‐dark cycle, with free access to food and water. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to use the minimal number of animals required for statistical validity.

C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Endothelium‐specific miR‐15a/16‐1 conditional knockout (EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO) mice were generated on a C57BL/6J background by crossing miR‐15a/16‐1 flox/flox mice (a generous gift from Dr. Riccardo Dalla‐Favera, Columbia University) [50] with VE‐Cadherin‐Cre transgenic mice. EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO mice were viable and fertile, exhibiting normal appearance, behavior, growth, and litter size. Age‐ and sex‐matched wild‐type (WT) littermates (8–12 weeks old, 20–34 g) were used as controls. EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO and WT mice were randomly assigned to sham or TBI groups using a lottery‐based randomization procedure. Male and female mice were distributed as evenly as possible across experimental groups to minimize potential confounding effects of sex. All behavioral, histological, and molecular assessments were performed by investigators blinded to both genotype and treatment.

CCI Model of TBI

TBI was induced in EC‐miR‐15a/16‐1 cKO and WT mice using a CCI device (Precision Systems and Instrumentation), as described previously [51]. Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane in a gas mixture of 25% O2 and 74% N2O and secured in a stereotaxic frame. After a midline scalp incision, a 4.0‐mm‐diameter craniotomy was made over the right parietotemporal cortex (centered 0.5 mm anterior and 2.0 mm lateral to bregma) using the Ideal Micro‐Drill (Cellpoint Scientific Inc.). The impact was delivered to the exposed dura using a 3‐mm flat‐tipped impactor at a depth of 1.5 mm, velocity of 3.5–3.7 m/s, and dwell time of 150 ms. Following impact, the scalp incision was closed with 6‐0 sutures. Core body temperature was maintained at 37.0°C ± 0.5°C throughout the procedure. For the sham group, identical surgical procedures were performed, including anesthesia, scalp incision, and craniotomy, but without cortical impact. The scalp was sutured in the same manner as the TBI group.

AAV Injection

For endothelial cell‐specific gene modulation, mice received systemic administration of AAV‐BI30 vectors via tail vein injection. AAV‐BI30 was selected based on previous studies demonstrating highly efficient and selective transduction of brain vascular endothelial cells throughout the central nervous system following systemic delivery, with minimal transduction of neurons and glial cells [52, 53]. Endothelial cell‐targeted AAV‐BI30 (AAV2 serotype) vectors (1 × 101 1 viral genomes per mouse) carried either AAV‐U6‐shRNA(mSyne1)‐CAG‐GFP (shSyne1) for endothelial knockdown of Syne1 or AAV‐U6‐shRNA(SCRM)‐CAG‐GFP (SC) containing a non‐targeting scrambled shRNA as a control. Both constructs shared identical backbones and regulatory elements, ensuring comparable expression efficiency. Viral particles were diluted in sterile phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to a final volume of 100 µL and injected into the lateral tail vein using a 31‐gauge insulin syringe. Mice were monitored until full recovery and maintained under standard housing conditions. All AAV injections were performed 1 month before CCI induction to ensure stable transgene expression in brain endothelial cells.

Behavioral Assessments

Behavioral assessments were conducted by a trained experimenter who was blinded to genotype and treatment, utilizing a series of tests to assess sensorimotor and cognitive functions before injury and up to 35 days post‐CCI.

Rota‐Rod Test

Motor coordination and balance were evaluated using an accelerating rota‐rod apparatus (IITC Life Science Inc.) as described previously [54]. Mice were placed on a rotating drum that accelerated from 5 to 40 rpm over 5 min. Each mouse underwent three trials per session, separated by 5 min intertrial intervals. Baseline performance was established over three consecutive days before surgery, and post‐injury testing was conducted at 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 days after CCI. The mean latency to fall across the three trials was recorded for each time point.

Adhesive Removal Test

Forepaw sensorimotor function was assessed using the adhesive removal test, as previously described [55]. Mice were placed individually in a transparent Plexiglas cylinder (30 cm height × 20 cm diameter) for a 60 s habituation period, after which a small adhesive tape (0.3 × 0.4 cm) was affixed to the left forepaw. The latency to first contact (time to touch) and to complete removal (time to remove) of the tape was recorded. Each mouse performed three trials per session, conducted 3 days before surgery and at designated post‐injury time points. If a mouse failed to touch or remove the tape within 120 s, a latency of 120 s was assigned.

Foot‐Fault Test

Sensorimotor coordination of the forepaw and hindpaw was evaluated using the foot‐fault test [51]. For 5 min, mice were free to move around a horizontal metal grid. Any time the left forepaw or hindpaw slipped through the grid openings, a foot‐fault was noted. To determine baseline performance, one trial was carried out daily for three days prior to surgery. Testing was then repeated at predetermined post‐injury time points. The percentage of foot‐fault errors compared to the total number of steps taken by the left forepaw or hindpaw was used to express foot‐fault performance.

MWM Test

Spatial learning and reference memory were assessed using the Morris water maze test according to previous studies [21, 23, 56]. Behavioral testing was conducted between 29 and 34 days after CCI. During the learning phase (days 1–5), mice were trained to locate a circular escape platform submerged 1.5 cm below the water surface and positioned at the center of the target (fourth) quadrant. For each trial, mice were released from one of three randomly selected start points located at the 3, 6, or 9 o'clock positions of the pool, and the latency to locate the hidden platform was recorded. On day 6, a memory trial was performed by removing the platform; mice were released from the 6 o'clock position and allowed to swim freely for 60 s. The time spent in the target quadrant and the average swimming speed were recorded. Behavioral data were acquired and analyzed using ANY‐maze tracking software (Stoelting Co.) with video monitoring.

Passive Avoidance Test

Long‐term cognitive performance was further assessed using the passive avoidance test. Passive avoidance test was performed based on a previous protocol with minor modifications [23]. On day 34 after surgery (training phase), each mouse was placed in the illuminated chamber of a two‐compartment apparatus and allowed to acclimate for 60 s before the door to the dark chamber was opened. Upon entering the dark compartment, the door was closed, and a mild foot shock (0.4–1.6 mA) was delivered. Mice remained in the dark chamber for 30 s to reinforce the association between the aversive stimulus and the dark environment. On the following day (day 35, testing phase), mice were returned to the light chamber, and the latency to enter the dark compartment within 180 s was recorded as a measure of memory retention.

Y‐maze Test

Spontaneous alternation behavior in the Y‐maze was used to assess spatial working memory [57]. The apparatus consisted of three identical arms (each 35 cm long, 5 cm wide, and 10 cm high) arranged at 120° angles from each other. Each mouse was placed at the end of one arm and allowed to explore all three arms freely for 5 min. The sequence and total number of arm entries were recorded using ANY‐maze tracking software. An alternation was defined as successive entries in all three arms without repetition. The percentage of spontaneous alternation was calculated as the ratio of actual alternations to the maximum possible alternations [(total arm entries – 2) × 100%]. The maze was cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues.

DTI

Ex vivo diffusion tensor imaging was used to evaluate brain white matter (WM) integrity as previously described [58]. At 35 days after CCI, mice were euthanized and imaged using an 11.7‐Tesla/89‐mm vertical‐bore Bruker AVANCE AV3 HD MicroImaging system equipped with a Micro2.5 gradient insert and a 20‐mm quadrature RF probe (Bruker Biospin), operating under ParaVision 6.0.1. DTI data were acquired using a multi‐slice spin‐echo sequence with five A0 images and 30 non‐collinear diffusion‐weighted directions. Imaging parameters were as follows: echo time (TE)/repetition time (TR), 22/2800 ms; two signal averages; matrix size, 160 × 160; field of view (FOV), 16 × 16 mm; 25 slices at 0.5‐mm thickness; b‐value, 3000 s/mm2; and Δ/δ, 11.0/5.0 ms. Fractional anisotropy (FA) and directionally encoded color (DEC) maps were reconstructed using DSI Studio software (http://dsi‐studio.labsolver.org/). Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated in the corpus callosum (CC) and external capsule (EC) for quantitative analysis.

TEM

As previously mentioned, TEM was used to analyze the ultrastructure of myelin sheaths in the CC and EC [20]. At 35 days after CCI, mice were deeply anesthetized, transcardially perfused, and the CC/EC region was dissected into 1‐mm3 tissue blocks. Samples were processed for ultrathin sectioning using an ultramicrotome (Reichert Ultracut; Leica Microsystems) and imaged with a Philips CM120 transmission electron microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, OR). Micrographs were acquired at identical magnifications for all groups. Approximately 100 axons per mouse were analyzed using ImageJ software. Myelin thickness, axonal abnormalities, and g‐ratios (axon diameter/fiber diameter) were calculated, and g‐ratio distributions were plotted as scatter plots for quantitative comparison among groups.

Brain Slice Preparation

Brain slices were processed as previously described [20, 59]. Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 0.9% NaCl followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were removed, post‐fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 2 days. Coronal sections (26 µm thick) were cut using a microtome (HM450, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored in cryoprotectant solution at ‐20°C until use.

LFB Staining

LFB staining was performed to assess WM demyelination according to the previous protocol [60]. Briefly, brain sections were incubated in LFB solution, differentiated sequentially with 70% ethanol and 0.05% lithium carbonate (Li2CO3), and then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Images were acquired using a light microscope (EVOS M7000; Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each brain, three randomly selected fields from three consecutive coronal sections were analyzed in the CTX, EC, and striatum. Relative optical density (OD) values were quantified using ImageJ software for statistical comparison.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described, with slight modifications [61, 62]. Free‐floating brain sections were washed three times in PBS (5 min each), once in 1% PBST (1% Triton X‐100 in PBS) for 20 min, and 5 min in 0.3% PBST. Sections were then blocked with 5% normal donkey serum in 0.3% PBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies diluted in 0.3% PBST overnight at 4°C. After washing, sections were incubated with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies. Details of primary antibodies, dilution ratios, and sources are listed in Table S1. Images were acquired using confocal microscopes (A1R, Nikon, Japan; or STELLARIS, Leica, Germany). Mean fluorescence intensity and cell counts were quantified using ImageJ software.

CV Staining

Cresyl violet staining was processed to evaluate neuronal loss [18]. Briefly, brain sections were deionized and immersed in cresyl violet solution, followed by sequential rinses in distilled water, 70%, 95%, and 100% ethanol, and then cleared in xylene. Images were acquired using EVOS M7000. For each brain, we analyzed three randomly selected fields from three consecutive coronal sections in the cortex, hippocampus CA1 and CA3. The CV‐positive area within each region was quantified using ImageJ software to assess neuronal preservation.

Assessment of BBB Permeability

BBB permeability was evaluated using the fluorescent tracer Alexa Fluor 555‐conjugated cadaverine (0.95 kDa; 180 µg per mouse; Invitrogen, A30677), as previously described [63]. Alexa 555‐cadaverine was injected into the femoral vein 60 min before perfusion to visualize tracer leakage through the injured BBB. Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 0.9% NaCl followed by 4% PFA. Brains were removed, post‐fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 2 days. Coronal sections (30 µm thick) were cut using the microtome and stored in cryoprotectant solution at ‐20°C until use.

For the detection of endogenous IgG extravasation, sections were sequentially incubated in avidin and biotin blocking solutions (Vector Laboratories; two drops of each reagent per 10 mL PBS, 15 min each), followed by blocking with 5% normal donkey serum for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with biotinylated anti‐mouse IgG antibody (1:500; Vector Laboratories), followed by Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated streptavidin (1:1000; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories).

Sections were processed for direct fluorescence detection of Alexa 555‐cadaverine and IgG. Images were acquired using EVOS M7000. Six equally spaced coronal sections encompassing the injury area were analyzed, and the cross‐sectional areas of Alexa 555‐cadaverine and IgG fluorescence were quantified using ImageJ software. Leakage volume (mm3) was calculated as the sum of fluorescent areas multiplied by the distance between sections (1 mm).

Isolation of Brain Vascular Endothelial Cells by FACS

Brain microvascular endothelial cells were isolated from adult mouse cortices following a modified protocol based on Giacobini et al. [64]. Mice were transcardially perfused with ice‐cold PBS to remove blood. The cerebral cortex was rapidly dissected on ice, and meninges and large surface vessels were carefully removed under a stereomicroscope. The cortex was minced into approximately 1 mm3 fragments and enzymatically dissociated in a collagenase/dispase solution (0.5 mL of enzyme stock in 14.5 mL of PBS containing 2% FBS) for 30 min at 37°C with gentle rotation. The digested tissue was gently triturated approximately 100 times with a P1000 pipette to obtain a single‐cell suspension.

Myelin and debris were removed by density centrifugation on 22% Percoll gradients (560 × g, 10 min, 4°C, no brake). The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in HBSS/BSA/glucose buffer and washed twice. Cells were then incubated on ice for 20 min with fluorophore‐conjugated antibodies against CD31 (PECAM‐1; endothelial marker), CD13 (pericyte marker), CD45 (pan‐leukocyte marker), and CD41 (platelet marker) in HBSS/BSA/glucose buffer, followed by washing and filtration through 40 µm cell strainers. DAPI (1:500) was added immediately before sorting to exclude dead cells.

Flow cytometric sorting was performed on a BD FACSAria II cell sorter using a 100 µm nozzle and 13 psi sheath pressure to preserve cell viability. Gating was established using unstained, single‐color, and fluorescence‐minus‐one controls. Doublets, debris, and DAPI+ (dead) cells were excluded. Endothelial cells were defined as CD31+CD13−CD41−CD45−, and pericytes as CD31−CD13+CD41−CD45−. Sorted cells were collected into a 96‐well plate with lysis buffer or 1.5 mL tubes prefilled with PBS containing 2% FBS and maintained on ice throughout the procedure.

Immediately after sorting, cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4°C and resuspended according to downstream applications. For bulk RNA sequencing, cells were processed immediately until RNA extraction. For the TBI group, the ipsilateral (injury‐side) cerebral cortex was collected for cell isolation, whereas in the sham group, the corresponding cortical region from the sham‐operated side was used.

RNA Sequencing

Messenger RNA for bulk RNA sequencing was isolated from FACS endothelial cells obtained from adult wild‐type and transgenic mice. Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the University of Pittsburgh HSCRF Genomics Research Core. Complementary DNA was synthesized using the SMART‐seq HT Ultra Low Input Kit, and sequencing libraries were generated with the Illumina DNA Prep (Nextera FLEX) kit following the manufacturer's protocols. Equimolar libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 P3 200‐cycle system using a 2 × 101‐bp paired‐end configuration.

RNA‐seq Data Processing

Raw sequencing reads were processed using the nf‐core RNAseq pipeline (v3.12.0). In brief, adapter contamination and read quality were assessed with FastQC (v0.11.9) and trimmed using Trim Galore! (v0.6.7). Filtered reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (Ensembl GRCm39 v110) using STAR (v2.7.9a). Gene‐level quantification was performed with Salmon (v1.10.1), and transcript assembly and quantification were carried out using StringTie (v2.2.1). Post‐alignment quality control was conducted using RSeQC (v3.0.1), Qualimap (v2.2.2‐dev), dupRadar (v1.28.0), Preseq (v2.0.3), and featureCounts (v2.0.1).

Differential Expression Analysis

DEG analysis was performed in R (v4.3.2) using DESeq2 (v1.42.0). Transcript‐level abundance estimates from Salmon were imported via tximport (v1.30.0) to generate gene‐level count matrices. Lowly expressed genes (counts <10 in fewer than four samples) were excluded. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change >1 and an adjusted p <0.05 were defined as differentially expressed. Variance‐stabilizing transformation (VST) was applied for normalization, and log fold‐change shrinkage for visualization and ranking was performed using the ashr method. Heatmaps were generated using ComplexHeatmap (v2.18.0). Volcano plots were created with EnhancedVolcano (v1.20.0), and Venn diagrams illustrating DEG overlaps were produced using venndir (v0.0.29.900). Additional plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.4.4).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GESA)

GSEA was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.10.0). Genes were ranked by log2 fold change regardless of differential expression status to capture subtle but coordinated shifts in biological processes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was analyzed across biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories using annotations from org.Mm.eg.db (v3.18.0). Enriched terms (adjusted p < 0.05) were visualized using bubble plots, dot plots, enrichment maps, and gene‐concept network plots generated with enrichplot (v1.22.0) and ggplot2 (v3.4.4).

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from FACS‐purified vascular endothelial cells from the cerebral cortex using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), as previously described [61]. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad) on a CFX Connect Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to Gapdh mRNA. Each PCR reaction was run in triplicate using independent FACS‐sorted samples. Primer sequences used in this study are provided in Table S2.

Public Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing

Single‐cell transcriptomic data from paired contusion‐core and pericontusional tissues of three independent human TBI patients (GSE182398) were obtained from the GEO public database. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.0) using the Seurat package (v5.3.0). Raw count matrices were imported into Seurat objects and subjected to quality control filtering to remove low‐quality cells and potential doublets. Data normalization was performed using the SCTransform function. Gene expression differences between contusion‐core and pericontusional vascular endothelial cells were visualized using the DotPlot function. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with the FindMarkers function, using a significance threshold of adjusted p < 0.05. Venn diagram comparisons between scRNA‐seq DEGs and the bulk RNA‐seq DEGs were generated using the eulerr package (v7.0.2). The pathway enrichment analysis was performed using EnrichR v3.4. The human scRNA‐seq data were obtained from the publicly available GEO database (GSE182398) and were analyzed as a secondary analysis of publicly available data.

Cell Culture and Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

Dual‐luciferase reporter assays were performed as described previously [20]. The mouse mSyne1 3′‐UTR dual‐luciferase reporter plasmid (pEZX‐MT01‐mSyne1 3′‐UTR) and its mutated construct (pEZX‐MT01‐mSyne1 3′‐UTR‐mut) were purchased from GeneCopoeia. Plasmids were propagated in E. coli cultured on kanamycin‐selective Luria–Bertani (LB) agar plates, and purified plasmid DNA was validated by restriction enzyme digestion and agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop One/OneC Microvolume UV–vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

HEK293 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and without antibiotics and seeded into 24‐well plates. When cells reached approximately 30% confluency, they were infected with lentiviruses for knockdown or overexpression of miR‐15a/16‐1. 72 h after infection, cells were transfected with either wild‐type or mutant mSyne1 3′‐UTR plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) in Opti‐MEM medium for 6 h. Following transfection, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 10% FBS, and cells were incubated for an additional 24 h.

Cells were lysed with Passive Lysis Buffer (PLB; Promega), and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a SpectraMax i3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity. Four independent experiments were performed to determine mean luciferase activity.

Statistics

Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test; homogeneity of variance with the Brown–Forsythe test. Data are presented as mean ± s.d. with individual data points shown. For comparisons among multiple groups, one‐way or two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used when assumptions were met. Two‐group comparisons used two‐tailed unpaired t‐tests. Body‐weight trajectories were analyzed by two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. When variances were unequal, Welch's ANOVA with Dunnett's T3 post hoc test was applied; for non‐normal data, Kruskal–Wallis tests were used with appropriate multiple‐comparison corrections. Pearson correlation coefficients (two‐tailed) were calculated for correlation analyses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Here, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; ns, not significant. Sample sizes (n) refer to biologically independent animals. For imaging‐based analyses, quantification was performed using representative sections or fields obtained from multiple animals, as specified in the corresponding figure legends.
