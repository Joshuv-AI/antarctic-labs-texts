Abstract
Liquid crystal (LC) technology enables low-power and cost-effective solutions for implementing the reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) . However, the phase-shift response of LC - RIS s is temperature-dependent, which, if unaddressed, can degrade the performance. This issue is particularly critical in applications such as secure communications, where variations in phase-shift response may lead to significant information leakage. In this paper, we consider secure communication through an LC - RIS and developed a temperature-aware algorithm adapting the RIS phase shifts to thermal conditions. Our simulation results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm significantly improves the secure data rate compared to scenarios where temperature variations are not accounted for.

II-B

LC phase shifter

In this subsection, we assume a fixed temperature and explain how LC molecules introduce a phase shift into the impinging wave. Based on this discussion, in Section
III-A
, we develop a model accounting for the impact of the temperature. LC-RISs control how signals reflect by leveraging the unique electromagnetic properties of LC molecules, which can be reoriented using an electric field (
\vec{E}_{\rm RF}
)
[
8
]
. This reorientation changes the permittivity of the LC, which in turn affects the phase shift induced by each RIS element. LC molecules have an elongated, rod-like shape, and their electromagnetic properties change depending on whether the electric field is aligned with their major or minor axis. When the electric field is aligned with the major axis, the permittivity is higher, resulting in a greater phase shift, see Fig.
2
for an illustration. By controlling the orientation of these molecules via the applied voltage controlled by the applied electrical field, the RIS can adjust the way signals reflect, allowing it to create a programmable wireless environment.

Fig. 2
:
The relationship between phase shift and applied voltage for an
LC
at different temperatures are depicted. The experimental data is taken from
[
14
,
15
]
, a piece-wise linear function is modeled similar to
[
11
]
.

The maximum range of phase shift that an
LC
unit can achieve depends on several factors such as the length of the
LC
cell, the difference in
permittivity between different molecular orientations, and the operating frequency, i.e.,

\Delta\omega_{\max}=2\pi l(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,\parallel}}-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,\perp}})\frac{f}{c},

(2)

where
l
,
f
, and
c
are the phase shifter length, frequency, and speed of the light in vacuum, respectively.
\varepsilon_{r,\parallel}
and
\varepsilon_{r,\perp}
are the maximum and the minimum relative permittivity, respectively. They correspond to when an electrical field is aligned or perpendicular to the vector
\vec{n}
in Fig.
2
.
The value of
\varepsilon_{r,\parallel}
and
\varepsilon_{r,\perp}
are scaled with temperature, which leads to a temperature-dependent phase-shift response for
LC
-
RIS
s, as will be discussed in detail in Section
III
.

II-C

Secrecy rate

The secrecy rate is a crucial metric used to quantify the performance of a physical-layer secure communication system. It is defined as the difference between the achievable rates of the legitimate user and the eavesdropper
[
16
]
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{SR}=[\log(1+\mathrm{SNR}_{u})-\log(1+\mathrm{SNR}_{e})]^{+},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SNR}_{u}=\frac{|(\mathbf{h}_{u}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{q}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SNR}_{e}=\frac{|(\mathbf{h}_{e}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{q}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}},

(5)

where
(\mathbf{h}_{g}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{\mathsf{H}}=\mathbf{h}_{d,g}^{\mathsf{H}}+\mathbf{h}_{r,g}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{H}_{t},\,g=\{u,e\}
is the end-to-end channel from the
BS
to the legitimate user and eavesdropper.

In this paper, our goal is to maximize the secrecy rate by optimizing the
RIS
phase shifts and
BS
beamformer, thus the system can create a favorable communication environment for the legitimate user while suppressing the signal strength in the area of the eavesdropper. We design the
RIS
phase-shift based on the approximate locations of both the legitimate user and the eavesdropper, with the following benefits:

•

Unlike most literature that assumes the eavesdropper’s
CSI
is fully known, we assume only that the eavesdropper’s location lies within a specified area,
\mathbf{p}_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{e}
. The size of this area,
|\mathcal{P}_{e}|
, accounts for potential errors in estimating this location.

•

Similarly, for the legitimate user, we assume only that their location is within a specific area,
\mathbf{p}_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{u}
. The area size,
|\mathcal{P}_{e}|
, can be adjusted not only to account for location estimation error but also to reduce reconfiguration overhead by decreasing the frequency of required
RIS
adjustments.

The
RIS
phase shifts will be designed based on the LOS paths to optimize the communication, as it offers the most significant contribution to signal strength at
mmWave
bands, where
LC
-
RIS
s are expected to be adopted.

III
Temperature-Aware LC-RIS Phase-shift Design

In this section, we start by introducing a model to describe the impact of the temperature on the
LC
-
RIS

III-A

Theory behind the temperature impact on LC

In this section, we analyze how temperature variations affect the maximum phase shift generated by an individual
LC
cell. Let us define
\Delta n\triangleq(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,\parallel}}-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,\perp}})
in (
2
), which is known in the literature as birefringence
[
10
]
. With changing the temperature,
\Delta n
changes leading to a different phase shift. The dependency of
\Delta n
on temperature is characterized by the Haller approximation
[
17
]
:

\Delta n=\Delta n_{0}\Big(1-\frac{T}{T_{c}}\Big)^{\beta},

(6)

where
\Delta n_{0}
is birefringence at the reference temperature, denoted by
T_{r}
,
T
is the current temperature and
T_{c}
is the clearing temperature of the
LC
, which is defined as the temperature at which the
LC
becomes isotropic, i.e.,
LC
molecule becomes completely disordered. Moreover,
\beta
is a constant which typically is around
0.2-0.25
for many
LC
materials
[
10
]
.
Let us assume at
T_{r}
,
LC
cells produce
2\pi
differential phase shift. Exploiting (
2
) and (
6
), it can be obtained:

\Delta\omega_{\max}(T)=2\pi l\Delta n_{0}\Big(1-\frac{T}{T_{c}}\Big)^{\beta}\frac{f}{c},

(7)

where
\Delta\omega_{\max}(T_{r})=2\pi
. Equivalently, (
7
) can be rewritten as

\Delta\omega_{\max}(T)=2\pi\Big(\frac{T_{c}-T}{T_{c}-T_{r}}\Big)^{\beta}.

(8)

Therefore, when the temperature decreases from
T_{r}
, the maximum achievable phase shift increases. In this case,
\Delta\omega_{\max}
exceeds
2\pi
, allowing the phase shifts to be adjusted back to those corresponding to
T_{r}
by tuning the voltages. However, with increasing temperature, the maximum achievable phase shift decreases. This case will be investigated in the following section.

III-B

Temperature-aware phase-shift design

Next, assume the setup in Fig.
1
where
RIS
wishes to maximize the secure rate defined in (
3

P1:

\displaystyle~\underset{\bm{\omega},\mathbf{q},\alpha}{\max}~\alpha

(9a)

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}~~\mathrm{SR}\geq\alpha,\,\forall\mathbf{p}_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{u},\,\forall\mathbf{p}_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{e}

(9b)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}0\leq[\bm{\omega}]_{n}<\omega_{\mathrm{max}},\forall n,

(9c)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}\|\mathbf{q}\|_{2}^{2}\leq P_{t}.

(9d)

Here, (

(10)

where
\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{RIS}}(\cdot)

-

P2:

\displaystyle~\underset{\mathbf{q},\alpha}{\max}~\alpha

(11a)

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}~~\mathrm{SR}\geq\alpha,\,\forall\mathbf{p}_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{u},\,\forall\mathbf{p}_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{e},

(11b)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}\|\mathbf{q}\|_{2}^{2}\leq P_{t}.

The optimal beamformer is obtained using the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Assuming a dominant

\mathbf{q}=\sqrt{P_{t}}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{BS}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{RIS}})

Proof.

Substituting

\mathrm{SR}=\Big[\log\big(\frac{1+\overbrace{|\mathbf{h}_{r,u}\bm{\Gamma}c_{0}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{RIS}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{BS}})|^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{n}}^{\zeta_{u}(\mathbf{p}_{u})}\times\overbrace{|\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{BS}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{RIS}})\mathbf{q}|^{2}}^{\mu}}{1+\underbrace{|\mathbf{h}_{r,e}\bm{\Gamma}c_{0}\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{RIS}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{BS}})|^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{n}}_{\zeta_{e}(\mathbf{p}_{e})}\times\underbrace{|\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{BS}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{RIS}})\mathbf{q}|^{2}}_{\mu}}\big)\Big]^{+},

where

RIS design:
Assuming a fixed beamformer, we maximize the secrecy rate by optimizing the
RIS
phase shifts. The phase shift configuration subproblem is given by:

P3:

\displaystyle~\underset{\bm{\omega},\alpha}{\max}~\alpha

(13a)

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}~~\text{C1:~}\mathrm{SR}\geq\alpha,\,\forall\mathbf{p}_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{u},\,\forall\mathbf{p}_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{e}

(13b)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}\text{C2:~}0\leq[\bm{\omega}]_{n}<\omega_{\mathrm{max}},\forall n.

(13c)

\frac{1+\mathrm{SNR}_{u}}{1+\mathrm{SNR}_{e}}\geq\gamma\Rightarrow(1-\gamma)+(\mathrm{SNR}_{u}-\gamma\mathrm{SNR}_{e})\geq 0.

Here, we can also decompose each

\displaystyle\mathrm{SNR}_{u}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{A}_{u}\mathbf{s},

(15a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SNR}_{e}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{A}_{e}\mathbf{s},

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{P3}}:\quad

\displaystyle~\underset{\bm{\omega},\gamma}{\max}~\gamma

(17a)

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}~~\widehat{\text{C1}},\text{ C2}.

\displaystyle~\underset{\mathbf{S},\gamma}{\max}~\gamma

(18a)

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}~~\widehat{\widehat{\text{C1}}}:\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}(\gamma)\mathbf{S})\geq\gamma-1,\forall\mathbf{p}_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{u},\,\forall\mathbf{p}_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{e},

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{\text{s.t.}}\text{C2: }0\leq[\bm{\omega}]_{n}<\omega_{\mathrm{max}},\forall n,

While constraint C2 is linear in

Lemma 2
.

Let us assume

(21)

Proof.

Recalling
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^{\mathsf{H}}
with
\mathbf{s}=[\mathsf{e}^{\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{1}},\,\cdots,\,\mathsf{e}^{\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{N}}]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}[\mathbf{S}]_{n,i}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{e}^{\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{e}^{-\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{i}}=\mathsf{e}^{\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathsf{e}^{-\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{i}}}{N}

(22)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{\mathsf{e}^{\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}N}{\omega_{\max}}\int_{0}^{\omega_{\max}}\mathsf{e}^{-\mathsf{j}\omega}\mathrm{d}\omega=\frac{\mathsf{e}^{\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}N(1-\mathsf{e}^{-\mathsf{j}\omega_{\max}})}{\mathsf{j}\omega_{\max}},

(23)

where

Lemma 3
.

The constraint
0\leq[\bm{\omega}]_{n}\leq\omega_{\mathrm{max}},

, where
\pi<\omega_{\max}<2\pi
, is equivalent to

\mathrm{Re}([\mathbf{s}]_{n})+\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})\mathrm{Im}([\mathbf{s}]_{n})\leq 1
,
where
[\mathbf{s}]_{n}=\mathsf{e}^{\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}
and
\mathrm{Re}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{Im}(\cdot)
denote the real and imaginary parts, respectively.

Proof.

We start by noting that since
[\mathbf{s}]_{n}=\mathsf{e}^{\mathsf{j}[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}
, we can express
\mathrm{Re}([\mathbf{s}]_{n})+\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})\mathrm{Im}([\mathbf{s}]_{n})
as
\cos([\bm{\omega}]_{n})+\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})\sin([\bm{\omega}]_{n})
. By substituting this into the inequality, it yields that

\displaystyle\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})\sin([\bm{\omega}]_{n})\leq 1-\cos([\bm{\omega}]_{n}).

(24)

Using the known trigonometric equations
\sin([\bm{\omega}]_{n})=2\sin(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})\cos(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})
and
1-\cos([\bm{\omega}]_{n})=2\sin^{2}(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})
, we can transform the inequality into:

\displaystyle 2\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})\sin(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})\cos(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})\leq 2\sin^{2}(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2}).

(25)

We can divide out
\sin(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})
from both sides of the inequality since
\sin(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})\geq 0
for
0\leq[\bm{\omega}]_{n}\leq 2\pi
. Consequently, the inequality simplifies to

\displaystyle\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})\cos(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})\leq\sin(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2}).

(26)

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\textbf{Case 1:}\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})\leq\tan(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2}),\,\,0<[\bm{\omega}]_{n}<\pi,\\
\textbf{Case 2:}\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})\geq\tan(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2}),\,\,\pi<[\bm{\omega}]_{n}<2\pi.\end{cases}

(27)

Case 1 always holds because
\tan(\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2})<0
when
\pi<\omega_{\max}<2\pi
while
\tan(\frac{[\bm{\omega}]_{n}}{2})>0
. Case 2 is satisfied as long as
[\bm{\omega}]_{n}\leq\omega_{\max}
, thus concluding the proof.
∎
