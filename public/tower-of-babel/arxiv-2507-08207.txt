A Dynamic Stackelberg Game Framework for Agentic AI Defense Against LLM Jailbreaking
Zhengye Han and Quanyan Zhu

Abstract
As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in critical applications, the challenge of jailbreaking, where adversaries manipulate the models to bypass safety mechanisms, has become a significant concern. This paper presents a dynamic Stackelberg game framework to model the interactions between attackers and defenders in the context of LLM jailbreaking. The framework treats the prompt-response dynamics as a sequential extensive-form game, where the defender, as the leader, commits to a strategy while anticipating the attacker’s optimal responses. We propose a novel agentic AI solution, the ”Purple Agent,” which integrates adversarial exploration and defensive strategies using Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT). The Purple Agent actively simulates potential attack trajectories and intervenes proactively to prevent harmful outputs. This approach offers a principled method for analyzing adversarial dynamics and provides a foundation for mitigating the risk of jailbreaking.



1
Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) become increasingly integrated into critical applications such as search engines, virtual assistants, code generation tools, and customer service platforms, there has been a growing interest in identifying and exploiting their operational boundaries. In this context, jailbreaking refers to the deliberate manipulation of an LLM to bypass its built-in safety mechanisms, ethical guidelines, and content moderation filters
[
6
]
. Often framed as a prompt engineering challenge or red-teaming task, jailbreaking aims to induce the model to generate restricted or harmful content.

The defense against jailbreaking is becoming a cat-and-mouse game
[
2
,
5
]
. Attackers continuously search for vulnerabilities and prompt-based exploits, while developers attempt to erect guardrails, retrain models, and deploy moderation layers to preempt such behavior. These interactions typically unfold through iterative trial-and-error, often lacking a principled framework to guide defense strategies. This paper seeks to bridge that gap by introducing a game-theoretic framework to formally model the interaction between attackers and defenders. We conceptualize the prompt-response dynamics as a sequential extensive-form game, where the attacker incrementally probes the LLM and the defender responds with strategies aimed at mitigation. The resulting formulation is a dynamic Stackelberg game
[
1
]
, where the defender (leader) commits to a policy while anticipating the attacker’s (follower’s) optimal response. Widely used in many applications
[
4
,
7
,
8
]
, the framework admits a well-defined equilibrium concept that captures the outcome of the recursive structure of optimal play.

However, due to the complexity and high dimensionality of modern LLMs, fully specifying and solving this game is intractable in practice. The complete structure of the interaction tree is not known a priori and must instead be discovered incrementally. In realistic settings, adversaries often engage in an exploratory phase before exploiting vulnerabilities, informed by model responses, trial feedback, and structural priors. This exploration process can be effectively modeled using sampling-based planning algorithms such as Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT)
[
3
]
, which allow an attacker to incrementally expand the space of prompts and identify paths that lead to successful jailbreaks.

By integrating both the exploratory search behavior of the attacker and the anticipatory planning of the defender, our framework provides a unified view of jailbreak dynamics rooted in behavioral and game-theoretic reasoning. This leads naturally to the design of agentic AI defenders, or Purple Agents, which simulate the attacker’s logic as part of their own reasoning architecture. The Purple Agent embodies a hybrid strategy: it proactively evaluates adversarial search trajectories while deploying carefully calibrated defenses, thereby operationalizing “thinking Red to act Blue.”

The rest part of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce a dynamic Stackelberg game formulation that captures the sequential nature of prompt-response interactions, wherein the defender commits to a policy while anticipating the best response of the attacker. Section 3 presents an agentic solution architecture based on RRT, which enables structured exploration of the prompt space. This framework supports distributed deployment and facilitates the synthesis of Purple Agents, defenders capable of internalizing attacker reasoning to proactively identify and secure vulnerable trajectories. In Section 4, we illustrate our approach through a concrete case study. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper and outlines directions for future work.

Sequential Stackelberg Game-Theoretic Framework for LLM Jailbreaking

We consider a two-player extensive-form game with perfect information that models the interaction between an attacker (Player 1) and a defender (Player 2), where the defender is instantiated as an LLM. The attacker attempts to elicit harmful or restricted outputs, while the defender aims to detect, mitigate, or block such adversarial prompts. The game unfolds over
T
rounds of interaction and is defined by the tuple:

\Gamma=(N,V,E,x_{0},(V_{t}^{i})_{t\in[T],\,i\in N},A,H,Z_{T},O_{T},o_{T},u_{T}),

•

x_{0}\in V

•

A

•

T

•

A

For a terminal history
h_{T}\in H_{T}

o_{T}(h_{T})\in O_{T}

u_{1,T}(o_{T}(h_{T}))=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if }o_{T}(h_{T})=\text{Jailbreak},\\

u_{2,T}(o_{T}(h_{T}))=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }o_{T}(h_{T})=\text{Jailbreak},\\
0&\text{if }o_{T}(h_{T})=\text{Safe Interaction},\\
+1&\text{if }o_{T}(h_{T})=\text{Blocked}.\end{cases}

Thus, the game is zero-sum at the terminal stage: for all

u_{1,T}(o_{T}(h_{T}))+u_{2,T}(o_{T}(h_{T}))=0.
Note that both players share the same observable history. The game is played under

and

t-1

After Player 2 takes action
a_{2,t}=s_{2,t}(h_{t-1})

t

h_{t-1}^{1}=h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t}\}.

A pure strategy for Player 1 at round

s_{1,t}:H_{t-1}^{1}\to A_{1,t},

H_{t-1}^{1}

After Player 1 responds with
a_{1,t}=s_{1,t}(h_{t-1}^{1})

h_{t}=h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t},a_{1,t}\}.
Note that although the full history
h_{t}

h_{t-1}

a_{2,t}

Dynamic Programming Formulation.

Let
v_{t,i}(h_{t})
denote the
value of the subgame

h_{t}\in H_{t}
for each player
i\in\{1,2\}

t

T

h_{T-1}\in H_{T-1}

v_{2,T-1}(h_{T-1})
, captures the maximum utility that Player 2 can obtain by acting optimally at round
T

v_{2,T-1}(h_{T-1})=\max_{a_{2,T}\in A_{2,T}}u_{2}\left(o_{T}\left(h_{T-1}\cup\{a_{2,T},\text{BR}_{1,T}(a_{2,T})\}\right)\right),

where
A_{2,T}
is the set of available actions for Player 2 at round
T
, and
o_{T}:H_{T}\to O_{T}
is the terminal outcome function, which assigns a semantic label (e.g.,
Safe Interaction
,
Jailbreak
,
Blocked
) to the complete terminal history
h_{T}\in H_{T}

u_{2}:O_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
maps terminal outcomes to Player 2’s numerical payoffs.

The function
\text{BR}_{1,T}(a_{2,T})\in A_{1,T}
denotes Player 1’s best response at round
T
, given that Player 2 has chosen action
a_{2,T}
. This best response is assumed to be unique and is defined as:

\text{BR}_{1,T}(a_{2,T})=\arg\max_{a_{1,T}\in A_{1,T}}u_{1}\left(o_{T}\left(h_{T-1}\cup\{a_{2,T},a_{1,T}\}\right)\right),

where
u_{1}:O_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is Player 1’s utility function. Let
a_{2,T}^{*}
denote the optimal action for Player 2 that solves equation (1), and let
a_{1,T}^{*}=\text{BR}_{1,T}(a_{2,T}^{*})
denote the corresponding best response from Player 1, as defined in equation (2). The outcome of the interaction at round
T
is thus the action pair
(a_{2,T}^{*},a_{1,T}^{*})
, leading to the terminal history
h_{T}=h_{T-1}\cup\{a_{2,T}^{*},a_{1,T}^{*}\}
.

The corresponding value function for Player 1 at round
T-1
is given by:

v_{1,T-1}(h_{T-1})=u_{1}\left(o_{T}\left(h_{T-1}\cup\{a_{2,T}^{*},a_{1,T}^{*}\}\right)\right),

which represents the optimal utility that Player 1 receives under the induced outcome of the Stackelberg interaction at round
T
. Thus, the value
v_{2,T-1}(h_{T-1})
represents the optimal payoff that Player 2 can secure at the second-to-last round of the game by choosing the best possible action
a_{2,T}
, under the assumption that Player 1 will respond optimally. The resulting terminal history
h_{T}=h_{T-1}\cup\{a_{2,T},\text{BR}_{1,T}(a_{2,T})\}
fully determines the outcome and payoffs.

Similarly, at any round
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, given the history
h_{t-1}\in H_{t-1}

v_{2,t-1}(h_{t-1})

v_{2,t-1}(h_{t-1})=\max_{a_{2,t}\in A_{2,t}}v_{2,t}\left(h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t},\text{BR}_{1,t}(a_{2,t})\}\right).

Here,
A_{2,t}
denotes the set of actions available to Player 2 at round

\text{BR}_{1,t}(a_{2,t})\in A_{1,t}

\text{BR}_{1,t}(a_{2,t})=\arg\max_{a_{1,t}\in A_{1,t}}v_{1,t}\left(h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t},a_{1,t}\}\right).

Similarly, the outcome of round

a_{1,t}^{*}=\text{BR}_{1,t}(a_{2,t}^{*})

v_{1,t-1}(h_{t-1})=v_{1,t}\left(h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t}^{*},a_{1,t}^{*}\}\right),

The function
v_{2,t}(h_{t})
denotes the value of the subgame beginning at the updated history
h_{t}=h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t},a_{1,t}\}

t

v_{2,t-1}(h_{t-1})

a_{2,t}^{*}

a_{1,t}^{*}

H_{t}

s_{2,t}^{*}
and
s_{1,t}^{*}

(s_{2}^{*},s_{1}^{*})
for the entire game.

Definition 1
(Subgame-Perfect Stackelberg Equilibrium (SPSE))

A strategy profile
(s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*})
is a
Subgame-Perfect Stackelberg Equilibrium (SPSE)
if the following conditions hold:

1.

At each round
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
and for every history
h_{t-1}\in H_{t-1}
, the defender (Player 2) selects an action
a_{2,t}^{*}=s_{2,t}^{*}(h_{t-1})\in A_{2,t}
that solves:

a_{2,t}^{*}\in\arg\max_{a_{2,t}\in A_{2,t}}v_{2,t}\left(h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t},\text{BR}_{1,t}(a_{2,t})\}\right),

where
\text{BR}_{1,t}(a_{2,t})=\arg\max_{a_{1,t}\in A_{1,t}}v_{1,t}\left(h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t},a_{1,t}\}\right)
is the unique best response of Player 1 to action
a_{2,t}
.

2.

Player 1 (the attacker) observes the defender’s action
a_{2,t}^{*}
and chooses their best response:

a_{1,t}^{*}=s_{1,t}^{*}(h_{t-1}\cup\{a_{2,t}^{*}\})=\text{BR}_{1,t}(a_{2,t}^{*}).

3.

The full strategy profile
(s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*})
induces a play path through the extensive-form game tree such that, at every proper subgame starting from any history
h_{t-1}
, the continuation of
(s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*})
forms a Stackelberg equilibrium of that subgame; i.e., optimal actions are chosen recursively as in (1) and (2).

This equilibrium concept captures sequential optimality under perfect information, where the leader (Player 2) commits to history-dependent actions first, and the follower (Player 1) best-responds after observing the leader’s move. The equilibrium is subgame-perfect, as optimality holds at every history of the game.

Stackelberg Reasoning for Agentic AI

The Stackelberg game framework provides a principled foundation for modeling agentic AI in adversarial environments. It naturally supports the development of Purple Agents, defensive systems that simulate attacker behavior to formulate robust and anticipatory strategies. A Purple Agent integrates the exploratory initiative of a Red Agent with the strategic caution and constraint of a Blue Agent, effectively bridging the two roles into a single, adaptive defender.

At the heart of this framework lies a leader-follower dynamic: the Purple Agent, acting as the defender, commits to a policy while anticipating the best response of the adversary. This anticipation is not reactive but embedded directly into the agent’s forward reasoning process. By simulating the attacker’s behavior, modeled through RRT-style planning, the Purple Agent is able to forecast potential adversarial paths and intervene before harm can materialize. Rather than merely imitating Red Agent strategies, the Purple Agent internalizes these dynamics to design and implement targeted interventions. At each decision point, it identifies areas of vulnerability, predicts how adversarial expansions might unfold, and proactively introduces safeguards such as guardrails, filters, or redirections to prevent jailbreaking.

The core capability of the Purple Agent is its hybrid reasoning loop. It performs two interwoven tasks: first, it simulates how an attacker might explore the prompt space to find a jailbreak; second, it monitors those exploratory paths for high-risk developments and, upon detection, deploys defensive measures to neutralize the threat. This process relies on two key internal functions. The first is a deployment mechanism triggered when a potential jailbreak is identified—this function enforces preemptive defenses such as blocking, redirecting, or sanitizing prompts. The second function simulates the likely continuation of the adversary’s reasoning from a given prompt over a planning horizon, enabling the defender to assess not only the immediate threat but also the downstream consequences of allowing a query to progress.

Case Study: Purple Agent Avoidance of Jailbreaking

In this section, we present a case study illustrating how a Purple Agent can proactively anticipate and prevent jailbreaks in large language models by extending the Red Agent’s RRT-based exploration with dynamic defensive reasoning and interventions. The Purple Agent begins its operation from a benign root prompt
p_{0}
, continuously monitoring the unfolding interaction tree. As user prompts evolve along the tree, the agent evaluates whether any path exhibits signs of leading toward a jailbreak. If such risk is detected early, before a harmful completion is generated, the agent deploys preemptive defenses. These may include rerouting to safer semantic regions, filtering potentially dangerous responses, or directly rejecting the query. This anticipatory behavior is depicted in Figure
2
(1), where the agent intercepts a risky trajectory before it escalates.

To enhance foresight, the Purple Agent simulates potential adversarial expansions using a function we refer to as
SimulateRedExpansion
. This simulation examines how the Red Agent might incrementally construct new prompts by sampling future branches within a fixed horizon
H
. For instance, it may explore
k=3
candidate continuations at each step to evaluate whether any lead to harmful outcomes. If the simulation reveals that any of these branches have a high probability of resulting in a jailbreak, as shown in Figure
2
(2), the Purple Agent immediately deploys targeted defenses to neutralize the threat.

Following each intervention or simulated threat, the Purple Agent updates its internal defense policy. This policy revision is informed by both preemptive actions and observed outcomes. It fine-tunes its filtering criteria, defense thresholds, and response-generation tactics in real time, maintaining alignment with safety objectives while minimizing false positives. Finally, upon reaching a terminal node, whether due to a successful query, a filtered response, or a blocked interaction, the Purple Agent conducts a post-hoc analysis. If a jailbreak was detected or narrowly averted, the agent refines its defense mechanisms to guard against similar future attempts. If the trajectory proved benign, the agent reuses this information to improve its predictive accuracy. In either case, the
SimulateRedExpansion
routine is rerun to continually improve the agent’s anticipatory capacity and maintain robust protection.

5
Conclusion

Jailbreaking presents a growing threat to the safe deployment of large language models (LLMs), driven by increasingly strategic prompt engineering. To address this, we introduced a dynamic game-theoretic framework based on Stackelberg interactions, capturing the sequential and asymmetric nature of adversarial dynamics. We further developed an agentic AI solution in the form of a Purple Agent, an anticipatory defender that simulates attacker behavior using a combination of extensive-form games and Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT). This enables the agent to identify and proactively harden vulnerable paths. The framework supports red team–informed defenses, targeted adversarial training, and policy refinement. It is also extensible to stochastic environments, multi-agent interactions, and inverse reinforcement learning. By unifying strategic modeling with adaptive planning, our approach offers a principled foundation for building resilient AI defenses against jailbreak threats.
