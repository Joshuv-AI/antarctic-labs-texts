Early mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ADAMTS-5 in an acute ischemia/reperfusion–induced pressure ulcer model, a pilot exploratory animal study
Authors: Mustafa Sağlam, Burcu Yanık, Eralp Erdoğan, Zeynep Sıla Yaşar, Özlem Cemeroğlu, Betül Bakan, Haşim Çakırbay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to determine the messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expression levels of interleukin (IL)-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and A Disintegrin and Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin Motifs (ADAMTS)-5 in an acute ischemia/reperfusion (I/R)-induced pressure ulcer (PU) model, in order to characterize the early inflammatory cytokine profile and explore the potential role of ADAMTS-5 in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling.Patients and MethodsTen BALB/c mice were randomly allocated to control and PU groups. Acute PUs were generated on the dorsal skin using a repeated I/R protocol achieved with ceramic magnetic plaques. Skin biopsies obtained from ulcerated and control areas were examined histologically using hematoxylin-eosin staining. mRNA expression levels of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and ADAMTS-5 were analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR and compared statistically between groups.ResultsCompared with controls, mRNA expression levels of IL-1α, IL-6, IL-17, and ADAMTS-5 were significantly increased in the PU group (p<0.05). In contrast, IL-1β expression showed an increase that did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionThese findings indicate that proinflammatory cytokines and ADAMTS-5 are activated early during PU formation following I/R injury. The observed upregulation of ADAMTS-5, a molecule that has been rarely examined in PU models, suggests a possible role for early ECM degradation in ulcer development. Further experimental and clinical studies are warranted to clarify the relevance of these pathways for PU progression and targeted local therapies.

A pressure ulcer (PU) arises from damage to the underlying tissue due to prolonged, unrelieved pressure. This generally transpires when tissue is subjected to compression between a bony prominence and an external surface for an extended duration.[1, 2] Pressure-induced skin injury can vary from non-blanchable erythema of intact skin to severe ulcers that extend to the bone.[3] Despite advancements in medicine, patient care, and education, PUs remain a significant cause of morbidity and mortality among high-risk patients. Moreover, they impose significant strain on individuals, their families, and the healthcare system.[4] Comprehending the pathophysiology and predisposing factors, identifying at-risk patients for PUs, and avoiding ulcer formation will alleviate the total load on the healthcare system.[3] Consequently, research aimed at diminishing disability, mortality, and healthcare expenditures associated with PUs is highly advocated.[5]

The etiopathogenesis of PUs involves both extrinsic and intrinsic factors. Extrinsic factors include pressure, shear force, friction, local microenvironment, and moisture, while intrinsic factors include immobilization, incontinence, nutritional deficiencies, anemia, circulatory disorders, and neurological diseases.[6] Members of the cytokine family secreted by the immune system, such as interleukins (IL) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), show altered levels in acute and chronic wounds and play critical roles as intrinsic factors in wound healing. Recent clinical and experimental evidence suggests that both local and systemic cytokine profiles may serve as predictive biomarkers for PU severity, progression, and chronicity.[7]

A Disintegrin and Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin Motifs (ADAMTS) constitute a homologous family of zinc-containing enzymes belonging to the MMP family, capable of degrading extracellular matrix (ECM) and basement membrane components. These enzymes participate in wound healing, ECM remodeling, and collagen degradation.[8, 9, 10, 11, 12] In particular, ADAMTS-5 has been suggested to regulate critical tissue repair and ECM turnover processes.[11, 12]

Recent studies indicate that PUs represent a distinct form of sterile ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, characterized by an early and dysregulated inflammatory cytokine response that plays a critical role in tissue damage and impaired healing.[13, 14]

Recent review studies have implicated the IL-17 signaling pathway in the amplification of early inflammatory responses and skin necrosis during pressure-induced I/R injury.[13]

Despite the increasing interest in the role of cytokines in PUs, there is limited evidence regarding the function of ADAMTS-5 in acute PU models, and its contribution to early ECM remodeling remains poorly elucidated.[7] We hypothesized that the early upregulation of ADAMTS-5 together with pro-inflammatory cytokines may contribute to ECM degradation during the initial stages of pressure-induced tissue injury. As PUs represent a pathophysiologically distinct type of wound, studies investigating cytokine and growth factor expression patterns in acute lesions remain limited. In particular, the simultaneous evaluation of classical pro-inflammatory cytokines together with ADAMTS-5 in an acute I/R–induced PU model may help address an important gap in the existing literature. Identifying these early molecular alterations is critical for the development of preventive and targeted local cytokine- and cytokine-antagonist-based therapeutic strategies.

The objective of this study was to determine the messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expression levels of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and ADAMTS-5 in PU tissue to enhance our understanding and facilitate the development of novel local treatment strategies.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design and animals

The study was conducted between July 1, 2013, and July 1, 2014. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Local Animal Ethics Committee of the Republic of Turkey Ministry of Health Ankara Training and Research Hospital, Ministry of Health, Türkiye (meeting no: 0014, decision no: 214, date: 04/06/2013). Twenty-eight-week-old male BALB/c mice were obtained from the Saki Experimental Animal Center (Ankara, Türkiye; License No: 32). The general health status of the animals was checked before the experiment, and they were fed with standard laboratory chow and water ad libitum.

This study was designed as a pilot animal experiment to assess feasibility and obtain preliminary data. Accordingly, the sample size was determined based on ethical considerations and practical feasibility, as commonly recommended for exploratory animal study designs. In the absence of a known expected effect size, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed.[15, 16]

Grouping

The animals were randomly divided into two groups, each consisting of five mice: a control group and an experimental group. In the experimental group, I/R injury was induced on the dorsal skin to create PUs.

Induction of pressure ulcer model

The mouse PU model was established based on the protocol developed by Stadler et al.[14] Mice were fasted for 12 hours prior to anesthesia. General anesthesia was achieved by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (60 mg/kg; Ketalar, Parke Davis) and xylazine (10 mg/kg; Rompun 2%, Bayer).[17] The anesthesia was adjusted to ensure loss of pain reflexes while maintaining spontaneous respiration, and additional doses were administered as needed.

During the procedure, body temperature was monitored rectally and maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad. Oxygen was supplied via a nasal mask at 1.5 L/min. After the procedure, mice were housed at room temperature (20-22 °C).

The dorsal skin of all mice was shaved. In the experimental group, the dorsal skin was gently lifted, and two sterile circular ceramic magnetic plaques (5×12 mm in diameter, 2.4 g, 1000 Gauss magnetic strength) were placed on opposite sides of the pinched skin. This setup produced a consistent pressure of 50 mmHg between the plaques. Pressure was applied for 2.5 hours, followed by a 30-minute reperfusion period. This I/R cycle was repeated five times. After 12.5 hours, biopsies were taken from the ulcerated dorsal skin of the experimental group and the corresponding normal skin of the control group. All animals were sacrificed by cervical dislocation at the end of the experiment.

Histopathological examination

Biopsy specimens were fixed in 10% formalin and processed using a tissue processor. Samples were embedded in paraffin at 60 °C and solidified at -10 °C. Sections 4 µm thick were obtained using a microtome and mounted on slides. After deparaffinization at 80 °C and xylene treatment, slides were stained with hematoxylin for 5-10 minutes, rinsed with distilled water for 10 minutes, and briefly washed under running tap water. Slides were subsequently counterstained with eosin for one minute, washed, dehydrated in graded alcohols (70%, 95%, 100%), and air-dried. One drop of Canada balsam was applied, covered with a coverslip, and left to dry. Prepared slides were examined under a light microscope.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction analysis

Biopsy tissues were homogenized in TriPure Reagent (1 mL per 10 cm2 tissue) using pipetting. After being incubated at 15-25 °C for 5 minutes, 0.2 mL of chloroform was added to every 1 mL of reagent, and the samples were homogenized thoroughly for 15 seconds. The samples were incubated at 15-25 °C for 2-15 minutes and then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 minutes at 2-8 °C. This resulted in three distinct phases. The colorless upper aqueous phase containing total RNA was carefully transferred to a new tube.

To precipitate RNA, 0.5 mL of isopropanol was added to 1 mL of reagent. The mixture was then incubated at 15-25 °C for 5-10 minutes and then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 minutes at 2-8 °C. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, centrifuged at 7,500 g for 5 minutes, air-dried, and then dissolved in 20-50 µL of DEPC-treated water. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically.

Complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA) was synthesized using the Thermo Scientific RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit. For cDNA synthesis, 2 µg of total RNA (10 µL) was used in a 20 µL reaction mixture containing 2 µL 10X reverse transcription (RT) buffer, 0.8 µL 25X dNTP mix, 2 µL random primers, 1 µL MultiScribe™ reverse transcriptase, 1 µL RNase inhibitor, and 3.2 µL nuclease-free water. The reaction was carried out at 25 °C for 10 minutes, 37 °C for 120 minutes, 85 °C for 5 minutes, and held at 4 °C using a MyGenie™ 96 Gradient Thermal Cycler (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea).

Quantitative RT-polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a Rotor-Gene Q system (Qiagen). Each 20 µL PCR reaction mixture contained 5 µL cDNA, 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL forward primer, 1 µL reverse primer, and 3 µL nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling parameters included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 minutes, succeeded by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 seconds and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 60 seconds. The expression levels of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and ADAMTS-5 were normalized to GAPDH and quantified using the 2-ΔΔCt technique. Table 1 displays the primer sequences and corresponding amplicon lengths. RT-PCR analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation.

Samples that could not be tested for mRNA expression due to insufficient RNA quantity or quality were excluded. The number of samples analyzed for each target gene is reported in the corresponding result tables.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. As the data demonstrated approximate normal distribution, differences between the control and experimental groups were evaluated using the Student’s t-test.

Given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample size, statistical analyses were primarily intended to describe group-level trends rather than to provide definitive inferential conclusions.

A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Macroscopic observation

In the experimental group, skin integrity was compromised, evidenced by erythema, surface erosion, and areas of necrosis in regions subjected to I/R injury. Conversely, the skin of the animals in the control group remained healthy and exhibited no discernible macroscopic issues.

H&E staining

Histopathological examination of control group samples revealed normal epidermal thickness, intact dermal architecture, and preserved muscle fibers in the underlying tissue. Mast cells with granular cytoplasm were dispersed across the dermis, in accordance with typical skin histology.

The experimental group had epidermal thinning, loosening, and epithelial degeneration. The dermis exhibited significant interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration, inflammatory infiltration surrounding skin appendages and hair follicles, as well as capillary dilation and congestion. In the muscular layer, edema, loosening, and degeneration of muscle fibers were observed, ranging from central nucleation and substantial degeneration to total loss of muscle fibers. Figure 1 shows some of the most important histological findings from blinded examinations.

RT-PCR analysis of IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and ADAMTS-5 mRNA expression in PU tissues

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis demonstrated distinct differences in cytokine and ADAMTS-5 mRNA expression levels between the experimental and control groups. In line with the exploratory design of the study, fold-changes and mean differences were interpreted alongside p values.

The mean IL-1α level was significantly higher in the experimental group compared to the control group (p<0.001). The mean IL-1α level in the control group was 27×10-10±18×10-10, whereas in the PU-induced experimental group it was 302×10-10±37×10-10 (Table 2).

Although IL-1β mRNA expression showed a 2.4-fold increase in the experimental group compared with controls, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.238). Mean IL-1β expression levels were 25×10-6±39×10-6 in the control group and 60×10-6 ±35×10-6 in the experimental group (Table 2). The lack of statistical significance for IL-1β, despite the observed fold-increase, is likely attributable to high inter-individual variability and the early time point of tissue sampling following I/R injury.

The mean IL-6 level was significantly higher in the experimental group compared to the control group (p<0.001). The mean IL-6 level was 0.199±0.164 in the control group and 2.078±0.662 in the experimental group (Table 3).

The mean IL-17 level was significantly higher in the experimental group than in the control group (p<0.001). The mean IL-17 level was 24×10-12 ±11×10-12 in the control group and 287×10-12 ±29×10-12 in the experimental group (Table 4).

The mean ADAMTS-5 level was significantly higher in the experimental group compared to the control group (p=0.014). The mean ADAMTS-5 level was 0.013±0.007 in the control group and 0.041±0.015 in the experimental group (Table 5).

Summary of results

Overall, IL-1α, IL-6, IL-17, and ADAMTS-5 mRNA expression levels were significantly upregulated in acute PU tissue, whereas IL-1β expression did not differ significantly between groups.

DISCUSSION

In our study, all samples from the PU group exhibited not only dermal alterations but also epidermal and muscular layer changes. In the experimental group, the epidermis showed loosening, edema, thinning, and epithelial degeneration. In the dermis, there was a marked interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration, inflammatory infiltration around skin appendages and hair follicles, capillary dilatation, and congestion. In the muscle layer, muscle fibers demonstrated edema, loosening, and degeneration, ranging from central nucleation and prominent degeneration to complete loss of fibers. These histopathological findings confirm that repeated I/R injury induces multi-layer tissue damage characteristic of acute PU formation. RT-PCR analysis revealed that IL-1α, IL-6, IL-17, and ADAMTS-5 mRNA expression levels were significantly higher in the experimental group compared to the control group. Although IL-1β expression was 2.4-fold higher in the experimental group, this difference was not statistically significant. This lack of statistical significance likely reflects the early time point of tissue sampling and inter-individual variability within small experimental groups.

Cui et al.[18] conducted a study similar to our paradigm, producing PUs in mice using I/R injury. Histologically, there were degenerative ruptures, transverse striations, and infiltrations of granulocytic and lymphocytic cells. Our findings indicate that IL-17 RNA expression, measured by RT-PCR, and protein expression, assessed by Western blot, were significantly elevated in the ulcer group compared to controls. These findings demonstrate that IL-17 is an essential component in the pathogenesis of PUs.

In another study by Barone et al.[19] involving patients with chronic ulcers, IL-1α and the MMP collagenase were measured by ELISA. Both were found to be low in acute PUs but elevated in chronic PUs. Following hydroactive wound dressing treatment, IL-1α and collagenase levels significantly decreased. In a systematic review published in 2023, all included studies reported significantly elevated IL-1α levels in PUs.[8] While these findings primarily relate to chronic wounds, our results indicate that IL-1α upregulation begins at an early stage following I/R injury.

Tsutakawa et al.[20] developed a PU model in rats that is similar to ours. RT-PCR analysis demonstrated significantly increased IL-1β and IL-6 mRNA levels compared to controls. Furthermore, repeated daily intraperitoneal nicotine injections led to significant elevations in IL-1 and IL-6 mRNA levels. In our study, IL-6 expression was significantly elevated, whereas IL-1β did not reach statistical significance. Differences in species, experimental conditions, and the acute timing of biopsy collection may explain these discrepancies. IL-1α and IL-1β are synthesized as precursor proteins in response to inflammation. IL-1β synthesis is induced via stimulation through toll-like receptors (TLRs), whereas IL-1α synthesis does not require such signaling. In sterile inflammatory conditions such as ischemia, inflammation proceeds through IL-1α signaling rather than TLR-mediated pathways. Therefore, IL-1β synthesis cannot occur through TLR activation under ischemic conditions. Instead, IL-1α induces IL-1β synthesis and secretion in such settings. This mechanism explains why IL-1α levels were significantly higher, while IL-1β levels were relatively low in the early phase of our acute model. In later stages of inflammation, IL-1β levels are expected to rise substantially. It is therefore likely that if biopsies had been obtained at a later stage, IL-1β levels would have been significantly higher. In a study by Jiang et al. comparing chronic PUs, acute wounds, and normal controls, IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) mRNA levels were significantly elevated in chronic PUs compared with both acute wounds and controls. IL-1β and TNF-α were also significantly elevated in acute wounds compared to controls.[21] This further supports the role of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α in the chronicity of PUs.

Gohel et al.[22] found no statistical correlation between chronic venous ulcer size and MMP-2 or MMP-9 levels. Conversely, Piatkowski et al.[23] indicated that MMP-2 and MMP-9 levels markedly diminished concomitant with ulcer healing. Our investigation revealed that ADAMTS-5, a member of the MMP family, was markedly increased in acute PU tissue. ADAMTS-5, predominantly investigated in cartilage degradation, has been infrequently assessed in PU models, especially during the acute phase of injury.

Proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 are very important for the healing of acute wounds because they cause inflammation and epithelialization. But keeping these cytokines high for a long time may slow down healing. Excessive metalloproteinase activity may also make it harder for the ECM to change shape. In an acute PU model, the simultaneous increase of proinflammatory cytokines and ADAMTS-5 indicates that the pathways for tissue breakdown are rapidly initiated following the injury. Ohura et al.[24] demonstrated that the application of fibroblast growth factor (FGF) to the skin accelerated the healing of PU by reducing the ulcer’s size, width, and depth, while also expediting epithelialization. Brem et al. [25] utilized local vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in a diabetic ulcer mouse model, resulting in markedly enhanced wound healing. Robson et al.[26] similarly reported that local administration of recombinant human platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) in chronic PUs significantly enhanced fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, neovascularization, and the healing rate.

Earlier research has demonstrated that the localized administration of growth factors, including FGF, PDGF, and VEGF, facilitates ECM synthesis and accelerates the healing process. Our findings indicate that antagonizing cytokines such as IL-1α, IL-6, and IL-17, along with inhibiting MMPs like ADAMTS-5, which are involved in ECM and collagen degradation, may aid in preventing ulcer chronicity and expediting the healing of established chronic PUs.

In the PU mouse model study conducted by Mine et al.[27], intraperitoneal administration of anti-IL-17 demonstrated a reduction in scar tissue formation. The increased cytokine levels observed in our PU samples indicate that cytokine-targeted therapeutic approaches may also be relevant in our model.

In 2025, a study examined the blood profiles of 54 patients with PUs classified as severity 2 to grades 3-4. The researchers found that patients with grade 3-4 PUs had more genes that are linked to inflammation and fewer genes that are linked to the immunomodulatory markers. They suggested that these systemic indicators might clarify the molecular pathways responsible for the chronic progression of this disease, as demonstrated in our study. They also noticed that these markers might help figure out illness severity.[7]

The main limitations of our study were the small sample size, the lack of a priori sample size calculation, and the exclusive evaluation of an acute PU model. As a pilot study with a small number of animals per group, the study was not powered to detect small-to-moderate effects. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and need confirmation in adequately powered future studies. Further large-scale studies comparing acute and chronic PUs are needed to establish cytokine profiles and evaluate the therapeutic potential of local cytokine antagonists or MMP inhibitors.

In conclusion, the expression levels of IL-1α, IL-6, IL-17, and ADAMTS-5 mRNA in the PU tissue, as measured by RT-PCR, were significantly higher in the experimental group than in the control group. This upregulation was observed in an acute I/R-induced PU model. The results of this study may contribute to understanding the cytokine profile involved in the pathogenesis of PUs, particularly by demonstrating increased ADAMTS-5 mRNA expression, a marker that has been scarcely investigated in PU biology and early ECM remodeling. These findings may also help inform the development of innovative local therapeutic approaches and support the potential relevance of early cytokine- and MMP-targeted interventions. Given the acute experimental design and exploratory nature of the study, further animal and clinical investigations are required to clarify the role of these molecular changes in PU progression and chronicity.
