Assessing Knowledge and Attitudes Regarding Celiac Disease and gluten-free Products Among Healthy Blood Donors: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Mohammed AW Almorish, Ahmed ME Elkhalifa, Elsharif A Bazie, Moataz Mohamedalhasan Ali, Muath Ahmed Aldomini, Mohammed Ahmed Alharbi, Reham Bakhsh, Baraa S Quronfulah, Hatim Matooq Badri, Abdullah Ahmed Alghamdi, Ahmad Salah Alkathiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCeliac disease (CD) is an autoimmune condition marked by intestinal mucosa inflammation due to gluten. Currently, the sole effective treatment for CD is a gluten-free diet. knowledge and attitudes towards CD are essential for timely diagnosis and management. This research seeks to explore the knowledge and attitudes of blood donors regarding CD and gluten-free products in Yemen.MethodsA cross-sectional study was executed involving 216 healthy male blood donors in Sana’a, Yemen. The questionnaire used in the study was a reliable survey instrument (with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.71) employed for the purpose of data collection. Demographic information, knowledge, and attitudes regarding CD were gathered through direct questionnaire administration. The questionnaire was divided into three parts: (1) demographic questions on age, sex, marital status, education level, blood groups, and body mass index (BMI); (2) knowledge assessment with 10 items; and (3) an attitude section with 4 questions. Statistical analysis utilized SPSS version 24 (IBM Chicago, IL, USA).ResultsA total of 216 blood donors were analysed. All participants were male, aged between 20 and 52 years. Among the participants, 14.8% exhibited familiarity with CD and gluten-free products. A comprehensive analysis revealed that 3.7% of participants possessed sufficient knowledge, 11.1% exhibited moderate knowledge, whereas 85.2% indicated a lack of knowledge regarding CD and gluten-free products. The vast majority 99.1% of participants demonstrated unfavourable attitudes towards CD and gluten-free products. A significant weak positive correlation was identified between knowledge scores and attitudes towards CD and gluten-free products (r = 0.370, P < 0.001). No correlation was found between socio-demographic factors and blood donors’ knowledge and attitudes regarding CD and gluten-free products.ConclusionThe research revealed a significant lack of awareness and ongoing misconceptions regarding CD within the Yemeni population. These results highlight the critical necessity for focused educational initiatives and enhanced availability of gluten-free products to promote timely diagnosis and efficient disease management.Keywords: Celiac disease, Knowledge, Attitude, Gluten-free products, Blood donors

Introduction

CD is a chronic autoimmune disorder triggered by gluten sensitivity in genetically susceptible individuals, exhibiting a global seroprevalence of 1.4%, with histological features of small intestinal villous atrophy and presenting with both gastrointestinal symptoms, such as diarrhea and weight loss, and extraintestinal manifestations, including anemia and arthritis, frequently coexisting with other autoimmune disorders, ultimately leading to significant distress and diminished quality of life [1, 2].

CD significantly impacts quality of life due to a combination of physical, psychological, social, and economic factors, with women often experiencing a more pronounced decline and the cost and availability of gluten-free products. These clinical and psychosocial burdens reduce work and daily functioning and are moderated but not fully solved by strict gluten-free diet adherence [3].

Given the profound influence of CD on individuals’ lives, it is imperative to enhance public awareness and education regarding this condition, as improved understanding not only elucidates its epidemiological impact across various communities but also facilitates early detection, intervention, and management; thereby enabling the public to better recognize symptoms, seek timely medical counsel, and adhere to a gluten-free diet, which constitutes the primary therapeutic approach for the disorder [3].

The prevalence of CD in Yemen is not explicitly detailed in the available literature, but it is part of a broader trend observed in the Middle East. The prevalence of CD in the region varies significantly, with estimates ranging from 0.1% in Tunisia to 3.2% in Saudi Arabia [4]. Yemen’s prevalence is likely to be similar to other Middle Eastern countries, where the disease is increasingly recognized as common, particularly among high-risk populations such as those with type 1 diabetes, where rates can reach 3–20% [5].

The genetic predisposition to CD among different groups in Yemen and other Middle Eastern countries reveals significant variations influenced by specific HLA-DQ genotypes. Research indicates that Arab populations, including those in Yemen, exhibit a unique genetic profile, primarily characterized by the DQ2.5 and DQ8 genotypes, which are strongly associated with CD susceptibility [6].

Dietary adherence is fundamental in the management of CD. Studies indicate a significant correlation between awareness of CD and the adoption of a gluten-free diet among adults, while also revealing a notable deficiency in physicians’ knowledge regarding its screening and diagnostic criteria, underscoring the imperative for enhanced education across various medical fields [7–10].

To this point, there have been no epidemiological investigations regarding CD conducted within Yemen. The prevalence of CD among individuals exhibiting gastrointestinal symptoms in Yemen has been determined to be considerable, with one investigation reporting a prevalence rate of 3.4% prevalence among pediatric patients at a children’s healthcare facility [11].

These findings suggest that the observed rates may only reflect a small portion of the actual prevalence, underscoring the imperative to enhance public awareness and perceptions to facilitate the adoption of screening and diagnostic services. There exists a notable deficiency of CD-related research in the Eastern region, which accentuates the necessity for additional studies to assess the burden of the disease and the public’s understanding and attitudes, thereby enabling effective allocation of resources. This preliminary study aims to assess public comprehension of CD and gluten sensitivity in Yemen, highlighting the imperative for enhancing awareness of this medical condition.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional study participants were selected from blood donors at the National Center for Blood Transfusion and Research in Sana’a, Yemen, during the period from January to April 2025.

Study Population

Convenience sampling was used to recruit the study participants. The study involved males aged over 18years. The inclusion criteria consisted of all eligible blood donors’ volunteers were consecutively included in the study, in accordance with the following criteria established at baseline: good health, aged between 18 and 60 years and weight greater than 50 kg. Participants who met the inclusion criteria were invited to complete the questionnaire, while those who did not meet the criteria were excluded from participation. Individuals possessing significant physical or mental disabilities that hindered participation were excluded.

Study Sample Size

The sample size was calculated using a formula: n = N/(1 + N (e) 2) with 95% confidence interval level. Where: n = minimum required sample size, N = the target population size was established based on institutional records of December 2024 blood donors (N = 470) and e = allowable error (%) which was set as 0.05. Substituting in the formula: n = 470/1 + 470*(0.05)2 = 216. Thus, minimum of 216 blood donors was required to be enrolled in the study. A sampling procedure was used to recruit subjects who met the inclusion criteria [12].

Questionnaire and Data Collection

The questionnaire was derived from a literature review to facilitate this research [13, 14].

The survey instrument encompassed 14 inquiries derived from an extensive literature review pertaining to the relevant subject matter. The survey instrument incorporated demographic information concerning age, gender, educational attainment, and marital status. It evaluated the level of awareness regarding CD, the understanding of its associated risk factors, the correlation between CD and gluten-free dietary practices, its implications for the quality of daily life, awareness of the gluten-free market, and societal sensitivity towards the condition.

The survey questionnaire was translated and subsequently administered in the Arabic language. The questionnaire underwent a pilot test with 50 adults to evaluate the question clarity in Arabic language, organization, and comprehension prior to data collection. The internal consistency of the questionnaire was evaluated via Cronbach’s alpha, with a threshold of 0.71 deemed satisfactory [15].

The knowledge dimension encompassed 10 items, whereas the attitude dimension comprised 4 items. Within the knowledge dimension, each correct response garnered 1 point, while incorrect or unclear responses received 0 points, yielding a potential score range of 0 to 10 points. In terms of the attitude dimension, a five-point Likert scale was employed; responses were evaluated on a scale of 5 to 1 points (5: highly positive; 4: positive; 3: neutral; 2: negative; 1: highly negative). The sum score range for the attitude dimension was 4 to 20 points. The aggregate of scores for each item within each dimension signified the overall score for that dimension. To categorize the scoring levels, attaining above 50% of the maximum score in each dimension indicated a high level, scores ranging from 25% to 50% were deemed to represent a medium level, and scores falling below 25% were classified as low [16].

A score within the range of 0 to 2points for knowledge signified a deficient level, conversely, a score from 3 to 5 points represented a moderate level of knowledge, and a score between 6 and 10 points was indicative of a good level of knowledge. Regarding attitudes, a score falling between 4 and 12 points denoted a poor attitude, while a score ranging from 13 to 20 points implied a good attitude.

Distribution of the questionnaire was conducted through face-to-face interactions. The interviewers elucidated the research objectives to qualified adult participants meeting the inclusion criteria. The criteria required participants to exhibit a willingness to engage in the survey.

BMI Measurement

BMI is computed with metric units. Weight in kilograms is divided by height in meters squared. The formula is BMI = kg/m². The normal weight classification is defined by a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 kg/m^2; overweight is indicated by a BMI of 25 to 29.9 kg/m^2; and obesity is characterized by a BMI of 30 kg/m^2 or higher [17].

Data Analysis

The data was analyzed using the SPSS version 24 from (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Normality tests.

(Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk) were conducted. Numeric, percentage were gathered to assess the knowledge and attitude of participants towards CD. Chi-square tests were employed to explore the significant relationship between knowledge and attitude. A significance level of p < 0.05 was utilized. Additionally, Pearson correlation tests were conducted to identify any significant correlation between knowledge and attitude scores. In.

accordance with the study questions and hypotheses, the results of the statistical analysis were presented in tables and graphs.

Results

Socio-demographic Characteristics

A total of 216 male participants aged 20–52 was analyzed. The age distribution showed 39.8% were 20–30 years old, 37.0% were 31–40 years, 19.4% were 41–50 years, and 3.7% were over 51 years. In terms of marital status, 83.3% were married, 15.7% single, and 0.9% divorced. A majority of 88.4% were urban residents, while 11.6% were rural.

Educational attainment revealed that 70.8% were university-educated, 20.8% had high school education, 6.0% were preparative, and 2.3% had primary education. The majority, 43.5%, were classified as overweight, 32.4% as normal weight, and 24.1% as obese. Blood type distribution indicated that 54.2% had O+, 30.1% had A+, and other blood groups (B+, O-, AB-, A-) comprised 7.4%, 5.1%, 1.9%, and 0.9%, respectively (Table 1).

Discussion

CD is characterized by an immune response to gluten, can lead to severe gastrointestinal health issues if left unmanaged. To the best of our knowledge, this investigation represents the first scholarly endeavor to analyze the Knowledge and attitude of blood donors concerning CD within the context of Yemen.

In our study, only 14.8% of participants exhibited prior knowledge of CD, revealing a pronounced knowledge gap within the Yemeni population compared to awareness levels of 44–82.1% in other studies [13, 14]. In Central Europe, healthcare practitioners averaged 50.9% on CD knowledge assessments [18]. This deficiency in understanding CD, which parallels trends in Western nations, underscores the urgent need for targeted educational initiatives to enhance regional awareness and improve health outcomes for affected individuals.

In this study, it is important to note that 85.2% of blood donors displayed significant unawareness of CD. Awareness of CD varies significantly, as evidenced by Bulgaria’s 94% recognition of its chronic status yet only 26.7% understanding its autoimmune aspect, Kazakhstan’s 59.4% of physicians exhibiting poor knowledge on symptoms and treatments, and Iran’s low specialist performance in diagnosis and management [19–21]. Highlighting the imperative for specialized educational strategies and training to mitigate regional inequalities in congenital disorder awareness and management.

In the present study, 99.1% of participants exhibited negative attitudes towards CD and gluten-free products. A recently research revealed that 69.5% of respondents possessed unfavorable attitudes toward CD management. Negative attitudes were significantly linked to demographic variables like age and occupation, but not sex [13]. In Turkey, a survey revealed that public awareness and sensitivity towards CD and gluten-free products were insufficient, with a large portion of the population unaware of the disease and its dietary requirements [14]. Despite prevalent negative perceptions of CD and gluten-free products, it is crucial to examine these attitudes within a broader context, as the challenges faced by CD patients are complex, necessitating a multifaceted approach that enhances public awareness, elevates the nutritional quality and accessibility of gluten-free options, and offers psychological support, thereby potentially transforming public perception and enhancing the quality of life for those affected by CD.

In this study, only 11.1% affirmed the availability of gluten-free products locally, merely 3.7% of participants recognized gluten-free product labeling, while only 6.5% considered these products affordable; additionally, 13.9% reported accessibility challenges, and 9.3% noted a lack of variety in gluten-free options. This perception is consistent with research indicating that cost and availability of gluten-free foods remain challenges for CD patients [22]. Contrastingly, a study found that 49% identified gluten-free items in stores, with only 10.3% viewing them as affordable, and 41.7% acknowledging the difficulties faced by celiac patients [23]. The limited supply of gluten-free products in Poland has led to dissatisfaction among patients and caregivers, reflecting significant variability in availability across supermarkets [24]. A recent study indicating only 233 gluten-free items versus 24 gluten-containing ones, while regional disparities in gluten-free product standards are noted globally [25]. A study from China exhibiting lower contamination rates in imports than domestically produced items [26]. Conversely, in Greece, the scarcity of processed gluten-free options for celiac patients is primarily due to inadequate food labeling and manufacturers’ hesitance to register in national databases [27]. Although the accessibility and adherence to gluten-free products are improving in certain areas, substantial obstacles persist, including elevated costs and limited product availability in underdeveloped markets, alongside labeling compliance and cross-contamination issues, which underline the need for enhanced regulatory oversight and consumer education to safeguard the safety and accessibility of such products for those with CD.

Limited public and healthcare understanding contributes to inadequate CD awareness. Prior research indicates primary care physicians often lack knowledge about CD symptoms and management. Such ignorance may delay diagnosis and treatment, causing prolonged distress and complications for patients. Despite increased awareness, many patients remain undiagnosed, with estimates suggesting 95% of affected individuals lack appropriate diagnosis [28, 29]. This study underscores participants’ low knowledge levels, highlighting the urgent need for targeted educational programs for public and healthcare professional awareness.

The significant a weak positive correlation between knowledge and attitude scores indicates that enhancing knowledge about CD may positively affect attitudes toward the disease. This aligns with Jyoti et al.‘s study, which highlights that structured educational programs improve understanding and compliance with dietary guidelines among CD patients [30]. Additionally, research indicates that public health initiatives, such as community awareness and nutrition education campaigns, can effectively enhance knowledge and alter negative perceptions of CD and gluten-free diets [19].

This investigation provides significant insights into CD awareness in Yemen, yet it is constrained by limitations such as a cross-sectional design that inhibits causal inference and an inadequate sample size. The study’s results are limited by a sample comprising only males, which hinders the generalizability of the conclusions and their applicability to females; thus, to enhance the credibility of these findings, it is essential to pursue additional research with diverse demographics for a comprehensive understanding of the topic.

Future research should utilize longitudinal and mixed-methods to investigate cultural obstacles and validate measurement instruments for Arab populations to enhance comprehension of CD knowledge differences among diverse Yemeni groups.

Conclusion

The study revealed significant gaps in knowledge and ongoing misconceptions about CD within the Yemeni population. These results highlight the urgent necessity for focused educational initiatives and enhanced availability of gluten-free products to support timely diagnosis and effective condition management.
