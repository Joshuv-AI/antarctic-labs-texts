Differential Effects of Psychogenic Stress on Bone Morphology and Metabolism in Wistar Rats
Authors: Sangun Lee, Haruka Tatehana, Takumi Saito, Issei Sugimoto, Yugo Kimura, Ikue Kondo, Atsunori Itagaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychogenic stress (PS) significantly contributes to the development of various pathological conditions, including psychiatric, cardiovascular, and metabolic disorders. Furthermore, PS has been shown to influence bone health. In this study, we aimed to elucidate PS effects on bone morphology and metabolism during distinct developmental periods in male Wistar rats.MethodsPS was induced by combined restraint and water immersion stress for 3 hr per day over 4 weeks. In the end of the experiment, femora and tibiae were analyzed to determine bone length, cross-sectional area, and bone mass. Internal organ weights were also recorded. Bone-specific alkaline phosphatase and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase activities were measured as markers of bone formation and resorption, respectively. Data were analyzed using the Student’s t-test and Scheffé’s multiple comparison test.ResultsPS significantly reduced body and organ weights at all developmental stages, indicating systemic growth suppression. The degree of stress-induced alterations in bone metabolism differed depending on the developmental stage: PS during growth impaired bone formation and enhanced resorption.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that PS exerts stage-specific effects on skeletal development and maintenance. The results highlight the importance of considering developmental stage in evaluating the impact of PS on bone health and suggest potential mechanisms linking chronic stress exposure to altered bone metabolism and increased risk of osteoporosis.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, psychogenic stress (PS) has been increasingly recognized as a significant contributor to a wide range of stress-related disorders, including psychiatric illnesses, cardiovascular diseases, and metabolic abnormalities.[1,2] Accumulating evidence indicates that PS exerts deleterious effects on health across all age groups, and PS impact during critical periods of growth and development is particularly concerning.[3,4]

Several studies have reported that PS adversely affects bone health, resulting in alterations in bone mass, structure, and strength.[1,5,6] However, the mechanisms by which PS influences bone morphology and metabolism at different developmental stages remain incompletely understood. Both aging and chronic stress have been shown to disrupt neuroendocrine and immune homeostasis, thereby impairing the body’s capacity to maintain internal balance.[7,8] A central component of the stress response is the activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, which leads to elevated glucocorticoid secretion and subsequent dysregulation of the immune and metabolic processes.[9,10] Glucocorticoids play a critical role in bone metabolism by regulating the balance between bone formation and resorption.[11,12]

Sustained elevation of stress hormones, as observed under chronic PS, has been shown to suppress osteoblast activity while promoting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, ultimately resulting in bone loss.[13–16] Moreover, chronic PS has been associated with increased production of inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which further exacerbate stress-induced impairment of bone remodeling.[15,16]

A substantial body of clinical and experimental research has shown that PS and stress-related psychiatric conditions are associated with altered endocrine and immune function, contributing to reduced bone mass and increased fracture risk.[17–19] Age clearly influences these effects of PS, as developmental stage modulates both endocrine responsiveness and skeletal adaptation to stress. In older individuals, increased oxidative stress and elevated sympathetic nervous system activity further compromise bone quality, whereas during growth, stress-related hormonal disturbances interfere with normal bone development. [20,21]

Despite these observations, comparative studies examining the effects of PS on bone morphology and metabolic activity across different developmental stages remain limited. Therefore, in the present study, we investigated the effects of PS on bone structure and turnover in distinct age groups, with a particular focus on stress hormone levels and bone metabolic markers. Elucidating age-dependent skeletal responses to PS may provide critical insights into the regulation of bone growth and maintenance throughout development.

METHODS

This study utilized bone tissue obtained from rats used in our previous experiment.[20] The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Aomori University of Health and Welfare (Animal ethics no. 20001). All experimental procedures strictly adhered to the principles of the 3Rs, Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement, which are internationally recognized ethical standards for the humane use of animals in scientific research.

By reusing tissue samples from the prior study, the present research minimized the need for additional animal experimentation, thereby meeting the ethical obligation to reduce the number of animals used in biomedical research while ensuring scientific validity and reproducibility.

Materials

The experimental animals were male Wistar rats (CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) representing five distinct life stages: growth period (GP; 3 weeks old, N=12), early adulthood (EA; 8 weeks old, N=12), adulthood (A; 6 months old, N=12), middle age (MA; 12 months old, N=12), and old age (OA; 24 months old, N=12). Within each age group, animals were randomly assigned to either a control (CON) group or PS-treated group.

Protocol of PS

PS was induced using the restraint water-immersion stress protocol originally developed by Lu et al. [22] Lee et al. [23] and Ohta et al. [24,25]. This method combines water aversion with physical restraint to elicit psychological stress while avoiding physical injury. Each rat was placed in a perforated acrylic cylinder, immobilized, and partially submerged in water maintained at 23°C to a depth of 2 cm. Stress exposure was conducted once daily between 9:00 am and 12:00 pm for three consecutive hours per session over four weeks.

Body weight and blood draw

Body weight was measured weekly from the beginning to the end of the study, at a consistent time before each stress session. Blood samples were collected from the tail vein on the first and final day of the experiment. Whole blood was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min to separate plasma, which was then stored at −80°C in an ultra-low temperature freezer until analysis.

Morphology

1) Organ and bone collection

At the end of the experimental period, following terminal blood collection, a mixed anesthetic solution (total volume, 25 mL) containing medetomidine (0.076 mg/mL), midazolam (0.08 mg/mL), and butorphanol (0.10 mg/mL) diluted in saline was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 0.5 mL per 100 g of body weight. Upon confirmation of deep anesthesia, the thoracic cavity was opened, and the animal was euthanized via transcardial perfusion with physiological saline through the left ventricle. The thymus, heart, pancreas, stomach, left adrenal gland, left kidney, femur, and tibia were harvested. Connective tissue and extraneous material were carefully removed, and the organs were gently blotted dry to remove surface moisture. Wet weights were measured using an analytical balance (MC210S; Sartorius, Goettingen, Germany).

2) Bone length and cross-sectional analysis

The right femur and tibia were dissected free of soft tissues, including muscles, ligaments, and tendons. The maximum bone length was measured using a digital ABS Digimatic Caliper CD-AX (Mitutoyo Co., Kawasaki, Japan) with a precision of 0.5 mm. A transverse cross section was prepared at the midpoint (50% of the total bone length) by making a perpendicular incision, and the cut surface was photographed with a digital camera. Cross-sectional images were analyzed using WinROOF image analysis software (Windows version; MITANI Co., Tokyo, Japan). Parameters measured included total cross-sectional area, cortical bone area, and perimeter.

3) Bone weight (Wet, Dry, Ash)

The left femur and tibia were cleaned to remove residual connective tissue and surface moisture, and their wet weights were recorded. The samples were then dried at 105°C for 36 hr in a convection oven (NDO-420; Tokyo Rikakikai Co., Tokyo, Japan) to determine the dry weight. The dried bones were subsequently ashed at 600°C for 24 hr in an electric furnace (EPTR-25K; Isuzu Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Niigata, Japan) to obtain the ash weight.[26,27]

Biochemistry

1) Bone metabolism markers

Serum bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BAP) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRACP) activity levels were measured as biochemical markers of bone metabolism using a TRACP-ALP assay kit (MK301; Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan).

2) BAP and TRACP assays

Each well was washed once with 200 μL of physiological saline, and the wash solution was completely aspirated. Subsequently, 5–50 μL of the cell extraction buffer was added to each well and gently pipetted to lyse the cells. Then, 50 μL of 0.2 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 9.5) containing 1 mM MgCl2 was added to the plate for ALP measurement, or 50 μL of 0.5 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.2) was added to the plate for TRACP measurement. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 15–60 min. The enzymatic reaction was stopped, and color development initiated by adding 50 μL of 0.5 N NaOH to each well. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 28; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Means and standard deviations were calculated for all measured parameters. In addition, the percentage change from baseline to post-experiment was calculated. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to analyze pre- and post-experimental differences in bone metabolic markers, while between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted. Statistical significance was set at P value of less than 0.05.

RESULTS

Body weight

As shown in Figure 1, body weights in the EA, A, MA, and OA stages were significantly higher in the CON group than in the PS group starting from week 1 of the experiment relative to baseline measurements (all P<0.05). In contrast, during the GP stage, the CON group exhibited significantly higher body weights than the PS group beginning in week 2 (all P<0.05) (Fig. 1).

At the end of the experiment, the PS group showed a significant reduction in body weight (213.2±26.28 g) compared to the CON group (270.7±10.13 g), representing a 21.2% decrease (all, P<0.05). Specifically, at the EA stage, the PS group (312.5±18.19 g) had a significantly lower body weight than the CON group (371.3±14.21 g), corresponding to a 15.8% decrease (P<0.001). Similarly, at the A stage, the PS group (389.0±15.30 g) was 14.4% lower than the CON group (454.3±21.50 g), and at the MA stage, the PS group (475.7±18.77 g) was 14.1% lower than the CON group (553.5±17.71 g; all P<0.001). At the OA stage, the PS group (528.0±26.63 g) was also significantly lower by 13.5% compared to the CON group (610.3±25.32 g; P< 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Organ weights

The wet weight of the thymus decreased with age across the GP, EA, and A stages. Compared with the organ weights in the CON group, the PS group had significantly lower thymus weight at all developmental stages, ranging from 38.2% to 44.5% (P<0.05). Similarly, the wet weights of the stomach (−11.5% to −28.4%), spleen (−14.4% to −32.7%), and kidneys (−11.6% to −27.6%) were significantly lower in the PS group than in the CON group at all developmental stages. Heart weight was significantly reduced in the PS group only at the GP (−15.0%, P<0.01) and EA (−11.5%, P<0.05) stages. In contrast, adrenal weight was significantly higher in the PS group than in the CON group at all stages (18.3 to 37.0%; P<0.05) (Table 1).

Bone length

The femur length was significantly shorter in the PS group than in the CON group at both the GP and EA stages. Specifically, at the GP stage, the PS group (29.0±1.05 mm) showed a 7.1% reduction compared with the CON group (31.2±0.43 mm; P<0.001). At the EA stage, the femur length in the PS group (34.2±0.85 mm) was 4.2% shorter than in the CON group (35.7±0.39 mm; P<0.01). Similarly, the tibial length was significantly lower in the PS group than in the CON group. At the GP stage, the PS group (32.6±1.26 mm) exhibited a 7.9% reduction compared with the CON group (35.4±0.71 mm), while at the EA stage, the PS group (37.8±0.45 mm) was 4.3% shorter than the CON group (39.5±0.47 mm) (P<0.001 for both) (Fig. 2).

Bone weight

At the GP stage, the PS group exhibited significantly lower femur weights compared with the CON group: wet weight (−19.2%, P<0.01), dry weight (−20.5%, P<0.01), and ash weight (−16.8%, P<0.05). At the EA stage, femur wet weight (−8.5%), dry weight (−10.9%), and ash weight (−11.8%) were also significantly reduced in the PS group compared with the CON group (P<0.05 for all).

Similarly, the tibia showed significant reductions in the PS group. At the GP stage, the PS group demonstrated lower wet (−18.3%, P<0.01), dry (−18.4%, P<0.01), and ash (−13.7%, P<0.05) weights compared with the CON group. At the EA stage, wet (−11.2%, P<0.01), dry (−11.2%, P<0.05), and ash (−10.5%, P<0.05) weights were also significantly lower in the PS group. No significant differences in femur or tibia weights were observed between the PS and CON groups at the A, MA, or OA stages (Table 2).

Cross-sectional area

The cross-sectional area at the mid-diaphysis (50% of the total bone length) of both the femur and tibia increased progressively with age. However, at the OA stage, the PS group exhibited significantly smaller cross-sectional areas than the CON group: −20.1% for the femur and −18.7% for the tibia (P<0.05 for both) (Table 3).

Biochemistry

1) Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)

The presence of ALP activity was confirmed across all developmental stages. Baseline ALP levels demonstrated no significant differences between the CON and PS groups. Within the CON group, ALP levels increased significantly only at the GP stage, reaching 206.4% (94.4±30.08→289.1±149.2; P<0.05). In contrast, in the PS group, ALP activity showed substantial increases at multiple developmental stages: at the GP stage, 101.8% (103.0±53.56→207.9±65.69; P<0.05); at the EA stage, 94.4% (88.6± 6.20→172.2±27.75; P<0.001); and at the OA stage, 143.1% (173.8±32.7→422.5±180.07; P<0.05) (Fig. 3).

2) TRACP

Similar to ALP, baseline TRACP levels exhibited no significant differences between the CON and PS groups across all developmental stages. Within the CON group, TRACP levels increased significantly at specific stages: at the A stage by 187.5% (0.200±0.014→0.575±0.185; P<0.01), and at the MA stage by 70.8% (0.212±0.031→0.362±0.127; P<0.05). In contrast, the PS group showed marked increases in TRACP activity at several stages: GP (71.4%, from 0.227± 0.018 to 0.389±0.136; P<0.05), A (150.0%, from 0.206± 0.008 to 0.515±0.276; P<0.05), and OA (301.6%, from 0.316±0.028 to 1.269±0.438; P<0.01), each demonstrating a statistically significant increase (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

The present study investigated the effects of PS on multiple biological parameters across five developmental stages, from juvenile to OA. Whereas previous studies have reported stress-related skeletal changes at specific life stages in murine models,[6,28] the distinctive feature of the present study is the systematic analysis of biochemical and morphological bone parameters across several ages within a single experimental framework. This design enabled direct comparison of stress susceptibility across developmental stages under standardized conditions, thereby providing novel insights into the age-dependent vulnerability and adaptive capacity of the skeletal system.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports,[29,30] which demonstrated that body and organ weights increase rapidly during the GP, followed by stabilization in adulthood and decline in OA. In contrast, in the PS group, increases in body and organ weights were attenuated during the growth and early adult phases, followed by substantial weight loss later in life. Stress-induced anorexia has been extensively documented as a contributing factor to weight loss,[31,32] and the present findings suggest that PS interacts synergistically with aging to exacerbate catabolic processes. Notably, after the accelerated somatic growth phase, PS was more closely associated with body weight reduction, an effect that became increasingly pronounced with age. In addition to changes in body weight, significant atrophy of the thymus, spleen, and kidneys was observed in the PS group across all developmental stages. Thymic involution was particularly pronounced, corroborating earlier findings that PS accelerates thymic atrophy. [33,34] In contrast, heart mass growth was comparatively more resistant to PS, persisting beyond the juvenile phase. These observations indicate organ-specific differences in stress sensitivity. Additionally, the adrenal glands were enlarged in all PS-exposed groups, reflecting the physiological impact of stress. Overall, these findings suggest that PS distinctly affected the development and maintenance of organs’ sizes, depending on both the development stage and intrinsic organ physiology.

The regulation of bone metabolism is a complex process involving intricate interactions between mechanical loading and endocrine signaling. Given the controlled housing and activity conditions in this study, it is reasonable to hypothesize that endocrine mechanisms played the dominant role. During the growth and early adult phases, both bone length and mass in the PS group were significantly lower than those in the CON, indicating that PS interferes with skeletal development. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that stress impairs not only bone morphology but also systemic metabolic functions, including hepatic dysfunction caused by oxidative stress.[35,36] Immature skeletal tissues may be particularly vulnerable to endocrine disruption, as rapid growth requires tightly coordinated hormonal regulation. Notably, reductions in the bone cross-sectional area were mainly observed in the OA group, whereas suppression of bone length was most pronounced during earlier developmental stages. Although differences did not reach the set level of statistical significance in all comparisons, PS-treated animals tended to have lower bone mass across most age groups, suggesting that stress-related bone loss cannot be attributed solely to aging. Circulating glucocorticoid levels are known to increase with age,[37] and age-related hypercortisolemia may potentiate the skeletal effects of PS, thereby contributing to cortical bone atrophy in older animals.

At baseline, no differences in ALP or TRACP activity were observed between the groups, confirming that initial conditions were comparable. Following PS exposure, both markers were significantly elevated across multiple developmental stages, indicating enhanced bone turnover. These results suggest that PS induces a state of accelerated bone remodeling, particularly during the transition from growth to juvenile stages, when skeletal plasticity remains high.

The equilibrium between osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-mediated resorption is pivotal to skeletal integrity. PS activates the HPA axis, leading to increased cortisol secretion, which in turn upregulates receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) and suppresses osteoprotegerin (OPG) expression levels.[38,39] Cortisol has been shown to induce apoptosis in osteoblasts and osteocytes,[40,41] inhibit osteoblast differentiation, and prolong osteoclast survival.[42,43] Furthermore, the suppression of parathyroid hormone-related protein has been reported to elevate circulating parathyroid hormone levels, thereby further reducing OPG expression and promoting bone loss.[44,45] In the present study, adrenal hypertrophy was consistently observed in the PS group across all developmental stages, supporting the hypothesis of the sustained HPA axis activation. Both osteoblasts and osteoclasts express adrenergic receptors,[46] and sympathetic nervous system activation suppresses osteoblast proliferation while enhancing RANKL-mediated osteoclastogenesis.[47] Experimental evidence indicates that stress-induced alterations in autonomic nervous system activity contribute directly to bone loss,[48] whereas neuropeptide signaling may modulate this effect.[49] The observed stage-specific differences in bone turnover markers in our study may therefore reflect age-dependent variations in autonomic regulation and neuroendocrine responsiveness.

Oxidative stress represents another critical mechanistic link between PS and skeletal pathology. PS has been shown to induce systemic oxidative stress, which can disrupt endocrine and immune homeostasis through inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and HPA axis modulation. [50,51] In our previous analysis, oxidative stress was significantly elevated across all age groups subjected to PS.[20] This persistent oxidative burden may amplify endocrine dysregulation and inflammatory signaling, thereby exacerbating imbalances in bone remodeling. Sex hormones also play a pivotal role in maintaining bone homeostasis. As reported in,[52] estrogen preserves cortical and trabecular bone mass, whereas testosterone is essential for trabecular bone maintenance in males. Chronic PS has been associated with reductions in circulating estrogen and testosterone levels,[53,54] which may contribute to stress-induced bone loss. Furthermore, elevated cortisol levels have been shown to increase leptin secretion.[55,56] Although leptin has been linked to reduced trabecular bone volume,[57,58] some evidence suggests it may exert protective skeletal effects under certain conditions.[59] These discrepancies highlight the multifaceted, context-dependent actions of leptin, particularly its divergent effects on bone metabolism between central and peripheral systems.

A salient finding of this study is the observation of bone loss during the growth phase, which correlated minimally with body weight changes. This suggests a direct effect of PS on the skeletal tissue, independent of mechanical unloading or nutritional status. In contrast, bone alterations observed during the OA may reflect a convergence of stress-related endocrine dysregulation, chronic inflammation, and age-associated metabolic decline. Distinguishing between these mechanisms is important when interpreting developmental differences in stress-induced bone pathology.

Although the animal model used in our study allowed for a controlled investigation of PS-induced skeletal changes across the lifespan, it did not fully capture the complexity of human PS. Previous models have been criticized for eliciting relatively modest HPA axis activation,[60] and it remains unclear whether our model sufficiently addressed this limitation. Further studies incorporating measurements of serum corticosterone, glucocorticoids, IL-6, and TNF-α would help clarify the magnitude and relevance of neuroendocrine activation. Moreover, intervention studies examining stress mitigation or exercise could enhance the translational relevance of this model and guide strategies for preserving bone health under chronic psychological stress.

In summary, this study demonstrates that chronic PS significantly impairs organ and bone development during the GP and adversely affects bone metabolism. In contrast, its impact appears limited during adulthood, when organs and bone structures have fully matured. However, notable fluctuations in bone metabolic markers were observed in older animals. These findings indicate that PS hinders organ development in immature stages and may contribute to age-related bone resorption with variability depending on individual physiological responses.
