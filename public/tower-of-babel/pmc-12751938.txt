Incidence of loss to follow‑up among East African adults on antiretroviral therapy: a systematic review and meta‑analysis
Authors: Mulugeta Shegaze Shimbre, Abebe Gedefaw Belete, Manaye Yihune Teshale, Nigus Kabtu Belete, Abakundana Nsenga Ariston Gabriel, Wei Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with human immunodeficiency virus receive antiretroviral treatment regardless of their clinical stage or CD4 count. While the “Universal Test and Treat” approach offers benefits, loss to follow-up remains a significant issue in sub-Saharan Africa, especially in East African countries, hindering treatment program goals. Due to inconsistent findings in previous studies, policymakers and healthcare providers struggle to implement effective interventions. This study aims to provide a pooled incidence of loss to follow-up among East African adults on antiretroviral therapy.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted among East African adults on antiretroviral therapy. Literature was sought from PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane, and Web of Science. Additionally, we conducted hand-searching of reference lists and citation tracking of included studies. The review encompassed both retrospective and prospective follow-up articles published in English. The data extraction was completed using Covidence software and later transferred to Stata™ Version 18.0 for further analysis. Heterogeneity was evaluated with the aid of forest plots with the I2 test. To explore the sources of heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses, meta-regression, assessments of publication bias, and sensitivity analyses. The incidence rate of loss to follow was estimated via a random-effects meta-analysis model.ResultsThe current meta-analysis examined 20 studies that satisfied the eligibility criteria out of the 3011 studies examined involving a total of 54,388 adults receiving antiretroviral therapy. The pooled analysis of these 20 studies established an overall incidence of loss to follow-up of 9.67 per 100 person-years of observation (95% CI, 7.84, 11.93) with I2 of 98.5% and P-value < 0.001. With sub-group analysis, the multicountry study that includes Rwanda, Kenya, and Tanzania had the highest incidence rate of 16.7 per 100 person-years, while lower in Ethiopia with an incidence rate of 8.9 per 100 person-years.ConclusionsAmong adult East Africans on antiretroviral therapy, the loss to follow-up rate is projected to fail to meet the requirement set by the UN. Such insight can assist in formulating policies by identifying important factors that can help retain adults with HIV in lifelong antiretroviral treatment services.Systematic review registrationCRD42024568267Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-03025-5.

Background

In 2023, an estimated 39.9 million individuals globally were living with HIV, of whom 38.6 million were adults aged 15 years or older. In East and Southern Africa, 20.8 million people were living with the disease, and 400,000 adults aged 15 years or older were newly infected. During the same year, approximately 1.3 million individuals became newly infected with HIV, while 630,000 people died from AIDS-related illnesses [1]. In December 2020, UNAIDS established the 95-95-95 targets to be achieved by 2030. These goals aim to ensure that 95% of individuals living with HIV know their status, 95% of those diagnosed are on antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 95% of those receiving treatment attain viral suppression. Meeting these interconnected milestones would result in at least 86% of the global population living with HIV achieving viral suppression by 2025 [2].

Effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) for HIV successfully suppresses viral replication, improves or sustains immune function, and reduces both morbidity and mortality. This enables individuals living with HIV (PLWH) to achieve a life expectancy comparable to that of the general population while also contributing to the prevention of new infections. As of 2023, a total of 30.7 million individuals worldwide were receiving antiretroviral therapy, with 77% of adults aged 15 years and older living with HIV having access to treatment. These figures highlight the continued global efforts to combat the HIV epidemic and expand access to life-saving care. Similarly, in 2022, 83% of people in East and Southern Africa were on ART [3]. Nevertheless, despite the remarkable achievements of ART, challenges such as patient attrition and loss to follow-up (LTFU) persist as significant obstacles to the sustained success of ART scale-up efforts [4].

Loss to follow-up (LTFU) refers to individuals who discontinue their HIV care or antiretroviral therapy (ART) for more than 3 months or longer after the last scheduled visit [5]. LTFU poses a critical challenge in the effective management of HIV, particularly in resource-limited settings. Its consequences are profound, impacting both individuals and communities. For individuals, LTFU is associated with poorer health outcomes and increased mortality, while at the community level, it elevates the risk of HIV transmission due to unsuppressed viral loads [6]. Studies conducted across various countries in East Africa, a region significantly affected by HIV [7], have reported varying rates of loss to follow-up (LTFU) among individuals receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART). The LTFU rate was 26.43% in Tanzania [8], 25.3% in Burundi [9], 20.2% in Uganda [10], and 40.6% in Kenya [11]. Common factors associated with LTFU included age, gender, marital status, the type and line of ART regimen, viral load suppression status, WHO clinical stage, and duration of ART [8–11]. Although previous individual studies in East Africa have examined the rate of LTFU, their findings have been inconsistent and varied across regions, making it challenging to determine an accurate overall estimate to make an informed decision on the area. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) aim to address these discrepancies by providing a unified and precise estimate of the loss to follow-up (LTFU) rates across East Africa. This comprehensive assessment will contribute to a more accurate understanding of LTFU patterns in the region, enabling the effective monitoring and evaluation of national and international policies and programs. Furthermore, the findings will support the development of evidence-based interventions aimed at achieving the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to know the combined incidence of LTFU among East African adults undergoing on ART. The study protocol for this Systematic review and Meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with ID of CRD42024568267. For the dissemination of our results, we adhered to the PRISMA guidelines (Supplementary Table 1).

Study setting

As per the list provided by the African Development Bank, we included the following East African countries in our research (Burundi, Comoros, Djibouti, Ethiopia, Eritrea, Kenya, Rwanda, Seychelles, Somalia, South Sudan, Sudan, Tanzania, and Uganda) (https://www.afdb.org/en/countries/east-africa).

Participants

We included all cohort research articles conducted on the incidence of LTFU among East African Adults on ART and published in English from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024.

Searching strategies

We utilized a step-by-step search strategy in this review. Primarily, electronic databases that comprise health system research including PubMed, Embase (Ovid), Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science were used to search for relevant studies. Afterward, hand-searching of reference lists and citation tracking of included studies were screened for additional studies. The search was performed by using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), and Boolean search operators “OR”, and “AND”. The main keywords utilized for the article search included the following: incidence, loss to follow‑up, antiretroviral therapy, adults, and East African countries. These keywords were selected because they aligned with the core concepts of the review objective. Various combinations and variations of these keywords were employed. We explored synonyms for each search term in the literature to create a comprehensive search strategy. The full search strategy used across the 5 databases is available as a supplementary file (Supplementary Table 2).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

All cohort studies conducted on the incidence of LTFU among East African Adults on ART and published in English from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024, were eligible.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded articles that did not have the outcome of interests, those published before 2015, published in non-English language and done in non-cohort study designs. The reasons for excluding primary studies from the review and analysis are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Screening procedure

Results from the search were uploaded into Covidence reference management software where duplicates were removed. The records that were preserved were then passed into title abstract and full-text screening, and data extraction. First four reviewers (MYT, AGB, ANAG, and NKB) reviewed each article for title and abstract in a dyad. Disagreements emerged in this process were resolved by MSS who analyzed and discussed the concepts in depth. The same reviewers carried out the second step based on full texts and disagreements were again resolve by MSS. Finally, all disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus to eliminate bias.

Quality appraisal

Two reviewers together (MSS and AGB) rated the quality of the studies passed for data extraction. We used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality appraisal checklist to determine the quality of the studies enrolled. The JBI appraisal checklist for cohort contains 11 study items for which the investigators’ specific and quantitative measures were taken in terms of answering the question to the item yes = 1, no = 0, and not sure or not applicable = 0. These scores were adjusted for every research and expressed as a percentage of the number of items that were scored and subsequently reached for each research a percentage that allowed categorization into different types of JBI bias evaluation checklist. Studies with a score of more than 50% were considered good quality (Supplementary Table 4).

Data extraction

The data were extracted by two authors together (AGB and NKB) using Covidence software. Any disagreements among the data extractors were discussed and it was handled by the lead author (MSS). The extracted data included the following information: the name of the primary author, year of publication, study setting, study design, the total number of participants, the duration for which the participants were followed, total person-years, and the rate of incidence with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome of interest was the pooled incidence density rate of loss to follow-up (LTFU) from the ART program. Loss to follow-up (LTFU) was defined as failure to collect for a period of 3 months or longer after the last visit for a collection refill, where the adult has not been declared dead or transferred out.

Data synthesis

The extracted data were exported to Stata version 18 for data synthesis and statistical analysis. The characteristics of the extracted data were presented in the table, and a summary of the pooled estimate was presented graphically in a forest plot. The pooled incidence of LTFU from ART was calculated by employing random effects with the Der-Simonian Laird approach. The studies’ heterogeneity was assessed via the I2 statistics. The I2 is considered low when it is less than 50%, moderate when it is between 50% and 75%, and high when it is greater than 75% [16]. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on study setting, year of publication (2015–2020 vs. 2021–2024), sample size (≤ 574 (median) vs. > 574), and period of follow-up (≤ 5 vs. > 5 years). Sensitivity analysis was done to check a single study effect on the overall pooled estimate, and publication bias was checked graphically using a funnel plot and statistically by Egger’s regression test [17]. The incidence density rate with its 95% confidence intervals was used to show the LTFU rate in the forest plot.

Result

Search results

Electronic database searches on PubMed, Embase (Ovid), Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, as well as hand-searching of reference lists and citation tracking of included studies, led to the discovery of a total of 3011 studies. After removing duplicates, 1588 studies remained, of which 65 were chosen for a full-text review. Out of these, 45 studies were excluded for various reasons. Specifically, 39 articles were excluded due to variations in study outcome measurements, 5 articles were excluded because of differences in study population, and 1 article was excluded due to study design. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis titled “Incidence of Loss to Follow-Up among East African Adults on Antiretroviral Therapy” presents a critical evaluation of LTFU rates and contributing factors among adults on ART in East Africa. The reported pooled incidence of LTFU was 9.67 per 100 person-years (95% CI, 7.84–11.93), indicating significant variation across studies. This variation shows how clinical, systemic, and demographic factors together shape ART retention.

The vast majority (80%) of the studies contained in this paper were conducted in Ethiopia and offered crucial insight into the outputs of ART undertakings in the nation. Studies from Ethiopia [5, 12] revealed that retention strategies and the type of healthcare facility contributed to the relatively low LTFU rates of 5.3 and 6.7 per 100 person-years, respectively. Other Ethiopian studies [22, 28] reported LTFU figures of 12.3 and 13.5 per 100 person-years, respectively, which suggests that there are country-wide fluctuations caused by regional disparities in healthcare and distribution of other essential resources.

A multicountry study conducted in Kenya, Rwanda, and Tanzania [25] reported the highest minimum LTFU incidence of 16.7 per 100 person-years. The outcomes of this research highlight how challenging alternative effortful retention strategies are to attempt in different healthcare systems and sociocultural settings. Studies from Rwanda [10, 24] noted that LTFU rates were reported as 25.2 per 100 person-years and 7.5 per 100 person-years, respectively. These differences were related to the different ART regimen types, levels of patient participation, and facility-level factors.

Most of the studies reviewed had common clinical and demographic predictors of LTFU, including WHO clinical stage and suppression of viral load, as well as age and gender. Studies from Rwanda [23] and Ethiopia [13] reported higher LTFU among younger individuals and those with unsuppressed viral loads. These findings indicate that these groups are at increased risk of LTFU. Healthcare access and being married were notable social factors. Studies’ findings show that stigma and transport barriers as contributing factors to patient dropout [19, 29].

There were significant evidence gaps from Burundi, Comoros, Djibouti, and Somalia, even though Ethiopia shows a reasonable amount of evidence in this assessment. This underrepresentation limits the generalizability of the findings, hence highlighting the need to increase research in these areas. More research is required in each country to understand their challenges regarding ART retention and inform tailored ART adherence strategies. Also, in view of the multifactorial determinants of loss to follow-up in East Africa, as demonstrated by the high level of heterogeneity in the meta-analysis (I2 = 98.5%), the complexity of the problem becomes more understandable. This heterogeneity is explained by subgroup analyses based on area, publication year, and follow-up time. These patterns suggest that additional factors, such as health-system and sociocultural influences related to HIV, may also be involved. This heterogeneity also calls for a locally relevant and appropriate response rather than a one-time solution.

To enhance the external validity of future research, efforts should be made to include more studies from underrepresented countries and to conduct multicountry collaborations. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of LTFU drivers across diverse contexts and inform tailored interventions. Until such evidence is available, policymakers and healthcare providers in non-Ethiopian East African countries should interpret these findings with caution and consider local contextual factors when designing retention strategies.

Limitations

The primary limitations of this study include the high heterogeneity observed across included studies. This heterogeneity limits the generalizability of the pooled estimates and indicates that unmeasured factors such as sociocultural influences and health system differences may significantly impact LTFU outcomes. Additionally, there were notable evidence gaps from several East African countries like Burundi, Comoros, Djibouti, and Somalia, which restrict the applicability of findings across the entire region. Most of the included studies (approximately 80%) originated from Ethiopia, which further constrains the generalizability of the results to broader settings with different health system contexts. Variations in outcome measurement definitions and study designs among included studies may also introduce bias and affect the robustness of the results.

Policy implications and recommendations

From the review’s findings regarding targeting 2025 goals in UNAIDS, there are important implications, such as the fact that there are comparatively low LFTU figures in some places, such as Ethiopia. But even then, such low figures are still a barrier to achievement in East Africa. These patients can potentially be reached or retained, but tailored ART support and follow-up approaches are required. Counselling, patient retention, and transport are examples of evidence-based strategies that will have to be addressed so that retention can be realized. There is evidence that community-based approaches can enhance patient retention by increasing social support while reducing stigma.

Achieving this goal requires addressing systemic barriers and aligning ART programs among regions. Enhancing the integration of ART into patient care could help monitor and improve adherence. Countries that are LTFU and are lower can leverage regional best practices, such as those implemented by Ethiopia, to enhance patient retention. Additionally, investigating countries such as these will help build the evidence base needed to fill the gaps in the data and support the type of policies that are required.

Conclusion

Despite Ethiopia demonstrating relatively high ART retention, East Africa as a whole remains off track to meet the 2025 UN targets. The pooled evidence shows very high heterogeneity (I2 = 98.5%), indicating that ART retention varies greatly across countries and settings. This variability, partly explained by differences in program implementation, healthcare capacity, and definitions of LTFU, limits the certainty and generalizability of the findings. To reduce loss to follow-up, strategies that may improve retention include enhanced counselling, adherence support, and patient follow-up systems, particularly in settings where these gaps were commonly reported. However, due to the high variability between studies, these interventions should be adapted to local contexts and implemented cautiously. Further standardized and high-quality research is needed to strengthen the evidence base and guide more definitive policy decisions.
