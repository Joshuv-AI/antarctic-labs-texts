Abstract
. We consider the numbers of positive and negative eigenvalues of matrices of squared distances between randomly sampled i.i.d. points in a given metric measure space. These numbers and their limits, as the number of points grows, in fact contain some important information about the whole space. In particular, by knowing them, we can determine whether this space can be isometrically embedded in the Hilbert space. We show that the limits of these numbers exist almost surely, are nonrandom and the same for all Borel probability measures of full support, and, moreover, are naturally related to the operators defining the multidimensional scaling (MDS) method. We also relate them to the signature of the pseudo-Euclidean space in which the given metric space can be isometrically embedded. In addition, we provide several examples of explicit calculations or just estimates of those limits for sample metric spaces. In particular, for a large class of countable spaces (for instance, containing all graphs with bounded intrinsic metrics), we get that the number of negative eigenvalues increases to infinity as the size of samples grows. However, we are able to provide examples when the number of samples grows to infinity and the numbers of both negative and positive eigenvalues increases to infinity, or the number of positive eigenvalues is bounded (but as large as desired), and the number of positive ones is fixed. Finally, we consider the example of the universal countable Rado–Erdős–Rényi graph.

A Polish (i.e., separable and homeomorphic to complete) space

2.2.
Spectral theory for matrices

In the sequel, we frequently use the Cauchy interlacing theorem

Proposition 2.1

Let

In particular, for any real

4.
Limit distance signature of separable metric spaces

We introduce the following notion crucial for this paper.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
We call the limit distance signature of

(s_{-}(X,d),s_{+}(X,d))

(4.1)

s_{\pm}(X,d)=\sup\{s_{\pm}(S(X_{N})):X_{N}\subset X\,\mbox{finite}\}.

In this section we prove the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a separable metric space. Then
the following assertions hold.

(i)

If

is at most countable, then

s_{\pm}(X,d)=\lim_{N}s_{\pm}(S(X_{N}))=\sup_{N}s_{\pm}(S(X_{N}))

.

(ii)

If

(iii)

are the respective random finite sets of points in

(iv)

If

4.1.
Deterministic characterization of limit distance signatures

The following purely deterministic statement is valid.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be an at most countable metric space.
Then for all sequences of finite subsets
X_{N}\subset X
such that
X_{N}\nearrow X
as
N\to\infty
,
the limits always satisfy

s_{\pm}(X,d)=\lim_{N}s_{\pm}(S(X_{N})).

In particular, the above limits are independent of the sequence
(X_{N})_{N}
.

Proof.

By the Cauchy interlacing theorem, the sequences
\bigl(s_{\pm}(S(X_{N}))\bigr)_{N}
are non-decreasing
and hence admit a finite or infinite limit. Moreover, by the definition of
s_{\pm}(X,d)
, we have

s_{\pm}(X,d)\geq s_{\pm}(S(X_{N}))
for every
N
, and hence

s_{\pm}(X,d)\geq\lim_{N}s_{\pm}(S(X_{N})).

To show the other side of the inequality, note for any finite subset

, there is an
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that
X^{\prime}\subset X_{M}
. Thus, by
the Cauchy interlacing theorem,
s_{\pm}(S(X^{\prime}))\leq s_{\pm}(S(X_{M}))
. Since
X^{\prime}
is arbitrary, we also have the reverse inequality

s_{\pm}(X,d)\leq s_{\pm}(S(X_{M}))\leq\sup_{N}s_{\pm}(S(X_{N}))=\lim_{N}s_{\pm}(S(X_{N})),

concluding the proof.
∎

.
∎

Proposition 4.6

Let

5.1.

Proposition 5.1

5.2.
Embedding in pseudo-Euclidean spaces

The pseudo-Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
with signature
(n,p)
is the linear space of
vectors
\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
equipped with the bilinear form

(u,v):=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}v_{i}+\sum_{j=n+1}^{n+p}u_{j}v_{j}.

The latter defines a pseudo-distance

d_{n,p}(u,v):=\sqrt{(u-v,u-v)}

on every set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
such that the Minkowski difference

\Sigma-\Sigma:=\{u-v:u\in\Sigma,v\in\Sigma\}
belongs to the positive cone

C_{n,p}:=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p}:(v,v):=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=n+1}^{n+p}v_{j}^{2}\geq 0\right\}.

The following statement is valid.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure with full support and finite
4
-th moment in the metric space
(X,d)
.
Suppose that

s_{-}(T_{\mu})=n\in\mathbb{N}
and
s_{+}(T_{\mu})=p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
be nonzero eigenvalues (counting multiplicities) and
u_{i}\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
be the respective eigenfunctions
of the operator
T_{\mu}\colon L^{2}(X,\mu)\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
(defined by (
2.2
)), normalized so that
\|u_{i}\|_{2}=1
,
i=1,\ldots,n+p
. Without loss of generality, assume
\lambda_{i}<0
for
i=1,\ldots n

and
\lambda_{i}>0
for
i=n+1,\ldots n+p
.
Then the map
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}^{n,p}

defined by

f(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n+p}\sqrt{|\lambda_{i}|}u_{i}(x),

is an isometry onto the image such that its image

\Sigma:=f(X)
satisfies
\Sigma-\Sigma\subset C_{n,p}
.
In particular, every finite metric space can be isometrically embedded into some pseudo-Euclidean space with the image
\Sigma

\Sigma-\Sigma
belongs to the positive cone.

Vice versa, if
\mu
is a Borel probability measure with finite

(with

In particular, we also get

\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\lambda_{i}u_{i}^{2}(x)=k_{T}(x,x)

for all

.

From (

) and (

), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\lambda_{i}\left(u_{i}(x)-u_{i}(y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\lambda_{i}u_{i}^{2}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\lambda_{i}u_{i}^{2}(y)-2\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\lambda_{i}u_{i}(x)u_{i}(y)

T_{N}:=\Pi_{N}S(X_{N})\Pi_{N}
is the matrix

T_{ij}=(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{x}_{j})_{n,p},\quad\mbox{where }\bar{x}_{i}:=x_{i}-\bar{x},\quad\bar{x}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}.

Thus, one can view
T_{N}
as the difference between two matrices,
T_{N}=T_{+}-T_{-}
, where

(T_{-})_{ij}=(P_{n}^{-}\bar{x}_{i})\cdot(P_{n}^{-}\bar{x}_{j}),\qquad(T_{+})_{ij}=(P_{p}^{+}\bar{x}_{i})\cdot(P_{p}^{+}\bar{x}_{j}),

P_{n}^{-}
(resp.
P_{p}^{+}
) standing for the projections from
\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
identified with the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
with all coordinates zero except the first
n
ones (resp. to
\mathbb{R}^{p}
identified with the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
with all coordinates zero except the last
p
ones). Also,
u\cdot v
stands for the usual dot product between two vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
.
Both
T_{-}
and
T_{+}
are clearly positive semidefinite with
s_{+}(T_{-})\leq n
,
s_{+}(T_{+})\leq p
, and
s_{-}(T_{-})=s_{-}(T_{+})=0
.
From Lemma
A.1
(i), we get that

s_{+}(T_{N})\leq s_{+}(T_{+})+s_{+}(-T_{-})=s_{+}(T_{+})+s_{-}(T_{-})\leq p.

A completely symmetric reasoning yields
s_{-}(T_{N})\leq n
.
Then Proposition
4.15
applied to the space
(\operatorname{supp}\mu,d)
yields the desired claim

s_{+}(T_{\mu})\leq p,\quad s_{-}(T_{\mu})\leq n,

since
L^{2}(X,\mu)
is isomorphic to
L^{2}(\operatorname{supp}\mu,\mu)
.
∎

Note that not every subset
\Sigma
of a pseudo-Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
is an image of an isometric embedding of a metric space.
In particular, the condition
\Sigma-\Sigma\in C_{n,p}
is a strong restriction, see the example below.

Example 5.7
.

If
n=1
, and
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
satisfies

\Sigma-\Sigma\subset C_{n,p}
, then the intersection of
\Sigma
with every line parallel to
x_{1}
axis must be either empty or a singleton.
In other words,
\Sigma
belongs to a hypersurface which, if seen in
\mathbb{R}^{n+p}=\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
, is a graph
G
of some function:
x_{1}=g(x_{2},\ldots,x_{p+1})
.
Clearly,
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
1
-Lipschitz function, since
G-x\subset C_{n,p}
for every
x\in G
.

5.3.
On feasible signatures

The following result shows that for a generic finite metric space there exists an arbitrarily small perturbation of the distance which preserves
s_{+}
and makes
s_{-}
maximum possible.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space with
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
such that the triangle inequality is strict for any distinct triplet from
X
. Consider the matrix
T:=\Pi_{N}S(X)\Pi_{N}
.
Then for any sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, there is a distance
d_{\varepsilon}
over
X
such that

\max_{i,j}|d(x_{i},x_{j})-d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i},x_{j})|\leq\varepsilon

and the corresponding matrix
T_{\varepsilon}
has signature

s_{+}(T_{\varepsilon})=s_{+}(T),\quad s_{-}(T_{\varepsilon})=N-1-s_{+}(T).

Proof.

Fix linearly independent vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
define

g_{\varepsilon}(x_{i},x_{j})=d^{2}(x_{i},x_{j})-\varepsilon\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|^{2}.

Note
g_{\varepsilon}\to d^{2}
uniformly as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. For sufficiently
\varepsilon>0
, we have
d_{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{g_{\varepsilon}}\geq 0
.
Moreover, since for any distinct
i,j,k
, the strict triangle inequality

d(x_{i},x_{j})<d(x_{i},x_{k})+d(x_{k},x_{j})

holds, then for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, one has the triangle inequality

d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i},x_{k})+d_{\varepsilon}(x_{k},x_{j}).

Thus,
d_{\varepsilon}
is a distance over
X
for small
\varepsilon>0
. We set then
X_{\varepsilon}:=(X,d_{\varepsilon}))
.

Now, it is easy to see that the matrix corresponding to
(X,d_{\varepsilon})
has the form

T_{\varepsilon}:=\Pi_{N}S(X_{\varepsilon})\Pi_{N}=T-\varepsilon G,

where
G:=\begin{pmatrix}\left(v_{i}-\bar{v},v_{j}-\bar{v}\right)\end{pmatrix}_{i,j=1}^{N}
with
\bar{v}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}
.
In particular,
G
is a Gram matrix with
s_{+}(G)=N-1
,
s_{-}(G)=0
, thus due to Lemma
A.1
(i) one has

\displaystyle s_{+}(T_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq s_{+}(T)+s_{+}(-\varepsilon G)=s_{+}(T),

\displaystyle s_{-}(T_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\geq s_{-}(-\varepsilon G)-s_{-}(-T)=N-1-s_{+}(T).

Moreover, for small enough
\varepsilon
, one has
s_{+}(T_{\varepsilon})=s_{+}(T)
by Lemma
A.1
(ii).
Since
\ker T
is non-trivial, we have
s_{-}(T_{\varepsilon})\leq N-1-s_{+}(T_{\varepsilon})=N-1-s_{+}(T)
. Thus, we get the equality for
s_{-}(T_{\varepsilon})
.
The claim follows.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

For any
p\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
, there is an
(n+p+1)
-point metric space
(X,d)
such that the corresponding matrix
T
has signature
s_{+}(T)=p
,
s_{-}(T)=n
.

Proof.

It is enough to apply the above Proposition
5.8
to
N:=n+p+1
distinct points in a general position (i.e. spanning
\mathbb{R}^{p}
) on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, endowed with the Euclidean distance.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

Under the conditions of the above Corollary
5.9
, one clearly has

s_{+}(X,d)\in\{p,p+1\}
,
s_{-}(X,d)\in\{n,n+1\}
in view of Theorem
4.2
(iv). Thus, in particular, this corollary gives a construction of hollow symmetric nonnegative (HSN) matrices (in this particular case even stronger, squared distance matrices)
with arbitrarily large number of positive eigenvalues.
This provides an alternative proof to the main theorem of
[
6
]
(the latter seems to be much more complicated and gives just HSN matrices, not necessarily related to any distances).

We are also able to provide the following example of an infinite metric space
(X,d)
with
s_{-}(X,d)=+\infty
and
s_{+}(X,d)
finite (arbitrary up to the error of
1
).

Example 5.11
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. In the following construction we assume the pseudo-Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
with different
n\in\mathbb{N}
to be
hierarchically embedded one into another, in such a way that a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
is identified
with the vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m,p}
,
m>n
, defined by

\displaystyle y_{i}:=x_{i},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle y_{i}:=0,

\displaystyle\quad i=n+1,\ldots,m,

\displaystyle y_{m+j}:=x_{n+j},

\displaystyle\quad j=1,\ldots,p.

This gives a natural embedding of
\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
into
\mathbb{R}^{m,p}
.
In the same way, we identify
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(resp.
\mathbb{R}^{p}
) with the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
with last
p
coordinates (resp. first
n
coordinates) zero.
As in the proof of Theorem
5.6
, we denote
by
P_{n}^{-}
(resp.
P_{p}^{+}
) the projections from
\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(resp.
\mathbb{R}^{p}
), both identified with subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n+p}
,
defined by
(P_{n}^{-}z)_{i}:=z_{i}
, and
(P_{n}^{-}z)_{j}:=0
(resp.
(P_{p}^{+}z)_{i}:=0
,
(P_{p}^{+}z)_{j}:=z_{j}
), where
i=1,\ldots,n
,
j=n+1,\ldots,n+p
).

We are going to construct inductively a sequence of increasing sets

X_{N}=\{z^{1},\ldots,z^{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1,p},\quad N\geq p+1,

with special properties.
For brevity, we denote

\displaystyle S_{N}:=S(X_{N}),\quad T_{N}:=\Pi_{N}S_{N}\Pi_{N}.

As in the proof of Theorem
5.6
, one has

\displaystyle T_{N}

\displaystyle=T_{N}^{+}-T_{N}^{-},\quad\mbox{where}

\displaystyle(T_{N}^{-})_{ij}

\displaystyle:=(P_{N-1}^{-}\bar{z}^{i})\cdot(P_{N-1}^{-}\bar{z}^{j}),

\displaystyle(T_{N}^{+})_{ij}

\displaystyle:=(P_{p}^{+}\bar{z}^{i})\cdot(P_{p}^{+}\bar{z}^{j}),\quad i,j=1,\ldots N,

\displaystyle\bar{z}^{i}

\displaystyle:=z^{i}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}z^{j},

where the dot product is the Euclidean one.
Clearly,
T_{N}^{\pm}
are both positive semidefinite.
We will construct
(X_{N})_{N=p+1}^{\infty}
in such a way that

(i)

both
P_{N-1}^{-}X_{N}
and
P_{p}^{+}X_{N}
are sets of points in a general position in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p}
respectively (i.e. not contained in any hyperplane in these spaces),

(ii)

d_{N-1,p}
is a distance over
X_{N}
and for every triple of distinct points in
X_{N}
, the triangle inequality for
d_{N-1,p}
is strict,

(iii)

P_{p}^{+}X_{N}\subset S
, where
S
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

(iv)

u\cdot P_{p}^{+}(z^{k}-z^{1})<d_{N-1,p}(z^{k},z^{1})|u|
for all
k=2,\dots,N
and non-zero
u\in T_{y^{1}}(S)
, where
T_{y^{1}}(S)
is the tangent space to
S
at point
y^{1}:=P_{p}^{+}z^{1}
, and
|\cdot|
stands for the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

In view of (i) we will have then

s_{+}(T_{N}^{-})=N-1,\quad s_{+}(T_{N}^{+})=p,\quad s_{-}(T_{N}^{-})=s_{-}(T_{N}^{+})=0.

Thus from
T_{N}=T_{N}^{+}-T_{N}^{-}
with the help of Lemma
A.1
(i) we get

(5.8)

\displaystyle s_{-}(T_{N})

\displaystyle\geq s_{-}(-T_{N}^{-})-s_{-}(-T_{N}^{+})

\displaystyle=s_{+}(T_{N}^{-})-s_{+}(T_{N}^{+})=N-p-1,

as well as
s_{+}(T_{N})\leq s_{+}(T_{N}^{+})+s_{-}(T_{N}^{-})=p
.

We start with
N:=p+1
: take
p+1
points
\{y^{1},\ldots,y^{p+1}\}
in a general position on the unit sphere
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Now, let
\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{p+1}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be in a general position and with the norms
|x^{k}|
,
k=1,\ldots,p+1
, so small that for
d_{p,p}
satisfies strict triangle inequalities over the set

X_{p+1}:=\{z^{1},\ldots,z^{p+1}\},\quad z^{k}:=(x^{k},y^{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{p,p},

the property (iv) holds for
N:=p+1

and
s_{+}(T_{p+1})=s_{+}(T_{p+1}^{+})=p
(where the last equality is due to Example
5.5
).
Then, in particular,

(5.9)

s_{+}(S(X_{p+1}))=p

in view of Theorem
4.2
(iv) and the fact that
s_{-}(S(X_{p+1}))\geq 1
.
The possibility to satisfy (iv) follows from the strict convexity of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
which implies

u\cdot(y^{k}-y^{1})<|y^{k}-y^{1}|\cdot|u|

for all
k\neq 1
and nonzero
u\in T_{y^{1}}(S)
, and

d_{p,p}(z^{k},z^{1})\to|y^{k}-y^{1}|\quad\mbox{ as $x_{k}\to 0$, $x_{1}\to 0$}.

Once
X_{N}=\{z^{1},\ldots,z^{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1,p}
is constructed, we denote

y^{k}:=P_{p}^{+}z^{k}\in S,\quad x^{k}:=P_{N-1}^{-}z^{k}.

We will choose
z^{N+1}=(x^{N+1},y^{N+1})
such that
y^{N+1}\in S
and
x^{N+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
has the form

\displaystyle(x^{N+1})_{i}

\displaystyle:=(x^{1})_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N-1,

\displaystyle(x^{N+1})_{N}

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon_{N},

where
\varepsilon_{N}>0
.
First, note that

\mathrm{dist}\,\left(\frac{y-y^{1}}{|y-y^{1}|},T_{y^{1}}(S)\right)\to 0\quad\mbox{as $y\to y^{1}$},

where
\mathrm{dist}
stands for the distance in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
between a point and a set.
Then, due to (iv), one can choose
y^{N+1}
close enough to
y^{1}
, so that for an auxiliary point
\tilde{z}^{N+1}:=(x^{1},y^{N+1})
one has

\displaystyle(\tilde{z}^{N+1}-z^{1},z^{1}-z^{k})_{N,p}

\displaystyle=(y^{N+1}-y^{1})\cdot(y^{1}-y^{k})

\displaystyle<d_{N,p}(z^{k},z^{1})|y^{N+1}-y^{1}|,

\displaystyle(z^{1}-\tilde{z}^{N+1},\tilde{z}^{N+1}-z^{k})_{N,p}

\displaystyle=(y^{1}-y^{N+1})\cdot(y^{N+1}-y^{k})

\displaystyle<d_{N,p}(z^{k},\tilde{z}^{N+1})|y^{N+1}-y^{1}|,

and

\displaystyle|y^{N+1}-y^{1}|

\displaystyle<d_{N,p}(z^{1},z^{k}),

hence

\displaystyle d_{N,p}(\tilde{z}^{N+1},z^{1})

\displaystyle=|y^{N+1}-y^{1}|<d_{N,p}(z^{1},z^{k})+d_{N,p}(z^{k},\tilde{z}^{N+1}),

for all
k=2,\dots,N
, with
a\cdot b
standing for the Euclidean dot product in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Thus, by Lemma
A.5
,

\displaystyle d_{N,p}(\tilde{z}^{N+1},z^{k})<d_{N,p}(\tilde{z}^{N+1},z^{1})+d_{N,p}(z^{1},z^{k}),

\displaystyle d_{N,p}(\tilde{z}^{1},z^{k})<d_{N,p}(z^{1},\tilde{z}^{N+1})+d_{N,p}(\tilde{z}^{N+1},z^{k}).

for all
k=2,\dots,N
, and hence
d_{N,p}
satisfies the strict triangle inequality on the set
X_{N}\cup\{\tilde{z}^{N+1}\}
.
Now, we can choose
\varepsilon_{N}
small enough, such that
d_{N,p}
is a distance over
X_{N+1}:=X_{N}\cup\{z^{N+1}\}
satisfying the strict triangle inequality, and

u\cdot(y^{N+1}-y^{1})<d_{N,p}(z^{N+1},z^{1})|u|

for all nonzero
u\in T_{y_{1}}S
.
The latter follows from the fact that

u\cdot(y^{N+1}-y^{1})<|y^{N+1}-y^{1}|\cdot|u|

due to the strict convexity of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and

d_{N,p}(z^{N+1},z^{1})=\sqrt{|y^{N+1}-y^{1}|^{2}-\varepsilon_{N}^{2}}\to|y^{N+1}-y^{1}|\quad\mbox{ as $\varepsilon_{N}\to 0$}.

Therefore,
X_{N+1}
satisfies (i)-(iv).

To conclude, let
X:=\bigcup_{N=p+1}^{\infty}X_{N}
, and equip this set with the distance coinciding with
d_{N,p}
over each
X_{N}
.
Then

s_{\pm}(X,d)=\lim_{n}s_{\pm}(S_{N}).

by Theorem
4.2
(i).
Clearly, in view of Theorem
4.2
(iv) and (
5.8
), one has

s_{-}(S_{N})\geq N-p-1,

and hence
s_{-}(X,d)=+\infty
.
On the other hand, by Theorem
5.6
combined with Theorem
4.2
(iv), we get
s_{+}(X,d)\leq p+1
.
Finally,
s_{+}(X,d)\geq s_{+}(S(X_{p+1}))=p
by (
5.9
), thus

s_{+}(X,d)\in\{p,p+1\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad s_{-}(X,d)=+\infty,

concluding the example.

The following example shows another construction of an infinite metric space
(X,d)
with
s_{+}(X,d)=+\infty
and
s_{-}(X,d)
finite but different from
1
.

Example 5.12
.

Let
X:=\mathbb{N}
, and define the distance

\displaystyle d(i,j):=\begin{cases}1,\ i<4,\ j=4\quad\mbox{or }i=4,\ j<4,\\
0,\ i=j,\\
2,\ \mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that the points
1,2,3,4
form the same tripod from Example
5.3
, which cannot be embedded in any Hilbert space.
The subset
X_{N}:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
has the
N\times N
squared distance matrix

B_{N}:=\left(\begin{tabular}[]{lllllll}0&4&4&1&4&\ldots&4\\
4&0&4&1&4&\ldots&4\\
4&4&0&1&4&\ldots&4\\
1&1&1&0&4&\ldots&4\\
4&4&4&4&0&\ldots&4\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
4&4&4&4&4&\ldots&0\\
\end{tabular}\right)

and by the Haynsworth inertia additivity formula
[
9

its signature satisfies

(s_{-},s_{0},s_{+})(B_{N})=(s_{-},s_{0},s_{+})(B_{4})+(s_{-},s_{0},s_{+})(B_{N}/B_{4}),

where
B_{N}/B_{4}
stands for the Schur complement of
B_{4}
in
B_{N}
. The explicit calculation shows

(s_{-},s_{0},s_{+})(B_{4})=(3,0,1)
and

B_{N}/B_{4}:=\frac{4}{3}\left(\begin{tabular}[]{lllllll}8&11&11&11&\ldots&11\\
11&8&11&11&\ldots&11\\
11&11&8&11&\ldots&11\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
11&11&11&11&\ldots&8\\
\end{tabular}\right).

For
N:=4+k
the latter
k\times k
matrix has one eigenvalue
-4
of multiplicity
k-1
and one strictly positive simple eigenvalue (the Perron–Frobenius one), that is,
(s_{-},s_{0},s_{+})(B_{4+k}/B_{4})=(k-1,0,1)
.
Thus,
(s_{-},s_{0},s_{+})(B_{4+k})=(k+2,0,2)
for

, and therefore,
s_{-}(X,d)=2
and
s_{+}(X,d)=+\infty
.

Now, we extend the previous Example
5.12
to show that
s_{-}
can be arbitrarily large.

Example 5.13
.

Let
(X_{i},d_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,m
, be a finite metric space with the squared distance matrix
D_{i}:=(d_{i}^{2}(x_{j},x_{k}))_{j,k}
.
Assume
\operatorname{diam}(X_{i})\leq 2h
for some
h>0
.

Denote for the sake of brevity
N_{i}:=\#X_{i}
,

S_{i}:=S(X_{i})
and

R_{i}:=\frac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbf{1}_{N_{i}}\mathbf{1}^{T}_{N_{i}}+S_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(h^{2}\mathbf{1}_{N_{i}}\mathbf{1}^{T}_{N_{i}}-D_{i}).

Now we define a metric space
(X,d)
with
X:=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}
and

d(x,y):=\begin{cases}d_{i}(x,y),&x,y\in X_{i},\\
h,&x\neq y\ \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The respective squared distance matrix is given by

D:=\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}&h^{2}&\dots&h^{2}\\
h^{2}&D_{2}&\dots&h^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
h^{2}&h^{2}&\dots&D_{m}\end{pmatrix}.

Let also

\begin{array}[]{rl}S&:=S(X)=-\frac{1}{2}D,\\
R&:=\frac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{T}+S=\frac{1}{2}(h^{2}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{T}-D)=\operatorname{diag}(R_{1},\dots,R_{m}),\end{array}

where
N:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}N_{i}
.
Note that
s_{\pm}(R)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{\pm}(R_{i})
.
Further, since
\frac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{T}
is positive semidefinite rank one matrix,
by Lemma
A.1
, we get

s_{+}(S)\leq s_{+}(R)\leq s_{+}(S)+1,\quad s_{-}(S)-1\leq s_{-}(R)\leq s_{-}(S).

Let us consider a specific example, where all
X_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m-1
, are isometric with

D_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}0&4&4&1\\
4&0&4&1\\
4&4&0&1\\
1&1&1&0\end{pmatrix}

as in the previous example,
and
X_{m}
is such that
d_{m}(x,y)=2
if
x\neq y
.
Since
\operatorname{diam}(X_{i})=2
for all
X_{i}
, one can set
h:=1
.
Thus, for
i=1,\dots,m-1

R_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-3&-3&0\\
-3&1&-3&0\\
-3&-3&1&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

with
\sigma(R_{i})=(-5/2,1/2,2,2)
and
s_{+}(R_{i})=3
,
s_{-}(R_{i})=1
.
Furthermore,
X_{m}
can be isometrically embedded into a Euclidean space, and

D_{m}=4\mathbf{1}_{N_{m}}\mathbf{1}_{N_{m}}^{T}-4\mathrm{Id}_{N_{m}},\quad S_{m}=2\mathrm{Id}_{N_{m}}-2,\quad R_{m}=2\mathrm{Id}_{N_{m}}-\frac{3}{2}\mathbf{1}_{N_{m}}\mathbf{1}_{N_{m}}^{T},

with
s_{+}(R_{m})=N_{m}-1
,
s_{-}(R_{m})=1
.
Thus,

s_{+}(R)=3(m-1)+N_{m}-1,\quad s_{-}(R)=m,

and therefore,

s_{+}(X,d)=s_{+}(S)\in\{3m-5+N_{m},3m-4+N_{m}\},\quad s_{-}(X,d)=s_{-}(S)\in\{m,m+1\}.

Note that the constructed space
X
can be represented as a graph endowed with its intrinsic distance.

\mathbb{R}^{3}
, equipped with their intrinsic distances.

Squared distance matrices are special cases of hollow symmetric non-negative (HSN) matrices, i.e., symmetric matrices with nonnegative entries and with zeros on the diagonal.
Using the spectral theory of HSN matrices from
[
6
]
, we obtain the following result
for countable metric spaces.

Proposition 6.4
.

Suppose
(X,d)
is a bounded infinite separable metric space, and there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that
\delta_{0}\leq d(x,y)
for all
x\neq y
.
Then
s_{+}(X,d)=+\infty
, in particular,
s_{+}(T_{\mu})=s_{+}(K_{\mu})=+\infty
for every
Borel probability measure
\mu
on
X
with full support.

Proof.

For every finite subset
X_{N}\subset X
,
the squared distance matrix of
X_{N}
is a HSN matrix with off-diagonal entries from the interval
[\delta_{0}^{2},\delta_{1}^{2}]
, where
\delta_{1}
is the diameter of
(X,d)
. Since
\delta_{0}>0
and
\delta_{1}<+\infty
, by theorem 3.3 from
[
6
]
, there exists some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that any HSN matrix of order at least
n_{0}
with off-diagonal entries from
[\delta_{0}^{2},\delta_{1}^{2}]
admits at least
m
strictly negative eigenvalues. Therefore, for any fixed
m
, there exists some
n_{0}
such that for any finite
X_{N}\subset X
with
\#X_{N}\geq n_{0}
, one has that
s_{+}(S(X_{N}))\geq m
, proving the claim.
∎

The assumptions of the above corollary are satisfied, for instance, when
the metric space
(X,d)
is induced by a connected countable graph with its intrinsic metric on vertices being bounded.

6.2.
Limits and tangent cones

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
(X_{k},d_{k})
be a sequence of separable metric spaces with
p_{k}\in X_{k}
, and the sequence of pointed metric spaces
(X_{k},d_{k},p_{k})
converge as
k\to\infty
to a pointed separable metric space
(X,d,p)
in the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff sense.
Assume that all
(X_{k},d_{k})
and
(X,d)
satisfy the Heine–Borel property (i.e., closed balls are compact).
Then

s_{\pm}(X,d)\leq\liminf_{k}s_{\pm}(X_{k},d_{k}).

Proof.

We prove the statement for
s_{+}
, and the proof for
s_{-}
is completely analogous.
Fix a finite set
\Sigma_{N}\subset X
and let
\bar{B}_{r}(p)\subset X
be a closed ball centered at
p
such that
\Sigma_{N}\subset\bar{B}_{r}(p)
.
It follows from the definition of the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence
[
5
, definition 8.1.1]
that for an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
there are
\bar{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and maps
f^{k}_{\varepsilon}\colon\bar{B}_{r}(p)\subset X\to\bar{B}_{r+\varepsilon}(p_{k})\subset X_{k}
with
f_{\varepsilon}^{k}(p)=p_{k}
such that

\left|d_{k}(f_{\varepsilon}^{k}(x),f_{\varepsilon}^{k}(y))-d(x,y)\right|\leq\varepsilon

for all
x,y\in\bar{B}_{r}(p)
and
k\geq\bar{k}
.
Choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, by Lemma
A.1
(ii) one has

s_{+}(S(f_{\varepsilon}^{k}(\Sigma_{N})))\geq s_{+}(S(\Sigma_{N}))

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large.
We obtain the bound

\liminf_{k}s_{+}(X_{k},d_{k})\geq\liminf_{k}s_{+}(S(f_{\varepsilon}^{k}(\Sigma_{N})))\geq s_{+}(S(\Sigma_{N})).

Taking a supremum with respect to all finite subsets
\Sigma_{N}\subset X
, we get the claim.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

Suppose that the metric space
(X,d)
with Heine–Borel property has a tangent cone
(T_{p},\tilde{d})
at
p\in X
.
Then
s_{\pm}(X,d)\geq s_{\pm}(T_{p},\tilde{d})
. In particular, if
(X,d)
is a smooth Riemannian manifold equipped with its intrinsic distance, then
s_{+}(X,d)\geq\dim X
.

Similarly, if
(X,d)
has a (Gromov–Hausdorff) asymptotic cone
(C_{p},\tilde{d})
at
p\in X
, then
s_{\pm}(X,d)\geq s_{\pm}(C_{p},\tilde{d})
.

Proof.

Recall that the pointed metric space
(T_{p},\tilde{d},p)
(resp.,
(C_{p},\tilde{d},p)
) is the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff limit of
the sequence
(X,kd,p)
(resp.,
(X,d/k,p)
) as
k\to+\infty
. Apply the above Proposition
6.5
, having in mind that

s_{\pm}(X,kd)=s_{\pm}(X,d/k)=s_{\pm}(X,d).

If
(X,d)
is a smooth Riemannian manifold,
it suffices to recall that
T_{p}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n=\dim X
, and
(T_{p},\tilde{d})
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean distance so that
s_{+}(T_{p},\tilde{d})=s_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=n

by Example
5.5
.
∎

7.
Limit signature of the Rado graph

7.1.
Preliminaries on the spectral theory of random matrices and the Wigner semi-circle law

Random matrices play an important role in the study of the Rado graph, so we outline the relevant results on random matrices used in our paper.

Recall the following terminology in matrix spectral distribution. Let
W_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be a real symmetric matrix. Denote by
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{N}
the eigenvalues of
W_{N}
counting multiplicity. Then the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of the normalized matrix
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}W_{N}
is the probability measure

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}:=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{j}/\sqrt{N}}.

Now, we consider a sequence of random matrices
(W_{N})_{N=1}^{\infty}
constructed as follows. Let
(Y_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables, and
(Z_{ij})
with
1\leq i<j
be a family of i.i.d. random variables. For each
N\geq 1
,
W_{N}
is a symmetric matrix of order
N
with entries given by

\displaystyle(W_{N})_{k,k}=Y_{k},\ (W_{N})_{i,j}=(W_{N})_{j,i}=Z_{ij},\ \quad\mbox{for all }1\leq k\leq N,\ 1\leq i<j\leq N.

Thus,
W_{N}
can be viewed as upper left submatrices of order
N
of the same infinite size random matrix.
For each
N\geq 1
, by taking the ESD of the normalized random matrix
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}W_{N}
, we obtain a random probability measure of the form (
7.1
). The following version of the Wigner semi-circle law from
[
2
, theorem 2.5]
indicates the ESDs of these normalized random matrices converge to the semi-circle distribution.

Proposition 7.1

(Wigner semi-circle law)
.

Let
W_{N}
be the order
N
random real symmetric matrix such that the diagonal terms are i.i.d. random variables, and the off-diagonal terms are i.i.d. random variables with variance
\sigma^{2}>0
, constructed as above. Then with probability
1
, the ESDs of normalized random matrices
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}W_{N}
weakly converge to the semi-circle law, i.e., to a measure with density

\rho_{\sigma}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{4\sigma^{2}-x^{2}},&-2\sigma\leq x\leq 2\sigma,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In particular, the above theorem implies that the signatures of the matrices
(W_{N})_{N=1}^{\infty}
almost surely satisfy

(7.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}s_{\pm}(W_{N})=\infty,\quad\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{s_{+}(W_{N})}{s_{-}(W_{N})}=1.

7.2.
Signatures of the MDS operators and limit signature of the Rado graph

The Rado graph
R
is a homogeneous, countable, and universal graph in the sense that it contains all finite graphs as its induced subgraphs. The Rado graph also has the following finite extension property: given any two finite disjoint subsets
U,V
of
R
, there exists an
x\in R\setminus(U\cup V)
such that
x
is adjacent to all points in
U
, but not adjacent to any point in
V
. The Rado graph is known to be unique up to graph isomorphism, see
[
7
, chapter VII]
for details.
Let
d
be the intrinsic (graph) metric on
R
, and
\mu
be any Borel probability measure on
(R,d)
having full support.
The above properties immediately imply that the intrinsic metric
d
of
R
is bounded with
d\leq 2
.

We also recall that the Rado graph admits the following random construction (infinite Erdős–Rényi graph).
Fix any real number
0<p<1
. Let
(Z_{ij})
with
i<j\in\mathbb{N}
be a family of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables:
Z_{ij}\sim Be(p)
.
Then we define a random graph
G
with the set of vertices
\mathbb{N}
and the random adjacency matrix
\mathcal{A}
defined by

(7.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{ij}=\mathcal{A}_{ji}=Z_{ij},\ \mathcal{A}_{ii}=0,\ \quad\mbox{for all }1\leq i<j.

With probability
1
, this construction yields a graph isomorphic to the Rado graph—thus, it can be considered as a “random enumeration” of
R
.
Furthermore, for each
N\geq 1
, let
S_{N}
be the random matrix of order
N
such that

(7.4)

(S_{N})_{ij}=\dfrac{3}{2}\mathcal{A}_{ij}-2,\quad(S_{N})_{ii}=0\quad\mbox{for all $1\leq i\neq j\leq N$}.

Then almost surely one has
(S_{N})_{ij}=-\frac{d^{2}_{G}(i,j)}{2}
for
1\leq i,j\leq N
, where
d_{G}
is the intrinsic metric on
G
. Therefore, provided that
G
is isomorphic to
R
, there is a sequence of finite subsets
R_{N}\subset R
such that
R_{N}\nearrow R
,
\#R_{N}=N
, and
S_{N}=S(R_{N})
.
Since the random matrices
(S_{N})_{N\geq 1}
also satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
7.1
, we obtain the following theorem on the limit distance signature of
(R,d,\mu)
.

Theorem 7.2
.

One has

s_{\pm}(R,d)=+\infty

and
s_{\pm}(K)=s_{\pm}(T)=+\infty
for every probability measure
\mu
over
R
having full support.
Moreover, the only functions in
L^{2}(R,\mu)
in the kernel of
T
are constants.

Proof.

By the Wigner semi-circle law (Proposition
7.1
), with probability
1
, we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}s_{\pm}(S_{N})=+\infty.

On the other hand, by the random construction of the Erdős–Rényi–Rado graph, almost surely there is a sequence of finite subsets
R_{N}\subset R
with
R_{N}\nearrow R
as
N\to\infty
such that

S_{N}=S(R_{N}).

Thus by Theorem
4.2
(i), we have

s_{\pm}(R,d)=+\infty
, which by Theorem
4.2
(v) implies
s_{\pm}(K)=+\infty
.
Finally, Proposition
4.14
yields
s_{\pm}(T)=+\infty
.

It remains to compute the dimension of the kernel
\ker T
of
T
. Since
T=PKP
, clearly
\mathbf{1}\in\ker T
.
Since
P
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(R,\mu)
, we have
0\neq f\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}\cap\ker T
if and only if
K(f)
is collinear to the constant function
\mathbf{1}
in
L^{2}(R,\mu)
. Number the vertices of
R
by positive integers. The fact
\mu
has full support implies
(Kf)_{i}=(Kf)_{j}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
. The sequence
(Kf)_{i}
has an infinite “matrix multiplication” representation. More specifically, set

S=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(d^{2}(i,j)\right)_{i,j=1}^{\infty},\ v_{j}=f_{j}\mu(\{j\}).

We then have

\displaystyle(Kf)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{ij}v_{j}.

For any
n\geq 1
, define

\displaystyle v_{n}^{+}=\sum_{j\leq n,\ v_{j}\geq 0}v_{j},\ v_{n}^{-}=\sum_{j\leq n,\ v_{j}<0}v_{j}

Because
\mu
has full support and
f\neq 0
, we see
v_{n}^{+}\nearrow v^{+}>0
and
v_{n}^{-}\searrow v^{-}<0
. Since
f\in L^{2}(R,\mu)
and
\mu(R)=1
, the sequence
(v_{j})
is absolutely convergent and
v^{+},v^{-}
are finite. Using the finite extension property of the Rado graph, for any fixed
n\geq 1
, we can find
i_{1}>n
such that
i_{1}
is adjacent to all vertices in the summation of
v_{n}^{+}
while non-adjacent to all vertices in the summation of
v_{n}^{-}
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle(Kf)_{i_{1}}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(v_{n}^{+}+4v_{n}^{-}+\sum_{j>n}v_{j}d^{2}(i_{1},j)\right)

Similarly we can find
i_{2}>n
such that

\displaystyle(Kf)_{i_{2}}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(4v_{n}^{+}+v_{n}^{-}+\sum_{j>n}v_{j}d^{2}(i_{2},j)\right)

Using
(Kf)_{i_{1}}=(Kf)_{i_{2}}
, we get

(7.5)

\displaystyle-3v_{n}^{+}+3v_{n}^{-}+\sum_{j>n}(d^{2}(i_{1},j)-d^{2}(i_{2},j))v_{j}=0

Since the diameter of
(R,d)
is
2
, we find

\displaystyle\biggr|\sum_{j>n}\left(d^{2}(i_{1},j)-d^{2}(i_{2},j)\right)v_{j}\biggr|\leq 4\sum_{j>n}|v_{j}|

Since
\{v_{j}\}
is absolutely convergent, we find a contradiction to (
7.5
) for sufficiently large
n
. It follows that
\ker T\cap\mathbf{1}^{\perp}=\{0\}
. Since
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
has codimension
1
in
L^{2}(R,\mu)
,
the dimension of
\ker T
cannot exceed
1
. Because
\ker T
contains constants, they are
the only elements of the kernel of
T
as claimed.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

In
[
12
]
, the authors showed for a homogeneous closed connected Riemannian manifold
X
equipped with the canonical distance
d
and Riemannian volume
\mu
, the constants are in the kernel of the MDS defining operator
T
for
(X,d,\mu)
. The results on such smooth manifolds given by these authors appear to share similarities with the theorem above. However, in contrast to the Riemannian volume measure on homogeneous spaces, a probability measure with full support on a homogeneous countable metric space can never be homogeneous, i.e., invariant under automorphisms of the graph.

7.3.
Signature ratio limit of i.i.d. generated finite subsets of the Rado graph

We examine the following question on the Rado graph. Let
(R_{N})_{N\geq 1}
be an increasing sequence of finite subsets of
R
such that
R_{N}\nearrow R
, endowed the restriction of the metric
d
. Denote by
\Delta(R_{N})
the ratio of the number of positive eigenvalues over the number of negative eigenvalues of the associated matrix
S(R_{N})
, that is,

\Delta(R_{N}):=\frac{s_{+}(S(R_{N}))}{s_{-}(S(R_{N}))}.

We ask whether the limit
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\Delta(R_{N})
exists on the extended real line, and whether this limit is independent of the choice of
(R_{N})_{N\geq 1}
. Note first that the Wigner semicircle law and the random construction of
R
imply there exists a sequence
R_{N}\nearrow R
such that
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\Delta(R_{N})=1
, see Section
7.2
. However, this is not true for all sequences, as the following example shows.

Example 7.4
.

Fix any point
R_{1}^{\prime}=\{r_{1}\}
. By the finite extension property, if
R^{\prime}_{N}\subset R
is finite such that all pairs of vertices are adjacent, there exists
r_{N+1}\in R\setminus R_{k}^{\prime}
such that
R^{\prime}_{N+1}=R^{\prime}_{N}\cup\{r_{N+1}\}
has all pairs of vertices adjacent. Thus, we obtain a strictly increasing sequence
(R^{\prime}_{N})_{N\geq 1}
of finite subsets of
R
such that the vertices of
R^{\prime}_{N}
are all adjacent for each
N\geq 1
. Each
R_{N}^{\prime}
with the metric induced by
d
can be isometrically embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
, but not in
\mathbb{R}^{N-2}
. By the Proposition
5.1
,
we have
s_{+}(S(R_{N}^{\prime}))=N-1
and
s_{-}(S(R_{N}^{\prime}))=1
for this sequence. Note that
R\setminus\cup_{N=1}^{\infty}R^{\prime}_{N}
is infinite, i.e.

R\setminus\cup_{N=1}^{\infty}R^{\prime}_{N}=\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots\}.

Define
R_{N}=R^{\prime}_{N^{2}}\cup\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\}
, so we have
R_{N}\nearrow R
.
using the Cauchy interlacing theorem, it is easy to show for the sequence
(R_{N})_{N\geq 1}
defined as above, we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\Delta(R_{N})=\infty
.

Thus we see the limit
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\Delta(R_{N})
is not unique. Moreover, similarly one can show that it does not always exist on the extended real line.
Although the limit of the ratios
\Delta(R_{N})
depends on the choice of
R_{N}\nearrow R
, they typically converge to
1
in the following sense.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
G
be a random Erdős–Rényi–Rado graph,
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}
with full support, and
\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,
be i.i.d. random elements with
\operatorname{law}(\xi_{i})=\mu
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, independent of
G
.
Then, for
X_{m}:=(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
one has

\lim_{m\to\infty}\Delta(X_{m})=1

almost surely.

Proof.

Since
\mu
has an infinite support, a random sequence
\xi=(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
a.s. has infinitely many distinct elements.
For such a realization of
\xi
, define an auxiliary sequence
\hat{\xi}=(\hat{\xi}_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{N}
which is obtained from
\xi
by cancelling the repeating elements in the order of their appearance.
Since
G
and
\xi
are independent, the conditional law of
G
given
\xi
(uniquely defined for a.e.
\xi
) does not depend on
\xi
and coincides with the marginal law of
G
, that is, the distributional law of an Erdős–Rényi–Rado random graph.
The matrices
\hat{S}_{N}
corresponding to
S_{N}
(defined by (
7.4
)) are given by the formulae

(\hat{S}_{N})_{ij}:=\dfrac{3}{2}\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\xi}_{i},\hat{\xi}_{j}}-2,\;(\hat{S}_{N})_{ii}=0,\quad 1\leq i\neq j\leq N.

Since all
\hat{\xi}_{i}
are by definition distinct, by the construction of an Erdős–Rényi–Rado graph and Proposition
7.1
we obtain that ESDs of
\hat{S}_{N}
converge to the semicircle law a.s. (conditioned on
\xi
, i.e. with
\xi
and hence also
\hat{\xi}
fixed). Therefore,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{s_{+}(\hat{S}_{N})}{s_{-}(\hat{S}_{N})}=1,

and by the Fubini theorem, this holds (unconditionally) a.s.

Finally, by the properties of the Rado graph, one a.s. has
(\hat{S}_{N})_{ij}=-\frac{1}{2}d^{2}_{G}(\hat{\xi}_{i},\hat{\xi}_{j})
, i.e.
\hat{S}_{N}=S(\hat{X}_{N})
, where
\hat{X}_{N}:=\{\hat{\xi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Then by Lemma
4.9
we get

\lim_{m\to\infty}\Delta(X_{m})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\Delta(\hat{X}_{N})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{s_{+}(S(\hat{X}_{N}))}{s_{-}(S(\hat{X}_{N}))}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{s_{+}(\hat{S}_{N})}{s_{-}(\hat{S}_{N})}=1\text{ a.s.}

as claimed.
∎

The above Proposition
7.5
shows that the limit of the ratios
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\Delta\left((\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\right)

is equal to
1
almost surely for almost every realization of the random Rado graph
G
, once the random points
\xi_{i}
in the Gromov–Vershik scheme are independent of
G
. This is however not the case when we fix a specific realization of the Rado graph as the following examples show. In fact, we show that
the limit of the ratios
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\Delta\left((\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\right)
may become as large as desired (Example
7.6
) or even infinity (Example
7.7
), all this of course, with some positive probability and depending on the choice of the measure
\mu
.

Example 7.6
.

Let
(R,d)
be as before. Let
E:=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots\}\subset R
be the infinite Hilbertian simplex, i.e.,
d(e_{i},e_{j})=\delta_{ij}
(we call it Hilbertian since
(E,d)
can be isometrically embedded in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}
).
The set
E_{0}:=R\setminus E
is clearly infinite, and we enumerate its elements so that

E_{0}=\{e_{1}^{0},e_{2}^{0},\ldots\}.

Fix any integer
j\geq 1
. For
k=1,\ldots,j
, define

\displaystyle E_{k}:=\{e^{k}_{l}:=e_{lj+k}:l\in\mathbb{N}\},

so that
E=\bigcup_{k=1}^{j}E_{k}
, and the sets
E_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,j
, are disjoint.
We construct a probability measure
\mu
of full support on
R
such that
\mu(\{e^{0}_{l}\})=\mu(\{e^{k}_{l}\})

for all
l\geq 0
and
k=1,\ldots,j
. Note that in this way

(7.6)

\mu(E_{0})=\mu(E_{1})=\ldots=\mu(E_{j})=\frac{1}{j+1}

Let
\xi=(\xi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
with
\xi_{i}\colon\Omega\rightarrow R
be the i.i.d. random sequence from
(R,d,\mu)
.
For each
k=0,\ldots,j
, denote by

N_{E_{k}}(m)
the number of
distinct
elements in
E_{k}
of the finite random sequence
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{m})
.

We show first that for
p_{j}:=1/(j+1)>0
, one has

(7.7)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{N_{E_{0}}(m,\omega)\leq N_{E_{k}}(m,\omega),k=1,\ldots,j\right\}\right)\geq p_{j}

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
In fact, setting

\Omega_{k}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:N_{E_{k}}(m,\omega)\leq N_{E_{i}}(m,\omega),i=0,\ldots,j\right\},

we clearly get

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})=\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})=\ldots=\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{j}),

and
\bigcup_{k=0}^{j}\Omega_{k}=\Omega
.
Therefore,

1=\mathbb{P}(\Omega)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{j}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{k})=(j+1)\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0}),

hence the claim (
7.7
).

Once (
7.7
) is proven, we consider the set
\hat{R}_{N(m)}
of distinct elements
of the finite random sequence
(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, and the respective square matrix

S({\hat{R}_{N(m)}})
as well as the square matrices

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{N_{(E_{0},m)}}^{0}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}(d^{2}(\xi_{i},\xi_{j}))_{i,j=1,\ldots,m,\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\in E_{0}\cap\hat{R}_{N(m)}},

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{N_{E}(m)}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}(d^{2}(\xi_{i},\xi_{j}))_{i,j=1,\ldots,m,\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\in E\cap\hat{R}_{N(m)}},

where the lower index stands for the size of the respective matrix.
One has

\displaystyle s_{+}\left(E_{N_{E}(m)}\right)

\displaystyle=N_{E}(m)-1,

since
E_{N_{E}(m)}
is
N_{E}(m)\times N_{E}(m)
matrix and
(E,d)
is isometrically embedded in a Hilbert space.
Hence

(7.8)

s_{+}\left(\hat{E}_{N_{E}(m)}\right)=N_{E}(m)-1.

We observe now that the matrix
S({\hat{R}_{N(m)}})
has the block form

\displaystyle S({\hat{R}_{N(m)}})=\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{S}_{N_{E_{0}}(m)}^{0}&&*\\
*&&\hat{E}_{N_{E}(m)}\end{matrix}\right)

(where
*
stands for some blocks).
Thus in view of the Cauchy interlacing theorem, we get

(7.9)

s_{+}(S({\hat{R}_{N(m)}}))\geq s_{+}(\hat{E}_{N_{E}(m)})=N_{E}(m)-1,

the latter equality due to (
7.8
).
On the other hand,
S({\hat{R}_{N(m)}})
is an

From (

Example 7.7
.

Let
(R,d)
be as before.
According to the Example

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\exists j\in\mathbb{N}:\xi_{j}>\max_{1\leq i<j}\xi_{i}+1\right\}\right)

Appendix A
Auxiliary lemmas on operators and their spectra

We collect here some technical results on the spectra of operators on Hilbert spaces used throughout the paper.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space.

(i)

If
A
,
B
are compact self-adjoint operators on
H
, then

s_{\pm}(A+B)\leq s_{\pm}(A)+s_{\pm}(B).

(ii)

If
A
and
\{A_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, are compact self-adjoint operators on
H
such that
A_{n}\to A
in operator norm, then

s_{\pm}(A)\leq\liminf s_{\pm}(A_{n}).

Proof.

We prove the claims for
s_{+}
. The proof for
s_{-}
is identical.

We start with the proof of (i). Assume that
s_{+}(A)<\infty
and
s_{+}(B)<\infty
, otherwise the claim is trivial. Suppose that
s_{+}(A+B)>s_{+}(A)+s_{+}(B)
.
Then there are at least
m+1=s_{+}(A)+s_{+}(B)+1
linearly independent eigenvectors
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
of
A+B
corresponding to positive eigenvalues.
Let
L_{A}
(resp.,
L_{B}
) be the linear span of all eigenvectors of
A
(resp.,
B
) corresponding to positive eigenvalues. Clearly, we have that
\dim L_{A}=s_{+}(A)
and
\dim L_{B}=s_{+}(B)
.
Then
\dim(L_{A}+L_{B})\leq s_{A}(A)+s_{+}(B)<m
and there is a non-zero vector
u\in\operatorname{span}\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\cap(L_{A}+L_{B})^{\perp}
. Since
A
and
B
are self-adjoint, this yields
(u,Au)\leq 0
,
(u,Bu)\leq 0
, hence
(u,(A+B)u)\leq 0
what leads to a contradiction. Therefore,
s_{+}(A+B)\leq s_{+}(A)+s_{+}(B)
as claimed.

Now, let us prove (ii). Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
be unit norm eigenvectors of
A
corresponding to positive eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{m}>0
. Define symmetric matrices
\bar{A}:=((u_{i},Au_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{m}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})
and
\bar{A}_{n}:=((u_{i},A_{n}u_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{m}
. Then for
\lVert A_{n}-A\rVert<\lambda_{m}
, we have

\lambda_{\min}(\bar{A}_{n})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\bar{A})+\lambda_{\min}(\bar{A}_{n}-\bar{A})\geq\lambda_{m}-\lVert\bar{A}_{n}-\bar{A}\rVert\geq\lambda_{m}-\lVert A_{n}-A\rVert>0.

In this case,
s_{+}(A_{n})\geq s_{+}(\bar{A}_{n})\geq m
, therefore,

m\leq\liminf_{n}s_{+}(A_{n}).

Taking supremum over all
m\leq s_{+}(A)
, we get the result.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\mu
be a Borel probability measure, and

A\colon L^{2}(X,\mu)\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
be an integral linear compact operator defined by the formula

(Au)(x):=\int_{X}a(x,y)u(y)\,d\mu(y)

with
a\in L^{2}(X\times X,\mu\otimes\mu)
satisfying
a(x,y)=a(y,x)
for
\mu\otimes\mu
-a.e.
(x,y)\in X\times X
, so that

A
is self-adjoint.
Let
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
u_{i}\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
be an enumeration of its eigenvalues (counting multiplicities) and the respective eigenfunctions of unit norm in
L^{2}(X,\mu)
.
Then

(A.1)

a(x,y)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}u_{i}(x)u_{i}(y),

the equality in the sense of
L^{2}(X\times X,\mu\otimes\mu)
.

Proof.

By the Hilbert–Schmidt theorem,
\{u_{i}\}
form a topological basis in
L^{2}(X,\mu)
. Thus
representing
a
in terms of the Fourier series in
u_{i}\otimes u_{j}
in
L^{2}(X\times X,\mu\otimes\mu)
,
we get

a(x,y)=\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}u_{i}(x)u_{i}(y).

For the Fourier coefficients
c_{ij}
, we have

\displaystyle c_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\left(\int_{X}a(x,y)u_{i}(u)\,d\mu(y)\right)u_{j}(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{X}\lambda_{i}u_{i}(x)u_{j}(x)\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\delta_{ij},

where
\delta_{ij}
stands for the Kronecker delta, which implies (
A.1
).
∎

.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\mu
be a Borel probability measure, and

A\colon L^{2}(X,\mu)\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
be an integral linear operator defined by the formula

(Au)(x):=\int_{X}a(x,y)u(y)\,d\mu(y)

with a continuous function
a\in L^{2}(X\times X,\mu\otimes\mu)
satisfying
a(x,y)=a(y,x)
. The following assertions hold.

(i)

For every finite subset
X_{N}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subset\operatorname{supp}\mu
, one has

(A.2)

\displaystyle s_{\pm}(A)\geq s_{\pm}(A(X_{N})),

(A.3)

\displaystyle s_{\pm}(P_{\mu}AP_{\mu})\geq s_{\pm}(\Pi_{N}A(X_{N})\Pi_{N}),

where
A(X_{N}):=(a(x_{i},x_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.

(ii)

If
\mu
is a Radon measure (which is automatically guaranteed when
(X,d)
is Polish), then there is a sequence of finite subsets
X_{N}\subset X
such that

(A.4)

\displaystyle s_{\pm}(A)\leq\liminf_{N}s_{\pm}(A(X_{N})),

(A.5)

\displaystyle\ s_{\pm}(P_{\mu}AP_{\mu})\leq\liminf_{N}s_{\pm}(\Pi_{N}A(X_{N})\Pi_{N}).

Proof.

To prove (i), let
\varepsilon>0
be such that all the positive eigenvalues of
A(X_{N})
belong to the interval
[\epsilon,+\infty)
.
Choose an
r>0
sufficiently small such that all open balls
B_{i}:=B_{r}(x_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,N
, are pairwise disjoint, and for all
i,j=1,\ldots,N
the estimate

(A.6)

\displaystyle\left|a(x_{i}^{\prime},x_{j}^{\prime})-a(x_{i},x_{j})\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad\mbox{ for all $x_{i}^{\prime}\in B_{i},\ x_{j}^{\prime}\in B_{j}$}.

holds. Define

v_{i}:=\frac{1}{\mu(B_{i})}\mathbf{1}_{B_{i}}\in L^{2}(X,\mu),\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

(recall that
\mu(B_{i})>0
since
x_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}\mu
).
Let
Q_{N}\colon L^{2}(X,\mu)\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
be the orthogonal projector onto
L_{N}:=\operatorname{span}\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
Define the linear operator
\tilde{A}_{N}\colon L^{2}(X,\mu)\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
by

(\tilde{A}_{N}u)(x):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\int_{X}a(x_{i},x_{j})\mathbf{1}_{B_{i}}(x)\mathbf{1}_{B_{j}}(y)u(y)\,d\mu(y).

For every
u\in L_{N}
, setting
U:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B_{i}
, we have

(A.7)

\|\mathbf{1}_{U}Au-\tilde{A}_{N}u\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{1}_{U}Au-\tilde{A}_{N}u\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|u\|_{1}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|u\|_{2},

where the first and the last estimates are just Höder inequality minding that
\mu
is a probability measure, and the second one is due to (
A.6
).
Since
Q_{N}v=Q_{N}(\mathbf{1}_{U}v)
for all
v\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
, then from (
A.7
) we get that
the operator norm of
Q_{N}AQ_{N}-\tilde{A}_{N}=Q_{N}(A-\tilde{A}_{N})Q_{N}
is bounded by
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
Now, let us define the embedding operator
E_{N}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to L_{N}
as
E_{N}x:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}v_{i}
, so that
\operatorname{Im}E_{N}=L_{N}
and

(E_{N}^{*}v_{i})_{k}=\frac{1}{\mu(B_{i})}\delta_{ik}

with
\delta_{ik}
standing for the Kronecker delta.
Since

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{N}(v_{k})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{B_{i}}(x)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{X}a(x_{i},x_{j})1_{B_{j}}(y)\dfrac{1}{\mu(B_{k})}\mathbf{1}_{B_{k}}(y)d\mu(y),

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{B_{i}}(x)a(x_{i},x_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a(x_{i},x_{k})\mu(B_{i})v_{i},

then

E_{N}^{*}\tilde{A}_{N}E_{N}=A(X_{N})
.
We have therefore

\displaystyle s_{+}(A)

\displaystyle\geq s_{+}(Q_{N}AQ_{N})

by Lemma
A.4
(i) with
Q:=Q_{N}
,
B:=Q_{N}AQ_{N}
,
H_{1}=H_{2}=L^{2}(X,\mu)

\displaystyle\geq s_{+}(\tilde{A}_{N})\quad\mbox{by the Weyl inequality}

\displaystyle=s_{+}(E_{N}^{*}\tilde{A}_{N}E_{N})\quad\mbox{by Lemma~\ref{lm spm_control1}(iii) since $(\ker\tilde{A}_{N})^{\perp}\subset L_{N}=\operatorname{Im}E_{N}$}

\displaystyle=s_{+}(A(X_{N})).

Now, let
\bar{Q}_{N}\colon L^{2}(X,\mu)\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
be the orthogonal projector onto
L_{N}\cap\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
.
In the same way as above,
\bar{Q}_{N}(A-\tilde{A}_{N})\bar{Q}_{N}
is bounded by
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
Furthermore,

(\mathbf{1},E_{N}x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{1},v_{i})x_{i}=(\mathbf{1}_{N},x),

hence
\operatorname{Im}(E_{N}\Pi_{N})=L_{N}\cap\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
and
\bar{Q}_{N}E_{N}\Pi_{N}=E_{N}\Pi_{N}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle s_{+}(P_{\mu}AP_{\mu})

\displaystyle\geq s_{+}(\bar{Q}_{N}A\bar{Q}_{N})\quad\mbox{by Lemma~\ref{lm spm_control1} since $P_{\mu}\bar{Q}_{N}=\bar{Q}_{N}$}

\displaystyle\geq s_{+}(\bar{Q}_{N}\tilde{A}_{N}\bar{Q}_{N})\quad\mbox{by the Weyl inequality}

\displaystyle\geq s_{+}(\Pi_{N}E_{N}^{*}\tilde{A}_{N}E_{N}\Pi_{N})\quad\mbox{by Lemma~\ref{lm spm_control1} since $\bar{Q}_{N}E_{N}\Pi_{N}=E_{N}\Pi_{N}$}

\displaystyle=s_{+}(\Pi_{N}A(X_{N})\Pi_{N}).

The proofs of
s_{-}(A)\geq s_{-}(A(X_{N}))
and
s_{-}(P_{\mu}AP_{\mu})\geq s_{-}(\Pi_{N}A(X_{N})\Pi_{N})
are completely symmetric.

We now prove (ii). To this aim, for an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, using that
\mu
is the Radon measure,
we can find a compact
K\subset X
with
\mu(X\setminus K)
be so small that

\int_{(X\times X)\setminus(K\times K)}a^{2}(x,y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)<\varepsilon^{2}.

This can be done in view of by absolute continuity of the integral and of the fact that

\displaystyle\mu((X\times X)\setminus(K\times K))

\displaystyle=\mu(((X\setminus K)\times X)\cup(K\times(X\setminus K)))

\displaystyle\leq 2\mu(X\setminus K).

We find a
\delta>0
, a
\delta
-net
X_{N}:=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subset K
, and a partition of
K
into
N
disjoint Borel subsets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{N}
such that

(A.8)

\displaystyle\left|a(x_{i}^{\prime},x_{j}^{\prime})-a(x_{i},x_{j})\right|<\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{ for all $x_{i}^{\prime}\in B_{i}$, $x_{j}^{\prime}\in B_{j}$}

(e.g.,
B_{i}
being a “Voronoi cell” for
x_{i}
).

Much similar to the prof of (i), let
Q_{N}\colon L^{2}(X,\mu)\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
be the orthogonal projection onto
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}_{B_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and denote by
\tilde{A}(X_{N})\colon L^{2}(X,\mu)\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
the linear operator defined by the formula

(\tilde{A}_{N}u)(x):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\int_{X}a(x_{i},x_{j})\mathbf{1}_{B_{i}}(x)\mathbf{1}_{B_{j}}(y)u(y)\,d\mu(y).

For every
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
, we have

\displaystyle\|Q_{N}AQ_{N}u-\tilde{A}_{N}u\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|Q_{N}AQ_{N}u-\tilde{A}_{N}u\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\|u\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\|u\|_{2},

and

\displaystyle\|Au-Q_{N}AQ_{N}u\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X}\,d\mu(x)\left(\int_{X}\left|a(x,y)-\mathbf{1}_{K}(x)a(x,y)\mathbf{1}_{K}(y)\right|\cdot|u(y)|\,d\mu(y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{2}^{2}\int_{X}\,d\mu(x)\left(\int_{X}\left|a(x,y)-\mathbf{1}_{K}(x)a(x,y)\mathbf{1}_{K}(y)\right|^{2}\,d\mu(y)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}.

This implies

\|Au-\tilde{A}_{N}u\|_{2}\leq\|Au-Q_{N}AQ_{N}u\|_{2}+\|Q_{N}AQ_{N}u-\tilde{A}_{N}u\|_{2}\leq 2\varepsilon\|u\|_{2}.

In other words, we constructed a sequence of
X_{N}\subset X
such that

\tilde{A}_{N}\to A
as linear bounded operators over

as

.
This implies

s_{\pm}(A)\leq\liminf_{N}s_{\pm}(\tilde{A}_{N})

by Lemma

(ii).
In the same way one can show that

hence concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
be Hilbert spaces,
A
and
B
be linear compact self-adjoint operators over
H_{2}
and
H_{1}
, respectively, such that

, where

Then

(i)

s_{\pm}(B)\leq s_{\pm}(A)
.

(ii)

If, moreover, the codimension of the image of

is

, then

s_{\pm}(B)\geq s_{\pm}(A)-k.

(iii)

If

B

Qu_{j}
,

are also linearly independent
since otherwise
\ker B\cap\operatorname{span}\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\neq\{0\}
which is impossible.
Hence,
(Av,v)>0
for every
v\in\operatorname{span}\{Qu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
,

.
This implies
s_{+}(A)\geq n
, and therefore, taking the supremum over all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

, we get

be a set of linearly independent eigenvectors of

\dim\left(\operatorname{span}\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\cap\operatorname{Im}Q\right)\geq m-k.

Thus we may choose at least

. Thus implies

, i.e., Claim (ii).

from

. Hence,

, and this again implies

(A.9)

Furthermore,

hence
\operatorname{Im}Q+\ker A
is dense in

, thus
\operatorname{Im}(P_{A}Q)

.
Let again
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\subset H_{2}

corresponding to strictly positive eigenvalues, thus the matrix

is positive definite.
Since all
w_{j}

, then one can find

with each

,
\tilde{w}\neq 0

Choosing

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\Sigma=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
be such that
\Sigma-\Sigma\subset C_{n,p}
(thus,
d_{n,p}(z_{i},z_{j})
is defined).
Then the strict triangle inequality

d_{n,p}(z_{1},z_{3})<d_{n,p}(z_{1},z_{2})+d_{n,p}(z_{2},z_{3})

is equivalent to the strict one-sided Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

(z_{1}-z_{2},z_{2}-z_{3})_{n,p}<d_{n,p}(z_{1},z_{2})d_{n,p}(z_{2},z_{3}).

Proof.

The strict triangle inequality is clearly equivalent to

d_{n,p}^{2}(z_{1},z_{3})<d_{n,p}^{2}(z_{1},z_{2})+2d_{n,p}(z_{1},z_{2})d_{n,p}(z_{2},z_{3})+d_{n,p}^{2}(z_{2},z_{3}).

Since

\displaystyle d_{n,p}^{2}(z_{1},z_{3})

\displaystyle:=(z_{1}-z_{3},z_{1}-z_{3})

\displaystyle=(z_{1}-z_{2},z_{1}-z_{2})+2(z_{1}-z_{2},z_{2}-z_{3})+(z_{2}-z_{3},z_{2}-z_{3})

\displaystyle=d_{n,p}^{2}(z_{1},z_{2})+2(z_{1}-z_{2},z_{2}-z_{3})+d_{n,p}^{2}(z_{2},z_{3}),

we get the result.
∎
