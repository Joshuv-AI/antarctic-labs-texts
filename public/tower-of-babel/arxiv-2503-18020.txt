A New Perspective on Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of Bicomplex Linear Operators
Anjali, Akhil Prakash, Amita, Prabhat Kumar

Abstract
. This paper deals with eigenvalues and eigenvectors of bicomplex linear operators defined on bicomplex space. We investigate the properties of these operators in the context of eigenvalues and eigenvectors, along with some relevant theorems. Several theorems are explored to establish conditions for bicomplex eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Additionally, we examine the structure of the eigenspaces corresponding to these eigenvalues,analyze their properties, and discuss relevant theorems.

1.
Preliminaries and Notations

This section introduces bicomplex numbers, covering key concepts such as idempotent representation and the cartesian product of bicomplex space. It provides a comprehensive overview of these fundamental notions and presents essential results related to bicomplex numbers.

Bicomplex numbers:

A bicomplex number is an element that can be expressed in the following form:

\xi={u}_{1}+{i}_{1}{u}_{2}+{i}_{2}{u}_{3}+{i}_{1}{i}_{2}{u}_{4},\;\;{u}_{k}\in\mathbb{R},\;\;1\leqslant k\leqslant 4,\;\;\mbox{with}\;\;\;{i}_{1}{i}_{2}\ =\ {i}_{2}{i}_{1},\;\;{i}_{1}^{2}={i}_{2}^{2}=-1,

where
\displaystyle u_{k}
are real numbers with
\displaystyle 1\leqslant k\leqslant 4
, and the unit vectors
\displaystyle i_{1}
and
\displaystyle i_{2}
satisfy the conditions
\displaystyle i_{1}i_{2}=i_{2}i_{1}
and
\displaystyle i_{1}^{2}=i_{2}^{2}=-1
. The set of all bicomplex numbers is denoted as
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
, referred to as the bicomplex space. The symbols
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1},\;\mathbb{C}_{0}
, for convenience, denote the set of all complex numbers and the set of all real numbers respectively. The bicomplex space
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
can be described in the following two ways:

By considering the real components form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{{u}_{1}+{i}_{1}{u}_{2}+{i}_{2}{u}_{3}+{i}_{1}{i}_{2}{u}_{4}\;:\;{u}_{1},{u}_{2},{u}_{3},{u}_{4}\in\mathbb{C}_{0}\}

and the complex components form:

(1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{{z}_{1}+{i}_{2}{z}_{2}\;:\;{z}_{1},{z}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}\}.

It is important to note that
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
contains zero-divisors, which means it is not a field but rather an algebra over
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}
. In
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
, there are exactly four idempotent elements;
\displaystyle 0,1,e_{1},
and
\displaystyle e_{2}
. The nontrivial idempotent elements
\displaystyle e_{1}
and
\displaystyle e_{2}
are defined as follows:

e_{1}\coloneqq\frac{(1+i_{1}i_{2})}{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad e_{2}\coloneqq\frac{(1-i_{1}i_{2})}{2}.

Notice that
\displaystyle e_{1}+e_{2}=1
,
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}=e_{2}e_{1}=0
and
\displaystyle e_{1}^{2}=e_{1},\;e_{2}^{2}=e_{2}
.

Definition 1.1
.

[
8
]
(Principal Ideal)
The proper principal ideals generated by
\displaystyle e_{1}
and
\displaystyle e_{2}
are denoted as
\displaystyle I_{1}
and
\displaystyle I_{2}
and defined as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{1}=:\{\xi e_{1}:\xi\in\mathbb{C}_{2}\}=\{\xi^{-}e_{1}:\xi^{-}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{2}=:\{\xi e_{2}:\xi\in\mathbb{C}_{2}\}=\{\xi^{+}e_{2}:\xi^{+}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}\}.

Singular and non-singular elements:

An element
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{C}_{2}
is said to be a non-singular if there exists
\displaystyle\eta\in\mathbb{C}_{2}
such that
\displaystyle\xi.\eta=1
. In other words,
\displaystyle\xi
possesses a multiplicative inverse within
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
. Also, an element in
\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{2}
that lacks such a multiplicative inverse is called a singular element. Moreover, there is an infinite number of elements that lack a multiplicative inverse.

Notably,
\displaystyle\xi=z_{1}+i_{2}z_{2}
is classified as singular if and only if the absolute value of
\displaystyle z_{1}+i_{2}z_{2}
equals zero, written as
\displaystyle|z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}|=0
. The set of all singular elements in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
is denoted as
\displaystyle O_{2}=I_{1}\cup I_{2}
.

The following result mentioned in
[
8
]
can be verified easily.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{C}_{2}
be a bicomplex number. Then we have

\displaystyle\xi
is singular
\displaystyle\text{if and only if}\quad\xi\in I_{1}\cup I_{2}

in other words,
\displaystyle\xi
is non-singular
\displaystyle\text{if and only if}\quad\xi\notin I_{1}\cup I_{2}
.

Idempotent representation of bicomplex numbers:

[
8
]

Every bicomplex number
\displaystyle\xi={z}_{1}+{i}_{2}{z}_{2}
can be uniquely represented as the complex combination of elements
\displaystyle e_{1}
and
\displaystyle e_{2}
in the following form:

\xi=(z_{1}-i_{1}z_{2})e_{1}+(z_{1}+i_{1}z_{2})e_{2},

where complex numbers
\displaystyle(z_{1}-i_{1}z_{2})
and
\displaystyle(z_{1}+i_{1}z_{2})
are called idempotent components of
\displaystyle\xi
and will be denoted by
\displaystyle\xi^{-}
and
\displaystyle\xi^{+}
respectively. Therefore the number
\displaystyle\xi={z}_{1}+{i}_{2}{z}_{2}
can also be written as
\displaystyle\xi=\xi^{-}e_{1}+\xi^{+}e_{2}
, where
\displaystyle\xi^{-}=z_{1}-i_{1}z_{2}
and
\displaystyle\xi^{+}=z_{1}+i_{1}z_{2}
.
Moreover the idempotent representation of the product of elements
\displaystyle\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{C}_{2}
can be seen easily to be as:

\xi\cdot\eta=\left(\xi^{-}\eta^{-}\right)e_{1}+\left(\xi^{+}\eta^{+}\right)e_{2}.

Cartesian product:

[
5
]

The
\displaystyle n
-times Cartesian product of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
denoted by
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
and consists of all
\displaystyle n
-tuples of bicomplex numbers of the form
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})
. Each component
\displaystyle\xi_{i}
belongs to
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
for
\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Formally, this is expressed as:

{\mathbb{C}}_{2}^{n}\eqqcolon\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}):\;\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{2};\;i=1,2,\ldots,n\}.

Furthermore, using idempotent representation, each element of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
can be expressed uniquely as follows:

(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})=(\xi_{1}^{-},\xi_{2}^{-},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{-})e_{1}+(\xi_{1}^{+},\xi_{2}^{+},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{+})e_{2},

such that
\displaystyle(\xi_{1}^{-},\xi_{2}^{-},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{-})
and
\displaystyle(\xi_{1}^{+},\xi_{2}^{+},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{+})
are n-tuples of complex numbers from the space
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n}
.

Cartesian idempotent product:

[
5
]

The Cartesian product in bicomplex spaces is defined as follows: for any subsets
\displaystyle S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n}
, their idempotent product, denoted
\displaystyle S_{1}\times_{e}S_{2}
, is defined as a subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
given by

(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle S_{1}\times_{e}S_{2}\eqqcolon\{e_{1}x+e_{2}y:x\in S_{1},y\in S_{2}\}.

Analogous to the concept of cartesian product of bicomplex number, for any subsets
\displaystyle H_{1},H_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{2}^{m\times n}
, their idempotent product, denoted as
\displaystyle H_{1}\times_{e}H_{2}
is defined to be a subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{m\times n}
given as:

(3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{1}\times_{e}H_{2}\eqqcolon\{e_{1}A+e_{2}B:A\in H_{1},B\in H_{2}\}.

Similarly for linear maps, for any non-empty subsets
\displaystyle A_{1},A_{2}
of the space of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}
-linear maps
\displaystyle\text{Hom}(\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n},\mathbb{C}_{1}^{m})
, their
idempotent product, written as
\displaystyle A_{1}\times_{e}A_{2}
, is defined to be a subset of
\displaystyle\text{Hom}(\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n},\mathbb{C}_{2}^{m})
given as

(4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{1}\times_{e}A_{2}\eqqcolon\{e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}:\;T_{1}\in A_{1},T_{2}\in A_{2}\}.

.

For the convenience, we denote the set of all
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}
-linear maps from
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n}
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{m}
by
\displaystyle L_{1}^{nm}
, instead of
\displaystyle\text{Hom}(\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n},\mathbb{C}_{1}^{m})

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}

. Clearly both
\displaystyle L_{1}^{nm}
and
\displaystyle L_{2}^{nm}
are vector spaces over

(5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dim(L_{1}^{nm})=mn\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\dim(L_{2}^{nm})=4mn.

.

(
Bicomplex matrix
):
A bicomplex matrix of order
\displaystyle m\times n
is denoted by
\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{m\times n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{m\times n}

2.
Eigenvalues and eigenvectors associated with
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}

Earlier,
[
5
]
had given an ”Idempotent method” for matrix representation of a linear map of the type
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}:\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{2}^{m}
. This method gives a one-one correspondence between the bicomplex matrices
\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times n}
and the linear operator’s
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}
on finite dimensional vector space
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
. This unique correspondence can help to develop the concept of eigenvalue and eigenvector of a bicomplex matrix
\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times n}
. For this reason, this linear operator
\displaystyle T
is vital for our analysis and plays an essential role from the bicomplex matrix point of view. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for further details. This section aims to analyze the facts and find the essential results related to eigenvalue and eigenvector of
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}
.

Definition 2.1
.

(Non-singular and Singular vectors in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
) :
A vector
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
is non-singular if at least one of
\displaystyle\xi_{i},i=1,2\ldots,n
is non-singular otherwise it is singular. Furthermore, for any
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
is non-singular then
\displaystyle(\xi_{1}^{-},\xi_{2}^{-},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{-})\neq 0
and
\displaystyle(\xi_{1}^{+},\xi_{2}^{+},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{+})\neq 0
but converse need not be true.

Example 2.2
.

Here,
\displaystyle(1,0,\ldots,0)
and
\displaystyle(0,1,\ldots,0)
are non-zero vectors of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n}
but
\displaystyle e_{1}(1,0,\ldots,0)+e_{2}(0,1,\ldots,0)=(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,0)
is a singular vector of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
.

Remark 2.3
.

If
\displaystyle 0\neq(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}

if and only if
either
\displaystyle(\xi_{1}^{-},\xi_{2}^{-},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{-})\neq 0
or
\displaystyle(\xi_{1}^{+},\xi_{2}^{+},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{+})\neq 0
.

Definition 2.4
.

(Modified eigenvalue and Modified eigenvector)
: Let
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}\in L_{1}^{n}\times_{e}L_{1}^{n}
be a linear operator on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}(\mathbb{C}_{1})
. A scalar,
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{1}
is an
eigenvalue
of
\displaystyle T
if there exist a non-zero vector
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
such that
\displaystyle T(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})=\lambda(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})
. Then the vector
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})
is called an
eigenvector
of
\displaystyle T
associated with the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{1}
. Furthermore, if
\displaystyle\kappa\in\mathbb{C}_{2}
with
\displaystyle T(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})=\kappa(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})
, then
\displaystyle\kappa
is called a
modified eigenvalue
of
\displaystyle T
and the non zero vector
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})
is called a
modified eigenvector
of
\displaystyle T
associated with the modified eigenvalue
\displaystyle\kappa
.

Remark 2.5
.

Every eigenvalue (eigenvector) of
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}
is a modified eigenvalue (modified eigenvector) of T but converse need not be true.

Example 2.6
.

Let
\displaystyle T_{1},T_{2}\colon\mathbb{C}_{1}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}_{1}^{2}
be two linear operators such that

T_{1}(x,y)=(x,0)\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{2}(x,y)=(x,y)\quad\forall\;(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}_{1}^{2}.

It is evident that
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}\colon\mathbb{C}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{1})\to\mathbb{C}_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{1})
such that
\displaystyle T(\xi,\eta)=(\xi,\eta e_{2})
. It is a routine matter to check that
\displaystyle(1,e_{2})
is an eigenvector (modified eigenvector) of T associated with the eigenvalue (modified eigenvalue) 1. Moreover,
\displaystyle(e_{1},0)
is a modified eigenvector of T associated with the modified eigenvalue
\displaystyle 1e_{1}+re_{2}
, for
\displaystyle r\in\mathbb{C}_{1}
.

Theorem 2.7
.

A linear operator
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}\in L_{1}^{n}\times_{e}L_{1}^{n}
is singular
if and only if
either
\displaystyle T_{1}
is singular or
\displaystyle T_{2}
is singular.

Proof.

Suppose that
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}\colon\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}(\mathbb{C}_{1})\to\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}(\mathbb{C}_{1})
is singular. Using
1.7
and
2.3
, it follows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle ker(e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2})\neq\{0\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle ker(T_{1})\times_{e}ker(T_{2})\neq\{0\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

there exists a non-zero vecctor

\displaystyle\displaystyle(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})e_{1}+(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})e_{2}\in ker(T_{1})\times_{e}ker(T_{2})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{such that either}\ 0\neq(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in ker(T_{1})\ \mbox{or}

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0\neq(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})\in ker(T_{2})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{either}\quad ker(T_{1})\neq\{0\}\quad\mbox{or}\quad ker(T_{2})\neq\{0\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{either}\quad T_{1}\quad\mbox{is singular}\quad\mbox{or}\quad T_{2}\quad\mbox{is singular}.

Thus the theorem is proved.
∎

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
\displaystyle T=e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}\in L_{1}^{n}\times_{e}L_{1}^{n}
,
\displaystyle I=e_{1}I+e_{2}I\in L_{1}^{n}\times_{e}L_{1}^{n}
be the identity linear operator and
\displaystyle\kappa=\kappa_{1}e_{1}+\kappa_{2}e_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}

\displaystyle(T-\kappa I)
is singular if and only if either
\displaystyle(T_{1}-\kappa_{1}I)
is singular or
\displaystyle(T_{2}-\kappa_{2}I)
is singular.

\displaystyle\displaystyle e_{1}\kappa_{1}(\eta_{1}^{-},\eta_{2}^{-},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{-})+e_{2}\kappa_{2}(\eta_{1}^{+},\eta_{2}^{+},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{+})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow{T}_{1}(\eta_{1}^{-},\eta_{2}^{-},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{-})=\kappa_{1}(\eta_{1}^{-},\eta_{2}^{-},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{-})

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle{T}_{2}(\eta_{1}^{+},\eta_{2}^{+},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{+})=\kappa_{2}(\eta_{1}^{+},\eta_{2}^{+},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{+})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\eta_{1}^{-},\eta_{2}^{-},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{-})\in{}^{T_{1}}(E)_{\kappa_{1}}

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\eta_{1}^{+},\eta_{2}^{+},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{+})\in{}^{T_{2}}(E)_{\kappa_{2}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\displaystyle{}^{T_{1}}(E)_{\kappa_{1}}\times_{e}{}^{T_{2}}(E)_{\kappa_{2}}

It shows that
{}^{T}(ME)_{\kappa}={}^{T_{1}}(E)_{\kappa_{1}}\times_{e}{}^{T_{2}}(E)_{\kappa_{2}}
.

Thus the theorem is proved.
∎
