Metabolomics of cerebrospinal fluid in pediatric neuroborreliosis: unraveling candidate immunometabolic signatures and diagnostic potential

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroborreliosis (LNB) poses a significant diagnostic challenge in paediatrics due to its non-specific clinical presentation and the limited sensitivity of standard tests. Introduction

Lyme neuroborreliosis (LNB) is a major manifestation of Borrelia burgdorferi infection within the central nervous system (CNS), with epidemiological data indicating a higher incidence in the pediatric population, likely due to increased outdoor exposure and differences in immune system maturation (Tveitnes et al., 2012). Establishing a definitive diagnosis of LNB remains difficult because its clinical presentation is often non-specific, frequently mimicking other inflammatory or infectious conditions of the CNS (Lohr et al., 2018). These diagnostic hurdles are compounded by the limited sensitivity of conventional serological assays, particularly during the early stages of the disease (Lohr et al., 2018). To address these limitations, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) metabolomics has emerged as a promising tool for identifying objective biomarkers. By quantifying low-molecular-weight metabolites, this approach provides a direct, time-sensitive snapshot of the biochemical alterations occurring within the CNS microenvironment (French et al., 2018; Plaatjie et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021).

Recent advancements in Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) have enabled more granular analysis of CSF metabolic profiles (French et al., 2018; Plaatjie et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021). A key area of interest is the tryptophan-kynurenine (Trp-Kyn) pathway, which is modulated by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). Previous studies have reported activation of this pathway in pediatric LNB, evidenced by elevated CSF concentrations of kynurenine and kynurenic acid (Sühs et al., 2019; Wickström et al., 2021). Furthermore, the kynurenine/tryptophan ratio has been suggested as a potential tool for distinguishing bacterial CNS infections from viral or autoimmune etiologies (Sühs et al., 2019). The activation of this pathway is also linked to neurotoxicity, with correlations observed between neurotoxic metabolites like quinolinic acid (QA) and established inflammatory markers (Quist-Paulsen et al., 2018; Wasilewski & Serrafi, 2026; Yan et al., 2021). Given that the early phase of LNB involves a distinct immunometabolic response, characterizing these shifts may provide insights into the mechanisms of blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption (Gasse et al., 1994; Wasilewski et al., 2025).

Despite these developments, pediatric-specific data remain limited, and the necessity of studying this demographic is underscored by age-dependent differences in neuroimmunological responses. While our broader research program includes investigations into systemic inflammatory markers and comparative blood-CSF analyses, the present study focuses specifically on characterizing the CSF metabolome to identify candidate metabolic signatures associated with pediatric LNB. Consequently, the primary aims of this pilot study are: (i) to perform a comprehensive metabolomic analysis of CSF in children with LNB; (ii) to evaluate the metabolic shifts associated with the disease; (iii) to correlate these findings with clinical parameters; and (iv) to integrate these results with known pathophysiological pathways, including immune activation and BBB integrity. We hypothesize that this approach will facilitate the identification of potential prognostic biomarkers and clarify the underlying immunometabolic mechanisms driving pediatric LNB.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval number KB 172/2026). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Study population and diagnostic criteria

The study cohort consisted of children recruited from the Lower Silesian Voivodeship in Poland. Demographic characteristics of the control group were as follows: mean age 14.25 ± 1.97 years (range: 11–17 years). The sex distribution of this group comprised 12 females and 8 males. The control group consisted of 20 healthy children, recruited from the same geographical region.

LNB was diagnosed based on clinical presentation, including neurological symptoms, and confirmed through established diagnostic parameters in accordance with the ESCMID guidelines. These criteria included the presence of specific anti-Borrelia burgdorferi antibodies in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and serum, as determined by ELISA and confirmed via Western Blot analysis, alongside pleocytosis in the CSF.

The pediatric patient group with LNB comprised 20 children with a mean age of 11.4 ± 2.8 years (range: 6–17 years), including 10 females and 10 males. The duration of symptoms prior to admission ranged from 5 to 21 days, with a median of 10 days. The primary clinical manifestations included facial nerve palsy, severe headache, and meningeal signs. Regarding the laboratory findings, the mean CSF pleocytosis was 112 cells per microliter. Intrathecal antibody production was confirmed by a positive antibody index in all enrolled patients. Crucially, all patients were treatment-naïve and had not received any antibiotic therapy prior to the biological sampling. Children with a history of other central nervous system infections, autoimmune disorders, or recent tick-borne co-infections were strictly excluded from the study.

Sample collection and study population

The present study examined untargeted metabolomic profiles in paired serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples obtained from pediatric patients evaluated for pediatric LNB. The study cohort included a total of 20 patients diagnosed with LNB and 20 healthy controls. Crucially, from each of the 20 LNB patients, paired serum and CSF samples were collected simultaneously prior to the initiation of any treatment. Serum samples were also collected from the 20 healthy controls to facilitate direct group comparisons.

To maximize metabolome coverage during LC-MS/MS analysis, every individual CSF sample (n = 20) was injected into two complementary chromatographic columns (BEH Amide and ZIC-pHILIC). Consequently, the metabolomic data generated from these two distinct analytical methods are referred to as the “BEH Amide” and “ZIC-pHILIC” datasets throughout the manuscript, representing complementary analytical runs of the exact same 20 patient samples rather than separate patient cohorts.

Venous blood specimens were collected using serum separator tubes containing a clot activator and gel for serum separation. These samples underwent centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 15 min, after which the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at − 76 °C. Cerebrospinal fluid was obtained via lumbar puncture under sterile conditions and subsequently centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting fluid was aliquoted into 0.1 mL volumes and maintained at − 76 °C until analysis. To ensure sample integrity, all specimens were thawed at 20 °C for one hour prior to analysis, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles were strictly avoided.

For blood-based biochemical analyses, the study consistently utilized serum. Whole blood was collected into serum-separator tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min. Following this interval, the samples were centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The serum was then separated, aliquoted, and preserved at − 80 °C. All centrifugation procedures were standardized to gravitational force (g-force) to enhance reproducibility and ensure consistency throughout the manuscript. Analytical data were processed utilizing standardized parameters to maintain the integrity and comparability of the metabolic profiles.

Metabolite extraction and spectrum acquisition

Blood samples were thawed on ice and homogenized by vortex mixing for 5 min at 4 °C. An aliquot of 100 µL was mixed with 300 µL of ice-cold LC-MS grade methanol (− 20 °C) and 5 µL of deuterated lysine internal standard (d4Lys), vortexed for 5 min, incubated at − 20 °C for 60 min, and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was transferred to LC-MS vials. Pooled quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining equal aliquots from all extracts.

CSF samples were prepared as follows: 100 µL of sample was mixed with 400 µL of ice-cold methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) and 5 µL of d4Lys. After incubation and centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C), 200 µL of the supernatant was evaporated to dryness, reconstituted in 100 µL of H₂O: MeOH: ACN (2:1:1, v/v), sonicated for 1 min, centrifuged, and transferred to LC-MS vials. Pooled QC samples were prepared by combining equal aliquots from all CSF extracts.

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific Dionex, Germany) coupled to a Compact Quadrupole Time-of-Flight mass spectrometer (Compact QTOF-MS, Bruker Daltonik, Bremen, Germany) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive and negative ionization modes. Pooled QC samples were injected periodically throughout the analytical sequence to monitor analytical stability.

Blood samples were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (130 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) fitted with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide VanGuard pre-column (Waters). The column and autosampler temperatures were maintained at 45 °C and 4 °C, respectively. In positive ionization mode, mobile phase A consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid, while mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. Separation was performed at a flow rate of 0.4 mL min⁻¹ with a 5 µL injection volume using the following gradient: 99% B (0–1 min), 99–80% B (1–4 min), 80–30% B (4–12 min), 30–99% B (12–12.5 min), followed by re-equilibration at 99% B until 18 min. In negative ionization mode, mobile phase A consisted of 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH 9.0) in water/acetonitrile (95:5, v/v), whereas mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile/10 mM ammonium bicarbonate (95:5, v/v). Separation was performed using the following gradient: 99% B (0–1 min), 99–30% B (1–7 min), 30–99% B (7–7.5 min), followed by re-equilibration at 99% B until 12 min.

To maximize metabolome coverage, each CSF sample was analyzed using two complementary chromatographic methods. The first employed the same BEH Amide method described above in both positive and negative ionization modes. The second employed an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column for positive ionization and a SeQuant ZIC-pHILIC column (5 μm, 100 × 2.1 mm; Merck) fitted with a ZIC-pHILIC guard column (20 × 2.1 mm, 5 μm). For the BEH Amide positive method, the column temperature was maintained at 25 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of 20 mM ammonium formate containing 0.1% formic acid in water (pH 3.8), whereas mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. Separation was performed using a linear gradient from 95 to 45% B over 19 min at a flow rate of 0.4 mL min⁻¹ with a 2 µL injection volume. For the ZIC-pHILIC negative method, the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of 20 mM ammonium acetate and 20 mM ammonium hydroxide in water (pH 9.3), whereas mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile. Separation was performed using the following gradient: 90% B (0–1.5 min), 50% B (8–11 min), and 45% B (12–15 min), followed by re-equilibration to the initial conditions. The flow rate was 0.3 mL min⁻¹ with a 2 µL injection volume.

The same mass spectrometric acquisition parameters were applied to all analytical methods within each ionization mode. In positive ESI mode, data were acquired over the m/z range 20–1000 using auto MS/MS with collision energies of 20–60 eV. The source parameters were as follows: capillary voltage 4.5 kV, drying gas temperature 220 °C, drying gas flow 10 L min⁻¹, and nebulizer pressure 2.2 bar. In negative ESI mode, data were acquired over the m/z range 70–1000 using auto MS/MS with collision energies of 25–35 eV. The capillary voltage was set to 4.5 kV, the drying gas temperature to 210 °C, the drying gas flow to 8 L min⁻¹, and the nebulizer pressure to 2.0 bar.

Raw LC-MS data were processed in MetaboScape 2021b (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Molecular features were detected using the T-ReX 3D algorithm, aligned across all samples, and grouped into bucket tables based on retention time and accurate m/z. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching accurate mass, isotopic pattern and MS/MS spectra against the Bruker MetaboBASE 3.0 spectral library. The resulting feature tables were exported for subsequent statistical analysis.

For the preparation of blood serum samples, the matrices were mixed with ice-cold HPLC-grade methanol in a 1:2 ratio to facilitate protein precipitation. The resulting mixture was vortexed, incubated on ice for 20 min, and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was subsequently collected and evaporated to dryness under vacuum to completely eliminate the methanol solvent. The dried residue was then reconstituted in 100 µL of sodium phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4) prepared in D2O containing 0.1% TSP. In contrast, the protein precipitation step was omitted for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples owing to their inherently low protein content. Instead, raw CSF was mixed directly with the identical D2O - based sodium phosphate buffer containing TSP in a 2:1 ratio. Regarding both biological matrices, TSP served exclusively as an internal standard for chemical shift referencing at δ = 0.0 ppm and was not utilized for absolute quantification purposes.All NMR experiments were conducted on a Bruker AVANCE III 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 1.7 mm TCI cryoprobe maintained at 298 K. One-dimensional (1D) 1H NOESY pulse sequences using the noesygppr1d program were utilized with 32 scans, a relaxation delay of 4.0 s, and an acquisition time of 2.7 s. This in-house optimized protocol was adapted and validated based on previously established metabolomics methodologies (Beckonert et al., 2007). The resulting NMR spectra were processed, phased, and baseline-corrected using TopSpin version 3.6.2 as well as MestReNova version 14.2 software. Finally, chemical shifts were verified through comparison against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB).

Data preprocessing: missingness and imputation

Raw metabolomics datasets were processed by extracting signal intensity matrices, where zero values were treated as missing. A filtering threshold of 75% data presence was applied across all datasets, meaning a maximum tolerated missingness of 25%. Metabolites exceeding this threshold were excluded from downstream analysis. Missing values were assumed to follow a Missing Not At Random (MNAR) mechanism due to the likelihood of concentrations falling below the limit of detection (Wei et al., 2018).

Missing data were imputed using the Random Forest (RF) algorithm, which consistently demonstrated the best performance compared to half-minimum, KNN (Troyanskaya et al., 2001), and QRILC methods (Wilson et al., 2022), yielding the lowest normalized root mean square error (NRMSE) and the most optimal NRMSE-based sum of ranks (SOR) (Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2012; Wilson et al., 2022). Imputation and preprocessing steps were performed in R (v4.5.0) using the metabolomicsR, imputeLCMD, and missForest packages. Following imputation and log2 transformation, the data were normalized utilizing probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) via the omicTools package. In the computational workflow, QC samples were incorporated as a major contributor in the selection of the reference spectrum used for PQN. Specifically, for each metabolite, the median signal intensity across QC samples was computed, then used to construct the reference spectrum [Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, 3]. This normalization method is advantageous because it relies on a representative profile compared to median signals obtained from the study samples as a whole. Additionally, PQN is suitable for NMR targeted metabolomics. Following this normalization, the data were mean-centered and subjected to Pareto scaling to stabilize variance prior to statistical modeling. We would like to underline that QC samples, as observed in the downstream analysis, were excluded from the samples list for a better and accurate univariate and multivariate statistical analysis.

Univariate and multivariate statistical analysis

Univariate statistical analysis was performed on blood datasets comparing case and control groups using a two-sided Welch’s t-test. P-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and significant metabolites were identified based on thresholds of P-value < 0.05, FDR < 0.05, and an absolute fold change (|FC|) > 1.5.

Multivariate analyses were conducted on all datasets using Principle Component Analysis and Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) implemented in the R package mixOmics. For blood samples, principle component analysis was performed for control and disease groups, respectively with QC samples. The optimal number of latent components was determined using repeated cross-validation (50 repetitions, using 3-fold or 5-fold CV depending on the number of metabolites). Model performance was evaluated via classification error rate, balanced error rate (BER), and prediction distances. To overcome overfitting challenges occurring in supervised statistical approaches, permutation testing was performed by randomly permuting class labels 1000 times and recomputing the model performance. The statistical significance of model discrimination was estimated using an empirical permutation p-value derived from the BER distribution of permuted models. The optimal number of components was selected from mixOmics, which bases on statistical testing to determine whether adding components significantly improves prediction performance. Important metabolites driving group discrimination were identified using Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores, utilizing thresholds between > 1.0 and > 1.5.

Enrichment analysis

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Metabolites identified as significant through univariate or multivariate thresholds were mapped to biological pathways using the KEGG database, using KEGG IDs as compound identifiers. A valid enrichment pathway was defined using a significance threshold of p-value < 0.05.

Results

Data quality and imputation assessment

During the data processing, missingness levels were first assessed across all datasets, including blood and CSF samples in both negative and positive ionization modes (Fig. 1A–F). This evaluation process is mandatory before normalizing the data, as multiple normalization algorithms consider missing values and zero values as a challenge (Sun & Xia, 2024).

Volcano Plot representing the most significant metabolites that were detected as up accumulated and down accumulated in infected patients with LNB disease, measured bu the log2 Fold Change and FDR significance, from different Ionization Approaches (Blood Samples). Left volcano plot representing metabolites from the Negative Ionization approach, whereas right volcano plot representing metabolites from the Positive Ionization approach. Up accumulated metabolites are coloured in blue, down accumulated metabolites are coloured in green, and non-significant metabolites are coloured in orange

In blood datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1 A and B), missingness was generally moderate, with a limited number of metabolites exceeding the threshold, particularly in negative ionization mode. In contrast, positive ionization datasets showed a broader distribution of missingness, with several metabolites approaching higher missingness levels. For CSF datasets, missingness patterns differed between acquisition platforms. The ZIC-pHILIC dataset displayed relatively high missingness for a subset of metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 2), while the BEH Amide dataset showed a more balanced distribution, although several metabolites still exceeded the 25% threshold (Supplementary Fig. 3). Given the targeted NMR-based metabolomics approach, missing values across our dataset were assumed to follow a Missing Not At Random (MNAR) mechanism (Shah et al., 2019). This assumption is supported by the fact that missing signals are likely associated with metabolite concentrations falling below the limit of detection, which results in the signal being attributed as NA.

The recommended method for addressing the data when missingness exists is to start omitting the highly missing data, then impute the metabolite with partially missing information (Dekermanjian et al., 2022). Data above 75% of data integrity did excel when imputed with Random Forest (RF) algorithm, as it consistently demonstrated the best performance compared to half-minimum, KNN, and QRILC methods (Supplementary Table 1 A). This was evidenced by the lowest normalized root mean square error (NRMSE) value. However, NRMSE wouldn’t be a unique metric to evaluate a MNAR for NMR-based metabolomic data analysis, as the missing value is not randomly distributed, using NRMSE directly might cause biased results especially for those imputation methods with determined values as we showed in the table (Supplementary Table 1 A). Thus, we derived another metric, which was NRMSE-based sum of ranks (SOR). In fact, by computing SOR across all the rest of the datasets, Random Forest still is considered the best imputation method for our current downstream analysis (Supplementary Table 1B).

Serum metabolome

Univariate statistical analysis allowed the identification of metabolites that significantly alters between case and control groups in blood samples. The metabolites signals across all the blood datasets, from each of the patients groups (Case/Control) share a common normal distribution, confirming the data normality prior statistical analysis. (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5, 6). Considering the thresholds used, we identified 17 metabolites that are significantly regulated (7 metabolites from the Negative Ionization approach, and 10 metabolites from the Positive Ionization Approach, Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Table 3). Volcano plots were used to visualize the distribution of statistically significant metabolites by comparing patients with LNB disease to control samples.

Among the significantly up-accumulated metabolites in patients, hypoxanthine (FDR = 2.33641815117303e-20, logFC = 2.40061649510292), L-tyrosine (FDR = 3.2472383897345e-23, logFC = 1.62609955114251), Acremin F (FDR = 2.4481177213364e-15, logFC = 1.175782659832732), and 1-arachidonoyl-2-hydroxy-sn-glycero-3-phosphate (FDR = 1.73794333407263e-13, logFC = 1.99413964790239) were consistently observed at higher levels. In fact, the high presence of hypoxanthine has been reflected to host–pathogen metabolic interactions, as demonstrated in mice models, Borrelia burgdorferi relies on host-derived purines such as hypoxanthine for survival and infection (Coburn et al., 2021) (Fig. 1). Additionally, elevated levels of L-tyrosine are more likely to be in tyrosine-dependent signaling (Parthasarathy & Philipp, 2017), as this amino acid FDR signal is the highest in significance among the other metabolites (Fig. 1).

The increase in Xanthine (FDR = 4.25756551616383e-23, logFC = 1.64964223013016) levels (Fig. 2) may suggest an involvement of the metabolite in purine metabolism. In fact, Xanthine dehydrogenases break Hypoxanthine into Xanthine (Phelan et al., 2023), then into Uric acid. In fact, uric acid (FDR = 9.6429850030316e-34, logFC = −2.53357326072535) is significantly accumulated, as identified in Supplementary Fig. 7. Given that uric acid acts as an antioxidant (Ames et al., 1981), in combination with the accumulation of upstream metabolites such as hypoxanthine and xanthine, may reflect alterations in this pathway breakdown. Evidence from mice models (Coburn et al., 2021; Phelan et al., 2023) indicates that this metabolic regulation is associated with bacterial resistance to reactive oxygen species (ROS), indicating an increased oxidative stress on the host organism.

PLS-DA plot stratifying blood samples into two separate groups (Patient and Healthy groups), A issued from Negative Ionization Approach, B issued from Positive Ionization Approach

Subsequently, metabolites that met the defined significance thresholds were visualized using heatmaps (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). These heatmaps reveal distinct clustering patterns, where groups of metabolites appear co-regulated in-patient samples, while showing lower or baseline expression levels in control groups. Overall, the heatmaps highlight clear differences in metabolite abundance and distribution across sample groups.

From a multivariate statistical analysis, Multivariate analysis further supported these observations. PCA demonstrated a clear separation between patients, controls, and QC samples in both negative and positive ionization datasets, primarily along PC1, which explained 40.88 and 39.99% of the total variance, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 10). These observed separation, majorly between the two groups are consistent with the supervised PLS-DA results, where the model effectively separated the samples into two distinct clusters based on both negative and positive ionization datasets (Fig. 2).

Following PLS-DA model evaluation, the optimal number of latent components for the blood PLS-DA models was determined to be n = 2, which represents the most informative structure underlying the covariance between compounds relative abundances and groups observed. Model robustness was further assessed through permutation testing, as described in the Materials and Methods section. Using 1000 random permutations, the observed balanced error rates (BER = 0.00) were significantly lower than those obtained from permuted models (empirical permutation p < 0.001), supporting that the observed class separation is unlikely to have arisen by chance (Supplementary Table 4). As shown in Fig. 2A, B, PLS-DA achieved a clear discrimination between patient and control samples in both negative and positive ionization datasets. In contrast, the broader confidence ellipses observed within the patient group suggest greater metabolic differentiations among neuroborreliosis patients, reflecting inter-individual differences throughout their metabolic compounds abundances.

This selection allowed further investigation of the metabolites contributing most strongly to each component. Following validation of the model’s performance, Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores were used to identify key metabolites. VIP thresholds were adapted between datasets to account for differences in metabolite numbers. Using this approach, several metabolites overlapped with those previously identified through univariate statistical analysis (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 11).

Notably, the number of significant metabolites retained through PLS-DA was lower compared to univariate results, reflecting the more stringent VIP threshold. This indicates that only metabolites contributing most strongly to the variance explained by the model were retained. Additionally, the contribution of each significant metabolite to the latent components allowed identification of group-specific patterns (case vs. control). In latent variable 1, uric acid (VIP > 1.5) and p-acetaminobenzoic acid (VIP > 1.0) showed negative correlations relative to the other significant metabolites identified in the negative ionization dataset. This observation is consistent with the patterns previously observed in the volcano plot (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 12). A similar behavior was observed in the positive ionization dataset, where metabolites such as citric acid (VIP > 1.5), xanthine (VIP > 1.5), and ADMA (VIP > 2.0) exhibited distinct contribution patterns compared to other significant metabolites (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 13). Furthermore, comparable clustering patterns were observed in heatmaps derived from the PLS-DA analysis and those obtained from univariate analysis, supporting the consistency and robustness of the model (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15).

For downstream enrichment analysis using the KEGG database, only metabolites with valid KEGG Compound IDs were retained. From the univariate analysis, 15 out of 17 significant metabolites identified across both positive and negative ionization modes were successfully mapped to KEGG entries. Similarly, from the multivariate analysis, 12 out of 13 significant metabolites were associated with valid KEGG Compound IDs. These subsets were subsequently used for pathway enrichment analysis.

Cerebrospinal fluid metabolome

To complement the univariate and multivariate analyses previously performed on the serum metabolome, an unsupervised hierarchical clustering heatmap was generated using the 68 metabolites consistently detected across all 20 cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples, expressed as log2 Z-score normalized abundances (Fig. 3). This exploratory approach enabled visualization of intrinsic clustering patterns among patients and metabolites, independent of pre-assigned clinical groupings.

Hierarchical clustering heatmap of the 68 metabolites detected across all 20 cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples. Rows represent individual metabolites and columns represent individual patients, both hierarchically clustered (dendrograms shown on the left and top margins, respectively). Color intensity reflects the log2 Z-score normalized metabolite abundance, ranging from blue (down-accumulated) to red (up-accumulated)

Sample-wise clustering (Fig. 3, top dendrogram) revealed that the majority of patients (n = 18) formed a broad, heterogeneous cluster characterized by comparatively modest and variable metabolite fluctuations. In contrast, three patients (115, 116, and 120) branched off at the earliest node of the dendrogram, denoting the largest overall dissimilarity in CSF metabolic composition within the cohort, while patients 096 and 079 occupied an intermediate position between these two groups.

This divergence was driven by a large, contiguous block of co-regulated metabolites (Fig. 3, row dendrogram) that were consistently and markedly up-accumulated (Z-score > 1) in patients 115, 116, and 120 relative to the rest of the cohort. This cluster comprised several amino acids (L-histidine, D-proline, D-ornithine, L-cystine, L-asparagine, glutamate, L-phenylalanine, L-tyrosine, L-arginine, L-alloisoleucine, and D-isoleucine), methylated osmolytes and related compounds (choline, betaine, carnitine, and asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), together with purine pathway intermediates (xanthine, uric acid) and citrulline. Notably, several of these metabolites – including carnitine, choline, ADMA, xanthine, uric acid, and the aromatic amino acids tyrosine and phenylalanine – overlapped with the metabolites found to be significantly altered in the serum metabolome (Sect. 3.2), suggesting that patients 115, 116, and 120 may present a more pronounced systemic-to-CNS metabolic disturbance. This is consistent with dysregulation of the purine degradation pathway and the associated increase in oxidative stress previously described in blood (Coburn et al., 2021; Phelan et al., 2023), as well as with tyrosine-dependent signaling alterations reported in LNB (Parthasarathy & Philipp, 2017), and may point to greater blood-CSF barrier permeability in these individuals.

Interestingly, dLys was the only metabolite displaying a near-uniform up-accumulation across virtually the entire cohort, irrespective of the two main sample clusters (Fig. 3, top row). This cohort-wide pattern suggests that dLys elevation could represent a shared feature of CSF metabolism in this patient population; however, given its broad distribution independent of the observed patient stratification, further targeted validation is required to clarify its biological versus pre-analytical origin.

The remaining metabolites, largely comprising lipid-related monoacylglycerols (1-monopalmitin isoforms and 1-stearoyl-rac-glycerol), polyols (ribitol, dulcitol), purine catabolites (hypoxanthine and its associated features), and other diverse compounds (p-tolyl sulfate, N-acetylaspartylglutamic acid, myo-inositol, adenine, N,N, N-trimethyllysine, and 5-d-aminolevulinic acid), displayed a more heterogeneous, patient-specific distribution without a consistent group-level trend. This heterogeneity was illustrated by isolated extreme values restricted to single individuals, such as the pronounced up-accumulation of 1-monopalmitin (feat.8) in patient 026 and the marked down-accumulation of the N-acetylaspartylglutamic acid-containing cluster in patient 117, rather than concordant regulation shared across a defined subgroup. These patterns likely reflect greater inter-individual variability, potentially influenced by non-disease-related factors such as diet, renal handling, or technical variance in lipid/polyol quantification, and should be interpreted with caution as candidate biomarkers.

Taken together, the unsupervised clustering of the CSF metabolome partially mirrors the metabolic disturbances identified in blood (Sect. 3.2), particularly regarding purine metabolism, methylated osmolytes, and aromatic amino acids, while also revealing a distinct subgroup of patients with a more pronounced global metabolic perturbation in CSF. These observations provide the basis for the spectral quality and metabolite annotation assessment presented in the following section.

NMR analysis

The assessment of 1H NMR spectral quality demonstrated excellent resolution and water suppression efficiency across the cohort, ensuring high data integrity prior to downstream statistical processing. To address the specific verification of structural annotations, metabolite identification was performed using the Chenomx NMR Suite database coupled with systematic comparison against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB). For biomarker candidates and congested regions, assignment accuracy was validated using two-dimensional (2D) 1H–1H Total Correlation Spectroscopy (TOCSY) and Heteronuclear Single Quantum Coherence (HSQC) experiments, providing Level 2 putative annotation according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines.

The analysis of chemical shifts demonstrated robust reproducibility across the paired biological matrices (Table 1), with the Coefficient of Variation (CV) remaining strictly below the 1% technical threshold for 25 out of 27 annotated biomolecules. Minor structural outliers like lysine and creatinine exhibited slightly elevated CVs, which is typical for protons susceptible to minor microenvironmental fluctuations. Overall, the robust technical stability observed validates the accuracy of the peak assignment strategy, ensuring that subsequent multi-group comparative modeling reflects genuine pathophysiological alterations associated with central nervous system infections.

Enrichment and pathways

Common pathways identification across the samples

Pathways obtained from enrichment analysis showed commonalities among the different samples, as well as the different methodologies used throughout the study. In blood and CSF samples, a strong overlap of pathways were identified. Notably, purine metabolism (p-value < 0.01) consistently appeared as the most significantly enriched pathway across both samples, and across the two statistical methods (univariate and multivariate statistical approaches), indicating reproducible signal independent of the statistical method used. Similarly, metabolite compounds observed in pathways related to phenylalanine metabolism (p-value < 0.05) have been observed more in the VIP significance in PLS-DA results both ZIC-pHILIC and BEH Amide datasets (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). The pathway of phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (p-value < 0.05) was repeatedly identified across both univariate and multivariate analyses, as well as across blood and CSF samples. Lastly, glycerophospholipid metabolism (p-value < 0.01) was identified in blood samples and within the CSF ZIC-pHILIC dataset, suggesting a partial overlap in lipid-related metabolic dysregulation between systemic and central compartments. These metabolites involved in glycerophospholipid metabolism were observed in CSF ZIC-pHILIC datasets and serum metabolome samples as significant contributors in VIP scores, as well as Loadings component contributions (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this pilot study, a detailed metabolomic analysis was conducted on blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from children with neuroborreliosis (LNB). Given the non-specific nature of clinical symptoms and the limitations of standard serological tests and CSF parameters, the search for new potential biomarkers of this disease is of paramount importance. Our results indicate profound changes in the host’s biochemical profile in response to infection, with particular emphasis on the purine pathway, amino acid metabolism and processes associated with neuroinflammation.

The most significant and consistent finding of this analysis was a marked disturbance of the purine metabolic pathway, observed in both the serum and cerebrospinal fluid of the patients. The elevated concentrations of hypoxanthine, xanthine, and uric acid in children with LNB may arise from multiple pathophysiological processes. Primarily, these elevations could reflect non-specific consequences of infection, such as increased cellular turnover, localized tissue injury, generalized inflammatory activation, and exacerbated oxidative stress. Alternatively, as a secondary hypothesis, this purine signature might also be linked to the specific metabolic dependencies of Borrelia spirochetes. It is well-established that Borrelia burgdorferi lacks a de novo purine synthesis pathway and relies entirely on salvaging host-derived purines for nucleic acid biosynthesis (Jewett et al., 2009; Pettersson et al., 2007). While it is conceptually plausible that the increased host purine pool could facilitate bacterial replication or reflect a pathogen-induced microenvironment (Phelan et al., 2023), our untargeted metabolomic data alone cannot establish causality. Therefore, this pathogen-driven purine scavenging should be considered merely as one of several potential contributing factors alongside the broader, nonspecific host inflammatory response.

Choline showed a high contribution to group discrimination in the PLS-DA models, with a VIP score exceeding 1.75. This indicates that choline contributed substantially to the separation between patient and control profiles, although a VIP score alone does not establish the direction or independent statistical significance of the metabolite change. According to previous literature, choline has been proposed as a potential biomarker of neuroinflammatory activity and treatment response (Huang et al., 2025). As choline participates in several cellular and mitochondrial processes, it also plays an important role in epigenetic regulation through its function as a methyl-group donor. These mechanisms may contribute to the regulation of inflammatory processes and oxidative stress (Bekdash, 2016). Therefore, an alternance of abundance is observed in our study between the two groups, which can subsequently highlight a potential neuroinflammation in our patients group. This pattern of differentiation was observed in an MRI study conducted in LNB patients, where levels of differentiation of tumors were observed directly from abundances of Choline (Garkowski et al., 2019, 2020). Therefore, further validation of choline participation to different metabolic pathways, it could be achieved through targeted proteomic quantification of choline oxidase involved in regulatory and inflammatory processes.

Beyond serving as a byproduct of purine catabolism, the elevated levels of uric acid could theoretically function as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP). In this framework, extracellular uric acid released from damaged cells could act as an endogenous danger signal that stimulates the immune response via NLRP3 inflammasome complexes, thereby amplifying localized inflammation. This proposed phenomenon aligns with previously reported alterations in the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway, hinting at a speculative metabolic synergy where purine and tryptophan metabolites co-modulate the immunometabolic landscape. These systemic perturbations echo prior metabolic signatures described in pediatric patients with bacterial infections of the central nervous system, where purine pathways represent a major metabolic hub (Wang et al., 2021).

Our targeted analysis also revealed prominent alterations in the biosynthesis and metabolism of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan. Elevated systemic levels of L-tyrosine emerged as a highly statistically significant feature, which we hypothesize may mirror the activation of tyrosine-dependent signaling cascades during acute inflammation (Chen et al., 2022; Russkikh et al., 2025). Furthermore, these results are conceptually consistent with the documented role of the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway in neuroborreliosis. Although tryptophan and kynurenine were not directly quantified in the cerebrospinal fluid in this study, the identified enriched pathways indirectly suggest a metabolic shift consistent with this pathway. The broader literature provides complementary context, as the acute phase of LNB is frequently characterized by significantly elevated cerebrospinal fluid concentrations of L-kynurenine and kynurenic acid (Wickström et al., 2021). This pathway is typically driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines, especially interferon-gamma, which induces indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase. Concurrently, elevated levels of neopterin, a established marker of macrophage activation, have been reported in acute neuroborreliosis (Gasse et al., 1994), although this metabolite exhibited a high coefficient of variation in our data.

Finally, unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the cerebrospinal fluid identified choline and glutamate as key metabolites driving the metabolic divergence in a specific subgroup of patients. Increased choline abundance has been associated with active inflammation and cellular membrane turnover, a finding supported by neuroimaging studies of LNB patients (Ustymowicz et al., 2004). While the alterations in glycerophospholipid metabolism are suggestive of blood-brain barrier disruption during active neuroborreliosis, the current metabolomic data cannot definitively confirm this structural damage without complementary biophysical or imaging evidence (Adams et al., 2022). Furthermore, due to the lack of a non-LNB neuroinflammatory control group, it remains to be determined whether these metabolic shifts in CSF are specific to Borrelia infection or reflect a generalized central nervous system response to neuroinflammation. Similarly, while glutamate is a primary excitatory neurotransmitter and its accumulation could theoretically precipitate excitotoxic cascades and exacerbate central nervous system pathology, its direct mechanistic link to the pathogenesis of LNB requires further functional validation, as current direct evidence remains limited (French et al., 2018).

Data reliability and study limitations

The use of advanced NMR and LC-MS/MS spectroscopy techniques has enabled a comprehensive characterisation of the biochemical phenotype. The high quality and stability of chemical shift measurements (CV below 1% for key metabolites, with the exception of lysine and creatinine) ensure that the observed differences reflect actual pathophysiological changes rather than instrumental errors. The use of the Random Forest (RF) algorithm for imputing MNAR (Missing Not At Random) missing data proved to be the optimal solution, allowing the reliability of the statistical analysis to be maintained.

The main limitation of this study is the lack of a matched control group for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples. This meant that conclusions regarding the CNS had to be based primarily on multivariate statistics and the identification of internal subgroups within the patient population. A methodological strength of this study is that serum and CSF samples were collected simultaneously from the same individuals, allowing for direct within-patient comparison between systemic and central compartments.

Conclusions

Metabolomic analysis of cerebrospinal fluid and blood serum allows for precise mapping of the body’s biochemical response during pediatric neuroborreliosis, thereby offering invaluable insights into pathophysiological mechanisms that remain inaccessible to standard serological testing. The most prominent and consistently reproducible metabolic alteration observed in the course of the disease involves a profound disruption of the purine pathway. This disruption is primarily manifested by significantly elevated concentrations of hypoxanthine, xanthine, and uric acid, which likely reflects a generalized infection-related response, such as increased cellular turnover, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress, while also remaining compatible with the known metabolic dependence of the Borrelia pathogen on host purines. Our findings are compatible with previously reported alterations of the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway in pediatric LNB; however, this pathway was not directly assessed in the present cohort. Concurrently, the identification of elevated levels of choline and glutamate in the cerebrospinal fluid suggests a possible association with an active inflammatory process and potential excitotoxicity. However, without an appropriate disease-control cohort, these observations must be treated as hypotheses requiring further validation. Additionally, notable changes in glycerophospholipid metabolism and aromatic amino acid biosynthesis suggest a transient compromise in the integrity of the blood-brain barrier during the active phase of neuroinfection, which directly correlates with the intensity of the localized immune response. Taken together, these newly identified immunometabolic signatures, particularly those governing purine metabolism and the inferred kynurenine pathway, represent a promising foundation for future controlled studies aimed at exploring candidate biomarkers for the pediatric population, in whom the clinical presentation of neuroborreliosis is frequently nonspecific.
