Hypoxia-preconditioning human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells induce high-quality mitochondrial transfer through gap junctions to alleviate ischemia-reperfusion injury in liver graft
Authors: Xinling Luo, Weiqi Zeng, Erfeng Xiong, Ziming Wang, Jingsheng Huang, Meiqi Luo, Zhongxian He, Jinyu Liu, Dongdong Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmid the widespread scarcity of donor livers, mitigating ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) of liver grafts is vital for ensuring early recovery of post-transplant liver function. Human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) have shown potential in alleviating IRI damage by regulating mitochondrial function. Hypoxia-preconditioning hBMSCs (hypo-hBMSCs) have shown considerable promise in enhancing therapeutic efficacy, yet the underlying mechanism remain to be elucidated. Therefore, this study aims to explore the role of hypo-hBMSCs in alleviating hepatic IRI and uncover their potential mechanisms, with the goal of offering new strategies for the application of hBMSCs in liver protection after transplantation.MethodsInitially, we investigated the impact of hypoxia preconditioning on the quality of hBMSCs mitochondria and whether hypo-hBMSCs can alleviate IRI damage in liver grafts by transferring mitochondria. Subsequently, by employing the enhancer RA and the inhibitor Gap26 to modulate the function of gap junctions (GJs) in vivo and in vitro, we confirmed their crucial role in the process of hypo-hBMSCs transferring mitochondria to hepatocytes. Ultimately, through bioinformatics analysis, Co-IP, siRNA and overexpression, we demonstrate that the up-regulated Cx43 and Cx32 in hypo-hBMSCs can form homotypic Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs with hepatocytes, thereby enhancing the transfer of mitochondria.ResultsThe results indicate that hypoxia preconditioning diminishes superoxides accumulation and elevates the mitochondrial membrane potential by inducing mitophagy in hBMSCs, consequently improving mitochondrial quality. Upon administration via portal vein injection, hypo-hBMSCs significantly mitigate hepatic IRI. Compared with hBMSCs, hypo-hBMSCs are capable of transferring more mitochondria to hepatocytes through GJs. When the function of GJs is modulated by the enhancer RA or the inhibitor Gap26, the efficiency of mitochondrial transfer correspondingly shifts. Further investigation uncovers that hypo-hBMSCs prompts an upsurge in the expression of Cx43 and Cx32 (not Cx26). Nevertheless, these proteins are unable to form heterotypic GJs (Cx43-Cx32-GJs) with hepatocytes; instead, they form homotypic Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs, which facilitate the transfer of mitochondria between hypo-hBMSCs and hepatocytes.ConclusionHypo-hBMSCs can enhance mitochondrial quality by inducing mitophagy. Meanwhile, they can up-regulate Cx43 and Cx32 to form homotypic Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs with hepatocytes, thereby transferring more high-quality mitochondria to hepatocytes to exert a protective effect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02497-1.Keywords: Hypoxia preconditioning, HBMSCs, Hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, Gap junctions, Mitochondrial transfer

Introduction

Amidst the current global shortage of donor livers and the rapid increase in the utilization rate of marginal livers, mitigating hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) in liver grafts has emerged as a crucial factor for improving the utilization rate and improving patient outcomes [1–4]. Although various treatment options for hepatic IRI are available, they remain largely symptomatic, with a significant lack of effective targeted therapies [2]. Studies have shown that during the ex vivo period of donor livers, ischemia and hypoxia disrupt the energy production structures, leading to severe deficiency in ATP synthesis [5, 6]. Upon reperfusion, the restoration of oxygen supply causes calcium overload in cells [6, 7], stimulates the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [8, 9] and mediates mitochondrial damage [10, 11]. This cascade of events further exacerbates the impairment of ATP synthesis and the repair of liver injury in liver grafts. Therefore, protecting mitochondrial function may hold the key to alleviating hepatic IRI and represent a potential therapeutic strategy.

Human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in protecting the liver and mitigating damage in numerous animal models of liver injury [12–15]. This is attributed to their unique biological properties, such as multipotent differentiation potential, immunomodulatory capacity, and paracrine function [16, 17]. Notably, recent studies have confirmed that in non-hepatic injury models, such as severe pancreatitis and acute lung injury, hBMSCs can repair damaged cells by transferring mitochondria [18, 19]. This process reduces ROS production, enhances ATP synthesis, and increases calcium buffering capacity, thereby restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and antioxidant systems in damaged cells.

In light of the significant potential of hBMSCs in organ protection, researchers have been actively investigating various strategies to enhance their functionality. For example, pretreatment with hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) and fibroblast growth factor 4 (FGF4) has been shown to effectively promote the directed migration of hBMSCs to lesions in a mouse model of acute liver fibrosis with carbon tetrachloride, thereby exerting therapeutic effects [20]. HGF-overexpressed BM-MSCs have demonstrated the ability to protect and promote the proliferation of graft livers in liver transplantation models [21]. BM-MSCs pre-treated with hypoxia exhibit stronger proliferative and reparative capabilities, which can effectively reduce oxidative stress and autophagy in liver fibrosis models [22]. In ischemic-hypoxia brain injury, hypoxia pre-treatment of hBMSCs attenuates the inflammatory response in the injured area, reduces neuronal apoptosis, and promotes the recovery of neurological function [23]. Given these promising findings, the present study focuses on elucidating the effects of hypoxia pretreatment on hBMSCs and investigating the protective effects of hypoxia-preconditioning hBMSCs (hypo-hBMSCs) on liver grafts injury.

In this study, we found that hypoxia preconditioning can improve the mitochondrial quality of hBMSCs and transfer them to hepatocytes to exert a protective effect. The mechanisms underlying the mitochondrial transfer efficacy of hBMSCs can be primarily ascribed to three pathways: extracellular vesicles (EVs), free mitochondria transfer, and cell-to-cell contact transfer [1, 24, 25]. However, the EVs pathway typically encapsulates only a small and limited quantities of mitochondria, or merely mitochondrial components, which results in low transfer efficiency [26, 27]. In the free mitochondria transfer pathway, the mitochondria and cell-derived mitochondria exhibit significant heterogeneity in both morphology and function, which remains poorly understood in current research [28]. In contrast, the cell-to-cell contact transmission pathway allows hBMSCs to form specialized channels such as tunneling nanotubes, gap junctions (GJs) and other conduits through direct contact between cells, thereby enabling efficient delivery of hBMSCs mitochondria to diseased cells and exerting therapeutic effects [29–31]. Among these channels, GJs are deemed one of the most likely to play a role in mitochondrial transfer. The direct transfer of substances between adjacent cells mediated by GJs constitutes the biological foundation for a series of pathophysiological activities. Our previous study, we found that not only confirmed that GJs composed of Cx32 can dually regulate hepatocyte injury [32], but also revealed in the autologous orthotopic liver transplantation (AOLT) rat model that hBMSCs can reside in the liver for an extended period and mitigate hepatocyte ferroptosis [1]. It has not been definitively reported whether hBMSCs can transfer mitochondria to hepatocytes through GJs to exert corresponding protective effects. Additionally, we have observed hBMSCs and hepatocytes predominantly express Cx43, Cx32 and Cx26, which can form both homogeneous and heterogeneous GJs (i.e., GJs composed of the same or different Cx proteins) [33–35]. It is imperative to further investigate which type of GJs predominantly mediates mitochondrial transfer. Addressing these questions will not only shed new light on the mechanisms by which hBMSCs protect against liver injury but may also uncover new targets for the modification of hBMSCs.

In summary, the purpose of this study was to compare the efficacy of hypo-hBMSCs with that of normal hBMSCs in hepatic IRI, and to further explore the precise mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of hypo-hBMSCs, thereby providing a scientific basis for their potential application in the treatment of hepatic IRI.

Materials and methods

Animal experiment

We purchased Sprague-Dawley male rats (weight: 300–320 g) from Sijiajingda BioTech Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). All rats were raised in a temperature-controlled barrier environment at the South China Agricultural University, where was under a 12-hour light-dark cycle and had access to normal chow diet and sterile water. Rats were injected with 106 normol hBMSCs or hypo-hBMSCs which were labeled with Dil and mitochondria were stained with MitoBright LT Green through portal vein (PV) 24 h before Autologous Orthotopic Liver Transplantation (AOLT).

Autologous orthotopic liver transplantation (AOLT) rat model

AOLT rat model was established successfully according to the standard procedure reported previously [1]. In summary, the rats were deeply anesthetized using isoflurane and administered a systemic heparinization of 50 U heparin in 1 mL normal saline (NS) via tail vein injection. The abdominal cavity was then opened, and the liver falciform ligament was resected initially. Subsequently, the left superior phrenic vein was ligated using a 6 − 0 absorbable suture. A 0 suture was placed under the suprahepatic vena cava (SVC) in preparation for later blockage. Next, the splenogastric vein and right suprarenal vein were carefully dissected and ligated using 4 − 0 absorbable sutures. After mobilizing the portal vein (PV) and infrahepatic vena cava (IVC), microvascular clamps were applied to both vessels. A trocar needle was introduced into the PV to infuse 1.5 mL of heparinized normal saline (25 U/mL) slowly, facilitating the clearance of hepatic blood into the right atrium until the liver became pale. The SVC was then tied off with the pre-placed 0 suture. Next, 20 mL of 4 °C NS with 12.5 U/mL heparin was continuously infused through the PV, while a 1 mm incision was made on the IVC wall to serve as an outflow tract. The anhepatic phase lasted approximately 20 ± 1 min. At the end of this phase, the openings of the PV and SVC were closed with 9 − 0 absorbable sutures. After ischemia, all clamps and ligatures were released, and rewarming was achieved via peritoneal lavage using preheated NS supplemented with epinephrine (0.002 mg/mL) and norepinephrine (0.004 mg/mL). Finally, compound lidocaine cream (Ziguang Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd, Beijing, China) was topically applied to the incision site.

Cell culture and treatments

Human bone marrow-derived MSCs (hBMSCs) were obtained from Cyagen, Jiangsu, China, and cultured in a OriCell complete culture medium containing with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin and streptomycin and culture supplement (Cyagen). Thereafter, when reaching the confluence, hBMSCs were passaged with an StemPro™ Accutase™ (ThermoFisher). Notably, hBMSCs of passages 3–8 were utilized in this study. Buffalo Rat Liver-3 A (BRL3A) cells were purchased from FUSHENBIO Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). They were cultured in a Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. BRL3A cells were passaged with trypsin/EDTA when cell confluence reached 90–100%. Cells were fostered in a sterilized cell incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) in the condition of 37 °C, 90% humidity, and 5% CO2.

To establish a hypoxia pretreatment cell model, hBMSCs were cultured in FBS/glucose-free medium (a Roswell Park Memo-rial Institute (RPMI)−1640 medium without FBS and glucose). Then the cells were placed in a Galaxy 48R hypoxia incubator (Eppendorf GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) with 5% CO2 and 1% O2 at 37°C for 2 h. Following incubation, the cells were dissociated using the StemProTM -AccutaseTM and subsequently seeded onto BRL3A cells to co-culture directly. Generally, the ratio of hBMSCs to BRL3A cells is 1:1. After BRL3A cells were co-cultured with hBMSCs for 4 h, the cells were used for hypoxia/reoxygenation (HR) experiment. As for the indirect co-culture experiment, 24-well transwells were employed with 24 inserts with 0.4 μm pore size filters (Corning, New York, USA). HBMSCs were seeded into the upper chamber at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 24 h and then preconditioned with a hypoxia pretreatment which described previously. Subsequently, the upper chamber was transferred to a 24-well plate coated with BRL3A cells (1 × 10⁵ cells per well). After co-culture of BRL3A cells with hBMSCs for 4 h, the cells were subjected to hypoxia/reoxygenation (HR) experiment.

To establish a HR cell model, BRL3A cells with or without hBMSCs were processed using the same protocol as previously described for the hBMSCs, and placed in a Galaxy 48R hypoxia incubator (Eppendorf GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) with 5% CO2 and 1% O2 at 37 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, the cells were placed in the normal cell incubator for 2 h.

To plumb the role of Cx43 and Cx32 in this study, retinoic acid (RA, 1 µM, MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA), the specific GJs agonist, and Gap26 (0.25 mg/mL, APExBIO, Houston, USA), the specific GJs inhibitor, were used in hBMSCs. To further knock down the expression, Cx43-siRNA and Cx32-siRNA (IGEbio, Guangzhou, China) were used in hBMSCs, and below is the sequence:

si-Cx43: 5’-GGAUUGAAGAGCACGGCAAdTdT;

si-Cx32: 5’-GAGUAUGGCUGUCCGUCAUdTdT.

The control group was administered a sequence-scrambled siRNA to serve as a negative control (NC-siRNA) for comparison. The siRNAs were introduced into hBMSCs using a transfection kit from RiboBio, following the manufacturer’s protocol.

For the generation of Cx43 or Cx32 stable overexpression in hBMSCs, hBMSCs were initially plated in 6-well plates. Once the cells reached 40–50% confluence, they were infected with a lentivirus containing the Cx43 or Cx32 overexpression vector (MiaoLing Plasmid Platform). As a negative control, cells were also infected with a lentivirus carrying the pLV3-3xFLAG vector (MiaoLing Plasmid Platform). Following 72 h of infection, the cells were selected with puromycin at a concentration of 4 µg/mL.

Histological analysis

The liver tissues were immersed in 10% (v/v) buffered formalin solution (Nanjing Keygen Biotech, Jiangsu, China) for fixation overnight at room temperature with gentle shaking. Subsequently, the samples were processed for paraffin embedding and sectioned into slices with a thickness of 5 μm. These sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and the extent of pathological injury was assessed using Suzuki’s scoring system to evaluate the pathological damage [36]. Briefly, through three indicators of sinusoidal congestion, cytoplasmic vacuolization, and hepatocyte necrosis, each is scored from 0 to 4 in severity, with a total score of 12 points.

The frozen liver tissues sections were washed 3 times in PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4) and 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was added, then coverslips were placed. Every section was observed using the fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The morphology of mitochondria in liver tissues were observed by TEM. The fresh liver tissues were fixed using malondialdehyde and stored at 4 °C. Subsequent procedures were carried out by Servicebio and the grids were visualized with Hitachi TEM system at 80.0 kV.

Mitochondrial superoxide (Mito-SOX) assay

Mito-SOX production was measured using the Mito-SOX Red kit (S0061S, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The detailed procedure is as follows: hBMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates until reaching 85–90% confluency. Then the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed once with PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4). Cells were incubated with 1mL of pre-prepared MitoSOX staining working solution (5 µM) and Mito-Tracker Green working solutions (green, Beyotime, 100nM) at 37 °C for 30 min. After removing the dye, PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4) was added to wash once. Subsequently cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 20 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4) three times, 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was added into the cells nuclei for 3 min at room temperature in the dark. Mitochondrial superoxide levels were detected using a fluorescence microscope. Four distinct yields will be captured on camera for every sample. The average fluorescence intensity calculated by ImageJ represents the fluorescence intensity. The experiment was repeated by three to five times (n = 3–5).

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

HBMSCs were seeded into 24-well plates separately and cultured until reaching 85%−90% confluency. Typically, PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4) was used to wash the cells to remove the culture medium. Pre-warmed Mito-Tracker Red (red, Beyotime, 100nM) was then added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C in the CO2 incubator for 30 min. Removing the liquid, washing the cells with PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4) once and next fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde in sequence at 37 °C for 20 min. Subsequently using PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4) to wash three times, pre-warmed Mito-Tracker Green (green, Beyotime,100nM) was added to the cells and incubated at 37° C for 30 min. Finally the cell nuclei were stained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 3 min at room temperature in the dark. Finally, Capturing four images per field of view according to the steps mentioned above. The average fluorescence intensity calculated by ImageJ represents the fluorescence intensity. Three to five independent experiments were conducted.

Mitochondrial transfer efficiency via cellular fluorescence

BRL3A cells or hBMSCs were seeded into 24-well plates separately and cultured until reaching 85%−90% confluency. HBMSCs were stained with a working fluid containing MitoBright LT Red Working Solution (red, DOJINDO, 0.1µmol/l) for 30 min and BRL3A cells were stained with a working fluid containing the fluorescent indicators Calcein-AM (green, Sigma-Aldrich, 25pM) in 1 ml serum-free growth media for 30 min. Then hBMSCs were digested with StemPro™ Accutase™ and transferred to BRL3A cells for co-culture. After 4 h of co-culture, cells were treated to HR experiments. After that, cells were rinsed with PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4) once, and then fixed 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) in sequence at 37 °C for 20 min. The average fluorescence intensity calculated by ImageJ represents the fluorescence intensity of the well. Independent experiments (n = 3–5) will be performed.

Mitochondrial transfer efficiency via flow cytometry

BRL3A cells or hBMSCs were seeded into 6-well plates separately and cultured until reaching 85%−90% confluency. Dil (red, Invitrogen, 2 µg/ml) and Mito-Tracker Green working solutions (green, Beyotime, 100nM) were added to hBMSCs, followed at 37 °C for 30 min in a cell incubator for staining. The cells were then digested with StemPro™ Accutase™, which were transferred to BRL3A cells for co-culture. After 4 h of co-culture, cells were treated to HR experiments. After that, the cells were digested with StemPro™ Accutase™. Next gently resuspend the cells in a RPMI − 1640 medium supplemented without FBS. Finally, mitochondrial transfer levels were measured using a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman, Pasadena, USA). The proportion of BRL3A cells with green fluorescence was used as the observation indicator. Three to five independent replicates will be repeated (n = 3–5). (Dil: Ex/Em = 549/565 nm, Mito-Tracker Green: Ex/Em = 490/516 nm).

“Parachute” dye-coupling assay

This experiment evaluates the function of GJs by transferring dyes between two cells. In short, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 85–90%. The “donor” cells - hBMSCs were stained with a working fluid containing the fluorescent indicators Calcein-AM (green, Sigma-Aldrich, 25pM) and Dil (red, Invitrogen, 2 µg/ml) in 1 ml serum-free growth media. After washing with PBS (Invitrogen, pH7.4), the “donor” cells were digested and centrifuged, resuspended and counted. The “recipient” cells-BRL3A cells received approximately 500 “donor” cells per well and were co-cultured in a cell culture incubator for 4 h to induce GJs formation. GJs allow Calcein (367Da) to spread to adjacent cells when Dil (>1 kDa) was prevented from being transferred to nearby cells via GJs because of its high molecular weight during this time. To assess GJs function, this study employed the ratio of the number of BRL3A cells (green) and hBMSCs (yellow). The image was taken under the previously stated conditions using a fluorescence microscope. Every sample will be captured on camera from four distinct yields. The experiment was conducted by three to five times (n = 3–5).

Gene expression analysis

A gene metabolism analysis visualization platform called Metabolic gEne RApid Visualizer (http://merav.wi.mit.edu/SearchByGenes.html) was utilized to analyze and compare the expression of GJs-related genes in hBMSCs and liver tissues.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using an RNA extraction solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and its quantity was determined via an ultramicro spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). For mRNA analysis, the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed with HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Real-time PCR amplification was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). β-actin served as the internal reference gene, and the relative mRNA expression levels were assessed using the ΔΔCt method. The following gene-specific primers were used for RT-qPCR: β-actin: F-AAATCGTGCGTGACATCAAAGA and R-GCCATCTCCTGCTCGAAGTC; Cx43: F-CTCAACTGCTGGAGGGAAGG and R-CCAGAAGCGCACATGAGAGA; Cx32: F-GGCACAAGGTCCACATCTCA and R-GGGTGGCTCTTTTGGCAGAAG; Cx26: F-GGCTCACCGTCCTCTTCATT and R-GGACACGAAGATCAGCTGCA.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-cultured cells (BRL3A cells and hBMSCs) are harvested and lysed in ice-cold buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 5% Glycerol) supplemented with protease inhibitors Deacetylase Inhibitor Cocktail (MCE, HY-K0030) and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (NCM, P001). After 30 minutes of incubation on ice, the lysate is centrifuged at 12500 rpm for 5 minutes at 4 °C to remove debris. Next, the primary antibody (Cx32, A10112, 1:1000, abclonal) is added to the Protein A/G agarose beads for 45 minutes to 1 hour, with a parallel negative control using non-specific IgG. Then, using 1xTBS to wash the mixture three times. Thereafter, 125uL lysis buffer was incubated 20uL mixture at 4 °C for overnight. The precipitate was washed three times with 1xTBS and then boiled with 1x loading buffer (YEASEN,20315ES20). The eluted samples are then analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot.

Then, centrifuged the lysate (12500 rpm, 4℃, 12 min) to extract the supernatant. The BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to measure the protein concentration, and a loading buffer (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was used to prepare the sample. Lastly, boil the sample for 5 min in a metal bath at 100 °C. Following that, an equivalent number of protein samples will be put to 8–12% SDS-PAGE (GeneScript, New Jersey, USA) and subsequently moved onto membranes made of polyvinylidene fluoride (Immobilon PSQ, Merck Millipore, Ltd., Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were sealed with 5% nonfat milk and allowed to be sealed for 2 h. They were then incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃ (mentioned below). Once TBST has been cleaned three times, incubated the secondary antibody in accordance with the species of the primary antibody and exposed to chemiluminescence analysis (Tanon5200, Shanghai, CN). Next, for calibration findings, incubated the internal reference protein (#380626, GAPDH, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, 1:10000 and R1207-1, β-actin, HUABIO, 1:10000). ImageJ software will be used to perform the final analysis of all the findings. The data were displayed as corrected values for the control and the internal reference protein. There will be three repetitions of the experiment (n = 3).

ATP assay

ATP production in liver tissues or hBMSCs was measured with an ATP assay kit (S0026, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

ATP detection in BRL3A cells was as follows: Firstly, cell populations were gated following the staining methods and gating strategies for flow cytometry described in Materials and methods 2.11. Results

Hypo-hBMSCs are more effective in alleviating hepatic IRI

Figure 1A demonstrated that the cell surivival rate of hBMSCs subjected to 2 h of hypoxia preconditioning (Hypo-2 h) remained unchanged, while ATP synthesis was enhanced (Fig. 1A). Further investigation revealed that hypoxia preconditioning significantly increased the expression of autophagy-related proteins such as PINK1 and LC3A, indicating that mitophagy had been activated (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, Fig. 1C-D demonstrated that hypoxia preconditioning not only reduced the accumulation of superoxide in the mitochondria on hBMSCs, but also elevated the mitochondrial membrane potential levels. Collectively, these findings lead us to speculate that hypoxia preconditioning may improve the quality of mitochondria in hBMSCs by initiating mitophagy.

Based on this discovery, we injected hypo-hBMSCs via the portal vein 24 h prior to the establishing AOLT models (Fig. 1E, Figure S1). The results found that the mitochondrial damage observed by TEM was effectively mitigated, characterized by orderly arrangement of cristae, dense matrix, and uniform distribution (Fig. 1F), and the hepatic ATP content was also significantly restored (Fig. 1G). More importantly, compared with non-preconditioned cells, hypo-hBMSCs exhibited a stronger effect in alleviating hepatic IRI, as evidenced by reduced Suzuki’s scores and decreased serum liver injury markers (Fig. 1H-I).

Hypo-hBMSCs transfer more mitochondria in alleviating hepatic IRI

We respectively employed Dil (red fluorescence) to label hBMSCs and utilized Mito-tracker Green (green fluorescence) for their mitochondria. The results revealed that after 24 h post-injection of hBMSCs, these cells not only persisted within the liver tissue, but facilitated the transfer of mitochondria to adjacent cells (Fig. 2A). Notably, when compared to their non-hypoxia-preconditioned counterparts, hypo-hBMSCs exhibited a markedly enhanced efficiency in mitochondrial transfer (Fig. 2B). This observation was consistently replicated in our in vitro experimental setup. Immunofluorescence imaging demonstrated that hypo-hBMSCs were capable of transferring a greater quantity of mitochondria to co-cultured BRL3A cells (Fig. 2C). We co-cultured BRL3A with hBMSCs which were labeled with Dil and Mito-Tracker Green. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that hypo-hBMSCs transferred a significantly higher number of mitochondria to BRL3A cells, as indicated by a substantial increase in the proportion of BRL3A cells exhibiting green fluorescence (Q1 group: 15.6% compared to 5.53%) (Fig. 2D, Figure S2A). Subsequently, we sorted out an equal number of these cells and measured the intracellular ATP levels. The results showed that BRL3A cells that had taken up hypo-hBMSCs mitochondria had higher ATP levels, indicating that hypo-hBMSCs mitochondria had superior functional metrics(Fig. 2E). However, the current method is not sufficient to completely rule out the possibility that the green fluorescence in population Q1 originates from non-specific uptake of Mito-Tracker Green from hBMSC debris or apoptotic bodies. Given the current literature reports that hepatocytes rarely actively engulf cell debris or apoptotic bodies [37], we believe that the green fluorescence in population Q1 indeed originates from intact, functionally normal mitochondria.

Intriguingly, when we implemented a transwell co-culture system to separate hypo-hBMSCs and BRL3A cells, thereby precluding direct cellular contact, mitochondrial transfer still occurred but at a reduced efficiency compared to direct co-culture conditions (2.8141 vs. 2.1376) (Figure S3A). This discrepancy suggests that the direct contact between these two cell types may establish a specialized conduit for mitochondrial transfer, thereby significantly influencing the overall efficiency of this process.

Hypo-hBMSCs facilitate efficient mitochondrial transfer to hepatocytes by enhancing GJs function

GJs serve as a crucial conduit for direct cell-to-cell communication and play a pivotal role in the direct intercellular material transfer [27]. Thus, we sued the “Parachute” dye-coupling assay to determine the function of GJs between hBMSCs and BRL3A cells. Fig. 3 A showed that the dye transfer from hypo-hBMSCs to BRL3A cells was more pronounced than that observed between hBMSCs and BRL3A. Concurrently, GJs’ enhancer RA increased the dye transfer between hypo-hBMSCs/normal hBMSCs and BRL3A cells, which was reversed by the inhibitor Gap26 (Fig. 3B). More importantly, the changes of mitochondrial transfer capacity between hypo-hBMSCs/normal hBMSCs and BRL3A cells was consistent with the changes in GJs function by immunofluorescence experiments. (Fig. 3C). And the same conclusion was further supported by flow cytometry, where we labeled the mitochondria of hBMSCs with Mito-Tracker Green and assessed the proportion of BRL3A cells exhibiting green fluorescence, finding that the proportion of BRL3A cells internalizing mitochondria from hypo-hBMSCs/normal hBMSCs changed in accordance with changes in GJs function. When the function of GJs was augmented by RA, the transfer of mitochondrial from hyp-hBMSCs/normal hBMSCs to BRL3A cells was significantly enhanced. Conversely, inhibitiong of GJs function with Gap26 markedly reduced the mitochondrial transfer to BRL3A cells (Figure S4A). These findings demonstrate that changes of GJs function could regulate mitochondrial transfer capacity, with the GJs on hypo-hBMSCs in particular demonstrating a higher efficiency in mitochondrial transfer.

Hypo-hBMSCs enhance GJs function through the upregulation of Cx43 and Cx32

GJs are composed of Cx [38]. The public databases show that Cx43, Cx32, and Cx26 are commonly expressed on both hBMSCs and liver tissues, among which, Cx43 is highly expressed on hBMSCs, while Cx32 is highly expressed in liver tissue (Fig. 4A). However, the literature indicates that Cx26 expression depends on the cellular state [39, 40]. Fig. 4B demonstrated that BRL3A cells highly expressed Cx32, while hBMSCs highly expressed Cx43. Given that GJs on hypo-hBMSCs demonstrated a higher efficiency in mitochondrial transfer, we further investigated the changes in the expression of Cx proteins on hypo-hBMSCs. The results of western blotting and RT-qPCR showed that both Cx43 and Cx32 expression were increased significantly, but Cx26 expression had no changes (Fig. 4C). Meanwhile, according to the existing studies, the structural domains of Cx43 and Cx32 are significantly different, preventing the formation of functional heterotypic channels (Cx43-Cx32-GJs) [33, 41], which our Co-IP experiments also verified the absence of interaction between Cx32 and Cx43, as Cx43 was not found in the immunoprecipitation complex of Cx32 (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that functional homotypic channels composed of Cx43 or Cx32 (Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs) between hypo-hBMSCs and BRL3A cells mediated the mitochondrial transfer.

Functional homotypic Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs mediate mitochondrial transfer from hypo-hBMSCs to hepatocytes

To further substantiate our research findings, we specifically knocked down or overexpressed Cx43 or Cx32 in hBMSCs to investigate how alterations in the function of GJs, composed of these proteins, influence mitochondrial transfer. The results indicated that as the expression of Cx43 or Cx32 in hBMSCs was reduced (Fig. 5A), the “Parachute” dye-coupling assay demonstrated a significant decline in fluorescence transfer between hBMSCs and BRL3A cells, signifying a diminished GJs function. Concurrently, mitochondrial transfer between these two cell types also experienced a substantial reduction (Fig. 5B-E). Conversely, when Cx43 or Cx32 was overexpressed in hBMSCs (Fig. 6A), both fluorescence transfer and mitochondrial transfer between hBMSCs and BRL3A cells exhibited a marked increase (Fig. 6B-C). Collectively, these results reaffirm the crucial role of GJs, composed of Cx43 or Cx32, in facilitating mitochondrial transfer between hBMSCs and BRL3A cells.

Discussion

Hepatic IRI is a significant challenge that affects the prognosis and success rate of patients undergoing LT [9]. Despite extensive research, effective clinical strategies to mitigate Hepatic IRI remain very limited [2]. In recent years, hypoxia preconditioning MSCs have demonstrated strong potential in alleviating organ damages due to their superior mitochondrial function and anti-inflammatory capabilities, but their mechanisms of action still limit their clinical application and translation [18, 23]. On this basis, we prepared hypo-hBMSCs and observed the therapeutic efficacy of hBMSCs against hepatic IRI in vivo and in vitro. We confirmed that hypoxia preconditioning treatment effectively enhanced the quantity and quality of mitochondria, while also promoting the release of ATP, through the induction of mitophagy. Meanwhile, hypo-hBMSCs could transfer mitochondria to the neighboring liver cells via functional homotypic Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs, protecting against hepatic IRI (Fig. 7). These findings not only elucidate the novel mechanisms underlying the protective effects of hypo-hBMSCs, but also offer fresh insights and directions for the clinical application and translational potential of hBMSCs.

Hypo-hBMSCs have emerged as a powerful tool in organ repair and regeneration, showcasing their remarkable therapeutic potential. For instance, hypo-hBMSCs have been shown to enhance anti-apoptotic capacity by activating the HIF-1α signaling pathway, leading to improved outcomes in myocardial infarction [42]. In ischemic brain injury models, these cells promote neurogenesis and angiogenesis through the secretion of various neurotrophic and anti-inflammatory factors, thereby facilitating functional neurological recovery [43]. Additionally, Hu et al. demonstrated that hypoxia preconditioned human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (hypo-hUC-MSCs) can mitigate severe acute pancreatitis injury by transferring functional mitochondria to damaged cells [18]. These findings collectively underscore the reliability and effectiveness of hypoxia preconditioning as a strategy to significantly enhance the therapeutic efficacy of hBMSCs [44]. During hepatic IRI, mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark event that leads to ATP depletion, oxidative stress, and ultimately cell death [6, 8, 10], a conclusion supported by our experimental results. Mitochondrial damage results in diminished ATP levels, which are negatively correlated with the severity of liver injury (Figure S1B-H). hypoxia preconditioning treatment can trigger mitophagy, reduce superoxide production, and stabilize mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby providing higher quality mitochondria for cellular repair and regeneration [18]. Most importantly, we have not only confirmed that hBMSCs can penetrate the liver sinusoidal endothelial cells to reside and function effectively in liver tissue over the long term, but also observed that hypo-hBMSCs exhibit enhanced capacity for transferring mitochondria to neighboring hepatocytes (Fig. 2B-C). These novel insights offer fresh perspectives on leveraging hBMSCs for the protection of liver grafts, highlighting their potential as a versatile and effective strategy in the realm of organ transplantation and tissue repair.

In the present study, we elucidated that hypoxia preconditioning treatment increased the expression of Cx43 and Cx32 on hBMSCs (Fig. 4C). The homotypic Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs formed by these proteins mediated the transfer of mitochondria between neighboring cells (Figs. 5B-E and 6B-C), thereby providing the target to further enhance the ability of hypo-hBMSCs to deliver mitochondria to hepatocytes. Beyond GJs, tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) and extracellular vesicles (EVs) are also widely recognized as efficient conduits for intercellular mitochondrial transfer [27]. TNTs are F-actin-based nanoscale membrane tubes, which are capable of long-distance mitochondrial transport [45]. However, due to their complex formation requirements and structural instability, mitochondrial transfer via TNTs is often inefficient and prone to interruption [46]. By comparison, characterized by their structural stability and relatively simple formation conditions, GJs stand out as a crucial structure for intercellular communication such as maintaining the normal rhythm of the heart [47]. Being one of the most powerful mechanisms for hBMSCs to exert their paracrine effects, EVs, encapsulated by a lipid bilayer, are limited by their small size to mediating the transfer of mitochondrial fragments or components, which in turn results in relatively low transfer efficiency [26]. Additionally, the non-selective nature of EVs may inadvertently deliver damaged mitochondrial components to recipient cells, potentially leading to adverse effects [48]. Besides, other paracrine effects of hBMSCs, such as IL-10 or TGF-B, promote mitochondrial transfer by regulating intercellular signaling pathways [49, 50]. However, due to their instability and selectivity for the microenvironment, the efficiency of mitochondrial transfer is relatively low. It could be acknowledged that other pathways like TNTs and EVs might also contribute to the overall effect in vivo, but based on the in vitro functional modulation experiments (Gap26/RA), the GJs pathway is demonstrated to be highly efficient and crucial for mitochondrial transfer.

GJs are formed by the docking of two connexin hemichannels, each composed of six connexin (Cx) subunits [38, 40]. As the basis for many biological effects, it mediates the direct transfer between adjacent cells, and mitochondria are also one of the important materials transferred [27]. GJs enable direct and efficient mitochondrial transfer from donor to recipient cells. Importantly, GJs exhibits selectivity, preferentially delivering healthy mitochondria while minimizing the risk of transferring dysfunctional ones, for example, healthy mitochondria with higher membrane potential are more likely to be transferred to recipient cells through GJs [51–53]. These characteristics indicate that GJs are an ideal pathway for mitochondrial transfer between neighboring cells. Thus, in our study, we demonstrated that hypo-hBMSCs enhance GJs function by upregulating the expression of Cx proteins, thereby mediating the transfer of a greater number of mitochondria from hypo-hBMSCs to neighboring hepatocytes in vitro. This might be one of the reasons why hypo-hBMSCs exhibit better therapeutic effects. However, this requires more experiments in vivo for validation.

To further elucidate the interactions, we investigated the composition of GJs between hypo-hBMSCs and neighboring hepatocytes. Utilizing immunofluorescence and Co-IP assays, we revealed that hypo-hBMSCs and hepatocytes specifically form homotypic Cx43-GJs or Cx32-GJs, rather than heterotypic Cx43-Cx32-GJs. However, there has a limitation in this study that we did not thoroughly investigate the potential association between the increased expression of Cx43/Cx32 and enhanced mitophagy. Based on the literature review, we speculate that there may be an interaction mechanism — The upregulated Cx43/Cx32 increase the expression of HIF-1ɑ through its C-terminal binding to HIF-1ɑ, and HIF-1ɑ subsequently can activate the expression of BNIP3 (a protein that promotes mitophagy), thereby promoting beneficial mitophagy in hypo-hBMSCs. We have preliminarily confirmed this viewpoint through western blot experiments in Supplementary Figure S5 (Figure S5A), but further in-depth research is needed, which will become one of our key research focuses in the future. Overall. this discovery not only identifies a novel target for genetic modification of hBMSCs, but also paves the way for future therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing mitochondrial transfer.

Throughout our study, we have demonstrated that hypoxia preconditioning treatment significantly enhances the quality of mitochondria in hBMSCs through protective autophagy, which selectively degrades damaged mitochondria. More importantly, hypoxia preconditioning treatment upregulates the expression of Cx43 and Cx32. A substantial number of high-quality mitochondria are efficiently transferred to neighboring hepatocytes via homotypic Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs, thereby effectively alleviating hepatic IRI (Fig. 7).

Conclusion

In summay, the study identifies hypo-hBMSCs induce mitophagy to enhance mitochondrial quality. At the same time, they also up-regulate Cx43 and Cx32 to form homotypic Cx43-GJs and Cx32-GJs with hepatocytes, which transfer more high-quality mitochondria to hepatocytes to exert a protective effect. Our findings highlight that hypoxia preconditioning treatment is a powerful strategy to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of hBMSCs, providing compelling new evidence to promote their clinical translation, especially in protecting against injury in liver grafts.
