Small bowel obstruction induced by migrated sacropexy mesh: a case report
Authors: Ahmed Ben Mahmoud, Mahdi Hammami, Amine Sebai, Yosr Hassine, Youssef Chaker, Anis Haddad, Montassar Kacem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSmall bowel obstruction (SBO) is a frequent cause of surgical emergency, most commonly due to adhesions, hernias, or tumors. Mesh-related SBO following laparoscopic sacropexy is an exceptionally rare complication, often presenting years after the initial procedure and difficult to diagnose preoperatively.Case presentationWe report a 69-year-old Caucasian woman with a history of laparoscopic sacropexy for cystocele repair using mesh eight years prior, presenting with abdominal pain, bilious vomiting, and distension. Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers. Abdominal radiography showed dilated small bowel loops, and computed tomography scan confirmed a mechanical SBO with a pelvic transition point, but the cause was unclear. Due to persistent symptoms despite medical management, laparoscopy was performed. Intraoperatively, a segment of small bowel mesentery was found incarcerated beneath a fibrous band, initially resembling an adhesion. Careful dissection revealed the band to be a migrated portion of the sacropexy mesh. The constricting segment was excised, the bowel released and inspected from the ligament of Treitz to the ileocecal valve, confirming viability and absence of additional lesions.ConclusionMesh-related SBO is extremely rare, with mechanisms including migration, erosion, and adhesion formation. Preoperative diagnosis is challenging since mesh is often poorly visualized on CT. Laparoscopy offers both diagnostic and therapeutic advantages, enabling targeted management with reduced morbidity compared to laparotomy, but requires advanced expertise to minimize the risk of enterotomy in distended bowel.

Introduction

Intestinal obstruction is a common and serious cause of acute abdomen, representing a significant proportion of surgical emergencies worldwide. Characterized by the partial or complete interruption of intestinal transit, it can rapidly lead to bowel ischemia, perforation, and sepsis if not promptly diagnosed and managed. The most frequent etiologies include postoperative adhesions, hernias, tumors, and, more rarely, congenital bands [1]. Laparoscopic sacropexy, widely performed for the treatment of pelvic organ prolapse such as cystocele, involves the placement of synthetic mesh to suspend the vaginal vault or bladder and restore pelvic anatomy. While this technique is generally safe and effective, it carries potential complications [2]. These include mesh erosion, infection, chronic pelvic pain, and, unusually, mechanical complications such as bowel obstruction.

In this report, we describe an unusual case of small bowel obstruction (SBO) in a 69-year-old woman with a prior history of laparoscopic cystocele repair using mesh.

This work is reported in line with the CARE criteria [3].

Case report

A 69-year-old Caucasian female presented to the emergency department with a two-day history of diffuse abdominal pain, nausea, and repeated episodes of bilious vomiting. She reported abdominal distension and an absence of bowel movements and flatus over the preceding 24 hours.

Her medical history was notable for an appendectomy at the age of 30 and a laparoscopic cystocele repair performed 8 years earlier. Unfortunately, detailed information regarding the cystocele repair, including the type of mesh used and the surgical technique (such as whether a sacropexy was performed), was not available.

On initial examination, the patient was alert, oriented, and hemodynamically stable. Her vital signs were within normal limits: blood pressure 125/75 mmHg, heart rate 85 bpm, respiratory rate 16 breaths/minutes, temperature 37 °C, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air.

Abdominal examination revealed marked distension without tenderness nor guarding. Bowel sounds were diminished. There were no palpable hernias, and surgical scars from prior procedures were noted. Rectal examination revealed an empty rectal ampulla.

Baseline laboratory tests showed leukocytosis with a white blood cell count of 12,000/mm3 and an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 100 mg/L, suggesting an inflammatory response. Renal function tests, serum electrolytes, liver enzymes, and coagulation profile were within normal ranges, indicating no significant metabolic disturbances or organ dysfunction at presentation.

An abdominal X-ray demonstrated multiple dilated loops of small bowel with prominent air-fluid levels, consistent with a diagnosis of SBO.

Initial management included supportive measures including intravenous fluid resuscitation with isotonic crystalloids to correct dehydration and maintain adequate perfusion, electrolyte monitoring and correction, and gastric decompression via a nasogastric tube, which yielded a significant amount of bilious content. Analgesics and antiemetics were administered for symptom relief.

The patient was stabilized and admitted for further evaluation and management, with a provisional diagnosis of mechanical SBO of unclear etiology pending further imaging and surgical exploration.

A contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography scan (CT scan) of the abdomen and pelvis revealed diffuse dilatation of small bowel loops, a decompressed distal ileum, and a transition point in the pelvis (Fig. 1). Initially, this band appeared as a dense, fibrous structure resembling an adhesive band or a congenital fibrous strand. The surgical team proceeded with meticulous dissection and sectioning of the band using Ligasure to relieve the obstruction. However, upon closer inspection of the freed segment, the dissected structure was recognized as a portion of a previously placed synthetic mesh, likely part of the sacropexy performed during the laparoscopic cystocele repair 8 years earlier. Once the mesh flange was sectioned, the incarcerated bowel loop was gently released from its entrapment. The affected segment was inspected thoroughly: after a short period of observation, it gradually regained its normal pink coloration and peristaltic movements, suggesting that the bowel remained viable with no irreversible ischemic injury or need for resection. No additional mesh erosion or intra-abdominal adhesions were observed. Intraoperative inspection of the whole small bowel was carried out, from the ligament of Treitz to the ileocecal valve revealing no lesions or lacerations.

The decision was made not to remove the entire mesh, as there were no signs of infection or widespread mesh-related complications. Instead, the entrapping portion was excised to prevent recurrence. Peritoneal lavage was performed, and two drains were placed for postoperative monitoring.

The patient recovered uneventfully. Bowel function progressively returned: she passed flatus by postoperative day two and had her first bowel movement by day four. Oral intake was resumed gradually without complications. The drains were removed once the output decreased and showed no signs of infection. The patient was discharged home in good general condition on postoperative day six, with recommendations for outpatient follow-up to monitor for any late mesh-related issues.

Discussion

Laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy (or sacropexy) is widely regarded as the gold-standard surgical treatment for apical pelvic organ prolapse, particularly cystocele and uterine prolapse [4]. This procedure restores pelvic anatomy by suspending the vaginal vault or uterus to the anterior longitudinal ligament of the sacrum using a synthetic mesh [5]. Numerous studies have confirmed its efficacy in achieving long-term anatomical support and improved quality of life, comparable to vaginal repairs [6, 7], but lower complication rate [8]. However, despite its benefits, sacropexy is not without complications, particularly those related to the implanted mesh.

The most commonly reported mesh-related complications include mesh erosion into the vagina or bladder, chronic pelvic pain, infection, and dyspareunia [2]. Less frequently, mesh may migrate or erode into the abdominal cavity, potentially causing mechanical complications such as bowel obstruction, fistulas, or adhesions.

Cases of bowel obstruction directly related to mesh used in sacropexy are exceptionally rare, with only isolated cases reported in the literature [9–13]. Most of these involve mesh migration, erosion, or the formation of fibrous bands tethering the bowel or mesentery. Such complications may manifest many years after the initial surgery, as illustrated in our case.

The mechanisms of mesh migration and erosion are multifactorial. Chronic foreign body reaction, low-grade inflammation, mechanical friction between the mesh and surrounding tissues, and insufficient initial fixation may contribute to progressive mesh displacement. Over time, the mesh may erode through fascial planes or peritoneal surfaces, creating bands or flanges that can trap bowel loops or mesenteric structures [14]. Additionally, factors such as impaired tissue healing, infection, or excessive mesh tension may accelerate erosion. In our patient, the absence of infection or extensive adhesions suggests a gradual erosion process, resulting in the formation of a fibrous mesh flange under which the mesentery became incarcerated.

Preoperative diagnosis of mesh-related SBO remains challenging. The clinical presentation is often non-specific and indistinguishable from more frequent etiologies such as postoperative adhesions, internal hernias, or strictures. Conventional abdominal X-rays generally demonstrate non-specific signs of intestinal obstruction, such as air-fluid levels and dilated loops, but they provide little information about the underlying cause.

Cross-sectional imaging, particularly CT scanning, is the modality of choice in the evaluation of suspected SBO obstruction. CT can determine the level and severity of the obstruction and may reveal associated complications, including ischemia or perforation. However, mesh-related etiologies are rarely identified preoperatively. The difficulty arises from the fact that synthetic meshes are often poorly visualized on CT unless they are associated with secondary changes such as localized inflammatory reaction, mass effect, or abnormal positioning suggestive of migration [11]. In most cases, the radiological findings point to mechanical obstruction without allowing precise etiological attribution to the mesh.

In our case, the preoperative CT scan confirmed the presence of SBO, showing dilated proximal loops and a transition zone, but failed to demonstrate mesh migration as the underlying cause. This limitation underscores the difficulty of relying solely on imaging to establish the diagnosis. Ultimately, surgical exploration remains the gold standard, both for diagnosis and management, as it allows direct visualization of the bowel, assessment of its viability, and identification of the offending foreign body.

Laparoscopy has increasingly emerged as a valuable tool in the diagnosis and management of SBO, particularly in hemodynamically stable patients without signs of diffuse peritonitis or severe bowel ischemia. Its minimally invasive nature allows for a complete exploration of the abdominal cavity, precise identification of the transition point, and, in selected cases, definitive therapeutic intervention [15]. Compared with open surgery, laparoscopy is associated with reduced postoperative pain, lower risk of wound complications, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery.

However, the feasibility of laparoscopy in SBO is highly dependent on patient selection and surgical expertise. Dense adhesions, massively distended loops, or compromised bowel wall can limit exposure and increase the risk of iatrogenic enterotomy. The procedure requires a careful and systematic approach, often beginning with an open technique or optical trocar to minimize the risk of bowel injury during entry. Gentle manipulation of dilated loops is essential to avoid serosal tears or perforations.

In the specific context of prior pelvic mesh implantation, laparoscopy offers distinct advantages. It permits direct visualization of mesh position, assessment of potential migration, and evaluation of mesh-related adhesions or erosions. Targeted adhesiolysis or mesh release can often be performed safely under laparoscopic guidance, thereby avoiding the morbidity of a full laparotomy [16]. Nonetheless, this approach demands significant surgical expertise. Surgeons must be proficient in advanced laparoscopic techniques, familiar with the altered anatomy following pelvic reconstructive procedures, and prepared to convert to open surgery if safe progress cannot be achieved [17].

Therefore, while laparoscopy represents a feasible and often preferable option for SBO management, its safe execution relies on adequate case selection, technical proficiency, and readiness to escalate to open surgery when necessary.

In our patient, laparoscopy provided both diagnostic clarity and therapeutic intervention. The incarcerated bowel loop was gently released, and the constricting mesh flange was excised without necessitating bowel resection. This approach likely contributed to the rapid postoperative recovery and preservation of bowel function.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but serious complication of laparoscopic sacropexy: SBO due to delayed mesh erosion and mesenteric entrapment. Although sacropexy remains an effective and generally safe procedure for pelvic organ prolapse, surgeons should be aware of the potential for late mesh-related complications, including mesh migration and erosion into the abdominal cavity.

This case underscores the importance of long-term surveillance in patients with pelvic mesh implants and reinforces the role of laparoscopy as the preferred approach in managing unexplained bowel obstruction in this context.
