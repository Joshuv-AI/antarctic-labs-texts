Visually detectable facial mimicry in response to android facial expressions
Authors: Dongsheng Yang, Wataru Sato, Chun-Ting Hsu, Takashi Minato, Shin’ya Nishida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFacial mimicry plays a crucial role in human communication. Previous studies have reported that observing human facial expressions induced spontaneous facial mimicry using facial electromyography (EMG) and video data. A previous study reported that the expressions of an android also elicited such mimicry, as reflected in EMG recordings. However, whether such android-induced mimicry could be visually detectable remains unknown. To investigate this issue, we showed dynamic facial expressions of anger and happiness from the android Nikola via a prompter system to participants. We recorded the EMG of their corrugator supercilii and zygomatic major and videotaped their faces, which were automatically coded for facial actions to evaluate brow lowering and lip-corner pulling actions. Nikola’s angry expressions induced stronger corrugator supercilii activity in the EMG and brow lowering in the video data, while happy expressions induced stronger zygomatic major activity and lip corner pulling. These results imply that the facial expressions of humanlike androids can induce visually detectable spontaneous facial mimicry in humans, which could be further utilized to coordinate human–robot interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25394-6.Keywords: Android, Automated Facial Action Coding System (FACS), Facial electromyography (EMG), Facial expressions of emotion: Facial mimicrySubject terms: Human behaviour, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Facial mimicry has been proposed to play a crucial role in human social interactions, acting as a foundation for eliciting empathy and social bonding1. Psychophysiological research that has employed facial electromyography (EMG) has revealed that exposure to facial expressions triggers facial muscle activity that matches the expressions displayed. For example, one study demonstrated that merely viewing photographs of angry and happy faces spontaneously activated the corrugator supercilii muscle (associated with brow lowering and typical of anger) and the zygomatic major muscle (associated with lip-corner pulling and typical of happiness), respectively2. Additional research indicated that such responses occurred even without conscious recognition of expressions, emphasizing their automatic nature3. Other studies reported that such responses correlated with the elicitation of emotional experiences and the recognition of emotional states, highlighting the crucial role of facial mimicry in intraindividual psychological processes4,5. Moreover, several studies that have videotaped the reactions of participants to facial expressions have reported visually detectable facial actions congruent with the stimulus expressions6–8. For example, a study that applied Facial Action Coding System (FACS) analysis9 to evaluate the action units (AUs) of brow lowering (i.e. AU 4) and lip corner pulling (i.e. AU 12) found that observing dynamic facial expressions of anger and happiness induced more frequent AU 4 and AU 12, respectively6. The data imply that facial mimicry is visible and serves interindividual social functions.

Hofree et al.10 found that spontaneous facial mimicry, as evaluated via facial EMG, can be elicited by the facial expressions of an android, a robot with a human-like appearance and movements11. Robots that can socially interact and communicate with humans have recently become popular to support human activity at home and in various industries worldwide12. Since social robots are expected to interact and communicate naturally with humans13, data showing the potential of such robots to induce facial mimicry in humans could be valuable. Hofree et al.10 showed an android’s dynamic facial expressions of anger and happiness in face-to-face situations and recorded participants’ facial EMG signals. EMG activity of the corrugator supercilii and zygomatic major muscles was stronger in response to angry and happy expressions, respectively. Relatedly, several studies reported congruent facial EMG activity in response to angry and happy facial expressions of virtual agents14, though there may be several differences between virtual and robotic artificial agents (e.g. physical presence15). These findings imply that the facial expressions of androids have the potential to induce facial mimicry in humans, as do human facial expressions.

However, issues remain regarding facial mimicry in response to android facial expressions. First, it remains unclear whether android facial expressions can robustly induce facial mimicry because one of the experiments in Hofree et al.10 showed unclear findings. In the experiment, the researchers presented videos of an android’s angry and happy facial expressions to participants and recorded their corrugator supercilii and zygomatic major muscle EMG. The results showed that only participants who rated the android high for human likeness showed stronger zygomatic major activity in response to happy than angry expressions. A difference between emotional expressions in corrugator supercilii EMG was not evident. The results suggested the possibility that facial mimicry in response to android facial expressions may not be evident under the display viewing condition. However, based on ample evidence showing that EMG activity can be interpreted as facial mimicry even in response to human expressions displayed on the monitor, together with the facilitative effect of human likeness on facial mimicry to android expressions in Hofree et al.10, we hypothesized that androids with sufficiently high human likeliness could induce facial EMG activity to show facial mimicry in humans.

Furthermore, it remains unknown whether facial mimicry in response to androids could be visually detectable. Since facial EMG activity in response to facial expressions is generally very weak (at a few microvolts16), some researchers have speculated that facial muscle activity detected by EMG signals may not be visually detectable17–19; hence, EMG activity in response to android facial expressions may also not be visually detectable. A previous study by Perugia20 videotaped participants’ facial reactions to robots’ emotional facial expressions and applied automated FACS analysis to the video data, suggesting the possibility of visually detectable facial mimicry. However, the researchers did not compare different facial expression conditions, and it remained inconclusive as to whether visually detectable facial mimicry occurred or not. This point is crucial because only visually detectable facial mimicry can serve as an intraindividual, social signal and coordinate reciprocal social relationships. If humans show visually detectable facial mimicry in response to an android, then the android can modify social behaviors via those signals (e.g., an android teacher in a classroom can smile at a student to induce a positive state and then have a conversation depending on the student’s response). Furthermore, visually detectable facial mimicry could be read by other humans, and in turn, an android–human pair interaction could modify the emotional states of other people in the vicinity (e.g., a student’s response to an android teacher could change the atmosphere among students in a classroom). Based on robust findings in humans6–8, we hypothesize that when an android’s facial expressions are similar in appearance and motion to those of humans, it may induce visually detectable facial mimicry.

To test these hypotheses, we experimented with an android (Fig. 1). We used the android Nikola, which has been validated to show humanlike facial expressions of six basic emotions, including anger and happiness21. We presented Nikola’s dynamic facial expressions of anger and happiness to participants. We used EMG to measure the activities of the corrugator supercilii and zygomatic major muscles and videotaped participants’ facial reactions. The video recordings were subsequently subjected to automated FACS analysis to evaluate the AUs of brow lowering (i.e. AU 4) and lip-corner pulling (i.e. AU 12) as described in a previous study8. We predicted that Nikola’s expressions of anger and happiness would more strongly induce corrugator supercilii and zygomatic major responses in EMG1, respectively, as well as brow lowering and lip-corner pulling actions, respectively. We also assessed subjective valence ratings to confirm the validity of Nikola’s negative and positive facial expressions and explored the ratings of arousal and human likeness. We also explored correlations between these measures.

Results

Facial EMG

We predicted that Nikola’s emotional expressions would induce greater activity in the corrugator supercilii muscle in response to angry expressions than in response to happy expressions and stronger activation of the zygomatic major in response to happy expressions than to angry expressions.

Indeed, a paired t-test (one-tailed) indicated significantly stronger corrugator supercilii activity (Fig. 2 left) in response to angry expressions than in response to happy ones (t[25] = 1.804, p < 0.001, d = 0.354). For the zygomatic major EMG activity (Fig. 2 right), significantly stronger activity was found in response to happy expressions than to angry expressions (t[25] = 4.53, p < 0.001, d = 0.89).

Mean (with standard error) standardized electromyography (EMG) activity of the corrugator supercilii muscle (left) and zygomatic major muscle (right) in response to Nikola’s angry and happy facial expressions.

Video data

We expected the video data results to be similar to those for EMG activity, and they aligned with our expectations. For AU 4, a paired t-test (one-tailed) indicated significantly greater activation in response to angry expressions compared to that in response to happy expressions (t[25] = 2.17, p = 0.020, d = 0.43); the opposite was true for AU 12 (t[25] = 1.98, p = 0.030, d = 0.39) (Fig. 3).

Mean (with standard error) for action unit 4 (left) and 12 (right) activity in response to Nikola’s angry and happy facial expressions. The y-axis represents the average score of the baseline-corrected action unit data from each trial processed using the Py-Feat 0.6.022 toolkit. Details are elaborated in the Methods section. Lower panes are illustrations of the action units created using FACSGen 2.023,24.

Subjective ratings

We analyzed the subjective valence ratings of participants as a manipulation check (i.e. negative vs. positive facial expressions) using the same analyses as for the EMG and video data. We also explored the ratings of arousal and human likeness using two-tailed t-tests.

Consistent with our predictions, a paired t-test (one-tailed) for the valence ratings (Fig. 4 left) indicated significantly higher positive ratings for happy than for angry expressions (t[22] = 3.89, p < 0.001, d = 0.81) in terms of valence. Regarding arousal (Fig. 4 middle), a two-tailed paired t-test indicated significantly higher ratings for the angry expressions than for the happy expressions (t[22] = 6.41, p < 0.001, d = 1.34). Finally, human-likeness ratings (Fig. 4 right) showed a nonsignificant tendency to rate the angry expressions higher than the happy expressions (t[22] = 1.86, p = 0.077, d = 0.39).

Mean (with standard error) subjective ratings of valence (left), arousal (middle), and human likeness (right) in response to Nikola’s angry and happy facial expressions.

Zygomatic major EMG and AU 12 were significantly positively correlated, and there were significant negative correlations between the zygomatic major EMG and AU 4, and between AU 4 and 12 activity (p < 0.05). The corrugator supercilii EMG was significantly positively correlated with the arousal ratings (p < 0.05).

Discussion

The results of facial EMG analysis indicated that stronger activity of the corrugator supercilii, which is related to brow-lowering actions and prototypical facial muscles associated with angry expressions, was elicited in response to Nikola’s angry expressions than in response to its happy expressions (shown via the prompter display). Stronger activity of the zygomatic major, which is related to lip-corner pulling actions and the prototypical facial muscles of happy facial expressions, was elicited in response to happy expressions. These findings are partially in agreement with a previous study that demonstrated greater EMG activity in these same areas in response to the same facial expressions from an android during face-to-face interactions10. However, in that study, the corrugator supercilii did not show clear differences between angry and happy expressions when the participants observed the android expressions in the display; stronger zygomatic major EMG activity was observed in response to happy vs. angry expression only in those who rated the android highly on human likeness. We speculate that this discrepancy may have been attributable to the degree of human likeness of the androids employed10. We used Nikola, which was developed and empirically validated to show humanlike facial expressions both spatially and temporally21; such validation was not reported for the android used in the previous study25. Therefore, Nikola’s humanlike facial expressions may have strongly induced facial mimicry, which was detected even via a display. Our results are consistent with previous studies that have reported congruent facial EMG patterns in response to human facial expressions in displays (e.g.,2). Our results extend these previous studies and indicate that facial expressions of humanlike androids can generally induce facial mimicry that is detected via EMG, as do the facial expressions of humans.

More importantly, the video data analysis demonstrated greater activation of AU 4, which indicates brow lowering, in response to angry expressions, and greater activation of AU 12, which is associated with lip-corner pulling, in response to happy expressions. These results are consistent with the facial EMG data as well as with findings showing that facial EMG and automated AU analyses based on video data can be congruent26. However, video data can provide unique insights, because only visually detectable facial mimicry can be used by androids to modulate subsequent android social behaviors. Therefore, our results suggest that androids have the potential to engage in reciprocal social interactions with humans. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported visually detectable facial mimicry in response to human facial expressions shown in the display (e.g.,6). However, no previous studies that employed robots have found that they can induce visually detectable facial mimicry in humans. To the best of our knowledge, our results provide the first evidence that androids can induce visually detectable facial mimicry in humans.

Our correlation analyses indicated some associations between measures. We found a correlation between zygomatic major EMG and AU 12 activity. This is consistent with a previous study that reported associations between facial EMG and automatically analyzed AU data in response to human facial expression stimuli8. We found negative correlations between brow and lip activity in the EMG and AU data, consistent with previous findings reporting the opposite patterns in these muscles using EMG2,3 and AU6–8 evaluations with human facial expression stimuli. We did not find evident correlations between facial EMG/AU activity and human likeness ratings. We speculate that the subjective impression of human likeness may not be linearly related to facial mimicry in response to android facial expressions.

Our findings have practical implications regarding the use of androids or social robots in real-world applications. Social robots have become increasingly common worldwide, supporting human activities at home and in the workplace12. Robots take on various roles, including education, caregiving, and customer service, where effective social interaction is key to their acceptance and success. To fulfill this purpose, social robots should be able to interact naturally with humans13. As facial mimicry plays a vital role in human interactions1, social robots should be equipped to induce facial mimicry in humans to enhance their ability to engage in effective and natural interactions. For example, robots capable of inducing facial mimicry could establish stronger social bonds and rapport with students in classrooms, elderly patients in caregiving settings, or customers in shopping situations. This capability would improve the acceptance and efficacy of such robots.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we tested only angry and happy facial expressions. Hence, the effects of other emotions remain to be examined. Previous studies that have tested human facial expression stimuli have reported that facial mimicry of other emotions, such as sadness and surprise27, induced congruent facial EMG activity. The android Nikola that we used in this study is appropriate to test this issue, because it has been validated as showing the facial expressions of all six basic emotions21, as well as complicated emotions states such as awe28. Investigating the android’s facial expression of other emotions is a promising direction for future research.

Second, we did not assess participants’ emotion recognition or other mind perception measures. Previous studies testing facial mimicry in response to humans’ facial expressions have reported that facial mimicry is associated with emotion recognition4,5. Our data imply that, from the observer’s perspective, Nikola’s facial expressions may induce the perception of emotional states in Nikola. At the same time, a previous study that assessed participants’ mental state perception in response to various agents found that humanoid robots were considered not to have subjective experience, i.e., their subjective experience was rated below that of a deceased woman29. These findings suggest that the explicit, rational evaluation of the subjective states of Nikola may deny the existence of its emotional experience. The assessment of emotion or mind perception related to spontaneous facial mimicry in response to androids is a subject for future research.

Third, we did not test whether participants’ facial reactions to Nikola’s angry and happy expressions could be recognized as congruent emotional expressions by other humans. Since we did not acquire participants’ consent to show their faces to others, we could not conduct additional rating experiments. To simulate this investigation, we conducted machine-based emotion-recognition analyses using Py-Feat 0.6.022, which recognizes the intensity of six basic emotions and the neutral emotion for facial images using ResMaskNet30. ResMaskNet employs a ResNet-50 backbone augmented with four residual masking blocks; each block is a U-Net based localization subnetwork that refines intermediate feature maps by generating spatial attention masks, allowing the network to focus on crucial facial regions (e.g., eyes, brows, and mouth)30. The results showed that the recognized emotion categories in participants’ faces congruent with Nikola’s facial expressions were subtle and did not differ significantly between conditions (recognized anger: mean ± SE = 0.00 ± 0.00 and 0.00 ± 0.00 for angry and happy expressions, respectively, t[25] = 0.09, p = 0.927, d = 0.02; recognized happiness: mean ± SE = 0.00 ± 0.00 and 0.00 ± 0.00 for angry and happy expressions, respectively, t[25] = 0.54, p = 0.593, d = 0.11). The most strongly recognized emotion was neutral for most trials for both angry and happy facial expression conditions (64.62% and 62.69%, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 3). The results suggested that participants’ facial reactions to Nikola’s facial expressions exhibit facial actions but not emotional expressions. However, it is also possible that the artificial neural network may not have detected subtle facial expressions because it did not learn from micro-expression databases. A previous study reported that human coders could recognize both facial actions and emotional expressions in participants’ facial reactions to human facial expressions6, suggesting the possibility that humans may detect subtle emotions in participants’ facial reactions to android expressions. This issue warrants empirical investigation in future studies using human ratings.

Finally, we conducted the experiment within a laboratory-controlled environment. This controlled setting, while ensuring experimental rigor, may not have fully captured the nuances of natural interactions. For example, during natural interactions, androids should express emotions not only via facial expressions but also through other modalities, including the prosody and verbal contents31; androids may need to face multiple observers32. Further investigation of these issues would provide deeper insights into how facial mimicry manifests in more natural and dynamic human–robot interactions.

In summary, Nikola’s facial expressions effectively induced both physiologically and visually detectable facial mimicry in humans. This implies that androids have the potential to engage in more natural and socially meaningful interactions with humans, paving the way for more effective integration of social robots into everyday life.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-six adults (12 men and 14 women) were recruited in Kyoto (mean age ± SD = 25.27 ± 5.49 years, age range 20–37 years). The sample size was determined through a priori power analysis using G*Power software (ver. 3.1.9.233). The analysis was based on paired t-tests (one-tailed) to detect facial mimicry, specifically higher brow-lowering activity in response to angry expressions and higher lip-corner-pulling activity in response to happy expressions. The parameters included an α-level of 0.05 and a power (1 – β) of 0.80, with an effect size (d) of 0.56, estimated from previous visually detectable facial mimicry findings in response to human dynamic facial expressions6. This analysis indicated that 22 participants were required. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of RIKEN and conducted following its ethical standards. Written informed consent, including consent for video recording and publication, was obtained from all participants. All participants were financially compensated for their involvement in the research.

Experimental design

We used a within-subjects one factorial design focusing on angry and happy emotions.

Apparatus

The android Nikola21 was used to show facial expressions. This android was developed at RIKEN to study human–robot emotional interactions. The android’s face is that of a human child; it is covered with soft, deformable silicone skin attached to the front of the skull. Underneath the skin, it is equipped with 35 pneumatic actuators that are controlled by pressure valves: 29 are dedicated to controlling facial muscle movements, 3 are used for head movements, and 3 manage eyeball movements. A previous study21 validated Nikola’s 17 AUs associated with prototypical facial expressions, including 4 (brow lowering) and 12 (lip-corner pulling). The android receives motor control commands from a notebook computer (Intel Core i7–10870H CPU, 2.20 GHz, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 laptop GPU, GDDR6 6GB) running the Ubuntu 20.04 operating system. Commands are transmitted via a USB cable, enabling Nikola to generate dynamic facial expressions. The android’s hair and make-up were the same throughout the experiment.

To display Nikola’s facial expressions to the participants and videotape the participants’ faces, the android and the participant each faced a prompter (MPL-21W3/HDMI, Life-On, Hikone, Japan), consisting of a mirror reflecting a TV screen, with a video camera (VIXIA HF R800, Canon, Tokyo, Japan) concealed behind the mirror (Fig. 5; Supplementary Fig. 1). The video signals were controlled by a switcher (SL-41C, Imagenics, Tokyo, Japan), which received serial port signals from a Windows 11 computer (Precision T3500, Dell, Round Rock, TX, USA) and Presentation 24.1 software (Neurobehavioral Systems, Berkeley, CA, USA) running the experimental paradigm and switched between the visual input from the PC and the android’s side camera. The resolution of the video recording was 1920 × 1080.

Experimental setup for the facial stimulus display and recording participants’ responses.

Stimuli

Nikola displayed dynamic facial expressions of anger and happiness. During each trial, the android presented a neutral face for 1 s, transitioned to an emotional expression over the next second (± 25 ms), and held the apex emotional expression, either happy or angry, for 1 s. We used 60 slightly different angry (30) and happy (30) facial expressions by changing the AU activation patterns (e.g. prototypical emotional expressions21), and adjusted the emotional expressions based on Bayesian optimization34 and the degree of mouth opening (e.g. closed, partially open, and open wide).

Procedure

The experiments were conducted individually in an electrically shielded soundproof room (Science Cabin, Takahashi Kensetsu, Tokyo, Japan). Upon arrival, the participants were briefed about the study and providing informed consent before signing the consent form. An assistant placed the EMG electrodes on the participants and seated them in front of the prompter.

In the initial phase of the experiment, the participants engaged in passive viewing. They were instructed to focus on a central cross on the screen, which appeared for a jittered duration between 2,000 and 3,750 ms, with an average intertrial interval of 2,604 ms. During each trial, the android presented dynamic facial expressions of anger and happiness for 3 s; neutral, dynamic, and apex expressions for each 1 s. This speed has been shown to reflect natural changes in the dynamic facial expressions of human35 and android21 faces. Then, the fixation cross reappeared on the screen. The participants completed four practice trials, composed of two trials for each emotion condition. Subsequently, they engaged in 30 passive viewing trials per condition, amounting to a total of 60 trials, with a break scheduled after 32 trials. The order of conditions within the trials was pseudo-randomized for each participant to prevent more than three consecutive trials of the same condition (either happy or angry). EMG data were gathered during these passive viewing trials.

In the second part of the study, participants completed the subjective experience ratings. They were instructed: “Please rate the subjective experience you feel when you see the dynamic emotions.” Participants were asked to evaluate valence (positive vs. negative), arousal (low vs. high), and human likeness (low vs. high) based on their feelings, using Likert scales ranging from 1 to 9, using the keyboard in front of the prompter. During each trial, participants rated the dynamic facial expressions in terms of valence, arousal, and human likeness. They completed four practice rating trials (two per emotion condition) before moving on to the 60 test rating trials. Afterwards, the electrodes were removed, and the participants received monetary compensation before being dismissed.

Facial EMG recording

The EMG data were recorded from the corrugator supercilii and zygomatic major muscles using pre-gelled, self-adhesive 0.7-cm Ag/AgCl electrodes with 1.5-cm of spacing between the electrodes (Prokidai, Sagara, Japan). The placement of the electrodes adhered to established guidelines36,37. A reference electrode was positioned on the left earlobe. The signals were amplified and subjected to online filtering (bandpass: 20–400 Hz) and were recorded at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz using an EMG-025 amplifier (Harada Electronic Industry, Sapporo, Japan) along with the PowerLab 16/35 data acquisition system running LabChart Pro v8.0 software (ADInstruments, Dunedin, New Zealand). A 20-Hz low-cut filter was used, as it effectively eliminates motion-related artifacts from facial EMG38,39.

Data analysis

EMG data preprocessing

EMG data analyses were performed using Psychophysiological Analysis Software 3.3 (Computational Neuroscience Laboratory of the Salk Institute) and in-house programs implemented in MATLAB 2020a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The data for each trial were 2.5–3.5 s, including the apex emotional expression period (i.e. 2–3 s) and considering a delay of about 500 ms in facial mimicry40. The data were rectified and baseline-corrected for each trial to the mean value over the neutral expression period (i.e. 0–1 s) as described in a previous study26. Trials with an absolute value of the standardized baseline-period activity greater than six were excluded as artifacts. EMG data were standardized within individuals for each muscle site based on previous studies (e.g.,41).

Video data preprocessing

The video data were subjected to automated FACS coding using the default models of Py-Feat 0.6.022. RetinaFace, a toolbox that performs 2D face alignment and 3D face reconstruction in a single shot, was used to detect faces. The feature pyramid network in RetinaFace obtains the input facial images and outputs five feature maps of different scales, computed from the output of the corresponding ResNet residual stage (pretrained classification network on the ImageNet-11k dataset). Cascade multitask loss was calculated, incorporating the loss-of-face classification, Box regression, five-facial-landmark regression, and 1 k 3D vertices regression. MobileFaceNets42 was used to detect facial landmarks, adopting a global depth-wise convolution layer to output a discriminative feature vector, in place of the global average pooling layer, after the last non-global convolutional layer of a face feature embedding convolutional neural network. The XGBoost classifier, Feat-XGB43, was used as the AU detector, which used PCA-reduced HOG features for the AU predictions. It analyzed 20 AUs (1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 17, 18, 20, 23, 24, 25, 26, 28, and 43) (see Supplementary Fig. 2 for output examples). We analyzed the data of AUs 4 and 12 based on our interest. As in the above-described EMG analysis, we baseline-corrected the data from each trial.

Subjective rating preprocessing

The mean rating was calculated for each rating for each emotion condition of each participant. Because three participants misunderstood the instructions, we analyzed the data of the remaining 23 participants.

Statistical tests

The emotion conditions were statistically compared using JASP 0.14.1 (JASP Team, 2020). Preprocessed EMG and AU data were averaged for each condition and each participant and then analyzed with paired t-tests comparing the angry and happy conditions. Our predictions were tested using one-tailed tests. In addition, we analyzed the subjective valence ratings to confirm our manipulation check in the same manner. We explored the ratings of arousal and human likeness using two-tailed t-tests. Although our preliminary analyses indicated that some measures violated the normality assumption (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.05), we reported the results of t-tests in the Results because paired t-tests with more than 25 observations are robust to the violation of the normality assumption44, and our permutation tests using the mult_comp_perm_t1(data,n_perm,tail,alpha_level,mu,reports,seed_state) function45 revealed that all of the significant results remained significant (i.e. p = 0.000, 0.040, 0.016, 0.029, 0.000, and 0.000 for the corrugator supercilii EMG, the zygomatic major EMG, AU 4, AU 12, valence, and arousal, respectively). We also explored the correlations among these measures (two-tailed), controlling the FDR of all tests using the Benjamini–Hochberg method46 with the open-source multiple test correction and formatted tables software47. The results were considered significant at p < 0.05.
