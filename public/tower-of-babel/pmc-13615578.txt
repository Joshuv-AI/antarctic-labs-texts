The role and mechanisms of mesenchymal stem cells in postoperative cognitive dysfunction via immune network regulation
Authors: Xuehao Liu, Chengran Wang, Lizheng Huang, Haiqi Song, Shanshan Yu, Fanglei Han, Jinlan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) is a common neurological complication in elderly patients after surgery. Its pathophysiology is complex. Neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and immune imbalance are all involved in this process. These changes can affect postoperative recovery and reduce patients’ quality of life. As population aging continues to increase, the prevention and treatment of POCD have become important clinical issues. In recent years, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have attracted attention in neurological diseases because of their immunomodulatory and tissue healing abilities. Current studies suggest that MSCs may relieve neuroinflammation, protect neurons, and improve cognitive function by regulating the immune network. This regulation includes modulating T cell function, promoting macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, and maintaining cytokine balance. However, the mechanisms by which MSCs act in POCD have not been fully clarified. Their clinical application also faces several challenges, including transplantation efficiency, long-term safety, and individualized treatment design. Therefore, this review summarizes how MSCs may intervene in POCD through immune network regulation. This review also aims to provide a theoretical basis and possible research directions for future studies in this field.

Introduction

Postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) is a common and serious complication after surgery. It is more frequently observed in elderly patients, and its reported incidence ranges from about 10% to 54%. Patients with POCD usually experience declines in memory, attention, and executive function. These cognitive changes can lower postoperative quality of life. They may also extend hospital stay, increase mortality, and raise the risk of long-term cognitive decline [1]. The development of POCD is closely related to systemic inflammation and immune activation in the central nervous system (CNS) after surgical trauma. Among these pathological changes, neuroinflammation plays a key role in both the onset and progression of POCD [2, 3].

Surgical stress can activate microglia and astrocytes. Activated glial cells then release pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [4]. These cytokines can damage the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). This process further triggers a neuroinflammatory cascade and finally affects synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis in the hippocampus [2, 5]. The hippocampus plays an important role in learning and memory. Zheng et al. found that increased M1 polarization of microglia promoted the release of extracellular vesicles (EVsM1) with high CCR5 expression. EVsM1 can bind CCL3/4/5 to CCR5 on the neuronal surface. This interaction activates the GPCRs-Ras-MAPK signaling pathway and leads to neuronal degeneration, synaptic loss, and eventually POCD [6]. In addition, surgery and anesthesia can induce systemic inflammation. These inflammatory signals may reach the CNS through several routes, such as the vagus nerve, and then promote neuroinflammation.

MSCs are adult stem cells with multi-directional differentiation potential. Because of their low immunogenicity, strong immunomodulatory ability, and tissue repair potential, MSCs have shown broad prospects in POCD treatment [7]. Their therapeutic effects are mainly achieved through paracrine mechanisms. MSCs can secrete several active components, including growth factors, extracellular vesicles, and conditioned medium (MSC-CM). These components can regulate immune cell function and suppress excessive inflammatory responses. Among them, MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) have received increasing attention in POCD treatment. MSC-EVs are enriched with bioactive molecules, such as miRNAs, lncRNAs, proteins, lipids, and metabolites. These molecules can regulate recipient cell function through intercellular communication [8]. MiR-140-5p carried by MSC-EVs can target HDAC7. Through this mechanism, MSC-EVs regulate M1 polarization of microglia and reduce brain injury and inflammation [9]. These results indicate that MSCs may protect against POCD by acting on several targets within the immune network. They also support the development of cell-free therapeutic strategies.

Biological characteristics and immunomodulatory functions of mesenchymal stem cells

Sources and surface markers of MSCs

MSCs are adult stem cells with multipotent differentiation potential. They have shown broad application prospects in regenerative medicine. MSCs can self-renew and differentiate into several cell types. More importantly, they also have clear immunomodulatory functions [10, 11]. Because of these biological features, MSCs have been widely studied in cell therapy. Their applications now cover many areas of disease, including immune and neurological disorders, diabetes, bone and cartilage diseases, and myocardial infarction [10], and many clinical trials in these areas are underway.

MSCs are widely distributed in various tissues, including bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and amniotic membrane [12]. MSCs derived from different sources have similar characteristics but exhibit variations in their proliferative capacity, immunomodulatory properties, and differentiation potential [13].

Bone Marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs): BMSCs are the earliest studied and applied type of MSCs and are considered the “gold standard“ [14]. They possess robust proliferative capacity and multipotent differentiation potential, capable of differentiating into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes, among others [11]. However, the bone marrow harvesting procedure is invasive [13]. Studies indicate that BMSCs have advantages in regenerating bone and cartilage [11].

Adipose-derived MSCs (ADSCs): ADSCs have become a research focus due to their easy accessibility, abundance, and minimally invasive harvesting procedure [15]. They can also be used in intraoperative medical procedures. ADSCs share a similar multipotent differentiation potential with BMSCs, including the ability to differentiate into adipocytes, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes [16]. They demonstrate potent immunosuppressive capabilities [11].

Umbilical Cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs): UC-MSCs have garnered significant attention owing to their non-invasive acquisition, low immunogenicity, high proliferative capacity, and strong immunomodulatory functions [17, 18]. Compared to MSCs derived from adults, UC-MSCs of neonatal origin generally exhibit stronger proliferative capacity, lower immunogenicity, and a lower incidence of mutations [18].

MSCs from different sources may show slight differences in marker expression. BMSCs are positive for CD73, CD90, and CD105, but show no expression of CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR [17, 19]. ADSCs usually express high levels of CD73, CD90, and CD105, while they show low expression of CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR [16]. UC-MSCs have a similar pattern, with high expression of CD73, CD90, and CD105 and low expression of CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR [20] (Table 1 and 2).

Immunomodulatory effects of MSCs in disease therapy

Immunomodulatory Mechanisms of MSCs: cell contact-dependent and independent pathways

The immunomodulatory function of MSCs is one of the main reasons why they have therapeutic potential. This function is mainly achieved through two routes: cell contact-dependent mechanisms and contact-independence, or paracrine, mechanisms [39].

Cell Contact-Dependent Pathway: MSCs can directly communicate with immune cells through surface molecules. This process helps limit excessive immune activation [39]. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is one of the key molecules expressed on the surface of MSCs. PD-L1 binds to programmed death-1 (PD-1) on T cells and then suppresses T cell activation and cytokine secretion [39, 40]. Intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) serves as a co-receptor and also participates in the interaction between MSCs and immune cells [39]. These contact-dependent signals work together to promote immune tolerance and reduce inflammatory responses.

Contact-Independent Pathway (Paracrine Action): The paracrine pathway is also an important way for MSCs to regulate immune responses. MSCs release many different soluble factors into the local microenvironment. These factors can affect the proliferation, differentiation, and function of immune cells. They can also inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokine production, enhance anti-inflammatory cytokine release, and promote the generation of immunosuppressive cells, such as Tregs [7].

Exosome (sEV)-Mediated Immunomodulation: In recent years, exosomes, also known as small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), secreted by MSCs have attracted increasing attention. They are now considered an important part of MSC-mediated paracrine regulation. Exosomes are nano-sized (30–150 nm) membranous vesicles and can travel long distances in the body. They contain many bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, mRNA, and miRNA. After being taken up by recipient cells, exosomes can deliver these molecules to immune cells. In this way, they regulate immune cell function and reshape the immune microenvironment [41].

Immunomodulatory Factors Secreted by MSCs

MSCs exert anti-inflammatory and immune homeostatic effects through a variety of immunomodulatory factors. Upon exposure to inflammatory signals, MSCs can dynamically adjust their paracrine profiles and the expression of surface immunomodulatory molecules. Therefore, the action of MSCs should not be understood as a linear response of a single factor targeting a single receptor, but rather as a networked regulation in response to multiple inflammatory signals.

Transforming Growth Factor-β (TGF-β): TGF-β is an important immunosuppressive cytokine secreted by MSCs. It can inhibit T cell activation and proliferation [39]. At the same time, TGF-β promotes the differentiation and function of Tregs. Tregs are important for maintaining immune tolerance and limiting autoimmune responses [39, 42].

Interleukin-10 (IL-10): IL-10 is an important anti-inflammatory cytokine. However, in MSC-mediated immune regulation, IL-10 should not be understood solely as a factor directly secreted by MSCs. Instead, MSCs can enhance IL-10-driven anti-inflammatory responses by modulating Tregs, regulatory B cells, and M2-like macrophages or microglia, thereby reducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors and restoring immune homeostasis [43].

Indoleamine 2,3-Dioxygenase (IDO): IDO is a rate-limiting enzyme expressed by MSCs. Under resting conditions, IDO expressions in MSCs may be low; however, upon exposure to inflammatory signals such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, or IL-1β, IDO expression can be induced or enhanced. It suppresses T cell proliferation and function by degrading the essential amino acid tryptophan [44]. In addition, the accumulation of tryptophan metabolites can induce T cell apoptosis. This effect further strengthens the immunosuppressive activity of MSCs [39].

Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2): MSCs can also secrete PGE2, which is a lipid mediator with broad immunomodulatory effects [39, 42]. PGE2 can inhibit T cell proliferation, B cell activation, and NK cell cytotoxicity. It can also promote M2 macrophage polarization, thereby reducing inflammation [44].

Hepatocyte growth factor (HGF): HGF is also an important factor in the paracrine effects of MSCs. Compared with immune-suppressive molecules such as IDO and PGE2, HGF is more inclined toward tissue repair, endothelial protection, and neural recovery [45].

These immunomodulatory factors form a complex regulatory network. Through this network, MSCs can produce therapeutic effects in several immune-related conditions. These conditions include inflammatory diseases, autoimmune diseases, and graft-versus-host disease [39] and MSCs are undergoing such testing in clinical trials.

Immune-inflammatory mechanisms of postoperative cognitive dysfunction

The inflammatory response in POCD follows a distinct temporal sequence. Following surgical trauma, tissue damage immediately triggers the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Within 1–3 h postoperatively, peripheral innate immune responses are activated, and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 begin to rise. Approximately 3–6 h later, systemic inflammation can impair the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), allowing peripheral inflammatory signals to more easily affect the central nervous system. During the subsequent 6–12 h, microglia and astrocytes become activated. By 24–48 h, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, mitochondrial damage, and activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome further amplify neuroinflammation. Over the next 5–7 days, inflammation may gradually subside; however, if repair mechanisms are insufficient, it could progress into persistent neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment.

Systemic inflammatory response and blood-brain barrier disruption induced by surgical trauma

Tissue damage during surgery can release DAMPs, including high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), mitochondrial DNA, and S100 proteins, etc. These DAMPs can activate the innate immune pathway through pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), inducing a systemic inflammatory response, and manifesting as elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the peripheral nervous system [46]. This systemic inflammatory response further damages the integrity of BBB.

The BBB is mainly formed by tightly connected endothelial cells. It controls the exchange of substances between the blood and the central nervous system (CNS). When the BBB is damaged, peripheral inflammatory factors and immune cells can enter the brain more easily or influence the brain microenvironment. This process may further activate microglia and astrocytes. Clinical studies have shown that patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery have increased serum HMGB1 levels after surgery. This increase is associated with cognitive decline. These findings suggest that HMGB1 is not only an inflammatory marker of tissue injury but may also participate in the development of postoperative cognitive impairment [47].

Microglial activation and the neuroinflammatory cascade

Microglia, as the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, plays a core role in the neuroinflammatory cascade associated with POCD. Surgical trauma and systemic inflammatory responses can prompt microglia to shift from a resting state to an activated state, releasing various inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and ROS, thereby contributing to hippocampal neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and cognitive decline [48].

Aging may further increase the vulnerability of microglia to postoperative inflammatory stimulation. In elderly individuals, microglia often show a stronger and more prolonged inflammatory response. This age-related microglial priming may partly explain why older patients are more susceptible to POCD.

The balance between the pro-inflammatory and reparative phenotypes of microglia also affects the pathological process of POCD. Pro-inflammatory M1-like microglia mainly secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, participating in neurotoxic reactions; while M2-like microglia can secrete protective factors such as TGF-β, IL-10, and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), participating in inflammation resolution and neural repair [49].

Pro-inflammatory cytokines and IL-17 A/MMP-9 axis-mediated barrier disruption and cognitive impairment

Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, play a key role in the pathophysiological process of POCD. These cytokines mainly originate from activated microglia, infiltrating peripheral immune cells and damaged tissues, and can further exacerbate and maintain postoperative neuroinflammatory responses [50]. TNF-α and IL-1β can directly damage neuronal function by influencing synaptic plasticity, glutamatergic transmission and neural network homeostasis [51]. Among them, IL-1β can enhance NMDA receptor function, especially by promoting GluN2B/NR2B-related signaling to enhance NMDA receptor-mediated excitotoxicity, thereby increasing the risk of neuronal damage [52]. IL-6, as an important mediator of systemic inflammation, is closely related to BBB dysfunction, the spread of peripheral inflammation to the central nervous system and postoperative cognitive decline [53]. However, the role of IL-6 may be context-dependent. Although MSCs can secrete IL-6, the overall effect of MSCs is usually anti-inflammatory because transplanted cells are limited in number and survival time, and their therapeutic effect mainly depends on broader paracrine immune regulation.

In addition, the IL-17 A/MMP-9 axis may further aggravate BBB damage. IL-17 A can induce the expression of matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9), and MMP-9 can degrade BBB-related extracellular matrix and tight junction structures, increase BBB permeability, and make peripheral inflammatory signals more likely to enter the central nervous system [54].

Oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury and sustained neuroimmune activation

During the progression of POCD, postoperative inflammation is closely linked to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Surgical trauma, anesthetic exposure, and systemic inflammation can increase ROS production. Excessive ROS may impair mitochondrial function and disturb synaptic transmission and neuronal network stability, especially in the hippocampus, which is highly sensitive to energy metabolic stress [55].

Mitochondrial injury can further amplify neuroinflammation. Damaged mitochondria may release mitochondrial DNA, cardiolipin, and other mitochondria-derived DAMPs. These signals can activate innate immune pathways and promote inflammasome-related responses. In a mouse model of POCD induced by hepatectomy, MSC treatment reduced IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and oxidative stress markers in brain tissue. It also increased BDNF expression and improved cognitive function [56].

Inflammasome activation and pyroptosis-mediated inflammatory amplification

Under the combined effects of DAMP release, increased ROS production, and mitochondrial injury, the NLRP3 inflammasome can be activated. The NLRP3 inflammasome is an important platform through which the innate immune system senses danger signals. Once activated, it recruits and activates caspase-1, thereby promoting the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18 and converting early injury signals into stronger inflammatory effector responses [57]. Previous studies have suggested that NLRP3 inflammasome activation and NLRP3-mediated IL-1β release are closely associated with learning and memory impairment in POCD, indicating that this pathway may represent a key node in maintaining postoperative neuroinflammation [58].

Caspase-1 can also cleave gasdermin D (GSDMD) and induce pyroptosis, a pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death. Pyroptotic cells release intracellular contents and new DAMPs, which can further activate surrounding glial cells and immune cells and form a sustained inflammatory amplification loop. In an aged mouse model of sevoflurane-induced cognitive impairment, hippocampal microglial pyroptosis was associated with activation of the ROS-NLRP3 pathway. Inhibition of pyroptosis or ROS reduced microglial pyroptosis and improved cognitive function [59] (Figs. 1 and 2).

Experimental evidence of MSCs regulating the immune network to ameliorate POCD

Effects of MSCs on modulating peripheral immune cells

MSCs exert immunomodulatory effects by regulating the balance of peripheral immune cells, particularly the ratio of Treg to Th17 cells. Previous studies have shown that MSCs can promote the generation of Treg cells and inhibit the differentiation of Th17 cells, thereby reducing IL-17 A-related inflammatory responses and restoring immune homeostasis [60]. MSC-derived extracellular vesicles can also regulate T cell proliferation, activation and differentiation by delivering active molecules such as miRNA, suppressing pro-inflammatory Th1/Th17 responses and promoting Treg generation [61].

MSCs metabolize tryptophan into kynurenine through the secretion of IDO [62]. Kynurenine is a profound immunometabolite that can activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR). The activation of AHR is considered a critical molecular switch that promotes the generation of Treg and inhibits Th17 cells [62]. Th17 and Treg cells play antagonistic roles in immune balance, with Treg cells capable of suppressing the proliferation of Th17 cells and the production of IL-17 A [63]. It can therefore be predicted that MSCs may bias T cell differentiation towards Treg via the IDO-kynurenine-AHR axis, thereby reducing IL-17 A production. One study indicated that MSCs suppress Th17 cell responses within lymph nodes via L-amino-acid oxidase. Furthermore, the CD73-IFNγ signaling axis is crucial for MSC-mediated inhibition of inflammatory macrophages; CD73-deficient MSCs were unresponsive to IFNγ-mediated “licensing”, suggesting that the CD73-IFNγ-IDO1 signaling axis is key for MSC-mediated suppression of M1 macrophages [64].

Mechanisms by which MSCs Suppress central microglia and peripheral macrophages

The mechanism by which MSCs inhibit the M1-type polarization (pro-inflammatory phenotype) of central microglia is a crucial pathway for improving POCD. Microglia play a dual role in CNS diseases, and their M1/M2 polarization is critical for disease progression [65]. M1-like pro-inflammatory microglia mainly release pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and ROS, thereby causing neuroallergy and neuronal damage; conversely, M2-like anti-inflammatory and reparative microglia can provide protective factors such as IL-10, TGF-β, and IGF-1participating in neuroregression, tissue repair and protection [66].

Furthermore, human amniotic membrane-derived MSCs (hAMSCs), after pre-activation with IFN-γ, promoted the polarization of microglia towards the M2-type (anti-inflammatory phenotype) and increased IL-10 production by upregulating PDL-1 expression and immunomodulatory miRNAs [67]. M2-type microglia possess anti-inflammatory and tissue healing functions [68].

In drug-induced liver injury models, following MSC or MSC-derived exosome intervention, a decrease in the activation markers of the cGAS-STING pathway (p-STING, p-TBK1, p-IRF3) and a reduction in IFN-β and ISG expression were detected in peripheral macrophages [69]. Concurrently, the expression of M2 macrophage markers was upregulated, and stress granule formation was observed [70]. It can therefore be predicted that MSCs may induce M2 macrophages to form stress granules, which in turn inhibit the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby reducing the inflammatory response [69]. MSCs can also regulate macrophage cytokine production by activating the Akt/mTORC1 pathway0 [71]. In summary, these studies indicate that MSCs suppress neuroinflammation and ameliorate the pathological progression of POCD by regulating the phenotypic transition and related signaling pathways of microglia and macrophages.

Mechanisms by which MSCs suppress natural killer (NK) cells

MSCs can suppress the proliferation and cytotoxicity of NK cells [72, 73]. The effect is mainly related to cytokines and soluble molecules released by MSCs, including PGE2, TGF-β, and IDO [74]. These factors can change the phenotype of NK cells and inhibit cytokine-induced NK cell proliferation. As a result, the cytotoxic activity of NK cells is reduced. For example, in COVID-19-related studies, MSCs were reported to help clear hyperactivated cytokine-secreting NK cells. This effect may contribute to the recovery of immune balance and reduce excessive inflammatory responses, such as the “cytokine storm” [75]. This regulatory effect is especially important under strong inflammatory conditions. It may help prevent NK cell-mediated injury to healthy tissues, including neurons.

Regulatory effects of MSCs on B Cells

MSCs can inhibit the proliferation of B lymphocytes and reduce their ability to produce antibodies [72]. This effect may help control autoimmune responses and chronic inflammation. MSCs may suppress B cell proliferation by secreting factors such as PGE2 and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL-2). This mechanism is particularly relevant in diseases such as multiple myeloma, because abnormal B cell activation and antibody production may contribute to disease progression [76, 77]. In advanced multiple myeloma, B cells can also release immunosuppressive cytokines, including IL-10 and TGFβ. This change may further enhance the immunosuppressive state [76].

MSCs can also regulate IDO expressions and promote the conversion of tryptophan into the kynurenine metabolic pathway [78]. Kynurenine is an endogenous ligand for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). It can activate AhR in B cells and induce B cells to shift from a pro-inflammatory effector phenotype toward a regulatory or tolerogenic phenotype [79]. This phenotypic change is usually accompanied by increased IL-10 expression, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and decreased antigen-presenting capacity [80]. The interaction between B cells and microglia may form a regulatory loop. Secretions from pro-inflammatory B cells can induce human microglia/macrophages to increase IL-12, IL-6, and TNF-α expression. At the same time, these secretions can reduce the expression of homeostatic molecules and weaken myelin phagocytic capacity. In contrast, secretions from IL-10-expressing B cells can enhance homeostatic molecule expression and phagocytic capacity in microglia/macrophages [81]. IL-10 can also promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype, which may support tissue healing and regeneration [82].

Regulatory effects of MSCs on neutrophils

Surgical stress or injury activates neutrophils, leading to the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which are primarily composed of DNA and granular protein complexes [83]. Excessive NETs not only cause tissue damage but also promote sustained inflammation and may directly or indirectly disrupt biological barriers, including the BBB [84]. Neutrophils are the primary producers of NETs, while monocytes/macrophages and microglia may be secondarily activated by NET components, and endothelial cells are closely related to BBB structure and adhesion molecule expression [85]. Therefore, MSCs regulating the activities of these immune cells, particularly by inhibiting neutrophil NETosis, may be a key strategy for alleviating POCD.

Recent studies have shown that human amniotic membrane-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hAM-MSCs) can inhibit NET formation by secreting tumor necrosis factor-stimulated gene 6 (TSG-6). TSG-6 is an important mediator for the immunomodulatory effects of MSCs [86]. Specifically, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) secreted by hAM-MSCs can regulate NET release by affecting peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) and NF-κB activity in neutrophils via EP2 and EP4 receptors [87]. This mechanism provides a clear molecular-level explanation for MSC-mediated inhibition of neutrophil NETosis.

TSG-6 has demonstrated immunomodulatory effects in various pathological conditions, including inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis and transendothelial migration, and modulating inflammatory responses in dendritic cells, macrophages, monocytes, and T cells. In a rat model of spinal cord injury, intrathecally transplanted Wharton’s jelly MSCs were able to inhibit the NLRP1 inflammasome, further underscoring the role of MSCs in inflammation regulation [88].

Challenges in clinical translation and future directions

Optimization of administration routes and dosage for MSCs

Optimizing the administration route, timing, dosage and frequency of MSCs and their derivatives is a key issue for promoting their clinical transformation in the prevention and treatment of POCD. Currently, preclinical studies mostly use intravenous injections, which are simple to operate, have low invasiveness and is suitable for repeated administration [93]. However, after intravenous administration, MSCs or MSC-EVs may be rapidly intercepted or cleared by the lungs, liver, spleen and mononuclear phagocytic system, thereby limiting the effective dose that enters the central nervous system [94]. As cell-free therapeutic carriers, MSC-EVs can enter recipient cells through membrane fusion, endocytosis or ligand-receptor interactions, and deliver active molecules such as miRNA, proteins, lipids and metabolites [95]. Their nanoscale structure, biocompatibility and potential ability to cross the blood-brain barrier give them certain advantages in the treatment of neurological diseases.

Artificial intelligence (AI) may play an important role in optimizing the administration route and dosage of MSCs. By combining multidimensional biological signals with neurofunctional indicators, AI may help build a more personalized and real-time treatment strategy. AI can analyze dynamic time-series data, including plasma inflammatory cytokine profiles, changes in immune cell subsets, microglial/astrocytic markers, and blood-brain barrier leakage indicators. It can also process neurofunctional metrics, such as electroencephalogram (EEG) spectra, functional connectivity, and event-related potentials [96, 97]. Based on these data, AI may predict the postoperative inflammatory peak, key turning points in immune responses, and changes in neurofunctional status. This information can help identify the optimal MSC administration time window, route, and dosage curve for each patient. Compared with the traditional model of “fixed dose → delayed clinical response”, this strategy may make MSC therapy more individualized and timelier [98, 99].

Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) can serve as real-time monitoring tools. They can continuously track the recovery of brain network function before and after MSC administration [100]. AI models can then adjust MSC dosage and dosing intervals according to real-time neural states captured by BCIs. In this way, treatment optimization can form a dual-layer closed-loop system that includes both immune dynamics and neural function [101]. This administration strategy is jointly guided by neural electrical activity and immune responses. Compared with adjustment methods based only on a single immune parameter or a single neural indicator, this approach may better reflect the complex pathological network of POCD.

VLong-term safety assessment and immunorejection risks

Long-term safety and possible immunorejection are still important issues in the clinical translation of MSCs and their derivatives for POCD. MSCs are usually considered to have relatively low immunogenicity. However, they are not completely immune-privileged. Their immunogenicity and therapeutic effects may be affected by several factors, including donor variability, tissue source, culture expansion, inflammatory priming, MHC compatibility, and repeated administration [102]. MSCs act through multiple mechanisms.

However, MSC heterogeneity, tissue source, and preparation procedures may change host immune responses [103]. For instance, induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) derived MSCs possess unlimited self-renewal capacity but may involve tumorigenic risks or immune activation during long-term use [103]. In addition, exosomes are a cell-free therapeutic strategy. They can reduce some risks related to live-cell transplantation. However, the long-term effects of the miRNAs carried by exosomes still need further validation [104].

Future efforts should involve large-scale animal models and clinical trials to monitor immune markers and histopathological changes, ensuring the safety and tolerability of MSC-based therapies for POCD [105]. The composition and function of exosomes change according to their parental MSCs; therefore, their therapeutic efficacy largely depends on the MSC donor, tissue source, and culture conditions. Aging can also induce fundamental changes in the MSC secretome, which has significant implications for the therapeutic application of exosomes [106].

Combined therapeutic strategies

The combined use of MSCs and anti-inflammatory drugs may provide a useful strategy for POCD management. This approach may improve therapeutic efficacy because POCD is not caused by a single pathological process, but by several interacting mechanisms [81].

AI and brain-computer interface (BCI) technologies may help integrate MSCs with other therapeutic approaches. This combination could support a more flexible multimodal intervention system. For example, MSC therapy may be combined with transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) or transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) [107]. BCI can record EEG features, including the θ/γ power ratio, network synchronization/desynchronization indices, and microstate analysis. These features can then be used as AI inputs to adjust stimulation parameters and treatment timing. This strategy is designed to strengthen the interaction between neuromodulation and MSC-mediated immunomodulation [108]. In this process, MSCs may regulate inflammatory networks, promote M2-type polarization, and reduce the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. At the same time, tACS can be applied at specific frequency bands to enhance hippocampal-prefrontal functional connectivity. This effect may help restore synaptic plasticity and cognitive function [109]. AI models may further identify the optimal time window for electrical stimulation according to BCI feedback. Therefore, this “dual neuro-immune targeting” strategy may overcome some limitations of MSC-based immunomodulation alone.

Summary

The therapeutic value of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in POCD has received broad attention. MSCs can regulate immune responses through several targets, so they may provide a useful strategy for treating neuroinflammation-related disorders. Their immunomodulatory effects are mainly reflected in suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [110, 111]. For example, MSCs reduce the infiltration and activation of M1-type macrophages. This effect further decreases the production of pro-inflammatory factors. Inflammatory models, such as the LPS (Lipopolysaccharide) induced mouse model, MSCs markedly reduce IL-1β and TNF-α levels in brain tissue.

MSCs also help form an anti-inflammatory microenvironment by increasing IL-10 and TGF-β production [112]. IL-10 is an important immunosuppressive factor. It can limit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. TGF-β is also involved in immune regulation, cell growth, and differentiation. It has anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects [113]. Under TNF-α stimulation, MSCs can increase the expression of TGF-β and IL-10, which strengthens their immunosuppressive activity [114]. In this way, MSCs reduce pro-inflammatory signals and enhance anti-inflammatory responses at the same time. This process may help relieve neuroinflammation, protect neurons, and support cognitive recovery [56]. Although MSCs show clear potential in POCD treatment, their clinical translation still faces several problems.

Cell source and heterogeneity remain important issues. MSCs can be obtained from bone marrow (BM-MSCs), adipose tissue (AD-MSCs), and umbilical cord (UC-MSCs). MSCs from different tissues may not have the same biological features or immunomodulatory ability. In addition, culture conditions, passage number, transplantation route, and dosage can all affect therapeutic outcomes [115]. These factors make study results less consistent. They also make it difficult to build a unified treatment protocol. Future studies need to improve cell preparation and administration strategies. This work may increase the reliability and repeatability of MSC-based therapy.

The timing and duration of MSC action also needs more study. It is still unclear whether the anti-inflammatory effect of MSCs is only a short-term response or a long-term remodeling of the immune microenvironment. This question is important because it directly affects clinical treatment design. Some studies suggest that MSCs may have lasting effects through paracrine mechanisms. However, researchers still need to explain how MSCs survive and maintain their function in vivo over a longer period.

Patient differences may also influence MSC efficacy. Age, disease status, and immune background can change the response to MSC treatment. For example, elderly patients may not respond to MSCs in the same way as younger patients. Therefore, future studies need to identify reliable biomarkers. These biomarkers may help select patients who are more likely to benefit from MSC therapy [116].

At the same time, single-cell sequencing and proteomics may help clarify how MSCs regulate immune networks. These methods can reveal cytokine profiles, gene expression patterns, and cell-cell interactions. They may also help explain how MSCs affect host immune responses and neural plasticity [117]. Future studies should further examine the interaction between MSCs and other immune cells, including Treg cells and macrophages. More attention should also be paid to how MSC-secreted components cooperate in neuroimmune regulation. This line of evidence may help promote the clinical translation and application of MSC-based therapies.

In summary, MSCs have promising prospects for POCD treatment. However, several barriers still need to be addressed, including cell source, standardization, duration of action, and patient variability. Future work should combine engineering strategies, biomaterials, and multi-omics analysis. These approaches may help clarify the mechanisms of MSCs and support their wider clinical application.
