Abstract
This paper studies the response of stock markets relative to the banking sector to innovation by using a panel of 75 countries from 1982 to 2021. We find that innovation increases the activity, efficiency and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector, moderated by proximity to technological frontier and institutional quality. The moderating effect of institutional quality is positive for activity and efficiency but negative for size. Moreover, the moderating effect can be nonlinear depending on specific indicators. The marginal effect of innovation on the activity is persistent over many years, but the moderating effect of institutional quality gradually fades away. Keywords: innovation; financial structure; institutional quality; proximity to technological frontier JEL Classification: E02, G10, O31

We can see that stock markets are more active and efficient than the banking sector in countries where innovation is higher, even though there is a significant variation at the low level of innovation. On the other hand, there is almost no relationship between innovation and the size of stock markets relative to the banking sector and a little variation in the relative size over innovation. Those contrasting stylized facts motivate us to study the relationship between innovation and financial system for the three dimensions of financial structure separately.

Regarding identification, we do not view a country’s financial structure as causing innovation in the first place. Stock markets do help companies grow and raise innovative capacity by financing R&D investments

To establish a causality from innovation to financial structure, we will employ a instrumental variable (IV) strategy.

Our premise is that patent activity makes innovation outputs observable and verifiable and reduces information asymmetry, thereby compelling revaluation of firms in capital markets. We view surges in patent activity as demand shocks for external finance that should raise the importance of stock markets relative to the banking sector. Framing the relationship between innovation and financial structure in this way allows us to study how the response of stock markets to innovation differs compared with the banking sector in terms of he three dimensions of financial structure. In addition, we analyze how those relationships depend on a country’s proximity to technological frontier
and institutional quality, incorporating views from the law-finance nexus literature

We use a panel of 75 advanced and emerging economies over 1982–2021 to show that innovation increases the activity, efficiency and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector and that those marginal effect are moderated by institutional quality and proximity to technological frontier. In our view,
whether the demand shocks for external finance is transmitted into stock market activity, efficiency, and size relative to the banking sector depends a country’s proximity to frontier as market signals might be stronger in more productive economies. Moreover, the transmission of the demand shocks depends
critically on institutional quality as markets may fail to process innovation signals with weak investor protection, poor enforcement, or limited disclosure
(
35
)
, whereas strong institutions enhance the ability of investors to interpret market signals and enforce contracts
(

Our main findings are as follows. First, the number of patent applications is positively associated with the activity, efficiency, and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector.
To establish causality from innovation to financial structure, we interact a country’s geographical distance to the regional innovation leader with regional patent growth and define the interaction terms as an IV, based on the idea that technology spills overs regionally, while financial structure is determined by country-specific factors

Second, the marginal effect of innovation on financial structure is amplified in countries closer to the world technological frontier, and also moderated by institutional quality indicators based on

The institutional quality indicators include regulatory quality, voice and accountability, rule of law, political stability, corruption control, and government effectiveness.
In particular, we find that the moderating effects of institutional quality are heterogeneous. While institutional quality indicators, when significant, have a positive moderating effect on the activity and efficiency of stock markets relative to the banking sector, they have, when significant, a negative moderating effect on the relative size.
This hints at the increasing size and role of the banking sector for financing innovation in high institutional economies.

Third, the moderating effect of institutional quality can be nonlinear. When significant, most institutional quality indicators show a U-shaped moderating effect.
This means that innovation can lead to higher activity and efficiency of stock markets relative to the banking sector
at low and high levels of institutional quality. Since the banking sector’s response to innovation does not vary much with institutional quality, stock markets are mostly responsible for the nonlinear moderating effect. The result of a large mediating effect of institutional quality at a low level of institutional quality is intriguing and suggests that there might be alternative financing channels not captured by the literature on law-finance nexus but are relevant for economies with low institutional quality. One example of such a channel is based on reputation and relationships explored for China in

Lastly, we employ the local project (LP) method with an IV approach, following

In addition, stronger institutional quality amplifies the marginal effect of innovation in the initial years, with the effect diminishing over longer horizons. Moreover,
we use alternative innovation proxies, financial structure indicators and instruments
to ensure that our findings are not driven by any single proxy and offer more robust and generalizable evidence on the role of innovation in influencing financial structure.

3
The panel instrumental variable approach

This section presents our main empirical results. We employ an IV approach to address several sources of endogeneity in the relationship between innovation and financial structure. This potential endogeneity mainly concerns reverse causality. Reverse causality might arise because while we hypothesize that innovation, proxied by the number of patent applications, increases the activity, efficiency, and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector, it is also plausible that the activity, efficiency, and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector promote innovation by easing access to equity capital and reducing financing constraints.

To address such concerns, we construct an instrument based on the idea of regional innovation spillover effects as in
27
and
8
, who highlight that innovation diffusion is region-specific due to geographical proximity and trade links, and
30
,
45
, and
11
, who confirm that regional characteristics significantly influence technological spillovers.

3.1
The baseline model

Let
S_{r}
denote the set of all countries in the region
r
. We separate the entire world into four regions: Africa, Asia, Europe and the Western Hemisphere. We first identify the regional innovation leader
l_{r}
in each region
r
as the country
j
with the highest number of patent applications in a chosen base year
t_{1980}
, formally defined as

l_{r}=\arg\max_{j\in S_{r}}\ \text{pat}_{j,t_{1980}}.

The regional leaders based on equation above are South Africa (Africa), Japan (Asia), Germany (Europe) and the United States (Western Hemisphere). For each country
i
in region
r
, we compute the average patent growth among all other countries in the same region except for country
i
, defined as

g_{r,t}^{-i}=\frac{1}{|S_{r}|-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S_{r},j\neq i\end{subarray}}\Delta\text{pat}_{j,t}.

We then define
d_{i,l_{r}}
as the logged geographical distance between the largest city of country
i
and the regional leader
l_{r}
. These distances are calculated using the great circle formula, based on the latitudes and longitudes of the most important cities in each country
(
24
)
. Finally, the instrument for country
i
in year
t
is constructed as

z_{i,t}=d_{i,l_{r}}\times g_{r,t}^{-i}.

This instrument captures regional innovation spillover effects that influence domestic innovation activities, while remaining plausibly exogenous to domestic financial market structures. The validity comes from the fact that the development of finance for an economy is not directly determined by regional knowledge flows but rather by country-specific factors, e.g, its legal origins
(
38
)
, while innovation can respond strongly to regional knowledge flows.
Thus, our instrument should remain relevant for domestic innovation while satisfying the exclusion restriction for financial structure. In addition to the above interaction-based IV, we incorporate regional dummy instruments indicating whether countries are from Africa, Asia, Europe or the Western Hemisphere to account for common non-time varying regional factors that affect patent applications.

We employ a 2SLS estimator to estimate the causal impact of innovation on financial structure. In the first stage, we regress current patent applications on our constructed instrument, regional dummies, control variables, and time-fixed effects. The fitted values obtained represent the exogenous variation in innovation activity. In the second stage, we regress our measures of financial structure on these fitted innovation values, excluding country fixed effects since regional fixed effects are included as instruments. This panel IV regression provides a structural interpretation that identifies the causal relationship between innovation and financial structure. The weak-instrument diagnostics reported confirms the validity and strength of our IV strategy. Furthermore, the coefficient estimates on innovation remain robust and statistically significant across specifications. This consistency suggests that our strategy effectively captures the exogenous variation, thereby supporting the conclusions drawn from our causal inferences in the empirical analysis.

Our baseline second-stage specification is given by

\ln(y_{i,t})=\beta_{1}x_{i,t}+\beta\mathbf{W}_{i,t}+\mu_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}

3.2
Proximity to technological frontier

Following
1
, we construct an indicator called proximity to frontier (PTF) to identify whether the effect of innovation depends on how far a country is from the world technological frontier.

To construct PTF, we take the ratio of a country’s TFP to the highest observed TFP in the dataset in any given year such that

\text{PTF}_{i,t}=100\times\frac{TFP_{i,t}}{\max_{j\in\mathcal{C}}TFP_{j,t}}

where
\mathcal{C}
denotes the set of all countries in the dataset. PTF takes values in the range
(0,100]
with 100 indicating that the country is at the world technological frontier. This measure allows us to quantify how closely a country follows the most productive economy over time.

We consider a regression interacting PTF with the instrumented number of patent applications. The second-stage equation is given by

\ln(y_{i,t})=\omega_{1}x_{i,t}+\omega_{2}\text{PTF}_{i,t}+\omega_{3}x_{i,t}\times\text{PTF}_{i,t}+\omega\mathbf{W}_{i,t}+\mu_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}.

3.3
Institutional quality channel

The preceding analysis demonstrates that the effect of innovation on the activity, efficiency and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector are larger as a country approaches the world technological frontier. However, PTF alone may not be sufficient to fully understand how innovation shapes the three dimensions of financial structure. Even countries at similar levels of innovation capability can exhibit different outcomes if institutional quality differs. Institutions influence how effectively innovation is translated into market activity. Strong institutions promote legal certainty, regulatory quality, and transparency, all of which are crucial for fostering investor confidence and enabling financial markets to respond to innovative activities.

Therefore, this section extends our empirical framework to examine whether institutional quality moderates the marginal effect of innovation on financial structure. We analyze six key institutional indicators: regulatory quality, voice and accountability, corruption control, rule of law, political stability, and government effectiveness based on
29
. Our second-stage empirical specification remains consistent with the approach used in the previous section such that

where
q_{i,t}
represents one of the institutional quality measures for country
i
in year
t
.

3.4
Nonlinear mediation effect of institutional quality

The linear interaction
x_{i,t}\times{q}_{i,t}
assumes that the marginal effect of innovation on financial structure varies linearly with institutional quality.
To test for a possible nonlinear relationship, we augment the interaction with a quadratic term in institutional quality and its interaction with innovation (e.g.,
x_{i,t}\times{q}_{i,t}^{2}
) following
42
. To capture the nonlinear effects of institutional quality on the relationship between the number of patent applications and financial structure, we estimate

\ln(y_{i,t})=\pi_{1}{x}_{i,t}+\pi_{2}({x}_{i,t}\times{q}_{i,t})+\pi_{3}({x}_{i,t}\times{q}_{i,t}^{2})+\pi_{4}{q}_{i,t}+\pi_{5}{q}_{i,t}^{2}+\pi\mathbf{W}_{i,t}+\mu_{t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}.

The marginal effect of innovation is

g({q})\equiv\frac{\partial\ln(y_{i,t})}{\partial{x}_{i,t}}=\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}{q_{it}}+\pi_{3}{q_{it}}^{2}.

If
\pi_{3}=0
, this slope varies linearly with institutional quality. If
\pi_{3}\neq 0
, the slope changes with institutional quality:
\pi_{3}>0
can produce a U-shape in
g({q})
, while
\pi_{3}<0
can produce an inverted-U. However, to confirm a presence of U (or inverted-U), we need: (i)
\pi_{3}
statistically different from zero; (ii) the endpoint slopes
g^{\prime}(L)
and
g^{\prime}(U)
are significant and have opposite signs:
g^{\prime}(L)<0<g^{\prime}(U)
for U shape and
g^{\prime}(U)<0<g^{\prime}(L)
for inverted-U shape; and (iii) the turning point
{q}^{\star}=-\frac{\pi_{2}}{2\pi_{3}}
lies within
(L,U)
, where
L\equiv\min({q}_{i,t})
and
U\equiv\max({q}_{i,t})
are taken over the estimation sample (i.e., the observations used in the regression).
The empirical results presented in Table
8
-
9
indicate that institutional quality can have a nonlinear effect on the relationship between innovation and financial structure.

For the activity dimension,
Table

shows that
innovation’s interaction with regulatory quality exhibits an inverted U-shape (Column 1), whereas its interactions with corruption control, rule of law, and government effectiveness are U-shaped (Columns 3, 4, and 6).
For the size dimension, Table

shows that voice and accountability shows an inverted U-shaped interaction with innovation (Column 2).
For the efficiency dimension, Table
9
shows that all indicators except voice and accountability exhibit U-shaped interactions with innovation (Columns 1 and 3-6).

4
Local projection with the instrumental variable

While the previous analysis establishes the marginal effect of innovation on financial structure, it remains unclear how quickly or persistently these effects unfold over time. Stock markets and banks may respond to innovation with delays due to adjustment costs, institutional frictions, or the time required for new technologies to diffuse. Static models capture only the contemporaneous average impact, potentially overlooking important dynamic adjustment paths. In this section, we focus on the dynamic responses of the activity dimension, as it is most widely used for capturing financial structure in related fields.

4.1
The dynamic effects of innovation

We implement a Local Projection (LP) framework following
28
, extended with the same instruments to address the endogeneity concerns. Unlike vector autoregressions (VARs), the LP method does not require imposing strong assumptions on the joint dynamics of the system and is suitable for estimating impulse response functions in panel data with heterogeneous units. We estimate the specification for each horizon
h=0,1,\ldots,H
given by

\ln(y_{i,t+h})=\rho^{h}x_{i,t}+\varphi^{h}\mathbf{W}_{it}+\mu_{t}^{h}+\varepsilon_{i,t+h}^{h}

where
y_{i,t+h}
denotes the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector in the country
i
at time
t+h
,
{x}_{i,t}
is the instrumented value of innovation (e.g. the number of patent applications) and regional dummies at time
t
, and
\mathbf{W}_{it}
represents a vector of control variables. Time fixed effects
\mu_{t}^{h}
control for the common shocks. The coefficient of interest,
\rho^{h}
, captures the period-specific response of financial structure
h
periods after an innovation shock at time
t
. We estimate the specification using White’s heteroskedasticity-robust standard error
(
54
)
.

Figure
5
displays impulse response functions tracing the dynamic effects of innovation shocks on the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector over a 10-year horizon. The left panel shows the ratio of stock value traded to bank credit, while the right panel shows the ratio of stock value traded to private credit from all financial institutions. We can see that innovation shocks result in a gradual and persistent increase in the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector. The effect begins modestly in the early years, becomes more pronounced from around the year 4 onward, and reaches approximately 0.7-0.8 percentage points by year 10, depending on the credit definition used. The upward trend indicates that innovation progressively strengthens the role of stock markets compared to banks or financial institutions. The findings suggest that innovation exerts not only a significant average effect but also a continually accumulating influence on the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector over time.

4.2
The dynamic effects of institutional quality

While earlier sections establish that innovation increases the activity, efficiency and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector on average, and that institutional quality conditions those relationships, it remains unclear how these effects unfold over time. Financial markets may not adjust instantaneously to innovation shocks, especially when institutional environments shape the speed and magnitude of the responses. For example, a respect for rule of law or effective regulation could either accelerate or delay the process by which innovation influences the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector. Understanding these dynamic channels is crucial for policymakers seeking to promote innovation-driven stock market development in diverse institutional contexts.

To capture these dynamics, we estimate the LP-IV model with interaction terms given by

\ln(y_{i,t+h})=\rho_{1}^{h}\,x_{i,t}+\rho_{2}^{h}\,q_{i,t}+\rho_{3}^{h}\,\left(x_{i,t}\times q_{i,t}\right)+\varphi^{h}\,\mathbf{W}_{i,t}+\mu_{t}^{h}+\varepsilon_{i,t+h}^{h}.

Figure
6
presents the dynamic responses of the interaction between innovation and institutional quality on the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector across six institutional indicators. For corruption control and political stability, the initial impact of innovation is strongest, reaching levels above 1.2 percentage points. Conclusion

This paper revisits the relationship between innovation and financial structure from a new perspective. We conceptualize innovation as a catalyst that gradually shapes financial structure of an economy. Drawing on a broad panel of 75 countries over 40 years, we provide empirical evidence that innovation increases the activity, efficiency, and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector. By employing instrumental variables, local projections, as well as various nonlinear specifications, we address endogeneity concerns and explore the heterogeneous and dynamic nature of those relationships.

Our results suggest that while innovation leads to more active, more efficient and larger stock markets relative to the banking sector, the banking sector actively finances innovation, for example, by using patents as collateral for loans, increasing banks’ assets relative to stock market capitalization, when institutional quality is high.
Furthermore, we show that the moderating effect of institutional quality on the relationship between innovation and the activity and efficiency of stock market relative to the banking sector can be nonlinear, strengthening the effect for high institutional quality.

Our local projection estimates further indicate that the influence of innovation on the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector is persistent over time. The dynamic effects tend to intensify around the fourth year after an innovation shock. Furthermore, stronger institutional environments enhance the initial marginal effect of innovation on the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector. However, this influence is not permanent. Over time, the positive effects diminish, with several institutional indicators showing responses that taper off and lose statistical significance by roughly the eighth to tenth year. These findings suggest that while sound institutions play a critical role in helping innovation impact the activity of stock markets relative to the banking sector in the short to medium term, their capacity to sustain this impact may weaken over longer horizons.

A.3
Alternative instruments

By employing alternative measures for the geographic distance between countries, we construct three additional instruments based on the following methods. First, we use the distance between capitals of countries instead of the distances between the largest cities. The other two measures are weighted calculations. To quantify the weighted distances between countries, we follow the methodology of
24
. Let
d_{k,\ell}
denote the distance between agglomeration
k
in country
i
and agglomeration
\ell
in country
j
. The population-weighted average distance between countries
i
and
j
is computed as

d_{i,j}=\left(\sum_{k\in i}\frac{\text{pop}_{k}}{\text{pop}_{i}}\sum_{\ell\in j}\frac{\text{pop}_{\ell}}{\text{pop}_{j}}\,d_{k,\ell}^{\theta}\right)^{1/\theta}

where
\text{pop}_{k}
and
\text{pop}_{\ell}
denote the populations of agglomerations
k
and
\ell
, respectively, and

determines how sensitive trade flows are to distance.

For the weighted measure in terms of population, we
produce a simple arithmetic mean of bilateral distances weighted by population shares by setting
\theta=1
. By contrast, we produce a harmonic mean of bilateral distances by setting

. The harmonic mean assigns greater weight to shorter distances and aligns with the negative coefficients typically observed for distance in gravity models of bilateral trade. Consequently, using
