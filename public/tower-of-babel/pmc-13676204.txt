Effect of animation-based edutainment compared with pamphlet-based education on hand hygiene and HAI prevention among surgical patients: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Raziyeh Ghafouri, Zahra Azizi, Zeinab Asghari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundHealthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are among the most common and serious complications in healthcare settings. Engaging patients in infection prevention through effective educational interventions is a key strategy for reducing the risk of HAIs. This study aimed to compare the effects of animation-based edutainment and pamphlet-based education on hand hygiene practices and HAI prevention among surgical patients.MethodsA quasi-experimental study was conducted with 170 surgical patients aged 18–65 years who met the inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria were refusal to continue the study or inability to access the animated content on the smartphone. To prevent contamination between groups, the Pamphlet group was sampled first, followed by the animation group. Educational content delivered in two formats: pamphlets and animation.ResultsThe paired t-test indicated that participants’ handwashing frequency before and after the intervention differed significantly in both groups (P<0.001). Additionally, the independent t-test showed that there was no significant difference in handwashing frequency between the groups before the intervention (t(167)=−0.14, P=0.99), whereas a significant difference was observed after the intervention (t(168)= −3.76, P<0.001), with greater improvement in the animation group. Also, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests demonstrated statistically significant improvements in several awareness behaviours related to HAIs within both groups (P<0.05), with greater improvement in the animation group.ConclusionEducational interventions using pamphlets and animation effectively improve hand hygiene practices and awareness of infection prevention among surgical patients. Animation-based education may reduce the risk of HAIs in surgical settings, potentially by enhancing patients’ motivation, engagement, and willingness to learn.Keywords: Healthcare-associated infections, Infection prevention, Edutainment-based education, Hand hygiene

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are among the most frequent complications of healthcare delivery and remain a major global patient safety challenge across all healthcare settings, from primary care facilities to hospitals [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. HAIs are defined as infections that are neither present nor incubating at the time of healthcare provision but develop at least 48 h after admission or healthcare exposure [3]. These infections adversely affect the quality of care [1,4], increase healthcare costs [1,5], prolong patients’ length of stay (LOS) [6,7], and contribute substantially to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare resource use [4]. Globally, HAIs impose a considerable burden on healthcare systems, with an estimated 2.6 million cases and over 91,000 deaths annually in the European Union and European Economic Area, and approximately 1.7 million cases and 99,000 deaths each year in the United States [1]. Furthermore, the overall HAI prevalence has been estimated up to 15.5%, reaching 34.1% among critically ill patients [2], with an even greater burden reported in developing countries [2,8]. As increasing evidence highlights the importance of the healthcare environment in infection prevention and control (IPC), the implementation of evidence-based IPC strategies has become essential for reducing HAIs, improving patient outcomes, lowering healthcare costs, and decreasing unnecessary antibiotic use, given that most HAIs are considered preventable through effective policies and practices [2,5,[8], [9], [10]].

Simple yet effective measures – such as proper hand hygiene and the use of personal protective equipment – can prevent a large proportion of HAIs [9]. Recommended practices include hand hygiene [8,11], environmental disinfection, safe injection and medication procedures, use of personal protective equipment, minimizing potential exposures, proper reprocessing of reusable medical devices, transmission-based precautions, removal of temporary medical devices where feasible, and occupational measures such as vaccination and sick leave for healthcare personnel [8]. Hand hygiene is central to IPC programmes [1], and the World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that contaminated hands are a major route of HAI transmission [11]. Adequate hand hygiene significantly reduces infection risk, whereas poor hand hygiene in overcrowded or understaffed healthcare settings is associated with increased HAI rates [9]. Pathogens are transmitted directly to patients’ skin or surroundings via patients’ and caregivers’ hands, or via healthcare workers’ hands, with or without gloves [12,13].

While staff education is essential [14,15], the role of patients and family caregivers in direct-care activities is often overlooked [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. The Joanna Briggs Institute recommends patient empowerment to reduce HAIs by enhancing patients’ hand hygiene knowledge and promoting participation in hand hygiene practices [13]. For example, Loveday et al. reported that after 48 h of hospital stay in acute care units, 39% of patients' hands were contaminated with at least one pathogen, including Clostridioides difficile, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus, and Gram-negative organisms [12]. Furthermore, patients are often unaware of HAIs and catheter-associated infections [17], and limited research exists regarding empowerment strategies for patients and family caregivers within IPC recommendations [13,18].

Patient empowerment is a relatively novel concept that has expanded into patient safety [15,18]. It is a core component of the WHO’s multi-faceted hand hygiene strategy [18] and supports care planning with infection prevention at the centre of the patient journey from admission onwards [4]. Empowering patients in safety initiatives requires educating them about potential risks and actionable measures to reduce those risks [15,19,20]. Given the widespread availability of mobile phones and internet access, mobile technology has emerged as a practical tool for education [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]], particularly for patient empowerment [19,22,25]. Mobile-based interventions allow self-directed, engaging, and accessible learning [15,20,21,26]. Despite its potential, challenges remain and further research is needed to optimize mobile-based patient education [15,19,22]. Considering the importance of patient empowerment in preventing HAIs and the applicability of mobile technology in education, the aim of the present study was to compare the effects of animation-based edutainment and pamphlet-based education on hand hygiene practices and HAI prevention among surgical patients.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a quasi-experimental study to compare the effects of animation-based edutainment and pamphlet-based education on hand hygiene practices and HAI prevention among surgical patients. The study was carried out in a tertiary-care hospital, with participants recruited from surgical wards. A single-blind design was applied, ensuring that the data collector and the analyst were unaware of group allocation.

Participants

Data were collected from early May through October 2025. During this period, out of 2100 hospital visitors, 500 individuals met the study inclusion criteria, of whom 200 were randomly selected. A total of 200 patients aged 18–65 years were initially assessed for eligibility for this study. Inclusion criteria included possession of a smartphone, literacy (ability to read and write), and willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria were refusal to continue the study or inability to access the animated content on the smartphone. Eligible participants were patients classified as requiring level I nursing care, who were ambulatory and independent in self-care. Patients who lacked the ability to ambulate or perform self-care at baseline, or who lost these abilities during the study, were excluded. Ultimately, 170 participants participated in this study (Figure 1).

Research process.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on the study by Ghafouri et al. [19], and sample length was calculated with α = 0.05 (type I error), β = 0.8 (type II error), observed effect size = (μ1−μ2) σ = 0.5, and the use of the subsequent formula

Based on this calculation, the minimum required sample size was 63 participants per group. By calculating a 40% dropout rate, the sample size was set at 100 people in each group. Initially, 100 hospitalized patients were randomly assigned to each group. The groups were comparable in age, sex hospitalization ward, LOS, length of hospitalization, and history of previous hospitalization.

Sampling

Participants were recruited using stratified random sampling with the Study Randomizer [Software Application], (2017) Available at: https://www.studyrandomizer.com. To prevent contamination between groups, the Pamphlet group was sampled first, followed by the animation group. Study parameters, including the number of hospital attendees, groups, participants per group, data collection method, and wards, were registered in the software. Participants were then randomly selected sequentially as they entered the wards. Eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria and provided written informed consent were enrolled in the study.

Educational intervention

The educational content was developed through expert panel consultation, including 10 specialists in infection control and surgical nursing. Core topics included the importance of hand hygiene, critical handwashing situations, prevention of respiratory infections, and identification of risk factors and early signs of infection. The content was delivered in two formats: pamphlet and animated video. The first group received the educational material via pamphlets. The second group received the educational material via animated video. Education emphasized patient empowerment for self-care and active participation in infection prevention. The educational content was identical in both groups and focused on the importance of hand hygiene and the appropriate moments for handwashing in healthcare settings. In the Pamphlet group, the material was presented in a written format accompanied by illustrative images, whereas in the animation group, the same content was delivered through a 5-min animated video. The educational intervention aimed to enhance patients’ knowledge and promote their active participation in infection prevention through proper hand hygiene practices.

Data collection

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee prior to data collection. Participants were informed about the study objectives and assured of confidentiality. After random selection, demographic information and written informed consent were obtained. Data were collected at two time points: pre-intervention (before education) and postintervention (one week after education). The educational intervention was delivered on the first day of hospitalization, immediately after completion of the pretest.

Primary outcomes included frequency of handwashing, critical handwashing situations, recognition of early signs of respiratory, urinary, and wound infections, cigarette consumption in the hospital. Data were collected using researcher-designed checklists. To maintain blinding, the data collector was unaware of group assignments, and the analyst was blinded to the groups (Figure 1).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to check the normality of the continuous variables. The frequency of handwashing before and after the intervention was compared using a paired-samples t-test. For comparisons for handwashing situations, recognition of early signs of respiratory and wound infections, and cigarette smoking during hospitalization (pre-interventionvs postintervention), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. The frequency of handwashing was compared between the two groups using an independent-samples t-test, while all other variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 170 participants were included in the study, of whom 93 were women (54.7%) and 77 were men (45.3%). The overall mean age of participants was 40.39 years (standard deviation [SD] = 11.99). The animation group comprised 89 participants with a mean age of 40.96 years (SD = 11.58), while the Pamphlet group included 81 participants with a mean age of 39.78 years (SD = 12.46). Independent-samples t-tests showed no statistically significant differences between the intervention groups in age (t (168) = −0.64, P = 0.52) or length of hospitalization (t (168) = −0.89, P = 0.37). Similarly, χ2-square analyses revealed no statistically significant differences between the two groups with respect to sex (χ2 [1] = 0.05, P = 0.83), hospital ward (χ2 [3] = 1.42, P = 0.70), or history of previous hospitalization (χ2 [1] = 0.29, P = 0.59). These findings indicate that the intervention groups were comparable at baseline in terms of demographic and clinical characteristics. In contrast, statistically significant between-group differences were observed, when the Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to compare the outcomes between the Pamphlet and animation groups after the educational intervention. Before the intervention, there were no statistically significant differences between the groups for any of the variables (all P > 0.05). Discussion

The present study demonstrated that education can significantly improve patients’ knowledge and behaviours in preventing HAIs. These findings align with existing evidence indicating that patient empowerment facilitates care planning and management, with infection prevention integrated into the patient journey from admission [4]. Empowering patients requires educating them about personal safety risks and the measures they can take to mitigate these risks [17]. Most healthcare facilities were found to have moderate to high levels of hand hygiene implementation, with facility budgets and national income levels being key determinants [1]. Availability of resources, leadership, and organizational support are critical elements for improving care quality and ensuring safe access to healthcare for all patients [1,15]. In regions with a higher proportion of low- and middle-income countries, data are limited; however, available evidence indicates higher rates of HAIs and more severe health and economic outcomes [1].

HAI risk is growing due to factors like an ageing population, complex treatments, and antibiotic resistance [4]. HAIs affect various organ systems, including urinary tract and bloodstream infections [1]. IPC is crucial for combating antimicrobial resistance, a rising public health threat that diminishes antimicrobial effectiveness, increases disease burden and mortality, and strains healthcare finances [9]. Many patients are unaware of the risks of catheter-related infections [16]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends hand hygiene, appropriate personal protective equipment, single-use devices, and environmental disinfection [8]. Direct contact, primarily via hands, is the main transmission route in clinical care settings [8,10,18]. Hand hygiene can prevent up to 50% of HAIs, including MRSA [8]. Patient empowerment, where individuals gain control over health decisions, has included reminding healthcare workers about hand hygiene, though patients may be hesitant to critique staff [13].

Smoking may adversely influence tissue repair and increase susceptibility to postoperative infection through several biological pathways, including impaired tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery, altered inflammatory responses, and compromised host defense. These effects may interfere with normal wound-healing processes and increase the likelihood of postoperative complications. Recent evidence supports an association between smoking and surgical-site infection; a 2024 meta-analysis involving 41,944 patients undergoing fixation of closed ankle and calcaneal fractures found that smokers had a higher risk of surgical-site infection than non-smokers (odds ratio [OR] = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.32–1.97), with an even greater risk for deep infections (OR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.42–3.09) [27]. However, evidence regarding whether preoperative smoking cessation directly improves healing remains less conclusive, particularly in oral surgery, highlighting the need for further high-quality studies [28].

The findings of the present study also indicate that participants may have had limited awareness of the potential role of blood and body-fluid contamination in creating an environment conducive to the persistence and transmission of micro-organisms. This finding highlights the importance of educating hospitalized patients about environmental contamination, particularly contamination involving blood and other bodily fluids. Increasing patients’ awareness of these potential sources of microbial transmission may encourage greater attention to hospital environmental hygiene and prompt reporting of visible contamination to healthcare personnel.

In the present study, no statistically significant between-group difference was observed in the recognition of wound and urinary tract infection symptoms. One possible explanation is that the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic increased public exposure to information about respiratory infections, their symptoms, and preventive measures, potentially resulting in greater familiarity with respiratory infection–related health information. In contrast, patients may have had less exposure to information about the early signs of non-respiratory infections. Recognizing symptoms such as redness, swelling, pain, or discharge from a wound, as well as urinary symptoms such as dysuria, increased frequency, urgency, hematuria, fever, and chills, is important for timely identification and management of infection. Therefore, future educational interventions should place greater emphasis on early warning signs of wound and urinary tract infections and encourage hospitalized patients to promptly report new or worsening symptoms to healthcare professionals. Overall, these findings underscore the need for targeted patient education on the early recognition and prompt reporting of respiratory infection symptoms, particularly wound and urinary tract infections, during hospitalization.

Consequently, providing patients and their family caregivers with engaging animation-based educational interventions may enhance awareness, foster active participation in infection prevention, and improve hand hygiene and other behaviours that reduce the risk of HAIs [15,19,20,22,26]. The COVID-19 pandemic has further raised public awareness regarding hygiene practices and infection prevention, creating a favourable environment to foster a culture of infection control and active patient engagement [29]. The present study investigated the impact of an educational intervention on hand hygiene practices, infection recognition, and smoking behaviour among hospitalized patients. The findings indicate that the animation-based educational intervention had a significant positive effect on several aspects of patient behaviour. Specifically, the frequency of handwashing before meals, floor disinfection, smoking in the hospital, and taking showers during hospitalization showed significant improvements in the intervention group compared with the Pamphlet group. These results suggest that targeted educational programmes may enhance patient engagement in infection prevention behaviours. The results of this study highlight that animation-based education may serve as an effective intervention to empower patients in HAI prevention, reinforcing patient safety and enhancing care quality. Such interventions may occupy a key role in comprehensive IPC programmes and the broader effort to reduce infection risks in healthcare settings.

Modern educational methods like entertainment, games, and organization, especially when delivered via mobile phones, enhance learning effectiveness [19,20,22,24]. This study, along with similar research [[10], [11], [12],18,19,26], recommends their use in patient education. Future studies should employ longer follow-up periods to confirm efficacy and consider individualizing education to accommodate patient preferences, particularly when using mobile phones or computer programs. Furthermore, the integration of technology should address potential barriers such as digital literacy and access to devices. Exploring diverse multi-media formats and interactive elements can cater to varied learning styles and maintain patient engagement [19,24,26,30]. It is also vital to assess the cost-effectiveness of these interventions, ensuring they provide optimal value in improving for empowering patients outcomes and promoting long-term adherence to treatment plans. Collaboration between healthcare professionals, educators, and technology developers is crucial to create patient educational programmes and empowering patients that are both effective and user-friendly [5,19,20].

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the follow-up period was limited to one week, which may not have been sufficient to evaluate the long-term sustainability of the educational intervention and its effects on adherence to infection prevention behaviours. Longer follow-up periods are warranted to determine whether the observed improvements are maintained over time. Second, only patients who were ambulatory and capable of independent self-care were included in the study. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to patients with mobility impairments or those requiring assistance with self-care. These patients often face greater challenges in adhering to infection prevention measures and hygiene protocols, and the effectiveness of educational interventions may differ in this population. It is possible that the observed effectiveness of the intervention was influenced, in part, by the participants’ ability to perform self-care independently. Future studies should include patients with mobility limitations and self-care dependency to better evaluate the effectiveness of educational interventions in these populations and to identify their specific educational and supportive needs for infection prevention.

Also, the findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, hand hygiene practices and HAI prevention behaviours were assessed using a self-reported checklist rather than objective measures. Self-reported data are inherently susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias, and participants may have over-reported their adherence to recommended preventive behaviours, particularly after receiving the educational intervention. Consequently, the magnitude of the observed improvements may have been overestimated. Future studies should combine self-reported measures with objective assessment methods, such as direct observation or electronic monitoring, to provide a more comprehensive and accurate evaluation of behavioural changes.

Both animation-based edutainment and pamphlet-based education were associated with significant improvements in hand hygiene frequency and several awareness and preventive behaviours related to HAIs among surgical patients. Although both educational approaches appeared to enhance patients’ participation in infection prevention, animation-based edutainment showed greater improvement than pamphlet-based education for selected outcomes, including hand hygiene frequency and some preventive behaviours. These findings suggest that animation-based edutainment may provide additional benefits for certain aspects of patient education; however, its overall advantage over conventional educational materials should be interpreted with caution.

Given its engaging format and ease of implementation, animation-based edutainment may be considered a complementary approach to routine patient education in surgical wards. Further studies with larger samples, multi-centre designs, and longer follow-up are needed to determine whether the observed improvements are sustained over time and whether they translate into meaningful clinical outcomes, such as reductions in HAIs.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Its single-centre design restricts generalizability. Reliance on self-reported measures introduces potential bias. The short follow-up limits assessment of long-term impact on knowledge retention, behaviour change, and HAI incidence. Participants’ varying digital literacy and motivation may have influenced engagement and outcomes. Finally, partial blinding could have introduced performance bias. Future research should employ multi-centre designs, longer follow-up, objective outcomes, and strategies to mitigate bias to better evaluate mobile-based gamified education for HAI prevention.

Another limitation was the use of a self-reported checklist for assessing hand hygiene practices and HAI prevention behaviours, which may have introduced recall and social desirability biases and potentially influenced the reported outcomes. Future studies using objective assessment methods, such as direct observation, are recommended to provide a more accurate evaluation of behavioural changes.

Another limitation of this study was the relatively short follow-up period, which limited the evaluation of the long-term sustainability of the observed improvements in hand hygiene practices and HAI prevention behaviours. Although significant behavioural changes were observed shortly after the intervention, it remains unclear whether these improvements would be maintained over time. Future studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to assess the durability of educational effects and determine whether sustained behavioural changes translate into meaningful clinical outcomes.

Another limitation of this study was that only patients who were ambulatory and capable of independent self-care were included. Consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to patients with mobility impairments or those who require assistance with daily care. Future studies are recommended to include patients with mobility limitations to better understand the challenges they face in adhering to infection prevention measures and hygiene protocols, and to identify their specific supportive and educational needs.

Another limitation of the present study was the relatively limited effect of the intervention on participants’ recognition of wound and urinary tract infection symptoms. Future studies should investigate targeted and repeated educational interventions focusing specifically on the early recognition of non-respiratory infection signs and symptoms, particularly wound and urinary tract infections, to determine more effective strategies for improving patients’ infection-related awareness.
