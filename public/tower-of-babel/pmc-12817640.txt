Knowledge, attitudes and practices levels of clinical nurses towards humanistic caring: the first nationwide multi-centre cross-sectional survey in China
Authors: Hongwei Chang, Danli Xiong, Deying Hu, Ruiying Yu, Yanjin Liu, Dongmei Dai, Zheng Lin, Yongyi Chen, Guanghong Zhao, Rong Tan, Hongyan Wu, Yilan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHumanistic caring in China has developed rapidly in recent years; however, patients’ satisfaction with nurses’ humanistic caring remains moderate. To date, there has been no nationwide, multi-centre, large-sample survey exploring nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding humanistic caring, which limits the identification of gaps and evidence-based strategies for improvement.AimThis study aimed to assess the levels of knowledge, attitude, and practices regarding humanistic caring among nurses in tertiary hospitals across China, and to analyze the associated influencing factors, thereby providing a data-driven foundation and practical evidence for developing targeted improvement strategies.MethodA multi-centre cross-sectional survey was conducted from July to August 2022 in 25 provinces (cities and districts) across China. Nurses meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were recruited through convenience sampling in tertiary hospitals. Data were collected using a self-developed demographic questionnaire and The Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices of Clinical Nursing Staff on Humanistic Care Questionnaire developed by Li Yuqin. The original scale demonstrated excellent reliability and validity (content validity index = 0.980; Cronbach’s α = 0.982; test–retest reliability = 0.983), and the Cronbach’s α in the present study was 0.991 (P < 0.001). Questionnaires were distributed and collected via the online platform Questionnaire Star. A total of 14,305 valid responses were obtained, yielding an effective response rate of 99.72%. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 for descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultAmong the 14,305 participants from 25 provinces, 95.48% were female, with a mean age of 32.37 ± 6.62 years; 85.53% held at least a bachelor’s degree. Notably, 42.17% had never received humanistic caring training. The mean scores for knowledge, attitude, and practice were 80.24%, 83.62%, and 87.95%, respectively, with practice scoring highest and knowledge lowest. Significant differences in KAP scores were observed across gender, region, department, professional title, work experience, and job-related factors (all P < 0.05). Nurses who expressed love for nursing, engaged in self-caring, received family support, and were satisfied with their work and salary or had undergone humanistic caring training achieved significantly higher scores in all three dimensions (P < 0.001). Stepwise multiple regression identified job and salary satisfaction, enthusiasm for nursing, self-caring awareness, and prior training as major positive predictors of knowledge and attitude, whereas knowledge and attitude significantly predicted humanistic caring practices. Correlation analysis confirmed strong positive associations among the three dimensions (r = 0.636–0.827, P < 0.001), aligning with the KAP theoretical model.ConclusionChinese clinical nurses demonstrate moderate levels of knowledge and attitudes toward humanistic caring but relatively strong humanistic caring behaviors. Training programs should be strengthened, with content emphasizing the concept, significance, and core competencies of humanistic caring. Hospitals are encouraged to integrate humanistic values into organizational management and promote a supportive humanistic caring culture to enhance nurses’ humanistic practice.

Introduction

With the shift from the traditional biomedical model to the biopsychosocial model, healthcare has increasingly emphasized patients’ psychological experiences, social support, and overall well-being rather than disease treatment alone [1]. Within this paradigm, patient-centered care has become a fundamental value in modern nursing. Humanistic caring reflects this philosophy by prioritizing respect, understanding, and empathy to deliver holistic care that addresses patients’ physical, psychological, and social needs [2]. Evidence indicates that implementing humanistic nursing care enhances patients’ satisfaction, improves recovery, and enriches their care experience [3]. Strengthening nurses’ humanistic caring competence is also essential for improving nurse–patient relationships, rebuilding trust, and promoting high-quality nursing services [4, 5]. Since 2010, Chinese health authorities have promoted the integration of humanistic caring into nursing practice through national policies and pilot programs [1]. Despite these efforts, patient satisfaction with humanistic caring remains only moderate [6], nurse–patient conflicts persist, and incidents of workplace violence against nurses continue to occur [7]. Moreover, excessive workloads and psychological stress often hinder nurses’ ability to consistently provide caring that aligns with patients’ humanistic expectations [5]. As a result, the effective implementation of humanistic caring in clinical settings remains a substantial challenge.

The Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) theory, a foundational model in behavioral science, proposes that inadequate knowledge and weak beliefs are key contributors to suboptimal behavior [8]. Applying this framework to humanistic nursing offers a systematic approach to analyzing deficiencies across cognitive, attitudinal, and behavioral dimensions. Guided by the KAP model, this study explores nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices concerning humanistic caring in tertiary hospitals across China, aiming to identify influencing factors and provide evidence for targeted education, optimized training programs, and the promotion of patient-centered nursing practice.

Background

The concept of humanistic caring was first introduced in the 1950s by American nursing theorist Madeleine Leininger and later refined by Dr. Jean Watson [9, 10]. Watson regarded humanistic caring as the essence and core of nursing, grounded in universal humanistic and altruistic values. Its ultimate goal is to help individuals achieve holistic well-being across physical, psychological, and socio-cultural dimensions [11]. She further identified ten fundamental components of humanistic caring: (1) cultivating humanistic–altruistic values; (2) instilling faith and hope; (3) fostering sensitivity to self and others; (4) establishing helping–trusting relationships; (5) facilitating the expression of emotions; (6) employing systematic problem-solving in clinical decision-making; (7) promoting interpersonal teaching and learning; (8) creating a supportive and spiritually nurturing environment; (9) meeting human needs; and (10) acknowledging the phenomenological and spiritual aspects of human existence. These ten elements have since become widely adopted guiding principles in clinical nursing worldwide [12].

Humanistic caring plays a pivotal role in clinical nursing, management, and education. In clinical practice, Watson emphasized that nurses with strong humanistic caring competence can enhance patients’ adherence to treatment and satisfaction with nursing care [2]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, humanistic nursing was vital in shelter hospitals, effectively mitigating patients’ psychological distress [13]. Similarly, integrating humanistic caring into stroke management has been shown to reduce psychological disorders and improve rehabilitation outcomes [14]. Physiological and rehabilitative support also constitute major components of humanistic caring for stroke patients and their families [15]. In intensive care units, the use of humanistic caring checklists to guide nurse–patient communication has significantly improved patient satisfaction [16]. The comprehensive implementation of humanistic caring in ophthalmic wards has been associated with improved communication skills, motivation, and care quality among nurses [17]. Moreover, incorporating humanistic caring into nurse training programs enhances professional identity, strengthens problem-solving skills, and promotes more harmonious nurse–patient relationships [5]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that humanistic caring not only facilitates disease recovery but also enhances the overall patient experience and fosters the sustainable development of nurse–patient relationships.

From a managerial perspective, Watson proposed that humanistic caring attitudes are transmitted through professional culture. The absence of humanistic values in nursing management can leave nurses feeling undervalued, resulting in dissatisfaction and decreased job fulfillment [18]. Gunawan (2022) further emphasized that humanistic caring is a core component of nursing leadership and its practice can improve the well-being of both nurses and managers [19]. In China, a nationwide survey by Liao et al. confirmed that the humanistic caring competence of nursing managers and clinical nurses is a crucial determinant of caring behaviors in clinical settings [20].

Humanistic caring education also plays a critical role in cultivating humanistic caring competence among nursing students. As a foundational professional attribute, humanistic caring requires intentional development through structured curricula and experiential learning. Wang used structural equation modeling to examine the formation of nursing students’ humanistic caring competence, providing a theoretical basis for pedagogical reform [21]. Similarly, Waterman et al. found that students’ humanistic caring competence improved progressively throughout targeted training programs [22]. Implementing structured humanistic caring curricula in nursing schools has further been shown to enhance students’ empathy, caring behaviors, and professional values [23].

In China, although the development of humanistic caring nursing is relatively recent, it has gained more momentum. Since 2010, the Chinese government has been promoting the development of care in hospital treatment and care through the enactment of policies [1]. In 2021 The Humanistic Care Committee in China, named Chinese Association for Life Care, published the “Expert Consensus on the Code of Practice for Humanistic Care Practice in Hospital Nursing” [24]. In 2022, they released the “001-2022 Management Norms for Humanistic Care in Ward Nursing” [25]. All of the above have greatly contributed to the development of humanistic nursing in China. But despite this, the results of a multi-centre, large-sample study by Chinese scholars in 2023 still show that Patient satisfaction with humanistic nursing in secondary and tertiary hospitals in China is at a moderate level [6]. Moreover, no national multi-centre, large-sample cross-sectional survey has yet examined nurses’ humanistic caring competence, making it difficult to analyze the underlying causes of low patient satisfaction from the perspective of nurses’ professional capacity.

The Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) theory offers a well-established framework for understanding behavioral formation and its determinants. It posits that knowledge acquisition, attitude development, and behavioral practice are interrelated and progressive processes, where accurate knowledge promotes positive beliefs that in turn drive appropriate behaviors [26]. In this study, Watson’s Theory of Human Caring provides the theoretical underpinning for the knowledge dimension within the KAP model. A thorough understanding of Watson’s ten Carative Factors-such as humanistic-altruistic values, faith-hope, empathy, helping-trusting relationships, and the creation of a healing environment-helps nurses internalize caring beliefs and translate them into practice. The interrelationship among knowledge, attitude, and practice is illustrated in Fig. 1. Understanding the levels of knowledge and attitudes of Chinese nurses toward humanistic caring therefore offers a valuable perspective and theoretical basis for enhancing their humanistic caring competence.

This study represents the first nationwide, multi-centre cross-sectional survey on nurses’ humanistic caring competence organized by the Chinese Association for Life Care. It aims to identify existing gaps in nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to humanistic caring and to generate evidence to support national strategies for improving humanistic caring competence and informing future interventions for nursing management and education.

Methods

Design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted from July to August 2022 using convenience sampling. Tertiary hospitals in 25 provinces (cities and districts) where the Chinese Association for Life Care is located were selected as study sites. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) possession of a valid nursing license; and (2) at least one month of nursing experience, currently engaged in clinical nursing or nursing management. Exclusion criteria included: (1) nurses undergoing further or standardized training; (2) nurses on leave; and (3) those who had recently experienced major life events. The sample size was calculated using the formula for estimating a population mean: n = (u α/2 × σ / δ)², where α = 0.05, u 0.05/2 = 1.96, σ = 33.55, and δ = 1. The minimum required sample size was 4,325 [27].

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using two instruments: a General Information Questionnaire and the Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) of Clinical Nursing Staff on Humanistic Careing Questionnaire, developed by Li Yuqin in 2019 [27]. The General Information Questionnaire included demographic and work-related variables such as region, gender, age, marital status, education, department, professional title, years of experience, and hospital type. It also comprised 10 additional questions on management role, teaching duties, interpersonal relations, family support, passion for nursing, satisfaction with salary and job, experience in humanistic caring pilot wards, attention to self-caring, and participation in humanistic caring training. Q1: Whether to hold a management position? Q2: Whether to undertake teaching tasks? Q3: Whether to hold good terms with colleagues? Q4: Is your family supportive of your work? Q5: Do you love nursing? Q6: Are you satisfied with your salary? Q7: Are you satisfied with your job? Q8: Have you ever worked in a humanistic caring pilot ward? Q9: Do you pay attention to self-caring? Q10: Have you attended any humanistic caring training?

The KAP Questionnaire consisted of three dimensions: knowledge (18 items), attitudes (22 items), and practices (23 items), totaling 63 items. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale:

Knowledge: 1 (“do not know at all”) to 5 (“know very well”);

Attitudes: 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”);

Practices: 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”).

The total possible score ranged from 63 to 315, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge, more positive attitudes, and better humanistic caring practices. The original scale demonstrated strong psychometric properties (content validity index = 0.980, Cronbach’s α = 0.982, test-retest reliability = 0.983; all P < 0.001). In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.991 (P < 0.001), indicating excellent internal consistency.

Data collection

Data were collected online using Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn), a professional platform for web-based surveys. Prior to distribution, permission was obtained from nursing administrators of participating hospitals. The questionnaire link was then disseminated via internal communication channels. To ensure data quality, all items were mandatory, and only one submission was permitted per IP address. The introductory page of the questionnaire provided detailed information about the study, including its purpose, participation rights, and informed consent. Participation was entirely voluntary. After collection, two researchers independently reviewed and cross-checked the responses. Questionnaires were excluded if they contained logical inconsistencies, were completed in less than 60 s, or had missing data. A total of 14,345 questionnaires were received, of which 14,305 were valid, yielding a valid response rate of 99.72%.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval

was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. S161). Permission was also granted by the directors of nursing in participating hospitals. All participants provided informed consent and participated voluntarily.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used for categorical variables. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicated that the data did not follow a normal distribution. Consequently, continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (P25, P75), and average score rate (mean score / full score × 100%). Between-group comparisons were conducted using non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis H test). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Stepwise multiple linear regression analyses were further performed to identify significant factors influencing nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding humanistic caring.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 14,305 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis. Among the respondents, 13,658 (95.48%) were female and 647 (4.52%) were male. Participants ranged in age from 22 to 59 years, with a mean age of 32.37 ± 6.62 years. The majority of nurses (85.53%) held a bachelor’s degree or above, and 38.95% had the title of supervisor nurse or higher. Notably, more than one-third of the respondents reported that they had not received any formal training in humanistic caring. Additional sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Questionnaire scores

The mean scores for the knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions were 80.24%, 83.62%, and 87.95%, respectively. Within the knowledge domain, the lowest-scoring items were related to understanding the current development of humanistic caring (65.20%), the essence of humanistic caring (77.80%), and the basic requirements for its implementation (78.60%). In the attitude dimension, lower scores were observed for items addressing whether humanistic caring can reduce hospitalization days, whether it represents the starting point of nursing care, and whether it should be included in nursing quality assessment indicators. For practice, nurses demonstrated relatively high overall performance in implementing humanistic caring behaviors. However, comparatively lower scores were reported for forming humanistic caring teams, arranging family visits and accompaniment, and participating in humanistic caring training. Multifactor analysis

Univariate analysis was first conducted to examine differences in KAP scores across demographic variables. Significant differences were found in nurses’ knowledge, attitude, and practice scores with respect to most demographic and occupational factors (all P < 0.05). Variables such as region, gender, age, marital status, department, professional title, years of experience, and hospital type were all significantly associated with at least one of the three dimensions.

Nurses who reported higher job satisfaction, salary satisfaction, family support, and harmonious workplace relationships achieved significantly higher scores in all dimensions (P < 0.001). Similarly, those who expressed passion for nursing, paid attention to self-caring, received humanistic caring training, or worked in humanistic caring pilot wards exhibited higher levels of knowledge, positive attitudes, and better humanistic caring practices (P < 0.001). Furthermore, nurses in management or teaching roles and those with higher professional titles showed greater humanistic caring competence. Gender differences were also evident, with female nurses outperforming males across all dimensions. Regional and departmental variations were notable, suggesting that contextual and organizational factors may influence nurses’ humanistic caring capacity.

Subsequently, stepwise multiple linear regression analyses (αentry = 0.05, αremoval = 0.10) were performed using the scores of each index as dependent variables. Variables found to be significant in univariate analyses were entered as independent variables, and the scores of the knowledge and attitude dimensions were additionally included as predictors. Discussion

This study represents the first nationwide, multi-centre, large-sample cross-sectional survey conducted by the Chinese Association for Life Care to assess clinical nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding humanistic caring. The results demonstrated significant correlations among the three dimensions, aligning with the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) theoretical framework.

Among the three dimensions, practice achieved the highest score, reaching an excellent level (>85%), whereas knowledge scored lowest. This pattern indicates that while nurses perform well in humanistic caring behaviors, their theoretical understanding of humanistic caring remains insufficient. The disparity underscores the need for continuous knowledge renewal and systematic educational interventions. The gap may be attributed to the relatively late development of humanistic caring education in China, the absence of a comprehensive training framework, and a low participation rate in related programs (57.83%). Historically, nursing education has prioritized the cultivation of hard skills—technical expertise and professional knowledge—over soft competencies such as empathy, communication, and humanistic caring. Consequently, the integration of humanistic caring into nursing curricula and clinical practice has lagged behind [28]. This structural imbalance suggests that while nurses may exhibit humanistic caring behaviors in practice, the lack of a solid theoretical foundation limits their ability to internalize and sustain such behaviors. Consistent with the KAP model [29], knowledge acquisition underpins the formation of positive beliefs, while attitudinal transformation serves as the driving force for stable, enduring humanistic caring behaviors. Therefore, education on humanistic caring should not only teach nurses how to humanistic caring but also help them understand why to humanistic caring, fostering reflective, conscious, and value-driven humanistic caring practices. Within the knowledge domain, gaps were observed in nurses’ understanding of the definition, current development, and essential requirements of humanistic caring, indicating that training should move beyond technical instruction to include conceptual and value-oriented content.

With respect to attitudes, nurses’ overall scores were moderate. Item-level analysis revealed that some participants perceived humanistic caring as having limited effects on reducing hospitalization days or improving treatment outcomes, and viewed its evaluation as unnecessary. However, previous evidence consistently shows that humanistic caring contributes to patient recovery, alleviates pain [14], and enhances the overall quality of the hospital experience [30]. Such humanistic caring facilitates both physical and psychological restoration and indirectly supports shorter hospital stays. These findings suggest that short-term training or performance assessments may improve knowledge and behaviors but are insufficient to fundamentally shift attitudes. True acceptance of and commitment to humanistic caring require sustained cultivation within a supportive organizational culture.

A positive organizational climate serves as an essential external condition for sustaining humanistic caring. Studies have shown that managerial support and a humanistic caring workplace environment can enhance nurses’ psychological capital, empathy, and professional engagement, thereby strengthening humanistic caring behaviors [31, 32]. Tian and colleagues further confirmed the positive correlation between organizational climate and humanistic caring performance [33–35]. These findings echo Watson’s view that “humanistic caring is transmissible; humanistic caring and love can generate humanistic caring and love” [36]. From the perspective of the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [37], humanistic caring represents a valuable yet depletable psychological resource. Without adequate external support, nurses may experience resource exhaustion, reducing their capacity for sustained caring. Conversely, organizational support, humanistic-caring leadership, and positive collegial relationships function as replenishing resources that mitigate stress and restore emotional energy. Cultivating a humanistic-caring organizational culture is thus crucial for maintaining long-term engagement in humanistic caring. Nursing managers should embed the spirit of humanistic caring into management, education, and interpersonal interactions, while providing psychological support and career development opportunities. These measures can gradually reshape nurses’ attitudes toward humanistic caring, transforming it from an externally motivated act into an intrinsic professional habit. Moreover, the evaluation of humanistic caring should differ from assessments of technical skills—rather than overt scoring, implicit and ongoing assessments may better capture authentic humanistic caring behaviors and guide targeted improvement.

In terms of behavior, although the practice dimension yielded the highest overall score, the lowest item was “the presence of a humanistic caring team within the nursing department.” This suggests limited institutional recognition of the importance of humanistic caring and ongoing shortages in human resources. Prior studies have emphasized that under-staffing and high workload pressures constrain the effective and sustainable implementation of humanistic caring behaviors [38].

Regression analysis further revealed that both individual and occupational factors significantly influenced humanistic caring competence. Nurses who had received humanistic caring training, demonstrated stronger professional identity, paid attention to self-caring, and were satisfied with their job and salary achieved higher scores in knowledge and attitude dimensions. These results highlight the combined influence of training and intrinsic motivation on the enhancement of humanistic caring competence. Attitude scores also emerged as significant predictors of behavioral performance, supporting the KAP model pathway whereby knowledge influences behavior through attitude [8]. Additionally, years of work experience and female gender were positively associated with humanistic caring behaviors, suggesting that professional maturity and gender-related traits—such as women’s higher empathy—facilitate humanistic caring practices. These findings are consistent with those of Lai-Kun Tong et al. [38], who reported that nurses with fewer years of experience tend to focus more on technical operations and less on empathetic communication. Departmental differences were also observed: nurses in surgical and obstetric–gynecologic units scored higher on humanistic caring behaviors, whereas those in operating rooms and intensive care units scored lower, likely due to fewer opportunities for interaction with conscious patients and families. Overall, these results indicate that individual characteristics, organizational support, and workplace environment jointly shape nurses’ humanistic caring competence.

In summary, this study integrated the KAP theory with the Conservation of Resources (COR) framework to elucidate the multifaceted factors influencing nurses’ humanistic caring competence. Sustained improvement requires a multilevel approach: at the individual level, strengthening theoretical knowledge and fostering belief formation; at the organizational level, cultivating a humanistic caring culture and establishing supportive systems; and at the educational level, optimizing training programs and embedding implicit evaluation mechanisms. Collectively, these strategies can promote conscious, consistent, and enduring engagement in humanistic caring.

This study has several limitations. First, although data were collected from 25 provinces and municipalities across China, the use of online questionnaires may have introduced sampling bias and affected representativeness. To address this, duplicate IP submissions were restricted and all items were mandatory to ensure data completeness. Future studies may employ stratified sampling to obtain more representative data. Second, as a cross-sectional design, causal relationships could not be established; longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to validate these findings. Third, the exceptionally high Cronbach’s α values, while indicating excellent reliability, may also suggest item redundancy; future research should consider scale refinement to improve efficiency without compromising validity. Finally, the analysis of influencing factors was limited to regression models; subsequent studies could employ path analysis or structural equation modeling to further explore the complex interrelationships among variables.

Implications of research

This study provides empirical evidence to inform educational and managerial strategies for enhancing nurses’ competence in humanistic caring. Nursing training programs should systematically integrate humanistic caring education, emphasizing not only technical skill development but also the cultivation of conceptual understanding, empathy, ethical awareness, and reflective capacity. At the organizational level, managers should proactively foster a supportive and humanistic caring culture that strengthens the humanistic caring atmosphere between leaders and nurses as well as among colleagues. Such an environment can help nurses alleviate stress and maintain sustained humanistic caring behaviors. Guided by the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) theory, continuous training initiatives should be implemented to reinforce knowledge, consolidate beliefs, and stabilize humanistic caring behaviors. Incorporating the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, healthcare institutions should also ensure the provision of adequate psychological and social support resources to help nurses sustain consistent caring performance. Future research could adopt longitudinal or interventional designs to verify the effects of educational and organizational interventions on the long-term sustainability of humanistic caring. Moreover, advanced analytical approaches such as path analysis or structural equation modeling may be employed to further elucidate the complex interrelationships among these variables.

Conclusions

This nationwide multicenter study revealed that Chinese nurses exhibited relatively strong performance in humanistic caring behaviors but demonstrated insufficient mastery of theoretical knowledge and only moderate levels of caring attitudes. These findings indicate an imbalance in nurses’ humanistic caring competence across the three dimensions of knowledge, attitude, and practice. To address this gap, continuing education programs should incorporate structured and comprehensive humanistic caring training that integrates behavioral guidance, theoretical enrichment, and the internalization of core humanistic caring values. At the organizational level, hospitals should foster a culture of humanistic caring through supportive leadership, constructive interpersonal relationships, and institutional mechanisms that embed humanistic caring into both management and clinical practice. The establishment of multidisciplinary humanistic caring teams and the enhancement of workplace support systems could further strengthen nurses’ humanistic caring competence, ensuring its sustainable development while creating a nurturing environment that benefits both nurses and patients.
