Transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement in a patient with a history of artificial aortic arch replacement and stent implantation: a case report and strategies for a super-stiff approach

Introduction

Transfemoral (TF) access is the most commonly used approach for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).1,2 However, in some patients, femoral calcification, tortuosity, or a small femoral diameter makes it difficult to use TF access. In such cases, alternative approaches—such as subclavian, trans-carotid—are often considered. However, these alternative approaches carry higher complication rates and operational complexity.3,4 Furthermore, they are not always applicable. Therefore, a proper strategy for managing tortuous TF access is urgently needed. Options such as an extra-stiff guidewire and a long sheath5–7 may be considered. Other techniques, including the buddy wire technique,8 balloon deflection technique, and balloon-assisted tracking technique,9 could also offer potential solutions. However, in patients with a history of artificial aortic arch replacement, aortic arch stent implantation, or plugging device implantation in the aortic arch, the aorta tends to be stiff, excessively tortuous, and inelastic. Successfully performing TAVR in such cases is difficult, and no prior studies have reported on this issue.

In the present study, we report a successful TF-TAVR procedure in a patient with a history of aortic arch-descending aorta bypass surgery and stenting and anastomotic fistula plugging.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old male with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis (peak velocity 478 cm/s, mean gradient 53 mmHg) presented with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, aortic arch-descending aorta bypass surgery and stenting (16 years ago), and anastomotic fistula plugging (4 years ago).

Computed tomography angiography assessment

Multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) was performed. A Type 0 bicuspid aortic valve was detected. The annular area measured 462 mm2. Multiplanar measurements were taken every 2 mm from the left ventricular outflow tract to 17 mm above the annulus. The restricted area was supra-annulus 17 mm (399.9 mm2) (Figure 1).

Aortic root assessment. (A1) Longitudinal view of aortic root. (A2) Type 0 bicuspid valve. (B) A multiplane measurement of the aortic root.

Approach assessment

Aortic valve replacement was deemed necessary for this patient. However, due to his previous surgical history, he was not considered a candidate for surgery by the cardiac surgeon. As a result, TAVR was proposed.

The condition of the iliofemoral arteries was satisfactory; however, the state of the aortic arch and thoracic aorta presented significant challenges for a TF-TAVR (Figure 2). Alternative approaches were also considered. The anterior/posterior Circle of Willis arteries were non-patent, excluding transcarotid access due to high cerebral ischaemia risk. Prior surgery precluded transapical access. Left subclavian access was obstructed by an aortic arch stent, while right subclavian access was excluded due to tortuosity.

Approach assessment. (A and B) 3D and calcification of the approach assessment to show the super-stiff aortic arch; (C) the minimum diameter of the aortic arch in the stent; (D) fluoroscopic image of the aortic arch to show the 3D distortion.

Transfemoral TAVR was finally pursued. The SAPIEN 3 valve (26 mm) was selected for its short-frame design and steerable delivery system.

Difficulties and solutions of transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement

A 6-Fr AL2 catheter initially failed to traverse the rigid stented aortic arch. Following aortic arch balloon dilation, persistent resistance was encountered when advancing the 20-mm valvuloplasty balloon. We then planned to advance the THV system without the prosthesis.

The THV delivery system (without the SAPIEN 3 prosthesis) was successfully advanced to the thoracic descending aorta. However, crossing the aortic arch proved difficult, even after adjusting the curvature of the delivery system. After multiple attempts, the THV system finally passed through the aortic arch, though significant resistance was encountered.

At this point, the question arose: What should be done next? We proposed the following five solutions: (i) SAPIEN 3 with steerable delivery system: The SAPIEN 3 with a steerable delivery system and a short stent frame design could potentially help navigate this complex anatomy. (ii) Buddy wire technique: By placing a second guidewire alongside the first, additional support is provided, facilitating passage of the THV. The buddy wire also serves as a guide to reduce frictional resistance. (iii) Balloon deflection technique: A balloon could be placed at the region of severe angulation via a buddy wire. Slightly inflating the balloon would straighten the curved vessel, while deflating the balloon would allow safer and more effective delivery of the device. (iv) Snare assistance: Using a snare, the direction of the THV head could be adjusted. (v) Long sheath option: Inserting a large-bore sheath (65 cm in length) through the femoral artery could cover the severe angulation of the aorta (Figure 3).

Difficulty and solutions of THV delivery pass. (A1, B1, C1, and D1) S3 is difficult to pass; (A2, B2, C2, C3, and D2) it passed smoothly using different techniques.

After a discussion, a long sheath was preferred due to a high risk of prosthesis unloading. Like the THV delivery system, the long sheath was unable to cross the aortic arch. We proposed the following four solutions: (i) buddy wire technique, (ii) balloon deflection technique, (iii) snare assistance, and (iv) balloon-assisted tracking technique: a balloon is positioned partially extended beyond the sheath tip. By inflating the balloon at low pressure while the surgeon gently pushing both the sheath and the balloon together, or by pushing the sheath alone while deflating the balloon. The balloon reshapes the tip of the sheath and the aorta, facilitating smoother navigation (Figure 4).

Difficulty and solutions of long sheath pass. (A1, B1, C1, and D1) Long sheath is difficult to pass; (A2, B2, and D2) it passed smoothly using different techniques.

Ultimately, the balloon-assisted tracking technique was applied. Guided by the Safari wire, we positioned the balloon in the region of severe angulation. After the balloon slightly inflated, the surgeon gently pushing both the sheath and the balloon together. By inflating the balloon, the delivery direction of the sheath was adjusted, ultimately enabling successful passage through the region of severe angulation (see Supplementary material online, Video S1).

After assembly in the ascending aorta, the 26 mm SAPIEN 3 valve was released. However, unexpectedly, the final position was found to be above the annulus. After a detailed assessment, the prosthesis was deemed stable. We then sealed the femoral access.

Multidetector computed tomography was performed 1 month later, confirming supra-annular anchoring of the prosthesis (Figure 5).

Discussion

This is the first report of TF-TAVR in patients with prior history of artificial aortic arch replacement, stent implantation of aortic arch, and plugging device implantation in the aortic arch. The enlightenments of our case report were (i) the strategies to deal with tortuous and stiff approach, which was often the stumbling stone for TF-TAVR. (ii) For bicuspid aortic valve, the anatomy of the supra-annulus is narrow enough to play as an anchoring zone.

In this challenging case, we presented our thinking and strategies to deal with tortuous and stiff approach. The technique of balloon-assisted tracking and long sheath was applied. The technique of balloon-assisted tracking was originally applied in the field of coronary intervention. Then, some researchers reported that this technique was successfully applied to cross tortuous radial artery, Vieussens valve, and other challenging approaches.9–11 In the present study, we expanded this technique to artificial tortuous vessels with a stent. The use of a long sheath was also a useful technique. Kadoya and colleagues5 have reported the use of a long sheath on a tortuous and shaggy aorta. They successfully performed TAVR and thoracic endovascular aortic repair. The feasibility of using a long sheath for tortuous approaches was also demonstrated by Professor Tomohiro Kawaguchi6 and Yu Yang.7 In the present case, we extended the use of a long sheath to patients with previous aortic surgery and stent implantation, where pass ability was extremely compromised.

The supra-annulus structure was used as the anchoring zone in our case. The annulus size was considered the ‘gold standard’ when selecting the prosthetic valve size.12 This is based on the notion that the annulus is the tightest part of the aortic root anatomy. While this theory may be accurate for tricuspid valves, it does not hold true for bicuspid valves. In bicuspid valves, the primary restricted area is typically located at the supra-annulus.13,14 In the present case, the area 17 mm above the annulus was 399.9 mm2, which was smaller than the annulus. Some researchers have focused on the anchoring effect of the supra-annular area. Tian Yuan and colleagues13 analysed post-TAVR MDCT data and demonstrated that the maximal compression of the stent occurs on the aortic valve leaflets. Xian Bao and colleagues14 also reported that the waist sign can indicate the anchoring ability of the supra-annular zone and guide prosthesis size selection. In the present case, even though the prosthesis landed above the annulus, the stent remained stable with no paravalvular leak. The success of our case highlights the anchoring effect of the supra-annular area and calls for further studies to explore the algorithm for prosthesis selection based on the supra-annular zone.
