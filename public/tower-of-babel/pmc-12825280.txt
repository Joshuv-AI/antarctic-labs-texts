Changes of physician–patient communication skills among general practitioners after intervention of targeted SEGUE training
Authors: Jia Zou, Yu Du, Cai Qin, Nian Wu, Hao Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies have shown that physician–patient communication is crucial in healthcare. This study investigated the level of physician–patient communication among general practitioners in a Chongqing district. Targeted training was implemented and the intervention effect of the training was analyzed.MethodsOne hundred twenty-eight general practitioners were selected from 10 primary hospitals that had established relationships with our hospital using convenience sampling. A targeted training plan for physician–patient communication (PPC) was created based on the ‘set the stage,’ ‘elicit information,’ ‘give information,’ ‘understand the patient’s perspective,’ and ‘end the encounter-based communication’ (SEGUE) framework and was provided to the general practitioners for 3 months from July-September 2021. The targeted training included lectures, case studies, seminars, and interactive training via on-site teaching and online teaching modes. The PPC evaluation scale based on the SEGUE framework was used on-site to observe, score, and evaluate PPC skills for the 128 general practitioners before and after instituting the training plan.ResultsThe total SEGUE score was significantly increased by 1.0 point after training compared to that before training (95% CI: 0.90–1.10; P < 0.001). Among the five SEGUE dimensions, the increase in the ‘set the stage’ (95% CI: 0.71–0.87; P < 0.001), ‘elicit information’ (0.14; 95% CI: 0.08–0.21; P < 0.001), and ‘give information’ (95% CI: 0.03–0.12; P < 0.001) were statistically significant (P < 0.05) after training. The senior physician subgroup had the most pronounced increase in total scores (1.05; 95% CI: 0.89–1.21; P < 0.001), followed by intermediate physicians (1.04; 95% CI: 0.87–1.2; P < 0.001), and junior physicians (0.92; 95% CI: 0.73–1.12; P < 0.001). The changes in the total scores among the three subgroups were statistically significant (P < 0.001).ConclusionAfter a 3-month training program, a higher SEGUE value was to be observed on the level of clinical communication skill among general practitioners from community healthcare centers. It can be assumed that this is due to an effect of the program.Keywords: Physician–patient communication, General practitioners, Training, SEGUE scale

Introduction

Physician–patient communication (PPC) is a crucial part of healthcare and effective communication skills are highly important for physicians [1, 2]. Studies have shown that physician–patient disputes are caused by poor communication between physicians and their patients [3, 4]. Patients may complain that they did not receive sufficient information from physicians and nurses to make correct decisions or receive satisfactory treatment [3]. Better PPC is helpful in improving patient satisfaction, bringing better and more accurate care, which leads to better clinical outcomes [5, 6].

The clinical communication skills of general practitioners in China do not match the needs of patients [7–9]. Community healthcare centers that serve as grass-roots medical institutions provide medical services to the community residents with common and chronic diseases, community emergency rescue, and rehabilitation medical services. The patients are mostly low income and elderly with chronic diseases in community healthcare centers [7–9]. However, community healthcare centers lack advanced equipment and professional physicians [7–9]. Many community general practitioners are originally pharmacists or are recent medical school graduates who lack knowledge, skills, and experiences for seeing patients [7–9]. Therefore, the trust in community healthcare centers by residents is low and communication difficulties exist between the patients and physicians [7–9]. In addition, general practitioners need to understand and treat common diseases. However, the lack of systematic training in the community health care centers due to financial constraints poses a challenge to professional capacity and communication skills for the general practitioners [7–9].

Chinese physicians need PPC training at community health care centers, where formal training organized by health commissions at or above the municipal level is infrequent [7–10]. The SEGUE framework is based on ‘set the stage,’ ‘elicit information,’ ‘give information,’ ‘understand the patient’s perspective,’ and ‘end the encounter-based communication.’ As reported, the SEGUE framework is effective in a PPC skill education program [11]. Poor PPC frequently occurs in community healthcare centers [7–9]. PPC skills are better among physicians in tertiary hospitals than primary hospitals because tertiary hospitals provide more training opportunities in communication skills and have better medical services and management than exist in primary hospitals [12]. It is necessary to establish a good basis for PPC among general practitioners and community residents in community healthcare centers to provide effective medical services and responsible health management for residents. Currently there are few studies that have investigated targeted training intervention based on the SEGUE framework on general practitioners in community healthcare centers of China and worldwide.

Therefore, this study investigated the current state of PPC skills among general practitioners in community healthcare centers in Chongqing, China and the intervention effect of the PPC training plan on general practitioners based on the SEGUE framework.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study design was approved by the Medical and Health Research Ethics Committee of the Fifth People’s Hospital of Chongqing, China (No. 2021-4−20; approved on 20 April 2021). All clinicians involved in the study provided informed written consent. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Information that could directly identify an individual, including the name, ID number, identifiable clinical images, were removed when collecting and using patient data. Access to the original data was minimized, while ensuring the purpose of the research. Encryption and permission control were used to protect data from unauthorized access. Participants received education and training in information security and privacy protection, then signed research and data confidentiality agreements.

Ten community healthcare centers in the area that had established a medical association relationship with our hospital were selected by convenience sampling due to the COVID-2019 control. Encounter sampling was adopted. Each center appointed a nurse who did not know about this study to collect the sample at the door of the medical center. When the sampling reached one quarter of the total number of people in the medical center, the sampling was ended. The 7–20 outpatient general practitioners from each service center were selected as the research participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) full-time employment at a community healthcare center; (2) passed the general practitioner qualification examination and worked as a general practitioner for > 1 year; (3) no prior knowledge of the assessment process and this study; and (4) no prior PPC training > 8 h in length systematically organized by health administrative departments, medical institutions, or professional societies. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) employed part-time in a community healthcare center; and (2) chose not to participate in the study during the study period.

Methods

Implementation methods

First, field investigations were performed to collect general information on the respondents, including the community healthcare centers where physicians worked, as well as gender and titles. The SEGUE framework [1] was used to determine participant PPC skills and the proficiency levels in communicating with patients before the intervention. Next, PPC training was provided to the participants. Finally, a second test was performed within 3 months after training ended using the SEGUE framework.

PPC training

Professional experts were invited from our hospital and other hospitals who had > 10 years of working experience, long-term engagement in clinical work, and who had accumulated rich experience in PPC to train the physicians. The training experts held senior titles. The targeted training course was set based on the book, Doctor–Patient Communication [13], and the SEGUE framework, which was also developed according to the current PPC skills and SEGUE scores of the participants. The training period was from July-September 2021 due to the COVID-2019 control in China in 2021 and to support COVID-2019 management. Each participating practitioner took part in the training for at least 2 h per week (≥ 24 h within 3 months) based on the book, Doctor–Patient Communication [13], (theoretical foundation) and the SEGUE framework (practical operation) to balance prevention and control demands and the effectiveness of training duration.

The participants were divided into 4 groups with 32 people in each group. Each group received training as a team. The main consideration was the actual need that “small-scale gatherings are safer” under the prevention and control of COVID-19 2019. There were four teachers. The first teacher was responsible for teaching the ‘set the stage’ in the SEGUE scale. The second teacher was responsible for teaching the ‘elicit information’ stage. The third teacher was responsible for teaching the ‘give information’ stage. The fourth teacher was responsible for teaching ‘understand the patient’s perspective’ and ‘end the encounter-based communication’ stage. This division of labor was designed to ensure that each SEGUE dimension was explained in depth by a dedicated person to avoid content overlap or omission.

The training methods combined on-site lectures and online teaching modes. The onsite-lectures were the main training methods and the on-line teaching modes were presented in the Wechat groups for daily discussions and material sharing and DingTalk platforms for centralized teaching. The training content delivery involved a combination of lectures, case studies, seminars, and interactive training. The training topics were issues related to PPC. By integrating multiple forms to cover the theoretical understanding-case application-interactive practice demands of PPC skills, especially focusing on PPC-related topics, interactive exercises were provided to help general practitioners quickly connect with clinical scenarios.

Training contents

Module 1 focused on basic theory of the PPC, as follows: (1) analysis of the importance and status quo of PPC; (2) psychological characteristics and needs of patients; (3) communication models and skills; and (4) medical humanities and empathy training. Module 2 focused on core communication skills, as follows: (1) verbal communication, including (a clear explanation of the condition and treatment plan, while avoiding technical terms, open questioning and listening skills, and expression of empathy; (2) non-verbal communication, including body language (eye contact, gestures, and posture) and emotion management (coping with anger/sadness in patients); and (3) special scenarios, including giving bad news, such as a cancer diagnosis, responding to questions or complaints from patients/family members, and dealing with cultural differences or language barriers. Module 3 focused on laws, regulations, and ethics, as follows (1) relevant provisions of the Regulations on the Prevention and Handling of Medical Disputes; (2) standardized communication and signing of informed consent; and (3) privacy protection and information confidentiality. Module 4 focused on practice and case analysis, as follows (1) discussion of classic physician-patient conflict cases (success/failure cases); (2) role-playing simulation (interaction among physicians, patients, and family members); and (3) feedback analysis of real patient satisfaction.

Evaluation scale

The Chinese version of the SEGUE scale was used to evaluate the PPC skills of participants who had undergone standardized training [14, 15]. The SEGUE scale is comprised of five dimensions (the ‘set the stage,’ ‘elicit information,’ ‘give information,’ ‘understand the patient’s perspective,’ and ‘end the encounter-based communication’) for a total of 25 evaluation indicators. Each indicator on the scale is assigned 1 point and the total score is 25 points. The higher the score for each dimension and the total score, the better the performance in that dimension and the overall performance. Related research showed that the reliability and validity of the SEGUE scale were all < 0.7 with respect to correlation coefficients. The correlation coefficients between the PPC content score, PPC skill score, and total score were all > 0.8. The scale is easy to use, can be used to quickly establish a physician–patient evaluation data system, and subjective factors can be effectively avoided [13, 14]. The Cronbach coefficient of the total scale was 0.86 and the Cronbach coefficients of each dimension were all > 0.75. The SEQGUE scale was retested in the 3rd week after the completion of the study. The retest reliability of the total scale was 0.83 and the retest reliability of each dimension was > 0.80.

Quality control

The assessment personnel included five members of the author group, all of whom received unified training in SEGUE scale use. and learned about unified assessment standards. To ensure the consistency of scoring results, each assessment evaluator pre-scored 10 medical workers randomly chosen within the health centers before the formal assessment. The study was initiated when the scoring results were consistent within the assessment personnels. The uniform evaluation criteria are the Chinese version of the SEGUE scale [1, 14, 15]. The assessment personnel evaluated the general practitioners when on duty. The assessors entered the consultation room directly with the patients and the patient families. There was no communication with the general practitioner who was evaluated throughout the process. The general practitioner did not know an evaluation was taking place.

Senior physicians were defined as those with more than 15 years’ experiences; intermediate physicians were defined as those with more than 5 years’ experiences; and junior physicians were defined as those with less than 5 years’ experiences.

Statistical methods

EpiData 3.1 software was used to establish a database. The database was captured by two researchers and verified in real time. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.2).

The scores were statistically described using means and standard deviations (). This study examined the total score, the 5 dimensions, and the 25 items separately. In addition, a subgroup analysis was performed based on participant job titles. Specifically, a t-test was used to statistically examine changes in the scores of senior, intermediate, and junior physicians before and after training. Statistical significance was set at a P < 0.05.

Discussion

In this study the communication skills of 128 general practitioners from 10 community healthcare centers were evaluated before and after systematic training using the SEGUE scale. These general practitioners had significantly improved scores in the ‘set the stage,’ elicit information,’ and ‘give information’ stages of the SEGUE scale after the targeted systematic training.

The current study focused on the general practitioners from community healthcare centers in Chongqing (southwest China). General practitioners shoulder the responsibility of looking after resident health and are the medical personnel who have the closest contact with most residents [7–9]. The average total SEGUE score of general practitioners in Chongqing was 18.73 before training, which is as low as reported in other studies [11, 12]. Targeted training could help improve the evaluation scores among the medical workers [10–12, 16, 17]. As reported, community skills training will help reduce attacks from patients against physicians and promote the patient-physician-relationship in the long term, especially in primary care, secondary care, and community hospital settings [10–12]. The current study showed a total improved SEGUE score of 19.73 after targeted training based on the SEGUE framework among general practitioners in the Chongqing district, which demonstrated the effectiveness of the targeted training plan and is consistent with previous studies [10–12, 16, 17]. The common finding of the current and previous studies [10–12, 16, 17] is that the PPC ability of physicians is being studied and investigated. But some studies focused on physicians from tertiary hospitals, while some studies focused on the physicians affiliated with secondary and tertiary hospitals [12]. Houwen et al. studied general practitioners [16], as in our study, but Houwen et al. used an intervention mapping framework to create the training plan, which differed from the current study using the SEGUE framework. The results of the current study may be helpful for those who focus on general practitioners in community healthcare centers.

Zeng et al. compared the total SEGUE scores between residents in the dimensions of age, gender, training years, education background, and marital status, but did not compare the scores of each dimension of the SEGUE framework [11]. As reported by Guo and Wang [12], physicians had significantly higher self-evaluated scores than patient evaluation scores in every dimension of the SEGUE framework. Moreover, physicians had much higher SEGUE scores in the eastern region of China than the central and western regions for both the physician self-evaluation and patient evaluation. However, this study did not compare the physician SEGUE scores post-training to the scores before training [12]. The current study showed that the scores that increased after training were significantly higher than before training ‘set the stage,’ ‘elicit information,’ and ‘give information,’ but minimal improvements were achieved in ‘understand the patient’s perspective’ and ‘end the encounter-based communication.’ Bai et al. reported that the total SEGUE and dimension scores increased significantly immediately after and 1 month after the program than before training [18]. The reason for these findings may be that community general practitioners are very familiar with the patients or even be neighbors or friends of the patients. The improvements of general practitioners in “‘understand the patient’s perspective” were not significant. Moreover, Chinese community healthcare centers have a large patient load every day and general practitioners have limited time to see each patient. Therefore, even after training, general practitioners still do not have enough time to communicate with patients and could not achieve better consultation conclusions in the ‘end the encounter-based communication’ dimension. The underlying factors for the nominal improvements in ‘understand the patient’s perspective’ and ‘end the encounter-based communication’ section still need to be investigated.

Communication skills training should be conducted more than 4 times each year, as suggested by previous studies [19]. Multiple training methods used in the current study, such as simulation teaching, clinical practice teaching, video viewing and analysis, standardized patient role-playing, Balint group, and teacher-standardized patient models, should be utilized and can effectively improve communication skills [17–22]. The current study also took different training methods combined with cumulative training time requirements of at least 24 h in 3 months. In this way, communication skills were promoted in an effective way. The results demonstrated the effectiveness of the targeted training in the 3-month training but the long-term effects need validation in the future.

As reported in another study, the senior physicians with lengthy work experience have more knowledge and more experience communicating with patients and achieve better practice outcomes and scores than junior physicians [22]. The current study showed that the communication skills of junior, intermediate, and senior physicians were not satisfactory before training and significantly improved through training. Despite the physician titles, the communication skills and scores improved after communication training, which is consistent with previous studies [11, 12, 18]. The communication skills of senior general practitioners improved relatively significantly. We speculate that the core reason for this finding is the combination of the structured framework of the SEGUE and practical experience. Senior general practitioners already possess rich clinical communication intuition and the SEGUE framework provides general practitioners with tools to transform experience into structured skills, enabling general practitioners to more accurately fill the normative shortcomings in their communication. These results confirmed that the targeted training plan is effective. Long duration effectiveness is expected after lengthy training in the future.

The current training plan based on SEGUE framework can be adopted by other community healthcare centers to enhance the PPC relationship and physician professional abilities. The training module can be set based on the following five dimensions of the SEGUE scale: (1) carry out phased training design; (2) adopt diversified teaching methods; (3) establish an assessment and evaluation system; and (4) adopt a combined online and offline teaching model.

This study was limited because the training focused on general practitioners in the Chongqing district with a small sample size. The regional limitation limited promotion at home and abroad. Second, the training duration in this study was short due to the COVID-2019 control. Therefore, although the total score improved in 3 months, the improvement in the long term needs further validation. Third, this study lacked a control group to compare the effects of the training plan. Fourth, the cost-effectiveness was not considered in the current study. In the future, a multicenter, large-sample size study with long-term follow up is required. Comparisons to a control group and the cost-effectiveness are also required. Moreover, this study did not quantitatively assess the communication skills accumulated from daily practice of general practitioners, such as through the Self-rating Scale of Communication Experience or Patient Satisfaction Score for auxiliary verification. Future research can further incorporate this indicator to more comprehensively analyze the synergistic effect of training and practice on communication ability.

Conclusion

The targeted training plan based on the SEGUE framework can effectively improve the SEGUE scores and PPC skills among general practitioners in community healthcare centers in a short-term intervention. Future studies can further explore the long-term impact of different training methods on PPC skills and combine more factors for in-depth analysis and evaluation, as follows: adding the control group; adding the cost-effectiveness analysis; adding the analysis on training resources; cultural factors; healthcare infrastructure; and adding large sample size from multi-centers.
