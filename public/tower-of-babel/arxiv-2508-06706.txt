Probabilistic Circuits for Knowledge Graph Completion with Reduced Rule Sets
Jaikrishna Manojkumar Patil, Nathaniel Lee, Al Mehdi Saadat Chowdhury, YooJung Choi, Paulo Shakarian

Abstract
Rule-based methods for knowledge graph completion provide explainable results but often require a significantly large number of rules to achieve competitive performance. This can hinder explainability due to overwhelmingly large rule sets. We discover rule contexts (meaningful subsets of rules that work together) from training data and use learned probability distribution (i.e. probabilistic circuits) over these rule contexts to more rapidly achieve performance of the full rule set. Our approach achieves a 70-96% reduction in number of rules used while outperforming baseline by up to 31 × \times when using equivalent minimal number of rules and preserves 91% of peak baseline performance even when comparing our minimal rule sets against baseline’s full rule sets. We show that our framework is grounded in well-known semantics of probabilistic logic, does not require independence assumptions, and that our tractable inference procedure provides both approximate lower bounds and exact probability of a given query. The efficacy of our method is validated by empirical studies on 8 standard benchmark datasets where we show competitive performance by using only a fraction of the rules required by AnyBURL’s standard inference method, the current state-of-the-art for rule-based knowledge graph completion. This work may have further implications for general probabilistic reasoning over learned sets of rules.



Introduction

Knowledge graph completion is a canonical problem in AI
(
Hayes 1977
;
Reiter 1980
)
which aims at inferring missing facts from incomplete data. It has applications spanning from search engines and recommendation systems to question answering systems and healthcare research. Recent developments
(
Xiong et al. 2024
;
Trouillon et al. 2016
;
Meilicke et al. 2020
)
have achieved remarkable improvements in this field. There are mainly two lines of work: high-performing but black box embedding-based approaches and explainable rule-based approaches. This research focuses on the latter. Rule-based methods like AnyBURL and AMIE learn first-order Horn rules from the knowledge graphs, providing explainable predictions through logical inference chains.

However, achieving this competitive performance with rule-based approaches leads to a fundamental challenge that undermines their core advantage. Figure
1
shows the number of rules learned by AnyBURL rule learner in 10 seconds for different datasets. The number of rules learned by AnyBURL to achieve competitive performance with embedding models across standard benchmark datasets is 1000 seconds, which will generate significantly more number of rules than shown here. This explosion of rules raises some major problems. First, the explainability that is the major motivating factor for rule-based approaches is weakened mainly because if humans need to comprehend thousands of logical inferences to understand a single prediction, then the system has failed to provide human understandable explanations that they were designed for. Furthermore, the computational complexity increases dramatically for reasoning tasks beyond simple deduction, such as abduction or even consistency checking mainly because the system then needs to explore a larger search space. The notion of requiring tens of thousands of rules for reasoning contradicts the principle of Occam’s Razor, which basically favors simple explanations if possible. This raises a critical question:
Can we achieve similar performance for knowledge graph completion tasks using rule-based approaches using a much smaller set of rules?

.

To achieve this, we propose a framework that uses probabilistic circuits to learn a distribution over rule contexts representing meaningful combinations of learned non-ground rules without requiring independence assumptions between rules. Using this, the framework then generates rule sets consisting of singleton rules or rule sets using greedy walks.
For inference, we use either the lower bound of true probability or the exact probability to assign probabilities to predictions based on the rule sets that entailed them. We also provide a suite of formal results characterizing the computation of the query probability and showing the adherence to formal probabilistic semantics.
Note that our framework is agnostic to the underlying rule sources and is not limited to AnyBURL, making it applicable to rules learned from any rule learning system.
We evaluated our framework on eight standard benchmark datasets.
We saw that the proposed framework achieved significant efficiency improvement. In these eight datasets, it reduces the size of the rule set by 70 - 96% (an average 12-fold reduction) and delivers around 31-fold performance improvement over the confidence-based baseline. Additionally, this dramatic rule reduction still attains an average of 91% of the highest baseline performance (i.e., baseline with maximum number of rules). This confirms that an improvement in explainability does not come at the cost of reasoning accuracy.
While our primary focus in this paper is leveraging PCs for knowledge graph completion task, our framework has general applicability to any other domains of symbolic AI that struggle with scalability of rule-based approaches.

Technical Preliminaries

Syntax.
We assume a logic programming framework in which we have a set of ground

atoms
\mathcal{A}
. We define negation and conjunction
\neg,\wedge
in the standard manner based on world semantics (where literals are atoms and negations of atoms). We allow rules of the form
h\leftarrow b
where the body
b
is a conjunction of literals and head
h
is a literal. When the body is omitted (e.g.,
h\leftarrow
) we assume that
h
is always true and call it fact. A program
\Pi
is a set of rules. For semantics, a world is a subset of atoms, and a world satisfies an atom if the atom is in the world, satisfies a negation of an atom if the atom is not contained in the world, and satisfies a conjunction of literals if each literal is satisfied by the world. A world satisfies rule
h\leftarrow b
if when it satisfies
b
, it also satisfies
h
. A world satisfies a program if it satisfies all elements of that program. We will say program
\Pi
entails some query
q
(which can be a program, rule, fact, conjunction, or literal) if for all worlds where
w\models\Pi
, we have
w\models q
(written
\Pi\models q
).

Rule Context.
We envision a set of rules learned from data mining algorithms (e.g., in the experiments of this paper we use AnyBURL as we focus on knowledge graph completion). However, we note that a set of learned rules may not be necessarily consistent. However, as the rules are learned from data, we know that there are certain subsets of rules that are valid in a given
context
. Further, such a context would need to be determined in a way such that rules associated with a given context are consistent. For example, we use a combination of training data and abduction to determine rules in a specific context (described in Experimental Setup).

We will introduce some special atomic propositions (denoted with Greek letters) and rules to allow for the addition of context. First, each rule
r
is of the form
h_{r}\leftarrow b_{r}\wedge\mu_{r}
where
\mu_{r}
is an atomic proposition that is used to activate rule
r
. We assume a finite number of contexts (set
C
). If rule
r
is associated with context
c\in C
, then we include rule
\mu_{r}\leftarrow\nu_{c}
in our program. If it is not included in context
c
then we include rule
\neg\mu_{r}\leftarrow\nu_{c}
. This ensures that rules are only associated with a given context. Additionally, for each context
c
and
c^{\prime}\neq c
we add rules
\neg\nu_{c^{\prime}}\leftarrow\nu_{c}
to the program. These additional logic constructs are essentially designed to ensure mutual exclusivity of the contexts. We will often use the notation
\Pi_{c}
to denote the program
\Pi\cup\{\nu_{c}\leftarrow\}
. In other words,
\Pi_{c}
is the program in context
c
. In practice, not all the constructs need to be implemented; for example, we can avoid the need for quadratic number of rules of the format
\neg\nu_{c^{\prime}}\leftarrow\nu_{c}
by simply ensuring that we reason with
\Pi_{c}
’s separately.

Learned Probability Distribution.
In this work, we take a set of rules
\Pi
with associated contexts (and the infrastructure rules described above) and learn a distribution over rule contexts (set
C
). We use maximum-likelihood learning to learn a probabilistic circuit
(
Choi, Vergari, and Van den Broeck 2020
)
for the probability distribution.
A key insight in this paper is that traditional confidence-based methods of selecting rules do not capture which rules work best together (i.e., the context). By learning a probability distribution over contexts and identifying the contexts that a rule is associated allows us to leverage this structure during inference.

We shall denote the distribution as
P_{\theta}
and when we use a context in the argument (e.g.,
P_{\theta}(c)
) we are describing the probability of a particular context (which is disjoint from any other context, hence
\sum_{c}P_{\theta}(c)=1
). When the argument is a set of rules (or facts), it will refer to a marginal probability. For set of rules, we have:

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(R)=\sum_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models R}P_{\theta}(c)

(1)

Throughout the paper, we will use
q
to denote a query (which can be a program, rule, fact, conjunction, or literal) and often the symbol
R
to denote a set of rules.

Formal Results and Methodology

In this paper, we shall often find it computationally advantageous to bound or find equivalences to the marginal. In this section, we prove a suite of results for this reason. Critically, none of the following results rely on any sort of independence assumption. We first show a lower bound on the marginal.

Proposition 1
.

For any set of rules
R
and a context
c
,

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(R)\geq\sum_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\subseteq R}P_{\theta}(c)

(2)

This proposition follows from the fact that if
\Pi_{c}
is contained in
R
then
R
must be a logical consequence.

We next look at the marginal of specific query. We assume that the query is
intentional
, meaning that it does not appear in any facts of
\Pi
but can appear in the head of non-fact rules.

Proposition 2
.

When
q
is intentional,

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(q)=1-P_{\theta}\left(\bigwedge_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models q}\neg\nu_{c}\right)

(3)

Proof.

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(\bigwedge_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models q}\neg\nu_{c})=\sum_{c^{\prime}\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c^{\prime}}\models\bigwedge_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models q}\neg\nu_{c}}P_{\theta}(c^{\prime})

(4)

Consider some
\Pi_{c^{\prime\prime}}
. If
\Pi_{c^{\prime\prime}}\models q
then it does not model
\bigwedge_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models q}\neg\nu_{c}
(as the fact
\nu_{c^{\prime\prime}}\leftarrow
is in
\Pi_{c^{\prime\prime}}
). If it does not model
q
, then for every other
c^{\prime\prime\prime}
the rule
\neg c^{\prime\prime\prime}\leftarrow c^{\prime\prime}
implies
\Pi_{c^{\prime\prime}}\models\neg c^{\prime\prime\prime}
. Therefore, in this case
\Pi_{c^{\prime\prime}}\models\bigwedge_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models q}\neg\nu_{c}
. Therefore, we obtain the following equivalence:

\displaystyle\{c^{\prime}\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c^{\prime}}\models\bigwedge_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models q}\neg\nu_{c}\}=\{c^{\prime}\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c^{\prime}}\not\models q\}

(5)

From this, we get the following:

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(\bigwedge_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models q}\neg\nu_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\not\models q}P_{\theta}(c)

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models q}P_{\theta}(c)

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-P_{\theta}(q)

(8)

Which in turn gives us the statement.
∎

We can also show properties to relate marginal probabilities to each other. This can be useful if we desire to only compute the marginal probability of a small number of rule sets. This is particularly useful when the number of contexts (the size of
C
) is very large (e.g., in our experiments we learned probability distributions with
10^{5}
contexts). Further, while it is advantageous to learn
P_{\theta}
over large numbers of program for reasons of accuracy, this is detrimental to explainability when considering a user.

Proposition 3
.

Given subsets of
\Pi
:
R_{1},\ldots,R_{j},\ldots,R_{max}
,

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(q)\geq sup\{P_{\theta}(R_{j})|R_{j}\models q\}

(9)

The corresponding upper bound mirrors the above proposition.

Proposition 4
.

Given subsets of
\Pi
:
R_{1},\ldots,R_{j},\ldots,R_{max}
,

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(q)\leq sup\{1-P_{\theta}(R_{j})|R_{j}\not\models q\}

(10)

Inference Procedure.
As we learn
P_{\theta}
over the set of contexts, if we have facts that are known to be true, then we can simply add those facts to
\Pi
and, for a given query, we can compute its probability based on the facts and learned probability distribution by using computation techniques shown in Propositions
1
-
4
. We note that propositions
3
and
4
require the identification of some subsets of rules to enable the calculation of the marginals. We performed experiments with three such methods:

•

PC1
.
Approximates the probability of a query with the lower bound of Proposition
3
and uses rule sets consisting of singleton rules.

•

PC2
.
Exactly computes the probability of a query using Proposition
2
.

•

PC3
.
Approximates the probability of a query with the lower bound of Proposition
3
and uses rule sets created from a greedy walk (more details in the Ruleset Generation section of Appendix). The walk is terminated once the marginal of the rule is below the predefined threshold. The new walk begins with the remaining non-ground rules.

Formal Probabilistic Semantics.
Here we show that the presented formalism can be shown to be equivalent to Nilsson Logic
(
Nilsson 1986
)
providing a formal probabilistic semantics. Let us consider the following construction in Nilsson logic, creating a logic program
\Pi_{nil}
and
P_{nil}
(where
P_{nil}
assigns a probability to all elements of
\Pi_{nil}
):

1.

For each
r\in\Pi
, add
r
to
\Pi_{nil}
and set
P_{nil}(r)=1.0
.

2.

For each
c\in C
add fact
\nu_{c}\leftarrow
to
\Pi_{nil}
and set

P_{nil}(\nu_{c}\leftarrow)=P_{\theta}(c)
.

Following
(
Khuller et al. 2007
;
Shakarian, Simari, and Subrahmanian 2012
)
, an interpretation
I
is a probability distribution over all possible world - which is the same semantic structure of
(
Nilsson 1986
)
. Given a logic program and associated probabilities assigned to all its elements (e.g., as in
\Pi_{nil}
and
P_{nil}
defined above), an interpretation
I
satisfies the structure (written
I\models(\Pi_{nil},P_{nil})
if for all
r\in\Pi_{nil}
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{w\textit{ s.t. }w\models r}I(w)=P_{nil}(r)

(11)

With this in mind, we first prove a technical lemma.

Lemma 1
.

For some formula
q
, and world
w
where a satisfying interpretation
I
assigns it a non-zero probability,
w\models q
if and only if for some
c
where
\Pi_{c}\models q
,
w\models\nu_{c}
.

Proof.

Claim 1: Any satisfying interpretation that assigns a world
w
a non-zero probability must satisfy exactly one
\nu_{c}
atom. We note that this follows from the fact that, by the construction, no two
\nu
atoms can be true in a given world (by the construction) and as they are associated with disjoint contexts, the probability mass assigned any single
\nu
atom must sum to
1
(by
P_{nil}
).

Claim 2: (
\rightarrow
) Suppose, BWOC, that
w\models q
and there is no
c
where
\Pi_{c}\models q
,
w\models\nu_{c}
. Claim 1 tells us that
w
must satisfy a single helper atom - let us call this
\nu_{c}
. Consider the rules of the form
\mu_{r}\leftarrow\nu_{c}
and
h\leftarrow b\wedge\mu_{r}
in
\Pi_{nil}
. As all other rules cannot possibly fire as only
\nu_{c}
is true in
w
(which by Claim 1 precludes other helper atoms), and the set of rules of second form can fire, and only these rules entail
q
. Note these are the same rules in
\Pi_{c}
. Hence, these rules together must entail
q
. This tells us that
\Pi_{c}\models q
- a contradiction.

Claim 3: (
\leftarrow
) Suppose BWOC, that there exists an
c
where
\Pi_{c}\models q
and
w\models\nu_{c}
but
w\not\models q
. Consider the rules of the form
\mu_{r}\leftarrow\nu_{c}
and
h\leftarrow b\wedge\mu_{r}
in
\Pi_{c}
. We know all these rules can also be found in
\Pi_{nil}
. Therefore, as
\Pi_{c}\models q
, any world that models
\nu_{c}
must also satisfy
q
- a contradiction.

The proof of the lemma follows from claims 1-3.
∎

Therefore, we have the following result to show that our syntactically-learned probability distribution ensures semantic entailment:

Theorem 1
.

Interpretation
I
that satisfies
(\Pi_{nil},P_{nil})
if and only if for any query formula
q
:

\sum_{w\textit{ s.t. }w\models q}I(w)=\sum_{c\textit{ s.t. }\Pi_{c}\models R}P_{\theta}(c)

Proof.

Let
W_{i}=\{w|w\models\nu_{c}\}
. Note that by claim 1 of Lemma 1, that no world (with non zero probability) in
W_{i}
can satisfy a helper atom other than
\nu_{c}
, so we can partition the set of non-zero probability worlds by helper atoms. Consider the set
W_{q}\equiv\{w|w\models q\}
(the set of worlds we sum over in the right-hand side of the equation). By Lemma 1, there is a precise set of
W_{i}
’s such that their union equals
W_{q}
and that we can characterize those as follows:

W_{q}=\bigcup_{\Pi_{c}\models q}W_{c}

Hence, we have the following equality:

\sum_{w\textit{ s.t. }w\models q}I(w)=\sum_{\Pi_{c}\models q}\sum_{w\in W_{i}}I(w)

By the construction, we have the following:

\sum_{w\textit{ s.t. }w\models q}I(w)=\sum_{\Pi_{c}\models q}\sum_{w\models\nu_{c}}I(w)

By step 2 of the construction, we then have:

\sum_{w\textit{ s.t. }w\models q}I(w)=\sum_{\Pi_{c}\models q}P_{\theta}(c)

Which gives us the statement of the theorem.
∎

Experimental Setup.

Our evaluation task is knowledge graph completion: given a knowledge graph and a set of learned non-ground rules, the goal is to infer missing triples that are likely to be in test set. We use standard metrics such as Hits@1, Hits@3, Hits@10 and Mean Rank Reciprocal (MRR) to compute the quality of predicted triples.
Hits@k gives the proportion of test triples that appear in top-k predictions, and MRR computes the average reciprocal rank of correct predictions across all test triples. (formal definition of both in Appendix)
We use AnyBURL rule learner with 10 seconds learning time and minimum support threshold
\geq 10
for consistent comparisons. AnyBURL assigns a confidence score to each of these learned rules as the ratio of instances where both the rule’s head and body were satisfied to the number of instances where only the body was satisfied.
We use training triples to establish contexts. We have used PyClause’s
(
Betz et al. 2024
)
abductive reasoning engine to determine rule-context associations to realize the infrastructure rules of form
\mu_{r}\leftarrow\nu_{c}
introduced in Technical Preliminaries. Note that in our implementation we do not represent these rules explicitly but instead rely on the underlying association matrix.
For PC learning, we use the Hidden Chow-Liu Tree
(
Liu and Van den Broeck 2021
)
for structure learning and expectation maximization (EM) for parameter learning using the Juice library for probabilistic circuits
(
Dang et al. 2021
)
. Note that 5 runs of the framework were conducted for Family dataset to check stability of results, rest were run once.

Hyperparameter Selection.

We tested confidence thresholds for input non-ground rules(50-90%) and number of EM iterations for circuit parameter learning(10, 50, 100). The final settings focused on showing framework effectiveness over large number of rules for each dataset and also ensuring computational tractability for evaluation. The confidence thresholds for each evaluated dataset are 50%(CODEX-S/Kinship/UMLS), 60%(FB15K-237), 70%(Nations), 0%(all mined rules for WN18, WN18RR, Family). Datasets with higher thresholds (e.g., Kinship at 90% with only 1300 rules) outperformed respective baseline too but does not prove scalability to larger rule base. The number of EM iterations are 100(Family/WN18/WN18RR), 50(Kinship), 10(Others).

Baseline/PC Inference.

Baseline sorts all the input rules by confidence and selects top-
n
rules for evaluation point
n
and infers using AnyBURL inference engine. Our PC-guided framework uses the PC1, PC2, and PC3 inference methods described in the Technical Preliminaries to obtain the prediction probabilities. AnyBURL evaluation engine was used to compute Hits@k and MRR for all to maintain consistency with results.

Results and Discussion

All experiments were conducted on high-throughput computing cluster nodes utilizing up to 16-core CPUs (single cores for simpler tasks), NVIDIA A100 GPU, and up to 500GB memory.
We empirically evaluated our framework with eight benchmark knowledge graphs and demonstrated that our framework maintained competitive performance with much higher efficiency (in terms of the required number of rules to reach there) than the baseline approach.
For these 8 datasets, the PC-guided framework reduced the required size of rule set between 70-96% (average 12-fold reduction in rules). With this reduced rule count, we outperform the baseline by approximately
31\times
. PC-guided inferences also preserved an average of around 91% of highest baseline performance (PC-guided performance with minimal rules vs. baseline’s confidence-based performance with maximum considered rules).

Hits@10.
In Figure
2
, we see consistent patterns of immediate performance for Hits@10 over all the datasets. Using minimal equivalent number of rules, PC2 inference shows substantial improvements for Hits@10 over the baseline (with PC1 showing similar trends): 214-fold (UMLS), 17-fold (Kinship), 6.4-fold (FB15K-237), 6.2-fold (CODEX-S), 4-fold (Family), 2.8-fold (Nations), 1.4-fold (WN18RR) and significant improvement in WN18 as baseline achieved zero performance for 500 rules.
In CODEX-S, PC2 inference achieved Hits@10 of 0.4259 using only 1000 rules by achieving 99.95% of the baseline’s highest Hits@10 (0.4261) that required 20000 rules, i.e. PC2 required only 5% of the total rules to reach there. UMLS gives the strongest evidence for the need of our framework where PC2 inference exactly achieves baseline’s peak Hits@10 of 0.9644 by using only 1000 rules compared to baseline that required 25000 rules to reach. This is a 25-fold reduction in rule usage. In WN18, we see both PC1 and PC2 inferences achieving near-peak performance (99.4% and 99.22%) of baseline’s using only 500 rules. Conclusion and Future Work

The embedding-based approaches have shown state-of-the-art performances for knowledge graph completion, but this comes at the cost of interpretability. In recent years, explainable rule-based methods have achieved competitive performances over embedding-based approaches. However, this requires grounding tens of thousands of non-ground rules, undermining the very explainability they were designed for. In this paper, we address the critical challenge of preserving reasoning performance while significantly reducing rule sets for interpretable inference.
We proposed a probabilistic circuit guided framework that discovers rule contexts from training data and learns the distribution over these rule contexts without assuming rule independence. We also provided a suite of formal results characterizing the computation of probability and showing the adherence to formal probabilistic semantics.
We introduced 3 PC-guided inference methods: PC1 (lower bound approximation of query probability - singleton rule sets), PC2 (exact query probability - singleton rule sets), and PC3(approximation of query probability - rule sets from greedy walks). Our framework reduced the rule requirement by 70-96%, and with these smaller rule sets, we got 31-fold performance (Hits@10, MRR) improvement over baseline with same number of baseline rules. We also preserve an average of 91% performance with a reduced rule set compared to the full rule set of the baseline.

Future Work.

As our framework does not depend on any graph-specific data structures, it can be applied to other symbolic AI domains that struggle with rule explosion and computational complexity, for example, the proof search module in the symbolic theorem proof system
(
Jiang, Fonseca, and Cohen 2024
)
.
As our framework is agnostic to the underlying rule sources and is therefore not limited to AnyBURL for rule learning, we will explore the integration of the framework with other rule learning systems
(
Cheng, Ahmed, and Sun 2023b
;
Jain et al. 2019
;
De Raedt et al. 2015
)
. We are mainly interested in integrating our framework with Inductive logic Programming systems to address their exponential search space challenges
(
Côrte-Real et al. 2018
)
. We would also like to investigate how our framework can fit in and improve neuro-symbolic systems
(
Weber et al. 2019
)
by providing reduced rule sets alongside sub-symbolic components while maintaining interpretability.

Evaluation Metrics.

This section provides formal definitions for the evaluation metrics used in our experiments. Let
\mathcal{T}_{test}
denote the set of test triples of particular dataset, and for each test triple
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{test}
, let
rank_{i}
denote the rank of the correct entity in the sorted list of predictions (where rank 1 is the highest scoring prediction).

Hits@k.
The Hits@k metric measures the proportion of test queries where the correct entity appears within the top-k predictions:

\text{Hits@k}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{test}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{T}_{test}|}\mathbf{1}[rank_{i}\leq k]

(14)

Mean Rank Reciprocal (MRR).
The MRR metric computes the average reciprocal rank of correct predictions across all test triples.

\text{MRR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{test}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{T}_{test}|}\frac{1}{rank_{i}}

(15)

Since we are using AnyBURL evaluation engine which approximates the MRR and it is based on the top-k only. Note that for maintaining consistency with the results for both PC framework and baseline, we have used k = 1000 for all our experiments. We define MRR as:

\text{MRR}=\text{MRR@k}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{test}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{T}_{test}|}\frac{\mathbf{1}[rank_{i}\leq k]}{rank_{i}}
