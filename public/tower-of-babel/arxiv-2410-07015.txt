A stabilization result for ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -harmonic 1-forms by constructing solutions on closed 3-manifolds with long cylindrical necks
Willem Adriaan Salm

Abstract
In this paper, we give an explicit construction of families of ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -harmonic 1-forms that degenerate to manifolds with cylindrical ends. We do this by considering certain linear combinations of L 2 L^{2} -bounded ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -harmonic 1-forms and by modifying the metric near the link. This construction works if number of L 2 L^{2} -bounded ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} -harmonic 1-forms is strictly more than twice the number of connected components of the link. This can always be done if we consider a connected sum with a 3-manifold with sufficiently large b 1 b_{1} .

Introduction

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms are a multivalued extension of the standard harmonic 1-forms. To define these, let
(M,g)
be a three-dimensional, Riemannian manifold,
\Sigma
a codimension 2 subspace and
\mathcal{I}
a real Euclidean line bundle over
M\setminus\Sigma
. In this paper we assume that
\Sigma
is a smoothly embedded 1-manifold, but we still refer
\Sigma
as the singular set. The line bundle
\mathcal{I}
can be equipped with a unique connection by requiring that
\nabla s=0
for any local section
s
with constant pointwise norm. For any
\omega\in\Gamma(T^{*}M\otimes\mathcal{I})
, we say that the triple
(\Sigma,\mathcal{I},\omega)
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form if
\operatorname{d}\!_{\nabla}^{*}\omega=\operatorname{d}\!_{\nabla}\omega=0
and
\mathcal{I}
has non-trivial monodromy for loops linking
\Sigma
. Similarly, one can define
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic spinors.

There is an equivalent definition of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms which we will use in this paper. Namely, the space of real line bundles is classified by
H^{1}(M\setminus\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
and so one can find a double cover
\hat{M}
of
M\setminus\Sigma
that trivializes
\mathcal{I}
. On this double cover,
\omega
becomes a harmonic 1-form that switches sign under the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action of the double cover. Although the metric on this double cover is degenerate along
\Sigma
, Hunsicker and Mazzeo
[
1
]
showed that there is a Hodge theory for these spaces. They showed that for every element in
H^{1}_{-}(\hat{M}):=\{\omega\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\hat{M})\colon\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cdot\omega=-\omega\}
there exists a unique, harmonic,
L^{2}
-bounded representative and this representative is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form.

For application purposes we require that
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms are
C^{0}
-bounded, unless specified otherwise. Namely, Taubes
[
2
,
3
]
found that sequences of
PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-connections on 3-manifolds can degenerate, but after a blow up, one can find a subsequence converging to a
C^{0}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form. According to Haydys and Walpuski
[
4
]
and Taubes
[
5
]
, this behaviour also shows up for Seiberg-Witten equations with multiple spinors in dimensions three and four. According to Taubes
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
,
C^{0}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms/spinors also play a role in the study of the Kapustin-Witten equations, Vafa-Witten equations and the study of complex anti-self dual equations on 4-manifolds.

Although
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms and spinors play an important role in many gauge theories, not much is known about them. Doan and Walpuski
[
9
]
gave an existence theory for
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic spinors using the wall-crossing formula for Seiberg-Witten equations with two spinors. By the work of Donaldson
[

-harmonic functions/1-forms/spinors. We also know there should be topological conditions distinguishing

-bounded and

-bounded solutions. For example, Haydys
[

gave a necessary condition for
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms on an integral homology sphere in terms of the Alexander polynomial of the singular set.

The reason that so little is known about these
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms, is that there are only a few known construction methods. For example, Taubes and Wu
[
14
]
gave some local examples where the singular set is not smooth. Haydys et al.
[
15

found some handcrafted examples in 3 and 4 dimensions. He
[
16

explained how to create
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms if there is an additional

-symmetry.
He and Parker
[

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms. Yan
[

created new families of examples where the branching set collapses.

In this paper we give an explicit construction of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms. We show that as long as
\Sigma
is smooth and
\dim H^{1}_{-}(\hat{M})
is strictly more than
2\>b_{0}(\Sigma)
, there always exist a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form by deforming the metric near the singular set.

To understand why
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms are so hard to find, we have to revisit weighted elliptic analysis on complete, non-compact manifolds. By studying the fundamental solutions for the model metric one can guess the correct decay rate for which the elliptic operator is Fredholm. For example, for a 3-manifold with a cylindrical end, the fundamental solutions of the Laplacian are linear combinations of
e^{\sqrt{n^{2}+m^{2}}r}e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta}
and so one uses the weighted norm
\|u\|_{C^{k,\alpha}_{\delta}}:=\|e^{-\delta r}u\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}
. One can show that except for a discrete set of values for
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, the Laplacian is Fredholm.

One can repeat this argument to find
L^{2}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms, as the model solutions have polynomial behaviour near the singular set. Using the monodromy condition one can show that for certain weights the Laplacian is an isomorphism. This is done by Hunsicker and Mazzeo
[
1
]
to classify
L^{2}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms.

The issues start when one additionally requires that these 1-forms are
C^{0}
-bounded. Using the same weighted analysis from before, one can check that the formal adjoint of the Laplacian—which is again the Laplacian but with different weight—has an infinite dimensional kernel. Therefore, for a generic metric there are no
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms. Sadly, this can’t be solved by changing the weight function. Namely, the model solutions are of the form
I_{n/2}(m\cdot r)e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta}
where
I_{\alpha}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind. This implies that for a fixed value of
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, all model solutions have the same decay rate and so they are not distinguishable using weighted norms.

Normally, one describes this cokernel using a local expansion near the singular set
\Sigma
. Namely, for any point on
\Sigma
we can trivialize a neighbourhood as
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
where
(\theta,0)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
is a parametrization of
\Sigma
.
Using the work of Donaldson
[
10
]
, any
L^{2}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form has a local expansion

\omega=\Re\left(\operatorname{d}\!\left(A(\theta)z^{\frac{1}{2}}+B(\theta)z^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(r^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)

(2)

near the singular set.
A
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form is
C^{0}
-bounded if and only if
A(\theta)
vanishes everywhere along
\Sigma
.

Because of these issues one often perturbs the singular set to have an extra set of parameters to control. If one sets up the problem correctly, one can show that small perturbations can kill off small values of
A
. This is done in
[
10
]
and in
[
11
]
. These methods are not trivial and for the perturbation argument to work the authors had to use the Nash–Moser theorem and they also had to assume that the
B
-term in
Equation

2
is nowhere vanishing.

With this in mind we make the following observations: First, in order to construct a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form it is sufficient to construct an
L^{2}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form with an sufficiently small obstruction term and repeat the argument given in
[
10
]
. (This is exactly the same idea as in He and Parker
[
17
]
, but they use the deformation theory of Parker
[
11
]
.) Secondly, to control the terms in the cokernel one has to make use of additional structures or symmetries. In the current paper we will control the cokernel by forcing cylindrical behaviour into this edge problem. To do this we replace the metric near
\Sigma
with a model metric and this model metric will have a cylindrical region of arbitrary length. Finding
L^{2}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms reduces to solving the Laplace equation on these cylinders and thus we expect to get exponential growth on these regions. We show that the Laplacian has a bounded inverse, uniform with respect to the length of the necks, and so this exponential growth behaviour gives exponential decay behaviour near
\Sigma
.

By slightly changing the problem we can make these estimates uniform, but this will yield two new conditions for each connected component of

1.

There is a complex-valued Fourier mode which will play a dominant role when inverting the linearised operator. We need this mode to be non-zero and we need to normalise everything such that this mode has unit norm. In a suitable trivialization and using the expansion in

In the last part of this paper we show that for a generic metric the first condition is satisfied. However, the same proof will show that the second condition is generically not satisfied. 2.1
Regularity estimates

According to Hunsicker and Mazzeo
[
1
]
, there is a Hodge theory for
L^{2}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms on
(M,g_{s})
with
\Sigma
as singular set. To find this
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-form, one picks an element of
H^{1}_{-}(\hat{M})
and chooses a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-antisymmetric smooth representative
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(\hat{M})
. Because the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-antisymmetry forces
\omega
to be exact on the boundary and neck regions, we can assume that
\omega
is compactly supported on the interior region. To find an harmonic representative, we need to find a

that is anti-symmetric and solves
\operatorname{d}\!^{*}(\omega+\operatorname{d}\!u)=0
.
Equivalently,
u
must solve

\Delta u=-\operatorname{d}\!^{*}\omega.

(4)

According to Donaldson
[
10
]

can always be found.

In this section we study the regularity properties of the Laplacian. Especially,
we want to show that in suitable Banach spaces, there is a constant
C>0
such that for all
u
in the domain of
\Delta
,
\|u\|\leq C\|\Delta u\|
. Our goal in this chapter is to show that
C
can be chosen uniformly for every parameter
s>0
. As an intermediate step we first show that there are regularity estimates with uniformly bounded constants.

Donaldson
[
10

defined his Hölder norms near the link as follows: For a given

and

to be the set differential operators, given by degree

,
\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}
, and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}

He defines his Hölder norm as

\|u\|_{D^{k,\alpha}}:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq k,\\
D\in\underline{\mathcal{T}}_{j}\end{subarray}}\|Du\|_{C^{0,\alpha}},

(5)

where the

.

In this paper, we extend this definition and assume that
\underline{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
is generated by

\tilde{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r},\quad\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}

on the boundary and neck regions in

We denote the restriction of this Hölder space to

First notice that

Assume that this theorem is false. Then there exist a sequence
u_{i}\in E^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})
such that

\|u_{i}\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}=1,\quad\operatorname{Supp}(u_{i})\subset U,\text{ and }\|\Delta_{g}u_{i}\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\to 0.

Fix
0<\beta<\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. We have a compact inclusion of
D^{k+2,\alpha}(U)
into
D^{k+2,\beta}(U)
. Therefore, without loss of generality we assume that
u_{i}
converges to some
u\in D^{k+2,\beta}(\overline{M})
. Moreover
\Delta u=0
. By the maximum principle
u=0
.

Next, let
\chi_{j}(\theta)
be a smooth, finite partition of unity of
\Sigma
. Assume that the support of each
\chi_{j}
is sufficiently small. By Proposition 2.2 and remark 2.3 (3) in
[
10
]
, for each
i\in\mathbb{N}

\|u_{i}\|_{D^{1,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq\sum_{j}\|\chi_{j}\cdot u_{i}\|_{D^{1,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq C\sum_{k}\|\Delta_{g_{s}}(\chi_{j}\cdot u_{i})\|_{D^{0,\beta}(\overline{M})}.

Expanding the Laplacian yields

\displaystyle\|u_{i}\|_{D^{1,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq C

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\|\chi_{j}\cdot\Delta_{g_{s}}u_{i}\|_{D^{0,\beta}(\overline{M})}+\|u_{i}\cdot\Delta_{g_{s}}\chi_{k}\|_{D^{0,\beta}(\overline{M})}+\left\|\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\theta}\frac{\partial\chi_{j}}{\partial\theta}\right\|_{D^{0,\beta}(\overline{M})}.

(7)

The right hand side of this inequality converges to zero as
i
converges to infinity.
Therefore
u_{i}
converges to zero in
D^{1,\alpha}(\overline{M})
. At the same time, a careful reading of the proof of Proposition 2.6 in
[
10
]
, gives us the estimate

\|u_{i}\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq C\left[\|\Delta_{g_{s}}u_{i}\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}+\|u_{i}\|_{D^{1,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\right].

This would imply
u_{i}
converges to zero in
D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})
, which contradicts the fact that
\|u_{i}\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}=1
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
Let
U\subset\overline{M}
be a small, closed tubular neighbourhood of
\Sigma
on which
g_{s}=\operatorname{d}\!r^{2}+4r^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\phi^{2}+\operatorname{d}\!\theta^{2}
.
There exists constant
C>0
, independent of the length
s
of the necks, such that for all
u\in E^{k+2,\alpha}(\hat{M})
,

\|u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq C\left(\|\Delta_{g}u\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}+\|u\|_{C^{0}(\overline{M}\setminus U)}.\right)

Proof.

Let
U^{\prime}\supset\supset U
be another tubular neighbourhood of
\Sigma
like
U
and let

\chi_{r}(r)
be a smooth step function that is supported in
U^{\prime}
and
\chi_{r}|_{U}=1
.
By splitting
u=\chi_{r}\>u+(1-\chi_{r})u
, we get that

\|u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq\|\chi_{r}\>u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}+\|(1-\chi_{r})u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}.

By Lemma
2.2
, there exists a uniform constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\chi_{r}\>u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq

\displaystyle C\|\Delta_{g_{s}}(\chi_{r}\>u)\|_{D^{k+,\alpha}(\overline{M})}

(8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\|\chi_{r}\Delta_{g_{s}}(u)\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}+\|u\Delta_{g_{s}}\chi_{r}\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}+\left\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\frac{\partial\chi_{r}}{\partial r}\right\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\right).

(9)

We can absorb (the derivatives of)
\chi_{r}
in to the constant
C
and so we get

\displaystyle\|\chi_{r}\>u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\|\Delta_{g_{s}}(u)\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}+\|u\|_{D^{k+1,\alpha}(\operatorname{Supp}(\operatorname{d}\!\chi_{r}))}\right).

(10)

Using an elliptic regularity estimate on the support of
\operatorname{d}\!\chi_{r}
, this can be written as

\displaystyle\|\chi_{r}\>u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\|\Delta_{g_{s}}(u)\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}+\|u\|_{C^{0}(\overline{M}\setminus U)}\right)

(11)

for some uniform constant
C>0
.

Next we consider the term
\|(1-\chi_{r})u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}
. We claim that there exists a uniform constant
C>0
, such that

\|(1-\chi_{r})u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M}\setminus U^{\prime})}\leq C\left(\|\Delta_{g_{s}}((1-\chi_{r})u)\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M}\setminus U^{\prime})}+\|(1-\chi_{r})u\|_{C^{0}(\overline{M}\setminus U^{\prime})}\right)

If this claim is true, we can repeat the first part of this proof to conclude the statement.

To show this claim, we show that
g_{s}
has uniform bounded geometry on
\overline{M}\setminus U^{\prime}
.
By Theorem 1.2 in Hebey
[
20
]
, it is sufficient to show that there is a uniform bound on (the derivatives of) the Ricci curvature and the injectivity radius has a uniform lower bound. The last condition is satisfied, as on the support of
1-\chi_{r}
, none of the circle fibres decay. The curvature is uniformly bounded, as on the neck the curvature is zero.

Because
g
has bounded geometry on
\overline{M}\setminus U
, we can cover
\hat{M}
with small balls with small, but fixed radius and on each ball the
D^{k,\alpha}
-norm is equivalent to the
C^{k,\alpha}
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Hence on each ball, we have a local Schauder estimate. Taking the supremum over all balls yield the estimate, given in this claim.
∎

2.2
Bounded inverse

With these regularity estimates, we now show that
\Delta
has a uniform bounded inverse. Although this will be a long and technical proof, it will be one of the most pivotal results, as this proposition will enable us to compare
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms for different lengths of necks.

Proposition 2.4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of the length
s
of the necks, such that for all
u\in E^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})

\|u\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq C\>\|\Delta_{g_{s}}u\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M})}.

Proof.

Suppose that this theorem is false. Then there exists a sequence
s_{i}>0
and
u_{i}\in E^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M},g_{s_{i}})
such that
s_{i}
diverges to infinity,
\|u_{i}\|_{D^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline{M},g_{s_{i}})}=1
, and
\|\Delta_{g_{s_{i}}}u_{i}\|_{D^{k,\alpha}(\overline{M},g_{s_{i}})}
converges to zero.

By
Lemma

2.3
, on a compact set
K\subset\hat{M}
the sequence
u_{i}
is bounded below in the
C^{0}
-norm, i.e. there is a constant
c>0
and a sequence
x_{i}\in K
such that
|u_{i}(x_{i})|>c
. Depending on the behaviour of
x_{i}
, we have the following 3 cases to consider:

1.

The sequence
x_{i}
converges up to a subsequence to a point
x
inside the interior region.

2.

The sequence
x_{i}
converges up to a subsequence to a point
x
inside a boundary region, but not does not converge to a point in
\Sigma
.

3.

The sequence
x_{i}
stays inside a neck region.

For each case, we will reach a contradiction using the following steps:

1.

Modify the sequence
u_{i}
into a new sequence that is defined on some fixed limiting space.

2.

Use Arzela–Ascoli to show that this new sequence converges up to a subsequence to some
u_{\infty}
. Use local elliptic regularity to show that
u_{\infty}
is non-zero a harmonic function.

3.

Show that on the limiting space there is no non-zero harmonic function.

Case 1:
x_{i}
converges up to a subsequence to
x
in the interior region.

Step 1
: For any
t>0
, let
N_{t}\subset\hat{M}
be the union of the interior and neck regions and assume that the necks on
N_{t}
have length
t
. By keeping the interior region fixed, we can view
N_{t_{1}}
is a subset of
N_{t_{2}}
if
t_{1}<t_{2}
. Hence, we can define
N_{\infty}
as the union of
N_{s_{i}}
, which is the interior space and an infinite long neck. We pick
N_{\infty}
as our limiting space. The metric
g_{s_{i}}|_{N_{s_{i}}}
induces a metric on
N_{\infty}
and because this metric has cylindrical ends, we use the standard Hölder norm on
N_{\infty}
.

Step 2
: For any
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the restriction
u_{i}|_{N_{s_{j}}}
of
u_{i}
on
N_{s_{j}}
. By fixing
N_{s_{j}}
, the Arzela–Ascoli theorem implies that there exists a subsequence which converges to a continuous function on
N_{s_{j}}
. Using compact exhaustion and the Arzela–Ascoli theorem repeatedly, one can find a subsequence of
u_{i}
that converges to some continuous function
u_{\infty}
on
N_{\infty}
. We still denote this subsequence by
u_{i}
by abuse of notation.
The function
u_{\infty}
is bounded and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-antisymmetric, as it is the limit of uniformly bounded and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-antisymmetric functions. Also,
u_{\infty}
cannot be identically zero, as

|u_{\infty}(x)|=\lim_{i\to\infty}|u_{i}(x)|
must be greater than
c>0
.

On any pair of compact sets
K\subset\subset K^{\prime}
inside
N_{\infty}
elliptic regularity states

\|u_{i}-u_{j}\|_{C^{k+2,\alpha}(K)}\leq C\left(\|\Delta(u_{i}-u_{j})\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(K^{\prime})}+\|u_{i}-u_{j}\|_{C^{0}(K^{\prime})}\right)

As the right-hand side converges to zero,
u_{i}
is a Cauchy sequence on
C^{k+2,\alpha}(K)
and thus
u_{\infty}
is at least twice differentiable and
\Delta u_{\infty}=0
.

Step 3
: We claim that any bounded,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-antisymmetric, harmonic function on
N_{\infty}
vanishes everywhere. Indeed, on a neck inside
N_{\infty}
, let
r^{\prime}
be the distance to the interior region and expand
u_{\infty}
into the Fourier modes

u_{\infty}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\hat{u}_{nm}(r^{\prime})e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta}.

(12)

Because
u_{\infty}
is harmonic, each Fourier mode must satisfy

\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{u}_{nm}}{\partial(r^{\prime})^{2}}-\left(\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}\right)\hat{u}_{nm}=0.

Hence there is a family of constants
u^{\pm}_{nm}
such that

\hat{u}_{nm}(r^{\prime})=u^{+}_{nm}e^{+\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}}r^{\prime}}+u^{-}_{nm}e^{-\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}}r^{\prime}}.

On each neck
U
inside
N_{\infty}
, the projection operator

\displaystyle\pi_{nm}\colon v\mapsto\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{T^{2}}v\>e^{-in\phi}e^{-im\theta}\operatorname{d}\!\phi\>\operatorname{d}\!\theta

(13)

that sends each function to its
(n,m)
Fourier mode is a bounded operator on
C^{0}(U)
. Therefore, the term

\lim\limits_{r^{\prime}\to\infty}|\hat{u}_{nm}(r^{\prime})|=|u^{+}_{nm}|\cdot\lim\limits_{r^{\prime}\to\infty}|e^{+\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}}r^{\prime}}|

can only be bounded if
u^{+}_{nm}=0
. This implies that

u_{\infty}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}u^{-}_{nm}e^{-\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}}r^{\prime}}e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta}

decays when
r^{\prime}
goes to infinity. Doing this for each cylindrical end, the maximum principle forces
u_{\infty}
to be zero everywhere.

We have reached a contradiction, as
u_{\infty}(x)
is bounded below away from zero. Therefore,
\{x_{i}\}
cannot converge up to a subsequence to some
x
in the interior region.

Case 2:
x_{i}
converges up to a subsequence to
x
in a boundary region, but it does not converge to the singular set
\Sigma

Step 1
: Without loss of generality
x_{i}
lies inside a single boundary region. Let
N_{s_{i}}
be the union of this boundary region and the neighbouring neck region. By keeping the boundary region fixed, we can say
N_{t_{1}}
is a subset of
N_{t_{2}}
if
t_{1}<t_{2}
. Hence, we can define
N_{\infty}
as the union of
N_{s_{i}}
, which is the boundary region and an infinitely long neck. We pick this as our limiting space.
The metric
g_{s_{i}}|_{N_{s_{i}}}
induces the metric

g=\operatorname{d}\!r^{2}+4\tilde{r}^{2}\operatorname{d}\!\phi^{2}+\operatorname{d}\!\theta^{2}

on
N_{\infty}
, which is an edge metric at one end and a cylindrical metric at the other side. We measure functions with the Hölder norm defined in
Equations

5
and
6
.

Step 2
: Using an identical argument as in the first case, one can find a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-antisymmetric, non-zero, bounded, twice differentiable, harmonic function
u_{\infty}
on
N_{\infty}
.

Step 3
: We claim that any bounded,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-antisymmetric, harmonic function on
N_{\infty}
vanishes everywhere. Like in the first case, we consider each Fourier mode
\hat{u}_{nm}(r)
separately and each Fourier mode is again bounded. This time, each Fourier mode must satisfy

\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\tilde{r}\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{nm}}{\partial r}\right)-\left(\frac{n^{2}}{4\tilde{r}^{2}}+m^{2}\right)\hat{u}_{nm}=0.

(14)

When
r
is sufficiently small, i.e. when
\tilde{r}=r
, then
Equation

14
is the defining equation for the modified Bessel function and so
\hat{u}_{nm}(r)
are linear combinations of
I_{|n|/2}(|m|\>r)e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta}
and
K_{|n|/2}(|m|\>r)e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta}
. The only modified Bessel functions that are bounded near zero, are modified Bessel functions of the first kind. Therefore,
\hat{u}_{nm}(r)
are scalar multiples of
I_{|n|/2}(|m|r)e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta}
.

Next, we consider the case when
r
is sufficiently large, i.e. when
\tilde{r}=2
.
Equation

14
simplifies to the same differential equation found in Case 1 and therefore,
\hat{u}_{nm}(r)
must be of the form
u^{+}_{nm}e^{+\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}}r}+u^{-}_{nm}e^{-\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}}r}
. Again, boundedness implies that
u^{+}_{nm}=0
and therefore,
\hat{u}_{nm}(r)
decays when
r
diverges to infinity.

We conclude, that each
\hat{u}_{nm}(r)e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta}
is an harmonic function that vanishes on the boundary of
N_{\infty}
. By the maximum principle
\hat{u}_{nm}(r)=0
. This is true for all Fourier modes and so
u_{\infty}=0
, which yields a contradiction.

Case 3:
x_{i}
stays inside a neck region.

Step 1
: Let
N_{s_{i}}
be the neck region where
x_{i}
resides and denote
r^{\mathrm{bdr}}_{i}
and
r^{\mathrm{int}}_{i}
the distance between
x_{i}
and the neighbouring boundary or interior region respectively. If
r^{\mathrm{int}}_{i}
is bounded, one can repeat the argument given in the first case. Similarly, if
r^{\mathrm{bdr}}_{i}
is bounded, one can repeat the second case. So now we assume that both
r^{\mathrm{bdr}}_{i}
and
r^{\mathrm{int}}_{i}
diverges.

On the neck
N_{s_{i}}
, we translate the
r
-coordinate such that
r=0
at
x_{i}
. With this reparametrization we can identify
N_{s_{i}}
with
[c_{i},C_{i}]\times T^{2}
where
c_{i}
converges to
-\infty
and
C_{i}
converges to
+\infty
. This way we can take the union of all
N_{s_{i}}
and get
N_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}\times T^{2}
as the limiting space. We equip
N_{\infty}
with the metric
\operatorname{d}\!r^{2}+16\operatorname{d}\!\phi^{2}+\operatorname{d}\!\theta^{2}
and we use the standard Hölder norms on
N_{\infty}
.

Steps 2 and 3
: Here we use an identical argument as in the second case. The only difference is that we have cylindrical metrics on both ends on
N_{\infty}
. Hence,
u_{\infty}
has to vanish everywhere, which yields a contradiction.
∎

2.3
The asymptotic expansion near the singular set

Now we have proven
Proposition

2.4
, we show its usefulness by applying it on the local expansion that is given in
Equation

2
. We do this by comparing this expansion to the expansion into Fourier modes. Explicitly, let
u\in C^{\infty}(\hat{M})
be a solution of
Equation

4
. As we have seen in the proof of
Proposition

2.4
, on a neck and boundary region near a connected component
\Sigma_{i}
of
\Sigma
the restriction of

can be expanded in its Fourier modes as

u=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}u_{nm}(s,\omega,\Sigma_{i})I_{nm}(r)e^{in\phi}e^{im\theta},

(15)

where
I_{nm}(r)
is the solution of
Equation

14
that vanishes at
r=0
. That is, up to a scalar multiple and on the region where
\tilde{r}=r
,
I_{nm}(r)
equals
r^{|n|/2}
when

or it is the modified Bessel function
I_{|n|/2}(|m|\>r)
of the first kind.
We normalise
I_{nm}(r)
, such that
I_{nm}(r)
is positive and
I_{nm}(r)=r^{|n|/2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{(|n|+1)/2})
. Also, depending on the context, we also write
u_{nm}:=u_{nm}(s,\omega,\Sigma_{i})
to simplify notation.

Comparing
Equation

15
with the expansion in the
A(\theta)
and
B(\theta)
, which is given in
Equation

2
, we conclude that the function
A(\theta)
depends on the
n=\pm 1
Fourier modes and that the function
B(\theta)
depends on the
n=\pm 3
Fourier modes. Even more, the fact that
u
is real-valued implies the condition
u_{nm}=\bar{u}_{-n,-m}
and thus we can ignore the negative
n
-modes. By identifying
z^{n/2}=r^{n/2}e^{in\phi}
, we conclude

\displaystyle A(\theta)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{1m}\>e^{im\theta}\quad\text{and}\quad B(\theta)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{3m}\>e^{im\theta}.

(16)

Therefore, to study expansion in
A
and
B
functions, it is sufficient to study the

and
u_{3m}
Fourier modes.

We estimate
u_{nm}
by using
Proposition

2.4
:

Lemma 2.5
.

Using the parametrization
(R_{0},R_{0}+s)\times T^{2}
of a neck region, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
s>0
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n
odd, such that for all compactly supported on the interior,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-antisymmetric
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(\hat{M})
, we have

|u_{nm}|\leq e^{-\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}}s}\frac{C}{I_{nm}(R_{0})}\|\operatorname{d}\!^{*}\omega\|_{D^{0,\alpha}}

for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and odd
n
.

Proof.

Let
u
be a solution of
Equation

4
.
According to
Proposition

2.4

\|u\|_{D^{2,\alpha}(\overline{M})}\leq C\|\operatorname{d}\!^{*}\omega\|_{D^{0,\alpha}(\overline{M})}

which implies
\|u\|_{C^{0}((R_{0},R_{0}+s)\times T^{2})}\leq C\|\operatorname{d}\!^{*}\omega\|_{D^{0,\alpha}(\overline{M})}.

The map that projects
u
to the Fourier mode

is a bounded map
and so

|u_{nm}|\cdot\sup_{r}I_{nm}(r)\leq C\|\operatorname{d}\!^{*}\omega\|_{D^{0,\alpha}(\overline{M})}.

Because
I_{nm}(r)
is positive and strictly increasing due to the maximum principle,

|u_{nm}|\cdot I_{nm}(R_{0}+s)\leq C\|\operatorname{d}\!^{*}\omega\|_{D^{0,\alpha}(\overline{M})}.

Let
\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{16}+m^{2}}
.
We claim that
\frac{I_{nm}(R_{0})}{I_{nm}(R_{0}+s)}e^{\alpha s}\leq 2
.
If this is true, then

\displaystyle|u_{nm}|\leq

\displaystyle e^{\alpha s}\frac{I_{nm}(R_{0})}{I_{nm}(R_{0}+s)}\cdot e^{-\alpha s}\frac{C}{I_{nm}(R_{0})}\|\operatorname{d}\!^{*}\omega\|_{D^{0,\alpha}(\overline{M})}

(17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\alpha s}\frac{2C}{I_{nm}(R_{0})}\|\operatorname{d}\!^{*}\omega\|_{D^{0,\alpha}(\overline{M})}.

(18)

which is what we need to show.

To prove the claim, recall that

solves
Equation

I_{nm}(r)=c_{nm}\left(e^{\alpha(r-R_{0})}+c^{\prime}_{nm}e^{-\alpha(r-R_{0})}\right).

(19)

As

cannot be bounded,

. Similarly, the positivity of

With these constrains, we calculate
\frac{I_{nm}(R_{0})}{I_{nm}(R_{0}+s)}e^{\alpha s}

is chosen such that
\frac{I_{nm}(R_{0})}{I_{nm}(R_{0}+s)}e^{\alpha s}

\frac{I_{nm}(R_{0})}{I_{nm}(R_{0}+s)}e^{\alpha s}=\frac{1+c^{\prime}_{nm}}{1+c^{\prime}_{nm}e^{-2\alpha s}}

Because

3
The Nash–Moser Theorem

Applying
Lemma

2.5
on the asymptotic expansion that is given in the
A
and
B
terms, we expect that the
A(\theta)
term will exponentially decay when
s
becomes arbitrary large. Next we show that when the neck is sufficiently long and the decay of the
A(\theta)
term is sufficiently fast, then the
A(\theta)
term can be set to zero by slightly perturbing the singular set
\Sigma
. For this we repeat the argument given by Donaldson
[
10
]
. Namely, in his paper he showed that when one has a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic function and one slightly perturbs the Riemannian metric, one can always find a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic function by slightly perturbing the singular set. Due to the similarities of the problems, we start with his setup and make some small alterations.

Donaldson showed his deformation theorem using the Nash–Moser theorem. Explicitly, he used the implicit function theorem given by Hamilton
[
21
]
. To explain this, we have to revisit the notion of Fréchet spaces and tame estimates.

Recall that
a graded Fréchet space
is a vector space
\mathcal{F}
equipped with an sequence of seminorms
\{\|\ldots\|_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\|f\|_{0}\leq\|f\|_{1}\leq\|f\|_{2}\leq\ldots

It is a metric space using the translation-invariant metric
d(f,g)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}\frac{\|f-g\|_{k}}{1+\|f-g\|_{k}}
.
Canonical examples of Fréchet spaces are Sobolev and Hölder spaces.

Next, let
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
be Fréchet spaces and let
\mathcal{U}
be an open subset of
\mathcal{F}
.
We say that a map
\mathbf{P}\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{G}
is a
tame map
if there exists
r,b\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every integer
n\geq b
there exists a constant
C_{n}>0
such that for all
f\in U

\|\mathbf{P}(f)\|_{n}\leq C_{n}(1+\|f\|_{n+r}).

If
P
is a smooth map and all derivatives are tame, we then say that
P
is a
smooth tame map
. Partial differential operators are examples of smooth tame maps.

In order to understand the work of Donaldson
[
10
]
, we need to introduce the notion of invertibility with quadratic error. Namely, let

By

∎

We finally show that

can be applied on our model problem.

Proposition 3.7
.

When
s
is sufficiently large and Assumptions

,

and

is invertible with

-quadratic error. Moreover, for each

, there exists a

\Sigma
, such that

has a unique

-harmonic 1-form with singular set

. The cohomology class of this

-harmonic 1-form is

, where

Proof.

Using

3.5
write

is
\tilde{B}(\theta)\cdot\nu+\mathbf{Q}(\tilde{\Sigma},a,\nu)

\mathbf{E}

In

Using

∎

4
The non-decaying modes

In the previous section we have seen that under three additional assumptions, one can construct a family of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-harmonic 1-forms on the model space
(M,g_{s})
. While Assumption
A1
can be regarded as a normalization and Assumption
A3
resembles a non-degeneracy condition, Assumption
A2
is highly non-trivial.
In this section we will show that for a generic metric and a suitable choice of cohomology class, all assumptions can be satisfied.

We prove this in three steps. First we consider a fixed cohomology class and we study the limiting behaviour of the Fourier modes
u_{n0}
. We show that in the limit we are actually doing analysis on manifolds with cylindrical ends, which is a much simpler problem as in
b
-calculus a cokernel is almost always finite dimensional.

Secondly, we show that for a generic metric this cokernel can be spanned if
H^{1}_{-}(\hat{M})
is sufficiently large. So using Gram–Schmidt one can satisfy the assumptions for the limiting case where the neck has infinite length. Finally, we show that the cohomology classes can be perturbed when the neck has an arbitrary long but finite length.

In order to prove the the second step, we have to revisit the proof of He
[
16
]
. Namely, we will calculate the variation of
u_{n0}
under the variation of the metric in the limit where the neck is infinitely long. We will see that for a generic metric and fixed cohomology class, Assumption
A3
is satisfied, while Assumption
A2
will likely fail. By generalizing this proof we show that the Gram–Schmidt process can be done for a generic metric.

4.1
The Fourier modes of
L^{2}
-bounded
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

In the previous section we considered a fixed model space

Outside the support of

Like we did in

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

of

For every positive odd integer

Remark 4.2
.

Recall that on the neck and boundary regions, the model solutions

The condition

\displaystyle\int_{\hat{M}}

\displaystyle G_{n}\Delta_{g_{s}}f\operatorname{Vol}^{g_{s}}

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8n\pi^{2}}\lim\limits_{r_{0}\to 0}\int_{\{r_{0}\}\times T^{2}}\left(r^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-in\phi}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}+\frac{n}{2}r^{-\frac{n}{2}-1}fe^{-in\phi}\right)2r\operatorname{d}\!\phi\wedge\operatorname{d}\!\theta

.

\displaystyle f_{n0}.

(40)

∎

The factor

We conclude

4.3
Proof of the assumptions

In

4.9

and

Lemma 4.12

Assume

Let

\begin{pmatrix}\Re(v^{\mathrm{cyl}}_{10}(\omega_{1},\Sigma))&\Re(v^{\mathrm{cyl}}_{10}(\omega_{2},\Sigma))\\
\Im(v^{\mathrm{cyl}}_{10}(\omega_{1},\Sigma))&\Im(v^{\mathrm{cyl}}_{10}(\omega_{2},\Sigma))\end{pmatrix}.

is not zero, then we can find a basis of

To simplify our calculations, we pick a basis

4.9

Next we consider the variation of the determinant under small variations of the metric. Using the basis

of

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}\det(V)=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}\Im(v^{\mathrm{cyl}}_{10}(\omega_{2},\Sigma))-\Im(v^{\mathrm{cyl}}_{10}(\omega_{1},\Sigma))\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}\Re(v^{\mathrm{cyl}}_{10}(\omega_{2},\Sigma)).
