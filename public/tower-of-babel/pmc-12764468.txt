Riboflavin-PLGA nanoparticles enhance the effectiveness of the pyruvate carboxylase inhibitor ZY-444 in targeting lipogenesis in colon and breast cancer
Authors: Siraprapa Siritutsoontorn, Preeyanuch Manohong, Natthapat Sawektreeratana, Chutima Kuhakarn, Kanlaya Prapainop Katewongsa, Sarawut Jitrapakdee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Targeting cancer metabolism has become a promising strategy for cancer treatment. Pyruvate carboxylase (PC) is an anaplerotic enzyme that replenishes tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates. PC is overexpressed in several types of human cancers, especially in aggressive cancers, including breast and colon cancer, where it promotes growth, survival, and metastasis. Recently, N4-((5-(4-(benzyloxy)phenyl)-2-thiophenyl)methyl)-N2-isobutyl-2,4-pyrimidinediamine (ZY-444) has been reported to possess anti-cancer activity by inhibiting PC activity in breast cancer. In this study, we expanded the potential of ZY-444 to highly aggressive colon cancer, HT-29 and HCT116, and further enhanced its efficacy using nanodelivery systems. The results reveal that ZY-444 not only inhibits the clonogenic growth of these colon cancer cells but also downregulates the expression of several key lipogenic enzymes, accompanied by a marked reduction in triglyceride and cholesterol levels. To improve its efficacy, ZY-444 was encapsulated in poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles with a surface coated with riboflavin (Rf)-functionalized chitosan (CSRf), a ligand known to target Rf transporters. The ZY-444-loaded PLGA nanoparticles exhibited a spherical morphology with an approximate size of 300 nm and a positive zeta potential, demonstrating their suitability for drug delivery applications. Compared with the free compound, ZY-444 encapsulated in nanoparticles demonstrated markedly enhanced efficacy being 11-, 14-, and 19-fold more potent in inhibiting the clonogenic growth of HT-29, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 cells, respectively. In addition, the encapsulated form was over 10 times more effective at suppressing the migration and invasion of HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 cells. Encapsulated ZY-444 also more strongly inhibited the expression of lipogenic enzymes, further reinforcing its effect on lipid metabolism. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of ZY-444-loaded PLGA nanoparticles as a promising drug delivery system for suppressing colon and breast cancer through targeted inhibition of the de novo lipogenesis pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29207-8.

Introduction

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, enabling tumor cells to alter their metabolism to support rapid proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance. These adaptations include enhanced aerobic glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, glutaminolysis, and de novo lipogenesis to fulfill the anabolic demands required for sustained growth and survival1–4. Targeting key metabolic enzymes in these pathways has gained significant attention as a strategy to inhibit cancer growth and metastasis5,6. Pyruvate carboxylase (PC) is an important anaplerotic enzyme that replenishes tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates by carboxylating pyruvate to form oxaloacetate. This reaction sustains the biosynthetic pathways necessary for tumor progression. Previous studies have shown that PC is overexpressed in various types of cancers, with its expression correlating with clinicopathological parameters and poor prognosis in cancer patients7–10. Both genetic ablation of the PC gene and pharmacological inhibition of PC activity have been shown to inhibit the growth of many cancers including breast7,11, lung12,13, colon14, prostate15–17, thyroid9,18–20, glioblastoma21–23, ovarian10, and liver cancer24, highlighting its extensive role as a pro-oncogenic enzyme. Therefore, effective PC inhibitors are promising candidates for cancer treatment. Recently, compound (N4-((5-(4-(benzyloxy)phenyl)−2-thiophenyl)methyl)-N2-isobutyl-2,4-pyrimidinediamine (ZY-444) has been reported to possess an anti-cancer activity in vitro and in vivo by inhibiting PC activity25. ZY-444 effectively inhibits breast cancer growth, demonstrating a lower IC50 against breast cancer cells compared to paclitaxel, a widely used chemotherapy drug. This compound also inhibits breast cancer growth and lung metastasis in nude mice, indicating its potential role as the anti-cancer agent25. Despite the well-established therapeutic potential of ZY-444 in breast cancer, its efficacy against colon cancer remains largely unexplored. Moreover, ZY-444 has not yet been incorporated into a nanoparticle-based delivery system, which could significantly enhance its therapeutic efficacy and targeted delivery.

Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is a widely used biodegradable and biocompatible polymer approved by the FDA for drug delivery applications. It is considered safe and can deliver a variety of therapeutic agents, including small RNAs, peptides, small molecules, and both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs26. To further enhance the delivery efficiency and specificity, the PLGA nanoparticles can be modified with antibodies27, peptides28, or ligands29. Encapsulating drugs in nanoparticles decorated with cancer-specific ligands not only enhances the bioavailability of these drugs but also sustains their release, prolonging their action at the targeted site. Recently, riboflavin (Rf) has been utilized as a conjugated ligand in various nanocarriers for drug delivery systems, including liposomes, PLGA nanoparticles, magnetic nanoparticles, and dendrimers30–34. These Rf-functionalized nanoparticles have successfully enhanced drug delivery to cancer cells.

We have demonstrated, for the first time, that Rf-functionalized PLGA nanoparticles effectively deliver ZY-444 to a highly invasive colon cancer cell line, enhancing its anticancer activity compared to the free form of the drug.

Results and discussion

Spectroscopic data of ZY-444

N4-((5-(4-(Benzyloxy)phenyl)-2-thiophenyl)methyl)-N2-isobutyl-2,4-pyrimidinediamine (ZY-444) was synthesized by following the previously published protocol reported by Fan et al.35 This compound was synthesized by five sequential steps as shown in Fig. 1A, and its identity was verified by 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, and HRMS analysis. 1H-NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) of ZY-444 exhibited resonances as the following: δ 7.83 (d, J = 5.5 Hz, 1H), 7.49–7.37 (m, 6H), 7.35–7.31 (m, 1H), 7.02 (d, J = 3.6 Hz, 1H), 6.99–6.94 (m, 2H), 6.92 (d, J = 3.6 Hz, 1H), 5.72 (d, J = 5.8 Hz, 1H), 5.08 (s, 2H), 4.99 (br s, 1H), 4.67 (d, J = 5.7 Hz, 2H), 3.23 (t, J = 6.4 Hz, 2H), 2.05 (br s, 1H), 1.91–1.84 (m, 1H), 0.96 (d, J = 6.6 Hz, 6H) ppm (Fig. 1B). 13C{1H} NMR (100 MHz, CDCl3) exhibited resonances as the following: δ 162.46, 162.25, 158.38, 156.05, 143.92, 140.26, 136.78, 128.61 (2C), 128.03, 127.45 (2C), 127.41, 126.96 (2C), 126.49, 121.60, 115.20 (2C), 94.62, 70.08, 48.94, 40.15, 28.52, 20.30 (2C) ppm (Fig. 1C). Molecular formula of ZY-444 was established by high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HRESIMS), which showed [M + H]+ peak at m/z 445.2058 (calcd. for C26H29N4OS, 445.2057) (Fig. 1D). The purity of synthesized ZY-444 was determined from the area fraction of LC chromatogram, which was observed at the retention time of 1.5 min showing 96.74%, which is acceptable for biological testing (> 95% purity)36.

ZY-444 inhibited breast and colon cancer cell growth

The inhibitory effect of ZY-444 on growth of cancer cells was examined in two colon cancer cell lines (HT-29 and HCT116) and two breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7), each with different degrees of aggressiveness. HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 are highly aggressive colon and breast cancer cell lines, respectively, whereas HT-29 and MCF-7 have low-to-moderate and low aggressiveness37,38. 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) and paclitaxel are first-line anti-cancer drugs for colon cancer39 and breast cancer40 treatment, respectively. To compare the efficacy of ZY-444 with both conventional anti-cancer drugs, an MTT assay was performed. ZY-444 inhibited the growth of these four cancer cell lines in a dose-dependent manner. As shown in Fig. 2A, the 50% inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of ZY-444 for HCT116, HT-29, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-231 cell lines, were approximately 1.36 µM, 1.53 µM, 1.80 µM, and 3.06 µM, respectively. In comparison, the IC50 of 5-FU for HCT116 and HT-29 were 8.66 µM and 18.78 µM, respectively, while the IC50 of paclitaxel for MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 were 0.20 µM and 7.19 µM, respectively. Interestingly, HT-29 and HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited greater sensitivity to ZY-444 compared to 5-FU and paclitaxel, whereas MCF-7 cells were less sensitive to ZY-444 than to paclitaxel. However, ZY-444 at a concentration of 5 µM demonstrated a more significant inhibitory effect on the growth of all cancer cell lines compared to both conventional anticancer drugs.

As reported by Lin et al.25, ZY-444 inhibits breast cancer cell line by inhibiting PC activity, suggesting that cell lines with higher PC protein would be more sensitive to this compound. Western blot analysis of PC expression in these cell lines indicated that the MCF-7, HT-29 and HCT116 had PC protein 25%, 50% and 75% lower than MDA-MB-231 cell line, respectively (Fig. 2B). This result is consistent with the lower IC50 for ZY-444 in the MDA-MB-231, HT-29, MCF-7, and higher IC50 in the HCT116 cell lines, which as a very low PC level.

Physical characterization of the ZY-444-loaded PLGA nanoparticles

Due to the presence of several hydrophobic functional groups, including the pyrimidine ring, thiophene ring, benzyl group, and isopropyl group (Fig. 1), ZY-444 exhibits poor water solubility, which limits its efficacy as a chemotherapeutic drug. Therefore, the encapsulation of ZY-444 within nanoparticles is employed to overcome these challenges. Additionally, the increased uptake of Rf to support the growth of several cancers makes it a promising ligand to improve bioavailability and cellular uptake of this compound into cancer cells through riboflavin transporters (RFVTs)41. Rf-citrate ester (CARf) was used as a ligand to conjugate with chitosan to produce the Rf-functionalized chitosan (CSRf). The CSRf was then coated onto the surface of PLGA (Fig. 3A) to create a CSRf-PLGA nanoparticle shell that can encapsulate ZY-444. The hydrodynamic diameters, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potentials of three coating PLGA including PLGA-CSRf, PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444, and PLGA-CSRf-TRITC, are shown in Table 1. The average sizes of PLGA-CSRf, PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444, and PLGA-CSRf-TRITC were approximately 253 nm, 295 nm, and 256 nm, respectively. The PDI of the Rf-functionalized PLGA was in range of 0.2–0.6, which represents a highly monodisperse, and acceptable for drug delivery systems. The zeta potential values of PLGA-CSRf, PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444, and PLGA-CSRf-TRITC were + 12.91, + 12.05, and + 9.69 mV, respectively, indicating the successful coating of chitosan on PLGA nanoparticles. The encapsulation efficiency of ZY-444 is represented in Table 1. Furthermore, the SEM images revealed the spherical shape with smooth surface of both PLGA-CSRf, and PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 (Fig. 3B). FTIR spectrum of PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 showed an increase in the -C = C- stretching peaks at 1450–1650 cm−1, representing the aromatic rings of the ZY-444 compound. The increased intensity at 1200–1300 cm−1 corresponded to the -C-O- stretching of the ZY-444 structure (Fig. 3C).

Cellular uptake of Rf-functionalized PLGA nanoparticles into cancer cells

An efficient cellular uptake of nanoparticles enhances the delivery of anti-cancer drugs to cancer cells. Upon internalization into cancer cells, the encapsulated drug is released from the polymeric cavity following the degradation of the polymeric nanoparticles. Interestingly, Rf-functionalized PLGA nanoparticles have been reported as an effective drug delivery system for breast cancer31 but their potential application in colon cancer and their intracellular fate remains unexplored. Here, we evaluated the uptake efficiency and intracellular trafficking of Rf-functionalized nanoparticles in both colon and breast cancer cells. TRITC, a commonly used fluorescent dye, was used to visualize the uptake of the nanoparticles to the cells due to its chemical stability and biocompatibility. To investigate cellular uptake of Rf-functionalized PLGA nanoparticles, PLGA-CSRf-TRITC was used to visualize the co-localization of the nanoparticles and lysosome in HT-29, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 cells using a fluorescence microscope. Once the nanoparticles enter cells, they are translocated to endosomes and eventually lysosomes. LAMP1 (Lysosome-Associated Membrane Protein 1), a well-established lysosomal marker, was used to confirm whether nanoparticles were translocated to lysosomes. As shown in Fig. 4, the TRITC-loaded nanoparticles were internalized into three cancer cell lines in a dose-dependent manner. Their uptake was likely mediated by the interaction between Rf-functionalized nanoparticles and RFVTs overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells41,42. In circulation, Rf binds to a soluble riboflavin carrier protein (RCP) before being transported into cells via RFVTs. Notably, elevated serum Rf levels have been associated with an increased risk of colon cancer43. Additionally, RCP levels were found to be elevated in the sera of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma44, breast cancer45, and prostate cancer46. Furthermore, co-localization of the TRITC-loaded nanoparticles within lysosomes was observed, showing merged yellow signals localized around the nuclei. Interestingly, the uptake of TRITC-loaded nanoparticles was higher in MDA-MB-231 and HCT116 cells than in HT-29 cells at the same concentrations (Fig. 4A-C). The lower uptake of Rf-functionalized nanoparticles in HT-29 cells compared to others may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, HT-29 cells have lower expression level of RFVTs, including RFVT1-3, than HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 cells (data not shown). Tutino et al.47 have revealed differential expression levels of RFVTs among different colorectal cancer cell lines, and loss of RFVT1 expression was observed in HT-29 cells. Moreover, HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 cells are known to have higher metabolic activity and often upregulate various nutrient transporters, including RFVTs, to meet their energy demands48. Apart from the RFVT-mediated uptake, the nanoparticles may be taken up by different endocytic mechanisms. HT-29 cells primarily use clathrin-mediated endocytosis49,50, while HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 cells exhibit multiple uptake pathways such as clathrin-, caveolae-, and macropinocytosis-mediated endocytosis50,51, increasing their ability to internalize Rf-functionalized nanoparticles. Lastly, HT-29 cells form tight epithelial-like junctions and secrete a dense extracellular matrix, acting as a physical barrier that limits nanoparticle access52. In contrast, HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 cells, with a more mesenchymal phenotype, possess looser extracellular matrix structures, allowing greater nanoparticle access to the cell membrane53.

ZY-444-loaded PLGA nanoparticles enhanced inhibitory effects on cell viability and colony formation

To examine whether the encapsulation of ZY-444 with Rf-functionalized nanoparticles enhances the inhibitory effect on the growth of both colon and breast cancer cell lines compared to free ZY-444 compound, cell viability was performed. As shown in Fig. 5A-C, ZY-444 inhibited the growth of all three cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner, with MDA-MB-231 cells being more sensitive than others. The IC50 of ZY-444 in HT-29, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 cells were 3.58 µM, 2.51 µM, and 2.38 µM, while the IC50 of PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 were 1.23 µM, 0.19 µM, and 0.13 µM, respectively. According to this result, the encapsulation of ZY-444 in Rf-functionalized nanoparticles resulted in 3-fold, 13.6-fold, and 19.2-fold improvement of drug sensitivity in HT-29, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 cell lines, respectively. However, growth inhibition was not observed in the group of cancer cells being exposed to unencapsulated PLGA, indicating that the inhibitory effect on the growth of cancer cells was not attributed to the PLGA shell.

Because the MTT assay only measures short-term metabolic activity, a clonogenic assay is necessary to determine whether cancer cells can survive and regrow after treatment, making it a critical assay for evaluating long-term therapeutic effects. To further investigate whether ZY-444 encapsulated in Rf-functionalized nanoparticles enhances its inhibitory effect on clonogenic growth, a clonogenic assay was performed. As shown in Fig. 6A-C, fewer concentrations of ZY-444 were required to inhibit clonogenic growth of all cancer cell lines. PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 at 0.1 µM was found to inhibit clonogenic growth by approximately 40–50% in colon cancer cells and 70% in breast cancer cells. All three cancer cell lines treated with PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 at 0.5 µM completely failed to establish the colony, while those being exposed to free ZY-444 at the same concentration were still able to establish their colonies. These data indicate that PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 nanoparticles enhanced the inhibitory effect on clonogenic growth in both colon and breast cancer cells. The enhanced inhibitory effects observed herein may be partly attributed to the improved cellular uptake of Rf-functionalized nanoparticles, facilitated by the binding of RCP and RFVTs.

Moreover, the enhanced inhibitory effect of Rf-functionalized PLGA on cancer growth was also consistent with many previous studies of vitamin-targeted PLGA nanoparticles. El-Hammadi et al.54 showed that the delivery of 5-FU by PLGA-PEG-folate improved the anti-cancer activity of 5-FU in MCF-7 and HT-29 cells with IC50 less than free 5-FU by 5- and 9-fold, respectively, suggesting that the PLGA acts as a potential drug delivery system for 5-FU in breast and colon cancers. Li et al.55 also demonstrated that folate-functionalized PLGA in the co-delivery of docetaxel and gemcitabine improved their cytotoxicity with a 3.6-fold decrease in IC50 in ovarian cancer, SKOV-3 cells. Moreover, the drugs-loaded PLGA-folate also showed a 4-fold reduction in tumor volume in nude mice bearing SKOV-3 compared with free drugs without toxicity to normal organs. Another study by Singh et al.56 showed that encapsulation of doxorubicin (DOX) in PLGA-PEG-biotin showed a 7-fold increase in half-life and lowered the IC50 for breast cancer 4T1 cells by 2-fold compared to free drug. Furthermore, a 3-fold greater reduction in tumor volume and burden was also observed in 4T1 breast cancer mice receiving PLGA-PEG-biotin-DOX compared to free drug.

ZY-444-loaded nanoparticles enhanced inhibitory effects on cell migration and invasion

We next assessed the inhibitory effect of ZY-444 loaded PLGA on migration and invasion, the two key oncogenic properties in the highly metastatic cell lines, HCT116 and MDA-MB-231. PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 at 0.25 µM and 0.5 µM showed inhibition of migration and invasion ability of HCT116 cells by approximately 25% and 65%, respectively while requiring up to 1–2 µM of free ZY-444 to achieve the inhibition by approximately 30% and 70%, respectively (Fig. 7A). Similarly, these two concentrations of PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 can inhibit migration and invasion of MDA-MB-231 cells by approximately 40%−70%, respectively (Fig. 7B). These results indicate that encapsulation of ZY-444 in Rf-functionalized nanoparticles enhances the inhibition of breast and colon cancer cell motility more effectively than free ZY-444.

ZY-444-loaded nanoparticles enhanced down-regulation of key lipogenic enzyme accompanied by reduced triglyceride and cholesterol levels

Up-regulation of de novo lipogenesis and cholesterol synthesis, which serve as a cellular structure and energy sources, is well- known to support cancer growth57. Previous studies have shown that genetic knockdown or knockout of PC in thyroid and colon cancer9,14 causes the down-regulation of key lipogenic enzymes. Similarly, pharmacological inhibition of PC activity by ZY-444 could further validate the effects observed with genetic suppression. To compare the effect of ZY-444-loaded nanoparticles on the expression of key lipogenic enzymes including acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1), fatty acid synthase (FASN), pyruvate carboxylase (PC), and HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR), with the free ZY-444 compound, the expression of these enzymes was examined by qRT-PCR and Western blotting. The qRT-PCR results showed that inhibition of lipogenic enzyme expression is enhanced in HT-29, HCT116 and MDA-MB-231 cells treated with PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 more than free ZY-444. Among cell lines, MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited the highest sensitivity to both free ZY-444 and PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 treatments, showing a significant reduction in ACC1, FASN, and HMGCR mRNA levels. PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 effectively suppressed the expression levels of ACC1 and FASN by 20–70% in all cell lines, while the expression levels of HMGCR were decreased by 50–60% in HCT116 and MDA-MB-23 but not affected in HT-29 cells. However, the expression level of PC was upregulated at the mRNA levels in all cell lines, suggesting that a compensatory mechanism may be operating at the transcriptional level in response to PC inhibition (Fig. 8A-C). Western blot analysis confirmed that PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 markedly reduced ACC1 and FASN protein levels in all cell lines, with the most pronounced inhibition observed in MDA-MB-231 cell line. In contrast, HMGCR protein expression was decreased in both MDA-MB-231 and HCT116 cell lines, while it was not affected in HT-29 cell line. Notably, PC protein levels remained unchanged across all cell lines. Collectively, these findings indicate that the nanoformulated PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 exerts a more potent inhibitory effect on lipogenic enzymes than the free ZY-444 compound (Fig. 8D–F).

To confirm the down-regulation of key enzymes in fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis, the triglyceride and cholesterol levels in ZY-444 treated cells were measured. For triglyceride measurement, the reduced triglyceride levels were correlated with the down-regulation of ACC1 and FASN mRNAs and proteins. In general, triglyceride levels in all three cell lines treated with PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 at 2.5 µM were lower than those treated with free ZY-444 at both 2.5 µM and 5 µM concentrations. The downregulation of lipogenic enzymes in response to PC inhibition may result from multiple interconnected mechanisms involving metabolic and transcriptional cross-talk58,59. PC inhibition is expected to reduce oxaloacetate levels, thereby limiting the availability of key intermediates required for lipogenesis. This reduction may activate feedback mechanisms that suppress the expression of lipogenic enzymes. Additionally, the downregulation of lipogenic enzymes in PC-deficient cells may be linked to the down-regulation of two key transcription factors: sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP1c) and carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein-α (ChREBPα). These transcription factors regulate lipogenic gene expression60, and disruptions in their activity due to altered metabolite levels or energy status could lead to decreased expression of lipogenic enzymes. These finding agree with previous studies of Liu et al.9, which demonstrated that PC was overexpressed to support fatty acid synthesis in thyroid cancer for its growth and metastasis. In PC-knockdown thyroid cancer cells, the expression of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis including SREBP1c and FASN, was decreased, accompanied by lower triglyceride and lipid droplet levels. Similarly, a study by Ngamkham et al.14 reported the down-regulation of ACC1 and FASN in PC knockout HT-29 colon cancer cell lines. Liu et al.9 also reported that PC promotes SREBP1c expression through activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling cascade in thyroid cancer cells. Furthermore, ACC1 and FASN expression was increased in HER2-overexpressing breast cancer cells via the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway61.

In this study, ZY-444-treated PC inhibition suppressed breast and colon cancer growth and motility through the down-regulation of key lipogenic enzymes, which was concomitant with reduced triglyceride and cholesterol levels. Lipids, including fatty acids and cholesterol, are well-known to play crucial roles in various biological processes, serving as an energy source, constituting the primary components of cell membranes, and acting as precursors for signaling molecules that drive cancer progression57. Additionally, cholesterol and sphingolipids, the major constituents of lipid rafts, are involved in regulating membrane fluidity and activating key signaling pathways, such as PI3K/Akt, MAPK, and FAK, to facilitate cancer cell survival, adhesion, and motility62. Therefore, the impairment of lipid synthesis can potentially disrupt cancer cell growth, migration, and invasion.

Conclusion

In summary, this study is the first to demonstrate that ZY-444, previously known for its anticancer activity in breast cancer, also has inhibitory effects on colon cancer cells. ZY-444 significantly reduced proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion of highly invasive colon cancer cells. The downregulation of key lipogenic enzymes and the reduction of intracellular triglyceride and cholesterol levels indicate a high potential for cancer lipid metabolism. Moreover, encapsulating ZY-444 in Rf-functionalized PLGA nanoparticles enhances its anticancer efficacy. The nanoparticles enhanced cellular uptake, resulting in a stronger suppression of proliferation and metastasis in both colon and breast cancer cells. These findings not only expand the therapeutic potential use of ZY-444 but also underscore the promise of this nanoparticle-based drug delivery system in developing personalized cancer therapies, offering a novel approach in the fight against breast and colon cancers.

Methods

Cell culture

All cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Colon cancer [HT-29 (ATCC-HTB-38) and HCT116 (ATCC-CCL-247)] and breast cancer [MDA-MB-231 (ATCC-HTB-26) and MCF-7 (ATCC-HTB-22)] cell lines were cultured in DMEM/F12, McCoy’s 5a and DMEM-high glucose medium, respectively. All media were supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 100 units/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Gibco). All cell lines were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. The confluent cells were sub-cultured every 2–3 days once they reached 80% confluency. The authenticity of the cell lines used in this study was confirmed by STR profiling with the PowerPlex Fusion 6 C System.

Chemical synthesis of ZY-444

The ZY-444 compound was synthesized by following the previously published protocol of Fan et al.35 with modifications (Supplementary material S1). General: 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE 400 spectrometer (400 MHz) in CDCl3 using tetramethylsilane (δ = 0 ppm) or the residual non-deuterated solvent peak as an internal reference. 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE 400 spectrometer (100 MHz). High-Resolution Mass Spectra (HRMS) were recorded on a Bruker Q-TOF spectrometer in the ESI mode. Reactions were monitored by Thin-Layer Chromatography and visualized by UV. All reagents and solvents were obtained from commercial sources and used without further purification. Chromatographic methods were performed using silica gel 60 (Merck, 70–230 mesh). LCMS analysis was performed on a Bruker Elute UHPLC coupled with a Bruker compact QTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co.KG) ESI QTOF-MS equipped with a Data Analysis version 6.0 System, and was operated by Hystar 5.0 software.

Preparation of riboflavin-functionalized PLGA nanoparticles

Riboflavin (Rf)-citrate ester (CARf) and Riboflavin-functionalized chitosan (CSRf) were synthesized and characterized following the procedures described by Mekseriwattana et al.31 Rf-functionalized PLGA nanoparticles were prepared using a single emulsion solvent evaporation method63. Fifty milligrams of PLGA, with or without 2.5 mg of ZY-444 (20:1), was dissolved in 0.5 ml of dichloromethane (oil phase) before adding dropwise into 2 ml of 5% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution (water phase) containing 15 mg of CSRf with vigorous mixing. The mixture was then emulsified by sonication at 38% amplitude for 30 s for 3 cycles. After that, the mixture was added to 25 ml of 0.3% PVA and stirred for 2 h to evaporate the organic solvent. The synthesized nanoparticles were separated and washed twice by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 min.

Physical characterization of nanoparticles

The nanoparticles were characterized by measuring their sizes and charges (zeta potential) using a Zetasizer instrument (Malvern Instruments, Melvern, UK). Their morphology was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Quanta250, FEI, US). The encapsulation of ZY-444 in Rf-functionalized nanoparticles was also characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Bruker ALPHA FTIR spectrometer, Bruker, US). To determine the amount of ZY-444 encapsulated in the nanoparticles, the particles were weighed and added to methanol solution before being sonicated and mixed for 2 cycles to release the ZY-444 dissolved in methanol. ZY-444 was then separated from the PLGA aggregates by centrifugation before being subjected to HPLC (Agilent 1110 Series HPLC system, Agilent Technologies, USA). An eclipse XDB-C8 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm) was used with a mobile phase consisted of methanol: water (95: 5). The concentration of encapsulated ZY-444 was quantitated from the standard curve of the free ZY-444 compound at different concentrations and is presented as microgram (µg) of ZY-444 per mg of nanoparticles. In vitro uptake assay

1 × 105 cells of HT-29, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 were plated into round coverslips in a 24-well plate and incubated in a CO2 incubator for 24 h. The cells were treated with 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/ml of PLGA-CSRf-TRITC, diluted in serum-free media for 4 h. After incubation, cells were washed three times with complete media and once with 1xPBS before fixing with 3.7% (v/v) formaldehyde for 10 min. The fixed cells were washed twice with 1xPBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X for 10 min. The permeabilized cells were washed three times with 1xPBS, followed by blocking with 1%BSA in 1xTBST for 30 min. The cells were then incubated with a 1:200 dilution of anti-LAMP1 (sc-19992, Santa Cruz) at 4 °C in the dark overnight. The cells were washed three times with 1xPBS before incubating with 1:200 dilution of secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (ab150165, Abcam) at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were washed three times with 1xPBS, stained for nuclei with 0.5 µg/ml DAPI for 5 min, and then washed with 1xPBST three times. Lastly, the stained cells were mounted with the antifade mounting medium and visualized under the fluorescent microscope (ZEISS Apotome 3, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany).

MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]−2,5 Diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) assay

To investigate the cytotoxicity of ZY-444, 5 × 103 of HT-29, HCT116, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-7 cells were plated into a 96-well plate and incubated in a CO2 incubator for 24 h. The cells were then treated with various concentrations of ZY-444 in complete media for 48 h. To investigate the effect of ZY-444-loaded nanoparticles, 5 × 103 of HT-29, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 cells were plated and incubated for 24 h before treating with various concentrations of free ZY-444, PLGA-CSRf, and PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 in serum-free media for 48 h. After incubation, cells were washed with complete media and 1xPBS before replacing with 100 µl of fresh medium containing 1 mg/mL MTT solution and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After that, the MTT solution was removed, the formazan product was dissolved by adding 100 µL of DMSO. The absorbance of formazan product, which is directly proportional to the number of living cells was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan skyhigh, Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK).

Clonogenic assay

650 cells of HT-29, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 were plated into 35 mm dishes in media containing 10% FBS for 24 h before treating with various concentrations of free ZY-444, PLGA-CSRf, and PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 in serum-free media for 48 h. After treatment, the culture media was replaced with fresh complete media, and the cells were allowed to grow until colonies were formed for 14 days. Once the colonies were visible, the culture media were removed, and the colonies were washed with 1x PBS. The colonies were then fixed with 700 µL of absolute methanol for 20 min and stained with 0.5% (w/v) crystal violet in 25% methanol overnight in the dark. Excess dye was removed, and the dishes were rinsed with tap water. The 35-mm culture dishes were air-dried before counting the colonies in five random fields under light microscope.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Migration and invasion assays were performed using a Boyden chamber assay with transwell cell culture inserts (Corning, Corning Incorporated, US). 2 × 105 cells were plated into a 6-well plate and incubated in a CO2 incubator for 24 h. Cells were treated with various concentrations of ZY-444, and PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444 in complete or serum-free media for 48 h. After treatment, cells were trypsinized for 2 min, and the digestion was stopped by adding medium containing 5% FBS. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 3 min, and resuspended in the serum-free medium. 1 × 105 or 1.5 × 105 cells for MDA-MB-231 or HCT116 cell, respectively, were diluted in 200 µl of serum-free medium were re-plated into the upper chamber of the Transwell, which was pre-coated with 20 µg of Matrigel for invasion. 600 µl of medium supplemented with 10–20% FBS was added into the lower chamber, and the chamber was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the non-migrated/invaded cells were removed with a cotton swab and washed twice with 1xPBS. The migrated/invaded cells were fixed with 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde in 1xPBS for 20 min and stained with 0.5% (w/v) crystal violet in 25% (v/v) methanol overnight. Finally, the migrated/invaded cells were washed twice with tap water before imaging under a microscope with a 10x magnification.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted and purified using an RNAspin Mini Kit (Cytiva), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of RNA was quantitated by a NanoDrop™ 2000/2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK). 500 ng of total RNA and 200 ng of random hexamers were mixed in a 10 µl-reaction and pre-heated at 70 °C for 5 min. The reverse transcription was carried out in a 20 µl-reaction mixture as described previously14. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR green system for measuring ACC, FASN, and HMGCR mRNA levels, while PC mRNA levels were determined by Taq-man probe as described previously14. The thermal profiles consisted of an initial reaction at 95 °C for 10 min followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 30 s in a CFX connect real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., USA). The expression of mRNAs of all target genes was normalized with that of the 18 s ribosomal RNA gene, shown as the relative mRNA expression. Fold change was calculated using the comparative CT method.

SDS-PAGE and Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.25% sodium deoxycholate, 1% (v/v) Nonidet® P-40, and 1x protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)). The concentration of extracted protein was determined using a Bradford assay. Twenty micrograms of proteins were separated on discontinuous 8% SDS-PAGE under denaturation conditions followed by Western blotting as previously described14. Proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane with 1x transfer buffer (25 mM Tris-Base, 0.192 M glycine, 10% methanol) at constant 100 volts, 100 min. Lipogenic enzymes including acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1), fatty acid synthase (FASN), pyruvate carboxylase (PC), HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) were detected with the following dilutions of primary antibodies: anti-ACC1 (67373-1-1 g, ProteinTech) (1:10,000), anti-FASN (66591-1-1 g, ProteinTech) (1:10,000), anti-yeast PC64 (1:5,000), anti-HMGCR (13533-1-AP, ProteinTech) (1:1,000), anti-β-actin (A5441, Sigma) (1:40,000) was used as internal control.

Triglyceride and cholesterol measurements

Triglyceride and cholesterol levels were measured using the Triglyceride-Glo™ Assay and Cholesterol/Cholesterol Ester-Glo™ Assay (Promega, USA), following the manufacturer’s. 1 × 104 cells of HT-29, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 were plated into 96-well, solid white, flat-bottom, polystyrene, TC-treated microplates (Corning, Inc., USA) containing growth medium. The cells were incubated in a CO2​ incubator for 24 h. After 24 h, the growth medium was replaced with serum-free medium containing various concentrations of free ZY-444 and PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444, and the cultures were maintained for 48 h. Following this incubation period, 50 µL of a glycerol lysis solution supplemented with lipase (12.5:1) was added to each culture well and to adjacent wells containing known amounts of cholesterol. The plates were then incubated for 30 min. Following lysis, 50 µL of a glycerol detection reagent, supplemented with a reductase substrate and a kinetic enhancer (100:1:1), was added to the lysate and standard wells before a 1-h incubation at room temperature. The luminescent signal was recorded using a Tecan Spark 10 M Multimode Plate Reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland). The amounts of triglyceride and cholesterol were determined from a standard curve, which was plotted based on the relationship between the concentrations of triglyceride or cholesterol and the fluorescent readout. The amounts of triglyceride and cholesterol in each sample were then normalized to cell numbers. These cell numbers were determined by an MTT assay performed on a separate plate that contained the same number of cells and was subjected to the same conditions as the plates used for the triglyceride and cholesterol assays. The normalized values for triglyceride and cholesterol in the ZY-444 and PLGA-CSRf-ZY-444-treated cells were expressed relative to those of the untreated cells, which were arbitrarily set to 100%.

Statistical analysis

All values are shown as mean ± S.D. The statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA and Student’s t-test where appropriate using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.3 (Systat Software, Inc., California, US).
