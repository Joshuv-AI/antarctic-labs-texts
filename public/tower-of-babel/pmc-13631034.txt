Risk of Recurrence in Patients with HR+/HER2− Early Breast Cancer: A Podcast
Authors: Maryam B Lustberg, Megan-Claire Chase, Stacey Tinianov, Kevin Kalinsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWithin 20 years of diagnosis, up to 27% of patients with hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative early breast cancer will experience recurrence, despite standard-of-care treatments. Of these recurrences, approximately half will occur within 5 years of diagnosis, regardless of disease stage or nodal status. The majority of recurrences are metastatic, for which there is currently no cure. Patients may not fully discern the incurable and potentially distant nature of disease recurrences, or they may be unaware the risk of recurrence exists. Gaps remain in awareness, understanding, and communication around recurrence risk between healthcare professionals and patients. Thus, in addition to understanding the clinical trial and real-world data on recurrences, it is also important to amplify the patient perspective on the impact of recurrence risk to facilitate effective shared decision making. This podcast will bring together physician and patient advocate experts to discuss the risk of recurrence for patients with hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative early breast cancer including early and late risk of recurrence, discuss how risk is assessed, and examine the evolving treatment landscape for reducing risk. Key Points

Many patients with stage II and III hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative early breast cancer will go on to experience disease recurrence, and the majority of breast cancer recurrences are metastatic and incurable.The rapidly evolving treatment landscape for hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative early breast cancer includes various ways to assess risk as well as targeted therapies to help reduce the risk of recurrence.Patient and advocacy perspectives on the risk of recurrence provide powerful insights that have important implications for shared decision making between healthcare professionals and patients.

Dr. Kalinsky:

But for early-stage disease, we have ribociclib, which is approved for patients where they take 3 years of ribociclib along with endocrine therapy [17]. And we also have abemaciclib where patients take that for 2 years along with endocrine therapy [18]. And the reason I’m bringing this up in the context to your question, Stacey, is really the point about understanding late recurrences. And there’s a term that is thrown around quite a bit and that’s dormancy [6, 26]. And there has been a continued discussion in the field about tumor dormancy. And so maybe it’d be helpful to explain a bit what we mean by dormancy. Essentially, it is characterized by having a long period of time without having any clinical symptoms. And then a recurrence occurs when those dormant cells essentially come out of hiding and then we identify that there’s metastasis. And there’s been a lot of work to really understand what is the mechanism behind tumor dormancy and when might a patient have a tumor that escapes dormancy. Though this remains something that is very much a research question. What’s the biology? Why are we seeing this? And one of the really important things that we’ve seen with the CDK4/6 inhibitors that are approved is that we are identifying a decrease in the risk of early recurrences [17, 27]. And we are hoping that with additional follow-up that we will see an impact on late recurrences.

At ESMO 2025, we saw important data including updated data from NATALEE and from monarchE [28, 29]. And so, for monarchE, we saw with a median follow-up of 76 months, 7-year landmark data [29]. And to be real specific, we saw an absolute invasive disease-free survival benefit at 7 years of 6.5%. And now we also see an overall survival benefit at 7 years at 1.8%. And these data were published in Annals of Oncology simultaneously with the reporting at ESMO. We also saw data from NATALEE where we saw 5-year landmark data with a median follow-up of around 55 months [28, 29]. And we see an invasive disease-free survival benefit at 5 years of 4.5%, and the overall survival data are not yet mature. The hazard ratio is 0.8 and we see that it slightly crosses the confidence interval at one, but we will await additional maturity. And I think, you know, both of these studies and the reason that these updates are important is that we’re seeing this continued carryover effect in both studies where once a patient is done with taking their CDK4/6 inhibitor, there continues to be a longer-term benefit. And that’s really exciting to see in both of these trials. So hopefully with additional follow-up, we will see whether the same overall survival translates for patients with ribociclib.

Dr. Lustberg:

It’s important to understand how these targeted therapies work. Activation of the CDK4/6 pathway causes increased cell division of tumor cells and can contribute to endocrine therapy resistance [30]. So, CDK4/6 inhibitors target and inhibit this pathway. This helps target risk of recurrence as well as resistance to endocrine therapy.

With adjuvant olaparib (PARP inhibitor), over 50% of patients had nausea issues [33]. So, these are wonderful therapeutic strategies. I think there are tremendous advances, but they do need active management. When we look at quality-of-life analyses specifically in the clinical trials, they do not show a decrement in quality of life [31, 34, 35]. So, this is reassuring.

We have options for dose reduction, and it’s really important to really find out what’s important to them and how they prefer to manage these various toxicities because there are a number of choices and priorities to choose from.
