Dynamical phase transitions in certain non-ergodic stochastic processes Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yogeesh Reddy Yerrababu, Satya N. Majumdar, Tridib Sadhu

Abstract
We present a class of stochastic processes in which the large deviation functions of time-integrated observables exhibit singularities that relate to dynamical phase transitions of trajectories. These illustrative examples include Brownian motion with a death rate or in the presence of an absorbing wall, for which we consider a set of empirical observables such as the net displacement, local time, residence time, and area under the trajectory. Using a backward Fokker-Planck approach, we derive the large deviation functions of these observables, and demonstrate how singularities emerge from a competition between survival and diffusion. Furthermore, we analyse this scenario using an alternative approach with tilted operators, showing that at the singular point, the effective dynamics undergoes an abrupt transition. Extending this approach, we show that similar transitions may generically arise in Markov chains with transient states. This scenario is robust and generalizable for non-Markovian dynamics and for many-body systems, potentially leading to multiple dynamical phase transitions.

The dynamical observable
Q

U=0

We explore these observables for two specific Brownian dynamics. In the first example, a mortal Brownian particle has a probability of dying rendering it immobile. In the second example, the Brownian motion is constrained to move between a fixed absorbing wall and a reflecting wall. For both examples, we show that the probability
P_{T}(Q)

P_{T}(Q=qT)\sim e^{-T\phi(q)},

\phi(q)

\phi(q)
using the framework in

These singularities are linked to the dynamical phase transitions, arising from the interplay between trajectories that persist without being dead or absorbed throughout the entire duration
T
and trajectories that do not survive. Depending on the value of
Q
, one of these two kinds of trajectories is optimal for large
T

\phi(q)
marks the transition between these two types of evolutions. This represents a path-space generalization of conventional equilibrium phase transitions, where singularity in thermodynamic free energy indicates transitions in static observables.

An insightful approach to quantify the differences in the nature of trajectories is in terms of an effective dynamics

;
Majumdar and Orland 2015
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
;
De Bruyne et al. 2021a
;
De Bruyne et al. 2021b
;

;

;

;

The theory of constrained dynamics

;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a

;

;

We illustrate the generality of this mechanism in Markov chains with transient state spaces where eigenvalue crossing results in DPTs of observables that are discrete analogs of (
1
). The mechanism extends to many-body systems and non-Markovian processes. Our work asserts earlier observations

Nyawo and Touchette 2018

Mukherjee and Smith 2023
;
Whitelam and Jacobson 2021

Szavits-Nossan and Evans 2015

Gingrich et al. 2014

II
Large deviation theory

The large deviation asymptotic in (
2
) precisely means

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln P_{T}(Q=qT)=-\phi(q).

(3)

This is equivalent to an asymptotic of the generating function

\langle e^{pQ}\rangle\sim e^{T\mu(p)}

(4)

for large
T
, where
\mu(p)
is the scaled cumulant generating function (scgf). The scgf relates to the large deviation function
\phi(q)
by a Legendre-Fenchel transformation

\mu(p)=\max_{q}\{pq-\phi(q)\}.

(5)

For an introduction to the large deviation theory see
Touchette 2009
;
Majumdar and Schehr 2017
.

III
Mortal Brownian motion

The simplest continuous model we consider is the motion of a one-dimensional Brownian particle with a probability rate
\alpha
of dying, after which the particle remains immobile forever. In a small time interval
dt
, the position
x_{t}
of an alive particle evolves via the Langevin equation

x_{t+dt}=\begin{cases}x_{t}&\textrm{with prob }\alpha dt\ \textrm{of death}\\
x_{t}+\eta_{t}dt&\textrm{with prob}\ 1-\alpha dt,\end{cases}

(6)

where
\eta_{t}
is a Gaussian white noise with zero mean and the correlator

\langle\eta_{t}\eta_{t^{\prime}}\rangle=2D\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. For simplicity, we henceforth set
D=1
for the rest of the paper. In the above equation, once the particle dies,
x_{t+dt}=x_{t}
for the rest of time. Similar diffusion processes with mortality have been studied in related contexts
Mazzolo and Monthus 2022
;
Berman and Halina 1996
;
Holcman et al. 2005
;
Frydman 2000
;
Chen et al. 2022
.

The dynamics can also be described by the Fokker-Planck equation for the probability
P_{t}^{(a)}(x)
for the particle to start at the origin and remain alive until time
t
, at which point it is at position
x
,

\partial_{t}P^{(a)}_{t}(x)=\partial_{x}^{2}P^{(a)}_{t}(x)-\alpha P^{(a)}_{t}(x)

(7a)

with the initial condition
P_{0}^{(a)}(x)=\delta(x)
.
The probability for the particle to be dead at position
x
at time
t
follows

\partial_{t}P^{(d)}_{t}(x)=\alpha P^{(a)}_{t}(x)

(7b)

with the initial condition
P_{0}^{(d)}(x)=0
.
The probability of a particle to be at
x
, irrespective of its status (alive or dead), corresponds to the combined probabilities of two types of trajectories: those that persist until the final time
t
and are located at
x
, and those that die at
x
before the final time,

P_{t}(x)=P^{(a)}_{t}(x)+P^{(d)}_{t}(x).

(8)

A straightforward solution of (
7b
) gives

P_{t}(x)=e^{-\alpha t}g_{t}(x)+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}d{t^{\prime}}\,e^{-\alpha t^{\prime}}g_{t^{\prime}}(x)

(9)

where
g_{t}(x)=(4\pi t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp(-x^{2}/4t)
is the probability for a free Brownian starting at the origin to reach
x
at time
t
. The first term in the solution is due to particles that remain alive throughout the entire duration, where
e^{-\alpha t}
is the corresponding surviving probability. The second term is the net contribution from particles that survive until any intermediate time
t^{\prime}<t
reaching location
x
and die in the interval between
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}+dt^{\prime}
.

For large time
t=T
with
x=qT
, the first term on the right hand side of (
9
) has an asymptotic
e^{-T(\alpha+q^{2}/4)}
, while the second term decays as
e^{-T\phi(q)}
with

\phi(q)=\begin{cases}|q|\sqrt{\alpha}&0\leq|q|<\sqrt{4\alpha}\\
\alpha+\frac{q^{2}}{4}&|q|\geq\sqrt{4\alpha}.\end{cases}

(10)

The latter asymptotic is obtained by introducing a change of variable
t^{\prime}=rT
and subsequently evaluating the integral in (
9
) using a saddle point approximation for large
T
. The non-analyticity in (
10
) arises because, for
|q|>\sqrt{4\alpha}
, the saddle-point value of
r=r^{\star}\equiv|q|/\sqrt{4\alpha}
falls outside the range of integration. As a result, the integration is dominated by the value of the integrand at
r=1
where it is maximum.

For large
T
, the asymptotic of
P_{T}(x)
is determined by the dominant contribution among the two terms in (
9
) leading to (
2
) for
Q\equiv x

with the ldf
\phi(q)
in (
10
).

The ldf is plotted in Fig.
1
revealing singularities at
q=\pm\sqrt{4\alpha}
, where
\phi^{\prime\prime}(q)
is discontinuous. Additionally, there is an extra singularity at
q=0
, where the first derivative
\phi^{\prime}(q)
has a jump discontinuity. Drawing an analogy with equilibrium phase transitions, we categorise the former type of singularities as second-order DPTs and the latter type as first-order DPTs.

The origin of the second-order transition becomes apparent through the saddle point approximation used in deriving (
10
). In this approximation, discussed immediately after (
10
), the re-scaled variable
r
denotes the fraction of time the particle remains alive. For
|q|<\sqrt{4\alpha}
, the dominant contribution comes from the trajectories that reach
x
and die at time
T\sqrt{4\alpha}
. In contrast, for
|q|\geq\sqrt{4\alpha}
, the dominant contribution arises from trajectories that remain alive until the final time
T
. This drastic shift in the nature of optimal trajectories gives rise to the second-order dynamical phase transition.

The first-order transition at
q=0
stems from a similar change in the nature of optimal trajectories. For small
q
, the optimal trajectory is a straight line from the origin to
x=qT
, reaching in time
Tr^{\star}=T|q|/\sqrt{4\alpha}
and then dying. The slope of the straight line
x/(Tr^{\star})=\textrm{sgn}(q)\sqrt{4\alpha}
is non-vanishing for
q\to 0
, but it changes discontinuously as
q
changes sign, leading to a first-order transition.

III.1
Area under the trajectory

Another interesting observable is the area covered by a trajectory
Majumdar 2005
, defined as
Q=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}X_{t}dt
, where
t_{f}
is the time at which the particle died. Similar to (
8
), the probability
P_{t}(Q)
measured at time
t
can be written as a combination of two parts

P_{t}(Q)=P^{(a)}_{t}(Q)+P^{(d)}_{t}(Q)

(13)

where
P^{(a)}_{t}(Q)
is the contribution from trajectories that are alive until the final time
t
, whereas the trajectories contributing to
P^{(d)}_{t}(Q)
have died earlier. It is straightforward to show that

P_{t}(Q)=e^{-\alpha t}\widehat{g}_{t}(Q)+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}d{t^{\prime}}\,e^{-\alpha t^{\prime}}\widehat{g}_{t^{\prime}}(Q)

(14)

where

\widehat{g}_{t}(Q)=\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi t^{3}\right)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{3Q^{2}}{4t^{3}}}

(15)

is the probability of area
Q
for a free Brownian particle (simple to derive using the linearity of the observable).
Following a similar saddle point analysis as used for the position distribution, we find that for large time
t=T
,
P(Q=qT^{2})\sim e^{-T\phi(q)}
, where the ldf is given by:

\phi(q)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{|q|}\,\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}}\alpha^{3/4}&\textrm{for }0\leq|q|<\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\alpha}\\
\alpha+\frac{3}{4}q^{2}&\textrm{for }|q|\geq\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\alpha}.\end{cases}

(16)

This scaling behaviour differs from the distribution in (
2
). For similar examples, refer to
Nickelsen and Touchette 2018
;
Krapivsky et al. 2014
. The singular ldf is depicted in Fig.
2
, which, for small
q
, grows as
\sqrt{q}
compared to the linear growth in (
10
).

III.2
A general class of observables

The origin of the singularities in the ldfs for position and area lies in the competition between trajectories that remained alive for the entire duration and trajectories that died at an intermediate time. Such competition could result in similar singularities for other path-dependant observables. To illustrate this aspect, we consider a subset of observables
Q
in (
1
) with
V(X_{t})=0
while

U(X_{t})
is a function of the alive part of trajectory
X_{t}
:

Q=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\;U(X_{t})

(17)

where
t_{f}
is the time until which the particle is alive.

We define
P_{T}(Q|x_{0})
as the probability of the observable
Q
at time
T
for a mortal Brownian motion starting at
x_{0}
. Analogous to (
8
), the contributions to
P_{T}(Q|x_{0})
are split into two classes of trajectories:

P_{T}(Q|x_{0})=P^{\text{(a)}}_{T}(Q|x_{0})+P^{\text{(d)}}_{T}(Q|x_{0})

(18)

where
P^{\text{(a)}}_{T}(Q|x_{0})
is the contribution from the living trajectories, while
P^{\text{(d)}}_{T}(Q|x_{0})
is the contribution from trajectories that died before time
T
. The two probabilities are related,

P^{\text{(d)}}_{T}(Q|x_{0})=\alpha\int_{0}^{T}dtP^{\text{(a)}}_{t}(Q|x_{0}).

(19)

For determining
P^{\text{(a)}}_{t}(Q|x_{0})
, we adopt a backward Fokker-Planck equation approach discussed in
Majumdar 2005
. We define a double Laplace transformation

\mathcal{D}_{t\to s}^{Q\to p}\odot f_{t}(Q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{-st}\int dQ\;e^{pQ}f_{t}(Q).

(20)

Expressing (
18
) in terms of the transformed variables and using (
19
) gives

R_{s}(p,x_{0})=S_{s}(p,x_{0})+\frac{\alpha}{s}S_{s}(p,x_{0})

(21)

where
R_{s}(p,x_{0})
and
S_{s}(p,x_{0})
are the
\mathcal{D}
-transform of
P_{T}(Q|x_{0})
and
P^{\text{(a)}}_{T}(Q|x_{0})
.

In line with the methods illustrated in
Majumdar 2005
, we find that
S_{s}(p,x_{0})
is a solution of the ordinary differential equation

\frac{d^{2}S_{s}}{dx_{0}^{2}}-(\alpha+s-pU(x_{0}))S_{s}=-1.

(22a)

The specific boundary condition hinges on the observable in question. For observables where
U(x)\to 0
for large
x
,

S_{s}(p,x_{0})=\frac{1}{\alpha+s}\qquad\textrm{for }x_{0}\to\pm\infty.

(22b)

In principle, obtaining a solution for
S_{s}(p,x_{0})
allows us to compute
R_{s}(p,x_{0})
in (
21
) and perform the inverse
\mathcal{D}
-transformation to derive the probability
P_{T}(Q|x_{0})
. However, performing these steps analytically for arbitrary
U(x)
is not always feasible. Nevertheless, a large deviation form of the probability
P_{T}(Q|x_{0})
can be deduced from the poles of
R_{s}(p,x_{0})
. This can be seen as follows. Assuming
P_{T}(Q=qT|x_{0})\sim e^{-T\phi(q)}
, the Laplace transformation

\int dQ\,e^{pQ}\,P_{T}(Q=qT)\sim e^{T\mu(p)}

(23)

for large
T
, where
\mu(p)
is the scgf related to
\phi(q)
by (
5
). An additional Laplace transformation gives

R_{s}(p,x_{0})\sim\frac{1}{s-\mu(p)}.

(24)

Therefore, a pole of
R_{s}(p,x_{0})
at
s=\mu(p)
suggests a large deviation asymptotic of
P_{T}(Q=qT|x_{0})
where the ldf
\phi(q)
is a Legendre-Fenchel transform (
5
) of
\mu(p)
. If
R_{s}(p,x_{0})
has multiple poles, the scgf
\mu(p)
is given by the maximum among all the poles.

This pole structure is seen in the ldf of
P_{t}(x)
in (
10
), corresponding to
Q=x
for which
U=0
and
V=1
in (
1
). Refering to (
9
), it is straightforward to demonstrate that the corresponding
R_{s}(p,0)=\frac{\alpha+s}{s\left(\alpha-p^{2}+s\right)}
, which has two poles at
s=0
and at
s=p^{2}-\alpha
. (Here,
x_{0}=0
is the initial position for this problem.) This results in the scgf,
\mu(p)=\max\{0,p^{2}-\alpha\}
; and its Legendre-Fenchel transform yields the ldf in (
10
).

V
Spectral method

The problem of the long-time asymptotics (
3
) of the probability
P(Q)
of empirical observables (
1
) can be approached using an alternative method that has recently gained considerable attention
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
. In this approach, large deviations are expressed in terms of largest eigenvalue of an operator, offering an intuitive analogy with free-energy calculations using the transfer matrix method
Goldenfeld 1992
;
Dhar 2011
;
Cuesta and Sánchez 2004
.

We present a brief introduction to this method using the simplest example of a standard Brownian motion
\dot{X}_{t}=\eta_{t}
with Gaussian white noise
\eta_{t}
of mean zero and covariance of
\langle\eta_{t}\eta_{t^{\prime}}\rangle=2\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. For the joint probability
P_{T}(Q,x_{T}|x_{0})
of the observable
Q
in (
1
) and the final position
x_{T}
, starting at
x_{0}
, the generating function

R_{T}(p,x_{T}|x_{0})=\int dQe^{pQ}P_{T}(Q,x_{T}|x_{0})

(45)

has a path integral representation
Majumdar 2005
,

R_{T}(p,x_{T}|x_{0})=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{T}}\mathcal{D}[x_{t}]e^{-S_{T}(x_{t})}

(46)

where the Feynman-Kac action

S_{T}(x_{t})=\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\left\{\frac{\dot{x}_{t}^{2}}{4}-p\dot{x}_{t}V(x_{t})-pU(x_{t})\right\}

(47)

is analogous to the action of a charged particle in an electromagnetic field. The corresponding analogue of the Schrodinger equation is the Feynman-Kac formula
Majumdar 2005

\frac{d}{dT}R_{T}(p,x|x_{0})=\mathcal{L}_{p}\cdot R_{T}(p,x|x_{0})

(48a)

where the operator

\mathcal{L}_{p}=\left(\frac{d}{dx}-pV\right)^{2}+pU.

(48b)

is often referred to as the tilted operator
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
.

If the operator
\mathcal{L}_{p}
has a spectral gap between its largest and the second-largest eigenvalues, then for large
T
,
R_{T}(p,x_{T}|x_{0})\simeq e^{T\mu(p)}r_{p}(x_{T})\ell_{p}(x_{0})
, where
\mu(p)
is the largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{p}
with the associated left eigenvector
\ell_{p}(x)
and right eigenvector
r_{p}(x)
satisfying

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{p}\ell_{p}(x)=\mu(p)\ell_{p}(x)\textrm{ ~~ and }\mathcal{L}_{p}r_{p}(x)=\mu(p)r_{p}(x),

(49)

respectively, where
\dagger
denotes the adjoint. Comparing the asymptotics of
R_{T}(p,x_{T}|x_{0})
with the asymptotics (
4
) one can recognise that the largest eigenvalue
\mu(p)
gives the scgf of
Q
.

For a strictly convex
\mu(p)
, the asymptotic for
R_{T}(p,x_{T}|x_{0})
corresponds
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
to the large
T
asymptotic of the joint probability

P_{T}(Q=qT,x_{T}|x_{0})\simeq\sqrt{\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(q)}{2\pi T}}e^{-T\phi(q)}r_{p}(x_{T})\ell_{p}(x_{0})

(50a)

with

\phi(q)=pq-\mu(p)\quad\textrm{and }\mu^{\prime}(p)=q,

(50b)

which is seen by a saddle point approximation of the integral in (
45
).

The relation (
50b
) is an explicit version of (
5
) for a strictly convex
\mu(p)
. It establishes an equivalence between the conditioned ensemble and the ensemble where evolution is weighted by the value of the observable. Such equivalence of ensemble also extends to the dynamics
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
, more precisely, to the joint probability
P_{t}(Q,x|x_{T},x_{0})
of being at location
x
at an intermediate time
0<t<T
, starting at
x_{0}
and ending at
x_{T}
, and yielding a value
Q
for the empirical observable (
1
) measured over the time window
[0,T]
. Analogous to (
50
), the joint probability can be estimated in the large time
T
limit by the asymptotics of the corresponding generating function
G_{t}(p,x|x_{T},x_{0})
, defined in a way similar to (
45
). This further implies
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
that the conditional probability

P_{t}(x|Q,x_{T},x_{0})=\frac{P_{t}(Q,x|x_{T},x_{0})}{\int dxP_{t}(Q,x|x_{T},x_{0})}

(51)

can be estimated in the large
T
limit by an analogous quantity in the weighted ensemble,

P_{t}(x|Q=qT,x_{T},x_{0})\simeq\frac{G_{t}(p,x|x_{T},x_{0})}{\int dxG_{t}(p,x|x_{T},x_{0})}

(52)

with
p
and
q
related by (
50b
).

To describe the dynamics followed by the conditional probability
P_{t}(x|Q,x_{T},x_{0})
, we follow a procedure similar to that described in
Majumdar and Orland 2015
for conditioned dynamics of a Brownian bridge. Using Markovianity,

G_{t}(p,x|x_{T},x_{0})=R_{T-t}(p,x_{T}|x)R_{t}(p,x|x_{0})

(53)

where
R_{t}(p,x|x_{0})
evolves according to (
48
) with
T
replaced by
t
, and
R_{T-t}(p,x_{T}|x)\equiv B_{t}(p,x|x_{T})
follows the backward evolution
Majumdar 2005

-\frac{d}{dt}B_{t}(p,x|x_{T})=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\cdot B_{t}(p,x|x_{T}).

(54)

It then requires a straightforward algebra to show that

G_{t}(p,x|x_{T},x_{0})
in (
53
) satisfies

\frac{dG_{t}}{dt}=-2\frac{d}{dx}\left\{\left(pV(x)+\frac{1}{B_{t}}\frac{dB_{t}}{dx}\right)G_{t}\right\}+\frac{d^{2}G_{t}}{dx^{2}}.

(55)

Integrating the above equation over
x
, we see that the denominator in (
52
) is independent of time, assuming that
G_{t}(p,x|x_{T},x_{0})
vanishes sufficiently quickly for
x\to\pm\infty
. Using these results in (
52
), it immediately follows that the effective dynamics of the conditional probability
P_{t}(x|qT,x_{T},x_{0})
is similar to (
55
), with
p
and
q
related by (
50b
).

The effective dynamics particularly simplifies for
1\ll t\ll T
, where, for typical observables, the system reaches a quasi-stationary state
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
, in which the conditional probability
P_{t}(x|qT,x_{T},x_{0})\simeq P_{t}(x|q)
is independent of
x_{T}
,
x_{0}
, and
T
. Moreover,
B_{t}(p,x|x_{T})\simeq e^{-t\mu(p)}\ell_{p}(x)r_{p}(x_{T})
. The resulting conditioned dynamics is a drifted Brownian motion
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
governed by the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{d}{dt}P_{t}(x|q)=-\frac{d}{dx}\Big(F_{t}(x,p)P_{t}(x|q)\Big)+\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}P_{t}(x|q)

(56a)

with a modified force

F_{t}(x,p)=2\left\{pV(x)+\frac{\ell_{p}^{\prime}(x)}{\ell_{p}(x)}\right\}

(56b)

where the left eigenvector
\ell_{p}(x)
is defined in (
49
), and the parameters
p
and
q
are related by (
50b
). This effective description for the conditioned dynamics suitably generalizes
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
for general diffusive processes and for Markov processes on discrete states and time.

In this spectral method, singularities in
\mu(p)
can arise from crossing of the two leading eigenvalues, denoted as
\mu_{1}(p)
and
\mu_{2}(p)
, of the tilted operator
\mathcal{L}_{p}
in (
48b
), such that the scgf
\mu(p)=\max\{\mu_{1}(p),\mu_{2}(p)\}
. This is analogous to the origin of singular free energy at the phase transition point of equilibrium lattice models from the crossing of leading eigenvalues of the associated transfer matrix
Goldenfeld 1992
;
Dhar 2011
;
Cuesta and Sánchez 2004
. Across the singular point, the largest eigenvector also changes abruptly indicating a sudden change in the effective dynamics (
56b
). This way the singularity of scgf
\mu(p)
and equivalently that of the ldf
\phi(q)
corresponds to a phase transition in the system’s dynamics as the condition on the observable value is varied.

We illustrate how the DPTs for the models we discussed in Section.
III
and Section.

VI
Markov Chains

The origin of DPTs, as discussed in our examples of Langevine processes, is relatively easier to comprehend in their discrete counterparts, which are discrete time Markov processes on a finite configuration space. For a Markov chain, evolution of the probability
P_{t}(C)
of a configuration
C
at time
t
is governed by

P_{t+1}(C^{\prime})=\sum_{C}M(C^{\prime},C)P_{t}(C),

(69)

where
M(C^{\prime},C)
denotes the transition probability from state
C
to
C^{\prime}
. A discrete analogue
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
of the empirical observable (
1
) is

Q=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(C_{t})+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g(C_{t+1},C_{t}),

(70)

where
f(C)
and
g(C,C^{\prime})
are arbitrary functions. For instance, the residence time at a configuration
C
corresponds to
f(C_{t})=\delta_{C_{t},C}
and
g(C_{t+1},C_{t})=0
, while
f(C_{t})=0
and
g(C_{t+1},C_{t})=\delta_{C_{t},C}\delta_{C_{t+1},C^{\prime}}
gives the total number of jumps from configuration
C
to
C^{\prime}
.

In the limit of large
T
, the probability of
Q
has the asymptotic (
2
) with the corresponding scgf
\mu(p)
in (
4
) relating to the largest eigenvalue
e^{\mu(p)}
of the tilted matrix
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
;
Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
:

M_{p}(C,C^{\prime})=M(C,C^{\prime})e^{p[f(C^{\prime})+g(C,C^{\prime})]},

(71)

which is a discrete analogue of the tilted operator (
48b
).

Analogous to (
56b
), the dynamics conditioned to yield a value of
Q_{T}
, in the large time limit, are described (excluding the non-quasi-stationary regimes near
t=0
and
t=T
) by an effective Markov chain

;
Jack and Sollich 2015
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015

Derrida and Sadhu 2019a

P_{t+1}^{(p)}(C^{\prime})=\sum_{C}W_{p}(C^{\prime},C)P_{t}^{(p)}(C),

(72)

with the transition matrix

W_{p}(C^{\prime},C)=\frac{L_{p}(C^{\prime})}{e^{\mu(p)}L_{p}(C)}M_{p}(C^{\prime},C),

(73)

where
L_{p}
is the left eigenvector of
M_{p}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
e^{\mu(p)}
.
The tilting parameter

is expressed in terms of the observable value

by the relation
\mu^{\prime}(p)=Q_{T}/T

Chetrite and Touchette 2013

Chetrite and Touchette 2015

Derrida and Sadhu 2019a
.

In this formalism, the crossing of two leading eigenvalues of
M_{p}
results a singular scgf and, consequently, an abrupt change in the effective transition matrix
W_{p}
. This draws an instructive analogy between DPTs and conventional equilibrium phase transitions relating to crossing of two largest eigenvalues of transfer matrix
Cuesta and Sánchez 2004
. There are known examples of eigenvalue crossings in the transfer matrix of one-dimensional models
Cuesta and Sánchez 2004

Kittel 1969
. Notably, the transfer matrices in these examples are reducible and therefore do not satisfy the criteria for the Perron-Frobenius theorem
Cuesta and Sánchez 2004

71

VII
A robust mechanism for DPTs

A common feature between the two Markov chain examples in Section.
VI
is the presence of an absorbing state, from which the system, once entered, can not escape. Their Markov matrices and the associated tilted matrices are reducible making them beyond the purview of the Perron-Forbenius theorem
Cuesta and Sánchez 2004
. As a result, crossing of largest eigenvalues is permitted, leading to a singularity in the scgf and, equivalently, a DPT.

This mechanism for DPTs is expected in general for processes with transient states (see illustration in Fig.
16
). The transient state space
\mathcal{T}
is not accessible from the recurrent state space
\mathcal{R}
. Corresponding Markov matrix has the form:

\mathbf{M}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\mathbfcal{T}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbfcal{I}&\mathbf{\mathbfcal{R}}\end{pmatrix},

(83)

where
\mathbfcal{T}
and
\mathbfcal{R}
are blocks representing transitions within the sub-spaces
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{R}
, respectively, while
\mathbfcal{I}
involves transitions from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{R}
.

The set of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}
is composed of eigenvalues from
\mathbfcal{T}
and
\mathbfcal{R}
. At large times, the transient state space
\mathcal{T}
becomes empty, and the system reaches the stationary state in
\mathcal{R}
. The stationary distribution is given by the eigenvector of
\mathbfcal{R}
with the largest eigenvalue
e_{1}=1
. This implies that the largest eigenvalue (denoted as
e_{2}
) of
\mathbfcal{T}
is less than
1
.

For an empirical observable measured in the transient state (as was the case for the examples discussed), only the block
\mathbfcal{T}
is weighted by the tilting parameter
p
. This means that the spectrum of
\mathbfcal{R}
remains unchanged with
e_{1}=1
for all
p
. However, for large
p
, it is expected that the scgf, which is logarithm of the largest eigenvalue of the tilted matrix
\mathbf{M}_{p}
, is positive, implying that
e_{2}>1
for large
p
. This would occur if
e_{2}
, which was smaller than
e_{1}=1
for
p=0
, crossed
e_{1}
at some intermediate value of
p
, resulting in a DPT.

VIII
Multiple phase transitions

Building upon the mechanism of DPT discussed in this article, it is possible to construct simple examples with rich phase behaviours. In the following sections, we explore two such simple examples.

VIII.1

IX
Non-Markov processes

The mechanism of DPT that we discussed in Section.
VII
extends for non-Markovian processes. The key idea is that a non-Markov process with finite memory can be described by a higher dimensional Markov process, which also poses transient sectors if the original process have them. One would expect DPTs for such processes. We give two such examples.

IX.1
Mortal Active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

The active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
Martin et al. 2021
is a simple model of persistent dynamics for a non-polar active particle. The position of the particle follows a Langevin dynamics

\ddot{X}_{t}+\frac{1}{\tau}\dot{X}_{t}=\eta_{t}

(87)

with a Gaussian white noise
\eta_{t}
with zero-mean and covariance
\langle\eta_{t}\eta_{t^{\prime}}\rangle=2\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. The non-zero auto-correlation
\langle\dot{X}_{t}\dot{X}_{t^{\prime}}\rangle\sim\tau e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|/\tau}
for large
t-t^{\prime}
makes the
X_{t}
dynamics non-Markovian, although on the
(X_{t},\dot{X}_{t})
space the process is Markovian.

The free propagator for the position of AOUP started at the origin
X_{0}=0
with
\dot{X}_{0}=0
is Gaussian:

g_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t\mathcal{D}_{t}}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4t\mathcal{D}_{t}}\right),

(88)

with the variance

\mathcal{D}_{t}=\tau^{2}\left(1-\frac{\tau}{2t}\left(3-4e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}+e^{-\frac{2t}{\tau}}\right)\right).

(89)

For a mortal AOUP with a death rate
\alpha
, the probability of the particle position
x
at time
t
can be written as (
9
) with the propagator
g_{t}(x)
given in (
88
). For large
t
, the probability has the asymptotic
P_{t}(x=qt)\sim e^{-t\phi(q/\tau)}
with the ldf that is identical to (
10
).

This coincidence of the ldf with the Brownian case is not surprising considering that the measurement time
t
is far larger than the persistance time
\tau
for the AOUP.

IX.2
Mortal fractional Brownian motion (fBm)

An fBm
Sadhu and Wiese 2021
;
Sadhu et al. 2018
is a non-Markovian Gaussian process
X_{t}
with mean zero and power-law auto-correlation:

\langle\dot{X}_{t}\dot{X}_{t^{\prime}}\rangle=2H(2H-1)(t-t^{\prime})^{2(H-1)}

(90)

for
t>t^{\prime}>0
with the Hurst exponent
0<H<1
. The free propagator for a fractional Brownian starting at the origin is given by the Gaussian

g_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t^{2H}}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4t^{2H}}\right).

(91)

For a mortal fBm with a death rate
\alpha
, the probability of position at time
t
is similar to (
9
) with the propagator in (
91
). In the limit of large
t
, the probability has the large deviation asymptotic
P_{t}(x=qt^{\nu})\sim e^{-t\phi(q)}
with the ldf

\phi(q)=\begin{cases}\beta\;|q|^{\frac{1}{\nu}}&\text{for}\ |q|\leq\sqrt{2\alpha/H}\\
\alpha+\frac{q^{2}}{4}&\text{for}\ |q|>\sqrt{2\alpha/H}\end{cases}

(92a)

where
\nu=H+1/2
and

\beta=\frac{\nu\alpha}{H}\left(\frac{H}{2\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\nu}}.

(92b)

Remark:
The same ldf was found for an fBm with resetting
Majumdar et al. 2015a
.

X
Conclusions

We constructed various illustrative simple models demonstrating DPTs in the statistics of time-integrated observables. These DPTs correspond to sudden changes in effective dynamics that correspond to fluctuations of the observables. For a mathematical origin, we have shown how such DPTs generically arise from the crossing of eigenvalues in reducible Markov operators for stochastic dynamics with transient states. Understanding this scenario aided us in constructing rich phase behaviors consisting of multiple DPTs and extensions for non-Markov processes.

Extending this work for interacting many-body systems and their higher-dimensional generalizations presents intriguing possibilities. For instance, one could explore microscopic models of the epidemiological process in Section.
VIII.1
and population dynamics
Ovaskainen and Meerson 2010
;
Ottino-Löffler and Kardar 2020
, where the density-dependent mortality rate emerges from interactions. Another avenue for investigation could involve studying fluctuations of empirical observables such as integrated current or entropy production in many-particle dynamics with absorbing states. Relying on the mechanism discussed in this work, it is reasonable to predict DPTs in such many-body generalizations. A well-known example
Di Bello et al. 2023
is the dynamical phase transition in the current fluctuations of resetting Brownian particles starting from a domain wall initial state on an infinite line. Similar transitions are anticipated for the many-particle generalization of mortal Brownian particles. For such extended systems, the framework of fluctuating hydrodynamics of conditioned dynamics
Derrida and Sadhu 2019b
would provide an appropriate theoretical foundation.

In the mechanism discussed here, non-ergodicity arising from the underlying transient state-space is responsible for DPTs. Non-ergodicity may also effectively emerge in the thermodynamic limit of many-body systems or in the low-noise limit of single-degrees of freedom. Exploring the nature and universality of DPTs in such non-ergodic dynamics warrants further investigation.

It is worth noting that that non-ergodicity is not a necessary criteria for DPTs. Singular ldfs are also observed in position distributions in run-and-tumble dynamics devoid of transient states. These singularities originate from a competition between trajectories with typical jumps and trajectories with a dominant atypical jump
Mori et al. 2021b
;
Mori et al. 2021a
. Generalization of DPTs in such democratic versus winner-takes-all scenarios would be an interesting future direction.
