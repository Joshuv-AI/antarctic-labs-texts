Theoretical / numerical study of modulated traveling waves in inhibition stabilized networks
Safaa Habib, Romain Veltz

Abstract
We prove a principle of linearized stability for traveling wave solutions to neural field equations posed on the real line. Additionally, we provide the existence of a finite dimensional invariant center manifold close to a traveling wave, this allows to study bifurcations of traveling waves. Finally, the spectral properties of the modulated traveling waves are investigated. Numerical schemes for the computation of modulated traveling waves are provided. We then apply these results and methods to study a neural field model in a inhibitory stabilized regime. We showcase Fold, Hopf and Bodgdanov-Takens bifurcations of traveling pulses. Additionally, we continue the modulated traveling pulses as function of the time scale ratio of the two neural populations and show numerical evidences for snaking of modulated traveling pulses.

Introduction

Cortical waves have been the subject of active studies as reviewed in
[
45
]
. Some of the latest results concern the stimulus evoked activity in the visual cortex of awake monkeys
[
46
]
where it was experimentally shown that this cortical activity is a traveling wave (see Figure

) thanks to a recent phase-based algorithm for wave detection. It was suggested in
[
45

that the main mechanism for the existence of this traveling wave is a non zero - distance dependent conduction - delay.

On the other hand, the visual cortex in cats was shown in
[

to be best modeled with an inhibition stabilized network (ISN) in which a strong recurrent inhibitory input onto the excitatory cells paradoxically shapes the dynamics
[
62
]
. The dynamics of traveling pulses and fronts in this type of network was very nicely studied in
[
29

as function of the inhibition space / time scales. Notably, the authors found modulated traveling pulses by direct simulation. They also narrowed the analysis to the specific exponential connectivity kernels in order to perform numerical continuation of the waves by relying on the spatial dynamics trick
[
56
]
.

Here, we theoretically and numerically investigate the traveling wave dynamics in ISNs in hope to elucidate key mechanisms in monkey visual cortex dynamics
[
46
]
. We thus do not impose the connectivity kernel function.
We do not study the existence of wave
[
16
,
19
,
20
]
and simply assume that one is provided. At Hopf bifurcations in the wave frame, modulated traveling waves (MTW) emerge which are the main subject of this study. We also aim to study how the principle of linearized stability applies to TW and MTW in the context of neural fields. This was for example conjectured in
[
19
]
although this result is well known in the context of reaction-diffusion models
[
60
,
56
]
. We based our analysis on the freezing method
[
9
,
51
]
.
When used numerically, this method allows us to simulate the long time asymptotic of waves without being limited by the simulation domain as in
[
29
]
.

The (numerical) study of waves has a long history in the context of neural fields as reviewed in
[
14
,
11
,
7
]
, starting with analytical expressions
[
2
]
in which the authors assumed a Heaviside nonlinearity, and spanning the theory as well as the numerical analysis. The proof of existence of waves is a fascinating albeit very technical domain of research

with some of the latest tools reviewed in

The study

[

albeit without the preconditioner discovered in this paper which greatly speeds up the convergence of the newton algorithm.

The literature on the numerical computation of MTW is more scarce in the context of neural fields (but see
[
69
]

2
The neural field model

We study a two populations neural field model
[
6
,
11
,
29
,
67
]
which describes the mean neural activity of excitatory and inhibitory populations. We focus on the case where the neural populations are located on a one-dimensional cortex
\mathbb{R}
with convolutional connectivity kernels

\displaystyle\frac{du}{dt}(x,t)=

\displaystyle-u(x,t)+S\left(a_{ee}K_{e}\cdot u(x,t)-a_{ei}K_{i}\cdot v(x,t)-\theta_{e}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle\tau\frac{dv}{dt}(x,t)=

\displaystyle-v(x,t)+S\left(a_{ie}K_{e}\cdot u(x,t)-a_{ii}K_{i}\cdot v(x,t)-\theta_{i}\right)

where

K_{k}\cdot u(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{k}(x-y)u(y,t)dy,\ k\in\{e,i\},\ \quad S(I):=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\beta I)}.

The parameter
\tau
describes the ratio of the inhibitory time constant by the excitatory one.
S
is a nonlinear function which represents the firing rate as function of the synaptic input and
\beta\geq 0
is the nonlinear gain. The kernel mappings
K_{k}
from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}
represent the spatial spread of the connections between the populations and are normalized to one:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{k}=1.

The constants
a_{jk}\geq 0
are the coupling strengths between the populations
k
and
j
and
\theta_{p}
are the thresholds.

We select the parameters so that the space clamped model (when the solutions are independent of
x
) has three equilibria
(u_{i},v_{i})
for
i=1,2,3
with
u_{1}<u_{2}<u_{3}
. When
(u_{3},v_{3})
is stable, the network is in an inhibitory stabilized regime
[
62
,
29
,
68
]
.

Remark 1
.

It is convenient to rewrite (
1
) in the more compact way

\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}=-\mathbf{L}_{0}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{L}_{0}S\left(\mathbf{W}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{\theta}\right)

(2)

where
\mathbf{L}_{0}
is the diagonal operator
\mathbf{L}_{0}:=diag(1,\tau^{-1})
,
\theta:=(\theta_{e},\theta_{i})
and
\mathbf{W}_{ij}:=a_{ij}K_{j}
.

3
Informal introduction to fronts, pulses and waves

We regroup here the different formulations and results concerning the traveling wave solutions that we shall discuss in this paper. For convenience, we rewrite (
1
) as a Cauchy problem

\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u};p)

(3)

where
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the parameters.

Remark 2
.

It is enlightening to view (
3
) as an equivariant Cauchy problem
[
12
]
with symmetry group composed of the translations of the real line. A traveling wave is thus a relative equilibrium and a modulated traveling wave (see below) is a relative periodic orbit. See
[
12
]
for more information on relative equilibria / periodic orbits.

We are interested in a specific type of solutions
(\bar{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c})
called
traveling wave
(TW) with speed
\bar{c}
and profile
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
, where
\mathbf{u}(x,t)=\bar{\mathbf{v}}(x-\bar{c}t)
and where the limits
\lim\limits_{\xi\to\pm\infty}\bar{\mathbf{v}}(\xi)
are one of the
(u_{i},v_{i})
. Depending on the limits, we shall call the TW differently. If these two limits are the same
(u_{i},v_{i})
for some
i\in\{1,2,3\}
,
i.e.
connecting one of the space clamped stationary states to the same one, we call such solution a
traveling pulse
(TP), see figure
2
top for an example. A
traveling front
(TF) is a TW where the two limits are different.

. Parameters for bottom row

.

Remark 3
.

The fact that the network is in an ISN regime is crucial for the definition of the different TWs. We would not necessarily find them in a different dynamical regime.

Remark 4
.

Note that the numerical methods of section
8
work the same for
traveling fronts
(see
[
19
]
). We will not numerically study them thoroughly in this work.

It is straightforward to derive an equation satisfied by the TW but it is more convenient to introduce the so-called
freezing method

[
9
,
52
,
4
]
by expressing (
3
) in a wave frame
\xi=x-\gamma(t)
with
\gamma(0)=0
. The wave ansatz
\mathbf{u}(x,t)=\mathbf{v}(x-\gamma(t),t)
for some phase
\gamma
and profile
\mathbf{v}
, yields the equations where we write
\partial:=\partial_{\xi}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}&=&\lambda\partial\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v};p),\\
\frac{d\gamma}{dt}&=&\lambda.\end{array}\right.

These equations are easy to get, they stem from the time derivative of
\mathbf{v}(x-\gamma(t),t)
.
However, the mapping
\gamma
is still arbitrary. We fix this degree of freedom by using a phase-condition. For a given reference profile
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
, we look for the profile
\mathbf{v}
that minimize
J(\gamma):=\|\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot-\gamma)-\mathbf{v}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}
. This leads to the phase condition:

0=\langle\mathbf{v}-\hat{\mathbf{u}},\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle_{L^{2}}.

All in all, we arrive at the differential algebraic equation (DAE):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}&=&\lambda\partial\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v};p),\\
\frac{d\gamma}{dt}&=&\lambda,\\
0&=&\langle\mathbf{v}-\hat{\mathbf{u}},\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle_{L^{2}}.\end{array}\right.

(4)

We observe that the second equation decouples from the other two.
Given a solution
\mathbf{u}
of (
3
), we can recover the phase
\gamma
by substituting
\mathbf{u}(x+\gamma(t),t)
in the phase condition and differentiating
w.r.t.
time:

\dot{\gamma}=g(t,\gamma(t)),\quad\quad g(t,\gamma):=\frac{\langle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u}(t);p),\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot+\gamma)\rangle_{L^{2}}}{\langle\mathbf{u}(t),\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot+\gamma)\rangle_{L^{2}}}.

Thus, the only freedom in finding those waves is to fix a reference profile
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
such that (
4
) is well posed.

Remark 5
.

It can be interesting, especially for numerical bifurcation analysis, to view the previous equation (
4
) as a DAE with mass matrix
\mathbf{M}=diag(\mathrm{I}_{2},0)

\mathbf{M}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\begin{matrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\lambda\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda\partial\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v};p)\\
\langle\mathbf{v}-\hat{\mathbf{u}},\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle_{L^{2}}\end{matrix}\right].

A TW with speed
\bar{c}
is thus a stationary point
(\bar{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c})
of (
4
) with appropriate boundary conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}0&=&\bar{c}\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}+\mathbf{F}(\bar{\mathbf{v}};p)\\
0&=&\langle\bar{\mathbf{v}}-\hat{\mathbf{u}},\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle_{L^{2}}.\end{array}\right.

(5)

Finally, we are interested in
modulated traveling waves
1
1

1

called MTP for pulses. We require that the limits at
\xi=\pm\infty
are equal at all time
MTW
(\tilde{\mathbf{u}},\bar{c},T)
which are
T
-periodic solutions of (
3
) satisfying

\forall t\geq 0,\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ \tilde{\mathbf{u}}(x,t+T)=\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(x-\bar{c}T,t)

or equivalently
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(\xi,t+T)=\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(\xi,t)
in the wave frame
\xi=x-\bar{c}t
. MTW can thus be seen as
T
-periodic solutions
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
of

\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=\bar{c}\partial\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v};p).

(6)

Similarly, a
T
-periodic solution
(\tilde{\mathbf{w}},\tilde{\lambda})
of (
4
) also yields a MTW.
Indeed, by definition, we have

\mathbf{u}(x,t+T)=\tilde{\mathbf{w}}(x-\tilde{\gamma}(t+T),t+T)=\tilde{\mathbf{w}}(x-\tilde{\gamma}(t)-\delta\gamma,t)=\mathbf{u}(x+\delta\gamma,t)

where
\delta\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle see\ \eqref{eq:pddae}}}{{:=}}\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{\lambda}=\bar{c}T
defines
\bar{c}
as mean value of
\tilde{\lambda}
. The numerical advantage of the formulation (
4
) is that the wave speed
\bar{c}
is a byproduct but we need to solve a DAE whereas when solving (
6

\bar{c}
must be known accurately.

4.2
Stability of traveling waves

Definition 1

(Orbital stability
[
61
]
)
.

A traveling wave
(\bar{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c})
is exponentially and asymptotically orbitally stable if there are
M\geq 0,\alpha>0
such that for all
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, there is
\delta>0
such that for each solution
\mathbf{u}
of (
3
) with
\left\|\mathbf{u}(0)-\bar{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot+\gamma_{0})\right\|\leq\delta
for some
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a phase shift
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\forall t\geq 0,\ \left\|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,t)-\bar{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot-\bar{c}t+\gamma)\right\|\leq\epsilon.

Additionally,

\left\|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,t)-\bar{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot-\bar{c}t+\gamma)\right\|\leq Me^{-\alpha t}.

The orbital stability of
(\bar{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c})
is determined (see
[
56
]
for reaction-diffusion equations) by the spectrum of

\mathbf{A}:=\bar{c}\partial+d\mathbf{F}(\bar{\mathbf{v}};p).

(7)

The zero eigenvalue is always present because
\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}
in the kernel of
\mathbf{A}
as a consequence of the translation symmetry. If
0
is a simple eigenvalue and the rest of spectrum lies in
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ \Re(z)\leq w\}
for some
w<0
, then the TW is (orbitally) stable. This is an example of the
principle of linearized stability
. We shall see that this holds for the neural field equations (
1
). Note that this stability result was conjectured in
[
19
]
in a slightly different setting.

We now collect the different hypotheses concerning the components of (
2
).

Hypothesis 1
.

S\in C^{r+1}(\mathbb{R},[0,1])
with
r\geq 2
is increasing with all derivatives bounded.

Hypothesis 2
.

We assume that

1.

\bar{c}>0
(
w.l.o.g.
using the reflection symmetry),

2.

\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}
,

3.

\hat{\mathbf{u}}\in H^{2}
,

4.

\langle\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}},\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{2}\neq 0
.

Additionally, because of the reflection symmetry
\kappa\cdot\mathbf{u}(x):=\mathbf{u}(-x)
of equation (
1
) for even spatial connectivity kernels
K_{p}
, if
(\bar{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c})
is a solution, then so is
(\kappa\cdot\bar{\mathbf{v}},-\bar{c})
which makes Hypothesis
2
.1 less restrictive.

Remark 6
.

Choosing
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
in
\ker(\mathbf{A}^{*})
so that
\langle\hat{\mathbf{u}},\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}\rangle=1
would simplify the analysis but in practical applications,
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
is not known beforehand.

Hypothesis 3
.

K_{k}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{+})
for
k\in\{e,i\}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let us consider a traveling wave
(\bar{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c})
.
Grant
1
,
2
and
3
.
Assume that
\ker\mathbf{A}=span\{\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}\}
.
Further assume that the non-zero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
are in the set
\{\Re<w\}\subset\mathbb{C}
for some
w<0
.
Then, the traveling wave is exponentially and asymptotically orbitally stable in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
.

This results implies that the norm in definition
1
is the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
one. In particular, for traveling pulses (which do not belong to
\mathrm{L}^{2}
), we only allow for neighborhoods in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
.

The theorem is especially useful in that we only have to characterize the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
close to the imaginary axis and not the full spectrum.

Remark 7
.

We prove stability of the TW for the DAE formulation (
4
) in theorem
3
which implies theorem
1
.

4.3
Bifurcations of traveling waves

The goal of this section is to provide a bifurcation theory for TW. To this end, we prove the existence of an (invariant) center manifold of finite dimension for (
4
). Then, the study of the dynamics on this invariant manifold through the use of normal form theory
[
33
]
gives access to a bifurcation theory for TWs.

We introduce the projector
\mathbf{P}
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}
onto
\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}
along
\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}

\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathsf{v}:=\mathsf{v}-\frac{\langle\mathsf{v},\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle_{L^{2}}}{\langle\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}},\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle_{L^{2}}}\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}

(8)

which is well defined under Hypothesis
2
.

Theorem 2
.

Let us consider a traveling wave
(\bar{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c})
.
Grant
1
,
2
and
3
.
Assume that
\sigma_{c}:=\Sigma(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A})\cap i\mathbb{R}\neq\emptyset
and denote by

\mathbb{E}_{c}
the sum of the generalized eigenspaces of
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}
for the zero real part eigenvalues
\sigma_{c}
.
Define
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathbf{P}\mathrm{H}^{1}
.
Then, there exists a map
\Psi\in C^{r}(\mathbb{E}_{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathcal{Z})
with
r\geq 2
such that

\Psi(0;0)=0,\ d\Psi(0;0)=0

and a neighborhood
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{u}}\times\mathcal{O}_{p}
of
(0,0)
in
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that for
p\in\mathcal{O}_{p}
, the manifold

\mathcal{M}(p)=\left\{\mathbf{v}_{0}+\Psi\left(\mathbf{v}_{0};p\right);\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{E}_{c}\right\}\subset\mathcal{Z}

has the following properties:

1.

\mathcal{M}(p)
is locally invariant, i.e., if
\mathbf{v}
is a solution to (
20
) satisfying
\mathbf{v}(0)\in\mathcal{M}(p)\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{u}}
and
\mathbf{v}(t)\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{u}}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, then
\mathbf{v}(t)\in\mathcal{M}(p)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

2.

\mathcal{M}(p)
contains the set of bounded solutions of (
20
) staying in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{u}}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e., if
\mathbf{v}
is a solution to (
20
) satisfying
\mathbf{v}(t)\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{u}}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\mathbf{v}(0)\in\mathcal{M}(p)
.

This theorem is the basis for the study of the Hopf bifurcation points that appear in the simulations in section
9
, it was also mentioned in
[
59
,
60
]
and in
[
31
]
. Indeed, assume that
\pm\T1\i\omega\in\Sigma(\mathbf{A})
for some
\omega>0
with associated eigenvectors
\zeta,\bar{\zeta}
and no other eigenvalue is present on the imaginary axis. Further assume that
\ker\mathbf{A}=span\{\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}\}
. Then, the previous theorem yields a two-dimensional invariant manifold on which we can apply normal form theory. Generically, we expect to see the following dynamics

\dot{z}=(\alpha+\T1\i\omega)z+\beta z|z|^{2}+h.o.t,\quad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C},

where
\mathbf{v}(t)=z(t)\zeta+\bar{z}(t)\bar{\zeta}+h.o.t.
and
h.o.t.
denotes higher order terms.
The analysis of bifurcations like the Fold, Hopf or Bogdanov-Takens ones is based on the previous theorem and is left for future analysis. Note that these bifurcations of TW appear in the numerical analysis (section
9
) of the neural fields model (
1
).

4.4
Linear stability of modulated traveling waves

Definition 2

(Orbital stability of MTW)
.

A modulated traveling wave
(\tilde{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c},T)
is exponentially and asymptotically orbitally stable if there are
M\geq 0,\alpha>0
such that for all
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, there is
\delta>0
such that for each solution
\mathbf{u}
of (
3
) with
\left\|\mathbf{u}(0)-\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot-\bar{c}t+\gamma_{0},\phi_{0})\right\|\leq\delta
for some
\gamma_{0},\phi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist shifts
\gamma,\phi\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\forall t\geq 0,\ \left\|\mathbf{u}(\cdot,t)-\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot-\bar{c}t+\gamma,t+\phi)\right\|\leq\epsilon.

Additionally,

8
Numerical methods

Owing to the number of space variables, we aim at performing the computations in a matrix-free setting freeing us from the limitation on the number of variables. In effect, for a space discretization of size
N
, the number of unknowns is
2N+1
for a TW.

Note that
pde2path

[
64
]
supports the computation of waves through the addition of constraints to the model formulation. However, the software lacks shooting capabilities and that is why we use
BifurcationKit.jl

[
66
]
instead. The implemented methods require very little modification of this Julia package and will be added to the official package in the near future.

8.1
Traveling waves

The traveling waves are numerically approximated as zeros of (
5
) with Neumann boundary conditions for
\partial
using a Krylov-Newton
[
35
,
50
]
algorithm with preconditioner
[
57
]

Pr:=(-I+c_{prec}\partial,-I/\tau+c_{prec}\partial)
for
c_{prec}
estimated with direct simulation. It is very cheap to cache the sparse LU decomposition of
Pr
even for large discretizations. Without this preconditioner, the Krylov-Newton algorithm converges very slowly or does not converge at all. The iterative linear solver used for the Newton iterations is GMRES
[
3
]
.

8.2
Stability of traveling waves

The linear stability of a TW
(\bar{\mathbf{v}},\bar{c})
is deduced from the computation of the spectrum of
\mathbf{A}/\bar{c}
in (
7
). This is performed using a shift-invert method
[
55
]
based on the preconditioner
Pr
in order to extract the right-most eigenvalue.
However, we found that the method sometimes misses eigenvalues with large imaginary parts stemming from the transport operator
\partial
, so we validate our results with the slower computation of the spectrum of
e^{t\mathbf{A}}
(in effect the semigroup) for a fixed and small
t>0
, see
[
63
]
. In all cases, the iterative eigensolver is based on the Arnoldi algorithm
[
55
]
.

Another method consists in computing the spectrum of
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{P}}
based on lemma
5
. In the context of bifurcation theory, this is really advantageous as it removes the
0
eigenvalue associated with
\partial\bar{\mathbf{v}}
with is nonzero (numerically) although quite small in practice (
\approx 10^{-9}
).

8.3
Numerical bifurcation analysis of traveling waves

To study the dependence of the TW on a parameter
p
, we use
a pseudo-arclength continuation
[
28
]
method which allows us to obtain curves of solutions
(\bar{\mathbf{u}}(s),\bar{c}(s),p(s))
,
s
being the arclength.
The wave reference
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
and the wave speed of the preconditioner
Pr
is updated with the previously found wave solution.

The detection of bifurcation points in a continuation run is performed using the bisection algorithm implemented in
[
66
]
. The continuation of codim 1 bifurcation points (Fold / Hopf) in the wave frame is based on a minimally augmented formulation of (
4
) with the diagonal mass matrix
\mathbf{M}=diag(I_{2N},0)
. Finally, the codimension 2 bifurcations are computed along the Fold/Hopf curve based on theorem
2
.

8.4
Direct simulations

The computation of the solutions of the DAE (
4
) and the ODE (
6
) is based on the adaptive and implicit time stepper Rodas4P2 of the Julia package
DifferentialEquations.jl

[
53
]
using a Jacobian-free formulation with preconditioner
Pr
updated at time steps for which the iterative solver GMRES has longer iterations than a prescribed number.

8.5
Modulated traveling waves

In this paragraph, we provide details for computing MTW using two general different methods, each one having its own pros and cons. We recall that MTW are periodic solutions with period
T
of (
4
) which we rewrite

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}&=&\lambda(t)\partial\mathbf{v}(t)+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v}(t);p)\\
0&=&\langle\mathbf{v}(t)-\hat{\mathbf{u}},\partial\hat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle_{L^{2}}\\
0&=&s(\mathbf{v}(0),T)\end{array}\right.

(MTW-DAE)

where
s(\mathbf{v}(0),T)
is a section to remove the time phase invariance. Analogously, the MTW can be found by solving the ODE

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}&=&\bar{c}\partial\mathbf{v}(t)+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{v}(t);p)\\
0&=&s(\mathbf{v}(0),T).\end{array}\right.

(MTW-ODE)

These problems are solved using a time discretization yielding a large spatio-temporal problem or using shooting. The spatio-temporal formulation is the fastest to solve in our experiments but it is not adaptive and fails to capture orbits with large periods or stiff changes. The shooting method is slower but more robust and simpler to implement.

8.6
Modulated traveling waves, finite differences

In this case, the box scheme
[
34
,
65
,
38
]
based on a trapezoidal one is applied to find the solutions
(\mathbf{v}(t_{i}),\lambda(t_{i}),T)
of (
MTW-DAE
). More precisely, using a uniform mesh of length
M
of the time interval
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{M}=1
, we discretize the first equation of (
MTW-DAE
) as follows:

\frac{T}{h}\left[\mathbf{v}(t_{i})-\mathbf{v}(t_{i-1})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\lambda(t_{i})\partial\mathbf{v}(t_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\lambda(t_{i-1})\partial\mathbf{v}(t_{i-1})+\mathbf{F}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathbf{v}(t_{i})+\mathbf{v}(t_{i-1})\right];p\right),

(25)

where
1\leq i\leq M,h:=\frac{1}{M}
and
\mathbf{v}(t_{1}):=\mathbf{v}(1)
.
Analogously, we denote by
(\mathbf{v}(t_{i}),T,\bar{c})
the discretized solution to (
MTW-ODE
). This gives a number of unknowns of
3MN+1
or
2NM+2
depending on the formulation. In both cases, the solution to the linear problem associated with the Krylov-Newton algorithm is unlikely to converge without a preconditioner.
When solving (
MTW-ODE
), we use the precondionner
Pr_{ode}(c_{prec})
where
c_{prec}
is close to the speed
\bar{c}
we are looking for.
Pr_{ode}(c_{prec})
is defined as the linear operator obtained by replacing
\mathbf{F}
in (
25
) by
-\mathbf{L}_{0}
which makes it sparse.
When solving (
MTW-DAE
), the precondionner
Pr_{dae}(\tilde{\mu}(t_{i}))
is built similarly with
\mathbf{F}
replaced by
(-\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathrm{I})
where the identity operator is for the
\lambda
component of (
MTW-DAE
). We found that these cheap preconditioners allows fast computation of the MTW. In practice, 1 Newton iteration takes roughly 1s for
M\approx 50
.

In this case, the stability of the MTW is assessed by the computation of the Floquet exponents.
Their computation is based on the Arnoldi algorithm
[
55
]
.

8.7
Modulated traveling waves, shooting

The second way to find MTW is to apply a shooting method
[
26
,
25
]
based on a preconditioned adaptive and implicit time stepper. More precisely, to solve
MTW-DAE
, we look for
(\mathbf{v},\lambda,T)
where
\mathbf{v}
is a (several) point(s) on the orbit. We note
\phi_{dae}^{t}(\mathbf{v},\lambda)
the flow of (
4
) and thus seek zeros of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}0&=&(\mathbf{v},\lambda)-\phi_{dae}^{T}(\mathbf{v},\lambda)\\
0&=&s(\mathbf{v},T).\end{array}\right.

(SH-DAE)

Finally, one last method is to apply a standard shooting to the ODE (
MTW-ODE
) to find the zeros
(\mathbf{v},\bar{c},T)
of the functional:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}0&=&\mathbf{v}-\phi^{T}(\mathbf{v};\bar{c})\\
0&=&s(\mathbf{v},T),\end{array}\right.

(SH-ODE)

where the flow of (
6
) is written
\phi^{t}(\mathbf{v};\bar{c})
.
In both cases, the linear problem associated with the Krylov-Newton algorithm is solved using the iterative linear solver GMRES where the jacobian operator of the functional is evaluated with centered finite differences.

The computation of the Floquet multipliers associated to (
6
) is based on the Arnoldi algorithm
[
55
]
applied to the monodromy operator along the MTW.

Discussion

In this work, we focused on the theoretical / numerical analysis of the dynamics of TW and MTW in a two populations neural field model with one dimensional cortex. We proved a principle of linearised stability for TW, develop a bifurcation theory of TW and characterize the spectral properties associated to MTW. We based our analysis on the freezing method yielding a DAE which allows an efficient description of the dynamics. The only point left for future study is the proof of a principle of linearized stability for MTW. Compared to reaction-diffusion systems, the analysis of MTW for neural field equations seems more challenging as the dynamics in the wave frame is not regularizing,
i.e.
the linearized operator is not sectorial. This prevents us from defining a Poincaré return map for example.

Then, we presented numerical methods to compute MTW. This is where the freezing method really shines as it allows to simulate the wave profile for as long as we want, we are not limited by the simulation domain as in
[
29
]
for example. We found these MTW from Hopf bifurcations of TP.
Interestingly, we numerically found Fold, Hopf and Bogdanov-Takens bifurcations of TP which were analytically studied in the case of a Heaviside nonlinearity in the very detailed work
[
22
]
. The study of stationary traveling waves for different “mean field” of neural populations has recently been performed in
[
40
,
49
,
8
,
41
]
. We are then able to follow the MTW as function of a parameter (in this case the time scale ratio
\tau
) and showed evidences for snaking of MTP.

We focused on single pulses and while our stability results do not depend on their specific shape, dedicated tools have been developed to study the existence of multi-pulse solutions. This would be an interesting area of future research in the context of MTPs.

We discarded the presence of conduction delays in the model albeit they seem to be very important in the modeling of visual cortex
[
46
]
. In this context, our analysis does not hold and a lot remains to be done and a dedicated framework seems to be lacking in this case (see
[
44
]
for a numerical study in the context of neural fields).

The extension of the theory to two-dimensional domains is not completely direct and we already expect the loss of exponential stability of TP based on
[
18
]
. The study of circular pulses seems of intermediate difficulty and of great interest given their relevance to visual cortex
[
46
]
.

Finally, the interplay between noise and wave dynamics is a fascinating topic
[
36
,
37
,
32
,
42
]
. A lot remains to be done in this context especially for MTW and 2d cortices.
