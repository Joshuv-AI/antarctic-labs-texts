Comparative assessment of five analytical methods for airborne microplastics highlights importance of identifying sub- methods
Authors: Alex Aves, Anna MacDonald, Sebastian Naeher, Dušan Materić, Deonie Castle, Perry Davy, Sally Gaw, Laura E Revell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroplastics are an emerging class of contaminants that are widespread in the environment. While studies on airborne microplastics are rapidly increasing, the lack of standardised sampling and analysis methods is hindering progress towards accurately quantifying their global distribution. This study used existing analytical methodologies to understand the variability in identifying and quantifying airborne microplastics. High-volume aerosol samples were collected at a remote site in New Zealand and were analysed by micro-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, micro-Raman spectroscopy, Nile red staining combined with fluorescence microscopy, pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry and thermal desorption-proton transfer reaction-mass spectrometry, with all methods reporting different concentrations and polymer types. No particles were larger than 100 µm, and 98% of particles were smaller than 10 µm, highlighting the importance of methods that can analyse sub-10 µm particles. This study highlights the need for caution when comparing airborne microplastic datasets analysed using different methods.Subject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Since the first published study on airborne microplastics in 20161 the field has grown rapidly, with more than 100 published studies to date2. While studies characterising airborne microplastics are essential for expanding knowledge of their presence, abundance, transport, distribution, and chemical composition, there is a serious lack of standardisation in sampling and analysis methods, hampering reliable identification and quantification of airborne microplastics. Understanding global concentrations of airborne microplastics is essential for assessing inhalation risk to humans and other animals3,4, as well as their potential role in climate change5 and the impact of deposition contamination on land, water and soil6. The lack of standardisation in this field stems from the numerous pathways in study design that accompany an evolving field, as well as sparse reporting, which limits the reproducibility and comparability of studies7. Choices must be made at every stage of study design, from sample collection, sample preparation, sample analysis, and data interpretation, which influence study outcomes8–12.

Commonly used analytical techniques and protocols for airborne microplastic research have been developed based on previous studies into microplastics in environmental matrices, such as water and soil. This limits applicability to aerosol samples, as the size range of interest is smaller than for other matrices. For example, marine microplastic studies often use neuston net tows, which commonly have a mesh size of 333 µm. This means that analysis methods with higher particle size limits of detection can be used13. Airborne particles, including microplastics, smaller than 10 µm in diameter, pose a potentially greater risk to human health through inhalation exposure (i.e. PM10) and can also remain suspended in the atmosphere long enough to be relevant to climate processes14–16. Depending on the analytical techniques and protocols used, microplastic results are reported as particle counts or mass concentrations, with distinct advantages for each17. Without standardisation within the airborne microplastics field, understanding global abundance and synthesising multiple datasets proves difficult. To report accurate and reliable results, the airborne microplastic community must produce progressive research to clearly define the best methods of practice in this field, or at least articulate the limitations associated with various methods used.

Through a collaborative project, we conducted a field study and applied five established methodologies to analyse a sample set to identify airborne microplastics. Here, we report results gained from the analysis of aerosol samples via Nile Red staining with fluorescence microscopy, micro-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (µFTIR), micro-Raman spectroscopy (µRaman), pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS) and thermal desorption-proton transfer reaction-mass spectrometry (TD-PTR-MS). Currently, out of these five methods, µFTIR is the most frequently used method in airborne microplastic research (n = 166), followed closely by µRaman (n = 143), then Py-GC/MS (n = 17), fluorescence microscopy (n = 11) and lastly TD-PTR-MS (n=4) (Supporting Information). We aim to advance understanding of current airborne microplastic analysis techniques and to identify the benefits, limitations, and unknowns associated with these methods in use.

Results and discussion

Fluorescence microscopy with nile red staining

As fluorescence microscopy cannot accurately identify polymers18, we have presented results from Nile red analysis as ‘particles’ rather than microplastics. A total of 4652 particles were counted across all imaged sections of the filter using fluorescence microscopy methods. The particle concentration across all samples ranged from 197-3896 particles m (mean: 1891 particles m) (Fig. 1; Table S4). High variability was shown across the replicates and days. The lowest concentrations were measured on day 2 () and the highest particle concentrations were measured on day 3 () (Fig. 1). No particles were detected in the imaged section for all blank samples, except for day 1, where a total of 14 particles were found across all subsampled images (n=10). To take a conservative approach, the total number of particles across the blank subsampled sections from day 1 was subtracted from all sample results before extrapolation. Of the total particles, 4595 were fragments (99%) and 57 were fibres (1%). Most particles (98%) had diameters smaller than ; however, it should be noted that the size detection limit is approximately 1 µm; therefore, the µm size bin only contains 1-2 µm particles. Given that previous research highlights smaller MPs as more abundant (by number) than larger MPs, this may lead to underestimated number concentrations of very small MPs (Fig. 1; Ref.19). There were no particles larger than 100 µm. This implies that studies that cannot detect particles smaller than are missing nearly all (98%) of the microplastic particles present.

Mean concentration (±SD) per day calculated from Nile red analysis and the corresponding size distribution observed across all samples.

Only one other study has used fluorescence microscopy with Nile Red staining to report the presence of airborne microplastics in New Zealand20, however comparison is difficult as20 reported deposition fluxes, not concentration as reported here, and the samples were collected within a large city (population 1.7 million), 1000 km northeast of the current study site. Despite this, similar particle morphologies were identified, with 95% of particles classified as fragments and 5% classified as fibres in Ref.20 and in the current study 99% of particles were identified as fragments and 1% as fibres, as mentioned above. Reference20 observed larger particle sizes overall, with most particles in the 10–50 µm range and fewer than 3% of particles larger than 100 µm, whereas most particles in the current study were between 2–10 µm, with no particles larger than 100 µm (Fig. 1). The smaller sizes observed in this current study suggest long-range transport of particles rather than local sources21.

A comparative methods study completed on a standard set of marine sediments identified the overestimation of Nile Red fluorescence microscopy when compared to common spectroscopy techniques22, with other research highlighting that there is a potential for up to 100% overestimation of microplastics from this method via the presence of other organic materials that Nile red can bind to Ref.18. The high rates of suspected microplastic detections identified via this method likely reflect misidentification, particularly due to the lack of a digestion step prior to sample analysis. The high results presented here from Nile red analysis support the findings of previous work, which reported overestimation of microplastics due to limitations and the method’s non-specificity.

Micro-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Microplastics were not detected using µFTIR analysis in this study. Although visual screening by microscope identified potential plastic particles, all such particles were confirmed to be non-plastic by µFTIR. The size limitation of the µFTIR method used, which involved manually moving particles using tweezers under a microscope, meant that no particles smaller than 32 µm could be transferred and therefore analysed. This highlights the limitations of this method for atmospheric aerosol samples, as most atmospheric particles (by number) are smaller than 100 µm23. It should be noted that previous research using the same analysis method successfully identified microplastics, but the smallest identified particle was 50 µm in diameter24. Other µFTIR methods, such as focal plane array µFTIR, have a lower detection limit (11 µm) and could therefore yield different results to those found in this study. Fundamentally, the size detection limit of the airborne microplastic analytical method used is essential in reporting concentrations.

Micro-Raman

Across all samples, a total of 6,694 particles were analysed via µRaman spectroscopy, with an average of 3.2% of these particles confirmed as plastic. µRaman results ranged from 78–391 MP m (mean: 161 MP m) (Fig. 2; Table S6). The lowest concentrations were measured on day 4 (96 MP m) and the highest concentrations were measured on day 2 (231 MP m). The highest variability between sampler replicates was also observed on day 3 (Fig. 2). Nineteen polymer types were identified across all samples. Polysulfone (16%), polyacrylonitrile (15%) and polymethyl methacrylate (11%) were the most common polymers identified (Fig. 2).

Mean concentration (±SD) per day calculated from Raman analysis and the corresponding polymer composition of those samples.

The µRaman method quantified the largest number of polymer types (19) among all methods. As µRaman provides information about individual particles, it is highly specific in its reporting. These results are comparable to those found in outdoor aerosol samples collected in Germany, with an average concentration of 91 MP m25, and a study carried out in an indoor environment in Denmark, which reported concentrations ranging from 58 to 684 MP m26. Our results are 1-3 orders of magnitude higher than obtained in previous studies using µRaman in Asia, the US and marine environments27–30 and are much lower than reported concentrations of 2502 MP m in London, UK31. These variations are likely due to the selection strategy for particle analysis (i.e. analysing all particles with no selection bias), the different samplers used for collection (i.e. active vs. passive), the study locations, and the sample preparation methods employed across the studies.

Pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry

The mean MP concentration measured by Py-GC/MS was 1 µg m, with concentrations ranging from <MDL–2.84 µg m across the samples (Fig. 3; Table S11). Four polymer types were confirmed across the sample set out of the eight analysed for, with PVC making up the majority, followed by Nylon-66 (N-66), PMMA and PS (Fig. 3). Across all samples, mean polymer concentrations for each identified polymer type were 0.74 µg m for PVC, 0.17 µg m for N-66, 0.08 µg m for PMMA and 0.01 µg m for PS (Table S10). Given the current uncertainties in quantifying PE and PVC via Py-GC/MS, we acknowledge that caution should be exercised when interpreting the PVC results presented32. If PVC results are omitted from reporting here, overall microplastic concentrations would range from <MDL–0.64 µg m. Here, we aimed to utilise existing quantification methods and to explore their applicability to airborne microplastic research; however, variations in results are plausible when other quantifying ions are used. Few previous studies have used Py-GC/MS analysis to quantify airborne microplastics. Indoor airborne microplastics have been reported in Refs.33 and34 at rates of 3 ng m (N-6) and 10–360 ng m (N-66, PP, NBR, PS), respectively. Reference35 collected samples using a high-volume air sampler in an outdoor environment with results ranging from 0.09–6.3 ng m (PS, PP, SBR). These concentrations are significantly lower than those found in the current study, likely because35 quantified only three polymer types and used an alternate sampler that size-fractionates the particles.

Mean concentration (±SD) per day calculated from Py-GC/MS analysis and the corresponding polymer composition of those samples.

Py-GC/MS analysis for microplastics has yet to be standardised. The range of quantification methods used to calculate MP concentrations using Py-GC/MS data makes it difficult to compare published studies. The use of singular quantifier pyrolysis products for polymer identification can lead to misidentification due to interferences from other sources, including natural organic matter, which may cause the concentrations reported here to be higher than those in other studies32,36. The discrepancies between existing airborne microplastic studies using Py-GC/MS highlight the need for caution in interpreting current results. The microplastic community needs to focus on developing standardised sampling, sample preparation, and analysis guidelines to enable the successful and accurate quantification of MPs using this analytical method.

Thermal desorption-proton transfer reaction-mass spectrometry

TD-PTR-MS identified mean concentrations of 91 ng m, ranging from 44-170 ng m (Fig. 4; Table S13). Of the six polymer types quantified in this study, four were identified. The frequency of polymer detection was PE (39%) > TWP (29%) > PP (21%) > PET (12%) at average concentrations of 37 ng m, 23 ng m, 21 ng m and 14 ng m respectively (Fig. 4). The most similar work37, used active pumped sampling to measure PM10 and PM1 at a high-alpine site in Europe, using the same analysis technique and reporting results in the same units as the current study. The reported average concentrations were 35 ng m for PM10 samples with maximum concentrations of 165 ng m. While we might expect higher concentrations at a Northern Hemisphere site than at a Southern Hemisphere site due to population density, the size fraction analysed will influence the results, as the samples in the current study were TSP, whereas those in Ref.37 were PM10. Reference37 identified the most abundant polymer types as PET, PE and PP, whereas in our study PE, TWP and PP were the most abundant, with PET present at the lowest concentrations. The high TWP concentrations identified in our study are likely related to the proximity of the sampling site to the nearby highway. In a similar study, the mean concentration of airborne microplastics and nanoplastics in urban PM2.5 was 238 ng m, with maximum concentrations of up to 557 ng m38. Although the samples in Ref.38 were PM2.5, the reported rates were higher than those measured in the current study, which used TSP samples. This suggests that the differing sampling locations between these studies (northern vs. southern hemispheres; remote vs. urban) may be a potential point of influence.

Overview of methods

The mean plastic concentrations across all samples were 1891 particles m for Nile red fluorescence microscopy, 161 MP m for µRaman spectroscopy, 1 µg m for Py-GC/MS and 91 ng m for TD-PTR-MS. The highest overall variation was observed on day 3 (Table S15), and the Py-GC/MS results on day 4 exhibited the highest variation across all analysis methods (Fig. 3; Table S15). All chemically specific analytical methods detected different polymer types, with minimal overlap. In the mass-based methods, PE was the most abundant polymer detected via TD-PTR-MS, whereas PVC was the most abundant polymer detected via Py-GC/MS, and neither method identified both. In contrast, Raman analysis identified polysulfone as the most abundant polymer with PE contributing only 4.3% of total plastics and PVC contributing only 0.3% of total plastics. The Raman analysis identified the largest number of polymer types, 19, followed by TD-PTR-MS and Py-GC/MS, which both identified 4 (Table S14). Nile red analysis cannot chemically identify polymer types and is therefore limited in this regard.

The mean results for Py-GC/MS were approximately 10 times higher than those from TD-PTR-MS. Previous research has explored the relationship between TD-PTR-MS and µRaman, with nanoplastics assessed via TD-PTR-MS and microplastics assessed via µRaman8. Reference8 obtained comparable results by mass between the methods (after mass conversion), however, the polymer types identified varied between the methods, and particle counts were orders of magnitude higher from TD-PTR-MS analysis (nanoplastics) compared to µRaman analysis (MP). A comparison between Py-GC/MS and µFTIR analyses of surface water, wastewater, and sediment samples revealed similar trends in microplastic concentrations across both methods39. It concluded that there was a good overall comparability between the methods, despite the variation in polymer type, and that the converted mass results were not in agreement39. The elevated results reported by Py-GC/MS relative to TD-PTR-MS (1000 vs 91 ng m) highlight discrepancies even within spectrometric methods. Quantification methods for TD-PTR-MS use 40 specific ions to identify each polymer type based on established libraries from a range of standards, making it much more specific in its identification compared to Py-GC/MS which uses single ions for quantification that can often be from non-polymeric origins, albeit with the assessment of other indicator ions. Specifically, PE and PVC pyrolysates are limited in their specificity due to the potential for interference from other sources32. In this study we confirmed the presence of PE and PVC using qualifying ion ratios from standards, which led to the exclusion of PE results and inclusion of all but one PVC result.

Concentrations of suspected microplastics identified by Nile red fluorescence microscopy were over 13 times greater than those measured by µRaman. The high abundance of suspected microplastics observed by Nile red fluorescence microscopy in this study highlights the limitations inherent to this method, as noted previously18. There is a need for caution when assessing the literature on global microplastic abundance data collected using this method, and the future exclusion of this technique from reporting airborne microplastic abundance should be considered. While this technique can still provide insight into particle size and shape, the certainty that the observed particles are of plastic origin remains limited, thereby reducing the integrity of this method.

The coefficient of variation (CV) varied considerably across both methods and sampling days (Supporting Information, Table S15), reflecting differences in method precision and, in some cases, concentrations approaching detection limits. TD-PTR-MS demonstrated the most consistent performance across all days (CV: 16–39%), while Raman exhibited notably high variability on day 3 (CV = 88%), consistent with the elevated overall variation observed on that day. As noted above, Py-GC/MS returned no quantifiable values for any day 3 sample and produced a CV of 103% on day 4, driven by the anomalously elevated D4 S3 replicate. Nile red showed the greatest range in CV across days (13–58%), further supporting the concerns regarding its reliability discussed above.

Spearman rank correlation analysis revealed that Nile red diverged systematically from all other methods, exhibiting significant negative correlations with both Py-GC/MS ( = −0.66, p = 0.005) and TD-PTR-MS ( = −0.64, p = 0.008). Raman, Py-GC/MS, and TD-PTR-MS showed positive pairwise correlations, with only the Raman–TD-PTR-MS pair reaching statistical significance ( = 0.59, p = 0.017). The positive correlations between the chemically specific methods likely reflect shared day-to-day trends in total plastic burden rather than agreement on polymer composition, which is consistent with the minimal overlap in polymer types identified across these methods. The systematic divergence of Nile red from all three chemically specific methods further reinforces the concerns regarding its reliability discussed above. Full correlation coefficients and p-values are provided in the Supporting Information (Table S16).

When assessing these results, it is evident that the methods cannot be directly compared because of the overarching differences in what they measure and how they measure it. The polymer types identified across all methods varied greatly, with minimal overlap; the reporting units differ, and the concentrations and fluxes are highly variable. This ultimately means that we should compare only results analysed using the same methods, even if the reporting units are comparable.

Limitations and future research

This study presents results from a set of airborne particulate matter samples analysed via five microplastic analytical methods. The results presented are specific to the study design outlined. Given changes to sample collection, preparation, sample analysis, and equipment setup, as well as quantification steps and result calculations, these results may vary40. We have presented the results in this study as the mean results collected from four samplers for each day. The variation presented for each day for each method thereby potentially demonstrates the heterogeneity of microplastics across a larger aerosol filter, rather than the variation between replicate results of each method. The normalisation of data to present the final concentrations relies on the filter size and the volume of air sampled. Here, only a small sub-sample of a much larger filter was analysed, with results extrapolated to the entire filter. The authors acknowledge the limitations of this process and recommend further work to explore errors across larger samples and differences between direct analysis and preparation steps prior to identification and quantification. The best methods for airborne microplastic sampling and analysis have yet to be determined, and best practices for active sampling should be prioritised moving forward. There is no single, all-encompassing analysis method for microplastic research at present, and the specific analysis, or combination of analyses, used in future studies will depend on the research question. For example, mass concentrations are useful for human health studies and air pollution reporting (the World Health Organisation sets air quality guidelines in terms of mass concentrations), whereas particle concentrations are required for climate modelling, as knowledge of the size distribution is needed to understand direct and indirect aerosol radiative effects5.

There are current limitations associated with each of the presented methods. Nile red fluorescence microscopy provides characteristic data but lacks chemical information and therefore may overestimate microplastic abundance. The µFTIR method used in this study was limited to particles larger than 50 µm based on previous research, limiting its relevance for airborne microplastic research. In contrast, several existing µFTIR methods and instruments have lower thresholds, allowing them to report smaller size ranges41,42. Yet the exclusion of sub-10 µm particles from airborne microplastic analysis remains a major limitation. For Py-GC/MS, standard methods have not yet been established, and quantification depends on the selection of specific diagnostic fragments, which vary across published studies. The use of certain diagnostic fragments may lead to incorrect abundances and overestimation of certain polymer types due to potential interferences from other substances that contain hydrocarbon chains32. Using µRaman, Py-GC/MS, and TD-PTR-MS, we were only able to quantify specific polymer types; therefore, these methods rely on libraries and standards for quantification. Calibrating for a wider range of plastics and open-source polymer libraries for mass-based quantification is necessary as the field advances. Further work is required to better understand the variation seen in polymer identification between the methods.

While studies have compared analysis methods for microplastic identification39,43,44, there is currently a lack of comparative studies for airborne samples. Due to the smaller size range observed in aerosol samples, certain methods present different challenges and opportunities; therefore, research completed in other matrices may not be relevant or transferable to our understanding of effective and reliable analysis techniques for airborne microplastics. All microplastic researchers need to be transparent in their workflows, the methods used, the quantification steps undertaken, the analytical window reported and the processes within individual methods. Given the differences associated with each method (such as the polymers calibrated for, the specificity of the technique, the size limitations of the method, the settings of the instruments and the calculation steps associated with each method) and the findings of this study, direct comparison is not possible, which is a continuing issue of importance in microplastic research across the field45. Each technique can provide distinct insights that contribute to our overall understanding and development of the issue, but they are not directly comparable and yield different insights. While this study has explored apparent variation in sample analysis of airborne microplastics, this is only one step in the study design, with sample collection, sample preparation, and result calculation all influencing the reported results. Future research that strives to standardise best practices for airborne microplastics, including sampling, preparation, analysis, and result calculations, will help advance this field.

Methods

Sample collection and preparation

Sampling site

Sampling was carried out at the University of Canterbury’s (UC) Cass Mountain Research Station (Fig. S1), which is located on 17.75 km of mountain land in the Cass River basin in the upper Waimakariri catchment (coordinates: E). Samples were taken from this location to explore findings across analytical techniques for identifying and quantifying airborne microplastics, but also to increase observational data on background airborne microplastic concentrations which may be typical of the remote Southern Hemisphere. Cass is a settlement at the foothills of the Ka Tiritiri-o-te-Moana (New Zealand Southern Alps) with five houses and one permanent resident. The sampling site was located less than 1 km from a highway and railway tracks, increasing the likelihood of sampling traffic-related microplastics. The UC field station is used intermittently throughout the year for university field trips and field studies, but for the duration of this study only two researchers were present at the station. Samplers were set up beside an unused building (for power access) approximately 200 m from the main field station building. The samplers were on private land behind a locked gate, with no movement other than the two researchers on site for one week.

Sample collection

Four TISCH TE-5170 (Ohio, USA) high-volume total suspended particulate (TSP) air samplers were used to collect samples for this study. Four 12-hour sampling periods were undertaken with all samplers running for the same duration each period (average flow rate of 0.83 m/min). Glass fibre filters (TISCH (TE-G653), USA, 203.2 mm 254 mm, 1.6 µm pore size) were removed from cardboard packaging and placed by researchers wearing powder free nitrile gloves onto the stainless-steel mesh in the TISCH filter holder, secured in place and the lid closed before power was turned on. At the end of each sampling period, power was turned off upon approach and the filters were removed and stored in aluminium trays covered with foil. Replacement filters were installed, and the sampler turned back on. To account for potential contamination from the field site and sample handling, field blanks were exposed to the sampling site and handled in the exact same manner as all filters throughout the entire sampling process. Field blank results were used to calculate a method detection limit (MDL), against which all results were assessed. Average flow rates and total volume sampled by each sampler each day are presented in Table S1, and all quality assurance and quality control information can be found in Supporting Information 2.2.

Sample preparation

The large (203.2 mm 254 mm) filters were subsampled using a 32 mm diameter sharpened wad press. Subsampling was undertaken in a pre-cleaned fume hood that was turned off during sample preparation to limit air movement and airborne contamination. Stamp outs (32 mm diameter) were obtained from each filter for each analysis method (i.e. 5 32 mm stamp outs from each filter, 1 per analysis method; Fig. S2). Each of these stamp-outs was wrapped in aluminium foil and stored in an aluminium tin. Altogether, 20 samples (all 32 mm diameter stamp outs) were prepared for each one of the five analysis techniques. In total, 100 sub-samples were analysed across the five analytical techniques: ([four days four samplers] + four field blanks) five analytical techniques.

Sample analysis

Nile red fluorescence imaging

Dye preparation and staining

Nile red fluorescence imaging was completed at the University of Canterbury, Christchurch, New Zealand. Following previously used Nile red imaging protocols46, a Nile red working solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of Nile red dye into 1 L of HPLC grade methanol ( 99.9%) to create a 1 µg mL Nile red dye solution. Five drops of the solution were added to each filter to coat the filter, using a Pasteur pipette. Filters were oven dried at 50C for 1-2 hours and placed in the refrigerator before analysis. All samples were stored in foil inside aluminium tins to protect them from light between staining and analysis. To prepare the samples for imaging, the 32 mm diameter stamp outs were cut in half to fit on a standard microscope slide. They were then covered with a cover slip and secured with tape on the edges of the slip to prevent movement of the sample or airborne contamination during movement and analysis. These slides were then stored in the dark prior to imaging.

Imaging

A Zeiss Axio Imager M1 with 10 magnification was used for fluorescence microscopy. Green fluorescence was used (excitation 565 nm, emission 630 nm), with corresponding brightfield images also taken as reference images. The exposure time for fluorescence was 0.5 seconds at 30%. A transect through the middle of each half of a filter was imaged, with each image 1 1.25 mm. For each image, both a fluorescence and a bright field z-stack image were taken to ensure all particles at each z-foci were visible. A bright-field image was also acquired to verify that the z-stack image was focused solely on the sample and not on the cover slip, which may have resulted from contamination after sample preparation. Each z-stack focus was manually adjusted for each image, and the average depth profile collected was approximately 100 µm. The size limit of detection (LOD) for this method was 1 µm.

Image processing

The Z-stack images were combined using extended depth of focus on ZenPro 2.6 software and exported as TIFF files for further processing. All images were processed using ImageJ to collect particle counts, size and shape information, with settings kept consistent for all image processing (ImageJ Macro adapted from46; Supporting Information 2.3). All processed particle-count images were saved, manually reviewed, and compared with the original bright-field and fluorescence images; any images in which particles were misidentified were deleted from further analysis. Results from 10 images per sample transect were averaged, and these averages were extrapolated to the total filter surface area. All quality assurance and quality control information can be found in Supporting Information 2.2.

Micro-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Visual screening

µFTIR analysis was completed at the University of Canterbury, Christchurch, New Zealand. Samples were initially screened visually using a Nikon stereomicroscope (SMZ1270). Each filter was imaged in overview, with visible potential microplastics imaged individually. Colour, size and shape were all recorded for each potential polymer. As filters used for air sampling were glass fibre filters, particles were required to be transferred onto a substrate compatible with the transmission of IR light for µFTIR analysis. Individual particles were manually moved using fine-tipped tweezers under the stereomicroscope onto a calcium fluoride disk before µFTIR analysis, with the aid of a drop of ethanol. The smallest spiked particle in this study was 32 µm.

µFTIR analysis

Using a previously established µFTIR method, as presented in Ref.24, which confirmed the successful analysis of microplastics , a HYPERION2000 µFTIR microscope (Bruker Optics), attached to a Vertex 70 (Bruker Optics) spectrometer, was used for polymer identification, using a liquid nitrogen cooled mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. An optical overview image was recorded prior to infrared measurements at 15 magnification. Scans were run in transmission mode (10 scans, 4 cm resolution, spectral range of 4000-1000 cm using OPUS 7.8 software. All spectra were baseline corrected with the CO spectral range excluded. Spectra were saved and compared against Wiley Spectral Databases (Supporting Information 2.4). All quality assurance and quality control information can be found in Supporting Information 2.2.

Micro-Raman spectroscopy

Sample analysis

µRaman analysis was completed at the University of Strathclyde, Glasgow, United Kingdom. Microplastic samples were placed onto the automated stage of an XploRA Plus Horiba Raman confocal microscope spectrometer. The µRaman spectrometer parameters were set to analyse between 200-2000 cm Raman shift for all particles down to 1 µm (785 nm laser, 1200 lines/mm grating). Each sample was analysed in triplicate (a minimum of three areas across the filter, approximately 1% of the filter in each sub-sample window). There was unbiased particle selection (no selection or identification bias; all shapes, sizes, and colours of particles were analysed, along with the background filter material for baseline spectra analysis) resulting in all visible particles in the frame analysed and particles per sub-sample analysed to be representative of the overall sample47,48. Spectra were collected for each particle over 15 s for a minimum of three accumulations at a maximum of 25% power (filter) with 1200 mm grating and 50 µm split settings.

Polymer identification

Resulting collected particle spectra (n = 6694) were saved in Horiba standard format (L6M or .spc format) and analysed using Spectragryph (version 1.2.15). Multivariate analysis of each spectrum was performed using the SIMPLE, SLOPP and SLOPPE databases, as well as the laboratory reference plastics spectral database31,49,50. All particles with plastic spectra HIT rate (hit quality index) of 80% or 0.8 were counted as plastic31,51, with all plastic spectra with 80% HIT rate and all particles with no plastic spectra counted as non-plastic particles. The total plastic particle count per filter was extrapolated from the actual plastic particle count in the sub-sampled area. The average of the triplicate sub-sampled sections was used for further calculations. All results presented have been blank corrected. Blank correction was performed by polymer type, with all presented results representing the final values obtained from direct blank subtraction (i.e. if 10 particles of PP were identified in a blank sample, 10 PP particles are removed from all sample polymer counts). Further quality assurance and quality control information can be found in Supporting Information 2.2.

Pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry

Calibration and internal standard

Py-GC/MS analysis was completed in the Organic Geochemistry Laboratory at GNS Science, Lower Hutt, New Zealand. Instrument calibration was performed using a Microplastics Calibration Standard Set for MPs analysis using Py-GC/MS from Frontier Laboratories, Ltd. This standard kit was used after preparing a 10-fold dilution, by homogenising 100 mg of the microplastic mix with 900 mg of calcium carbonate powder from the standard kit with a Retsch Ball Mill (MM200). The calibration mix contained 12 different polymers, with eight used for quantification in this study (PE, PP, PS, PMMA, PC, PVC, N-6, N-66). Known amounts of calibration mix were weighed out using a Mettler Toledo XPR2 (1 µg readability) with 10 points used between 0.260-12.825 mg of total calibration mix. A calibration curve was obtained for each polymer based on pre-selected pyrolysis products from previous work (Supporting Information Table S3). All calibration curves had an R value of , except for PS with an R value of 0.86. Quantification was undertaken using the adjusted internal standard ratio results from each sample, with 10 µL of 0.02 mg/mL deuterated polystyrene (Polymer Sources, P40555-d-PS in DCM) added as an internal standard to every sample. No derivatisation agent was used in this study, which excluded polymers requiring derivatisation, such as PET.

Sample analysis

Pyrolysis cups (Eco-cup LF; PY1-EC80F) were filled with half of a 32 mm diameter stamp out and analysed in duplicate. Filters were halved using a pre-cleaned scalpel, then placed into the pyrolysis cups with pre-cleaned stainless steel tweezers. Each sample was analysed using a previously established double-shot method for microplastic analysis35,52 which combines thermal desorption (TD) and flash pyrolysis (Pyr). The Py-GC/MS system consisted of an auto-shot sampler (Frontier Laboratories, Ltd), a Microjet Cryo-Trap (MJT-1030E, Frontier Laboratories, Ltd) and a Double-Shot pyrolyzer (PY-2020 iD, Frontier Laboratories, Ltd). The first-shot, thermal desorption step ramped from 100-300 C at 20 C min, then held for 1 min. The second-shot pyrolysis step was set to 650 C and held for 0.2 min. The GC-MS setup used an Agilent 7890A GC equipped with an Agilent J&W DB-5MS GC capillary column (60 m 0.25 mm inner diameter 0.25 µm film thickness) coupled to an Agilent 5975C inert MSD quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS). The GC oven program was 40 C (2 min), 40-320 C at 20 C min, then held for 14 min. The MS was operated in full scan (m/z 40–600) and electron impact ionization mode at 70 eV using a source temperature of 230 C (Supporting Information Table S2).

Polymer identification

Polymer-specific fragment ions were selected based on previously published research, analysis of standards and a comparison of best practice quantification steps for PE and PVC. Specific retention times were recorded from standards, with the peak areas between these times used for quantification. Details of indicator ions used for the detection and quantification of MPs can be found in Supporting Information Table S3. Polymers were quantified using the ratio of the peak area of the selected quantifying ions to the internal standard (Agilent ChemStation Data Analysis). Quantification was calculated using the calibration equations for each polymer type and the resulting ratio. The mean of the two duplicate analyses for each sample was calculated, and values below the method detection limit (MDL) and the limit of detection (LOD) were omitted from further analysis. The results were multiplied by the total area of the filter and then divided by the total flow rate of each sampler, yielding a mass of each polymer type in µg m. All quality assurance and quality control information can be found in Supporting Information 2.2.

Thermal desorption-proton transfer reaction-mass spectrometry

Sample analysis

Thermal desorption-proton transfer reaction mass spectrometry analysis was performed at UFZ in Leipzig, Germany. Analysis was performed using previously published methods from53,54. Samples were analysed in triplicate (3 10 mm diameter stamp outs), with each triplicate placed into pre-cleaned glass vials. Each sample was manually attached to the instrument and instrument analysis was run. Thermal desorption of the sample was performed starting from 35 C for 3 min, then ramping to 360 C at a rate of 40 C per minute, stabilising at 360 C for 5 min. The TD step used a zero-air stream (50 mL/min) while the PTR-TOF-MS measured the outflow in real-time (1 Hz resolution). During the thermal desorption organic ions signals were monitored continually with a PTR-TOF-MS (PTR8000, IONICON Analytik, AU) using previously described settings53,54. Other operation parameters of the PTR-TOF-MS can be found in Supporting Information 2.5.

Polymer identification and quantification

Mass spectra were extracted and processed using the PTRwid software package55. To reduce the data to one mass spectrum per TD run, the signal for each ion was integrated for 7 minutes from the time the TD oven reached 200 C. The mean of the standard blanks was then subtracted from each, and a 3-sigma detection limit was applied. Resulting mass spectra of samples were analysed for plastic fingerprints based on the 40 most abundant ions as detailed in previous publications54,56,57. A positive fingerprint was accepted if the algorithm score was above 2-sigma compared to 1000 synthetic spectra54,56. Quantification steps are described in Ref.54. In brief, concentrations of organic ions present in the sample are obtained by estimating them from known reaction rates during proton transfer reactions55. Formulas are available in Ref.54. As the PTR ionisation efficiency is not 100%, we assume these values are the minimum amounts present. For reported result calculations, zeros were excluded from further calculations. Results were then multiplied to the area of the filter and converted to ng m using sampler flow rates. All quality assurance and quality control information can be found in Supporting Information 2.2.

Limit of detection and blank correction

All data were corrected for negative values from blank samples, then a blank average result and corresponding LOD (3 times the standard deviation of the standard blanks) were calculated. The results were then checked against the standard blank average and LOD. The standard blank average was subtracted from the result to obtain the final parts per billion (ppb) value, which was then used to calculate the final concentrations.

Data analysis

For each method, results were first calculated individually to examine the concentrations and variation within each method. Then, the concentrations and variation between the samplers were examined, which were the four replicates for each day of sampling. Finally, the overall variation across all samples was investigated, reporting the overall mean for each method and the standard deviation between the days. All results presented have been checked against the limit of detection (LOD) and the calculated method detection limits (MDL), calculated as the mean of the blanks plus three times the standard deviation of the blanks. The CV was also calculated for each day and method and is discussed in the main text to assess variation between the methods. To further evaluate inter-method agreement, Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed across all pairwise method combinations; Spearman’s rank was selected as it does not assume normally distributed data or comparable units between methods, making it appropriate for comparing across fundamentally different analytical techniques.
