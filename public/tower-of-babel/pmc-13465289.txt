Development and partial validation of a TaqMan quantitative PCR assay to detect Cervidpoxvirus
Authors: Lillian G. Maxwell, Kuttichantran Subramaniam, Samantha M. Wisely, Juan M. Campos Krauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn emerging disease known as mule deerpox virus (Cervidpoxvirus muledeerpox) (DPV) has spread across North America in both wild and captive cervid populations. While little is known about its prevalence in wild cervids, research in domestic populations indicates a high mortality associated with this pathogen, particularly in fawns. Most recently, a new Cervidpoxvirus has been reported in semi-domestic populations of reindeer in Sweden and Norway, expanding its geographical range internationally. Currently, there is no standard protocol to detect this virus, and the increasing range of this pathogen highlights the importance of a standard validated diagnostic test.ResultsIn this study, we developed and partially-validated a TaqMan quantitative PCR assay to detect Cervidpoxvirus based on the major capsid protein using the World Organization for Animal Health’s diagnostic assay guidelines. Aligning the three available whole genomes of DPV from GenBank W-848-83 (GenBank: NC_006966.1); W-1170-84 (GenBank: AY689437.1); MDPV-F (GenBank: MF966153), showed that the major capsid protein gene was highly conserved (> 99%) and primers and probe were designed to amplify a 83 bp region of this gene with 100% agreement. Analytical performance estimated that the assay was exclusive for DPV and highly sensitive (limit of detection ≥ 10 DNA copies), while diagnostic performance found that the assay was 100 times more sensitive than a standard conventional PCR assay.ConclusionWith this assay, detection of Cervidpoxvirus will be faster and more efficient than current methods, allowing both researchers and veterinarians to diagnose and prevent the further spread of this disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05597-3.

Background

Poxviruses are known to be a significant concern to public health for both animals and humans. The family Poxviridae includes large, double-stranded DNA viruses that are divided into two subfamilies: Chordopoxvirinae affecting vertebrates and Entomopoxvirinae affecting insects. Currently, 18 genera within Chordopoxvirinae are described affecting numerous vertebrate hosts [1]. Poxvirus genomes are linear, comprised of double-stranded DNA. There is a high level of variability within poxvirus genomes, but they are typically comprised of 130,000–360,000 base pairs (bp) and contain more than 150 genes on average [2]. Members of Poxviridae are further divided into two classes: high G-C content and low G-C content. The percentage of G-C content varies between species but is generally classified as greater than 60% G-C content for high, and between 30 and 40% G-C content for low [3].

Mule deerpox virus (Cervidpoxvirus muledeerpox) (DPV) is the sole-member of the genus Cervidpoxvirus with a genome size of 163 Kb and a G-C content of 27% [4, 5]. While originally reported in wild mule deer in Wyoming in 1983 [6], it was not until 2005 that DPV was first characterized [4]. Since then, this disease has been detected across North America with cases reported in six U.S. states and one Canadian province [6–11]. In these regions, DPV has been confirmed in free-ranging mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus), white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus), and black-tailed deer (Odocoileus hemionus columbianus), with additional cases reported in managed white-tailed and mule deer populations [6–11]. As many poxviruses display high host specificity, DPV is generally considered exclusive to cervid species. However, one study reports DPV in a goitered gazelle (Gazella subgutturosa), a member of the Bovidae family, in a zoological facility, indicating the possibility of species spillover [12].

Most recently, this pathogen has expanded globally, and was reported in reindeer in Norway and Sweden [13]; however, there is ambiguity about the identity of this and other newly described Cervidpoxviruses. In Norway and Sweden reports confirm the pathogen as a member of the Cervidpoxvirus genus but not DPV specifically. In 2020, a novel Cervidpoxvirus was reported in moose from Minnesota [14] and suggested as a new species of Cervidpoxvirus, moosepox virus (MPV). Compared to DPV genomes W-1170-84, MPV has a nucleotide identity of 98.5% which suggests that the viral sequences are highly conserved among host species. Consequently, while MPV has been suggested as a distinct species, it may actually represent a variant strain and has not yet been recognized as a new species by the International Committee on Virus Taxonomy (ICTV).

Cervidpoxvirus infection is primarily associated with crusted lesions on the skin of the head and neck, as well as on the mucous membranes and coronary bands [6–11]. Other reported signs include alopecia, conjunctivitis, and ulcers of the gastrointestinal tract, including the mouth, esophagus, rumen, and rectum [6, 9, 10]. It has also been reported that severe cases can cause the hooves to slough off [11]. Histopathological analysis of skin lesions showed signs of epidermal hyperplasia, necrotizing dermatitis, and folliculitis, with sporadic mixed bacteria in the crust of the lesion [7, 9–11].

Diagnosis heavily relies on gross examination and detection of clinical signs as well as through conventional PCR and serum neutralizing assays (SNA) [4, 8–11]. Using SNA, the seroprevalence of DPV was found to be 40.2% in wild adult Odocoileus spp. of Oregon [15], while signs and associated mortality in managed populations were only observed in animals less than one year old [10, 11]. Based on this evidence, it is assumed that Cervidpoxvirus is underreported across North America in both captive and wild populations, and its prevalence is largely unknown. One of the primary factors contributing to this lack of reporting is the absence of validated testing protocols for Cervidpoxvirus. Generally, diagnosis relies on clinical signs and conventional polymerase chain reaction (cPCR) [4, 8–11]. However, cPCR assays are unreliable as a diagnostic test due to their limitations, including low sensitivity of the assays, long protocol time, and lack of specificity to account for the variability between virus strains. In addition, SNA cannot distinguish between current and past infections. There are currently ten primer sets targeting seven different genes that have been used for Cervidpoxvirus cPCR detection (Additional File 1). This variation in diagnostic methods and the limitations of cPCR testing confirm the need for a standardized and validated quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay as a more rapid and more sensitive method of detection.

Methods

The World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) upholds guidelines for developing and validating diagnostic assays for numerous animal diseases [16]. The purpose of this research was to develop and partially validate a TaqMan qPCR assay for Cervidpoxvirus based on the WOAH guidelines. Partial validation included the estimation of analytical and diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for the qPCR and estimation of the qPCR parameters [16].

In silico TaqMan qPCR primer and probe design

All available sequences of the mule deerpox virus (DPV) rifampicin resistance protein also known as the major capsid protein (MCP) (1653 bp) were downloaded and aligned with default setting using Multiple Alignment using Fast Fourier Transform (MAFFT) [17]. Initial analysis of the aligned sequences showed that the MCP gene was highly conserved across the genus with > 99% nucleotide identity to all DPV strains W-848-83 (GenBank: NC_006966.1); W-1170-84 (GenBank: AY689437.1); MDPV-F (GenBank: MF966153) and the unclassified moosepox virus strain (GeneBank: MG751778.1). The consensus sequence for the MCP was then imported into PrimerExpress v2.0 to design primers and a [6FAM]-minor groove binder/nonfluorescent quencher-labeled TaqMan hydrolysis probe using default settings (Table 1). Given the low G-C content of Cervidpoxvirus, the primers were scrutinized to ensure the ability to be synthesized, selecting those with the lowest penalty value.

Generation of Cervidpoxvirus DNA standards

The analytical sensitivity of this assay was determined based on 10-fold dilution series of linearized plasmid DNA standards carrying a 395 bp fragment of the DPV MCP gene. To generate these standards, DNA was extracted from three spleen homogenates collected from captive white-tailed deer that were infected with DPV and confirmed by whole genome sequencing [18]. The target region was amplified by PCR using the MCP-Cloning primers (Table 1) designed outside of the qPCR assay target region, cloned into a vector, and replicated in competent Escherichia coli (E. coli) cells as described below. The total reaction volume of the MCP-Cloning PCR was 30 µL, containing 17.55 µL of molecular grade water, 3.0 µL of 10X PCR Buffer, 1.2 µL of 50 µM MgCl2, 0.6 µL of 10 µM dNTPs, 1.5 µL of 20 µM of forward and reverse primers, and 0.15 µL of Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen). The reaction was performed on a SimpliAmp thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems) using the conditions as described in Sayler et al. [11]. The PCR product from each sample was then purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) and quantified using a Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer and the high-sensitivity dsDNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies). Following purification, PCR products were cloned using the TOPO TA Cloning Kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample was screened using colony PCR using M13 forward and reverse primers using the same total volumes and reagents as the conventional PCR reaction described above. The reaction was performed on a SimpliAmp thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems) using the following conditions: 94 °C for 10 min for initial denaturation, 25 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 46 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, followed by final elongation at 72 °C for 10 min, then held at 4 °C. PCR products were confirmed on a 1% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide (EtBr) and colonies identified with the correct insert were placed into 5 mL of warmed LB broth treated with 50 µg/mL ampicillin and grown overnight (12–16 h) in a orbital shaker at 37 °C. The next day, all 5 mL of broth were centrifuged at 3,000 X g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was discarded, and the recombinant plasmid was extracted from the pelleted bacteria using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each extracted recombinant plasmid was then linearized using the NotI enzyme (New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer’s guidelines and visualized on a 1% agarose gel stained with EtBr to assess the purity of the linearized plasmid. Each linearized plasmid product was then purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen), and DNA was quantified three times as described above. The linearized plasmid with the highest average DNA concentration was diluted in TE buffer to 10^7 copies using the formula described by Krieg [19]. The diluted 10^7 product was then aliquoted into 30 µL and stored at -80 °C until use.

Detection of Cervidpoxvirus DNA using the designed qPCR assay

To estimate optimal extension temperature, a total of nine qPCR experiments were run each containing serial dilutions of the DPV Standard (10^7 to 10^1) and a negative control in triplicate with three plates run per three different extension temperatures 58 °C, 60 °C, and 62 °C. Reactions were performed as described above and analyzed to determine which temperature resulted in the highest mean efficiency. After preliminary temperature analysis, 62 °C had the highest mean efficiency and was selected for all subsequent qPCR experiments.

Each qPCR assay reaction volume was 20 µL and contained 2 µL of molecular grade water, 10 µL of a universal PCR master mix (TaqMan® Fast Universal PCR Master Mix 2X, Applied Biosystems), 1 µL of each 18 µM forward and reverse primers, 1 µL of 5µM FAM probe, and 4 µL of DNA standard dilution. In addition, 50 ng of DPV-negative deer DNA was added to the qPCR reactions with DNA standards. Each reaction was plated using the QIAgility (Qiagen) on a MicroAmp™ Fast Optical 96-Well Reaction Plate, 0.1 mL (Applied Biosystems), before being sealed with a MicroAmp™ Optical Adhesive Film (Applied Biosystems). The plate was then placed on a 96-well plate shaker for 1 min at 1,000 rpm and then spun down before being run on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) under the following standard qPCR protocol conditions: 95 °C for 20 s for initial denaturation followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 2 s and 62 °C for 20 s. Results were automatically imported into the QuantStudio™ Design & Analysis Software v1.4.1, and a cycle threshold (Ct) was automatically calculated by the software. To be considered positive, the Ct generated in at least two of three wells must exceed the threshold assigned by the software.

Estimation of qPCR assay analytical sensitivity and specificity

Ten experiments were performed using serial dilutions of the previously generated DNA standards from 10^7 to 10^1 copies and conditions described in Detection of Cervidpoxvirus DNA using the designed qPCR assay section to estimate efficiency, slope, y-intercept, correlation coefficient (R2), repeatability, and reproducibility. The limit of detection (LOD) of the qPCR assay was defined as the lowest dilution at which amplification was detected in at least 50% of wells [16]. Repeatability (intra-assay) and reproducibility (inter-assay) were calculated as the percent of coefficient variation (CV% = [SD/mean] × 100%) from the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the Ct values generated from a single representative experiment and among the 10 experiments, respectively.

To determine the analytical specificity, we first performed an in silico analysis, aligning the MCP gene from the four DPV strains (W-848-83 (GenBank: NC_006966.1); W-1170-84 (GenBank: AY689437.1); MDPV-F (GenBank: MF966153); MPV (GenBank: MG751778.1)) with three genetic relatives, Suipoxvirus swinepox (SWPV), Leporipoxvirus shope (RFV), and Leporipoxvirus myxoma (MYXV) as determined by the genetic analysis in Sayler et al. Figure 5 [11]. Based on the results, synthetic gene fragments for RFV (GenBank: NC_001266.1), MYXV (NC_001132.2), and SWPV (GenBank: AF410153.1) were produced for a 305 bp region of the MCP gene, which includes the primer and probe binding sites. Post testing, the three members of the Capripoxvirus genus and Parapoxvirus reddeerpox sequences were analyzed in silico to provide more information on other poxviruses affecting cervids and other small ruminants (Fig. 1).

Estimation of qPCR assay diagnostic sensitivity and specificity

To estimate diagnostic performance, the developed qPCR assay was compared to the in-house standard cPCR assay used by Sayler et al. [11] to characterize DPV in Florida.

In total 140 individual organ homogenates were chosen from the University of Florida Cervidae Health Research Initiative (CHeRI) necropsy sample archive that were suspected to have been infected with Cervidpoxvirus based on presence of gross skin lesions. Each tissue sample represented one individual deer. Cheng et al. [18] determined that lesion and spleen tissue are most reliable for Cervidpoxvirus molecular detection; however, very few individuals had lesion tissue remaining. For consistency, spleen tissue was used for 137 individuals, however, two samples used lung tissues and one used liver tissue. Samples were collected up necropsy as described in Cheng et al. [18] and homogenized by CHeRI technicians using buffer ATL and proteinase K digestion protocol adapted from the Qiagen DNeasy® extraction kit. DNA was extracted from these samples using a KingFisher™ (Thermo Scientific) in combination with the MagMAX™ DNA Multi-Sample Ultra Kit (Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions and tested using the Sayler et al. [11] cPCR protocol and the designed qPCR assay following the same conditions as described above in Detection of Cervidpoxvirus DNA using the designed qPCR assay section. In both assays, the sample order and ID were randomized and prepared for PCR by a different technician to minimize bias. For each qPCR experiment, the VetMAX™ Xeno™ Internal Positive Control (IPC) VIC Assay (Applied Biosystems) was used in the fourth well of each sample to rule out false negatives from PCR inhibition. The IPC assay included 10 µL of the same universal PCR master mix (TaqMan® Fast Universal PCR Master Mix 2X, Applied Biosystems) used in the samples, 4.2 µL molecular grade water, 1 µL of 1:10 diluted Vet Max Xeno IPC (Applied Biosystems), and 0.8 µL of VetMAX™ Xeno™ Internal Positive Control–VIC™ Assay (Applied Biosystem), and 4 µL of template DNA at 50 ng/well. Samples were considered positive when Ct values generated in at least two of three wells exceeded the threshold assigned by the software.

Results

In silico TaqMan qPCR primer and probe design

Aligned sequences of the MCP gene from DPV isolates W-848-83 (GenBank: NC_006966.1), W-1170-84 (GenBank: AY689437.1), and MDPV-F (GenBank: MF966153) revealed a similarity of > 99%. The forward primer and reverse primer targeted an 83 bp region for amplification within the MCP gene. In silico analysis of the primers and probe specificity against Cervidpoxvirus sequences showed no mismatches within binding site, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Estimation of qPCR assay analytical sensitivity and specificity

Amplification of the generated DPV standard dilutions was linear over 7 orders of magnitude (10^7–10^1 copies) (Fig. 2A). The means parameters (SD+) from the 10 replicated qPCR experiments were as follows: efficiency = 94.3% ± 2.37%, slope = -3.47 ± 0.06, Y-intercept = 39.75 ± 0.54, R2 = 0.998 ± 0.001(Fig. 2B). The LOD (analytical sensitivity) was determined to be 10 copies of DPV DNA, with a positive result in 29 out of 30 reactions. The coefficient of variation (CV) within inter-assay ranged between 0.88 and 2.82, while CV within intra-assay ranged between 0.08 and 2.5 (Table 2). As for the analytical specificity, no amplification of the bacterial or genetic relative viral MCP regions was detected.

Discussion

A summary of reports of mule deerpox virus shows that it is spreading to new geographic areas and potentially affecting new species [6, 8, 11–14]. The availability of a cost-effective, rapid, and validated molecular diagnostic test is crucial for detecting Cervidpoxvirus. In this study, a Cervidpoxvirus TaqMan qPCR targeting an 83 bp region of the conserved MCP was developed, partially validated, and shown to successfully amplify DPV in Florida farmed white-tailed deer tissue homogenates. Currently, this is one of only two qPCR assays available to detect Cervidpoxvirus, and it is the only assay validated to Stage 2 of the WOAH guidelines. To further validate this assay, interlaboratory testing (WOAH Stage 3) should be done so that this assay can be used for routine testing of Cervidpoxvirus. Further, this assay should be tested against the other samples of proposed different species of Cervidpoxvirus [13, 14] to further assess its utility in detecting variability within the Cervidpoxvirus genus.

Based on analytical performance, the DPV qPCR assay showed high efficiency (94.3%) with a high correlation coefficient that was sensitive (LOD ≥ 10 copies), specific (100%), repeatable, and reproducible. Most importantly, the low LOD of this assay is advantageous for detecting low-level infection or viremia, especially in animals with subclinical infections. Currently, no LOD is known for any of the cPCR assays used to detect Cervidpoxvirus; however, for Capripoxvirus, a developed qPCR assay was 100 times more sensitive than cPCR when tested against clinical samples [20]. In our developed assay, we saw similar results when estimating the diagnostic sensitivity; several samples that initially tested negative by cPCR were subsequently amplified in the qPCR with a low copy number.

For the samples that tested cPCR positive, the qPCR Ct values were between 13.9 and 37.2. Half of the qPCR-positive samples had Ct values between 35.1 and 39.5, estimating a range of DNA between 1 and 10 copies per uL. This range is well below the threshold for cPCR detection. With limited information on Cervidpoxvirus, further analysis is needed to accurately estimate diagnostic performance. In addition, evaluating the diagnostic assay’s performance in other Cervidpoxvirus host species is critical to fully validate the assay [16]. Currently, DPV has been reported in several cervid species [4, 9, 15]. Two reports primarily diagnose viruses of the Cervidpoxvirus genus in both moose and reindeer [13, 14]. While these studies suggest the possibility of a different species than DPV, these results are currently unconfirmed. In addition, DPV has been confirmed in a member of the Bovidae family (a goitered gazelle) in a zoological facility [12]. This report not only suggests the possibility of DPV species spillover but also suggests the possibility of other Cervidpoxvirus species. In the future, it is crucial to test this assay for samples within other host species and against other possible Cervidpoxvirus species. Based on the in silico analysis of the primers, they do amplify the moosepox MCP available on GenBank, providing evidence that a Cervidpoxvirus-specific molecular test could contribute to the discovery of other strains within the genus. Finally, while this partial validation used several sample types to test for Cervidpoxvirus, further sample types such as skin, heart, or kidney should be used to test and confirm the robustness of this assay. As mentioned above, all samples used in this study came from deceased animals. Further research is needed to determine the best tissue type to test live animals, including those collected using minimally invasive techniques.

The reported Cervidpoxvirus qPCR assay provides a cost-effective, rapid, and robust diagnostic tool. This assay is highly sensitive, efficient, and quantitative compared to other diagnostic tools for this disease, such as cPCR and SNA. Analysis of the in silico alignment between the developed primers and the three available DPV MCP sequences revealed no mismatches. While this assay requires further testing for additional sample types and host species, it begins to address the need for a validated diagnostic test for DPV. Primarily, the ability of this assay to diagnose low-level infection of DPV and all Cervidpoxvirus strains is crucial to the deer farming industry in North America and possibly other parts of the world. Poxviruses are typically highly transmissible, and while the primary mode of transmission of Cervidpoxvirus is unknown; identifying early infections before clinical signs appear is extremely important for informing management actions and preventing the further spread of this disease.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we designed and partially validated a TaqMan qPCR based on the major capsid protein gene. This results in primers and probe amplifying an 83 bp segment of the highly conserved gene. The resulting assay was generally robust, being highly exclusive and sensitive to Cervidpoxvirus. While further validation is necessary, this work begins to address the need for a standardized diagnostic test for DPV.
