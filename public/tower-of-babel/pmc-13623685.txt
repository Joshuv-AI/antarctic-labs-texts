Inflammation, coagulation, and nutritional reserves refine 90-day prognostic stratification after ruptured intracranial aneurysm repair
Authors: Jian Li, Zhen Wang, Hongwen Li, Xinyu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRuptured intracranial aneurysm is a major cause of neurological disability. Existing prognostic models primarily rely on neurological and radiological severity and may overlook systemic biological alterations.ObjectiveTo evaluate whether integrating systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), D-dimer, and prognostic nutritional index (PNI) with clinical-radiological factors improves prediction of 90-day unfavorable neurological recovery after aneurysm repair.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included 209 patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage undergoing clipping or coiling between January 2023 and December 2025. The primary outcome was unfavorable 90-day functional recovery (modified Rankin Scale 3–6). Admission biomarkers were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression. An admission model and an updated in-hospital model incorporating delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) were developed and evaluated using discrimination, calibration, decision curve analysis, and bootstrap validation.ResultsAmong 209 patients, 66 (31.6%) had unfavorable outcomes. Independent predictors included Hunt–Hess grade IV–V (aOR 3.85, 95% CI 1.84–8.06), modified Fisher grade 3–4 (aOR 2.76, 95% CI 1.28–5.95), acute hydrocephalus (aOR 2.62, 95% CI 1.25–5.48), higher SII (aOR 1.32 per 500-unit increase), higher D-dimer (aOR 1.22 per 1 mg/L increase), and lower PNI (aOR 0.68 per 5-point increase). The admission model achieved an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.76–0.88), while the updated model incorporating DCI improved the AUC to 0.86 (95% CI 0.80–0.91), with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.83 after bootstrap validation.ConclusionAn integrated model combining inflammatory, coagulation, nutritional, and clinical-radiological factors improved risk stratification after ruptured intracranial aneurysm repair. Incorporation of DCI provided additional prognostic value, although external validation is needed.

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) remains a devastating cerebrovascular emergency, with substantial mortality and long-term disability that disproportionately affect working-age populations (1). Contemporary guidelines emphasize that comprehensive management targeting early brain injury, delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), and systemic complications is essential to optimize functional recovery (2, 3). Despite advances in surgical clipping, endovascular coiling, and neurocritical care, many patients experience persistent neurological deficits even after successful aneurysm occlusion (4). Accurate early identification of high-risk patients therefore remains an urgent priority to guide perioperative monitoring and individualized interventions.

Current prognostic tools such as the Hunt–Hess, World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS), and modified Fisher grades are widely applied in clinical practice (5). These scores primarily capture initial neurological impairment and radiological hemorrhage extent, providing a static picture that does not reflect the dynamic, multisystem pathophysiological cascades triggered by aneurysm rupture (5, 6). Functional recovery is influenced not only by the primary bleeding event but also by secondary insults including early brain injury, neuroinflammation, microthrombus formation, and postictal hypercatabolism, all of which critically affect tissue repair and neurological outcomes (6, 7). Because, conventional grading systems omit systemic biochemical responses, their prognostic capacity remains inherently limited.

Accumulating evidence suggests that systemic biological markers reflecting inflammatory activation, coagulation abnormalities, and host nutritional reserve offer valuable prognostic information (8, 9). The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) integrates neutrophils, platelets, and lymphocytes, thereby capturing the interplay between hyperinflammation and immune suppression (10–12). D-dimer reflects prothrombotic tendency and microthrombus activation, mechanisms closely implicated in DCI and microcirculatory disturbance (13, 14). The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) represents systemic metabolic reserve and immune resilience, serving as a marker of host recovery capacity (15, 16). Although individual markers within these pathways have been separately associated with poor outcomes, most prior investigations focused on isolated biological dimensions. Whether integrating these three complementary dimensions, namely inflammation (SII), coagulation (D-dimer), and nutrition (PNI), provides significant incremental predictive value over conventional clinical and radiological tools remains unknown. Furthermore, while machine learning models have been proposed, they often lack bedside interpretability and clinical transparency (17).

To address this gap, we conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of patients with ruptured intracranial aneurysms undergoing microsurgical clipping or endovascular coiling. We hypothesized that an integrated multidimensional biomarker profile would improve 90-day prognostic accuracy beyond traditional clinical tools. Our objectives were to identify independent predictors of unfavorable 90-day neurological recovery, evaluate the incremental prognostic value of combining SII, D-dimer, and PNI with baseline clinical and radiological factors, and establish a clinically interpretable risk prediction model for early stratification and perioperative care.

Materials and methods

Patient population and study design

Study design and cohort derivation

This single-center retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Department of Neurosurgery, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. Patients with aSAH consecutively admitted between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2025, were screened. A total of 267 patients with aSAH were initially identified based on non-contrast cranial computed tomography (CT) demonstrating subarachnoid blood, with the causative intracranial aneurysm verified by CT angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), or digital subtraction angiography (DSA).

Consecutive enrollment was adopted to minimize selection bias. No formal sample size calculation was performed a priori; however, sample size adequacy was evaluated post -hoc based on events-per-variable (EPV) principles for multivariable prognostic modeling. The follow-up cutoff date was set as March 31, 2026, ensuring that all patients completed the 90-day neurological assessment. The analytic database was locked on April 15, 2026. This study strictly adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) reporting guidelines.

Ethics and waiver of informed consent

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (IRB2022-YX-175-01) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective cohort study utilizing secondary analysis of anonymized routine clinical and laboratory data without modifying patient care or introducing additional interventional risks, the Medical Ethics Committee approved a formal waiver of written informed consent. All personal identifying information was removed prior to analysis.

Multidisciplinary decision-making and standardized perioperative care

Treatment allocation for aneurysm occlusion (microsurgical clipping vs. endovascular coiling) was determined by a multidisciplinary team comprising senior neurosurgeons and neurointerventionalists, considering aneurysm anatomy (location, size, neck width, and arterial branching), hemorrhage extent, clinical grade, patient age, and comorbid conditions.

All patients received standardized neurocritical care according to institutional protocols derived from AHA/ASA and Neurocritical Care Society guidelines:

Vasospasm and DCI prophylaxis: oral nimodipine (60 mg every 4 h) was administered routinely upon admission unless severe refractory hypotension intervened.

Hemodynamic management: systolic blood pressure (SBP) was strictly maintained below 160 mmHg prior to aneurysm securing; post-securing, normovolemia was maintained, and induced hypertension (SBP target 140–180 mmHg) was initiated if symptomatic vasospasm or DCI developed.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) management: external ventricular drainage (EVD) or lumbar drainage was indicated for acute obstructive hydrocephalus (Evans' index >0.3) or severe intraventricular hemorrhage.

Nutritional and critical care: enteral nutrition was initiated within 24–48 h of admission for intubated or dysphagic patients; mechanical ventilation and sedation were standardized across ICU stays.

Data collection, variable definitions, and quality control

Data extraction and quality control

Data were extracted independently by two trained researchers using a pre-standardized data collection form from the hospital electronic medical records, PACS, ICU records, and neurosurgical registry. A senior neurosurgeon adjudicated discrepancies. After entry, 20% of cases were randomly re-audited against primary hospital records to ensure data fidelity.

Baseline clinical and imaging parameters

Initial neurological status (GCS, Hunt–Hess grade, WFNS grade) was recorded upon presentation. For patients undergoing emergency intubation or sedation, pre-sedation scores were prioritized. Radiological severity was appraised on initial CT scans by two blinded neurosurgeons, including modified Fisher grade, intraventricular hemorrhage, intracerebral hematoma, and acute hydrocephalus. Acute hydrocephalus was operationally defined as biventricular enlargement (Evans' index >0.3) on initial CT accompanied by clinical signs of elevated intracranial pressure or requirement for CSF diversion.

Aneurysm parameters (maximum diameter, neck width, location, and morphology) were measured using 3D reconstruction workstations. Irregular morphology was defined as lobulated domes, daughter sacs, wall protrusions, or asymmetrical configurations.

Laboratory biomarkers and composite indices

All baseline laboratory parameters were obtained from the first routine venous blood sample collected within 24 h after hospital admission and before initiation of major therapeutic interventions whenever clinically feasible. Potential confounding treatments before blood sampling, including corticosteroid administration, hyperosmolar therapy, and intravenous crystalloid resuscitation, were reviewed from electronic medical records. The median interval between symptom onset and blood sampling was recorded. Laboratory measurements included complete blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), coagulation parameters [prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen, and D-dimer], and liver/renal function indicators (serum albumin and total protein).

Composite inflammatory and nutritional indices were calculated as follows:

Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII): platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count;

Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII): platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count;

Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI): neutrophil count × monocyte count / lymphocyte count;

Secondary brain injury definitions DCI

Defined according to the 2023 consensus criteria as the occurrence of new focal neurological deficits, a decrease in GCS score ≥2 points, or new cerebral infarction detected by CT/MRI that was not attributable to aneurysm occlusion, surgical injury, hydrocephalus, or metabolic disturbances.

Symptomatic vasospasm: Defined as neurological deterioration accompanied by arterial narrowing on CTA/DSA or a mean flow velocity >120 cm/s on transcranial Doppler.

Rebleeding: Defined as new intracranial hemorrhage detected on CT with acute clinical deterioration occurring before or after aneurysm occlusion.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint was unfavorable neurological recovery at 90 days post-intervention, defined as an mRS score (18) from 3 to 6 (mRS 0–2 denoted favorable recovery). Outcomes were assessed at 90 days via outpatient clinic visits or structured telephone interviews conducted by two outcome assessors who were strictly blinded to admission laboratory markers, imaging details, and perioperative courses.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary endpoints included 90-day all-cause mortality, functional independence (mRS 0–2), complete functional recovery (mRS 0–1), mRS score at hospital discharge, ICU length of stay, and total hospital length of stay.

Statistical analysis

General statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent student's t-test. Non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U- test. Categorical variables are reported as counts (%) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to assess baseline covariate balance.

Management of missing data

Variables with < 5% missing values were analyzed using complete-case analysis. For variables with 5%−20% missingness, Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) was performed to generate 20 imputed datasets. The imputation model included all candidate predictors, baseline auxiliary variables, and the primary outcome (90-day mRS). Estimates from multivariable regression models were pooled using Rubin's rules. Variables with >20% missing data were excluded a priori.

Predictor selection, collinearity, and two-step modeling framework

Candidate predictors were screened using univariable logistic regression with a selection threshold of P < 0.10 combined with clinical relevance. Treatment modality (microsurgical clipping vs. endovascular coiling) was prespecified as a clinically relevant covariate because treatment selection may be influenced by aneurysm anatomy, morphology, and patient characteristics, and was therefore retained in multivariable models regardless of its crude association with outcome. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). No substantial multicollinearity was observed among variables retained in the multivariable models, with all VIF values below 2.5. SIRI was excluded because of strong collinearity with SII (VIF = 6.82). Detailed VIF values are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

To distinguish baseline risk prediction from subsequent in-hospital complications, a two-step modeling framework was applied:

Admission Model (Step 1): Constructed using only variables available within 24 h of admission, including Hunt–Hess grade, modified Fisher grade, acute hydrocephalus, SII, D-dimer, and PNI.

Updated In-Hospital Model (Step 2): Further incorporated the occurrence of in-hospital DCI to evaluate overall prognostic performance.

Multivariable logistic regression models were developed using backward stepwise selection. Effect estimates are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Nonlinear associations and cutoff determination

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses with 3–4 knots were performed to evaluate potential nonlinear associations between continuous biomarkers (SII, D-dimer, and PNI) and poor 90-day outcomes. Optimal cutoff values for continuous biomarkers were determined using Youden's index (sensitivity + specificity – 1) based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Model performance, internal validation, and overfitting assessment

Discrimination: model discrimination was assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), with differences between models compared using DeLong's- test.

Calibration: model calibration was evaluated using 1,000-resample bootstrap calibration curves, calibration slopes, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. A bootstrap shrinkage factor was calculated to estimate model optimism.

Clinical Utility: decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify net clinical benefit across threshold probabilities ranging from 10% to 70%.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Robustness was assessed through sensitivity analyses including complete-case analysis, exclusion of in-hospital mortality cases, alternative definitions of poor functional outcome (mRS ≥2 and mRS ≥4), and penalized logistic regression using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Interaction analyses were performed to evaluate whether treatment modality (clipping vs. coiling) modified the observed associations. All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Study population and missing data profile

A total of 267 patients with confirmed aSAH were screened during the study period. After applying the predefined eligibility criteria, 58 patients were excluded, resulting in a final analytical cohort of 209 patients (Figure 1). During the 90-day follow-up, 143 patients (68.4%) achieved favorable functional outcomes (mRS 0–2), whereas 66 patients (31.6%) experienced unfavorable outcomes (mRS 3–6). 13 patients were excluded because of loss to 90-day follow-up. Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, Hunt–Hess grade, modified Fisher grade, acute hydrocephalus, DCI occurrence, and admission biomarker levels, were comparable between patients included in the final analysis and those lost to follow-up (all P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S2), suggesting limited evidence of systematic differences related to follow-up completeness.

Missing data were identified for 12 candidate variables, with proportions ranging from 1.4% (3 / 209 for CRP) to 14.8% (31 / 209 for PCT) (Supplementary Table S3). Variables with < 5% missing values were analyzed using complete-case analysis, while variables with 5%−20% missingness were imputed using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) based on 20 imputed datasets, including the primary outcome. No candidate predictor had >20% missing data.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics according to 90-day functional outcomes are presented in Table 1. Compared with patients with favorable outcomes, those with unfavorable outcomes were older (64.5 ± 11.4 vs. 55.8 ± 10.9 years, P < 0.001; SMD = 0.78), had higher admission systolic blood pressure (161.8 ± 27.5 vs. 151.6 ± 24.8 mmHg, P = 0.008), and more frequently had a history of hypertension (68.2% vs. 48.3%, P = 0.008) and previous ischemic stroke (22.7% vs. 8.4%, P = 0.003). No significant differences were observed in sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes mellitus, or pre-admission medication use.

Admission neurological severity and radiological characteristics

Initial neurological impairment and radiological bleeding burden were markedly worse in the unfavorable recovery group (Table 2). Multidimensional biomarker profiles: inflammation, coagulation, and nutritional reserves

Admission laboratory parameters obtained within 24 h (median interval from ictus to blood sampling: 8.5 h; IQR: 4.2–14.0) demonstrated marked differences in systemic inflammatory status, coagulation activity, and nutritional reserves between patients with favorable and unfavorable outcomes (Figure 2A; detailed values are provided in Supplementary Table S4).

Patients with poor outcomes exhibited significantly higher inflammatory and coagulation-related biomarkers and lower nutritional status. Specifically, SII was significantly elevated in the unfavorable group compared with the favorable group (2,150 × 109/L [IQR: 1,480–2,980] vs. 1,120 × 109/L [IQR: 760–1,540], P < 0.001; SMD = 0.91), as was D-dimer (3.82 mg/L [IQR: 2.10–6.45] vs. 1.45 mg/L [IQR: 0.82–2.60], P < 0.001; SMD = 0.83). In contrast, PNI was significantly lower among patients with poor outcomes (41.2 ± 4.8 vs. 48.6 ± 4.2, P < 0.001; SMD = −1.02).

When analyzed across ordinal 90-day mRS categories (mRS 0–6), SII and D-dimer showed a stepwise increase with worsening functional status, whereas PNI demonstrated a progressive decline (Figure 2B). Furthermore, the proportion of patients with poor 90-day outcomes decreased consistently across PNI quartiles (Q1: < 43.1; Q2: 43.1–46.5; Q3: 46.6–50.2; Q4: >50.2), from 61.5% in Q1 to 9.6% in Q4 (Ptrend < 0.001).

Aneurysm features, interventions, and secondary complications

Posterior circulation location (31.8% vs. 14.7%, P = 0.004), larger maximum aneurysm diameter 9.7 mm [6.8–13.5] vs.6.8 mm [4.9–9.4], P < 0.001), and irregular morphology (56.1% vs. 34.3%, P = 0.003) were significantly associated with unfavorable recovery (Table 3). Aneurysm occlusion modality did not significantly differ between clipping (42.4% vs. 46.2%) and coiling (57.6% vs. 53.8%, P = 0.609). However, time from onset to treatment was longer in the poor outcome group (28 h [15–46] vs.17 h [9–28], P < 0.001).

Association between admission PNI and in-hospital nutritional trajectory

Because nutritional reserve may influence recovery after acute neurological injury, we further explored the association between admission PNI and subsequent nutritional support intensity as well as changes in serum albumin levels during hospitalization.

Patients were stratified according to the previously identified optimal PNI cutoff of 45.2. Compared with patients with PNI ≥45.2, those with lower admission PNI (< 45.2) demonstrated greater nutritional vulnerability during hospitalization. Patients with lower PNI required longer enteral nutritional support and exhibited greater cumulative nutritional intervention requirements. Specifically, the duration of enteral feeding was significantly longer in patients with PNI < 45.2 than in those with PNI ≥45.2 [median 12 (IQR: 8–18) days vs. 8 (IQR: 5–12) days, P < 0.001]. Furthermore, patients with lower admission PNI experienced a greater decline in serum albumin concentration from admission to discharge (−6.8 ± 3.1 g/L vs. −3.2 ± 2.5 g/L, P < 0.001), suggesting accelerated nutritional depletion during the acute recovery phase.

In multivariable linear regression analysis adjusted for age, Hunt–Hess grade IV–V, modified Fisher grade 3–4, DCI occurrence, and ICU length of stay, lower admission PNI remained independently associated with greater in-hospital albumin decline (β = −0.42, 95% CI: −0.61 to −0.23, P < 0.001).

Furthermore, logistic regression analysis demonstrated that lower admission PNI was independently associated with prolonged enteral nutrition requirement (>14 days) after adjustment for potential clinical confounders (adjusted OR=2.31, 95% CI: 1.34–3.98, P = 0.003) (Supplementary Figure S2).

The association between longer onset-to-treatment time and poor recovery was attenuated after adjustment for baseline neurological severity, suggesting that treatment delay may partly reflect initial disease severity rather than an independent causal pathway. A supplementary model retaining onset-to-treatment time demonstrated consistent effect estimates (Supplementary Table S6).

Step 2: Updated in-hospital model (Incorporating DCI)

The updated in-hospital model incorporated DCI, an in-hospital event occurring after admission, to evaluate whether secondary complications provided additional prognostic information beyond baseline predictors. After adding DCI to the model (EPV = 9.4), DCI emerged as an independent predictor of unfavorable neurological recovery (aOR 3.58, 95% CI: 1.62–7.91, P = 0.002). Other independent predictors remained significant, including Hunt–Hess grade IV–V (aOR 3.12), modified Fisher grade 3–4 (aOR 2.41), acute hydrocephalus (aOR 2.36), SII (aOR 1.28 per 500-unit increase), D-dimer (aOR 1.19 per 1 mg/L increase), and PNI (aOR 0.71 per 5-point increase) (Table 4).

Treatment modality was retained as an adjustment covariate because of potential confounding by indication but was not independently associated with unfavorable recovery (coiling vs. clipping: aOR=1.10, 95% CI: 0.59–2.05, P = 0.760).

Sensitivity analysis according to timing of biomarker measurement

To evaluate whether the timing of biomarker measurement influenced the observed associations, a sensitivity analysis was performed by stratifying patients according to the interval between symptom onset and the first blood sampling ( ≤ 6 h vs. 6–24 h). The associations of SII, D-dimer, and PNI with unfavorable neurological recovery remained directionally consistent across both subgroups, with no significant interaction effects observed (all P for interaction >0.10), suggesting that the prognostic associations were not substantially modified by the timing of baseline biomarker assessment.

Among patients with blood samples obtained within 6 h after symptom onset, elevated SII remained independently associated with poor recovery [adjusted OR (aOR) = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.10–1.56, P = 0.003], as did higher D-dimer levels (aOR=1.20, 95% CI: 1.02–1.42, P = 0.028). Higher PNI remained protective against unfavorable recovery (aOR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.53–0.89, P = 0.006).

In patients whose blood samples were obtained between 6 and 24 h after symptom onset, the associations remained stable, with SII (aOR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.09–1.48, P = 0.002), D-dimer (aOR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.03–1.35, P = 0.016), and PNI (aOR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.58–0.90, P = 0.004) showing similar associations with unfavorable neurological recovery (Supplementary Table S7).

A worst-case sensitivity analysis was performed by assigning all patients lost to follow-up as having unfavorable neurological outcomes (mRS 3–6). Under this conservative assumption, the associations between admission biomarkers and poor neurological recovery remained consistent with the primary analysis. The adjusted effect estimates were minimally changed for SII (aOR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.12–1.49, P < 0.001), D-dimer (aOR=1.20, 95% CI: 1.06–1.37, P=0.004), and PNI (aOR=0.70, 95% CI: 0.57–0.86, P < 0.001), supporting the robustness of the findings against potential outcome misclassification caused by missing follow-up (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of patients with ruptured intracranial aneurysms, we found that high Hunt–Hess grade (IV–V), high modified Fisher grade (3–4), acute hydrocephalus, in-hospital DCI, elevated SII, elevated D-dimer, and low PNI were independently associated with unfavorable 90-day neurological recovery. The addition of these three biological markers to conventional clinical and radiological scores significantly improved model discrimination, calibration, and net clinical benefit. These findings suggest that a multidimensional prognostic framework integrating inflammatory, coagulation, and nutritional parameters can refine risk stratification after aneurysm repair.

The prognostic importance of initial hemorrhage severity and neurological impairment was reaffirmed in our cohort. Hunt–Hess grade IV–V and modified Fisher grade 3–4 remained robust independent predictors, consistent with extensive evidence linking severe early brain injury and extensive subarachnoid blood to microcirculatory failure, cerebral edema, and subsequent DCI (19, 20). Acute hydrocephalus independently increased the odds of poor recovery, likely through impaired cerebrospinal fluid circulation and elevated intracranial pressure. Rather than acting as isolated variables, these clinical and radiological scores reflect both acute metabolic disruption of cerebral tissue and susceptibility to secondary complications.

To address the conceptual challenge of using in-hospital events for early prognostication, we adopted a two-step modeling framework. The admission model, which incorporated Hunt–Hess grade, modified Fisher grade, acute hydrocephalus, SII, D-dimer, and PNI, achieved good discrimination (AUC 0.82) within 24 h of arrival, providing clinicians with an immediate tool for early risk triage. When DCI occurred as a secondary event, the updated in-hospital model further improved performance (AUC 0.86), indicating that dynamic monitoring of delayed complications adds prognostic value. This staged approach resolves the temporal ambiguity between baseline variables and post-admission events and supports a dynamic risk monitoring strategy throughout hospitalization.

Systemic inflammation and coagulation activation were strongly correlated with functional outcomes. SII integrates neutrophil-driven inflammatory cascades, platelet-mediated microthrombosis, and lymphocyte-related immune exhaustion (21, 22). The restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a marked non-linear dose-response pattern, with the risk of poor recovery escalating rapidly when SII exceeded approximately 1500 × 109/L. Elevated admission D-dimer was independently associated with poor functional status and 90-day mortality, corroborating prior evidence that early fibrinolysis and intravascular microthrombosis contribute to secondary microcirculatory failure (23). Although admission measurements provide a useful baseline snapshot, they represent a single time point in a rapidly evolving process. Dynamic changes in these markers during hospitalization may better capture persistent inflammatory activation or resolution, and future studies incorporating serial measurements are warranted to determine whether longitudinal trajectories outperform single assessments.

The nutritional dimension, as reflected by PNI, deserves particular attention. PNI combines serum albumin and lymphocyte count, serving as a proxy for both metabolic reserve and immunocompetence. We observed an inverse association in which each 5-point increase in PNI conferred a 29% reduction in the risk of unfavorable outcome, with a non-linear relationship identifying a clinical threshold around 45.2. Patients with admission PNI below this cutoff experienced greater albumin decline and required longer enteral nutritional support during hospitalization, suggesting that baseline nutritional vulnerability is associated with accelerated metabolic stress after aneurysm rupture. Acute brain injury frequently induces hypercatabolism, hypoalbuminemia, and immunoparalysis, which collectively impair neuronal repair and increase susceptibility to infections (24, 25). From a translational perspective, early identification of patients with low PNI on admission may facilitate targeted nutritional screening, structured early enteral nutrition protocols, and close monitoring of albumin trajectories during intensive care. Whether such nutritional interventions can directly modify neurological outcomes requires prospective investigation, but our findings support PNI as a potentially useful marker for identifying patients at higher metabolic risk.

Our extended prediction model demonstrated strong discrimination, bootstrap-corrected calibration (calibration slope 0.94), and consistent net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities (10% to 70%). Unlike opaque machine learning algorithms, the logistic regression structure maintains full interpretability, allowing clinicians to weigh individual risk factors transparently at the bedside (26, 27). Sensitivity analyses using complete-case analysis, exclusion of in-hospital deaths, alternative mRS cutoffs, and LASSO penalized regression all confirmed the robustness of the observed associations. Subgroup analyses stratified by treatment modality showed no significant interaction, further supporting the stability of these systemic biomarkers across clipping and coiling populations.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective single-center design may introduce selection bias and residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as fluid balance, sedation protocols, and intraoperative hemodynamics. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Second, although only 13 patients (4.9%) were lost to 90-day follow-up and baseline characteristics were comparable between included and excluded patients, we cannot completely exclude the possibility of survivorship bias. Third, biomarkers were measured only at admission; repeated measurements might provide additional prognostic insight by capturing dynamic changes in inflammation, coagulation, and nutrition. Fourth, although we performed rigorous internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples, external validation in independent cohorts is essential before clinical implementation. We note that a fully comparable external cohort from our collaborating institution was not available for the current analysis because of differences in data availability and case selection during the study period. Therefore, our model should currently be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than a definitive clinical decision tool. Finally, the relatively modest number of outcome events (66 events) yielded an events-per-variable ratio of approximately 9.4 for the updated in-hospital model, and some optimism or overfitting cannot be entirely excluded. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and independent validation cohorts are needed to confirm generalizability and clinical utility.

In conclusion, unfavorable 90-day functional recovery after ruptured intracranial aneurysm repair is associated with initial hemorrhage severity, acute hydrocephalus, in-hospital secondary brain injury, and systemic biological imbalances. Beyond conventional clinical and radiological grading, admission SII, D-dimer, and PNI reflect complementary pathophysiological dimensions encompassing inflammatory-immune activation, prothrombotic tendency, and metabolic reserve. Integrating these routinely available, low-cost biomarkers into a transparent prediction model significantly improves prognostic discrimination and net clinical benefit over traditional tools alone. Future prospective, multicenter studies adhering to TRIPOD guidelines are warranted to externally validate this framework and to evaluate whether targeted perioperative interventions, including early nutritional optimization and anti-inflammatory strategies, can improve long-term neurological recovery for aSAH patients.
