Minimal-access exoscopic approach for caudate hemorrhage with intraventricular extension using the ViewSite Brain Access System
Authors: Noboru Ishino, Takuma Maeda, Yusuke Yamaki, Kokyo Sakurada, Omar F. Jbarah, Hiroki Kurita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Surgical management of intracerebral hemorrhage has evolved with advances in minimally invasive endoscopic techniques and minimal-access exoscopic approaches to deep-seated hematomas. We report two cases of caudate head hemorrhage with extensive intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) and acute obstructive hydrocephalus, successfully treated using a minimal-access exoscopic transcortical transventricular approach with the ViewSite Brain Access System, a tubular retractor.Case Description: The patients were a 57-year-old woman presenting with sudden coma and a 79-year-old man receiving dual antiplatelet therapy and hemodialysis who developed abrupt vomiting followed by progressive impairment of consciousness. Computed tomography demonstrated a left caudate head hemorrhage and massive IVH filling the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles with total hematoma volumes of 63 mL in Case 1 and 126 mL in Case 2, accompanied by acute hydrocephalus. A trajectory through the left Kocher’s point enabled evacuation of the intraparenchymal caudate head hematoma and extensive intraventricular hematoma through a single corridor under exoscopic visualization in each case. The hematomas were satisfactorily evacuated, with evacuation rates of 85.7% in Case 1 and 89.7% in Case 2, without intraoperative complications or postoperative rebleeding. In Case 2, hemodialysis was withheld for the first 24 h after onset because of concern for acute hemodynamic and osmotic fluctuations. Hydrocephalus did not recur after removal of the external ventricular drain.Conclusion: The minimal-access exoscopic transcortical transventricular approach using a tubular retractor provided a stable working corridor with favorable maneuverability and hemostatic control, and may be effective for combined intracerebral and intraventricular hematomas complicated by acute hydrocephalus, particularly when neuroendoscopic evacuation is not readily available.

INTRODUCTION

Microsurgical hematoma evacuation remains a standard surgical option for selected patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). In parallel, minimally invasive endoscopic evacuation has been increasingly adopted, and its indications continue to expand.[10,19] For intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), endoscopic techniques have also enabled direct clot removal in addition to cerebrospinal fluid diversion.[17] However, endoscopic surgery requires specialized equipment and technical expertise and therefore may not be available on a 24/7 basis at every center. Moreover, because hemostatic maneuvers are inherently limited during endoscopic procedures, conversion to open craniotomy may be necessary in some cases.

We use a minimal-access exoscopic approach for hematoma evacuation, with a transparent tubular retractor as an adjunct to maintain a less disruptive corridor for deep-seated lesions. Herein, we describe two cases of caudate head hemorrhage with intraventricular extension and acute hydrocephalus treated with exoscopic transcortical transventricular hematoma evacuation using the ViewSite Brain Access System (VBAS).

CASE DESCRIPTION

Case 1

A 57-year-old woman with a history of hypertension developed sudden impairment of consciousness and was transported to our hospital. On arrival, she was comatose (Japan Coma Scale [JCS] III-200; Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] E1V1M4), with a blood pressure of 184/121 mmHg and anisocoria (2/4 mm). Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a left caudate head hemorrhage and massive IVH filling the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles, accompanied by acute hydrocephalus [Figure 1a]. The total hemorrhage volume was 63 mL. CT angiography revealed no vascular malformation or aneurysm, and hypertensive ICH was diagnosed. Laboratory data showed a platelet count of 327,000/μL, prothrombin time–international normalized ratio (PT-INR) of 0.94, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of 28.9 s, fibrin degradation products of 8.1 μg/mL, D-dimer level of 4.91 μg/mL, and serum creatinine level of 0.58 mg/dL. Emergency surgical evacuation was planned, and the time from symptom onset to surgery was 113 min.

Surgical procedure

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position, and an external ventricular drain (EVD) was inserted into the right anterior horn. A linear skin incision centered on the left Kocher’s point was made, followed by a small frontal craniotomy using two burr holes [Figure 1b]. After a 20-mm cortical incision, a VBAS tubular retractor (17 mm in diameter, 11 mm in height, and 70 mm in length) was inserted toward the caudate head hematoma and the frontal horn [Figure 1c]. After evacuation of the intraparenchymal clot [Figure 1d], active arterial bleeding was identified from the anterolateral aspect of the caudate head. Based on its anterolateral location, the bleeding source was considered most likely to involve the lenticulostriate arterial system, and hemostasis was achieved using bipolar coagulation. The surgical corridor was then advanced into the left lateral ventricle, and the intraventricular clot was removed [Figure 1e]. The clot in the third ventricle was partially removed through the foramen of Monro. An additional EVD was placed through the surgical tract. The procedure was completed without complications. The patient did not receive any blood transfusion during the preoperative or intraoperative period. Operative time was 100 min, and estimated blood loss was 50 mL.

Postoperative CT confirmed evacuation of both the caudate hematoma and the IVH [Figure 1f]. Postoperative residual hemorrhage volume was 9 mL, corresponding to an evacuation rate of 85.7%, with the residual hematoma located mainly in the third and fourth ventricles. Because clearance of the fourth ventricular hematoma was relatively slow, with continued high-volume cerebrospinal fluid drainage of approximately 150 mL/day at a drainage pressure of 15 cmH2O, the EVD was maintained until postoperative day 10. After its removal, hydrocephalus did not recur. However, the patient’s level of consciousness did not improve. She was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital on postoperative day 39 with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 5. At 90-day follow-up, the mRS score was 4.

Case 2

A 79-year-old man with a history of myocardial infarction and end-stage kidney disease on maintenance hemodialysis developed sudden vomiting followed by progressive deterioration of consciousness and was transported to our hospital. On arrival, he was comatose (JCS III-200; GCS E1V1M4), with a blood pressure of 247/130 mmHg and equal pupils (2/2 mm). CT demonstrated a left caudate head hematoma with massive IVH and acute hydrocephalus [Figure 2a]. The total hemorrhage volume was 126 mL. Laboratory data showed a platelet count of 283,000/μL, PTINR of 0.84, APTT of 27.4 s, D-dimer level of 4.81 μg/mL, and serum creatinine level of 9.34 mg/dL. Hemodialysis was not performed during the first 24 h after onset because rapid hemodynamic and osmotic fluctuations in the acute phase of ICH may adversely affect cerebral edema and intracranial dynamics.[5,8] Emergency surgical evacuation was planned, and the time from symptom onset to surgery was 81 min.

Surgical procedure

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position. A linear skin incision centered on the left Kocher’s point was made, and a small frontal craniotomy was performed using two burr holes, as in Case 1. After a 25-mm cortical incision, a VBAS tubular retractor (21 mm in diameter, 15 mm in height, and 70 mm in length) was inserted toward the caudate head hematoma and the frontal horn [Figure 2b]. Following evacuation of the caudate hematoma, active arterial bleeding was identified from the anterolateral aspect of the caudate head, possibly arising from a lenticulostriate arterial branch, and hemostasis was achieved using bipolar coagulation [Figure 2c]. The trajectory was then extended into the lateral ventricle, and the left ventricular clot was evacuated [Figure 2d]. The clot in the right lateral ventricle was also removed through the septal defect. The clot in the third ventricle was partially evacuated through the foramen of Monro [Figure 2e]. An EVD was placed through the surgical tract. No perioperative transfusion, including platelet transfusion, was administered, and no specific reversal of dual antiplatelet therapy with agents such as desmopressin was performed. Operative time was 110 min, and estimated blood loss was 20 mL.

Postoperative CT confirmed adequate hematoma evacuation [Figure 2f]. Postoperative residual hemorrhage volume was 13 mL, corresponding to an evacuation rate of 89.7%, with the residual hematoma located mainly in the third and fourth ventricles. The EVD was removed on postoperative day 3 without recurrence of hydrocephalus. The patient’s level of consciousness improved to JCS I-3 and GCS E4V1M5. He was transferred for further rehabilitation on postoperative day 63 with a mRS score of 4. At 90-day follow-up, the mRS score was 4.

DISCUSSION

We report two cases of caudate head hemorrhage with extensive IVH and acute hydrocephalus that were successfully treated using a minimal-access exoscopic transcortical transventricular approach with the VBAS. This approach enabled evacuation of both the intraparenchymal and intraventricular components through a single trajectory through Kocher’s point, without complications. Although we previously reported the utility of the exoscopic VBAS approach for thalamic hematoma,[16] the present two cases expand this strategy to caudate head hemorrhage with intraventricular extension and further develop it into a more reproducible approach using a frontal trajectory through Kocher’s point.

VBAS is a transparent, large-caliber tubular retractor designed to balance preservation of the surgical corridor with adequate visualization and instrument maneuverability.[16,18] Its uniform circumferential pressure distribution along the tract may reduce focal brain contusion compared with traditional blade retractors while maintaining a stable operative cavity. These features may be particularly advantageous for deep lesions, where the working space tends to collapse as clot and cerebrospinal fluid are removed. In caudate head hemorrhage with IVH, a trajectory through Kocher’s point provides access to both the parenchymal hematoma and the frontal horn. Using the VBAS, we were able to maintain the same corridor for sequential parenchymal evacuation, ventricular clot removal, and hemostasis.

Neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuation has become increasingly common; however, access to endoscopic equipment and trained personnel may be limited across institutions, particularly during nighttime emergency surgery. In addition, secure hemostasis is critical during deep hematoma evacuation and can be challenging under endoscopic visualization because of restricted instrumentation,[4] occasionally necessitating conversion to open surgery.[15] In contrast, an exoscopic setup combined with the VBAS allowed the use of standard microsurgical instruments and bipolar coagulation through a minimal-access corridor compared with conventional craniotomy, enabling reliable hemostatic control in both cases. Although the VBAS diameter (17–21 mm) requires a larger cortical incision than typical endoscopic sheaths (approximately 8–10 mm), the Kocher’s point trajectory generally avoids eloquent cortex, and we considered the additional cortical incision acceptable given the benefits in working space and hemostatic control.

A limitation of this dry-field, straight Kocher’s point trajectory is restricted access to the deep third ventricle. Flexible endoscopy in a wet field may allow deeper inspection toward the cerebral aqueduct through the foramen of Monro.[9] With our approach, deeper manipulation within the third ventricle carries a risk of fornix injury, and access near the aqueduct was limited. Accordingly, clot removal from the third ventricle was partial and performed only when safely visible through the foramen of Monro.

For acute hydrocephalus caused by IVH, EVD placement alone is commonly performed. However, in cases with a large intraventricular clot burden, EVD-only management may be complicated by catheter obstruction and prolonged drainage, which in turn may increase the risk of ventriculostomy-related infection. Prior reports have suggested that direct IVH evacuation can facilitate earlier EVD removal and may reduce shunt dependency in selected patients.[3,13] In our cases, the EVD could be removed relatively early without subsequent shunt dependence.

Nevertheless, the effect of hematoma evacuation on functional outcome in deep-seated ICH appears limited based on prior randomized evidence. STICH did not demonstrate an overall benefit of early surgery for spontaneous supratentorial ICH,[7] and the recent ENRICH and MIND trials did not establish clear functional superiority of surgical evacuation for deep hemorrhages.[1,12] Accordingly, we consider the role of surgery in these cases to be primarily life-saving rather than function-restorative. Our rationale for intervention was the immediate need to decompress acute obstructive hydrocephalus, reduce ventricular clot burden, and obtain direct control of deep bleeding in two moribund patients.

Another important anatomical consideration is the presumed bleeding source. Caudate hemorrhage is generally attributed to hypertensive small-vessel arteriopathy involving perforating arteries, although aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, and moyamoya-related vasculopathy have also been described.[2,14] The caudate head receives arterial supply from several perforator systems, including the recurrent artery of Heubner, anterior lenticulostriate arteries, and lateral lenticulostriate arteries.[6,11] In the present cases, the bleeding points were located at the anterolateral aspect of the caudate hematoma cavity. Given this location, the bleeding source was considered most likely to be a perforating artery within the lenticulostriate system. Although involvement of the recurrent artery of Heubner cannot be completely excluded because of its contribution to the vascular supply of the caudate head, a primary hemorrhage arising directly from this named artery could not be confirmed and should not be inferred from the present intraoperative observations alone.

CONCLUSION

Exoscopic transcortical transventricular hematoma evacuation using the VBAS enabled controlled minimal-access removal of caudate head hemorrhage with massive intraventricular extension and acute hydrocephalus in two patients. This approach provides a stable corridor with sufficient visualization and working space for deep hemostasis using familiar microsurgical instruments. Although its contribution to functional outcome in deep-seated ICH is likely limited, this approach may still be useful as a life-saving procedure and may facilitate earlier ventricular drain removal by reducing intraventricular clot burden. Prospective comparisons with conventional microscopic surgery and neuroendoscopic evacuation are warranted.
