Asymptotics and zero distribution of geometric polynomials
M. Bello-Hernández, M. Benito, Ó. Ciaurri, E. Fernández

Abstract
We obtain some results on the asymptotic behavior and zero distribution of the so-called geometric polynomials. The asymptotics is given both on compact subsets of ℂ ∖ [ − 1 , 0 ] \mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0] and on compact subsets of the interval ( − 1 , 0 ) (-1,0) . The zeros of these polynomials are simple and lie in ( − 1 , 0 ] (-1,0] ; moreover, the zeros of consecutive polynomials interlace. Its zero distribution is a measure whose density is similar to Cauchy weight. Some orthogonality properties of these polynomials are also proved.



Keywords:

Geometric polynomials, Asymptotics, Zero distribution, Orthogonality

Introduction

Geometric polynomials play a significant role in combinatorics, special functions, and analytic number theory. They form a distinguished sequence of polynomials characterized by their close connection to Stirling numbers of the second kind and to the structure of set partitions, making them a useful tool for encoding combinatorial information in algebraic form.

The geometric polynomials
(P_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
are defined by

f(z,t)=\frac{1}{1-z(e^{t}-1)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(z)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

(1)

This functional identity determines uniquely the sequence

(P_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
and provides a convenient framework for deriving explicit formulas, recurrence relations, and structural properties of these polynomials. See for example
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
16
]
. This representation highlights the interplay between exponential growth and rational functional forms, a feature that underlies many applications in enumerative combinatorics and asymptotic analysis.
The polynomials
(P_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
can be given in term of Stirling number of second kind
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
, namely

P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}z^{k},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,

(2)

since
\frac{(e^{t}-1)^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{j}{k}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}
. Stirling number of second kind stands for the number of ways to partition a set of
n
objects into
k
non-empty subsets. Euler’s work
[
8
, p. 485]
seems to be where these polynomials first appear. Geometric polynomials are also called Fubini polynomials because Fubini numbers are given by

P_{n}(1)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{n}}{2^{k}},\quad n=0,1,\ldots

(3)

These numbers enumerate various combinatorial objects, including ordered partitions, which are partitions that also take the order of blocks into account. See for example
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
.

Our objective is to obtain asymptotic behavior and zero distribution of geometric polynomials. Next we set their asymptotics in compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0].
In Theorem

Crux Mathematicorum

Theorem 1
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P_{n}(z)(\log\frac{1+z}{z})^{n+1}}{n!}=\frac{1}{1+z}

(4)

uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0].

For
z>0
Theorem
1
is proved in
[
3
]
. Hereafter
\log(\cdot)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mu_{P_{n}}
be the normalized counting measure on the zeros of polynomial
P_{n}
; i.e.,
\mu_{P_{n}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{k,n}}
, where

Theorem 2
.

The zeros of
P_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, are simple and lie in
(-1,0]
; the value
z=0
is a zero of all polynomials
P_{n}
,
n\geq 1
. The zeros of
P_{n+1}
different from

interlace with the zeros of
P_{n}
. Moreover, we have that the sequence of measures
(\mu_{P_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in the weak-* topology to the measure with density

\frac{1}{(1+x)|x|((\log\frac{1+x}{|x|})^{2}+\pi^{2})},\quad x\in(-1,0).

(5)

Thus for each

it holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}|\{1\leq k\leq n:a<x_{k,n}<b\}|=\int_{a}^{b}\frac{dx}{(1+x)|x|((\log\frac{1+x}{|x|})^{2}+\pi^{2})},

where
|\{1\leq k\leq n:a<x_{k,n}<b\}|
stands for the number of zeros of
P_{n}
in the interval
(a,b)
.

Lemma 2
.

1.

If
m
and
n
are positive integers of different parity we have

\int_{-1}^{0}\left(\frac{P_{2j}(x)}{x}\right)^{m}\left(\frac{P_{2k}(x)}{x}\right)^{n}\,dx=0,\quad j,k\in\mathbb{N}.

2.

\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{P_{2j}(x)}{x}\frac{P_{2k-1}(x)}{x}\,dx=0,\quad k,j\geq 1.

3.

If
n\geq 2
, we have

\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x}dx=0.

(11)

Proof.

The first two statements follow from the symmetry property of the polynomials
P_{n}
with respect to
-1/2
given by (
8
).

For the third statement, observe that

f(z,t)-1=z(1-e^{t})f(z,t),

and

\int_{|t|=2}\frac{dt}{t^{n+1}}=0,\quad n\geq 1.

If
n\geq 1
, by Cauchy integral formula we have

\frac{P_{n}(z)}{n!}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|t|=2}\frac{(f(z,t)-1)}{t^{n+1}}\,dt=\frac{z}{2\pi i}\int_{|t|=2}\frac{(1-e^{t})f(x,t)}{t^{n+1}}\,dt.

As

\int_{-1}^{0}f(x,t)\,dx=\frac{t}{e^{t}-1},

it follows

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\int_{|t|=2}\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{f(x,t)(1-e^{t})}{t^{n+1}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{-n!}{2\pi i}\int_{|t|=2}\frac{dt}{t^{n}}=0,\quad n\geq 2.\qed

For
n\geq 2
we write
P_{n}(z)=n!\prod_{k=1}^{n}(z-x_{k,n})
, with
x_{n,n}=0
.

Lemma 3
.

There exist positive numbers
\lambda_{k,n}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1,
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{k,n-1}=1
and

n\frac{P_{n-1}(z)}{P_{n}(z)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\lambda_{k,n}}{z-x_{k,n}}.

(12)

Moreover the sequence of functions
\left(n\frac{P_{n-1}(z)}{P_{n}(z)}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0]
; i.e. if
K\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0]
is a compact set, then there exists a constant
C=C(K)>0
such that for all
z\in K
we have

\left|n\frac{P_{n-1}(z)}{P_{n}(z)}\right|\leq C.

Proof.

According to Lemma
1
the zeros of
P_{n}
are simple and lie in
(-1,0]
. We ordering these zeros such that
x_{n,n}=0
. Let

Q_{k}(z)=\frac{P_{k}(z)}{z},\quad k=1,2,\ldots

The zeros of
Q_{n}
are
(x_{k,n}:k=1,2,\ldots,n-1)
. By the Lagrange interpolation formula we have

nQ_{n-1}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}nQ_{n-1}(x_{k,n})\frac{Q_{n}(z)}{Q_{n}^{\prime}(x_{k,n})(z-x_{k,n})}.

Thus, the equality (
12
) follows with

\lambda_{k,n}=\frac{nQ_{n-1}(x_{k,n})}{Q_{n}^{\prime}(x_{k,n})},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,n-1.

The polynomials
Q_{n}
y
Q_{n-1}
have degree exactly
n-1
and
n-2
, respectively, with leading coefficients
n!
and
(n-1)!
. Multiplying (
12
) by
z
and taking limits as
z\to\infty
, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{k,n}=1.

According to Lemma
1
, the zeros of
Q_{n}
and
Q_{n-1}
interlace in
(-1,0)
. From Rolle’s theorem
Q_{n}
and
Q_{n}^{\prime}
have also interlacing zeros, so

Q_{n}^{\prime}(x_{k,n})Q_{n}^{\prime}(x_{k-1,n})<0,

the same happen with
Q_{n-1}
. As
Q_{n-1}
and
Q_{n}
have positive leading coefficient, we have

Q_{n}^{\prime}(x_{k,n})Q_{n}(x_{k,n})>0
and hence
\lambda_{k,n}>0
,
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1.

If
K\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,1]
is a compact set,

d(K,[0,1])=\inf\{|z-x|;x\in[0,1],\,z\in K\}>0.

From (
12

\left|n\frac{P_{n-1}(z)}{P_{n}(z)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{d(K,[0,1])}.\qed

Proof of Theorem

The function

has a simple pole at each point
t=t_{k}=\log\frac{1+z}{z}+2\pi ki,

k\in\mathbb{Z}
. These poles are simple and

\lim_{t\to\log\frac{1+z}{z}}(t-\log\frac{1+z}{z})f(z,t)=\frac{-1}{1+z}.

Let
L(z)=\min\{|\log\frac{1+z}{z}+2\pi i|,|\log\frac{1+z}{z}-2\pi i|\}
. The principal branch of the logarithmic function
\log\frac{1+z}{z}
has imaginary part in
(-\pi,\pi)
,

\log\frac{1+z}{z}=\log\left|\frac{1+z}{z}\right|+i\arg\left(\frac{1+z}{z}\right),\quad\arg\left(\frac{1+z}{z}\right)\in(-\pi,\pi)

for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,1]
. Observe that
\log\frac{1+z}{z}+2\pi i
and
\log\frac{1+z}{z}-2\pi i
are the singularities of
f(z,t)
different from
\log\frac{1+z}{z}
with smaller modulus. Then

\left|\log\frac{1+z}{z}\right|<L(z)

and we can take
R=R(z)
such that
|\log\frac{1+z}{z}|<R<L(z)
.
Thus,

g(z,t)=f(z,t)+\frac{1}{(z+1)(t-\log\frac{1+z}{z})}

is an analytic function in the disk

\{t\in\mathbb{C}:|t|<L(z)\},

which contains to
t=\log\frac{1+z}{z}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{P_{n}(z)}{n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|t|=2}\frac{g(z,t)}{t^{n+1}}\,dt-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|t|=2}\frac{dt}{(1+z)(t-\log\frac{1+z}{z})t^{n+1}}

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|t|=R}\frac{g(z,t)}{t^{n+1}}\,dt+\frac{1}{(1+z)(\log\frac{1+z}{z})^{n+1}}.

(14)

Since
g(z,t)
is an analytic function in
\{t\in\mathbb{C}:|t|<L(z)\}
we have

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|t|=R}\frac{g(z,t)dt}{t^{n+1}}=O\left(\frac{1}{R^{n}}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{|\log\frac{1+z}{z}|^{n}}\right),\,\text{as }n\to\infty,

(15)

and then from (

4
).

Now let
K

. Since the translation and the principal branch of logarithm are continuous functions, for each point

\left|\log\frac{1+z}{z}\right|<R(z_{0})<L(z).

Thus (

\lim_{n\to\infty}(R(z_{j}))^{n}\left(\frac{P_{n}(z)}{n!}-\frac{1}{(1+z)(\log\frac{1+z}{z})^{n+1}}\right)=0,

and

. Therefore (

\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0]
.
∎

Lemma 4

We have

\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{dx}{(z-x)|x|(1+x)((\log\frac{1+x}{|x|})^{2}+\pi^{2})}=\frac{1}{z(1+z)\log\frac{1+z}{z}},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0].

Proof.

Define

F(z)=\frac{1}{z(1+z)\log\frac{1+z}{z}},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0];

this function is in Nevalinna clase (
F
is an analytic function in
I_{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im(z)>0\}
and
\Im(F(z))<0
for
z\in I_{+}
), and
F(z)=1/z+o(1/z)
as

is the Stieltjes transform of a probability measure
\omega
in
[-1,0]
(see
[

). According to the Stieltjes inversion formula (see
[

) the density of

\rho(t)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\Im F(t+i\epsilon),\quad t\in(-1,0).

(21)

Since
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{(t+i\epsilon)(1+t+i\epsilon)}=\frac{1}{t(t+1)}
, and

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\log\left(\frac{t+1+i\epsilon}{t+i\epsilon}\right)=\ln\left|\frac{t+1}{t}\right|-i\pi,

we have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}F(t+i\epsilon)=\frac{1}{t(1+1)(\log\frac{1+t}{t}-i\pi)}=\frac{\log\frac{1+t}{|t|}+i\pi}{t(1+t)((\log\frac{1+t}{|t|})^{2}+\pi^{2})}.

Hence, from (

\rho(t)=\frac{1}{|t|(1+t)((\log\frac{1+t}{|t|})^{2}+\pi^{2})},\quad t\in(-1,0).

Because of

\int_{-1}^{0}\rho(t)\,dt=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{2}+\pi^{2}}=1,

the singular term of the measure
\omega
equals to zero, and the lemma follows.

\mathcal{C}([a,b])
(
\mathcal{R}_{n-1,n}=\{P/Q:P,Q\text{ polynomials of degree }\leq n-1,n,\text{ respectively}\}
, see
[
12
, pp. 204-205]
) we have that the sequence
(\mu_{P_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in the weak-* topology and the limit is the measure with the weight (
5
); i. e. all
f\in\mathcal{C}([-1,0])
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f\,d\mu_{P_{n}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_{k,n})

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{0}f(x)\,\frac{dx}{(1+x)|x|((\log\frac{1+x}{|x|})^{2}+\pi^{2})}.

Since the limit measure is a measure absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, for each
[a,b]\subset[-1,0]
it holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}|\{1\leq k\leq n:a<x_{k,n}<b\}|=\int_{a}^{b}\frac{dx}{(1+x)|x|((\log\frac{1+x}{|x|})^{2}+\pi^{2})},

where
|\{1\leq k\leq n:a<x_{k,n}<b\}|
stands for the number of zeros of
P_{n}
in the interval
(a,b)
(see
[
15
, Theorem 1.5, p. 5]
).
∎

Remark 4
.

An alternative proof of Theorem
2
is the following. By the Banach–Alaoglu theorem the set of Borel probability measures in
[-1,0]
is weak-* compact. So to prove that the sequence
(\mu_{P_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is weak-* convergent it is enough to prove the equality of two of its weak-* partial limits,
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
.

According to (
18
) we have

\int\log|z-t|\,d\mu_{1}(t)=\int\log|z-t|\,d\mu_{2}(t)

almost everywhere with respect to two dimensional Lebesgue measure. By the unicity theorem (see
[
15
, p. 97]
)
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
and Theorem
2
follows.

From Lemma
3
and (
16
) we obtain the following result.

Corollary 3
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_{k,n})\lambda_{k,n}=\int_{-1}^{0}f(x)dx,

for all
f\in\mathcal{C}([-1,0])
, and the Lebesgue measure in
[-1,0]
is the weak-* limit of the sequence of measures
(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k,n}\delta_{x_{k,n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and for all
[a,b]\subset[-1,0]

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k:a<x_{k,n}<b}\lambda_{k,n}=b-a,

where for each
n
the sum is in
k=1,2,\ldots,n
such that
a<x_{k,n}<b
.

From Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
we get the the following result.

Corollary 4
.

If
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0]
, we have

\int_{-1}^{0}\log|z-x|\frac{dx}{|x|(1+x)((\log\frac{1+x}{x})^{2}+\pi^{2})}=-\log\left|\log\frac{1+z}{z}\right|.
