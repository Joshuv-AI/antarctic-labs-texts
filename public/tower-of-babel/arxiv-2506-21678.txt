On the role of connectivity in Linear Logic proofs
Raffaele Di Donna, Lorenzo Tortora de Falco

Abstract
We investigate a property that extends the Danos-Regnier correctness criterion for linear logic proof-structures. The property applies to the correctness graphs of a proof-structure: it states that any such graph is acyclic and that the number of its connected components is exactly one more than the number of nodes bottom or weakening. This is known to be necessary but not sufficient in multiplicative exponential linear logic ( 𝑀𝐸𝐿𝐿 \mathit{MELL} ) to recover a sequent calculus proof from a proof-structure. We present a geometric condition on untyped proof-structures allowing us to turn this necessary property into a sufficient one: we can thus isolate several fragments of 𝑀𝐸𝐿𝐿 \mathit{MELL} for which this property is indeed a correctness criterion. We also recover as by-product some known results.

Introduction

It is widely acknowledged that one of the main contributions of Linear Logic (
\mathit{LL}
, see
[
9
]
) to the field of proof-theory is the shift from a term-like or tree-like representation of proofs to a more general
graph
-like one. Graphs representing proofs in
\mathit{LL}
are usually called
proof-nets
: their syntax is richer and more expressive than the one of the
\lambda
-calculus, or the one of sequent calculus, or natural deduction. Contrary to the mentioned formalisms, proof-nets are special inhabitants of the wider land of
proof-structures
. A proof-structure is any “graph” that can be built in the language of proof-nets, and it need not represent a proof. Proof-structures are well-behaved for performing computations: right from the start, it appeared clearly that the procedure of cut elimination can be applied to proof-structures, and that they can be interpreted in denotational models thanks to the notion of experiment (see
[
9
]
).

Two of the main research lines in this area, to which nowadays one often refers as “the theory of proof-nets”, are the study of their rewriting properties, like confluence and the normalization of the cut elimination procedure (see
[
9
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
2
]
, …) and the study of
correctness criteria
for proof-structures: it is obviously an interesting theoretical question to find abstract properties allowing us to isolate, among proof-structures, exactly those that are indeed proofs. The present work is a contribution to this second line of research in the theory of proof-nets.

A strong divergence quickly appeared: on one hand the ideal (but very restricted) multiplicative fragment
\mathit{MLL}
, for which a lot of alternative correctness criteria (of various nature) were introduced (see
[
9
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
3
]
, …), and on the other hand the wild “rest of the world” for which no really convincing correctness criterion has ever been found. Some rare notable exceptions are the multiplicative fragment with quantifiers (see
[
10
]
) and the (classical and intuitionistic) polarized fragments (see
[
19
]
and
[
16
]
); we can also quote the multiplicative and additive fragment (see
[
11
]
and
[
15
]
), provided however one is not too picky.

The property
\mathsf{ACC}
(also called Danos-Regnier criterion) states that every “correctness graph” of the structure is acyclic and connected: it is one of the most famous correctness criteria for
\mathit{MLL}
(see
[
5
]
). In the multiplicative and exponential fragment
\mathit{MELL}
(the fragment of
\mathit{LL}
in which it is natural to represent and study the majority of logical and computational phenomena), one often considers proof-structures satisfying the property
\mathsf{AC}
, obtained from
\mathsf{ACC}
by dropping connectivity. Like
\mathsf{ACC}
for
\mathit{MLL}
proof-structures,
\mathsf{AC}
is stable wrt cut elimination of
\mathit{MELL}
proof-structures; but
\mathsf{AC}
allows us to isolate, among
\mathit{MELL}
proof-structures, the ones that are proofs only in an extended sense: essentially, the proofs of
\mathit{MELL}
sequent calculus with (some variants) of the
\mathit{mix}
rule (see for example
[
8
]
and
[
1
]
). The several attempts to add some other condition(s) to
\mathsf{AC}
in order to obtain a correctness criterion for
\mathit{MELL}
(without
\mathit{mix}
) were never really successful, and for very good reasons: it has been eventually proven in
[
14
]
that no feasible correctness criterion can ever be found as soon as one adds just the multiplicative units to
\mathit{MLL}
(this fragment is denoted by
\mathit{MLL}_{u}
).

Since connectivity is undeniably a simple and solid geometric property, one can try to reverse the point of view and ask what is the logical meaning of connectivity: for example, in
[
13
]
a remarkable property (that does not hold for general
\mathit{MELL}
proof-structures) is proven for connected
\mathit{MELL}
proof-structures. In the same spirit, in this paper we revisit an old property which is in between
\mathsf{AC}
and
\mathsf{ACC}
. Connectivity fails in
\mathit{MELL}
for the presence of the weakening rule of logic and of the rule for the multiplicative constant bottom: it is then rather natural to relate the number of connected components (
\mathit{cc}
) and the nodes weakening or bottom (
w
) through the equality
\smash{\mathit{cc}=w+1}
. We obtain in this way the property denoted by
\mathsf{ACC}_{\sharp w}
in this paper: to our knowledge, it was first considered in
[
25
]
, where the author shows that it is a necessary property for a proof-structure to be a real proof, but it is not sufficient due to the counterexample in
\Cref
fig:counterexample. It is common knowledge in the community that
\mathsf{ACC}_{\sharp w}
is stable wrt cut elimination of
\mathit{MELL}
proof-structures (
\Cref
theorem:stability-cut-elimination: in the absence of a precise reference, we give a full proof in
\Cref
subsec:cut-elimination-steps). Thus, following the idea that simple and solid geometric properties are likely to have a logical meaning, we wondered whether it was possible to identify interesting subsystems of
\mathit{MELL}
for which
\mathsf{ACC}_{\sharp w}
is a correctness criterion.

We found a geometric condition on
\mathit{MELL}
proof-structures, which we called
(\neg w\otimes)
, such that every
(\neg w\otimes)
-proof-structure satisfies
\mathsf{ACC}_{\sharp w}
iff it is a proof: this is our
\Cref
thm:untyped-sequentiality, which is obtained in an untyped framework. Indeed, in recent years it has become quite common in the theory of proof-nets to drop types when they are not needed: like for the
\lambda
-calculus, there are properties of proof-structures (like confluence:
[
4
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
12
]
) that do not rely on the presence of types, and some others (like normalization:
[
9
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
2
]
) for which the presence of types is crucial. It turns out that the essence of the characterization of the proof-structures that are indeed proofs has nothing to do with types (this was known, but maybe clearly expressed for the first time in
[
1
]
): correctness criteria allow us to present proof-structures in a sequential way (
\Cref
sec:ps), which, in presence of types, yields the traditional “sequentialization theorems” describing how a proof of sequent calculus can be built from a proof-structure satisfying some correctness criterion. We retrieve the typed framework in the last part of the paper, in which we present some interesting fragments of
\mathit{MELL}
the cut-free proof-structures of which are
(\neg w\otimes)
-proof-structures, so that our theorem applies. In the restricted framework of
\mathit{MLL}_{u}
,
(\neg w\otimes)
-proof-structures had already been considered: in Appendix B of
[
25
]
, a sequentialization result is proven with a technique different from the one used in this paper (see
\Cref
remark:AppendixRegnier for more details).

Remark 80
.

One could think of proving
\Cref
itm:tenll-context-par,itm:tenll-not-provable of
\Cref
prop:tenll as an immediate consequence of
\Cref
itm:tenll-context and of the reversibility of the
\invamp
rule in linear logic. However, the
\invamp
rule might not be reversible in the fragment
(\neg?\otimes)
\mathit{LL}^{*}
. Indeed, the standard proof of this statement fails because
(\neg?\otimes)
\mathit{LL}^{*}
is not closed under linear negation.
