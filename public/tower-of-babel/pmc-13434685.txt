Identification and diagnosis of flow instability in parallel channel systems using machine learning
Authors: Cheng Peng, Xingchen Wang, Runxin Tian, Jiang Wu, Jian Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDensity wave oscillations in parallel thermal-fluid systems threaten operational safety due to uneven flow and instability. Predicting these oscillations is a critical challenge for both forced and natural circulation systems. Here, we demonstrate an intelligent framework that accurately identifies such hazardous states. We combine numerical modeling of open and closed circulation systems with a hybrid method integrating Genetic Algorithm (GA) optimization and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classification. For open systems, we map oscillation patterns across a wide operational space. For closed systems, we efficiently pinpoint DWO-sensitive regions. Our results show that the flow state recognition method, validated against manual and signal analysis, achieves an overall accuracy of 99.19%. Furthermore, GA-optimization of the KNN model achieved 96.4-100% accuracy with superior blind-test generalization, while spatial non-uniformity of heat load distribution critically governs instability patterns, informing differentiated safety strategies. This confirms the framework’s effectiveness and reliability for practical engineering applications.Subject terms: Fluid dynamics, Characterization and analytical techniques

Introduction

In the context of global warming and pressing challenges posed by energy security, nuclear energy has emerged as an essential alternative for low-carbon energy transition due to its negligible carbon emissions1–3, while nuclear safety remains the essential assurance for its sustainable development4. Parallel channel systems constitute an essential design framework in nuclear reactors, engineered to distribute high-volume flows for velocity reduction, resistance alleviation, as well as enhanced redundancy and operational reliability5,6. However, this configuration simultaneously introduces inter-branch heterogeneities, which may provoke flow distribution imbalance and thermo-hydraulic instabilities under specific operational conditions7. Among these, self-sustained periodic oscillations represent a pivotal concern in nuclear reactor safety design and operation. While such oscillations fall under the general category of limit cycle oscillations (LCO) in nonlinear dynamics, in the specific context of two-phase flow systems, the underlying physical mechanism is more accurately described as density wave oscillation (DWO). DWO arises from the intrinsic stability deficiencies of nonlinear dynamical systems within parameter-sensitive regimes, which render their complete elimination infeasible. Nevertheless, through mechanistic analysis, stability boundary delineation, and operational parameter control, their amplitude can be effectively suppressed and associated risks mitigated8,9.

Leveraging multi-branch architectures to optimize system-wide efficiency, parallel channel configurations are widely deployed in fluid transport and heat transfer applications10,11. Their operational robustness is inextricably linked to facility safety and economic performance, underscoring the imperative for rigorous investigation into flow instability mechanisms to ensure reactor integrity and sustainable energy generation. Early research primarily explored the influencing factors of parallel channel instability, which relied on experimental measurements and empirical formulas12,13. With the advancement of fluid mechanics theory, research evolved toward classical theoretical analysis, establishing foundational models and analytical frameworks for such systems14–16. This development markedly improved the capability to predict system stability and oscillation behavior. Subsequently, numerical simulation methods were widely adopted due to advances in computational technology, rendering finite element analysis, computational fluid dynamics (CFD), and one-dimensional (1D) system simulations essential research instruments. Relevant studies have validated the coupling efficacy between system thermal hydraulics and CFD codes through simulation, while the influence of manifold structures on flow distribution has been systematically analyzed and iteratively optimized17. Furthermore, the nonlinear interrelationship between turbulence parameters (e.g., Reynolds stresses) and both flow uniformity and pressure drop has also been quantitatively explored18. Building on these findings, comparative evaluations of flow characteristics across distinct manifold configurations have been conducted, revealing critical performance instability under equivalent operational conditions19. Notably, the volume-of-fluid model has been strategically deployed to investigate two-phase flow instabilities20. These advancements have established a basic framework for high-fidelity simulations of complex transient processes, multiphase interactions, and nonlinear thermal-hydraulic phenomena. In recent developments, researches have reflected a progression toward the convergence of multidisciplinary approaches—notably digital twins, intelligent integrated management, and multi-physics coupled simulations21. This methodological integration, when synergized with optimization algorithms, enables collaborative refinement of system design and control strategies, exhibiting distinct multiscale resolution, high precision, and intelligent predictive capabilities.

Within this context, sustained periodic pressure-flow oscillations have been observed in both experimental and engineering applications. In two-phase flow systems, such oscillatory behavior is physically rooted in the density wave feedback mechanism—hence the term DWO. Owing to its inherent limitations, classical linear stability theory fails to explain such self-sustained periodic solutions with finite amplitudes—namely, limit cycle behavior, which reveals the system’s intrinsic strongly nonlinear dynamic nature. In a bid to deeply investigate its mechanisms, a nonlinear dynamical systems theory framework was introduced. The simulation investigation by Ozawa et al.22 elucidated the triggering conditions and dynamic evolution patterns of nonlinear oscillations. Through numerical simulations, Beran et al.23 discovered that the LCO induced by external forcing originates are triggered by a weakly subcritical Hopf bifurcation, concurrently elucidating the formation mechanisms of stable limit cycles governed by supercritical bifurcations. Contemporary research replicated and analyzed oscillation phenomena and their parametric sensitivity through low-order nonlinear lumped-parameter models24–27. Driven by advances in computing capabilities, Zhou et al.28 investigated the impacts of manifold configurations on flow maldistribution and LCO in centralized parallel heat exchangers via CFD, thereby proposing optimized designs. Subsequently, Brouwer et al.29 uncovered the differential oscillation and thermo-hydraulic coupling collaborative regulation mechanism of multi-channel limit cycles under nonuniform heat load through thermal-hydraulic numerical simulation. Building upon these computational strategies, Jin30 revealed the dominant influence of discrete heat source positions in parallel pipes on the stability of two-phase flow LCO using CFD-based numerical simulations, while Guo et al.31 clarified the mechanisms of instability transmission and LCO generation in parallel channel systems through fluid-structure interaction numerical simulations. The coupling of CFD with system simulations captures transient responses and spatial effects, uncovering complex dynamic behaviors, such as multimodal limit cycle competition.

While the complex mechanisms of DWO have been revealed through nonlinear dynamic theories and numerical simulations, traditional physics-based prediction methods still exhibit limitations in computational complexity and adaptability when confronting high-dimensional, nonlinear, and time-varying operational conditions. Recent review papers further show that machine learning is increasingly reshaping the study of multiphase hydrodynamics, transport, and reactions. Yan et al.32 reviewed soft-computing techniques for multiphase flow measurement, highlighting their roles in phase-flow metering, phase-fraction estimation, and regime-related tasks. Zhu et al.33 comprehensively summarized ML applications in multiphase flows and reactors, including closure modeling and CFD acceleration, flow/regime identification and parameter prediction, as well as reaction kinetics modeling and optimization. More recent reviews extended this picture from artificial intelligence (AI)-CFD integration for multiphase thermochemical reacting systems to broader artificial intelligence-enhanced modeling, flow regime recognition, intelligent parameter prediction, and control in complex multiphase flow systems34,35. Collectively, these studies indicate that the state of the art is moving from empirical correlations and purely data-driven prediction toward hybrid and physics-informed frameworks with stronger computational efficiency. However, they also emphasize persistent limitations, including the dependence on high-quality labeled data, limited interpretability and physical consistency, and insufficient generalization across flow regimes and operating conditions. Against this background, rapid diagnosis of DWO in thermo-hydraulic systems with strongly coupled flow states, varying operating conditions, and high-dimensional nonlinear responses remains a challenging task, for which data-driven methods offer a promising complementary route. Since the early 2000s, machine learning approaches have been introduced to enable efficient oscillation behavior prediction, among which the KNN algorithm demonstrates promise due to its nonparametric nature and adaptability to small-sample data36. Capable of processing nonlinear data, KNN directly learns intricate mapping relationships between oscillation patterns and system states without requiring complex parameter estimation or strong assumptions, making it suitable for regression prediction and pattern recognition of key characteristics of DWO, such as amplitude and frequency. By computing inter-sample distances, KNN flexibly adapts to multi-operational condition instability, effectively capturing dynamic characteristic evolution patterns37. Despite this, standard KNN models face challenges with feature redundancy and hyperparameter sensitivity when handling high-dimensional features, necessitating optimization algorithms to enhance their predictive performance and generalization capability.

The genetic algorithm (GA), a global optimization methodology rooted in natural selection principles, has been applied across domains, including reactor design38, nuclear safety system optimization39 and flexibility of nuclear energy systems40, demonstrating robust global search and multi-objective optimization capabilities41–44. Kööp et al.45 innovatively proposed a two-stage global search-grid refinement intelligent analysis framework, achieving efficient identification of natural circulation-induced LCO by coupling GA with the RELAP5 thermal-hydraulic system code. Building upon this methodology, this study develops a hybrid predictive architecture synergistically integrating KNN with GA. The GA component collaboratively optimizes two critical KNN hyperparameters: the neighbor cardinality K and the distance metric calculation method. This intelligent parametrization mechanistically circumvents the exponential computational complexity (O(n²)) inherent to conventional grid search methods, particularly efficacious in high-dimensional optimization landscapes. Relevant research encompasses feature selection with weighted KNN46, similarity metric optimization in collaborative filtering47, and systematic reviews of KNN enhancement strategies for high-dimensional data applications48. These studies demonstrate that this hybrid predictive framework provides an effective technical approach for real-time monitoring, early warning, and intelligent diagnostics of DWOs in parallel channel systems, markedly enriching the methodological framework in this research domain.

Despite existing challenges in predicting DWOs, this study develops a robust intelligent framework integrating GA global search with an optimized K-nearest neighbors (KNN) classifier. The results demonstrate that this combined approach effectively discriminates among stable flow, irregular oscillations, and DWO regimes within defined parameter spaces. The framework thus provides a reliable, computationally efficient tool for identifying flow stability, supporting the safe operation of critical thermal-hydraulic systems in nuclear and energy engineering.

Modeling of open parallel channel systems

The open parallel channel system is configured as a parallel system consisting of three branch pipes, with its channel topology depicted in Fig. 1a. A constant mass flow rate of 0.1 kg s−1 of water is supplied at the inlet (inlet flow boundary), with the fluid pressure preset to 5 MPa as an initial condition to ensure a smooth transition during system startup. Heating elements implement a delayed activation strategy, initiating abrupt thermal input via a step signal 20 s post-simulation start to replicate industrial equipment startup and shutdown processes. The main channel diameter is 20 mm, whereas the three branch pipes (pipes 6–8) each have a diameter of 10 mm. With the aim of achieving uniform heating along the tube, the heating elements were arranged tightly against its outer surface, and a prescribed heat flux (q″) boundary condition was applied externally, thus minimizing local temperature differences.

Time step selection and numerical model validation

The numerical methodology employed in this study was implemented using the system code NUMAP, which adopts a control volume-based approach rather than a full CFD framework. The computational mesh was generated by axially dividing the piping system into discrete control volumes. For the heating tubes—each with an inner diameter of 10 mm and a total length of 1.5 m—a default axial discretization of 20 nodes was applied. This discretization strategy is consistent with a previously validated modeling approach developed for a high-temperature, high-pressure steam-water two-phase flow experimental facility, as illustrated in Fig. 1c49. Temporal discretization was handled through an adaptive time-stepping algorithm. An automatic step reduction mechanism was activated to ensure numerical stability during rapid transients, such as the initiation of heating or the onset of flow instability.

The numerical configuration adopted in this work was benchmarked against previously published dual-channel validation results reported in ref. 49. In that benchmark, a high-temperature, high-pressure steam-water two-phase facility was considered, which was established with reference to a high-temperature, high-pressure steam-water loop using deionized water under P = 0.89–1.32 MPa, G = 500–750 kg m−2 s−1, and ΔT sub, in = 58.5–132.3 °C, with heated tubes of 1400 mm length, 8 mm outer diameter, and 2 mm wall thickness. For the benchmark validation cases, the loop was operated at P = 1.0 MPa, G = 500 kg m−2 s−1, and ΔT sub, in = 100 °C, and the heating power was increased stepwise until periodic flow oscillation first appeared. The compared quantity was the critical outlet vapor quality at the onset of instability. The published comparison comprised 10 uniform-heating cases and 16 nonuniform-heating cases, for which the maximum relative deviations between simulation and experiment were 18.87% and 15.7%, respectively. Supplementary statistics based on digitized published points indicate an overall MAE of approximately 0.017 and an RMSE of approximately 0.023. In addition, the same numerical configuration was extended to 2-, 3-, and 4-channel systems, and the predicted instability boundaries in the Npch-Nsub plane remained within ±5% of the dual-channel reference in ref. 49. The detailed information regarding this validation can be found in Supplementary Note 1. These benchmark results support the discretization scheme and adaptive time-stepping strategy adopted in the present NUMAP simulations.

Based on these validation results, the discretization scheme and time-step selection strategy derived from the experimentally verified dual-channel case were adopted in the present model. This ensures computational reliability and accuracy in resolving thermal-hydraulic transients, thereby providing a robust foundation for the large-scale parametric analyses and subsequent machine learning training and evaluation conducted in this study.

Algorithm design and construction

FMI-PyFMI integration and co-simulation control

To enable efficient coupling between the system thermal-hydraulic simulations and external data-driven modules, the Functional Mock-up Interface (FMI) standard was adopted for model encapsulation and co-simulation control. The NUMAP-generated open and closed parallel channel models were exported as Functional Mock-up Units (.fmu) compliant with the FMI 2.0 standard, each containing the model equations, parameters, and standardized interface definitions. Within the PyFMI environment, parameter transfer, data synchronization, and simulation control were implemented through structured Python-FMI interaction. Each.fmu package contains a modelDescription.xml file defining the model variables, including heating power, flow rate, pressure, and boundary conditions. Using the PyFMI library, the load_fmu() function was invoked to instantiate the model object, while model parameters were updated in real time via set() methods prior to each simulation run. This standardized approach facilitated seamless integration between the thermal-hydraulic simulations and machine learning workflows, enabling automated batch execution of parametric studies while maintaining precise control over simulation inputs and outputs.

The Python-based PyFMI library served as the execution environment. Prior to each simulation, the modelDescription.xml file within the FMU was programmatically parsed to access parameter lists, boundary conditions, and output variables. Input parameters-including heating power PHeater1, PHeater2, and total power combinations-were dynamically assigned to the FMU instance via the setReal() interface before simulation initialization. This mechanism ensured deterministic configuration of each case within large-scale automated parameter sweeps.

Data synchronization across the co-simulation was governed by PyFMI’s internal time-stepping solver. During runtime, state variables were advanced using NUMAP’s internal integrator, while PyFMI extracted flowrate, temperature, and pressure outputs at uniform sampling intervals (Δt = 0.02 s). To guarantee temporal consistency, all runs shared identical total durations and integration step sizes, enabling direct cross-case comparison and Fourier/autocorrelation analyses on uniformly aligned time-series data.

The co-simulation controller was parallelized using the Joblib multiprocessing framework. Each heating-power combination was treated as an independent simulation task executed in an isolated PyFMI instance, allowing concurrent evaluation of multiple parameter sets. This configuration enhanced throughput and computational efficiency during GA-driven global searches for DWO regions.

In brief, the FMI-PyFMI-Joblib configuration established a scalable and reproducible co-simulation platform, ensuring synchronized data exchange and efficient parameter management across thousands of simulation runs.

Flow regime identification via Fourier and autocorrelation analysis

In parallel channel systems, accurate flow regime identification is essential for operational safety, particularly for detecting DWO that may threaten structural integrity. Traditional physical models often face difficulties in capturing the nonlinear characteristics of such systems. Therefore, the Fourier transform (FT) and autocorrelation function (ACF) analysis are employed as nonparametric signal-processing tools to extract periodic features directly from time-series flow data. FT converts time-domain signals into the frequency domain to identify dominant oscillation frequencies, while ACF evaluates temporal self-similarity at different time lags to reveal periodic structures in the time domain. The combined application of FT and ACF compensates for the limitations of each individual method-FT may emphasize transient spectral peaks, whereas ACF may lose sensitivity for long-period oscillations in finite sampling windows. Their complementary use enables more robust identification of DWO through cross-validation between frequency-domain and time-domain characteristics.

The specific operational procedure is as follows: The flow regime identification process begins with data acquisition, in which mass flow rates from representative branch channels (pipes 6, 7, and 8) are continuously sampled at a frequency of 50 Hz over simulation intervals ranging from 145 to 150 s. Each run, therefore, produces 251 discrete data points that describe the temporal evolution of the flow field.

Finally, cross-validation between Fourier and autocorrelation results determines the flow regime classification based on joint quantitative criteria. A flow is identified as stable when the absolute flow rate variation satisfies ΔQ < 0.0002 kg s−1 and the normalized fluctuation intensity ΔQ/Qavg < 0.002. If no matching frequency pair between DFT and ACF results falls within a 20 % relative error tolerance, the regime is categorized as an irregular oscillation. Conversely, when at least one frequency pair matches within this 20 % margin, the condition is classified as a DWO, with the highest-energy Fourier frequency component designated as the representative oscillation signature. Invalid numerical runs, including cases in which the solver exceeded the admissible thermophysical-property range or failed to converge, were not treated as physical flow regimes. These cases were excluded from classifier training, hyperparameter optimization, and final confusion-matrix evaluation.

Additional multi-window consistency analyses (10, 15, and 20 s versus the 5 s baseline) show that the labeling results (5 s) remain highly stable across the dataset, with >99.1% consistency in the open system and 100% consistency in the closed system, confirming the robustness of the adopted 5 s window. Detailed comparison results are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

The methodology was validated on 3721 open-system power combinations (11163 pipe datasets) and 1596 closed-system high-density parameter scans. For open systems, DWO detection achieved 99.19% accuracy with zero missed DWO cases (see “Results and Discussion”). In closed systems, GA-optimized KNN models confirmed 96.4–100% accuracy across pipes (see “Results and Discussion”).

Configuration of the genetic algorithm

The GA is a stochastic optimization method inspired by the natural processes of evolution, including selection, crossover, and mutation. By imitating the mechanisms of biological inheritance, GA iteratively explores the search space to progressively approach the global optimum. Starting from an initial population, each individual (chromosome) is evaluated according to a predefined fitness function, which quantifies its performance with respect to the optimization objective. Individuals with higher fitness values are assigned greater probabilities of being selected for reproduction. Through crossover, genetic material from two parent individuals is recombined to generate offspring that inherit a mixture of traits, while the mutation operator introduces small random perturbations to the chromosome structure, thereby maintaining genetic diversity and preventing premature convergence to local optima.

In this study, the GA was employed for two principal tasks: identification of DWO conditions in the parameter space and hyperparameter tuning of the KNN model. Both problems exhibit complex nonlinear characteristics and large, multimodal search domains, where traditional deterministic or gradient-based optimization methods are prone to entrapment in local minima. Owing to its population-based search mechanism, GA provides robust global exploration and does not rely on explicit mathematical formulations or derivative information of the objective function. This makes it particularly suitable for optimizing flow-regime recognition problems under intricate thermal-hydraulic conditions.

A dedicated class, GeneticAlgorithm, was developed to implement the complete GA workflow. During initialization, the class receives the core configuration parameters-population size, chromosome length, fitness function, and random seed. The random seed ensures reproducibility by fixing the pseudorandom number generation sequence. The initial population is generated differently depending on the task type: for the dwo_finding problem, each gene value is randomly sampled within the range [0, 300,000]. For other tasks (e.g., KNN tuning), chromosomes are initialized as all-ones vectors.

In the fitness evaluation stage, the algorithm adaptively invokes the appropriate fitness function according to the problem category. For dwo_finding, each chromosome is directly passed to the evaluation function to compute its fitness value. For KNN optimization, additional contextual parameters—such as pipeline type and auxiliary variables-are incorporated to accurately assess each individual’s predictive accuracy. These fitness scores serve as the quantitative basis for the subsequent selection process.

The selection operation adopts a tournament selection mechanism. A subset of individuals is randomly chosen to form a tournament, and the individual with the highest fitness value is designated as a parent. This process is repeated to obtain two parents for crossover. The crossover operator applies a single-point crossover strategy: a random crossover point is determined along the chromosome, and the gene segments of the two parents are exchanged at this point to produce two offspring with newly combined genetic information.

The mutation process is dynamically configured according to both the problem type and the current generation number. For the dwo_finding task, chromosomes undergo Gaussian perturbations with a large variance during early generations to encourage global exploration. Mutation amplitudes are progressively reduced as iterations proceed, balancing exploration and exploitation. Each mutated gene is constrained within the [0, 300,000] interval to preserve physical feasibility. For the KNN hyperparameter optimization task, a fixed mutation probability is applied to perform binary bit-flipping (0 ↔ 1), introducing new genetic diversity into the population.

Throughout the iterative evolution, the algorithm continually computes the fitness of each generation, records the best and worst individuals, and updates the global optimum. Selection, crossover, and mutation operations generate successive populations, with elitism ensuring that the best-performing solution of the current generation is preserved in the next, thus preventing the loss of superior solutions. Upon termination—after the specified number of generations or convergence threshold—the algorithm outputs the global best solution, the corresponding fitness value, and comprehensive lists of generation-wise best individuals, their fitness trajectories, and population statistics.

For the dwo_finding application, the algorithm further incorporates population deduplication, grouping, and parallel computation to accelerate fitness evaluation and reduce runtime complexity. Parallel execution distributes the evaluation of individual chromosomes across multiple threads, leveraging modern computational resources for efficiency gains.

To further assess whether the dataset size was sufficient for reliable prediction within the studied configuration, an additional split-ratio sensitivity analysis was conducted. The comparisons indicate that the structured simulation datasets provide adequate coverage of the present operating domain and that the predictive performance is not strongly sensitive to reasonable variations in the train/test partition. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Note 3.

Distance metrics for KNN and their mathematical characteristics

In this study, the KNN model employs one of eight alternative distance calculation methods to determine the similarity between two points in a two-dimensional feature space. The specific metric ultimately adopted is selected automatically by the GA according to the distance formulation that achieves the highest fitness value after iterative evolution. The GA simulates the biological processes of evolution-encoding, selection, crossover, and mutation—to optimize and screen the most appropriate distance metric for the dataset under analysis.

At the outset, all eight-candidate metrics are binary-encoded to form the chromosome genes within the GA population. A population is then initialized, and a fitness function is constructed based on the prediction accuracy returned by the fitness_calculate function. Each distance metric’s effectiveness is quantitatively assessed through this fitness evaluation. The algorithm proceeds through repeated tournament selection, crossover, and mutation operations, continuously updating the population across generations. When the pre-defined number of iterations is reached, the individual with the highest fitness is identified, and the corresponding distance metric is selected as the final configuration of the KNN model—thereby realizing a fully self-adaptive optimization of the distance-measurement method.

Summary of algorithm integration

The complete computational workflow, illustrated schematically in Fig. 2, unifies co-simulation, signal analysis, and intelligent optimization into a single automated pipeline. Simulation data generated via FMI-PyFMI are first processed through Fourier autocorrelation analyses to label each parameter combination as stable, irregular, or DWO. The labeled dataset then serves as input for GA-optimized KNN model training. To avoid information leakage during model assessment, performance was evaluated on held-out blind-test subsets that were not used during hyperparameter optimization or classifier fitting. Specifically, 500 randomly generated power combinations were reserved for the open parallel channel system and 309 for the closed parallel channel system. Their reference labels were assigned independently using the same Fourier autocorrelation criteria and were kept hidden from the classifier until prediction was completed, after which they were released for confusion-matrix construction and metric calculation. No overlap was permitted between the training and blind-test subsets.

Analysis and identification of flow instability in the open parallel channel systems

Building upon the 150-s simulation duration determined through pilot studies, this study implemented a co-simulation framework using PyFMI to integrate FMI models (model_1.fmu) with Python-based control logic. This architecture enables automated batch adjustment of heat flux and parallel simulation execution. To systematically investigate flow instability regimes, the heat flux range for dual heaters was configured as 0–1.2 MW m−2 with 0.02 MW m−2 increments, generating 3721 parameter combinations. Leveraging multicore processing capabilities, the Joblib library facilitated 10 threads of parallel computation through intelligent task partitioning: distributing workloads into optimized batches where each thread autonomously executes simulations with real-time data archiving, thereby ensuring the stability of the computation and substantially improving the efficiency at the same time.

Global analysis of flow in pipe conditions

The flow regime identification method based on Fourier analysis and autocorrelation functions was applied to analyze the working fluid flow characteristics in individual pipes. By systematically integrating 3721 sets of simulation data, the analytical procedure described in Supplementary Note 5 was performed for pipe 6, pipe 7, and pipe 8, and the corresponding flow regime distributions are presented in Fig. 3A–C, respectively. These figures visualize the distribution of stable flow and oscillatory regimes across the entire heater heat flux range of 0–1.2 MW m−2. Each color point in Fig. 3 corresponds to a simulation case obtained at a discrete combination of heater powers. The blue points denote stable flow conditions, the red points represent oscillatory (DWO) regimes identified through the Fourier-autocorrelation criteria, and the white points appear only near the transition boundary between regimes. The black points correspond to invalid numerical runs in which the coolant temperature exceeded the material-property limits of the numerical code. These cases were excluded from the physical-regime dataset and were not used for classifier training or evaluation.

Flow regime distribution of pipes across the entire heat flux interval in an open parallel channel system.

A Pipe 6. B Pipe 7. C Pipe 8.

A systematic quantitative analysis of the flow distribution in the pipes demonstrates that the system exhibits pronounced dynamic stability when the heat flux of both heaters falls within the range of 0–0.6 MW m−2. Under these conditions, over 95% of the fluid operating states are characterized by steady flow, with only sporadic irregular oscillations and regular oscillations (DWO) occurring near the boundary regions of the heat flux combinations. This phenomenon demonstrates that balanced low-power inputs sustain thermodynamic equilibrium, while flow disturbances induced by heat flux instability are rapidly attenuated through the inherent damping characteristics of the pipes.

The system’s dynamic behavior undergoes substantial changes under asymmetric heater heat flux configurations, when one heater operates in the 0.3–0.8 MW m−2 range, and the other remains within 0–0.3 MW m−2. Although stable flow remains dominant (approximately 70%), the occurrence rate of DWO conditions surges sharply when the heater heat flux ratio (heater 1: heater 2) falls within narrow intervals of [0.25, 0.375] and [8/3, 4]. This reveals the existence of power ratio-sensitive zones where asymmetric heating induces resonance effects between fluid density gradients and flow in pipes inertia, markedly lowering the threshold for regular oscillations. The nonlinear relationship between DWO frequency and power ratio further confirms the complex nonlinear characteristics of system dynamics.

When the heat flux of one of the heaters is increased to 0.8–1.2 MW m−2, and the other heater fluctuates in the range of 0–1.2 MW m−2, the system fully enters the region of a strongly nonlinear dynamic regime. In this state, DWO conditions dominate over 85% of operational scenarios, while stable flow persists only sporadically. Intensive thermal input causes drastic fluid density instability, generating sustained pressure oscillations that prevent stable flow maintenance. Simultaneously, increasing power asymmetry amplifies flow instability, further promoting DWO formation and persistence.

The above results indicate that the absolute value of heater power, relative ratio, and power gradient together constitute the key factors affecting the kinetic stability of the open parallel channel system. Besides, the overall prediction of red and blue regions is highly accurate. However, the few irregular points near the boundary arise from local perturbations in the discretized parameter space (finite power interval) and from the intrinsic sensitivity of nonlinear systems close to bifurcation. The Fourier autocorrelation algorithm used for regime classification identifies such cases as irregular oscillations, because their frequency components do not fully satisfy the periodicity criteria within the analyzed time interval. Hence, the white points do not represent physical errors or solver instabilities. Instead, they correspond to the transient manifestation of attractor competition between the steady and limit-cycle states near a Hopf-bifurcation boundary. Similar behaviors-quasi-periodic, intermittent, or weakly damped oscillations-are well documented in nonlinear thermal-hydraulic systems that exhibit bifurcation hysteresis or subcritical transitions. Therefore, these transitional regions are naturally more responsive to small disturbances than the fully stable domains, leading to such isolated misclassifications. Notably, this phenomenon reflects the stochastic uncertainty of the Fourier-based identification method near regime interfaces.

In other words, the Fourier-autocorrelation algorithm, while achieving an overall identification accuracy of 99.19%, exhibits an inherent limitation in unambiguously classifying these borderline cases. Within the finite observation period, the flow may exhibit weakly damped or quasi-periodic oscillations that do not fully satisfy the strict periodicity criteria for DWO, nor the stability thresholds for a steady classification, resulting in their designation as irregular oscillations. This phenomenon accounts for the scattered points at the regime interfaces and the sparse oscillatory points within predominantly stable regions, revealing the stochastic uncertainty of the identification method near critical boundaries.

This behavior is consistent with findings in similar nonlinear systems, such as those reported in GA-based searches for instabilities in natural circulation loops45. The high overall predictive accuracy of the GA-KNN framework confirms that the core physical mechanisms are robustly captured. Future work will aim to improve boundary-region prediction by extending the simulation duration, incorporating time-frequency analysis techniques, and employing nonlinear time-series diagnostics to enhance the resolution of these critically sensitive transitional zones.

Prediction of operating conditions for arbitrary heating power in the open parallel channel systems

With the aim of establishing a high-reliability KNN model, 3721 simulated flow regime datasets from open parallel channel systems constitute the training set, supplemented by 500 randomly generated power combinations for testing, forming a comprehensive dataset. Hyperparameter optimization employs GAs to simulate natural selection mechanisms, efficiently exploring parameter spaces for iterative model refinement.

During GA-based KNN hyperparameter optimization, key parameters (K-value and distance metrics) are systematically investigated. The K-value search range extends up to 2048 (211), while eight predefined distance metrics are encoded using binary representation to facilitate genetic operations. The chromosome structure combines distance metric indices and K-values, with crossover and mutation operations enabling efficient parameter space exploration. The prediction accuracy of KNN serves as the fitness function, driving the evolutionary search toward superior hyperparameter configurations to enhance overall model performance.

Figure 4 illustrates the evolutionary trajectories of the GA fitness functions for pipe 6 (Fig. 4A), pipe 7 (Fig. 4B), and pipe 8 (Fig. 4C) during the hyperparameter optimization process of the KNN model. As shown in Fig. 4A, the optimal configuration for pipe 6 utilizes the Chebyshev distance metric with K=12, achieving a prediction accuracy of 97.6%. Figure 4B shows that the highest accuracy for pipe 7 (98.6%) is obtained using the Chebyshev distance with K=7. For pipe 8, the optimal performance is achieved using the Chebyshev distance with K=7, as illustrated in Fig. 4C, resulting in an accuracy of 98.8%. These curves not only demonstrate the GA-driven performance enhancement across generations but also validate the effectiveness of adaptive hyperparameter optimization in complex channel systems. Analysis and identification of flow instability in the closed parallel channel systems

Search for DWO regions in the closed parallel channel systems

Based on the closed-form topological model of parallel channel systems established in Fig. 1b, this section investigates the occurrence regions of DWO within such systems. A two-stage GA strategy was employed: firstly, leveraging its global optimization capability to intelligently search and preliminarily identify potential DWO regions, thereby narrowing the scope of study. Subsequently, intensive sampling is carried out within these candidate regions to accurately delineate DWO-triggering boundary conditions and critical parameter thresholds. This two-phase approach achieves an optimal balance between exploration efficiency and result precision.

In the implementation of GAs for identifying regions susceptible to DWO in the closed parallel channel systems, this study defines the fitness function as the maximum value of the ratio (Qmax − Qmin)/Qmin across the three pipes. This formulation characterizes the amplitude of flow instability while decoupling the influence of inherent channel oscillatory behaviors. By emphasizing pronounced deviations in flow distribution, the proposed fitness metric facilitates the efficient identification of parametric regions exhibiting DWO characteristics.

In the GA implementation, the chromosome is designed to encode the heating power combination of heater 1 and heater 2 as (PHeater 1, PHeater 2), with power values specified in watts (W). This approach ensures real-time heat dissipation from the system, thereby mitigating the risk of pipe rupture due to overheating. Considering the current industrial standard of 0.8 MW m−2 heat flux for advanced commercial heaters, and considering necessary safety margins, parameter scalability, and the trade-off between computational resources and simulation accuracy, the heating power for each PHeater [i] (i = 1, 2) is constrained to an upper limit of 0.3 MW. This threshold is chosen to optimally balance experimental safety and the resolution of system dynamics.

This study conducts system simulation by constructing individuals through a GA, with each group containing 10 individuals undergoing 60 generations of iterative evolution, totaling 600 simulated individuals. During the initialization phase, the power combinations of first-generation individuals were randomly generated, while subsequent generations strictly adhered to the core operational mechanisms of GAs, including selection, crossover, and mutation, to continuously explore the heater power parameter space. Following complete iterative processes, the final algorithmic search results are visualized in Fig. 6. The figure clearly reveals the mapping relationship between DWO operating condition zones and heater power configurations.

Through analysis of the GA optimization results in Fig. 6, it was observed that the algorithm exhibits a pronounced search bias toward the region of heater power combinations depicted in the right subplot, indicating a high probability of DWO conditions occurring in this zone. To clarify the operational pattern distribution within this region, this study conducted a refined exploration across parameter ranges of heater 1 [46,000, 54,000 W] and heater 2 [0, 30,000 W], employing a sampling interval of 400 W. This configuration yields 21 discrete values for heater 1 and 76 for heater 2, resulting in 21 × 76 = 1596 simulation datasets through combinatorial permutations of the two heaters’ power settings. Furthermore, the power combinations previously explored by the GA within this domain were integrated with newly generated sampling points, collectively forming a high-density parameter scanning matrix that achieves fine-grained coverage of the DWO-prone operational region.

The distributions in Fig. 7 reveal marked pipe-dependent differences in the closed parallel channel system. Pipe 6 exhibits a narrow, highly aggregated DWO strip, indicating a well-defined triggering zone, whereas pipe 8 shows a localized island-like DWO region surrounded by stable and invalid numerical cases. By contrast, pipe 7 remains almost DWO-free across the entire investigated subspace. Because pipes 6–8 share identical geometry and local resistance layout, this DWO-free behavior cannot be attributed to structural differences. Instead, it is primarily governed by heat-load allocation: pipe 7, located downstream of heater 1, experiences a relatively high but narrow heating range (46,000–54,000 W). A Ledinegg-type stability analysis further shows that the outlet vapor quality remains within xexit=0.214−0.251, while the total pressure-drop derivative satisfies dΔPtotal/dm°>0 throughout this range, preventing the system from entering the unstable region required for DWO onset. Detailed derivation and parameter evaluation are provided in Supplementary Discussion 2. The high identification accuracy obtained for pipe 7, based on the FT-ACF approach, should therefore be interpreted in the context of a physically verified DWO-free operating domain.

The above results reveal the differences in the dynamic response of different pipes under the same power combination, which provides an important basis for the prediction of the working conditions and safe operation of the closed parallel channel system.

To validate the practical applicability of the genetically optimized KNN model, this study conducted operational-condition prediction experiments on 309 held-out blind-test cases. The findings substantiate the effectiveness of the optimization strategy and underscore the model’s practical value in engineering applications, offering a reliable technical approach for monitoring the operational states of closed parallel channel systems.

Conclusions

This study addresses the issues of flow distribution imbalance and DWO caused by uneven heating in parallel channel systems, proposing an intelligent identification and diagnostic method that integrates a GA and the KNN algorithm. Based on a simulation platform (NUMAP), numerical models of both open and closed parallel channel systems were established, with datasets generated through systematic simulations. For the open parallel channel system, a full parameter space scan was conducted to analyze the distribution of flow oscillations. Regarding the closed parallel channel system, a GA was utilized for a global search of DWO-sensitive regions, followed by refined simulations to determine heating parameters triggering DWO. Based on the simulation data, KNN working condition prediction models were established for both systems, respectively. A GA was novelly introduced to optimize the hyperparameters of the KNN model, substantially improving the accuracy of working condition identification for both types of systems and providing an efficient solution for engineering safety operations.

The principal conclusions of this paper are as follows:

(1) Open parallel channel systems exhibit regular flow pattern transitions with heating power variations: stable flow dominates in low-power regions (0–0.3 MW m−2), DWO-sensitive zones exist in medium-high power regions (0.3–0.8 MW m−2), and DWO dominates in high-power regions (0.8–1.2 MW m−2). The flow pattern identification method combining the Fourier transform and autocorrelation analysis achieved 99.19% accuracy in DWO detection.

(2) For the closed parallel-channel system, the proposed two-stage GA-assisted strategy efficiently located a DWO-prone subregion in the heater-power space: stable flow prevails in low-power regions (0–30 kW), DWO-sensitive zones appear in medium-high power regions (30–100 kW), within which pipe 6 exhibited a narrow DWO strip and pipe 8 showed a localized island-like DWO region, whereas pipe 7 remained DWO-free throughout the investigated domain. These results highlight strong pipe-dependent differences in instability sensitivity under the refined operating window considered in this study.

(3) The integrated Fourier-autocorrelation method effectively identifies three flow states (stable flow, irregular oscillations, and DWO). Through GA-optimized KNN hyperparameters, the model achieves high-accuracy operating condition identification in both open and closed parallel channel systems: prediction accuracies of 97.8%, 97.6%, and 97.6% for the three pipes in the open system, and 96.76%, 100%, and 97.09% in the closed system, respectively. The model maintains robust performance on the blind test dataset, validating effective generalization from training data to unseen operating conditions. Compared with conventional methods (i.e., RF model), the GA-based adaptive hyperparameter optimization strategy enhances the discriminative reliability of the model in complex nonlinear thermal-hydraulic systems, providing an effective technical approach for real-time monitoring and safe operation of parallel channel systems.

(4) Investigations of the closed system reveal that different pipe positions exhibited markedly distinct dynamic responses under identical heating power combinations. Pipe 6 displays a narrow DWO strip, and pipe 8 shows a localized island-like DWO region, whereas pipe 7, located downstream of heater 1, remains DWO-free throughout the entire investigated domain. This phenomenon arises from the spatial nonuniformity of heat load distribution, where pipe 7 satisfies the Ledinegg stability criterion. This finding elucidates the critical mechanism of structural symmetry but asymmetric dynamic behavior, providing essential guidance for differentiated safety design of parallel channel systems.

Although the present GA-KNN framework has demonstrated reliable performance in identifying flow regimes within the investigated parallel-channel systems, several extensions would further strengthen its generality and engineering applicability. The current study focuses on identical channel geometries and a fixed range of boundary conditions, within which the structured parameter sweeps, blind-test evaluations, and supplementary sensitivity checks provide adequate coverage and support the robustness of the proposed methodology. Nevertheless, because the present feature space does not explicitly incorporate dimensionless thermo-hydraulic parameters, direct extrapolation to systems with different geometries, scales, or operating levels would still require additional retraining or recalibration. Future work will therefore introduce characteristic dimensionless groups, such as the Reynolds number, phase change number (Npch), subcooling number (Nsub), and aspect ratio (L/D), to enhance cross-configuration adaptability and improve the physical transferability of the model. In parallel, broader sensitivity analyses will be expanded to cover geometric parameters, boundary-condition variations, numerical discretization, sampling-window selection, labeling thresholds, and algorithmic settings, thereby providing a more systematic understanding of the factors influencing prediction stability. Reproducibility will also be further reinforced through more standardized reporting of simulation settings, dataset construction, random initialization, train-test partitioning, and model-training protocols. In addition, advanced sequence-learning models, such as long short-term memory networks and Transformer architectures, may be incorporated to capture more intricate nonlinear dynamics and long-range temporal correlations in transient flow signals.

These extensions are expected to strengthen the robustness and engineering applicability of the framework, providing intelligent and reliable tools for early detection and prevention of flow instabilities in nuclear thermal-hydraulic and energy processing systems.
