Abstract
We present theoretical and practical results on the order theory of lattices of functions, focusing on Galois connections that abstract (sets of) functions – a topic known as higher-order abstract interpretation . We are motivated by the challenge of inferring closed-form bounds on functions which are defined recursively, i.e. as the fixed point of an operator or, equivalently, as the solution to a functional equation. This has multiple applications in program analysis (e.g. cost analysis, loop acceleration, declarative language analysis) and in hybrid systems governed by differential equations. Our main contribution is a new family of constraint-based abstract domains for abstracting numerical functions, 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} -bound domains , which abstract a function f f by a conjunction of bounds from a preselected set of boundary functions. They allow inferring highly non-linear numerical invariants , which classical numerical abstract domains struggle with. We uncover a convexity property in the constraint space that simplifies, and, in some cases, fully automates , transfer function design. We also introduce domain abstraction , a functor that lifts arbitrary mappings in value space to Galois connections in function space. This supports abstraction from symbolic to numerical functions (i.e. size abstraction ), and enables dimensionality reduction of equations. We base our constructions of transfer functions on a simple operator language , starting with sequences , and extending to more general functions , including multivariate, piecewise, and non-discrete domains.

Introduction

Functions are fundamental objects in program semantics and formal
descriptions of dynamical systems, and are thus of prime interest to
static analysis. However, classical abstract interpretation has often
focused on state-oriented perspectives, emphasising “zeroth-order”
abstract domains, which describe sets of values.
As a result, properties and abstractions of lattices of

functions
– a topic sometimes called
higher-order
abstract interpretation

cousot1994higher-short
– have received
comparatively little attention.
In this paper, we explore a perspective treating functions as
first-class objects, and we build abstract domains of abstract functions,
using functions as the basic building block.
They allow us to use abstract interpretation as a solving strategy to
infer closed-form bounds on the solutions to functional equations,
viewed as abstractions of programs and systems.

Our original motivation stems from
static cost analysis
: the
automated inference of bounds on a program’s resource consumption
(e.g. execution time, memory, energy consumption).
In static cost analysis, unlike classical worst-case execution time
(WCET) analysis, we wish to infer
cost functions
that are

parametric
in some abstraction of program inputs (e.g.
numerical
sizes
), and are correct both asymptotically and for
small inputs.
One established approach to static cost analysis, used in the static analyser

;

;
ciaopp-sas03-journal-scp-shortest
;

;

;
gen-staticprofiling-iclp16-extrashortest
;
resource-verification-tplp18-extrashortest
;
mlrec-tplp2024-nourl
;
order-recsolv-sas24-nourl

and other related tools,
Wegbreit75-short-plus

of the program itself.
Solutions or bounds on the solutions to these equations then
provide information on the cost of the program.

Of course, functional equations are ubiquitous in static analysis and
computer science in general, from symbolic semantic equations, to
numerical recurrence equations for cost analysis, and even
differential equations in dynamical systems.
Thus, the work presented in this paper is applicable
in many contexts beyond cost analysis.

In particular, higher-order abstract interpretation has recently

We argue that it is worthwhile to explore the design of

Our intuition is that functions

In fact, while classical numerical domains struggle to express

The ideas presented in this paper build upon investigations

Most

2.2
Equations as Operators Viewpoint

The work presented in this paper builds upon the equation as operator
viewpoint developed in
order-recsolv-sas24-nourl
, as well as
further insights developed in the study of this order theory framework
of functional equations.

The basic idea of this approach is to recall that a functional
equation with unknown

and

may be equivalently described as a fixpoint problem

\forall\vec{n}\in\mathcal{D},\,f(\vec{n})=\mathrm{\Phi}(f)(\vec{n}),

is used to define

2.3
Orders in function space

Given an equation
\mathrm{\Phi}
, what order can we impose on our set of
functions to make Theorem
2.1
usable and useful?

In this paper, we always use the most natural order structure on
sets of functions: the
pointwise order
. It gives us a
notion of upper and lower bounds that is useful for program analysis,
as well as an intuitive notion of function comparison.

Definition 2
(Pointwise lattice structure)

For
X

(L,\leq,\vee,\wedge)
a complete lattice, the
set of functions

can be equipped with a
natural lattice structure
(X\to L,\mathop{\dot{\leq}},\dot{\vee},\dot{\wedge})

by

\centering\begin{gathered}f\mathop{\dot{\leq}}g\overset{\mathrm{\Delta}}{\iff}\forall x\in X,\,f(x)\leq g(x)\\
f\,\dot{\vee}\,g\overset{\mathrm{\Delta}}{=}\big(x\mapsto f(x)\vee g(x)\big),\quad\,\dot{\bot}\,\overset{\mathrm{\Delta}}{=}\big(x\mapsto\bot\big)\\
f\,\dot{\wedge}\,g\overset{\mathrm{\Delta}}{=}\big(x\mapsto f(x)\wedge g(x)\big),\quad\,\dot{\top}\,\overset{\mathrm{\Delta}}{=}\big(x\mapsto\top\big)\\
\end{gathered}\@add@centering

This order structure, however, appears in several
different flavours, depending on the order used in

(L,\leq)

We are interested in functions arising in numerical contexts
where three main types
of order appear

the usual order on the real numbers, set inclusion orders
(e.g. interval inclusion), and the

Example 2
(Flat order, denotational semantics)

It is common to see pointwise orders in classical denotational

Example 8
(Size abstraction)

Suppose we are interested in the cost (a real number, e.g. the

of a deterministic procedure as a function of
its inputs, which are elements of a large set

of symbolic
data structures (e.g. lists of numbers, trees with nodes

The cost function we are interested in is an element

Thus, we perform an abstraction step, by introducing a

from the large set of data

\texttt{Sizes}=\mathbb{N}
. For example,

We are thus left with a problem about an abstract cost function

procedure as a function of data
sizes
.
If we can infer the best possible

access to the exact cost of the procedure called on an arbitrary

.
This explains both the intuition behind the precomposition step in

\gamma
, and the usage of join in
\alpha

We now explain how to treat an interval valued function

\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{R})
as a pair of lower and upper bound functions in

Definition 3
(Bounds of interval-valued function)

Consider an interval-valued function
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{R})
, which never
outputs the empty interval. Then, we can uniquely define the lower
bound and upper bound of each

, which can be gathered into two
functions

, so that

\forall x\in\mathcal{D},\,f(x)=[f_{lb}(x),f_{ub}(x)]
.

To simplify notations, we may use the notion of
an interval of
functions
, and suggestively write this relationship as

f=[f_{lb},\,f_{ub}]
.

If we allow

to output the empty interval, a convention must be
chosen, e.g. that

and
f_{ub}(x)=-\infty

whenever
f(x)=\varnothing
.

Remark 5

If we use classical intervals
\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{R})
, the notions of
interval-valued functions, pairs of functions, and interval of
functions are not exactly equivalent.

This comes from the issue of the empty interval, and its multiple
representations via a pair of bounds.
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{R})
admits
several valid representations as
(f_{lb},f_{ub})\in(\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})^{2}
,
while the notion of interval of functions

is weaker
than that of interval-valued function, because the notion of

\bot
is more granular in the latter case.

These uninteresting complications disappear if we accept to work
with Kaucher intervals (see Ex.

where
(\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{I}_{K}(\mathbb{R}),\dot{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathcal{I}_{K}})\cong\big(\mathcal{D}\to({\overline{\mathbb{R}}}\times{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}),\overset{.}{\overline{\geq_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}\times\leq_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}}}\big)\cong(\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}},\dot{\geq}_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})\times(\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}},\dot{\leq}_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})

which may also be suggestively written
\mathcal{I}_{K}(\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})
.

Remark 6
(From interval equations to classical equations)

Thanks to these isomorphisms, an interval functional equation on

f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{I}_{K}(\mathbb{R})
can always be viewed as a
system
of
classical numerical function equations on the pair of unknowns

(f_{lb},f_{ub})\in(\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})^{2}
.

Importantly, this transformation
preserves monotonicity
, from

\dot{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathcal{I}_{K}}
to

\dot{\geq}_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}\times\dot{\leq}_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Because of this, techniques to bound the solutions of numerical
systems of equations (as in

or this
paper) can also be used to bound the solutions of interval
functional equations.

Note that with this construction, the equation
\mathrm{\Phi}_{\mathcal{I}}
is
transformed into a
coupled system of equations
.

In some favourable cases this system can be
separated
, i.e.
decoupled, into two independent equations
\mathrm{\Phi}_{\mathbb{N},lb}
and

\mathrm{\Phi}_{\mathbb{N},ub}
.
It can be shown that this occurs when, in addition to being

\dot{\sqsubseteq}_{\mathcal{I}}
-monotone (where

\sqsubseteq_{\mathcal{I}}=\geq_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}\times\leq_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
), the operator

\mathrm{\Phi}_{\mathcal{I}}
is also
\dot{\leq}_{\mathcal{I}}
-monotone
, for the new order

\leq_{\mathcal{I}}\,:=\,\leq_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}\times\leq_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
, defined as in

ModalItvBook2014-short

Further favourable properties can be exploited to transform
numerical-set equations
\mathrm{\Phi}_{\mathcal{P}}
or
\mathrm{\Phi}_{\mathcal{I}}
into purely numerical
functions equations, trying to make these equations as precise as
possible. However, these subtle analyses are outside the scope of this
paper, and will be described in future work.

3.4
Towards
\mathfrak{B}
-bounds domains: reminders on
constraint domains
, and the case of functions

We are now ready to set the stage for
\mathfrak{B}
-bounds
, the
key family of abstract domains discussed in this paper, and the last
Galois connection of Fig.
2
.

Our
\mathfrak{B}
-bounds are an instance of the idea of

constraint domains
: complex concrete objects are abstracted by
conjunctions of constraints taken in some preselected set

\mathfrak{C}
of simpler, manageable constraints.
This is an ubiquitous concept in abstract interpretation, and we apply
it to the case of functions, leading to abstractions of the shape

(\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}},\mathop{\dot{\leq}})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\smash{\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-3.57222pt}{\hbox to30.37247pt{$\smash{-}\mkern-7.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\smash{-}\mkern-2.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-7.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}}\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\smash{\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-0.01563pt}{\hbox to30.37247pt{$\mathord{\leftarrow}\mkern-7.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\smash{-}\mkern-2.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-7.0mu\smash{-}$}}}\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\smash{\enskip\raisebox{-4.73608pt}{\ $\scriptstyle\alpha_{\mathfrak{B}}$\ }}\hss}\hbox to0.0pt{\smash{\hskip 5.49913pt\raisebox{7.50104pt}{\ $\scriptstyle\gamma_{\mathfrak{B}}$\ }}\hss}\rule[-5.73608pt]{0.0pt}{15.82043pt}\hskip 30.37247pt}(\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B}),\supseteq),

where
\mathfrak{B}
is a preselected set of
boundary functions
,
as will be detailed in the next section (abstracting pointwise-ordered
functions, or interval-valued functions, or sets of functions).
The main appeal of these
\mathfrak{B}
-bound domains is that,
for functions, we will be able to work relatively easily with

classes
of highly non-linear numerical invariants, which is a
challenge for classical numerical abstract domains.

In this section, we simply wish to draw the reader’s attention to this
reversed inclusion order
\supseteq
, to avoid confusion, and to
quickly recall a few classical examples of constraint domains to build
intuition.

The reversed inclusion is simply a consequence of the duality between
objects and constraints (or between generators and constraints), which
may be schematised as

Bigger object (
\subseteq
)
\longrightarrow
less constraints (
\supseteq
),

Smaller object (
\supseteq
)
\longrightarrow
more constraints (
\subseteq
).

Example 11
(Classical numerical abstract domains)

Most classical numerical abstract domains follow this pattern:
the domain of polyhedra

abstracts sets of
values by conjunctions of
affine inequalities
, so that the
inclusion of a set
E
in the concretisation of a polyhedron can be
written as

\forall x\in E,\,\bigwedge_{i}\sum_{j}a_{i,j}x_{j}\leq b_{i}
.

Similarly, abstract values in the domains of hyperboxes (intervals),
zones and octagons may be seen as conjunctions of inequalities taken
in classes of constraints that are smaller than the full
class of affine inequalities
CousotCousot76-short
;
Mine2001-zones-short
;
Mine2001-octagons-short
.

Example 12
(Template domains)

Instead of considering an infinite family of functions to generate
the set of constraints
\mathfrak{C}
, it is also possible to start
from finitely many functions, for efficiency or expressivity

Domains constructed in this way are often called
template
domains
, e.g.
the

-level sets

of

, and are an example

For

-level sets, a finite family

of
possibly non-linear functions is selected, and

is then
abstracted by conjunctions on inequalities of the form

\bigwedge_{p\in P}p(x)\leq v(p)
, where

.

Example 13
(Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz

The reversion of the inclusion order is very visible in the case of

Theorem 4.1
(Convexity of the set of constraints)

Suppose that the class of boundary functions
\mathfrak{B}

Then, for all

\alpha_{\mathfrak{B}}(f)
is also convex (i.e.

\alpha_{\mathfrak{B}}(f)\cap(\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R})
is convex in function space).

Proof

Let

b_{1},b_{2}\in\alpha_{\mathfrak{B}}(f)\cap(\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R})
.
Take any
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
For all

, we have
f(\vec{n})\leq b_{1}(\vec{n})
and

f(\vec{n})\leq b_{2}(\vec{n})
, so

f(\vec{n})\leq\lambda\cdot b_{1}(\vec{n})+(1-\lambda)\cdot b_{2}(\vec{n})
.
Thus,
\big(\lambda\cdot b_{1}+(1-\lambda)\cdot b_{2}\big)\in\alpha_{\mathfrak{B}}(f)

e.g.

\lambda\cdot\big(n\mapsto\sum a_{k}n^{k}\big)+(1-\lambda)\cdot\big(n\mapsto\sum b_{k}n^{k}\big)=\big(n\mapsto\sum(\lambda a_{k}+(1-\lambda)b_{k})n^{k}\big).

Remark 9

In practice, to work with
\mathfrak{B}
-bounds, we may need to
replace
(\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B}),\supseteq,\cap,\cup)
with a more
computable representation

(\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow,\text{fin}}(\mathfrak{B}),\sqsubseteq^{\sharp},\sqcup^{\sharp},\sqcap^{\sharp})
,
where
A\sqsubseteq^{\sharp}B
is a sound approximation of

\mathop{\uparrow}{A}\supseteq\,\mathop{\uparrow}{B}
.

This typically involves approximating the pointwise order

\mathop{\dot{\leq}}
on
\mathfrak{B}
using a coefficient-wise comparison, derived from a parametrisation of

\mathfrak{B}
(e.g. comparing polynomials via their coefficient vectors).

By parametrisation of
\mathfrak{B}
via
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we simply mean a
function
\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathfrak{B}
, such as the mapping that describes a
polynomial
by its vector of coefficients.

Remark 10

Note that if
\mathfrak{B}
satisfies the descending
chain condition (i.e. there is no infinite strictly decreasing
chain of elements of
\mathfrak{B}
), then for every

U\in\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}(\mathfrak{B})
, there exists a minimal (w.r.t. inclusion) set

A\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})

such that
U=\,\mathop{\uparrow}{A}
.
Moreover,
A
forms an antichain.
If, in addition,
all antichains in
\mathfrak{B}
are
finite, it follows
that all sets
\alpha_{\mathfrak{B}}(f)
are finitely representable as
the upper closure of a finite antichain.

This phenomenon, reminiscent of Dickson’s lemma, can occur when

\mathfrak{B}
is parameterised by
\mathbb{N}^{k}
, such as in the case of
polynomial or affine bounds with non-negative integer coefficients.
In this paper, however, we focus instead on
\mathfrak{B}
that can
be parameterised by vectors of
real numbers
, leveraging

convexity properties
and aiming
to identify convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
that admit finite
representations.

5
Abstract Domains of Sequences

We now present
examples of
\mathfrak{B}
-bound domains, starting with the simple case of
the function space
\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e. univariate
sequences
.
We begin
in Section
5.3
with simple affine bounds, then
proceed
to
non-linear abstract domains
. We first
let
\mathfrak{B}
be
the set of polynomials of bounded degree (parameterised initially in
the monomial basis, then in the binomial basis) in
Section
5.4
. Next, we move on to exponential
bounds and products of polynomials with exponentials in
Section
5.5
.

As a reminder, our ultimate
goal is to compute
an overapproximation of the solution
\mathrm{lfp}\,\mathrm{\Phi}
of a concrete
numerical equation
\mathrm{\Phi}\in\mathrm{End}_{\mathop{\dot{\leq}}}(\mathbb{N}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})
. In this paper,
we aim to achieve this via
an abstract equation

\mathrm{\Phi}^{\sharp}\in\mathrm{End}_{\sqsubseteq^{\sharp}}(\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}(\mathfrak{B}))
,
operating on
the abstract domain of
\mathfrak{B}
-ubs.
Computing abstract Kleene sequences in this setting yields
an overapproximation of
\mathrm{lfp}\,\mathrm{\Phi}^{\sharp}
, which itself
overapproximates
the solution
\mathrm{lfp}\,\mathrm{\Phi}
to our equation.
We exemplify this using
an
ideal, best abstraction

\mathrm{\Phi}^{\sharp}
in
Section
5.1
.

However, in practice, we need an automated method
to compute the abstract operator
\mathrm{\Phi}^{\sharp}
from the definition of

\mathrm{\Phi}
. We follow
the classical compositional approach of abstract interpretation.
We first define the syntax of a simple language for defining

\mathrm{\Phi}
, then compute the abstract semantics (i.e.
transfer
functions
) for its basic constructs, and finally compose them
to obtain a sound abstraction
\mathrm{\Phi}^{\sharp}
of
\mathrm{\Phi}
.
For this purpose, we introduce a simple operator language in
Section
5.2
.

5.4
Polynomial Bounds

The situation is very similar in the case of polynomials, which are
also closed under most operations in our language. The main
differences are the increased number of coefficients, and the fact
that the coordinate-wise order is now incomplete, potentially limiting
simplifications in generator sets.

Definition 11
(Polynomial bounds, bounded degree, monomial basis)

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. We consider

\mathfrak{B}=\big\{n\mapsto\sum_{k\leq d}a_{k}n^{k}\,\big|\,a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\big\}\cup\big\{n\mapsto\mathbf{ite}(n=0,a_{0},+\infty)\\

d

\mathfrak{B}\cong\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\,\cup\,\{+\infty\}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\,\cup\,\{\top_{\mathfrak{B}}\}
.
We order
\mathfrak{B}
with the corresponding coordinate-wise
(product) order
\sqsubseteq_{monom}
.

Proposition 10

\sqsubseteq_{monom}
is a sound approximation of
\mathop{\dot{\leq}}
, but it is
not complete.

Proof

For
f=n\mapsto\sum a_{k}n^{k}
and
g=n\mapsto\sum b_{k}n^{k}
,
if
\forall i,\,a_{i}\leq b_{i}
, then
\forall n,\,f(n)\leq g(n)
.

However, the converse does not hold.
For example,
n^{2}\mathop{\dot{\geq}}n
, i.e.

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\,n^{2}\geq n
, but
(1,0,0)\not\sqsupseteq(0,1,0)
.
(Note that however, on the real numbers,
(1/2)^{2}\ngeq(1/2)
.)

Remark 14

If desired, it is still possible to compute
\mathop{\dot{\leq}}
for polynomials, but
we do not need this fact here.

Like before, there is no difficulty in computing the abstract
semantics of our constants and of the arithmetic operations, using
standard polynomial arithmetic.
The situation is a bit more complex for

and

For example, for

\sum_{k\leq d}a_{k}(n+1)^{k}=\sum_{k\leq d}\Big(\overbrace{\sum_{j\leq d}a_{j}\binom{j}{k}}^{p_{k}}\Big)n^{k}.

We can also set up a convex problem to generate the transfer function
for

, but it is quite dense.

To simplify, we can use a
useful trick from the literature on type-based amortised cost

basis of polynomials (

, where shift operations behave more simply.

Definition 12
(Binomial basis)

The binomial basis of the set

\big\{n\mapsto\binom{n}{k}\,\big|\,k\in\mathbb{N},\,0\leq k\leq d\big\}

For example,
\binom{n}{0}=1
,
\binom{n}{1}=n
,

\binom{n}{2}=\frac{1}{2}n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n
, and

\binom{n}{3}=\frac{1}{6}n^{3}-\frac{1}{2}n^{2}+\frac{1}{3}n
.

5.5
Beyond Polynomials: Exponential Polynomials

We may go even further and consider sets
\mathfrak{B}
of boundaries
composed of all elements of the form
n\mapsto\sum_{b=1}^{m}a_{b}b^{n}

(exponentials, spanned by bounded integer bases), or, even better,

n\mapsto\sum_{b=1}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{d}b^{n}n^{k}
(exponential polynomials,
i.e. products of polynomials with exponentials).
In full generality, it would be desirable to allow arbitrary bases
rather than a finite number of them, or even work with more expressive
templates such as
n\mapsto\sum a_{b,k,e,f}b^{n}n^{k}\log(en+f)
.
However, for practical reasons,
it is convenient to let
\mathfrak{B}
form a
finite-dimensional
vector space
, which simplifies
transfer function design.

The principles that allow us to compute abstract semantics
\llbracket.\rrbracket^{\sharp}

of
Seq
for such exponentials and exponential polynomials are very
similar to those in previous sections: in several cases (e.g.
+
) we
immediately obtain it from the stability properties of
\mathfrak{B}
.
To go further, we can apply convexity arguments
and some transfer function synthesis.

In the case of exponentials and exponential polynomials, we can also
simplify (and make the optimisation problems more sparse) by using a
trick similar to the binomial basis used for polynomials. Once again,
this technique originates in the context of type-based automated
amortised resource analysis (AARA), in more recent
work
Kahn20-short
. The idea is to use the
Stirling numbers of
the
2^{\text{nd}}
kind
, which grow exponentially, and satisfy a
Pascal-like identity that makes them well-behaved under shifts.

Definition 14

Stirling numbers of the
2^{\text{nd}}
kind
, defined for
each
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and denoted by
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
, are
given by the following recurrence relations

\begin{gathered}\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n}=1\text{ for }n\geq 0,\qquad\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{0}=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{n}=0\text{ for }n>0,\\
\textstyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{k}=k\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}+\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k-1}\text{ for }0<k<n.\end{gathered}

They grow exponentially in
n
for fixed
k
:

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
is asymptotically equivalent to
\frac{1}{k!}k^{n}
.

Moreover, the sets of functions on
n
spanned (as vector spaces) by
\{1,2^{n},3^{n},...m^{n}\}

and by
\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{1},...,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{m}\}
are the same.
We can change basis between these representations and provide
explicit formulas for
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
, see
e.g.
ConcreteMathematics
.

Using this, we can work in the vector space

\mathfrak{B}=\mathrm{Vec}\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{1},...,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{m}\}
,
extended with appropriate top elements, as before.
The
Pop
operation is simple.

Proposition 12

\llbracket\mathtt{Pop}\rrbracket\big(\sum_{b\leq m}a_{b}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{b}\big)
is the function

\sum_{b}p_{b}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{b}
, with coefficients given by
p_{b}=b\cdot a_{b}+a_{b+1}
.

This approach extends naturally
to exponential polynomials in
stirling+binomial basis, using

\mathfrak{B}=\mathrm{Vec}\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{b}\binom{n}{k}\;\middle|\;1\!\leq\!b\!\leq\!m,0\!\leq\!k\!\leq\!d\right\}.

Here, similar computations can be performed by observing

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{b}\binom{n+1}{k}=b\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{b}\binom{n}{k}+b\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{b}\binom{n}{k-1}+\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{b-1}\binom{n}{k}+\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{b-1}\binom{n}{k-1}
.

5.6
Widenings

We have not yet discussed widenings, which are crucial in practice for
ensuring the convergence of ascending Kleene sequences in the domain

(\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}(\mathfrak{B}),\supseteq)
.
However, designing widenings is a highly heuristic task, best
approached in parallel with implementation and (automated)
experimentation. We therefore present here only a few core ideas –
sufficient to guarantee finite-time convergence – and defer their
evaluation and tuning to future work.

There are two primary sources of non-termination when working in

\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}(\mathfrak{B})
, especially when sets are represented
as upper closures of a finite set of generators.

1.

The cardinality of the generator set may grow without bound
(no ascending chain condition for sets of generators, no finite
antichain condition in
(\mathfrak{B},\mathop{\dot{\leq}})
, no descending chain
condition for sets of valid bounds).

2.

The generators themselves may be repeatedly updated and grow
unboundedly
(no ascending chain condition in
(\mathfrak{B},\mathop{\dot{\leq}})
),
as illustrated in Section
5.1
.

To address the first issue, we adopt a classical strategy used for
powerset
domains:
4
4

4

See
DBLP:journals/sttt/BagnaraHZ07
for references and advanced discussions
on widenings for powerset domains.

fix an arbitrary integer bound
max-nb-constraints
on the
number of generators. When this bound is exceeded, we either drop or
join new constraints, selecting them in arbitrary order.

To address the second issue, as mentioned in
Section
5.1
, we only consider the case where

\mathfrak{B}
is parameterised by finite vectors of numbers,
and propose using parameter-wise
stability widening
,
possibly combined with thresholds and delayed widening – this is the
most common widening for intervals
CousotCousot76-short
;
MineTutorialBook2017-short
, where it is
called
standard widening
.
Intuitively, the effect of
\nabla_{\text{stability}}
is to track the
evolution of each parameter of each constraint: once a parameter is
updated repeatedly, its value is increased to the
next threshold, and ultimately
to
+\infty
.

We may also try to directly use precise polyhedra
widenings
BHRZ05-SCP
on the convex set of parameter vectors.

Finally, to accelerate convergence and potentially improve precision,
we may choose to do as mentioned in
Section
5.1
, and exit early if we detect

(post)fixed generators
, i.e. generators
b\in\mathfrak{B}
of
an abstract value
F^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}(\mathfrak{B})
such that

b\in\llbracket\texttt{Prog}\rrbracket^{\sharp}(b)
for the formulation

\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket^{\sharp}:\mathfrak{B}\to\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}(\mathfrak{B})
of the
abstract semantics.

6
Convexity and Transfer Function Synthesis

As observed in Theorem
4.1
, when the class
of boundary functions
\mathfrak{B}
is convex, the set

\alpha_{\mathfrak{B}}(f)
of all constraints in
\mathfrak{B}
satisfied
by a concrete
f
is itself a convex set.

Moreover, designing an (optimal) abstract transfer function

T^{\sharp}:\mathfrak{B}\to\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}(\mathfrak{B})
corresponding
to a construct with concrete semantics

T:(\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})\to(\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})
can be stated as the
following problem.

Given a function
b\in\mathfrak{B}
,

find
all

f^{\sharp}\in\mathfrak{B}
such that

f^{\sharp}\mathop{\dot{\geq}}T(b).

This can be easily generalised to multi-input constructs,
and the set of solutions to this problem
is convex.
Note that it is acceptable to discover
some
but not all
solutions: this yields
a non-optimal but sound transfer function.
In particular, it is acceptable to replace
\mathop{\dot{\leq}}
in the above
problem with a sound approximation
\sqsubseteq_{\mathfrak{B}}
, and/or
to perform integer-to-real relaxations.

Now, assume we are given a parametrisation
p:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathfrak{B}

which preserves the notion of convexity from constraint space

\mathfrak{B}
to parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, i.e. such that

F^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})
is convex whenever

p^{-1}(F^{\sharp})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is convex, e.g. if
p
is linear,
as in all prior examples.
Transfer function design can then be reformulated
as the following problem, whose set of solutions is convex in

\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Given a vector
\vec{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

find
all

\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

p(\vec{x})\mathop{\dot{\geq}}T(p(\vec{y})).

Therefore,
transfer function synthesis
reduces to the
question of (automatic) discovery of a set of generators for this

finite-dimensional convex set
, or more exactly this family of
convex sets parameterised by
\vec{y}
.

For many classes of expressions allowed
in
p
and
T
, the above problem is tractable for tools such
as CAS
augmented with SMT solvers and
advanced algebraic decision procedures, e.g. for quantifier
elimination.
For example, we have performed transfer function synthesis experiments
in two CAS,
Mathematica

mathematica-v13-2-short
and

Sage

sagemath-short
, by explicitly encoding the semantics of
several concrete transfer functions
T
and explicitly giving
various families of boundaries
\mathfrak{B}
.
These CAS then possess primitives that allow to

(1)
query for instances of parameters that solve the above problem,

(2)
provide a finite representation of the set of solutions of the
above problem (via quantifier elimination and other reductions),
or even
(3)
discover minimal solutions and express these sets via
generators.

Of course, the problem does not always admit a minimal
set of solutions that generate all others under
convexity, even when the solution set is finitely representable.
In such cases, we may (arbitrarily or automatically) select a
finite number of generators.
5
5

5

This is reminiscent of the classical example of
approximating a disk with a polyhedron.
This is not a coincidence (see
Rem.
15
).

As the reader might expect, in general, these synthesis operations can
become computationally expensive.
Nevertheless, it is important to note that synthesis is required

only once
, during the
design
our abstract domain. For
example, we may begin by specifying that we want to consider the case
where
\mathfrak{B}
is the set of bivariate quartic polynomials

\mathbb{R}_{4}[X,Y]
. We can then apply transfer function synthesis
a single
time
to derive all relevant abstract transfer functions,
e.g. for some extension of the language
Seq
. Finally, the abstract
domain can be implemented, using the automatically generated transfer
functions, with no further calls to the CAS required at analysis
time.
6
6

6

In addition to alleviating the burden of manual abstract domain

design
, transfer function synthesis can be used to improve

precision
, by generating transfer function for more than just
the most basic syntactic constructs, see related
work
Reps2004-short
;
Monniaux_LMCS10-short
;
BrauerKingLMCS12-short
;
AmurthOOPSLA22-short
.

Nevertheless, it is desirable to speed up the process of transfer
function synthesis. By relaxing full automation and allowing ourselves
to reformulate the above optimisation problems, we can drastically
simplify the queries sent to solvers.
Indeed, a key difficulty of the above problem lies in the use of the
order
\mathop{\dot{\leq}}
, which introduces an extra quantifier of the form
“
\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\,\texttt{<...>}
” in our queries (or possibly a
relaxed variant like
“
\forall n\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
”).
The problem becomes significantly easier
if we find a way to discard the variable
n
, and formulate the
problem purely in terms of inequalities over the parameters
\vec{x}
and

\vec{y}
.
This is precisely what we did in our “warm-up” example of
Remark
13
, which gave the
abstract semantics of
\llbracket\mathtt{Push}~c\rrbracket^{\sharp}
in
Proposition
9
from
Section
5.3
.
We now present two further examples, corresponding to transfer
function synthesis in
Sections
5.4
and
5.5
.

Example 17

For univariate
polynomials of degree
d
written in the monomial basis, the convex
problem for synthesising
\llbracket\texttt{Push}~x\rrbracket^{\sharp}
on the input
\sum a_{k}n^{k}
, with unknowns
(p_{d},...,p_{1},p_{0})
, can be written

{\small\begin{cases}&\hfill p_{0}\geq x\\
\wedge&\forall k\in[0,d],\;{\displaystyle\sum_{j\leq d}p_{j}\binom{j}{k}}\geq a_{k},\end{cases}}

following the same Pop/Push trick used in
Remark
13
, and using
relaxation to only enforce coordinate-wise order.
Note, however, that the corresponding matrix is very dense.
By contrast, using the
binomial
basis presentation, we obtain

\small\begin{cases}&\hfill p_{0}\geq x\\
\wedge&\forall k<d,\;p_{k}+p_{k+1}\geq a_{k}\\
\ \wedge&\hfill p_{d}\geq a_{d},\end{cases}

which is much more sparse, and whose generators can in fact even
reasonably be computed by hand.

For exponential polynomials in the basis of binomials and Stirling

\llbracket\texttt{Push}~x\rrbracket^{\sharp}
applied to the function

\big(\sum_{k,b}a_{b,k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{b}\binom{n}{k}\big)

\vec{p_{b,k}}

p_{m+1,k}=p_{b,d+1}=0
for all

\small\begin{cases}&\hfill p_{1,0}\geq x\\

Remark 15
(Convexity for Relational Domains)

One may wonder what enabled us to design non-linear numerical
abstractions for functional constraint domains, since this
is a challenge in the classical case of numerical relations (c.f.
Section
3.4
).
The convexity property of Theorem
4.1
is
clearly a key ingredient.

Does this mean that Theorem
4.1
no longer
holds for relational constraints?
No.
In fact, a variant of Theorem
4.1
can be
formulated in the relational case. We conjecture that this approach
may prove useful to enable transfer function synthesis in classical
numerical abstract domains, and perhaps even enable novel non-linear
abstractions of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

We leave these directions to future work.
For now, we simply offer the reader a “constraint space
perspective” on the example of abstracting of a disk using
polyhedra – the classical example used to demonstrate that
polyhedra do not come from a full Galois connection.

From a purely theoretical perspective, we can in fact build a Galois
connection from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to the set of
all
affine
constraints, just as we did for
\mathfrak{B}
-bounds, allowing
infinitely many constraints in principle.
For such affine constraints, however, closed convex polyhedra in
state space correspond dually to closed convex polyhedra
in constraint space.

Example 19
(Dual of a Disk)

We may abstract the disk

D=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\}
by the infinite set

\alpha(D)=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,\forall(x,y)\in D,\,ax+by\leq c\}

of affine inequalities satisfied by
D
.

We can compute the shape of
\alpha(D)
in constraint space,
and obtain
\alpha(D)=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,c\geq\|(a,b)\|_{2}\}
,
where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidean norm.
In other words, the abstraction of the disk may be visualised in
constraint space as an upward, closed convex cone.
In fact, for any object
E\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the set
\alpha(E)
would
always be a convex cone in constraint space.

There is, however, a redundancy in the parametrisation of affine
inequalities by
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
If we remove this
redundancy, we are left with the
projective space

\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, in which
\alpha(D)
looks like a disk (its points
are rays of the above cone).
To recover a finite set of constraints (at the cost of precision),
one must choose a finite number of points of the disk, and they
should be
extremal
– i.e. belong to its boundary, which is a
circle.
Finally, if these points are chosen in an equipartition of the
circle, and interpreted back from constraint space to state space, we
obtain the classical approximation of a disk by a regular
polyhedron.

More generally, this final example suggests the viewpoint of transfer
function synthesis via tessellations of boundaries in constraint
space.

7
Abstract Domains of Functions

We now succinctly present how the ideas introduced in
Section
5
can be extended beyond the case
of
sequences

\mathbb{N}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
, defined over a minimalistic operator
language based on
Push
/
Pop
constructs, to more
general
functions

\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
on more general
domains
\mathcal{D}
.
This extension is presented as a generalisation of the
sequence case, highlighting the additional features required.

7.1
Multivariate Functions

Handling multivariate functions of the form
\mathcal{D}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
with

\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{N}^{d}
, is relatively straightforward, at least when starting
from a finite-dimensional vector space
\mathfrak{B}\cong\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{R}\cdot f_{k}
(extended with top elements), as in the case of the
examples in Section
5
.

To extend such
\mathfrak{B}
-bound domain, initially defined for
univariate
functions
\mathbb{N}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
to an abstract domain for multivariate
functions
\mathbb{N}^{d}\to{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}
, we can simply perform a
(tensor)
product
(using the coordinate-wise order on parameters), i.e.

\mathfrak{B}^{\otimes d}:=\bigotimes_{i<d}\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{R}\cdot f_{k}^{(i)}=\bigoplus_{k_{1},...,k_{d}}\mathbb{R}\cdot f_{k_{1}}^{(1)}\otimes...\otimes f_{k_{d}}^{(d)}.

The main significant change is that more memory is required to
store abstract values: if the univariate
case was dealt with an
r
-dimensional parameterisation, we now need

r\times d
numbers to describe an element of

\mathfrak{B}^{\otimes d}
.

Example 20

Univariate
polynomials
\mathbb{R}[n]
in the monomial basis
P(n)=\sum_{k}a_{k}n^{k}

simply give rise to multivariate polynomials
\mathbb{R}[x,y]=(\mathbb{R}[n])^{\otimes 2}
in the monomial basis
P(x,y)=\sum a_{k_{x},k_{y}}x^{k_{x}}y^{k_{y}}
.

We can easily define new recursive constructs in each
direction
i
, written
\llbracket\texttt{Pop}_{i}\rrbracket
and

\llbracket\texttt{Push}_{i}~c\rrbracket
, e.g.
.

\texttt{Push}_{y}~c:\;\;f(x,y,z)\rightsquigarrow\mathbf{ite}\big(y=0,\,c,\,f(x,y-1,z)\big),

which may be composed to construct more complex recursive calls.
Transfer functions (e.g.
\llbracket\Diamond\rrbracket^{\sharp}
,
\llbracket\texttt{Cst~c}\rrbracket^{\sharp}
,

\llbracket\texttt{Pop}_{i}\rrbracket^{\sharp}
, etc.) generalise naturally.

Example 21

For polynomials,
1\cdot(x+1)^{2}yz=(x^{2}+2x+1)yz=1\cdot x^{2}yz+2\cdot xyz+1\cdot yz
.
Similarly, in the binomial basis,

\binom{x+1}{k_{x}}\binom{y}{k_{y}}\binom{z}{k_{z}}=\binom{x}{k_{x}}\binom{y}{k_{y}}\binom{z}{k_{z}}+\binom{x}{k_{x}-1}\binom{y}{k_{y}}\binom{z}{k_{z}},

so more generally,
\llbracket\texttt{Pop}_{x}\rrbracket^{\sharp}\big(\sum a_{(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})}\binom{x}{k_{x}}\binom{y}{k_{y}}\binom{z}{k_{z}}\big)=

\uparrow\!\!\Big\{\sum\big(a_{(\mathbf{k_{x}},k_{y},k_{z})}+a_{(\mathbf{k_{x}+1},k_{y},k_{z})}\big)\binom{x}{k_{x}}\binom{y}{k_{y}}\binom{z}{k_{z}}\Big\}.

7.2
Piecewise Functions, for Complex Control Flows

Extending our domains to support control flows beyond the ones
associated with
sequences
is more interesting, and requires
adding new features.

Note that for now, we can only represent recursive calls of the form

f(\vec{n}-\vec{k})
, where
\vec{k}
is a constant vector, in a way that
is identical in the whole domain
\mathcal{D}
, except for base
cases which occur only when at least one input variable is
0
.
In the cost analysis of symbolic declarative programs, it is reasonable to
assume that base cases are defined for data structures of small size.
It is also reasonable to assume that recursive cases consists in taking
data, deconstructing it, and making recursive calls on data
structures of smaller size. Such assumptions are made, implicitly or
explicitly, in many cost analysers, such as
DBLP:journals/toplas/0002AH12-shortest
;
caslog-shortest
;
vh-03-shortest
.

However, this is not a sound assumption for more general static
analysis: variables inside loops, for example, may not evolve in a
global monotone way. This assumption is also a limitation in the

cost
analysis of typical
imperative
programs, as
thoroughly
discussed in
montoya-phdthesis-short
.

Example 22

Let us consider a simple multiphase loop
presented in
montoya-phdthesis-short
, and the corresponding cost
equation
\mathrm{\Phi}
below, which gives the number of loop iterations as
a function of the initial values of the variables.
Crucially, observe that the domain
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}^{3}
must be partitioned
into several subdomains
\mathcal{D}_{i}

Related Work

Higher-order abstract interpretation

cousot1994higher-short
,
can be defined as the study of abstract domains whose
values are interpreted as functions and operators.
Its relevance is clear for the abstract interpretation of higher-order
functional languages, a topic first studied in the early 90s (e.g.

BurnSCP86
;
BurnFP91-short
;
HuntPhd91-short
;
cousot1994higher-short
),
and which has recently received renewed attention for practical
analysis of languages such as

OCaml

montagu-icfp2020-short
;
montagu-salto-prelim-ml23-short
;
Bautista-FMSD2024-short
;
valnet-ECOOP25-short
,
where more refined value properties are explored.

Abstractions of higher-order objects are valuable
in other settings,
including static analysis of dynamical systems, such as simple loops
(e.g. for summarisation, acceleration, and in numerical filters) and
differential equations in hybrid systems. In dynamical systems,
non-linear properties quickly arise by iteration of simple (linear)
dynamics.
Sequence domains are also relevant to static analysis of data-flow
languages in synchronous programming.

For the analysis of filters written in
C
,

Feret05-short
introduced an abstract domain of

arithmetico-geometric sequences
, which gave us the initial
inspiration for
\mathfrak{B}
-bounds.
This domain abstracts sets of functions, but is intended to be used as
a classical “non-relational” value domain: variables in a loop are
(independently) bounded by a (non-linear) function of a local loop
counter. As in

\mathfrak{B}
-bounds, the finite representation of
non-linear functions is chosen to admit precise abstraction of simple
arithmetic and recursive structures, e.g. the operation
next
,
which is similar to our
Push
.

For differential equations, the
flowpipes

of
GoubaultHSCC17-short
;
GoubaultCAV18
provide inner and outer
interval-valued approximations of functions. The interval version of

\mathfrak{B}
-bounds is similar to outer flowpipes in both spirit and
concretisations, though their
inner machinery and order theory differs.

Non-linear abstract domains.
Few domains go beyond the
expressiveness of polyhedra. Notable exceptions include those
for polynomial
equalities
RodriguezKapurSas04-short
;
MullerOlm04-short
,
polynomial
inequalities
by reduction to
polyhedra
BRZ-polyineq-SAS05-short
, and the
wedge

domain
kincaid2018-shortest
.
Outside pure abstract interpretation, semialgebraic reasoning
techniques (e.g. cylindrical algebraic
decomposition
Collins75-CAD-short
or more tractable
optimisation-based sums-of-squares methods
RouxVS18-short
)
have been used to reason on polynomial inequality invariants,
especially for continuous and hybrid systems.
Efficient domains with lower expressivity than general (semi)algebraic
sets have also been designed, e.g. the ellipsoid
domain
Feret04-short
and the conic extrapolation of higher
ellipsoids of
MV-conic-ellipsoids-CAV15-short
.
Finally, incomparable domains have been considered, such as tropical
polyhedra
allamigeonTropicalSAS08-short
, or the
arithmetico-geometric sequences of
Feret05-short
, which are a
particular case of exponential polynomials.

Transfer function synthesis
is a growing family of techniques
enabled by the emergence of powerful decision procedures, quantifier
elimination techiques, as well as approaches for optimisation, search
and learning, among others. These techniques help design abstract
domains and improve their precision.
Important early work includes
GrafSaidi97-short
for
predicate-abstraction domains and
Reps2004-short
for domains with no
infinite ascending chains.
For numerical domains, the power of quantifier elimination and
optimisation techniques was demonstrated in a line of
work by Monniaux
Monniaux_POPL09-short
;
Monniaux_CAV09-shortest
;
Monniaux_CAV10-short
;
Monniaux_LMCS10-short
.
Strategies to avoid expensive quantifier elimination were discussed
in
BrauerKingLMCS12-short
, which also contains a recommended
introduction to the literature on the topic.
More recent
works
AmurthOOPSLA22-short
;
PasadoOOPSLA23-short
;
PruningOOPSLA24-short
;
USSTAD-preprint-25
;
ZixinHuangPhD25-chap5

have explored many additional
strategies, e.g. based on search or
numerical optimisation, and inspired by approaches from program
synthesis or machine learning.

Exact recurrence solvers.

Centuries of work have created a large body of
knowledge on recurrence solving, with classical results including closed forms of
C-recursive
sequences

Petkovsek2013sketch-short
,
or more recently,
hypergeometric solutions of
P-recursive
sequences

Petkovsek92
.
Several algorithms have been implemented
in CAS, based on mathematical frameworks such as

Difference Algebra

;
Levin08
,
Finite
Calculus

, or

However, as explained in the introduction, these techniques have a

Beyond classical CAS, specialised tools such as

PURRS

Applications of recurrence solving

invited to consult our references and previous contributions for more

9
Conclusions and Future Work

Motivated by the inference of closed-form bounds for
recursively defined functions, we have presented both theoretical and
practical results on the order theory of lattices of functions, within
an equation-as-operator viewpoint.
In particular, we proposed a
domain abstraction
mechanism,
which formalises the abstraction of a symbolic semantic equation by a
numerical functional equation, also also paves the way towards
applications to dimensionality reduction.
Most importantly, we introduced
\mathfrak{B}
-bound domains
–
constraint-based abstract domains using predefined boundary functions
(e.g. polynomials, exponentials) – enabling the inference of

non-linear numerical invariants
. A key convexity property
simplifies transfer function design and, in some cases, enables
automation.
We demonstrated
their application to
concrete domains of
sequences
, and
sketched how they may be used for more general
functions
.

This work complements our previous contributions, where we showed
that our results have multiple applications in program analysis, such
as static cost analysis, loop acceleration, and the analysis of
higher-order declarative languages.
Still, this paper is a stepping stone towards broader applications and
generalisation. We have provided theoretical constructions and
illustrative examples, laying the groundwork for future
implementations. Experimental work will be essential to refine these
ideas – especially regarding
widening operators and efficient representations of abstract
values. In particular, it would be valuable to develop tools based on

\mathfrak{B}
-bound domains for cost or value analysis of numerical
higher-order programs, and compare them with
existing approaches.

Our investigations also raise several open questions.
To move beyond minimal operator languages with hardcoded recursive
structure, Section
7.2
highlights a
compelling problem: designing
practical techniques to compute “best
\mathfrak{B}
-approximations of

\mathfrak{B}
-cases by
\mathcal{C}
-parts”.
Another key challenge lies in the operator abstraction induced by
domain abstraction (Section
3.2
), which
is a priori non-constructive. Developing automated techniques to
select appropriate mappings
m
and compute their abstractions –
especially across broader classes of
m
– is of central importance
in static cost analysis.
So far, we have focused on sets
\mathfrak{B}
that form finitely
generated vector spaces. Extending the framework to richer families of
bounds is another natural direction.
Finally, as outlined in Section
7.3
, we
plan to explore applications to continuous systems. While this paper
focuses
on discrete domains to develop the theoretical foundations of

\mathfrak{B}
-bounds, their generality makes them a promising framework
for continuous domains, where the need for such abstractions
may
be even greater than in cost analysis.
