Magnetic helicity fluxes in dynamos from rotating inhomogeneous turbulence
Axel Brandenburg, Ethan T. Vishniac

Abstract
We analyze direct numerical simulations of large-scale dynamos in inhomogeneous nonhelically driven rotating turbulence with and without shear. The forcing is modulated so that the turbulent intensity peaks in the middle of the computational domain and drops to nearly zero at the two ends above and below the midplane. A large-scale dynamo is driven by an α \alpha effect of opposite signs in the two hemispheres. In the presence of shear, the hemispheric magnetic helicity flux from small-scale fields becomes important and can even overcompensate for the magnetic helicity transferred by the α \alpha effect between large and small scales. This effect has not previously been observed in nonshearing simulations. Our numerical simulations show that the hemispheric magnetic helicity fluxes are nearly independent of the magnetic Reynolds number, but those between large and small scales, and the consequent dynamo effect, are still found to decrease with increasing Reynolds number—just like in nonshearing dynamos. However, in contrast to nonshearing dynamos, where the generated mean magnetic field declines with increasing magnetic Reynolds number, it is now found to remain independent of it. This suggests that catastrophic dynamo quenching is alleviated by the shear-induced hemispheric small-scale magnetic helicity fluxes that can even overcompensate the fluxes between large and small scales and thereby cause resistive contributions.



I
Introduction

Many astrophysical bodies harbor large-scale magnetic fields.
Late-type stars and galaxies are the main examples where a dynamo converts
kinetic energy into large-scale magnetic energy
[
24
,
17
]
.
Disks around young stars and compact objects are additional examples,
where the existence of large-scale magnetic fields has so far only been
inferred from simulations
[
1
,
40
,
26
]
.
In all these cases there is turbulence, the bodies rotate, and they are
stratified in the sense that the density and/or the turbulent velocity
vary in space.
This, together with the overall rotation, causes the turbulence to
become helical, which leads to an
\alpha
effect
[
49
,
67
]
,
where the coefficient
\alpha
relates the mean electromotive force to
the mean magnetic field.
Also, the magnetic field attains helicity, which affects the
\alpha

effect
[
53
]
.
The underlying theory has been the subject of textbooks
[
47
,
50
,
46
,
75
]
, but later it became clear that magnetic helicity
conservation causes such dynamos to saturate at progressively lower values
as the microphysical resistivity decreases or the conductivity increases

[
32
,
39
,
44
,
7
,
72
,
29
,
5
]
.
The resulting mean magnetic field would then be very weak for
astrophysically relevant resistivities.

[
6
]
coined the term catastrophic quenching, which in its
original form refers to the actual value of
\alpha
becoming very
small at low resistivities.
In particular, numerical simulations by
[
23
]
have shown that for
mean fields defined as volume averages, the value of
\alpha
diminishes
to zero as the conductivity increases.
It was therefore thought to be difficult to explain the generation of
the large-scale magnetic fields observed in many astrophysical bodies
with an
\alpha
effect.
It quickly became clear that the problem of catastrophic quenching
is connected with the homogeneity of the turbulence in such simple
numerical setups.
In those cases, there can be no magnetic helicity flux and magnetic
helicity is then well conserved in the limit of large conductivity.
To avoid this difficulty,
[
72
]
envisaged an
\alpha

effect that is computed from a specifically designed magnetic helicity
flux such that the magnetic helicity is conserved exactly.
However, the anticipated magnetic helicity fluxes have not yet been
found in numerical simulations
[
37
]
.
With just inhomogeneous turbulence, many numerical simulations show
that the amplitude of the resulting mean magnetic field decreases with
increasing conductivity
[
27
,
57
,
4
]
.
This phenomenon is then generally also still referred to as the
catastrophic quenching problem, even though
\alpha
itself may no longer
be catastrophically small.
On the other hand, corresponding analytic calculations of the mean
magnetic helicity fluxes by
[
45
]
have not shown a dependence
of the magnetic helicity flux on the microphysical conductivity.
The reason for catastrophic quenching remains therefore obscure.

Many previous numerical simulations have employed helical forcing.
The purpose of the present work is to avoid this by adopting a more
natural setup in which a nonhelical flow is being driven.
Kinetic helicity of the flow can then emerge self-consistently as a
result of stratification and rotation.
We also consider the effect of shear and how it contributes to the

\alpha
effect
[
60
]
.
Shear may also be responsible for driving magnetic helicity fluxes

[
72
]
.

In Section
II
, we describe our numerical simulations and the
test-field method that is used to compute the turbulent transfer coefficient.
We also discuss the decomposition of magnetic helicity fluxes into
contributions between hemispheres and between large and small scales.
In Section
III
, we describe the results without and with shear.
We conclude in Section
IV
.

II
Description of the model

II.1
Setup of our model

In this paper, we focus on the analysis of the magnetic helicity fluxes
resulting from a simulation in slab geometry with horizontal
xy

averages depending on time and disk height
z
.
In the middle of the domain at
z=0
, the averaged turbulent intensity
has a maximum.
The angular velocity vector points in the positive
z
-direction, which
allows us to associate the regions above and below the midplane with
north and south.
This geometry can also be applied to the two sides around the equator
of a sphere, where the
z
-coordinate corresponds to latitude.

For our numerical simulations, we employ the
Pencil Code

[
51
]
.
Since it advances the magnetic vector potential, the magnetic field is
always solenoid and the code is well suited for the task of analyzing
magnetic helicity and its flux.
Other codes that instead evolve the magnetic field and use divergence
cleaning to keep the magnetic field solenoidal can spontaneously produce
or destroy small-scale magnetic helicity
[
21
]
, although schemes
have been devised to conserve magnetic helicity at the expense of
modifying the magnetic field in neighboring places
[
76
]
.
The
Pencil Code
has also been used successfully in various
earlier studies of magnetic helicity fluxes
[
35
,
36
,
27
,
10
]
.

II.2
Governing equations

We consider nonhelically driven inhomogeneous turbulence of an isothermal
gas with constant sound speed
c_{\rm s}
in the presence of rotation with the
angular velocity vector
\bm{\Omega}\equiv(0,0,\Omega)
.
In some cases, we include an additional shear flow,
\bm{V}=(0,Sx,0)
,
where
S=-q\Omega
is a constant and
q
is the shear parameter.
Shear flows with
q<2
are Rayleigh stable, but unstable to the
magnetorotational instability for
q>0
; see
[
2
]
for a review.
Keplerian shear corresponds to
q=3/2
, while shear in galactic disks
corresponds to
q=1

[
3
]
.
The turbulence is stochastically driven with a forcing function

\mbox{\boldmath$f$}{}(\bm{x},t)
, whose intensity is modulated in the
z
-direction
with a profile function
f_{\mathrm{prof}}(z)={\textstyle{1\over 2}}(1+\cos k_{1}z)
, where

k_{1}=2\pi/L
is the lowest wavenumber in a cube of size
L^{3}
.
To assess the sensitivity of the results upon this
choice, we also consider a case with a top-hat profile by using

f_{\mathrm{prof}}(z)={\textstyle{1\over 2}}[1+\tanh(5\cos k_{1}z)]
, which has steep flanks
at
z=\pm\pi
, as quantified by the factor
5
in front of the
cosine function.
The forcing is applied on the right-hand side of the evolution equation
for the velocity
\bm{U}
, which then reads
[
16
,
18
]

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal D}{}\bm{U}}{{\cal D}{}t}=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$f$}{}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{U}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\bm{U}-SU_{x}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}{}-2\bm{\Omega}\times\bm{U}

(1)

\displaystyle-c_{\rm s}^{2}{\bm{\nabla}}\ln\rho+\frac{1}{\rho}\left[\bm{J}\times\bm{B}+{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot(2\rho\nu\mbox{\boldmath${\sf S}$}{})\right],

where
{\cal D}{}/{\cal D}{}t=\partial/\partial t+\bm{V}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}
is the advective
derivative with respect to the shear flow,
\rho
is the density,

\bm{B}
is the magnetic field,
\bm{J}={\bm{\nabla}}\times\bm{B}/\mu_{0}
is the current density,

\mu_{0}
is the vacuum permeability,
\nu
is the viscosity,
and
{\sf S}
is the traceless rate-of-strain tensor with the components

{\sf S}_{ij}=(\partial_{i}U_{j}+\partial_{j}U_{i})/2-\delta_{ij}{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{U}/3
.
The tensor
{\sf S}
is not to be confused with the constant scalar
S
,
which denotes the uniform background shear when
q\neq 0
.
The logarithmic density obeys the continuity equation in the form

\frac{{\cal D}{}\ln\rho}{{\cal D}{}t}=-\bm{U}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\ln\rho-{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{U}.

(2)

The magnetic field
\bm{B}={\bm{\nabla}}\times\bm{A}
is solved in terms of the
magnetic vector potential
\bm{A}
,

\frac{{\cal D}{}\bm{A}}{{\cal D}{}t}=-SA_{y}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}{}-\bm{E}-{\bm{\nabla}}\Phi,

(3)

where
\bm{E}=\eta\mu_{0}\bm{J}-\bm{U}\times\bm{B}
is the electric field, with

SA_{y}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}{}\equiv\bm{E}_{\mathrm{S}}
being the contribution from the shear,

\eta
is the magnetic diffusivity, and
\Phi
is the electrostatic potential.
In Equation (
3
), we have adopted the advecto-resistive gauge, in
which
\Phi=-V_{y}A_{y}-\eta{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{A}

[
22
]
.
As shown in
[
16
]
, the inclusion of the advective term
V_{y}A_{y}
is necessary for being able to adopt shearing–periodic boundary
conditions.
This means that the magnetic diffusion operator reduces to

\eta\nabla^{2}\bm{A}
; see
[
22
]
for further details.
In some cases, we also compute the vector potential in the
Coulomb gauge,
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{Cou}}=\bm{A}-{\bm{\nabla}}\Lambda
, by solving

\nabla^{2}\Lambda={\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{A}
with appropriate boundary conditions.

The nonhelical forcing function
f(\bm{x},t)
is given by
[
33
]

f(\bm{x},t)=f_{0}c_{\rm s}\,(c_{\rm s}k/\delta t)^{1/2}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}{}\times\bm{k}}{|\mbox{\boldmath$e$}{}\times\bm{k}|}\,e^{{\rm i}\bm{k}(t)\cdot\bm{x}+{\rm i}\varphi(t)},

(4)

where
e
is a random vector that is not aligned with
\bm{k}
,

\varphi(t)
is a random phase (
|\varphi|\leq\pi
), and
f_{0}
is the
amplitude.
At each time step, a new forcing vector
\bm{k}(t)
is chosen randomly from
a set of wavevectors
\bm{k}
whose lengths
|\bm{k}|
lie in a narrow band

k_{\rm f}-\delta k/2\leq k<k_{\rm f}+\delta k/2
, where
k_{\rm f}=8
and
\delta k=1

is used for all the runs discussed in this paper.

To analyze the possibility of large-scale dynamo action, it is useful
to compute planar averages.
Owing to the inhomogeneity in the
z
-direction, we adopt

xy
averages, which are denoted by an overbar, e.g.,

\overline{\bm{U}}(z,t)=\int\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)\,{\rm d}{}x\,{\rm d}{}y/L^{2}
.
Fluctuations about the average are then denoted by lowercase symbols,
e.g.,
\bm{u}=\bm{U}-\overline{\bm{U}}
,
\bm{b}=\bm{B}-{\overline{\bm{B}}}
, and
\bm{j}=\bm{J}-{\overline{\bm{J}}}
.

II.3
Control parameters and initial conditions

The value of the overall root-mean square (rms) velocity
u_{\rm rms}
is
characterized by the Mach number,
\mbox{\rm Ma}=u_{\rm rms}/c_{\rm s}
.
When there is shear, the value of
u_{\rm rms}
does not include this shear flow.
Since we are here not interested in studying compressibility effects,
we adopt subsonic Mach numbers and take
\mbox{\rm Ma}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr<\cr\sim\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$\textstyle#$\hfil\cr<\cr\sim\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr<\cr\sim\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr<\cr\sim\cr}}}}0.1
for all runs.
The values of
\nu
and
\eta
are characterized by the fluid and
magnetic Reynolds numbers,

\mbox{\rm Re}=u_{\rm rms}/\nu k_{\rm f}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}=u_{\rm rms}/\eta k_{\rm f},

(5)

respectively.
The ratio
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}=\nu/\eta
is the magnetic Prandtl number.
In the following, we vary
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}
by keeping the value of
Re
fixed.
Another control parameter is the relative forcing wavenumber,
k_{\rm f}/k_{1}
.
The amount of rotation and shear are quantified by the Coriolis and
shear numbers,

\mbox{\rm Co}=2\Omega/u_{\rm rms}k_{\rm f}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{\rm Sh}=S/u_{\rm rms}k_{\rm f},

(6)

respectively.
As initial condition, we use
\bm{U}=\ln\rho/\rho_{0}=0
, so the initial density
is equal to some reference density
\rho_{0}
.

The initial magnetic vector potential is calculated from a weak
Gaussian-distributed field with an rms value
B_{\rm rms}
such that the
rms Alfvén speed
v_{\rm A}=B_{\rm rms}/\sqrt{\mu_{0}\rho_{0}}
is a small fraction
of
c_{\rm s}
.
When
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
exceeds a certain critical value, there is dynamo action,
i.e.,
v_{\rm A}/c_{\rm s}
grows exponentially and saturates eventually at a value
around 0.1.
Instead of quantifying
v_{\rm A}/c_{\rm s}
, it is useful to quantify the ratio

v_{\rm A}/u_{\rm rms}
, or, equivalently, the value of
B_{\rm rms}
in units of the
equipartition field strength,
B_{\rm eq}=\sqrt{\mu_{0}\rho_{0}}\,u_{\rm rms}
.
The rms value of the large-scale field is denoted by
\overline{B}_{\rm rms}
.

Owing to the presence of rotation and stratification of the
turbulent intensity, we expect the generation of kinetic helicity,

\overline{\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}}
, where
\bm{\omega}={\bm{\nabla}}\times\bm{u}
is the vorticity of
the fluctuating velocity.
Following
[
38
]
, we determine the resulting kinetic helicity in
terms of the nondimensional ratio

\epsilon_{\rm f}(z)=\overline{\bm{\omega}\cdot\bm{u}}/u_{\rm rms}^{2}k_{\rm f},

(7)

which is characterized primarily by the amplitude of its variation,
defined here as

\epsilon_{\rm f0}=2\left\langle\epsilon_{\rm f}(z)\sin k_{1}z\right\rangle.

(8)

As in
[
38
]
, we expect
\epsilon_{\rm f0}
to increase linearly with
increasing rotation rate and with increasing stratification of turbulent
intensity, provided these values are not too large.

The simulations are performed with the
Pencil Code

[
51
]
.
Numerical results are usually presented as averages over a statistically
steady stretch in time.
Error margins are estimated as the largest departure over any one third
of the full time series.

II.4
Quasi-kinematic test-field method

To characterize the nature of large-scale dynamo action, we need to obtain
the mean-field dynamo coefficients that characterize the dependence of
the mean electromotive force
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}
on
{\overline{\bm{B}}}
and
{\overline{\bm{J}}}
.
The most accurate choice is the test-field method

[
62
,
63
,
8
,
20
]
.

In the test-field method, we solve the equations for the fluctuations

\bm{b}_{\mathrm{T}}
that result from a certain test field
\overline{\bm{B}}_{\mathrm{T}}
.
We represent it by
\bm{b}_{\mathrm{T}}={\bm{\nabla}}\times\bm{a}_{\mathrm{T}}
and solve for the vector
potential
\bm{a}_{\mathrm{T}}
, which, in the Weyl (or temporal) gauge with zero
electrostatic potential, obeys

\frac{\partial\bm{a}_{\mathrm{T}}}{\partial t}=\bm{u}\times\overline{\bm{B}}_{\mathrm{T}}+\overline{\bm{U}}\times\bm{b}_{\mathrm{T}}+\bm{u}\times\bm{b}_{\mathrm{T}}-\overline{\bm{u}\times\bm{b}_{\mathrm{T}}}+\eta\nabla^{2}\bm{a}_{\mathrm{T}}.

(9)

This allows us to compute
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}^{\mathrm{T}}=\overline{\bm{u}\times\bm{b}_{\mathrm{T}}}
.
We adopt the parameterization
\overline{\cal E}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}=\alpha_{ij}\overline{B}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}-\eta_{ij}\mu_{0}\overline{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
Since only the
x
- and
y
-components are significant, we have 8 unknowns
for the 4 components of
\alpha_{ij}
and the 4 components of
\eta_{ij}
.
To obtain all unknowns, we use the 4 vectorial test fields
\overline{\bm{B}}_{\mathrm{T}}=(c,0,0)
,

(s,0,0)
,
(0,c,0)
, and
(0,s,0)
, where
c=\cos k_{\rm T}z
and
s=\sin k_{\rm T}z
.
Since only the
x
- and
y
-components are significant, we have exactly
8 independent equations for the 8 unknowns.
We choose
k_{\rm T}=k_{1}
and refer to
[
20
]
regarding the
significance of also studying
k_{\rm T}>k_{1}
to obtain full integral kernels in
a parameterization involving integral kernels.

When
\bm{u}
in Equation (
9
) is a solution of the nonlinear Equation (
1
)
with the Lorentz force included, we talk about the quasi-kinematic
test-field method.
This method is nonlinear in the sense that it describes correctly the
modifications of the velocity field in response to the actual magnetic
field in the simulations
[
19
,
43
]
.
However, it is not fully nonlinear in the sense that it does not
include the fluctuating magnetic field from a small-scale dynamo

[
55
,
41
]
.
On the other hand, there are so far no clear cases of practical interest
where the quasi-kinematic method is known to fail; see
[
11
]

for a review.
The only exception is the case where magnetic fluctuations are produced
by applying externally maintained currents to drive the system.
Those cases are mainly of academic interest and not relevant to our
problem at hand.
The success of the quasi-kinematic method lies probably in the fact that
the small-scale dynamo-generated magnetic fields are not well correlated
with the large-scale field.

In the present case, the time-averaged turbulent transport coefficients
depend on
z
.
In addition to plotting the individual components of
\alpha_{ij}

and
\eta_{ij}
, we also compute the traces

\alpha\equiv(\alpha_{xx}+\alpha_{yy})/2
and

\eta_{\rm t}\equiv(\eta_{xx}+\eta_{yy})/2
, as well as the antisymmetric parts,

\gamma\equiv(\alpha_{yx}-\alpha_{xy})/2
and

\delta\equiv(\eta_{xy}-\eta_{yx})/2
.
In the following, we fit
\eta_{\rm t}
to Legendre polynomials of
\cos k_{1}z
.
Since these are orthogonal polynomials, a decline of the coefficients with
increasing order can be interpreted as convergence.
Another quantity of interest is the
z
-dependent dynamo number,

C_{\alpha}=\alpha/\eta_{\rm t}k_{1}
.
In the present case, it turns out that, to a good approximation, it has
a linear profile.
The values and slopes are given in tabular form below.

II.5
Mean-field evolution

To assess the importance of the aforementioned turbulent transport
coefficients
\alpha
,
\eta_{\rm t}
,
\gamma
, and
\delta
, we consider
numerical mean-field models, where we can rescale the coefficients to
learn about their relative importance.
In that case, we solve the one-dimensional mean-field equation, again in the
Weyl gauge,

\frac{\partial{\overline{\bm{A}}}}{\partial t}=\alpha{\overline{\bm{B}}}-\eta_{\rm T}\mu_{0}{\overline{\bm{J}}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}{}\times{\overline{\bm{B}}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}{}\times\mu_{0}{\overline{\bm{J}}}-S\overline{A}_{y}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}{},

(10)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}{}=(0,0,\gamma)
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}{}=(0,0,\delta)
are vectors that
only have a
z
-component and
\eta_{\rm T}=\eta_{\rm t}+\eta
is the total magnetic
diffusivity.
It should be remembered, however, that the values and profiles of the
turbulent transport coefficients have been computed under the assumption
of steady mean fields.
This is obviously not the case; see
[
34
]
for the treatment
of time-dependent mean fields.

II.6
Magnetic helicity fluxes

The saturation level of the resulting mean magnetic field is known to
be severely limited by the ability to shed magnetic helicity from
the dynamo through magnetic helicity fluxes; see
[
78
]

for a recent assessment.
It is therefore of interest to consider the evolution equation
for the magnetic helicity balance separately for the large-scale
and small-scale contributions by splitting the total mean
magnetic density,
\overline{\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}}
, into two parts:

{\overline{\bm{A}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
and
\overline{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}}
.
The evolution equation for
\overline{\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}}
is obtained by
dotting Equation (
3
) with
\bm{B}
and adding its curl dotted with
\bm{A}
,
which yields

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\overline{\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}}=-2\overline{\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}}-{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal F}$}}{},

(11)

where
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal F}$}}{}=\overline{\bm{E}\times\bm{A}}+\overline{\Phi\bm{B}}
is the
total magnetic helicity flux.
Note also that
\overline{\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}}=\eta\nu_{0}\overline{\bm{J}\cdot\bm{B}}
,
i.e., the induction term does not contribute.
The evolution equation for
{\overline{\bm{A}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
is obtained from
the evolution equation for the mean field
\partial{\overline{\bm{A}}}/\partial t=-\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{}-{\bm{\nabla}}\overline{\Phi}
, where

\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{}=\eta\mu_{0}{\overline{\bm{J}}}-\overline{\bm{U}}\times{\overline{\bm{B}}}-\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}

(12)

is the averaged electric field, and
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}=\overline{\bm{u}\times\bm{b}}

is the mean electromotive force from the fluctuating fields.
This yields

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\overline{\bm{A}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}=2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}-2\eta\mu_{0}{\overline{\bm{J}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}-{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal F}$}}{}_{\mathrm{m}},

(13)

where
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal F}$}}{}_{\mathrm{m}}=\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{}\times{\overline{\bm{A}}}+\overline{\Phi}\,{\overline{\bm{B}}}

is the magnetic helicity flux from the mean field.
Note, however, that in our cases
\overline{B}_{z}=0
at all times owing to the
fact that
{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}=0
, the use of planar averages, and the fact
that
\overline{B}_{z}=0
initially.
Therefore,
\overline{\Phi}\,{\overline{\bm{B}}}=0
.
Furthermore, the mean-field shear contribution,
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{}_{\mathrm{S}}
,
to the mean electric field only leads to a lateral magnetic helicity
flux and is therefore irrelevant.

Finally, the evolution equation for
\overline{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}}
is obtained
from the difference
\overline{\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}}-{\overline{\bm{A}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
,
which yields

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\overline{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}}=-2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}-2\eta\mu_{0}\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}-{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal F}$}}{}_{\mathrm{f}},

(14)

where
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal F}$}}{}_{\mathrm{f}}=\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}{}\times\bm{a}}+\overline{\phi\bm{b}}

is the magnetic helicity flux of the fluctuating field,
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}{}=\bm{E}-\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{}

is the fluctuating electric field, and
\phi=V_{y}a_{y}-\eta{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{a}
.
Contrary to
[
10
]
, we use here the more natural and more compact
notation where
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}
is included in the definition of
\bm{E}
;
see Equation (
12
).

In the statistically steady state, we can drop the time derivative.
Instead of considering volume-integrated quantities separately for the
northern and southern hemispheres, it is convenient to plot them as
fluxes in terms of undetermined integrals,

\int_{z_{-}}^{z}2\eta\mu_{0}{\overline{\bm{J}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}\,{\rm d}{}z=+\int_{z_{-}}^{z}2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}\,{\rm d}{}z-\overline{\cal F}_{\mathrm{m}z},

(15)

\int_{z_{-}}^{z}2\eta\mu_{0}\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}\,{\rm d}{}z=-\int_{z_{-}}^{z}2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}\,{\rm d}{}z-\overline{\cal F}_{\mathrm{f}z},

(16)

in the range
z_{-}\leq z\leq z_{+}
, where
z_{\pm}=\pm\pi/k_{1}
are the upper
and lower boundaries of the cube.
In the following, we refer to these as “integrated terms.”

Note that in both Equations (
15
) and (
16
) there are three terms of which
two are manifestly gauge invariant.
Therefore, the third term also,
\overline{\cal F}_{\mathrm{m}z}
and
\overline{\cal F}_{\mathrm{m}z}
in each
equation, respectively, must be gauge invariant.
This argument was already applied by
[
35
]
in their work on magnetic
helicity fluxes from a dynamo embedded in a conducting halo.

It is convenient to present the magnetic helicity fluxes in normalized form.
For the following, we define the reference flux as

F_{\mathrm{m0}}=\eta_{\rm t0}k_{1}\int_{z_{-}}^{z_{+}}{\overline{\bm{B}}}^{2}\,{\rm d}{}z.

(17)

This is analogous to the work of
[
10
]
, except that there

k_{1}^{2}
instead of
k_{1}
was written by mistake.

To compare the current helicity with the kinetic helicity, we define

\epsilon_{\rm f}^{\mathrm{M}}(z)=\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}/u_{\rm rms}^{2}k_{\rm f}
and its amplitude
as
\epsilon_{\rm f0}^{\mathrm{M}}=2\left\langle\epsilon_{\rm f}^{\mathrm{M}}(z)\sin k_{1}z\right\rangle
.
Finally, the ratio between the small-scale current and
magnetic helicity densities is characterized by the ratio

k_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}=\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}/\overline{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}}
,
where the two terms have been computed from a sinusoidal fit, analogously
to
\epsilon_{\rm f0}^{\mathrm{M}}
.
These quantities are discussed in Section
III
.
It should be noted, however, that the departure of
k_{\mathrm{eff}}
from
the value of
k_{\rm f}
is mainly a measure of the departure of the magnetic
vector potential from the Coulomb gauge, because the term
{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{A}

in the expression for
\bm{J}=-\nabla^{2}\bm{A}+{\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{A}
can be
important.
By contrast, the ratio
\overline{(-\nabla^{2}\bm{a})\cdot\bm{b}}/\overline{\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}}
is typically found to be close to the actual
value of
k_{\rm f}=8\,k_{1}
, even when the Coulomb gauge is not used.

This can still be important, especially at small scales, and therefore

II.7
Magnetic helicity cycle

In this section, we explain that there is a continuous flux of magnetic
helicity both between hemispheres and between scales.
This is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Owing to the presence of rotation and a finite gradient in the turbulent
intensity, an
\alpha
effect is expected based on the formula by

[
67
]
,

\alpha\approx-\ell^{2}\bm{\Omega}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\ln(\rho u_{\rm rms}),

(18)

where
\ell
is a suitable length scale.
We refer here to
[
61
]
for analytical calculations based on the
consideration of homogeneous background turbulence that is being affected
by stratification and rotation, and
[
14
]
for simulation results
over a broad range of astrophysical settings.
Equation (
18
) predicts a positive (negative) value of
\alpha

in the upper (lower) disk plane.
This, in turn, implies negative (positive) kinetic helicities of the
small-scale velocity and magnetic fields in the upper (lower) disk plane.
It is known that, at least in the absence of shear, the magnetic helicity
of the small-scale field is then also negative (positive) in the upper
(lower) disk plane
[
27
,
57
]
.
Assuming that small-scale magnetic helicity is transported down the
gradient of the magnetic helicity density and/or magnetic energy density

[
45
]
, we expect a small-scale magnetic helicity flux from south
to north; see the dashed line in Figure
1
.
It is shown here as a dashed line, because in our simulations without
shear this flux appears to be too weak, while in our runs with shear
it appears to be too large to balance the corresponding fluxes at
large scales.

There is also a magnetic helicity flux from small to large scales,
which is given by the integral of
2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
.
This term has a contribution
\alpha{\overline{\bm{B}}}^{2}
, which is positive in
the north, but since it enters with a minus sign, the associated flux
points from small to large scales, and in the opposite direction in the
south where
\alpha
is negative; see Figure
1
.

III
Results

We have performed a series of runs with different parameters; see
Table
1
for a summary.
In all cases without shear, we use
256^{3}
mesh points and keep the
viscosity fixed (
\nu k_{1}/c_{\rm s}=5\times 10^{-4}
), so the level of turbulence
stays unchanged but
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
is increased by decreasing the value of
\eta
.
This makes the dynamo stronger, so
B_{\rm rms}/B_{\rm eq}
increases.
This quenches the velocity field, and therefore the values of
Re

decrease with increasing values of
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}
.
We notice, however, that as
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}
and
B_{\rm rms}/B_{\rm eq}
increase, the
normalized rms value of the mean field,
\overline{B}_{\rm rms}/B_{\rm eq}
, decreases.
As already emphasized above, this is at the core of the problem of
catastrophic quenching.

For Runs A–E, we also have obtained test-field results,
while for Runs F–H, we have focussed on the analysis of
the contributions to the magnetic helicity balance.
In some of those runs, we also increased the resolution.
Those runs have uniform shear with
q=0.5
.
This choice is motivated by demanding that
|\bm{V}|/c_{\rm s}
does not exceed unity.
Given that the value
q=3/2
is of particular interest for accretion
disks, we also consider one such case using then, however, a correspondingly
larger sound speed of
c_{\rm s}=3
so that
|\bm{V}|/c_{\rm s}
is unchanged.
The values of
Re
and
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
are given for the saturated state.

III.1
Dynamos without shear

For all the cases in Table
1
, there is dynamo action.
The resulting magnetic field has spatiotemporal coherence with
a systematic large-scale magnetic field oscillation on a time
scale long compared with the turnover time of the turbulence.
As noted above, however, although
B_{\rm rms}
is seen to increase with
increasing magnetic Reynolds number,
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}=\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}\mbox{\rm Re}
, the rms magnetic
field contained in the mean field,
\overline{B}_{\rm rms}
, is seen to decrease.

In Figure
2
, we show
zt
diagrams, also known
as butterfly diagrams, for the two relevant components of
{\overline{\bm{B}}}

for Run D with
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}=10
.
We see migration of the magnetic field away from the boundaries at

k_{1}z_{\pm}=\pm\pi
.
This is expected for
\alpha^{2}
dynamos with a nonuniform distribution
of
\alpha

[
68
,
25
]
, or even for a uniform

\alpha
, but with different boundary conditions on two opposite ends;
see
[
9
]
.

III.2
Turbulent transport coefficients

To identify the nature of the large-scale dynamo seen above, it is
useful to compute the turbulent transport coefficients for the horizontal
averages applied in this study.
All our test-field runs have
\nu=5\times 10^{-4}c_{\rm s}/k_{1}
.
We adopt the expansion

\eta_{\rm t}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{3}\eta_{\rm t}^{(\ell)}P_{\ell}(\cos k_{1}z)
.
In the range
k_{1}|z|\leq 2.5
, we determine a linear fit to

C_{\alpha}(z)\approx\alpha/\eta_{\rm t}k_{1}
.
For larger values of
|z|
,
C_{\alpha}
varies no longer linearly, so
this part is ignored in the fit.

In Figure
3
, we show time-averaged profiles of
\alpha(z)
,

\gamma(z)
,
\eta_{\rm t}(z)
, and
\delta(z)
for Run D with
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}=10
.
For normalization purposes, we adopt the following estimates
for the
\alpha
effect and the turbulent magnetic diffusivity:

\alpha_{0}=-\epsilon_{\rm f0}u_{\rm rms}/3,\quad\eta_{\rm t0}=u_{\rm rms}/3k_{\rm f}.

(19)

Note that in all our simulations (both with and without shear)
\alpha_{0}

is negative.
In Figure
3
, the ratio
\alpha/\eta_{\rm t}k_{1}
shows local
extrema at
k_{1}z=\pm 2
of about
\pm 5
, but has here a more linear profile
as a function of
z
than for Run A.
The profile
\gamma(z)
quantifies turbulent pumping.
It is negative in the southern hemisphere and positive in the northern
hemisphere.
Since
\gamma
plays the role of an advection vector (albeit without
any material motion), this corresponds to magnetic field pumping away
from the midplane; see
[
65
]
for a review.
The profile
\delta(z)
describes the
\bm{\Omega}\times{\overline{\bm{J}}}
or Rädler
effect
[
54
]
, which is known to contribute to dynamo action,
although this term alone cannot contribute to a change in
{\overline{\bm{B}}}^{2}
.
The sign of
\delta
is here as expected from theory, and it also
agrees with earlier test-field results
[
18
]
.

For the corresponding results for Run A with
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}=0.2
, the ratio

\alpha/\eta_{\rm t}k_{1}
again shows local extrema at
k_{1}z=\pm 2
of about

\pm 5
.

we have solved the relevant mean-field model with Equation (

seen before; see

for simulations in spherical geometry,

III.4
Interpretation of magnetic helicity fluxes

We are interested in the magnetic helicity flux between the northern
and southern hemispheres.
It is convenient to plot the magnetic helicity flux through any
xy

plane as a function of
z
.
In Figure

, we see that for Run D most of the
total magnetic helicity flux is contained in the large-scale contribution,

\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{}\times{\overline{\bm{A}}}
.
The small-scale magnetic helicity flux is nearly negligible.
The large-scale component is nearly balanced by the fluxes

2\int_{-\pi}^{0}\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}\,{\rm d}{}z
and

2\int_{0}^{\pi}\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}\,{\rm d}{}z
between different scales in
the southern and northern hemispheres.
At small scales, however, almost the entire flux is absorbed by the
ohmic diffusion term
2\eta\mu_{0}\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}
, which was also
found for most of the earlier work
[
27
,
57
]
, when
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}

was not yet very large.

Butterfly diagrams for
\overline{B}_{x}
(left) and
\overline{B}_{y}
(right)
for Run E with shear and
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}=10
.

For the much more diffusive Run A with
\mbox{\rm Pr}_{\rm M}=0.2
, the situation
is a bit different; see Figure

.
Here, the integrated
2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
term is still
balanced by the integrated
-2\eta\mu_{0}\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}

term, but now there is also a significant contribution from the
integrated
2\eta\mu_{0}{\overline{\bm{J}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
term, which balances

\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{}\times{\overline{\bm{A}}}
.
Looking at Figure
1
, this means that the microphysical
magnetic diffusivity is important not only at small scales, when the
integrated
2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
term is entirely supplied by the
integrated
-2\eta\mu_{0}\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}
term and not much by the

\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}{}\times\bm{a}}
or integrated
\overline{\phi\bm{b}}
terms, but
also at large scales, when the integrated
2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
term
is supplied to 50% by the integrated
\eta\nu_{0}\overline{\bm{J}\cdot\bm{B}}

term and to another 50% by

III.5
The effect of shear

We now consider models with finite shear using
q=0.5
(so
V_{y}=-q\Omega x
).
This value is less than what is used to model Keplerian shear,
where
q=3/2
.
Nevertheless, one such case will be considered in Section
III.7
.
As emphasized above, this is because we want to avoid supersonic speeds
on the shearing boundaries at
x=\pm\pi
.
In the present case with
\Omega/c_{\rm s}k_{1}=0.5
and
q=0.5
, we have

V_{y}(\pm\pi)=\mp 0.8c_{\rm s}
.
Run E is an example of a model with shear.
The fluxes for this and a few other runs with shear are summarized in
Table
3

III.6
Superequipartition with shear

The reason why our runs with shear show strong small-scale magnetic
helicity fluxes is probably connected with the fact that in those runs,
the magnetic field reaches superequipartition strengths.
This is seen in Table
3
, where
B_{\rm rms}/B_{\rm eq}>1
, and in
Figure
12
, where we plot for Run E the
temporal evolution of
\langle\bm{B}^{2}\rangle
,
B_{\rm eq}^{2}=\mu_{0}\rho_{0}\,u_{\rm rms}^{2}
,

\langle B_{y}^{2}\rangle
, and
\langle B_{x}^{2}\rangle
.
We see that the total (small-scale and large-scale) magnetic field
reaches superequipartition field strengths at
t\approx 300/c_{\rm s}k_{1}
,
which is clearly before the large-scale magnetic field saturates at

t\approx 1000/c_{\rm s}k_{1}
, which is when
\langle B_{y}^{2}\rangle
has reached a
statistically steady state.
Interestingly, the large-scale magnetic field displays an approximately
exponential growth at a rate
10^{-2}\,u_{\rm rms}k_{\rm f}
for the squared
mean-field strength.
The growth rate of the mean field is then half that value, which is
comparable to the growth rates found in Table
2
, where

\lambda/c_{\rm s}k_{1}\approx 0.005
, corresponding to

\lambda/u_{\rm rms}k_{\rm f}\approx 0.01
.

The slow exponential growth of the mean field may hint at a new type
of instability that is responsible for the emergence of the large-scale
magnetic field in a regime in which the total magnetic field reaches
superequipartition strengths.
Analogous evidence for exponential growth of a secondary mean-field
instability has previously been seen in other circumstances; see

[
15
]
for an example in the context of density-stratified
turbulence in which large-scale structures were found to form.

In the shear-induced superequipartition regime, the rms values of the
resulting large-scale magnetic field are found to be independent of
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
.
In the absence of shear,
\overline{B}_{\rm rms}
clearly declined with
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
, albeit
only like
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}^{0.3}
; see Figure
13
(a).
With shear, however,
\overline{B}_{\rm rms}
is nearly independent of
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
; see
Figure
13
(b).
The fact that resistive contributions through the integrated

2\eta\mu_{0}\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}
term become increasingly important can
be regarded as a consequence of the shear-induced hemispheric magnetic
helicity fluxes that become extremely efficient at exchanging small-scale
magnetic helicity between hemispheres.
We can therefore say that they overcome catastrophic quenching so as to
guarantee an
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
-independent large-scale magnetic field.

The strong contribution of the small-scale current helicity term might
raise concerns whether the simulation is sufficiently well resolved.
To check this, we plot in Figure
14
an
xy

slice of
J_{x}
through
k_{1}z=1
, but no signs of ringing, i.e.,
no oscillations on the grid scale are seen.
Instead, it shows the typical inclined patterns associated with the
shear flow,
\overline{U}_{y}(x)=-q\Omega x
.

III.7
Sensitivity to a steeper profile and larger shear

As alluded to in Section
II.2
, we adopted a sinusoidal modulation
for the turbulent intensity.
To get an idea about the sensitivity of our results upon this choice,
we now consider the top-hat profile defined in Section
II.2
.
The result for Run Q is shown in
Figure
16
.
The differences to Run E in Figure
8
are
relatively minor, except that the flanks of
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}{}\times\bm{a}}

at
z=\pm\pi/2
show a sharper onset at
z=\pm\pi/2
.
Also, the profile of the integrated
2\eta\mu_{0}\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}
term
shows marked humps at these positions.

Another particular choice was that of the value of
q
, which corresponds
to the local double-logarithmic shear derivative in other astrophysical
contexts.
In astrophysical bodies such as stars, the value of
q
can have either
sign in different regions.
Values in the range from
\pm 0.1
to
\pm 1
are not uncommon.
In galactic disks with a constant linear velocity, we have
q=1
,
whereas in Keplerian disks, we have
q=3/2
.
As discussed in Section
II.2
, with our choice of angular velocity
and domain size, our compressible simulations yield transonic velocities
for
q>0.5
.
To assess the sensitivity of our results upon the choice
q=0.5
, we
now consider a simulation similar to Run E, but with
q=3/2
and
a larger sound speed of
c_{\rm s}=3
.
The result is listed in Table
3
as Run R.
The values of
Re
and
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
are larger than for Run E, because we
left the viscosity and magnetic diffusivity unchanged.
The kinetic helicity parameter is now nearly vanishing, but the current
helicity parameter remains unchanged compared to that for Run E.
All the other parameters describing the mean magnetic field and magnetic
helicity fluxes are also nearly unchanged.
We therefore conclude that our particular choice of the value of
q

had no decisive effect on the results.

IV
Conclusions

In this paper, we have considered a volume containing a modulated level
of turbulence, which drops to zero at the vertical boundaries, so that
the internal transport coefficients of the medium
go to zero smoothly at those boundaries.
Consequently, the results of the simulations should be insensitive to
the vertical boundary conditions.
The properties of the medium, including rotation and shear, are otherwise
uniform within the volume.

The existence of a systematic flux of small-scale
helicity, as seen in Figure
8
, is consistent
with a simple dimensional estimate.
A magnetic helicity flux arising from turbulence should scale with

\overline{\bm{u}^{2}}\overline{\bm{B}^{2}}\tau
, where
\tau
is a correlation
time
[
45
]
.
However, the magnetic helicity flux is a pseudo-vector, with a direction
that requires a scaling with either the rotation or the shear (or more
particularly with the local vorticity), which implies an extra factor
of
\Omega\tau
or
S\tau
.
For a uniform driving scale of the turbulence, and a uniform shear and
rotation, we expect a magnetic helicity flux proportional to

\overline{\bm{B}^{2}}
, which is consistent with the evidence in case E.
Our results suggest that this flux is significantly more sensitive to shear than to rotation.

We have shown that in a large-scale dynamo, the magnetic helicity
fluxes between large and small scales can even become overcompensated by
those between the two hemispheres when microphysical resistive effects
are small.
In the absence of shear and at small magnetic Reynolds numbers (Run A,

\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}=3.5
), these fluxes are comparable to the reference flux defined
in Equation (
17
).
For Run D with
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}=160
, the magnetic helicity fluxes are about 30%
of the reference flux.
However, while the magnetic helicity flux at large scales
is large, that at small scales is virtually absent; see
Figures
4
and
5
.
In the presence of shear and for similar values of
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
(Run E),
the flux drops to 1–5% of the reference flux, but now the fluxes are
approximately the same at large and small scales.

The correspondence between the magnetic helicity flux between hemispheres
and between scales is not a coincidence.
The small-scale flux between hemispheres depends on the total magnetic
field strength, although the small- and large-scale magnetic fields
contribute to the flux with different coefficients.
The flow of magnetic helicity between scales is proportional to the
square of the large-scale field.
Consequently, the saturation strength of the large-scale field may be
set by the magnetic helicity flux between hemispheres.

While the large fraction of small-scale magnetic helicity fluxes in the
presence of shear is indicative of alleviating catastrophic quenching,
we do not find that the resistive term becomes unimportant at large
magnetic Reynolds numbers.
This does not a priori mean that such dynamos are not viable in the
large-
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
regime.
The fact that
2\int\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}\,{\rm d}{}z\to 0
in the large magnetic
Reynolds number limit was thought to reflect the basic catastrophic
quenching problem of large-scale dynamos with helicity.
However, while the hemispheric small-scale magnetic helicity flux
stays constant as
\mbox{\rm Re}_{\rm M}
increases, it is not being used to balance the
integrated
2\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}\cdot{\overline{\bm{B}}}
term, but it either drives or is
driven by the integrated
2\eta\mu_{0}\overline{\bm{j}\cdot\bm{b}}
term.

Our models have demonstrated that interesting flux dynamics can occur
entirely without boundaries.
The magnetic helicity fluxes occur within the volume due to gradients
of the turbulent intensity and turbulent kinetic and magnetic helicities,
as was also found by
[
45
]
.

Our work has also shown that the dynamos in the present setups are of

\alpha^{2}
or
\alpha\Omega
type, i.e., they are driven by an

\alpha
effect and supported by shear, if shear is present.
The ratio of the local value of
\alpha
to the product of the local
turbulent magnetic diffusivity and the lowest wavenumber of the
domain, i.e., the
z
-dependent dynamo number, which is found to be
an approximately linear function with a coefficient of 2.8 and 2.2 for
Runs A and D, respectively.
This suggests that the dynamo number decreases with increasing conductivity.
For Run E with shear, the ratio is 1.4.
Turbulent pumping points in the direction away from the midplane.
There is also a Rädler effect with the theoretically expected sign,
i.e.,
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal E}$}}{}
has a contribution proportional to
\bm{\Omega}\times{\overline{\bm{J}}}

with a positive coefficient.
In the presence of shear, the effect becomes anisotropic and the
component that is relevant for the shear–current effect, namely the

\eta_{yx}
component for shear of the present form
S={\rm d}{}\overline{U}_{y}/{\rm d}{}x
,
is essentially zero, which is consistent with earlier findings

[
18
,
41
]
.

Our results have applications to stellar and galactic dynamos, where
gradients of kinetic and magnetic helicity fluxes are expected to occur
through the equator.
Even the boundary between convecting and nonconvecting regions both
in late-type stars and in massive stars is an example where magnetic
helicity fluxes can be expected to encounter dynamical boundaries of the type idealized here.
