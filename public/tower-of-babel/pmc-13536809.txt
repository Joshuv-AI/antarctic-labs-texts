Spatial non-stationarity in son preference: a district-level geographically weighted regression analysis of NFHS-5 in India
Authors: Soumen Barik, Dewaram A Nagdeve, Anuj Singh, Mayank Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSon preference remains a key driver of gender inequality in India, yet most studies treat its determinants as uniform across space, obscuring critical subnational variation. Addressing this gap, this study investigates the geographic heterogeneity of son preference and examines how its predictors vary spatially.Data and methods Using district-level data on 102,045 ever married women aged 15–49 from the National Family Health Survey-5 (2019–2021), we applied a spatially explicit analytical framework, including choropleth mapping, Global Moran’s I, hotspot analysis, Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) cluster mapping, kriging interpolation, and Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR). The use of GWR was justified by significant spatial non-stationarity (Koenker BP = 32.78, p < 0.001) detected in OLS diagnostics.ResultsSon preference prevalence ranged 8.2%-42.3%. Global Moran’s I (0.397, z = 57.86, p < 0.001) confirmed significant clustering. OLS identified five significant predictors: parity 2, no mass media exposure, household size 5–8, illiterate mothers, and younger maternal age, explaining 64% variance. GWR demonstrated superior fit (AICc: 4459.98 vs 4478.46; adjusted R2: 0.65 vs 0.64). Local R2 ranged 0.45–0.71, highest in northern/central districts. Women’s illiteracy (β: 0.40–0.62), large household size (β: 0.35–0.83), and younger mothers showed strongest associations in northern/central India, but negligible effects in southern regions, confirming spatial heterogeneity.ConclusionSon preference is a spatially embedded social process, shaped by localized patriarchy, economy, and institutions. Geographically targeted, gender-transformative policies such as conditional incentives for girls’ schooling and localized media campaigns effectively address the structural devaluation of daughters.Keywords: Son preference, Spatial analysis, GWR, Patriarchy, India, NFHS-5

Introduction

The preference for sons over daughters is a deeply entrenched demographic and social phenomenon with pronounced spatial concentration, particularly across South and Southeast Asia [1]. Far from being uniformly distributed, son preference varies markedly across regions, reflecting localized configurations of culture, economy, and institutional norms. Son preference remains a persistent manifestation of gender inequality. It operates through discriminatory reproductive behaviours, including sex-selective practices and differential care, contributing to imbalances in child sex composition [2–4]. Wherein sons are systematically accorded greater value and investment than daughters [5]. Surname inheritance, expectations of old-age support, and the perceived social value of male offspring form the core of the boy-utility framework underlying son preference. Historical evidence demonstrates its persistence across regions, from succession rules in royal lineages to land inheritance practices in agrarian households, where lineage continuity and property transmission are tied to male descent.[6, 7]. The persistence of this bias is sustained by intersecting socioeconomic, cultural, and institutional forces [8]. In South Asia, for instance, daughters are often perceived as an economic liability due to the dowry system, which obliges the bride’s family to transfer substantial assets at marriage [9, 10]. Additionally, region-specific religious beliefs concerning the afterlife particularly the expectation that sons perform ancestral rites to ensure familial and spiritual continuity further reinforce the preference for male offspring [11, 12].

With the advent of industrialization and urbanization, patterns of son preference have undergone significant transformation across regions [13]. In many Western countries, son preference has largely disappeared, reflecting shifts toward gender-egalitarian norms and institutional support for women [14]. In settings where son preference was once deeply entrenched, evidence indicates a shift toward more gender-balanced child preferences. For example, the share of Taiwanese women expressing a preference for more sons than daughters declined markedly from 48% in 1973 to 12% in 2002, reflecting a substantial shift in gender norms, while in South Korea the sex ratio at birth (SRB) defined as the number of male births per 100 female births fell from a peak of 116.5 in 1990 to near-biological normal levels (around 105) by 2013 [12, 15].

Son preference is an attitudinal and normative orientation favoring male offspring, whereas the SRB is a downstream demographic outcome that emerges only when this preference intersects with access to prenatal sex-determination technology and contracting family size norms [11, 16]. In contrast, India presents a more complex trajectory. While son preference as a social norm has shown gradual change, the SRB has remained elevated. Recent estimates suggest that the Sex Ratio at Birth (SRB) in India remains outside the natural range, standing at 917 females per 1000 males during 2021–23. Despite an overall downward trend, son preference continues to manifest through distorted sex ratios at birth, underscoring its resilience as a structural and normative force [12, 17, 18]. Still, son preference remains a critical social issue in several parts of the India, as Generation Z and subsequent cohorts enter the marriage market they face as persistent female deficits, marriage squeeze, and lifelong bachelorhood. These imbalances elevate marriage costs, destabilize family structures, distort labour markets, constrain women’s employment opportunities, and increase vulnerability to trafficking and fraudulent marriages [19]. [20].

The earlier conducted research has firmly established son preference as a key driver of sex-selective abortion, resulting in persistently skewed population sex ratios in societies where male-biased norms are deeply rooted [21–25]. As consensus has emerged regarding the link between son preference and distorted sex ratios, recent scholarship has shifted attention toward the subtler, yet equally consequential, forms of discrimination experienced by surviving girls [26, 27]. Using advanced statistical techniques, studies demonstrate that parental son preference translates into unequal allocation of critical household resources including breastfeeding duration, nutrient intake, healthcare utilization, and parental time systematically disadvantaging daughters [28–31]. Importantly, these discriminatory practices are not spatially random,they cluster across regions characterized by entrenched patriarchy, socioeconomic deprivation, and limited access to public services, underscoring the need for geographically explicit analyses that move beyond national averages to uncover the spatial determinants shaping gendered child outcomes.

Existing literature conceptualizes son preference as emerging from the interaction of institutional structures, economic incentives, and cultural norms, rather than as independent factors [7, 11, 16, 32]. Institutional arrangements such as patrilineal inheritance and gendered property rights, prevalent in India and many Muslim-majority societies, create structural constraints that privilege sons in the intergenerational transfer of assets [7, 33]. These institutional constraints generate economic incentives, particularly in agrarian contexts where male labor yields higher returns and sons are expected to provide old-age support [16, 24]. Crucially, these structural and economic factors are reinforced and legitimized through cultural and religious norms, including patrilocal residence, marital exogamy, and the expectation that sons perform funeral and ancestral rites, especially among Hindus in India further entrench son preference by providing the normative legitimacy that sustains these economic and institutional incentives [11, 16, 32].

While the institutional, economic, and cultural drivers of son preference are well documented, most studies assume these effects are spatially uniform, overlooking India’s significant regional diversity. In reality, the strength and direction of key predictors vary across space—factors that intensify son preference in one region may be weak or even reversed in another. Existing research, largely based on state- or national-level analyses, masks district-level clustering and context-specific dynamics [6, 34, 35]. This gap is critical in India, where gender norms, kinship systems, and development trajectories differ sharply. Northern, central, and western regions characterized by patrilineal systems, lower female autonomy, and agrarian economies show stronger associations between son preference and predictor. In contrast, southern and northeastern regions, with higher female literacy and more flexible social structures, exhibit weaker or negative associations [36–38]. By using a geographically explicit, district-level approach, this study captures spatial non-stationarity in son preference and its determinants. The findings highlight that son preference is not uniform but context-specific, underscoring the need for targeted, place-based policy interventions.

Methods and materials

Data source

The study uses data from the National Family Health Survey–5 (NFHS-5, 2019–2021), a nationally representative household survey conducted across all Indian states and union territories. NFHS-5 provides detailed information on women’s reproductive behavior, fertility intentions, household characteristics, and socio-demographic conditions. The survey follows a stratified, two-stage sampling design and is representative at national, state, and district levels. The analysis focuses on ever married women aged 15–49 years, from whom information on ideal sex composition of children and fertility preferences was collected. All women’s responses were weighted using the DHS sampling weight (V005), rescaled by dividing by 1,000,000 to maintain consistency across districts. District shape files for spatial analysis were obtained from the Survey of India and aligned with the NFHS administrative boundaries.

Conceptual framework

Figure 1 illustrates the conceptual framework, positioning son preference as a spatially embedded outcome shaped by structural patriarchy, regional kinship systems, and localized economic incentives favoring male offspring. macro-level determinants shape proximate fertility behaviors including parity-specific stopping rules and sex-composition targets which manifest as geographically clustered patterns of son-biased norms [36–38]. Individual-level factors, such as maternal education, mass media exposure, household wealth, and age at marriage, operate as contextual moderators that either attenuate or amplify son preference depending on regional institutional arrangements [16, 24].

Study sample and selection criteria

Data for this cross-sectional analysis were derived from the National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5), conducted across India during 2019–2021. As depicted in Fig. 2, a sequential exclusion criterion was applied to arrive at the final analytical sample. From the total cohort of 724,115 women interviewed, we excluded 181,285 women who had never been in a union, as fertility preference measures are most relevant within marital contexts, yielding a sample of 542,830 ever-married women. Subsequently, 6,034 mothers who reported uncertainty regarding their ideal number of children were excluded to ensure response validity, resulting in 536,796 mothers with definitive fertility preferences as the final analytical sample used for the analysis. Furthermore, son preference is defined as an ideal number of boys exceeding the ideal number of girls, was observed among 102,045 mothers (17.43%), while 440,785 mothers (82.57%) did not exhibit son preference.

Flowchart of sample selection process for analysis of son preference among reproductive aged ever married women

Outcome variable: In NFHS, the ideal number of sons and daughters reported by women aged 15–49. A respondent is classified as son preference if she reports an ideal number of sons greater than the ideal number of daughters [39, 40]. A binary indicator was constructed as follows: 1 = Son preference (ideal number of boys > ideal number of girls) and, 0 = No son preference (ideal number of boys ≤ ideal number of girls).

Explanatory variable: A comprehensive set of demographic and socioeconomic variables widely documented as predictors of gender preference, fertility behaviour, and reproductive decision-making were included as explanatory factors. The relevance of these covariates is well established in prior research on gender norms, fertility intentions, and son preference [16, 17, 32, 39, 41, 42]. Studies have shown that educational attainment [32], maternal nutritional status, early marriage and early childbearing, exposure to media and family planning messages [43], and parity, especially the number of living daughters [16] are strongly associated with gender-based fertility preferences and reproductive decision-making. Likewise, socioeconomic and cultural dimensions such as caste, religion, wealth, and regional location have been shown to shape gender norms and attitudes toward son preference in India [22, 40, 44]. Statistical analysis

Descriptive and bivariate analyses: Univariate analysis was used to summarize district-level prevalence of son preference and the distribution of explanatory variables. Bivariate analyses examined associations between each explanatory variable and the outcome using Pearson’s chi-square test, which evaluates departures from independence between categorical variables. These analyses provide insights into the socioeconomic gradients of son preference and help identify patterns that warrant deeper spatial investigation.

Spatial analysis

A. Mapping of district level son preference: choropleth maps display the spatial distribution of son preference. district-level proportions were calculated and plotted using GIS software to identify broad geographic patterns. All individual-level variables were aggregated to the district level using sampling weights to derive district-level proportions, and both OLS and GWR models were fitted at the district level using district centroids; thus, coefficients represent ecological (area-level) associations rather than individual-level effects.

B. Global spatial autocorrelation analysis: Global spatial autocorrelation was conducted to determine whether the spatial distribution of son preference across districts in India was dispersed, clustered, or randomly distributed. Global Moran’s I is a spatial statistic used to measure spatial autocorrelation by analyzing the entire dataset and producing a single output value ranging from − 1 to + 1. A Moran’s I value close to − 1 indicates that son preference among districts is dispersed, while a value near + 1 signifies clustering. A Moran’s I value close to 0 suggests that son preference is randomly distributed across geographic space [45, 46].

C. Hotspot analysis (Getis–Ord Gi*): To examine spatial variations in clustering across the study area, a hotspot analysis was performed using the Getis–Ord Gi* statistic. Which identifies hotspots and cold spots by comparing local means against the global distribution. This method is widely used in demographic and public health studies to detect localized clustering of social indicators [47]

A high positive z-score indicates a hotspot (high clustering of high values), while a low negative z-score indicates a cold spot (high clustering of low values). Statistical significance is determined using associated p-values.

D. Local indicators of spatial association (LISA): LISA based on local Moran’s I were employed to detect statistically significant district-level clusters and spatial outliers of son preference. Using a queen-contiguity weights matrix and 999 permutations, significant local associations were classified into four spatial regimes: high–high clusters (hotspots of elevated prevalence), low–low clusters (coldspots of low prevalence), and high–low/low–high spatial outliers, with non-significant districts indicating no local spatial dependence. This approach enabled the identification of geographically concentrated pockets of son-preferring norms and contextual anomalies across India’s districts.

D. Spatial regression analysis: To examine the relationship between son preference and potential predictor variables, a spatial regression analysis was conducted using the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method and Geographically Weighted Regression. OLS regression was applied to assess how various socioeconomic, demographic, and household factors influence son preference prevalence across Indian districts. The model is specified as: Y = β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + … + βₙXₙ + ε; where: Y = dependent variable (son preference); β₀ = intercept; β₁, β₂…βₙ = regression coefficients; X₁, X₂…Xₙ = explanatory variables; ε = residual term. The OLS model relies on key assumptions: explanatory variables should exhibit anticipated directional relationships and statistical significance; residuals should not exhibit spatial clustering, tested using Global Moran’s I; and residuals should follow a normal distribution, assessed via the Jarque–Bera test. Prior to fitting the GWR model, multicollinearity among explanatory variables was evaluated using Variance Inflation Factors in the OLS framework, with all values falling below the threshold of 5, indicating no serious multicollinearity. Since GWR inherits variables from the OLS model without introducing new predictors, this assessment was considered sufficient for both models. Finally, model performance was evaluated using adjusted R2 and the corrected Akaike Information Criterion to compare global versus locally varying specifications.

E. Geographically weighted regression (GWR): OLS regression was applied to assess how various socioeconomic, demographic, and household factors influence son preference prevalence across Indian districts. Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) was used to model spatially varying relationships between son preference and district-level covariates. Unlike global regression models, GWR allows coefficients to vary across geographic locations, accounting for local context and heterogeneity in social norms. Result

Table 2 presents bivariate associations between son preference and maternal background characteristics. Son preference significantly increased with advancing maternal age, rising from 13.25% among ever married women aged 15–24 years to 19.93% among those aged ≥ 35 years (p < 0.001). A strong inverse gradient was observed with education, with prevalence highest among illiterate women (27.65%) and lowest among those with higher education (8.68%). Early marriage (< 18 years), higher parity, absence of mass media exposure, and lower wealth quintiles were all significantly associated with elevated son preference (p < 0.001). Rural residents exhibited higher prevalence (19.58%) compared to urban women (12.76%). Marked regional heterogeneity was evident, with the Central region showing the highest prevalence (24.57%) and the Southern region the lowest (10.13%). These findings underscore significant socioeconomic and geographic disparities in son preference across India.

Figure 3 shows geographic distribution of son preference in India at the district level using NFHS-5 data-set. The majority of districts fall within the 10–20% range (281 districts), indicating moderate prevalence nationally. High-prevalence clusters (30–40% and 40–50%) appear prominently in parts of northern and central India, particularly across Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, and some pockets of Jharkhand, West Bengal, Rajasthan, Haryana and Ladakh and Jammu. Several districts in these regions exceed 40%, representing the strongest concentration of son-preferring norms. In contrast, southern and western states including Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Maharashtra show predominantly low levels of son preference, with many districts falling below 10%. Eastern and northeastern regions exhibit mixed patterns, with low to moderate prevalence of son preference.

Geographic distribution of son preference in India at the district level (NFHS-5, 2019–2021)

Figure 4 presents the results of the Global Moran’s I spatial autocorrelation analysis for son preference across the study area. The Moran’s I value of 0.397 indicates a moderate positive spatial autocorrelation, suggesting that districts with similar levels of son preference tend to be geographically clustered rather than randomly distributed. The z-score of 57.86 is extremely high and lies in the right tail of the normal distribution, while the p-value (< 0.001) confirms that the observed spatial pattern is statistically significant and unlikely to have occurred by chance. This indicates the presence of significant spatial clustering, where districts with high son preference are located near other high-value districts and low-value districts tend to cluster together. The significant clustering pattern suggests that spatial processes and regional socio-cultural factors influence the distribution of son preference, supporting the use of further spatial analytical techniques such as Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) and Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) to examine local spatial patterns and spatially varying relationships.

District-level spatial autocorrelation of son preference

Figure 5 presents the spatial clustering of son preference across Indian districts using the Getis–Ord Gi* hotspot analysis. The results reveal statistically significant geographic clustering, with distinct hotspots and coldspots observed across regions. High-value clusters (hotspots), significant at the 95% and 99% confidence levels, are predominantly concentrated in the central and eastern regions of India, particularly across Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Jharkhand, parts of Madhya Pradesh, and West Bengal. These areas exhibit consistently higher levels of son preference and are surrounded by districts with similarly high values. In contrast, low-value clusters (coldspots), significant at comparable confidence levels, are largely located in southern and western India, including Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and parts of Maharashtra and Gujarat. These districts demonstrate significantly lower levels of son preference and are spatially clustered with neighboring districts exhibiting similar patterns. Several districts across northern and northeastern regions fall within the non-significant category, indicating the absence of strong local spatial clustering.

Spatial regression analysis: Table 3 presents the results of the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model examining the determinants of son preference. The Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression analysis was conducted to examine the determinants of son preference among households across 707 districts in India. The model explains 64% of the variance in son preference practices (Adjusted R2 = 0.640; Multiple R2 = 0.644) and the joint F-statistic (180.53, p < 0.001) indicates that the overall model is statistically significant. Similarly, the Wald chi-squared test (1255.24, p < 0.001) further confirms the robustness of the model. Diagnostic tests indicate acceptable model specification. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values range from 1.27 to 4.96, suggesting low correlation among the independent variables. As all VIF values are below 5, there is no evidence of serious multicollinearity; therefore, there is no justification for excluding any independent variables. The Table 4 Koenker (BP) test (χ2 = 32.78, p < 0.001) confirms the presence of heteroskedasticity and spatial non-stationarity. The Jarque–Bera test (χ2 = 45.84, p < 0.001) indicates that the residuals deviate from normality. However, this deviation is common in large sample sizes and does not necessarily invalidate the model’s findings. Therefore, we relied on robust probability values to assess the significance of the coefficients. Five of seven explanatory variables emerged as statistically significant predictors of son preference is no mass media exposure (β = 0.300, p < 0.001); Parity 2 (β = 0.969, p < 0.001); No maternal education (β = 0.137, p < 0.001); Household size 5–8 members (β = 0.156, p < 0.001): Mother’s age 15–24 years (β = − 0.214, p = 0.0005): The poorest wealth quintile (β = − 0.032, p = 0.13) and rural residence (β = − 0.001, p = 0.94) were not statistically significant predictors of son preference in the global model. These findings establish a robust global baseline for examining spatial heterogeneity in the determinants of son preference through geographically weighted regression.

Discussion

This study mapped the geographic distribution of son preference and spatial determinants across India’s districts using NFHS-5 (2019–21) data. Applying a spatially explicit approach choropleth mapping, hotspot analysis (Getis–Ord Gi*), kriging interpolation, and geographically weighted regression (GWR) we found marked spatial heterogeneity: 17.43% of ever married women aged 15–49 expressed son preference, with high-prevalence clusters (30–50%) in northern and central India (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Haryana, and parts of West Bengal) and low levels (< 10%) in southern and western states (Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Maharashtra). Hotspot analysis and LISA map confirmed that significant clustering intense hotspots in the Hindi and Bengali belts, cold spots in the South and Northeast. While earlier studies such as Upadhyay et al. [34] map district-level prevalence, they assume spatially uniform effects. Our approach addresses this gap by showing that the relationships between son preference and key factors maternal age, illiteracy, parity, mass media exposure, and household size vary sharply across space, highlighting the context-specific nature of gender norms. By capturing spatial non-stationarity, we reveal that both the strength and direction of these drivers differ regionally.

Son preference in India is sharply clustered, reaffirming the well-documented north–south divide in gender norms, women’s autonomy, patriarchal family structures, and gendered kinship systems [6, 34, 49]. High-prevalence districts exceeding 30–50% are concentrated in the Indo-Gangetic Plain (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, West Bengal) and parts of Rajasthan, Haryana, and Jharkhand, where male-labor-intensive agrarian economies reinforce patrilineal kinship, dowry norms, and low female education and labor force participation [32, 50, 51], Similar patterns emerge in the Central–Northwestern Uplands, marked by arid ecologies, joint-family structures, land-based inheritance, and rigid gender hierarchies [52] all underscoring how patrilineal institutions and sons’ perceived economic utility sustain son preference [16, 39]. In contrast, southern and northeastern states exhibit son preference below 10%, reflecting decades of investment in female education, selective matrilineal traditions, and progressive social reforms in Kerala and Tamil Nadu [6, 32, 53]. Crucially, spatial clustering transcends administrative borders, suggesting cross-district diffusion of gender norms consistent with theories of social learning and norm transmission [54, 55] a dynamic still underexplored in the Indian context.

In high-patriarchy districts of Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, West Bengal, and Jharkhand, young (15–24) and illiterate mothers show the strongest positive association with son preference. This pattern likely reflects early entry into marital unions, where patrilocal residence, limited media exposure and low female autonomy expose women to intense pressure from husbands and in-laws, fostering the internalization of son-biased norms [13, 56, 57]. Limited education and young age further weaken women’s bargaining power, making the birth of a son a key pathway to securing status and acceptance within the household [51, 58, 59]. Moreover, sons are perceived as providers of long-term economic security, particularly in contexts of uncertainty and the financial burden associated with daughters. Prevailing gender norms also equate successful motherhood with bearing a male child. Together, these factors intensify son preference among young, illiterate mothers [6, 34, 35]. In contrast, southern and northeastern districts This reflects a context where baseline son preference is already low, associations are weak, neutral, or even slightly negative associations,, high female literacy, and social safety nets decouple early childbearing from son-biased goals [34, 37, 53].

Parity is among the most robust predictors of son preference, with a prevalence of 26.5% among women at higher parities. GWR estimates show consistently positive coefficients, reflecting unmet desired sex composition of children, particularly across districts in the Hindi and Bengali Belts. This reflects high-patriarchy regions, constrained reproductive autonomy, where women continue childbearing until a son is born under intense familial and community pressure [6, 35, 60, 61]. While the association weakens in southern and northeastern states having more children does not strongly correlate with a desire for sons, reflecting greater gender equity and less pressure to achieve a specific sex composition indicating that development alone does not eliminate gender bias but may render it more strategic. National small-family norms risk being counterproductive in high-prevalence regions if they ignore the imperative to produce sons within fewer births. Thus, son preference endures not as overt tradition but as a covert, adaptive practice shaped by local patriarchal structures [34, 58, 62].

Nationwide, the absence of mass media exposure is associated with higher son preference, particularly in eastern, northern, and parts of southern states. Limited media access restricts exposure to modern and egalitarian gender norms. This reinforces traditional son-biased attitudes within close-knit family and community networks. Women without media exposure are often more socially isolated and embedded in patriarchal structures. As a result, intergenerational transmission of son preference remains strong and less challenged [57, 63], such households are typically poorer and more dependent on agriculture. This increases reliance on sons for labor and old-age security. Daughters, in contrast, are often perceived as financial burdens due to dowry practices. The absence of media also limits women’s awareness and autonomy [8, 64]. Consequently, traditional reproductive norms persist, including continued childbearing until a son is born [19]. In northern and central India, larger households exhibit stronger son preference, driven by agricultural labor demands, patrilineal inheritance, expectations of old-age support, low mass media exposure, and predominantly rural economic structures. High levels of patriarchy, particularly in co-resident extended families with older cohorts, further intensify pressure for sons. Sons are culturally valued for continuing family lineage and ensuring economic security [11, 16, 32]. Additionally, dowry practices, limited female employment opportunities and autonomy, and weak social security systems continue to reinforce son preference in rural areas [6, 7]. In contrast, in the South and Northeast, both factors show weaker coefficients, reflecting greater female agency, equitable kinship norms, and autonomy rather than vulnerability [16, 40, 65].

This study exposes critical limitations in India’s uniform policy approach to addressing son preference. Despite flagship programs like Beti Bachao Beti Padhao and Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana, sex ratios at birth worsened between the 2001 and 2011 censuses, reflecting policies that overlook profound spatial heterogeneity and limited ground-level intervention [66]. Our GWR results reveal that predictors operate non-stationarily: young mothers (15–24 years), illiteracy, parity two, and large household size exhibit strong positive associations with son preference in northern, central, and eastern hotspots, yet show weak or neutral effects in southern and northeastern districts [49]. This spatial contingency indicates that patriarchal dynamics—patrilineal inheritance, dowry burdens, and son-dependent old-age security intensify gender bias only where institutionalized patriarchy remains entrenched [67]. Consequently, universal interventions waste resources in low-prevalence regions while under-serving high-burden clusters that transcend administrative borders (e.g., UP-Bihar-MP-Rajasthan). We recommend that existing policies could benefit from spatial targeting, particularly in identified hotspot districts, through geographically tiered, gender-transformative strategies. These include conditional cash transfers linked to girls’ secondary enrolment, prioritized in hotspot areas with district-level decentralized action plans; localized media campaigns that challenge lineage-based son preference; stricter enforcement of anti-dowry and inheritance laws in high-patriarchy districts; and cross-district coordination mechanisms to facilitate the diffusion of progressive norms (Upadhyay et al., [68, 6, 35]). Critically, recent social security schemes providing financial assistance to families with only daughters, alongside universal old-age pensions, may prove more effective by directly addressing the economic utility of sons. Policies accounting for local normative values and women’s empowerment rather than centralized, one-size-fits-all approaches are essential to curb the spatially embedded social menace of son preference.

While this study has several limitations that warrant caution. First, district-level aggregation masks intra-district heterogeneity and risks ecological fallacy, as unobserved community-level dynamics and urban–rural divides remain unresolved. Second, although GWR accounts for spatial non-stationarity, it cannot fully disentangle the intersectional interplay of caste, class, education, and religion, which likely exert heterogeneous, context-dependent effects across districts. Crucially, spatial clustering should not be misinterpreted as geographic determinism; observed disparities are driven by underlying socio-economic structures, institutional access, and cultural norms rather than location itself. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and unmeasured factors such as local policy enforcement, migration, and evolving gender attitudes may further shape these patterns. Future work should integrate longitudinal, sub-district, and qualitative data to trace how intersecting vulnerabilities co-constitute localized gender norms. Additionally, the findings may be influenced by the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP), as district-level aggregation can affect observed spatial patterns and associations. GWR may also be prone to local overfitting, and the assumption of spatial independence may not fully align with the complex survey design of NFHS-5. Furthermore, hotspot and LISA analyses involve multiple local statistical tests without formal correction for multiple comparisons, which may increase the likelihood of type I error.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that son preference in India is not a static cultural artifact but a spatially embedded social process, dynamically shaped by localized intersections of structural patriarchy, agrarian economies, kinship systems, and institutional access. Using GWR on NFHS-5 data, we reveal stark district-level heterogeneity: while 17.43% of ever married women nationally express son preference, prevalence soars to 30–50% across the Indo-Gangetic and Central–Northwestern belts, driven by entrenched patrilocality, an agriculture-based, labour-intensive economy, dowry burdens, low female autonomy, and son-dependent old-age security. In contrast, southern and northeastern districts consistently exhibit prevalence below 10%, reflecting historically flexible kinship structures, higher female education, and stronger institutional safety nets. Critically, GWR exposes the non-stationarity of conventional predictors: secondary education, mass media exposure, parity, and household size operate with markedly different intensities and directions across geographic space. Factors such as younger and illiterate mothers, parity, and larger household size strongly amplify son preference in northern hotspots, but are neutral or attenuated in southern cold spots, underscoring that gender bias is context-dependent rather than universally deterministic. These findings challenge the efficacy of uniform national gender policies, which consistently overlook the profound spatial contingency of patriarchal norms. Instead, we advocate for a paradigm shift toward spatial targeting through place-based interventions. District-level action plans with robust monitoring and evaluation, along with initiatives to enhance the value of the girl child, conditional incentives for girls’ schooling, localized normative campaigns, and strengthened old-age security, should be strategically deployed in identified high-burden corridors. By mapping how institutional patriarchy translates into localized fertility behavior and spatial clustering, this study advances a place-sensitive understanding of son preference. Dismantling gender-biased fertility norms ultimately require moving beyond aggregate narratives to implement precisely targeted, spatially informed policies that address the economic and cultural utilities of sons where they remain most entrenched.
