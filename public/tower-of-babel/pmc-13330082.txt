The contribution of rare germline variants to the immune landscape of breast cancer

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMany breast cancer predisposition genes are involved in DNA damage repair, leading to genome instability that can impact immunosurveillance, neoantigen formation, and the composition of the tumor immune microenvironment.MethodsHere, we explored associations between germline protein truncating variants (PTVs) in 34 (putative) breast cancer predisposition genes, of which 26 involved in DNA damage repair, with the abundance of four immune cell markers, i.e., CD8 + , FOXP3 + , CD20 + and CD163 + , across 7,969 invasive breast tumors of women of European ancestry.ResultsThe most apparent associations were those of CD163, a marker of M2-like tumor-associated macrophages, with genes involved in double- and single-strand break DNA repair, and with the 12 known breast cancer predisposition genes combined. Specifically, DNA damage repair genes, BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, RAD51D, and MSH6 were associated with a 1.3 to twofold abundance of CD163-positive cells. Estrogen receptor status was found to mediate associations to a limited extent.ConclusionsOur findings support a role of rare pathogenic germline variants involved in DNA damage repair, and particularly those predisposing to breast cancer, in the immune landscape of breast tumors. These insights may help guide the development of immunomodulatory strategies for breast cancer prevention and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-026-01662-6.

Background

In breast cancer, the composition of the tumor immune microenvironment, namely the type and abundance of lymphocytes, monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils, influences tumor progression [1]. A high number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes has been associated with improved responsiveness to neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone [2] or in combination with immunotherapy [3]. However, the unique activities of individual lymphocytic and monocytic subtypes suggest nuanced associations with clinical outcomes. For instance, elevated levels of cytotoxic T-cells (CD8-positive cells) are associated with improved patient survival [4, 5]. Increased levels of pro-tumorigenic immune cell types such as regulatory T-cells (Treg-cells; FOXP3-/CD25-positive cells) and M2 tumor-associated macrophages (M2-TAMs; CD163-/CD206-positive cells) are associated with worse clinical outcomes [6, 7]. Contradictory evidence exists on the association between abundance of B-cells (CD20-/CD19-positive cells), and breast cancer prognosis (reviewed by [8]). However, a recent meta-analysis suggests that increased B-lymphocyte abundance correlates with improved overall and breast cancer-specific survival [9].

The abundance of immune cells is associated with intrinsic tumor characteristics, particularly the estrogen receptor (ER)-status of the tumor. ER-positive tumors tend to have a lower number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes compared to ER-negative tumors [10]. Among the ER-negative subtype, triple-negative tumors with increased levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes associate with a more favorable survival benefit even in the absence of chemotherapy [11–13]. Importantly, germline genetic factors may also influence the abundance of anti- and pro-tumorigenic immune cells [14]. Tumors (specifically triple-negative ones) in carriers of germline pathogenic variants in BRCA1, exhibit a heightened mutational burden resulting in an increased lymphocytic infiltration [15]. Similarly, an association study has linked BRCA1 germline variants with increased dendritic cells, interferon-gamma, MHC-I and STAT1 immune features [14]. Additionally, a pre-clinical study has suggested that breast tissue of non-affected BRCA1/2 pathogenic variant carriers has a higher immune infiltration compared to non-carriers [16]. This evidence agrees with the hypothesis that genomic instability due to DNA damage repair deficiency, which might be caused by germline variants, leads to altered tissue immune microenvironment [16, 17]. There is also, seemingly contrasting, evidence that patients with pathogenic variants in BRCA1/2 do not seem to benefit more from immunotherapy compared to non-carriers patients [18]. Another, preclinical, study showed an association with response to immunotherapy for truncating mutations in BRCA2, but found no association for BRCA1 [19].

Understanding the role of germline pathogenic variants that predispose to breast cancer, specifically those in the DNA damage repair domain, in shaping the immune microenvironment of tumor development and progression, will contribute to preventative and treatment strategies for high-risk groups of women. In this analysis, we used data from the BRIDGES study [20], which characterized and quantified risk association of rare protein truncating variants (PTVs) and missense variants (MSVs) in 34 (putative) breast cancer predisposition genes, 26 of which are involved in DNA damage repair. Approximately 8,000 cases from BRIDGES had data on a series of immune markers obtained from standardized scoring of tumor tissue microarrays. The main objective of this study was to investigate the association between rare germline variants in these DNA damage repair genes and the abundance of CD8-, CD20-, FOXP3- and CD163-positive cells. The extent to which these associations might be mediated by subtype, based on ER-status, was also investigated. In addition, we assessed associations in the subgroup of triple-negative breast cancers. A secondary objective was to assess whether any of the remaining eight genes, unrelated to DNA damage repair but including some involved in PI3K signaling, cell adhesion and proliferation, were associated with the composition of the tumor immune microenvironment.

Methods

Patients

Women diagnosed with breast cancer from 11 studies participating in the BRIDGES study within the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC) were included [20]. Analyses were restricted to women of self-reported European ancestry, diagnosed with primary invasive breast cancer and at least 18 years of age at diagnosis (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Demographic data and tumor characteristics were obtained from the central BCAC database: each individual study collected data that was later centralized, after quality control and harmonization (BCAC database version 15.01). All women involved provided written informed consent, and each study met local ethical committee and/or institutional review board approvals.

Patient and tumor characteristics

Tumor data for a total of 7,969 women were included, of which 7,232 had scores for CD8, 7,326 for CD20, 7,239 for FOXP3, and 7,213 for CD163 immunohistochemical staining. A flow diagram describing inclusion and exclusion criteria is shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S1. The number of cases included for each of the 11 studies are given in Additional file 2: Table S1, while the baseline characteristics of all participants, and across immune cell types, are shown in Additional file 1: Table S3. The median age at diagnosis was 55 years of age [(Min–Max): 18–93]; 22% of the tumors were ER-negative, 71% ER-positive tumors and 7% with unknown ER-status (Additional file 1: Table S3). Subgroup analysis was performed on 765 (approximately 11%) tumors that were triple negative. The distributions of relevant tumor characteristics were similar across the slightly different subsets of patients with available data for each immune cell type.

Quantification of immune cell abundance

Each study collected formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue blocks containing primary breast tumor tissue. Whole tumor slides were obtained from the tumor blocks and representative tumor areas were manually identified after staining with hematoxylin and eosin. Tissue microarrays were constructed from the tumors by collecting tissue cylinders with a diameter ranging from 0.6 to 2 mm at each study site (Additional file 2: Table S1). Tumor tissues were from surgical resections, prior to commencement of adjuvant therapy; except potentially from 3.2% of the cases that were noted in the database to have received neo-adjuvant chemotherapy. Each study sent unstained tissue microarray slides for central immunohistochemical staining of CD8, FOXP3, CD20 and CD163 at the Cancer Research UK Cambridge Institute (Additional file 2: Table S2; Additional file 1: Table S3). Tissue microarray sections that were successfully stained using cytoplasm monoclonal antibodies for CD8, CD20 or CD163; and nuclear monoclonal antibody for FOXP3 were scanned using Leica Aperio AT2 at the Cancer Research UK Cambridge Institute (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Digitized slides were quality controlled and linked to data in the central BCAC database.

Quantification of positively stained areas of core images from de-arrayed tissue microarrays was performed using a custom HALO algorithm [21]. The abundance of immune cells was quantified across the entire core, by combining stromal and intra-tumoral regions. The abundance of immune cells in each core was assessed as the percentage of the total tissue area occupied by positively stained cells. This score was defined as the ratio of the area covered by positive staining out of the total area composing the tumor core. Tumor area was composed by positively stained regions, non-stained areas and artifact regions that were attributed to overstaining, non-specific staining, widespread debris, or tissue folding. Any image that contained more than 40% of the total region detected as artifact was excluded. For each patient, a single score on each immune marker was obtained by averaging across all cores for that patient. Only a fraction of tumors (n = 755 of 7,969 tumors) had a single image available, and for these tumors scores from the single score was used. The distributions of percentages of positively stained area for the four immune cell types are shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S3.

Genotype and quality control

Details of sequencing, variant calling and quality control have been described previously [20]. Briefly, genotype data for PTVs and rare MSVs were obtained for 34 (putative and established) breast cancer predisposition genes, using a targeted panel. Of these, 12 were considered definitively associated with breast cancer risk in the BRIDGES study and/or analyses based on the ClinGen clinical validity framework: ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, CDH1, CHEK2, PALB2, PTEN, RAD51C, RAD51D, STK11, TP53 [22]. The CHEK2 c.1100delC PTV was also analyzed separately because of its abundance and earlier reports of strong associations with breast cancer risk [23].

MSVs that were described as deleterious by having a Phred-like Combined Annotation–Dependent Depletion (phred-CADD; version 1.4) score higher than 20 were included [24]. This score predicts how much more deleterious a MSV is compared to the remaining human genome. A score higher than 20 suggests that a MSV is likely to be among the top 1% of deleterious substitutions [25]. In a previous study involving BRIDGES genotype data [26], the best method to classify missense variants differed per gene, but Phred-CADD showed good overall performance. In line with the previous BRIDGES studies published, for BRCA1, BRCA2, and TP53 MSV classification we used the more reliable ClinGen/ENIGMA guidelines [20], and for ATM and CHEK2 MSV classification we combined multiple in-silico algorithms [26].

Classification criteria for DNA damage repair genes

Among the 34 genes included in the BRIDGES gene panel, 26 are associated with DNA damage repair either directly or indirectly. Among these genes, 16 genes are involved in double-strand break repair as part of the homologous recombination pathway, seven genes are involved in single-strand break repair (i.e., Fanconi anemia/DNA crosslinking, mismatch repair or base excision repair) and three genes are indirectly involved in DNA damage repair, either as DNA damage sensors (ATM and CHEK2) or as downstream targets (TP53). The remaining eight genes are involved in proliferation (NF1 and STK11), PI3K signaling (AKT1, PIK3CA and PTEN), epigenetic regulation (MEN1) or cell adhesion (CDH1 and EPCAM). A detailed annotation of the function of each of the 34 genes included in the analyses and a grouping in terms of four DNA repair types are available in Additional file 1: Table S4.

Statistical analyses

In the primary analyses we evaluated the association between each immune marker and PTVs of each gene. We also included analyses of MSVs. As individual variants are rare, we performed burden analyses in which the genotype of each gene was defined as 1 or 0 according to whether the individual carried a germline variant in the gene. Non-carriers were defined as women without pathogenic germline variants in the gene of interest, irrespective of germline variants in other genes. For illustration purposes, raw immune cell abundances grouped by germline genotype for the 12 breast cancer predisposition genes are shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S4. A sensitivity analysis was conducted in which the non-carriers were only women with no other pathogenic germline mutation, either PTV or MSV, in any other gene to investigate whether individual gene effects were attenuated if the non-carrier group included individuals with PTVs or MSVs in other genes. We also performed analyses based on the burden of variants across a set of genes, grouped by the mechanisms of DNA damage repair in which they are involved. This approach aimed to identify associations that might be missed due to a low number of cases for each individual gene. Similarly, we also grouped the 12 known breast cancer predisposition genes together to identify their association with immune abundance.

The percentage of immune cells was transformed to a proportion by scaling it to a non-inclusive unit interval score (0, 1). For samples where the score was either 0 or 1, 10−5 was added or subtracted, accordingly. Associations between the percentage of immune cells and germline genotypes were assessed using beta regression with a logit link [27]. Beta regression was chosen as it accurately models outcomes bounded by the unit interval. The regression coefficients can be interpreted as the change in the log(odds) of the proportion of stained cells, per unit increase in the covariate [28, 29].

The primary analyses included study and age at breast cancer diagnosis as covariates (reported here as unadjusted models). To assess the extent to which the association between germline variants and immune cell abundance is mediated by the ER-status of the tumor, an adjusted model including ER-status of the tumor as an additional covariate was included. This model will highlight which associations are influenced by ER subtype. If an association from the unadjusted model remains largely unchanged and statistically significant in the adjustment model, it indicates that the observed effect is irrespective of the ER-status. Subgroup analysis was performed for women with triple-negative breast cancer (defined as ER-negative, progesterone receptor (PR)-negative, and HER2-negative). We did not perform subgroup analysis on ER-positive samples because of the low abundance of immune cells in these tumors, often referred to as “cold tumors” [30], coupled with the low variability of the immune abundance in our data (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Given the low prevalence of the PTVs, we report statistical significance at a nominal level of 0.05; though to account for the many comparisons and small numbers in some analyses we focused our discussion on those with p < 0.01 and with more than 10 cases. All analyses were performed in R v.3.6 [31] using the betareg v.3.1.4 R package [32].

Results

The number of cases, OR and 95% CI for all associations between germline genotype and abundance of immune cell types are provided in Additional file 1: Tables S5-S12.

Double-strand break repair genes

Compared to non-carriers, carriers of germline PTVs in four individual double-strand break repair genes, BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, and RAD51D, were found to be associated with breast cancer immune cell abundance (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Fig. S3 and Fig. S5; Additional file 1: Tables S5-S12). PTVs in BRCA1 were associated with higher FOXP3-positive and CD163-positive cells [OR (95%CI): 1.25 (1.04–1.5) p = 0.017 and 1.66 (1.41–1.95) p < 0.001, respectively]. PTVs in BRCA2 were associated with CD8-positive and CD163-positive cells [OR (95% CI): 1.27 (1.09–1.49) p = 0.003 and 1.29 (1.11–1.5) p = 0.001, respectively]. PTVs in PALB2 and RAD51D were associated with CD163-positive cells [OR (95% CI): 1.36 (1.08–1.73) p = 0.01 and 1.71 (1.05–2.79) p = 0.031, respectively]. MSVs in MRE11 were also associated with CD163-positive cells. There was no evidence of consistent or strong associations between PTVs or MSVs in other genes with any of the immune cell types when considering all tumors together. Other DNA damage repair genes

Carriers of germline PTVs and/or MSVs in mismatch repair or single-strand break repair genes compared to non-carriers were to a more limited extent found to be associated with breast cancer immune cell abundance with some evidence for MLH1 and MSH6, but not for MSH2 and PMS2 (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Tables S5-S24).

Discussion

The risk of developing breast cancer is strongly associated with deleterious variants in predisposition genes, many of which play a role in DNA damage repair and related pathways [20, 26]. Beyond impacting risk, germline PVs have also been shown to influence tumor biological behavior, predisposing more strongly to risks of aggressive (triple-negative and/or high-grade) breast cancers [33]. The immune composition of the tumor microenvironment has emerged as an important component of breast tumor biological behavior, but the impact of germline PVs on tumor immune composition is less-well understood. In this study, we explored the association between rare germline variants and the abundance of four immune cell types that have been shown to associate with breast cancer prognosis [4–7, 9]. We found most convincing evidence for associations between germline variants in DNA double-strand break repair in aggregate, and in particular BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, RAD51D, and CD163-positive cell abundance in the tumor. When PTVs across all genes involved in homologous recombination were grouped, we observed an association with CD8-positive and CD163-positive cells. In alignment with recent evidence [14, 34], these associations suggest that CD8-positive and CD163-positive cell abundance in breast tumors might be linked to a heritable genetic component beyond BRCA1 and BRCA2.

In our study, PTVs in BRCA1 were associated with an increased abundance of FOXP3-positive and CD163-positive cells, which tend to promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Contrastingly, results from a previous pan-cancer analysis by Sayaman and colleagues that investigated germline genetic contribution to the immune landscape of cancer, including breast cancer, showed that BRCA1 germline variants were associated with elevated interferon and major histocompatibility complex class-I traits, which play a role in immune activation [14]. These apparent contradictions demonstrate the complex biology of immune mechanisms in mammary carcinogenesis. Conceivably, our observation of increased abundance of CD163 and FOXP3-positive cells in relation to PTVs in BRCA1 might reflect tumor promoting chronic inflammatory processes, while the observations by Sayaman and colleagues might reflect ongoing cancer immunoediting, processes that might coexist during tumor development and progression [35]. The association of PTVs in BRCA1 with CD163-positive cells slightly attenuated after adjusting for the ER-status of the tumor, suggesting that the ER-status might be partially mediating the association. We also found PTVs in BRCA2 to be associated with increased levels of CD8-positive and CD163-positive cells. This is consistent with recent evidence that BRCA2-deficiency results in an increase of interferon responses through cGAS/STING micronuclei accumulation [36], which ultimately can regulate macrophage polarization and T-cell differentiation [37]. Similarly, long-term exposure to BRCA2 abrogation in cancer-derived human cells was associated with an upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes [38]. Moreover, pre-clinical studies demonstrated that healthy breast lobules of BRCA1/2 germline carriers have 2.3 times more CD8-positive T-cells compared to non-carriers [16]. The associations of PTVs in BRCA2 with CD8-positive and CD163-positive cells remained largely unchanged after adjusting for ER-status of the tumor, suggesting that ER-status might not be a mediator. These associations were not statistically significant in the triple-negative subgroup analysis, which was expected considering the low abundance of BRCA2 carriers in that subgroup. Moreover, it is important to note that other genetic, epigenetic, and non-genetic mechanisms might contribute to the immunogenicity of triple-negative breast cancer and further studies will be required to disentangle these. Recent evidence showed that ER-positive tumors display reduced number of immune cells, low HLA class I expression and favor the recruitment of lymphoid or myeloid cells other than T-cells [30]. The few immune-rich ER-positive tumors show an increased relative fraction of macrophages compared to immune-low ER-positive tumors [39].

While the direct effect of some of the genes we studied on breast cancer immune cell abundance remain largely unknown, evidence in other cancer types might suggest potential mechanisms of action. For example, BRCA1-PALB2 deficiency has been associated with T-lymphocyte infiltration and the activation of cGAS-STING in macrophages in hepatocellular carcinoma [40]. In ovarian cancer, patients with germline homologous recombination deficiency were enriched for CD68 + tumor associated macrophages and CD3 + lymphocytes, with the latter associated with longer median overall survival [41]. These findings concur with reports showing that impairment of homologous recombination increases the rate of DNA defects, potentially leading to an enhanced immunogenic response against tumors [42] and higher leukocyte fractions [14]. In sum, these results suggest that partial impairment of innate DNA damage repair can lead to increased immunological signaling and recruitment of CD163-positive cells.

Variants in breast cancer risk genes, especially those involved in homologous recombination, are more strongly associated with risk for ER-negative and in particular triple-negative breast cancer [33]. These subtypes show both increased immune infiltration [43] and a higher frequency of homologous recombination deficiency [44]. In our analyses, most associations between germline variants and immune cell abundance remained significant after adjustment for ER-status. In the triple-negative subgroup analysis, the association of PTVs in RAD51D with CD163-positive cells remained significant, but associations for other genes failed to reach significance. Reduced power due to smaller sample sizes may explain this for some genes, but this will need to be confirmed in larger datasets of triple-negative breast cancers.

While our study is the largest association analysis of rare MSVs and PTVs in breast cancer predisposition genes and immune cell abundance, it remains open to further improvement. For example, to our surprise we did not observe a statistically significant association between PTVs in BRCA1 and CD8-positive cells, even though the association [OR (95% CI): 1.15 (0.96–1.39)] was in the same direction as previously reported [15]. While we observe an association between CD163-positive cells and grouping germline genetic variants on breast cancer risk genes, the effect is mainly driven by BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2 and RAD51D. This may be due to limited power for several genes, although our cohort was large. Another possible explanation for the absence of some expected associations might be the leftward-skewed distribution of the percentages, where limited variability in the outcome makes it challenging to detect the effect of rare germline variants. This might result from utilizing tissue microarrays instead of whole-slide images to estimate immune cell abundance, as tissue microarrays have been shown to markedly underestimate immune cell levels compared to the original whole-slide from which they are derived [45]. Other potential limitations of using TMAs include intratumoral heterogeneity and lack of spatial information regarding where the cores were sampled in the tissues. Notably, however, emerging data, including two studies from the current analysis, suggest that evaluation of immune cell subsets on TMAs may closely approximate tumor biology assessed on whole sections [46]. However, further studies might be required to clarify the etiologic relevance of spatial immune localization in breast tissues. Due to the original data source and study design (aimed to detect novel breast cancer genes) our analysis focused exclusively on (putative) predisposition genes, potentially excluding genes with a strong effect on immune response, such as IL7 [47, 48].

Beyond impacting immune composition, it is important to be aware that perturbations in BRCA1 may also impact the function of immune effector cells. For example, in-vivo evidence suggests that cytotoxic T-cell antitumor immune activity is impaired in BRCA1-knockout mice [49]. Similarly, macrophages with deficiency in nucleotide excision repair show functional and morphological abnormalities, displaying heightened glucose intake and pro-inflammatory stimuli [50]. Collectively, these prior results suggest that DNA damage repair deficiencies can compromise immune cell function and exhibit a bi-directional effect, influencing the activity of immune cell as well as their interaction with tumor cells, which is something we could not address in our analyses.

Conclusions

Our findings underpin a potential driver role of rare pathogenic germline variants involved in DNA damage repair, and particularly those predisposing to breast cancer, in the immune landscape of breast tumors, most notably with CD163-positive cells. With further elucidation, these findings have the potential to improve the selection of patients that may benefit from immunomodulatory therapies, especially considering recent developments pointing towards tumor associated macrophages as potential targets for PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy [51]. Our results lend support to the notion that germline impairment of DNA damage repair machinery interferes with the immunological component of breast cancer.
