Association of liver fibrosis scores with all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with heart failure
Authors: Ziyu Guo, Zixiang Ye, Qinfeng Xu, Yike Li, Jingang Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to examine the relationship of liver fibrosis (determined via fibrosis scores) with all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in HF patients. The study examined demographic and clinical data were collected from NHANES database (1999 to 2018). A total of 1356 HF patients were enrolled in our analysis. During a median follow‐up time of 70 months, 455 patients died. Compared to the survivors, the death group showed significantly elevated LFSs levels. RCS analysis revealed a linear relationship between various LFSs and all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. KM curves and Cox regression models indicated that higher FIB‐4 (≥ 1.637), NFS (≥ −0.064), and AST/ALT ratio (≥ 1.172) were linked to higher risk of all‐cause mortality [Cox model 2: FIB‐4 adjusted hazard ratio (aHR), 1.24; 95% CI, 1.04–1.48; NFS aHR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.01–1.38; AST/ALT ratio aHR, 1.25; 95% CI, 1.07–1.47] and cardiovascular mortality in heart failure patients (FIB‐4 aHR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.07–1.67; AST/ALT ratio aHR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.08–1.79). ROC curves indicated that FIB‐4, NFS, and the AST/ALT ratio were important predicators of all‐cause mortality (AUC: 0.715, 0.707, and 0.715, respectively) and cardiovascular mortality (AUC: 0.658, 0.657, and 0.659, respectively) in heart failure patients. Random survival forests showed that FIB‐4, AST/ALT ratio, and NFS emerged as important factors potentially influencing mortality of HF. Consistent associations were observed in subgroup analysis. Liver fibrosis scores (FIB‐4, NFS, and AST/ALT ratio) were strongly linked to all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in heart failure patients.

INTRODUCTION

Heart failure (HF), a common disease worldwide, has high morbidity and mortality rates. 1 As of 2023, global statistics indicate an estimated 64 million individuals grappling with HF worldwide. 2 Although treatment advancements have been made, patients with HF still face elevated mortality rates and frequent hospital readmissions, resulting in a significant healthcare burden. 3 Identifying new risk factors for adverse outcomes in HF is crucial for improving patient prognosis.

Recently, liver fibrosis has garnered attention. There are several risk factors that are common to both liver fibrosis and cardiovascular disease, such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidemia. Besides liver‐related negative effects, liver fibrosis is linked to various non‐liver complications, including cardiovascular incidents, a higher mortality risk, and extrahepatic cancers. 4 , 5 , 6 Although liver biopsy is regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing liver fibrosis, it is impractical for widespread screening. Noninvasive scoring systems, derived from routinely available clinical, laboratory, and anthropometric parameters, offer a potentially safe and accessible alternative method for the initial assessment of fibrosis, particularly in asymptomatic individuals or those without a history of liver diseases. 7 The liver fibrosis scores (LFSs) are calculated using various parameters, including demographics factors, metabolic factors, and biochemical factors. 8 For instance, the fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4) index is determined using age, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and platelet levels. 9 , 10 LFSs not only reflect pathogenesis and progression of liver fibrosis but also be used as independent predictors of cardiovascular disease (CVD). 4 , 11 As Alon et al. found that patients with liver fibrosis have a higher probability of developing several CVD, including coronary heart disease (CHD), myocardial infarction (MI), atrial fibrillation (AF), and heart failure. 12

Previous reports suggested that liver fibrosis and HF are highly correlated, both clinically and pathophysiologically. 11 , 13 , 14 However, limited research has explored the connection between liver fibrosis and negative outcomes or death in HF individuals. In this study, we sought to investigate the associations of liver fibrosis, determined via LFSs, and all‐cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) in HF population. Additionally, we assessed the predictive value of various LFSs for these mortality outcomes in the HF patients.

METHODS

Study population

Data of this study were extracted from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, a vital nationwide research project and approved by NCHS Institutional Review Committee. Informed consent was secured from all individuals involved in the NHANES study before participation. Data collection for NHANES is an ongoing process, with information gathered biennially by trained interviewers. The present investigation extracted participants’ data from NHANES 1999–2018. In this cohort, 101,316 participants completed the interviews. The exclusion criteria were: (a) age < 20 years old; (b) without self‐reported HF; (c) incomplete data of age, platelet, BMI, diabetes, albumin, AST, and ALT; (d) missing data more than 30%; (e) missing survival data. Ultimately, the analysis incorporated 1356 individuals in total (Figure 1).

Flowchart of study population.

Definition of indicators

Liver scarring was evaluated using FIB‐4, the nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) fibrosis score (NFS), and the AST/ALT ratio. FIB‐4 and NFS were determined using the following formulas reported previously. 15

Clinical characteristics and variables

In this study, variables related to HF according to clinical relevance or statistical significance were selected. Baseline characteristics, including demographics, lifestyle information, and comorbidities were gathered via questionnaire. Initial demographic data encompassed factors like age, gender, ethnicity, educational background, and smoking habits. BMI and biochemical parameters were obtained from medical and laboratory examinations measured in a mobile examination van. BMI was calculated from height (m2) and weight (kg). Heart failure was defined by a synthesis of self‐reported physician diagnosis and standardized medical condition questionnaire completed during a personal interview with a positive response to the question, “Has a doctor ever told you that you had congestive heart failure?” Hypertension was characterized by self‐reported hypertension later confirmed by a doctor, a systolic blood pressure (BP) > 140 mmHg or diastolic BP > 90 mmHg, or taking BP‐lowering medications. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was characterized by a self‐reported diagnosis from doctor, HbA1c levels of 6.5% or higher, fasting blood glucose levels of 7.0 mmol/L or higher, or the administration of oral diabetes medications or insulin. Hyperlipidemia was described as an increase of serum total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL‐cholesterol, and HDL‐cholesterol, or self‐reported hyperlipidemia and taking cholesterol‐lowering medications. Individuals who indicated a past diagnosis of COPD and CHD were identified according to the questionnaire responses.

Assessment of ACM and CVM

To evaluate mortality, we integrated data from the National Death Index (NDI) with mortality records. The study outcomes encompassed ACM as well as CVM. Causes of death were categorized according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD‐10). CVM was identified as death due to heart diseases (ICD‐10 codes I00‐I09, I11, I13, and I20‐I51) and stroke (ICD codes I60–I69).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were displayed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical variables were displayed as frequency and percentages (%), with hypothesis testing conducted via the Chi‐squared test. The connection between LFSs and death rates was analyzed using restricted cubic spline (RCS) methodology. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were utilized to evaluate the impact of LFSs on mortality risk in heart failure, using inflection points derived from the RCS as thresholds to categorize patients into low‐ or high‐LFSs groups. Cox proportional hazard models were constructed to assess the relationships between the FIB‐4, NFS, AST/ALT ratio, and ACM and CVM, presenting the findings as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). There were three models: the first was a crude model with no covariate adjustments; Model 1 was adjusted for age, gender, ethnicity, and education; Model 2 was adjusted for all factors from Model 1 and additionally adjusted for smoking habits, BMI, alcohol use, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, DM, CHD. The predictive ability of each LFS was assessed by plotting receiver‐operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Random survival forests, an ensemble tree method for the analysis of right censored survival data, were used to investigate the importance of clinical predictors and rank them by their importance. To explore the links between the AST/ALT ratio and the risk of mortality, subgroup analysis was conducted, with the data categorized by age, gender, DM, and CHD. Data analysis was conducted with R 4.3.2 software, and p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The NHANES protocol was revised, approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the National Center for Health Statistics.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

This study included 1356 qualified participants diagnosed with heart failure. Their median age was 70 years, with 42.3% being female. During the follow‐up period (median follow‐up of 70 months), 455 participants died. There were significant differences between the survival and death groups across several variables, including age, ethnic composition, educational levels, drinking status, BMI, ALT, bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, total protein, globulin, creatinine, uric acid, blood urea nitrogen, and eGFR. The death group showed markedly elevated FIB‐4, NFS, and AST/ALT ratio levels in comparison to the survival group. The two groups showed no significant differences regarding gender, smoking status, HbA1c levels, AST, albumin, HDL‐C, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, CHD, and DM. Associations between the LFSs and survival status

We employed a RCS model to delineate the dose–response associations between FIB‐4, NFS, AST/ALT ratio and all‐cause and CV mortality. The spline curves revealed notable linear associations between LFSs and mortality (all p for nonlinear associations > 0.05), indicating that higher LFSs are positively correlated with increased mortality, thereby suggesting a detrimental impact of elevated LFSs on the lifespan of individuals with heart failure (Figure 2). Figure 3 presented the KM survival curves for all‐cause and CV mortality among heart failure participants stratified by LFSs. Patients were grouped with low or high LFSs (higher FIB‐4 ≥ 1.637; higher NFS ≥ −0.064; higher AST/ALT ratio ≥ 1.172) based on the inflection points derived from the RCS, which were applied as cutoff points. These findings indicated that participants with elevated LFSs levels had notably higher rates of mortality (log‐rank test p < 0.05) (Figure 3).

Associations between different LFSs and mortality risk

Cox regression analysis was employed to evaluate the associations between different LFSs and adverse outcomes in heart failure patients. The unadjusted Cox analysis revealed that FIB‐4 (HR 1.17; 95% CI 1.14–1.21), NFS (HR 1.27; 95% CI 1.20–1.33), and the AST/ALT ratio (HR 1.82; 95% CI 1.56–2.11) were significantly associated with all‐cause death (Table 2). The multivariable Cox regression, accounting for other confounding variables, validated these links (Cox model 2: FIB‐4 adjusted hazard ratio (aHR), 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04–1.15; NFS aHR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.03–1.18; AST/ALT ratio aHR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.12–1.65). Simultaneously, FIB‐4, NFS, and the AST/ALT ratio were linked to cardiovascular mortality both before and after the adjustment for confounding variables (Cox model 2: FIB‐4 aHR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.01–1.18; NFS aHR, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.00–1.22; AST/ALT ratio aHR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.21–2.02) (Table 2). We also observed similar findings using the categorical LFSs in the models (Table 2). FIB‐4, NFS, and AST/ALT ratio were converted to categorical variables based on RCS cutoff points (FIB‐4 < 1.637 and ≥ 1.637; NFS < −0.064 and ≥ −0.064; AST/ALT ratio < 1.172 and ≥ 1.172). In the multivariate Cox regression analysis examining the relationship between all‐cause mortality and the LFSs, groups with elevated LFSs exhibited increased risks of all‐cause mortality (Cox model 2: FIB‐4 aHR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.04–1.48; NFS aHR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.01–1.38; AST/ALT ratio aHR, 1.25; 95% CI, 1.07–1.47). Likewise, we noted similar trends for cardiovascular mortality in relation to FIB‐4 and the AST/ALT ratio (Cox model 2: FIB‐4 aHR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.07–1.67; AST/ALT ratio aHR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.08–1.79). Nevertheless, the proportional‐hazards assumption was violated for cardiovascular mortality of NFS (Table 2).

Predictive performance of the various LFSs for mortality

We assessed and contrasted the effectiveness of different LFSs in forecasting mortality risk, as illustrated in Figure 4. After adjusting for covariates, the AUC values for the FIB‐4, NFS, and AST/ALT ratio in relation to all‐cause mortality risk were 0.715, 0.707, and 0.715, respectively. In addition, the FIB‐4, NFS, and AST/ALT ratio had AUCs of 0.658, 0.657, and 0.659, respectively, in predicting the risk of cardiovascular mortality. These results indicated that FIB‐4, NFS, and the AST/ALT ratio showed reliable and moderate effectiveness in forecasting all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. The DeLong test showed no notable variation among the AUCs of the different LFSs (p > 0.05).

Subgroup analysis

Considering clinical practicality and ease of measurement, we selected the AST/ALT ratio for our study and performed a stratified analysis by age, gender, diabetes status, and CHD. Our analysis revealed that the relationship between the AST/ALT ratio and the likelihood of ACM and CVM remained stable across various subgroups (Table S1). Significant interaction p values were observed in age and diabetes subgroups for ACM and CVM. Additionally, a significant correlation was observed between gender and all‐cause mortality. In the CHD subgroup, no notable interactions were detected (Table S1).

DISCUSSION

This research assessed the links between various LFSs (including FIB‐4, NFS, and AST/ALT ratio) and all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in heart failure patients. And this research identified that the FIB‐4, NFS, and AST/ALT ratio were crucial independent predictors of adverse outcomes in heart failure patients. These associations remained robust even after adjusting for the confounders.

HF causes significant mortality, public health, and financial burden. As is well known, numerous novel biomarkers, including serum protein alterations and genetic variation have developed in recent years. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 However, the predictive capacity of these biomarkers for HF patients' prognosis is suboptimal due to methodological constraints, including higher costs and complexity. Thus, identifying cost‐effective and straightforward tools for forecasting long‐term survival outcomes in patients with HF has become a crucial focus in the cardiovascular field.

Liver fibrosis, mainly associated with NAFLD, is a progressive condition. 20 Numerous research have recognized NAFLD as a major contributor to the development of HF. 14 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Filippo et al. further demonstrated that NAFLD and the severity of its fibrosis were independently correlated with an all‐cause mortality among elderly patients with acute HF. 25 Although liver biopsy still regarded as a gold standard for evaluating liver fibrosis severity, non‐invasive fibrosis scoring systems are widely utilized for their non‐invasiveness. 10 , 26 , 27 Previous research has revealed that LFSs were not only independent risk factor of liver disease mortality but also increased major adverse cardiac events. 28 Additionally, some investigations have assessed and revealed the predictive ability of LFSs in predicting morbidity and mortality in HFpEF patients. 24 , 25 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 A subsequent review of the TOPCAT study identified notable correlations between increased NFS and FIB‐4 levels and the likelihood of HF hospitalization. However, these associations were diminished after adjustments for potential covariates. 29 , 35

In our study, we found that FIB‐4, NFS, and AST/ALT ratio were all related to mortality in HF patients and could predict adverse clinical outcomes of HF patients. And LFSs did emerge as variables of importance in the random survival forest analysis. Considering accessibility to clinical testing, we suggest that AST/ALT ratio seemed to be a preferable screening tool to predict adverse outcomes in HF patients. This finding is meaningful in that it can help clinicians to better understand risk factors for HF mortality and implement effective preventive measures as early as possible. A universally accepted cutoff point for AST/ALT ratio has not yet been established. Our study revealed that noteworthy increased mortality risks were observed in individuals with AST/ALT > 1.172. This finding suggests that AST/ALT value of 1.172 could potentially serve as an effective cutoff point for predicting all‐cause and CV mortality within HF patients.

The exact mechanism connecting hepatic fibrosis with HF remains inadequately understood. Previous research showed that insulin resistance, subclinical inflammation, and dyslipidemia were associated with hepatic fibrosis. 24 , 35 , 36 Given the significant link between liver fibrosis and various metabolic disorders, 37 , 38 we adjusted for traditional cardiovascular risk factors like smoking, hypertension, and diabetes. Despite these adjustments, the independent significant associations between LFSs and mortality of HF were not attenuated. Subgroups analysis revealed that the influence of the AST/ALT ratio on mortality was more obvious in individuals younger than 65 years old. All‐cause mortality was more obvious in males, which could be due to estrogen's role in cardiovascular protection. Notably, the link between the AST/ALT ratio and the risk of death was more significant in individuals with diabetes. One possible explanation is, as predisposing factors of liver fibrosis, metabolic abnormalities (e.g., insulin resistance, obesity, and dyslipidemia) may play an intermediary role in the connection between liver fibrosis and other metabolic disease. 24 , 39

Our research investigated the connections between LFSs and prognosis of heart failure, revealing that FIB‐4, NFS, and the AST/ALT ratio could potentially forecast adverse outcomes in heart failure patients. However, our study also had several limitations as follows. First, due to the nature of the investigation, the results are observational rather than causal. And the data in this research were not a very large sample size and relevant solely to the general American adult population, which may limit the broader applicability of our results. Additionally, the small sample size restricted the feasibility of conducting subgroup analyses with multifactorial adjustment. Further prospective, large‐scale, multi‐center, and multi‐sited investigations are needed to validate our findings. Second, although we adjusted for relevant covariates, there may still be unmeasured confounding factors that cannot be entirely excluded. Next, the data of LFSs or other variables were single without repeated measurements, which may not fully reflect the long‐term status and introduce instability when calculating LFS indicators. Last, some variables, such as heart failure, hypertension, and DM, were based on data obtained from the participants' self‐report, which may have led to an underestimation of true prevalence, recall bias, and self‐report bias. Furthermore, the accuracy of diagnosis determination could have been affected by patients' recall and the type and severity of heart failure were not specifically studied. Therefore, our study results may not be generalizable to populations outside of the NHANES sample. However, given the inherent challenges in objectively defining these variables in large‐scale studies and the strict adherence to the protocol in the NHANES, the level of information bias is likely to be acceptable. Further research is necessary to validate our findings.

CONCLUSION

Our study found that high noninvasive LFSs were significantly correlated with all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in heart failure patients and performed well to predict the risk of mortality. In addition, our findings suggest that AST/ALT ratio has good predictive capabilities and is easy to derive, making it more clinically useful.
