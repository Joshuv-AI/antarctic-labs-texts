Introduction

In patients with structural heart disease, incessant narrow-complex tachycardia may indicate ventricular tachycardia (VT) originating from the septal region and involving Purkinje-related re-entry mechanisms.1 Misdiagnosis of these arrhythmias can delay definitive therapy and increase the risk of progressive heart failure.2 Although advances in high-resolution electroanatomic mapping have improved the delineation of re-entrant VT circuits, entrainment pacing remains an essential method for confirming re-entry as the mechanism of tachycardia and for localizing a critical component of the circuit.3,4 Mapping the upper septal region and delivering radiofrequency (RF) energy within the intra-septal substrate, particularly near the conduction system, remains challenging. Pacing maneuvers and their responses in this region may still be crucial in guiding effective catheter ablation. We present a case of slow VT originating near the His–Purkinje system that was successfully ablated by targeting the septal exit. This report highlights important diagnostic and electrophysiological considerations, demonstrating the value of pacing maneuvers in identifying viable ablation targets within this complex region.

Case report

A 54-year-old patient with ischemic cardiomyopathy and a history of coronary artery bypass grafting and prophylactic implantable single-lead cardioverter defibrillator with atrial sensing (VDD-ICD) implantation was transferred from another hospital with a suspected diagnosis of persistent supraventricular tachycardia (SVT). The patient reported a 1-week history of epigastric discomfort and dizziness. Clinical examination revealed signs of acute heart failure and transthoracic echocardiography showed a severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of around 10%. The 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) showed continuous narrow-complex tachycardia with a cycle length of 510 ms, a QRS duration of 110 ms and P waves following the QRS complexes, compatible with either retrograde ventriculo-atrial conduction or an atrial tachycardia with long anterograde conduction. (Figure 1A). ICD interrogation revealed a pattern of persistent tachycardia with a short RP interval over several weeks. The tachycardia could be terminated by ventricular overdrive pacing via the ICD but would immediately reinitiate with a sinus beat. Following the exclusion of progression of coronary artery disease, an electrophysiological study was performed.

Mapping was performed using the EnSite X mapping system (Abbott, St. Paul, MN). After obtaining informed consent, catheter ablation of the tachycardia was undertaken. A decapolar electrode catheter (coronary sinus [CS] catheter) (Inquiry, Abbott) was placed in the great cardiac vein and a quadripolar catheter (Supreme, Abbott) in the right ventricular (RV) apex. Heparin was administered intravenously to maintain an activated clotting time of between 250 and 350 seconds. Entrainment pacing from the RV during tachycardia showed constant and progressive fusion, supporting a re-entrant mechanism of a VT. Entrainment from the atrium was not possible. Following transseptal puncture using a steerable sheath (Agilis, Abbott), a multielectrode mapping catheter (HD Grid, Abbott) was advanced into the left ventricular (LV).

Voltage mapping of both LV and RV, as well as high-density activation mapping during VT, were performed using the HD Grid catheter. Low-voltage areas, defined as bipolar electrogram amplitudes of less than 0.5 mV, were identified within the ventricular septum (Figure 1B). Total ventricular activation time was 510 ms, which was equal to the tachycardia cycle length (TCL). Activation mapping of LV and RV demonstrated a centrifugal spread with the earliest activation site (EAS) located in the upper septal region on both LV and RV (Figure 2), showing retrograde His–Purkinje activation. At the EAS on the RV septum, the local bipolar electrogram (D3–4) preceded QRS onset by 58 ms, whereas at the earliest LV septal site (A2–3) the electrogram preceded QRS onset by 51 ms. Therefore, RV activation was slightly earlier than LV activation at the septal exit (Figure 2B and 2C).

Low-amplitude, fractionated electrograms were recorded within the scar tissue at the LV EAS; however, ventricular entrainment pacing was not possible at this site. Using the HD Grid catheter, entrainment from the contralateral RV EAS demonstrated manifest fusion and a stimulus to QRS interval (S-QRS) equal to the EAS to QRS interval (EAS-QRS). The post-pacing interval (PPI) approximated the TCL (PPI-TCL = 10 ms), suggesting outer-loop participation in the re-entry circuit (S-QRS <30% TCL; S-QRS = electrogram-QRS; PPI-TCL = 10 ms). During entrainment at the RV EAS, the stimulus-to-atrium interval was equal to the His-to-atrium interval (Stim. to CS = His to CS) indicating local capture of the VT and proximal His–Purkinje system with retrograde atrial activation (Figure 2). Therefore, ablation was targeted at the LV EAS septal site to reduce the risk of direct injury to the His–Purkinje system. RF energy was delivered at 35 W using an irrigated ablation catheter (TactiCath, Abbott), leading to termination of the VT within 3.6 seconds (Figure 3). Additional lesions were applied without inducing atrioventricular (AV) block, despite transient left bundle branch block (LBBB), which completely resolved by the end of the procedure. The procedure was completed without complications, and clinical VT was non-inducible by the end because of successful substrate modification. The patient's condition improved rapidly, and he maintained a stable sinus rhythm with intact AV conduction; no VT recurrence was observed during the 2-month follow-up period.

Discussion

This case highlights a rare variant of slow VT with a short QRS duration in a patient with ischemic cardiomyopathy, involving the His–Purkinje system within the upper septal region. The diagnosis of intra-septal VT involving the His–Purkinje network was supported by 3 key findings: (1) activation mapping showed that the VT exit was confined to the basal septal scar, with a PPI almost identical to the TCL; (2) manifest entrainment at the EAS in the septal RV showed His–Purkinje capture; and (3) RF ablation on the LV septal side opposite the conduction system successfully terminated the VT.

The VT was initially misdiagnosed as SVT due to the presence of narrow-QRS complexes with LBBB morphology and retrograde P waves on the 12-lead ECG. Bogun et al1 compared narrow-QRS (QRS <145 ms) and wide-QRS (QRS >145 ms) VTs in post-infarction patients. They demonstrated that involvement of the Purkinje system accounts for relatively narrow QRS complexes during these VTs. Although most patients exhibited the right bundle branch block pattern typically associated with an inferior wall myocardial infarction, only 1 patient showed LBBB morphology (as in our case). In these cases, ablation near the His bundle suggested that a proximal portion of the His–Purkinje system, before bifurcation into the right and left bundle branches, was incorporated into the re-entry circuit. Evidence of involvement of the His–Purkinje system in our patient, in the form of retrograde His activation and an LBBB pattern, provides a mechanistic explanation for the unusually narrow-QRS complex despite the presence of extensive scar tissue. The His–Purkinje network may be a key component of many post-infarction VT circuits, highlighting the importance of detailed diagnostic mapping and entrainment maneuvers to accurately identify the re-entrant pathway and the most suitable ablation target.

VT ablation traditionally targets the region from the critical isthmus to the exit, identified by concealed entrainment, a PPI equal to or very close to the VT cycle length, and measurement of the stimulus-QRS interval. Concealed entrainment has long been considered the defining feature of a critical circuit site, whereas manifest entrainment is usually attributed to bystander capture or activation of the outer loop.3,4 However, increasing evidence suggests that these criteria are not mandatory for identifying a critical isthmus for successful ablation through pacing maneuvres.5, 6, 7 Manifest entrainment with a PPI matching the TCL in patients with post-infarction VT and intra-mural septal substrate may be sufficient to locate sites where ablation is nonetheless effective.8 Capture of myocardium surrounding the catheter tip appears to generate a QRS complex with local fusion, differing from the morphology of the clinical intra-septal VT. These observations indicate that manifest entrainment in septal intra-mural VT does not necessarily exclude proximity to a critical segment of the circuit, particularly when ablation at that site results in VT termination. However, we cannot safely exclude the possibility that the RV EAS exhibiting manifest entrainment represented part of an inner loop of the circuit near the exit, with VT termination resulting from conductive heating of an adjacent critical isthmus. Entrainment of the VT from the exactly opposite RV EAS demonstrated capture of the conduction system with ventriculo-atrial conduction. The identical intervals (Stim-CS = His-CS) therefore indicate true capture of the His–Purkinje system and may also explain the presence of manifest fusion despite entrainment response that otherwise resembled those of a VT exit.

As pacing at the contralateral LV EAS failed to capture the His–Purkinje system, ablation was performed at this site to minimize the risk of injury to the His–Purkinje network. A similar strategy is employed for patients with para-Hisian accessory pathways, where ablation from the non-coronary or right coronary cusp increases the distance from the AV node and His bundle.9 This improves procedural safety and long-term efficacy. VT terminated 3.6 seconds after RF energy was delivered to the EAS in the basal LV septum. This was preceded by a single ectopic beat with a shorter cycle length and slightly different QRS morphology, suggesting either catheter-induced or spontaneous ectopy at the ablation site just before termination. Nevertheless, given the incessant nature of the VT prior to ablation and its subsequent non-inducibility, we considered the termination to be related to energy delivery on the LV side. In a VT where most of the re-entry circuit is confined to the intra-septal myocardium, the full TCL is usually not expressed on the LV endocardial surface (total activation time <TCL).5 In our case, however, the full TCL of 510 ms was present on the LV activation map despite basal intra-septal involvement, suggesting that a small part of the VT circuit was located within the septum. Nevertheless, this intra-septal segment appears to constitute the critical isthmus near the His–Purkinje system, whereas the remainder of the re-entrant pathway propagates along the LV. Overall, these findings support the existence of a slow re-entry circuit involving the basal septal scar in LV and RV involving the His–Purkinje system.

This case highlights the importance of high-density activation mapping, anatomical knowledge, ablation response and integrating pacing maneuver interpretation when diagnosing and treating Purkinje-related septal VTs.

Conclusion

Narrow-QRS tachycardia in structural heart disease may represent ventricular re-entry involving the His–Purkinje system. Recognition of this rare but important entity is essential for correct diagnosis and therapy. This case highlights that entrainment can help identify a critical part of the re-entry circuit when corroborated by activation mapping and ablation response. High-density mapping enabled precise localization and safe ablation in this case, leading to lasting arrhythmia suppression and clinical improvement.
