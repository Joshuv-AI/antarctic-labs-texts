Electrocatalytic removal of ciprofloxacin using modified Ti/TiO2 anodes with ZrO2 and Bi2O3 nanoparticles modeled via CCD-RSM approach
Authors: Mahdieh Najarian, Fatemeh Mahmoudian, Farideh Nabizadeh Chianeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research examined the electrocatalytic removal of the antibiotic ciprofloxacin (CIP) using Ti/TiO2 anodes modified with ZrO2 and Bi2O3 nanoparticles. The anodes were prepared utilizing the simple electrophoretic deposition technique, leading to the development of a uniform, dense, and stable nanoparticle coating. Physicochemical characterization through field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) demonstrated a porous morphology and confirmed the presence of Ti, O, Zr, and Bi within the coating, while X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed the crystalline TiO2 matrix and the presence of ZrO2 and Bi2O3 phases. Furthermore, electrochemical assessments indicated that the ternary nanocomposite Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 anode exhibited reduced charge transfer resistance (Rct = 8925 Ω/cm2), enhanced electrocatalytic performance, and a significantly longer lifespan (approximately 150 h) compared to other samples. Moreover, according to the results derived from modeling with R2 = 0.9781, the influence of operational variables was examined, revealing that acidic pH, minimal Na2SO4 concentration, moderate current density, and suitable process duration significantly enhanced the removal efficiency. Ultimately, the results suggest that the proposed anode has significant potential to provide researchers with new opportunities for the design and improvement of anodes.Keywords: Anode, TiO2, ZrO2, Bi2O3, Ciprofloxacin, RemovalSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Pharmaceuticals, especially antibiotics, are one of the most important emerging water pollutants and pose a major environmental challenge, and their high persistence has led to their long-term presence in water resources1–3. The widespread use of antibiotics in treating human and animal diseases, and even as livestock growth promoters, has led to the release of large amounts of these compounds into the environment4–6. Estimates suggest that up to 95% of consumed antibiotics are not metabolized in the body and are excreted into the environment7,8. One of the most important groups of these compounds is the fluoroquinolones, widely used in human and veterinary medicine due to their broad spectrum of activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria9,10. Reports indicate that fluoroquinolones have been detected in hospital effluents, municipal wastewater, and pharmaceutical industrial effluents at varying concentrations11. In particular, ciprofloxacin (CIP) is one of the most widely used fluoroquinolones for treating various infections, and a significant portion of is excreted unchanged from the body and enters the aquatic environment8,12. In addition to threatening human health, this has extensive negative effects on aquatic ecosystems, including algae, zooplankton, fish, and aquatic plants13. Therefore, the presence of these compounds in water resources underscores the need for new and efficient technologies to remove them. Since conventional treatment methods (such as biological and chemical processes) have limited capacity to completely remove these compounds, developing and applying advanced technologies for their removal has become one of the main challenges in sustainable water resource management14–16.

In line with the need for efficient removal of persistent compounds such as ciprofloxacin, the electrochemical oxidation (EO) method has been recognized as one of the most advanced pollutant removal technologies. In addition to requiring no external chemical additives, this method is easily controllable and applicable to various types of wastewater17,18. The efficiency of the EO process depends on multiple factors. The anode plays a key role because the production of electrical oxidants and the oxidation of pollutants occur in the vicinity of the anode surface. Therefore, the selection and development of a suitable anode is one of the important challenges in these processes and can significantly influence efficiency19,20. To date, several types of anodes, including platinum, boron-doped diamond (BDD), graphite, and mixed metal oxide (MMO) anodes, have been introduced for EO processes. Among them, MMO anodes typically consist of a substrate coated with one or more active metal oxides (RuO2, SnO2, TiO2, etc.), which are widely used in treatment21,22. Titanium is one of the most common substrates for these types of anodes due to its high mechanical and chemical resistance, thermal stability, and non-toxicity. However, its stability and electrocatalytic activity are limited by the formation of an inactive layer on the surface. To overcome this problem, surface modification of the substrate with metal oxides has been proposed, which can provide suitable properties for use in EO processes23,24. In this regard, Ti/TiO2 anodes have received considerable attention due to their low cost, abundance, and environmental friendliness; however, improving their electrocatalytic performance remains challenging25,26. As an effective strategy to address these challenges, Ti/TiO2 anodes can be modified with various nanostructured modifiers, each of which has its own characteristics and advantages. Among these, the use of ZrO2 nanoparticles (forming a TiO2–ZrO2 composite coating) can increase the specific surface area and enhance the catalytic activity of the anode, while providing improved stability. This improvement is attributed to the outstanding properties of ZrO2, such as high mechanical strength, favorable corrosion resistance, and electrochemical activity27–29. However, these anodes also have limitations in terms of service lifetime, which can be mitigated by incorporating additional modifying nanoparticles. In this way, Bi2O3 nanoparticles have been proposed as a suitable modifier to improve the performance of Ti/TiO2-ZrO2 anodes. Owing to their high chemical and electrochemical stability, abundance, non-toxicity, and relatively low cost, Bi2O3 nanoparticles is able to significantly increase the stability and electrochemical activity of the anode30,31. Therefore, Bi2O3 was chosen as a co-modifier to enhance both the activity and durability of Ti/TiO2–ZrO2 anodes. Accordingly, in this study, the electrophoretic deposition (EPD) method was employed to prepare a TiO2-ZrO2-Bi2O3 nanoparticle composite coating on a titanium substrate, which is regarded a suitable method for anode preparation due to its significant advantages, such as thickness control, coating uniformity, and ease of process24,31. Hence, more detailed studies on the simultaneous incorporation of ZrO2 and Bi2O3 nanoparticles as functional modifiers on the surface of Ti/TiO2 anodes can provide valuable insights for researchers in this field. Moreover, to further investigate the performance of the proposed electrode in the electrochemical removal of CIP, the Central Composite Design (CCD), as a subset of Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to model the operating conditions and analyze the interaction between effective parameters in the removal process. This statistical approach adds significant value to the present work. Despite the various studies on Ti-based anodes for antibiotic degradation, the combined modification of Ti/TiO2 with both ZrO2 and Bi2O3 nanoparticles via EPD has not been systematically investigated, particularly for fluoroquinolone antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin. This study uniquely explores the synergistic effect of co-modification on the electrocatalytic performance and stability of Ti/TiO2 anodes. Moreover, the process optimization through a CCD-RSM modeling approach provides valuable insights into the interactive influence of operating parameters on ciprofloxacin removal efficiency. It is hoped that the findings of this research will serve as an effective step toward the advancement of electrode materials.

Experimental

Material

In this study, titanium dioxide (TiO2), zirconium dioxide (ZrO2), and bismuth oxide (Bi2O3) nanoparticles were utilized to modify the surface of the titanium substrate. Table 1 shows the specifications of these nanoparticles, all of which were purchased from Neutrino. Oxalic acid (C4H2O4) with a purity of 98%, purchased from Merck, was used to clean the surface of the titanium substrate. Nickel (II) chloride (NiCl2·6H2O), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH), all obtained from Merck, were utilized in the coating process, as electrolytes in the removal tests and electrochemical analyses, as well as to adjust the pH under different removal conditions, respectively. CIP was obtained as a model pollutant from Sanamed Pharmaceutical Company. It is worth noting that isopropanol (C3H8O) was used as a solvent obtained from Dr. Mojallaly Chemical Industries Company to prepare the coating suspension. Double-distilled water was used in all stages of the experiments.

Preparation of electrodes

Titanium sheets were cut into two different dimensions (1 cm × 1 cm and 2 cm × 2 cm) and attached to a titanium rod. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the prepared electrodes were initially immersed in a sodium hydroxide solution at 90 °C, followed by immersion in a boiling oxalic acid solution for a specific time to clean the substrate surface32. Then, the electrodes were washed in an ultrasonic bath. The next step involved preparing a suspension for coating the Ti/TiO2-ZrO2-Bi2O3 nanocomposite electrode via the EPD method, utilizing a suspension of TiO2:ZrO2:Bi2O3 nanoparticles in a proportion of (80:10:10) with isopropanol solvent. Additionally, a small amount of NiCl2 (charging salt) was added to the solution to enhance the adhesion of the nanoparticles. The resulting suspension was placed in an ultrasonic bath to effectively disperse the nanoparticles within the solvent. Following the preparation of the suspension, two titanium electrodes served as anodes while a single titanium electrode functioned as the cathode in the suspension. Subsequently, a nanocomposite coating was formed on both sides of the cathode surface by applying a voltage. In the final stage, the prepared electrode was placed in a furnace for 3 h at 350 °C. The same process was repeated to prepare electrodes coated with different nanoparticles, namely Ti/TiO2, and Ti/TiO2–ZrO2, for comparison purposes.

Stepwise illustration of the electrode preparation and coating process using EPD.

Electrodes’ characterization

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was employed to investigate the surface morphology of Ti/TiO2, Ti/TiO2–ZrO2, and Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 electrodes. Microscopic images were captured with a TE-SCAN MIRA3 model device made in the Czech Republic at magnifications of 1 μm and 500 nm. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was utilized for both quantitative and qualitative assessment of the elements found in the coatings with an Oxford instrument produced in the United Kingdom. In order to identify the crystal structure and phase characteristics of the samples, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed with a German-made Bruker D8 instrument, utilizing CuKα radiation with a wavelength of 1.5406 Å. To examine the electrochemical properties and electrocatalytic performance of the electrodes, a series of electrochemical tests, including linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), chronoamperometry (CA), and chronopotentiometry (CP), were conducted. These evaluations were recorded using an Origa Flex device (Origalys, France). EIS analysis was also performed using an IviumState potentiostat–galvanostat model Vertex (Netherlands), and the equivalent circuit was plotted and analyzed using Z-View software. All electrochemical tests were performed using a three-electrode cell in which the Ag/AgCl electrode served as the reference electrode, platinum acted as the auxiliary electrode, and each of the Ti/TiO2, Ti/TiO2–ZrO2, and Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 electrodes with dimensions of 1 × 1 cm2 was placed in the electrolyte solution as the working electrode.

Results and discussion

Physio-chemical characterization

Based on digital and FESEM images of the electrodes at 1 μm and 500 nm scales (Fig. 2), the TiO2 coating structure on the Ti/TiO2 electrode clearly has a granular morphology. The addition of ZrO2 nanoparticles to the Ti/TiO2–ZrO2 binary nanocomposite electrode altered the morphology, resulting in a more uniform and denser coating surface. In addition to improving the density of the coating, these changes have led to a structure with finer grains and a more uniform distribution of particles. The finer grain size and improved uniformity can lead to an increase in the available active surface area of the electrode and, as a result, improve its efficiency in electrochemical reactions. On the other hand, this dense and uniform microstructure, as an effective barrier, increases the resistance to electrochemical degradation. In particular, the addition of ZrO2 improves the stability of the coating by reducing the number of surface cracks and pores and can play a role in increasing the durability and longevity of the electrode. In addition to improving the stability, these changes also improve the electrocatalytic activity of the electrode34–36. Furthermore, the incorporation of Bi2O3 to form the ternary Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 nanocomposite electrode has a significant effect on the microstructure of the coating. Examination of the images shows that the presence of this oxide creates a much more homogeneous surface, and the particles are dispersed with a more uniform distribution on the surface. Such a structure, in addition to optimizing the surface roughness, can provide better conditions for charge exchange and electron transfer in electrochemical reactions, and directly contribute to improving the electrode performance in these reactions37–39. Overall, it is expected that these structural changes resulting from the addition of ZrO2 and Bi2O3 will not only improve the physical and mechanical properties of the coating but also increase the electrochemical activity and enhance the electrode performance in the long term.

EDS analysis is among the most commonly employed analytical techniques for identifying and quantifying the percentage of elements found in a sample. Consequently, this analysis was conducted to verify the existence of the desired elements in the composition of the nanocomposite coatings. The findings of this analysis, illustrated in Fig. 3, clearly show the existence of Ti, O, Zr, and Bi elements in the corresponding coatings.

Figure 4 illustrates the XRD patterns of Ti/TiO2, Ti/TiO2–ZrO2, and Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 nanocomposite electrodes prepared through electrophoretic deposition. In this figure, the distinctive diffraction peaks of TiO2 on the crystal planes (101), (004), (200), (105), (211), (204), and (220) can be observed at angles of 2θ = 25.28°, 37.8°, 48.2°, 53.1°, 55.1°, 62.7°, and 70.6°, in the Ti/TiO2 electrode, which are also present in the other modified electrodes34. Moreover, the peaks associated with the (110), (111), and (011) planes at angles of 2θ = 24.03°, 28.07°, and 31.34° for ZrO2 nanoparticles in the Ti/ZrO2 electrode are similarly detected in the binary and ternary nanocomposite electrodes40. The diffraction peaks (112), (121), (113), and (222) observed at angles of 2θ = 26.14°, 28.32°, 38.43°, and 39.95° suggest the existence of Bi2O3 nanoparticles within the ternary nanocomposite electrode structure31,41. The findings confirm the existence of TiO2, ZrO2, and Bi2O3 nanoparticles in the coating that has developed on the titanium substrate’s surface. It is also evident that some peaks partially overlap, influencing the intensity variation of the peaks in the modified electrodes or causing the peaks to be broader than those in the unmodified electrodes.

Electrochemical characterization

Figure 5 displays the LSV curves for Ti/TiO2, Ti/TiO2–ZrO2, and Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 electrodes, each measuring 1 × 1 cm2, recorded at a scan rate of 50 mV/s in a 0.1 M Na2SO4 electrolyte solution (in potential range of 0—3 V). According to this test, the values for oxygen evolution potential (OEP) were assessed for each electrode. As illustrated in this figure, incorporating ZrO2 and Bi2O3 nanoparticles into the TiO2 coating enhanced the OEP value of the ternary nanocomposite electrodes. The rise in OEP value in the modified electrodes can be linked to the enhancement of the electrode’s active surface area, and the synergistic interactions among TiO2, ZrO2, and Bi2O331. A higher OEP indicates that the electrode can sustain a broader potential window before the onset of oxygen evolution, allowing more of the applied current to be utilized for generating reactive oxygen species instead of gas evolution. These reactive species are primarily responsible for the oxidation and degradation of ciprofloxacin molecules.

LSV of the prepared anodes in 0.1M Na2SO4 at room temperature.

Establishing the lifespan of the electrode is one of the essential factors in optimizing and altering its structure. The CP test is employed as a technique to assess the electrochemical stability of the electrode. In this test, a steady current density is used, and the potential variations are evaluated over time. Nonetheless, at the laboratory scale and with low current densities, the electrode lifespan cannot be directly predicted for industrial settings with elevated current densities. Consequently, Eq. (2) is employed to determine the real electrode lifespan, with i1 and i2 representing the actual and accelerated currents, while τ1 and τ2 denote the actual and accelerated lifetimes, respectively42,43.

CP testing was conducted for Ti/TiO2, Ti/TiO2–ZrO2, and Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 electrodes, with dimensions of 1 × 1 cm2, in 0.1 M Na2SO4 electrolyte solution and a constant current density of 50 mA/cm2. The graphs of this test are shown in Fig. 6. The findings indicated that the measured lifetimes of the electrodes were determined to be 20.4, 21.4, and 150.78 h, respectively (according to Eq. 2 and Fig. 6). It is evident that incorporating ZrO2 nanoparticles, particularly with a minor addition of Bi2O3 in the nanocomposite framework, has enhanced conductivity and extended the lifespan. Moreover, the incorporation of Bi2O3 nanoparticles has resulted in a more even coating on the surface of the Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 electrode. This is likely a result of decreased surface cracking, which increases the active sites and thereby improves the electrocatalytic activity. The CP test outcomes suggest that the ternary TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 composite may serve as an effective choice for enhancing the stability and efficiency of electrodes employed in electrochemical oxidation processes.

The CA test was conducted to further assess the electrocatalytic performance and electrochemical stability of the electrodes. In this method, the current density is recorded over time at a constant potential, and a decrease in current signifies surface passivation or degradation. The smaller this reduction, the greater the electrocatalytic performance and chemical stability of the electrode44,45. The prepared electrodes, serving as working electrodes with dimensions of 1 × 1 cm2, underwent the CA test in a 0.1 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution at a constant potential of 4 V. The results illustrated in Fig. 7 indicate that the modified Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 electrode displayed a greater current density compared to the other electrodes, and this value remained more stable throughout the testing period. According to these findings, the inclusion of ZrO2 and Bi2O3 modifying nanoparticles within the nanocomposite framework has elevated the count of active surface sites, boosted electrical conductivity, and strengthened the chemical stability of the electrode.

CA curve of Ti/TiO2, Ti/TiO2–ZrO2, and Ti/TiO2–ZrO2-Bi2O3 electrodes in 0.1M Na2SO4.

To precisely examine the electrochemical properties and particularly the charge transfer resistance (Rct) in the electrodes, EIS measurements were conducted on three electrode samples: Ti/TiO2, Ti/TiO2–ZrO2, and Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3. This approach is a crucial instrument for examining surface characteristics and assessing the kinetics of reactions between the electrode and electrolyte, offering both quantitative and qualitative insights into charge transfer processes and resistances within the system46,47. Measurements were conducted in a Na2SO4 electrolyte solution at a concentration of 0.1 M, covering a frequency range from 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz, and at open circuit potential. Figure 8 shows that the EIS spectra for all three samples feature a semicircular arc in the high-frequency area. The arc is directly connected to the charge transfer resistance; reducing the arc enhances the electron transfer process and boosts the electrocatalytic efficiency48,49. The analysis of the results reveals that the Ti/TiO2-ZrO2-Bi2O3 electrode exhibits the smallest semicircular arc in the high-frequency region, indicating a lower charge transfer resistance (Rct) and consequently faster electron transfer compared to the other two electrodes (Table 4). The reduced Rct is attributed to the even distribution of ZrO2 and Bi2O3 nanoparticles on the Ti/TiO2 substrate surface, resulting in a rise in active site numbers, enhanced conductivity, and improved surface interaction between the electrode and the electrolyte. Lower Rct indicates more efficient electron transfer at the interface and correlates with the improved current responses observed in CA. The EIS data were fitted according to the equivalent circuit model Rs\[CPE(Rct)]. The parameter Rs denotes the ohmic resistance of the solution, whereas Constant Phase Element (CPE) accounts for the non-ideal capacitive behavior of the electrode surface due to surface heterogeneity and roughness50,51.

Taken together, the observed improvements in coating morphology, stability, and electrochemical behavior can be mechanistically attributed to the complementary roles of ZrO2 and Bi2O3 which ZrO2 primarily improves coating density, reduces crack/porosity formation, and contributes to mechanical/electrochemical stability, while Bi2O3 further optimizes surface homogeneity and active-site accessibility. Together, these effects reduce charge-transfer resistance, raise the OEP, limit passivation, and increase the number of active sites, providing a consistent mechanistic explanation for the observed enhancements in electrocatalytic activity and electrode longevity.

The removal and oxidation of pollutants in electrochemical systems can be achieved in two pathways: direct oxidation, where the reaction takes place directly on the anode surface, and indirect oxidation, which happens through generating strong oxidant species in the electrolyte medium, such as hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and sulfate radicals (SO4•⁻)24. To determine the prevailing mechanism and examine the electrochemically “active” behavior (direct participation in electron transfer) or “inactive” behavior (primarily facilitating indirect oxidation) of the final Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 anode surface, CV tests were conducted in both modes: one containing the antibiotic CIP in the electrolyte and another without it. As illustrated in Fig. 9, following the addition of CIP to the Na2SO4 solution, no emergence of a new peak was noted when compared to the pure electrolyte solution. This suggests that the Ti/TiO2-ZrO2–Bi2O3 anode exhibits inactive behavior and that the process of pollutant oxidation mainly occurs indirectly.

Modeling of removal process

RSM is a powerful mathematical and statistical method employed to model, enhance, and examine the impacts and operational variables on a process’s response. This method, by concurrently assessing the effects and interrelations of variables, enables the determination of optimal conditions and, particularly in intricate processes, results in considerable reductions in time and costs52. In this study, RSM was utilized to enhance the operational parameters influencing ciprofloxacin (CIP) removal using the Ti/TiO2-ZrO2-Bi2O3 anode. The data obtained were analyzed statistically utilizing Minitab software, evaluating multiple indices, with the correlation coefficient and adjusted correlation coefficient being the key metrics for assessing model fit quality; values near one signify a strong agreement of the model with the experimental data. According to the findings of this study, the values of R2 = 0.9781 and R2adj = 0.9589 were achieved, with both being quite near to one. These numbers suggest that more than 97% of the variability in CIP removal is explained by the proposed quadratic model, leaving only about 2.19% of unexplained variation, which is quite minimal. Consequently, it can be inferred that the created model aligns closely with the experimental data53,54. Subsequently, Analysis of variance (ANOVA) results further confirmed the adequacy of the quadratic model. Table 5 shows the outcomes of this test for the CIP elimination process.

Along with the regression coefficients, residuals (the discrepancy between observed and predicted values) are crucial for evaluating the model’s adequacy. Residuals represent the portions of data variation that the model cannot account for, and these variations are anticipated to follow a normal distribution. A typical approach for assessing the normality of residuals is to utilize a normal probability plot55. In Fig. 10, the actual residual values are graphed against the anticipated values according to a normal distribution. The experimental data exhibit a linear trend, with points closely aligning along a straight line; this suggests a normal distribution of the residuals and, in turn, confirms the stability of the normality assumption for the errors in the model. Moreover, Fig. 10 presents a graph displaying the residuals in relation to the fitted values. The irregular spread of the residuals around the zero line, lacking a consistent pattern, shows that there is no systematic relationship between the residuals and the predicted values. This aspect is viewed as evidence of the model’s effectiveness in forecasting the removal of the CIP antibiotic. A random oscillatory pattern is seen in the residual histogram, aligning with the assumptions of traditional statistical methods. In the observation order graph, the residuals fluctuate randomly around the central line, signifying no evident trend and uniformity of error variances55,56.

(7) was utilized to assess the influence of variables on the removal efficiency of pharmaceutical pollutants and determine the percentage contribution of each factor to the response. Figure 11 illustrates the Pareto analysis regarding the removal of CIP antibiotics. Electrolysis time exhibited the most pronounced effect (31.23%), indicating that longer reaction times enhance radical generation and pollutant degradation. The significant effects of current density (11.25%) and pH–electrolyte concentration interaction (11.83%) suggest that electrochemical activity and solution conductivity jointly determine the formation of reactive oxidants such as hydroxyl (•OH) and sulfate radicals (SO4•–). In the quadratic model, positive coefficients suggest that the variable enhances removal efficiency, whereas negative coefficients imply a reduction in efficiency with a rise in the variable. Three-dimensional response surface analysis

Figure 12a illustrates the response surface of the interaction between current density and the concentration of Na2SO4 electrolyte. The current density is a crucial factor in the electrochemical oxidation process in terms of economics, as it influences the generation of oxidant species and electron transfer. Conversely, a higher concentration of Na2SO4 enhances the process’s efficiency by boosting the solution’s conductivity and introducing oxidant species. Considering the negative interaction coefficient, the effectiveness of these two parameters is greater when they act in opposing directions. However, excessive current or salt concentration may cause side reactions or oxidant recombination, thereby slightly lowering efficiency24,58. Figure 12b shows the interaction effect between Na2SO4 concentration and removal process time. The negative value of the interaction coefficient indicates that enhancing one while reducing the other results in greater efficiency. At lower salt levels, extended reaction time allows sufficient radical generation and diffusion, maintaining high removal efficiency. Figure 12c displays the relationship between pH and the concentration of Na2SO4. The positive interaction coefficient suggests a combined effect of these two factors on the CIP removal efficiency. This outcome arises from the formation of potent oxidant species in an acidic environment and the influence of pH in promoting electron transfer and the breakdown of pollutants. Lower pH enhances the formation of highly reactive •OH radicals, and reduced sulfate ion concentrations diminish the capability of SO42- ions to scavenge OH radicals, leading to a greater availability of radicals for CIP degradation59,60.

3D plots of various factors’ effect on CIP removal.

Figure 12d provides the interaction effect between removal process time and current density. Due to the negative interaction coefficient, enhancing current density and decreasing time results in increased efficiency. A shorter treatment duration with a higher current density still results in better removal, as the enhanced radical production offsets the decrease in contact time. The rate of oxidation increases considerably as a greater number of oxidizing agents are generated per unit time61. Figure 12e illustrates the relationship between pH and current density. The negative interaction coefficient shows that optimal efficiency occurs under high current density and low pH conditions. This outcome is attributed to the heightened rate of electron transfer and greater production of hydroxyl radicals in more acidic conditions and at increased currents59,60.

Optimum conditions

A primary goal of the experimental design and optimization process is to identify and determine the optimal values of the effective variables in order to achieve the highest efficiency of removing pharmaceutical contaminants from the aqueous environment. This plays an important role in improving the efficiency of the process, reducing operating costs, and minimizing environmental impacts. Accordingly, using the proposed models obtained from the CCD method, the optimal values of each of the key parameters were determined. The results of the analyses showed that the optimal conditions for the effective removal of CIP antibiotics by the Ti/TiO2-ZrO2–Bi2O3 anode include pH equal to 5, current density of 1.75 mA/cm2, Na2SO4 electrolyte concentration equal to 1 g/L, and removal process time of 240 min. Under these conditions, the model prediction for CIP removal efficiency was 77.3%, while the experimental removal efficiency obtained was 73.2%.

Conclusion

In this study, a Ti/TiO2 anode co-modified with ZrO2 and Bi2O3 nanoparticles (denoted as Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3) was fabricated using the EPD method, and its physicochemical and electrochemical properties were investigated. The physicochemical analyses confirmed the formation of a uniform coating and the successful presence of the modifying nanoparticles. Electrochemical analyses further revealed that the simultaneous incorporation of ZrO2 and Bi2O3 nanoparticles reduced the charge transfer resistance and enhanced the electrode stability of the Ti/TiO2 anode. The operational lifetime of the anode reached approximately 150 h, indicating improved resistance to electrochemical corrosion and extended operational durability. To evaluate the electrochemical performance of the developed electrode, it was applied for the removal of pharmaceutical pollutant CIP. The process was modeled and optimized using a CCD–RSM, achieving an R2 value of 0.9781 and CIP removal efficiency of 73.2% under optimal conditions (values of 5, 1.75 mA/cm2, 1 g/L, and 240 min for pH, current density, Na2SO4 electrolyte concentration, and removal process time, respectively). Considering the aim of this work, which was to propose a new approach for Ti/TiO2 modification, the Ti/TiO2–ZrO2–Bi2O3 anode represents an innovative ternary structure demonstrating synergistic effects between the two nanoparticles. The anode exhibited promising performance at the laboratory scale, paving the way for further research. Therefore, future studies should focus on testing in real matrices, identifying intermediate by-products, energetic evaluation of the process, and conducting scale-up investigations to facilitate the transfer of this technology toward industrial applications.
