Schwann cell-secreted frizzled-related protein 1 dictates neuroinflammation and peripheral nerve degeneration after neurotrauma
Authors: Xiangyun Yao, Lingchi Kong, Yi Qiao, David Brand, Juehong Li, Zhiwen Yan, Song Guo Zheng, Yun Qian, Cunyi Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeurotrauma in limbs can induce sustained neuroinflammation, resulting in persistent disruption of nerve tissue architecture and retardation of axon regrowth. Despite macrophage-mediated inflammation promoting the removal of necrotic neural components and stimulating neo-vessel ingrowth, detrimental shifts in macrophage phenotype exacerbate nerve degeneration. Herein, we find that peripheral nerve injuries (PNIs) result in abundant secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (sFRP1) expression, particularly by Schwann cells (SCs). Heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) in macrophages recognizes sFRP1 and triggers a dysregulated secretion of inflammatory mediators. Single-cell atlas of human injured peripheral nerves reveals the appearance of sFRP1-expressing SCs with mesenchymal traits and macrophages with a proinflammatory genetic profile. Deletion of either SC-specific sFRP1 or macrophage-specific HSP90 alleviates neuroinflammation and prevents the progression of nerve degeneration. Together, our findings implicate the response of macrophages to SC-derived sFRP1 in exacerbating nerve damage following PNIs.Keywords: axon regeneration, neuroinflammation, secreted frizzled-related protein 1, heat shock protein 90, peripheral nerve injury

Introduction

Neurotrauma in limbs is commonly encountered in patients admitted to trauma centers (2%–3%).1 Following peripheral nerve trauma, crushed nerves mount a robust regenerative response. However, the intrinsic regeneration capacity is extremely limited in transected nerves and long-gap nerve defects, which inevitably confronts axonal regrowth retardation and irreversible degenerative changes.2 In this context, surgical intervention always leads to unsatisfied clinical outcome, including motor and sensory function loss, paralysis, and neuropathic pain.3

Peripheral nerve regeneration relies on axon guidance and neurotrophic support from Schwann cells (SCs).4 SC-produced pro-regenerative signals are transmitted within nerve extracellular matrix (ECM) and thus provide a supportive milieu for axon regrowth.5 Injury can also be sensed by tissue as a damage signal, which initiates detrimental ECM remodeling.6 In posttraumatic nerve tissue, SCs respond to the injury signal by shifting their phenotype to an earlier developmental stage to initiate a regeneration program.7 Detrimental shifts in SC phenotype and function can also lead to persistent neuroinflammation underlying neuronal regenerative decline.8 Despite all of these information, our understanding of the growth suppressors in nerve tissue remains poor, hindering progress in the development of effective therapies for nerve repair.

Upon peripheral nerve injuries (PNIs), macrophages accumulate in the injured site and represent the most important immune effector cells in peripheral nerves.9 While proinflammatory macrophages remove necrotic neural components and induce the ingrowth of neo-vessels, defective macrophage programs extend the local disruption of nerve tissue architecture produced by the initial insult.10 Overactive neuroinflammation by macrophages is destructive to nerve regeneration while an antagonist-based treatment holds great promise to enhance neurite outgrowth.11 However, the identification of a specific molecular target which can dictate the detrimental programs of macrophages in PNIs is less clear and likely complex.

In this study, we seek to identify a therapeutic target for blockade to improve the nerve healing process. We find that SCs secrete abundant secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (sFRP1) in response to neurotrauma. sFRP1 is a highly hydrophilic, secreted protein recognized as the antagonist of Wnt signaling.12 In the central nervous system (CNS), elevated sFRP1 binds to ADAMs and interferes with the activation of amyloid precursor protein, thus causing amyloid plaque deposition.13 In addition, sFRP1 can be secreted by astrocytes to activate microglia and amplify inflammation in the aged brain.14,15 Despite its role in CNS diseases, sFRP1 remains unexplored in the context of peripheral nervous system (PNS) diseases.

Here, we identify a detrimental role for sFRP1 in exacerbating nerve degeneration. Genetic deletion of sFRP1 in SCs improves axon regeneration in mice by alleviating overactive neuroinflammation. Mechanistically, sFRP1 binds to heat shock protein (HSP90) and subsequently activates downstream nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling. Depletion of HSP90 in macrophages partly abrogates the proinflammatory activities in vitro and attenuates neuroinflammation exerted by sFRP1 in vivo. Furthermore, we confirm that the sFRP1-neutralizing antibody can improve axon regeneration. Therefore, sFRP1-targeted therapy holds great promise for treating PNIs.

Results

The temporospatial patterning of peripheral nerves following neurotrauma

In an independent patient cohort of neurotrauma (19 females, 26 males), distal stumps of tibial nerves were freshly collected from mangled limbs during amputation surgeries, for the preparation of pathologic specimens. Patient demographics are summarized in Table S1. We evaluated the Wallerian degeneration of patients with PNI in pathologic specimens derived at 0–3, 3–12, and >12 h post injury. Based on transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results, degenerative changes were exacerbated along the time course, in terms of myelin sheath swelling, dissociation, and impaired axon integrity (Figures S1A–S1C). Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining findings further confirmed that the degenerative changes worsened in human nerves at different time points (at 0–3, 3–12, and >12 h post injury, n = 6 biological replicates, respectively) (Figures S1D and S1E). Nerve samples collected at later time points due to delayed amputation surgery showed worsened degenerative changes in the distal nerve specimens. The TEM images also revealed that mitochondria in injured nerves deteriorated along the time course into punctate and swell morphology with damage to cristae (Figures S1F and S1G). Therefore, following severe nerve trauma, Wallerian degeneration in human distal nerves showed temporal progression.

Proinflammatory macrophages dominated the early nerve injury response and accumulated at the injured site and distal nerves (Figure S1H). The posttraumatic nerve microenvironment provides an inductive niche for SC phenotype switching from a differentiated state to a proliferative state.7 P75NTR is a signaling adapter molecule that mediates apoptosis induced by nerve growth factor (NGF) and distributes mainly in SCs.16 For this study, the expression of p75NTR, a marker of dedifferentiated SCs,17 was also analyzed by immunofluorescence (IF). Abundant expressions of p75NTR were shown in injured nerves from patients (Figure S1I), which indicated the activation of repairing program and phenotypic transition in SCs. Apart from nerve degenerative changes, PNIs also activate endogenous reparative processes including increased proliferation and migration of support cells in the injured area.18 Previous studies reported that acute insults could induce the proliferation of nerve mesenchymal cells and fibroblasts at the distal nerve stumps that supported axon regrowth.19 In this study, we also analyzed Ki67 expression in human nerves at different time points to identify whether regenerative responses were initiated following nerve injuries. For patients who came to the hospital within the first 3 h post injury, slight expression of Ki67 was seen in human nerve sections. Marked expression of Ki67 was noticed in 3–12 h post injury and was continuously increasing for more than 12 h. This possibly explained the posttraumatic remodeling of human nerves at the early stages (Figures S1J and S1K).

These results indicated that human peripheral nerves underwent progressive degeneration, accompanied by regenerative changes, under the context of neurotrauma. Considering the complexity of posttraumatic nerve microenvironment, the key extracellular signal that dictates nerve degenerative or regenerative responses awaits discovery.

sFRP1 is associated with posttraumatic nerve degeneration and incites axon demyelination and SC degeneration

PNIs initiate the execution of a series of repair programs that consist of a temporal sequence of structural, functional, and compositional changes. To identify key proteins that could hinder nerve regeneration, we constructed a mouse sciatic nerve injury model and harvested the distal nerves at 24 h post transection, a time point when the expression of injury-specific cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) reaches peak levels.20,21 Then, we employed mass spectrometry to perform proteomic analysis on the nerve tissue from normal mice and nerve-transected mice (Figure 1A). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed a distinct clustering distribution between injured and uninjured nerve samples (Figure 1B). We identified 740 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) with at least a 1.5-fold change in a total of 6,584 proteins examined from all samples. Among these proteins, the levels of 238 proteins were significantly downregulated while the levels of 502 proteins were significantly upregulated in the injured sciatic nerves (Figures 1C and S2A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis showed that metabolism-related pathways were enriched in injured nerves (Figure S2B). Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the extracellular exosomes and extracellular space were highly enriched cellular components in injured nerves (Figure S2C). Based on GO analysis, DEPs were enriched in cell proliferation and immune responses in terms of biological process and molecular function (Figures 1D and S2D). The ECM provides abundant biomechanical and biochemical signals to control fundamental cellular processes in nerve regeneration.22 Considering that ECM protein changes can directly affect regeneration, we then characterized and compared injury-induced protein changes in ECM, which might underlie the complex nerve repair process (Figure 1E). Of these candidates, we found that sFRP1, a non-canonical WNT antagonist, was one of the most differentially secreted factors in the ECM. This difference was confirmed in a western blot analysis of healthy and injured mouse nerves, in which a significant increase of sFRP1 levels was seen in three injured nerve samples (Figures 1F and 1G). This finding was noteworthy, as previous neuroscience studies revealed that sFRP1 upregulation was associated with the pathogenesis of several neurodegenerative diseases and inflammatory conditions.13,15

We next examined the role of increased sFRP1 on nerve repair. We used a sFRP1 inhibitor, WAY-316606 (500 nM, 5 μL), for intraneural administration. We analyzed the reparative outcome of injured nerves at 3 weeks post injury, a later time point to measure long-term nerve regeneration. An increased myelin sheath thickness was observed in WAY-316606-treated nerve in comparison with PBS-treated controls by TEM (Figures 1H and 1I). HE and toluidine blue (TB) staining revealed that mice treated with WAY-316606 showed more myelinated axons (Figures 1H, 1J, and 1K). Moreover, sFRP1 was also abundantly expressed in human injured nerves at 12 h post injury, as revealed by immunohistochemistry (IHC), indicating that sFRP1 level was an early detectable indicator after nerve injuries (Figure 1L). Together, these results put forth a detrimental role of sFRP1 in post-injury peripheral nerve tissue.

Dedifferentiated SCs produce abundant levels of sFRP1 to potentiate nerve degeneration

Although sFRP1 levels were found to be elevated in nerve tissue following injury, it was unclear which cells expressed sFRP1 in response to nerve transection. Thus, we performed bioinformatics analysis based on a single-cell RNA sequencing dataset (GSE120678) from a previous study,19 which used samples from sham-operated and transected mouse sciatic nerves. Based on the single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, we found that cells from injured and uninjured nerves had different subsets (Figure 2A) and that sFRP1 was mainly expressed in fibroblasts and SCs, in both uninjured and injured mice (Figure 2B). To verify the contribution of these disparate cellular sources to sFRP1 production, we quantified the abundance of SC-sourced and fibroblasts-sourced sFRP1 in injured nerves and uninjured nerves (Figure S3A). Flow cytometry analysis of sFRP1+ cells in uninjured nerves revealed no significant difference between SC-sourced and fibroblast-sourced sFRP1 (Figure S3C). In comparison, following nerve injury, SC-sourced sFRP1 increased robustly and accounted for nearly all of the elevated sFRP1 induced by injury (Figures S3B and S3D).

To investigate the spatial distribution of sFRP1 in injured nerves, we next coimmunostained sFRP1 with the fibroblast marker, fibronectin, and the SC marker, S100β. As shown in the longitudinal nerve sections, a uniform distribution of sFRP1 was presented at the injured site and distal nerves following nerve trauma (Figure 2C). In the transverse sections of distal nerves, an abundant number of sFRP1+/S100β+ SCs could be detected, but only few fibroblasts were coimmunostained for sFRP1 (Figures 2D, 2E, and 2F).

The neuronal cell body of the sciatic nerve is found in the L3–L5 spinal cord segments and dorsal root ganglions (DRGs).23 Therefore, we also explored sFRP1 expression in L3–L5 DRGs to figure out whether sFRP1 could be produced in the neuronal cell body and delivered to sciatic nerves via anterograde axonal transport. However, in DRG immunofluorescent staining, neurons (marked by NeuN) were not coimmunostained for sFRP1 (Figure S3E). These results indicated that SCs were likely the most abundant source of sFRP1, which was significantly upregulated following nerve transection injury.

Acute inflammatory insult triggered sFRP1 expression in SCs

Next, we studied what drove sFRP1 expression in SCs. Neuroinflammation fosters dysfunctional extracellular molecular component, in which sFRP1 secretion is enhanced to amplify the inflammatory signaling and neurodegenerative changes.15 Considering sFRP1 is a critical mediator that underlies neuroinflammation, we hypothesized that inflammation activated the reprogramming of SCs and thus generated a different SC phenotype with higher sFRP1 expression. We then treated mice in control group with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (15 mg/kg) for 3 days and found higher sFRP1 expression in SCs (Figures 2G, 2H, and 2I). We stimulated SCs derived from sciatic nerves with LPS and then tested their sFRP1 expression. We found that LPS-treated SCs showed dramatic elevation of sFRP1 expression in a concentration-dependent manner (Figures 2J and 2K). Endoneurial PDGFRα marks diverse mesenchymal precursors.24 These data suggested that injury signals might be sensed by SCs as an inflammatory insult and thus initiated inflammation-induced cytokine release. It is noteworthy that SCs challenged with LPS showed higher levels of PDGFRα and sFRP1, indicating more mesenchymal traits in these sFRP1-expressing SCs (Figures 2L–2N).

Mice with deletion of sFRP1 in SCs alleviated immune insults and improved axon regrowth

Myelin-specific proteolipid protein (PLP) is expressed in myelinating SCs from the precursor stage and lasts throughout their mature stages.25 To decipher the contribution of SC-sourced sFRP1 to nerve regeneration perturbation, we crossed Sfrp1flox/flox mice with PlpcreErt1 mice to generate tamoxifen-inducible SC-specific sFRP1-deficient mice (Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1) (Figure 3A). The transgenic mice were validated prior to being evaluated for nerve regeneration (Figures S4A–S4D). We then used a tamoxifen injection strategy to delete SC-sourced sFRP1 in adult mice prior to peripheral nerve transection. The post-injury sFRP1 expression was significantly reduced in tamoxifen-injected Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice compared with their tamoxifen-injected Sfrp1flox/flox littermates, which indicated successful deletion of sFRP1 and also implied that SCs were the major cell source of elevated sFRP1 induced by injury (Figure S4E).

The specificity of sFRP1 antibody was tested using SCs derived from Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice. As expected, the sFRP1 immunosignals were not observed in SCs isolated from Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice (Figure S5A). To verify whether the deletion of sFRP1 from SCs influences the myelinating function of the SCs themselves, we transfected sFRP1-small interfering RNA (siRNA) into SCs and analyzed the SC myelination 16 h post transfection. Myelin basic protein (MBP) is responsible for adhesion of the cytosolic surfaces of multilayered compact myelin and its expression level is positively correlated with the myelinating capacity of SCs.26 We then tested whether sFRP1 deletion influenced the myelinating function of SCs. Successful knockdown of sFRP1 in SCs was confirmed by western blot analysis (Figure S5D). In our results, deletion of sFRP1 mediated by siRNA did not significantly reduce the MBP expression level of SCs (Figures S5B and S5C).

To address whether the inhibition on sFRP1 secretion improves long-term regeneration of peripheral nerves, we analyzed the nerves of Sfrp1flox/flox mice and Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice at later points following injury as previously reported.27 At 2 weeks after nerve transection, most axons succeeded in crossing the nerve bridge and regrew into the distal stump in the mutant mice. Sciatic nerve axonal regeneration was significantly improved in Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice (Figures 3B and 3C). Improved nerve recovery after transection injury was also confirmed in transgenic female mice, suggesting that the improved regeneration after nerve injury due to sFRP1 deficiency in SCs was independent of gender (Figures S4F and S4G).

Conditional deletion of sFRP1 had great impact on the accumulation of immune cells within injured nerves that were dominated by macrophages. The accumulation of macrophages peaks at about 2 weeks and then gradually declines.9 In this regard, we compared the macrophage infiltration in nerves of wild-type (WT) and mutant mice at 14 days post injury. Noteworthy, massive reduction of macrophages in nerves of Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice was observed (Figures 3D and 3E). TNF-α and IL-1β have been implicated as the main effector of inflammatory cascade. Following nerve injuries, the expression of IL-1β and TNF-α reaches peak levels within 1 day after injury.20 In our results, a marked decrease of TNF-α was observed in the nerves of Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice at 24 h post injury, compared with that in WT mice (Figures 3F and 3G).

Due to the reduced macrophage accumulation in injured nerves, we then focused on the macrophage-neuron interaction in Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice. In the distal nerve, macrophages trigger neuroinflammation, including clearing myelin debris and secreting inflammatory factors. In the region of the axotomized neuronal cell bodies in sciatic DRGs, macrophages are recruited by chemokine C-C motif ligand 2 (CCL2), which mediates macrophage-neuron interaction and thus promotes the regenerative capacity of neurons.20,28 However, in our results, the expression of CCL2 in sciatic DRG neurons showed no significant difference between Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 and Sfrp1flox/flox mice (Figures 3H and 3I). At 24 h after nerve injuries, sciatic DRG neurons were isolated and cultured in vitro. In our results, DRG neurons from Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice exhibited significantly longer axon regrowth length after 4 days and 7 days of culture (Figures 3J–3L). Activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) is required for transcriptional reprogramming induced by axon injury and thus its expression is commonly used to substantiate the potential axonal damage of DRG neurons.29 Herein, we observed that the induction of ATF3 at 24 h post injury was apparent in the sciatic DRG of Sfrp1flox/flox mice, but not in the DRG of Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice (Figures 3M and 3N).

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma coactivator 1 α (PGC-1ɑ) is a crucial transcriptional coactivator that regulates mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis.30 Fluorescence staining for PGC-1ɑ and hexokinase 1 (HK1) confirmed that deletion of sFRP1 increased the expression of PGC-1ɑ and HK1, suggesting a higher level of oxidative metabolism (Figures S5E, S5G, and S5H). Mitofusin2 (MFN2) is determinant to mitochondrial fusion and predominantly localized in the mitochondrial outer membrane, by which neuronal mitochondria regulate neuroinflammation.31 From IF images, MFN2-immunostaining colocalized with the mitochondria label voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1). The colocalization percentage of VDAC1 and MFN2 in the Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 neurons was 1.833 times higher than that in Sfrp1flox/flox neurons (Figures S5F and S5I), indicating less mitochondria fusion in WT neurons in response to nerve injury.

PDGFRαhighP75NTR+JUN+ cells as pathogenic sources of sFRP1 potentiate the accrual of myeloid cells in human nerve microenvironment and exacerbate neuroinflammation

Having established a role for SC-derived sFRP1 in neuroinflammation and nerve regrowth in a murine model of PNIs, we returned our attention to humans and check whether the changes from the rodent model mapped to the human pathology. To investigate cell dynamics in the peripheral nerves of patients with traumatic PNI, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on tibial nerve tissues from 3 patients with traumatic limb amputation (Figure S6A). A total of 26,114 cells were sequenced that clustered into 19 subjects. To identify their cell types, we overlaid cells in the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embeddings (t-SNEs) with well-characterized marker genes, showing relative levels of expression as a color gradient. This approach identified Schwann lineage cells expressing S100B, PLP1, and CDH19 (clusters 1, 7, 13, and 17); fibroblasts expressing sFRP4, DCN, and COL1A1 (clusters 2 and 12); endothelial cells expressing PECAM1, VWF, and JAM2 (clusters 6, 10, and 15); mast cells expressing TPSAB1 and KIT (cluster 19); T cells/NK cells expressing CD3D, SKAP and Themis/NKG7, and KLRC1 and KLRD1 (cluster 8); B cells expressing CD79 and MS4A (cluster 18); smooth muscle cells/pericytes expressing TAGLN, ACTA2, and MYH11 (clusters 3, 11, 14, and 16); and monocytes/macrophages expressing CD68 and LYZ (clusters 4 and 9) (Figure S6B). CDH19 is a specific marker for Schwann lineage cells and is expressed along the SC differentiation. S100β and MPZ are frequently used as markers of mature SCs while c-Jun, p75NTR, and nestin are used as markers of immature, dedifferentiating SCs.32 The sequencing data confirmed that SFRP1 was expressed by fibroblasts and dedifferentiating SCs, characterized by the expression of CDH19, P75NTR, and JUN and a high level of PDGFRA (Figures S6C, S6D, and S6G). This group of cells is also a major source of neurotrophic factor (NGF) and nestin (Figures S6E and S6F).

We then assessed the immune cell composition in injured nerve tissues and found few lymphocytes (Figures S6L1, S6L2, and S6M) but more myeloid cells (Figures S6H–S6K), reflective of the inflammatory and immunogenic milieu of injured nerves in human patients. Noteworthy, macrophages were the most abundant immune cells in posttraumatic nerve tissues. Genes encoding inflammatory factors, typically TNF-α and IL-β, were abundantly expressed in macrophages (Figure S6N). Transcriptional analysis revealed that macrophages in injured nerves profoundly induced pathways associated with immune cell recruitment, activation, and antigen presentation (Figures S6O–S6T). Moreover, the top 10 abundant genes expressed in cluster 4 and cluster 9 macrophages are related with inflammation, antigen presentation, and enzymatic degradation (Figures S6U and S6V). These results inspired us to further investigate whether sFRP1 exerted strong proinflammatory program on macrophages.

sFRP1 coordinates the phenotypic switch and metabolic alterations of macrophages

Next, to acquire a more targeted understanding of sFRP1-induced macrophage reprogramming, we interrogated the phenotypic, genetic, and metabolic changes of macrophages in response to sFRP1 treatment. Mouse DRG neurons were isolated and cultured in sFRP1-supplemented medium for 4 days. However, no significant difference was observed between sFRP1-treated and PBS-treated neurons in terms of neurite outgrowth (Figures 4A and 4B). TEM images further revealed that sFRP1 treatment did not damage the mitochondria of DRG neurons either (Figure 4C). We therefore hypothesized that sFRP1 did not directly influence the axon regrowth but exerted its effect on non-neuronal cells in nerves.

We next searched for the target cell in peripheral nerves that responded to sFRP1 release. Axon regeneration is highly influenced by support cells, particularly SCs and macrophages.33 Following sciatic nerve injury, circulating macrophages infiltrate injured nerves to remove debris, promote regeneration, and build cytokine microenvironment in nerves.34 It has been previously reported in CNS disease that astrocyte-specific sFRP1 exacerbated the proinflammatory phenotype of macrophages to neural damage.15 In bone marrow microenvironment, sFRP1-incorporated Wnt/β-catenin signaling is integral to granulocyte/macrophage lineage differentiation.35 In response to bacterial infection, Wnt3a promotes the activation of β-catenin and anti-inflammatory polarization of primary macrophages.36 In order to verify the role of macrophages in sFRP1-regulated axonogenesis, we depleted circulating monocytes/macrophages through intraperitoneal injection of clodronate liposomes before the administration of sFRP1 into injured nerves. Injecting mice with clodronate liposomes can greatly reduce macrophage accumulation in nerve tissues.37 Control group mice were injected with PBS liposomes. Axon regrowth and remyelination were poor under the treatment of sFRP1 (Figures 4D and 4E). Such growth suppression was partly abolished by clodronate and WAY-316606 treatment.

To determine whether the in vivo effects of sFRP1 could be replicated in vitro, we stimulated bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) with LPS or IL-4 in the presence of different doses of sFRP1 ranging from 1 to 500 nM. Monocytes were cultured with macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF) for a week to induce their differentiation into macrophages as confirmed by F4/80 staining (Figure S7A). After 24 h of culture with LPS, IF showed higher expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, three prototypic proinflammatory cytokines, in both 50 and 500 nM sFRP1-treated BMDMs (Figures 4F, 4H, 4I, S7B, and S7C). Macrophages decreased the production of anti-inflammatory factors, IL-10 and IL-13, after 48 h of culture with IL-4 in the presence of 50 and 500 nM sFRP1 (Figures S7D–S7F). Monocyte differentiation and macrophage polarization under sFRP1 treatment were also revealed by flow cytometry. After LPS pretreatment, the percentage of F4/80+ CD86+ proinflammatory macrophages was significantly higher in the sFRP1-treated group (37.83%) compared with that of the PBS-treated group (20%) (Figures 4G and 4L). We then primed macrophages with IL-4 and further confirmed a lower percentage of F4/80+ CD206+ BMDMs in the sFRP1-treated group (13.5%) than in the PBS-treated group (19.7%) (Figures 4G and 4M).

Given that we observed a prominent role of sFRP1 in triggering macrophage phenotypic switch in vitro, we next investigated whether such effect could be reproduced in vivo. sFRP1 recombinant protein (5 μL, 500 nM) was delivered post injury to distal nerves through intraneural injection versus a PBS control (5 μL). The expression levels of CD86, CD206, IL-1β, and TNF-α were measured in injured nerves of WT, sFRP1-treated, and sFRP1-knockout mice (Figure S8A). sFRP1 treatment significantly induced the infiltration of CD86+ cells and increased the TNF-α and IL-1β level in injured murine nerves. Depletion of sFRP1 markedly reduced the infiltration of proinflammatory CD86+ cells (Figure S8D) and the level of TNF-α and IL-1β (Figures S8B and S8C) but increased the infiltration of pro-resolving CD206+ cells, compared with WT mice (Figure S8E). These results further affirmed that sFRP1 exerted an inflammatory program on macrophages which led to dysregulated secretion of inflammatory mediators.

We also observed attenuated Wnt3a and arginase expression in BMDMs treated with high-dose sFRP1 (Figure 4J). Phagocytosis of myelin debris is a key function of macrophages to restore homeostasis in posttraumatic nerve microenvironment. Whereas the phenotypes and cytokine activities in macrophages were greatly altered, the phagocytic function of macrophages remained unchanged in the treatment of sFRP1 as evaluated by pHrodo BioParticles (Figure 4K).

To comprehensively explore the effects of sFRP1 on BMDMs, we next performed RNA sequencing analysis. PCA analysis clearly differentiated the clustering distribution between transcriptomes of sFRP1-treated and PBS-treated BMDMs (Figure S9A). The molecular signature of sFRP1-treated and PBS-treated BMDMs was compared. We found 479 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with at least 2-fold change (407 upregulated and 72 downregulated) in sFRP1-treated BMDMs compared with PBS-treated controls (Figure 4N). GO enrichment analysis, KEGG enrichment analysis, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that sFRP1-treated macrophages expressed a distinct transcriptional program associated with inflammatory responses, cytokine activities, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Figures 4O, 4P, S9B, and S9D–S9L). As shown in Figures 4O and 4P, sFRP1 treatment enhanced the expression of genes related to CCR chemokine receptor binding. Circulating macrophages engraft efficiently in the pool of nerve-resident macrophages as recruited by CCL2, which acts on the macrophage receptor CCR2.9 We therefore tested the CCL2-related activities of macrophages under sFRP1 treatment. As expected, sFRP1 significantly increased the CCR2 expression in macrophages (Figures S7G and S7H).

Phenotypic switching of macrophages is determined by both gene expression and metabolic pathways. In order to meet the energy requirements, proinflammatory macrophages are characterized by increased glycolysis and decreased oxidative metabolism.38 Our GSEA results also identified that sFRP1 treatment elevated the glycolysis level while suppressing oxidative metabolism in macrophages (Figures S10A–S10D). We next sought to probe metabolic alterations of BMDMs treated by sFRP1. Glycolysis and oxidative metabolism were quantified as the rate of extracellular cellular acidification rate (ECAR) and real-time oxygen consumption rate (OCR), respectively (Figure 4Q). Our results showed that baseline OCRs were decreased in macrophages treated with 100, 200, and 500 nM sFRP1. Then, macrophages were treated with oligomycin, an adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthase inhibitor, as well as carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP) and antimycin A and rotenone. Following the serial administration of inhibitors, the OCR values in the control group changed greatly while the change of OCR values was mild in sFRP1-treated groups (Figure 4R). These findings suggest that ATP production and maximal respiration were inhibited in sFRP1-treated macrophages. We also evaluated the glycolysis of macrophages induced by 50, 100, 200, and 500 nM sFRP1. Samples were sequentially treated with saturated glucose (10 mM), oligomycin, and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG). The ECAR results showed that sFRP1 treatment enhanced both basal and maximal glycolysis as well as the glycolytic reserve of macrophages (Figure 4S). In this regard, sFRP1 elevated glycolysis of BMDMs but impaired their oxidative respiration. It might result in the decrease of bioenergetic reserve and the consequent vulnerability to reactive oxygen species in sFRP1-treated BMDMs. Together, our results support that sFRP1 treatment of BMDMs led to changes of intracellular signaling cascades and metabolic pathways. These results led us to identify the binding target of sFRP1 in macrophages.

sFRP1 bound to HSP90 and thus governed macrophage phenotypic switch

Although the direct interaction between sFRP1 and other factors has been reported, such as integrin, RANKL, Wnt ligands, and ADAM,17,39 its binding proteins in BMDMs under the context of PNIs remained unknown. We then identified which protein interacted with sFRP1 and thus further elucidated the underlying mechanism by which sFRP1 dysregulated cytokine secretion and metabolic pathway of macrophages. We incubated BMDM-derived total proteins with His-labeled sFRP1 and performed immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis. A total of 59 His-binding proteins were identified. Nuclear proteins were excluded for the absence of nuclear localization signal in sFRP1 peptides. We then selected the 10 candidates with top 10 scores in the liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) for further analysis (Figure 5A). Among them, both HSP90ɑ and HSP90β, two isoforms of HSP90, were listed as the interaction partner of sFRP1 (Figure 5B). HSP90, as a type of chaperone protein, has been reported to bind to inflammasomes as a cofactor, leading to the abnormal activation of these inflammasomes in BMDMs.40,41 Thus, we chose HSP90 for further study. We next transfected human embryonic kidney 293T cells with Myc-sFRP1 and HA-HSP90 expressing plasmids and then incubated these cells in complete culture medium for another 48 h. Thereafter, we collected the total cell lysate for coimmunoprecipitation. Myc-tagged proteins were precipitated by anti-Myc antibody and immunoblotted by anti-HA antibody. A strong band of HA was observed in Myc immunoprecipitants (Figure 5C). Conversely, HA-tagged proteins were precipitated using anti-HA antibody followed by western blot assay using anti-Myc antibody. We also observed a strong band of Myc in HA immunoprecipitants (Figure 5D). These results confirmed that sFRP1 interacted with HSP90. We also performed the immunoprecipitation (IP) experiment to assess the direct binding of sFRP1 to HSP90 using an HSP90 antibody, followed by validation of sFRP1 in BMDM lysates by immunoblotting. We observed that HSP90 knockdown in BMDMs caused less binding of sFRP1 and HSP90 compared with the sFRP1-treated group (Figure 5E). Gene and protein expression of HSP90 were also verified in human injured nerve samples by IHC and t-SNE plots (Figures 5F and 5G).

To investigate the role of HSP90 in regulating the effect of sFRP1 on BMDMs, we transfected BMDMs with siRNA-HSP90 or siRNA control and then primed BMDMs with LPS in the presence of sFRP1. Successful knockdown of HSP90 in BMDMs was validated by western blot analysis (Figure S11A). As expected, sFRP1 treatment exacerbated TNF-α secretion in BMDMs in the siRNA-control group but its effect was partly abolished in the siRNA-HSP90 group (Figures 5H and 5I). TNF signals through two receptors, TNFR1 and TNFR2. TNFR1 is expressed on DRG neurons and mediates pronociceptive neuronal hyperexcitability and axonal degeneration.42,43 However, the specific receptors that meditated axonal degeneration and the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

NF-κB has long been considered to promote the expression of proinflammatory genes, including TNF-α, IL-1β, CXCL13, and ICAM-1.44 Here, we discovered that the knockdown of HSP90 reduced the activity of the p65 subunit NF-κB (Figures 5H and 5J). Also, knockdown of HSP90 significantly reversed the proinflammatory phenotype caused by sFRP1 treatment and promoted a pro-resolving phenotype in macrophages (Figures 5K–5M). We further explored the macrophage-neuron interaction in vitro by coculturing LPS-stimulated macrophages for 4 days and 7 days (Figure 5N). As expected, macrophages inhibited axon growth in the presence of sFRP1 and such inhibition was abrogated after the knockdown of HSP90 in macrophages (Figures 5O and 5P). Therefore, we proposed the HSP90-NF-κB signaling pathway as the downstream mechanism of sFRP1 to suppress neuronal regrowth.

Macrophage-specific HSP90 deletion protected against neuroinflammation in PNIs

To test for a consequential role of HSP90 in peripheral nerve repair, we crossed Hsp90aa-floxed mice with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of Lyz2 (Lyz2-cre), thus generating mice with the deletion of Hsp90aa gene in monocytes and macrophages (Figure 6A). In our preliminary study, we found that heterozygous knockout of Hsp90aa in such cells led to a remarkable improvement of nerve regeneration in mice (data not shown). In this regard, we utilized mice with heterozygous Hsp90aa deletion (Lyz2Hsp90aa−/+) in Lyz2+ cells for further experiments. The transgenic mice were validated prior to being evaluated for nerve regeneration (Figures S11B–S11D). We confirmed that Hsp90aa gene expression was lower in BMDMs derived from Lyz2Hsp90aa−/+ mice compared with those from Hsp90aaflox/+ mice (Figure S11E). Then, we subjected Lyz2Hsp90aa−/+ and Hsp90aaflox/+ mice to nerve transection and monitored nerve repair outcomes. We observed an improvement in axon regrowth at 2 weeks after nerve transections in Lyz2Hsp90aa−/+ mice, while the infiltration of macrophages was reduced (Figures 6B–6E). Improved nerve repair outcomes were also confirmed in transgenic female mice, suggesting the underlying mechanism was independent of gender (Figures S11F and S11G). Moreover, genetic deletion of HSP90 in macrophages robustly decreased the level of inflammatory factors especially TNF-α (Figures 6F–6I). Despite what was observed in Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 and Sfrp1flox/flox mice, the deletion of HSP90 in macrophages did not affect the regenerative capacity of DRG neurons (Figures 6J–6L).

Evaluation of a sFRP1-neutralizing antibody therapy for axon injury

In this study, we characterized the comprehensive protein expression profile of injured nerves and identified the growth-suppressive protein, sFRP1 in nerve microenvironment, which was secreted by SCs following injuries. The data aforementioned led us to investigate the therapeutic effect of neutralizing antibody (NAb) against sFRP1 on nerve repair.

We tested the effects of sFRP1-NAb on axon regrowth in vivo. Following nerve transection, mice were treated with sFRP1-NAb (1 mg/kg) or control IgG by intraneural injection per 3 days for 2 weeks. Intraneural injection of WAY-316606 (500 nM, 5 μL) was also administered to compare the therapeutic effect with sFRP1-NAb. Both sFRP1-NAb and WAY-316606 promoted the axon regrowth of transected nerves in mice, and sFRP1-NAb showed better nerve repair than WAY-316606 (Figures 7A and 7B).

To investigate the role of sFRP1-NAb in neuron-macrophage interaction, we adopted the microfluidic device to coculture neurons and macrophages (Figure 7C). In the microfluidic chip, DRG neuronal bodies were restricted in soma chambers and macrophages were cultured in the terminal chamber (Figure 7D). Axons could regrow from the soma chamber into the terminal chamber through the 450-μm-long microgrooves. sFRP1 and antibodies were applied to the terminal chamber, in which axons were removed by vacuum aspiration at 7 days to mimic in vitro injury model. We found that sFRP1-NAb significantly increased the length of regrown axons as compared to an IgG control (Figures 7E and 7F). A direct neuron-macrophage coculture model was also constructed by culturing neurons on the culture dish pre-seeded with a monolayer of macrophages (Figure 7G). After 4 days and 7 days of culture, increased axon length was also observed in neurons that were seeded on macrophage monolayers treated with sFRP1-NAb (Figures 7H and 7I). Thus, sFRP1 antagonism reversed the macrophage-dependent regenerative decline and promoted axonal regrowth following nerve transections.

Discussion

Neurotrauma to peripheral nerves causes the destruction of severed axons distal to the injury site via Wallerian degeneration.45 Although post-injury peripheral nerves retain a certain regenerative capacity, complete nerve transection generally has a poor outcome with minimal clinically meaningful recovery. One possible explanation is that nerve transection leads to the massive production of growth-suppressing proteins, which determine degenerative changes in nerves. Previous articles have identified the regeneration-limiting proteins in the CNS whose suppression supported post-injury axon regeneration.46 However, the identification of regeneration-limiting protein in the PNS is less clear. Our work indicates that the suppressive nerve microenvironment primes severed axons for regenerative retardation by a sFRP1-dependent mechanism, which intensifies neuroinflammation induced by macrophages. Elevated sFRP1 expression is a consequence of SC dedifferentiation, which intensifies macrophage recruitment and activation via binding to HSP90 in macrophages. Excessive inflammation extends the local disruption of nerve structure produced by the initial insult. SC-specific depletion of sFRP1 or macrophage-specific depletion of HSP90 gene expression abolishes these excessive inflammatory changes and improves nerve repair.

Improved proteomic techniques provide fundamental insights into a substantial proteomic change following nerve transection. We observed that sFRP1 was the cytokine most increased in post-injury nerve ECM. sFRP1 is a small (35 kDa), secreted, and highly dispersible protein typically expressed in the brain. As an endogenous negative modulator of Wnt signaling and metalloprotease ADAM10, sFRP1 has strong correlation with several neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease and glaucoma.13,47 In addition, sFRP1 exacerbates synaptic dysfunction, CNS inflammation, and the formation of dystrophic neurites.48 However, its role in the PNS is still not fully understood and remains to be explored. Here, we show that dedifferentiating SCs are the major source of elevated sFRP1 in PNS. Unlike SCs at homeostasis, posttraumatic SCs dedifferentiate into immature mesenchymal-like state and release a spectrum of proteins into nerve ECM.7,49 SC-specific knockout of sFRP1 prevents macrophage-mediated inflammatory damage and thus promotes axon regrowth following nerve transection.

NAbs directly targeting the disease-associated protein show promising therapeutic effects in the clinic. Many NAb-based drugs have been tested in phase 1 and phase 2 clinical trials, revealing a good safety profile, and some of them have received Food and Drug Administration approval.50,51 In this study, we took advantage of a sFRP1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) that specifically neutralizes sFRP1 but not sFRP2, the other known Wnt antagonist. In line with the data from Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice, sFRP neutralization through intraneural delivery of NAbs also promotes axonal regeneration. sFRP1 mAbs were evaluated as therapeutics for nerve transection in mice and axotomy in microfluidic devices. Based on our experimental data, sFRP1 mAbs effectively restored the axonal regeneration in these models. Therefore, sFRP1-NAbs represent a translational opportunity, and sFRP1-NAb drug delivery systems, such as nerve scaffolds and hydrogels containing sFRP1-NAbs, may stand as an efficient and precise clinical strategy for the treatment of nerve transection.

Posttraumatic inflammation is a consequence of multicellular host response to infectious or physical tissue damage. It involves the recruitment of immune cells, the amplification of hematopoietic cell response with collateral tissue damage, and the clearance of inflammatory cells.52 Moreover, aberrant neuron-immune crosstalk inhibits regenerative signals, causing the axonal regenerative decline.53 Based on our single-cell sequencing data, macrophages and neutrophils are the most abundant immune cells in the inflammatory and immunogenic milieu of injured nerves in human patients. Neutrophil infiltration appears quickly after PNI as an acute immune response within the nervous system, but they function mainly within DRGs where the neuronal cell bodies reside, rather than at the nerve crush site.54 By contrast, infiltrating macrophages enter distal nerve segments from bone marrow, regulate immune homeostasis within peripheral nerves, and are about three times in quantity over that of resident macrophages.9 Macrophages from sFRP1-deficient (SFRP1−/−) mice demonstrated less polarization and reduced infiltration relative to WT mice.55 We also found that the injury-induced macrophage swarms and inflammatory factors were ameliorated in Sfrp1flox/floxPlpcreErt1 mice. These data indicate that excessive neuroinflammation and regenerative decline rely on SC-macrophage interactions, which elicit proinflammatory signals.

Previous studies detected the sFRP1 immunosignals localized in neurons and microglia, reflecting the uptake of glial cell-secreted sFRP1 by neurons and microglia.13,15 Moreover, in our study, no receptor protein was observed among the top ten binding proteins with highest scores in the LC-MS/MS analysis. Considering the secreted and dispersible nature of sFRP1, we propose that sFRP1 can be transported directly across the cellular membrane to the cytoplasm without receptor binding.

A previous study revealed that all endoneurial cells, including SCs, axons, and macrophages, display a mitochondrial response following acute nerve injury to promote repair.56 Local signals and circulating cytokines participate in shaping the metabolic environment within peripheral nerves upon injury.56 Despite sFRP1’s mediation of the inflammatory process in nervous tissue,15 its regulatory function in macrophage metabolism has not been described thus far. Interestingly, we observed pronounced metabolic alterations of macrophages provoked by sFRP1, with robust suppression on mitochondrial respiration and promotion of glycolysis.

Given that sFRP1 is a canonical Wnt antagonist, we investigated the relationship between sFRP1 and Wnt in macrophages. We observed attenuated Wnt expression in macrophages treated with high-dose sFRP1. However, the interaction of sFRP1 with Wnt was not detected in the IP-MS experiments, by which HSP90 was screened as a major interacting molecule of sFRP1 in macrophages. Therefore, despite the fact that sFRP1-Wnt interaction might also function in macrophage reprogramming, we proposed sFRP1-HSP90 as the main axis in regulating neuroinflammation. HSP90 family includes four members HSP90α, HSP90β, the mitochondrially localized TRAP1, and an endoplasmic reticulum-resident paralog Gp96.57 HSP90 chaperone protein is abundantly expressed in macrophages and causes a variety of human inflammatory diseases.40,58 From previous reports, it is known that HSP90 activates the NF-κB pathway to promote inflammatory responses during osteoarthritis pathogenesis.59 Macrophage-specific depletion of HSP90α using Hsp90flox/+Lyz2cre mice blocked the sFRP1-mediated activation of NF-κB and reversed the exacerbated neuroinflammation. However, Hsp90 deletion in macrophages did not affect the regeneration of DRG neurons, suggesting that HSP90 was not the only downstream binding protein of sFRP1. Thus, antagonizing sFRP1 secretion represents the optimal strategy to counteract regenerative retardation following nerve transection.

Collectively, we reveal that, in the context of nerve transection, elevated secretion of sFRP1 by SCs interacts with HSP90 in macrophages to initiate excess neuroinflammation, thereby causing axonal regenerative retardation. sFRP1 antagonism reverses the growth-suppressing effect and improves axonal regrowth, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for posttraumatic nerve repair.

Limitations of the study

In this study, the interaction of sFFRP1 and HSP90 in macrophages was revealed by the IP-MS experiments. We did not elucidate the pathway by which sFRP1 was transported into macrophage cytoplasm. As we detected no receptor proteins in the LC-MS/MS analysis, we propose that sFRP1 can be transported directly across the cellular membrane to the cytoplasm without receptor binding. Future studies will have to assess whether sFRP1 is transported through passive diffusion, active transport, or phagocytosis. Also, we did not discover the downstream molecule of HSP90-NF-κB axis which triggered axon degeneration. Future studies that characterize the in vivo DRG transcriptome by RNA sequencing would be helpful toward validating these results.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patient samples

Patient samples were collected from Department of Orthopedics, Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. The use of human specimens in this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital (2022-KY-200(K)). This study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2300068077) on Feb 6, 2023. Patients with uncontrolled hypertension and metabolic dysfunction were excluded as these chronic diseases could also lead to neurodegeneration. In detail, peripheral nerves were collected during traumatic limb amputation, from patients with different types of PNIs (e.g., N. tibialis, N. medianus or N. ulnaris). Nerve leftovers (∼1 cm) in the amputated limbs were quickly frozen on dry ice in order to limit injury responses as much as possible. Histological analysis was performed in 45 paraffin-embedded pathologic specimens. Patients included in this study were listed in Table S1.

Mice

The sFRP1-floxed mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc (CAT: CKOCMP-20377-Sfrp1-B6J-VA). For genotyping, genomic DNA was extracted from tail tips. The sFRP1flox/flox mice were generated by intercrossing of sFRP1flox/+. The primers of genotyping for sFRP1-floxed mice were: F1-5′-AATGCTGACTGGACATAGGTTAGTT-3’; R1-5′-CTCCCAAATAGAAGGATGTGGGTC-3’; F2-5′-TCTAAGCCCCAAGGTAAGGGTAT-3’; R2-5′-AGCTTTGCTTTCTTTCAGGTATGG-3’. The primers of genotyping for PLP-Cre mice were: F-5′-AGAATGGGACAGCTGGGACAAAGT-3’; R-5′-GAACATCTTCAGGTTCTGCGGGA-3’. We obtained PLP1-CreERT mice (Stock No: C001031) from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. PLP-Cre:sFRP1flox/flox mice were generated by crossing sFRP1flox/flox mice and PLP-Cre mouse line. For PLP-Cre:sFRP1flox/flox mice, tamoxifen was administered at 6-week-old for 7 consecutive days.

The Hsp90aaflox/+ mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc (CAT: CKOAIP230801XB1). The primers of genotyping for Hsp90aaflox/+ mice were: F-5′-AGGCTACCTGTAACTAACATAAGGG-3’; R-5′-CATTCAAACCCTTTAGCTGAGGC-3’; We obtained Lyz2cre mice (Stock No: C001003) from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. The primers of genotyping for Lyz2cre mice were: F-5′-CCCAGAAATGCCAGATTACG-3’; R-CTTGGGCTGCCAGAATTTCTC-3’; The Hsp90aaflox/+Lyz2cre mice were generated by crossing Hsp90aaflox/+ mice and Lyz2cre mouse line. All mice were kept in C57BL/6J background. The mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free conditions and kept on in a regular 12 h:12 h light:dark cycle.

Cell culture

SCs were isolated and cultured as previously described.60 Briefly, murine sciatic nerves were resected and quickly cut into small slices using and digested with 0.05% collagenase/dispase (Worthington, USA) solution for 1 h in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 humidified incubator. Then, the digested slices were centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min and resuspended using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution. After 15 min, the digestion was stopped and the pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 ng/mL heregulin-β-1, 2 μM forskolin and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Finally, SCs were inoculated onto poly-L-lysine (PLL)-coated coverslips.

DRG neurons were derived and cultured as previously described.38 DRGs were carefully dissected and digested in 0.25% collagenase (Collagenase Type 1, LS004194, Worthington, USA) at 37°C for 30 min. Following the termination of digestion and centrifuge procedure, cell pellets were resuspended in neurobasal media (Gibco, USA) added with 2% B-27 supplement (Gibco, USA) and 2mM GlutaMAX (Gibco, USA). DRG neurons were seeded onto PLL-coated coverslips or microfluidic chambers (XC150, Xona Microfluidics).

BMDMs were purchased from the Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (CP-M141). For phenotypic polarization induction, BMDMs were treated with LPS (SigmaAldrich, 1 μg/mL) for 24 h to induce a proinflammatory phenotype and IL-4 (574302, Biolegend, 20 ng/mL) for 48 h to induce an anti-inflammatory phenotype.

For neuron-macrophage coculture modeling, macrophages were seeded onto the upper chamber of the Transwell system (3450; Corning) while neurons were seeded in the lower chamber (two chambers were separated with a 0.4-μm pore size polyester membrane). The serum-free medium DMEM/F12 (Gibco) added with 2% B-27and GlutaMAX was used to culture both macrophages and DRGs.

Method details

Murine nerve sample preparation

Murine sciatic nerves were harvested from 6-week-old mice. The procedures of animal maintenance and experiments were in strict accordance with the ethics and policy of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital (DWLL2023-0286). For the injured nerve model, complete transection injury was performed 5 mm proximal to the trifurcation of sciatic nerves. After injury, proximal and distal nerve ends were reconnected by end-to-end neurorrhaphy with 6-0 sutures. Thereafter, wounds were closed and animals were sent back to closed cages.

For sFRP1 inhibition treatments, WAY-316606 (HY-10858, MedChemExpress, 500nM, 5μL) and LPS (15 mg/kg) were administered into different mice through intraneural injection, while control mice received PBS injection. We performed repeated intraneural administration of WAY-316606 and PBS every 3 days for 2 weeks. For recombinant sFRP1 protein treatment, mice were intraneurally injected with sFRP1 protein (HY-P73413, MedChemExpress, 500nM, 5μL) or PBS (5μL), immediately after nerve transection procedure.

To efficiently deplete the monocytes and macrophages in vivo, clodronate liposome (5 mg/mL, 200μL) and PBS liposome (5 mg/mL, 200μL) injection was given intraperitoneally at 48 h before nerve transection and maintained every 4 days until the end of the experiment.

Upon harvest, mice were killed with CO2 and subsequent cervical dislocation, and sciatic nerves were dissected with scissors. For proteomic analysis and protein expression comparison, murine nerves were frozen on dry ice as soon as possible. For histological analysis, nerve samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and embedded in paraffin to be cut into 5 μm paraffin microtome slice. For TEM analysis, human and murine nerve samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C overnight followed by post-fixation in 1% osmic acid at 4°C for 1.5 h

Histology and immunocytochemistry (ICC)

IHC was performed using primary antibodies and the biotin-conjugated secondary antibody (Goat anti-rabbit, 1:500, ab207995, Abcam, USA) and DAB as substrate. Primary antibodies used for IHC included anti-sFRP1 (rabbit, 1:200, 26460-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Ki67 (rabbit, 1:200, ab15580, Abcam) and anti-HSP90 (1:150, ab13495, Abcam).

For IF analysis, the sections were stained with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies including goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 (1:200, ab150115, Abcam), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 (1:200, ab150079, Abcam), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200, ab150113, Abcam) and goat anti-mouse Alexa 488 (1:500, ab150113, Abcam). For triple IF staining, tyramide signal amplification (TSA) was performed. After the incubation with a specific primary antibody, samples were added with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated polymer before visualization of the antibody using CY3-TSA, FITC-TSA, or CY5-TSA. Antibody stripping was performed before the next antibody detection procedure.

For ICC analysis, Schwann cells, macrophages and DRG neurons were fixed with 4% PFA (Solarbio, China) for 20 min and permeabilized by 0.25% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature. Cell samples were blocked with 5% normal goat serum followed by incubation with primary antibodies. Afterward, cell samples were incubated with above-mentioned secondary antibodies. Finally, the nuclei of cell samples were stained with DAPI solution (No. H-1200; Vector Laboratories Inc, USA) and observed with a fluorescent microscope.

Primary antibodies included anti-cJun (rabbit, 1:500, 9165S, Cell signaling), anti-p75NTR (rabbit, 1:200, ab52987, Abcam), anti-S100 (1:100, 90393S, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-fibronectin (rabbit, 1:100, ab2413, Abcam), anti-sFRP1 (rabbit, 1:200, 26460-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Neurofilament (rabbit, 1:200, 55453, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-NeuN (mouse, 1:250, ab104224, Abcam), anti-PDGFRɑ (rabbit, 1:200, ab203491, Abcam), anti-CCL2 (mouse, 1:200, 66272-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-F4/80 (rabbit, 1:250, 28463-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-MAP2 (rabbit, 1:200, 17490-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-ATF3 (rabbit, 1:150, ab256534, Abcam), anti-PGC-1α (mouse, 1:200, 66369-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-HK-1 (rabbit, 1:200, 19662-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-VDAC1 (rabbit, 1:200, 55259-1-AP, Proteintech), MFN2 (rabbit, 1:200, 12186-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-TNF-ɑ (rabbit, 1:200, ab183218, Abcam), anti-IL-1β (rabbit, 1:200, ab254360, Abcam), anti-Wnt3a (rabbit, 1:200, ab219412, Abcam), anti-Arg-1(rabbit, 1:200, ab96183, Abcam), anti-beta III Tubulin (rabbit, 1:200, ab18207, Abcam), anti-NF-κB p65 (rabbit, 1:300, ab32536, Abcam, USA), anti-SCG10 (rabbit, 1:200, 10586-1-AP, Proteintech).

TEM analysis

Neurons and nerve tissue samples were fixed and dehydrated to be embedded in acrylic resin. Cut sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and then imaged using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Proteomic analysis

Nerve samples were frozen and then ground into fine and uniform powder in liquid nitrogen for protein extraction. Then the samples were digested and fractionated. The data independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry analysis was acquired by TimsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer (Bruker) and nanoElute (Bruker). For data independent acquisition (DIA), 56 DIA windows were acquired and the collision energy was ramped linearly as a function of the mobility from 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.6 Vs/cm2 to 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.6 Vs/cm2. The MS/MS spectra were recorded from 100 to 1700 m/z. The default factory settings were used for the Spectronaut Pulsar 15.3.210906.50606 (Biognosys, Swiss) search and library generation. The DIA data were analyzed with Spectronaut searching the above constructed spectral library. The thresholds of fold change (>1.5) and P-value <0.05 were used to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Annotation of all identified proteins was performed using GO and KEGG pathway. DEPs were further used for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis.

Genotyping

To isolate DNA for genotyping, tail biopsies was incubated at 55°C in 1X Modified Gitschier Buffer containing 0.5% Triton X-100 and 1 mg/mL proteinase K for at least 2 h under constant shaking. The Proteinase K was subsequently heat inactivated at 90°C for 10 min. Thereafter, genotyping was performed via PCR amplification of genomic DNA. PCR primers were employed in a co-amplification reaction.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing of human injured nerves was carried out using Chromium Single Cell 3′ Solution (10X Genomics). Libraries were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (performed by CapitalBio Technology, Beijing). The human nerve datasets were integrated and then analyzed for clustering and t-SNE visualization using the Seurat 4.0 package. Cell types were annotated based on the expression of canonical cell markers listed in Table S2.

Experimental test of phagocytic function

BMDMs were incubated with 100 μg/mL pHrodo Zymosan A Green BioParticles (P35365, Invitrogen) for an hour according to the instruction of manufacturer and visualized by immunostaining with anti-F4/80 antibody (red), and were imaged by confocal microscopy to observe the internalized fluorescent microspheres.

RNA sequencing of BMDMs

Total RNA of BMDMs was isolated using the Trizol reagent. RNA extracted from non-treated BMDMs was marked as control while RNA from sFRP1-treated BMDMs was marked as experiment group. Then, transcriptome sequencing and analysis were performed at Oebiotech Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (1.24.0) analysis. p value < 0.05 and fold change>2 were set as the threshold for significantly differential expression. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs were performed to analyze enriched gene sets in macrophages in response to sFRP1 treatment.

Flow cytometry

To identify the proportion of proinflammatory and pro-resolving BMDMs, single cell suspensions were incubated with surface biomarkers using FITC-conjugated F4/80 (157309, Biolegend), PE-conjugated CD206 (568273, BD Biosciences) and APC-conjugated CD86 antibodies (105011, Biolegend) for 30 min at 4°C according to the manufacturer’s instruction and labeled cells were determined using flow cytometry.

To characterize sFRP1-positive cells, injured nerves and contralateral control nerves were collected and finely chopped prior to digestion, in accordance with a previous report.7 To assess the percentage of sFRP1+ SCs and sFRP1+ fibroblasts, nerve single cell suspensions were incubated with anti-sFRP1 antibodies (rabbit, ab267466, Abcam), followed by goat anti-rabbit AlexaFluor594 secondary antibodies (ab150080, Abcam). Cell suspensions were then centrifuged and washed with PBS thrice. Thereafter, cells were acutely stained with CoraLitePlus 488-conjugated S100β antibodies (CL488-15146, proteintech) and CoraLitePlus 647-conjugated prolyl-4-hydroxylase antibodies (4PHL, CL647-11245, proteintech) and subjected to flow cytometry to assess the coexpression of sFRP1, S100β and 4PHL. Data were analyzed by FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc, USA).

Seahorse bioenergetic analysis

Measurements of oxygen consumption and extracellular acidification were performed using Seahorse XF24 Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Macrophages were plated at 50,000 cells/well on Seahorse XF24 cell culture microplates in DMEM/F12 media with 5.5 mM D-glucose for 24 h. OCR was measured by sequential addition of 1 μM oligomycin, 1.5 μM fluoro-carbonyl cyanide phenylhydrazone (FCCP) and 100 nM rotenone plus 1 μM antimycin A. ECAR was measured after the sequential addition of 10 mM glucose, 1 μM oligomycin, and 100 mM 2-DG.

Immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP/MS)

BMDMs were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer and incubated with His-labeled sFRP1 (Ag24844, Proteintech). Then, the protein sample was precleared with protein A/G-agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 1 h. Thereafter, cell lysates were incubated with antibodies against His (2365S, 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology), HA (3724S, 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology), MYC (2276S, 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology), and HSP90 (ab203126, 1:100, Abcam) respectively followed by adsorption to protein A/G-agarose beads. The immunoprecipitates were then eluted and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) for immunoblot analysis. The His-binding immunoprecipitates were evaluated by mass spectrometry analysis.

Immunoblot analysis

Proteins were subjected to thorough centrifugation prior to electrophoresis. Following SDS-PAGE procedure, protein samples were transferred onto PVDF membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). PVDF membrane was then blocked with goat serum and subjected to incubation with primary antibodies against His (2365S, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), HA (3724S, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), MYC (2276S, 1:1500, Cell Signaling Technology), HSP90 (ab203126, 1:1000, Abcam), sFRP1 (rabbit, 1:1000, 26460-1-AP, Proteintech), TNF-ɑ (rabbit, 1:1000, ab183218, Abcam). Thereafter, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies and subjected to the addition of enhanced chemiluminescent reagent (Millipore) before visualization.

Transfection study

HSP90 siRNA was synthesized by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China) and transfected into BMDMs through Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Thereafter, BMDMs were cultured in a serum-free medium overnight and then were treated with or without sFRP1 (500 nM) for another 24 h, with 500 nM bovine serum albumin as a control.

To investigate the myelinating capacity of sFRP1-deficient SCs, sFRP1-siRNA (Origene, SR410016) were transfected into SCs at 10 nM concentration, using Attractene Transfection Reagent (QIAGEN, 301005) for 16 h. The expression of MBP was analyzed 16 h post transfection.

Plasmids expressing Myc-tagged sFRP1 and HA-tagged HSP90 were generated by cloning their ORFs with the N-terminal Myc or HA sequence (Genomeditech, Shanghai). Plasmids were also transfected into HEK 293T cells via Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen).

Antibody mediated sFRP1 neutralization

The neutralizing antibodies used in this study were anti-sFRP1 (Sigma-Aldrich, ABS1593) and IgG control (R&D Systems, AB-105-C). For in vivo experiments, 1 mg/kg sFRP1-NAb or 1 mg/kg IgG control was administered through intraneural injection every 3 days for 2 weeks. For in vitro experiments, BMDMs were preconditioned with 500 nM sFRP1 followed by incubation with 300 ng/mL anti-sFRP1 neutralizing antibody or IgG control for 24h.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD and contained at least three independent biological replicates. We utilized GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software) for statistical analysis. For two-group comparisons, a two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For greater than two-group comparisons, a one- or two-way ANOVA was utilized. Differences were regarded as significant at a p-value <0.05.
