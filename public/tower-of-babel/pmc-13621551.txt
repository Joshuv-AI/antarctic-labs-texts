Preferences towards digital health technologies: a scoping review
Authors: Aimée Kingsada, Silvana Briones-Piedrafita, Thomas Rapp, Jonathan Sicsic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital Health Technologies (DHTs) are expanding rapidly, offering new opportunities to support care delivery. Their adoption, however, depends on how well they match patients’ needs and expectations. Accurately assessing patient preferences is challenging due to diverse user profiles and varying methods used to measure preferences. A synthesis of current evidence is needed to clarify what patients value in DHTs.ObjectivesThis study synthesizes evidence on patient preferences for DHTs—including eHealth, telehealth, telemedicine, and mHealth—and examines the methods used to elicit these preferences, highlighting opportunities to improve adoption and design.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review of literature published from 2000 to 2026 following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Searches were performed in PubMed, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, and Web of Science. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts for eligibility. Data were charted and narratively synthesized, with study characteristics categorized by methodology (qualitative, quantitative, mixed methods) and preference elicitation techniques.ResultsOf 2,419 records identified, 115 underwent full-text screening and 85 met all inclusion criteria: 27 qualitative studies (31.76%), 45 quantitative studies (52.95%), and 13 mixed-methods studies (15.29%). Quantitative studies primarily applied attribute-based methods (e.g., Discrete Choice Experiments, Conjoint Analysis, Best-Worst Scaling), and six studies used the Contingent Valuation method to estimate total willingness-to-pay for DHTs. Qualitative studies employed thematic analysis, deductive, inductive, and immersion–crystallization approaches. Across studies, patients consistently emphasized cost, privacy, convenience, and personalization of DHTs as key concerns. Only a few studies estimated the relative importance of the attributes or the marginal willingness to pay for the device’s features. Considerable heterogeneity in preferences was observed by DHT type, patients’ health condition and age.ConclusionsQuantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method approaches provide complementary insights into how patients perceive and value DHTs. Patients consistently seek personalized, easy-to-use, secure, and affordable solutions, which is particularly important for older adults managing chronic conditions. Given the diversity of patient preferences, it is essential to consider these differences when developing digital health technologies to ensure they effectively support patients and address their needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13561-026-00792-2.Keywords: Scoping review, Digital health technologies, Patient preferences, Discrete choice experiment

Introduction

Billions of people worldwide have access to mobile phones and computers, and there has been a growing interest in using these technologies in healthcare. In 2025, approximately 60% of Europeans aged 16–74 reported using the internet to seek health-related information [1]. Digital Health Technologies (DHTs) are emerging as solutions to various challenges facing healthcare systems. DHTs cover various information and communications technologies (ICT) applications to enhance health and healthcare services (see Fig. 1). These challenges include the aging population, chronic diseases, human errors in medical records and treatments, and fragmented healthcare delivery [2]. Demographic ageing, in particular, is expected to substantially increase demand for care while the supply of healthcare and long-term care workers is not increasing at the same rate. Across OECD countries, the ratio of working-age individuals to older adults is declining, and the number of care workers per older person has stagnated in many European countries, raising concerns about system capacity. In this context, digital health technologies are increasingly seen as a means to improve productivity, support task delegation, and enable remote care delivery, thereby helping to mitigate workforce shortages [3, 4]. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic has significantly accelerated the adoption of DHTs, enhancing healthcare providers’ capabilities and supporting patients’ healthcare needs [5–7].

Definition and scope of Digital Health Technologies (DHTs). DHTs encompass a broad range of ICT-based applications in healthcare. eHealth refers to the use of digital technologies across health systems, including telehealth services. Telehealth includes both clinical and non-clinical remote services, while telemedicine refers specifically to clinical services. mHealth overlaps with eHealth and refers to the use of mobile and wireless devices such as smartphones and connected objects. Source: Authors

DHTs provide several key benefits for patients [8]. They have the potential to make healthcare more accessible [9], improve health monitoring [10, 11], and reduce costs by saving time and cutting down on travel [12], while emerging evidence suggests that these benefits may not be evenly distributed across populations and could contribute to widening health inequalities, particularly along sociodemographic gradients in access and use [13]. Beyond these aspects, DHTs can benefit patients by relieving health systems struggling with a diminishing workforce and increasing health demand and expenditures. Another advantage of DHTs is that they can be personalized and are thus suitable for promoting a patient-centered approach to healthcare. In particular, eHealth and mHealth have specific characteristics that enhance this approach to healthcare delivery, allowing public health professionals and medical practitioners to interact with patients to address health concerns. Technological medical progress has been recognized to enhance healthcare management, focusing on empowering patients, from delivering tailored information to implementing smartphone applications for disease prevention and management [14]. Overall, the benefits shown by DHTs could be a new way to improve individual well-being [15].

Beyond benefits at the individual level, DHTs hold substantial potential to enhance healthcare system performance. Evidence from OECD countries indicates that tools such as electronic health records can improve care coordination, reduce medical errors, and avoid unnecessary duplication of services, thereby contributing to greater efficiency [16]. In parallel, economic evaluations indicate that many digital health interventions can improve health outcomes while maintaining or even reducing costs, supporting their potential to enhance resource allocation [17]. However, such evaluations remain relatively scarce and methodologically challenging, notably due to difficulties in capturing medium- to long-term outcomes and attributing effects over extended time horizons. Across European countries, empirical evidences further shows that digital transformation is associated with improved health outcomes and system performance, although these effects depend on levels of investment and the availability of adequate infrastructure [18]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the benefits of digital health are not automatic but depend on effective implementation and use. In this context, understanding patient preferences is essential to inform digital health policies, as aligning technologies with patients’ needs and expectations may support more efficient allocation of resources.

Despite their potential, the widespread adoption of DHTs remains constrained by several barriers. A systematic review [19] identified 33 barriers to the use of telemedicine, including discomfort with technology among staff, resistance to change, costs, reimbursement issues, patients’ age, and education level. At the system level, OECD analyses also point to persistent regulatory and financing challenges, particularly regarding public funding and reimbursement mechanisms [20]. In response, the WHO has developed a global strategy to support the sustainable development of digital health, emphasizing coordination across stakeholders and alignment of investments with evidence-based approaches [21]. These initiatives aim to promote the large-scale implementation of person-centered digital health programs. However, beyond technological and institutional constraints, the uptake of DHTs ultimately depends on their acceptability and usability for end users. In this respect, patient preferences play a key role in shaping engagement with digital tools and their integration into routine care.

Patient preferences are increasingly acknowledged as vital in health technology assessment (HTA), key in public funding of health technologies across EU countries, and influencing payer decisions. These preferences provide essential social, ethical, and organizational perspectives that complement the insights from professionals and researchers [22, 23]. They ensure that technologies align with user needs and expectations, enhancing their effectiveness and adoption. Therefore, health outcomes can also be improved when patients are more willing to engage in DHTs [24–27]. Moreover, findings in the literature indicate that most digital health interventions are cost-effective [28].

However, assessing DHTs while considering patient preferences accurately can be challenging. For example, the variety of methods to elicit preferences increases the difficulty of assessing the value of digital technologies [29, 30]. Research by van Overbeeke et al. (2021) [31] shows that while HTA representatives from Canada, Belgium, and Germany value patient preference studies, they struggle to quantify their impact on decisions. Additionally, DHTs significantly differ from standard technologies. They evolve rapidly and necessitate ongoing monitoring and updates. As a result, traditional cost-utility analyses typically applied to drugs and medical devices may not suit DHTs [32]. Overall, the literature provides limited guidance on the optimal methods for integrating patient preferences into assessments. To address this gap, it is essential to understand the preferences of various stakeholders and the methods used to elicit these preferences.

This study provides a scoping review of the literature addressing patient preferences regarding medical innovations such as eHealth, telehealth, and mhealth. It explores the methods used to elicit patients’ preferences toward medical innovations and compares the results across various study settings. Despite the growing interest in patient preferences, research specifically targeting medical innovations is still limited. This scoping review surveys existing literature on patient preferences for various medical innovations, documenting the primary research’s scope, diversity, and critical aspects [33]. First, it aims to outline the methods used to elicit preferences and identify critical themes relevant to patients when adopting and using DHTs. Second, it explores how these themes vary across the study context (e.g., type of DHTs), the method used (e.g., qualitative vs. quantitative), and study populations. Given the increasing support for qualitative research in health economics due to its in-depth insights into quantitative findings, this review highlights the importance of including qualitative and mixed-methods studies to enhance the comparison of patient preferences across different methodologies [34]. This review contributes to the literature in two main aspects. First, it provides a useful cartography of DHT characteristics included in (quantitative) stated preferences studies, to help future researchers identify key attributes and their relevance. Second, it provides a methodological comparison of results found across quantitative and qualitative methods, thus allowing for assessing the strengths of each approach for eliciting patients’ preferences.

Methods

This scoping review is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews [35] (Appendix A in Online Supplementary Materials (OSM)). A review protocol was not registered since it is not a systematic literature review.

Search study

A comprehensive search of published literature was conducted using the following databases: PubMed, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, and Web of Science. The inclusion of the first three databases was based on recommendations from Bahaadinbeigy et al. (2010) [36], who suggested them as the most suitable sources for reviewing telemedicine-related topics. The addition of Scopus and Web of Science was motivated by the potential for relevant studies in journals outside the health domain. We restricted our search to studies published between 2000 and 2026 to ensure the inclusion of recent publications and provide a comprehensive review of the research. The search strategies for each database are detailed in Appendix B in SupplementalMaterials.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We focused on quantitative studies employing standard preference elicitation tools and excluded studies that did not report preference measures or those using non-choice-based methods like Likert-type scales or ranking, in which it is impossible to quantify trade-offs between various DHT dimensions. Our review focuses on medical innovations, encompassing information and communication technology in health delivery. We did not exclude qualitative studies, provided that they explored preferences for DHTs and reported the method used (e.g., semi-structured interviews and focus groups). Our search was restricted to peer-reviewed publications in English and excluded the grey literature, commentaries, editorials, protocols, and conference abstracts.

Search terms

The search method underwent a pilot test to assess the suitability of the chosen databases and keywords. The search terms were carefully selected due to their relevance to the research topic and broad application across various domains, aiming to minimize the likelihood of excluding relevant articles. However, additional terms were incorporated when needed (see Appendix B in OSM). The chosen keywords suited two distinct categories:

Preferences: Choice Behaviour, Patient Preference, Stated preference, Preference-based, Choice experiment, DCE, Best-worst scaling, Conjoint analysis, time trade-off, choice-based, contingent valuation, standard gamble.

Preferences: Choice Behaviour, Patient Preference, Stated preference, Preference-based, Choice experiment, DCE, Best-worst scaling, Conjoint analysis, time trade-off, choice-based, contingent valuation, standard gamble.

Medical innovations: Medical innovation, m-Health, mHealth, eHealth, e-Health, Telehealth system, Telehealth, Remote monitoring system, Connected object, Teleconsultation, digital therapeutic, Remote assistance.

Medical innovations: Medical innovation, m-Health, mHealth, eHealth, e-Health, Telehealth system, Telehealth, Remote monitoring system, Connected object, Teleconsultation, digital therapeutic, Remote assistance.

Record screening and data extraction

A two-tier screening process was implemented, starting with two reviewers’ initial review of titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text screening of selected studies. Eligible articles were managed using Zotero software (version 6.0.37), where duplicates were identified and removed. Studies that met the inclusion criteria advanced to full-text review. After the full-text review, efforts to obtain full texts of unavailable articles were documented, along with reasons for exclusion. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, involving two additional reviewers when necessary. The two authors responsible for study identification also extracted information and classified the included references. A data extraction framework was established to assess study relevance and extract characteristics. Pertinent data from each included study were collected electronically using a data charting form.

Results

Description of findings

The database searches identified 2,419 records for review, as shown in Fig. 2. Among these, 358 were from PubMed, 394 from Embase, 252 from CINAHL, 547 from Web of Science, and 868 from Scopus. After removing 711 duplicates, 1,708 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Out of these, 1,593 articles (92.26%) were excluded. The main reasons for exclusion included lack of reported preferences (38.41%), focus on non-medical innovations (15.56%), absence of standard preference elicitation tools (12.55%), and conference abstracts (11.92%) —other reasons for exclusion were related to studies focusing on health providers, protocols, and non-English papers. In total, 115 records met the inclusion criteria and underwent full-text screening, consisting of 48 qualitative papers (41.7%) and 67 quantitative papers (58.3%). Following the full-text screening, the total number of papers included in the analysis was 85, comprising 27 qualitative papers (31.76%), 45 quantitative papers (52.95%), and 13 mixed-methods papers (15.29%). Data were synthesized separately using a matrix format for each type of study. This format includes details such as the authors, date, country, preference elicitation tool, study design, and main findings, organized by the type of research. In the appendix C (OSM), quantitative studies are presented in Table C1, while qualitative and mixed-methods studies are presented in Table C2 and Table C3, respectively.

Flowchart of search results

The studies included in this review span from 2005 to 2026, with over half (60%) published in the recent five-year period from 2022 to 2026. Research on patients’ preferences towards medical innovation using qualitative methods first appeared in 2014. Since 2018, there has been a noticeable trend towards mixed-methods research that integrates both qualitative and quantitative approaches. In geographic terms, most patient preference studies originated from the United States (US), with 24 [37–60] studies accounting for 28% of the included studies. Australia [61–77] and the United Kingdom [54, 78–87] contributed 17 and 11 studies, respectively. The dataset consists of three studies from Canada [88–90] and one from South Africa [91]. In Asia, contributions came from China [92–99], Japan [100, 101], South Korea [102, 103], and Singapore [104]. A study also focused on the Iranian population from West Asia [105]. In Europe, the Netherlands led with seven studies [106–112], followed by Spain with three [106, 111, 113] and Germany with four [106, 111, 114, 115]. Single studies were identified from Norway [116], Finland [117], Switzerland [118], Belgium [119], Ireland [120] as well as a single study conducted across Albania and Bulgaria [113]. Specifically, most qualitative studies were conducted in the United States (44.4%), followed by Europe (25,92%), Australia (11.11%) and China (7.41%), with one study each from South Africa and Canada.

The analysis highlights the heterogeneity in sample sizes, reflecting the diversity of approaches and scales employed in preference elicitation across various research contexts. In qualitative studies, the average sample size is 38 individuals, ranging from 9 to 152. Quantitative studies report an average sample size of 631, spanning from 17 to 6,271. Mixed-methods studies, meanwhile, have an average of 351 observations, ranging from 22 to 1,226. Specifically, attribute-based studies have an average sample size of 494, whereas non-attribute-based studies typically have a larger average of 1,780.

The included papers are divided into two main categories: 35.3% focus on the general characteristics of DHTs, while 64.7% examine preferences related to various pathologies. Among these, the most studied areas include mental health (11 studies), cardiology (7), and dermatology (4). Other health conditions covered include arthritis, cancer, neurological disorders, sexual and reproductive health, asthma, diabetes, HIV, prenatal care, rheumatic diseases, addiction, and chronic diseases. Specifically, qualitative research predominantly investigates preferences for the general features of DHTs, accounting for 40% of qualitative studies, and for specific conditions, including cancer, chronic diseases, sexual and reproductive health, diabetes, mental health, and rheumatic diseases.

Four categories were established based on the type of digital health technologies (DHTs) addressed: eHealth, telehealth, telemedicine, and mHealth. MHealth emerged as the most frequently studied category, represented in 29 studies (34.1%), followed by telehealth with 24 studies (28.2%) and telemedicine with 21 studies (24.7%). EHealth was the focus of 11 studies. While no specific type of DHTs was predominantly linked to any particular health condition, cardiology is an exception; 6 studies related explicitly to telemedicine (5) and mHealth (1) focused on applications such as blood pressure monitoring, cardiac rehabilitation, and hypertension management. Qualitative research has primarily concentrated on telehealth, representing 40% of studies (10 studies), while quantitative research focuses extensively on telemedicine and mHealth, accounting for 68% of quantitative studies. Mixed-methods research predominantly addresses mHealth in 38% of studies (5 out of 13) and telehealth in 30% (4 out of 13).

Methods used to elicit preferences

Quantitative studies employed four different methods to elicit preferences, which we classified into attribute-based and non-attribute-based approaches (Table C4 in appendix C, OSM). The majority (86.67%) utilized attribute-based methods, with discrete choice experiment (DCE) being the most commonly used approach, appearing in 35 studies [38, 41, 56, 60–67, 70–74, 78, 79, 81, 84–88, 92–94, 96, 97, 104, 106, 113, 114, 118, 121] (71.9%). The first DCE included in this analysis dates back to 2010. However, there has been a marked increase in publications since 2023. Notably, 21 out of 35 studies (60%) were published from 2023 onwards, including 8 studies in 2023 and 8 in 2025. Conjoint analysis [102, 103] and Best-Worst Scaling (BWS) [90, 95] were less frequently used to elicit patient preferences for DHTs, with only two studies employing each method. Furthermore, contingent valuation was used in 6 studies to estimate willingness to pay for DHTs, accounting for approximately 15% of all quantitative studies [37, 38, 50, 54, 100, 101].

Qualitative research typically involves collecting data through individual interviews and focus groups conducted in-person, via telephone, or online. Among the studies reviewed, personal interviews, predominantly semi-structured, were used in 13 studies (55%) [39, 43, 46, 48, 52, 59, 75, 98, 99, 107, 115, 117]. Focus groups alone were used in 8 studies (29%), while surveys were used in 3 studies (11%) [59, 72, 77]. A combination of focus groups and individual interviews was employed in 3 studies (11%) [55, 82, 89]. Additionally, one study applied the Delphi method to gather data and quantify survey scores for analysis [109]. Most qualitative studies employed three main analytical approaches to assess preferences: thematic content analysis, deductive approach, and inductive approach, which are sometimes used together. Thematic content analysis is the most common, used in 10 studies [39, 51, 57, 83, 89, 91, 116, 117, 120], followed by the inductive approach in 5 studies [42, 46, 53, 75, 98], and the deductive approach in 4 studies [40, 48, 82, 107]. Only 3 studies [55, 99, 115] integrate deductive and inductive approaches, and one employs immersion-crystallization [43].

Mixed-methods studies integrate both qualitative and quantitative approaches to elicit preferences. For the qualitative component, most studies utilize interviews and focus groups. In contrast, surveys [44, 47, 49, 58, 68, 80, 105, 110–112, 119] are predominantly used in the quantitative component, accounting for 80% of the methods, while DCE [108] and administrative data [45] are each employed once. Quantitative results (aside from those derived from DCE) are typically presented as descriptive statistics to complement the qualitative analysis.

Patients’ preferences

A word cloud was created to examine the attributes mentioned in studies using DCE (see Figure C1 in Appendix C, OSM). The data for the word cloud was prepared by extracting attributes from Table C1 (appendix C, OSM), followed by a cleaning process where common stop words, punctuation, and numbers were removed. Subsequently, the frequency of each term was calculated to generate the word cloud displayed in Figure C1. The analysis identified several terms frequently repeated across the included studies, including “time,” “cost,” “care,” “program,” “privacy,” “price,” “service,” “type,” “health,” and “consultation.” We extracted three key attributes that recur with notable frequency from these: data protection and privacy, time management, and price/cost.

Focusing on the three primary areas identified, privacy is featured as an attribute in 14% of the studies, time in 41%, and cost in 63%. Notably, when including non-attribute-based studies that examine willingness-to-pay, the percentage of studies addressing cost rises to 85%. Although cost is frequently chosen as an attribute, the payment vehicle and the payment context vary across studies (see Table D1 in Appendix D, OSM). The payment vehicle may relate to the digital tool itself (preferences to buy a device [80, 82] or a mobile application [75, 76, 84, 93]), the service fee (either one-time [55, 65] or monthly [38, 86, 96, 103, 114]), and the overall cost of care (including out-of-pocket expenses [51, 57, 59, 60, 62], specific fees for teleconsultations [63, 65, 71, 72, 74, 92, 97, 121], or costs to the health care system [79]). Such heterogeneity restricts the assessment of the relative importance of cost in individuals’ decisions regarding DHTs.

Preferences for health technology vary depending on the type of DHT assessed. In mHealth technologies, quantitative studies show that patients prioritize ease of use, utility, and data security [84, 85, 93, 94, 106, 118], with older adults focusing more on clinical support and diagnostic accuracy [73, 85, 121], and acceptance being influenced by physician endorsements [93, 94, 106]. Qualitative studies add that patients value simplicity, personalization, and interactive features, receiving information and communicating with providers or peers [43, 46, 51, 52, 91, 115]. Patients also report concerns about excessive dependence on technology [43], reduced personal contact with healthcare staff [51, 52, 91, 98, 115], data security [77, 115], and a preference for hybrid models combining digital and human support [51, 52, 115]. Mixed-methods studies illustrate that these preferences vary by condition and user group: for example, patients with chronic arthritis preferred medication support but rejected emotional support [119], trauma patients emphasized psychosocial support whereas physicians emphasized data integration into medical records [108], asthma patients favored tailored monitoring [80], and mental health patients largely preferred in-person therapy, with middle-aged adults being more open to DHTs [58].

In eHealth technologies, which include telehealth and telemedicine, quantitative studies indicate that patients seek guided follow-up and affordability [81], though evidence on adoption across age groups remains limited. Telehealth is well accepted for chronic disease management due to its convenience and cost-effectiveness [56], but less favoured for conditions that require physical evaluations [61, 66, 67]. Telemedicine is appreciated across all age groups for follow-ups or monitoring chronic conditions [64, 104], particularly in areas with limited access to care [63]. Qualitative studies emphasize secure [42, 53], interaction with healthcare professionals [39, 117], feedback [42, 53, 89, 117], and multimedia content [83, 89]. Patients express concerns about privacy, technical difficulties [39, 53, 59], and the need for in-person interactions for complex issues or initial sessions [39, 48, 82, 109, 120]. Mixed-methods studies provide further nuance: telehealth and telemedicine are recognized for saving travel time [49, 68], reducing costs [105], and providing access to care, yet in-person visits remain preferred for sensitive topics, physical examinations, or complex diagnoses [44, 45, 58, 111].

The type of health issue also influences patient preferences. Those with chronic diseases are often open to remote consultations for routine monitoring, viewing them as equivalent to in-person visits when cost is a favourable factor [43, 80, 82, 83, 89, 104, 119]. Patients with mental health conditions prioritize confidentiality and tend to prefer apps or teleconsultations over in-person therapy, provided that data security is guaranteed [56, 62, 109]. Conversely, individuals with cardiovascular diseases or rehabilitation needs generally prefer in-person interactions due to the perceived importance of physical evaluations [61]. Cancer patients prefer personalized digital tools tailored to their cancer stage, enabling self-monitoring and peer exchange among survivors, while valuing optional features and remaining concerned about reduced personal contact and data security [42, 46, 98, 115]. In cases of severe or urgent symptoms, patients of all ages consistently prefer in-person consultations [41, 64, 66].

Qualitative studies focus on different topics than quantitative studies, but they complement each other by revealing unique patient needs. Standard terms such as “personalized,” “hybrid,” “interactive,” “privacy,” and “convenience” appear often in qualitative research, showing a strong preference for tailored and customizable digital health solutions [40, 42, 46, 52, 53, 99, 116]. Across all ages, patients and care providers appreciate a mix of digital tools and in-person care, valuing face-to-face interactions for complex health issues and the convenience of digital tools for everyday health management [46, 51, 55, 120]. Interaction and engagement are also crucial for patients who want to participate in their healthcare journeys. They value DHTs designed with engaging interfaces and interactive learning opportunities, facilitating direct communication with health professionals.

Mixed-methods studies are often conducted to investigate preferences for customizing DHTs based on patients’ needs or to compare preferences for DHT versus in-person visits. These studies reveal that, although telemedicine provides convenience and accessibility, many patients still prefer in-person visits due to their superior communication and privacy, which are crucial for detailed diagnostics [44, 45, 47, 58, 105]. However, another preference is self-management tools that provide personalized coaching and actionable feedback [68, 80, 119]. Mixed-methods research often includes heterogeneity analyses, which investigate variations in preferences across different demographic groups. For instance, one study [58] specifically examined how various age groups engage with mental health DHTs. The results show that older adults are less likely to use mental health apps compared to younger and middle-aged adults, preferring traditional in-person therapy instead.

Few studies investigated preferences for people whose average age is over 60 years. Four qualitative studies [39, 53, 89, 99] indicate that older adults favor simple, multimodal educational tools (written, visual, video) and value face-to-face interactions for privacy and comfort. At the same time, technical issues hinder the uptake of video consultations. They also call for personalized telehealth services, but remain cautious about sharing personal data. Two quantitative studies [37, 63, 72] further show preferences for comprehensive, low-cost telehealth models, especially for those living far from hospitals, with face-to-face assessments before telehealth being particularly important. Willingness to pay for telemedicine decreases with cost, with patients suffering from chronic heart failure less price-sensitive than those with hypertension [49, 58, 110, 111]. Overall, older adults are generally willing to use telehealth technologies when these are simple, clearly explained, and tailored to their needs. They particularly appreciate multimodal educational tools and initial face-to-face interactions, which reassure and build trust. However, technical difficulties, cost, privacy, and data security concerns remain significant barriers that limit adoption. These findings suggest that while older adults are not inherently resistant to new technologies, practical and perceptual obstacles must be addressed to facilitate their use [99].

Such findings emphasize the importance of heterogeneity analyses in designing digital health interventions effectively tailored to meet distinct user groups’ diverse needs and preferences. Preferences vary even within the same demographic groups, reinforcing the importance of offering digital care as an option rather than a default.

Discussion

Recent advances in medical technology have led to increased research on patient preferences for DHTs. Initially dominated by quantitative methods with well-established methodologies [122], the field has progressively embraced qualitative research, offering richer insights into how patients perceive and value these tools [34]. Our scoping review indicates that both approaches yield broadly consistent results; however, qualitative research is more effective in exploring nuanced aspects, such as how personalization, usability and interaction features influence perceived value, dimensions that quantitative analyses often overlook. While mixed-methods designs are intended to leverage the strengths of both approaches, studies that rigorously integrate quantitative models with qualitative insights remain scarce. Among the mixed-methods studies identified, only one discrete choice experiment among the mixed-methods studies that successfully implemented this combination [108].

Cost emerged as a central determinant of DHT adoption, discussed in 85% of the included studies. However, the variety of payment schemes used to address the cost of DHTs complicates the comparison of study findings. Accurate comparisons are only feasible among studies with similar cost perspectives, whether patient out-of-pocket expenses for devices, mobile applications, care, or costs from the public payer’s viewpoint. Among the 27 quantitative studies reviewed, only 13 assessed the relative weight of attributes [123–125], and just three found cost to be one of the most valued features (Table D1 in Appendix D). This finding highlights the need for greater transparency and methodological rigor in reporting preference structures, particularly for economic attributes. Understanding how patients value costs is essential to inform reimbursement policies and improve both uptake and efficiency in DHT implementation.

The heterogeneity observed across study settings (i.e., country, healthcare system characteristics, target populations, types of DHTs assessed, and methodological approaches) may reflect differences in national reimbursement and regulatory frameworks for DHTs. Evidence from European countries indicates that reimbursement pathways for DHTs remain fragmented, with substantial variation in eligibility criteria, pricing mechanisms, and funding approaches [126, 127]. For instance, Germany is often considered a pioneer, having introduced a structured and mandatory reimbursement pathway for digital health applications through the Digital Health Care Act, whereas other countries such as England, Belgium, and France have developed alternative approaches, including pilot programs, conditional reimbursement schemes, or evolving assessment frameworks. Such differences are likely to influence both access to DHTs and the contexts in which patients interact with them, thereby shaping their preferences. This suggests that patient preferences should be interpreted within their institutional context rather than as universally transferable findings. From a policy perspective, incorporating preference evidence into decision-making may help design reimbursement and implementation strategies that are better aligned with patient expectations, ultimately supporting more efficient and context-sensitive allocation of healthcare resources. More specifically, our findings suggest that patient preferences for attributes such as usability, personalization, affordability, and data security capture dimensions that are not fully reflected in traditional HTA metrics, such as clinical outcomes or PROMs. Integrating these preference-based insights into HTA processes could improve the assessment of real-world acceptability and uptake of DHTs, thereby supporting more informed reimbursement decisions [128].

Patients consistently express preferences for customizable DHTs that support self-management through educational content, feedback, and communication with healthcare providers. These tools can be effectively integrated into blended care (BC) models that combine digital interventions with in-person consultations, increasingly applied in prevention, rehabilitation, chronic disease management, and mental health [129–131]. This approach is consistent with the ongoing digital transformation of healthcare systems in Europe, including initiatives such as the European Health Data Space (EHDS) and national pilots [132]. For instance, Germany has established a structured reimbursement pathway for digital health applications [133], enabling their integration into routine care, while the Netherlands increasingly promotes BC models for chronic conditions, positioning them as part of broader system modernization [134]. These cases illustrate how policy frameworks can drive BC adoption, enhancing care continuity and efficiency amid workforce pressures and aging populations. However, BC can only deliver its full potential under certain conditions. Successful implementation requires engagement from both patients and healthcare professionals [129]. While healthcare professionals generally view BC positively, they need adequate training and must overcome challenges related to remote care delivery, long-term follow-up, and evolving roles such as educator or coach [114, 135]. From the patient perspective, material access to technology, time availability, and trust in remote care are crucial determinants of engagement [136]. Overall, realizing the potential of BC within Europe’s digital transformation requires aligning patient and provider preferences with supportive policy frameworks and appropriate infrastructure. Addressing these constraints is essential for the effective integration of DHTs into routine care.

Privacy and data security are also fundamental to DHT acceptance [137]. Patients demand high levels of confidentiality and transparency in managing their medical information, particularly in mHealth applications [138]. Risks such as hacking, phishing, and weak encryption can undermine trust in digital systems. Providers must therefore adopt robust measures, including encryption, multi-factor authentication, and regular security updates [139]. This concern is echoed in the WHO’s global strategy on digital health (2020–2025), which emphasizes the need for legal and regulatory frameworks ensuring privacy, confidentiality, integrity, and data availability [21]. Building trust in data management is not only an ethical requirement but also a practical condition for widespread DHT adoption.

Preferences for DHTs show both convergence and variation across study designs and populations. Quantitative studies consistently highlight core factors influencing adoption: ease of use, utility, cost, and data security, with older adults emphasizing clinical support and reliability [37, 38, 41, 50, 54, 56, 61–67, 78, 79, 81, 84, 85, 88, 90, 92–95, 100–104, 106, 114, 118]. Qualitative research is particularly valuable for eliciting preferences beyond what quantitative models can capture, revealing nuanced needs such as personalization, interactivity, hybrid models combining digital and face-to-face care, and concerns about reduced personal contact or overreliance on technology [39, 40, 42, 43, 46, 48, 51–53, 55, 57, 82, 83, 89, 91, 107, 109, 115, 117, 120]. Mixed-methods studies bridge these perspectives, showing that preferences can differ by health condition, user group, and age [44, 45, 47, 49, 58, 68, 80, 105, 108, 110, 111, 119]. For example, older adults prefer in-person interactions and simple, tailored tools, while middle-aged adults may be more open to digital solutions.

Our scoping review of preferences for DHTs has several limitations. First, it includes various devices and services with distinct features and contexts of use, limiting the comparison of individual preferences across these different technologies. In addition, our review primarily assesses the quality of result reporting in quantitative studies, a focus that aligns with the recent checklist for DCE, but does not include a formal appraisal of the quality of qualitative research. Future research could address this gap by applying established frameworks such as the CASP Qualitative Checklist or JBI-QARI for structured quality assessment [140, 141]. The use of reporting guidelines such as COREQ may also further enhance transparency and comparability across qualitative studies [142]. Furthermore, the review mainly includes published studies, potentially omitting unpublished research or negative results, which introduces the risk of publication bias. Our analysis focused on specific quantitative preference analysis methods and did not encompass more exploratory methods such as the allocation of points, the analytic hierarchy process, or constant sum scaling [143]. However, our search terms were broad enough to include these methods under the umbrella of ‘patient preferences’ had they been present in the literature. We adhered to the PRISMA-scR checklist to ensure comprehensiveness (Appendix A).

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that patients’ preferences for DHTs are shaped by usability, personalization, affordability, and trust in data management. Tailoring DHTs to individual health conditions is particularly valued, especially by older adults who may initially be reluctant to use digital tools but can benefit from technologies that support chronic disease management. While quantitative studies quantify trade-offs and willingness to pay, qualitative research provides richer insights into personalization needs and the acceptability of hybrid care models combining digital and face-to-face interactions. Integrating these complementary approaches through mixed methods can better capture the diversity of patient preferences across age groups and health conditions.

Incorporating patient preferences into the design, evaluation, and reimbursement of DHTs can enhance engagement, satisfaction, and health outcomes. Future research should further explore trade-offs from both patient and payer perspectives, particularly regarding cost, convenience, and privacy guarantees, and support the harmonization of digital health frameworks across Europe. Recent updates in countries such as Germany, France and Finland illustrate the potential for these findings to inform policy and the development of interventions tailored to diverse patient needs [144, 145].
