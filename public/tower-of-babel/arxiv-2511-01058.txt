Limit profiles and cutoff for the Burnside process on Sylow double cosets
Michael Howes

Abstract
This article gives sharp estimates for the mixing time of the Burnside process for Sylow p p -double cosets in the symmetric group S n S_{n} . This process is a Markov chain on S n S_{n} which can be used to uniformly sample Sylow p p -double cosets. The analysis applies when n = p ​ k n=pk with p p prime and k < p k<p . The main result describes the limit profile of the distance to the stationary distribution as p p goes to infinity. From the limit profile, we get the following two corollaries. First, if k k remains fixed as p → ∞ p\to\infty , then order p p steps are necessary and sufficient for mixing and cutoff does not occur. Second, if k → ∞ k\to\infty as p → ∞ p\to\infty , then cutoff occurs at p ​ log ⁡ k p\log k with a window of size p p . The limit profile is derived from explicit upper and lower bounds on the distance between the Burnside process and its stationary distribution. These non-asymptotic bounds give very accurate approximations even for p p as small as 11 11 .

Introduction and main results

The Burnside process is a Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm for uniformly sampling orbits from a group action
[
16
]
. Recent works have shown empirically that the Burnside process mixes quickly in a number of complex and interesting examples
[
3
,
8
,
14
]
. This paper is perhaps the first to provide a sharp analysis of the mixing time of an instance of the Burnside process for double cosets.

To describe the Burnside process, let
\mathscr{X}
be a finite set and let
G
be a finite group acting on
\mathscr{X}
with
x^{g}
denoting the image of
g
acting on
x
. The set
{\mathscr{X}}
splits into disjoint orbits

{\mathscr{X}}=\mathscr{O}_{x_{1}}\sqcup\mathscr{O}_{x_{2}}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\mathscr{O}_{x_{Z}},

where
\mathscr{O}_{x}=\{x^{g}:g\in G\}
is the orbit containing
x
and
Z
is the number of orbits. The Burnside process is a Markov chain on
{\mathscr{X}}
with stationary distribution
\pi(x)=\frac{1}{Z|\mathscr{O}_{x}|}
. Thus, sampling
X
from
\pi
and reporting the orbit containing
X
gives a way of uniformly sampling an orbit. Furthermore, taking many steps of the Burnside produces random elements of
{\mathscr{X}}
that are approximately distributed according to
\pi

The Burnside process transitions from

to

according to the following two-step procedure:

1.

From
x\in\mathscr{X}
, choose
g
uniformly from the set
G_{x}=\{g\in G:x^{g}=x\}
.

2.

Given

, choose
y
uniformly from the set
{\mathscr{X}}^{g}=\{y\in{\mathscr{X}}:y^{g}=y\}
.

The probability of transitioning from
x
to

is thus given by the following transition kernel

P(x,y)=\frac{1}{|G_{x}|}\sum_{g\in G_{x}\cap G_{y}}\frac{1}{|{\mathscr{X}}^{g}|},

(1)

The kernel

is ergodic and reversible with respect to

is the unique stationary distribution of

[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
. For

P_{x}^{1}(y)=P_{x}(y)=P(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad P_{x}^{t}(y)=\sum_{z\in{\mathscr{X}}}P^{t-1}_{x}(z)P_{z}(y).

This paper focuses on a particular case of the Burnside process called the

is the symmetric group of degree

Under this group action the orbits are Sylow

If

is an ergodic transition kernel, then the distribution

converges to

The field of Markov chain mixing is focused on proving upper bounds on

\displaystyle d(t)

1.1
Proof overview

Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
rely on two main ideas. The first idea is that the Sylow–Burnside process on
S_{pk}
lumps to the size of the double coset. The second idea is that most double cosets are of maximal size.

1.1.1
Lumping to double coset size

A Markov chain lumps under a function
T
if the following holds:

Definition 1.5
.

Let
P
be a transition kernel on a set
{\mathscr{X}}
and let
T:{\mathscr{X}}\to\mathscr{Y}
be a function. The transition kernel
P

lumps under
T
if for all Markov chains
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
generated by
P
, the process
Y_{t}=T(X_{t})
is a Markov chain on
\mathscr{Y}
. The process
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is called a
lumped process
.

If
P
lumps under
T
, then we will let
\overline{P}
denote the transition kernel for the lumped process. The kernel
\overline{P}
is called the
lumped kernel
. Furthermore, if
P
has a unique stationary distribution
\pi
, then
\overline{P}
has a unique stationary distribution
\overline{\pi}
. The distribution
\overline{\pi}
is called the
lumped stationary distribution
and
\overline{\pi}(y)=\pi(\{x:T(x)=y\})
.

Lumping a Markov chain can help analyze the mixing time of a Markov chain. This is because the lumped kernel
\overline{P}
has a smaller state space and is often easier to analyze. In the current example, the Sylow
p
-double cosets in
S_{pk}
can have size
p^{k},p^{k+1},\ldots,p^{2k}
. This means that mapping a permutation to the size of its double coset reduces the problem from a state space of size
(pk)!
to a state space of size
k+1
. The following result gives an exact formula for the lumped transition kernel of the Sylow–Burnside process.

Theorem 1.6
.

Define
T:S_{pk}\to\{k,\ldots 2k\}
by

T(\sigma)=a\Longleftrightarrow|H\sigma H|=p^{a}.

(7)

Then the Sylow–Burnside kernel
P
lumps under
T
and the lumped transition kernel is given by

\overline{P}(a,b)=\sum_{y=0}^{2k-\max\{a,b\}}\binom{2k-a}{y}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{y}\frac{p^{a+b-2k}}{(p(k-y))!}f(b-y;k-y),

(8)

where
f(a;k)
is the number of Sylow
p
-double cosets in
S_{pk}
of size
p^{a}
. Furthermore, if
Z=\sum_{a=k}^{2k}f(a;k)
is the total number of Sylow
p
-double cosets, then the lumped stationary distribution is given by

\overline{\pi}(a)=\frac{f(a;k)}{Z}.

(9)

Some aspects of a Markov chain can be “lost” when going from
P
to
\overline{P}
. For instance, it is always true that if
P
is a transition kernel on
{\mathscr{X}}
that lumps under
T
, then for all
x\in{\mathscr{X}}

\|\overline{P}^{t}_{T(x)}-\overline{\pi}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|P^{t}_{x}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

However, the reverse inequality does not always hold—for example it fails when
T
is a constant. For the Sylow–Burnside process with
T
defined as in (
7
), Lemma
4.2
states that reverse inequality holds up to a small error that goes to zero as
p\to\infty
. Thus, for the purposes of studying
d(t)
, we can replace
P
with
\overline{P}
and reduce our problem to a much smaller state space.

Lumping has played an important role in the study and implementation of other examples of the Burnside process.
[
10
]
showed that the Burnside process always lumps to the orbit—meaning the Markov chain lumps under
T(x)=\mathscr{O}_{x}
. In
[
10
]
an explicit formula for the lumped transition kernel was used to bound the mixing time of the Burnside process for
S_{n}
acting on binary strings.
[
13
]
also study this example and compute the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the lumped process. More recently,
[
12
]
compute the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the unlumped process and show that the mixing time depends heavily on the starting state. In a different direction,
[
8
]
study the Burnside process for contingency tables and integer partitions and show that the lumped process can be enormously more efficient to run than the original process. The present example shows that sometimes it is useful to lump the Burnside process even further.

1.1.2
Most double cosets are large

To analyze the lumped Sylow–Burnside process, we need to understand
f(a;k)
—the number of Sylow
p
-double cosets in
S_{pk}
of size
p^{a}
. As claimed in Theorem
1.6
, the stationary distribution of the lumped process is given by

\overline{\pi}(a)=\frac{f(a;k)}{Z}.

[
7
]
give a formula for
f(a;k)
that we restate in (
11
).
[
7
]
also give strong bounds on
f(a;k)
. These bounds show that most Sylow
p
-double cosets have size
p^{2k}
in the sense that
f(2k;k)/Z\to 1
as
p\to\infty
. This means that the lumped stationary distribution
\overline{\pi}
is well approximated by a point mass at
2k
. Using this approximation, we get that

\|\overline{P}^{t}_{a}-\overline{\pi}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\approx 1-\overline{P}^{t}_{a}(2k).

That is, the total variation distance between
\overline{P}^{t}_{a}
and
\pi
is roughly equal to the probability that
\overline{P}^{t}_{a}
assigns to
\{k,\ldots,2k-1\}
. To compute
\overline{P}^{t}_{a}(2k)
we use another approximation that replaces
\overline{P}
with a second transition kernel
Q
. The approximating transition kernel
Q
has a nice probabilistic description. To move from
a
to
b
under
Q
do the following:

1.

Flip
2k-a
independent coins each with heads probability
1/p
.

2.

Set
b=a+X
where
X
is the number of coins that land on heads.

From this description, it follows that
Q_{a}^{t}(2k)
is equal to the probability that the maximum of
2k-a
independent geometric random variables is at most
t
. Thus,

\|\overline{P}^{t}_{a}-\overline{\pi}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\approx 1-Q^{t}_{a}(2k)=1-\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}\right)^{2k-a}.

Proving the above formula for
Q^{t}_{a}(2k)
and bounding the distance between
\overline{P}^{t}_{a}
and
Q^{t}_{a}
gives Theorem
1.3
and in turn Theorem
1.1
.

2
Background

2.1
The Burnside process

This section reviews some important properties of the Burnside process and surveys recent work on other instances of the Burnside process. Let
G
be a finite group acting on a finite set
{\mathscr{X}}
. As described in the introduction, the Burnside process is a Markov chain on
{\mathscr{X}}
with transitions from
x
to
y
according to the following two-step rule:

1.

From
x\in\mathscr{X}
, choose
g
uniformly from the set
G_{x}=\{g\in G:x^{g}=x\}
.

2.

Given
g
, choose
y
uniformly from the set
{\mathscr{X}}^{g}=\{y\in{\mathscr{X}}:y^{g}=y\}
.

The Burnside process can equivalently be described as a random walk on a large bipartite graph. The bi-partition is
{\mathscr{X}}\sqcup G
and an edge connects
x
to
g
if and only if
x^{g}=x
. The two steps of the Burnside process are equivalent to picking
g
uniformly from the neighbors of
x
and then picking
y
uniformly from the neighbors of
g
. Since the identity group element is connected to every
x\in{\mathscr{X}}
, this two-step walk is ergodic on
{\mathscr{X}}
. Furthermore, the stationary distribution of the random walk is given by:

\pi(x)=\frac{\deg(x)}{|\{(x,g):x^{g}=x\}|}=\frac{|G_{x}|}{|\{(x,g):x^{g}=x\}|}=\frac{|G|}{|\mathscr{O}_{x}||\{(x,g):x^{g}=x\}|}=\frac{1}{Z|\mathscr{O}_{x}|}.

(10)

In (
10
), the last equality holds because
|\{(x,g):x^{g}=x\}|=Z|G|
by the lemma that is not Burnside’s
[
20
]
. The second last equality uses the orbit–stabilizer theorem (for all
x\in{\mathscr{X}}
,
|G|=|\mathscr{O}_{x}||G_{x}|
).

The definition of the Burnside process and the above results were introduced in
[
16
]
as an approach to computational Pólya theory.
[
16
]
proposed using the Burnside process to estimate the number of orbits
Z
— a task that has applications in chemistry, statistics and computer science. To make the Burnside process a tractable algorithm for estimating
Z
, two things must be satisfied. First, it must be possible to draw uniform samples from both
G_{x}
and
{\mathscr{X}}^{g}
as in the definition of the Burnside process. Second, it is necessary to have a bound on the mixing time of the Burnside process. Starting with
[
16
]
, different works have answered these questions for various group actions and found both positive and negative results.

In the negative direction
[
15
]
showed that there exist group actions where the Burnside process mixes slowly. They construct their examples by connecting the Burnside process to the Swendsen–Wang algorithm for sampling from Pott’s models. Other works have studied the mixing time of the Burnside process for different group actions and found more positive results. These examples include Bose–Einstein statistics
[
10
,
13
,
12
]
, large sparse contingency tables
[
11
]
, set partitions
[
21
]
, and conjugacy classes in CA groups
[
22
]
.

There also exist a number of works that focus on the implementation of the Burnside process for different applications. These include
[
3
]
for sampling Pólya trees,
[
8
]
for sampling contingency tables and integer partitions and
[
14
]
for counting conjugacy classes in uni-triangular matrix groups. These papers derive implementations of the Burnside process and empirically find that the Burnside process mixes quickly.

Beyond the above examples, properly understanding the mixing time of the Burnside process is an open problem. For instance, Theorem
1.1
is the first time a limit profile has been computed for a natural instance Burnside process. We hope that our analysis can inform the study of the Burnside process for other group actions.

2.2
Sylow
p
-double cosets in the symmetric group

In this section we describe the Sylow
p
-double cosets in the symmetric group and state some results from
[
7
]
. Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group of permutation of size
n
. A Sylow
p
-subgroup of
S_{n}
is a subgroup
H\subseteq S_{n}
of order
p^{m}
where
p^{m}
is the largest power of
p
that divides
|S_{n}|=n!
. The value of

is given by

m=\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{a}}\right\rfloor.

The structure of the subgroup
H
was determined by
[
17
]
and can be described as the automorphism group of a
p

[
23
]
. The product group
H\times H
acts on the symmetric group by

\sigma^{h,g}=h^{-1}\sigma g.

The orbits under this group action are the
Sylow
p
-double cosets
. A Sylow
p
-double coset is a set of the form

H\sigma H=\{h\sigma g:h,g\in H\}.

The Sylow
p
-double cosets form a partition of
S_{n}
and the collection of all double cosets is represented as
H\backslash S_{n}/H
. By the orbit–stabilizer theorem, the size of
H\sigma H
is given by

|H\sigma H|=\frac{|H|^{2}}{|H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma|}.

The subgroup
H
has size
p^{m}
and, by Lagrange’s theorem
|H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma|
must divide
p^{m}
. It follows that the size of
|H\sigma H|
must be one of
p^{m},p^{m+1},\ldots,p^{2m}
.
[
7
]
show that, except for a handful of values of
n
and

, each possible size occurs as
\sigma
ranges over
S_{n}
. They also show that, as long as
p\geq 3
, the proportion of permutations
\sigma
in double coset of size
p^{2m}
goes to
1
as
n\to\infty
. Thus, most permutations are in large double cosets.

The results in this paper are restricted to the “Abelian case” when
n=pk
with
k<p
and so

. The name refers to the fact that the Sylow p-subgroup

is Abelian if and only if

. When
n=pk
with
k<p
, the Sylow
p
-subgroup

is isomorphic to

.

As in the general case, the possible values for

are
p^{k},p^{k+1},\ldots,p^{2k}
.
[

]

k<p
,

Z\left(1-\frac{2p^{4}}{(p-1)!}\right)\leq f(2k;k)\leq Z.

Both Theorem
2.2
and Proposition
2.3
are quantitative versions of the statement “most Sylow
p
-double cosets are of size
p^{2k}
.” However, the two results correspond to different meanings of “most.” Theorem
2.2
states that if
\sigma
is chosen uniformly from
S_{pk}
, then the probability that
\sigma
is in a large double-coset goes to 1 as
p
goes to infinity. That is,
p^{2k}f(2k;k)/(pk)!\to 1
. On the other hand, Proposition
2.3
states that if the double coset is chosen uniformly at random, then the probability that double coset is of size
p^{2k}
goes to
1
. That is,
f(2k;k)/Z\to 1
. Both these results hold in a very strong sense. They are uniform in
k<p
and the error terms go to zero super-exponentially. Theorem
2.2
and Proposition
2.3
are both used to prove the mixing time result in Theorem
1.3
.

There are many other things to say about Sylow
p
-subgroups and double cosets in
S_{n}
. See
[
23
]
and
[
7
]
for a growing literature.

3
The Sylow–Burnside process

In this section we study the Sylow Burnside process. Section
3.1
contains an implementation of the Sylow–Burnside process and Section
3.2
proves that the Sylow–Burnside process lumps to double coset size. We begin by setting some notation and by fixing a particular Sylow
p
-subgroup to work with.

For integers
a\leq b
,
[a:b]
will be the set
\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
of size
b-a+1
. Through-out
H
will be a fixed Sylow
p
-subgroup in
S_{pk}
with
p
prime and
1\leq k<p
. Without loss of generality, we will take
H
to be the subgroup

H=\langle\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}\rangle,

(12)

where
\eta_{j}
is the cyclic permutation
((j-1)p+1,\ldots,jp)
. The permutations
\eta_{j}
have order
p
and commute. This means that each
g\in H
can be written uniquely as

g=\eta_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\eta_{k}^{i_{k}}\quad i_{j}\in[0:p-1].

(13)

It follows that if
|\{j\in[1:k]:i_{j}\neq 0\}|=y
, then
g
has
y

p
-cycles and
(k-y)p
fixed points.

3.1
Implementation of the Sylow–Burnside process

Let
H
be the Sylow
p
-subgroup defined by (
12
). The goal of this section is to describe how to implement the Burnside process for
H\times H
acting on
S_{pk}
by
\sigma^{h,g}=h^{-1}\sigma g
. That is, how to draw uniform samples in the following two steps:

1.

Given
\sigma\in S_{pk}
, sample
h,g
uniformly from the stabilizer
(H\times H)_{\sigma}=\{(h,g):\sigma^{h,g}=\sigma\}
.

2.

Given
h,g
, sample
\tau
uniformly from the set of fixed points
S_{pk}^{h,g}=\{\tau:\tau^{h,g}=\tau\}
.

These two steps are described formally in Algorithms
1
and
2
respectively and their derivations are given in the next two subsections. Both algorithms are based on results from Section 3 of
[
8
]
which gives a general implementation of the Burnside process for double cosets. The key observation is the equivalence

\sigma^{h,g}=\sigma\Longleftrightarrow h=\sigma g\sigma^{-1}.

(14)

Algorithms
1
and
2
are easy to implement and quick to run. The GitHub repository
https://github.com/Michael-Howes/BurnsideProcess
contains an implementation of the Sylow–Burnside process in the Julia Programming Language
[
4
]
. This implementation is used to perform the simulations in Section
5.2
.

3.1.1
Sampling from a stabilizer

Equation (
14
) implies that the map
g\mapsto(\sigma g\sigma^{-1},g)
is a bijection between
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
and
(H\times H)_{\sigma}
. Thus, to perform the first step it is sufficient to be able to sample from
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
. By definition

H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma=\langle\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}\rangle\cap\langle\sigma^{-1}\eta_{1}\sigma,\ldots,\sigma^{-1}\eta_{k}\sigma\rangle.

Furthermore, the permutations
\sigma^{-1}\eta_{j}\sigma
are all cyclic permutations of order
p
. Thus, if
\sigma^{-1}\eta_{j}\sigma\in H
for some
j
, then it must be the case that
\sigma^{-1}\eta_{j}\sigma=\eta_{j^{\prime}}^{i}
for some
j^{\prime}\in[1:k]
and
i\in[1:p-1]
. It follows that if
A=\{j:\sigma\eta_{j}\sigma^{-1}\in H\}
, then

H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma=\langle\eta_{j}:j\in A\rangle.

And thus every element of
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
can be written uniquely as
\prod_{j\in A}\eta_{j}^{i_{j}}
for some choice of
i_{j}\in[0:(p-1)]
. From this observation, it is easy to draw uniform samples from
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
and hence
(H\times H)_{\sigma}
. This idea is formalized in Algorithm
1
.

3.1.2
Sampling from a set of fixed points

Now we will describe how to sample

for some

Let

3.2
Proof of Theorem
1.6

Algorithms
1
and
2
will be used to show that the Sylow–Burnside process lumps to double coset size. Let
P
be the transition kernel for the Sylow–Burnside process and let
T:S_{pk}\to[k:2k]
be given by
|H\sigma H|=p^{T(\sigma)}
. Theorem
1.6
states that
P
lumps under
T
and gives expressions for the lumped kernel
\overline{P}
and stationary distribution
\overline{\pi}
. By Dynkin’s criteria
[
18
, Section 6.3]
it is sufficient to show that for all
a,b\in[k:2k]
if
T(\sigma)=a
, then

\displaystyle\overline{P}_{a}(b)

\displaystyle:=P_{\sigma}(\{\tau:T(\tau)=b\})=\sum_{y=0}^{2k-\max\{a,b\}}\binom{2k-a}{y}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{y}\frac{p^{a+b-2k}}{(p(k-y))!}f(b-y;k-y),

(16)

\displaystyle\overline{\pi}(b)

\displaystyle:=\pi(\{\tau:T(\tau)=b\})=\frac{f(b;k)}{Z},

(17)

where
f(a;k)
is the number of double cosets in
H\backslash S_{pk}/\penalty H
of size
p^{a}
and
Z
is the total number of double cosets. To show (
16
), we will introduce a second function
R:H\to[0:k]
and then prove two lemmas about the relationship between
R
and
T
. To define
R(g)
write
g=\eta_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\eta_{k}^{i_{k}}
as in (
13
) and set

R(g)=|\{j\in[1:k]:i_{j}\neq 0\}|.

(18)

That is,
R(g)
is the number of
p
-cycles in
g
. The first lemma, Lemma
3.1
, describes the distribution of
R(g)
when
g
is uniformly sampled from
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
as in Algorithm
1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix
\sigma\in S_{pk}
such that
T(\sigma)=a
. If
g
is uniformly sampled from
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
, then

\mathbb{P}(R(g)=y)=\binom{2k-a}{y}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{y}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)^{2k-a-y}.

In words,
R(g)
is binomially distributed with parameters
2k-a
and
\frac{p-1}{p}
.

Proof.

By Algorithm
1
, we know that
g
can be represented as

g=\prod_{j\in A}\eta_{j}^{i_{j}},

where
A=\{j:\sigma\eta_{j}\sigma^{-1}\in H\}
and
\{i_{j}:j\in A\}
are independent and uniformly distributed on
[0:(p-1)]
. Furthermore, we have that

p^{|A|}=|H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma|=\frac{|H|^{2}}{|H\sigma H|}=p^{2k-a}.

Thus,
R(g)=\sum_{j\in A}I[i_{j}\neq 0]
is equal in distribution to the sum of
2k-a
independent binary variables each with expected value
\frac{p-1}{p}
. Thus,
R(g)
is binomially distributed with parameters
2k-a
and
\frac{p-1}{p}
as claimed.
∎

The next result, Lemma
3.2
, gives the distribution of
T(\tau)
when
\tau
is sampled from
S_{pk}^{h,g}
as in Algorithm
2
. It shows that the distribution of
T(\tau)
can be described in terms of uniformly sampling
\tau^{\prime}\in S_{pk^{\prime}}
with
k^{\prime}\leq k
and looking at the size of the
p
-Sylow double coset containing
\tau^{\prime}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(h,g)\in H\times H
be such that
S_{pk}^{h,g}\neq\emptyset
and
R(g)=R(h)=y
. Let
\tau
be uniformly distributed in
S_{pk}^{(h,g)}
, then

\mathbb{P}(T(\tau)=b)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{(p(k-y))!}p^{b-y}f(b-y;k-y)&\text{if }b\leq 2k-y,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

First note that

T(\tau)=2k-|\{j:\tau\eta_{j}\tau^{-1}\in H\}|.

Next write
g=\eta_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\eta_{k}^{i_{k}}
and set
J_{0}(g):=\{j\in[1:k]:\eta_{j}=0\}
and
J_{1}(g):=[1:k]\setminus J_{0}(g)
. Define
J_{0}(h)
and
J_{1}(h)
analogously.

By assumption, we have that
|J_{1}(g)|=|J_{1}(h)|=y
and
|J_{0}(g)|=|J_{0}(h)|=k-y
. In particular,
g
and
h
both have
(k-y)p
fixed points and
y

p
-cycles. By Algorithm
2
we know that
\tau
must map each cycle
\eta_{j}^{i_{j}}
with
i_{j}\neq 0
to a power of a
p
-cycle of
h
. It therefore follows that if
j\in J_{1}(g)
, then
\tau\eta_{j}\tau^{-1}\in H
. Therefore,

T(\tau)\leq 2k-|J_{1}(g)|=2k-y.

It remains to show that if
b\leq 2k-y
, then

\mathbb{P}(T(\tau)=b)=\frac{1}{(p(k-y))!}p^{b-y}f(b-y;k-y).

To see this, let
F(g)
and
F(h)
be the set of fixed points of
g
and
h
. Recall that by Algorithm
2
,
\tau
induces a random bijection from
F(g)
to
F(h)
. It follows that the number of
j\in J_{1}(h)
such that
\tau\eta_{j}\tau^{-1}\in H
is equal in distribution
|\{j\in[1:(k-y)]:\rho\eta_{j}\rho^{-1}\in H^{\prime}\}|
where
\rho
is uniformly sampled form
S_{p(k-y)}
and
H^{\prime}
is a
p
-Sylow subgroup of
S_{p(k-y)}
. It follows that for
b\leq 2k-y
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T(\tau)=b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(2k-|\{j\in J_{0}(h):\tau\eta_{j}\tau^{-1}\in H\}|+|\{j\in J_{1}(h):\tau\eta_{j}\tau^{-1}\in H\}|=b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(2k-y-|\{j\in[1:(k-y)]:\rho\eta_{j}\rho^{-1}\in H\}|=b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(2(k-y)-|\{j\in[1:(k-y)]:\rho\eta_{j}\rho^{-1}\in H\}|=b-y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(|H^{\prime}\rho H^{\prime}|=p^{b-y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{((k-y)p)!}p^{b-y}f(b-y;k-y),

where the last line holds because there are
p^{b-y}f(b-y;k-y)
permutations in
S_{(k-y)p}
in double cosets of size
p^{b-y}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.6
by showing (
16
) and (
17
). This is done by marginalizing over the distribution of
R(g)
where
h,g
are sampled in the first step of the Sylow–Burnside process. Lemma
3.1
gives the distribution of
R(g)
and Lemma
3.2
gives the conditional distribution of
T(\tau)
given
R(g)
. The distribution of
T(\tau)
when
\tau\sim P_{\sigma}
is derived by combining these two results.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

To prove (
16
), fix
\sigma\in S_{pk}
with
T(\sigma)=a
. To sample
\tau\sim P_{\sigma}
, we first sample
(h,g)
as in Algorithm
1
and then sample
\tau
as in Algorithm
2
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}(T(\sigma)=b)=\sum_{y=0}^{k}\mathbb{P}(R(g)=y)\mathbb{P}(T(\tau)=b\mid R(g)=y)

By Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{y=0}^{k}\mathbb{P}(R(g)=y)\mathbb{P}(T(\tau)=b\mid R(g)=y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=0}^{k}\binom{2k-a}{y}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{y}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)^{2k-a-y}\frac{1}{(p(k-y))!}p^{b-y}f(b-y;k-y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=0}^{2k-\max\{a,b\}}\binom{2k-a}{y}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{y}\frac{p^{a+b-2k}}{(p(k-y))!}f(b-y;k-y).

Thus,
P
lumps under the map
T
and the lumped kernel is given by (
16
). For the stationary distribution of
\overline{P}
, suppose that
\tau\sim\pi
, then

\overline{\pi}(b)=\mathbb{P}(T(\tau)=b)=\frac{1}{Zp^{b}}p^{b}f(b;k)=\frac{f(b;k)}{Z},

and so
\overline{\pi}
is given by (
17
).
∎

4
Proofs of mixing time results

In this section we first prove the non-asymptotic result in Theorem
1.3
and then take a limit to derive the limit profile in Theorem
1.1
.

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1.3

As explained in Section
1.1
, there are two ideas behind the proof of Theorem
1.3
. The first idea is to approximate the mixing time of
P
with the mixing time of
\overline{P}
. This approximation is based on the fact that most double cosets are large and is proved in Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
.

The second idea is that the mixing time of
\overline{P}
is approximately the time it takes reach
2k
(corresponding to the Burnside process reaching the large double cosets). To compute the time it takes
\overline{P}
to reach
2k
we approximate
\overline{P}
by a simpler transition kernel
Q
. In Lemma
4.3
we show that
Q
is a good approximation to
\overline{P}
. Finally, in Lemma
4.5
, we compute the time it takes
Q
to reach
2k
. Combining this calculation with the various approximations gives Theorem
1.3
.

The first lemma describes the conditional distribution of
\tau
when
\tau
is sampled via the Burnside process and conditioned on being in a large double coset. Specifically, it states that if
\tau\sim P^{t}_{\sigma}
and
T(\tau)=2k
, then
\tau
is uniformly distributed over the set of such
\tau
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
B=\{\sigma\in S_{pk}:T(\sigma)=2k\}
and fix
\sigma\in S_{pk}
and
t\geq 1
. Then, the conditional distributions
P^{t}_{\sigma}(\cdot\mid B)
and
\pi(\cdot\mid B)
are both uniform on
B
. That is, for any,
A\subseteq S_{pk}

P^{t}_{\sigma}(A\mid B)=\pi(A\mid B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}.

Proof.

Note that
\pi(\tau)=Z^{-1}p^{-T(\tau)}
. Thus,
\pi(\tau)
depends only on
T(\tau)
and so
\pi(\cdot\mid B)
is uniform on
B
.

To show that
P^{t}_{\sigma}(A\mid B)=|A\cap B|/|B|
we can assume without loss of generality that
t=1
. This is because for any
t\geq 1

P^{t}_{\sigma}(A\mid B)=\sum_{\tau\in S_{pk}}P^{t-1}_{\sigma}(\tau)P_{\tau}(A\mid B).

Thus, if
P_{\sigma}(\cdot\mid B)
is the uniform distribution for every
\sigma\in S_{pk}
, then
P^{t}_{\sigma}(\cdot\mid B)
is also the uniform distribution. When
t=1
, we have that

P_{\sigma}(\tau)=\frac{1}{|(H\times H)|_{\sigma}}\sum_{(h,g)\in(H\times H)_{\sigma}\cap(H\times H)_{\tau}}\frac{1}{|S_{pk}^{h,g}|}.

If
T(\tau)=2k
, then
(H\times H)_{\tau}
only contains the identity. Thus, for
\tau\in B
,

P_{\sigma}(\tau)=\frac{1}{|(H\times H)_{\sigma}|}\frac{1}{|S_{pk}|}.

This shows that
\tau\mapsto P_{\sigma}(\tau)
is constant on
B
and hence the conditional distribution
P_{\sigma}(\cdot\mid B)
is uniform.
∎

Lemma
4.2
is used to compare the mixing time of the lumped chain to the mixing time of the original process. The key idea is that the distance
\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
is well approximated by
\pi(B)-P^{t}_{\sigma}(B)
. This approximation is justified by the fact that
\pi(B)\approx 1
and by Lemma
4.1
which states that
P^{t}_{\sigma}(\cdot\mid B)=\pi(\cdot\mid B)
. The proof below formalizes this idea.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
p\geq 11
,
t\geq 1
and
\sigma\in S_{pk}
. If
T(\sigma)=a
, then

\|\overline{P}_{a}^{t}-\overline{\pi}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|P_{\sigma}^{t}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|\overline{P}_{a}^{t}-\overline{\pi}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\frac{2p^{4}}{(p-1)!}

Proof.

The first inequality holds for any lumping of a Markov chain. To prove the second inequality, let
B=\{\tau:T(\tau)=2k\}
as in Lemma
4.1
. Also let
\varepsilon=\frac{2p^{4}}{(p-1)!}
. By Proposition
2.3
we have

\pi(B^{c})=\frac{Z-f(2k;k)}{Z}\leq\varepsilon.

Thus,

\displaystyle\|P_{\sigma}^{t}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{A\subseteq S_{pk}}\{\pi(A)-P_{\sigma}^{t}(A)\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{A\subseteq S_{pk}}\{\pi(A\cap B)+\pi(A\cap B^{c})-P_{\sigma}^{t}(A\cap B)-P_{\sigma}^{t}(A\cap B^{c})\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{A\subseteq S_{pk}}\{\pi(A\cap B)-P_{\sigma}^{t}(A\cap B)\}+\varepsilon.

By Lemma
4.1
, for any
A\subseteq S_{pk}
,

\displaystyle\pi(A\cap B)-P_{\sigma}^{t}(A\cap B)

\displaystyle=\pi(B)\pi(A\mid B)-P_{\sigma}^{t}(B)P_{\sigma}(A\mid B)

\displaystyle=(\pi(B)-P^{t}_{\sigma}(B))\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}

\displaystyle\leq\pi(B)-P^{t}_{\sigma}(B)

\displaystyle=\overline{\pi}(2k)-\overline{P}^{t}_{a}(2k)

\displaystyle\leq\|\overline{\pi}-\overline{P}^{t}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Thus,

\|P_{\sigma}^{t}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\sup_{A\subseteq S_{pk}}\{\pi(A\cap B)-P_{\sigma}^{t}(A\cap B)\}+\varepsilon\leq\|\overline{\pi}-\overline{P}_{a}^{t}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\varepsilon.

∎

Let
Q
be the following transition kernel on
[k:2k]

Q(a,b)=\begin{cases}\binom{2k-a}{2k-b}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{2k-b}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)^{b-a}&\text{if }b\geq a,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(19)

The transition kernel
Q
is upper triangular and
2k
is the unique absorbing state. This implies that the stationary distribution of
Q
is
\delta_{2k}
–a point pass at
2k
. The next lemma states that transition matrix
Q
is a good approximation to the lumped transition matrix
\overline{P}
. This approximation is based on the fact that most double cosets are large and so the sum in (
8
) is dominated by the largest term.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in[k:2k]

\|\overline{P}^{t}_{a}-Q^{t}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{t}{(p-2)!}.

Proof.

By induction, it suffices to show that for all
a\in[k:2k]
,

\|\overline{P}_{a}-Q_{a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{1}{(p-2)!}.

By Theorem
1.6
we have that for
b\in[k:2k]
with
b\geq a

\displaystyle\overline{P}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=0}^{2k-b}\binom{2k-a}{y}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{y}\frac{p^{a+b-2k}}{(p(k-y))!}f(b-y;k-y)

\displaystyle\geq\binom{2k-a}{2k-b}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{2k-b}\frac{p^{a+b-2k}}{(p(b-k))!}f(2(b-k);b-k)

By Theorem
2.2
, we have that
\frac{1}{(p(b-k))!}f(2(b-k);b-k)\geq\frac{1}{p^{2(b-k)}}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\right)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\overline{P}(a,b)

\displaystyle\geq\binom{2k-a}{2k-b}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{2k-b}p^{a+b-2k}\frac{1}{p^{2(b-k)}}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\right)

\displaystyle=\binom{2k-a}{2k-b}\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{2k-b}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)^{b-a}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\right)

\displaystyle=Q(a,b)\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\right).

And for
b<a
,
\overline{P}(a,b)\geq 0=Q(a,b)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|\overline{P}_{a}-Q_{a}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{b=0}^{k}(Q(a,b)-\overline{P}(a,b))_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b=a}^{k}(Q(a,b)-\overline{P}(a,b))_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{b=a}^{k}\left(Q(a,b)-\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\right)Q(a,b)\right)_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\sum_{b=a}^{k}Q(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p-2)!}

∎

The next lemma states that
\overline{\pi}
is well-approximated by
\delta_{2k}
. That is the stationary distributions of
\overline{P}
and
Q
are close to each other.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
p\geq 11
, then

\|\overline{\pi}-\delta_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{2p^{4}}{(p-1)!}

Proof.

By Proposition
2.3
we have

\|\overline{\pi}-\delta_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=1-\overline{\pi}(2k)=\frac{Z-f(2k;k)}{Z}\leq\frac{2p^{4}}{(p-1)!}.

∎

The chain
Q
is much easier to analyze than
\overline{\pi}
. In particular, the time it takes
Q
to reach
2k
is exactly equal in distribution to the maximum of several independent geometric random variables.

Lemma 4.5
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in[k:2k]

\|Q_{a}^{t}-\delta_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=1-Q_{a}^{t}(2k)=1-\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}\right)^{2k-a}.

Proof.

We will prove this result by giving an equivalent description of the chain
Q
. Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Markov chain with transition kernel
Q
and
X_{0}=a
. Then, given
X_{t}=b
,
X_{t+1}
is equal in distribution to
a
plus a binomial variable with parameters
2k-b
and
1/p
. The process
X_{t}

1.

Set
X_{0}=a
and start with
2k-a
independent coins each with probability of heads equal to
1/p
.

2.

For each
t\geq t
, flip all the coins available coins and remove the ones that land on heads. Let
X_{t}
be equal to
X_{t-1}
plus the number of removed coins.

From the above description,
X_{t}=2k
is equivalent to all
2k-a
coins landing on heads in the first
t
steps. This gives the equality

\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=2k)=\mathbb{P}(\max\{Z_{j}:1\leq j\leq 2k-a\}\leq t),

(20)

where
Z_{j}
is the time it takes the
j
th coin to land on heads. Thus,
\{Z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2k-a}
are independent geometric random variables with
\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}>t)=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}
.

By independence, we have

\displaystyle\|Q^{t}_{0}-\delta_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle=1-Q_{0}^{t}(2k)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=2k)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\max\{Z_{j}:1\leq j\leq 2k-a\}\leq t)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}\leq t)^{2k-a}

\displaystyle=1-\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}\right)^{2k-a},

as claimed.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem

\sigma\in S_{pk}
and

(21)

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Note that

\left|\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}-\|Q_{a}^{t}-\delta_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right|\leq\left|\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}-\|\overline{P}_{a}^{t}-\overline{\pi}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right|+\left|\|\overline{P}_{a}^{t}-\overline{\pi}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}-\|Q_{a}^{t}-\delta_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right|,

Furthermore, by Lemma

\displaystyle\left|\|\overline{P}_{a}^{t}-\overline{\pi}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}-\|Q_{a}^{t}-\delta_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right|\leq

Thus,

5.1
Exact computation when
k=1

In the case when
k=1
, all Sylow
p
-double cosets either have size
p
or size
p^{2}
. Theorem
2.1
from
[
7
]
shows that there are
n_{1}=p-1
double cosets of size
p
and
n_{2}=\frac{(p-1)!-(p-1)}{p}
of size

. The stationary distribution of the Sylow–Burnside process in this case is

\pi(\sigma)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{(n_{1}+n_{2})p^{2}}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p^{2},\\
\frac{1}{(n_{1}+n_{2})p}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p.\end{cases}

In Section
B
of the appendix, it is shown that the entries of the transition kernel are given by

P(\sigma,\tau)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p!}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p^{2},\\
\frac{1}{pp!}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p\text{ and }H\sigma H\neq H\tau H,\\
\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{pp!}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p\text{ and }H\sigma H=H\tau H.\end{cases}

Section
B
also contains an eigenvalue decomposition of
P
. The transition kernel has four distinct eigenvalues. These eigenvalues are
1
,
1-\frac{1}{p}
,
1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}
and
0
with multiplicities
1,p-2,1
and
p!-p
respectively. From the eigenvalue decomposition, it is possible to exactly compute
\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
. Section
B
proves that for all
\sigma\in S_{p}

\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p.\\
\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p^{2}.\end{cases}

(22)

If
p
is even moderately large, then

and

. This gives

\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}\approx\frac{1}{n_{1}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t},\quad\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\approx 0.

Thus, in the

\displaystyle\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle\approx\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p,\\
0&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p^{2}.\end{cases}

Which agrees with the approximation in Theorem
1.3
when

6
Related Markov chains

The present argument gives a new approach to the analysis of Burnside processes. Previous efforts used Doeblin conditions
[
16
,
10
]
, explicit diagonalization
[
13
,
12
]
and coupling
[
2
,
21
,
22
]
.

The approach used here is most similar to that of
strong stationary times

[
1
,
6
]
. Lemmas
4.1
and Lemma
4.2
can be restated as follows. Let
(\sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be generated according to the Sylow–Burnside process and let
T
be the smallest
t
such that
\sigma_{t}
is in a large double coset. Then, the random permutation
\sigma_{T}
is independent of
T
and approximately distributed according to
\pi
. If
\sigma_{T}
was exactly distributed according to the stationary distribution
\pi
, then
T
would be a strong stationary time and we would have the bound

\|P_{\sigma}^{t}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}(T>t\mid\sigma_{0}=\sigma)

(23)

see
[
1
,
6
]
or
[
19
, Chapter 6]
. Since
\sigma_{T}
is only approximately distributed according to
\pi
, (
23
) holds up to a small error. Controlling this error and approximating
\mathbb{P}(T>t\mid\sigma_{0}=\sigma)
is essentially how Theorem
1.3
was proved.

The analysis of the Sylow–Burnside process is also reminiscent of a natural Markov chain for sampling
k
-dimesional subspaces of an
n
-dimension vector space over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This Markov chain was analyzed in
[
5
]
. The Markov chain lumps distance to the initial subspace and most of the subspaces are at the maximal distance
k
. The distance typically increases and so
k
steps are necessary and sufficient. It would be interesting to investigate the connection with
[
5
]
further. One direction would be to study the Burnside process for Sylow
p
-double cosets in the matrix group
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

7
The non-Abelian case

We end with some conjectures and questions about the behavior of the Sylow–Burnside process in the non-Abelian case when
n\geq p^{2}
. Even answering these questions for
n=p^{2}
is an interesting problem.

The main challenge appears to be generalizing Section
3.1
on the implementation of the Burnside process. Although some aspects of Section
3.1
go through, it seems difficult to describe the subgroup
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
. If there is a method for uniformly sampling from
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
, then the algorithms in Section
3.1
can be easily adapted to run the Sylow–Burnside process in general. Unfortunately, even computing
|H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma|
seems hard.

One important property about the Abelian case was a simple description of the distribution of the cycle type of
g
when
g
was uniformly sampled from
H\cap\sigma H\sigma^{-1}
(Lemma
3.1
). This leads to the following question.

Question 7.1
.

Let
H
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
S_{n}
. Does there exist a simple description of the cycle type of
g
when
g
is uniformly sampled from
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
? Relatedly, is there a formula for
|H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma|
or a simple algorithm for sampling from
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
?

Assuming that one can sample from
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
and hence run the Sylow–Burnside process, we have the following conjectures about the behavior of the Markov chain. These two conjectures correspond to the two main tools used in the Abelian case as described in Section
1.1
.

Conjecture 7.2
.

Let
P
be the transition kernel for the Sylow–Burnside process and let
T
be a function that records the conjugacy type of
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
as a subgroup of
S_{n}
, then
P
lumps under
T
.

Conjecture 7.3
.

Let
Z
be the total number of Sylow
p
-double cosets in
S_{n}
and let
Z_{0}
be the number of double cosets of maximal size
p^{2m}
, then if
p,n\to\infty

\frac{Z_{0}}{Z}\to 1

Remark 7.4
.

1.

In the Abelian case, the conjugacy type of
H\cap\sigma^{-1}H\sigma
is exactly determined by the size of
|H\sigma H|
. When
n\geq p^{2}
, we believe that Conjecture
7.2
is the correct generalization of Theorem
1.6
.

2.

Conjecture
7.3
is similar to Theorem 1.2 in
[
7
]
which can be equivalently stated as
\frac{Z_{0}p^{2m}}{n!}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. The difference between Conjecture
7.3
and Theorem 1.2 in
[
7
]
is the same as the difference between Proposition
2.3
and Theorem
2.2
.

In addition to proving Conjectures
7.2
and
7.3
, a generalization of the current paper would require a way of approximating the time it takes the Sylow–Burnside process to reach the large double cosets. In the Abelian case, this approximation was done with the monotone transition kernel
Q
. Assuming that Conjecture
7.2
is true, there may exist an analogous transition matrix that satisfies a similar monotonicity property. We state a more precise version of this an open question.

Question 7.5
.

Call a transition kernel
Q
on
S_{n}

monotone
if
Q(\sigma,\tau)>0
implies
|H\sigma H|\leq|H\tau H|
. Does there exist a monotone approximation to the transition kernel for the Sylow–Burnside process?

Answers to these questions and proofs of the conjectures seem to be the most promising path to generalizing beyond the Abelian case.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
2.3

To prove Proposition
2.3
we will prove a series of lemmas that bound
f(a;k)
. As stated in Section
2.2
, these proofs are based on the comments given in
[
7
, Remark 3.4]
.

Lemma A.1
.

For all
a,p,k

f(a;k)\leq\frac{1}{p^{a}}((a-k)p)!(2k-a)!\binom{k}{2k-a}^{2}(p(p-1))^{2k-a}.

Proof.

By equation (
11
) we have

f(a;k)=\frac{1}{p^{a}}\sum_{j=2k-a}^{k}(-1)^{j-(2k-a)}\Gamma_{j,a},

(24)

where

\Gamma_{j,a}=((k-j)p)!j!\binom{k}{j}^{2}(p(p-1))^{j}\binom{j}{2k-a}.

For
j\in[2k-a:k-1]
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{j+1,a}}{\Gamma_{j,a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{((k-j-1)p)!(j+1)!\binom{k}{j+1}^{2}(p(p-1))^{j+1}\binom{j+1}{2k-a}}{((k-j)p)!j!\binom{k}{j}^{2}(p(p-1))^{j}\binom{j}{2k-a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(j+1)\left(\frac{k-j}{j+1}\right)^{2}p(p-1)\frac{j+1}{j+1-2k-a}}{((k-j)p)((k-j)p-1)\cdots((k-j-1)p+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-j)^{2}p(p-1)}{(j+1-2k-a)((k-j)p)((k-j)p-1)\cdots((k-j-1)p+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-j)(p-1)}{(j+1-2k-a)((k-j)p-1)\cdots((k-j-1)p+1)}.

Since
j<k
, we have that
(k-j)(p-1)\leq(k-j)p-1
. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{j+1,a}}{\Gamma_{j,a}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(j+1-2k-a)}{((k-j)p-2)\cdots((k-j-1)p+1)}\leq\frac{1}{(j+1-2k-a)(p-2)!}\leq 1.

This means that
0\leq\Gamma_{j+1,a}\leq\Gamma_{j,a}
and so the sum in (
24
) is an alternating sum of non-negative, non-increasing terms. This implies that

f(a;k)\leq\frac{1}{p^{a}}\Gamma_{2k-a,a}=\frac{1}{p^{a}}((a-k)p)!(2k-a)!\binom{k}{2k-a}^{2}(p(p-1))^{2k-a}.

(25)

∎

The next lemma gives a bound on
f(a;k)
that is independent of

.

Lemma A.2
.

For
p\geq 11
and
k\leq a<2k
,

f(a;k)\leq\frac{1}{p^{2k-2}}((k-1)p)!k^{2}(p-1).

(26)

Proof.

Let
\Lambda_{a}=\frac{1}{p^{a}}((a-k)p)!(2k-a)!\binom{k}{2k-a}^{2}(p(p-1))^{2k-a}
and note that by Lemma
A.1
we have that
f(a;k)\leq\Lambda_{a}
. Furthermore,

\Lambda_{2k-1}=\frac{1}{p^{2k-1}}((k-1)p)!k^{2}p(p-1)=\frac{1}{p^{2k-2}}((k-1)p)!k^{2}(p-1)

is the right-hand-side of (
26
). Therefore, for the current lemma, it suffices to show that
(\Lambda_{a})_{a=k}^{2k-1}
is a decreasing sequence as this would imply

f(a;k)\leq\Lambda_{a}\leq\Lambda_{2k-1}.

Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda_{a+1}}{\Lambda_{a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{a}((a+1-k)p)!(2k-a-1)!\binom{k}{2k-a-1}^{2}(p(p-1))^{2k-a-1}}{p^{a+1}((a-k)p)!(2k-a)!\binom{k}{2k-a}^{2}(p(p-1))^{2k-a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{((a+1-k)p)!\left(\frac{2k-a}{a+1-k}\right)^{2}}{((a-k)p)!(2k-a)p^{2}(p-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{((a+1-k)p)((a+1-k)p-1)\cdots((a-k)p+1)(2k-a)}{(a+1-k)p^{2}(p-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{((a+1-k)p-1)\cdots((a-k)p+1)(2k-a)}{(a+1-k)p(p-1)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(p-1)!(2k-a)}{(a+1-k)p(p-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p-2)!(2k-a)}{(a+1-k)p}

We know that
a+1-k\leq k<p
and
2k-a\geq 1
. Thus,
\frac{\Lambda_{a+1}}{\Lambda_{a}}\geq\frac{(p-2)!}{p^{2}}
. If
p\geq 11
, then
(p-2)!\geq p^{2}
and so
\Lambda_{a+1}\geq\Lambda_{a}
as required.
∎

The final lemma compares
f(a;k)
to
f(2k;k)
.

Lemma A.3
.

For
p\geq 11
and
k\leq a<2k
,

\frac{f(a;k)}{f(2k;k)}\leq\frac{2p^{3}}{(p-1)!}

(27)

Proof.

By Lemma
A.2
we know that

f(a;k)\leq\frac{1}{p^{2k-2}}((k-1)p)!k^{2}(p-1)

Furthermore, from Theorem
2.2
we have

f(2k;k)\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-2)!}\right)\frac{(pk)!}{p^{2k}}\geq\frac{(pk)!}{2p^{2k}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{f(a;k)}{f(2k;k)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2((k-1)p)!k^{2}(p-1)p^{2k}}{p^{2k-2}(pk)!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2((k-1)p)!pk^{2}(p-1)}{(pk)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{2pk^{2}(p-1)}{(pk)(pk-1)\cdots(p(k-1)-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2p^{4}}{(pk)(pk-1)\cdots(p(k-1)-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2p^{4}}{p!}

\displaystyle=\frac{2p^{3}}{(p-1)!}.

∎

Finally, we can prove Proposition
2.3
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

By Lemma
A.3
if
Z=\sum_{a=k}^{2k}f(a;k)
, then

\displaystyle f(2k;k)

\displaystyle=Z-\sum_{a=k}^{2k-1}f(a;k)

\displaystyle\geq Z-(k-1)\frac{2p^{3}}{(p-1)!}f(2k;k)

\displaystyle\geq Z-\frac{2p^{4}}{(p-1)!}Z,

And so

1-\frac{2p^{4}}{(p-1)!}\leq\frac{f(2k;k)}{Z}\leq 1,

as required.
∎

Appendix B
Derivation of total variation distance when
k=1

In the section we prove (
22
) by proving a series of lemmas. We first prove the formula for
P
given in Section
5
, then we compute an eigenvalue decomposition for
P
. This eigenvalue decomposition is used to give an exact formula for
P^{t}(\sigma,\tau)
which is then used to compute the total variation distance as in (
22
).

Lemma B.1
.

Suppose that
k=1
, then the transition kernel for the Sylow–Burnside process is given by

P(\sigma,\tau)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p!}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p^{2},\\
\frac{1}{pp!}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p\text{ and }H\sigma H\neq H\tau H,\\
\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{pp!}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p\text{ and }H\sigma H=H\tau H.\end{cases}

Proof.

The matrix
P
is given by

P(\sigma,\tau)=\frac{1}{(H\times H)_{\sigma}}\sum_{h,g\in(H\times H)_{\sigma}\cap(H\times H)_{\tau}}\frac{1}{|S_{p}^{h,g}|}

If
|H\sigma H|=p^{2}
, then
(H\times H)_{\sigma}
only contains the identity and thus

P(\sigma,\tau)=\frac{1}{1}\times\frac{1}{|S_{p}^{\mathrm{Id},\mathrm{Id}}|}=\frac{1}{p!}.

If
|H\sigma H|=p
and
H\sigma H\neq H\tau H
, then
(H\times H)_{\sigma}\cap(H\times H)_{\tau}
again only contains the identity and so

P(\sigma,\tau)=\frac{1}{|(H\times H)_{\sigma}}\times\frac{1}{|S_{p}^{\mathrm{Id},\mathrm{Id}}|}=\frac{1}{pp!}.

Finally, if
H\times H=H\tau H
and
|H\sigma H|=p
, then
|(H\times H)_{\sigma}\cap(H\times H)_{\tau}|=p
and

\displaystyle P(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|(H\times H)_{\sigma}}\left(\frac{1}{|S_{p}^{\mathrm{Id},\mathrm{Id}}|}+\sum_{(h,g)\in(H\times H)_{\sigma}\setminus\{(\mathrm{Id},\mathrm{Id})\}}\frac{1}{|S_{p}^{h,g}|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{1}{p!}+(p-1)\frac{1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{pp!}.

∎

The following notation is useful for describing the eigenvectors of
P
. Let
A\subseteq S_{p}
be the set of permutations in double cosets of size
p
and let
B=S_{p}\setminus A
. We will fix
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n_{1}}
such that

A=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{n_{1}}H\rho_{j}H.

Finally, for a subset
C\subseteq S_{p}
, let
\mathbf{1}_{C}
be the vector that is
1
on
C
and
0
otherwise.

\displaystyle(P\psi)(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in A}\frac{1}{n_{1}}P(\sigma,\tau)-\sum_{\tau\in B}\frac{1}{n_{2}}P(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n_{1}}{p!}+\frac{p-1}{p}-\frac{n_{1}p}{p!}\right)\frac{1}{n_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p-1}{p!}+\frac{p-1}{p}-\frac{(p-1)p}{p!}\right)\psi(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)\psi(\sigma).

n_{1}=p-1

\psi(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{pn_{1}}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{p!}-\frac{n_{2}p^{2}}{n_{2}}\frac{1}{p!}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n_{2}}{(p-1)!}-\frac{n_{2}p}{(p-1)!}\right)\frac{1}{n_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{(p-1)!-(p-1)}{p!}+\frac{(p-1)!-(p-1)}{(p-1)!}\right)\psi(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-(p-1)!+(p-1)+p!-p(p-1)}{p!}\right)\psi(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)\psi(\sigma).

. There are

Lemma B.3
.

Let
P
be the transition kernel for the Sylow–Burnside process when
k=1
and let
\tilde{\psi}=\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}-\frac{1}{p^{2}n_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{B}
. If
|H\sigma H|=p
, then

\displaystyle P^{t}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}\left(\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}-\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right)+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}\tilde{\psi}+\pi,

(28)

and if
|H\sigma H|=p^{2}
, then

P^{t}_{\sigma}=-\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}\tilde{\psi}+\pi.

(29)

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma\}}
and note that
P^{t}_{\sigma}=\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}P^{t}
. Our goal is thus to write
\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}
in terms of the left eigenvectors of
P
and then apply Lemma
B.2
. Suppose that
|H\sigma H|=p
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}\right)+\frac{1}{p}\left(\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right)+\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}.

And

\displaystyle\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}+\frac{1}{p(n_{1}+n_{2})}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right)+\left(-\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\frac{1}{p^{2}n_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{B}+\frac{1}{p^{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\mathbf{1}_{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}-\frac{1}{p^{2}n_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{B}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}+\frac{1}{p^{2}n_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{B}\right).

Recall that
\tilde{\psi}=\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}-\frac{1}{p^{2}n_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{B}
and
\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}+\frac{1}{p^{2}n_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{B}=\pi
. We thus have

\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}=\left(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}\right)+\frac{1}{p}\left(\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right)+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\tilde{\psi}+\pi.

The vector
\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}
is a left eigenvector of
P
with eigenvalue 0 and
\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}
is a left eigenvector of
P
with eigenvalue
1-\frac{1}{p}
. Finally,
\tilde{\psi}
and
\pi
are left eigenvectors with eigenvalues
1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}
and 1 respectively. It follows that

\displaystyle P^{t}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}P^{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}\right)+\frac{1}{p}\left(\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right)+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\tilde{\psi}+\pi\right)P^{t}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}\left(\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}_{H\sigma H}-\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right)+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}\tilde{\psi}+\pi.

Since
\tilde{\psi}=\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}-\frac{1}{p^{2}n_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{B}
. We have shown (
28
). Now suppose that
|H\sigma H|=p^{2}
, then a similar calculation gives

\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}=\left(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{n_{2}p}\mathbf{1}_{B}\right)-\frac{n_{1}}{(n_{1}+n_{2})}\tilde{\psi}+\pi.

The vector
\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{n_{2}p}
is a left eigenvector of

with eigenvalue
0

\displaystyle P^{t}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}P^{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{n_{2}p}\mathbf{1}_{B}\right)-\frac{n_{1}}{(n_{1}+n_{2})}\tilde{\psi}+\pi\right)P^{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}\tilde{\psi}+\pi.

Since
-\tilde{\psi}=\frac{1}{p^{2}n_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{B}-\frac{1}{pn_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}
, we have also shown (

∎

Lemma B.4
.

Let
P
be the transition kernel for the Sylow–Burnside process when
k=1
. Let
\pi
be the stationary distribution for

. Then for all

\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p.\\
\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}&\text{if }|H\sigma H|=p^{2}.\end{cases}

Suppose
|H\sigma H|=p
. Then, Lemma
B.3
shows that
P^{t}_{\sigma}(\tau)>\pi(\tau)
if and only if
\tau\in H\sigma H
we have

\displaystyle P^{t}_{\sigma}(\tau)-\pi(\tau)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\right)\frac{1}{p}+\frac{n_{2}}{pn_{1}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}.

Since
|H\sigma H|=p
it follows that

\displaystyle\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in H\sigma H}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\right)\frac{1}{p}+\frac{n_{2}}{pn_{1}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\right)+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}

If
|H\sigma H|=p^{2}
, then Lemma

if and only if
\tau\in B
. Furthermore, for

\displaystyle P^{t}_{\sigma}(\tau)-\pi(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}}{p^{2}n_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}.

Since

\|P^{t}_{\sigma}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\sum_{\tau\in B}\frac{n_{1}}{p^{2}n_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}=\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{p-1}{p(p-2)!}\right)^{t}.

∎
