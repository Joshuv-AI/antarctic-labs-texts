INTRODUCTION

Education in regional anesthesia has rapidly evolved, especially with technological advances over the last 20 years. Technology has not only influenced how regional anesthesia is taught, but also how information is shared, evaluated, and implemented in clinical practice. However, the ultimate goal is widespread implementation of basic regional anesthesia and analgesia with global patient access and consistent, reliable, and safe utilization. Today, technology drives curriculum design, teaching and learning methods, competency assessment, and clinical practice change. This article reviews recent technological developments in regional anesthesia education and considers their potential implications for training current and future generations of anesthesiologists to provide regional anesthesia worldwide.

THE BASICS: PLAN A BLOCKS

This article builds on the ‘Plan A blocks’ concept [1■■,2], which was proposed in 2019 with the goal of promoting ‘few blocks for the many’ within the anesthesiology community [2]. Although there has been much discussion about the suggested blocks themselves, this was only one part of a three-step pathway to ensure the future of regional anesthesia quality and safety: (a) selecting and teaching a small number of high-value regional blocks; (b) disseminating standardized curricula and assessment tools to verify competence; and (c) incorporating Plan A blocks into clinical pathways to increase patient access to regional anesthesia and improve patient care [2]. The proposed list of Plan A blocks is amenable to both anesthesiology trainees and consultants who do not yet perform regional blocks routinely and may be adapted based on the training program or anesthesiology practice [1■■]. Achieving competence in a set of basic blocks worldwide is an ambitious goal but would provide a common curricular focus for residency training and continuing medical education for all anesthesiologists. The Royal College of Anaesthetists in the UK has already incorporated the Plan A blocks framework into its trainee curriculum [1■■]. With Plan A block dissemination and implementation in mind, we present how emerging technology can accelerate learning and practice change.

TECHNOLOGY TO MODERNIZE TEACHING METHODS

Emerging technologies are reshaping how regional anesthesia is taught, with the most transformative applications involving artificial intelligence, immersive simulation, and integrated visual displays. The development of artificial intelligence software embedded within ultrasound equipment [3■] has been shown to assist the general anesthesiologist (i.e. nonexpert in regional anesthesia) with the identification of sonoanatomy relevant to Plan A blocks [4,5]. While artificial intelligence may not necessarily aid the expert, there are potential benefits for general anesthesiologists who infrequently perform regional blocks and their patients in terms of procedural precision and safety [6■]. According to Jo et al. [6■], artificial intelligence–assisted ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia (UGRA) uses trained deep learning algorithms that can create color-coded overlays in real time on ultrasound images, enabling the proper recognition of relevant sonoanatomy during the procedure. As this technology develops, it may even bridge the performance gap between the novice and the expert regional anesthesiologist.

In another domain of artificial intelligence, large language models (LLMs) such as Claude (Anthropic, San Francisco, California, USA), ChatGPT (OpenAI, San Francisco, California, USA), Copilot (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA), and Gemini (Alphabet Inc., Mountain View, California, USA) can be helpful in regional anesthesia education at many levels. For physicians who are learning regional anesthesia, LLMs can quickly generate summaries on peripheral nerve block landmarks and sonoanatomy, nerve physiology, local anesthetic pharmacology, and existing evidence, including recently published articles and clinical trials. For educators, LLMs can draft curricular outlines, generate assessment tools, make slide presentations for didactic lectures, and more. LLMs can create educational materials for patients and caregivers, nurses, surgeons, pharmacists, physical therapists, and any other healthcare group. While LLMs offer many potential benefits, they cannot and should not replace physician decision-making and are best used as a supplemental resource in the clinical setting [7■]. Multiple limitations exist with current LLMs, including the lack of judgment, fabricated information, and data ‘hallucinations’ that can potentially lead to inappropriate conclusions and patient harm [8]. OpenEvidence (Miami, Florida, USA) is an LLM designed for healthcare that synthesizes answers from the scientific literature [9]. This platform is gaining in popularity within the medical community as a decision support tool [9,10], but still has some limitations in terms of interface, depth on complex or rare topics, and visual aids [11].

Anesthesiology as a specialty has been a pioneering leader in simulation-based education, and high-fidelity simulation utilizing computer-enhanced manikins has been fully integrated into anesthesiology residency training in the USA and many other parts of the world [12,13]. However, there are challenges in continuing manikin-based simulation beyond residency, such as access to physical simulation centers, cost, and competition for time with clinical responsibilities [14]. Advances in simulation technology have moved beyond the physical environment of the simulation center into head-mounted devices, which incorporate the use of either fully immersive virtual reality or augmented reality, combining real-world visual input with computer-generated overlay. These systems have demonstrated applicability in UGRA training [15■■]. Simulation via head-mounted display maintains the advantages of teaching realistic patient care scenarios and even procedures in a controlled and safe learning environment [16]; learners still acquire knowledge and develop teamwork and communication skills but do not need to travel to a dedicated facility. This may be particularly advantageous for the consultant anesthesiologist in practice who may wish to use simulation to maintain regional anesthesia skills or learn new techniques. In a randomized trial, trainees receiving instruction in UGRA via an immersive three-dimensional virtual reality platform achieved procedural skill acquisition comparable to those receiving instruction from a remote proctor with access to two-dimensional ultrasound and external camera views [17]. From the perspective of personalization, simulation via head-mounted display allows the learner to customize the experience by selecting scenarios and educational tools (e.g. anatomy models) to facilitate their learning [18].

TECHNOLOGY TO DISSEMINATE UPDATED INFORMATION

Since medicine is ever-changing, technology can facilitate the widespread dissemination of new information. Virtual collaboration tools include, but are not limited to, videoconferencing platforms and social media.

Videoconferencing platforms were already being used for remote meetings and digital learning before the COVID-19 pandemic [19], but worldwide utilization has increased since then, even within regional anesthesia [20]. Academic departments have largely adapted their grand rounds and other didactic lectures to a fully virtual or hybrid (i.e. in-person and virtual) format across medical specialties [21–26]. Medical societies now offer regular webinar content, including the American Society of Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine (https://asra.com/events-education/webcasts) and the European Society of Regional Anaesthesia and Pain Therapy (https://esraeurope.org/esra-webinar/). Teaching in a videoconferencing platform differs from live in-person ‘classroom’ instruction, and educators may require additional training to effectively deliver information and maintain learner engagement.

Social media platforms can be used in the educational context to share and curate information as well as facilitate networking and scholarly discussion [27]. Education in regional anesthesia has benefited from the active participation of regional anesthesiologists around the world on various platforms such as X (Bastrop, Texas, USA), Instagram and Facebook (Meta, Menlo Park, California, USA), YouTube (Alphabet, Mountain View, California, USA), and LinkedIn (Sunnyvale, California, USA) [28–30]. Each platform can be characterized by its own unique culture and audience, and the posting formats and engagement features differ between platforms. Therefore, there is no single ideal social media application for regional anesthesia education, and educators should choose a particular platform based on the intended audience, content format, and anticipated outcomes [29,31,32]. For example, Instagram is a social media platform featuring images and videos; it is popular in the age range encompassing anesthesiology trainees and early career practicing anesthesiologists and has been used for medical education [33]. In the USA, the top two social media platforms in terms of utilization are YouTube and Facebook [31]. YouTube is the most popular video hosting site on the planet, and consumers can subscribe to individual YouTube channels to get notifications when new video content is posted. When learners seek ‘just in time training’, they are much more likely to search YouTube than a traditional text book; yet, posted content for medical education has been deemed ‘moderate quality and reliability’ and may differ from other published sources of information [34■]. X (formerly Twitter) is a microblogging platform in which posts are still primarily text-based, although images and videos can also be shared [31]. Although X is not one of the most popular platforms in the general population, it continues to be used in academics, particularly during scientific meetings, and a recent study has shown X engagement to be the primary driver of higher alternative bibliometrics among anesthesiology journals [35■].

With the exception of society-hosted webinars, which may have preapproval of content for continuing education credit, other forms of online dissemination and social media do not typically undergo a peer review process or editorial oversight [35■,36]. There is an inherent risk of spreading and consuming misinformation, whether this is intentional or not, and this can potentially result in patient harm. For this reason, researchers, educators, and clinical experts in regional anesthesia and acute pain medicine should be active on social media and other digital platforms to contribute trustworthy content [27]. It is important to note that the peer review process for scientific journals is also imperfect and subject to bias, errors, and even fraud [37]. An argument can be made that freely accessible online materials may in fact undergo more ‘peer review’ given that they can be read or watched by anyone, and any consumer (including the general public) can potentially post a response or question.

TECHNOLOGY TO ASSESS COMPETENCE

While there are many innovative medical education tools that teachers can use to teach, there have been relatively fewer technologies developed to determine how well a learner has learned [38]. Assessment of competence is an essential requirement in graduate medical education and is necessary in practice to track the progression of regional anesthesia skills from novice to expert. There are validated assessment tools available in regional anesthesia, some more detailed and others more global [39–44]. However, the application of these tools is still dependent on humans, specifically expert regional anesthesiologists, who must observe each trainee’s procedural performance in real time or review video recordings [40,42]. Conducting these reviews is both time- and labor-intensive and therefore ripe for technological innovation.

With an established checklist, such as the direct observation of procedure skills [39], a computer can be ‘trained’ to score video recordings. While not yet tested in regional anesthesia skills assessment, this type of machine learning technology has been applied to perform gait analysis using video recordings [45,46]. In veterinary medicine, analysis of videos using artificial intelligence performed similarly to human veterinarians in assessing pain in cattle [47].

Another technology that may be useful for assessment in regional anesthesia is eye-tracking, given the predominant use of ultrasound visualization during procedural performance [48,49]. In UGRA, the target nerve or fascial plane represents an ‘area of interest’ for eye-tracking software, and this technology has the capability to measure the eye movements of the user as well as the number and proportion of gaze fixations on a target [48]. Eye-tracking has been shown to differentiate novices in regional anesthesia from experts who were shown the same set of ultrasound images representing common block techniques [48]. Both eye-tracking and artificial intelligence–enabled video review (perhaps even used together) have the potential to greatly improve the efficiency and reliability of assessment related to both technical and nontechnical skills [50].

Although the focus of this article is expanding education in basic regional anesthesia through technology to increase patient access, advanced training in regional anesthesia and acute pain medicine through a dedicated fellowship after anesthesiology residency deserves a brief discussion. Within the USA, structured regional anesthesia fellowships began in the 1980s [51] but rapidly expanded after the incorporation of ultrasound into regional anesthesia practice and eventually established a national curriculum with accreditation standards in 2016 [52]. Since fellowship graduates are likely to take on leadership roles in their clinical practices, especially in regional anesthesia education, fellowship training should also incorporate the technologies previously mentioned for learning to teach, sharing of new information, and assessing competence.

TECHNOLOGY TO FACILITATE PRACTICE CHANGE AND EXPANDED PATIENT ACCESS

Where emerging technology in regional anesthesia education may have the most profound impact is in expanding the cohort of general anesthesiologists who can offer basic regional blocks to their patients in clinical practice. Ultrasound guidance only began its incorporation into regional block procedures in the past 20 years; yet, physician careers tend to last 30 or more years, which means that a significant number of practicing anesthesiologists globally were not trained to use ultrasound for nerve blockade as residents.

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and rural areas of higher-income countries, opportunities for even basic UGRA training may be limited by cost, geography, and a shortage of local expert faculty. Technologies have emerged in recent years that facilitate remote training, which has been described as ‘telesimulation’ in regional anesthesia [17,53,54].

While telesimulation in regional anesthesia may be conducted using videoconferencing platforms with an instructor or facilitator located remotely and part-task trainers on site for learner practice locally, clinical practice may not change. Taking new regional anesthesia skills learned in the classroom and applying them in clinical practice requires a longer-term commitment in the form of ‘telementoring’ [55]. Telementoring may involve real-time, remote participation by an expert who can observe the performance of a procedure by a learner via videoconference and provide timely and relevant feedback [55]. With emerging technology, ultrasound imaging may be livestreamed, and the remote ‘telementor’ may be able to annotate objects on the screen for the learner to see almost immediately. Alternatively, a remote telementor may review procedural videos asynchronously and provide feedback to the learner in the form of a debriefing session. A 2025 systematic review of telementoring for trainees in low-resource settings describes a clear evolution from early examples of simple live video with two-way audio to real-time annotation of shared video feeds [55]. In the future, telementoring systems may be able to integrate haptic feedback to the remote expert to further facilitate procedural guidance with near-physical presence over mobile networks. While much of this work to date has focused on surgery [55], the potential benefits are easily extrapolated to regional anesthesia, a heavily procedure-based subspecialty that routinely employs image guidance.

A successful example of telesimulation followed by telementoring to change clinical practice has been described in Guatemala, where the anesthesiologists had no previous experience with UGRA [56■]. After 4 weeks of virtual teaching, one in-person conference, and 1 month of directly supervised clinical practice followed by ongoing remote mentorship, participating anesthesiologists demonstrated measurable gains in both knowledge and procedural skills, with an overall block success rate over 96% and no reported patient complications in the months following implementation [56■]. Most importantly, the program was designed to transition educational leadership to local faculty, addressing the sustainability problem that has historically limited the durability of short-course educational interventions in LMICs. These technologies may also be applied in lower-resourced regions (e.g. rural, inner city) of high-income countries to expand UGRA training and patient access to regional anesthesia and analgesia.

There are potential medicolegal considerations. Specifically, credentialing for medical direction or supervision, laws, and regulations that relate to virtual care across state lines or international borders, where applicable, should be thoroughly reviewed for compliance.

LIMITATIONS OF TECHNOLOGY IN REGIONAL ANESTHESIA EDUCATION

While there are exciting new advances and innovations in technology for regional anesthesia education (Fig. 1), there are many limitations. First, and perhaps most importantly, access to new technologies is not universal and may be limited by cost, expertise, or logistics (e.g. network bandwidth) depending on the setting. Next, digital tools have the potential to democratize education but can also be prone to error. Artificial intelligence chatbots have been known to hallucinate or fabricate information, including references [8,57]. LLMs are limited to available data; they cannot incorporate scientific articles that are inaccessible behind paywalls, and they cannot discriminate between good and bad studies. In a similar way, social media platforms can offer valuable educational content in many different formats; however, the lack of peer review or other editorial quality control can lead to the dissemination of erroneous information. Finally, medical educators have generally learned to teach in the context of clinical care, facilitate case-based discussions, and deliver didactic in-person lectures. The transition to virtual teaching and innovative applications of telesimulation and telementoring requires dedicated training to develop these novel skills.

CONCLUSION

In summary, educators in regional anesthesia have an abundance of technological tools available to advance the mission of training anesthesiology residents and practicing anesthesiologists in a basic set of high-value regional techniques (Plan A blocks). The appropriate and ethical application of digital platforms in regional anesthesia may be guided by available resources and careful review and curation by educators. Achieving the ambitious goals of widespread dissemination of Plan A blocks, with reliable competency assessment and successful implementation in clinical practice worldwide to ensure patient access, will require technological integration as well as further innovation.
