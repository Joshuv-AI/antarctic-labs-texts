Synchronous Cardiac and Central Nervous System Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma
Authors: Jack Bradbury, Cassandra Spanos, Michael Shipton, Penny McKelvie, Alex Lin-I Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Cardiac lymphoma, Ventricular mass, Heart failure, Echocardiography, Case report

Introduction

Primary cardiac lymphoma is a rare diagnosis.1 Patients most commonly present with heart failure symptoms and are at times simultaneously infected with HIV/AIDS.2 Histopathological findings obtained from cardiac biopsy are the reference standard for diagnosis in these patients; however, the benefits of the biopsy approach must be weighed against a patient's individual values as well as the overall risks and yield of the procedure. Given the positive response to treatment, cardiac lymphoma should be considered when patients present with new heart failure symptoms and a right-sided mass on imaging. This report details a presentation of synchronous cardiac diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with central nervous system (CNS) involvement, the challenges involved with appropriate means of diagnosis, and the rapid response that can be achieved when treatment is initiated in a timely manner.

Case Presentation

A 59-year-old woman presented to hospital with a progressive 2-month history of lethargy, subjective fevers, unintentional weight loss, and exertional dyspnea, rendering them bed bound over the week prior to presentation. This was complicated by a 1-day history of altered mental status and hypoxia. The patient's medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, chronic hepatitis B with fatty liver, and hypothyroidism. Physical examination revealed dual heart sounds with no murmurs. There were left basal crepitations with no other features of heart failure, as well as altered mental status with a Glasgow coma score of 11 (M6, V2, E3) and no other focal neurological signs. Electrocardiogram showed a prolonged PR interval of 240 ms with no suggestion of ischemia or tamponade. Chest x-ray showed an enlarged cardiac silhouette with evidence of pulmonary congestion but no focal consolidation or pleural effusion. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, ordered to investigate for lower respiratory tract infection, demonstrated a 55 mm right atrial (RA) mass with no evidence of mediastinal, hilar, or axillary lymphadenopathy and no parenchymal changes. The patient was started on intravenous ceftriaxone, benzylpenicillin, and acyclovir for presumed meningoencephalitis, and mental status subsequently improved. A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed a large (5 × 4 × 8 cm), irregular, and heterogeneously echodense mass involving the right atrium and ventricle, resulting in moderate right ventricular (RV) dysfunction and RV free wall akinesis. There was also a pericardial effusion without evidence of tamponade, measuring 2.0 cm lateral to the right ventricle and 1.7 cm inferior to the left ventricle (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). Lumbar puncture was performed, and aside from a slightly elevated glucose (4.4 mmol/L, ref. 2.0-3.9), was otherwise normal. Human immunodeficiency virus serology was negative. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain was performed to investigate for suspected encephalitis, which revealed an 18-mm left thalamic lesion with mass effect and a 3-mm left frontal lobe nodule, suggesting lymphoma of the CNS. A whole-body positron emission tomography (PET) scan to assess for extent and metabolic activity of these lesions showed intense uptake of the RA and RV mass (Figure 1). Symmetrical, intense uptake of bilateral mediastinal and right hilar lymph nodes was also seen; however, this was attributed to previous granulomatous disease given the presence of internal calcification and subcentimeter size. There were no other sites of occult primary lymphoma identified. This was supported following endobronchial ultrasound with washings and lymph node fine needle aspirate, which revealed no malignant cells.

Pretreatment two-dimensional TTE, apical 4-chamber (A) and parasternal short-axis (B) systolic views, demonstrates a large irregular, heterogenous, echodense RA (yellow arrow) and RV (red arrows) mass measuring 5.0 × 4.0 × 8.0 cm with associated pericardial effusion. Pretreatment cardiac PET-fluorodeoxyglucose scan (C) demonstrates increased radiotracer uptake (red arrow) consistent with the large right-sided cardiac mass seen on TTE.

Balancing likely diagnostic yield and the potential risks, particularly the patient's informed decision to decline blood products in case of bleeding for cultural and religious reasons, an RA endomyocardial biopsy through a transjugular approach was undertaken. This was performed under fluoroscopy and TTE guidance with no complications. Histopathology showed a diffuse myocardial infiltration of large atypical lymphoid cells (CD20+, CD10–, BCL6+, BCL2+, MUM1+, cMYC–, EBER ISH–, cyclin-D1–) consistent with a diagnosis of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), nongerminal center-type, nondouble expressor (Figure 2).

Hematoxylin and eosin stain (A) demonstrates diffuse infiltrate of large atypical lymphoid cells (×600 magnification). CD20 immunohistochemistry stain (B) demonstrates diffuse membranous activity within the lymphoma (×400 magnification).

The patient was subsequently treated with dose-attenuated chemotherapy (anthracycline-containing hyperCVAD (cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, dexamethasone, methotrexate, cytarabine, and rituximab), selected to also treat for CNS involvement empirically alongside darbepoetin alfa and eltrombopag support to mitigate risk of cytopenia. There was early clinical improvement with no evidence of adverse effects. Repeat imaging with TTE, CT, and PET 2 months after initiating treatment showed good response to chemotherapy with complete resolution of the cardiac and CNS masses as well as resolution of the altered mental status (Figures 3 and 4, Videos 3 and 4).

Discussion

This case adds to the body of knowledge of a very rare presentation of synchronous cardiac and CNS DLBCL, including the significant size these tumors can increase to and the extent of anatomical involvement. Hematologic etiologies should be considered in the event of a new right-sided cardiac mass, as these often have implications for diagnostic or therapeutic procedures. This is especially important in patients who decline transfusions for reasons including cultural or religious beliefs, where periprocedural support with blood products is not feasible, affecting the benefit-risk ratio. In this case, a cardiac biopsy was favored over pericardial aspirate as the latter has modest diagnostic yield and would likely subject the patient to additional procedures. The role of TTE with or without ultrasound-enhancing agents (UEAs) in the diagnostic approach to cases such as this is not extensively documented but may be helpful in assessing mass vascularity, which favors the diagnosis of malignancy. Further, the remarkable response of this patient to chemotherapy highlights the importance of accurate and prompt diagnosis.

Cardiac lymphoma is rare, comprising 1% of all primary cardiac tumors and less than 1% of all extranodal lymphomas.1,3 Approximately 80% of primary cardiac lymphomas are of the DLBCL subtype.4 Analysis of the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results database in the United States showed that there have been 123 documented cases of primary cardiac DLBCL between 1975 and 2016.1 While primary cardiac lymphoma is defined as that which occurs exclusively within the heart, secondary lymphoma is more common where CNS involvement is seen either at time of diagnosis or at relapse; this affects up to 25% of patients,5 underscoring the importance of detecting extracardiac involvement when cardiac lymphoma is diagnosed. A subset of secondary lymphoma, synchronous lymphoma, describes non-Hodgkin lymphoma occurring simultaneously within the CNS and systemically at the time of initial diagnosis; it is very rare and not well described in the literature.6 Clinical presentations among patients with either primary or secondary cardiac DLBCL include heart failure, chest pain, constitutional symptoms, heart block, or noncardiac symptoms including abdominal pain, dizziness and hemoptysis, superior vena cava syndrome, and stroke, in descending order of frequency. Patients presenting with heart failure have an overall worse prognosis.2 Men are twice as likely to develop primary cardiac DLBCL, and up to 15% of patients with cardiac DLBCL have HIV/AIDS.2 Transthoracic echocardiography and cardiovascular MRI are useful for diagnosis, with up to 77% of primary cardiac lymphomas presenting as RA masses.7 The administration of UEAs during echocardiography may be utilized to help differentiate malignant and stromal tumors as well as thrombi, which are cardiac masses enhanced by UEAs indicate hypervascularity, and would suggest a malignant process.8,9 Electrocardiogram-gated postcontrast cardiac CT has several benefits in the investigation of suspected cardiac lymphoma, allowing for detailed characterization of the mass itself, assessment of mass calcification and valve involvement, which is rare in primary cardiac lymphomas, and assessment for localized extracardiac spread.10 Cardiovascular MRI, in addition to functional and structural information, also allows greater tissue characterization of cardiac masses, which is helpful in differentiating benign from malignant tumors.11 Current societal recommendations state that 18F fluorodeoxyglucose PET/CT scanning is required for accurate staging of DLBCL and also in the assessment of adequate response to treatment.12

Endomyocardial biopsy is required for diagnosis of primary cardiac lymphoma, in line with the 2007 joint statement by the American Heart Association, American College of Cardiology, and European Society of Cardiology. Endomyocardial biopsy is recommended when diagnosis cannot be made by noninvasive modalities, if the biopsy result would change the course of treatment, and if there is a reasonably high chance of biopsy success (Class IIa, level C evidence).13 Endomyocardial biopsy is typically performed under fluoroscopy with transesophageal echocardiogram assistance; however, biopsy under fluoroscopy with concurrent TTE guidance has been shown to be useful in obtaining a definitive diagnosis of cardiac lymphoma and at the same time reducing periprocedural risks of more invasive procedures.14 This contrasts with cytology performed on pericardial aspirate, which has been shown to have a diagnostic yield of only 67% in cases of primary cardiac lymphoma.15 While there is no established management algorithm given the rarity of synchronous primary cardiac lymphoma, early diagnosis and treatment is paramount to improve prognosis, and vigilance is required on commencement of chemotherapy for acute cardiac events.7

Conclusion

Synchronous cardiac lymphoma is rare and should be suspected when there is a new diagnosis of a right-sided cardiac mass. Multimodal imaging should be incorporated to assist in diagnosis; however, histopathological assessment via endomyocardial biopsy remains the reference for diagnosis. Emerging evidence supports the use of TTE imaging alongside fluoroscopic guidance in obtaining a tissue diagnosis. While this is a rare disease, treatment is effective with timely investigation and therapy.
