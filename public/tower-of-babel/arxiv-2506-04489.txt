Spectrally accurate and efficient convolution with the 3D free-space Laplace Green’s function via the super-potential
Lukas Exl, Sebastian Schaffer

Abstract




Abstract.

We present a high-accuracy spectral method for solving the unbounded three-dimensional Poisson equation with smooth, compactly supported sources. The approach is based on a super-potential formulation, where the solution is obtained by applying the Laplacian to a convolution with the biharmonic Green’s function. A separable Gaussian-sum (GS) approximation enables efficient FFT-based computation with quasi-linear complexity. Owing to the improved regularity of the biharmonic kernel, the GS cutoff error is of order four, eliminating the need for correction terms or Taylor expansions required in standard GS or Ewald-type methods. Numerical benchmarks demonstrate that the method achieves machine-precision accuracy and outperforms existing GS-based schemes in both error and runtime, making it a robust and efficient tool for free-space Poisson problems on uniform grids.

Keywords.
super-potential, fast convolution, fast Fourier transform (FFT), free-space Coulomb and Newton potential, biharmonic equation, separable Gaussian-sum (GS) approximation

1
Introduction

This paper presents a method for solving the unbounded three-dimensional Poisson equation

\displaystyle-\Delta u(\bm{x})=\rho(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\lim_{|\bm{x}|\rightarrow\infty}|u(\bm{x})|=0,

(1)

based on the classical representation of the solution as the convolution of the source density
\rho
with the free-space Green’s function
U(\bm{x})=\tfrac{1}{4\pi}\tfrac{1}{|\bm{x}|}
,

\displaystyle u(\bm{x})=(U\ast\rho)(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,\rho(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y},\quad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(2)

We assume the smooth and compactly supported density/source distribution
\rho
to be defined on a uniform grid, as it is common in many applications. This assumption enables the use of spectral methods based on trapezoidal quadrature, where the rapid decay of Fourier coefficients leads to high accuracy even on relatively coarse grids
[
30
,
31
]
.

The problem (
2
) is a fundamental model that appears in a wide range of physical applications
[
7
]
, including the simulation of Bose-Einstein condensates
[
4
,
25
,
3
,
28
]
, quantum chemistry
[
26
,
27
,
19
,
18
,
17
]
, particle physics
[
1
,
2
,
23
]
, electro- and magnetostatics
[
21
,
16
,
11
,
15
]
and astrophysics
[
29
,
6
]
.

Common approaches to compute the nonlocal convolution (
2
) are based on Fourier transforms of the
truncated Green’s function

[
32
]
or fall within the class of
Ewald-type methods

[
9
,
24
,
27
,
13
]
, which decompose the singular Green’s function
U
into a smooth long-range component
U_{s}
and a localized singular correction
U_{c}
. This decomposition allows the smooth part of the convolution to be efficiently evaluated using the convolution theorem

U\ast\rho\approx\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}(U_{s})\cdot\mathcal{F}(\rho)\right),

typically on an equispaced Cartesian grid using the
fast Fourier transform
(FFT), which offers quasi-linear scaling. The smoothness of
U_{s}
ensures rapid convergence of the Fourier series, making the spectral approximation both accurate and efficient. The remaining correction term involving
U_{c}
retains the singularity but is locally supported, and thus can be evaluated using direct summation or FFT-based correction via a Taylor expansion of the density
[
13
,
10
]
. A naive direct evaluation of the full convolution (
2
) scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
on a grid with
N
points. In contrast, the original Ewald method
[
9
]
and optimized variations scale as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})
. A fully spectral method as introduced in
[
13
]
is based on a smooth and separable
Gaussian-sum
(GS) approximation of the Green’s function
[
22
,
5
,
11
,
20
]
to regularize the singularity, that is,

\displaystyle U(\bm{x})\approx U_{GS}(\bm{x})=\sum_{s=0}^{S}\omega_{s}\prod_{p=1}^{3}e^{-\alpha_{s}x_{p}^{2}},\quad|\bm{x}|\in(\varepsilon,\delta],\,0<\varepsilon<\delta.

(3)

Owing to the tensor-product structure of the approximation, a significant portion of the computational effort can be accurately precomputed in the form of one-dimensional integrals during the setup phase, where the effort is affordable since problem (
2
) is typically solved many times in applications. However, the cutoff error introduced by the Gaussian-sum approximation is of the order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
, which requires the evaluation of a correction integral to maintain high overall accuracy. In
[
13
]
, this correction was performed using a Taylor expansion of the density function followed by FFT evaluation, introducing additional computational effort.

In contrast, the method proposed in this work achieves
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
complexity by applying FFT-based convolution only to a smooth component of the biharmonic Green’s function, eliminating the need for a correction term while maintaining accuracy up to machine precision.

The key component of the method is the introduction of a
super-potential

v

[
8
]
, which satisfies the simple relation

\displaystyle u(\bm{x})=\Delta v(\bm{x}),

(4)

where
v
solves the biharmonic equation
-\Delta^{2}v=\rho
and is given as the convolution with the biharmonic Green’s function.
Approximating this convolution using a rank-3 separable Gaussian-sum of the biharmonic Green’s function results in a truncation error of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})

[
14
]
. After computing
v
via Fourier-based convolution, the solution
u
to (
2
) can then be efficiently obtained through spectral differentiation, maintaining high precision.

2
Method

For the sake of simplicity, we assume a (rescaled) density compactly supported in the unit cube
\bm{B}_{1}=[-1/2,1/2]^{3}
(a general rectangular domain could also be considered
[
10
]
). The problem (
2
) gets for
\bm{x}\in\bm{B}_{1}

\displaystyle u(\bm{x})=(U\ast\rho)(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U(\bm{y})\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}=\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}U(\bm{y})\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}

(5)

with
\bm{B}_{2}:=2\cdot\bm{B}_{1}=[-1,1]^{3}
.
According to (
4
) we have

\displaystyle u(\bm{x})=\Delta\big(V\ast\rho\big)(\bm{x})=\Delta\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}.

(6)

After a Fourier series approximation of the density, we can apply the convolution theorem utilizing FFT. Note that
\bm{x}-\bm{y}\in 3\cdot\bm{B}_{1}
. However, it is enough to consider a (truncated) Fourier series approximation of
\rho
on
\bm{B}_{2}
(with simple zero padding)
[
13
]
. More precisely, let the
2
-periodic (
L=2
) smooth density
\rho
be approximated as

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x})\approx\sum_{\bm{k}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{k}}\,e^{2\pi i\,\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}{L}},

(7)

\bm{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
,
k_{j}=-\tilde{n}_{j}/2,\ldots,\tilde{n}_{j}/2-1,\,\tilde{n}_{j}=2n_{j}
with the Fourier coefficients

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{1}{8}\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}\rho(\bm{x})\,e^{-2\pi i\,\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}{L}}\,d\bm{x},

(8)

which are approximated by trapezoidal quadrature utilizing FFT.
After substitution into (
6
) we get

\displaystyle u(\bm{x})=-\pi^{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}(|\bm{k}|^{2}\,\hat{V}_{\bm{k}}\,\hat{\rho}_{\bm{k}})\,e^{\pi i\,\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}

(9)

with

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\bm{k}}:=\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|\,e^{-\pi i\,\bm{k}\cdot\bm{y}}\,d\bm{y},

(10)

where (
9
) is computed by (inverse) FFT.

In principle, the integrals in (
10
) could be precomputed. However, accurate evaluation of the three-dimensional integrals is both computationally expensive and numerically challenging. To overcome this and efficiently shift a significant portion of the computational cost to the setup phase, we employ a separable Gaussian-sum approximation of the kernel
|\bm{y}|
. This enables effective and accurate precomputation by reducing the problem to sums of one-dimensional tensor-product integrals.

Figure 1
:
Relative errors of the GS approximation for
|y|
with
S=460
terms.

We begin with the integral representation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n}e^{-r\,t^{2}}\,\text{d}t=\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{n+1}{2}\right)}{2r^{\tfrac{n+1}{2}}},\quad r>0,\quad n>-1,

(11)

and consider the case
n=0
. Following the
sinc quadrature
approach
[
22
]
, this yields an exponentially convergent, separable approximation of the free-space Green’s function
U
in (
2
) for
r=|\bm{y}|^{2}
. Specifically, for
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
0<\varepsilon<1\leq\delta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi}\frac{1}{|\bm{y}|}\approx\sum_{s=0}^{S}\omega_{s}\,e^{-\alpha_{s}|\bm{y}|^{2}}=\sum_{s=0}^{S}\omega_{s}\prod_{p=1}^{3}e^{-\alpha_{s}y_{p}^{2}},\quad|\bm{y}|\in(\varepsilon,\delta],

(12)

where the approximation is given as a sum of separable Gaussians.

Multiplying both sides of (
12
) by
|\bm{y}|^{2}
yields an approximation of the biharmonic kernel

\displaystyle V(\bm{y})\approx V_{\mathrm{GS}}(\bm{y})=\sum_{s=0}^{S}\omega_{s}|\bm{y}|^{2}e^{-\alpha_{s}|\bm{y}|^{2}}=\sum_{s=0}^{S}\sum_{q=1}^{3}\omega_{s}\prod_{p=1}^{3}\begin{cases}y_{p}^{2}\,e^{-\alpha_{s}y_{p}^{2}},&\text{if }p=q,\\[2.15277pt]
e^{-\alpha_{s}y_{p}^{2}},&\text{if }p\neq q,\end{cases}

(13)

which is a sum of rank-3 separable functions. The sinc quadrature parameters and the approximation interval
(\varepsilon,\delta]
can be adapted based on the desired accuracy and domain size
[
12
]
. Figure
1
illustrates the relative error of such a GS approximation using
461
terms, which shows double machine precision in
(1
E-
04,\delta],\,\delta>1
.

In the super-potential convolution (
6
), replacing the kernel
|\bm{y}|
with
V_{\mathrm{GS}}(\bm{y})
results in an overall approximation error of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
, provided that the pointwise difference
|\,\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{\mathrm{GS}}(\bm{y})\,|
is negligible in the interval
(\varepsilon,\delta]
. This leads to the assumption that the correction integral can be localized

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{\mathrm{GS}}(|\bm{y}|)\Big)\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}\approx\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})}\Big(\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{\mathrm{GS}}(|\bm{y}|)\Big)\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y},

(14)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})
denotes the ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at the origin.

Lemma 1

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0,\,\rho
be bounded, and
|\,\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{\mathrm{GS}}(\bm{y})\,|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
for
|\bm{y}|\in(\varepsilon,1]
. Then there holds

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}=\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}V_{GS}(\bm{y})\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

(15)

Proof.
We have

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}\big(\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{\mathrm{GS}}(|\bm{y}|)\big)\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}=\Big(\int_{\bm{B}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})}+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{\mathrm{GS}}(|\bm{y}|)\Big)\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y},

(16)

where due to the accuracy of the GS approximation

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\bm{B}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})}\Big(\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{\mathrm{GS}}(|\bm{y}|)\Big)\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}\Big|\leq|\bm{B}_{2}|\,\|\rho\|_{\infty}\,\max_{|\bm{y}|\in(\varepsilon,1]}|\,\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{\mathrm{GS}}(\bm{y})\,|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

(17)

For
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})
we have
|\bm{y}|\leq\varepsilon
and
V_{GS}(|\bm{y}|)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
and hence,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})}\big(\frac{1}{8\pi}|\bm{y}|-V_{GS}(|\bm{y}|)\big)\,\rho(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}\Big|\leq\varepsilon\,\big(\frac{1}{8\pi}+C\,\varepsilon\,+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\big)\,|\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})|\,\|\rho\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}),

(18)

with
|\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{0})|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})
denoting the volume of the
\varepsilon
-ball.

\square

The approximations of the integrals (
10
) can now be precomputed as a sum of tensor products of one-dimensional quadratures (e.g. Gauss-Kronrod) up to machine precision. In fact, we have

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\bm{k}}\approx\int_{\bm{B}_{2}}V_{GS}(\bm{y})\,e^{-\pi i\,\bm{k}\cdot\bm{y}}\,d\bm{y}=\sum_{s=0}^{S}\sum_{q=1}^{3}\omega_{s}\prod_{p=1}^{3}\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{1}2\,y_{p}^{2}\,e^{-\alpha_{s}y_{p}^{2}}\,\cos(\pi k_{p}y_{p})\,dy_{p},&\text{if }p=q,\\[4.26773pt]
\int_{0}^{1}2\,e^{-\alpha_{s}y_{p}^{2}}\,\cos(\pi k_{p}y_{p})\,dy_{p},&\text{if }p\neq q.\end{cases}

(19)

In general (
19
) needs the computation of
2\times(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})\times(S+1)
one-dimensional integrals with
n_{j}=\tilde{n}_{j}/2
.
In contrast, the tensor (
10
) would need the accurate computation of
\mathcal{O}(n_{1}n_{2}n_{3})
three-dimensional integrals.

Conclusion

We have presented a fast and accurate method for solving the unbounded Poisson equation in three dimensions using a spectral convolution approach based on a super-potential formulation. By expressing the solution as the Laplacian of a convolution with the biharmonic Green’s function, the method avoids the singularity of the original Green’s function and achieves high-order accuracy without the need for explicit correction terms. The convolution is regularized via a separable Gaussian-sum approximation, enabling efficient precomputation and the use of simple zero-padded FFT with quasi-linear overall complexity. Unlike earlier Gaussian-sum methods, which require a Taylor-based correction to mitigate cutoff errors, our approach achieves machine-precision accuracy solely from the convolution and spectral differentiation.

Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed method significantly improves upon the original GS approach in both error and runtime performance, particularly in anisotropic regimes. The super-potential formulation leads to fast convergence and reduced computational overhead, making the method especially attractive for repeated evaluations as commonly encountered in physical simulations. These advantages establish the method as a robust and efficient tool for high-fidelity solutions of the free-space Poisson problem on uniform grids.
