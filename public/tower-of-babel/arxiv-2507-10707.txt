Pinning models with contact number constraint: the effect of disorder
Giambattista Giacomin and Marco Zamparo

Abstract
. Disordered pinning models are statistical mechanics models built on discrete renewal processes: renewal epochs in this context are called contacts . It is well known that pinning models can undergo a localization/delocalization phase transition: in the localized phase the typical density of contacts is positive and the largest gap between contacts is at most of the order of the logarithm of the size n n of the system, whereas the system is void of contacts in the delocalized phase. When disorder is absent and the phase transition is discontinuous, conditioning the contact density to be positive but smaller than the minimum typical density in the localized phase has the effect of forcing to create one, and only one, macroscopic gap between two contacts, while the rest of the configuration keeps the characteristics of a localized state. However it is known that, in the presence of (bounded) disorder, this big jump phenomenon is no longer observed, in the sense that the largest gap in the conditioned system is o ⁡ ( n ) o(n) . Under minimal integrability conditions on the disorder we show that the conditioned system is localized in a very strong sense, in particular the largest gap is O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) . The proof is achieved by exploiting the improved understanding of the localized phase of disordered pinning models developed in [ 34 ] and by establishing some refined estimates, in particular a quenched Local Central Limit Theorem. We also present an analysis of the effect of disorder on the large deviation rate function of the contact density and, as an important ingredient for the generality of our results, we establish a smoothing inequality for pinning models under minimal integrability conditions, thus generalizing [ 32 , 11 ] . Introduction and main results

1.1.
The pinning model

Let
T_{1},T_{2},\cdots
be i.i.d.
\mathbb{N}
-valued random variables on a
probability space
(\mathcal{S},\mathfrak{S},\mathsf{P})
. Here and below

\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Looking upon

T_{1},T_{2},\ldots
as inter-arrival times, the associated random walk
defined by
S_{0}:=0
and
S_{i}:=T_{1}+\cdots+T_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
identifies
the renewal times of a renewal process. Hence the number of renewals
by time
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is

L_{n}:=\sup\big\{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\,S_{i}\leq n\big\}\,.

The model we consider is built on the renewal sequence

S:=\{S_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and depends on a parameter
h\in\mathbb{R}
and on a
sequence of real numbers
\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, which we
call
charges
. In fact, the
pinning model
is defined
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
by the probability
\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}
that satisfies

(1.1)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}}:=\frac{1}{Z_{n,h}(\omega)}\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{i=1}^{L_{n}}(h+\omega_{S_{i}})}\mathds{1}_{\{n\in S\}}\,,

provided that the
partition function

Z_{n,h}(\omega):=\mathsf{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{i=1}^{L_{n}}(h+\omega_{S_{i}})}\mathds{1}_{\{n\in S\}}]
is not 0. The set
\{n\in S\}\in\mathfrak{S}
is the
event that a renewal time coincides with
n
. Moreover, here and in
what follows, the convention is made that empty sums are 0 and empty
products are 1.

The assumptions on the law of inter-arrival times and the charges are
the following.

Assumption 1.1.

p(t):=\mathsf{P}[T_{1}=t]={\ell(t)}/{t^{\alpha+1}}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
with

\alpha\geq 1
and a slowly varying function at infinity

\ell
. Moreover,
p(t)>0
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

We recall that a real measurable function
\ell
, defined on

[1,+\infty)
in our case, is slowly varying at infinity if

\ell(z)>0
for all sufficiently large
z
and
\lim_{z\uparrow+\infty}\ell(\lambda z)/\ell(z)=1
for every
\lambda>0

[
7
]
. We assume that
p(t)>0
, i.e.,
\ell(t)>0
, for
all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, which in particular guarantees that

Z_{n,h}(\omega)>0
for every
n
and every
\omega
.

Assumption 1.2.

The charges
\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are sampled
from a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
in such a way
that the canonical projections
\omega\mapsto\omega_{a}
form a
sequence of i.i.d. random variables. Furthermore,

\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{\eta|\omega_{0}|}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]<+\infty
for some
number
\eta>0
and
\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]=0
.

We underline the
quenched
nature of the model, i.e., the fact
that the model is defined for every
n
once the charges are sampled
and we look for results that hold for typical realizations of them. Of
course the charges introduce a non translation invariant character in
the model: they are meant to take into account
impurities
, more
modernly called
disorder
, that should not be forgotten when
going toward more realistic models. In our case
h
represents the
average pinning potential, but the true pinning potential
h+\omega_{a}

is not constant and it is site dependent. With this language, we say
that the model is
pure
or
non disordered
if

\mathbb{P}[\{\omega\equiv 0\}]=1
. The pure pinning model is summarized
by
\mathsf{P}_{n,h,0}
and is explicitly solvable (see

[
27
,
28
,
38
]
).

There exists an extensive literature on pinning models, with or
without disorder, starting from the
60
s. We refer to the reviews

[
27
,
28
,
38
,
46
]
for extended
bibliographies and for discussions on the origin and relevance of
pinning models in applied sciences, notably physics and biology. In
short, the renewal set is interpreted as the ensemble of sites, the

contacts
, at which an interface, a polymer or another
fluctuating linear structure touches a
defect line
, receiving a
penalty, respectively a reward, at the contact site
a
if

h+\omega_{a}<0
, respectively if
h+\omega_{a}>0
. With this language,
the size
L_{n}
of the contact set is referred to as
contact
number
.

It is possible, and convenient when deriving correlation estimates, to
rewrite the model in terms of the binary random variables

X_{a}:=\mathds{1}_{\{a\in S\}}
for
a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, which
take value 1 at the contact sites and value 0 at the other sites. Note
that
X_{0}=1
as
S_{0}:=0
by definition. With this notation, we have
that (
1.1
) is equivalent to

(1.2)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}}=\frac{1}{Z_{n,h}(\omega)}\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a=1}^{n}(h+\omega_{a})X_{a}}X_{n}

with
Z_{n,h}(\omega)=\mathsf{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h+\omega_{a})X_{a}}X_{n}]
, and
the contact number reads
L_{n}=\sum_{a=1}^{n}X_{a}
.

1.2.
The free energy density

Under Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
, a
superadditivity argument shows that the limit defining the
free
energy density
(we will simply say
free energy
henceforth)

f(h):=\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\log Z_{n,h}\bigg]

exists and is finite for all
h\in\mathbb{R}
(see
[
27
]
,
Theorem 4.6). We list a number of elementary, but important facts:

(1)

the function
f
that maps
h
to
f(h)
is non-decreasing and
convex. Moreover, it is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
equal to 1;

(2)

Z_{n,h}(\omega)\geq\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{n}}p(n)
and

Z_{n,h}(\omega)\geq\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a=1}^{n}(h+\omega_{a})}p(1)^{n}
for all

n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Omega
,
so
f(h)\geq\max\{0,h+\log p(1)\}
;

(3)

Z_{n,h}(\omega)\leq\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a=1}^{n}\max\{0,h+\omega_{a}\}}
for
all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Omega
, so
f(h)\leq\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,h+\omega_{0}\}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]
.

These facts entail that if we set
h_{c}:=\inf\{h\in\mathbb{R}:f(h)>0\}
, then

-\infty\leq h_{c}<+\infty
,
f(h)=0
for
h\leq h_{c}
(when

h_{c}>-\infty
), and
\lim_{h\downarrow h_{c}}f(h)=0
both when

h_{c}>-\infty
and when
h_{c}=-\infty
. We refer to

[
34
, Proposition 1.1]
for an almost optimal
criterion to decide whether
h_{c}>-\infty
or not: in particular,

h_{c}>-\infty
if
\eta>1/(1+\alpha)
with
\eta
given in
Assumption
1.2
. With the definition of
h_{c}
at hand,
we say that the model is

•

delocalized
, or that it is in the
delocalized phase
, if
h<h_{c}
(when
h_{c}>-\infty
);

•

critical
if
h=h_{c}
(when
h_{c}>-\infty
);

•

localized
, or that it is in the
localized phase
, if
h>h_{c}
.

Note that
h_{c}=-\infty
implies that the model is localized for every
h
.

The free energy function in the localized phase has been investigated
in
[
31
,
34
]
. We reproduce here the
following findings that are particularly relevant for us (see

[
34
, Theorem 1.2]
).

Theorem 1.1.

The free energy
f
is strictly convex and infinitely
differentiable on
(h_{c},+\infty)
, and the following property holds
for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
: for every compact set
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
and

r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\adjustlimits{\lim}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\partial^{r}_{h}\log Z_{n,h}(\omega)-\partial_{h}^{r}f(h)\bigg|=\adjustlimits{\lim}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\partial^{r}_{h}\log Z_{n,h}\bigg]-\partial_{h}^{r}f(h)\bigg|=0\,.

Moreover,
f
is of class Gevrey-3 on
(h_{c},+\infty)
, i.e., for
every compact set
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
there exists a constant

c>0
such that for all
r\in\mathbb{N}

\sup_{h\in H}\Big|\partial_{h}^{r}f(h)\Big|\leq c^{r}(r!)^{3}\,.

We will refer to the first derivative
\rho:=\partial_{h}f
of
f
in
the localized phase as the
contact density
because, bearing
in mind that
\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}]=\partial_{h}\log Z_{n,h}(\omega)
,
Theorem
1.1
shows that the following holds for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
: for
every
h>h_{c}

(1.3)

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\frac{L_{n}}{n}\bigg]=\partial_{h}f(h)=:\rho(h)\,.

The second derivative
v:=\partial_{h}^{2}f
of
f
in the localized phase
is the limiting scaled variance of the contact number. In fact, as

\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\cdot}[(L_{n}-\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\cdot}[L_{n}])^{2}]=\partial_{h}^{2}\log Z_{n,h}
, Theorem
1.1
implies that also the following is
valid for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
: for every
h>h_{c}

(1.4)

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{L_{n}-\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}]}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)^{\!\!2}\bigg]=\partial_{h}^{2}f(h)=:v(h)\,.

We see that
\rho
takes values in
[0,1]
and the strict convexity of
the free energy in the localized phase implies that
\rho
is strictly
increasing and
v
strictly positive over
(h_{c},+\infty)
. Then we
infer that
\rho(h)>0
for all
h>h_{c}
, in agreement with the
intuitive notion of localized phase and in contrast with the fact that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}/n]=0
in the delocalized
phase. We note that
\lim_{h\uparrow+\infty}\rho(h)=1
since combining
convexity and differentiability of
f
with the bound

f(h)\geq\max\{0,h+\log p(1)\}
we deduce that
f(0)\geq\max\{0,h+\log p(1)\}-h\rho(h)
for
h>h_{c}
, which in turn gives

\liminf_{h\uparrow+\infty}\rho(h)\geq 1
.

The limit
\rho_{c}:=\lim_{h\downarrow h_{c}}\rho(h)
is of main
importance for us. When
h_{c}=-\infty
, the above bound

f(0)\geq\max\{0,h+\log p(1)\}-h\rho(h)
for
h>h_{c}
shows that

\limsup_{h\downarrow h_{c}}\rho(h)\leq 0
, and hence
\rho_{c}=0
. When

h_{c}>-\infty
, the model undergoes a localization/delocalization phase
transition that is
continuous
if
\rho_{c}=0
and

discontinuous
, or
first order
, if
\rho_{c}>0
.

1.3.
Large deviations for the contact number

The function
I_{h}
that maps
r\in\mathbb{R}
to

(1.5)

I_{h}(r):=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{r(k-h)-f(k)+f(h)\big\}

turns out to be a rate function in a Large Deviation Principle
(LDP). We note that
I_{h}
is a way to write the convex conjugate of

f(h+\cdot)-f(h)
, the latter being the limiting scaled cumulant
generating function of the contact number. In fact, we have the
following result under Assumptions
1.1

and
1.2
(see
[
39
, Theorem 1.4 and Corollary
1.11]
).

Proposition 1.2.

The following bounds defining a full LDP for the contact density
hold for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
:

(i)

\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}(1/n)\log\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}/n\in G]\geq-\inf_{r\in G}I_{h}(r)
for all
h\in\mathbb{R}
and
G\subseteq\mathbb{R}
open;

(ii)

\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}(1/n)\log\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}/n\in F]\leq-\inf_{r\in F}I_{h}(r)
for all
h\in\mathbb{R}
and
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}
closed.

One can show that
I_{h}(r)=+\infty
for
r\notin[0,1]
in a number
of ways, so the effective domain of
I_{h}
, i.e., the region where

I_{h}
is finite, is contained in the closed interval
[0,1]
. This
is, for example, a consequence of part
(i)
in
Proposition
1.2
since
0\leq L_{n}\leq n
. Of course a bounded
effective domain for
I_{h}
directly entails that, in the language of
large deviation theory, the rate
I_{h}
is
good
, i.e., its
sub-level sets are compact.

The effective domain of
I_{h}
is exactly the closed interval

[0,1]
. In fact
I_{h}
is convex and finite at the boundary of such
interval:
I_{h}(0)=f(h)
and
I_{h}(1)=f(h)-h-\log p(1)
. To see this we
note that both
f(k)
and
k-f(k)
are non-decreasing with respect to

k
with
\lim_{k\downarrow-\infty}f(k)=0
. Moreover, using that

p(t)\leq p(1)^{2-t}
for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
one can demonstrate that

Z_{n,k}(\omega)\leq p(1)\mathrm{e}^{k+\omega_{n}}\prod_{a=1}^{n-1}[1/p(1)+\mathrm{e}^{k+\omega_{a}}p(1)]

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Omega
, so
f(k)\leq\int_{\Omega}\log[1/p(1)+\mathrm{e}^{k+\omega_{0}}p(1)]\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]
. Combining the
latter with the bound
f(k)\geq k+\log p(1)
one finds that

\lim_{k\uparrow+\infty}[k-f(k)]=-\log p(1)
.

Of major interest is the shape of
I_{h}
over the interior of its
effective domain. To determine it, we recall that
\rho:=\partial_{h}f

is strictly increasing over
(h_{c},+\infty)
from
\rho_{c}
to 1, so it
has an inverse function
\imath_{\rho}
that maps the interval

(\rho_{c},1)
onto the interval
(h_{c},+\infty)
. Since the free energy

f
is infinitely differentiable in the localized phase by Theorem

1.1
,
\imath_{\rho}
is infinitely differentiable because of
the inverse function theorem. One can easily verify that

\imath_{\rho}
allows us to write
I_{h}
for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
as follows:

(1.6)

I_{h}(r)=\begin{cases}r(h_{c}-h)+f(h)&\mbox{if $r\in[0,\rho_{c}]$}\\
r[\imath_{\rho}(r)-h]-f\circ\imath_{\rho}(r)+f(h)&\mbox{if $r\in(\rho_{c},1)$}\\
f(h)-h-\log p(1)&\mbox{if $r=1$}\end{cases}

with the convention that
r(h_{c}-h)=0
when
r=0
and

h_{c}=-\infty
.

The rate function
I_{h}
of the contact density turns out to be
continuously differentiable on
(0,1)
and infinitely differentiable
and strictly convex on
(\rho_{c},1)
, the strict convexity being
manifest from the formula
\partial_{r}^{2}I_{h}(r)=1/\partial_{h}^{2}f(\imath_{\rho}(r))>0
valid for all

r\in(\rho_{c},1)
. Furthermore,
I_{h}
is of class Gevrey-3 on

(\rho_{c},1)
, inheriting this property from
f
thanks to Theorem

1.1
since Gevrey classes are closed under inversion and
composition (closure under composition is already in the work by
Maurice Gevrey
[
26
]
and one can find closure under
differentiation and passing to reciprocal, hence also about inversion,
in
[
40
, pages 94-95]
, along with the references to the
original literature). Importantly, when
\rho_{c}>0
, i.e., when the
model undergoes a first order phase transition, the rate function

I_{h}
exhibits an affine stretch terminating at
\rho_{c}
, so two
different regimes of large deviations appear corresponding to strict
and non-strict convexity of
I_{h}
. A natural question concerns the
meaning of these two regimes.

Remark 1.3.

We stress that Proposition
1.2
, like Theorem

1.1
and all the results that follow, is uniform with
respect to the free parameter
h
: we identify a set of charges of
full probability for which the results hold for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
or
every
h>h_{c}
depending on the situation. In the case of Proposition

1.2
, such a uniformity is not immediate from the results in

[
39
]
, where there is no a free parameter like

h
. What
[
39
, Theorem 1.4 and Corollary 1.11]

really state is that for each
h\in\mathbb{R}
there exists a set of
charges
\Omega_{h}\in\mathcal{F}
with
\mathbb{P}[\Omega_{h}]=1
such that
the large deviation bounds
(i)
and
(ii)
hold for all

\omega\in\Omega_{h}
. But then one realizes that the set
\Omega_{0}

corresponding to no free parameter works for all
h
. In fact, the
model
\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}
can be regarded as an exponential change
of measure of the model
\mathsf{P}_{n,0,\omega}
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{n,0,\omega}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{hL_{n}}}{\mathsf{E}_{n,0,\omega}[\mathrm{e}^{hL_{n}}]}\,.

Since the change of measure is governed by the contact number, the
full LDP for the contact density with good rate function in the model

\mathsf{P}_{n,0,\omega}
with a given
\omega
implies a full LDP with
good rate function in the model
\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}
with the same

\omega
(see
[
37
, Theorem III.17]
). It therefore
follows that
\Omega_{h}\supseteq\Omega_{0}
because of the uniqueness of
the rate function.

1.4.
The big jump phenomenon for pure models

The pure model is explicitly solvable, as mentioned earlier. In the
absence of disorder we have
h_{c}=0
and the free energy
f(h)
for

h>h_{c}
is the unique positive real number that solves the equation

\mathsf{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-f(h)T_{1}}]=\mathrm{e}^{-h}
(see
[
27
, Proposition
1.1]
). The real analytic implicit function theorem
shows that the function
f
is analytic for
h>h_{c}
, and one can
easily verify that
\rho_{c}:=\lim_{h\downarrow h_{c}}\rho(h)=\lim_{h\downarrow h_{c}}\partial_{h}f(h)=1/\mathsf{E}[T_{1}]
if

\mathsf{E}[T_{1}]<+\infty
and
\rho_{c}=0
if
\mathsf{E}[T_{1}]=+\infty
. So there is
a localization/delocalization phase transition, which is first order
when
\mathsf{E}[T_{1}]<+\infty
. In this case the rate function
I_{h}
of the
contact density has an affine stretch terminating at
\rho_{c}

according to formula (
1.6
) and two different
regimes of large deviations occur. In all cases the rate function is
strictly convex and analytic in the region
(\rho_{c},1)
.

An explanation of the two different regimes of large deviations is
achieved by investigating the maximal gap between contacts

M_{n}:=\max\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{L_{n}}\}
conditional on a given number
l
of
contacts. The following proposition, which is proved in Appendix

A
, lists the main results of such investigation.
We note that the presence of the parameter
h
in this proposition,
and in Theorem
1.7
below, is just a presentation choice
because the sharp conditioning disables such parameter:

\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[\,\cdot\,|L_{n}=l]
is independent of
h
whatever

n
,
l
, and
\omega
are. Let
\imath_{\rho}
be the inverse function
of the contact density
\rho
as previously defined.

Proposition 1.4.

The following conclusions hold:

(i)

for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
, closed set
R\subset(\rho_{c},1)
, and
\epsilon>0

\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{l\in nR\cap\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,0}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{M_{n}}{\log n}-\frac{1}{f\circ\imath_{\rho}(l/n)}\bigg|>\epsilon\bigg|L_{n}=l\bigg]=0\,;

(ii)

if
\rho_{c}>0
, then for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
, closed set
R\subset(0,\rho_{c})
, and
\epsilon>0

\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{l\in nR\cap\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,0}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{M_{n}}{n}-\left(1-\frac{l/n}{\rho_{c}}\right)\bigg|>\epsilon\bigg|L_{n}=l\bigg]=0\,.

Proposition
1.4
states that the regime of large
deviations corresponding to the affine stretch of
I_{h}
involves a

big jump phenomenon
, i.e.,
M_{n}
is
O(n)
, as the most
likely mechanism to achieve a small number of contacts. On the
contrary,
M_{n}
is
almost microscopic
, i.e.,
O(\log n)
, in
the regime of large deviations where
I_{h}
is strictly convex. The big
jump phenomenon is a well-known feature of heavy-tailed random
variables, such as the inter-arrival times used here: large values of
sums of independent heavy-tailed random variables are attained by a
large value of just one of the terms in the sum

[
3
,
16
,
24
,
42
]
.

1.5.
Main results: smoothing, contact number conditioning, and disorder effect

One interesting question concerns the effect of disorder on the
conditioned model we consider and, in particular, on the big jump
phenomenon. The presence or the lack of an affine stretch in the graph
of
I_{h}
is related to the event
\rho_{c}>0
, which in turn depends on
the occurrence of a phase transition, i.e., on
h_{c}>-\infty
, and on
the differentiability properties of the free energy
f
at
h_{c}
: in
fact, by convexity
\rho_{c}:=\lim_{h\downarrow h_{c}}\rho(h)
with

\rho:=\partial_{h}f
is the right derivative of
f
at
h_{c}
, whereas
the left derivative is 0. For this reason, we propose first a
smoothing inequality under Assumptions
1.1
and

1.2
, generalizing previous works (see

[
32
,
33
,
11
]
).

Theorem 1.5.

Suppose that
\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}^{2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]>0
. If

h_{c}>-\infty
, then there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all
sufficiently small
\delta>0

f(h_{c}+\delta)\leq c\,\delta^{2}\,.

We recall that the smoothing bound in
[
11
, Theorems 1.5 and
1.8]
works for charges of the form
\beta\omega_{a}

with
\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}^{2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]=1
and
\beta\in(0,\xi/2)
,
\beta
being a free parameter that controls the amplitude of
the charges and
\xi>0
being a number such that
\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{z\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]<+\infty
for all
z\in(-\xi,\xi)
. In
our setting, where there is no
\beta
but the variance of the charges
can be any, the hypothesis
\beta<\xi/2
becomes
\eta>2
with
\eta

as in Assumption
1.2
. Therefore
Theorem
1.5
is already proved in
[
11
]

if
\eta>2
, but here we extend the smoothing bound to the case

\eta\leq 2
and, in particular, to
1/(1+\alpha)<\eta\leq 2
for which we
have
h_{c}>-\infty
as recalled in Section
1.2
.

We are now in the position to state that, in the presence of disorder,

\rho_{c}=0
irrespective of whether
\mathsf{E}[T_{1}]
is infinite or not. In
fact, either disorder washes out the phase transition entailing

h_{c}=-\infty
or the phase transition is still present but it is
necessarily continuous as the right derivative of
f
at
h_{c}
is 0
according to Theorem
1.5
. Consequently, the affine
stretch in the graph of
I_{h}
is washed out by disorder and the two
different regimes of large deviations of the pure model merge into a
single one. The following corollary of formula
(
1.6
) and Theorem
1.5
collects what
we know about the rate function
I_{h}
.

Corollary 1.6.

Suppose that
\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}^{2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]>0
. For
every
h\in\mathbb{R}
the rate function
I_{h}
is strictly convex,
infinitely differentiable, and of class Gevrey-3 on
(0,1)
.

Our main finding is that disorder prevents any form of big jump
phenomena, as stated by the following theorem.

Theorem 1.7.

Suppose that
\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}^{2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]>0
. The
following property holds for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
: for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
and closed
set
R\subset(0,1)
there exists a constant
c>0
(independent of

\omega
) such that

\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{l\in nR\cap\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[M_{n}>c\log n\big|L_{n}=l\big]=0\,.

Theorem
1.7
substantially strengthens the results in

[
29
]
where in particular it is shown (assuming bounded
disorder) that for every closed set
R\subset(0,1)
and
\epsilon>0

and for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega

(1.7)

\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{l\in nR\cap\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[M_{n}>\epsilon n\big|L_{n}=l\big]=0\,.

In words, (
1.7
) just states that there is no macroscopic
big jump in the presence of disorder, i.e., the largest gap between
contacts is not macroscopic. Theorem
1.7
instead says that
the largest gap between contacts is almost microscopic and it is of
the same order of the largest gap between contacts in the localized
phase of the (pure or disordered) pinning model (see

[
31
, Theorem 2.5]
and

[
34
, Proposition 1.9]
). Other results analogous to
(
1.7
) are proved in
[
29
]
, notably in the
directions of generalized pinning models proposed in physics. Also
these results can be strengthened to obtain that the largest gap
between contacts is almost microscopic and
Section
4
is devoted to this.

We can refine Theorem
1.7
in the direction of saying that,
not only there is no trace of the big jump, but also that the contact
density is constant through the system even down to scale lengths that
diverge faster than
\log n
. This is the content of the next theorem.

Theorem 1.8.

The following property holds for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
: for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
and

\epsilon>0
, closed set
R\subset(0,1)
, and sequence

\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive numbers such that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\zeta_{n}=+\infty

\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{l\in nR\cap\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\Bigg[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\frac{l}{n}\bigg|>\epsilon\Bigg|L_{n}=l\Bigg]=0\,.

Proving results like those in Theorem
1.7
and in
Theorem
1.8
on the conditional measure requires a
good control of the probability mass function of
L_{n}
. In fact, a
crucial ingredient is a quenched local Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for
the contact number in the localized phase. This local result is
interesting in itself and we state it here among the main results.

Theorem 1.9.

The following property holds for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
: for every
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
compact

\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}\,\sup_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\Bigg|\sqrt{2\pi v(h)n}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}=l]-\exp\bigg\{\!-\frac{(l-\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}])^{2}}{2v(h)n}\bigg\}\Bigg|=0

with
v(h):=\partial_{h}^{2}f(h)>0
.

Theorem
1.9
is established by estimating the characteristic
function of the relevant random variable. This is a well-known
approach for independent random variables and it has been extended for
spin averages in non-disordered Gibbs models under suitable conditions
on the interaction and assuming that the CLT holds: the case of local
interactions is discussed in
[
21
]
and the case of
infinite-range pair interactions is tackled in

[
10
]
. The pinning model can be recast in a binary spin
model language and the contact number becomes the total magnetization
(see, e.g.,
[
48
]
), but the potentials involved contain
arbitrarily many body interactions (this will be taken up again in
Section
1.6
). Moreover, our model is
disordered. So we cannot apply the results in

[
21
,
10
]
and we have to exploit the specific
structure of one-dimensional pinning models.

1.6.
First order transitions, big jump phenomena, and phase separation droplet

Big jump phenomena are widespread and they are the crucial mechanisms
in problems studied in depth from fundamental mathematics to applied
sciences: in fact, they have attracted attention in the mathematical
community in connection with several condensation phenomena under
conditioning (see
[
23
]
and references therein) and
anomalous transport (see
[
50
]
and references
therein). We refer to
[
35
,
47
,
49
]
and
references therein for big jump regimes in the physical literature.
Investigating the stability of big jump phenomena in random media is a
special case of the general and challenging issue of understanding the
effect of disorder on phase transitions and critical phenomena (see,
e.g.,
[
28
, Chapter 5]
and references therein). To the best
of our knowledge, this has been done only for condensation phenomena
in the zero-range process with site-dependent jump rates

[
36
,
43
]
, exploiting the fact that this process has a
factorized steady state with single-site marginals that depend solely
on the local disorder. Coming to the pinning context, the way in
which disorder enters the model is more spatially structured than in
the zero-range process and this is one of the challenges of the
problem we face. The problem for pinning models is challenging to the
point that the most natural generalization of the model we tackle, and
(to a large extent) solve here, is widely open as we explain in
Subsection
1.6.3
.

We now discuss further these and related issues.

1.6.1.
Analogy with the phase separation droplet phenomenon

Proposition
1.4
and our main result,
Theorem
1.7
, are actually results about
canonical
Gibbs measures
and in this sense there is a direct link with the
problem much studied in statistical mechanics

[
20
,
14
,
8
]
that we now outline. Consider the
(non disordered) nearest-neighbor ferromagnetic Ising model, without
external field, in the box

\Lambda_{n}:=\{-n,\ldots,n\}^{d}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with dimension
d\geq 2
and at a temperature below the critical one. For such value of the
temperature the infinite-volume Gibbs measure is not unique and there
are two (extremal) translational invariant measures, one with average
magnetization
m_{+}>0
and the other one with average magnetization

m_{-}=-m_{+}<0
: this phenomenology is related to the first order
character of the phase transition. If
+1
boundary conditions are
imposed, then as
n
goes to infinity the
\Lambda_{n}
Gibbs measure
converges to the extremal Gibbs measure with average magnetization

m_{+}
. But if we condition the same model to have a given total
magnetization which is asymptotically equivalent to
m|\Lambda_{n}|
with
m\in(m_{-},m_{+})
, then sending
n
to infinity the
system rearranges in a phase separation pattern so that at a
macroscopic level, i.e., when the box is rescaled to
[-1,1]^{d}
, the
system is partitioned into a region of the minus phase and a region of
the plus phase (and an interface or hypersurface separating the two
phases). In particular, if
m
is not too far from
m_{+}
, then there
is a droplet – the
Wulff crystal
– of the
m_{-}
phase in a
sea of the
m_{+}
phase. This is analogous to the big jump phenomenon
in the one dimensional pinning model and, in both cases, this
phenomenon is directly linked to the presence of a first order phase
transition. Of course in the pinning model the two phases are the
localized phase
h>h_{c}
, with contact density
\rho(h)
, and the
delocalized phase in which not only the contact density is zero, but
the system is really void of points. Instead in the Ising model the
two phases are perfectly symmetrical: statistically they can be mapped
one into the other by switching the spin signs. This difference is
discussed further in Subsection
1.6.2
.

First order phase transitions are present in Ising models also when

d=1
, but we have to step to long-range interactions and in this
context the droplet phenomenon has been studied in
[
12
]

where it is shown that, under suitable hypotheses, it appears a phase
separation droplet that takes precisely the shape of a single interval
– a big jump – of the minus phase in the sea of the plus phase.

Results addressing the persistence of the phase separation droplet in
disordered Ising models have been developed in the context of diluted
models
[
45
]
. But the case we consider is more closely
related to external random field as disorder. In the nearest-neighbor
Ising context that we have just outlined, it is known that the first
order phase transition persists if
d\geq 3
and it does not if
d=2

(see
[
2
,
9
,
19
,
18
]
and
references therein). For the
d=1
long-range case the situation is
more complex and the first order transition may or may not withstand
disorder
[
2
,
17
]
. In this sense the pinning
model has a parallel in the
d=2
Ising model and in some
d=1

long-range cases, with the first order transition washed out by
disorder.

We present results on the canonical measure of the pinning model that
hold uniformly in the volume and down to the microscopic scale,
showing that the system is localized in a very strong sense, i.e., in
the sense that there is no trace of the delocalized phase: in making
this statement we play of course on the very different nature of the
two phases in the pinning model. We are not aware of a detailed
pathwise analysis of the canonical Gibbs Ising measure with random
external field, even if it may be approachable with the recent
considerable progress in this domain

[
1
,
17
,
19
]
.

1.6.2.
More about disorder smoothing and the connection with Ising models

As mentioned in Section
1.1
, it is possible to write
the pinning model in terms of the binary random variables

X_{0},X_{1},\ldots
, with the minor difference with respect to the Ising
case that the single spin state space is
\{0,1\}
instead of

\{-1,1\}
. The major difference is that the reference measure is not
the product measure, but a discrete point process. By this we mean
that, under the law
\mathsf{P}
, the sequence
\{X_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a
regenerative process with heavy-tailed inter-arrival distribution
(which is precisely the probability mass function
p
of
Assumption
1.1
), and of course we interpret a site
a
such
that
X_{a}=1
as a renewal epoch. In order to compare with the Ising
model, the variables
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots
should be i.i.d. Bernoulli
variables: in this case the inter-arrival times would be geometric
random variables. We can change the reference measure to the measure
of i.i.d. Bernoulli variables, but the Hamiltonian of the pinning
model passes from containing only one body interactions to arbitrarily
many body interactions (see
[
48
, formula (4)]
). The
structure and properties of this long-range and many-body Hamiltonian
and the associated specification has been only partially studied and
here we limit ourselves to the following considerations:

(1)

of course the long-range and many-body nature of the pinning
interactions is at the very origin of the
localization/delocalization phase transition, with the two phases
that are very asymmetrical (one concentrates on all
zeros). Despite this asymmetry, we point out the partial analogy
with the one-dimensional Ising model with
long-range two-body interactions, which exhibits a first order phase
transition if the interactions decay slow enough. It is precisely in
this context that the droplet model has been investigated (see

[
12
]
), as well as the effect of a random external field
on the transition (see in particular

[
2
,
13
,
17
]
that include also recent
progress);

(2)

this discussion brings to the spotlight the analogy between the
smoothing phenomenon for pinning models, i.e.,
Theorem
1.5
and

[
32
,
33
,
11
]
, and the

Imry–Ma smoothing phenomenon
first mathematically
established for Ising spin systems (and beyond) in the seminal work
by Aizenman and Wehr
[
2
]
(see also the recent progresses

[
1
,
19
]
). The arguments leading to these two
smoothing phenomena appear to be different, but there might be a
bridge between them (beyond the fact that both mechanisms wash out
the first order transition). A path for linking the two smoothing
phenomena could be via studying larger classes of long-range
many-body interactions for Ising spin systems in one dimension.

1.6.3.
On higher dimensional pinning models and open problems

There is a natural generalization of the pinning model that consists
in considering pinning for renewals in dimensions larger than one (see

[
25
,
30
]
and references therein). This is directly
stimulated by the applications and by the very first motivation, at
least from the historical viewpoint, for the pinning model: the DNA
denaturation transition. In fact, in the biophysics community the
pinning model goes under the name of
Poland-Scheraga model

[
44
]
. And, always in the biophysics community, also a

generalized Poland-Scheraga model

[
25
]
has been
introduced to account for mismatches in the pairing of the two DNA
strands, and, in mathematical terms, this model is precisely a pinning
model based on a two dimensional renewal process
[
30
]
. For
this higher dimensional renewal system one can actually show that the
localization transition is present also in the disordered model (see

[
5
,
41
]
and references therein), but the pure model has a
regime in which a big jump appears if the two stands are of
sufficiently different lengths. We stress that the big jump phenomenon
in the generalized Poland-Scheraga model is present without resorting
to conditioning, but it demands that the two polymer strands have
different lengths, and this is analogous to forcing the longer strand
to have less contacts than the
natural amount
. Whether disorder
washes out the big jump phenomenon in this generalized context is
open.

We conclude this section by pointing out that the main results we
present, Theorems
1.7
and
1.8
, hold
uniformly in contact densities belonging to closed subsets of
(0,1)
.
We do believe that the results should hold also for closed subsets of

(0,1]
, i.e., for contact densities that can be arbitrarily close to
the fully packed system. This is particularly intuitive for
Theorem
1.7
: in fact if the contact number is larger than
n-c\log n
for a constant
c>0
, then there is no room for a contact
gap of more than
c\log n
. At the expense of a nontrivial amount of
technical work and supposing that disorder is bounded, we can extend
our results to contact densities that approach
1
as
n
becomes
large, but we are unable to cover all disorder laws in Assumption

1.2
. This appears to be a limit of the local CLT
approach we adopt.

2.
Smoothing of disorder under subexponential conditions

The proof of Theorem
1.5
follows the line of

[
32
,
33
,
11
]
, and notably
exploits the
rare stretch strategy
applied to a model in which
the charges are
tilted
, according to the distribution
(
2.1
) below, in order to achieve mean
h
, while in the
original model the mean
h
is achieved by a shift, i.e., by adding

h
to
\omega_{a}
for every
a
. In fact, we explicitly exploit the
optimized result in
[
11
]
for the free energy smoothing
of the model with tilted disorder. But tilting is equivalent to
shifting only in the Gaussian case, and this is used in

[
33
]
to give a very compact proof. For general
disorder the control of shifting by tilting is obtained in

[
32
]
supposing that the charges are bounded. In

[
11
]
the boundedness is weakened to
Assumption
1.2
, at least if the variance of the charges
is below a certain threshold, which is infinite if the tail of the
charge law has super-exponential decay. We generalize these results
by removing any condition beyond Assumptions
1.1
and

1.2
. From the technical viewpoint, unlike the arguments
in
[
32
,
11
]
, our argument is direct in the
sense that we do not go through the convex conjugate of the free
energy function.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

For
s\in(0,\eta)
, with
\eta
as in Assumption
1.2
,
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
define

f_{s}(h):=\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Omega}\log Z_{n,h}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{n,s}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\,,

where we have introduced the
tilted measure

(2.1)

\mathbb{P}_{n,s}[\mathrm{d}\omega]:=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{s\sum_{a=1}^{n}\omega_{a}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}{\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{s\sum_{a=1}^{n}\omega_{a}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}]}\,.

A smoothing inequality with respect to this disorder tilt is provided
under Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
by

[
11
, Theorem 1.5]
, which states in particular that if

h_{c}>-\infty
, then there exists
s_{o}\in(0,\eta)
such that for all

s\in(0,s_{o})

(2.2)

f_{s}(h_{c})\leq(1+\alpha)s^{2}\,.

To control shifting by tilting, taking advantage of
(
2.2
), we shall demonstrate that there exist constants

\zeta>0
and
\eta_{o}\in(0,\eta)
such that

(2.3)

\int_{\Omega}(\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma})\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\geq\zeta\int_{\Omega}\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),\omega}[X_{a}]\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
s,\sigma\in(0,\eta_{o})
, and

a\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Since by direct computation we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\sigma}\int_{\Omega}\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

\displaystyle\penalty\ =\sum_{a=1}^{n}\Bigg\{\int_{\Omega}(\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma})\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]-\zeta\int_{\Omega}\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),\omega}[X_{a}]\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\Bigg\}\,,

bound (
2.3
) and an integration with respect to

\sigma
from 0 to
s
imply that

\int_{\Omega}\log Z_{n,h+\zeta s}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\leq\int_{\Omega}\log Z_{n,h}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{n,s}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
, and
s\in(0,\eta_{o})
. Dividing the
latter by
n
and then letting
n
go to infinity, we deduce that

f(h+\zeta s)\leq f_{s}(h)
for all
h\in\mathbb{R}
and
s\in(0,\eta_{o})
. It
therefore follows from (
2.2
) that if
h_{c}>-\infty
, then

f(h_{c}+\delta)\leq(\alpha+1)(\delta/\zeta)^{2}
for all

\delta\in(0,\min\{s_{o},\eta_{o}\}/\zeta\})
, which proves the theorem
with
c:=(\alpha+1)/\zeta^{2}
.

The bound (
2.3
).
The hypotheses

\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]=0
and

\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}^{2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]>0
assure us that

\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{0}\}^{\!2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]>0
. Set

\zeta:=\min\Bigg\{\frac{1}{8}\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{0}\}^{\!2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega],\frac{1}{4}\Bigg\}>0\,,

and for
s\in(0,\eta)
define

m_{s}:=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}\mathrm{e}^{s\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}{\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{s\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}\,.

The dominated convergence theorem shows first that
\lim_{s\downarrow 0}m_{s}=0
and then that

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{0}-m_{s}\}^{\!2}\mathrm{e}^{s\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}{\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{s\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}=\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{0}\}^{\!2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\,,

so

(2.4)

\frac{\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{0}-m_{s}\}^{\!2}\mathrm{e}^{s\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}{\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{s\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{0}\}^{\!2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\geq 4\zeta

for all
s\in(0,\eta_{o})
with a suitable choice of

\eta_{o}\in(0,\eta)
. We claim that (
2.3
) holds
with such constants
\zeta
and
\eta_{o}
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
s,\sigma\in(0,\eta_{o})
, and

a\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and denote by
{}^{a}\omega
the family of charges
where
\omega_{a}
is replaced by
m_{\sigma}
:
{}^{a}\omega_{b}=m_{\sigma}
if

b=a
and
{}^{a}\omega_{b}=\omega_{b}
if
b\neq a
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma})\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Omega}(\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma})\Big[\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)-\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}({}^{a}\omega)\Big]\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

(2.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma}\}\Big[\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)-\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}({}^{a}\omega)\Big]\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\,,

where the equality is due to the fact that
\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}({}^{a}\omega)
is independent of
\omega_{a}
,
while the inequality is due to the fact that

Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)
is increasing with respect to

\omega_{a}
. Convexity gives for all

\omega:=\{\omega_{b}\}_{b\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Omega

\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)-\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}({}^{a}\omega)\geq\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),{}^{a}\omega}[X_{a}](\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma})\,.

At the same time, the binary nature of
X_{a}
allows us to write for
every
\omega:=\{\omega_{b}\}_{b\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Omega

\displaystyle\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)-\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}({}^{a}\omega)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\mathsf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{b=1}^{n}\{h+\zeta(s-\sigma)+\omega_{b}\}X_{b}}X_{n}\big]}{\mathsf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{b=1}^{n}\{h+\zeta(s-\sigma)+{}^{a}\omega_{b}\}X_{b}}X_{n}\big]}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\mathsf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{b=1}^{n}\{h+\zeta(s-\sigma)+\omega_{b}\}X_{b}}X_{n}\big]}{\mathsf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{(m_{\sigma}-\omega_{a})X_{a}+\sum_{b=1}^{n}\{h+\zeta(s-\sigma)+\omega_{b}\}X_{b}}X_{n}\big]}

\displaystyle=-\log\Big\{1-(1-\mathrm{e}^{m_{\sigma}-\omega_{a}})\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),\omega}[X_{a}]\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\mathrm{e}^{m_{\sigma}-\omega_{a}})\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),\omega}[X_{a}]\,.

Thus, we deduce from (
2.5
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma})\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma}\}^{\!2}\,\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),{}^{a}\omega}[X_{a}]\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma}\}(1-\mathrm{e}^{m_{\sigma}-\omega_{a}})\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),\omega}[X_{a}]\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{0}-m_{\sigma}\}^{\!2}\mathrm{e}^{\sigma\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}{\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{\sigma\omega_{0}}\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]}\int_{\Omega}\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),{}^{a}\omega}[X_{a}]\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

(2.6)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\max\{0,\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma}\}(1-\mathrm{e}^{m_{\sigma}-\omega_{a}})\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),\omega}[X_{a}]\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\,,

where the equality is due to the fact that the expectation

\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),{}^{a}\omega}[X_{a}]
is independent of

\omega_{a}
. Now we restore the charge
\omega_{a}
in this expectation
by appealing again to the binary nature of
X_{a}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),{}^{a}\omega}[X_{a}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{E}\big[X_{a}\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{b=1}^{n}\{h+\zeta(s-\sigma)+{}^{a}\omega_{b}\}X_{b}}X_{n}\big]}{\mathsf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{b=1}^{n}\{h+\zeta(s-\sigma)+{}^{a}\omega_{b}\}X_{b}}X_{n}\big]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{E}\big[X_{a}\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{b=1}^{n}\{h+\zeta(s-\sigma)+\omega_{b}\}X_{b}}X_{n}\big]}{\mathsf{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-(\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma})(X_{a}-1)+\sum_{b=1}^{n}\{h+\zeta(s-\sigma)+\omega_{b}\}X_{b}}X_{n}\big]}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\max\{0,\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma}\}}\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),\omega}[X_{a}]\,.

Thus, combining (
2.7
) with (
2.6
) and
using (
2.4
), as well as the fact that
1/2\geq 2\zeta
by construction, we find

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma})\log Z_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma)}(\omega)\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \geq 2\zeta\int_{\Omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\max\{0,\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma}\}}+\max\{0,\omega_{a}-m_{\sigma}\}(1-\mathrm{e}^{m_{\sigma}-\omega_{a}})\Big]\mathsf{E}_{n,h+\zeta(s-\sigma),\omega}[X_{a}]\,\mathbb{P}_{n,\sigma}[\mathrm{d}\omega]\,.

This bound proves (
2.3
) because

\mathrm{e}^{-\max\{0,z\}}+\max\{0,z\}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-z})\geq 1/2
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

3.
Effect of disorder under contact number conditioning

3.1.
Results from the companion paper
[
34
]

The probability measure
\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}
associated to the model
we consider is absolutely continuous with respect to the renewal law

\mathsf{P}
and the relative density is the exponential of an energy term
which contains only one body potentials (see
(
1.2
)). From this it is not difficult to infer a
fundamental conditional independence property between consecutive
stretches of the polymer. In order to formulate such property, let

\mathcal{M}_{n}
be the set of functions
\phi:\{0,1\}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{C}

such that
|\phi(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})|\leq 1
for every

x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in\{0,1\}
and denote by
\vartheta
the left shift
that maps the sequence
\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
to

\vartheta\omega:=\{\omega_{a+1}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. The following
factorization, which implies the mentioned conditional independence,
is provided by
[
34
, Lemma 2.1]
: for every

h\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, integers
0\leq a\leq n
, and functions

\phi\in\mathcal{M}_{a}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{n-a}

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\phi(X_{0},\ldots,X_{a})\psi(X_{a},\ldots,X_{n})\Big|X_{a}=1\Big]

(3.1)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathsf{E}_{a,h,\omega}\big[\phi(X_{0},\ldots,X_{a})\big]\mathsf{E}_{n-a,h,\vartheta^{a}\omega}\big[\psi(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n-a})\big]\,,

so that

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\phi(X_{0},\ldots,X_{a})\psi(X_{a},\ldots,X_{n})\Big|X_{a}=1\Big]

(3.2)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\big[\phi(X_{0},\ldots,X_{a})\big|X_{a}=1\big]\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\big[\psi(X_{a},\ldots,X_{n})\big|X_{a}=1\big]\,.

When aiming at decorrelation estimates we will make use of the next
result, which is the first part of
[
34
, Lemma
3.1]
. Given two measurable complex functions

\Phi
and
\Psi
over
(\mathcal{S},\mathfrak{S})
we define their
covariance under the polymer measure
\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}
as

\mathsf{cov}_{n,h,\omega}[\Phi,\Psi]:=\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\big[\Phi\overline{\Psi}\,\big]-\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\big[\Phi\big]\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\big[\,\overline{\Psi}\,\big]\,,

provided that all the expectations exist. As usual,
\bar{\zeta}
is
the complex conjugate of
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
. The variance

\mathsf{cov}_{n,h,\omega}[\Phi,\Phi]
is denoted by

\mathsf{var}_{n,h,\omega}[\Phi]
.

Lemma 3.1.

For every
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega:=\{\omega_{c}\}_{c\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Omega
,
integers
0\leq a\leq b\leq n
, and functions
\phi\in\mathcal{M}_{a}

and
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{n-b}

\bigg|\mathsf{cov}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\phi(X_{0},\ldots,X_{a}),\psi(X_{b},\ldots,X_{n})\Big]\bigg|\leq 2\sum_{i=0}^{a-1}\sum_{j=b+1}^{\infty}\mathsf{E}_{j-i,h,\vartheta^{i}\omega}^{\otimes 2}\bigg[\prod_{k=1}^{j-i-1}(1-X_{k}X_{k}^{\prime})\bigg]\,.

Lemma
3.1
is demonstrated in
[
34
]
by
exploiting the trick of working with two
replica
of the
system and then of using the conditional independence property
(
3.2
) in the event that the two replica meet in between
the sites
a
and
b
. In fact, the lemma involves two independent
copies of the renewal process,
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and

\{S_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with associated contact indicators

\{X_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
\{X_{a}^{\prime}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, defined on the
measurable space
(\mathcal{S}^{2},\mathfrak{S}^{\otimes 2})
. The
product measure
\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}^{\otimes 2}
on

(\mathcal{S}^{2},\mathfrak{S}^{\otimes 2})
is the two-polymer measure
and
\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}^{\otimes 2}
is the corresponding expectation.

It will not come as a surprise then that the following estimate, which
is
[
34
, Lemma 3.2]
, is of crucial importance. Here
and below the assumptions in force are Assumptions
1.1
and

1.2
.

Lemma 3.2.

For every closed set
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
there exist constants
\gamma>0
and
G>0
such
that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sup_{h\in H}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\cdot}^{\otimes 2}\bigg[\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(1-X_{k}X_{k}^{\prime})\bigg]\Bigg]\leq G\,\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma n}\,.

One of the ingredients of Lemma
3.2
is an

alternative free energy
\mu(h)
, which can be defined by

(3.3)

\mu(h):=-\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\cdot}[T_{1}=n]\Big]\,.

Like
f(h)
, we have
\mu(h)>0
for

and
\mu(h)=0
for
h\leq h_{c}
(when

) (see
[
34
, Proposition 1.7 and Corollary
2.5]
). The proof of these facts relies on a
version of the McDiarmid’s inequality for functions of independent
random variables under subexponential conditions, which enables to
show the following concentration inequality for the finite-volume free
energy (see
[
34
, Theorem 2.4]
).

Theorem 3.3.

There exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
u\geq 0

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\Big|\log Z_{n,h}-\mathbb{E}\big[\log Z_{n,h}\big]\Big|>u\bigg]\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\kappa u^{2}}{n+u}}\,.

Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
are at the basis of Theorem

. The latter implies that the contact number
L_{n}

satisfies the following quenched CLT when

(see
[

, part

Theorem 3.4.

The following properties hold for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
: for every compact set

\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}\,\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\,\Bigg|\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\frac{L_{n}-\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}]}{\sqrt{nv(h)}}\leq u\bigg]-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{u}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}}\mathrm{d}z\Bigg|=0

with

.

3.2.
The local CLT for the contact number

The proof of the quenched local CLT for the contact number requires
that we investigate the characteristic function of
L_{n}
with respect
to the polymer measure
\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}
. Put for brevity

\#_{n}:=\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor
and

J_{n,h,\omega}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\#_{n}}\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2k}}p(1)^{2}p(2)}{[p(2)+\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2k}}p(1)^{2}]^{2}}X_{2k-1}X_{2k+1}\,.

The following lemma, which is partly inspired by
[
10
, Lemmas 2.2 and
2.3]
, deals with the mentioned characteristic
function and is the first step towards Theorem
1.9
.

Lemma 3.5.

For every integer
n\geq 3
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, and

z\in[-\pi,\pi]

\Big|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zL_{n}}\big]\Big|\leq\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi^{2}}J_{n,h,\omega}}\Big]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]}+4\frac{\mathsf{var}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]}{\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]^{2}}\,.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Fix an integer
n\geq 3
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, and

z\in[-\pi,\pi]
. The second inequality is due to the first and to
Chebyshev’s inequality, which entail

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi^{2}}J_{n,h,\omega}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]}+\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[J_{n,h,\omega}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]}+4\frac{\mathsf{var}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]}{\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]^{2}}\,.

Regarding the first inequality, denoting by
\mathfrak{O}
the

\sigma
-algebra generated by the odd binary variables

X_{1},X_{3},\ldots
, we can state that

\Big|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zL_{n}}\big]\Big|\leq\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z\sum_{k=1}^{\#_{n}}X_{2k}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|\bigg]\,.

Therefore, it suffices to show that

(3.4)

\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z\sum_{k=1}^{\#_{n}}X_{2k}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi^{2}}J_{n,h,\omega}}\,.

This bound is trivial if at most one variable among

X_{1},X_{3},\ldots,X_{2\#_{n}+1}
takes value 1 since
J_{n,h,\omega}=0
in
this case. Then, suppose that at least two of these variables take
value 1 and let
2l_{1}+1<\cdots<2l_{r}+1
with
r\geq 2
be the sites
where the values 1 are attained. Expliciting
J_{n,h,\omega}
we see
that (
3.4
) is tantamount to the following bound to be
proved:

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z\sum_{k=1}^{\#_{n}}X_{2k}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|

(3.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\prod_{s=2}^{r}\Bigg(\mathds{1}_{\{l_{s}\neq l_{s-1}+1\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{l_{s}=l_{s-1}+1\}}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{2z^{2}\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}p(2)}{\pi^{2}[p(2)+\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}]^{2}}\bigg\}\Bigg)\,.

Let us verify (
3.5
). Because of (
3.2
),
conditioning to the odd binary variables makes the sums

\sum_{k=1}^{l_{1}}X_{2k},\sum_{k=l_{1}+1}^{l_{2}}X_{2k},\ldots,\sum_{k=l_{r-1}+1}^{l_{r}}X_{2k},\sum_{k=l_{r}+1}^{\#_{n}}X_{2k}

independent, so that

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z\sum_{k=1}^{\#_{n}}X_{2k}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z\sum_{k=1}^{l_{1}}X_{2k}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{s=2}^{r}\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z\sum_{k=l_{s-1}+1}^{l_{s}}X_{2k}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z\sum_{k=l_{r}+1}^{\#_{n}}X_{2k}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|

(3.6)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{s=2}^{r}\Bigg\{\mathds{1}_{\{l_{s}\neq l_{s-1}+1\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{l_{s}=l_{s-1}+1\}}\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zX_{2l_{s}}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|\Bigg\}\,.

Under the hypothesis
l_{s}=l_{s-1}+1
two applications of
(
3.1
) yield

\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zX_{2l_{s}}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]=\mathsf{E}_{2,h,\vartheta^{2l_{s}-1}\omega}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zX_{1}}\big]=\frac{p(2)+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z+h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}}{p(2)+\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}}\,,

and the bounds
\cos z\leq 1-\frac{2z^{2}}{\pi^{2}}
, as
z\in[-\pi,\pi]
,
and
\sqrt{1-\zeta}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\zeta}{2}}
for
\zeta\in[0,1]

give

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zX_{2l_{s}}}\Big|\mathfrak{O}\Big]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{p(2)^{2}+\mathrm{e}^{2h+2\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{4}+2\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}p(2)\cos z}}{p(2)+\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{4z^{2}\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}p(2)}{\pi^{2}[p(2)+\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}]^{2}}}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{2z^{2}\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}p(2)}{\pi^{2}[p(2)+\mathrm{e}^{h+\omega_{2l_{s}}}p(1)^{2}]^{2}}\right\}\,.

We find (
3.5
) by combining (
3.6
) with
(
3.7
).
∎

The next step is to investigate the mean and the variance of

.

Lemma 3.6.

The following properties hold for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
:

(i)

\displaystyle{\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}\inf_{h\in H}\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]>0}
for all

compact;

(ii)

\displaystyle{\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}\sup_{h\in H}\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{var}_{n,h,\omega}[J_{n,h,\omega}]<+\infty}

for all
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
compact.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

The properties of slowly varying functions (see
[
7
, Theorem 1.2.1
and Proposition 1.3.6]
) imply that there exists a
constant
\xi>0
such that for all
t,\tau\in\mathbb{N}

(3.8)

\frac{p(t+\tau)}{p(t)p(\tau)}\leq\xi\min\{t^{\xi},\tau^{\xi}\}\,.

The constant
\xi
enters a technical lower bound on the probability
that there is a contact at a given site. In fact, for all
h\in\mathbb{R}
,

\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Omega
, and integers

we have (see
[

(3.9)

\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{a}]\geq\frac{1}{1+\xi\mathrm{e}^{-h-\omega_{a}}\min\{a^{\xi},(n-a)^{\xi}\}}\,.

Such lower bound is at the basis of the following estimate
that we need to prove the lemma: for every integer
n\geq 3
,

h\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Omega
, and

k\in\{1,\ldots,\#_{n}\}
with
\#_{n}:=\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}X_{2k+1}]

(3.10)

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2|h|}}{2}\frac{1}{1+\xi 2^{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{2k-1}}}\frac{1}{1+\xi\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{2k+1}}}\Big(\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k}]+\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k+1}]\Big)\,.

In order to prove (
3.10
) we appeal to
(
3.1
) to state that for a given integer
n\geq 3
,

h\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Omega
, and

k\in\{1,\ldots,\#_{n}\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}X_{2k+1}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}|X_{2k+1}=1]\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k+1}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{2k+1,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}]\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k+1}]

and

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k+1}]\geq\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k}X_{2k+1}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k+1}|X_{2k}=1]\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{n-2k,h,\vartheta^{2k}\omega}[X_{1}]\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k}]\,,

so that

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}X_{2k+1}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}_{2k+1,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}]\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k+1}]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}_{2k+1,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}]\mathsf{E}_{n-2k,h,\vartheta^{2k}\omega}[X_{1}]\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}_{2k+1,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}]\mathsf{E}_{n-2k,h,\vartheta^{2k}\omega}[X_{1}]\Big(\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k}]+\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[X_{2k+1}]\Big)\,.

This gives (
3.10
) because
(
3.9
) shows that

\mathsf{E}_{2k+1,h,\omega}[X_{2k-1}]\geq\frac{1}{1+\xi 2^{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{-h-\omega_{2k-1}}}\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-|h|}}{1+\xi 2^{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{2k-1}}}

and

\mathsf{E}_{n-2k,h,\vartheta^{2k}\omega}[X_{1}]\geq\frac{1}{1+\xi\mathrm{e}^{-h-\omega_{2k+1}}}\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-|h|}}{1+\xi\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{2k+1}}}\,.

Part (i).
Let
\Lambda
be the random variable that
maps
\omega:=\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Omega
to

\Lambda(\omega):=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\omega_{1}}p(1)^{2}p(2)}{[p(2)+\mathrm{e}^{\omega_{1}}p(1)^{2}]^{2}}\frac{1}{1+\xi 2^{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{0}}}\frac{1}{1+\xi\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{2}}}\,,

\xi
being the constant introduced in (
3.8
). Note the

\Lambda
is independent of the parameter
h
. Bounds
(
3.10
) gives for every integer
n\geq 3
,
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
and

\omega\in\Omega

increases continuously with respect to
h
and the limits

\lim_{h\downarrow-\infty}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}]=1
and

\lim_{h\uparrow+\infty}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}]=n
hold true. Thus, for
each
l\in\{2,\ldots,n-1\}
there exists a unique number

h_{n,l,\omega}
such that

\mathsf{E}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[L_{n}]=l
. This number allows us a
profitable change of measure, which in turn originates the following
bound that we use to prove the theorem: for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

h,c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, and
l\in\{2,\ldots,n-1\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[M_{n}>c\log n\,\big|L_{n}=l\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[M_{n}>c\log n\,,L_{n}=l]}{\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[L_{n}=l]}

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[M_{n}>c\log n]}{\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[L_{n}=l]}\,.

Theorems
1.1
and
1.9
and Lemma

3.7
assure us that there exists

\Omega_{o}\in\mathcal{F}
with
\mathbb{P}[\Omega_{o}]=1
such that the
following properties hold for all
\omega\in\Omega_{o}
,

H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
compact, and some constant
c>0
possibly
depending on
H
but not on
\omega
:

(1)

\displaystyle{\adjustlimits{\lim}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}\bigg|\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\frac{L_{n}}{n}\bigg]-\rho(h)\bigg|=0\,;}

(2)

\displaystyle{\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}\,\sup_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\Bigg|\sqrt{2\pi v(h)n}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}=l]-\exp\bigg\{\!-\frac{(l-\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}])^{2}}{2v(h)n}\bigg\}\Bigg|=0\,;}

(3)

\displaystyle{\adjustlimits{\limsup}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}\,n\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[M_{n}>c\log n\big]<+\infty\,.}

Fix
\omega\in\Omega_{o}
and suppose without restriction
that the compact set
R
in the statement of Theorem
1.7
is
of the form
[\delta,1-\delta]
with some
\delta\in(0,1/2)
. In this
way,
nR\cap\mathbb{N}
becomes the set of all integers
l
such that

\delta n\leq l\leq(1-\delta)n
. Since

\int_{\Omega}\omega_{0}^{2}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]>0
, according to Theorem

1.5
we have
\rho_{c}=0
and, whatever
\delta
is, there
are values of the free parameter
h
in the localized phase where the
contact density attains the values
\delta/2
and
1-\delta/2
. Such
values are
\imath_{\rho}(\delta/2)>h_{c}
and

\imath_{\rho}(1-\delta/2)>\imath_{\rho}(\delta/2)
,
\imath_{\rho}
being the inverse
function of the contact density introduced in Section
1.3
.
Property
(1)
tells us that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathsf{E}_{n,\imath_{\rho}(\delta/2),\omega}[L_{n}/n]=\delta/2

and

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathsf{E}_{n,\imath_{\rho}(1-\delta/2),\omega}[L_{n}/n]=1-\delta/2
,
so that
\mathsf{E}_{n,\imath_{\rho}(\delta/2),\omega}[L_{n}]\leq\delta n
and

\mathsf{E}_{n,\imath_{\rho}(1-\delta/2),\omega}[L_{n}]\geq(1-\delta)n
for all
integers
n\geq n_{o}
with some number
n_{o}
. Setting

H:=[\imath_{\rho}(\delta/2),\imath_{\rho}(1-\delta/2)]
, it therefore follows that
the above number
h_{n,l,\omega}
belongs to
H
for all integers

n\geq n_{o}
and
l\in nR\cap\mathbb{N}
. Let
c>0
be a constant
(independent of
\omega
) such that Property
(3)
holds. For all
integers
n\geq n_{o}
and
l\in nR\cap\mathbb{N}
we have

(3.17)

\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}\big[M_{n}>c\log n\big]\leq\sup_{h\in H}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[M_{n}>c\log n\big]

and, recalling that

\mathsf{E}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[L_{n}]=l
by construction,

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi\sup_{h\in H}v(h)n}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[L_{n}=l]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\sqrt{2\pi v(h_{n,l,\omega})n}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[L_{n}=l]

(3.18)

\displaystyle\qquad\geq 1-\sup_{h\in H}\,\sup_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\Bigg|\sqrt{2\pi v(h)n}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}=l]-\exp\bigg\{\!-\frac{(l-\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}[L_{n}])^{2}}{2v(h)n}\bigg\}\Bigg|\,.

Combining bounds (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) together
and invoking Properties
(2)
and
(3)
we get

\adjustlimits{\limsmallspace}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{l\in nR\cap\mathbb{N}}\,\frac{\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[M_{n}>c\log n]}{\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{n,l,\omega},\omega}[L_{n}=l]}=0\,.

This proves the theorem thanks to bound (
3.16
).
∎

3.4.
Mesoscopic contact averages

Theorem
1.8
follows from the next finding,
Theorem
3.8
, exactly in the same way as
Theorem
1.7
follows from Lemma
3.7
. But
Theorem
3.8
is a result of interest in its own right
as it shows that the macroscopic behavior of the (unconditioned)
pinning model is already detectable at mesoscopic scales that are just
required to grow faster than
\log n
.

Theorem 3.8.

The following property holds for
\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.e.\ }\omega
: for every compact set

H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
, all numbers
\gamma,\epsilon>0
, and any
sequence
\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive numbers such that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\zeta_{n}=+\infty

\adjustlimits{\lim}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}n^{\gamma}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\Bigg[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon\Bigg]<+\infty\,.

We present a proof that relies on the following preliminary lemma.

Lemma 3.9.

For every compact set
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
and
\epsilon>0
there
exist constants
\gamma>0
and
G>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

\sup_{h\in H}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\cdot}\big[|L_{n}-\rho(h)n|>\epsilon n\big]\leq\Gamma_{n}

with a non-negative measurable function
\Gamma_{n}
on
\Omega
that
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{n}]\leq G\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma n}
.

Proof Lemma
3.9
.

Fix
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
compact and
\epsilon>0
. We shall show
that for every
h\in H
there exist constants
\gamma_{h}>0
and

G_{h}>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

(3.19)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\cdot}\big[|L_{n}-\rho(h)n|>\epsilon n/2\big]\Big]\leq G_{h}\mathrm{e}^{-2\gamma_{h}n}\,.

This proves the lemma as follows. Since
\rho:=\partial_{h}f
is
uniformly continuous on the compact set
H
by Theorem

1.1
, there exists
\delta_{o}>0
such that

|\rho(h)-\rho(h^{\prime})|\leq\epsilon/2
if
h,h^{\prime}\in H
satisfy

|h-h^{\prime}|<2\delta_{o}
. Put
\delta_{h}:=\min\{\gamma_{h},\delta_{o}\}
and
note that we can find finitely many points
h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}
in
H

such that

H\subset\cup_{s=1}^{r}(h_{s}-\delta_{h_{s}},h_{s}+\delta_{h_{s}})
. For all

n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\in(h_{s}-\delta_{h_{s}},h_{s}+\delta_{h_{s}})\cap H
, and

\omega\in\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[|L_{n}-\rho(h)n|>\epsilon n\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[|L_{n}-\rho(h_{s})n|>\epsilon n/2\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{|L_{n}-\rho(h_{s})n|>\epsilon n/2\}}\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a=1}^{n}(h+\omega_{a})X_{a}}X_{n}]}{\mathsf{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a=1}^{n}(h+\omega_{a})X_{a}}X_{n}]}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{\delta_{h_{s}}n}\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{s},\omega}\big[|L_{n}-\rho(h_{s})n|>\epsilon n/2\big]\,,

so

\sup_{h\in H}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[|L_{n}-\rho(h)n|>\epsilon n\big]\leq\sum_{s=1}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{\delta_{h_{s}}n}\mathsf{P}_{n,h_{s},\omega}\big[|L_{n}-\rho(h_{s})n|>\epsilon n/2\big]=:\Gamma_{n}(\omega)\,.

The function
\Gamma_{n}
that maps
\omega\in\Omega
to

\Gamma_{n}(\omega)
is non-negative and measurable and, due to
(
3.19
), satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{n}]\leq G\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma n}
with

\gamma:=\min\{\gamma_{h_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{h_{r}}\}
and

G:=G_{h_{1}}+\cdots+G_{h_{r}}
.

The bound (
3.19
)
. Pick

h\in H
and let
\zeta>0
be a number so small that

f(h+\zeta)-f(h)-\rho(h)\zeta\leq\epsilon\zeta/8
and

f(h-\zeta)-f(h)+\rho(h)\zeta\leq\epsilon\zeta/8
, which exists because

\rho(h):=\partial_{h}f(h)
. Put
c:=\epsilon\zeta/32
for brevity. If

\log Z_{n,h}(\omega)\geq\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h}]-cn
and
\log Z_{n,h+\zeta}(\omega)\leq\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h+\zeta}]+cn
, then a
Chernoff–type bound gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[L_{n}>\rho(h)n+\epsilon n/2\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\big[\mathrm{e}^{\zeta L_{n}}\big]\mathrm{e}^{-\rho(h)\zeta n+\epsilon\zeta n/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{n,h+\zeta}(\omega)}{Z_{n,h}(\omega)}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho(h)\zeta n+\epsilon\zeta n/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h+\zeta}]-\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h}]-\rho(h)\zeta n-\epsilon\zeta n/2+2cn}.

This allows us to state that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\omega\in\Omega

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[L_{n}>\rho(h)n+\epsilon n/2\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{1}_{\{\log Z_{n,h}(\omega)<\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h}]-cn\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{\log Z_{n,h+\zeta}(\omega)>\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h+\zeta}]+cn\}}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h+\zeta}]-\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h}]-\rho(h)\zeta n-\epsilon\zeta n/2+2cn}\,.

Similarly, we can state that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\omega\in\Omega

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[L_{n}<\rho(h)n-\epsilon n/2\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{1}_{\{\log Z_{n,h}(\omega)<\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h}]-\delta n\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{\log Z_{n,h-\zeta}(\omega)>\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h-\zeta}]+\delta n\}}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h-\zeta}]-\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h}]+\rho(h)\zeta n-\epsilon\zeta n/2+2cn}\,.

Integrating with respect to
\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}\omega]
, appealing to the
concentration inequality given by Theorem
3.3
, and
using that
\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h+\zeta}]\leq nf(h+\zeta)+cn
,
\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h-\zeta}]\leq nf(h-\zeta)+cn
, and
\mathbb{E}[\log Z_{n,h}]\geq nf(h)-cn
for all sufficiently large
n
, we get for those
n

(3.20)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\cdot}\big[L_{n}>\rho(h)n+\epsilon n/2\big]\Big]\leq 4\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\kappa c^{2}}{1+c}n}+\mathrm{e}^{[f(h+\zeta)-f(h)-\rho(h)\zeta-\epsilon\zeta/2+4c]n}

and

(3.21)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\cdot}\big[L_{n}<\rho(h)n-\epsilon n/2\big]\Big]\leq 4\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\kappa c^{2}}{1+c}n}+\mathrm{e}^{[f(h-\zeta)-f(h)+\rho(h)\zeta-\epsilon\zeta/2+4c]n}\,.

By construction we have
f(h+\zeta)-f(h)-\rho(h)\zeta-\epsilon\zeta/2+4c\leq-\epsilon\zeta/4
and

f(h-\zeta)-f(h)+\rho(h)\zeta-\epsilon\zeta/2+4c\leq-\epsilon\zeta/4
, so (
3.20
) and
(
3.21
) show that
(
3.19
) is demonstrated with

\gamma_{h}:=\min\{\kappa c^{2}/(1+c),\epsilon\zeta/4\}
and some
constant
G_{h}>0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

For
s\in\mathbb{N}
define
h_{s}:=-s
if
h_{c}=-\infty
and
h_{s}:=h_{c}+1/s
if

h_{c}>-\infty
and note that
h_{s}>h_{c}
,

\lim_{s\uparrow\infty}h_{s}=h_{c}
, and

\lim_{s\uparrow\infty}(h_{s}+2s)=+\infty
. According to Lemma

3.9
there exist constants
\gamma_{s}>0
and

G_{s}>0
such that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}

(3.22)

\sup_{h\in[h_{s},h_{s}+2s]}\mathsf{P}_{j,h,\cdot}\big[|L_{j}-\rho(h)j|>j/s\big]\leq\Gamma_{s,j}

with a non-negative measurable function
\Gamma_{s,j}
on
\Omega

that satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{s,j}]\leq G_{s}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{s}j}
. The
random variable
\Lambda_{s}:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{s}j/2}\,\Gamma_{s,j}
possesses finite expectation, so Birkhoff’s
ergodic theorem assures us that there exists a set

\Omega_{o}\in\mathcal{F}
with
\mathbb{P}[\Omega_{o}]=1
such that for all

\omega\in\Omega_{o}
and
s\in\mathbb{N}

(3.23)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{s}(\vartheta^{i}\omega)<+\infty\,.

According to Lemma
3.7
we can also suppose that

\Omega_{o}
has the following property: for every
\omega\in\Omega_{o}
,
compact set
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
, and
\gamma>0
there exists a
constant
c>0
(independent of
\omega
) such that

(3.24)

\adjustlimits{\limsup}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}\,n^{\gamma}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\big[M_{n}>c\log n\big]<+\infty\,.

Fix
\omega\in\Omega
,
H\subset(h_{c},+\infty)
compact, and

\gamma,\epsilon>0
. Also fix an arbitrary sequence

\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive numbers such that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\zeta_{n}=+\infty
. Let
c>0
be as in
(
3.24
) and put
m_{n}:=\lfloor c\log n\rfloor
for
brevity. Let
n_{o}
be a number so large that
4(1+\epsilon)m_{n}+2\leq\epsilon\zeta_{n}\log n
for all integers
n>n_{o}
. For every integer

n>n_{o}
and
h\in H
we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\Bigg[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[M_{n}>c\log n]

(3.25)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\}}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon,\,M_{n}\leq m_{n}\bigg]\,.

Let us elaborate the terms in the double sum. Under the conditions

X_{0}=X_{n}=1
and
M_{n}\leq m_{n}
we have the identities

\sum_{i^{\prime}=\max\{0,i-m_{n}+1\}}^{i}X_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{k=i^{\prime}+1}^{i}(1-X_{k})=\sum_{j^{\prime}=j}^{\min\{j+m_{n}-1,n\}}\prod_{k=j}^{j^{\prime}-1}(1-X_{k})X_{j^{\prime}}=1\,,

so for
0\leq i<j\leq n
with
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n
we can write down

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon,\,M_{n}\leq m_{n}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}=\max\{0,i-m_{n}+1\}}^{i}\sum_{j^{\prime}=j}^{\min\{j+m_{n}-1,n\}}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\Bigg[X_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{k=i^{\prime}+1}^{i}(1-X_{k})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\mathds{1}_{\big\{\big|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\big|>\epsilon\big\}}\prod_{k=j}^{j^{\prime}-1}(1-X_{k})X_{j^{\prime}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}=\max\{0,i-m_{n}+1\}}^{i}\sum_{j^{\prime}=j}^{\min\{j+m_{n}-1,n\}}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[X_{i^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\big\{\big|\frac{1}{j-i}\left(\sum_{a=i^{\prime}+1}^{j^{\prime}-1}X_{a}+\mathds{1}_{\{j^{\prime}=j\}}\right)-\rho(h)\big|>\epsilon\big\}}X_{j^{\prime}}\bigg]\,.

Now we note that

|\sum_{a=i^{\prime}+1}^{j^{\prime}-1}X_{a}+\mathds{1}_{\{j^{\prime}=j\}}-\rho(h)(j-i)|>\epsilon(j-i)

with
i-m_{n}<i^{\prime}\leq i
and
j<j^{\prime}\leq j+m_{n}
implies

|\sum_{a=i^{\prime}+1}^{j^{\prime}}X_{a}-\rho(h)(j^{\prime}-i^{\prime})|>\epsilon(j^{\prime}-i^{\prime})-2(1+\epsilon)m_{n}-1
, where we have used that
0\leq\rho(h)\leq 1
. But for the
integers
n>n_{o}
the condition
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n
entails

4(1+\epsilon)m_{n}+2\leq\epsilon\zeta_{n}\log n\leq\epsilon(j-i)\leq\epsilon(j^{\prime}-i^{\prime})
. In conclusion, we can state that for

n>n_{o}
and
0\leq i<j\leq n
with
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon,\,M_{n}\leq m_{n}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}=\max\{0,i-m_{n}+1\}}^{i}\sum_{j^{\prime}=j}^{\min\{j+m_{n}-1,n\}}\mathsf{E}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[X_{i^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\big\{\big|\sum_{a=i^{\prime}+1}^{j^{\prime}}X_{a}-\rho(h)(j^{\prime}-i^{\prime})\big|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\epsilon(j^{\prime}-i^{\prime})\big\}}X_{j^{\prime}}\bigg]\,.

At this point, two applications of (
3.2
) give

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon,\,M_{n}\leq m_{n}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}=\max\{0,i-m_{n}+1\}}^{i}\sum_{j^{\prime}=j+1}^{\min\{j+m_{n},n\}}\mathsf{P}_{j^{\prime}-i^{\prime},h,\vartheta^{i^{\prime}}\omega}\Big[\big|L_{j^{\prime}-i^{\prime}}-\rho(h)(j^{\prime}-i^{\prime})\big|>\epsilon(j^{\prime}-i^{\prime})/2\Big]\,.

Combining this bound with (
3.25
) we finally deduce
that for all integers

n>n_{o}
and
h\in H

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\Bigg[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[M_{n}>c\log n]

(3.26)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+m_{n}^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathds{1}_{\{j\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\}}\mathsf{P}_{j,h,\vartheta^{i}\omega}\Big[\big|L_{j}-\rho(h)j\big|>\epsilon j/2\Big]\,.

For the last step we pick
s\in\mathbb{N}
so large that

H\subseteq[h_{s},h_{s}+2s]
and
\epsilon/2\geq 1/s
. Bounds
(
3.26
) and (
3.22
) show that for
all integers
n>n_{o}

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in H}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\Bigg[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{h\in H}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[M_{n}>c\log n]+m_{n}^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathds{1}_{\{j\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\}}\Gamma_{s,j}(\vartheta^{i}\omega)\,,

and a Chernoff–type bound, together with the definition

\Lambda_{s}:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{s}j/2}\,\Gamma_{s,j}
, yields

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in H}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\Bigg[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{h\in H}\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}[M_{n}>c\log n]+m_{n}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{s}\zeta_{n}\log n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{s}(\vartheta^{i}\omega)\,.

From here we find

\adjustlimits{\lim}_{n\uparrow\infty}{\sup}_{h\in H}n^{\gamma}\,\mathsf{P}_{n,h,\omega}\Bigg[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
j-i\geq\zeta_{n}\log n\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{1}{j-i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{j}X_{a}-\rho(h)\bigg|>\epsilon\Bigg]<+\infty

thanks to (
3.23
) and (
3.24
) and
thanks to the fact that
\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\zeta_{n}=+\infty
.
∎

4.
Generalizations

4.1.
The soft conditioning case

The findings about sharp conditioning, i.e., Proposition

1.4
and Theorem
1.7
, allow easily to
obtain similar results for a soft conditioning that restores the role
of the parameter
h
. Here we discuss this issue for the disordered
model on the basis of Proposition
1.2
, which is used to
restrict the range of the contact number, and Theorem

1.7
. The pure model is not considered for conciseness,
but the same arguments based on Propositions
1.2
and

1.4
apply to it. The following proposition
exemplifies two forms of soft conditioning.

Proposition 4.1.

4.2.

we can state that

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}\big[M_{n}\geq z_{n}-\epsilon n,\,S_{l_{n}}=n\big]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{l_{n}}\mathsf{P}\big[T_{i}\geq z_{n}-\epsilon n,\,S_{l_{n}}=n\big]

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{l_{n}-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{l_{n}}\mathsf{P}\Big[T_{i}\geq z_{n}-n\epsilon,\,T_{j}\geq z_{n}-\epsilon n,\,S_{l_{n}}=n\Big]

\displaystyle\geq l_{n}\mathsf{P}\big[T_{l_{n}}\geq z_{n}-\epsilon l_{n},\,S_{l_{n}}=n\big]

(A.12)

\displaystyle\quad-l_{n}^{2}\mathsf{P}\Big[T_{l_{n}-1}\geq z_{n}/2,\,T_{l_{n}}\geq z_{n}/2,\,S_{l_{n}}=n\Big]\,.

The last term in (
A.12
) can be bound as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}\Big[T_{l_{n}-1}\geq z_{n}/2,\,T_{l_{n}}\geq z_{n}/2,\,S_{l_{n}}=n\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}}\mathds{1}_{\{t^{\prime}\geq z_{n}/2\}}\mathds{1}_{\{t^{\prime\prime}\geq z_{n}/2\}}p(t^{\prime})p(t^{\prime\prime})\mathsf{P}\big[S_{l_{n}-2}=n-t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Big[\sup_{z\geq z_{n}/2}p(z)\Big]^{2}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}(t-1)\mathsf{P}\big[S_{l_{n}-2}=n-t\big]\leq n\Big[\sup_{z\geq z_{n}/2}p(z)\Big]^{2}\,.

So, using that
\sup_{z\geq z_{n}/2}p(z)\sim p(z_{n}/2)\sim 2^{\alpha+1}p(z_{n})=(2/z_{n})^{\alpha+1}\ell(z_{n})
(see
[
7
, Theorems
1.5.3 and 1.5.2]
), we see that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\frac{l_{n}\mathsf{P}[T_{l_{n}-1}\geq z_{n}/2,\,T_{l_{n}}\geq z_{n}/2,\,S_{l_{n}}=n]}{p(z_{n})}=0

because either
\alpha>1
and
\ell(z)\leq z^{(\alpha-1)/2}
for all
sufficiently large
z
(see
[
7
, Proposition 1.3.6]
) or

\alpha=1
and
\lim_{z\uparrow+\infty}\ell(z)=0
as

\mathsf{E}[T_{1}]<+\infty
. In this way, in order to prove
(
A.10
) from (
A.12
) it remains to
show that

(A.13)

\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}\frac{\mathsf{P}[T_{l_{n}}\geq z_{n}-\epsilon l_{n},\,S_{l_{n}}=n]}{p(z_{n})}\geq 1\,.

To this aim, pick a number
\delta>1
and, recalling that
z_{n}\geq 2\epsilon n
, note that
z_{n}+2\epsilon(\delta-1)l_{n}\leq\delta z_{n}
. For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}\big[T_{l_{n}}\geq z_{n}-\epsilon l_{n},\,S_{l_{n}}=n\big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq z_{n}-\epsilon l_{n}\}}p(t)\mathsf{P}\big[S_{l_{n}-1}=n-t\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\mathds{1}_{\{z_{n}-\epsilon l_{n}\leq t\leq z_{n}+2\epsilon(\delta-1)l_{n}\}}p(t)\mathsf{P}\big[S_{l_{n}-1}=n-t\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\mathsf{P}\Big[n-z_{n}-2\epsilon(\delta-1)l_{n}\leq S_{l_{n}-1}\leq n-z_{n}+\epsilon l_{n}\Big]\inf_{z\in[1,\delta z_{n}]}p(z)\,.

The law of large numbers yields

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\mathsf{P}[l_{n}/\rho_{c}-2\epsilon(\delta-1)l_{n}\leq S_{l_{n}-1}\leq l_{n}/\rho_{c}+\epsilon l_{n}]=1
, so using that

\inf_{z\in[1,\delta z_{n}]}p(z)\sim p(\delta z_{n})\sim p(z_{n})/\delta^{\alpha+1}
(see
[
7
, Theorems 1.5.3 and
1.5.2]
) we find

\liminf_{n\uparrow\infty}\frac{\mathsf{P}[T_{l_{n}}\geq z_{n}-\epsilon l_{n},\,S_{l_{n}}=n]}{p(z_{n})}\geq\frac{1}{\delta^{\alpha+1}}\,.

This demonstrates (
A.13
) because
\delta
can be
taken arbitrarily close to 1.
