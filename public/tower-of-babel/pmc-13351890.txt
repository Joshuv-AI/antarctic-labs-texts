A high amylose to amylopectin ratio in a low-protein diet promoted growth performance and protein deposition of breast muscle in geese
Authors: Xucheng Zheng, Zhi Yang, Xiyuan Xing, Xuan Li, Wenfeng Liu, Usman Nazir, Haiming Yang, Zhiyue Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This experiment was conducted to investigate the effects of different dietary protein levels and amylose to amylopectin (AM/AP) ratios on the growth performance and muscle development of geese. A total of 240 healthy male Jiangnan White geese (body weight [BW] 2124.8 ± 91.4 g, 35-d-old) were randomly assigned to 4 groups, with 6 replicates per group and 10 birds per replicate. The experimental period was 21 d. The 2 × 2 factorial design included two protein levels (16% and 14%) and two AM/AP ratios (0.34 and 0.44). The 4 groups were: control group, normal protein (16%) with normal AM/AP ratio (0.34) (NPNR); normal protein (16%) with high AM/AP ratio (0.44) (NPHR); low protein (14%) with normal AM/AP ratio (0.34) (LPNR); and low protein (14%) with high AM/AP ratio (0.44) (LPHR). The results showed that compared with normal protein diet, low protein diet increased average daily feed intake (ADFI; P = 0.008). Increasing the AM/AP ratio increased BW of geese at 56 d (P = 0.003), improved average daily weight gain (ADG; P = 0.003) and decreased feed-to-gain ratio (F/G; P = 0.009). Increasing the AM/AP ratio led to an increase in crude protein content in the breast muscle (P = 0.015), also enriched the amino acids composition in the liver and muscles, specifically elevated the Lys content in the liver (P = 0.004) and leg muscle (P = 0.031), and Glu, Leu, Lys, and Pro contents in breast muscles (P < 0.05). Both a low protein diet and a high AM/AP ratio increased short-term breast muscle protein deposition. High AM/AP ratio diets accelerated the protein fractional synthesis rate (P = 0.036) and fractional growth rate (P = 0.002) in breast muscles. Antagonistic interactions between AMPK and mTOR pathways were observed. Compared with the control group, the LPHR group showed down-regulation of AMPKα1 mRNA (P = 0.020) and up-regulation of mTOR mRNA in the breast muscle (P = 0.047). High AM/AP ratio in the diets induced hypertrophy and thickening of leg muscle fibers. In conclusion, increasing the AM/AP ratio in a low-protein diet promoted protein deposition in breast muscles and benefited geese growth.

Introduction

Low-protein diets have been shown to mitigate environmental impact by decreasing nitrogen excretion (Belloir et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017). Additionally, they conserve water, thereby reducing litter moisture and the incidence of footpad dermatitis for female broiler breeders (Li et al., 2018). A key challenge in reducing dietary protein levels is maintaining growth performance and physiological functions in poultry.

Animals' demand for protein is essentially a demand for amino acids (Wu, 2014). However, amino acid wastage remains a concern due to asynchronous digestion and absorption, as well as oxidation for energy in the intestines (Liu and Selle, 2015; Wang et al., 2019). Feed-grade amino acids differ fundamentally from protein-bound amino acids in digestive dynamics (Selle and Liu, 2019). The absorption rate of feed-grade amino acids in the intestines is nearly 100% (Wu, 2009); however their rapid absorption may lead to losses of protein-bound amino acids and reduce nitrogen utilization (Liu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2018).

Starch is the primary source of energy in poultry diets. The small intestine, as the main digestive organ of poultry, has high energy demands, partly due to the rapid renewal of epithelial cells to maintain its function (Macelline et al., 2021; Van Der Schoor et al., 2002). Dietary starch and protein are broken down in the small intestine into glucose and amino acids, respectively, which are then metabolized by intestinal cells to produce energy. Glucose has a higher energy supply efficiency than amino acids, but in most cases, amino acids (especially Gln and Glu) often serve as the main energy source in the intestine (Dudley et al., 1998; He et al., 2021), leading to amino acids wastage.

The concept of starch-protein digestive dynamics provides a framework to explain the impaired growth performance observed under extremely low-protein diets and how it might be remedied. This concept links the synchronized availability of glucose and amino acids to intestinal energy metabolism and, ultimately, to whole-body protein accretion (Liu and Selle, 2017). Amylose cannot be degraded or degraded very slowly by intestinal amylase and is mainly digested in the posterior end of the small intestine (Bello-Perez et al., 2018). Dietary amylose supplementation reduces endogenous amino acid losses, promoting amino acid entry into the portal circulation for protein synthesis (Zhou et al., 2023). Different grains have different amylose to amylopectin (AM/AP) ratios. The AM/AP ratio of glutinous rice and cassavais is approximately 0.02 to 0.20 (Yang et al., 2022), corn and wheat is approximately 0.32 to 0.35 (Pinheiro et al., 2013), and indica rice and peas is approximately 0.45 to 0.54 (Li et al., 2017). Some experiments have revealed the effectiveness of slowly digestible starch (high AM/AP ratio) in digestive dynamics (Herwig et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2019).

This experiment utilized low-protein diets supplemented only with L-Lys and DL-Met, adjusting the starch structure to investigate the effects of a high dietary AM/AP ratio on growth performance and muscle development in geese from 35 to 56 d of age. The aim was to provide practical insights into goose meat production.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All animal care and experimental procedures in the study were performed according to the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals of the People's Republic of China and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Yangzhou University (No. 202403131).

Experimental diets and design

A total of 240 healthy commercial male Jiangnan White geese with similar body weight (BW; 2124.8 ± 91.4 g) at 35 d of age, supplied by Changzhou Siji Poultry Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, Jiangsu, China), were randomly divided into 4 groups, each with 6 replicates of 10 geese. The experiment was designed as a 2 × 2 factorial array of dietary treatments with two protein levels (16% and 14%) and two AM/AP ratios (0.34 and 0.44). The dietary composition and nutrient levels are shown in Table 1. High-amylose corn starch (74.6% amylose and 25.4% amylopectin) supplied by Shanghai Quanwang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), was used to replace grain in diets, increasing AM/AP ratio while avoiding the influence of different protein sources on the experiment. The AM/AP ratios of corn and wheat bran were 0.34 and 0.93, respectively. Soybean meal and rice husk are almost starch-free, so their AM/AP ratios were negligible. The amylose and amylopectin content in the diets were determined using colorimetric assay kits (A152-1-1 for amylose and A152-2-1 for amylopectin, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Amylopectin can be separated from starch by 80% ethanol and determined by dual-wavelength colorimetry. The determination of crude protein and crude fiber followed GB/T 14924.9-2001 (China National Standard, 2001), and the determination of calcium followed GB/T 6436-2018 (China National Standard, 2018). The apparent metabolizable energy (AME) of feed was analyzed by tube feeding and all excreta collected method (McNab and Blair, 1988). Geese were raised on plastic nets throughout the experiment (single pen area: 1.9 m × 1.5 m, 2.85 m2), and the stocking density of geese was 4 birds/m2. The ambient temperature was around 25 °C. Birds had ad libitum access to water and feed under natural light. The experimental period was 21 d.

Data and sample collection, chemical analyses, and calculations

At the end of the experiment (56 d), one bird was randomly selected from each replicate and slaughtered. The entire right liver, breast muscle, and leg muscle were collected and stored at −40 °C for routine nutrient determination. A section of left tissue was stored in 4% paraformaldehyde for morphological observation. A small number of liver and muscle samples were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −70 °C for the determination of amino acid content.

Growth performance

All geese were weighed after fasting for 6 h at 35 and 56 d to calculate 56 d BW and average daily gain (ADG). The feed intake was recorded weekly to calculate average daily feed intake (ADFI) and the feed-to-gain ratio (F/G).

Nutrient composition in liver and muscles

Moisture (method 925.09; using a DHG-9246A Electro-thermal Blast Air Drying Oven, Shanghai Jing Hong Laboratory Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), CP (method 990.03; using a KN620 Automatic Kjeldahl Nitrogen Analyzer, Jinan Alva Instruments Co., Ltd. [ALVA], Jinan, Shandong, China), and crude fat (method 920.39; using a RD40 Fat Extraction System, OMNILAB-LABORZENTRUM GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany) content in liver and muscle were analyzed according to proximate analysis methods (AOAC, 2023), and amino acid (LA8080 High-Speed Amino Acid Analyzer, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) content was determined by following GB 5009.124-2016 (China National Standard, 2016).

Protein turnover in muscles

At 54 d, after feeding the experimental diets for 3.5 h, one bird was selected from each replicate according to the average BW and injected with L-[15N]-Phe solution (120 μmol/mL) through the jugular vein using a one-time large-dose injection method. The injection dose was 1 mL/100 g BW. After the injection of the isotope tracer for 15 min, the birds were slaughtered. The liver, breast muscle, and leg muscle were excised and weighed. Small samples of each tissue were quickly collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −70 °C for analysis of protein relative fractional synthesis rate (FSR). The time from the end of injection to sample freezing was recorded accurately. The abundance of L-[15N]-Phe in free and protein-bound pools of tissue samples was determined following Krebs et al. (2023).FSR%/d=EBound×1440×100%/Efreet×t,where t is the time from the end of injection to sample freezing; EBound is the abundance of L-[15N]-Phe in the protein-bound pool of incoming tissues (mol, %); Efree t is the abundance of L-[15N]-Phe in the free pool at time t (mol, %); and 1440 min = 1 d.

Two days before and after the isotope injection experiment (at 52 and 56 d), one bird per replicate, selected based on average BW, was slaughtered. The liver, breast muscle, and leg muscle were excised and weighed, and their CP content was determined for the analysis of the protein fractional growth rate (FGR).

FGR (%/d) = Protein deposition/Average amount of protein × 100;

Protein deposition = (Tissue weight at 56 d × CP% – Tissue weight at 52 d × CP%)/4;

Average amount of protein = (Tissue weight at 56 d × CP% + Tissue weight at 52 d × CP%)/2.

The formula for protein fractional degradation rate (FDR) is as follows:

FDR = FSR – FGR.

The formula for calculating relative muscle weight is as follows:

Relative weight (%) = Absolute muscle weight (g)/Live BW (g) × 100.

Muscle fiber characteristics

The right breast and leg muscles of geese were collected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and then subjected to drying, dehydration, embedding, sectioning, staining, and mounting. Optical microscope (Eclipse Ci-L, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was used to select the target muscle tissue area for imaging at 100 × magnification. After imaging, Image-Pro Plus 6.0 analysis software (Media Cybernetics, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) analysis software was employed to measure the number of transverse muscle fibers within three fields of view per section, using 1 μm as the standard unit, and to calculate muscle fiber diameter and density.

Statistical analysis

The data for all parameters were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The general linear model procedure in SPSS 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used, and Fisher's least significant difference (LSD) test was employed for multiple comparisons. The statistical model was as follows:Yijk=μ+αi+βj+(αβ)ij+εijk,where Yijk is the observed value for the k-th replicate under the i-th level of factor A and the j-th level of factor B; μ is the overall population mean; αi is the fixed effect of the i-th level of the first factor (Factor A); βj is the fixed effect of the j-th level of the second factor (Factor B); (αβ)ij is the interaction effect between the i-th level of Factor A and the j-th level of Factor B; εijk is the random experimental error term. The error terms (εijk) were independent of each other and followed a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a constant variance (εijk ∼ N [0, σ2]). Statistical significance is considered at P < 0.05 for all analyses. Data are presented as means and standard error of the mean (SEM). The results of relative gene expression were analyzed by one-way ANOVA and figures were created using Graph-Pad Prism 8 (Graph Pad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) software.

Discussion

It was foreseeable that reducing dietary protein levels would not impair the growth of geese in the present experiment, consistent with Liang et al. (2023) where low-protein diets without essential amino acid supplementation (except L-Lys and DL-Met) did not reduce weight gain. As herbivorous birds, geese are less protein-sensitive than chickens or ducks (Zheng et al., 2025). Their digestive system efficiently utilizes high-fiber and low-protein plant feed. Geese naturally require less protein and derive nutrients partly from microbial cellulose fermentation. Under the condition that energy and key limiting amino acids (Met and Lys) levels are the same, 14% protein might be closer to the “optimal protein level” for geese during the fattening phase. Under a high-protein diet (16%), geese consume more amino acids than needed. Excess amino acids cannot be stored and must be deaminated and converted into energy or fat. This process generates a higher heat increment, meaning the effective energy utilization rate decreases. When the protein level is reduced to a level closer to the actual requirement (14%), unnecessary deamination and metabolic costs are reduced, thus improving net energy utilization and promoting weight gain.

Although a 14% protein diet may be close to the optimal requirement for geese, this low level can still be insufficient to meet their specific amino acid needs. Consequently, geese often compensate by increasing feed intake to secure an adequate amino acid supply for growth and vital functions.

Xu et al. (2023) investigated the effects of different starch source diets on the growth and glucose metabolism of geese. The results showed that corn (AM/AP ratio = 0.340) and indica rice (AM/AP ratio = 0.456) diets improved the growth performance of geese. Glutinous rice (AM/AP ratio = 0.021) diet led to the rapid release of glucose, enhanced liver glycogen synthesis, and further influenced lipid metabolism. The present experiment used a combination of corn and high-amylose corn starch to simulate the indica rice diet and achieved satisfactory results. Increasing the AM/AP ratios in the diets had a positive effect on the production performance of geese, with ADG improving by 4.51% and F/G decreasing by 3.45%. This improvement is likely attributable to the sustained and stable intestinal release of glucose from amylose, optimizing starch and protein digestibility (Li et al., 2017).

The benefits of slowly digestible starch on poultry growth have been highlighted; however, this does not imply that rapidly digestible starch has the opposite effect. In the early growth stage, poultry require rapid release of glucose to supply energy for organ development. Luo et al. (2025) demonstrated that waxy corn starch enhanced the BW of broilers aged 0 to 3 weeks and improved intestinal health. Compared to corn starch diets, waxy corn starch diets significantly increased the apparent digestibility of amino acids. This indicated that rapidly digestible starch diets could improve the growth performance of broilers by enhancing the digestion and absorption of amino acids, reducing the oxidative energy supply of amino acids in the intestinal mucosa, and promoting muscle protein synthesis. Another study (Li et al., 2024) revealed that decreasing the AM/AP ratio in the diet of Hy-Line Gray laying hens improved the average egg weight and feed conversion rate, with linear increases in amino acid apparent digestibility, apparent metabolic energy, and intestinal villus area. Therefore, when selecting different starch sources, factors such as age, variety, and practical production requirements should be considered.

The liver is highly active in protein and amino acid metabolism. By 52 d, the liver of Jiangnan White goose is essentially developed with mature amino acid transport. Under experimental conditions, the high AM/AP group exhibited enriched tissue amino acid composition, particularly increased Glu, Lys, and Pro content in muscles. This indicated that a high amylose diet could prevent the breakdown of amino acids in the intestine, thereby providing energy (Moss et al., 2018). Consequently, these amino acids entered the portal vein and reached the liver to contribute to protein synthesis. In geese fed low-protein diets, the concentrations of Thr and Phe in the liver increased. Thr metabolism in the liver is involved in several key physiological processes, including gluconeogenesis and immune function (Qin et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2021). Phe is an aromatic amino acid that participates in the synthesis of Thr, which is crucial for growth and nervous system function (Fernstrom and Fernstrom, 2007). Therefore, under a low-protein condition, these amino acids may be preferentially regulated to ensure essential physiological functions.

Glu is one of the preferred energy substrates used by the intestines. When the AM/AP ratio increases and glucose provides a more sustained energy supply, the intestinal mucosa's oxidative demand for Glu/Gln decreases, allowing more Glu to be “spared” and enter the portal vein. Therefore, it is reasonable that the Glu content in the breast muscles is higher. Pro is closely linked to Glu metabolism (it can be converted from Glu through related pathways). Leu is a branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) and an important nutritional signal for mTOR. Therefore, the increase in Leu in the breast muscles may reflect “better supply/absorption” and is consistent with its role in promoting anabolic signaling. Leg muscle BCAAs (Leu, Ile, and Val) responded strongly, showing a significant protein level × AM/AP ratio interaction with the LPHR group exhibiting the highest concentrations. Compared to the breast muscles (which are primarily composed of fast-twitch muscle fibers and rely on glycolysis), the leg muscles contain a higher proportion of oxidative or intermediate muscle fibers, have a higher mitochondrial density, and possess greater oxidative metabolic capacity (Petracci and Cavani, 2011). The primary site of BCAAs breakdown is skeletal muscle (not the liver), and their transamination and subsequent oxidation mainly occur in muscle mitochondria. Therefore, when energy supply or nutrient availability changes, the BCAAs content in the leg muscles, rather than the breast muscles, is often the first and most significantly affected (Mann et al., 2021).

Protein turnover reflects the dynamic changes in animal proteins, and the protein deposition rate, determined by FSR and FDR, plays an important role in animal growth and development (Weng et al., 2025). Under normal nutritional conditions, muscle is the largest protein reservoir in animals, accounting for 40% to 45% of the total body protein (Wu, 2009), and largely determines the overall protein deposition. In the protein turnover experiment, breast muscles showed significant differences among treatments, whereas leg muscles were almost unaffected. According to the growth and developmental pattern of Jiangnan White geese (Xing et al., 2026), leg muscle develops earlier and faster than breast muscle. By week 8, the leg muscle is almost fully developed, whereas the breast muscle is still in a phase of rapid growth. The protein turnover experiment was conducted from 52 to 56 d; therefore, during this period, different dietary treatments had a greater impact on breast muscle development but had little effect on leg muscle (Tesseraud et al., 2000).

Changes in weight gain tended to be consistent with muscle protein deposition in this experiment, consistent with Weng et al. (2025). Thus, the hypothesis that a high AM/AP ratio diet promotes muscle protein deposition in geese was verified. Similar effects have been observed in mammals as well. For example, Doti et al. (2014) reported that feeding pea starch to growing pigs induced a gradual postprandial increase in blood glucose, resulting in sustained insulin release and enhanced lean tissue deposition. Interestingly, low-protein diets unexpectedly accelerated the synthesis and deposition of muscle protein in the breast of geese. This might be due to the short-term nature of the experiment, and further in-depth research will be conducted on this phenomenon. Research by Remignon et al. (1995) showed that the total number of muscle fibers in birds was determined before hatching, and the increase in muscle mass depended on the lengthening and thickening of existing muscle fibers. The diameter of muscle fibers was significantly positively correlated with BW. The enlargement and thickening of muscle fibers increased muscle mass and increased animal weight. The results of the present experiment were consistent with this view.

There is a view that protein degradation is more important than protein synthesis in muscle development (Macelline et al., 2025; Tesseraud et al., 2000). However, the dietary treatments in this experiment had a greater impact on FSR than on FDR, which may be related to differences in breeding status between broilers and geese. Broilers exhibit rapid muscle growth, with protein synthesis approaching its maximal rate; therefore, dietary interventions primarily influence protein degradation. In contrast, the breeding work for geese is still underdeveloped. The breast muscle percentage at the time of geese marketing does not exceed 10% (while broilers are close to 20%), and the muscle growth in geese is slower. Therefore, there is still significant potential for improving the muscle synthesis speed during the production cycle of geese. Besides, from a mechanistic perspective, the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway regulates protein degradation (Li et al., 2011; Rostagno et al., 2007), and the response mechanisms of poultry with different growth rates to the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway may differ. Saneyasu et al. (2015) reported that, regarding skeletal muscle protein degradation, there are significant differences between fast-growing broilers and regular layer chicks in the expression of genes related to the regulation of the ubiquitin-proteasome system, such as ubiquitin ligases. Future research should focus on comparative studies between broilers and geese.

An increased proportion of amylose tended to make starch digestion and glucose release steadier and more sustained, attenuating short postprandial peaks that impact energy metabolism. This helped maintain a more favorable cellular energy charge (ATP/AMP) and reduced energy-deficit signaling, thereby weakening AMPK activation (Sorrenti et al., 2022). In the LPHR group, AMPK was suppressed partly through improved energy status; additionally, greater peripheral availability of amino acids, together with moderate insulin/IGF-1 signaling, likely co-activated the mTOR pathway (Luo et al., 2023; Yoon, 2017), thereby accelerating translation initiation and elongation and increasing protein deposition and yield in skeletal muscles such as the breast.

Conclusion

In conclusion, for growing geese from 35 to 56 d, reducing dietary CP to 14% while supplementing only L-Lys and DL-Met, did not compromise growth. Incorporating a high AM/AP ratio (0.44) further enhanced weight gain, feed efficiency, and breast muscle protein deposition, particularly under the low-protein conditions. These findings demonstrate that optimizing dietary starch structure is an effective nutritional strategy to improve nitrogen utilization and support muscle yield in geese fed reduced-protein diets.
