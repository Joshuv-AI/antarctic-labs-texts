Abstract
. We consider the class of Hopfield models of associative memory with activation function F F and state space { − 1 , 1 } N \{-1,1\}^{N} , where each vertex of the cube describes a configuration of N N binary neurons. M M randomly chosen configurations, called patterns, are stored using an energy function designed to make them local minima. If they are, which is known to depend on how M M scales with N N , then they can be retrieved using a dynamics that decreases the energy. However, storing the patterns in the energy function also creates unintended local minima, and thus false memories. Although this has been known since the earliest work on the subject, it has only been supported by numerical simulations and non-rigorous calculations, except in elementary cases. Our results are twofold. For a generic function F F , we explicitly construct a set of configurations, called mixed memories, whose properties are intended to characterise the local minima of the energy function. For three prominent models, namely the classical, the dense and the modern Hopfield models, obtained for quadratic, polynomial and exponential functions F F respectively, we give conditions on the growth rate of M M which guarantee that, as N N diverges, mixed memories are fixed points of the retrieval dynamics and thus minima of the energy. We conjecture that in this regime, all local minima are mixed memories.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Hopfield models

Written in the mathematical framework of statistical mechanics, Hopfield networks are a family of dynamical models of associative memory that find their origin in the Hebbian theory of learning. Pioneered over forty years ago
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
they have had a profound influence on several scientific disciplines, from physics to contemporary machine learning, as has recently been recognised
[
40
]
.

In their simplest form, such models consist of
N
binary neurons taking values in
\{-1,1\}
. Possible
configurations
of the memory then are vertices
\sigma=(\sigma_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}\in\Sigma_{N}
of the
N
-dimensional discrete cube
\Sigma_{N}=\{-1,1\}^{N}
, and the objects to be memorised are
M
specific configurations
\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{M}
in
\Sigma_{N}
, called
patterns
.
Given a smooth function
F:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, the patterns are stored through an
energy function

E_{N,M}
, defined on
\Sigma_{N}
by

E_{N,M}(\sigma)=-\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}F\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\sigma_{i}\right),\quad\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}.

(1.1)

Thus
E_{N,M}(\sigma)
depends on the patterns only through their
overlap
with
\sigma
,
N^{-1}\sum_{i}\xi^{\mu}_{i}\sigma_{i}
.
The guiding idea is to choose the
activation function

F
in such a way that each pattern lies in a deep (ideally global) minimum of an energy valley of
E_{N,M}
and is surrounded by high energy barriers. An associative memory is then obtained by setting up a dynamics that decreases the energy so that, starting from a configuration that resembles a given pattern,
the dynamics converges to that pattern. One cannot hope that such properties will hold for every possible choice of patterns, but rather for a choice that is considered typical. This can be achieved by selecting the patterns at random. For this purpose, we let
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
be a probability space on which is defined a doubly infinite sequence

(\xi^{\mu}_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}},\mu\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of independent and identically distributed Bernoulli random variables, which satisfy

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\xi^{\mu}_{i}=1\right)=1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\xi^{\mu}_{i}=-1\right)=1/2.

(1.2)

The patterns are thus uncorrelated and unbiased.
Note that the energy function
E_{N,M}
now is a random function defined on
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
.

Several choices of the function
F
have been considered in the literature. In the 1982 paper introducing the now
classical
Hopfield model
[
17
]
,
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
. This choice was soon generalised to any polynomial of degree
p
in analogy to the
p
-spin models of statistical mechanics
[
32
]
,
[
29
]
. Three decades later, a series of papers
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
brought the case
F(x)=\frac{1}{p}x^{p}
back into the limelight under the name of
dense
Hopfield model. Concomitantly, a
modern
Hopfield model was proposed in
[
15
]
, where
F(x)=e^{Nx}
.

Each of these models is effectively a whole class of models parametrised by
M
. An issue of great practical and theoretical importance is that of
memory (or storage) capacity
,
i.e.
the maximum number
M
of patterns that a model can store and reliably retrieve. Using computer simulations, it was found in
[
17
]
that the memory capacity of the classical Hopfield model scales linearly with the number of neurons,
N
, and that memory is completely lost beyond
\sim 0.15N
.
Recent interest in dense and modern models stems from the fact that their memory capacity grows much faster, polynomially and exponentially in
N
respectively. We return to this topic in Section
1.2.2
.

This intriguing transition, together with the strong similarity between these models and mean-field models of disordered systems has sparked great interest among theoretical physicists and mathematicians working in statistical mechanics,
[
28
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
38
]
,
[
39
]
(see also the references therein). In this context, the key object of interest is the Gibbs measure associated to
E_{N,M}
. This is the random probability measure defined on
\Sigma_{N}
by

G_{\beta,N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}}e^{\beta NE_{N,M}(\sigma)},\quad\sigma\in\Sigma_{N},

where
\beta>0
is a parameter that physically represents the inverse of a temperature and

Z_{\beta,n}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}e^{\beta NE_{N,M}(\sigma)}
. An important first step towards understanding the asymptotic properties of
G_{\beta,N}
for large
N
is to compute the limiting free energy

f_{\beta}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\beta N}\log Z_{\beta,N}.

In a seminal paper, Amit
et al.

[
2
]
obtained a complete picture of the phase diagram of the classical Hopfield model at all temperatures using the non rigorous replica trick to compute the free energy. The loss of memory occurs at
M\sim 0.138N
and is linked to the appearance of a spin glass phase of the same nature as that found by Parisi
[
31
]
in the paradigmatic Sherrington and Kirkpatrick (SK) model of a mean-field spin glass
[
36
]
. Several aspects of their results have been understood with mathematical rigour (see
[
10
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
37
]
, Chap. 4 in
[
38
]
and Chap. 10 in
[
39
]
for the most advanced results, and references therein). All these results concern the static properties of the models and are governed, more or less explicitly, by the property that the global minima of the energy
E_{N,M}
lie at or near the patterns.

Clearly, the effective functioning of an associative memory depends not only on the properties of the deepest minima of its energy landscape, but more generally on the entire structure of its critical points, with local minima being fixed points of deterministic gradient descent dynamics or giving rise to metastable states in random dynamics
[
7
]
. In particular, it has been known since the first work on the subject that the process of storing the patterns in the energy function also creates spurious, unintended memories that overlap with multiple patterns and can be retrieved by the dynamics just like the patterns themselves
[
19
]
. Several strategies have been developed to “unlearn”
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
3
]
(see also the references therein) or to mitigate the effects of these spurious memories
[
24
]
,
[
23
]
, mostly by modifying the energy function. However, except for the simplest cases
[
21
]
,
[
12
]
, the literature offers only an empirical understanding of these extraneous memories based on numerical simulations and non-rigorous calculations
[
1
]
.

In this paper, we explicitly construct a class of spurious memories of the energy function (
1.1
) arising from mixtures of the initial patterns. These so-called
mixed memories
are configurations of the form

\xi_{i}(m)=\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left(\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\xi^{\mu}_{i}F^{\prime}(m_{\mu})\mathbbm{1}_{m_{\mu}\neq 0}\right),\quad 1\leq i\leq N,

(1.3)

where the
mixture coefficients
,
m=(m_{\mu})_{1\leq\mu\leq M}\in[-1,1]^{M}
, form a deterministic vector with finitely many non-zero components,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and where
\mathbbm{1}_{m_{\mu}\neq 0}=1
if
m_{\mu}\neq 0
and is zero else.
Furthermore, with a
{\mathbb{P}}
-probability that tends to one as
N
diverges, for all
1\leq\mu\leq M
such that

m_{\mu}\neq 0
, mixed memories have overlap
m_{\mu}
with the pattern
\xi^{\mu}
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}(m)\xi^{\mu}_{i}=m_{\mu}.

(1.4)

Our results are twofold:
(i) First, we construct solutions to the system of equations defined through (
1.3
) and (
1.4
), whose unknowns are the
n
non-zero components of
m
. More precisely, we construct a class of
admissible mixture coefficients,
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{all}
, which does not depend on
F
and is thus common to all models of Hopfield type. The solutions of (
1.4
) for a given energy function (
1.1
) are then obtained as the subset
{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{all}
of the mixture coefficients that satisfy a particular system of inequalities,
{\mathcal{S}}_{n,F}
, that depend on
F
.

(ii) We then give conditions on the growth rate of
M
as a function of
N
which guarantee that mixed memories

(\xi_{i}(m))_{1\leq i\leq N}
with
m\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
are local minima of the energy function (
1.1
) for the main models of interest,
i.e.
the classical, the dense and the modern Hopfield models. We ask two questions: whether this is true for each mixed memory or for all of them simultaneously, with
{\mathbb{P}}
-probability one as
N
diverges.

The set
{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
contains all the local minima found numerically. This supports the conjecture that we have obtained the complete set of all local minima.

The existence of a common set
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{all}
of mixture coefficients from which mixed memories are constructed appears to confirm recent numerical findings by Hopfield
et al.

[
23
]
,
[
24
]
, that spurious states can be “transported” within the class of dense models, from one model to another.

These results provide a first insight into mixed memories in Hopfield models with analogue (or continuous) neurons. Indeed, it has been known since
[
18
]
that there is a correspondence between the sets of local minima of the continuous and binary neuron models, with the two sets coinciding in the so-called high-gain limit, an analogue of the zero-temperature limit in statistical mechanics. This is of practical relevance both in computer science, where Hopfield models are commonly integrated into deep learning architectures
[
22
]
,
[
35
]
, and in statistical mechanics.

1.2.
Main results

In the classical Hopfield model, the existence of local minima corresponding to mixtures of a finite number of original patterns was fully formalised by Amit
et al.

[
1
]
through an extensive numerical study of the critical points of the free energy associated with the model at all temperatures. They discovered that these mixed memories are not random, but are given by well-defined, deterministic mixtures of patterns, and classified them into three main groups:
symmetric
,
continuous asymmetric
and
discontinuous asymmetric
memories (referring to the way they emerge when the temperature is varied). The class of continuous asymmetric memories appears to be the largest. Together with the symmetric memories, it is expected to contain all local minima. In contrast, the discontinuous asymmetric memories found were much rarer, with none being a local minimum. Symmetric memories were mathematically understood a few years later
[
21
,
12
]
. In this paper, we construct a class of mixed memories that encompasses all examples of the symmetric and continuous asymmetric memories obtained in
[
1
]
at zero temperature. We simply call this class
mixed memories
.

1.2.1.
Mixed memories

Throughout the paper,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is chosen to be independent of
N
and
M
is chosen to be a non-decreasing function of
N
. We begin with a formal definition of mixed memories for energy functions of the form (
1.1
).

Definition 1.1

(Mixed memories of type
F
)
.

Let
F
be a smooth function whose derivative satisfies
F^{\prime}(x)>0
, for all
x>0
. Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
independent of
N
,
n
-mixed memories of type
F
are configurations in
\Sigma_{N}
denoted by
\xi^{(N)}(m)=\left(\xi_{i}(m)\right)_{1\leq i\leq N}
and defined as

\xi_{i}(m)=\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left(\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\xi^{\mu}_{i}F^{\prime}(m_{\mu})\mathbbm{1}_{m_{\mu}\neq 0}\right),\quad 1\leq i\leq N,

(1.5)

where
m=(m_{\mu})_{1\leq\mu\leq M}\in[-1,1]^{M}
is a deterministic vector with the following properties:

(i)

m
has exactly
n
non-zero components,
i.e.
there exists a subset
V\subset\{1,\dots,M\}
of cardinality
|V|=n
such that
m_{\mu}\neq 0
if and only if
\mu\in V
.

(ii)

Let
\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\}
be an enumeration of the elements of
V
and, for each
1\leq\nu\leq n
, set
m_{\mu_{\nu}}=\hat{m}_{\nu}
.
Then, for each
1\leq\nu\leq n
, the normalised overlap of
\xi^{(N)}(m)
with the pattern
\xi^{\mu_{\nu}}
converges to
\hat{m}_{\nu}
as
N
diverges,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}N^{-1}\left(\xi^{(N)}(m),\xi^{\mu_{\nu}}\right)=\hat{m}_{\nu}\right)=1,\quad\forall 1\leq\nu\leq n,

(1.6)

and it converges to zero else,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}N^{-1}\left(\xi^{(N)}(m),\xi^{\mu}\right)=0\right)=1,\quad\forall\mu\in\{1,\dots,M\}\setminus V.

(1.7)

Remark
.

Since
n
is independent of
N
, the set
V
is countable and (
1.6
) implies

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\bigcap_{1\leq\nu\leq n}\left\{\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}N^{-1}\left(\xi^{(N)}(m),\xi^{\mu_{\nu}}\right)=\hat{m}_{\nu}\right\}\right)=1.

(1.8)

Remark
.

The techniques developed in this paper
a priori
allow us to treat cases where
n
is a growing function of
N
, albeit with the important limitation that this growth is logarithmic at most. See also the remark at the end of the proof of Proposition 3.1.

Let us now describe the set that we will prove to be a set of
n
-mixed memories. Recall that a
composition
of the integer
n
into
\ell
summands, or
\ell
-composition, is any solution
(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})
of
n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell}

with
n_{k}\geq 1
for each
1\leq k\leq\ell
. Note that unlike integer partitions, the order of the summands counts. We call an
\ell
-composition
(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})

allowable
if
n_{k}\geq 2
is even for all
1\leq k\leq\ell-1
and
n_{\ell}\geq 1
is odd. Thus, there is no allowable composition of an even integer
n
. Given an allowable
\ell
-composition set

\alpha^{(n_{k})}=2^{-n_{k}+1}{{n_{k}-1}\choose{\lfloor(n_{k}-1)/2\rfloor}}

(1.9)

for each
1\leq k\leq\ell
, and let
\gamma_{n}=\left(\gamma^{(k)}\right)_{1\leq k\leq\ell}
be the vector of components

\gamma^{(k)}\equiv\prod_{l=1}^{k}\alpha^{(n_{l})}.

(1.10)

Denote by
\Gamma^{all}_{n}\subset[-1,1]^{n}
the set of all such vectors, one for each allowable
\ell
-composition

\Gamma^{all}_{n}=\bigcup_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\left\{\gamma_{n}\mid(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\text{ is an allowable $\ell$-composition of $n$}\right\},

(1.11)

and define the subset

\Gamma_{n,F}=\left\{\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma^{all}_{n}:\gamma_{n}\text{ is a solution of }{\mathcal{S}}_{n,F}\right\},

(1.12)

where
{\mathcal{S}}_{n,F}
is the system of
\ell-1
inequalities

2F^{\prime}\left(\gamma^{(k)}\right)>n_{k+1}F^{\prime}\left(\gamma^{(k+1)}\right)+\dots+n_{\ell}F^{\prime}\left(\gamma^{(\ell)}\right)\,\text{ for all }1\leq k\leq\ell-1.

(1.13)

Given
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma_{n,F}
, let
m(\gamma_{n})=(m_{\mu}(\gamma_{n}))_{1\leq\mu\leq M}
be the vector whose components are constant and equal to
\gamma^{(k)}
on consecutive blocks of length
n_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq\ell
, and are
0
beyond,

m_{\mu}(\gamma_{n})=\begin{cases}\gamma^{(k)}&\text{if\, $1\leq\mu-(n_{0}+\dots+n_{k-1})\leq n_{k}$ \,for some $1\leq k\leq\ell$,}\\
0&\text{if\, $\mu>n$,}\end{cases}

(1.14)

where by convention
n_{0}\equiv 0
. With this, define

{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}_{n,F}=\left\{m(\gamma_{n}):\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma_{n,F}\right\}.

(1.15)

Finally, the above set is extended to include all possible permutations and (depending on the parity of
F
) all possible signs of the coordinates of each of its elements. More precisely, setting
a=1
if
F^{\prime}
is an odd function and
a=2
otherwise,

{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}=\bigcup_{\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma_{n,F}}\bigcup_{\pi\in\Pi_{M}}\bigcup_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{M}}\left\{m^{\prime}:m^{\prime}_{\mu}=\left(\varepsilon_{\mu}\right)^{a}m_{\pi(\mu)}(\gamma_{n}),{1\leq\mu\leq M}\right\},

(1.16)

where
\Pi_{M}
denotes the set of all permutations of
\{1,\dots,M\}
and

\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{\mu})_{1\leq\mu\leq M}\in\{-1,1\}^{M}
is a sequence of signs.
Therefore, if
F^{\prime}
is not an odd function, the coordinates of the vectors
m
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
are all non-negative.
The statement made in point (i) of Section
1.1
can now be put into concrete form.
Define

{\mathcal{M}}^{all}_{n}=\bigcup_{\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma^{all}_{n}}\bigcup_{\pi\in\Pi_{M}}\bigcup_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{M}}\left\{m^{\prime}:m^{\prime}_{\mu}=\varepsilon_{\mu}m_{\pi(\mu)}(\gamma_{n}),{1\leq\mu\leq M}\right\}.

(1.17)

Then, for each
F
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
is the subset of
{\mathcal{M}}^{all}_{n}
constructed from the sequences
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma_{n,F}\subseteq\Gamma^{all}_{n}
satisfying the system of inequalities
{\mathcal{S}}_{n,F}
and restricted to the positive orthant in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
unless
F^{\prime}
is odd.

We are now ready to state our first theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(Mixed memories of type
F
)
.

Let
F
be a smooth function whose derivative
F^{\prime}
satisfies
F^{\prime}(x)>0
, for all
x>0
.
For any odd
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if
m\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
then
\xi^{(N)}(m)
is an
n
-mixed memory of type
F
.

The set
{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
decomposes into the disjoint union of two subsets containing the symmetric and asymmetric mixed memories, respectively. We call symmetric the memories resulting from the trivial
\ell
-compositions given by
\ell=1
and
n_{1}=n
. In this case, (
1.14
) becomes
m(\gamma_{n})=(\gamma^{(1)},\dots,\gamma^{(1)},0,\dots,0)
, where
\gamma^{(1)}
is repeated
n
times. Note that this set contains the
M
original patterns themselves: these are obtained for
n=1
.
All other memories are called asymmetric.

It is clearly of interest to know how many of these mixed memories are present in a given model. The next proposition gives estimates of their growth rate in
M
.

Proposition 1.3

(Bounds on the number of mixed memories)
.

Let
F
be a smooth function whose derivative
F^{\prime}
satisfies
F^{\prime}(x)>0
, for all
x>0
.
For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
odd

A_{n,F}M^{n}\left(1-\frac{n}{M}\right)^{n-1}\leq\left|{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}\right|\leq A_{n,F}M^{n},

(1.18)

where
A_{n,F}
only depends on
n
and
F
and not on
N
.

We conclude this subsection by noting that the numerical work of Amit
et al.

[
1
]
for the classical Hopfield model strongly suggests that if
\xi^{(N)}(m)
is an
n
-mixed memory of type
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
, then
m\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
. Extrapolating to the case of general type
F
memories suggests that the converse of Theorem
1.2
is true. This leads to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.4
.

Given any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
odd,
\xi^{(N)}(m)
is an
n
-mixed memory of type
F
if and only if
m\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
.

Implications of this conjecture are discussed at the end of this section.

In Section
6
, we solve the system of inequalities
{\mathcal{S}}_{n,F}
defined in (
1.13
),
and explicitly enumerate the set of
n
-mixed memories of type
F
for the classical and the dense Hopfield models. We then draw consequences for their energy functions (
1.1
).
In particular, we prove (see Lemma
6.2
) that, for
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
, if
M\ll N
, then, with
{\mathbb{P}}
-probability one, the energy of the corresponding
n
-mixed memories becomes confined to the strip

(-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{\pi}]
as
N\rightarrow\infty
if
n>1
, while the energy of the original patterns converges to
-\frac{1}{2}
.

1.2.2.
Mixed memories are local minima

We are interested in finding the local minima of
E_{N,M}
. This can be achieved by setting up a deterministic dynamics
\sigma(t)
on
\Sigma_{N}
as follows: at time step
t+1
, an index
i
is selected at random from
\{1,\dots,N\}
(
e.g.
uniformly and independently from previous selections), and
\sigma(t)
is updated to

\sigma_{j}(t+1)=\begin{cases}T_{i}(\sigma(t))&\text{if $j=i$,}\\
\sigma_{j}(t)&\text{if $j\neq i,$}\end{cases}

(1.19)

where
T=(T_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}:\Sigma_{N}\rightarrow\Sigma_{N}
is some map that decreases the energy.
Then
\sigma
is a minimum of
E_{N,M}(\sigma)
if and only if it is a fixed point of this dynamics,
i.e.
if and only if

\sigma_{i}=T_{i}(\sigma)\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq N.

(1.20)

Two types of maps
T_{i}
(or update rules) have been considered in the literature: the so-called gradient map,

T^{\text{G}}_{i}(\sigma)=\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left\{\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\xi^{\mu}_{i}F^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq j\neq i\leq N}\xi^{\mu}_{j}\sigma_{j}\right)\right\},

(1.21)

and, more recently, the Hopfield-Krotov map
[
24
]
.

T^{\text{HK}}_{i}(\sigma)=\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left\{\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\left[F\left(\frac{\xi^{\mu}_{i}}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq j\neq i\leq N}\xi^{\mu}_{j}\sigma_{j}\right)-F\left(-\frac{\xi^{\mu}_{i}}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq j\neq i\leq N}\xi^{\mu}_{j}\sigma_{j}\right)\right]\right\}.

(1.22)

It is clear that the dynamics
T^{\text{HK}}
decreases the energy (
1.1
) and therefore its fixed points are local minima.

T^{\text{G}}
was Hopfield’s original choice
[
17
]
. In statistical mechanics, a variant is considered in which the sum (
1.21
) is over all
1\leq j\leq N
. It is obtained by taking the zero temperature limit of a gradient dynamics minimising the free energy functional associated with the energy (
1.1
). Although there is little difference between this definition and
T^{\text{G}}
, we cannot claim that
T^{\text{G}}
decreases the energy for all
F
. One of the motivations for studying both
T^{\text{HK}}
and
T^{\text{G}}
is to prove that for the main models of interest,
i.e.
the classical, the dense and the modern models, their fixed points coincide. As we will see, the question is only open for the dense model, since it turns out that
T^{\text{G}}=T^{\text{HK}}
for the classical and modern Hopfield models.

The theorems below give sufficient conditions on the growth rate of
M\equiv M(N)
for mixed memories to be local minima of the energy function, asymptotically, for three models: the classical, the dense and the modern Hopfield models.
Having stated these results, the obtained conditions are then compared to the known conditions for original patterns to be local minima.

Remember that
n
is independent of
N
. Furthermore, a vector
m\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}
is an
M
-dimensional vector with
n
non-zero components (see (
1.14
) and (
1.16
)).

Theorem 1.5

(Classical Hopfield network)
.

Take
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
in (
1.1
). For this model
T^{\text{HK}}=T^{\text{G}}\equiv T

Given

{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{1}(n)
. In this case the dilatation sequence reduces to

.
From the previous two facts we deduce that the two pairs

completely determine one and the same the Rademacher matrix

The conclusion of the lemma will now follow from the fact that

is a binary tree, and that such a tree can be constructed recursively from any sequence of

, where

is an
\ell
-composition of

. Simply replace each leaf of

with a copy of

{\mathcal{T}}^{(n_{2})}_{n_{2}}
, then replace each leaf of each tree
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n_{2})}_{n_{2}}
with a copy of
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n_{3})}_{n_{3}}
, and so
on

times. It remains to use the fact established above that, for each

, the pair

\bigl\{{\mathcal{T}}^{(n_{k})}_{n_{k}},(2^{n-(n_{0}+\dots+n_{k-1}+l)})_{1\leq l\leq n_{k}}\bigr\}

where

labels the levels of the tree

\bigl\{{\mathcal{T}}^{(n_{k})}_{1}(n_{k}),(2^{n-(n_{1}+\dots+n_{k})})\bigr\}

This gives the claim of the lemma.
∎

1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}
, then we have

\begin{split}{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}=&\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n)}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i\in(Z^{n}_{n_{1}})^{c}\right\}}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left(a_{1}{\sum_{1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}}}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\right)\\
=&\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n)}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left(a_{1}{\sum_{1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}}}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\right)\\
=&\frac{1}{d(n_{1})}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n_{1})}r^{(n_{1}),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[a_{1}\left(r^{(n_{1})}_{i},\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{1}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(3.19)

The first equality in (
3.19
) follows from the definition of
{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}
and (
3.18
). To go from the first to the second, we used that by (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), the sum of the terms satisfying the condition

\left\{i\in Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\right\}
vanishes. The last equality then follows from (
3.11
). Note that since
a_{1}>0
, it can be removed. Doing so, the last line of (
3.19
) reduces to the right-hand side of the mixed-memory equation (
2.24
) for the
n_{1}
-symmetric solution (see the more explicit formula (
3.3
) in the proof of Lemma
2.6
).
Thus, by Lemma
2.6
, (i), for all even integer
n_{1}\geq 2
, all
a_{1}>0
satisfying (
3.16
) and all

1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}
, we have

{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}=\alpha^{(n_{1})}.

(3.20)

Now, let us check that if
n_{1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+n_{2}
, then

{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}=0.

(3.21)

This uses the following two facts. Firstly, as we just saw, the sequence of signs (
3.18
) is piecewise constant over intervals of length
d(n_{2})
,

\begin{split}&\left(\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},m\right)\right]\right)_{i\in\left(Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\right)^{c}}=d(n_{2})\otimes\left(\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n_{1})}_{j},a_{1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{1}}\right)\right]\right)_{\left\{j\in\left(Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}\right)^{c}\right\}},\end{split}

(3.22)

where in the right-hand side,
\left(Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}\right)^{c}=\left\{1,\dots,d(n_{1})\right\}\setminus Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}
and

Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}=\left\{1\leq i\leq d(n_{1}):\sum_{1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}}r^{(n_{1}),\nu}_{i}=0\right\}.

(3.23)

Note that (
3.23
) is obtained by taking
n=n_{1}
in (
3.12
). Secondly, by (
3.11
), the configurations

\{r^{(n),\nu},n_{1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+n_{2}\}
, restricted to any such constancy interval, reduce to the
n_{2}
Rademacher configurations
\{r^{(n_{2}),\nu-n_{1}},n_{1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+n_{2}\}
of
R^{(n_{2})}
.
By the first of these two facts,

\begin{split}&{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}\\
=&\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d(n_{1})}\,\sum_{(j-1)d(n_{2})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{2})}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i\in(Z^{n}_{n_{1}})^{c}\right\}}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},m\right)\right]\\
=&\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d(n_{1})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{j\in\left(Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}\right)^{c}\right\}}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n_{1})}_{j},a_{1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{1}}\right)\right]\sum_{(j-1)d(n_{2})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{2})}r^{(n),\nu}_{i},\end{split}

(3.24)

and by the second,

\sum_{(j-1)d(n_{2})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{2})}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n_{2})}r^{(n_{2}),\nu-n_{1}}_{i}=\left(r^{(n_{2}),\nu-n_{1}},r^{(n_{2}),0}\right)=0,

(3.25)

where the last equality is (
2.7
) of Lemma
2.1
, (i). Inserting (
3.25
) into (
3.24
) then yields the claim of (
3.21
).

We now turn to the term
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}
in (
3.15
). For all
1\leq\nu\leq n
, by definition of

Z^{n}_{n_{1}}

\begin{split}&\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}\\
=&\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n)}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i\in Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\right\}}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{1}}\oplus a_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{2}}\right)\right]\\
=&\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d(n_{1})}\,\sum_{(j-1)d(n_{2})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{2})}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i\in Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\right\}}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{1}}\oplus a_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{2}}\right)\right]\\
=&\frac{1}{d(n_{1})}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d(n_{1})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{j\in Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}\right\}}{\mathcal{U}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{2},j},\end{split}

(3.26)

where
Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}
is defined in (
3.23
) and where, for each
j\in Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}
and
1\leq\nu\leq n
,

{\mathcal{U}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{2},j}\equiv\frac{1}{d(n_{2})}\sum_{(j-1)d(n_{2})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{2})}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{1}}\oplus a_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{2}}\right)\right].

(3.27)

As before, we distinguish two cases. First, if
n_{1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+n_{2}
then, by (
3.11
),

r^{(n),\nu}
is the concatenation of
d(n_{1})
identical copies of
r^{(n_{2}),\nu}
, and so,

\begin{split}{\mathcal{U}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{2},j}&=\frac{1}{d(n_{2})}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n_{2})}r^{(n_{2}),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[a_{2}\left(r^{(n_{2})}_{i},\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{2}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(3.28)

After removing the quantity
a_{2}
, which is possible since it is strictly positive, we again recognise in (
3.28
) the right-hand side of the mixed-memory equation of the
n_{2}
-asymmetric solution (see (
3.3
) in the proof of Lemma
2.6
). Thus, by Lemma
2.6
, (i), we have for all
a_{2}>0
, all
j\in Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}
and all

n_{1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+n_{2}

{\mathcal{U}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{2},j}=\alpha^{(n_{2})}.

(3.29)

Inserting (
3.29
) into (
3.26
) and using the last equality of (
3.14
) to evaluate
|Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}|
, we get

\begin{split}\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}&=\frac{\alpha^{(n_{2})}}{d(n_{1})}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d(n_{1})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{j\in Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}\right\}}=\frac{\alpha^{(n_{2})}}{d(n_{1})}|Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}|=\frac{\alpha^{(n_{2})}}{d(n_{1})}{{n_{1}}\choose{n_{1}/2}}\\
&=\alpha^{(n_{1}+1)}\alpha^{(n_{2})}.\end{split}

(3.30)

Second, if
1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}
, then by (
3.11
)
r^{(n),\nu}
is piecewise constant over intervals of length
d(n_{2})
,
i.e.

r^{(n),\nu}_{i}=r^{(n_{1}),\nu}_{j}
for all
i
in the interval
(j-1)d(n_{2})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{2})
. Thus,

\begin{split}{\mathcal{U}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{2},j}&=r^{(n_{1}),\nu}_{j}\frac{1}{d(n_{2})}\sum_{(j-1)d(n_{2})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{2})}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{1}}\oplus a_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{2}}\right)\right]\\
&=r^{(n_{1}),\nu}_{j}\frac{1}{d(n_{2})}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n_{2})}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[a_{2}\left(r^{(n_{2})}_{i},\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{2}}\right)\right]\\
&=0,\end{split}

(3.31)

where we used that due to the axial symmetry (
2.8
) of Lemma
2.1
, (ii), the sum in the second line of (
3.31
) is zero. Plugging (
3.31
) into (
3.26
), we get that for all
1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}=0.

(3.32)

We can now collect our estimates.
Combining (
3.20
), (
3.21
), (
3.30
) and (
3.32
), we obtain

{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}=\begin{cases}\alpha^{(n_{1})}&\text{if $1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}$},\\
0&\text{if $n_{1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+n_{2}$},\end{cases}

(3.33)

and

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}$},\\
\alpha^{(n_{1}+1)}\alpha^{(n_{2})}&\text{if $n_{1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+n_{2}$}.\end{cases}

(3.34)

Recall that we seek solutions to the system (
2.24
) of the form (
3.10
).
By (
3.15
), the right-hand side of (
2.24
) is equal to the sum of the above two terms,

m_{\nu}={\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}+\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}},

(3.35)

and so, inserting (
3.33
) and (
3.34
) in (
3.35
), we obtain that for all
n_{1}\geq 2
even,
n_{2}\geq 1
of arbitrary parity and all
a_{1},a_{2}>0
that satisfy (
3.16
),

m_{\nu}=\begin{cases}\alpha^{(n_{1})}&\text{if $1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}$},\\
\alpha^{(n_{1}+1)}\alpha^{(n_{2})}&\text{if $n_{1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+n_{2}$}.\end{cases}

(3.36)

Thus, observing that for
n_{1}
even,
\alpha^{(n_{1}+1)}=\alpha^{(n_{1})}
(see also (
3.99
)-(
3.100
)),
and choosing

\begin{split}a_{1}&=\gamma^{(1)}\equiv\alpha^{(n_{1})},\\
a_{2}&=\gamma^{(2)}\equiv\alpha^{(n_{1})}\alpha^{(n_{2})},\end{split}

(3.37)

the vector
m=\gamma^{(1)}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{1}}\oplus\gamma^{(2)}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{2}}
verifies the mixed-memory equation (
2.24
), provided that
n_{1}\geq 2
is even,
n_{2}\geq 1
has any parity and

2\gamma^{(1)}>n_{2}\gamma^{(2)}.

(3.38)

This proves Proposition
2.7
, (i), in the case of an asymmetric solution constructed from two symmetric solutions.

General construction step.

We now take
s=n
,
\ell>2
and assume that
(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})
is an
\ell
-composition of
n
such that
n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}\geq 2
are even and
n_{\ell}\geq 1
has any parity.
We seek solutions of the system (
2.24
) of the form

m=a_{1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus a_{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{\ell}},

(3.39)

for some numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}>0
satisfying the system of conditions

2a_{k}>n_{k+1}a_{k+1}+\dots+n_{\ell}a_{\ell},\,\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\,1\leq k\leq\ell-1.

(3.40)

To formulate the general construction step, the following definitions are needed. The first one extends the definition
(
3.12
) to each of the
1\leq k\leq\ell-1
groups of configurations of
R^{(n)}
. Using the
k
-th group, namely, the configurations
r^{(n),\nu}
with
n_{0}+\dots+n_{k-1}+1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}
, we let

Z^{n}_{n_{k}}
be the set

Z^{n}_{n_{k}}=\left\{1\leq i\leq d(n):\sum_{1\leq\nu-(n_{0}+\dots+n_{k-1})\leq n_{k}}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}=0\right\},\quad 1\leq k\leq\ell-1,

(3.41)

with the convention that
n_{0}=0
.
Since
n_{k}
is even for all
1\leq k\leq\ell-1
, these sets are non-empty. We denote by
\left(Z^{n}_{n_{k}}\right)^{c}
their complements,

\left(Z^{n}_{n_{k}}\right)^{c}=\left\{1,\dots,d(n)\right\}\setminus Z^{n}_{n_{k}},\quad 1\leq k\leq\ell-1.

(3.42)

These sets are then used to decompose the sum in the right-hand side of (
2.24
), for each
1\leq\nu\leq n
, into
\ell
terms:

{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1}}\equiv\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n)}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i\in\left(Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\right)^{c}\right\}}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},m\right)\right],

(3.43)

for all
2\leq k\leq\ell-1
,

{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}\equiv\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n)}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i\in Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\cap\dots\cap Z^{n}_{n_{k-1}}\cap{\left(Z^{n}_{n_{k}}\right)^{c}}\right\}}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},m\right)\right],

(3.44)

and

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}\equiv\frac{1}{d(n)}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n)}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i\in Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\cap\dots\cap Z^{n}_{n_{\ell-1}}\right\}}r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},m\right)\right].

(3.45)

With these definitions, the system of equations (
2.24
) can be rewritten as

m_{\nu}=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}+\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}\quad 1\leq\nu\leq n.

(3.46)

To construct solutions to this system, we evaluate each of the sums separately, except for the last two
(
i.e.
, (
3.44
) whith
k=\ell-1
and (
3.45
)), which we treat simultaneously. The gist of the proof is that for each
k
, the problem of evaluating the sum
{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}
can be reduced to a situation analogous to the one we encountered in the first step. As will become clear later, there is little difference in the treatment of
{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}
for

1\leq k\leq\ell-2
and
k=\ell-1
. We therefore start by considering the case
k=\ell-1
and evaluate the last two sums,

{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}
and
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}
.

The case
k=\ell-1
.

More precisely, let us establish that under the condition that

2a_{\ell-1}>n_{\ell}a_{\ell},

(3.47)

for all
n_{\ell-1}\geq 2
even and
n_{\ell}\geq 1
of arbitrary parity, we have

\begin{split}{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}=&\begin{cases}0&\text{if $1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}$},\\
\alpha^{(n_{\ell-1})}\prod_{l=1}^{\ell-2}\alpha^{(n_{l}+1)}&\text{if $1\leq\nu-(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})\leq n_{\ell-1}$},\\
0&\text{if $1\leq\nu-(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-1})\leq n_{\ell}$},\end{cases}\end{split}

(3.48)

and

\begin{split}\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}=&\begin{cases}0&\text{if $1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-1}$},\\
\alpha^{(n_{\ell})}\prod_{l=1}^{\ell-1}\alpha^{(n_{l}+1)}&\text{if $1\leq\nu-(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-1})\leq n_{\ell}$.}\end{cases}\end{split}

(3.49)

For this, note that
{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}
and
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}
are functions of a sequence of signs,

\begin{split}&\left(\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},m\right)\right]\right)_{\left\{i\in Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\cap\dots\cap Z^{n}_{n_{\ell-2}}\right\}}\\
=&\left(\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}}\oplus a_{\ell-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{\ell-1}}\oplus a_{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{\ell}}\right)\right]\right)_{\left\{i\in Z^{n}_{n_{1}}\cap\dots\cap Z^{n}_{n_{\ell-2}}\right\}},\end{split}

(3.50)

which, by definition of the sets
Z^{n}_{n_{k}}
, no longer depends on the first
n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}
configurations of
R^{(n)}
.
By Lemma
2.5
, we can write
R^{(n)}
using a 2-level tree as

R^{(n)}=\begin{pmatrix}2^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}\otimes R^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})}\\
R^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}\oplus\dots\oplus R^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}\end{pmatrix},

(3.51)

where the concatenation is over
2^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})}
matrices
R^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}
. Thus, the matrix obtained from
R^{(n)}
by removing the first
n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}
configurations is a concatenation of
2^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})}
identical matrices
R^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}
. Reasoning as in the first step (see in particular (
3.26
)-(
3.27
)), the terms

{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}
and
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}
can be rewritten as follows.
The role of the set
Z^{n_{1}}_{n_{1}}
is now played by the set

\begin{split}Z^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-2}}\equiv&\bigcap_{1\leq k\leq\ell-2}Z^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}}_{n_{k}}\\
\equiv&\Bigg\{1\leq i\leq d(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}):\textrm{for all}\,\,1\leq k\leq\ell-2\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\sum_{1\leq\nu-(n_{0}+\dots+n_{k-1})\leq n_{k}}r^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}),\nu}_{i}=0\Bigg\},\end{split}

(3.52)

with the convention that
n_{0}=0
. Given

j\in Z^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-2}}

and a subset

{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq\left\{1,\dots,d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})\right\}
,
define, for all
1\leq\nu\leq n

\begin{split}&{\mathcal{U}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell},j}({\mathcal{Z}})\\
\equiv&\,\,\frac{1}{d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}\sum_{(j-1)d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i-(j-1)d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})\in{\mathcal{Z}}\right\}}\\
&\,\,r^{(n),\nu}_{i}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n)}_{i},\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}}\oplus a_{\ell-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{\ell-1}}\oplus a_{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{\ell}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(3.53)

and

\begin{split}&{\mathcal{V}}^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}),\nu}({\mathcal{Z}})\\
\equiv&\,\,\frac{1}{d(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{j\in Z^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-2}}\right\}}{\mathcal{U}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell},j}\left({\mathcal{Z}}\right).\end{split}

(3.54)

Then, for
1\leq\nu\leq n
,

\begin{split}{\mathcal{S}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}&={\mathcal{V}}^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}),\nu}\left(\bigl(Z^{n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}}_{n_{\ell-1}}\bigr)^{c}\right),\\
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(n),\nu}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-1}}&={\mathcal{V}}^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}),\nu}\left(Z^{n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}}_{n_{\ell-1}}\right),\end{split}

(3.55)

where
Z^{n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}}_{n_{\ell-1}}
is the set

Z^{n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}}_{n_{\ell-1}}=\left\{1\leq i^{\prime}\leq d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}):\sum_{1\leq\nu^{\prime}\leq n_{\ell-1}}r^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}),\nu^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}}=0\right\}

(3.56)

obtained by taking
n=n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}
and
k=\ell-1
in (
3.41
).

As can be seen from the right-hand sides of (
3.48
) and (
3.49
), we now must distinguish between several cases. Let us start with the case
1\leq\nu\leq n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}
. For such
\nu
, it follows from (
3.51
) that for each

1\leq j\leq d(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})
,

r^{(n),\nu}_{i}=r^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}),\nu}_{j}

for all
i
in the constancy interval

(j-1)d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})+1\leq i\leq jd(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})
.
Thus,
r^{(n),\nu}_{i}
can be taken out from the sum over
i

3.53
).
Consequently, for any subset

{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq\left\{1,\dots,d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})\right\}
,
{\mathcal{V}}^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}),\nu}\left({\mathcal{Z}}\right)
factorises into

{\mathcal{V}}^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}),\nu}\left({\mathcal{Z}}\right)={\mathcal{X}}^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}),\nu}\,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}),\nu}\left({\mathcal{Z}}\right),

(3.57)

where

\begin{split}&{\mathcal{Y}}^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}),\nu}\left({\mathcal{Z}}\right)\\
\equiv&\frac{1}{d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i\in{\mathcal{Z}}\right\}}\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits\left[\left(r^{(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell})}_{i},\oplus a_{\ell-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{\ell-1}}\oplus a_{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{\ell}}\right)\right],\end{split}

(3.58)

and

\begin{split}&{\mathcal{X}}^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}),\nu}\\
\equiv&\frac{1}{d(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{j\in Z^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-2}}\right\}}r^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}),\nu}_{j}.\end{split}

(3.59)

To evaluate
{\mathcal{X}}^{(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}),\nu}
, we use that, in the light of the definition (
3.52
),
the axial symmetry of Lemma
2.1
, (ii), is preserved on
Z^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-2}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell-2}}

Given an integer

n

N

Recalling that the patterns

m(\gamma_{n})
is

q(\gamma_{n})\equiv{M\choose n_{1}}{M-n_{1}\choose n_{2}}\dots{M-(n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell-1})\choose n_{\ell}}.

(4.20)

This obeys the bounds

\frac{M^{n}}{n_{1}!\dots n_{\ell}!}\left(1-\frac{n}{M}\right)^{\ell-1}\leq q(\gamma_{n})\leq\frac{M^{n}}{n_{1}!\dots n_{\ell}!}\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{\ell-1}.

(4.21)

Thus,

\left|{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}\right|=(2^{n})^{a}\sum_{m(\gamma_{n})\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}_{n,F}}q(\gamma_{n}),

(4.22)

where
a=1
if
F^{\prime}
is an odd function and
a=2
otherwise.
Since
{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}_{n,F}
only depends on
n
and
F
and not on
M
, taking

A_{n,F}=(2^{n})^{a}\sum_{m(\gamma_{n})\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}_{n,F}}\frac{1}{n_{1}!\dots n_{\ell}!},

(4.23)

gives

A_{n,F}M^{n}\left(1-\frac{n}{M}\right)^{n-1}\leq\left|{\mathcal{M}}_{n,F}\right|\leq A_{n,F}M^{n},

(4.24)

which is (
1.18
). The proof is done.
∎

Proof of Lemma

We have to estimate the tail probability of the sum

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{A}}^{(p)}_{N}(n,i,m,\lambda)\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\frac{p-1}{2}}}\sum_{n+1\leq\mu\leq M}Y_{i}^{\mu}>\lambda\right),

diverges for all

\mathfrak{p}_{p,N}(\lambda)\equiv{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\frac{p-1}{2}}}\sum_{n+1\leq\mu\leq M}\overline{Y}_{i}^{\mu}>\lambda\right).

(5.40)

To bound the sum appearing in the right-hand side of (

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left|Y_{i}^{\mu}\right|>\gamma^{p-1}(N)\right]&={\mathbb{P}}\left[\left|Y_{i}^{\mu}\right|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}>\gamma^{2}(N)\right]\leq e^{-t\gamma^{2}(N)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{t\left(X_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{2}}\right],\end{split}

(5.41)

and note that the last expectation is equal to

is the the partition function of the Curie-Weiss model at inverse temperature

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{t\left(X_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{2}}\right]<c_{0}N,

(5.42)

(see,

6

).
Combining (
5.41
) and (

(5.43)

for some constants
c_{0}>0
and
0<t_{0}\leq 1/2
.

Now consider (
5.40
). To bound this term, the underlying idea is to choose the truncation threshold
\gamma(N)
in such a way that the density of
\overline{Y}_{i}^{\mu}
is well approximated by that of a standard Gaussian. A first bound can be derived from a second order Chebyshev inequality,
i.e.
, using that the variables
\left(\overline{Y}_{i}^{\mu}\right)_{n+1\leq\mu\leq M}
are independent with mean
{\mathbb{E}}(\overline{Y}_{i}^{\mu})=0
.

\begin{split}\mathfrak{p}_{p,N}(\lambda)&\leq\left(\lambda N^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\right)^{-2}\sum_{n+1\leq\mu\leq M}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\overline{Y}_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(5.44)

Next,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\overline{Y}_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{2}&=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N}:|x|\leq\gamma(N)}x^{2(p-1)}{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{i}^{\mu}=x\right),\end{split}

(5.45)

where

{\mathcal{S}}_{N}=\bigl\{(-N+k)/\sqrt{N}:0\leq k\leq 2N\bigr\}
.
By de Moivre-Laplace local limit theorem (see,
e.g.
Lemma 2 p. 46 in
[
14
]
), if
\gamma(N)=o\bigl(N^{1/6}\bigr)
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\overline{Y}_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{2}\leq\sigma_{N,2(p-1)}\equiv(1+o(1))\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N}:|x|\leq\gamma(N)}x^{2(p-1)}{\textstyle{1\over\sqrt{2\pi N}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}},\end{split}

(5.46)

which is a Riemann sum, and so,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{N,2(p-1)}=\sigma_{2(p-1)}\equiv(2p-3)!!,

(5.47)

where
\sigma_{2(p-1)}
is the moment of order
2(p-1)
of a standard Gaussian. We thus arrive at

\mathfrak{p}_{p,N}(\lambda)\leq\sigma_{N,2(p-1)}\lambda^{-2}MN^{-(p-1)}.

(5.48)

This bound can be improved by using Chebyshev’s inequality of order
2k
,
k>1
. Proceeding as above, it follows from classical combinatorics that there is a constant
c(k)>0
, independent of
N
, such that

\mathfrak{p}_{p,N}(\lambda)\leq c(k)\left(\sigma_{N,2(p-1)}\lambda^{-2}MN^{-(p-1)}\right)^{k}.

(5.49)

Clearly, this bound will be useful only when
M
is small enough compared to
N^{p-1}
.

To complement this result, we use that by the exponential Chebyshev inequality, for all

\begin{split}\mathfrak{p}_{p,N}(\lambda)&\leq e^{-\lambda t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(tN^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}\sum_{n+1\leq\mu\leq M}\overline{Y}_{i}^{\mu}\right)\right].\end{split}

By (
5.37
)-(
5.38
), first using the independence in

and then integrating the variables

,

\mathfrak{p}_{p,N}(\lambda)\leq e^{-\lambda t}\left\{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\cosh\left(tN^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(X_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{p-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\left|X_{i}^{\mu}\right|\leq\gamma(N)\right\}}\right)\right]\right\}^{M-n}.

(5.51)

Next, the expectation within braces in (
5.51
) is equal to

(5.52)

where

\mathfrak{e}_{p,N}(t)\equiv{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\left|X_{i}^{\mu}\right|\leq\gamma(N)\right\}}\cosh\left(tN^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(X_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{p-1}\right)\right].

(5.53)

We first deal with

and that

(5.54)

for all

as defined in (

\begin{split}\mathfrak{e}_{p,N}(t)&=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N}:|x|\leq\gamma(N)}\left[1+\frac{1}{2}(1+o(1))tN^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}x^{2(p-1)}\right]{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{i}^{\mu}=x\right)\\
&\leq 1+\frac{1}{2}(1+o(1))t^{2}N^{-(p-1)}\sigma_{N,2(p-1)}\leq e^{\frac{1}{2}(1+o(1))t^{2}N^{-(p-1)}\sigma_{N,2(p-1)}}.\end{split}

(5.55)

Inserting this bound into (
5.52

1.13
) reads

2\left(\gamma^{(1)}\right)^{p-1}>n_{2}\left(\gamma^{(2)}\right)^{p-1}+\dots+n_{\ell}\left(\gamma^{(\ell)}\right)^{p-1}.

(6.28)

Equivalently,

1>\sum_{r=2}^{\ell-1}k_{r}\left(\prod_{s=2}^{r}\alpha^{(2k_{s})}\right)^{p-1}+(k_{\ell}+{\textstyle{1\over 2}})\left(\prod_{s=2}^{\ell}\alpha^{(2k_{s})}\right)^{p-1}\equiv P+Q.

(6.29)

For
k\geq 2
, recall the estimate on
\alpha^{(2k)}
from (
6.2
) and note that for
k=1
,

\alpha^{(2k)}\big|_{k=1}=\frac{1}{2}<\frac{e^{-\varepsilon_{2k}}}{\sqrt{\pi k}}\big|_{k=1}
. Therefore, we have the bound, valid for all
k\geq 1

\alpha^{(2k)}\leq\frac{e^{-\varepsilon_{2k}}}{\sqrt{\pi k}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi k}}.

(6.30)

With this, with have, for all
k_{s}\geq 1
,
\,2\leq s\leq\ell-1

\begin{split}P&\leq\sum_{r=2}^{\ell-1}\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{p-1}{2}(r-1)}\left(\prod_{s=2}^{r-1}k_{s}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}k_{r}^{\frac{p-3}{2}}},\\
Q&\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{p-1}{2}(\ell-1)}\left(\prod_{s=2}^{\ell-1}k_{s}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}}\left({\textstyle{3\over 2}}k_{\ell}^{-\frac{p-3}{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{k_{\ell}\geq 1\}}+{\textstyle{1\over 2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{k_{\ell}=0\}}\right).\end{split}

(6.31)

We now can see that for
p\geq 3
, the right-hand sides of
P
and
Q
in (
6.31
) are decreasing both in
p
and in each
k_{s}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}P\leq\sum_{r=2}^{\ell-1}\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\right)^{(r-1)},\quad Q\leq\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\right)^{(\ell-1)},\end{split}

(6.32)

and summing the geometric progression

P+Q\leq\frac{1}{\pi-1}\left[1-\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\right)^{(\ell-2)}\right]+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\right)^{(\ell-1)}\leq\frac{1}{\pi-1}+\frac{3}{2\pi}<0.95<1.

(6.33)

From this we conclude that (
6.29
) is satisfied for all
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma^{all}_{n}
and all
n
odd. In the same way, one checks that the remaining
\ell-2
inequalities of the system
{\mathcal{S}}_{n,F}
are satisfied. This yields the claim of the lemma.
∎

We will not state the counterpart of Lemma
6.2
for the dense model, but simply observe that the contribution
to
E_{N,M}(\xi^{(N)}(m))
of the first
n
patterns increases to zero with
p
. More precisely, for all

m\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}_{n,F}=\left\{m(\gamma_{n}):\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma^{all}_{n}\right\}
,
by Theorem
1.2
, proceeding as in the proof of Lemma
6.2
, with
{\mathbb{P}}
-probability one

\begin{split}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{1\leq\nu\leq n}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi^{\nu}_{i}\xi^{(N)}_{i}(m)\right)^{p}=\,&\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}n_{r}\left(\prod_{s=1}^{r}\alpha^{(n_{s})}\right)^{p},\end{split}

(6.34)

and this quantity is bounded from above and below by constants times

\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{pr}{2}}\frac{1}{\left(\prod_{s=1}^{r-1}n_{s}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}n_{r}^{\frac{p-2}{2}}}.

(6.35)

Therefore, the contribution to
E_{N,M}(\xi^{(N)}(m))
of the first
n
patterns as
N\rightarrow\infty
decreases
as
p
decreases. This confirms the observation made in
[
24
]
that, asymptotically as
N\rightarrow\infty
, the energy gap,

\left|\sup_{1\leq\mu\leq M}E_{N,M}(\xi^{\mu})-\inf_{m}E_{N,M}(\xi^{(N)}(m))\right|,

(6.36)

increases as
p
increases, provided that
M
is sufficiently small for the contribution to the energy of the remaining patterns decays to zero.
