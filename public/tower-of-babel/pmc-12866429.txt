The role of postoperative ketamine infusion in ERAS protocols following elective open abdominal surgeries, a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yohannis Derbew Molla, Kidist Hunegn Setargew, Hirut Tesfahun Alemu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEnhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols are multimodal, evidence-based pathways designed to accelerate postoperative recovery and reduce complications. A key component of ERAS is optimizing pain management to minimize opioid reliance. Ketamine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, has demonstrated potential as a multimodal analgesic for reducing opioid consumption and postoperative pain; however, its specific role and efficacy within comprehensive ERAS protocols remain under-evaluated, with variations in dosing and patient response. The current literature lacks a clear consensus on the efficacy of postoperative ketamine infusion for critical ERAS outcomes, including pain control, opioid consumption, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and length of hospital stay. This meta-analysis evaluates the impact of postoperative ketamine infusion on pain, opioid use, and recovery outcomes within ERAS protocols for elective open abdominal surgeries.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted by screening PubMed, Scopus, and EMBASE up to June 2024. Randomized controlled trials involving adult patients undergoing elective open abdominal surgery were included if they compared postoperative ketamine infusion with standard care or placebo within an ERAS setting. The primary outcomes were postoperative pain intensity, measured using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and opioid consumption, converted to morphine equivalents. The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, and evidence quality was graded using the GRADE approach. A random-effects model was applied to account for heterogeneity across studies.ResultsFourteen RCTs involving 1067 patients met inclusion criteria. Ketamine significantly reduced postoperative pain scores (standardized mean difference − 0.53, 95% CI [-0.84, -0.22], p < 0.05) and opioid consumption within the first 24 h post-surgery (standardized mean difference − 0.44, 95% CI [-0.75, -0.13], p < 0.05) compared to control treatments. However, effects on PONV and other adverse events were not statistically significant (Log OR -0.19, 95% CI [-0.81, 0.43], p = 0.55), with considerable heterogeneity across studies (I² = 72.69%).ConclusionPostoperative ketamine infusion within ERAS protocols appears effective in reducing both pain and opioid consumption without significantly impacting PONV or adverse effects. These findings suggest ketamine may be a valuable adjunct in ERAS for elective open abdominal surgeries, though variability in results indicates that optimal dosing and administration strategies require further investigation to maximize patient recovery benefits and minimize side effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03463-x.Keywords: Ketamine, Enhanced recovery after surgery, Open abdominal surgery, Abdominal operation, Elective, Laparotomy, Postoperative pain, Abdomen, Opioid, Adult

Introduction

Over 80% of patients undergoing surgical procedures experience acute postoperative pain, and around 75% of those affected describe the intensity of this pain as moderate, severe, or extreme [1, 2]. Research indicates that fewer than 50% of patients who have surgery experience sufficient relief from postoperative pain [2, 3]. Poorly managed pain can adversely impact quality of life, daily functioning, recovery outcomes, the likelihood of complications following surgery, and the chances of developing chronic pain after the procedure [4].

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) denotes a patient-focused, evidence-informed approach developed by multidisciplinary teams and tailored to specific surgical procedures, with variations in implementation influenced by institutional resources and practices. The primary aim is to minimize the surgical stress response in patients, enhance their physiological functioning, and promote recovery. These care pathways create a cohesive continuum that guides patients from their home environment through various stages: pre-hospital/pre-admission, preoperative, intraoperative, postoperative, and ultimately back home [5, 6].

The management of postoperative pain, along with complications like nausea and vomiting, remains a significant challenge in postoperative care. Addressing these issues is crucial for facilitating early mobilization and ensuring timely discharge, as inadequate symptom control is among the leading reasons patients remain hospitalized after surgery [7]. Opioid analgesics, despite their widely recognized side effects, remain a fundamental component of postoperative pain management. Investigating new analgesics and exploring combinations of existing ones to minimize opioid reliance is an essential focus in acute pain research [4, 8]. Effective management of postoperative pain alleviates suffering, facilitates earlier mobilization, shortens hospital stays, reduces healthcare costs, and enhances patient satisfaction [9–12]. Numerous ERAS protocols currently recommend a narcotic-sparing strategy that includes regular, scheduled administration of NSAIDs and acetaminophen [13, 14].

Ketamine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is a valuable adjunct in multimodal analgesia due to its unique pharmacological properties [15, 16]. Low-dose ketamine infusion has shown notable analgesic advantages across different surgical scenarios, with research indicating decreases in postoperative pain levels and overall reliance on opioids [17]. The opioid-sparing properties of ketamine are especially pertinent within ERAS protocols, where the aim is to reduce opioid consumption to lessen the potential risks associated with opioid-related side effects, including sedation, respiratory depression, and gastrointestinal issues [18].

Moreover, the potential advantages of ketamine go beyond merely managing pain. In postoperative contexts, its administration has been linked to a lower occurrence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), probably because it leads to a decrease in opioid use, an important factor in PONV [19]. Additionally, reduced reliance on opioids correlates with quicker functional recovery and possibly shorter hospital stays, which aligns with the ERAS objectives of facilitating early discharge and promoting efficient recovery [9]. While these encouraging results have emerged, the specific role and standardization of ketamine use within ERAS protocols remain inconsistently defined across studies and guidelines [20].

Consequently, this systematic review and meta-analysis seek to assess the effects of postoperative ketamine infusion incorporated into ERAS protocols on important outcomes, such as pain management, opioid-sparing benefits, incidence of PONV, duration of hospital stay, and overall recovery results after elective open abdominal surgeries. By synthesizing data from various studies, we seek to clarify the efficacy and potential advantages of ketamine infusion within ERAS protocols, thereby providing a comprehensive evidence base that may inform future recommendations and enhance current ERAS guidelines. While ERAS protocols apply to a wide range of surgical contexts, this review specifically focuses on elective, open abdominal surgeries in adult patients, as these were the populations studied in the included randomized trials.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was guided by the methodological principles described in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses, and our results were reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Fig. 1) [21].

Protocol registration

This systematic review protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) under registration number CRD420251047361. The protocol is publicly available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251047361. No amendments were made to the original registered protocol during the review. A detailed protocol document was not uploaded to the PROSPERO record; however, eligibility criteria, search strategy, and planned analyses were established a priori and consistently applied throughout the review process.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was performed in PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Google Scholar up to June 2024. Controlled vocabulary terms (MeSH in PubMed and Emtree in EMBASE) and free-text keywords were combined using Boolean operators. The final search string used in PubMed was as follows:

(“Ketamine”[MeSH Terms] OR ketamine[tiab] OR “postoperative ketamine”) AND (“Enhanced Recovery After Surgery” OR ERAS OR “enhanced recovery”[tiab]) AND (“Postoperative Pain” OR “Pain, Postoperative”[MeSH Terms]) AND (“Abdominal Surgery” OR “Laparotomy” OR “Open Surgery”) AND (randomized controlled trial OR RCT). Similar search strategies were adapted for Scopus, EMBASE, and Google Scholar. The reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and included trials were also screened manually to identify any additional eligible studies. No language or publication date restrictions were applied. The search was conducted using combinations of terms in titles, abstracts, and keywords, rather than full-text search, to ensure reproducibility. The term ‘postoperative ketamine infusion’ was included to capture studies focusing on postoperative administration. Studies that qualified included randomized clinical trials assessing the effects of postoperative ketamine in comparison to a control group, with at least one reported outcome (Table 1). We included “postoperative ketamine” to better capture trials focusing on the continuation or initiation of ketamine administration after surgical closure.

Eligibility criteria and analysis

The search strategy was structured around the PICO framework: Population (P): Adults (≥ 18 years) undergoing elective open abdominal surgery, Intervention (I): Postoperative ketamine infusion (initiated after wound closure or in the PACU), Comparator (C): Placebo, standard ERAS care without ketamine, or other analgesic strategies, Outcomes (O): Postoperative pain (VAS or equivalent), opioid consumption, PONV, and adverse effects. These elements informed the selection of search terms and Boolean combinations used across databases. For this review, we defined “postoperative ketamine infusion” as ketamine administered after the surgical procedure, either initiated in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) or continued immediately after wound closure.

The trials were evaluated, and the following data points were extracted: study design, publication year, number of patients, pain assessment methodologies, types of rescue narcotics utilized, and adverse outcomes observed. Most studies documented pain assessment using the VAS, where pain was measured on a scale from 0 (indicating no pain) to 10 (indicating maximum pain). In instances where pain ratings were recorded on a 0–100 scale, these scores were converted to a 0–10 scale for consistency. However, studies that used the verbal reported scale were excluded.

Primary outcomes were predefined as a 24-hour cumulative opioid consumption (converted to morphine milligram equivalents, MME), Pain scores at 24 h postoperatively (VAS 0–10). These time points were selected a priori because they align with ERAS milestones for early recovery and are consistently reported in elective open abdominal surgery trials. Where studies reported multiple time points, 24-hour data were extracted to standardize comparisons. Most trials reported cumulative narcotic consumption; therefore, we compared the 24-hour total narcotic doses between the ketamine group and the control group (Control groups received either placebo or standardized ERAS care (defined as protocolized multimodal analgesia without ketamine, typically including multimodal analgesia, early mobilization, PONV prophylaxis). Given that different narcotics were utilized as rescue analgesics (morphine, fentanyl, dextropropoxyphene, etc.) in the trials, we standardized all narcotics to morphine equivalents for comparison based on recent recommendations [37]. The visual analog scale and cumulative narcotic doses were presented graphically in only a subset of studies. We made efforts to extract the values from the accessible graphs utilizing GetData Graph Digitizer [38].

Secondary outcomes encompass PONV and drug-related adverse effects, significant complications that may influence patient comfort and recovery. Furthermore, patient satisfaction is measured to gain insights into the overall experience and quality of care provided during the postoperative phase, although it is rarely reported in the included studies.

The meta-analysis was conducted using the STATA 17 software package, specifically the metaphor module. Due to the presence of clinical heterogeneity, as indicated by the Q-test, among the studies—which included variations in initial dosages and dosage strategies—a random effects model was employed. The significance level was established at 0.05. The studies were aggregated under the assumption of a common variance component across the different studies [39]. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic, with values exceeding 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity [21]. Additionally, the possibility of publication bias was investigated through funnel plots when there were 10 or more trials available for a specific outcome.

The findings from the meta-analysis studies were presented as the standardized mean difference accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for continuous outcomes such as opioid consumption and VAS scores. For binary outcomes, specifically postoperative nausea and vomiting, and drug-related adverse effects, the relative risk was reported along with 95% CIs. The use of standardized mean difference, rather than the original mean difference, was implemented to maintain a consistent scale of outcomes across the various studies. This is to account for the fact that morphine consumption and VAS varied significantly across the studies selected in this meta-analysis. The minimally clinically significant difference (MCID) for postoperative pain intensity was defined as a reduction of 1.0 on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), based on established guidelines for acute pain management. Predefined MCIDs were used to contextualize SMDs: For VAS scores, an MCID of 1.0 (on a 0–10 scale) corresponds to an SMD of ~ 0.50, based on pooled standard deviations (SDs) from included studies (mean SD = 2.0; MCID/SD = 1.0/2.0 = 0.50) [40]. The 20% reduction threshold in morphine milligram equivalents (MME) was considered clinically meaningful based on prior studies in postoperative analgesia. To translate this into SMD, we used pooled standard deviations from included studies (mean SD ≈ 40% of the mean opioid dose). Therefore, 20% ÷ 40% ≈ 0.5, providing an approximate equivalence between a 20% clinical reduction and an SMD of 0.5 [41]. SMDs ≥ 0.50 were thus interpreted as clinically significant. These thresholds were defined to allow evaluation of whether the reported outcomes were both statistically and clinically meaningful.

Strength of evidence

The strength of evidence for each outcome was assessed in accordance with the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) working group statement, utilizing the GRADEpro guideline development tool (McMaster University, 2015, created by Evidence Prime, Inc., Canada) [42]. The GRADE approach assessed the quality of evidence on a continuum ranging from high to moderate, low, or very low for each outcome, following a structured methodology. Results

14 randomized controlled trials were included in the overall systematic review after relevant literature review, involving a total of 1067 (510 patients on the ketamine group and 557 on the control group) patients [22–35]. The standardized background postoperative analgesic regimen—common to both ketamine and control groups—varied across studies, ranging from systemic opioid-based patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) to epidural or multimodal techniques (Supplementary Table 2).

Postoperative pain intensity

The meta-analysis examines the effects of ketamine on postoperative pain at 24 h postoperatively, as assessed by Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, in patients undergoing elective open abdominal surgery, relative to control treatments, encompassing 14 studies [22–35]. The aggregated overall effect reveals an effect size of -0.53, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [-0.84, -0.22]. This finding is statistically significant but only marginally clinically meaningful, suggesting that ketamine is effective in alleviating pain (reflected by lower VAS scores) during the postoperative phase when compared to control interventions. GRADE: low certainty, wide CI, and high heterogeneity suggest the true effect may be modest.

Nonetheless, there exists significant heterogeneity among the studies, reflected by an I² value of 82.33%. This variability is further corroborated by a significant Q-test (p = 0.00), indicating that such differences are unlikely to be attributed to chance alone (Figs. 2 and 3).

Postoperative opioid consumption

The meta-analysis assessing the effect of ketamine on average opioid consumption within the first 24 h following elective open abdominal surgery, in comparison to control treatments, comprised 11 studies [22–27, 29–33]. The overall pooled effect shows an effect size of -0.44 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [-0.75, -0.13], suggesting that ketamine notably decreases opioid use in the early postoperative phase relative to the control group. GRADE: low certainty.

Nonetheless, there is also considerable heterogeneity among the studies, as evidenced by an I² value of 75.98%, which reflects significant variability in the impact of ketamine on opioid reduction across different investigations. This finding is further corroborated by a substantial Q-test result (p = 0.00), indicating that the variability observed is unlikely to be attributed to random chance (Figs. 4 and 5).

Postoperative nausea and vomiting and adverse effects

The overall odds ratios (OR) for adverse effects, including PONV, between the ketamine and control groups during the first 24 h following elective open abdominal surgery reveal a log OR of -0.19, accompanied by a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [-0.81, 0.43] ( [22–24, 26–33, 35]. This result suggests that there is no statistically significant difference in adverse effects between the ketamine and control groups within this period. Nonetheless, significant variability exists among the studies, indicated by an I² value of 72.69%. Subgroup analysis by type of surgery

To assess whether the efficacy of postoperative ketamine differs by surgery type, we performed a subgroup analysis comparing studies involving gynecologic abdominal surgeries versus other major abdominal procedures (e.g., gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary). In the gynecologic subgroup, ketamine was associated with a statistically significant reduction in both postoperative pain and opioid consumption, with high heterogeneity (I² = 74.91%). In contrast, non-gynecologic abdominal surgeries exhibited a broader range of outcomes and substantial heterogeneity (I² = 96.76%). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (Q = 10.02, p = 0.0016), suggesting that surgery type may influence ketamine’s analgesic efficacy. We performed sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. Specifically, we conducted leave-one-out analyses for both pain and opioid consumption outcomes, sequentially removing each study to determine its influence on the pooled effect size. The results showed no single study unduly influenced the overall estimates.

Discussion

The role of ketamine within ERAS protocols remains ambiguous despite its potential benefits. While ketamine has shown efficacy in reducing postoperative pain and opioid consumption, critical components of ERAS pathways, its integration into these protocols is hindered by inconsistencies in study designs, dosing regimens, and patient selection criteria. The existing literature highlights significant variability in its impact on key ERAS outcomes such as PONV, time to mobilization, and hospital length of stay [9, 44]. Furthermore, most studies do not explicitly evaluate ketamine within the context of comprehensive ERAS protocols, which emphasize a multimodal and synergistic approach to recovery.

A comparison of our findings with other systematic reviews focusing on the effects of postoperative ketamine in abdominal surgeries indicates both consistent advantages and significant discrepancies in outcomes. These differences can be influenced by variations in patient demographics, dosing of ketamine, and the type of procedures. The considerable heterogeneity among studies presents a notable limitation when conducting a systematic review of this subject. We employed two strategies to explore the heterogeneity of RCTs more comprehensively. We sought to reduce heterogeneity by restricting the review to RCTs that administered postoperative ketamine. Second, we identified the most severe pain documented in each study to assess whether the severity of pain influences the efficacy of ketamine.

Across the included RCTs, ketamine administration varied notably in both dosage and duration. Most studies used continuous postoperative infusions typically initiated in the recovery room and continued for 12–24 h. Others employed short-term bolus dosing or combined intraoperative and postoperative infusions, reflecting inconsistency in timing and delivery methods. Additionally, multimodal analgesic regimens differed across studies: some incorporated NSAIDs and acetaminophen, whereas others used local anesthetic infiltration, epidural morphine, or adjunctive lidocaine. Variation in antiemetic prophylaxis likely contributed to heterogeneity in postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) outcomes. These discrepancies collectively explain part of the between-study variability in pain reduction and opioid-sparing effects observed in our analysis.

Postoperative pain intensity

Our results regarding postoperative pain alleviation align with other reviews concentrating on abdominal surgeries. In a systematic review conducted by Bell et al. (2020) [45] examining the use of ketamine following abdominal procedures, a decrease in VAS pain scores was also documented. This finding is analogous to our observed effect size of -0.53 (95% CI [-0.84, -0.22]). Bell et al. noted a somewhat reduced heterogeneity (I² approximately 60%), which may be attributed to more strict inclusion criteria that restricted variability in both surgical types and dosing protocols. This highlights that while ketamine demonstrates strong efficacy in managing postoperative pain, differences in study design and patient demographics may lead to varying effect sizes across different reviews.

Postoperative opioid consumption

Our analysis of ketamine’s opioid-sparing effect is consistent with the findings of Laskowski et al. [46], who noted moderate decreases in opioid use among patients undergoing abdominal surgery. Their calculated pooled effect size closely resembles our own result of -0.44 (95% CI [-0.75, -0.13]). Both our review and the study by Laskowski et al. observed significant heterogeneity (I² > 70%), suggesting a considerable degree of variability, which is likely affected by factors including variations in perioperative ketamine administration protocols, dosing practices, and the use of concurrent analgesic regimens. Two studies indicated that ketamine not only decreased the need for opioids but also demonstrated greater effectiveness in patients with elevated baseline opioid consumption. This suggests that individual patient factors, including previous opioid usage, could affect the degree of ketamine’s ability to reduce opioid requirements [47, 48].

The efficacy of ketamine appears modulated by two key factors: the type of surgery and the use of concomitant regional analgesia. Our subgroup analysis suggests a more pronounced analgesic effect in gynecologic abdominal surgeries compared to other major abdominal procedures. This may relate to differences in surgical stress, incision characteristics, or patient populations. Furthermore, the background analgesic regimen is critical. The majority of included studies utilized systemic opioid-based analgesia without routine neuraxial or regional blocks. In this context, ketamine provides a valuable opioid-sparing adjunct. In contrast, its additive benefit when superimposed on a robust regional analgesic foundation (e.g., epidural, TAP block) remains uncertain and is likely to be smaller, as the potential for further pain reduction diminishes. Therefore, ketamine’s role within an ERAS protocol may be most impactful in surgical pathways reliant on systemic multimodal analgesia or for patients in whom regional techniques are contraindicated or insufficient.

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and adverse effects

Regarding postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), the pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference between ketamine and control groups. In contrast to our study, which did not identify a statistically significant decrease in adverse effects such as PONV, other reviews, including those by Niesters et al., have indicated that ketamine may alleviate PONV in specific patient populations, although the results can vary widely [48]. The observed differences may arise from variations in ketamine dosing and inconsistencies in the use of adjunct anti-emetics across the studies. They indicated that administering lower doses of ketamine in conjunction with multimodal antiemetic strategies could potentially affect PONV outcomes. The high heterogeneity we noted (I² = 72.69%) underscores this variability, suggesting that specific factors related to each study might influence the impact of ketamine on adverse outcomes. An important source of heterogeneity is the variable baseline use of systemic opioids across the included protocols. While all studies aimed for multimodal, ERAS-consistent analgesia, the extent of opioid minimization differed. This variation directly impacts the interpretation of PONV outcomes, as the background opioid dose can be a primary driver of PONV. Therefore, ketamine’s potential to reduce PONV can be intrinsically linked to its ability to lower this opioid dose, an effect that may be more pronounced in protocols with higher baseline opioid use.

Patient satisfaction and time to mobilization

Limited reviews specifically examine the effects of ketamine on patient satisfaction or the time it takes for patients to mobilize following elective open abdominal surgery. Hung et al. noted that ketamine enhances patient-reported satisfaction scores in the immediate postoperative period; however, this effect tends to decrease after 24 to 48 h [49]. In terms of mobilization timing, studies such as those conducted by Laskowski et al. did not demonstrate a significant improvement with ketamine. This suggests that the advantages of ketamine are mainly associated with early pain relief and a decrease in opioid usage, rather than contributing to long-term functional recovery or mobilization outcomes [46].

Consequently, the administration of ketamine after elective open abdominal surgery did not elevate the risk of PONV or drowsiness. Furthermore, ketamine has been shown to reduce the occurrence of hyperalgesia [25], postpartum depression [23], and disturbances in postoperative sleep [50]. Despite the increasing use of ketamine for pain management following abdominal surgery, the optimal dosage remains undetermined. Therefore, further clinical research is necessary to identify the ideal dosage of ketamine.

The primary outcomes of this review—pain intensity and opioid consumption—are not merely isolated endpoints but are established critical mediators of functional recovery. Severe pain and opioid side effects (sedation, nausea, ileus) are dominant barriers to early mobilization and timely discharge, which are core objectives of ERAS protocols. Therefore, an intervention that significantly improves these analgesic outcomes provides a plausible and powerful mechanism for facilitating the broader ERAS recovery trajectory. While direct evidence from the included trials on mobilization and length of stay is limited, the consistent reduction in the opioid burden demonstrated here suggests ketamine can mitigate key opioid-related impediments to recovery.

While ketamine offers various advantages in ERAS protocols, its use is not without difficulties. Although it is generally well-tolerated at sub-anesthetic doses, ketamine has been linked to psychotomimetic side effects, including hallucinations and dysphoria, which may affect patient comfort and satisfaction [51]. Nonetheless, these adverse effects tend to be dose-dependent, and research indicates that the low-dose ketamine utilized in ERAS protocols is associated with a minimal occurrence of such negative effects [52].

A further limitation of ketamine infusion within ERAS protocols is the inconsistency in dosing regimens reported in various studies, which hinders efforts for standardization and comparison of outcomes. Another important source of heterogeneity in this review lies in the timing and administration protocol of ketamine. While all included studies incorporated ketamine as part of postoperative care, initiation times ranged from immediately after surgical closure to early postoperative recovery in the PACU. In some cases, intraoperative administration was followed by continuation into the postoperative period. These variations may influence both analgesic efficacy and the incidence of adverse effects.

Moreover, the majority of studies included in systematic reviews have concentrated on particular surgical populations, such as patients undergoing thoracic or orthopedic procedures, thereby restricting the applicability of the findings to other surgical types [53–55]. With the increasing adoption of ERAS protocols in diverse surgical specialties, it is essential to conduct further research to identify the most effective dosing and administration strategies for ketamine. This will help maximize its benefits while reducing potential risks.

In summary, our systematic review is consistent with the prevailing agreement found in similar studies, indicating that ketamine alleviates postoperative pain and decreases opioid usage after elective open abdominal surgery, despite the observed variability in these effects. The variations in outcomes related to PONV and adverse effects highlight the necessity for standardized dosing and supplementary protocols. In summary, although ketamine demonstrates potential as a component of a multimodal strategy for pain management, the inconsistent results observed in various reviews suggest that its effective application may necessitate customization according to individual patient characteristics and surgical considerations to enhance its postoperative advantages in elective open abdominal surgery.

It is important to contextualize the ERAS framework. While the term ‘ERAS’ formalizes a bundle of evidence-based practices, the core objectives—minimizing surgical stress, optimizing physiology, and accelerating recovery—have long been guiding principles in perioperative care. The multimodal analgesic strategies evaluated in some of the included studies, even if not explicitly conducted under a modern ‘ERAS’ protocol label, directly align with these objectives. Therefore, the evidence synthesized here on ketamine’s opioid-sparing and analgesic efficacy is directly applicable to the analgesic component of contemporary ERAS pathways.

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis have clinical implications for the integration of ketamine into ERAS protocols for elective open abdominal surgeries. ERAS emphasizes multimodal analgesia to minimize opioid use, enhance recovery, and reduce postoperative complications. Ketamine’s demonstrated ability to reduce both postoperative pain and opioid consumption aligns with the core goals of ERAS protocols. While our analysis did not show a direct, statistically significant effect on time to mobilization or hospital length of stay, the significant reduction in opioid use is a critical intermediate outcome. By mitigating key barriers to recovery such as opioid-induced sedation, ileus, and respiratory depression, ketamine has the potential to facilitate and support a faster recovery process. Future studies designed with functional recovery as a primary endpoint are needed to confirm whether this pharmacological potential translates into measurable improvements in mobilization and length of stay.

Despite these benefits, the variability in ketamine’s impact on postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and other adverse effects limits its broader application. Furthermore, the potential sedative effects of ketamine raise an important consideration for its use in ERAS pathways, which prioritize early mobilization. Our analysis found no significant improvement in time to mobilization, suggesting that the sedative properties might counterbalance the mobilization benefits afforded by superior analgesia. This underscores the importance of using low, sub-anesthetic doses to maximize the analgesic benefit while minimizing sedation, a key area for future dose-finding studies. For ERAS protocols to fully leverage ketamine, future studies should explore optimal dosing regimens, timing of administration, and patient selection criteria to maximize its benefits while minimizing risks. Furthermore, the lack of significant improvements in secondary outcomes, such as patient mobilization and length of hospital stay, highlights the need for a more comprehensive evaluation of ketamine’s role in achieving broader ERAS objectives. Addressing these gaps could help guide clinicians in incorporating ketamine more effectively into ERAS pathways. Future trials should adopt procedure-specific designs with harmonized perioperative protocols to better isolate the effect of ketamine.

Recommendations for future research

To definitively establish the role of ketamine within ERAS protocols and address the limitations identified in this review, future studies require a more standardized and nuanced design. First, there is a critical need to shift the primary focus from surrogate endpoints to standardized, patient-centered functional outcomes. Time to readiness for hospital discharge, based on achieving specific functional milestones, would be a more meaningful endpoint than length of stay alone. Second, research must move beyond heterogeneous regimens to identify the optimal dosing and timing. Head-to-head comparisons of different strategies for instance, a single bolus versus a continuous infusion are needed to identify the minimal effective dose, while the optimal timing of initiation (intra-operative versus immediate post-operative) also requires clarification. Furthermore, given the variability in response, future trials should aim to identify targeted patient populations that derive the most benefit, potentially by stratifying enrollment based on factors such as preoperative opioid use, or surgical complexity. To build a robust safety profile, comprehensive safety reporting is non-negotiable; all trials must prospectively monitor and standardize the reporting of ketamine-specific adverse events using validated scales. Finally, to ensure clinical relevance, studies should be conducted within a well-defined and audited ERAS protocol, which will clarify ketamine’s true additive benefit within a multimodal context rather than as an isolated intervention. Addressing these specific areas will provide the clarity needed to guide the formal integration of ketamine into future ERAS guidelines.

Conclusion

Ketamine shows promise as an adjunctive analgesic in postoperative pain management, with statistically significant reductions in pain scores and opioid consumption within the first 24 h. However, variability in ERAS protocol adherence across studies warrants cautious interpretation. Furthermore, while the reductions were statistically significant, their clinical significance remains uncertain due to the lack of predefined minimally clinically significant differences. Clinicians should consider ketamine as part of a multimodal approach tailored to individual patient needs, while future research should focus on refining its application to enhance overall recovery outcomes and align with ERAS principles. Future studies are needed to evaluate ketamine’s broader role in ERAS pathways and its impact on recovery outcomes beyond pain and opioid use.
