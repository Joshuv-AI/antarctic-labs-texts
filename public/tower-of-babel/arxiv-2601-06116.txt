Structure-Aware Diversity Pursuit as an AI Safety Strategy against Homogenization
Ian Rios-Sialer

Abstract
Generative AI models reproduce the biases in the training data and can further amplify them through mode collapse. We refer to the resulting harmful loss of diversity as homogenization. Our position is that homogenization should be a primary concern in AI safety. We introduce xeno-reproduction as the strategy that mitigates homogenization. For auto-regressive LLMs, we formalize xeno-reproduction as a structure-aware diversity pursuit. Our contribution is foundational, intended to open an essential line of research and invite collaboration to advance diversity.

Theoretical Framework

3.1
LLMs as trees of strings

Let
\{t_{a},t_{b},\ldots\}
denote the finite token alphabet, with special tokens
\bot
(start-of-sequence) and
\top
(end-of-sequence). A
string
is a finite sequence of tokens beginning with
\bot
; a
trajectory
is a string ending with
\top
. We write
prompts
,
continuations
, and
trajectories
as:

\displaystyle x_{p}

\displaystyle=\bot t_{1}\ldots t_{p}

\displaystyle x_{p+k}

\displaystyle=x_{p}t_{p+1}\ldots t_{p+k}

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=x_{T}=x_{T-1}\top

We denote the set of strings that are
continuations
of a prompt string
x_{p}
as
\mathsf{Str}(x_{p})
. The
unprompted
scenario corresponds to
x_{p}=\bot
. Then, we write the set of all strings as
\mathsf{Str}:=\mathsf{Str}(\bot)
. Similarly, we denote the set of strings that are
trajectories
of a prompt string
x_{p}
as
\mathsf{Str}_{\top}(x_{p})\subseteq\mathsf{Str}(x_{p})
, and the set of all trajectories as
\mathsf{Str}_{\top}:=\mathsf{Str}_{\top}(\bot)\subseteq\mathsf{Str}
.

Any LLM induces a tree on
\mathsf{Str}
: the root is
\bot
, each node is a string, the leaves are trajectories, and the edges connect strings by their next-token continuations with probability
p(t_{p+1}|x_{p})
. Probabilities chain and decompose as
p(y|x_{p})=p(x_{p+k}|x_{p})p(y|x_{p+k})
.

For any prompt
x
, we have a
probability mass function
on the trajectories for
any
particular prompt
[
17
]
. For simplicity, we assume all
terminal
strings
finish
within a
finite context window

8
8

8

This is a simplifying assumption for exposition. To be fully precise, we would instead formulate this as
y\in Terminating(x_{p})
where
\mathsf{Str}_{\top}(x_{p})\subseteq Terminating(x_{p})

Appendix Appendix B

In this appendix, we explore how our theoretical framework connects to other frameworks.
To that purpose, we consider an

\displaystyle\Lambda_{*}(x):=(\alpha_{*}(x))\qquad\alpha_{*}(x)\in\{0,1\}

Then, the structure core represents the probability of compliance being exactly

\mu:=\!\langle\alpha_{*}\rangle=\sum_{\mathclap{c\in\{0,1\}}}c\,\Pr(\alpha\!=\!c)=\Pr(\alpha\!=\!1)

Our singleton deviance is expressed as:

B.1

To calculate the

, we consider two choices for

\mathbb{E}[|\alpha_{*}-\mu|]=2\mu(1-\mu)=\mathrm{GS}

\mathbb{E}[\|\alpha_{*}-\mu\|^{2}_{2}]=\operatorname{Var}[\alpha_{*}]=\mu(1-\mu)=\frac{\mathrm{GS}}{2}

If we interpret
\mathrm{GS}
as the
degree of mixing
in outcomes, then increasing the expected deviance drives
heterogeneity
rather than

.

Appendix Appendix C
Trade-off between diversity and fairness

In this appendix, we show the fundamental tension between diversity and fairness by proving the existence of a
Pareto
trade-off between them. A Pareto trade-off says that among
efficient solutions
, improvement on one criterion
necessarily worsens
the other
[
32
]
. To establish such a trade-off, it suffices to exhibit two efficient solutions, each of which is better than the other on a different criterion. This shows that no single solution can
dominate
both.

Taking into account
\rho_{d}
and
\rho_{f}
defined as in
Equation 17
and
Equation 21
with a
\lambda
-weights and
\beta
-tunable-parameter set to
1.0\;
:

Definition C.1

(Pareto Dominance)
.

An intervention
w

Pareto-dominates
intervention
w^{\prime}
if
\rho_{d}(w)\geq\rho_{d}(w^{\prime})
and
\rho_{f}(w)\geq\rho_{f}(w^{\prime})
with at least one strict inequality.

Theorem C.2

(Trade-off Between Diversity and Fairness)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
w_{0}
induce a non-uniform baseline core with
\langle\alpha_{n}\rangle(w_{0})<1/n<\langle\alpha_{1}\rangle(w_{0})
.
Then there exist interventions
w_{d},w_{f}
such that neither Pareto-dominates the other:

\rho_{d}(w_{d})>\rho_{d}(w_{f})\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{f}(w_{d})<\rho_{f}(w_{f})

(C.1)

This demonstrates the existence of a
fundamental trade-off
between the two criteria.

Proof.

We construct two interventions that exhibit opposite strengths.

For simplicity, we assume
deterministic
generation
; adding stochasticity would only increase
\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{diverge}}
and strengthen the trade-off
14
14

14

For deterministic generation, the core equals the sole trajectory’s compliance, so both the expected deviance and its variance are zero.
. Proving our trade-off requires just one counterexample to dominance, so our deterministic pair suffices.

We also say
w_{0}
induces a non-uniform system core
\langle\Lambda_{n}\rangle(w_{0})=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
and assume that
\|\cdot\|_{\theta}
is the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}

Let
w_{d}
induce
\langle\Lambda_{n}\rangle(w_{d})=(0,\ldots,0,1)
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{d})\;

\displaystyle=\;\sqrt{(1-\mu_{n})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\mu_{i})^{2}}\kern 5.0pt>0

\displaystyle\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{diverge}}(w_{d})\;

\displaystyle=\;0\qquad\text{(deterministic)}

\displaystyle\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{even}}(w_{d})\;

\displaystyle=\;H(0,\ldots,0,1)=0

\displaystyle\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{inverted}}(w_{d})\;

\displaystyle=\;1\quad\text{(all pairwise orderings change)}

Thus:

\rho_{d}(w_{d})=\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{d})>0\qquad\rho_{f}(w_{d})=1

Let
w_{f}

. Then:

\displaystyle\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{f})\;

\displaystyle=\;\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1/n-\mu_{i})^{2}}\kern 5.0pt>0

\displaystyle\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{diverge}}(w_{f})\;

\displaystyle=\;0\quad\text{(deterministic)}

\displaystyle\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{even}}(w_{f})\;

\displaystyle=\;H(1/n,\ldots,1/n)=\log n

\displaystyle\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{inverted}}(w_{f})\;

\displaystyle=\;1\quad\text{(all orderings flatten)}

Thus:

\rho_{d}(w_{f})=\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{f})>0\qquad\rho_{f}(w_{f})=1+\log n

We now check if
\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{d})>\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{f})\;
. We assume the inequality and verify if it holds:

assuming

\displaystyle:\quad\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{d})>\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{f})

squaring

\displaystyle:\quad(1-\mu_{n})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\mu_{i})^{2}>\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1/n-\mu_{i})^{2}

rearranging

\displaystyle:\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mu_{i}>(n-1)\left(\mu_{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)

Since
\mu_{n}<1/n\leq 1/2
for
n\geq 2
, the right-hand side is negative. Since
\mu_{1}>1/n>0
and
\mu_{i}\in[0,1]
, the left-hand side is positive. Thus,
\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{d})>\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{explore}}(w_{f})
holds. Since
\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{diverge}}(w_{0})=\mathsf{score}_{\mathsf{diverge}}(w_{f})=0
, we conclude
\rho_{d}(w_{d})>\rho_{d}(w_{f})\;
.

Noting

\rho_{f}(w_{d})<\rho_{f}(w_{f})\;
.

Since
\rho_{d}(w_{d})>\rho_{d}(w_{f})
and
\rho_{f}(w_{d})<\rho_{f}(w_{f})
