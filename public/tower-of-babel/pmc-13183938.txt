Virulence insights from ancient strep A
Authors: Ananya Dash, Christopher N LaRock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNot many pathogens are as uniquely adapted as Streptococcus pyogenes to cause a variety of infections ranging from strep throat to serious flesh-eating skin infections. Yet, scientists, to date, have had limited knowledge into the extent of diseases strep caused in the past. In a new study, Valverde et al. found the signature of strep in the tooth of a mummified individual from a sea of sequencing data and traced the past of the pathogen.

Infections by Streptococcus pyogenes (group A Streptococcus or strep) have long plagued humanity. Throughout the centuries, mild infections like ‘strep throat’ pharyngitis have been common, punctuated by periodic epidemics of scarlet fever with high mortality. The advent of sanitation and antibiotics in the past decades have decreased the incidence, while surveillance during this shows that a range of genetic changes is associated with increased capacities to cause serious disease1. Yet, we have no clue about what strep was like in the pre-Columbian Americas: was it present, and was it asymptomatic, or could it cause severe disease?

In a recent study2, Valverde and colleagues launched an ancient DNA sampling campaign of mummies from the Late Intermediate Period (1100–1450 AD) at the National Museum of Archaeology (MUNARQ) in La Paz, Bolivia. A loose tooth from the last mummy they sampled brought them a surprising discovery: the remains of strep3. The strep genome is fundamentally similar to modern circulating strains and would show up in the clinical lab as positive by the rapid agglutination test and exhibit β-hemolysis on blood agar, hallmarks of group A Streptococcus. Based on sequence, it is closest to modern strains with a propensity toward strep throat infections over skin infections4, as is typical in cool dry climates like the Bolivian highlands. A limited number of strains cause scarlet fever, a serious and transmissible form of pharyngitis. In modern times, this is associated with expression of proinflammatory superantigen toxins5 that were not encoded in the Bolivian sample, possibly indicating a limited ability to cause severe infection.

The sequence, however, places the ancient strep genome in a clade with classical rheumatogenic strains associated with rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease (RHD)6. Autoimmunity during RHD permanently damages the heart valves and is to this day a leading cause of death by strep. But was RHD an issue in the pre-Columbian Americas? Deaths from RHD most commonly occur in young adults, as was the estimated age of the mummy whose tooth harbored strep. The study, therefore, highlights the need to dig deeper into the past of strep to better understand what this pathogen is capable of.
