Key practical aspects of perinatal palliative care: a scoping review mapping and grading their coverage in the literature
Authors: Belén Doucet, Agustín Silberberg, Ana Martín-Ancel, Guadalupe Grimaux, Juana Moure, Albert Balaguer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite growing recognition of the importance of perinatal palliative care (PnPC) as a comprehensive approach to supporting seriously ill fetuses or neonates and their families, its implementation in clinical practice remains nascent and heterogeneous. A synthesis of the literature may clarify which aspects are being addressed and how they are operationalized. We aimed to map how the key practical aspects (KPAs) of PnPC are reported in the literature and to assess the level of detail with which they are described.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review of PubMed in May 2025. We included articles that described, analyzed, or conceptualized practical aspects of PnPC programs or dedicated units. Following independent screening, 137 articles were included. We developed a KPA coding framework informed by landmark publications and guideline documents, and rated the depth of coverage for each KPA as 0 (not mentioned), 1 (mentioned), or 2 (discussed).ResultsThe proposed KPA coding framework comprised 42 items across seven domains. The most frequently mentioned KPAs included the composition and competencies of the interdisciplinary core team (80% of included articles), patient- and family-centered care, and support for memory-making practices. However, substantial variability was observed in the depth of coverage across KPAs. Patient- and family-centered care and shared decision-making were the most consistently discussed in depth (≥ 25% of articles). In contrast, internal organizational processes—including emotional support for healthcare professionals (14%), continuous quality-improvement systems (14%), and release from other clinical responsibilities during the dying process (1%)—were infrequently reported and rarely examined analytically. The role of the extended interdisciplinary team was discussed with limited depth compared with that of the core team.ConclusionsThe PnPC literature appears more developed in relational and family-centered approaches and in defining the interdisciplinary core team. However, the formalization of organizational processes, structured support for healthcare professionals, and system-level consolidation remain limited. These findings suggest that current priorities should focus less on identifying new care components and more on organizing already recognized elements into standardized, evaluable structures, including internal coordination processes, team support mechanisms, and the systematic integration of psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions of care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02188-9.

Background

Perinatal Palliative Care (PnPC) can be defined as a multi-component approach to caring for fetuses or neonates with a serious condition during the perinatal period, as well as their mother/partner, families, and involved healthcare providers, aimed at relieving pain, controlling symptoms, and improving the quality of care and overall well-being of fetuses and infants and all those involved. It is holistic, family-centered, comprehensive, and multidimensional, addressing not only physical needs but also psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions [1–3]. This type of care emphasizes quality of life, symptom management, skilled communication, the management of anticipatory grief, and continuous support for families when there is a known or suspected adverse pregnancy outcome or a fetal condition with a limited or uncertain prognosis for life [4–6]. Although the concept of PnPC is not new, its practical implementation in clinical care is still in its early stages [7–9].

The perinatal setting introduces considerable complexity into practice due to the wide variety of possible scenarios, the different recipients of care (the pregnant woman, the fetus in the antenatal and intrapartum periods, the neonate, the postpartum woman, the partner and the extended family), and the range of professionals involved [2–5]. Each case is highly specific and varies according to the timing and circumstances of these actors and their clinical contexts [1, 6, 7]. Therefore, implementing a PnPC program is challenging. Success depends on securing commitment across professional groups and delivering coordinated support to patients and families facing a novel, time-sensitive situation [3, 10–13]. Barriers to program design, integration, and implementation are driven primarily by organizational and cultural factors rather than material constraints. Overcoming these barriers requires a sustained interdisciplinary approach, continuous care coordination, and comprehensive education for the healthcare team [12, 14–17].

Given this complexity and the challenges of implementation, it is necessary to delineate the program’s key practical aspects to facilitate its uptake in clinical practice. Moreover, a heterogeneous body of work—drawing on expert opinions and working groups—has described core elements of PnPC and highlighted key settings for its application [1, 10, 18].

Accordingly, using a scoping review (ScR) approach, we aimed (1) to identify the key practical aspects of PnPC from the relevant literature, and (2) to evaluate how consistently each aspect is addressed and the level of detail with which it is reported.

Methods

Study selection

A ScR is a type of literature review used to explore and map the breadth of research on a topic, identifying key concepts, definitions, and boundaries within a field. It is particularly useful for summarizing existing evidence and highlighting gaps to inform future research. A ScR on PnPC was conducted using the PubMed database following the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [19]. The search was performed in May 2025, with no lower date limit applied. The full search strategy is provided in the supplementary files (see Supplemental File 1). The ScR aimed to identify articles that described or analyzed the key aspects, elements, components, or foundational concepts of PnPC units or programs. Eligible studies included qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods designs, including observational, interventional, and descriptive studies, as well as relevant reviews providing conceptual or structural insights into PnPC units or programs. No language restrictions were applied.

A total of 325 articles were initially identified. Two authors (BD and AS) independently screened titles and abstracts using Rayyan software. Articles were excluded if they did not focus specifically on PnPC; if PnPC was mentioned only as a therapeutic option without any conceptual development; or if it was addressed solely as an ethical approach. As a result of this screening process, 179 articles were selected. Discrepancies between authors were resolved by consensus and, when necessary, through consultation with two expert reviewers (ABS and AMA). In addition, 3 relevant articles identified through manual reference checking and expert recommendations in PnPC were included [19–22].

Subsequently, full texts were reviewed. At this stage, two additional authors (GG and JM), previously trained by the expert reviewers, joined the process. This step led to the exclusion of further articles, resulting in a final sample of 137 included articles (See Fig. 1. PRISMA-ScR flowchart and Supplemental File 2). A protocol for this ScR was developed a priori to ensure methodological rigor and transparency; however, it was not registered in a publicly accessible database.

Review process of the included studies

In the first stage, we developed a list of key practical aspects (KPAs) of PnPC, based on reference normative documents and key publications. This process was primarily guided by the Clinical Practice Guidelines for Quality Palliative Care issued by the National Coalition for Hospice and Palliative Care (NCP Guidelines) in the United States [18], adapted to the perinatal context by integrating the contributions of Dombrecht et al. [1] and Wilkinson et al. [10]. These two other articles were selected for their methodological and conceptual relevance: Dombrecht et al. conducted an integrative review analyzing core components of PnPC programs, whereas Wilkinson et al. proposed a framework with recommendations to support the integration of palliative care into perinatal medicine. Two authors (BD and AS) independently identified NCP domains and key elements from these sources and synthesized them into a preliminary list of KPAs adapted to PnPC. The KPAs were then organized according to NCP domains, with wording simplified to enhance applicability. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion among all authors.

In the second stage, once a KPA coding framework had been defined (Tables 1 and 2), we conducted a structured analysis process for the 137 included articles with two specific aims: (1) to identify which articles mentioned each KPA, and (2) to assess the extent to which each KPA was discussed in depth. A KPA was considered “mentioned” when it was briefly referenced without further elaboration, and “discussed” when it was explicitly characterized and developed in detail. Each KPA was therefore assigned a score in each article as follows: 0 = not mentioned; 1 = mentioned; and 2 = discussed. For this evaluation, two independent reviewer pairs (BD–AS and GG–JM) assessed 69 and 68 articles, respectively. Each pair first reviewed 10 articles to ensure concordance. During the remaining analysis, regular meetings were held to verify agreement. To enhance objectivity, reviewers systematically highlighted the exact text passages supporting the identification of each KPA in every article, allowing direct comparison of interpretations. Disagreements were resolved through joint reexamination until consensus was reached.

Results

A total of 137 articles were included in the final analysis. (See Fig. 1. PRISMA-ScR flowchart). Following the framework proposed by the NCP, the 42 identified KPAs were grouped into seven categories, as the ethical category—despite its importance—was excluded as a specific focus of this ScR. To enhance readability, the data are divided into two tables (1 and 2).

The most frequently mentioned KPAs concerned the composition and competencies of the core interdisciplinary team, identified in 80% of the included articles. This was followed by references to a patient- and family-centered approach and to support for memory-making practices. KPAs reported in more than 55% of articles are summarized in (Fig. 2).

Several aspects were reported infrequently (< 8% of articles), including team accessibility during the time preceding death, organisational arrangements to free the professional accompanying the dying process from other clinical responsibilities, and support for healthcare professionals’ spiritual and cultural needs and beliefs.

Across all KPAs, the proportion of articles providing in-depth discussion ranged from low (< 10% of articles) to comparatively high (≥ 25%), depending on the specific aspect. The patient- and family-centered approach was the KPA most frequently discussed in detail. Among KPAs that were frequently mentioned, the proportion of articles providing detailed operational descriptions varied across aspects. Discussion

This ScR advances a proposal of 42 key practical aspects (KPAs) of PnPC, derived from a comprehensive synthesis of landmark literature and aligned with the NCP domains [18]. In addition, the ScR examines both the visibility of each KPA within the literature (i.e., frequency of “mentions”) and the depth of its development (i.e., extent of “discussions”). By contrasting thematic prominence with analytical depth, this approach allows a nuanced understanding of which components of PnPC are widely recognized, which have been sufficiently elaborated to support structured clinical practice, and which—despite their acknowledged relevance—remain insufficiently developed as operational processes.

Utilizing a systematic and reproducible methodology, our analysis of 137 included articles suggests a relatively advanced development of patient- and family-centered palliative approaches, shared decision-making, and the interdisciplinary core team—typically comprising professionals from obstetrics, neonatology, nursing, and midwifery [1, 7, 10–11, 18, 23–26]. Conversely, few studies offer an in-depth exploration of internal organizational processes, structured team-support mechanisms, or the systematic integration of psychosocial, spiritual, and cultural dimensions [1, 25, 27–29]. Furthermore, specific elements of end-of-life care—beyond memory-making and immediate bereavement support—are acknowledged but remain less frequently detailed in the literature [2, 10, 31–33]. These findings exhibit strong concordance, from a complementary methodological perspective, with a recent bibliometric analysis of PnPC publications. While the bibliometric study reflects author-identified priorities, our ScR adds an external measure of how consistently topics are substantively developed across the literature [34].

A significant finding is the discrepancy between the most frequently mentioned KPAs and those receiving the most extensive discussion. While four KPAs—the interdisciplinary core team, patient- and family-centered approaches, support for memory-making, and psychological support—showed high visibility (> 65% frequency), our analysis of depth reveals that the literature tends to prioritize extensive discussion primarily for the first two categories, alongside shared decision-making (Fig. 2) despite its lower frequency of mention [1, 6, 35, 36].

The consistent depth afforded to these specific KPAs points toward the predominance of a relational model in PnPC [10, 23–25], grounded in the quality of the relationships formed among clinicians and parents, wherein individual values and preferences are honored during the decision-making processes [1, 10, 11]. However, it is important to note that even these relatively well-developed KPAs are examined in depth within only a limited subset of the literature. This may reflect both methodological heterogeneity across studies and the persistent absence of standardized frameworks for the systematic evaluation of PnPC components [10, 18, 23–25, 34]. Importantly, this model may not be fully feasible in all settings, particularly in resource-limited contexts where care is delivered by smaller core teams. Moreover, not all patients wish to—or are able to—access tertiary centers or engage in extensive interactions with multiple team members. Nonetheless, it should be emphasized that, beyond structural or resource constraints, the most essential element is the underlying culture: a shared awareness—among professionals, institutions, and families alike—that PnPC is possible, that it has value, and that it can be offered in some meaningful form regardless of the available means.

Regarding Structure and Processes of Care (NCP Guidelines, domain 1, KPA 1–17), the composition of the core team emerges as a KPA characterized by both high visibility and significant developmental density [23, 24]. In contrast, the integration of the extended team—comprising social workers, psychologists, pediatric and palliative specialists, and spiritual care providers— appears to receive a markedly lower level of analytical development, despite appearing in approximately half of the reviewed literature [10, 25]. This pattern suggests that while multidimensionality is widely endorsed as a foundational principle of PnPC, it has yet to be consistently operationalized, in contrast with the more established structures described in adult palliative care (NCP Guidelines) [18, 23–25].

Several factors may contribute to this developmental gap, such as the limited availability of professionals specifically trained in perinatal psychosocial or spiritual support [21], and the natural evolution of PnPC programs where consolidation of a specialized “core” team tends to precede the incremental expansion of interdisciplinary roles [1, 10]. Furthermore, the contributions of extended team members may be underreported if their activities are embedded within routine clinical workflows rather than documented as discrete interventions.

A number of complementary elements also receive relatively limited attention. These include community integration (such as familial peer-support networks), the structured coordination of care transitions, formalized interdisciplinary case reviews, and parallel care planning. Collectively, these may represent nascent domains and emerging theoretical priorities that, if further developed, could potentially strengthen the quality and continuity of care within the PnPC framework [10, 20, 34].

Support for healthcare professionals remains one of the least developed domains [8, 25]. While the importance of emotional support is widely mentioned, it is seldom addressed with depth. Furthermore, key structural practices—such as formal debriefings following a patient’s death or temporary relief from other clinical responsibilities during the dying process—are rarely addressed in the literature [1]. In contrast, aspects of early patient identification receive more sustained attention. Although the findings of this ScR might suggest that early intervention is underdeveloped, this should be interpreted with caution; recent influential guidance has expanded eligibility criteria beyond simple diagnosis to explicitly incorporate prognostic uncertainty and parallel planning from the outset [11].

Similarly, elements essential to systemic robustness—such as regular interdisciplinary meetings, structured coordination of care transitions, and continuous quality improvement— appear to remain modestly developed [23, 25, 26]. Overall, the limited depth of development observed across many KPAs regarding care structure and processes aligns with the research priorities recently identified in an international Delphi study [34].

Physical Aspects of Care (NCP Guidelines, domain 2, KPA 18–22), show a moderate yet heterogeneous level of development. While neonatal and maternal interventions focused on symptom relief are relatively prominent—suggesting that the management of physical suffering is a consistently addressed component of PnPC—other critical elements, such as systematic symptom screening and the routine updating of prenatal and postnatal care plans—including fetal care plans and maternal delivery plans within a framework of parallel planning—are less frequently detailed in the literature [10, 20, 23–26]. These gaps highlight important opportunities for strengthening the organization and delivery of high-quality clinical care.

Although frequently mentioned, the systematic screening of physical symptoms and psychosocial needs are less discussed in depth in the literature (NCP Guidelines, domains 3 and 4, KPA 23–28). While planned psychological support exhibits an intermediate level of development, the integration of social workers into formal care pathways is rarely articulated [30].

Spiritual, Religious, and Existential Aspects of Care (NCP Guidelines, domain 5, KPA 29–32), are commonly invoked as a component of holistic care, yet its operationalization remains limited [24, 25, 30]. Spiritual history-taking, screening, and planned interventions are seldom described in detail; furthermore, the recognition and support of healthcare professionals’ own spirituality are virtually unaddressed. This pattern may reflect the challenges of translating spirituality from a broadly acknowledged value into standardized clinical practice, particularly when compared with the more mature approaches reported in adult palliative care [10, 18].

A similar trend is observed regarding Cultural Aspects of Care (NCP Guidelines, domain 6, KPA 35–36). Systematic assessment of cultural contexts and the strategic adaptation of care to family values are infrequently documented, and support for healthcare teams in recognizing and addressing cultural biases receives little attention [23, 25, 26, 30], suggesting that cultural competence in PnPC may be at an early stage of development in the literature.

Regarding the Care of the Patient Nearing the End of Life (NCP Guidelines, domain 7, KPA 37–42), the need to develop, in collaboration with families, a documented care plan addressing possible scenarios—often integrated into the birth plan—is widely recognized, but its formalization remains limited [1, 6, 10]. Specifically, guaranteed access to the healthcare team and the comprehensive assessment and management of physical, psychological, spiritual, and cultural needs in the time preceding death are insufficiently elaborated. Finally, breastfeeding care appears weakly developed in the literature, suggesting that despite its clinical and symbolic importance, it has not yet been fully integrated into operational care frameworks [4, 10, 20, 37].

While we acknowledge that, given the exploratory nature of a ScR, definitive practice recommendations fall outside its scope, we believe the structured framework of KPAs proposed here may offer value at three levels: (1) it could provide clinical teams and institutions with a reference map for reflecting on the comprehensiveness of existing or planned PnPC programs; (2) it may offer educators and professional bodies a starting point for structuring curriculum development in perinatal palliative care training; and (3) it helps identify specific domains that remain critically underrepresented in the literature, which may inform future research prioritization. We are aware that translating this theoretical foundation into actionable clinical guidance is a complex and gradual process.

The findings of this ScR should be interpreted in light of several limitations, including heterogeneity in study designs and variability in terminology. Importantly, this ScR evaluates the representation of PnPC components in the literature, rather than their real-world implementation or clinical effectiveness. Although a homogeneous external criterion was independently applied by two reviewers, the identification of “in-depth development” inevitably involves some degree of interpretive judgment. In addition, the robustness of reported developments varies across articles, and some particularly influential publications (e.g., Wilkinson, Wang) may contribute disproportionately to the apparent maturity of certain components, despite being addressed in depth in relatively few studies. While the domain-based framework aligns with established standards, it may not fully capture contextual, legal, or cultural variability across settings. Furthermore, the literature search was limited to the PubMed database, selected for its broad coverage, and was complemented by manual review of reference lists and relevant websites. Finally, Ethical and Legal Aspects (NCP Guidelines, domain 8) were not examined in this ScR by design, in order to limit analytical complexity.

Conclusions

This ScR provides a structured synthesis of how key practical aspects (KPAs) of PnPC are represented in the literature. A major strength of this ScR is its quantitative assessment of both the frequency and depth of KPA development, offering a nuanced picture of the field’s level of consolidation. The findings shows that PnPC has achieved a significant degree of maturity in its core relationship with families and in the definition of the interdisciplinary core team, but remains at an early stage in the formalization of organizational processes, team support mechanisms, and system consolidation. One of the main current challenges lies not so much in identifying new elements to improve care for these highly vulnerable patients and their families, but rather in translating already identified components into evaluable structures, processes, and systems. Our findings suggest three priority lines of action: developing and implementing standardized internal processes (such as intra-team communication, regular interdisciplinary clinical meetings, and coordination of care transitions); integrating professional support as a structural component of the care model; and advancing toward systematic assessment and planning in the psychological, social, spiritual, and cultural dimensions of care.
