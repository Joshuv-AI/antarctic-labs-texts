Exploring early community exposure among first-year medical students in a peripheral Indonesian medical school: a qualitative case study
Authors: Ingolda Nadaa Nabiilah, Yoyo Suhoyo, Titi Savitri Prihatiningsih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical education has increasingly emphasized social accountability and community-oriented learning to prepare graduates for complex health system challenges. Early Community Exposure (ECE) introduces medical students to real-world community and primary care contexts early in training, with the potential to foster empathy, professionalism, and contextual understanding of health. While ECE has been widely implemented, evidence remains limited regarding its impact on first-year medical students in newly established medical schools located in peripheral regions of Indonesia. This study explored how novice medical students experienced and interpreted their first exposure to community-based learning.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative exploratory case study design. Participants were first-year medical students who completed the inaugural ECE program at Universitas Borneo Tarakan, a newly established medical school in North Kalimantan, Indonesia. Using purposive and snowball sampling, 15 students participated in in-depth semi-structured interviews conducted between January and March 2025. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed thematically using a hybrid inductive–deductive approach informed by community-based education, experiential learning, and social cognitive theory. Strategies to enhance trustworthiness included member checking, reflexive journaling, and peer debriefing.ResultsSeven interrelated themes were identified: (1) meaningful experiences, (2) early learning transformation, (3) bridging theory and practice, (4) professional identity formation, (5) internalization of professional values, (6) adaptation and motivation, and (7) program optimization. Students described ECE as a transformative and humanistic learning experience that enhanced empathy, responsibility, teamwork, and confidence. ECE enabled students to contextualize preclinical knowledge within real community settings while fostering early professional identity formation. Challenges included limited time allocation, scheduling constraints, and accessibility issues for some community participants.ConclusionsEarly Community Exposure provided first-year medical students with a pivotal learning experience that integrated cognitive, affective, and professional development from the outset of training. Embedding structured reflection, mentorship, and institutional support may further strengthen the educational impact of ECE, particularly in peripheral and resource-limited settings. These findings highlight the value of early community-based learning in preparing socially accountable physicians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09402-5.Keywords: Early community exposure, Community-based medical education, Professional identity formation, Qualitative research, Indonesia

Introduction

Medical education has undergone a global shift from a predominantly biomedical model toward approaches that emphasize social accountability, contextual learning, and humanistic values. Contemporary medical graduates are expected not only to demonstrate scientific competence but also to understand the social determinants of health, communicate effectively with diverse populations, and respond to community health needs with empathy and professionalism. Community-based education (CBE) has therefore been widely adopted as a curricular strategy to bridge theoretical knowledge with real-world health contexts by situating learning within communities and primary care settings [1–3].

Social accountability (SA) in medical education refers to the obligation of medical schools to direct their education, research, and service activities toward addressing the priority health concerns of the communities they serve. First articulated by the World Health Organization, this concept emphasizes responsiveness to local health needs, equitable health workforce distribution, and partnership with communities and health systems [33–35]. In practice, socially accountable medical education requires curricular structures that expose students to underserved populations, promote understanding of health inequities, and foster a commitment to community-oriented practice [36, 37].

Early Community Exposure (ECE) represents a structured form of community-based education in which medical students—often during their first year—engage directly with patients, families, and community members under guided supervision [38–40]. ECE aims to contextualize biomedical knowledge while fostering non-technical competencies such as communication, teamwork, reflection, and ethical sensitivity [1, 4–7]. For novice learners transitioning into medical training, early exposure to authentic health care environments offers an opportunity to experience medicine as a social practice rather than solely an academic discipline.

The theoretical relationship between ECE and social accountability lies in the mechanism through which early exposure shapes students’ understanding of health and professional responsibility. By situating learning within real community contexts, ECE enables students to observe the social determinants of health, patterns of health inequity, and practical constraints within local health systems [41, 42, 5]. Through experiential engagement and guided reflection, students may begin to internalize professional values aligned with equity, responsiveness, and service [42, 43, 5]. In this way, ECE functions not only as a pedagogical approach but also as a structural mechanism through which medical schools operationalize social accountability within their curricula [5].

Evidence from multiple educational settings indicates that early clinical and community exposure contributes to students’ cognitive, affective, and professional development. Studies from high-income countries have reported that early patient contact enhances patient-centred communication, increases interest in primary care, and supports long-term professional motivation [8–10]. More recent research from Asian and other low- and middle-income country (LMIC) contexts suggests that early community-based learning also strengthens students’ awareness of health inequities, promotes community-oriented attitudes, and fosters commitment to underserved populations [11, 12]. Through engagement with community realities, students begin to recognize health as a multifactorial phenomenon shaped by socioeconomic, cultural, and environmental factors rather than solely by biological mechanisms.

Beyond knowledge acquisition, ECE has also been associated with early professional identity formation (PIF). Immersion in authentic clinical and community contexts allows students to observe professional role models, reflect on their personal values, and begin integrating the norms and responsibilities of the medical profession into their evolving self-concept [9, 13, 14]. Experiential learning theory suggests that such authentic encounters facilitate deeper learning through reflection and meaning-making, while social cognitive theory highlights the importance of observational learning, feedback, and supportive environments in developing self-efficacy and motivation [15, 16]. Recent scholarship further conceptualizes professional identity formation as a dynamic and socially situated process shaped by interactions between learners, institutions, and the broader health system [17–23]. Early community engagement may therefore serve as a formative catalyst for identity development, particularly in contexts where community health needs are highly visible.

In Indonesia, the relevance of ECE is heightened by persistent disparities in health care access and workforce distribution across its geographically dispersed archipelago. The country comprises more than 17,000 islands with substantial variation in infrastructure, socioeconomic conditions, and health system capacity [45, 46]. Peripheral regions—including rural districts, border provinces, outer islands, and remote inland areas—continue to experience shortages of physicians, limited specialist services, and logistical barriers to health care delivery [46, 47]. Recent national reports and empirical studies highlight ongoing inequities in workforce distribution and access to primary care across eastern and border regions, underscoring the importance of locally responsive medical education strategies [5, 24, 25].

In response, Indonesian medical schools have increasingly incorporated early community exposure and community-oriented curricula to strengthen students’ understanding of primary care, cultural diversity, and population health needs [6, 7, 26]. Previous studies conducted in Indonesian contexts have reported improvements in students’ communication skills, empathy, and awareness of social determinants of health following community-based learning experiences, although logistical constraints, supervision challenges, and curricular integration issues have also been noted [27–29]. However, most existing studies have been conducted in well-established institutions located in relatively developed regions where educational infrastructure and institutional culture are already mature. Consequently, limited evidence exists regarding how ECE is experienced in newly established medical schools located in peripheral settings.

Recent national initiatives aimed at improving health workforce distribution have led to the establishment of new medical schools in underserved regions of Indonesia. Universitas Borneo Tarakan (UBT), located in North Kalimantan, launched its medical program in the 2024/2025 academic year with a strong emphasis on social accountability and community-oriented learning. As part of its foundational curriculum, Early Community Exposure was introduced as a mandatory first-year activity designed to immerse students in community and primary care environments from the beginning of their training. Within this context, ECE serves not only as a learning strategy but also as an institutional commitment to align medical education with the health priorities of a peripheral border region.

The first year of medical school represents a critical transitional period characterized by identity exploration, emotional adjustment, and the formation of early professional values [48, 49]. Understanding how students interpret and make meaning of early community exposure during this stage is therefore essential for informing curriculum design and student support mechanisms [49, 50]. Furthermore, examining ECE within a newly established peripheral medical school provides an opportunity to explore how institutional development, geographic marginality, and community engagement intersect in shaping students’ early professional formation [39–50].

This study therefore aimed to explore the experiences and perceptions of first-year medical students participating in the inaugural Early Community Exposure program at Universitas Borneo Tarakan. By situating ECE within the framework of social accountability and examining it in an underrepresented peripheral context, this research seeks to contribute updated and context-sensitive insights to the growing literature on early community-based medical education in Indonesia and comparable LMIC settings.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative case study design informed by an interpretivist paradigm to explore how first-year medical students experienced and interpreted participation in an Early Community Exposure (ECE) program. A qualitative approach was appropriate for capturing students’ perceptions, emotional responses, and meaning-making processes associated with early community-based learning.

The study was framed as a bounded case study, with the case defined as the inaugural implementation of the ECE program during the 2024/2025 academic year at Universitas Borneo Tarakan (UBT). A case study design allowed in-depth examination of this educational phenomenon within its institutional and sociocultural context, including curriculum structure, community partnerships, and the geographic characteristics of the setting. Although the study explored students’ lived experiences, the aim was not to identify the universal essence of a phenomenon, as in phenomenological research, but rather to understand how ECE operated within a specific institutional context and how that context shaped students’ interpretations.

The study was conceptually informed by experiential learning theory and social cognitive theory, which guided interpretation of how community engagement contributed to learning, value development, and emerging professional identity.

Research setting

The study was conducted at Universitas Borneo Tarakan (UBT), a public university located in Tarakan, North Kalimantan, Indonesia. The medical program at UBT was established in the 2024/2025 academic year as part of a national initiative to expand medical education into peripheral and border regions. The curriculum was developed in collaboration with Universitas Gadjah Mada to ensure alignment with national accreditation standards and to emphasize social accountability and community-oriented learning.

Tarakan is an island city characterized by geographic isolation, cultural diversity, and uneven health service distribution. Health care delivery in the region relies heavily on primary health centers (Puskesmas) and community-based health programs involving community health volunteers. These contextual characteristics provide an authentic environment for community-based medical education.

The ECE program was conducted during the first semester over an eight-week period. Students participated in weekly half-day community visits and were assigned in small groups to a primary health center and surrounding neighbourhoods. The program aimed to: (1) introduce students to primary health care in peripheral settings; (2) develop foundational communication skills through supervised interaction with patients and community members; (3) increase understanding of social determinants of health; (4) support early professional identity formation through role modelling and reflection; and (5) promote teamwork, responsibility, and ethical awareness. Learning activities included household visits, community health surveys, patient interviews, basic health assessments, and group-based health promotion activities conducted in collaboration with primary health center staff and community health volunteers. Structured briefing and debriefing sessions were incorporated to facilitate reflection on students’ experiences.

Participants and sampling

Participants were first-year medical students who had completed the ECE program during the 2024/2025 academic year. Eligibility criteria included enrollment as a first-year medical student at UBT and participation in the ECE program. Purposive sampling was used to recruit students who were able to provide rich and reflective accounts of their experiences. Recruitment was conducted through an invitation distributed via email and the cohort’s messaging group. The invitation included information about the study and a link to a registration form for interested students.

Of the 38 eligible first-year students, 20 expressed initial interest in participating, and 15 ultimately completed interviews. Sampling aimed to include students from different ECE groups and community placement sites to capture variation in experiences. To further enhance diversity of perspectives, snowball sampling was used whereby participants suggested peers who might offer additional insights. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new concepts or insights emerged from subsequent interviews. The final sample consisted of 15 participants.

Data collection

Data were collected through individual, semi-structured, in-depth interviews conducted between January and March 2025. A semi-structured interview guide was developed specifically for this study and informed by literature on community-based education, experiential learning, social cognitive theory, and professional identity formation. The guide included open-ended questions exploring students’ expectations prior to ECE, emotional responses during community encounters, experiences interacting with patients and supervisors, perceived learning outcomes, reflections on professional values, and perceived challenges during the program.

Interviews were conducted in Bahasa Indonesia in a private setting, lasted between 45 and 70 min, and were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. All recordings were transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were reviewed for accuracy by a trained research assistant. To preserve contextual meaning, data analysis was conducted using the original Bahasa Indonesia transcripts. Selected quotations included in the manuscript were translated into English and subsequently back-translated to ensure semantic accuracy. The English version of the interview guide is provided as supplementary material.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following the six-phase approach described by Braun and Clarke [51–53]. Analysis was conducted using a hybrid inductive–deductive strategy. First, all transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve familiarization with the data. During this phase, initial notes and reflections were recorded. Second, open coding was performed to generate preliminary codes directly from participants’ narratives. These inductively derived codes captured meaningful units of text relevant to students’ experiences and interpretations.

The analysis was simultaneously informed by theoretical concepts derived from experiential learning theory, social cognitive theory, and literature on professional identity formation. These theoretical perspectives functioned as sensitizing frameworks that guided interpretation but did not constrain the coding process. Primary coding was conducted by the first author (INN). To enhance analytic rigor, a second researcher (YS) independently reviewed a subset of transcripts (approximately 30%) and the corresponding codes. Coding discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. Codes were then iteratively compared and grouped into categories, which were subsequently refined into broader themes representing shared patterns of meaning across participants. Theme development involved multiple iterative discussions among the research team (INN, YS, and TSP) to ensure coherence, internal consistency, and alignment with the study’s research aims. A qualitative data analysis software was used to support data organization and coding.

Trustworthiness

Several strategies were employed to enhance the rigor and trustworthiness of the study. Credibility was supported through prolonged engagement with the data, triangulation between interview transcripts and reflexive field notes, and member checking. For member checking, a summary of preliminary findings was shared with several participants to confirm that the interpretations accurately reflected their experiences. Reflexivity was incorporated throughout the research process. Interviews were conducted by the first author (INN), a physician and medical educator with formal training in qualitative research methods. Prior to conducting the interviews, the interviewer engaged in reflexive practices to identify and critically examine potential assumptions and biases related to early community-based education. Reflexive journaling was maintained during data collection and analysis to document evolving perspectives and to support critical self-awareness in interpreting the data. Dependability was strengthened through the maintenance of an audit trail documenting coding decisions, theme development, and analytic discussions among the research team. Confirmability was further supported through reflexive journaling and peer debriefing with researchers who were not directly involved in data collection, helping to minimize the influence of researcher assumptions on data interpretation. Transferability was facilitated through detailed descriptions of the research context, participant characteristics, and educational setting, enabling readers to assess the applicability of findings to other contexts.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences, Universitas Borneo Tarakan (Approval No. 15/KEPK-UBT/2025). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed that their decision to participate or withdraw would not affect their academic standing. Confidentiality was ensured through the use of pseudonyms, and all identifiable information was removed from transcripts. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored securely on password-protected devices accessible only to members of the research team.

Results

Fifteen first-year medical students participated in the interviews. Analysis of their narratives revealed seven interrelated themes that describe how students experienced, interpreted, and integrated Early Community Exposure (ECE) into their emerging understanding of medical practice (Table 1). Collectively, these themes illustrate a developmental process in which authentic community engagement stimulated learning transformation, value internalization, and early professional identity formation (Fig. 1).

Developmental relationship among themes emerging from ECE

Meaningful and engaging learning experiences

Students consistently described ECE as a meaningful and emotionally engaging learning experience characterized by a supportive environment. Interactions with supervising physicians, community members, and peers created a sense of psychological safety that enabled students to participate actively in learning activities. Many participants emphasized feeling welcomed by health professionals and trusted by community members. This sense of acceptance helped reduce anxiety and fostered confidence during their first encounters with patients.

“I felt welcomed by the doctor… it was as if they truly accepted us as part of the team.” (S11).

Students also described patient interactions as particularly memorable. Listening to patients’ stories and building rapport were perceived as powerful learning experiences that differed from classroom-based instruction.

“When we talked directly with patients and heard their experiences, it felt very real. It made me realize that medicine is not only about theory.” (S8).

The informal and community-based environment enabled students to engage more authentically with patients and health professionals, creating a learning atmosphere that students described as both enjoyable and meaningful.

Early learning transformation through authentic experience

ECE represented students’ first authentic encounter with medical practice. Activities such as interviewing patients, observing consultations, and conducting basic health assessments transformed abstract biomedical knowledge into tangible experiences. Students frequently described these experiences as transformative learning moments. Experiential engagement allowed them to connect theoretical concepts with real situations, which they perceived as more memorable than classroom instruction alone.

“This became our first learning experience for what lies ahead as medical students.” (S14).

Several participants also reported that ECE prompted early reflection on potential career interests. Exposure to community health issues and preventive health activities encouraged students to consider roles beyond hospital-based practice.

“Seeing how health promotion works in the community made me think about the importance of prevention, not just treatment.” (S6).

These reflections suggest that early exposure not only facilitated skill acquisition but also supported early professional orientation.

Bridging theory and practice

Students consistently highlighted ECE as an effective bridge between theoretical learning and real-world practice. Applying recently learned concepts—such as measuring blood pressure or understanding cardiovascular physiology—helped reinforce their comprehension of foundational science.

“It became clearer when linked with real hypertension cases that we saw in the community.” (S8).

Students also recognized that real patient presentations often differed from textbook descriptions. This prompted them to adapt their thinking and appreciate the complexity of clinical realities.

“What we learn in class is often very structured, but in the community every patient has a different story.” (S3).

Through these experiences, students reported a stronger appreciation of the relevance of basic science knowledge and its application within authentic health contexts.

Early professional identity formation

Participation in ECE contributed to the early development of students’ professional identity. Through observing physicians, interacting with patients, and participating in community activities, students began reflecting on what it means to become a doctor. Many participants described gaining new perspectives on the physician’s role, emphasizing relational, ethical, and community-oriented dimensions of practice.

“After meeting patients directly, I realized that being a doctor is not only about treating disease but also about understanding people’s lives.” (S5).

ECE also strengthened students’ motivation to pursue a medical career. Participants frequently described feeling inspired by their experiences and developing clearer aspirations about the kind of physicians they hoped to become.

“This experience strengthened my resolve to pursue medicine and become a doctor who cares about the community.” (S2).

These narratives reflect the early stages of professional identity formation, as students began integrating professional values and expectations into their evolving self-concept.

Internalization of professional values

Closely related to identity formation, students described the internalization of professional values during their community encounters. This theme reflects the emotional and moral processes through which students personally embraced values such as empathy, responsibility, teamwork, and cultural sensitivity. Many students reported experiencing spontaneous empathy when learning about patients’ life circumstances.

“When I listened to their stories, my empathy just emerged naturally.” (S14).

Students also described developing a stronger sense of responsibility toward patients and communities. Even simple activities such as conducting health surveys or providing health education were perceived as meaningful contributions. Teamwork and peer collaboration were also emphasized as important aspects of the experience. Students frequently described how working in small groups strengthened mutual support and shared learning. Exposure to diverse community members further enhanced students’ awareness of cultural differences and reinforced the importance of respectful and inclusive communication.

Adaptation, confidence, and motivation

Many participants reported experiencing anxiety before their first community visits, particularly regarding communication with patients and fear of making mistakes. However, these concerns gradually diminished as students adapted to the environment and received support from peers and facilitators.

“At first I was nervous because it was my first time speaking directly with patients.” (S11).

Repeated exposure, supportive supervision, and peer collaboration helped students gain confidence in their abilities.

“At first I was nervous… but it turned out I didn’t need to be afraid after interacting with patients and getting guidance.” (S11).

Students reported that these experiences increased both confidence and motivation. Intrinsic motivation was fostered through personal growth and meaningful patient interaction, while extrinsic motivation was supported by teamwork and encouragement from supervisors.

Suggestions for program optimization

Although students described ECE as highly beneficial, participants also identified several areas for improvement. Time constraints were the most frequently mentioned challenge. Students felt that the limited duration of activities restricted deeper engagement with community members.

“If the duration were longer, it would be even better because we could interact more with the community.” (S6).

Participants also highlighted the importance of clearer scheduling and improved preparatory briefings before community visits. Some students suggested involving students more actively in program organization, for example through student-led committees, to improve coordination and foster a greater sense of ownership. Accessibility concerns were also noted, particularly regarding elderly community members who experienced difficulty participating in certain activities. Participants recommended considering transportation and mobility needs when planning future community engagement activities.

Suggestions for program optimization are presented as cross-cutting considerations to strengthen the ECE experience.

Discussion

This study explored first-year medical students’ experiences of Early Community Exposure (ECE) in a newly established medical school located in a peripheral Indonesian region. The findings indicate that ECE functioned as a developmental learning process integrating cognitive understanding, emotional engagement, and emerging professional identity formation. Rather than being perceived as a simple introductory activity, students experienced ECE as a meaningful encounter that shaped how they understood medicine, patients, and their future professional roles.

Principal findings and theoretical integration

Students consistently described ECE as a meaningful and relational learning experience characterized by psychological safety and supportive interactions with supervisors, peers, and community members. Feeling welcomed by health professionals and trusted by patients enhanced students’ confidence and willingness to participate actively in learning activities. These findings reinforce previous research demonstrating that early patient and community contact can foster empathy, engagement, and motivation among novice medical learners [8–10]. In particular, relational acceptance within community settings appeared to facilitate emotional engagement and reflective learning, highlighting the importance of supportive educational environments during the early stages of professional training.

ECE also functioned as a bridge between theoretical learning and real-world practice. Students described how applying biomedical concepts during community encounters helped them contextualize and better understand foundational knowledge. This observation aligns with previous literature suggesting that experiential learning strengthens integration between theoretical knowledge and practical application, thereby reducing the fragmentation often observed in traditional preclinical curricula [9, 30]. Early community-based activities may therefore enhance students’ perception of the relevance of foundational sciences and support the development of adaptive clinical reasoning.

A key conceptual contribution of this study lies in distinguishing between the internalization of professional values and professional identity formation (PIF). Students frequently described emotionally grounded experiences—such as empathizing with patients’ life circumstances or recognizing social inequities—which reflected the internalization of professional values including empathy, responsibility, and respect for patients. In contrast, when students articulated clearer aspirations about becoming compassionate and community-oriented physicians, they demonstrated the integration of these values into their evolving professional identity. In this sense, value internalization can be understood as an affective and moral process that supports the broader developmental trajectory of professional identity formation.

These findings suggest a developmental pathway in which authentic community exposure stimulates emotional engagement, which in turn facilitates value internalization and contributes to emerging professional identity. This layered interpretation contributes to contemporary discussions of PIF by illustrating how experiential encounters within community contexts may initiate early identity development among novice learners [31].

From an experiential learning perspective, ECE provided concrete experiences that encouraged reflection and conceptual integration. Students’ descriptions of memorable community encounters correspond with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, particularly the progression from concrete experience to reflective observation and conceptual understanding [15]. Learning occurred not only through observation but also through active participation in health-related activities, allowing students to construct meaning from real-world interactions.

From a social cognitive perspective, students’ transition from initial anxiety to increasing confidence illustrates the role of observational learning, mastery experiences, and supportive feedback in the development of self-efficacy [16]. Observing clinicians interacting with patients, receiving encouragement from supervisors, and collaborating with peers helped students gradually adapt to unfamiliar professional situations. These findings underscore the importance of mentorship and supportive supervision in facilitating early professional development.

Contribution and contextual novelty

Although early clinical and community exposure has been widely examined in medical education research, this study contributes new insights by examining ECE within a newly established medical school located in a peripheral border region. Three aspects of contextual novelty are particularly noteworthy.

First, the study was conducted during the inaugural year of a new medical program. Students’ experiences were therefore shaped not only by their exposure to community-based learning but also by the simultaneous development of institutional culture. This dual process—student professional formation occurring alongside institutional formation—has rarely been examined in existing Indonesian medical education literature.

Second, the geographic setting represents a peripheral island region characterized by uneven health service distribution and logistical challenges. Exposure to such contexts may intensify students’ awareness of health inequities and reinforce the relevance of socially accountable medical practice.

Third, the study offers a conceptual interpretation linking experiential learning, emotional engagement, value internalization, and professional identity formation within a resource-constrained educational environment. While previous Indonesian studies have primarily reported improvements in communication skills or empathy, this study provides a more integrated explanation of how early community exposure may contribute to broader developmental processes.

Implications for newly established and peripheral medical schools

The findings have several practical implications for medical schools seeking to implement early community-based learning programs, particularly in newly established or resource-constrained settings.

First, alignment between institutional mission and community engagement is essential. In new medical schools, ECE can function as a foundational mechanism for operationalizing social accountability by connecting educational activities directly with community health needs.

Second, structured reflection should be incorporated into ECE programs. Emotional experiences alone may not automatically translate into professional growth; guided reflection and debriefing sessions can help students critically interpret their encounters and integrate these experiences into their developing professional identity.

Third, strong partnerships with primary health centers and community stakeholders are crucial for sustainable implementation. Early engagement with community partners, clear role delineation, and collaborative planning can enhance program feasibility and long-term sustainability.

Fourth, logistical planning should consider manageable group sizes, consistent scheduling, and accessibility for community participants. These factors are particularly important in peripheral settings where transportation, infrastructure, and resource limitations may affect program implementation.

Finally, mentorship and peer-support structures should be strengthened during early institutional phases. In newly established schools, both students and faculty may be adapting to evolving systems, and supportive relationships can facilitate a positive learning environment [32].

Strengths and limitations

This study provides contextually grounded insights into early community exposure in a newly established medical school in a peripheral Indonesian region—an underrepresented context in the medical education literature. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small cohort of participants, which may limit the transferability of findings to other settings. Participants’ responses may also have been influenced by social desirability bias, particularly given their early stage of professional training. In addition, this study focused on short-term reflections following completion of the ECE program; future longitudinal research is needed to examine whether early community exposure influences longer-term outcomes such as career orientation, professional identity development, and commitment to underserved communities.

Conclusions

Early Community Exposure (ECE) provided first-year medical students at Universitas Borneo Tarakan with a formative and multidimensional learning experience extending beyond knowledge acquisition to encompass emotional engagement, value internalization, and early professional identity development. Through authentic interaction with patients and communities, students were able to contextualize preclinical knowledge while developing empathy, responsibility, and awareness of the social dimensions of health.

Despite initial anxiety, supportive supervision, peer collaboration, and positive community engagement enabled students to adapt and participate confidently in community-based learning activities. At the same time, logistical and structural challenges highlight the importance of careful program planning, institutional support, and sustainable community partnerships.

For newly established medical schools in peripheral or resource-limited settings, early community exposure may serve not only as an educational strategy but also as a practical expression of social accountability. Integrating structured reflection, mentorship, and collaborative community engagement from the early stages of curriculum development may strengthen both student learning and institutional responsiveness to local health needs.

Further longitudinal and multi-institutional research is warranted to examine the sustained impact of early community exposure on professional identity trajectories, workforce distribution, and socially accountable medical practice.
