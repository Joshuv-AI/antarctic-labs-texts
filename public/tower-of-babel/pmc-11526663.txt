Patient and public involvement and engagement: Do we need an ‘ethical anchor’?
Authors: Sophie Suri, Samantha L Harrison, Alex Bevin-Nicholls, Felicity Shenton, Sandra Atkinson, Jenny Earle, Gaynor Williams, Joanne Lally
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorking alongside patients and the public to shape and engage with research, Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement (PPIE), facilitates more impactful research outcomes. The UK Standards for Public Involvement provides a framework for conducting PPIE; however, they do not refer directly to ethical conduct. Research ethics involve the moral principles that govern researchers’ actions, and securing ethical approval from a research ethics committee is necessary before starting a study. Nonetheless, in the UK, ethical approval is not needed for PPIE activities.Main textBy its very nature, PPIE requires interaction with patients and public about their lived/living experience of often sensitive and emotional topics. We need to consider ethical principles of PPIE, and potential for harm to those involved. The authors call for ethics guidance to be included in the UK Standards for Public Involvement and discuss this with regards to (a) emotional risk (b) physical risk (c) confidentiality and personal data (d) support versus paternalism (e) marginalisation and (f) building and maintaining healthy relationships.ConclusionThe argument presented here has been informed by the authors’ own experiences in topic areas ranging from transplantation, respiratory disease and health inequalities to women living with domestic abuse, and suggests that although requiring ethical approval for PPIE would be a step too far, and stifle research progress, PPIE needs to be conducted with an ‘ethical anchor’ to facilitate ethical practice and mitigate risk.Keywords: Patient and public involvement, Ethics, Ethical anchor, Standards

Background

Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement (PPIE) provides a vehicle to ensure that research is not only relevant to its intended audience but also aligns with broader democratic principles of citizenship, accountability, and transparency [1]. Public involvement is defined by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) [1] as research being carried out ‘with’ or ‘by’ members of the public rather than ‘to’, ‘about’ or ‘for’ them and public engagement as “the myriad of ways in which the activity and benefits of higher education and research can be shared with the public” [2]. The emergence of PPIE in research dates to the 1970s [3] and has grown both nationally and internationally over the decades to being an often-expected part of funding applications to major funding bodies such as the NIHR in the UK, Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) in Canada and the Patient-Centred Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) in the US [4, 5]. The UK Standards for Public Involvement provides a framework for conducting PPIE, highlighting the importance of inclusive opportunities, working together, support and learning, communications, impact, and governance [6]. However, they do not refer directly to ethical conduct in PPIE.

Research ethics are the moral principles guiding researchers on how they should conduct their work [7]. Research ethics committees (REC) are responsible for ensuring that researchers adhere to these principles (a REC is a group of people responsible for formally reviewing research proposals to assess whether the research is ethical) [8]. These committees may be part of a university (sometimes without public representatives) or a public body such as the Health Research Authority (HRA) in the UK (which includes public representatives). However, unlike in research studies, formal ethical approval from a REC is not required for PPIE activities in the UK [9]. Similarly, the CIHR state that “ethics approval is not required for involving patients in the planning or design stages of research” [10].

Intrinsically, PPIE requires interaction with patients and the public and consideration should be given to ethical principles and potential for harm to those involved [11]. The argument presented here has been informed by the authors’ own experiences in health and care research, ranging from transplantation to respiratory disease and health inequalities to women living with domestic abuse. We believe that REC approvals for PPIE would hinder a timely research process, place additional pressures on already overburdened RECs and be a disincentive for researchers to engage in PPIE, opinions shared by recent authors on this topic area [9]. However, we suggest that PPIE still needs to embody ethical principles to facilitate good practice and mitigate risk [12]. The authors discuss this pertinent issue with regards to (a) emotional risk (b) physical risk (c) confidentiality and personal data (d) support versus paternalism (e) marginalisation and (f) building and maintaining healthy relationships (Table 1 offers potential solutions to the ethical concerns raised). Emotional risk

A fundamental part of PPIE involves patients and public members sharing their personal experiences of health and social challenges. Researchers must be informed about the potential emotional risks associated with this aspect of PPIE and have a moral duty to address these risks with everyone involved. This is crucial to avoid re-traumatising people when discussing issues such as the reality of living with chronic mental/physical health conditions or experience of domestic abuse [13]. Whilst offering a cathartic element and sense of purpose and belonging, sharing these experiences can be stressful and trigger strong emotional responses [14]. Planning and facilitating interactions to ensure people are supported prior to, during and after the activity can foster feelings of safety, respect and care, mitigating unnecessary harm and providing space for richer, more impactful conversations (Table 1, Section A) [14]. Emotional risk to the researcher also requires consideration, particularly when the topic is distressing (e.g., child sexual abuse, cancer survival, traumatic birth experience) or has relevance to the researcher involved [15]. Post activity de-briefs with a colleague (e.g., a colleague with experience/training in dealing with similar topics, a mentor) may give the researcher a chance to process triggering content and avoid emotional harm. This approach should not be limited for research areas deemed ‘sensitive’; asking any patient to recount their lived/living experience can be sensitive, and perhaps to others listening, therefore this approach of ethical working needs to be a value embedded across all PPIE.

Physical risk

Physical risks in public involvement work relate to physical harm that can impact anyone involved in PPIE, researcher or public member. These could pertain to risks in the environment where the activity is held or to the activity itself, especially if it involves more innovative and ‘hands-on’ PPIE approaches that may increase the likelihood of harm. For example, in a recent body of work two authors from this paper aimed to design a walking football intervention for people with chronic breathlessness. The authors conducted PPIE work that involved people with breathlessness taking part in a walking football taster session so that they could comment on the intervention from a position of experience. Physical risk considerations for this project included assessing the environment where the walking football was held, considering potential risk factors prior to taking part (e.g., contraindications to exercise, use of mobility aids/oxygen) ensuring facilitators were appropriately trained (e.g., in first aid) and had knowledge of procedures should an adverse event had occurred. Risk when working in more remote locations, working alone or in people’s houses should also be evaluated. Working with children or other groups at higher risk of exploitation and harm may raise further ethical concerns (e.g., safeguarding) and requires careful consideration [11, 13, 16].

Confidentiality and personal data

Confidentiality in PPIE is paramount and is an ethical principle that needs to be upheld by all involved. The UK Standards for Public Involvement instructs researchers in the ‘governance’ standard to ensure that “…the privacy of personal information is protected by collecting and using it in a suitable way” [6]. However, this needs to extend to include concerns identified by the authors in Table 1 section C, especially as recent PPIE work highlights privacy as a major concern [16]. In certain populations (e.g., rare disease/organ transplantation) the community is very small, and anonymity might be difficult, therefore measures are required to address this with all involved. Issues such as permission when taking/using PPIE members’ images should also be considered, a point especially relevant with the evolution of social media. Embedding these confidentiality considerations into guidelines and training is fundamental to building public trust in partnerships with researchers.

Support versus paternalism

Power dynamics are of concern in PPIE, where a balance is sought between support versus paternalism [17]. Who decides what the public members should be ‘protected’ from when sharing their lived/living experience? If researchers make these decisions on people's behalf, however well-intentioned, they risk disempowering public contributors by ignoring their agency and reinforcing hierarchical, elitist, paternalistic cultures [3, 17]. Respecting people’s autonomy is a principle at the heart of medical ethics and should be at the core of PPIE [18]. However, guidance on how to approach making these decisions together is needed to ensure that risk is addressed both transparently and supportively.

Marginalisation

Including marginalised communities and community members in PPIE is paramount to ensuring that research is informed by a “diversity of public experience and insight” [6]. Ethical approaches and practices must underpin partnership work with under-served or marginalised communities to ensure meaningful involvement. Superficial efforts that provide the illusion of inclusivity are highly unethical and serve to perpetuate harm and reinforce historical oppression [3, 19]. Examples include inviting people from diverse backgrounds to PPIE activities but not valuing or acting upon their input or using one person to represent an ethnically minoritised group with little acknowledgement for the heterogeneity within their heritage (e.g., within the broader label of ‘South Asian’) or intersectionality in their own identity [20]. We need to go beyond unethical practices such as tokenism and truly learn about, listen to and value people from a wide range of backgrounds to avoid compounding experiences of marginalisation and to ensure their voices make a real and much-needed impact on research.

Building and maintaining healthy relationships

Building and maintaining healthy relationships with public members is at the core of PPIE [21]. We suggest that researchers have a moral obligation to ensure that the foundations of these relationships are rooted in practices that foster long-term wellbeing for all parties involved. Overstepping boundaries such as assuming a public member will want to build a very personal relationship or trying to be their first port of call for health and social care advice can place excessive pressure on all parties and create feelings of discomfort and unhealthy dependencies. Consideration must be given to retaining professional distance and maintaining boundaries when involved in PPIE activities, and early conversations with public partners around mutual expectations can support this. Care should also be taken to ensure that public members are not just ‘used’ and ‘dropped’ once projects are complete. Making people aware of the impact they have had on a project can help maintain strong relationships. Signposting people to other opportunities or groups can help them continue the personal benefits experienced in PPIE and cultivates further partnerships to support people-centred research [1].

Conclusion

The vision to place people at the heart of healthcare is slowly being realised. Researchers are pushing the boundaries using creative and innovative ways of working to unlock the wealth of knowledge that lived/living experience brings, yet without proper ethical guidance the integrity of this public involvement could be compromised. Providing ethical guidance for PPIE is therefore a “strategic priority” [5, p. 1]. The authors by no means advocate for ethical approvals to be sought prior to PPIE but instead propose that an ‘ethical anchor’ is added to the UK Standards for Public Involvement to facilitate good practice and mitigate risk [6]. Future work should look to co-produce this framework with public members to encourage open discussion on ethical considerations from diverse perspectives.
