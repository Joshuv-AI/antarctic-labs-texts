Measuring fluxes between wave and geostrophic features in rotating non-hydrostatic flows with variable stratification
Jeffrey J. Early, Gerardo Hernández-Dueñas, Leslie M. Smith, Cimarron Wortham, M.-Pascale Lelong

Abstract
A challenge in physical oceanography is quantifying the energy content of waves and balanced flows and the fluxes that connect these reservoirs with their sources and sinks. Methodological limitations have prevented decompositions for realistic flows with non-hydrostatic motions and variable stratification. We present a framework that separates the flow into wave and geostrophic components using the principle that waves have no Eulerian available potential vorticity signature. Starting from new expressions for available energy and potential vorticity conservation, we construct a basis of wave and geostrophic modes, complete and orthogonal with respect to quadratic approximations of the conserved quantities. Using the resulting non-hydrostatic projection operators, the nonlinear equations of motion are expressed as coupled wave and geostrophic equations, quantifying cascade and transfer fluxes of wave and geostrophic energy. We apply the method to non-hydrostatic mid-ocean simulations with geostrophic mean-flow, near-inertial, and tidal forcing. From these experiments, we construct source-sink-reservoir diagrams for exact and quadratic fluxes, quantifying the fluxes between geostrophic and wave components. Because the cascade fluxes obey total energy conservation, we construct energy flow diagrams within the wave and geostrophic reservoirs and diagnose nonlocal transfers. The simulations show a geostrophic inverse cascade, a forward wave cascade, and a direct transfer of geostrophic to wave energy, with no indication of a forward geostrophic cascade. The mean-flow-only simulation shows weak spontaneous wave emission during spin-up, which diminishes to zero. Finally, we evaluate the decomposition by comparing linearized and fully conserved available potential vorticity, finding that errors become significant at scales below 15 km.



Key Points

•

A decomposition of wave and geostrophic total energy applicable to non-hydrostatic flows with arbitrary stratification

•

Application to realistic mid-ocean simulations with geostrophic-only and geostrophic-plus-wave forcing

•

Identification of inverse geostrophic cascade, forward wave cascade, and strong geostrophic-to-wave transfer

Introduction

Identifying energy pathways, from sources to sinks through the different reservoirs, remains one of the central challenges of oceanography.
63
constructed a ‘strawman energy budget’ from numerous observational sources with large uncertainties. In numerical models, on the other hand, producing a detailed energy budget hinges on (1) partitioning the flow into distinct constituents, i.e., the wave and balanced flow, and (2) precisely measuring their respective energies and associated energy fluxes. While it is relatively easy to compute
total

total

Here, the term ‘vortex’ refers to the geostrophic or balanced part of the flow with dynamics linked to potential vorticity conservation.
Not all linear flow decompositions satisfy energy orthogonality, yet this property is essential for uniquely interpreting energy transfers between reservoirs.

Attempts at understanding and quantifying wave-vortex interactions have a long history and use a variety of methodologies. The concepts of completeness and orthogonality are critical for discussing spectra and fluxes between flow components. To demonstrate this, let the state of a geophysical fluid be given by
(u,v,w,\eta,p)
for fluid velocity
(u,v,w)

\eta

\mathcal{E}\left[(u,v,w,\eta)\right]=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2}+N^{2}\eta^{2}\right)dV

(1)

where

is the squared buoyancy frequency. If the fluid can be linearly decomposed into two distinct parts such that
\left(u,v,w,\eta\right)=\left(u_{1}+u_{2},v_{1}+v_{2},w_{1}+w_{2},\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\right)
then

\mathcal{E}\left[\left(u,v,w,\eta\right)\right]=\mathcal{E}\left[\left(u_{1},v_{1},w_{1},\eta_{1}\right)\right]+\mathcal{E}\left[\left(u_{2},v_{2},w_{2},\eta_{2}\right)\right]+\epsilon_{12}

(2)

where
\epsilon_{12}
is a quadratic cross-term. For a sparse-data decomposition, the hope is that the cross-term
\epsilon_{12}

\epsilon_{12}=0
and the decomposition is
energetically orthogonal

With energy orthogonality as a guiding principle, we can define a set of
reservoirs
as a partition of the full fluid state, each with distinct energy.
Because potential enstrophy is also quadratic at lowest order, a wave–vortex decomposition must satisfy enstrophy orthogonality following rules analogous to (
2

When a basis is both complete and energetically orthogonal then each basis member has a unique energy signature—a feature that allows one to uniquely interpret that energy is moving
from
somewhere
to
somewhere else. Moreover, the lack of cross-terms also allows us to write energy spectra for separate flow components because the total energy is the sum of the squares of the amplitudes of the basis functions. This is exactly analogous to a Fourier series which partitions the total variance of a periodic function in terms of orthogonal Fourier modes. Importantly, energy orthogonality is not just a choice, but a requirement for constructing an energy spectrum.

Finding a complete and orthogonal basis that represents wave and geostrophic flows in a realistic domain is not trivial.
While the formulation of the internal gravity wave energy spectrum from orthogonal wave modes by
18
is sufficient for describing the energy content of interior flow, eigenmodes with an explicit free-surface are necessary when surface (or barotropic) waves are present.
25

35

45

67

Recent work has extended the normal mode decomposition to the shallow-water equations on the sphere including theoretical advances to include variable stratification
69
;
53
. In this case, the geostrophic mode is now interpreted as a Rossby mode, and the method has been applied both numerically and diagnostically. As precursor to the work in this manuscript,
13
extended prior wave-vortex decompositions to include non-hydrostatics with variable stratification, although without explicit derivation of the projection operators necessary to recast the non-hydrostatic equations of motion.

The purpose of this paper is to develop a method for precisely measuring the energy fluxes, including the inertial cascades and transfers, in a non-hydrostatic model with realistic stratification and forcing from a mean flow, winds, and tide. We define waves as the flow component with no Eulerian signature of available potential vorticity and decompose the flow according to the linear available potential vorticity. The result is a complete decomposition of the fluid flow where each mode satisfies quadratic energy and potential enstrophy orthogonality, the lowest order approximation of conserved nonlinear quantities. With this method we map the total energy flow for our semi-realistic simulations, and compare them to other similar results.

Wave forcing may introduce some form of ‘stimulated loss of balance’, either causing a direct transfer of geostrophic energy to the wave field, or causing a forward cascade of energy within the geostrophic reservoir toward smaller scales. 3.1
The no-motion and flattened isopycnal states

The most trivial, but important, solution to the equations of motion (

{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}},t)=(0,0,0)

(4)

A fully general energy budget relative to the no-motion density would include time dependence, allowing diapycnal mixing to modify the background state. Although some diapycnal mixing occurs in our simulations, we restrict the analysis to 200 days (Figure
2

The total density is partitioned into

Alternatively, following

\frac{d}{dt}\left(z-\eta\right)=\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}_{\textrm{nm}}(z-\eta)}\mathcal{S}_{\rho}.

In the absence of forcing,

3.3
Available potential enstrophy conservation with forcing

Here we derive a novel form of potential enstrophy conservation, based on available potential vorticity (APV). The term APV was first used in
54
, but later co-opted by
10
for a qualitatively similar quantity in closed form used here. In order to compute the potential enstrophy fluxes, we rederive the quantity defined in
10
, but now include the forcing terms.

The derivation of APV conservation begins in the usual way, by taking the curl of the momentum equations (
3a
)-(
3c
) and forming an evolution equation for absolute vorticity
\vec{\omega}+f\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
, where
\vec{\omega}\equiv\nabla\times{\mathbf{u}}
is the relative vorticity. APV follows from using
z-\eta
as the conserved quantity, rather than
\rho_{\textrm{tot}}
so we re-express (
7
) as

\frac{d\eta}{dt}-w=\mathcal{S}_{\eta}

(16)

by redefining the forcing as
\mathcal{S}_{\eta}=-\mathcal{S}_{\rho}/\rho^{\prime}_{\textrm{nm}}(z-\eta)

for notational convenience. To form an evolution equation for APV then requires taking the dot product of the absolute vorticity equation with
\nabla(z-\eta)
, and adding that to the dot product of the gradient of the thermodynamic equation (
7
) with absolute vorticity
\vec{\omega}+f\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
. The result is the expression,

\frac{d}{dt}\textrm{APV}=-\left(\vec{\omega}+f\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}\right)\cdot\nabla\mathcal{S}_{\eta}+\nabla(z-\eta)\cdot\left[\nabla\times\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{u}}\right]

(17)

where

\textrm{APV}\equiv\partial_{x}v-\partial_{y}u-f\partial_{z}\eta-\left(\nabla\times{\mathbf{u}}\right)\cdot\nabla\eta

(18)

and the constant
f
has been dropped, following the more rigorous treatment in
11
.

Available potential enstrophy is defined as,

Z\equiv\frac{1}{2}\textrm{APV}^{2},

(19)

so that conservation of available potential enstrophy is given by,

\frac{d}{dt}Z=\textrm{APV}\cdot\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{S}_{v}-\partial_{y}\mathcal{S}_{u}-f\partial_{z}\mathcal{S}_{\eta}\right)+\textrm{APV}\cdot\left(-\vec{\omega}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{S}_{\eta}-\nabla\eta\cdot\left[\nabla\times\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{u}}\right]\right).

(20)

The first term on the right-hand-side is approximately the leading order term in quasigeostrophic scaling, and the second term is the nonlinear correction required to make the expression exact.

The volume-averaged potential enstrophy fluxes follow from the right-hand side of the volume integral,

\partial_{t}\int Z\,dV=\int\left[\textrm{APV}\cdot\left(\partial_{x}\mathcal{S}_{v}-\partial_{y}\mathcal{S}_{u}-f\partial_{z}\mathcal{S}_{\eta}\right)+\textrm{APV}\cdot\left(-\vec{\omega}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{S}_{\eta}-\nabla\eta\cdot\left[\nabla\times\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{u}}\right]\right)\right]dV.

(21)

Just as with the energy budget derived above, we have neglected temporal changes in the no-motion state.

The available potential enstrophy spectral fluxes are shown in Figure
6
. The figure shows that the available potential enstrophy flux for two simulations are almost identical, both following the standard quasigeostrophic potential enstrophy picture where potential enstrophy cascades from mean flow forcing to smaller scales. The most notable difference between the two simulations is the weaker flux in MFW. Note also that bottom friction has a net positive potential enstrophy flux, consistent with classic picture that topography and friction generate potential enstrophy.

4
Linearization

To complete the sources, sinks and reservoirs diagram in Figure
4
requires that we decompose the fluid into wave and geostrophic parts, and derive energy and potential enstrophy conservation statements that include those reservoirs. To proceed, we linearize the equations of motions and determine the associated conservation statements in section
4.1
. A key feature of our approach is that the quadratically conserved quantities for the linear dynamical equations follow directly from their nonlinear counterparts—the approximations made here are central to understanding the limitations of the method. Appendix
A
formally derives the projection operators that take the fluid state and project onto the wave and vortex solutions, the result of which are summarized in section
4.2
. Section
5
applies the projection operators to the nonlinear equations of motion in order to derive the energy fluxes, and thereby complete the analysis used in Figure
4
.

4.1
Perturbation equations and their conservation laws

Perturbation equations are constructed by expressing the equations of motion (
3
) relative to the no-motion solution. It is helpful to express the buoyancy as
b=-g\rho_{\textrm{e}}/\rho_{0}=-N^{2}\eta_{\textrm{e}}
, with total density and pressure given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\textrm{tot}}(\mathbf{x},t)=

\displaystyle\rho_{\textrm{nm}}(z)+\frac{\rho_{0}}{g}N^{2}(z)\eta_{\textrm{e}}(\mathbf{x},t)

(22a)

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{tot}}(\mathbf{x},t)=

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{nm}}(z)+p_{\textrm{e}}(\mathbf{x},t).

(22b)

Using (

\psi

\psi({\mathbf{x}},t)=\begin{bmatrix}u({\mathbf{x}},t)\\

\left(\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{L}\right)\psi+\mathbf{\nonlinop}\left[\psi\right]={\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}},

\mathbf{L}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-f&0&0&\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\partial_{x}\\
f&0&0&0&\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\partial_{y}\\
0&0&0&N^{2}&\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\partial_{z}\\
0&0&-1&0&0\\
\partial_{x}&\partial_{y}&\partial_{z}&0&0\end{bmatrix},\,\mathbf{\nonlinop}\left[\psi\right]=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla u\\
{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla v\\
{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla w\\

and rescaled the buoyancy forcing so that

The operator
\mathbf{T}
is simply the time derivative along the diagonal, except in the entry for pressure:
\mathbf{T}\equiv\partial_{t}\circ\left(\delta_{ij}-\delta_{i5}\delta_{j5}\right)
where
\delta_{ij}

\left(\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{L}\right)\psi=0
and do not contain any quadratic combinations of the variables.

The linearization of APE follows exactly the approach laid out in
23
. Starting from the definition of APE in (
14

\textrm{APE}=-\frac{g}{2}\eta^{2}\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}(z-\eta)+\frac{g}{2}\int_{0}^{\eta}\xi^{2}\partial^{(2)}\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}(z-\xi)d\xi.

(26)

Next, by using the relations
\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}(z-\eta)=\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}(z)+\rho_{\textrm{e}}

\rho_{\textrm{e}}=\rho_{0}g^{-1}N^{2}\eta_{\textrm{e}}
together with the expansion

\eta=\eta_{\textrm{e}}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{z}\log\left(N^{2}(z)\right)\eta_{\textrm{e}}^{2}+O\left(\eta_{\textrm{e}}^{3}\right),

(27)

one arrives at the linearized version of APE

\textrm{APE}_{\textrm{lin}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}N^{2}\eta_{\textrm{e}}^{2},

(28)

where terms of order
O\left(\eta_{\textrm{e}}^{3}\right)
are neglected. From this we define the volume-integrated energy
\mathcal{E}
as

\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{2L_{x}L_{y}}\int(u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2}+N^{2}\eta_{\textrm{e}}^{2})\,dV,

(29)

which is the quadratic counterpart to the exact volume integrated energy defined in (
14
) and (
15
).

Linearizing APV in (
18

(u,v,w,\eta)
and expanding
\eta
in terms of
\eta_{\textrm{e}}=-\rho_{\textrm{e}}\left(\partial_{z}\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}\right)^{-1}
using (
27

\textrm{QGPV}\equiv\partial_{x}v-\partial_{y}u-f\partial_{z}\eta_{\textrm{e}},

(30)

as shown in
10

\mathcal{Z}

\mathcal{Z}=\frac{1}{2L_{x}L_{y}}\int\left(\partial_{x}v-\partial_{y}u-f\partial_{z}\eta_{\textrm{e}}\right)^{2}\;dV.

(31)

which is the quadratic counterpart to the volume integral of exactly conserved available potential enstrophy defined in (
19

4.2
The wave-vortex decomposition

The primary tool that enables the analysis are the complete set of energetically orthogonal solutions to linearized equations of motion
\left(\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{L}\right)\psi=0
with their associated projection operators. Mathematical details are relegated to appendix
A
, so here we summarize the essential features.

For our numerical simulations with a bounded domain and finite resolution there is a countable set of eigenmode solutions to
\left(\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{L}\right)\psi=0
which are indexed by horizontal wavenumbers
k,\ell
and vertical mode
j
that correspond to geostrophic, internal gravity wave, inertial oscillation, and mean density anomaly solutions. This solution set is
complete
, which means that we can represent any ocean state as a sum of the individual modes

\psi=\sum_{k\ell j}A_{0}^{k\ell j}(t)\Psi_{0}^{k\ell j}+A_{+}^{k\ell j}(t)\Psi_{+}^{k\ell j}+A_{-}^{k\ell j}(t)\Psi_{-}^{k\ell j}.

(36)

The
\Psi_{0}
modes are the geostrophic and mean-density anomaly (mda) solutions with a non-zero signature of QGPV, the
\Psi_{\pm}
modes are the internal gravity wave and inertial oscillations solutions with no QGPV, and
A_{0}^{k\ell j}
(t),
A_{\pm}^{k\ell j}(t)
are the coefficients. While any complete basis can be used to represent the ocean state, two key features make this base useful:

1.

each solution satisfies all boundary conditions and the conservations laws in Table
1
and is thus a physically realizeable state of the fluid and,

2.

the solutions are all energetically orthogonal.

Energy orthogonality means that the volume integral of the total quadratic energy is the sum of the squares of the energy of the individual solutions,

\frac{1}{2L_{x}L_{y}}\int(u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2}+N^{2}\eta_{\textrm{e}}^{2})\,dV=\sum_{k\ell j}\epsilon_{g}^{k\ell j}\left|A_{0}^{k\ell j}\right|^{2}+\epsilon_{w}^{k\ell j}\left|A_{+}^{k\ell j}\right|^{2}+\epsilon_{w}^{k\ell j}\left|A_{-}^{k\ell j}\right|^{2}

(37)

where
\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{w}
are the total energy of each mode noted in the second column of Table
5
and combined such that,

\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\equiv\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(\kappa^{2}+L^{-2}\right)^{-1}h_{g}^{j}&k>0,j\geq 0\\
\frac{g}{2}&k=0,j\geq 1\end{cases},\quad\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\equiv\begin{cases}h_{\kappa}^{j}&k>0,j\geq 1\\
h_{\textrm{io}}^{j}&k=0,j\geq 0\end{cases}.

(38)

It is worth emphasizing that any complete basis could be used to partition the fluid flow (a linear partitioning). However, the fact that it is energetically orthogonal (a quadratic partitioning) is what gives this decomposition meaning.

Energy orthogonality provides the prescription for projecting the fluid-state
\psi
onto the orthogonal modes
\Psi_{0}
,
\Psi_{\pm}
and recovering the coefficients
A_{0}
,
A_{\pm}
. The projection operators are summarized in Table
5
and require
u
,
v
,
\eta_{\textrm{e}}
—both pressure and vertical velocity are determined diagnostically for this system. Other combinations of the state variables are not sufficient for a complete description of the fluid.

5
Energy fluxes and the wave-vortex decomposition

The key result from section
4
is the projection operators which take the fluid state
(u,v,\eta)
and return the fields separated into their wave and geostrophic parts. However, to compute the energy fluxes between the wave-vortex modes, we must express the nonlinear equations of motion in terms of the wave-vortex basis using the projection operators
\mathcal{P}^{0}_{\textrm{g}}
,
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{w}}^{+}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{w}}^{-}
as defined in appendix
A.9
. In terms of the state-vector
\psi
, the equations-of-motion were expressed as

\left(\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{L}\right)\psi+\mathbf{\nonlinop}\left[\psi\right]={\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}}.

(
24
)

Starting by projecting the forcing, we define the forcing terms in wave-vortex space as,

\displaystyle F_{0}^{k\ell j}\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{0}_{\textrm{g}}\left[\mathcal{S}\right]

\displaystyle F_{+}^{k\ell j}\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\textrm{w}}\left[\mathcal{S}\right]

\displaystyle F_{-}^{k\ell j}\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}_{\textrm{w}}\left[\mathcal{S}\right]

and then use a shorthand to express the nonlinear advection terms,

\displaystyle\left[{\mathbf{u}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}^{k\ell j}\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{0}_{\textrm{g}}\left[{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla u,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla v,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla w,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\eta_{\textrm{e}}+w\eta_{\textrm{e}}\partial_{z}\ln N^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\left[{\mathbf{u}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\right]_{+}^{k\ell j}\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\textrm{w}}\left[{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla u,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla v,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla w,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\eta_{\textrm{e}}+w\eta_{\textrm{e}}\partial_{z}\ln N^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\left[{\mathbf{u}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\right]_{-}^{k\ell j}\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}_{\textrm{w}}\left[{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla u,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla v,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla w,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\eta_{\textrm{e}}+w\eta_{\textrm{e}}\partial_{z}\ln N^{2}\right]

resulting in the nonlinear equations of motion,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A_{0}^{k\ell j}=

(39a)

(39b)

5.1
Energy fluxes

With the nonlinear equations of motion expressed in wave-vortex space (
39
), it is now possible to compute the energy flux into the wave and geostrophic reservoirs, completing Figure
4

39

\displaystyle\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\partial_{t}\left|A_{0}^{k\ell j}\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle\Re\left[\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\bar{A}_{0}^{k\ell j}\left[{\mathbf{u}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\right]^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\right]+\Re\left[\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\bar{A}_{0}^{k\ell j}F_{0}^{k\ell j}\right]

(40a)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\partial_{t}\left|A_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle\Re\left[\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\bar{A}_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\left[{\mathbf{u}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\right]^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\right]+\Re\left[\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\bar{A}_{\pm}^{k\ell j}F_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\right]

(40b)

where we have combined the two sets of wave coefficients into one (
\pm
and w). These expressions can be compared to equation 2.48 in
17
written for the Navier-Stokes equations and, because both expressions use an energetically orthogonal basis, the same analysis techniques will apply.

There are two quasi-conservation laws related to (
40

15

\sum_{k\ell j}\Re\left[\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\bar{A}_{0}^{k\ell j}\left[{\mathbf{u}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\right]^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\right]+\Re\left[\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\bar{A}_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\left[{\mathbf{u}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\right]^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\right]\approx 0.

(41)

As a result, this implies that that total change in quadratic total energy should be entirely from the forcing,

\sum_{k\ell j}\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\partial_{t}|A_{0}^{k\ell j}|^{2}+\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\partial_{t}|A_{\pm}^{k\ell j}|^{2}\approx\Re\left[\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\bar{A}_{0}^{k\ell j}F_{0}^{k\ell j}\right]+\Re\left[\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\bar{A}_{\pm}^{k\ell j}F_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\right].

(42)

The arrows from the sources and sinks to the reservoirs in Figure

40

41

4

41

\Pi_{\textrm{w}}^{<}(k,\ell,j)\equiv\sum_{k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\Re\left[\epsilon^{k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}j^{\prime}}_{\textrm{g}}\bar{A}_{0}^{k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}^{<k\ell j}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}^{<k\ell j}\right]_{\textrm{g}}^{k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\right]

(45)

is the total amount of energy fluxed into the geostrophic reservoir from two waves with scales less than
k\ell j
. This is the most computationally expensive operation of this manuscript, as it requires considering all combinations of
k\ell j
(in practice we restrict to logarithmically-spaced
k_{h}
and
j
suitable for analysis and visualization). The source wave modes for
\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}
is the derivative of
\Pi_{\textrm{w}}^{<}(k,\ell,j)
which we call the ‘mirror flux’,

\mathcal{M}\left[\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}\right]\equiv\partial_{k}\partial_{\ell}\partial_{j}\Pi_{\textrm{w}}^{<}(k,\ell,j)/\left(\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\bar{A}_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\right)

(46)

where
\partial_{k}\Pi(k,\ell,j)\equiv\Pi(k,\ell,j)-\Pi(k-1,\ell,j)
is the first order difference and
\Pi_{\textrm{w}}^{<}(k,\ell,j)=0
for any
k,\ell,j<0
. Just as
\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}
has implicit indices
k\ell j
of the geostrophic modes,
\mathcal{M}\left[\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}\right]
has implicit indices
k\ell j
of the wave modes—with the property that both the flux term and its mirror sum to the same total energy. The mirror operation
\mathcal{M}
can also be applied to the
\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\right]_{\textrm{w}}
triad component to produce a flux from the geostrophic modes. This construction allows us to rewrite the triads in their most useful form, which separates the effects of cascades within the fields from transfers between the two fields:

geostrophic ggg-cascade:

F_{\textrm{ggg}}^{k\ell j}\equiv\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}

geostrophic ggw-cascade:

F_{\textrm{ggw}}^{k\ell j}\equiv\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}+{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}+\mathcal{M}\left[\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\right]_{\textrm{w}}\right]

geostrophic transfer:

F_{\textrm{g-tx}}^{k\ell j}\equiv\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}-\mathcal{M}\left[\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\right]_{\textrm{w}}\right]

wave www-cascade:

F_{\textrm{www}}^{k\ell j}\equiv\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{w}}

wave wwg-cascade:

F_{\textrm{wwg}}^{k\ell j}\equiv\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}+{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{w}}+\mathcal{M}\left[\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}\right]

wave transfer:

F_{\textrm{w-tx}}^{k\ell j}\equiv-\mathcal{M}\left[\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{w}}\right]_{\textrm{g}}\right]+\left[{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\nabla{\mathbf{u}}_{\textrm{g}}\right]_{\textrm{w}}
.

The four cascade terms inherit the triad flux conservation laws from (
44
) that

\sum\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\bar{A}_{0}^{k\ell j}F_{\textrm{ggg}}^{k\ell j}\approx 0,\quad\sum\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\bar{A}_{0}^{k\ell j}F_{\textrm{ggw}}^{k\ell j}\approx 0,\quad\sum\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\bar{A}_{\pm}^{k\ell j}F_{\textrm{www}}^{k\ell j}\approx 0\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sum\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\bar{A}_{\pm}^{k\ell j}F_{\textrm{wwg}}^{k\ell j}\approx 0

(47)

and, of course, the transfer terms are opposite and equal,

\sum\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{g}}\bar{A}_{0}^{k\ell j}F_{\textrm{g-tx}}^{k\ell j}=-\sum\epsilon^{k\ell j}_{\textrm{w}}\bar{A}_{\pm}^{k\ell j}F_{\textrm{w-tx}}^{k\ell j}

(48)

thereby conserving energy.

6.1
One-dimensional fluxes

With no- or constant-stratification the linear, energetically orthogonal solutions are comprised of sines and cosines with clearly defined vertical scales, and it is easy to define a total wavelength. However, in variable stratification, the energetically orthogonal solutions have a vertical structure with variable length scales, as described by the vertical modes in Table
4
of appendix
A

For example, an internal wave packet initially localized to the surface, will change its apparent length scale as it propagates downward entirely under linear dynamics. Thus, we can no longer rely on changes in length scale to indicate nonlinearity. One approach is to stretch the vertical modes on a WKB scaled vertical coordinate
6

For variable stratification, we define a length scale combining vertical mode and horizontal scales. Just as the horizontal wavelength is
2\pi/\kappa

is
\lambda_{d}=2\pi\sqrt{gh}/f
for both the geostrophic modes (using

\sqrt{gh_{g}}/f

\lambda_{p}\equiv 2\pi/k_{p}
where
k_{p}^{2}\equiv k^{2}+\ell^{2}+f^{2}/(gh)

To visualize and interpret the fluxes (both forced and inertial) we use pseudo-wavelength to collapse the three-dimensional values
(k,\ell,j)
onto the one-dimensional value,

This visualization has the important caveat that for the inertial fluxes transfers may not be local, and could jump across wavelength, making the local cascade interpretation incorrect.

The geostrophic fluxes can also be visualized in terms of kinetic energy fraction, the ratio of kinetic energy to total energy, and similarly the wave fluxes can be visualized in terms of intrinsic wave frequency

6.3
Separating the closure scales

The inertial fluxes in Figure

In particular, the wwg transfer term for the geostrophic energy shows no significant transfer at scales below 100 km, until the damping scales below 6 km, and shows increasing total transfer to wave energy until the same scales. 7
Discussion

As discussed in the introduction, estimating energy fluxes within and between wave and geostrophic flows requires two conditions: 1) a method for separating waves and vortices and 2) energy orthogonality between the two flow components. Here we examine our proposed approach in the context of these two conditions.

7.1
Waves and APV

An interpretation of the wave-vortex decomposition as fundamentally about ‘PV-inversion’ emerges from the form of the projection operator for internal-gravity waves (fourth row in Table
5
). To separate the internal gravity waves from the rest of the flow, one must
first
compute the potential vorticity, deduce the associated geostrophic streamfunction (a PV-inversion
24
), and only then can the internal gravity wave structures be deduced. In the case of constant stratification or hydrostatic flows, the vertical mode projection operators all commute, and this fact is quickly obscured in the derivation. At least conceptually then, it is helpful to think about the wave-vortex decomposition as originating from a definition of potential vorticity.

Our theoretical framework is that we define a wave as flow that has no Eulerian signature of available potential vorticity. The strength of this approach is that it does not rely on assumptions about the frequency of oscillation of flow features, but can be diagnosed at any instant in time. The decomposition, however, does not use APV (
18
), but the lower order approximation of QGPV (
30
) to separate wave and vortical flow. Figure
13
shows that the QGPV is fairly good approximation to APV, although some of the small-scale features are artifacts of the approximation. To quantify fidelity of QGPV as a diagnostic for APV we compare the spectrum of APV and QGPV with respect to the orthogonal QGPV modes as defined in appendix
B
.

There are two sources of error in the approximation of APV, equation

. The relative vorticity error scales with Rossby number, and becomes a significant error at the submeoscales. The planetary vortex stretching error is a generalization of the height nonlinearity terms identified by
1

5

11

A higher order correction to QGPV inversion can be found using optimal balance, an approach that starts from the linear eigenmodes used here, and uses an iterative procedure to improve decomposition of the potential vorticity part of the flow
33
;
8
. Application of a single backward-forward iteration of optimal balance over one inertial period reduces the APV error of the balanced field by an order of magnitude compared to the linear geostrophic modes, but appears to come at the cost of energy orthogonality.

7.2
Quadratic energy

The second major condition for measuring energy and energy fluxes is orthogonality between the energy content of the wave and vortex fields. The method described here has formal depth-integrated orthogonality with respect to quadratic energy, and not the fully conserved non-linear energy, equation (
13
). However, for the particular problems considered here, we find no significant differences between these two quantities. Based on the linearization of APE, we would only expect significant discrepancies when the vertical displacements are large.

Potential enstrophy fluxes

The potential enstrophy fluxes are mostly a simple story, as seen in the similarity between the simulations in Figure

7.4
Helmholtz decomposition

When presenting this work we are often asked how the wave-vortex decomposition relates to the Helmholtz decomposition of the horizontal velocities. This wave-vortex decomposition reduces to a Helmholtz decomposition if you neglect the buoyancy anomaly, as can be seen by zeroing out
\eta_{\textrm{e}}
in the projection operators shown in Table
5
. The consequence of this approximation is that one now requires additional assumptions to make a decomposition because waves have both rotational and divergent components and, simply by degrees-of-freedom counting, a straight decomposition is not possible without more information. In both
2
and
43
a temporal filter is applied which relies on a temporal scale separation between the waves and geostrophic flow. This provides an orthogonal decomposition of horizontal kinetic energy in the time domain and, having neglected buoyancy, total energy is no longer decomposed, and the full equations of motion cannot be projected. Notably the same temporal filter could be applied to the wave-vortex decomposition here, as no temporal information is used in this decomposition.

7.5
Hydrostatics

In the course of this work, the MFW simulation was also run under hydrostatic dynamics (not shown). The results were quite similar, with only a few key differences. First, the hydrostatic simulation produced fluxes from the NIO,
M_{2}
, and damping that are 22%, 25%, and 17% greater than the non-hydrostatic fluxes, respectively. The differences are statistically significant given our precision. Second, and somewhat unexpectedly, the hydrostatic and non-hydrostatic simulations showed nearly perfectly overlapping wave spectra of depth-integrated total energy. The key difference between the hydrostatic and non-hydrostatic simulations appears in the mooring spectra, where the distribution of wave energy above the local buoyancy frequency is significantly higher under hydrostatic dynamics, an effect obscured by the depth-integrated spectrum. We conjecture that the inertial energy cascade preserves total energy fluxed in both the hydrostatic and non-hydrostatic simulations, hence the spectra match, even though the vertical spatial distribution does not.

8
Conclusions

This work establishes a framework for computing total energy and potential enstrophy fluxes between orthogonal reservoirs of a fluid in variable stratification.
A key aspect is that the wave–vortex decomposition is derived from the fully conserved available potential vorticity, enabling its fidelity to be tested.
Secondly, because the decomposition is complete, the full nonlinear equations of motion are expressed in this basis without approximation.
Within this framework there are two wave cascade mechanisms, two geostrophic cascade mechanisms, and two mechanisms for transferring between waves and geostrophic motions.

Application of the decomposition to mid-ocean simulations with mean-flow, tidal, and near-inertial forcing shows a robust geostrophic inverse cascade with significant transfer of large-scale geostrophic energy to small-scale wave energy, along with forward cascade of wave energy. Further decomposing the fluid into damped (small-scale) and un-damped wave and geostrophic reservoirs, we find no evidence for a forward geostrophic cascade in this forcing regime. In the broadest sense this agrees with other work where large-scale low-frequency motions flux energy to small-scale motions
49
;
50
;
51
;
43
. The work here points to transfers from the wwg triad as the primary energetic pathway from large-scale geostrophic energy to small-scale damped motions as seen in Figure
4
.

Linear momentum evolution in (

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}\lvert\psi\rangle+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{L}\lvert\psi\rangle=

A.2
The solutions

The central idea is that any observable state-vector,
\lvert\psi\rangle

\lvert{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle

\lvert{\Psi}_{\textrm{io}}\rangle

\lvert{{\Psi}}_{\textrm{g}}\rangle

A.3
Projection and notation

The ultimate outcome of this work are a set of orthogonal solutions and projection operators, which take the real-value observable dynamical fields
(u,v,\eta_{\textrm{e}})
and project them onto real-valued observable orthogonal solutions. The other two dynamical variables,
w
and
p
, are diagnostically determined from the continuity equation and the vertical momentum equation. In fact, it is worth noting that the full state cannot be recovered from other combinations of the variables, only
(u,v,\eta_{\textrm{e}})
contain the complete information of the state. Thus, before even solving the equations of motion, we describe the projection operators.

The projection operators onto the eigenmode solutions are also composed of two parts: the horizontal projections, which take the form of Fourier transforms, and the vertical projections, which result from various Sturm-Liouville problems.

The solutions and projection operators require specification of
\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}(z)
and there is a strict physical requirement that
\eta_{\textrm{e}}
must adhere to the condition that

\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}(z)>\partial_{z}\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}(z)\eta_{\textrm{e}}(\mathbf{x})>\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}(z)-\tilde{\rho}_{\textrm{nm}}\left(-D\right),

(67)

to maintain the adiabatic constraint. Although not a physical requirement, for the work in this manuscript we make the additional simplifying assumption that
\eta_{\textrm{e}}(0)=0=\eta_{\textrm{e}}(-D)
. In notable contrast to
\eta_{\textrm{e}}
, the physical fields
(u,v)
do not have bounds on their values.

A.3.1
Horizontal transforms

A simplifying assumption for this problem is that the two horizontal dimensions
(x,y)
are periodic, which means that we can use a Fourier basis in the horizontal direction for our linear solutions. We now define the operators that move us between the spatial domain
(x,y)
and wavenumber domain
(k,\ell)
.

The horizontal structure of all real-valued functions in the domain have the form,

f(x,y)=\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{xy}\left[\hat{f}(k_{n},\ell_{m})\right]=\hat{f}(0,0)+\sum_{n\geq 0,m\in\mathcal{N}(n)}\left[\hat{f}(k_{n},\ell_{m})e^{ik_{n}x+i\ell_{m}y}+c.c\right]

(68)

where
k_{n}=2\pi n/L_{x}
and
\ell_{m}=2\pi m/L_{y}
and
\mathcal{D}^{-1}\left[\cdot\right]
is the inverse Fourier transform in two-dimensions. We have defined

\mathcal{N}(n)=\begin{cases}m=-\infty\ldots\infty&\textrm{for }n\geq 1\\
m=1..\infty&\textrm{for }n=0\end{cases}

(69)

so that the summation runs over unique coefficients, exploiting the Hermitian symmetry of real-valued functions that allows us to assume
c.c.={\hat{f}}^{\ast}(k_{n},\ell_{m})e^{-ik_{n}x-i\ell_{m}y}
. To recover a coefficient we define the forwards Fourier transform projection operator
\mathcal{D}\left[\cdot\right]
,

\hat{f}(k_{n},\ell_{m})=\mathcal{D}_{xy}\left[f(x,y)\right]=\frac{1}{L_{x}L_{y}}\int_{A}f(x,y)e^{-ik_{n}x-i\ell_{m}y}\,dA

(70)

which follows from the orthogonality, i.e., given two solution with wavenumbers
(k_{a},\ell_{a})
and
(k_{b},\ell_{b})

\frac{1}{L_{x}L_{y}}\int_{A}e^{ik_{a}x+i\ell_{a}y}e^{-ik_{b}x-i\ell_{b}y}\,dA=\delta_{k_{a}k_{b}}\delta_{\ell_{a}\ell_{b}}.

(71)

With these definitions, variance is preserved in both domains,

\frac{1}{L_{x}L_{y}}\int_{A}f^{2}(x,y)\,dA=\hat{f}(0,0)+\sum_{n\geq 0,m\in\mathcal{N}(n)}2\left|\hat{f}(k_{n},\ell_{m})\right|^{2}.

(72)

To simplify notation, anytime we write
\hat{f}
, that should be assumed to be shorthand for
\hat{f}(k_{n},\ell_{m})=\mathcal{D}_{xy}\left[f(x,y)\right]
and
\bar{f}
is shorthand for the
k=\ell=0
component that is simply a horizontal average.

because there
\bar{\rho}(z)
was used to define the background state whereas here we use
\rho_{\textrm{nm}}(z)
(an anonymous reviewer of
13
first brought this mode to our attention). However, the presence of the mean density anomaly is important for constructing a complete basis when linearizing about the no-motion state. Simply swapping two fluid parcels in the water column results in an mda solution and changes the potential energy and enstrophy of the system.

Depth-integrating (
86
),
one finds that
the mda solutions are constrained by global conservation of potential density

\int_{-D}^{0}N^{2}\bar{\eta}_{e}dz=-\int_{-D}^{0}\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\partial_{z}\bar{p}_{e}=\bar{p}_{e}(0)-\bar{p}_{e}(-D)=0,

(87)

and they are also constrained by global conservation of QGPV,

\int_{-D}^{0}\partial_{z}\bar{\eta}_{e}dz=\bar{\eta}_{e}(0)-\bar{\eta}_{e}(-D)=0.

(88)

In the rigid-lid problem, (
87
) tells us that the average pressure anomaly at the two boundaries must be zero, with
\bar{p}_{e}(-D)=\bar{p}_{e}(0)=0.
In addition, (
88
) says that the average buoyancy anomaly at the boundaries must vanish, with
\bar{\eta}_{e}(-D)=\bar{\eta}_{e}(0)=0
.

Even though the mda solutions have no fluid velocity, their energy and enstrophy are both nonzero, and the
G_{\textrm{g}}(z)
eigenmodes found from (
78
) diagonalize both quantities. Thus it is natural to describe the mda solutions using (
78
) together with appropriate boundary conditions
that also satisfy the constraints
(
87
)-(
88
).
However, if we impose both
p_{\textrm{e}}(-D)=p_{\textrm{e}}(0)=0

and

\eta_{\textrm{e}}(-D)=\eta_{\textrm{e}}(0)=0
, then the boundary value problem is overdetermined.
Therefore we must ask: what is the correct choice?

Enforcing (
87
) would disallow diabatic processes, which are included in our formulation (
3
). In contrast, if we impose (
88
), then no additional restrictions beyond the boundary conditions
\eta_{\textrm{e}}(-D)=\eta_{\textrm{e}}(0)=0
are imposed. The choice here is clear. Proceeding with the choice
G(-D)=G(0)=0
, projection of
\bar{\eta}_{\textrm{e}}
onto the
G_{\textrm{g}}(z)
eigenmodes follows immediately. Using integration by parts, it can be shown that

\mathcal{G}_{\textrm{g}}[\bar{\eta}_{\textrm{e}}]=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}g}\mathcal{F}_{\textrm{g}}[\bar{p}_{\textrm{e}}],

(89)

as noted in the third row of Table
5
, where we denote the coefficients of the mda solution
\lvert{{\Psi}}_{\textrm{mda}}\rangle
as
A_{0}^{00j}
.

A.6
IGW projection

The required relationships between the vertical modes
F_{\kappa}
(for
u
,
v
, and
p
) and
G_{\kappa}
(for
w
and
\eta_{\textrm{e}}
) are

F_{\kappa}=h_{\kappa}\partial_{z}G_{\kappa}\textrm{ and }(N^{2}-\omega_{\kappa}^{2})G_{\kappa}=-g\partial_{z}F_{\kappa}

(90)

which follow from the continuity equation and the vertical momentum equation, respectively. These relationships lead to the eigenvalue problem

\partial_{zz}G_{\kappa}^{j}-\kappa^{2}G_{\kappa}^{j}=-\frac{N^{2}-f^{2}}{gh_{\kappa}^{j}}G_{\kappa}^{j}

(91)

with boundary conditions
G_{\kappa}(0)=0=G_{\kappa}(-D)
, where we used that

\omega_{\kappa}^{j}\equiv\sqrt{gh^{j}_{\kappa}\kappa^{2}+f^{2}}.

(92)

The quantity
\omega_{\kappa}^{j}
should be viewed as a shorthand for the wave frequency that is dependent on the eigenvalue
h_{\kappa}^{j}
and the total wavenumber
\kappa=\sqrt{k^{2}+\ell^{2}}
.
Unlike constant stratification or hydrostatics, posing the eigenvalue problem in terms of
\omega
does not lead to a complete basis of orthogonal solutions.

Vertical modes
G_{\kappa}
therefore satisfy the orthogonality condition

\frac{1}{g}\int_{-D}^{0}\left(N^{2}-f^{2}\right)G_{\kappa}^{i}G_{\kappa}^{j}\,dz=\delta_{ij}

(93)

which leads to projection operator
\mathcal{G}^{j}_{\kappa}[\eta]
and its inverse
\mathcal{G}^{-1}_{\kappa}[\eta_{w}^{j}]
as defined in the fourth row of Table
4
. Unlike the geostrophic modes, there is no eigenvalue problem associated with the
F_{\kappa}
modes as discussed in
13
.

To determine the wave coefficients
A_{\pm}^{k\ell j}
we again exploit orthogonality and project the observed state
\lvert\psi\rangle
onto the internal gravity wave mode. This particular calculation is more challenging and we save the details for appendix
D
. The net result is

A_{\pm}^{k\ell j}=\frac{\langle{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}|\mathbf{H}|\psi\rangle}{\langle{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}|\mathbf{H}|{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle}\\
=\frac{e^{\mp i\omega_{\kappa}^{j}t}}{2\kappa h_{\kappa}^{j}}\left(i\mathcal{G}^{j}_{\kappa}\left[\hat{w}\right]\mp\omega_{\kappa}^{j}\mathcal{G}^{j}_{\kappa}\left[\hat{\eta}-\hat{\eta}_{g}\right]\right)

(94)

where
\hat{\eta}_{g}
is the isopycnal deviation of the geostrophic mode at this same wavenumber. This solution projection operator is shown in the fourth row of Table
5
. The vertical velocity can be replaced in favor of the horizontal divergence using that

\hat{w}=-\mathcal{G}_{g}^{-1}\left[h_{g}^{j}\mathcal{F}^{j}_{g}\left[ik\hat{u}+il\hat{v}\right]\right],

(95)

where we have used the relationships in (
80
) and the continuity equation,
-\partial_{z}\hat{w}=ik\hat{u}+il\hat{v}
.

A.7
IO projection

The inertial oscillations exist at the IGW limit where
\kappa=0
and
\omega=f
, such that
G_{\textrm{io}}
satisfies

\partial_{zz}G^{j}_{\textrm{io}}=-\frac{N^{2}-f^{2}}{gh^{j}_{\textrm{io}}}G^{j}_{\textrm{io}},

(96)

with
G^{j}_{\textrm{io}}(0)=0=G^{j}_{\textrm{io}}(-D)
.
The corresponding boundary value problem for
F^{j}_{\textrm{io}}
is given by

\partial_{z}\left(\frac{\partial_{z}F^{j}_{\textrm{io}}}{N^{2}-f^{2}}\right)=-\frac{1}{gh^{j}_{\textrm{io}}}F^{j}_{\textrm{io}},

(97)

with
\partial_{z}F^{j}_{\textrm{io}}(0)=0=\partial_{z}F^{j}_{\textrm{io}}(-D).

Thus for the inertial oscillations with
\kappa=0

F^{j}_{\textrm{io}}
may be stated as

\int_{-D}^{0}F_{\textrm{io}}^{i}F_{\textrm{io}}^{j}\,dz=h_{\textrm{io}}^{i}\delta^{ij},

(98)

as indicated in the last row of Table

Projection onto the inertial oscillation solution follows with,

\langle{\Psi}_{\textrm{io}}|\mathbf{H}|\psi\rangle=\frac{e^{-ift}}{2}\int_{-D}^{0}\left(\bar{u}-i\bar{v}\right)F_{\textrm{io}}(z)dz=\frac{e^{-ift}}{2}h_{\textrm{io}}^{j}\mathcal{F}^{j}_{\textrm{io}}\left[\bar{u}-i\bar{v}\right]

which means that,

A_{-}^{00j}=\frac{\langle{\Psi}_{\textrm{io}}|\mathbf{H}|\psi\rangle}{\langle{\Psi}_{\textrm{io}}|\mathbf{H}|{\Psi}_{\textrm{io}}\rangle}=\frac{e^{-ift}}{2}\mathcal{F}^{j}_{\textrm{io}}\left[\bar{u}-i\bar{v}\right]

(100)

as shown in the last row of Table

A.8
Summary of solutions

These four solutions fall into two obvious categories: 1) wave solutions which include the internal gravity waves
\lvert{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle
and inertial oscillations
\lvert{\Psi}_{\textrm{io}}\rangle
and 2) potential vorticity (or vortex) solutions which include geostrophic motions
\lvert{{\Psi}}_{\textrm{g}}\rangle
and the mean-density anomaly
\lvert{{\Psi}}_{\textrm{mda}}\rangle
. The key feature separating these solution types being whether or not they have potential enstrophy.

A.9
Generalized projection operators

Inserting the wave-vortex projection operator/identity operator
\mathcal{P}
from (
64
) into the linear momentum equations reduces to the almost trivial statement

\left(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{L}\right)\mathcal{P}\lvert\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}=\partial_{t}\lvert\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}=0,

(101)

which tells us that the coefficients of the linear eigenmodes are constant in time.
For example, the equation for the linear evolution of the inertia-gravity waves is given by

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{L}\right)\frac{\lvert{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle\langle{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}|\mathbf{H}|\psi\rangle}{\langle{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}|\mathbf{H}|{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=0

(102a)

\displaystyle A_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\left(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{L}\right)\lvert{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle+\partial_{t}A_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\lvert{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=0

(102b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A_{\pm}^{k\ell j}\lvert{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle

\displaystyle=0

(102c)

where we used (
62
) and
\left(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{L}\right)\lvert{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}\rangle=0
from (
59
).

For the purposes of this manuscript we will define two primary projection operators,
\mathcal{P}^{0}_{\textrm{g}}

\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{w}}^{\pm}

which isolate the part of the flow with and without qgpv. To be more concrete we unravel the definitions of the inner-product and express the projection operators relative to the wave-vortex basis (indexed by

\partial_{z}p(x,y,0)=0

\partial_{z}p(x,y,-D)=0

\hat{p}_{k\ell j}=\mathcal{F}_{g}\left[\mathcal{D}_{xy}\left[p\right]\right]

\frac{1}{L_{x}L_{y}}\int_{V}p^{2}(x,y,z)\,dV=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(h_{g}^{j}\left|\hat{p}_{00j}\right|^{2}+\sum_{m,n}^{\mathcal{N}}2h_{g}^{j}\left|\hat{p}_{k\ell j}\right|^{2}\right).

(109)

where we have used subscripts
k\ell j
as shorthand for
(k_{n},\ell_{m},j)
. The cross-spectrum of two real-valued functions
p(x,y,z)
,

with the same boundary conditions follows with,

\operatorname{SF}\left[p,q\right](k_{n},\ell_{m},j)=\begin{cases}2h_{g}^{j}\Re\left[\hat{p}_{k\ell j}\hat{q}^{\ast}_{k\ell j}\right]&n\geq 0,m\in\mathcal{N}\\

(111)

then the cross-spectrum that results can be defined as,

Appendix C
Transfers from triad fluxes

Consider a decomposition of the flow into three energetically orthogonal reservoirs of energy,
u=u_{a}+u_{b}+u_{c}
. The energy flux into reservoir
a
is,

\frac{d}{dt}E_{a}=\sum_{k\ell j}\Re\left[\epsilon_{a}^{k\ell j}A^{k\ell j}[(u_{a}+u_{b}+u_{c})\nabla(u_{a}+u_{b}+u_{c})]_{a}^{k\ell j}\right]

(114)

where
\epsilon_{a}
is the energy multiplier and
A
is the amplitude for the modes in that reservoir. Using the notation that,

T_{abc}^{(a,b)\mapsto c}\equiv\Re\left[\epsilon_{c}^{k\ell j}A^{k\ell j}[u_{a}\nabla u_{b}+u_{b}\nabla u_{a}]_{c}^{k\ell j}\right]

(115)

is the energy transfer from reservoirs a and b to reservoir c, the triad conservation laws can be written as

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle T_{abc}^{(a,b)\mapsto c}+T_{abc}^{(a,c)\mapsto b}+T_{abc}^{(b,c)\mapsto a}

(116a)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle T_{aac}^{a\mapsto c}+T_{aac}^{(a,c)\mapsto a}

(116b)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle T_{aaa}^{a\mapsto a}

(116c)

where
T_{aac}^{a\mapsto c}\equiv T_{aac}^{(a,a)\mapsto c}
and we have used subscripts label the triad to which that term belongs. Note that in the special case of the
aaa
triad, equation (
116c
), the transfer is unambiguously closed—energy stays within the a reservoir. In the special case of the
aac
triad, equation (
116b
), even though the term
T_{aac}^{(a,c)\mapsto a}
is ambiguous in its transfer source, the other term
T_{aac}^{a\mapsto c}
resolves the ambiguity, in contrast to the triad components for three unique reservoirs, equation (
116a
). The total energy fluxes of the three reservoirs can now be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{a}=

\displaystyle T_{aaa}^{a\mapsto a}+T_{bba}^{b\mapsto a}-T_{aab}^{a\mapsto b}+T_{cca}^{c\mapsto a}-T_{aac}^{a\mapsto c}+T_{abc}^{(b,c)\mapsto a}

(117a)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{b}=

\displaystyle T_{aab}^{a\mapsto b}-T_{bba}^{b\mapsto a}+T_{bbb}^{b\mapsto b}+T_{ccb}^{c\mapsto b}-T_{bbc}^{b\mapsto c}+T_{abc}^{(a,c)\mapsto b}

(117b)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{c}=

\displaystyle T_{aac}^{a\mapsto c}-T_{cca}^{c\mapsto a}+T_{bbc}^{b\mapsto c}-T_{ccb}^{c\mapsto b}+T_{ccc}^{c\mapsto c}+T_{abc}^{(a,b)\mapsto c}

(117c)

satisfying the total energy conservation law that

\frac{d}{dt}E_{a}+\frac{d}{dt}E_{b}+\frac{d}{dt}E_{c}=0.

(118)

With the goal of drawing arrows connecting the reservoirs, the terms in (
117
) are sorted according to which reservoir they are transferring from. The ambiguity in the
abc
triad is not resolvable in general
9
, so we make a good practical choice. We would like to express the triad in terms of unambiguous transfers
T^{b\mapsto a}
,
T^{a\mapsto c}
, and
T^{c\mapsto b}
such that,

\displaystyle T_{abc}^{(b,c)\mapsto a}=

\displaystyle T^{b\mapsto a}-T^{a\mapsto c}

(119a)

\displaystyle T_{abc}^{(a,c)\mapsto b}=

\displaystyle-T^{b\mapsto a}+T^{c\mapsto b}

(119b)

\displaystyle T_{abc}^{(a,b)\mapsto c}=

\displaystyle T^{a\mapsto c}-T^{c\mapsto b}

(119c)

which satisfies the conservation law (
116a
). The solution to this equation is

\begin{bmatrix}T^{b\mapsto a}\\
T^{a\mapsto c}\\
T^{c\mapsto b}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}T_{abc}^{(b,c)\mapsto a}-T_{abc}^{(a,c)\mapsto b}\\
T_{abc}^{(a,b)\mapsto c}+T_{abc}^{(b,c)\mapsto a}\\
-T_{abc}^{(a,c)\mapsto b}-T_{abc}^{(a,b)\mapsto c}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}T_{0}\\
T_{0}\\
T_{0}\end{bmatrix}

(120)

with the ambiguity up to the arbitrary constant
T_{0}
.

To choose an appropriate value for
T_{0}
, it is helpful to consider two limits: the
catalyst limit
and the
equipartition limit
. The catalyst limit occurs when one of the triad terms is zero, e.g.
T_{abc}^{(b,c)\mapsto a}\approx 0
. The usual interpretation here is that the
a
reservoir is acting as a catalyst, neither gaining or losing energy, but facilitating the transfer between reservoirs
b
and
c
and thus
T^{b\mapsto a}=T^{a\mapsto c}=0
with only
T^{c\mapsto b}\neq 0
. The equipartition limit occurs when two triad terms are equal, e.g.,
T_{abc}^{(b,c)\mapsto a}=T_{abc}^{(a,c)\mapsto b}
, in which case the transfer in or out of reservoirs
a
and
c
should be equal,
T^{a\mapsto c}=-T^{c\mapsto b}
. As a consequence, this also requires that
T^{b\mapsto a}=0
, just as in the catalyst limit. We there for make the choice that whenever, e.g.,
T_{abc}^{(a,b)\mapsto c}
has the largest magnitude, one should take
T^{b\mapsto a}=0
such that

\begin{bmatrix}T^{b\mapsto a}\\
T^{a\mapsto c}\\
T^{c\mapsto b}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-T_{abc}^{(b,c)\mapsto a}\\
T_{abc}^{(a,c)\mapsto b}\end{bmatrix}

(121)

and similarly when the magnitudes of

The wave mode projection operator is derived by first noting that because
\langle{\Psi}^{\pm}_{\textrm{w}}|\mathbf{H}|{{\Psi}}_{\textrm{g}}\rangle=0
