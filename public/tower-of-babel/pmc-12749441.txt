Intravenous acellular human amniotic fluid prevents ischemic cardiac remodeling
Authors: Grace Mitchell, Hadi Javan, Ian Nickel, Steven Valdez, Joseph A. Palatinus, Marta Szulik, David Eberhardt, Sarah Franklin, Dipayan Chaudhuri, Mikhail Skliar, Carla Valenzuela Ripoll, Jan Pierce, Craig H. Selzman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Amniotic products are potent immunomodulators used clinically to repair tissue injury. This study sought to determine the impact of acellular human amniotic fluid (hAF) on cardiac remodeling. Amniotic fluid was obtained from volunteer donors at the time of elective caesarean section, proprietarily processed into a sterile filtered acellular hAF, and administered to rats following coronary ligation. Compared to controls, hAF treated animals had a nearly sixfold decrease in both infarct size and fibrosis. Under hypoxic stress, hAF-treated H9C2 cells demonstrated higher cell viability and mitochondrial membrane stability and less apoptosis compared to saline-treated cells. Here we demonstrate that a single intravenous dose of acellular hAF provides functional cardioprotection that is mechanistically associated with cellular tolerance to hypoxic insult likely afforded by the plethora of naturally produced cytokines, chemokines, and immune-modulating proteins present in hAF. The ubiquitous availability of acellular hAF offers promising potential for its use as a cardioprotective adjunct.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28735-7.

Introduction

Despite significant advances in pharmacologic and interventional therapies, ischemic insults continue to result in cardiac remodeling and the development of heart failure1,2. Many pathophysiologic targets have been experimentally identified to modulate the impact of myocardial ischemia, but few have been implemented clinically. Identifying accessible, safe, and effective adjuncts to prevent or reverse ischemic heart failure remains a priority3,4.

Amniotic products, including membrane and fluid, have diverse immune-modulatory properties that have been utilized to treat a variety of conditions including burns, epithelial wounds, and chronic inflammatory conditions5–9. There is a paucity of data examining the influence of amniotic products in cardiovascular disease, with the majority of reports focusing on using amniotic fluid-derived stem cells and their secretome to augment myocardial regeneration10–13. Translation of these cell-based therapies are historically limited secondary to concerns for immunogenicity, malignancy, and regulatory challenges. Alternatively, we have developed a proprietary method of high throughput generation of a cell-free, non-antigenic solution from patients requiring term, elective caesarean sections14. This human amniotic fluid (hAF) is devoid of any cellular products yet carries a host of naturally-derived proteins that retain their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activity15. We recently performed detailed proteomic analysis by mass spectroscopy of several hAF donors that imparted cardioprotection from ischemia reperfusion injury and confirmed the presence of numerous immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory proteins16.

We previously demonstrated that hAF significantly protected the myocardium and preserved left ventricular function when rodents were subjected to ischemia–reperfusion (IR) injury17. Compared to controls, there was preservation of cardiac function on echocardiography (at the same level of sham animals), and a 75% decrease in infarct size. Protein arrays in hAF treated animals demonstrated augmented expression of a plethora of immune-modulating genes. In addition, our findings were not dependent on a single hAF donor, but were reproducible across 6 different amniotic fluid donors. Importantly, these results were seen with both intramyocardial injection as well as the more clinically relevant intravenous injection of hAF.

Having observed such potent cardioprotection in a model of reperfusion injury, we herein sought to determine the influence of hAF on the development and reversal of heart failure associated with pure myocardial ischemia.

Results

To investigate the influence of hAF on ischemic heart failure, rats were subjected to left anterior descending (LAD) coronary ligation with or without intravenous injection of hAF. We examined multiple time points of hAF administration to reflect several clinically-relevant situations. At the outset, we were curious if hAF could reverse existing heart failure (HF) and promote reverse remodeling. Hence, hAF was delivered 2 weeks after LAD ligation for 5 consecutive days. In this scenario, hAF was unable to reverse the deterioration in left ventricular function or promote reverse ventricular remodeling (Fig. 1A). Quite possibly, we did not give enough time for the hAF to impart its effect, so a separate cohort of animals was observed with the similar experimental condition for 12 weeks. We next sought to determine if hAF could prevent the development of ischemic heart failure. Animals were given intravenous hAF at several time-points: pre-ligation (one dose), 24 h post-ligation (one dose), and immediately post-ligation at chest closure for 5 consecutive days). As depicted in Fig. 1B,D (and Supplementary Table 1), serial echocardiography demonstrated preservation of left ventricular (LV) function for the pretreatment and immediate post-ligation group. Conversely, there was no effect when delivered 24 h after ligation (Fig. 1C).

Recognizing that cardioprotection was afforded when administered either pre- or immediately following ligation for 5 days, we wondered if a similar effect would occur with only a single dose of hAF following coronary occlusion. Compared to controls, and similar to shams, peri-infarct delivery of hAF preserved LV function, prevented ventricular dilation, and decreased heart and lung weights (Fig. 2). To better quantify the extent of injury, animals were ligated and administered hAF immediately following ligation and 24 h later hearts were procured with subsequent triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 2). Histopathologically, hAF markedly reduced the size of the infarct both visually and quantitively (Fig. 3C) as a ratio of LV size (NS, 0.40 ± 0.06 vs hAF, 0.07 ± 0.02, p < 0.01). Biochemically, (Fig. 3D), troponin release was markedly decreased in those animals receiving hAF (13,340 ± 1006 ng/ml vs 3214 ± 1411 ng/ml, p < 0.01). To further assess cardiac remodeling, we examined the influence of hAF on myocardial fibrosis 4 weeks after IR (Fig. 3B,E). Qualitatively, compared to those that received hAF, NS treated animals had marked wall thinning and chamber dilation as well as replacement fibrosis of the anterior wall consistent with a phenotype of ischemic cardiomyopathy. Following these observations, we undertook several investigations to analyze factors which might confer cardioprotection including immunomodulation, hypoxia resistance, reduction of apoptosis, and extracellular vesicle content. We first examined the impact of hAF on the immune response following LAD ligation by assessing the transcription of a series of well-known cytokines and biomarkers in whole heart tissues. Compared to control animals, hAF treatment was associated with reduced expression of several inflammatory cell surface markers representing macrophage and lymphocyte cell infiltration including CD45, CD11, and Cx3cr1 (Fig. 3F). hAF similarly decreased expression of the inflammatory cytokines IL-18 and IL-6.

To better understand how hAF profoundly decreased myocardial infarction size after coronary ligation, we utilized a more controlled, in vitro, environment to hone-in on mechanism. Our overarching hypothesis was that hAF provided protection from or resistance to hypoxic stress. We first performed kinetics studies utilizing H2C9 cells and MTT cell viability assays to identify 16 h of hypoxia as optimal stress. A live/dead assay was used to assess plasma membrane integrity. The polyanionic dye calcein is well retained within live cells, producing an intense uniform green fluorescence. On the other hand, ethidium homodimer-1 dye (EthD-1) enters cells with damaged membranes and undergoes a 40-fold enhancement of fluorescence upon binding to nucleic acids, producing a bright red fluorescence in dead cells. Assays were performed in both normoxic and hypoxic conditions to better quantify the influence of hAF on cell viability (Fig. 4B). In normoxic conditions, hAF stimulated cellular proliferation. While hypoxia decreased cell viability in both cohorts, the hAF group nearly maintained its original growth potential and remained much more viable than even normoxic control cells. We further quantified and sub-categorized cell stages by flow cytometry (representative flow diagram, Supplementary Fig. 3). As depicted in Fig. 4C, hAF had no impact on the number of necrotic cells exposed to hypoxia, but maintained more viable cells and markedly decreased the number of apoptotic cells compared to cells bathed in culture media without hAF (7.3 ± 0.5% versus 15.7 ± 1.4%, p < 0.001).

Observing the signal that hAF is protecting cells from apoptosis, we returned to the in vivo rodent model to examine whether or not our in vitro results would be confirmed in tissue samples. Utilizing myocardial tissue from the LAD-ligated hearts, we identified that hAF treated animals demonstrated significantly lower levels of Caspase-3 expression versus controls (Fig. 5: hAF: 4.0 ± 2.7 vs. saline: 16.2 ± 6.1, p < 0.0001) and was similar to that of control (2.2 ± 2.3) and sham (5.3 ± 3.4) animals. In order to better define aspects of cell death, cultured H9C2 cells exposed to hypoxic conditions were examined for mitochondrial membrane stability to characterize apoptotic cells. Tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester, perchlorate (TMRE) is a cell membrane permeant, positively charged dye that accumulates in active mitochondria due to their relative negative charge. In early apoptosis however, the programmatic opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) leads to depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential which prohibits the sequestration of TMRE. Combined with DAPI, which binds strongly to A-T-rich regions in DNA, and does so particularly well in dead cells, this dual staining technique can identify the ratio of apoptotic to viable cells under certain experimental conditions. Figure 6 illustrates the results of TMRE and DAPI staining for cells treated with hAF under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Figure 6A, provides individual representative flow cytometry images which are then quantified and analyzed in Fig. 6B,C. These data collectively demonstrate that cells cultured in normoxic conditions cluster in the bottom right of the TMRE/DAPI axes to indicate the presence of viable, healthy cells regardless of treatment with CM or hAF (p > 0.05). When cells are exposed to hypoxia, though, cells treated with CM alone demonstrate a shift to the bottom left (TMRE-/DAPI-; apoptotic), indicating an increase in the presence of apoptotic cells. Meanwhile, cells treated with hAF demonstrate a preservation of the bottom right (TMRE+/DAPI−; viable) distribution seen in cells cultured under normoxia, indicative of greater cell viability in this population. While it is known that extracellular vesicles (EVs) are robust components of amniotic fluid12,18,19, we sought to determine the role of EVs in our filtered, acellular hAF. We first identified that indeed, hAF has a significant EV component with nanoparticles that predominately ranged in the 35–200 nm in hydrodynamic diameter (Fig. 7A). This finding is consistent with previous reports identifying nanoparticles in hAF as small EVs expressing canonical exosomal markers20. To assess whether the cytoprotective effects of acellular hAF under hypoxic conditions are mediated by EVs, soluble factors, or their combined activity, we separated hAF into EV-enriched and EV-depleted fractions using size exclusion chromatography (SEC). Supplementary Table 5 summarizes the relative nanoparticle and protein content of each fraction in comparison to unfractionated hAF. The high ratio of EVs to proteins in the EV-enriched fraction indicates successful isolation of extracellular vesicles with minimal protein contamination, consistent with high-purity EV preparation21. The total number of EVs in the enriched fraction was comparable to, and slightly exceeded, the estimated EV content in the starting hAF sample, indicating near-complete recovery. Specifically, based on nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), 8.8 × 1011 EVs were estimated to be present in 200 μL of the enriched fraction, compared to 5.3 × 1011 EVs in 2.6 mL of unfractionated hAF. This level of agreement falls within the expected variability of NTA-based concentration measurements, which is influenced by sample handling, dilution steps, and instrument settings22. The effects of EV-enriched and EV-depleted fractions on the survival of H2C2 cells following a 16-h hypoxic challenge were evaluated and compared to those of unfractionated hAF using a six-well MTT assay. Hypoxic cells treated with 25 µL of unfractionated hAF received approximately 5.075 × 10⁹ EVs and 173.75 µg of total protein. To isolate the effect of EVs alone, a second was treated with 1.2 µL of the EV-enriched fraction, delivering an equivalent number of EVs but substantially lower protein content (2.64 µg). A third group received 122.3 µL of the EV-depleted fraction, closely matching the total protein content of the unfractionated hAF but a reduced, though still substantial, number of EVs (2.925 × 10⁹). As shown in Fig. 7C, all treatment groups improved cell viability relative to untreated hypoxic control. However, unfractionated hAF conferred the greatest protective effect, suggesting that both vesicular and non-vesicular components contribute synergistically to the cytoprotective activity of amniotic fluid in rescuing cells after hypoxic stress.

Discussion

Herein, we demonstrate that purified, cell-free human amniotic fluid significantly mitigates the development of heart failure associated with permanent coronary ligation. Experiments were intentionally designed to mimic several clinical scenarios. We first explored the ability of hAF to treat existing heart failure by administering hAF well after the ischemic insult. Neither pathologic nor functional improvements were observed. While we had previously demonstrated that hAF was able to salvage ischemic-reperfusion injury when delivered post-injury17, one could surmise that the damage associated with permanent ligation was too advanced to reverse. Indeed, ejection fraction roughly dropped from 75 to 25% in a 2-week period. In a similar manner, hAF delivered 24 h after ligation was still unable to influence the development of remodeling and reduced left ventricular function.

Conversely, if present around the time of acute ischemia—either before or immediately after—a single intravenous dose of hAF was able to profoundly limit infarct size, fibrosis, and preserve cardiac function to nearly normal levels. We were admittedly surprised at the extent of protection afforded by hAF: the proximal left anterior descending artery was completely occluded, yet the infarct size was < 5% of the LV and the ejection fraction was > 70%. To our knowledge, this level of cardioprotection in rat LAD ligation models is uncommon. In general, studies that are considered positive and promote adjunct therapy, including cell therapy, cytokines, growth factors, and targeted drugs both as mono and poly-therapy, demonstrate increases in ejection fraction between 3 and 9%23,24. In the present study, we observed an absolute increase, or maintenance of LV function by more than 30%. Troponin levels in hAF animals were still greater than sham, meaning there was myocyte injury, but it was markedly reduced compared to controls, suggesting that despite some myocardial injury, there is a substantial difference in the subsequent response to that initial injury.

In order to assign mechanism to these observations, several different pathways were assessed. Recognizing prior work implicating extracellular vesicles (EVs) in critical biologic functions as well as their presence in amniotic fluid25,26, we sought to determine the role of EVs in our processed, acellular hAF. When assessing viability under hypoxic conditions, neither the EV-rich or EV-depleted fraction could match the cellular protection compared to soluble hAF. These studies reinforce the notion that it is not a singular component of hAF which provides its cardioprotection, but rather the interplay of numerous survival ingredients evolutionarily designed to protect the fetus.

We have previously demonstrated that hAF contains a plethora of immune and anti-inflammatory proteins15–17. Bioinformatic analysis of global proteomic assays on 6 hAF donors revealed clusters of proteins involved in immunity, inflammatory responses, cell adhesion, and apoptotic signaling pathways16. In addition, these proteins were highly modified, with methylation and deamidation being the most abundant modifications. This study represented the first mass-spectrometry-based characterization of full-term, acellular hAF, suggesting that hAF offers a wide array of immune-modulating proteins working together to provide robust cardioprotection.

Indeed, in the current study, when examining some early biologic signals that affect the remodeling process, we saw significant decreases in expression of inflammatory cell cytokines and immune-modulatory surface markers in vivo. Although it is well known that immune modulation can significantly impact cardiac remodeling after myocardial infarction27, the observed reduction in infarct size and fibrosis appeared out of proportion to changes in cytokines and cell infiltration alone. If hAF is not regenerative (based on the inability to reverse established heart failure), yet preserves myocardial structure and function after significant ischemic injury, we subsequently surmised that hAF must provide cellular resistance to acute injury.

To study the role of hAF on cell death, several different techniques using an in vitro model of hypoxia demonstrated that hAF-treated cells maintain viability almost at the same level of cells cultured in a normoxic environment. Flow cytometry interrogation for both Annexin V and TMRE demonstrated that hAF appeared to stabilize both plasma and mitochondrial cell membranes to prevent cell death28,29. While there was no significant difference in the number of necrotic cells, hAF treated cells were less likely to experience apoptosis. Seeking further confirmation, we returned to the in vivo model and identified that these observations were not isolated to the in vitro environment, but were confirmed in the Caspase-3 staining of harvested myocardial tissue. hAF is comprised of numerous growth factors and hence is it is not surprising that at its baseline and under hypoxic stress, it can act as a growth stimulant, but the recognition of its protection against apoptotic cell death is novel and represents a promising direction for future mechanistic exploration.

Acellular human amniotic fluid therapy offers significant potential clinical relevance. Indeed, we could envision hAF being administered at the time of myocardial infarction, cardiac surgery, and organ transplantation to decrease ischemic injury, despite the level of subsequent reperfusion. Compared to other amniotic products referenced in the literature that suggest the benefits of amniotic fluid were due to its cellular aspects10–13, hAF is devoid of insoluble components (i.e. cells, vernix, and lanugo). Issues related to immunogenicity or tumor-potential are eliminated. Our preclinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of a single intravenous infusion of hAF (rather than the initial approach of intramyocardial delivery). Furthermore, we have completed clinical trials of intravenous hAF in humans and observed an attenuated acute phase response without safety signals30,31. Importantly, despite inherent differences in human hAF donors, our findings were consistent across multiple donors (Supplementary Table 3), suggesting that the effect of hAF is not donor-specific, and that large supplies of hAF could be processed for therapeutic use.

Despite encouraging results, a number of limitations to the current study and its potential translation must be acknowledged. Several challenges remain to better understand the in vivo kinetics and distribution of intravenously administered hAF. While we have demonstrated some elemental (proteomic) and functional (infarct response) homogeneity amongst donors, our studies only included 6 different donors. If we were to scale the needs for human use, whereby larger volumes of hAF would be needed, we would need to have a process to ensure biologic reproducibility across thousands of potential donors. Amniotic fluid quite possibly has subtle differences between donors based on gestational age, maternal age, and parity that the current studies were unable to detect32. In the original ischemia–reperfusion studies, intramyocardial injections were used before transitioning to intravenous administration17. To date, it remains unknown how intravenous hAF is distributed in the body. For example, how much of the hAF, and its corresponding functional elements, is actually making it to the heart (versus sequestration in the liver).

In conclusion, hAF robustly protects the acutely ischemic heart following permanent coronary ligation. A putative mechanism for hAF-afforded cardioprotection is maintenance of cell viability and prevention of apoptosis in response to hypoxia, a phenomenon consistent with the role of amniotic fluid in the womb. Importantly, we admittedly have not identified a singular factor to account for the salutatory effects of hAF, as the multiple natural components of hAF make such identification difficult. However, this does not deter from the clinical translatability of our findings. hAF has been utilized in clinical trials for other conditions without negative side effects, and thus readily translatable to patients with ischemic heart disease and subsequent interventions. Summarily, hAF is a natural product containing a multitude of soluble factors that has been crafted evolutionarily to synergistically provide innate tissue protection and that no pharmaceutical company could ever recreate.

Methods

Human amniotic fluid (hAF)

Allogenic hAF was procured and processed at the time of elective caesarean-section. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Utah (IRB#036454). Informed consent was obtained from all donor volunteers. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Briefly, an aliquot of hAF was removed and designated for pre-processing microbiologic testing for aerobic, anaerobic, and fungal microorganisms. Then, the hAF was transferred via aseptic techniques and processed using a proprietary filtration technology to obtain a sterile acellular hAF.

Myocardial infarction model

Male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA) at 7–8 week of age with a body weight of 201–225 g and housed at the accredited University of Utah animal facility. All experiments were approved by the University of Utah Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and followed the guidelines provided by the National Institutes of Health in Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals as well as ARRIVE guidelines. All rats had access to food and water ad libitum and were housed in plastic cages on standard 12/12 h light/dark cycles.

Ischemic heart failure was induced by performing a double-loop ligation of the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery as previously described33. Surgeon and investigators were blinded to treatment group (syringes were covered). Either normal saline (NS, control group) or hAF (experimental group) was injected over 60 s into the jugular vein at the respective experimental timepoints. The volume of injected hAF was 500 uL, (~ 5% of total blood volume) and was chosen based on previous testing in our ischemia–reperfusion model17. Sham animals had a similar procedure with placement of a loose suture around the coronary artery with our impairing blood flow.

Echocardiography

Echocardiograms were performed using a small animal echocardiography system (Vevo2100 High-Resolution Imaging System, Visual Sonics) equipped with a 13- to 24-MHz linear-array transducer (MS250, MS400 MicroScan Transducer, Visual Sonics). Heart rates were tightly monitored while isoflurane was titrated between 1 and 2% at 1–2 L/min of supplemental oxygen flow to maintain heart rates within a narrow window of 350–400 beats per minute as measured by continuous electrocardiography. Two axis views (short axis and parasternal long axis) were obtained in accordance with recommendations from the American Physiological Society for all animals34. All measurements were obtained by a blinded investigator (HJ) and averaged over three consecutive cardiac cycles.

Acute infarct size

Twenty-four hours after LAD ligation, hearts were harvested and rinsed stepwise in PBS and potassium chloride (2.56 M KCl) solution. Hearts were cut into 2 mm sections along short axis and placed in individual wells of a 24 well plate loaded with 2 ml of 2% 2,3,5 triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) solution per well, and incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking for 30 min. Heart sections were transferred to 10% phosphate buffered formalin for 90 min and rinsed before imaging using a stereomicroscope (Olympus SZX10 and U-TV0.5XC-3, Tokyo, Japan) to calculate infarct size as a ratio of the left ventricle (IF/LV) size34,35.

Measurement of cardiac-specific troponin

In addition to visual changes of the heart following coronary ligation (distal blanching and akinesis), myocardial injury was assessed by serum troponin collected 24 h after ligation. Ten percent of the animal’s total blood volume (TBV) was withdrawn via the jugular vein and serum collected. Troponin (TnI) levels were determined via a commercial ELISA kit (ab246529, Abcam).

Histopathology

After 4-week echocardiography was performed, hearts were quickly harvested following euthanasia under isoflurane. Hearts and dried lungs were weighed to calculate body weight ratios. Hearts were then paraffin embedded and serial sagittal (longitudinal) sections of rat myocardium were cut, fixed, and stained with Masson’s trichrome. Whole-field digital microscopy was performed using the Aperio ScanScope XT (Aperio Technologies) as previously described36. A pixel count algorithm with optimized color hue and saturation values (Aperio Image Scope 12.4, Leica Biosystems Imaging, Inc.) was set to accurately identify blue-colored collagen fibers versus red cardiomyocytes, thereby allowing for calculation of percent fibrosis.

For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin embedded heart sections were deparaffinized, followed by heat-induced epitope retrieval. Slides were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. Primary antibody (Rabbit anti-rat Caspase 3, Invitrogen, PA5-778871:100) was diluted in 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day slides were washed 3 times with 1X PBS and incubated for 2 h with secondary antibody (Donkey anti-rabbit AF594, Invitrogen, A327524, 7.5 μg/ml). Slides were then washed with 1X PBS two times, added DAPI-containing mounting media (ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI, Invitrogen, P36941), and cover-slipped. Images were acquired using the Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope (Thornwood, NY) and then quantified using the ImageJ software (NIH).

Quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction

At the time of necropsy, apical segments of hearts were removed and immersed in RNA-later for processing. RNA was isolated using Trizol. After RNA isolation, reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript™ IV VILO™. Real time PCR was performed using Applied Biosystems 7900HT with TaqMan™ Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, 4444964). Expression of mRNA targets were quantified using TaqMan gene expression assays (Thermo Scientific, see Supplementary Table 6) and were calculated in accordance with the 2-ΔΔct method (fold change = 2(−ΔΔct)) and normalized to expression of 18 s rRNA.

In vitro hypoxia studies

H9C2 cells (American Type Culture Collection) were cultured and experiments were performed up to 10 passages. After 24 h at 70% confluence, cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with or without hAF and placed into the normoxia incubator (95% air and 5% CO2 at 37 °C) or the hypoxia incubator (95% nitrogen and 5% CO2 at 37 °C). After 16 h of normoxia or hypoxia, cell viability was evaluated using a 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide assay (MTT, Invitrogen). Optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm using a Cytation 5 microplate reader on area-scan mode (Agilent Technologies, Inc., CA, USA). Measurements were repeated 3 times in triplicate.

Cell viability was also measured qualitatively using a 2-color fluorescence live/dead assay (Molecular Probes, MP03224). Briefly, after experimental conditions, cells were exposed to 150 µl of 2 µM Calcein solution in 4 µM EthD-1 solution and mounted on the stage of a Leica SP8 TCS confocal microscope system (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Two dyes, Calcein and EthD-1, were measured with an excitation and emission at 494/517 nm and 528/617 nm respectively.

Flow cytometry

Following 16 h of normoxia or hypoxia, apoptosis was evaluated using the eBioscience™ Annexin V-APC Apoptosis Detection Kits (Invitrogen). Annexin V fluorescent conjugates and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) were used to detect dead and apoptotic cells. DAPI- Annexin V− cells are considered viable or healthy, while DAPI- Annexin V+ staining indicates apoptotic cells, and DAPI + Annexin V+ cells are generally considered dead or non-viable. Cell aliquots were exposed to heat shock injury at 48 C for 5–10 min to serve as positive single-color controls for DAPI and Annexin V for compensation setup. To measure mitochondrial membrane potential, H9C2 cells were re-suspended in 300 μl flow cytometry buffer (PBS pH7.4 with 2% BSA and EDTA) in ice box. Cells were labeled with Tetramethylrhodamine, Ethyl Ester, Perchlorate (TMRE) (Cat. no. T669, Invitrogen) and DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) for 15 min.

Cell samples were processed with a BD FACS Canto II (BD Bioscience), using BD FACSDiva™ software (version 9.0). Data was analyzed using FlowJo™ (version 10.6.2) with a gating strategy that first eliminated debris and doublets before assaying cell health (Annexin V-APC vs DAPI or TMRE vs DAPI). Quadrants were used to delineate cell subsets and to report percentages of each.

Isolation of hAF nanoparticles and concentration of extracellular vesicle fractions

To determine the concentration and size distribution of nanoparticles in acellular hAF, we performed nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) following established protocols21,37. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) were isolated from thawed human amniotic fluid (hAF) using size-exclusion chromatography coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography. Samples were centrifuged at 3000×g for 15 min at 4 °C and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove debris. The clarified supernatant was applied to a pre-equilibrated SEC column (qEVoriginal/70 nm, Izon Science) connected to an HPLC system with UV detection at 280 nm. Elution was performed with sterile, endotoxin-free PBS at 0.5 mL/min, and 0.5 mL fractions were collected. Collected fractions were concentrated using Amicon® Ultra-15 centrifugal filter units (MilliporeSigma) with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 100 kDa for the EV-enriched and 3 kDa for the EV-depleted fraction. EV-enriched fractions (typically fractions 7–10) were pooled and concentrated using 100 kDa MWCO centrifugal filters (4000×g for 20 min). EV-depleted fractions were separately concentrated using 3 kDa filters for comparative analyses (4000×g for 80 min).

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between groups for serial echocardiographic measurements were made using 2-way repeated measures ANOVA with Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons using the statistical package, Prism 6.07 (GraphPad). Troponin, qPCR, TTC, flow cytometry, MTT assay, and immunofluorescence results were analyzed using 1-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons for data with normal and non-normal distribution, respectively. Two-tailed Student t test or Mann–Whitney test was used for comparisons between groups in all other experiments, with normal and non-normal distribution respectively. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was declared with a p-value < 0.05.
