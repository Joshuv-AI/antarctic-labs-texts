Prognostic Factors in Cochlear Implant in Otosclerosis: Radiologic and Clinical Variables
Authors: Giulia Zambonini, Sara Ghiselli, Giuseppe Di Trapani, Daria Salsi, Domenico Cuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to determine whether radiologic and clinical data can predict the cochlear implant (CI) benefit in patients with otosclerosis, with a focus on the prognostic value of computed tomography (CT) features and relevant clinical variables.Methods:A retrospective single-centre analysis was conducted on 126 otosclerotic patients (143 implanted ears) who underwent CI between 2009 and May 2025. Preoperative and postoperative CT scans were reviewed and classified using established grading systems (Symons/Fanning, Rotteveel, Veillon). Clinical variables included age at implantation, duration of hearing loss (HL), preoperative audiometric thresholds, history of stapes surgery, electrode design, and scala of insertion. Audiological outcomes were evaluated at 12 months postactivation using aided pure tone average and aided speech perception score. Univariate and multivariate statistical analyses were performed.Results:No significant correlation was found between CT-based otosclerosis staging or radiologic features and post-CI audiological outcomes. Multivariate analysis identified increased age at implantation, electrode insertion into the scala vestibuli, and use of straight electrodes as predictors of poorer speech perception. A longer duration of HL also negatively impacted outcomes. Precurved electrodes and scala tympani placement were associated with superior results. Previous successful stapes surgery predicted better performance.Conclusions:While CI provides effective auditory rehabilitation in otosclerotic patients, radiologic CT staging does not predict functional outcomes. Instead, clinical factors—age, duration of HL, electrode design, and array placement—are more influential. Preoperative CT remains essential for surgical planning, but patient counselling should focus on clinical history and procedural details.Level of Evidence:Level III.

Introduction

Otosclerosis is a disorder of bone remodeling within the otic capsule and a common cause of conductive or mixed hearing loss (HL); occasionally progressing to sensorineural forms. Otosclerosis begins with an “otospongiotic” phase, osteoclasts resorb the lamellar bone of the otic capsule, followed by a “sclerotic” phase, deposition of dense lamellar bone. Both often coexist in the same focus1.

Otosclerotic plaques are most frequently located anterior to the oval window (OW), in the region of the “fissula ante fenestram” (80%). Other common sites include the round window (RW, 30%), the pericochlear region (21%), or the anterior wall of the internal auditory canal (IAC, 19%). Less common involvement includes ossicles, facial nerve canal, semicircular canals, or endolymphatic duct2.

Diagnosis relies on clinical history, audiometric evaluation, and impedance testing, with high-resolution computed tomography (CT) playing a supportive role. Computed tomography (CT) allows visualization of bony abnormalities of the stapes, cochlea, and labyrinth. CT can identify reduced bone density in otospongiotic phase, and increased density in sclerotic phase with a sensitivity of ∼80% versus histopathologic findings and ∼90% versus clinical features3,4.

Several CT-based otosclerosis staging systems exist, although none is universally accepted5. The most cited are those by Symons and Fanning et al.6, Rotteveel et al.7, and Veillon et al.8 (Table 1). The first two have been described in patients with advanced otosclerosis undergoing cochlear implantation (CI). The Symons/Fanning classification6 focuses on cochlear involvement, the distribution of foci around the cochlear turns. In contrast, Rotteveel et al.7 highlights the “double ring” sign (hypodensity surrounding the cochlear profile) and the basal turn narrowing. The Veillon et al. classification8 highlights fenestral extension and involvement of the cochlear endosteum (Table 1).

HL management depends on severity and type, ranging from hearing aids (HA) and stapes surgery (SS) when to CI. SS is indicated in cases of conductive HL related to stapes fixation, whereas HA are recommended for patients who prefer not to undergo surgery or who present with mixed or sensorineural HL. CI is considered in cases of far advanced otosclerosis, characterized by severe-to-profound HL in which conventional amplification with HA is no longer effective. CI outcomes vary and can influenced by cochlear ossification, duration of deafness, and prior surgeries9. However, prognostic factors remain incompletely defined.

This retrospective study on a large CI cohort aims primarily to assess the predictive value of radiologic findings on functional outcomes. The secondary objective is to evaluate the prognostic impact of various clinical variables on functional outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This single-center retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary referral CI center and included consecutive patients with otosclerosis undergoing cochlear implantation between 2009 and May 2025. Diagnosis was based on clinical history, audiological and radiologic findings, with intraoperative confirmation when available. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Area Vasta Emilia Nord (969/2022/OSS*/AUSLPC; February 7, 2023).

Study population and unit of analysis

Overall, 126 patients (143 implanted ears) were included; 17 underwent sequential bilateral implantation. Because some patients contributed two ears, the ear was the unit of analysis, with statistical models accounting for within-patient correlation. Audiological outcomes at 12 months were available for 131 ears. Preoperative CT scans were available for 88 ears, while 24 had only postoperative imaging.

Preoperative assessment and clinical variables

All candidates underwent standard preoperative assessment, including high-resolution temporal bone CT and MRI to evaluate cochlear anatomy and patency. Clinical and audiological variables were retrospectively extracted from medical records, including age at implantation, preoperative PTA (0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz), duration of profound auditory deprivation, total duration of HL, and previous stapes surgery (SS) on the implanted side. “Years of deafness” was defined as the period of profound HL during which HA no longer provided meaningful benefit. “Years between first identification of HL and CI” estimated total hearing impairment duration. Previous SS was categorized as noSS (SS not performed), SSwb (SS with benfit), or SSchl (SS with complete HL, “dead ear”).

Radiologic assessment

CT scans were independently reviewed by two experienced otologic surgeons; disagreements on staging or specific radiologic features were resolved with a neuroradiologist until consensus was reached. Radiologic staging was performed using the CT-based classifications of Symons and Fanning, Rotteveel, and Veillon6–8. Involvement of specific anatomic subsites was also recorded, including the OW, RW, fissula ante fenestram, cochlear basal, middle, and apical turns, IAC, vestibule, and semicircular canals. Additional radiologic features were recorded as binary variables, affected/unaffected area: basal turn narrowing, contact between otosclerotic foci and the cochlear endosteum, double-ring sign, and diverticulum of the anterior IAC wall. When only postoperative CT imaging was available, assessment accounted for metallic artifacts from the electrode array; RW involvement and basal turn narrowing were not evaluated. For descriptive purposes, ears without radiologic evidence of otosclerosis were classified as grade 0.

Surgical variables

Operative reports were reviewed to collect intraoperative and device-related variables. These included scalar location of insertion, classified as scala tympani (ST) or scala vestibuli (SV), and electrode design.

Although implants from different manufacturers were used, electrode arrays were grouped according to design into straight (lateral wall) and precurved (perimodiolar/mid-scala) arrays. Complete versus partial insertion was also recorded.

Postoperative outcome measures

Postoperative audiological outcomes were assessed 12 months after CI activation. The primary functional outcome measure was the aided speech perception score (aSPS). The secondary outcome measure was aided pure tone average (aPTA), calculated across 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz.

Speech perception testing was performed in quiet using lists of 60 recorded disyllabic words presented at 65 dB SPL10. In cases of unilateral or asymmetric HL, the contralateral ear was masked with speech-weighted noise in order to isolate the performance of the implanted ear.

Statistical analysis

The main objective of the statistical analysis was to assess the association between radiologic and clinical variables and postoperative speech perception performance.

Because the unit of analysis was the implanted ear and some patients contributed bilateral sequential implants, analyses were performed using methods that accounted for nonindependence of observations within the same patient. Associations between radiologic staging systems and 12-month aSPS were, therefore, assessed using linear mixed-effects models, with patient included as a random effect.

The same modeling strategy was used for the analysis of specific binary radiologic findings and anatomic subsites. For staged classifications, categories were also dichotomized for clinically oriented comparisons: Symons/Fanning and Rotteveel as grade 1 versus grade ≥2, and Veillon as grade ≤2 versus grade ≥3. These groupings were defined to distinguish limited from more extensive disease while avoiding excessively small subgroup sizes.

Clinical and surgical predictors of outcome were first explored in univariate analyses. Variables associated with postoperative performance and considered clinically relevant were then entered into a multivariable model with 12-month aSPS as the dependent variable. Candidate predictors included age at implantation, preoperative PTA, years of deafness, total duration of HL, previous stapes surgery category, scalar location of insertion, electrode design, and radiologic variables.

To derive a more parsimonious explanatory model, an additional stepwise selection procedure was performed as an exploratory analysis. Results from this analysis were interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with the main multivariable model.

A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study cohort comprised 126 patients with otosclerosis who underwent CI at the study center between 2009 and May 2025. Seventeen patients received bilateral sequential CIs, resulting in a total dataset of 143 implanted ears. Preoperative CT scans were available for review in 97 patients, corresponding to 113 implanted ears.

Of the 143 implanted ears, 131 completed the 12-month follow-up and were included in the functional analysis. The gender distribution was fairly balanced, with 54.5% females and 45.4% males. The mean age at the time of surgery was 60.9 years, with a range spanning from 23.7 to 83.6 years.

Regarding surgical history, 92 patients had undergone previous SS on the implanted side. Of these, 77 had a single procedure, 14 underwent one revision, and one patient underwent two revisions. Poststapedoplasty anacusis (SSchl) was recorded in 35 cases (38.0%). Before implantation, 48% of the cohort utilized HA. The mean unaided preoperative PTA was profound, calculated at 112.6 dB HL (range: 80–130 dB HL), with unmeasurable thresholds assigned a value of 130 dB HL. Six cases of facial nerve stimulation during the first year of follow-up were recorded, four in patients with lateral wall arrays and two in those with precurved arrays. All cases resolved after adjustment of the stimulation parameters.

Surgical findings

All procedures were performed by the same surgical team via a standard transmastoid approach with posterior tympanotomy. Intraoperative ossification of the Scala Tympani (ST) was encountered in 25 cases (17.5%), which necessitated electrode insertion into the Scala Vestibuli (SV). Complete electrode insertion was achieved in the vast majority of cases (141 ears), with partial insertion occurring in only two instances.

The distribution of implant brands was: Cochlear (79.0%), MED-EL (9.1%), Advanced Bionics (7.0%), and Oticon Medical (4.9%). In terms of electrode design, straight electrodes were utilized in 15.4% of cases, while precurved electrodes were favored in 84.6% (113 perimodiolar, 8 midscalar).

Audiological outcomes

At the 12-month postoperative mark, the cohort demonstrated significant functional improvement. The mean aPTA was 32.8 dB (range: 25–60 dB), and the mean aSPS was 67.7% (range: 0%–100%).

Analysis of clinical prognostic factors

Hearing deprivation

The study examined the impact of auditory deprivation using two distinct variables: “years of deafness” and “years between first HL identification and CI.” Both variables exhibited a significant negative correlation with aSPS. Longer durations of HL and extended periods of total deafness were statistically associated with poorer speech perception outcomes (P=0.0005 and P=0.0124, respectively; Spearman rank test).

Previous stapes surgery

The influence of prior SS was analyzed by comparing the noSS group with the combined SS group (SSwb + SSchl). Preoperatively, patients with prior SS had significantly worse PTA (mean 118.0 dB HL) compared with the noSS group (mean 104.2 dB HL; P=0.00028). However, despite this preoperative disparity, postoperative outcomes were comparable. The aPTA was 31.8 dB HL in noSS, 33.7 dB HL in SSwb, and 33.1 dB HL in SSchl. Similarly, aSPS values were 68.8%, 66.0%, and 69.5%, respectively. Univariate analysis confirmed no significant differences, suggesting that previous SS per se did not negatively impact CI outcomes.

Electrode position

Outcomes were stratified by electrode scalar placement. Patients requiring SV insertion demonstrated lower mean aSPS (57.2%) compared with those with ST insertion (70.1%). This difference showed a strong trend toward statistical significance (P=0.051). Aided thresholds (aPTA) were nearly identical between the two groups (32.8 dB for ST vs. 32.9 dB for SV).

Electrode design

The design of the array significantly influenced outcomes. Precurved electrodes yielded significantly better aPTA (31.3 dB HL) compared with straight electrodes (40.2 dB HL; P<0.001). Furthermore, precurved electrodes were associated with significantly higher speech perception scores (71.2% vs. 50.1%; P=0.0058, t test).

Analysis of radiologic factors

A total of 88 preoperative and 24 postoperative CT scans were analyzed. Radiologic signs of otosclerosis were identified in 84.8% of ears, while 15.2% appeared radiologically normal. Otosclerotic involvement was staged according to the Symons, Rotteveel, and Veillon systems6–8 with case distributions proving heterogeneous across the various stages (the cases without evident damage on CT were classified as grade 0) (Fig. 1). Multivariate analysis

A multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to predict aSPS based on combined clinical and radiologic variables. The model explained 30% of the variability in aSPS (R^2=0.30), with an adjusted R^2 of 0.14, indicating modest explanatory power. The F-statistic was 1.872 (P=0.053), approaching statistical significance.

Notably, the multivariate analysis highlighted that patients in the SSwb group (successful previous stapes surgery) showed higher aSPS values (estimate: 18.2; P=0.0228) compared with other groups.

Discussion

This study represents one of the largest single-center cohorts of CI recipients with otosclerosis, providing a robust dataset for prognostic evaluation. The primary aim was to assess whether preoperative radiologic findings predict functional outcomes to improve counseling and surgical planning. In addition, the study aimed to integrate radiologic and clinical variables to better understand their relative contribution to postoperative speech perception outcomes in this specific population. Collaboration between radiology, audiology, and surgical teams remains essential for comprehensive patient management in this complex patient group overall care.

The role of radiology in prognosis

Preoperative CT is standard in CI work-up, with 80% to 90% sensitivity for otosclerosis3,4. In our cohort, 83.3% showed radiologic signs. However, normal CT does not exclude disease, as small otospongiotic foci may be undetected11. Biochemical bone-remodeling changes may affect cochlear endosteum, spiral ligament, and stria vascularis4, contributing to SNHL beyond imaging resolution. These microscopic changes may contribute to sensorineural HL but are not captured by routine imaging.

Our study applied Symons/Fanning, Rotteveel, and Veillon classifications6–8 to account for disease extent. None correlated with aSPS at 1 year. This aligns with Symons and Fanning6,12, but contrasts with Rotteveel et al., who reported a nonsignificant trend toward worse outcomes in advanced stages7. Overall, staging systems improve descriptive consistency but appear insufficient as standalone prognostic tools for functional CI outcome prediction.

We also extended the analysis to individual radiologic features, such as the “double ring” sign and IAC diverticula, finding no association with postoperative speech perception. Overall, CT is essential for diagnosis and planning, but radiologic burden alone does not predict CI outcome. This reinforces the multifactorial nature of CI outcomes in otosclerosis beyond radiologic severity alone.

Clinical and surgical prognostic factors

Duration of deafness is a key predictor13. Longer HL and profound deprivation correlated with poorer speech perception, consistent with auditory deprivation and cortical reorganization limiting rehabilitation. Precise onset in otosclerosis is difficult to define due to its progressive course and because HA use does not equate to preserved auditory input14. These findings emphasize the importance of timely referral for implantation when progressive deterioration is observed.

Age showed a decline in aSPS of ∼0.5% per year. This aligns with literature on central auditory and cognitive decline affecting speech perception, especially in complex listening15,16. Murr et al. reported ∼5% per decade decline in quiet17, with larger effects in noise18. Older patients can still achieve meaningful benefit. However, variability among individuals suggests that age alone should not be used as a strict exclusion criterion for implantation.

Prior stapes surgery remains debated19,20. Some studies report worse outcomes16,20, others no difference15,19. In our cohort, univariate analysis showed no clear effect, but multivariate analysis suggested better outcomes in patients with successful prior stapes surgery (SSwb) compared with postsurgical dead ear or no surgery, possibly reflecting better-preserved cochlear/neural function. These results suggest that preimplant cochlear status and disease course, rather than surgery itself, may better explain variability in outcomes across patients.

ST insertion yielded better outcomes than SV. Although cochlear ossification is no longer a CI contraindication21, SV is used in difficult cases. Earlier concerns included higher thresholds and insertion trauma22–24, but useful outcomes are achievable25. In our cohort, ST still performed better, likely due to closer spiral ganglion proximity23,24 and lower impedance from ossification/fibrosis26. SV remains valid when ST is not feasible. Nevertheless, careful case selection and surgical expertise remain critical in optimizing outcomes regardless of insertion route.

Precurved electrodes were associated with better thresholds and aSPS, though literature is mixed. Some retrospective studies show benefits27, while prospective data are inconsistent28. In otosclerosis, altered otic capsule properties may affect current spread29. Precurved arrays may provide closer modiolar positioning and focused stimulation but may increase insertion trauma risk30. Selection bias may influence results; overall outcomes depend more on clinical/surgical factors than CT staging alone. These considerations highlight the need for individualized electrode choice based on anatomy, disease extent, and surgeon experience.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Its retrospective single-center design and long inclusion period may limit external validity due to potential heterogeneity in surgical practice, device selection, and imaging assessment, vestibular evaluation, as preoperative CT was not available for all patients. In addition, the multivariate model showed modest explanatory power and should be considered exploratory. Nevertheless, the single-center setting ensured relative consistency in clinical and radiologic protocols, supporting internal coherence. Overall, these findings suggest the need for further investigation of potential correlations between surgical factors, electrode insertion, and radiologic findings, which are currently under further study.

Conclusion

CI is effective in patients with otosclerosis and profound HL, often leading to good audiological outcomes. CT classification does not predict benefits, but preoperative CT is crucial for planning. Comprehensive counseling is essential, as clinical history and duration of HL significantly influence postimplant results.
