Morphometric analysis and weighted sum priority based prioritization of micro-watershed, kiltie watershed, Upper Blue Nile basin, Ethiopia
Authors: Getu Abey Denekewu, Derege Tsegaye Meshesha, Enyew Adgo Tsegaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Micro-watershed prioritization using morphometric parameter analysis is a systematic approach to identifying and ranking micro-watersheds based on their susceptibility to soil erosion. This helps in implementing effective soil and water conservation measures. Soil erosion is one of the earth’s surface environmental problems. The objectives of this study were to (1) analyze 27 morphometric parameters in kiltie watershed. (2) Prioritize micro watersheds using weighted sum priority techniques. A morphometric analysis of 10 micro-watersheds was conducted, assessing characteristics such as linear, areal, shape, and relief, features to estimate erosion vulnerability. Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) digital elevation model (DEM) with 30*30 m spatial resolution was used to delineate the micro-watersheds and drainage networks through ArcGIS 10.7.1 software. Micro watersheds were ranked based on their soil erosion susceptibility by using a weighted sum priority (WSP) value derived from various morphometric parameters. The morphometric analysis and erosion evaluation results showed that six micro watersheds (MW1, MW2, MW3, MW4, MW5, and MW8) contributed very high soil erosion in the study area with 78.07% of the total area of the watershed (17.74 km2), three micro watersheds (MW6, MW7, and MW10) contributed high soil erosion with 20.64% of the total area of the watershed(17.74 km2), and one micro watershed (MW9) contributed medium soil erosion with 1.66% of the total area of the watershed (17.74 km2). Therefore, in the study area where soil erosion is significantly very high and we recommend the implementation of effective erosion control techniques that can contribute to long-term soil and water conservation efforts.

Introduction

Analyzing watershed morphometric parameter is crucial for evaluating erosion risks and prioritizing soil and water conservation initiatives1. Soil and water are vital for life and development plans2, especially in developing countries like Ethiopia, where agricultural production systems are prevalent3,4. Soil erosion poses significant environmental challenges, including loss of fertile soil, reduced crop yields, and degraded water quality5. Therefore, scientific studies are necessary to identify areas prone to erosion and suitable conservation strategies.

Watershed prioritization is a recognized method for pinpointing erosion and flood-prone areas, as well as sites for groundwater exploration6. This approach is critical for managing natural resources and addressing land degradation7. Various scientific methods, such as field surveys, expert judgment, morphometric analysis, multi-criteria decision analysis, satellite imagery, and hydrological modeling, can identify erosion-prone watersheds8,9. However, each method has its advantages and limitations, necessitating careful comparison based on accuracy, cost, data availability, and implementation feasibility10. Morphometric analysis, along with multi-criteria decision analysis and satellite imagery, is particularly effective for identifying and prioritizing erosion-prone areas11,12. While satellite imagery can be costly to obtain and process13, morphometric analysis offers a simpler, more affordable alternative that can yield reliable results when combined with expert consultation14,15.

In summary, morphometric analysis plays a crucial role in watershed prioritization by assessing drainage features, soil erosion potential, surface runoff, infiltration capacity, and groundwater availability16. Therefore, morphometric analysis is still an essential technique for prioritizing watersheds, even given the lack of extensive soil mapping data, land use cover data and climatic variability or hydrogeological measurements, because it can accurately assess both surface and sub-surface conditions14. Previous studies in Ethiopia have employed compound parameter approaches for watershed prioritization17–19, but these often assume equal weight among morphometric parameters, which may not accurately capture each watershed’s unique characteristics7,20. To overcome this, a non-subjective and statistically-based weighting approach is needed to enhance the accuracy of prioritization. Furthermore, there is a lack of research specifically examining the relationship between morphometric characteristics and soil erosion in the context of prioritizing micro-watersheds at risk of erosion in our study area.

To address the identified limitations in previous studies, this research focuses on a comprehensive analysis of various morphometric parameters, including linear, areal, shape, and relief aspects. Specifically, the study aimed to evaluate morphometric parameters to prioritize micro-watersheds in the kiltie watershed based on their erosional potential. This prioritization was utilized a weighted sum priority (WSP) value derived from a combination of these morphometric parameters, employing advanced geospatial and statistical techniques. The significance of this research is seeks to fill existing gaps in understanding the relationship between morphometric characteristics and soil erosion. By enhancing our knowledge of how these parameters influence soil erosion distribution and severity, this study aims to improve the assessment and quantification of erosion risks in the region.

A significant aspect of this research is its novelty, as it applies a non-subjective, statistically computed WSP value approach for watershed prioritization in a humid highland area characterized by strong ecological and hydrological constraints. This methodology has not been previously implemented in the kiltie watershed, making this study a pioneering effort in utilizing morphometric watershed prioritization in this context. By integrating geospatial data with statistical analysis, this research seeks to provide a more objective framework for evaluating micro-watersheds’ erosional potential. The findings are expected to contribute valuable insights for land management practices and soil conservation strategies, ultimately supporting sustainable agricultural practices and mitigating soil erosion in the kiltie watershed.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in the kiltie watershed (11° 18′ 30′′ N–11° 21′ 30′′ N and 36° 47′ 0′′ E–36° 53′ 0′′ E), located in the northwestern part of the Upper Blue Nile basin of Ethiopia (Fig. 1). The watershed covers 17.74 km2 with the altitude ranges between 2023 and 2208 a.m.s.l., which can be found in Dangila district, it is situated at 11° 16′ 00′′ N latitude and 36° 50′ 00′′ E longitude. The district represents midland with altitudes ranging from 1353 to 2454 m above sea level, at a distance of 471 km to the Northwest direction of Addis Ababa and 70 km from Bahir Dar city.

According to the Koppen-Geiger21 classification, the climate is humid subtropical. Moreover, the annual minimum and maximum temperature is 10.42 and 25.65 °C, respectively (Fig. 2)22.

Topography and major soil types

The study area topographically, it is characterized by flat lands and moderately steep rolling topography. According to FOA23 guideline the study area has different slope gradients, such as: 10.44% of the watershed areas have flat slope gradients (0–2%), 44.53% of the watershed areas have medium slopes (5.1–10%), 14.55% of the watershed areas have moderate slopes (10.1–15%), and 9.12% of the watershed areas have moderately steep slopes (15.1–24.33%) (Fig. 3). According to the FAO/UNESCO classification legend the dominant soil type in the study area is Eutric Nitisols, characterized as shallow, moderate to deep, and very deep in depth and sandy clay to clay textural types24.

Geology and lithology map of the study area

According to the World Geologic Maps of the United States Geological Survey (USGS), the study area is composed of quaternary igneous and tertiary igneous rocks, and the lithology of the watershed is composed of colluvium and volcanic ash, tuff, and mudflow (Fig. 4).The dominate geological unit of the study area was Tertiary igneous rocks. Depending on the specific lithology and weathering characteristics of Tertiary igneous rocks, the bifurcation ratio could be higher or lower than that of Quaternary igneous rocks. The presence of colluvium can lead to a higher bifurcation ratio in areas where it contributes to slope instability. The loose nature of colluvium may allow for rapid erosion and the formation of multiple small channels as water flows over it, increasing the complexity of the drainage network.

Data source and method of collection

In our study, a Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) digital elevation model (DEM) with a spatial resolution of 30*30 m was obtained from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) via the Earth Explorer website (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/). The DEM was transformed from the Geographic coordinates of the World Geodetic System (WGS)−1984 to the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) Zone-37 North (N) projected coordinate system, which enabled the effective processing and extraction of morphometric parameters in the metric system.

Morphometric parameter analysis

In this study, morphometric parameters were assessed using Digital Elevation Models (DEM). The DEM facilitated the determination of various parameters related to basic, linear, areal, shape, and relief aspects, which is helped to identify soil erosion hotspot areas and delineate the micro-watersheds. By computing the preprocessing of DEM and generating stream networks with ArcGIS 10.7.1 software (http://www.esri.com/en-us/arcgis), the analysis provides valuable insights into regions at high risk for soil erosion, thereby aiding in the prioritization of conservation measures (Fig. 5).

Weighted sum priority (WSP) approach for micro-watershed prioritization

Priority ranking is essential for understanding the morphological characteristics of individual watersheds25. This study was employed the weighted sum priority (WSP) approach to prioritize micro-watersheds(MWs), which combines statistical methods with geospatial technologies to identify critical parameters for analysis26. The WSP approach effectively generates weights for objective ranking, reducing subjective biases through a two-step process: the preliminary priority ranking (PPR) based on selected morphometric parameters, followed by the final priority ranking (FPR) utilizing weighted factors derived from a cross-correlation matrix20,26,27.

Preliminary priority ranking (PPR)

A total of 27 parameters were initially considered for micro watershed prioritization; however, only 21 were ultimately utilized for micro watershed prioritization because some parameters are subsets of others. For instance, area and perimeter parameters are subsets of areal and shape aspect parameters; the bifurcation ratio is a subset of the mean bifurcation ratio, and the stream length ratio is a subset of the mean stream length ratio. Additionally, maximum and minimum elevation is subsets of relief. Thus, 21 parameters were utilized for micro watershed prioritization, which included 21 morphometric parameters that capture various characteristics such as linear, areal, shape, and relief features. Linear, areal, shape, and relief features are four different morphometric features that have been used to rank MWs in order of risk because they are either directly or inversely related to the risk of soil erosion, runoff, and peak discharge. The areal, linear, and relief features are directly associated with the risk of soil erosion, runoff, and peak discharge, while the shape features are inversely associated with the risk of soil erosion, runoff, and peak discharge27,28. The higher values of shape parameters illustrate the less likely erosional status and vice versa27. The highest value is ranked first for areal, relief and linear features, and so on, while the lowest value is ranked first for the shape feature and so on27,29.

Weighted factor (WF)

The correlation matrix among all selected morphometric parameters was estimated to measure the linear relationship between morphometric pairs for calculating WF. This study was used the Pearson correlation coefficient method to perform a correlation matrix using the R software environment (version R 4.4.1.). Final priority ranking (FPR)

The final priority ranking and its categorization were made on the basis of the weight factor value. It was computed by multiplying the ranks from morphometric analysis and their weights obtained using cross-correlation analysis. The results obtained from the PPR were then multiplied by the WF to determine the WSP factor for the final prioritization of micro-watersheds. The highest priority ranking, ‘1,’ is assigned for the lowest weighted summation value, while the lowest priority ranking is assigned for the highest summation value, and so on7,30. Validation and ground-truthing

Researchers such as Kadam et al.27 and Shekar et al.31 have used this morphometric analysis approach even without validation for the assessment of soil erosion. Validation and ground-truthing was verified using Google Earth images and several field observations to illustrate erosion signs such as rills, active gully formation sites, landslide and vegetation covers within the watershed.

Results

Results from the morphometric analysis of the kiltie watershed provide important insights into the susceptibility of various micro watersheds to soil erosion. The study identified 10 distinct micro watersheds (designated MW 1 to MW 10) using hydrological tools in ArcGIS software and specific mathematical expressions were used for morphometric parameter analysis (Table 1; Fig. 6). A quantitative evaluation of the morphometric characteristics of these micro watersheds and their drainage network was conducted, which aids in understanding their morphology and in prioritizing areas vulnerable to soil erosion for effective conservation and management practices. In this regard morphometric parameters (MPs) that represent linear, basic, areal, shape and relief characteristics of the micro watersheds were examined. Linear aspect

In this study the highest and lowest stream order was 5th order and 2nd order in MW4 and MW9, respectively (Figs. 7, 8, 9). Stream number (Nu) is the number of stream segments of various orders in watersheds, ranges from 7 in MW7 to 121 in MW8 (Table 2). Stream length (Lu) is defined as the mean length of the stream of each of the dissimilar orders in a catchment. In the present research, the largest and smallest of the stream lengths values were 19.88 km in MW8 to 1.46 km in MW9 (Table 2). Bifurcation Ratio (Rb) is the ratio of the number of stream segments of given order to the number of segments of the next higher order35. We found that the maximum and minimum bifurcation ratio (Rb) values were 16.4 (MW5) and 1.3 (MW9) (Table 3).

Stream length ratio (Rl) is defined as the ratio of the given order’s average stream length to the next smaller order’s mean stream length33. In the current investigation, the highest and lowest stream length ratio values had MW4 (18.12) and MW9 (0.46), respectively (Table 3). Mean stream length ratio (Rlm) is defined as the ratio of the length of the stream to the number of streams33. We found that, the maximum and minimum mean stream length ratio values had MW7 (0.370) and MW8 (0.164). All the results related to these linear morphometric parameters for each micro watershed was summarized in Tables 2 and 3.

Basic aspect of morphometric parameters

Watershed area (A) is the overall area enclosed by the basin boundary and is referred to as the watershed area. The watershed covers 17.74 Km² in total. The largest and smallest micro-watershed regions in the current study were MW3 and MW9, respectively (Table 4). Watershed perimeter (P) is the full extent of a watershed’s boundaries. The watershed has a total perimeter of 62.98 km. The largest and smallest micro-watershed perimeters were 10.705 km (MW 8) and 2.701 km (MW 9), respectively (Table 4), reflecting variability in watershed shape and flow paths. Watershed length (Lb) represents the longest dimension parallel to the main drainage line within the basin and is utilized to measure water travel time from the watershed outlet to the watershed boundary39. In the present study the highest and lowest watershed lengths was 2.758 km (MW8) and 0.656 km (MW9) (Table 4), indicating variability in hydrological response times.

Areal aspect of morphometric parameters

Drainage density (Dd) is the ratio of total channel segment lengths cumulated for all orders within a basin to the entire basin area33. The highest and lowest values of Dd in the study area were 6.224 km/km² in MW2 and 4.949 km/km² in MW9, respectively (Table 4). Stream frequency (Fs) is the ratio of the total number of stream segments of all the orders in the watershed to the total area of the watershed33. We found that, the highest and lowest stream frequency values of the micro watersheds were 15.521 (MW7) and 32.844 (MW1), respectively. Drainage texture (Dt) is the ratio of stream number per perimeter of the basin33. The Dt of the river basin is classified into four categories: very coarse (< 2), coarse (2–4), moderate (4–6), and fine or highland topography (> 6)40. The drainage texture values of micro-watersheds range from 1.839 (MW7) to 12.511 (MW3) (Table 4).

Drainage intensity (Di) is the proportion of Fs to Dd36. The present study, the highest and lowest Di values were 6.087 (MW8) and 2.703 (MW7), respectively (Table 5). Infiltration number(If) is the outcome of drainage density and stream frequency reflecting the infiltration potential of a surface41. We found that, infiltration number ranges from 89.134 (MW7) to 196.787 (MW4) (Table 4). Constant of channel maintenance (Ccm) is defined as the inverse of Dd35. In the current study, the maximum and the minimum Ccm values were 0.202 km²/km (MW9) and 0.161 km²/km (MW2), respectively (Table 4).

Shape aspect of morphometric parameters

Form factor (Ff) is defined as the ratio of basin area to the square of the basin length33. The value of form factor would always be less than 0.7854 (for a perfectly circular basin)42. We found that Ff values have been varying from 0.486 (MW8) to 0.686 (MW9) (Table 5). Elongation ratio (Re) is defined as the ratio of diameter of a circle of the same area as the watershed to the maximum watershed length33. Elongation ratio can be classified into different categories i.e., circular (> 0.9), oval (0.8–0.9), less elongated (0.7–0.8), elongated (0.5–0.7), and more elongated (< 0.5)43. In this study Re values vary from 0.787 (MW8) to 0.934 (MW9), higher values of Re show high infiltration capacity and low runoff, whereas the lower Re values which are characterized by high susceptibility to erosion and sediment load43.

Circularity ratio (Rc) is termed as the proportion of the area of a catchment to the area of the circle with an equal circumference as the catchment’s perimeter38. Circularity ratio can be grouped in to three: elongated shape (Rc < 0.4), intermediate shape (0.4 to 0.7), and circular shape (Rc > 0.75)28. The Rc values of the study areas varying from 0.391(MW7) to 0.696(MW4) (Table 5). A higher Lof value indicates low relief and slower runoff, whereas a low Lof value indicates high relief, greater drainage, and lesser runoff14. Compactness coefficient (Cc) is the ratio of the watershed’s perimeter to the circumference of its equivalent circular area33. The values of Cc in the study area vary from 1.199 (MW4) to 1.599 (MW7) (Table 5). The highest Cc value indicates the highest infiltration capacity and lowest runoff, while the lowest Cc values indicate the highest and lowest runoff and infiltration.

Prioritization of micro-watersheds based on morphometric parameters and weighted sum priority (WSP) approach

The prioritization of watersheds for soil and water conservation is a critical task to ensure effective allocation of resources and targeted conservation efforts. Watershed prioritization based on morphometric parameters is a useful tool for identifying micro-watersheds that are at high risk of soil erosion44. No individual morphometric parameter can fully explain the level of erosion vulnerability of micro-watersheds. As a result, the weighted sum priority (WSP) approach of morphometric parameters is a more comprehensive measure of a watershed’s erosion vulnerability. In this study, a prioritization scheme based on the weighted sum priority (WSP) was implemented to rank the 10 micro-watersheds in terms of their soil and water conservation priority.

Preliminary priority ranking (PPR)

The study prioritizes micro-watersheds based on morphometric parameters and correlation coefficient matrix. The PPR is based on 21 morphometric parameters directly and inversely related to soil erosion (Table 7). The areal, linear, and relief morphometric parameters show a direct correlation with soil susceptibility to erosive processes27,29. In this context, high values of these parameters are associated with grater soil erodibility within the watershed. Consequently, micro watersheds exhibiting the highest values are assigned the highest priority (rank 1), followed in descending order, with the micro-watershed presenting the lowest value receiving the lowest rank (rank 10). In contrast, parameters related to shape display an inverse relationship with soil erodibility27; in this case, lower values indicate greater vulnerability to erosion. According to this criterion, micro-watersheds with the lowest values for these parameters are assigned the highest priority ranks, whereas those with higher values receive lower ranks.

Final priority ranking (FPR)

The final priority ranking and its categorization were made on the basis of the weighted factor value for each parameter, estimating WSP ranking values (Table 9; Fig. 9). It was computed by multiplying the ranks from morphometric analysis and their weights obtained using cross-correlation analysis using (Eq. 2). The highest priority ranking ‘1’ is assigned for the lowest weighted summation value, while the lowest priority ranking is assigned for the highest summation value, and so on7,30. The watershed is categorized into five priority categories according to their WSP approach i.e., very high (2.954 to 5.871), high (5.872 to 8.788), medium (8.789 to 11.705), low (11.706 to 14.622), and very low (> 14.623)30,46. According to Table 9, MW5 had the highest priority rank with the lowest weighted sum value (2.44) whereas; MW9 was assigned the lowest priority rank based on highest weighted sum value (9.36). Therefor MW5 had the highest priority ranking, indicating that there is more soil erosion and that soil and water conservation techniques were needed.

Discussion

Analysis of morphometric parameters

Morphometric parameters were analyzed using DEM, remote sensing, and Arc GIS software to understand surface development and soil erosion risks of the watershed47,48. The study explored four morphometric aspects: linear, areal, shape, and relief parameters, aiming to identify high-risk soil erosion areas. Understanding these factors is crucial for accurate erosion prediction and effective mitigation strategies. It was found that shape factors indirectly affect soil erosion, whereas linear, areal, and relief parameters have direct relationships, influencing water flow and speed7,26.

Linear parameters analysis

The linear aspect is a one-dimensional characteristic of morphometric analysis of a watershed, and it is concerned with the streams and their network. Linear parameters have a direct relationship with erosion potential27. The higher values of linear parameters illustrate the high likely erosional status and vice versa (Table 7). Different linear aspect of morphometric parameters such as; stream order, stream number, stream length, and bifurcation ratio, can be used to determine which watersheds are most vulnerable to soil erosion and to evaluate the influence of physical watershed characteristics on soil erosion49.

Stream order (U) is arranged in a hierarchical system, according to the connections between their tributaries44. In this study, stream ordering was conducted using Strahler’s method32, to analyze the drainage pattern of the area. Based on this approach, determination of stream order classification on a hierarchical system is carried out through the flow system of the smallest tributary in the upper reaches of the first order. Furthermore, the second-order classification is determined when the two smallest streams in the upstream meet each other; the meeting of the second-order basin determines the third-order classification, and so on. In the study area, MW9 had 2nd order, MW7 and MW10 had 3rd order, MW1, MW2, MW3, MW5, MW6, and MW8 had 4th order, and MW4 has 5th order (Fig. 5). It indicates, higher-order streams are less permeable and have less infiltration than those in lower orders50.

Stream number (Nu) is the number of stream segments of various orders in watersheds. Every segment of the stream was numbered preliminarily from the first order to the maximum order present in the sub-basins33. Furthermore, the availability of a large number of streams directs that the topography is still undergoing erosion. However, a lower number of streams indicate mature topography. We found that, the highest and lowest stream numbers were MW8 (121) and MW7 (7) and MW9 (7), respectively (Table 2). Stream length (Lu) is defined as the mean length of the stream of each of the dissimilar orders in a catchment. As a result, the length of the stream is greater in a first-order stream, and also it increases as stream order increases33. In the present research, the largest and smallest of the stream lengths are MW8 (19.88 km) and MW9 (1.46 km), respectively. The total stream length decreases with increasing stream order, i.e., first-order streams have the longest lengths and last-order streams have the shortest lengths. The irregularities in this trend are indicative of discrepancies in lithology. This finding is consistent with previous studies of Abdeta et al.1.

Bifurcation Ratio (Rb) is the ratio of the number of stream segments of given order to the number of segments of the next higher order35. The Rb differs from one order to the next; these irregularities depend on the drainage basin’s geological and lithological development32. In the current study, the highest and lowest bifurcation ratio (Rb) values have 16.4 (MW5) and 1.3 (MW9) (Table 3). This highest value indicates that the shape of the basin that had structural disturbance and the lowest value indicates that humble structural disturbance and the drainage patterns have not been distorted32. The MW5 shows the highest Rb of 16.4 and had high erosion potential and is thus assigned rank 1; similarly, MW9 had the minimum Rb value(1.3) and has the lowest erosion and is thereby assigned rank 10 (Table 8). Typically, Rb values within a range of 3.0 to 5.0 are common in drainage basins where the geological structures have no significant impact on the drainage pattern, and similarly, if the bifurcation ratio is less than 3.0, the geological structure of the basin is flat and homogenous32. However, in the study area, the bifurcation ratio (Rb) varies from 1.3 to 16.4, indicating that the geological structures have somewhat disturbed the drainage pattern in the study area; notably, except for MW9, all micro watersheds exhibit values that exceed the common range (Table 3), suggesting a greater disturbance in this particular area. This finding aligns with the research conducted by Abera et al.7, who reported that areas with higher Rb values tend to have a higher erosion risk due to geological disturbances. Low mean Rbm is also indicative of water stress condition for the watershed. A constant decrease of Rb throughout the different stream order as well as low mean Rb MW9 (1.3) indicates low flood potentiality for the watershed.

Stream length ratio (Rl) is defined as the ratio of the given order’s average stream length to the next smaller order’s mean stream length33. We found that, the highest and lowest stream length ratio values had MW4 (18.12) and MW9 (0.46), respectively (Table 3). This ratio reveals how efficiently water is transported. A high ratio indicates slower flow and potential infiltration, reducing erosion. Conversely, a low stream length ratio shows rapid water movement and increased erosion risk. Mean stream length ratio (Rlm) is defined as the ratio of the length of the stream to the number of streams33. In the current study, the maximum and minimum mean stream length ratio values had MW7 (0.370) and MW8 (0.164).

Basic parameters analysis

The basic aspect of morphometric parameters such as; Area (A), Perimeter (P), basin length (Lb) are directly related to soil erosion51. The area of the study watershed ranges from 0.295 km2 to 3.698 km2 for MW9 and MW8, respectively, the total area covers 17.74 km2 in the kiltie watershed (Table 4). The total perimeter of the study area is 62.976 km. Individual micro watershed values range from 2.701 km for MW9 to 10.705 km for MW8 (Table 4). Most authors suggest that basins large in area and perimeter have high risk of soil erosion27,52.

However, in our study, MW8 and MW9 has high values of this parameters, it is less severely impacted by soil erosion in overall rank weighted sum priority value than micro watershed (MW5) showing lower values. Similarly, this finding is in line with the findings of Duressa et al.14. Therefore, this paper suggests that this is not generally the case for all the micro watersheds of our study area. Due to this reason in our study not consider Area and perimeter factors for identifying soil erosion risk area. Basin or Watershed length (Lb) represents the longest dimension parallel to the main drainage line within the basin and is utilized to measure water travel time from the watershed outlet to the watershed boundary39. In the present study the highest and lowest watershed lengths are 2.758 km (MW8) and 0.656 km (MW9) (Table 4).

Areal parameters analysis

The areal aspect of morphometric parameters; drainage density (Dd), stream frequency (Fs), Drainage texture (Dt), Drainage intensity (Di), infiltration number(If), and Constant channel maintenance (Ccm) are directly related to soil erosion27,46,51. In this current study all areal morphometric parameter analysis results tabulated in Table 4 and the higher values of areal parameters illustrate the high likely erosional status and vice versa (Table 7).

Drainage density refers to the total length of all streams within the watershed divided by the total area of the watershed33. It indicates the overall density of the stream network in the area. In the present study, the drainage density parameter plays a vital role in identifying areas with high erosion risks. The results of this parameter range from 4.95 to 6.22Km/km2 for in MW 9 and MW 2, respectively (Table 4). Different authors reported that mountainous topography, scarce vegetation, and impermeable underlying material are the causes of high drainage density where low relief and highly permeable underlying material have low drainage density14,51. Therefore, MW9 with their highly permeable soil, low elevation, minimal runoff, and high infiltration capacity have a lower Dd value of 4.95 km/km2. Additionally, the higher Dd values reflect high runoff with high flood peak, thin soil cover, low permeability of soil, and steep to moderate land slope in MW2 (ranked 1), whereas the low drainage density values are attributed to relatively permeable soil cover, land having gentle slopes, and less runoff in micro-watershed 9 (ranked 10) (Table 7). This finding aligns with the research conducted by Arabameri et al.51, who reported that areas with higher Dd values tend to have a higher erosion risk.

Stream frequency (Fs) is the ratio of the total number of stream segments of all the orders in the watershed to the total area of the watershed33. In the study area, the highest and lowest stream frequency values of the micro watersheds were 15.521 (MW7) and 32.844 (MW1), respectively, suggesting micro-watersheds having lower Fs values represent low relief and permeable sub-surface material, whereas micro-watersheds with higher Fs values show resistant/low-conducting subsurface material, sparse vegetation, and high relief.

Drainage texture (Dt) is the ratio of stream number per perimeter of the basin33. It depends on several physical factors such as climate, rainfall, vegetation, rock, soil type, infiltration capacity, and soil evolutionary stage51. The Dt of the river basin is classified into four categories: very coarse (< 2), coarse (2–4), moderate (4–6), and fine or highland topography (> 6)40. The drainage density values of micro-watersheds range from 1.839 (MW7) to 12.511 (MW3) (Table 4). Drainage intensity (Di) is the proportion of Fs to Dd36. In the current study, the highest and lowest Di values are 6.087 (MW8) and 2.703 (MW7), respectively (Table 5).

Infiltration number is the outcome of drainage density and stream frequency reflecting the infiltration potential of a surface41. The higher number of infiltrations indicates a lower infiltration and higher runoff43. In the present study, infiltration number ranges from 89.134 (MW7) to 196.787 (MW4) (Table 4), with maximum values (MW4) indicating increased surface runoff and lower infiltration rates. Constant of channel maintenance (Ccm) is defined as the inverse of Dd35. A higher Ccm value indicates a low infiltration rate and a high runoff rate. In the current study, the maximum and the minimum Ccm values have 0.202 km²/km (MW9) and 0.161 km²/km (MW2), respectively (Table 4).

Shape parameter analysis

Shape morphometry is defined as the geometric features of a landform configuration53. Soil erosion is inversely related to shape morphometric parameters such as; form factor (Ff), elongation ratio (Re), circulatory ratio (Rc), length of overland flow(Lof), and compactness coefficient (Cc)27,30. Table 5 shows the values of all shape morphometric parameter analysis result. Form factor (Ff) is defined as the ratio of basin area to the square of the basin length33. The value of form factor would always be less than 0.7854 (for a perfectly circular basin)42. In the current study Ff values have been found varying from 0.486 (MW8) to 0.686 (MW9). The Ff describes the shape of the basin and its flow intensity26. The smaller the value of the Ff, the more it will elongate the shape, and the more excellent value of the Ff indicates the circular shape of the basin.

Elongation ratio (Re) is defined as the ratio of diameter of a circle of the same area as the watershed to the maximum watershed length33. Elongation ratio can be classified into different categories i.e., circular (> 0.9), oval (0.8–0.9), less elongated (0.7–0.8), elongated (0.5–0.7), and more elongated (< 0.5)43. In this study Re values vary from 0.787 (MW8) to 0.934 (MW9), higher values of Re show high infiltration capacity and low runoff, whereas the lower Re values which are characterized by high susceptibility to erosion and sediment load43. Circularity ratio (Rc) is termed as the proportion of the area of a catchment to the area of the circle with an equal circumference as the catchment’s perimeter38. It is influenced by the length and frequency of streams, geological structures, land use/land cover, climate and slope of the basin. Circularity ratio can be grouped in to three: elongated shape (Rc < 0.4), intermediate shape (0.4 to 0.7), and circular shape (Rc > 0.75)28. The Rc values of the study areas varying from 0.391(MW7) to 0.696(MW4) (Table 5). A higher Rc suggests a denser network of stream channels, improving drainage efficiency and dispersing runoff effectively, which can reduce surface runoff and erosion.

Length of overland flow (Lof) is half of the reciprocal of drainage density33. It affects a basin’s hydrological and physiographical processes because it suggests the length of water flow over the ground before it reaches a definite stream channel26. We found that the highest and lowest values of Lof had 0.101 (MW9) and 0.080 (MW2), respectively (Table 5). A higher Lof value indicates low relief and slower runoff, whereas a low Lof value indicates high relief, greater drainage, and lesser runoff14. Compactness coefficient (Cc) is the ratio of the watershed’s perimeter to the circumference of its equivalent circular area33. The values of Cc in the study area vary from 1.199 (MW4) to 1.599 (MW7) (Table 5). The highest Cc value indicates the highest infiltration capacity and lowest runoff, while the lowest Cc values indicate the highest and lowest runoff and infiltration.

Relief parameters analysis

Relief is a three-dimensional parameter of a drainage basin which expressed in terms of area, volume and altitude of watershed landforms to analyze terrain characteristics and can helps to identify areas in risk of soil erosion more effectively19,54. Relief morphometric parameters includes elevation, Relief, Ruggedness number, Relative relief, and Relief ratio are directly related to soil erosion, where the high rate of the parameters designates a higher risk of soil erosion and vice versa51.

The basin relief (Bh) is a vertical difference between a basin’s greatest and lowest elevation. It plays a crucial role in shaping landforms, drainage patterns, surface runoff, groundwater recharge, permeability, and erosional properties of the terrain55. The values of Bh in this study varies from 0.016 km (MW9) to 0.171 km (MW3) (Table 6), with a higher value of Bh indicating greater susceptibility to soil erosion and lower soil infiltration rate. The higher value of relief shows that it has lower infiltration and higher runoff and the lower value of relief shows that it has higher runoff and lower infiltration14.

Ruggedness number (Rn) is the product of the watershed relief and Drainage density34.In this study the highest and lowest Rn values have 1.045 (MW3) and 0.079 (MW9), respectively (Table 6), with a higher value of Rn (MW3) indicating greater erosion risk than MW9. According to Asode et al.56, the complexity of the relief characteristics shows a structural and denudation landform with the development of a landform that is prone to erosion. A high Rn indicates the existence of long and steep slopes, and a high degree of slope indicates a time of concentration of surface runoff and a higher susceptibility to erosion than watersheds with low Rn values57.

Relative relief (Rhp) is the ratio of basin relief to the perimeter of the drainage basin34. A relative relief (Rhp) is an important parameter to access any topography’s morphometric characteristics58. The values of higher and lower Rhp in the current study was 0.024 (MW5) and 0.005 (MW10), respectively (Table 6). Relief ratio (Rh) is the ratio between relief and watershed length35. When the relief ratio decreases, the drainage network becomes branched, so the bifurcation ratio becomes high59. Rh is directly related to slope and measures the watershed’s steepness; it also impacts the hydrological process51. In this study the values varies from 0.018 (MW10) to 0.083(MW7).

Micro watershed prioritization based on morphometric parameters and weighted sum priority (WSP) value approach

The prioritization of micro-watersheds is essential for identifying areas most susceptible to soil erosion and for effective implementation of soil and water conservation strategies. The final priority ranking was made on the basis of the weighted factor value. It was computed by multiplying the Preliminary priority ranks from morphometric analysis and their weights obtained using cross-correlation analysis. The results, thus, obtained was the final priority ranking of watersheds using WSP approach7,20,27,30,46. Our findings identifying micro watersheds with a very high to high risk of soil erosion can be carried out with the help of morphometric parameter-based micro watershed prioritizing techniques.

The level of micro watershed erosion vulnerability cannot be adequately explained by a single morphometric measure. As a result, a more effective, objective ranking of micro-watersheds while minimizing subjective biases and successful indicator of a micro watersheds erosion risk is the weighted sum priority value of morphometric characteristics7,26,27. This study is the first in the study area to analyze morphometric parameters, including areal, linear, shape, and relief aspects and utilized a non-subjective statistically computed WSP approach for micro-watershed prioritization.

Furthermore, the result of this study was in consistent with research findings from other basins conducted in Ethiopian highlands1,6,7,14,17–19. As result, for this study revealed that 10 micro-watersheds in the kiltie watershed exhibit significant susceptibility to soil erosion where approximately 78% of micro-watersheds from the total area of watershed (17.74 km2 were at very high risk of soil erosion and subsequent land degradation. Similar results were observed in the MOGR basin, located in the south-central Ethiopian highlands, the WSP factor-based final priority classifications of sub-watersheds illustrate that 19 of all sub-watersheds fall under the very high soil erosion priority classes, which account for 43% of the land area, equivalently about 5693 km2 total areas7.

Additionally, similar results were observed in the Jemma sub-basin (JSB) of the Abay Basin, Ethiopia, using morphometric parameter analysis and GIS, where that most SWs are classified into very high and high-priority categories for erosion52. Thus, these studies offer a valuable way of defining the priority areas to be identified for intervention to mitigate soil erosion. It provides a dynamic, cost-effective, and sustainable alternative to watershed prioritization approaches. The high to very high soil erosion-prone micro- watersheds identified in this study urgently require comprehensive watershed management strategies to mitigate the risk of soil erosion and land degradation in the kiltie watershed. It is recommended that government and non-government organizations implement various soil and water conservation practices such as terraces, soil bands, fanayaju, agronomic measures, vegetative practices, and area closure techniques, mainly targeting farm and grazing lands, as well as degraded lands.

Effective mitigation measure for soil and water conservation in the study area

Managing the whole watershed at once is very difficult, thus, these studies offer a valuable way of defining the priority areas to be identified for intervention to mitigate soil erosion. It provides a dynamic, cost-effective, and sustainable alternative to conventional watershed prioritization approaches. The final priority ranks of micro watersheds guides for phase wise implementation plan for management. The very high to high soil erosion-prone micro watersheds identified in this study urgently require comprehensive watershed management strategies to mitigate the intensifying risk of soil erosion and land degradation in the Kiltie watershed. It could be recommended that government and non-government organizations implement various soil and water conservation practices such as terraces, soil bands, and traditional rainwater harvesting methods like fanayaju, agronomic measures, vegetative practices, reforestation, and area closure techniques, mainly targeting agricultural lands, as well as degraded lands of the study area. Furthermore, policy measures and exploring alternative options to alleviate population pressure and reduce dependency on limited land resources are essential considerations.

Limitations and future work

The present study shows that morphometric analysis and weighted sum priority based prioritization of micro-watersheds based on their susceptibility to erosion and helps in implementing effective soil and water conservation measures. This methodology directly supports the objective of identify erosion prone micro watershed for strengthening soil and water conservation and territorial resilience in the face of climate threats and growing demographic pressures. Beyond the specific case of kiltie watershed, this methodological frame work offers a transferable tool for morphometric watershed prioritization in other similar agro ecological areas. Its application can inform integrated soil and water conservation in territories with limited resources, steep topography, and sensitive ecosystems. Addationally, we acknowledge the use of Google Earth imagery and field observations to illustrate erosion signs, gully formation sites, landslide and vegetation covers within our study area.

However, this study has certain limitations, including reliance on morphometric parameters alone, without direct incorporation of climatic variability, land cover data, or hydrogeological measurements. These factors, while partly inferred, should be integrated into future work to enhance the precision and applicability of prioritization efforts. Future research should focus on incorporating multi-criteria decision analysis frame works, coupling morphometric data with socio-economic and ecological parameters to create holistic watershed management plans. Addationally, we acknowledge the use of a 30 m Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) Digital Elevation Model (DEM) for extracting morphometric parameters and delineating very small micro-watersheds presents certain limitations. The spatial resolution of the DEM may not capture fine-scale topographic variations, particularly in areas with steep slopes or intricate drainage patterns. The use of remote sensing time series and field-based hydrological monitoring can further validate and refine prioritization models, thus enhancing adaptive management in the face of climate variability and human pressures.

Conclusion

The results of identifying soil erosion risk using morphometric analysis and a geospatial approach provide valuable insights into erosion vulnerability in kiltie watershed, Upper Blue Nile Basin Ethiopia. Additionally, identifying soil erosion plays a crucial role in identifying micro-watersheds that require priority attention for effective mitigation measures. By highlighting these critical areas, effective strategies can be implemented to control soil loss, enhance water resource management, and promote long-term environmental sustainability in the watershed. Traditional approaches for evaluating these factors can be expensive, laborious, and time-consuming. Nevertheless, identifying soil erosion by using morphometric parameters through geospatial technologies have revolutionized this procedure and less labor-intensive and cost-effective methods.

The study utilized DEM for morphometric analysis in an ArcGIS environment is a suitable method for identifying erosion prone micro watersheds. Based on morphometric analysis identifying erosion prone areas, the linear, areal, and relief aspects were the most influencing erosion risk assessment parameters. Depending on the morphometric analysis result, priority ranks were developed for each micro watershed to know the patterns of soil erosion potential. A combination of the whole morphometric analysis result using WSP values was developed and classified as very high, high, and medium to allocate erosion-prone areas. This study’s findings indicate that MW1, MW2, MW3, MW4, MW5 and MW8 are classified under the very high-priority soil erosion category; MW6, MW7, and MW10 are categorized as high-priority, and MW9 are designated as medium-priority for soil erosion.

This prioritization framework allows decision-makers to allocate resources efficiently and implement conservation measures to address environmental challenges. Because, managing the whole watershed at once is very difficult; thus, these studies offer a valuable way of defining the priority areas to be identified for intervention to mitigate soil erosion. The very high- to high-soil erosion-prone micro watershed is urgently requiring comprehensive watershed management strategies to mitigate the intensifying risk of soil erosion and land degradation in the kiltie watershed. Based on this we advised that government and non-government organizations implement soil and water conservation practices like terraces, soil bands, fanayaju, agronomic measures, vegetative practices, and area closure techniques, focusing on farm, grazing, and degraded lands. Finally this study concludes that utilized DEM for morphometric analysis and WSP values in ArcGIS software is logical, time and cost effective method for identifying erosion prone micro watersheds.
