Non-integrase mechanisms for dolutegravir resistance
Authors: Alan N Engelman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntiretroviral inhibitors that target specific aspects of human immunodeficiency virus 1 (HIV-1) replication have made a huge impact on the management of the global AIDS pandemic and the health and well-being of people that live with HIV. Current maintenance therapies commonly consist of one or two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors co-formulated with a second-generation integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI), and regimens containing the second-generation INSTI dolutegravir are in wide-use across the globe.Main bodyINSTIs are active site inhibitors that block HIV-1 DNA integration– more precisely, the ability of the integrase enzyme to join the 3′ ends of the viral reverse transcript with host chromosomal DNA. High-level resistance to first-generation INSTIs readily occurs via amino acid substitutions proximal to the integrase active site. Second-generation INSTIs, by contrast, impart comparatively high genetic barriers to the generation of drug resistance. When observed, such changes within integrase tend to confer comparatively low level resistance to both first- and second-generation INSTIs. Moreover, there are several reports of clinical failure to dolutegravir in the absence of known integrase drug resistance mutations. Laboratory-based research has at the same time identified three non-integrase pathways that can lead to dolutegravir resistance, including changes within the viral surface and transmembrane envelope glycoproteins, the nucleocapsid protein, and the genomic RNA 3′ polypurine tract that serves to prime plus-strand DNA synthesis during reverse transcription. Each type of change has been shown to confer resistance to dolutegravir through a unique molecular mechanism. Herein, I review the non-integrase changes that are known to occur and the proposed mechanisms that lead to the generation of dolutegravir resistance. The potential for these different types of changes to impact INSTI drug resistance in the clinic is also discussed.ConclusionThe unexpected observation that multiple non-integrase pathways can contribute to the generation of dolutegravir resistance highlights the remarkable plasticity of HIV-1 to circumvent challenge with a highly efficacious small molecule inhibitor. Given its current global use as a frontline anti-HIV inhibitor, this research informs regions of sequence surveillance for the continued safe and efficacious use of dolutegravir-based antiretroviral therapies.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Dolutegravir, Human immunodeficiency virus 1, Integrase, Nucleocapsid, Strand transfer inhibitor, Envelope glycoprotein, INSTI resistance, Polypurine tract

Background

Retroviral replication requires the activities of three virally-encoded enzymes: protease (PR), reverse transcriptase (RT), and integrase (IN). PR functions during virus maturation to cleave Gag and Gag-Pol precursor polyproteins, converting immature virions into infectious virus particles. Mature orthoretroviral particles harbor cores built from shells of repeating units of capsid protein. The capsid shells encase viral ribonucleoproteins RT, IN, and nucleocapsid (NC) along with two copies of plus-sense genomic RNA (reviewed in [1]).

Clinically efficacious drugs that target RT and PR first became available in 1987 and 1995, respectively [2]. Since the rollout of the initial IN strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) raltegravir (RAL) in 2007, the anti-IN compounds have become mainstays of antiretroviral therapy (ART) formulations that are used to treat people with HIV (PWH). Today, the second-generation INSTI dolutegravir (DTG) is widely-prescribed with a backbone formulation of RT nucleotide/nucleoside inhibitors (NRTIs) tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and lamivudine, collectively known as TLD [3]. Understanding DTG resistance mechanisms is accordingly critical to help curb the worldwide HIV/AIDS pandemic via the widespread administration of TLD.

Before getting into the details of the non-IN pathways that lead to DTG drug resistance, it is important to describe the central players involved with the integration of human immunodeficiency virus 1 (HIV-1) DNA into host chromosomal DNA – namely the IN enzyme, which as mentioned enters the cell as part of the viral core – and the viral DNA substrate that is synthesized by reverse transcription during the early phase of HIV-1 replication. Because one of the non-IN drug resistance pathways entails changes to the 3′ polypurine tract (3′PPT) – which functions during reverse transcription – I will first overview this namesake process of retroviral replication. Readers interested in a detailed review of HIV-1 reverse transcription are directed to ref [4].

Following virus infection, genomic RNA is converted into a single molecule of linear, double-stranded DNA by the RT enzyme, which is an RNA/DNA-dependent DNA polymerase with associated RNase H activity [5]. The primer for HIV-1 DNA synthesis is cellular tRNALys3, which is selectively packaged into HIV-1 virions [6]. The tRNALys3 at its 3′ end displays 18 bases of complementarity to the viral primer binding site (PBS), which abuts the unique 5′ (U5) region of retroviral genomes [7, 8] (Fig. 1, top). Initially-synthesized minus-strand DNA, which is known as minus-strand strong-stop (MSSS), consists of RU5 sequences (Fig. 1, step 1). Reverse transcription is a multi-step process requiring two nucleic acid translocation or strand transfer steps (Fig. 1, steps 2 and 7). The initial transfer of RU5 DNA, which can occur to either copy of genomic RNA, is driven by RT’s RNase H activity and downstream R region complementarity [9–11] (Fig. 1, step 2). Following translocation, RT extends the minus-strand out to the PBS while RNase H degrades the brunt of the RNA genome (Fig. 1, step 3). The 3′PPT, which is comparatively resistant to RNase H degradation, serves as the primer for plus-strand DNA synthesis [12]. Lentiviruses, such as HIV-1, harbor a second PPT, called the central PPT (cPPT) [13] (Fig. 1, step 4). Figure 1 details the intramolecular nature of the second strand transfer event and in total 9 steps required to yield blunt-ended double-stranded HIV-1 DNA. Notably, while the RNA genome harbors RU5 and U3R elements at its 5′ and 3′ termini, respectively, the reverse transcript harbors U3RU5 at both ends, which together comprise the long terminal repeat (LTR) (Fig. 1, step 9).

After gaining access to cell nuclei, retroviral DNAs are rapidly heterochromatinized, resulting in transcriptional silencing [14–16] (reviewed in [17]). Various host factors, such as nuclear protein 220 [14] and the structural maintenance of chromosomes 5 (SMC5)/SMC6 complex [18], regulate transcriptional silencing of unintegrated retroviral DNAs. Heterochromatinization can be thought of as an intrinsic immune response to foreign DNA, and, by extension, the cellular machineries that silence episomal DNA as antiviral restriction factors [19]. While comparatively large DNA viruses can encode for gene products to counteract transcriptional silencing (reviewed in [20]), retroviruses lack this capacity and instead leverage integration into host chromatin. Various viral factors, including HIV-1 protein R [21, 22] and human T-lymphotropic virus 1 (HTLV-1) transactivator of transcription and transformation (Tax) [23, 24], can stimulate gene expression from unintegrated HIV-1 DNA.

Retroviral INs possess two catalytic activities that are required for integration: 3′ processing and DNA strand transfer (see [25] for a detailed review of retroviral IN structure and function). During 3′ processing, IN hydrolyzes the LTR termini adjacent to phylogenetically-conserved CA dinucleotides, which, for HIV-1, liberates a GT dinucleotide from each 3′ end, resulting in chemically-reactive CAOH termini [26–28] (Fig. 1, step 10). A kinetic study during HIV-1 infection that focused on the relationship between reverse transcription and 3′ processing revealed that 3′ processing of the viral DNA ends can occur comparatively early– perhaps even concomitant with viral DNA end synthesis [29]. After locating a suitable target DNA acceptor site in the cell nucleus, IN uses the viral dA-3′ hydroxyl groups to cleave opposing strands of chromosomal DNA that are separated by 5 bp, which joins the viral DNA ends to the cleaved target DNA 5′-phosphates [27, 30, 31] (Fig. 1, steps 11 and 12). Host-mediated repair of the resulting hemi-integrated DNA yields the integrated HIV-1 provirus flanked by the duplication of the 5 bp of host DNA sequence that was cleaved by IN for DNA strand transfer (Fig. 1, steps 12 and 13).

Several forms of circular DNA, which require specialized conditions to be expressed efficiently in the absence of integration [23, 32], also form during retroviral infection. Ligation of linear viral DNA yields 2-LTR circles containing tandemly-repeated LTRs while 1-LTR circles form via homologous recombination or as a side-product of reverse transcription [33–38]. IN-mediated integration of the LTR ends into internal regions of linear viral DNA, which is termed autointegration, yields replication-defective subgenomic circular DNAs [39, 40].

Retroviral INs are composed of three independently-folded domains: (i) the N-terminal domain engages a single Zn2+ atom to fold into a helix-turn-helix [41, 42]; the C-terminal domain (CTD) adopts a β-barrel-like structure reminiscent of the Src homology 3 domain [43, 44]; and the catalytic core domain (CCD), which adopts an RNase H fold, harbors phylogenetically-conserved Asp and Glu residues (DDE motif) that comprise the enzyme active site [45–47]. The DDE residues coordinate the positionings of two Mg2+ ions for SN2 chemistry: metal ion A during 3′ processing enhances the nucleophilicity of water oxygen while ion B destabilizes the scissile ApG phosphodiester bond in viral DNA (Fig. 1, step 10); during strand transfer, the roles of the two metal ions reverse. Metal ion B enhances the nucleophilicity of the viral dA-3′ hydroxyl group, while ion A destabilizes the scissile phosphodiester bond in chromosomal DNA (Fig. 1, step 12) [48].

HIV-1 IN mutations can exert far-reaching phenotypic consequences. Two classes of replication-defective IN mutant viruses, class I and II, are distinguished based on proficiencies of reverse transcription [49]. IN engages RNA during HIV-1 assembly to encapsulate the viral genome into the core [50, 51]. Class II IN mutations disrupt IN-RNA binding due to specific alteration of RNA contact residues or indirectly by disrupting IN tetramerization, which significantly increases the proportion of eccentric virions with ribonucleoproteins located outside of the capsid shell [50, 52] and accounts for the reverse transcription defect of class II IN mutant viruses [53]. Class I IN mutant viruses, typified by changes of DDE active site residues, are, by contrast, predominantly blocked at the integration step. As a consequence, fractional increases in 2-LTR circle DNAs are observed in cells infected with class I IN mutant viruses [54–56]. Inhibition of integration with INSTIs similarly increases the proportion of 2-LTR circle formation [57].

Mechanism of INSTI action

Initial attempts to discover clinically-applicable HIV-1 IN inhibitors were unsuccessful due to the types of assays that were used during screening [58]. A key advance came from Hazuda and colleagues, who used IN prebound to oligonucleotide LTR DNA containing “pre-cleaved” or recessed CAOH-3′ ends (instead of free IN), which led to the discovery of the INSTI drug class [57, 59]. This discovery was rooted in the functional separation of IN’s 3′ processing and strand transfer activities. LTR substrates harboring recessed CAOH-3′ ends – and thus having bypassed the 3′ processing step – supported efficient strand transfer activity in vitro, highlighting the mechanistic separability of the two chemical reactions [31, 60, 61]. And, as discussed above, 3′ processing could occur comparatively early during HIV-1 infection, seemingly before chromosomal DNA would be encountered in the cell nucleus [29]. INSTIs display limited ability to engage apo IN on its own (unbound to DNA); the key drug-binding target is the cleaved synaptic complex (CSC) intasome (Fig. 1, step 10) with IN bound to processed CAOH-3′ ends [62] – indeed, the substrate used to select for initial INSTI compounds. HIV-1 is susceptible to INSTIs after 3′ processing but before target DNA engagement in the cell nucleus – both the unprocessed DNA end and, following cleavage and nuclear translocation, the target DNA, occupy similar active site-proximal positions – effectively eliminating functional INSTI engagement of the IN active site [48, 62]. The spatiotemporal separation of IN 3′ processing and strand transfer activities defines a viral Achilles’ heel that is exploited by INSTIs. And, the shortening of this spatiotemporal separation seemingly underlies one of the non-IN pathways of DTG resistance (see the section on NC resistance mutations below).

It became clear in the 2000s that structures of IN bound to processed CAOH-3′ ends would be critical to inform the mechanistic basis of INSTI action – and, by extension – mechanisms of INSTI resistance. INSTIs harbor two common functionalities: adjacent co-planar heteroatoms, which are most usually oxygen but can also be nitrogen, and a halo-benzyl group connected to the main pharmacophore via a flexible linker (shown for DTG in Fig. 2A). Structures of INSTI-bound CSC intasomes revealed that drug heteroatoms engage the active site-bound metal ions, as initially concluded from solution-based biochemical measurements [63]. Additionally, the halo-benzyl group assumes the position of the terminal adenine rings of 3′-processed viral DNA, the consequences of which eject the dA and its associated 3′-OH group, which is required to cleave host chromatin, from the active site (Fig. 2B-C) [64–67].

Due to the need to displace the terminal adenine ring, INSTIs bind to HIV-1 IN-viral DNA complexes in vitro comparatively slowly [68]. Once bound, INSTIs also display comparatively slow off rates. While the dissociative half-lives of first generation INSTIs RAL and elvitegravir (EVG) from IN-viral DNA complexes were ~ 9 h and 3 h, respectively, second-generation compounds DTG and bictegravir (BIC) remained associated for significantly longer periods, dissociating at half-lives of ~ 71–96 h and 163 h, respectively [69, 70]. Likely contributing to the superior efficacy of second-generation INSTI compounds, the extended drug-association periods would seemingly exceed the time required to translocate and integrate 3′-end processed viral DNA during HIV-1 infection of activated target cells [71, 72].

IN changes that confer INSTI resistance

Although the main theme of this paper is viral changes outside of IN that confer resistance to DTG, I will first briefly overview changes that occur within IN to (i) frame the extent that such changes confer resistance to first-generation versus second-generation INSTIs and (ii) introduce pharmacological concepts such as drug dose escalation, viral fitness, and fold changes (FCs) in drug susceptibility. Readers interested in more in-depth reviews of IN changes that confer INSTI drug resistance are directed to refs [73] and [74].

Typical approaches to FC measurements assess drug concentrations required to inhibit 50% of WT versus mutant virus infection (EC50, for effective concentration 50%) under single-round infection conditions, where either the test HIV-1 strain is genetically altered to preclude multi-round replication, or assays are terminated sufficiently early to preclude subsequent rounds of virus replication [75, 76]. EC50 values can also be determined from multi-round replication data [77].

Alterations in IN that confer INSTI resistance have been generated in the laboratory using dose escalation approaches where drug concentrations in cell culture are increased incrementally over time, which can span from weeks to months, as HIV-1 replicates and accumulates resistance changes. The IN region of the pol gene is then sequenced after reaching plateau drug concentrations, and such sequences are often compared to longitudinal viral samples to inform the evolution of resistance changes that contributed to the end-point genotype. In vivo drug resistance data comes from clinical trials, at times conducted under monotherapy conditions where PWH are prescribed just the test INSTI, and, after regulatory agency approval, in much larger populations that receive INSTI-containing ART regimens. Overall trends from lab-based tests and the clinic are generally similar. Second-generation INSTIs, such as DTG and BIC, impart much higher barriers to generate drug resistance as compared to first generation INSTI compounds. In other words, resistance to the first generation INSTIs RAL and EVG occurs much more readily than does resistance to DTG and BIC. However, high levels of resistance to the second-generation compounds can build off the types of IN changes that confer high level RAL/EVG resistance, observations that reinforce the importance of sequence surveillance for prescribing optimal ART formulations.

By now, several IN amino acid substitutions have been documented to confer INSTI resistance. The HIV Drug Resistance Database, which is an excellent informational source [73, 78, 79], demarcates changes based on the extent that they confer resistance to each of the five approved INSTIs – which additionally includes the long-acting compound cabotegravir (CAB). Resistance to RAL/EVG typically involves changes of amino acid residues proximal to the IN active site, including Q148H/K/R (Fig. 2B), N155H, Y143R/C/H (specifically for RAL), and E92Q (more important for EVG) [80–83]. High-level resistance is often associated with changes at Gln148, which is exacerbated by secondary substitutions of Gly140. For example, the Q148H change confers approximately 20- to 70-fold resistance to RAL, but at the same time weakens viral fitness, by about 30–90%, compared to wildtype (WT) reference strains [82, 84, 85]. The G140S change, which also negatively impacts fitness (~ twofold vs. WT), on its own confers marginal RAL resistance (~ 1.5 to 3-fold). However, G140S/Q148H mutant viruses are fully fit (infect cells at WT HIV-1 levels) and can confer > 1,000-fold resistance to both RAL and to EVG [82, 84, 85]. Although G140S/Q148H changes confer moderate ~ 3.5-fold resistance to DTG, other changes at these positions, such as G140A/Q148K, can confer > 50-fold DTG resistance [67, 74, 86]. Acquisition of a third active site-proximal amino acid change, such as E138K or N155H, can additionally significantly increase the resistance of G140S/Q148H strains to DTG [67, 74, 86].

The interaction between INSTI heteroatoms and the divalent metal ions at the IN active site has been described as practically covalent [87]. Visualization of INSTI-bound CSC intasomes constructed from drug-resistant IN mutant proteins has revealed that resistance changes generally effect the polarization of the Mg2+ ions, weakening the chelation of the bound drug [67, 87]. INSTIs accordingly dissociate from IN mutant-viral DNA complexes significantly faster than they do from WT IN-viral DNA complexes [69, 70].

Under INSTI-naïve conditions – that is, conditions where there was no prior clinical failure to first-generation compounds or no predisposing INSTI resistance changes in ex vivo infection experiments – DTG challenge often selects for IN changes R263K and G118R [88–90]. G118R, which alters CCD residue Gly118 proximal to the IN active site (Fig. 2B), reduces viral fitness ~ 4-fold and confers ~ 9-fold and between ~ 3 and 26-fold resistance to DTG and RAL, respectively [73, 74, 91]. The R263K CTD change, which has less than a 2-fold effect on HIV-1 fitness, fails to confer significance resistance to RAL while imparting ~ 3-4-fold resistance to DTG [73, 74, 86]. Thus, commonly-selected IN mutations confer maximally 10-fold resistance to DTG. Drug dissociation rates from IN mutant G118R and R263K-viral DNA complexes, together with cryogenic electron microscopy structures of drug-bound G118R and R263K IN mutant intasomes, which have not been reported, should help to inform the biochemical and structural bases for these commonly selected DTG resistance changes.

Altogether, IN changes that lead to high level drug resistance under INSTI-naïve conditions occur much more readily to first-generation compounds and can confer > 1000-fold resistance to these compounds. DTG resistance under similar conditions occurs much less frequently and these associated IN changes typically confer significantly lower levels of drug resistance. Still, as discussed, DTG can readily leverage first-generation resistance changes to select for multiply-mutated viruses that confer significant cross resistance to all INSTIs [67, 86, 92].

Non-IN pathways to DTG resistance

There are several reports of PWH that experienced virological failure while on an INSTI-containing regimen without a detectable IN drug resistance mutation (DRM) [93–98]. I will now segue to discuss non-IN changes that can confer DTG resistance. The following sections will follow the order through which the associated viral changes were first reported in the literature, starting with 3′PPT changes [99], which are then followed by changes in the envelope (Env) glycoproteins [77] and NC [100, 101].

3′PPT changes that confer DTG resistance

Fifteen consecutive ribopurine nucleobases comprise the 3′PPT (Fig. 3A, gray highlight). The 3′PPT is preceded by 5 ribouridines, called the U-box, which also contributes to reverse transcription [102]. The 6 riboguanosines that comprise the 3′ end of the 3′PPT, which are referred to as the G-tract [103], are the most conserved part of the 3′PPT [104–106].

The results of DTG dose escalation experiments conducted in MT4 cells, which are cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4)-expressing T cells co-infected with HTLV-1 [107], resulted in the isolation of mutant viral strain 9053 at 80 days after the initiation of the experiment. While 9053 lacked obvious IN DRMs, it contained 5 changes within and around the 3′PPT [99]. One of these changes, C > T (to be consistent with the literature, mutant viral names are DNA-based), occurred upstream of the U-box, effectively extending this motif by one nucleotide. The remaining 4 changes, which included a single nucleotide deletion, occurred within the G-tract (Fig. 3A). When transferred into subtype B strain HIV-1NL4−3, the 9053 changes conferred 23- to > 29-fold resistance to DTG [99, 108] (Fig. 3A).

Initial attempts to confirm the Malet et al. 2017 findings met with limited success due to the high fitness cost of the 9053 mutations (< 5% of WT infectivity), which precluded some laboratories from obtaining accurate infection measurements [109, 110]. Smith et al. also analyzed some of the changes that were observed from day 55 samples of the Malet et al. study. The AGT mutant virus, with G > A and G > T changes at the 4th and 6th positions of the G-tract, respectively, was also severely defective (7% WT infectivity) yet retained full sensitivity to DTG inhibition [109] (Fig. 3A). These investigators also tested the analogous AGC mutant virus, which mimicked the AGT mutant in that it was highly defective (5% of WT infection) yet fully sensitive to inhibition by DTG. The AGC mutant harbors the same G-tract changes as an isolate identified by Wijting et al. in one of 10 patients that experienced virological failure in the DOMONO DTG monotherapy trial [95] (Fig. 3A). The inability for AGC mutant viruses to confer significant DTG resistance in vitro was reported by two additional research groups [108, 110].

A comprehensive mutagenesis study that deconstructed the G-tract changes identified by Malet et al. helped to define the minimal number of base changes, as well as the positions of these changes, required to confer significant DTG resistance [108]; a sampling of these mutants appears in Fig. 3A. In addition to DTG, this study included tests for RAL, EVG, and BIC resistance. Viruses harboring each of the single nucleotide G-tract changes remained fully sensitive to all INSTIs. Indicating the importance of minimally two mutations, the 2C5T mutant virus, which compromised HIV-1 infection ~ 4-fold, conferred significant resistance (defined in this study as > 3-fold) to all INSTIs: DTG, > 29-fold (Fig. 3A); RAL, > 125-fold; EVG, 6.4-fold; and BIC, 3.4-fold. By contrast, the 2C5C mutant virus, which only differed from the 2C5T mutant by the T > C transition at G-tract position 5, was fully sensitive to all INSTIs, highlighting a requirement for ribouridine at G-tract position 5 in genomic RNA for INSTI resistance. The pairing of the ribouridine at G-tract position 5 with ribocytidine at position 2 was moreover important for high level resistance to some INSTIs, as the 2T5T mutant conferred intermediary resistance levels of 3.9-fold and 5.2-fold to DTG (Fig. 3A) and RAL, respectively. Adding the G > A change at position 3 of the G-tract further enhanced the resistance of the 2C5T strain to inhibition by EVG and BIC to > 29-fold and > 27-fold, respectively, while reducing fitness of the 2C5T virus ~ 5-fold (Fig. 3A). Based on this study, the 2C3A5T changes, carrying 3 of the 4 original G-tract mutations, accounted for the lion-share of broad-spectrum INSTI resistance profile of the 9053 mutant virus [99, 108]. At the same time, these 3’PPT mutant viruses were comparatively unfit (≤5% of WT infection) (Fig. 3A).

The HTLV-1 Tax protein is seemingly an important cofactor to select for 3′PPT-mutated, DTG resistant viral strains in cell culture experiments. The passaging of subtype B HIV-1LAI in SupT1 T cells, which do not express Tax, in the presence of 50 nM or 500 nM of DTG failed to select for known IN DRMs or dominant 3′PPT changes, though minor 3′PPT sequence variants were detected at 81 and 135 days after the start of the experiment [111]. Tax-expressing CD4+ T cells can support the replication of class I IN-defective mutant viruses carrying DDE active site mutations [23, 32]; class II IN-defective mutant viruses, by contrast, lack this capacity due to associated reverse transcription defects [32, 49]. Dekker at al. accordingly leveraged C8166-45 T cells, which express Tax [112], to select for DTG resistant strains starting from a randomized swarm of HIV-1LAI G-tract mutations (4,096 theoretical mutant viruses). Dominant outgrowth mutants included M1, selected at 87 days in the presence of 50 nM DTG, M2 (same conditions), and M3 (142 days with 500 nM DTG) (Fig. 3A); M6 and M7 variants of M1 and M2, respectively, harbored an additional 4 bp deletion and 65 bp duplication upstream of the 3′PPT. The M1, M2, and M3 mutants conferred significant > 29-fold resistance to DTG [111, 113] (Fig. 3A). Analyses of viral DNA replication intermediates in C8166-45 cells co-infected with WT plus M6 or WT + M7 viruses, with or without DTG present, revealed increased levels of mutant 1-LTR circular DNAs without detectable levels of mutant viral DNA integration [111]. These data were fully consistent with an independent study that concluded that aberrantly-high levels of 1-LTR circles (up to 50% of total viral DNA) supported the replication of the original 9053 3′PPT mutant strain in MT-4 cells in the absence of detectable levels of HIV-1 integration [114]. Curiously, Richetta et al. [114] reported that the 9053 mutant virus also replicated in CEM-SS T cells, which do not express Tax, although at somewhat slower kinetics than it replicated in Tax+ MT-4 cells.

My laboratory previously defined CD4+ T cell lines as permissive, semi-permissive, or non-permissive based on their abilities to support continual replication of class I IN-defective mutant viruses [32]. We can now appreciate that Tax recruits nuclear factor κB to counteract repressive heterochromatin at the LTR of unintegrated HIV-1 DNA, explaining the permissivity of Tax+ cells to support efficient replication of class I IN and 3′PPT mutant viruses in the absence of integration [23, 24, 32, 99, 111, 114]. Jurkat and CEM-12D7 T cells, which failed to support detectable levels of class I IN mutant viral replication, were defined as non-permissive [32]. The activities of class I IN mutant viruses, relative to matched WT viruses, can under single-round infection conditions vary as much as 1,000-fold across cell lines and primary cells [23, 32, 54, 55, 115]. In one study, class I IN mutant virus D64V infected CEM-SS and Jurkat T cells poorly (~ 1% of WT infection) [23], which is consistent with the non-permissive cellular phenotype. Plausibly, variations within CEM-SS cell isolates across laboratories may account for the substantial differences reported in the ability for these cells to support efficient replication of class I IN-defective and 3′PPT mutant viruses in the absence of Tax [23, 113, 114]. DTG resistance, however, can nevertheless be conferred from comparatively unfit 3′PPT mutant viruses in the absence of Tax expression, as long as the low levels of mutant viral expression lie within scorable ranges of virus infection and drug susceptibility assays [99, 108–111].

CEMx174 cells, which also do not express Tax, upon virus serial passage supported self-limiting replication of a class I IN mutant virus and were accordingly typed as semi-permissive [32]. Long term culture of HIV-1 in CEMx174 cells in the presence of 300 nM CAB selected for predominant 3′PPT G-tract mutants GGAGCA and AGCAGT without detectable IN DRMs, indicating that Tax may not be a critical cofactor for the selection of INSTI-resistant 3′PPT mutant viral strains in vitro [116]. Molecularly-cloned G-tract variant viruses for determination of FCs in CAB resistance were unfortunately not assessed in this study.

Various explanations have been put forth to account for the DTG resistance profiles of 3′PPT mutant viruses in the absence of detectable HIV-1 integration. Alterations in LTR end sequences are commonly inferred by sequencing the LTR-LTR junction within 2-LTR circles [117–119]. G-tract mutations can alter sites of RNase H cleavage within the 3′PPT, which can shorten the primer for plus-strand DNA synthesis and, reciprocally, extend the U3 end of the LTR [101, 103, 120] (Fig. 1). Because extending LTR ends beyond the natural CA/GT terminus (/, 3′ processing site) reduces the efficiency of IN 3′ processing activity in vitro [28, 121], G-tract mutations that yield significant 3′PPT extensions of the U3 LTR end would predictably decrease the efficiency of HIV-1 integration [122]. G-tract mutations would also weaken overall RNA/DNA complementarity needed for the comparative resistance of the 3′PPT to RNase H-mediated degradation [123, 124], which could lead to the loss of the element as a primer for plus-strand synthesis [111]. Along these lines, recombinant 3′PPT RNA containing the 9053 mutations failed to prime RT-directed DNA synthesis in vitro [114]. Plus-strand DNA synthesis initiated from the cPPT in the absence of effective 3′PPT priming can yield fractional increases in 1-LTR circle formation to confer resistance to DTG in the absence of viral DNA integration [111, 114] (Fig. 3B).

Env glycoprotein changes that confer DTG resistance

HIV-1 Env is expressed as a precursor 160 kDa glycoprotein (gp) that is processed by cellular furin into gp120 surface (SU) and gp41 transmembrane (TM) components [125]. SU and TM, which interact with one another on the virion surface non-covalently, comprise a metastable heterotrimer that engages cellular receptors to initiate virus infection (see [126] and [127] for recent reviews). In brief, the interaction of SU with cellular CD4 [128, 129] induces conformational changes that expose the binding site for a 7-transmembrane-spanning co-receptor, typically C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) or C-C motif chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) [130–134]. Co-receptor binding in turn promotes the insertion of the TM fusion peptide (FP) into the cellular membrane and the refolding of TM heptad repeat (HR) regions 1 and 2 into a 6-helical bundle to promote the fusion of the viral lipid envelope with the cell membrane [135–137]. Single molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (smFRET) experiments have defined SU conformational state 1 (closed; prior to CD4 engagement), state 2 (CD4-triggered, partially opened), and state 3 (fully opened/fusion-activated) [138, 139]. SU and TM harbor various subdomains, including variable regions (VRs) and constant regions (CRs) 1–5 for SU [140] and FP, HR, membrane proximal, membrane-spanning, and cytoplasmic tail regions for TM [136, 137, 141] (Fig. 4A).

HIV-1 can infect cells in vitro as a cell-free virion or via cell-to-cell transfer from a virus-producer cell through the virological synapse (VS) [142–145]. VS-mediated entry is much more efficient than cell-free infection, effectively increasing the multiplicity of infection (MOI), which in turn translates to a higher copy number of integrated viral DNA genomes per cell [146]. While some studies indicate that cells from PWH also possess multiple copies of integrated proviruses [147, 148], others have concluded that the majority of infected cells contain a single provirus [149, 150]. Although these observations muddle the ability to firmly conclude a contribution from VS-mediated entry to HIV-1 viremia and pathogenicity in vivo, many investigators would believe that at least some virus infection in vivo occurs via direct cell contact.

Initial indications of a role for Env in DTG resistance came from studying revertant viruses of HIV-1NL4−3 (which utilizes CXCR4 for cell entry) Gag p6 mutant viruses [77]. In these experiments, multi-round HIV-1 replication was initiated by transfecting Jurkat T cells with molecularly-cloned viral DNA. While revertant viruses were shown to harbor changes in vpu and env, new changes were not identified in p6. By constructing specific mutant viral strains, these investigators showed that changes Y61H and P81S, both of which occurred within the C1 region of SU, and A556T, which mapped to TM HR1, in large part rescued the replication defects of the initial p6 mutant viruses (Fig. 4A). The Env changes alone – when tested without the p6 changes – displayed a remarkable phenotype to improve multi-round replication kinetics in comparison to the WT while at the same time significantly reducing the efficiency of cell-free HIV-1 infection. Each Env change additionally improved the growth kinetics of the previously-described IN CCD mutant N155E virus [151]. Based on these results, the authors tested sensitivity to inhibition by DTG. Each Env change conferred significant resistance to 1.5 nM and 3.0 nM DTG, and TM change A539V was additionally selected from WT virus propagated in the presence of 3 nM DTG without noticeable IN DRMs. While A539V similarly improved the kinetics of WT replication in the absence of DTG, it, unlike Y61H, P81S, and A556T, displayed close to the WT level of cell-free HIV-1 infection. Although the Env mutant viruses remained sensitive to DTG inhibition under single-round infection conditions, the A556T and A539V changes were shown to confer 4.6- and 5.3-fold resistance to DTG, respectively, under multi-round replication conditions [77] (Fig. 4C).

Based on the requirement for multi-round HIV-1 replication, infection via the VS was proposed as the mechanism underlying Env mutant viral resistance to DTG [77]. Indeed, prior studies had demonstrated that cell-mediated infection conferred selective resistance to various types of antiretroviral inhibitors as well as neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) [152–154]. Along these lines, the A539V mutant was shown in a subsequent paper under multi-round infection conditions to confer resistance to a variety of antiretroviral inhibitors, including INSTIs (RAL and DTG, 5.4 and 8.1 FCs, respectively), non-nucleoside RT inhibitor (NNRTI) efavirenz (FC = 28), NRTI emtricitabine (FC = 5.4), PR inhibitor nelfinavir (FC = 24), and allosteric IN inhibitor (ALLINI) BI224436 (FC = 2.3). By contrast, the A539V mutant remained sensitive to inhibition by Env-targeting inhibitors T20 [155], which inhibits virus entry by blocking HR1-HR2 6-helix bundle formation, and BMS-378,806 [156], which blocks conformational changes induced by CD4 binding [157]. These findings were consistent with prior reports that cell-mediated HIV-1 infection remained sensitive to inhibition by entry inhibitors [158, 159].

SU region C1 and TM region HR1 play important roles in mediating the SU-TM interaction [160–163] and SU residues Tyr61 and Pro81 and TM residues Ala539 and Ala556 cluster near the SU-TM interface (Fig. 4B). SU readily dissociates from HIV-1 particles [164], a process that can be exacerbated by a soluble form of CD4 (sCD4) [165]. Consistent with stabilization of the SU-TM binding interface, Env changes Tyr61, Pro81, Ala539, and Ala556 conferred ~ 3.5- to > 10-fold resistance to sCD4-induced SU shedding from virions [157]. Env mutant conformation was probed by determining sensitivities to neutralization by different classes of NAbs including those that recognize: (i) unliganded, closed state 1 (VRC01, PGT145, PGT121, PG16, and 2G12), (ii) the SU-TM interface (35022), which served as an internal control due to conformationally-independent NAb recognition [166], and (iii) the CD4-bound, state 2 conformation (F105, 17b, 447-52D, and 10E8). Although Ala593 did not contribute directly to any of these NAb recognition epitopes, the A593V change nonetheless conferred significant resistance to neuralization by all CD4-bound targeting NAbs and significant sensitivity to PGT145 neutralization [157].

Hikichi and colleagues expanded upon the spectrum of changes that can confer DTG resistance by selecting for resistant strains over comparatively long-term (~ 32–46 weeks) dose escalation experiments in SupT1 T cells. The results of these experiments yielded a cacophony of Env changes spanning all SU and TM regions (except MPER in TM) as well as changes in NC (described in detail in the next section), and, occasionally, in IN [100]. A subset of Env changes, K6N (in the gp160 signal peptide), V85A (SU C1), S162K (SU V2), R298K (SU V3), Q363R (SU C3), A541V (TM HR1, analogous to the A539V change from the previous paper), V693I (TM MS), and G825E (TM CT) were co-assembled into 7xEnv* molecular clones for testing. In the absence of DTG, HIV-1NL4−3 7xEnv* displayed the same growth advantage in SupT1 cells as virus containing the solo A541V change. However, 7xEnv* uniquely replicated in the presence of 100 nM and 1 µM DTG. Replication under all conditions moreover preceded via IN-mediated integration, as adding the class I D116N active site mutation precluded 7xEnv* mutant viral replication. Similar to their previous findings, 7xEnv* displayed poor single-round infectivity and failed to convey significant DTG resistance under these infection conditions. Parallel work with a subtype C virus, as well as an HIV-1NL4−3 variant engineered to enter cells via the CCR5 co-receptor, revealed that these findings were HIV-1 subtype and co-receptor type independent.

Under multi-round replication conditions, 7xEnv* conferred highly impressive levels of INSTI resistance: DTG, 2,306-fold (Fig. 4C); CAB, > 2,000-fold; and RAL, > 400-fold. Although also conveying significant resistance to other antiretroviral inhibitors, these fold-change values paled in comparison: RT inhibitors, ~ 4 to 12-fold; PR inhibitors, ~ 6 to 11-fold; and T20, 2.4-fold. 7xEnv*, moreover, remained sensitive to inhibition by entry inhibitor BMS-378,806 and ALLINI BI224436. As previously documented for the A541V mutant virus, 7xEnv* resisted neutralization by NAbs that recognize the CD4-bound conformation (17b, 447-52D, and F105) as well as sCD4-induced shedding of SU from virions. SmFRET-based experiments revealed little change in 7xEnv* states before versus after CD4 binding, indicating that the Env changes practically negated the CD4-induced state 1-to-state 2 transition that is important for cell-free infection. At the same time, this “pre-determined” state 2 Env greatly favored infection via the VS [100].

The role of high MOI in conferring DTG resistance could be recapitulated under cell free infection conditions using high viral doses and pseudotyping with the vesicular stomatitis G envelope glycoprotein to enhance HIV-1 entry [167]. While these conditions also conferred resistance to RAL and to CAB, they failed to convey resistance to inhibitors from other drug classes. At present, it is unclear how high MOI confers preferential INSTI resistance. INSTIs, rather uniquely, have a limited number of targets to inhibit per replication cycle per cell – at most, two IN active sites performing two DNA strand transfer reactions. By comparison, the same overall complement of RT enzymes, at slightly early times in the infection cycle, polymerizes > 19,000 dNTPs to make one molecule of double-stranded viral DNA. Although the minimal number of RT molecules required to complete this task is unknown, a single IN active site – within the context of an asymmetrically inhibited intasome – can still support HIV-1 infection – with help from cellular DNA recombination machineries to integrate the viral DNA end that is unavailable for IN-mediated catalysis [168]. Based on the slow-off rates of second-generation INSTIs from intasome complexes in vitro, one possibility is that a comparatively large number of intasomes per cell helps to soak up the effective population of INSTI molecules [100].

Changes in NC that confer DTG resistance

NC proteins play numerous important roles in retroviral replication. HIV-1 NC, which is composed of 55 residues, harbors two Cys-Cys-His-Cys (CCHC) zinc-binding motifs that are separated by a short, basic linker (Fig. 5A). As part of the Gag precursor protein, the zinc-binding and linker regions play important roles in genomic RNA packaging and virion assembly [169, 170]. As the main viral ribonucleoprotein, NC functions as a chaperone to condense RNA within the core to facilitate reverse transcription (reviewed in ref [171]). NC in turn engages the neo-synthesized DNA to condense it into a tight globule [172, 173], which helps to prevent premature rupture or uncoating of the protective capsid shell [173–177]. Several studies additionally indicate that NC protein can stimulate HIV-1 IN strand transfer activity in vitro [178–181], though this has not been universally observed [182]. While some HIV-1 NC mutant viruses appear to be defective for integration, associated reverse transcription defects can muddy the interpretation of integration-specific phenotypes [183–186].

Alongside Env changes, several changes in NC arose during long-term DTG dose-escalation experiments in SupT1 cells [100] and in peripheral blood mononuclear cells [101], a primary source of human CD4+ T cells. Several of these changes, including N17S, G19S, N27I, G35D, G34E, and M46I, conveyed significant ~ 2 to 3-fold and ~ 3 to 8-fold DTG resistance during multi-round HIV-1NL4−3 replication and single-round infection of SupT1 T cells, respectively; single-round infection of primary CD4 + T cells derived from blood donors yielded similarly significant ~ 2 to 6 FCs in DTG resistance [101] (Fig. 5A-C). The participating amino acid residues generally mapped within the zinc-coordinating regions of NC. When visualized on an NMR structure of NC bound to HIV-1 RNA, the residues do not seem to cluster to a particular part of the structure or RNA-binding interface (Fig. 5A-B). Structural alterations that may occur when NC undergoes biomolecular condensation are largely uncharacterized [172, 173, 187]. Unlike the above discussed Env mutant viruses, these NC mutant viruses displayed the WT level of single round infectivity under cell-free infection conditions [100]. Also unlike the Env changes, alterations in NC appeared to specifically convey INSTI resistance, as dose escalation with the potent EFdA RT inhibitor [188] yielded RT and Env changes without any changes in NC [100].

Some NC mutations, such as H44C in the downstream zinc-binding region, disrupt proper viral DNA end formation needed for efficient IN-viral DNA binding and 3′ processing activity [185, 186]. Such a scenario could contribute to DTG resistance, akin to how 3′PPT extensions might block IN access to the viral U3 DNA ends of G-tract mutant viruses [122]. The sequencing of NC mutant N17S, G19S, and N27I viral 2-LTR circle junctions however failed to detect any significant differences from the WT, while the H44C mutant, as expected, yielded significant increases in junctional insertions and deletions. These results were importantly confirmed in the backdrop of the IN D116N active site mutation, which increases the frequencies of 2-LTR circle formation and the proportion of unprocessed consensus GT/AC junction sequences [54, 119, 185]. Viral DNA end sequences were also analyzed via an integration site sequencing pipeline that maps sites of HIV-1 integration into the human genome [189]. WT and NC mutant G19S, which was highlighted in this study due to the frequency at which it arose in DTG dose escalation experiments [100], were determined to harbor ~ 7.2% and 7.9% aberrant LTR ends, respectively. Although highly similar values, this difference was statistically significant due to the large number of integration junctions sequenced (~ 50,000 for each virus). Taken together, these investigators concluded that the NC mutations that confer resistance to DTG did not alter the integrity of the vial DNA end sequences required for intasome formation and IN catalysis [101] (Fig. 1, steps 9 and 10).

Though most investigators tend to assess single-round HIV-1 infectivity at ~ 2 days postinfection, shortening the assay readout to 1 day revealed significant ≥2-fold increases in the levels of NC mutant N17S, G29S, and G35D viral infection compared to the WT virus. Moreover, fold-increases in infection at 24 h positively correlated with DTG EC50 concentration across a set of 8 viruses (WT plus 7 NC mutants). Based on these observations, the kinetics of reverse transcription, 2-LTR circle formation, uncoating, and integration were analyzed for the WT and G19S NC mutant viruses. While both viruses displayed indistinguishable levels of reverse transcription and kinetics of reverse transcription and uncoating, the NC mutant virus yielded significantly increased levels of integration and 2-LTR circle formation at early times post-infection (6 and 9 h) that dissipated by 12 h post-infection. Integration kinetics were also assessed as the time required after the initiation of infection to overcome DTG inhibition. As controls, times required to overcome 50% of the levels of inhibition imposed by inhibitors of virus entry (BMS-378806), reverse transcription (NNRTI rilpivirine), and uncoating (lenacapavir; [177, 190, 191]) were assessed in parallel. While the times required to lose sensitivities to these inhibitors were indistinguishable for the WT and G19S mutant viruses, the NC mutant lost its sensitivity to DTG much faster than the WT virus. The WT virus experienced a 5.5 h lag between the completion of reverse transcription/uncoating and integration, while the G19S NC mutant viral lag, 2.4 h, was significantly shorter [101].

As discussed in the background section, the spatiotemporal separation of IN 3′ processing and strand transfer activities during the early phase of virus infection defines a viral Achilles’ heel that is exploited by INSTIs. Remarkably, NC mutations seemingly shorten this time frame to such an extent that they can confer INSTI resistance. INSTI binding rates to HIV-1 IN-viral DNA complexes in vitro have been most carefully described for a pre-clinical compound from Bristol-Myers Squibb [68]. Determination of such biochemical data for the approved INSTIs would be expected to help inform the mechanistic basis of NC mutant viral resistance to DTG. Although the work from Hikichi and colleagues perhaps most clearly supports a role for NC in HIV-1 integration during virus infection, the mechanistic basis for how NC regulates integration is unclear. It would be instructive to determine the kinetics of IN 3′ processing activity [29] in WT and G19S NC mutant-infected cells. If differences are seen, the kinetics of intasome formation could be assessed by cell-based methods such as bacteriophage Mu-mediated PCR footprinting [192, 193]. Possibly, such approaches could be extended to in vitro systems using purified components. Previous analyses of the role of NC on HIV-1 IN activity [178–182] predated advances employed for subsequent intasome studies, including solubility-enhanced forms of IN and assembly and purification conditions [66, 194–198]. It would be highly informative to assess the kinetics of intasome formation, IN 3′ processing and strand transfer activities, and INSTI association and dissociation rates as a function of WT versus G19S NC mutant protein. Such systems could in turn be leveraged to inform the structural basis of NC-mediated INSTI resistance.

Implications for clinical resistance to DTG

Previous studies have revealed that ~ 3 to 4-fold and 10-fold reductions in susceptibility to DTG correlated with reduced responses and markedly reduced responses to the associated therapies, respectively [73, 199]. Each of the non-IN resistance pathways described herein could accordingly seriously impact the clinical efficacies of DTG-containing regimens.

As discussed above, 3′PPT G-tract mutations were identified in one individual that experienced virological failure in the DOMONO DTG monotherapy trial without noted IN DRMs [95]. The associated AGC mutations, however, failed to confer significant resistance to DTG in follow-up drug susceptibility experiments [108–110]. Other G-tract mutations, including G > T transversion at the first position, or deletion of the 6th G, were identified in patients receiving DTG-based therapy [104]. In subsequent work, the deletion mutant virus remained fully susceptible to inhibition by four INSTIs, which was also observed for single nucleotide substitutions of positions 2–6 of the G-tract. It would apparently follow that viruses harboring the first position G > T change would also be sensitive to INSTI inhibition [108].

Of the three known pathways that can generate non-IN-mediated DTG resistance in vitro, 3′PPT mutations may be the least likely to have widespread clinical implications. The generation of DTG resistant viruses ex vivo has required specialized conditions that optimally benefit, and may require, co-expression of the HTLV-1 Tax protein [99, 100, 111]. Although some G-tract mutant viruses were also noted to arise in Tax-negative CEMx174 cells, the extent that these mutations conferred INSTI resistance was not rigorously tested [116]. Moreover, 3′PPT G-tract mutant viruses that confer significant DTG resistance are comparatively unfit, displaying ~ 5%-45% of WT activity under single-round infection conditions and < 0.001% of WT fitness under multiple rounds of replicative spread (Fig. 3A). Still, certain conditions may aid the selection of clinically-relevant 3′PPT mutant viruses. While the worldwide prevalence of HIV-1/HTLV-1 coinfection varies widely, certain populations experience rates as high as 10% [200, 201]. Because both viruses infect CD4+ T cells and macrophages [128, 129, 202, 203], HIV-1 could in some cases be exposed to cellular Tax in vivo. Additional sequence analyses, together with follow-up drug susceptibility profiles, are required to determine the relevance of 3′PPT changes to clinical INSTI resistance. The inclusion of PWH failing INSTI regimens without noticeable IN DRMs and that are coinfected with HTLV-1 would benefit such analyses.

Changes in Env could very well serve to amplify both IN and non-IN pathways to the development of clinically-relevant DTG resistance. HIV-1 infection is highly cytopathic to T cells [204, 205], which would be exacerbated by the high MOIs demonstrated for INSTI-resistant Env mutant viruses in vitro [100, 150, 206]. Cells infected at correspondingly high MOIs in vivo may be expected to have quite limited lifespans. Env mutations, therefore, may serve more as a conveyance or “steppingstone” to enhance the formation and fixation of other INSTI resistance changes [100]. Along these lines, Env mutations were routinely observed to occur prior to the appearance of both NC and IN resistance changes during long-term dose escalation experiments [100, 101]. Recent analysis of a British cohort identified Env changes Y61H and A539V, NC change N8S, as well as the 3′PPT G-tract proximal C > T transition mutation at frequencies of ~ 4–10% among 375 PWH on an INSTI-containing regimen. While none of these changes correlated to time to viral blip or time to viral suppression, the A539V change was highly predictive of virus strains harboring one of the other non-IN mutations [207].

Viral samples from PWH failing second-generation INSTIs without noticeable IN DRMs should be monitored for changes in Env and NC. The comparatively high variability of Env sequences among circulating HIV-1 strains makes env sequence surveillance inherently challenging. The NC region, by contrast, as well as some seemingly important Env residues, such as Ala539, are comparatively well conserved [77]. Sequence surveillance of discrete gag (NC), pol (IN) and env (SU and TM) amplicons will necessarily increase the amount of follow-up work needed to determine contributions of individual versus combination mutations to INSTI resistance. Approaches that leverage long-read sequencing to capture linked gag, pol, and env (and, for that matter, the 3′PPT) changes would optimally inform these investigations [208–210].

Conclusions and perspectives

The INSTI molecular target is the CSC intasome that is produced by the processing of linear viral DNA LTR ends by IN [62, 64] (Fig. 1, step 10). While IN drug resistance mutations generally increase the rate at which INSTIs dissociate from such complexes [69, 70], I have herein reviewed three novel non-IN resistance pathways that either bypass the need of the CSC intasome for HIV-1 replication (3′PPT changes; [111, 114]), seemingly swamp out effective INSTI molecules via high MOI (Env changes; [77, 100]), or apparently shorten the time frame under which CSC intasomes are available for INSTI binding (NC changes; [101]) (pictorially summarized in the graphical abstract). While each of these end-around pathways is in its own right striking, the identification of three such pathways highlights the remarkable plasticity of HIV-1 to evolve drug resistance to a highly efficacious second-generation INSTI. While changes in NC and the 3′PPT are likely INSTI-specific, mutations in Env TM have also notably been linked to virological failure to second-generation PR inhibitors [211, 212]. The ability of Env mutations to amplify drug resistance pathways may predictably become more problematic as additional antiretroviral inhibitors with high barriers to the generation of drug resistance become available [100]. Comprehensive determination of non-IN pathways that lead to clinical failure to second-generation INSTIs will aid optimal management of the current global rollout of TLD and establish methodologies to monitor and understand virological failure to other ART regimens where DRMs are routinely observed outside of the direct drug target.
