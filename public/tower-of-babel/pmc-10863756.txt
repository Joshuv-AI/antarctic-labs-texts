Association Between New‐Onset Type 2 Diabetes and Cardiac Conduction Diseases: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Maoxiang Zhao, Jingli Gao, Shuohua Chen, Siyu Yao, Miao Wang, Chi Wang, Sijin Zhang, Zekun Feng, Lu Tian, Yanjie Li, Yang Liu, Shouling Wu, Hao Xue
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac conduction diseases can lead to life‐threatening outcomes. However, the evidence on risk factors for conduction disease that is needed to underpin prevention strategies is limited. The present study aimed to determine the association between type 2 diabetes and cardiac conduction diseases.Methods and ResultsThis study included 101 080 participants free of prevalent diabetes and cardiac conduction diseases at baseline from the Kailuan Study. All participants were monitored biennially until December 31, 2020. During follow‐up, 14 397 participants were diagnosed as having type 2 diabetes. For each case subject, 1 control subject was randomly selected, matched for age (±1 year) and sex. The final analysis comprised 10 744 case‐control pairs. Cox regression models with age as the underlying time scale were used. During a median follow‐up of 5.46 years, 571 incident events occurred, including 164 atrioventricular blocks, 414 bundle‐branch blocks (BBBs), 274 right BBBs, and 210 left BBBs. After adjustment for potential confounders, participants with type 2 diabetes diagnosed had greater relative risks for most outcomes relative to controls, with hazard ratios of 1.42 (95% CI, 1.18–1.67) for conduction diseases, 1.40 (95% CI, 1.00–1.96) for atrioventricular blocks, 1.43 (95% CI, 1.16–1.75) for BBBs, and 1.69 (95% CI, 1.15–2.49) for left BBBs. In contrast, no association between diabetes and right BBB was observed.ConclusionsIn this study, participants with type 2 diabetes are at an increased risk of cardiac conduction disease but not associated with the development of right BBB.Keywords: atrioventricular block, cardiac conduction disease, left bundle‐branch block, right bundle‐branch block, type 2 diabetesSubject Categories: Diabetes, Type 2; Epidemiology; Primary Prevention; Arrhythmias

Cardiac conduction disease, a common arrhythmia seen on the ECG, can cause a delay or interruption in any part of the conduction system. 1 , 2 , 3 A series of recent studies has found that cardiac conduction abnormalities can lead to various adverse outcomes. Left bundle‐branch blocks (LBBBs) and right bundle‐branch blocks (RBBBs) are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases and mortality. 4 , 5 , 6 Even left anterior fascicular block, previously thought to have limited prognostic significance, has been associated with a heightened risk of atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, and death. 7 Although pacemaker implantation is currently the effective way of treating patients with life‐threatening conduction disease, it carries a heavy financial burden and a risk of serious complications. 8 However, there are no proven preventive strategies to reduce the risk of this common disorder. Considering that treatment of conduction disease is an enormous medical and socioeconomic task, a deep understanding of risk factors for conduction diseases is imperative.

Type 2 diabetes, a disease with dramatically increasing prevalence worldwide, is suggested to induce cardiac fibrosis and inflammation, which have been shown to underlie conduction abnormality development. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 However, limited evidence is available for the relationship between type 2 diabetes and conduction disease. The findings of the CHS (Cardiovascular Health Study) have shown that type 2 diabetes increased the risk of conduction disease. 13 However, the previous study did not compare risks from the time of diagnosis, and the researchers did not fully adjust for the impact of diabetes duration, which may be associated with greater risk of conduction diseases. A recent national nested case‐control study from Denmark reported that patients with new‐onset type 2 diabetes are at higher risk of complete atrioventricular block (AVB) compared with age‐ and sex‐matched control subjects, but the study did not investigate the relationship between diabetes and other subtypes of conduction block, and all the subjects enrolled were from the Western population. 14 Whether type 2 diabetes is an independent risk factor of conduction diseases in the Asian population remains unknown.

We therefore sought to examine longitudinal data from the population‐based Kailuan Study to investigate the association between type 2 diabetes and cardiac conduction diseases.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from Kailuan community, but restrictions apply to the availability of these data, which were used under license for the current study, and so are not publicly available. Data are, however, available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request and with permission of corresponding author. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Kailuan General Hospital. All participants gave written informed consent before study inclusion.

The current analysis was on the basis of data from the Kailuan cohort, an ongoing, dynamic, large community‐based cohort study in Tangshan, China. The details of the study design and procedures were described previously. 15 , 16 In brief, from June 2006 to December 2017, a total of 179 328 participants, aged ≥18 years, completed questionnaires by interviews, and had clinical/laboratory examinations that were conducted biennially in the 11 hospitals responsible for health care of this community.

In the current study, participants were included if they (1) attended the biennial examination; (2) did not have conduction disease and diabetes at baseline; and (3) had no missing important covariates (eg, fasting blood glucose, history of diabetes, use of hypoglycemic drugs, and ECG). Of the 98 054 participants who met these criteria, 14 397 were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes during follow‐up. Type 2 diabetes debut time was defined as the date of the examination at which type 2 diabetes was first diagnosed. For each case of new‐onset diabetes, 1 control subject was randomly selected from the population who attended the examination in the same year and matched for age (±1 years) and sex. The control subjects had to be without diabetes throughout the whole study period to be eligible. Ultimately, data from 10 777 case‐control pairs were analyzed (Figure). The follow‐up period started from the date of diagnosis for the case subjects with type 2 diabetes and from the same year for the matched control subjects.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was defined as any conduction disease, including atrioventricular and intraventricular conduction disturbances, assessed from 12‐lead standard ECG (ECG9130P; Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan) at each examination. Before receiving 12‐lead ECG in supine position, participants rested for 5 minutes in a quiet state. All ECGs were read by well‐trained physicians. ECG abnormalities, including evidence of conduction disease, were classified using the standards of the Minnesota ECG classification (Table S1). In case of controversy, a panel of 3 experienced physicians who were blinded to the study design reviewed the medical records and adjudicated the cases annually. Mortality information was obtained from provincial vital statistics offices and updated annually. Participants were followed up until the date of death, the occurrence of conduction diseases, or December 31, 2020.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as means±SDs for continuous variables and numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using the ANOVA, whereas categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. We used multiple imputation by chained equations to impute missing values for covariates. The incidence rate of conduction diseases was calculated by dividing the number of incident cases by the total follow‐up duration (person‐years). To explore the relationship between diabetes‐related AVB and pacemaker implantation, we divided participants into 4 groups: (1) nondiabetes and without AVB; (2) diabetes and without AVB; (3) nondiabetes and with AVB; and (4) diabetes and with AVB. Proportions of pacemaker implantation were compared between groups using the χ2 test.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models with age as the underlying time scale variable were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs of conduction disease for participants with new‐onset type 2 diabetes, compared with control subjects. The proportional‐hazards assumption was satisfied in the models. The covariates that were adjusted for were selected a priori based on previous studies and included physical activity, smoking status, alcohol drinking status, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, obese status, estimated glomerular filtration rate, hypertension, use of lipid‐lowering agents, use of antihypertensive agents, and use of hypoglycemic agents.

We performed several sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of the findings. These analyses included the following: (1) excluding outcome events occurring within the first year of follow‐up to address the potential reverse causation; (2) excluding participants who had a history of cardiovascular disease at baseline and developed cardiovascular disease during follow‐up; (3) excluding participants under treatment of hypoglycemic agents; and (4) considering the competing risk of death, Fine‐Gray models were used to repeat our analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). All statistical tests were 2 sided, and the significance level was set at P<0.05.

Discussion

In this large community‐based cohort study, we found that participants with type 2 diabetes had a higher risk of cardiac conduction diseases, but no differences in the risk of RBBB were observed by type 2 diabetes status.

As of today, only a few studies have investigated the association between type 2 diabetes and cardiac conduction disease. Two cross‐sectional studies found that type 2 diabetes was associated with third‐degree AVB in patients with hypertension and patients with ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction. 17 , 18 Recently, longitudinal data from the CHS showed that patients with type 2 diabetes had a significantly higher risk of cardiac conduction diseases. 13 However, these studies only enrolled White patients. The current study was the first prospective study to investigate the association between type 2 diabetes and cardiac conduction disease in an Asian country. In addition, previous studies mainly focused on participants with prevalent type 2 diabetes and did not start follow‐up at the time of diagnosis. In the present study, new‐onset type 2 diabetes occurred during the follow‐up period, which might reduce the impact of potential prevalence‐incidence bias. In line with our results, a more recent study, conducted by Haxha et al, reported that people with type 2 diabetes had higher risk for complete AVB compared with age‐ and sex‐matched control subjects. 14

Epidemiology

Our study extends the present knowledge as we further explore the association between type 2 diabetes and BBB. We found that type 2 diabetes was associated with a significantly higher risk of developing LBBB, but no significant association between type 2 diabetes and risk of RBBB was detected. Our findings are partly supported by the results from a recent post hoc analysis of the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT) study, where blood pressure control was associated with lower risk of LBBB, but no differences in the risk of RBBB were observed between treatment groups. 19 These findings might indicate that the association between type 2 diabetes and higher risk of LBBB was not a chance finding.

Our findings have important implications for the public health policy on the prevention of cardiac conduction disease. Growing evidence suggests that the progression of cardiac conduction diseases can lead to life‐threatening outcomes, including heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and all‐cause mortality. Severe AVB has a substantial mortality if left untreated and accounts for approximately half of the 1.25 million pacemakers implanted each year globally. 20 However, the only available effective therapy is pacemaker implantation, which has high medical cost and carries a risk of serious complications. Results of our study indicated that diabetes, a preventable risk factor, intensifies the risk of conduction diseases. Thus, increased efforts in the pervasive prevention of type 2 diabetes may lead to a substantial reduction of conduction disease–related medical and socioeconomic burden in China, where the prevalence of type 2 diabetes has dramatically increased over the past 3 decades, and approximately one‐third of adults were prediabetes in 2018. 21 , 22

The main strengths of our study include its large sample size, prospective cohort design, and the ability to track people from the time at diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. In addition, we included age‐ and sex‐matched control subjects without diabetes, which could fully consider and reduce the effects of such confounding factors.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because this is an observational study, we cannot establish the causality between type 2 diabetes and the development of conduction disease. Second, all the participants in the present study were Chinese, which minimized the confounding effect of racial background but may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, the onset age of type 2 diabetes was calculated as the date of the physical examination when diabetes was first diagnosed minus the patient's birth date, which slightly differed from the actual age at diagnosis. However, participants in our study underwent physical examination biennially; therefore, discrepancies in the onset age would not have a meaningful influence on the results. Fourth, the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes was based on a single measurement of fasting blood glucose, rather than oral glucose tolerance testing or the measurement of hemoglobin A1c, and therefore the incidence of type 2 diabetes might have been underestimated. Fifth, we did not distinguish between type 1 and type 2 diabetes in our study. However, according to the Chinese clinical guideline for preventing diabetes, type 2 diabetes represents ≈95% of all cases of diabetes. In addition, the average age at the diagnosis of diabetes was 55.6 years in our study, so misclassification of other types of diabetes was minimal.

In conclusion, participants with type 2 diabetes are at an increased risk of cardiac conduction disease but not associated with the development of RBBB. The findings of our study highlight the need to allocate more medical resources to the prevention of type 2 diabetes.
