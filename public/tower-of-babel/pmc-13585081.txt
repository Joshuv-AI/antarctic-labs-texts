Introduction

Dental caries is a biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, multifactorial disease characterized by alternating cycles of demineralization and remineralization of tooth structure.1 In primary teeth, it progresses rapidly, often resulting in pain, infection, and compromised quality of life and is referred to as early childhood caries (ECC). It is a major public health problem; the global burden of disease study 2017 reported untreated caries in deciduous teeth as the tenth most prevalent condition, affecting over 532 million children.2

Prevention of caries relies on controlling plaque biofilm and enhancing enamel resistance to acid attack. Other than modifying the oral ecology, various agents are also used for the prevention of ECC, which target either bacteria, sugar metabolism, or the strengthening of the tooth structure. Among different caries-preventive agents, fluoride is the most widely used and effective strategy, exerting its benefit primarily through topical action, promoting remineralization, reducing demineralization, and inhibiting bacterial metabolism.3,4 The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry and other professional organizations strongly recommend brushing twice daily with a fluoridated toothpaste with fluoride in the range of 1000–1500 ppm.5 However, toothbrushing in preschool children is highly dependent on parental supervision and may be inconsistent, limiting its protective effect.6 To overcome this, the use of professionally applied fluoride and other adjunctive strategies has been recommended.

Professionally applied fluoride varnishes are highly recommended, as they offer distinct advantages for young children. They deliver high concentrations of fluoride directly to tooth surfaces, are quick to apply, are moisture-tolerant, are safe, and are well accepted.4,7,8 Varnishes form a thin film that hardens on contact with saliva, allowing slow release of fluoride over several hours, thereby extending its protective action.8 Clinical trials and systematic reviews have consistently shown fluoride varnish to be effective in reducing caries incidence in primary teeth, particularly in high-risk groups.9,10

However, sometimes the use of fluoride as the only agent for caries prevention does not provide a complete solution, since the formation of fluoride reservoirs and the remineralization potential of saliva are limited by the availability of calcium and phosphate ions.11 For such situations, the use of other adjuncts is recommended. Hence, in addition to fluoride, other products like xylitol, casein phosphopeptide–amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP), and other calcium- and phosphate-based products are being added to enhance the effect of fluoride varnishes.12,13

The addition of CPP-ACP to fluoride varnish is known to increase the acid resistance of primary enamel as compared to fluoride varnish alone.14–16 It acts as a reservoir of bioavailable calcium and phosphate ions, promoting enamel repair and increasing acid resistance. Secondly, xylitol, a nonfermentable sugar alcohol that inhibits mutans streptococci and reduces plaque acidogenicity, is also known to contribute to caries prevention.8,13,17 The biological mechanism of action of xylitol in preventing dental caries is similar to other sugar alcohols, as these compounds are not readily metabolized by cariogenic micro-organisms.18 While each has shown promising effect individually, direct comparisons between such varnishes and standard home-based fluoride toothpaste regimens for ECC prevention remain limited.

Therefore, the present study was planned with the aim to compare and evaluate the clinical effectiveness of CPP-ACP and xylitol varnishes vs fluoride toothpaste in preventing dental caries, assessed using the decayed, missing, and filled surfaces in primary teeth/permanent teeth (dmfs/DMFS) index.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was a three-arm parallel randomized controlled trial done to compare and evaluate the clinical effectiveness of two varnishes, the first containing fluoride and CPP-ACP and the other containing fluoride and xylitol, along with the use of fluoride toothpaste in preventing dental caries as assessed using the dmfs/DMFS index. The third group included the use of brushing twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, without the use of any varnish. This was a unicentric, assessor-blinded, superiority trial with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio, conducted among 3–6-year-old children studying in kindergarten schools. The patient recruitment and treatment were done from December 2023 to December 2024.

Study Subjects

Ethical approval was sought from the Institutional Review Board of DAV(C) Dental College and Hospital, Yamunanagar, and from the Review Board of Pandit Bhagwat Dayal Sharma University of Health Sciences, Rohtak (Ref. no. F\IEC\23\0295). Patient screening was initiated only after due approvals were granted. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the committees and is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Children for the study were selected from kindergarten schools in the Yamunanagar district. Before this, consent was obtained from the school authorities, and then a letter of intent was sent to the parents to request their consent too. The children were recruited only after signed consent forms were received from their parents. In addition to that, the children were screened using the following inclusion and exclusion criteria:

Patients who fulfilled these criteria and whose parents gave informed consent were finally selected. Assent from children was also sought, followed by randomization of patients into three different groups.

Sample size was based on the primary outcome, which included the change in mean DMFS score. A preset type I error of 5% (p < 0.05 as significant) and a power of the study of 80% (type II error being 20%) were assumed for sample size determination. Accordingly, a total of 60 participants were included in the study.

Initially their baseline factors were recorded, which included demographic characteristics and caries statuses using dmfs/DMFS scores. Their randomization was carried out for this. Research Randomizer software was used for random sequence generation of three groups, and the “sealed opaque envelope technique” was used for allocation concealment.

Further, due to apparent differences in the interventions, neither the patients nor the operator could be blinded. However, the assessor for the indices was blinded. In addition to this, the statistician was also blinded, as he was informed about the group number assigned to the group and was unaware of the treatment techniques used in each group.

Group I included children who were provided with a varnish containing fluoride and xylitol (Enamelast, Ultradent, United States). Group II included children who were provided with a varnish containing fluoride and CPP-ACP (MI VarnishTM, GC America, Japan). Participants in both these groups were also advised to brush their teeth twice daily using a fluoridated toothpaste. Children in group III were advised to brush their teeth twice daily using fluoridated toothpaste and were not provided with any brush or other adjunct treatment.

The Clinical Procedures Are as Follows

Procedure for Application of Enamelast Varnish in Group I

Enamelast Varnish was applied to group I patients according to the manufacturer's instructions. Before placement, teeth were cleaned with gauze and dried using a chip blower. A thin layer of varnish was then applied to all tooth surfaces with a brush applicator (Fig. 1). Patients were instructed to avoid brushing, flossing, and consuming hot foods or drinks for 4–6 hours, preferably until the next morning. They were also advised to brush twice daily with fluoridated toothpaste. The application of Enamelast was repeated at the 6-month recall (Fig. 2), and group I patients were additionally instructed to use fluoride toothpaste, similar to group III.

Procedure for Tooth Brushing with Fluoridated Toothpaste in Group III

Group III patients were instructed to brush their teeth twice daily with 1000 ppm fluoridated toothpaste (Fig. 5). They were educated about its benefits and guided on the appropriate amount to use according to age (Fig. 6). As noted earlier, this instruction was also given to group I and group II patients.

All patients were recalled at their 6th month and 12th month for their follow-up visits. At each recall, dmfs/DMFS scores were recorded, and subsequently the changes in dmfs/DMFS were evaluated. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 16. Intergroup comparisons at each time point were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction. Intragroup changes were assessed using linear trend analysis.

To assess the rate of change in caries status over time, linear regression models were fitted for each group, with time (in months) as the independent variable and mean dmfs/DMFS score as the dependent variable.

The steeper slope observed in group III (β = 0.12), compared to groups I and II, indicated a faster rate of increase in dmfs scores over time, suggesting that groups I and II achieved better outcomes in maintaining lower dmfs scores (Table 3). Discussion

Dental caries in preschool children is a major global health concern, with early-onset disease leading to pain, functional impairment, and reduced quality of life. Preventive interventions during this period are critical, as lifelong oral health habits are being established. The present randomized controlled trial compared the effectiveness of Enamelast™ fluoride varnish, MI Varnish™ containing CPP-ACP, and twice-daily brushing with fluoridated toothpaste among children aged 3–6 years.

At 12 months, both varnish groups demonstrated significantly lower dmfs/DMFS increments compared with brushing alone. Intergroup analysis confirmed that both varnish groups performed better than brushing only, while no statistically significant difference was observed between Enamelast™ and MI Varnish™. These findings are consistent with previous reviews showing that professional varnish applications reduce caries progression more effectively than brushing alone.16,19,20

The linear trend analysis provided additional insights. Group III (brushing only) showed the steepest slope (β = 0.12), reflecting the fastest caries progression,16,20 while group II (MI Varnish™ with CPP-ACP) showed the least slope (β = 0.025), indicating the slowest progression. This aligns with studies reporting that CPP-ACP varnishes reduce lesion advancement by maintaining sustained calcium and phosphate ion availability at lesion sites.21 Group I (Enamelast™) also exhibited slower progression compared with brushing alone (β = 0.08), supporting earlier findings that fluoride varnish prolongs fluoride availability and enhances caries prevention.22

The brushing-only group demonstrated greater increments, likely due to variability in brushing frequency, parental supervision, and technique. This also indicates that other adjuncts also play a vital role. Similar observations were reported by Munteanu et al.,23 who concluded that brushing with fluoridated toothpaste, while essential, may be insufficient in high-risk children without professional adjuncts.

Our findings support earlier studies showing that professional varnish application consistently reduces caries incidence in young children.24 Although both varnishes were comparably effective in this trial, the least slope in the CPP-ACP group suggests potential incremental benefit, echoing previous evidence of superior lesion stabilization with CPP-ACP-containing varnishes.25

The role of xylitol varnishes in reducing bacterial activity and enhancing fluoride effectiveness has also been demonstrated in vitro and clinical studies,23 supporting the favorable outcomes observed in group I.

The strengths of this study include its randomized controlled design, allocation concealment, use of validated indices, and a 12-month follow-up that allowed observation of meaningful changes in caries progression. Limitations include the modest sample size (n = 60), single-institution setting, and reliance on parental reporting for brushing practices. Larger multicenter studies with longer observation are warranted to confirm sustained effectiveness.

Overall, this study reinforces the value of fluoride varnishes, particularly when combined with adjunctive remineralizing agents, in preschool caries prevention.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that both Enamelast™ fluoride varnish and MI Varnish™ provided superior protection against caries progression compared with brushing alone. Although both varnishes were similarly effective, MI Varnish™ demonstrated the slowest caries progression over time. These findings support the inclusion of CPP-ACP in fluoride varnish as effective, particularly for children at high risk of ECC.

Clinical Significance

Fluoride varnishes containing CPP-ACP or xylitol were more effective than brushing with fluoridated toothpaste alone in preventing ECC. MI Varnish™ (fluoride + CPP-ACP) demonstrated the slowest caries progression, highlighting the added benefit of remineralizing agents. Professional varnish applications, combined with daily brushing, provide superior protection for high-risk preschool children.
