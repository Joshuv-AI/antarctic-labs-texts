Surgical repositioning of a SING IMT following haptic dislocation: Restoration of alignment and visual function

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a common progressive disorder of the macula characterized by drusen, pigmentary changes, and, in its late stages by either geographic atrophy or disciform scarring.1 It is the leading cause of permanent central vision loss in developed countries, and, in the European Union, the number of people living with late-stage AMD is projected to climb to 12 million by 2050.1,2 Loss of central acuity significantly impairs high-resolution tasks like reading, face recognition, driving, computer use, etc., thereby reducing quality of life.3

Pharmacological treatment options remain limited: intravitreal anti-VEGF agents are effective only in neovascular AMD, and recently approved complement inhibitors (pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol) reduce the area of atrophy but their visual benefit is very modest.4 As a result, many late-stage AMD patients rely on low vision assistance (e.g., spectacles, magnifiers, telescopes) to cope with daily life. However, current external devices can be difficult to use as they require continuous head movement, which can provoke vestibular discomfort.5

The Smaller-Incision New-Generation Implantable Miniature Telescope (SING IMT™; SAMSARA VISION, Inc.) is a foldable, preloaded telescopic IOL designed to help patients with late-stage AMD improve their central vision. Based on a Galilean telescope principle, the device provides a x2.7 magnification, relocating the central image onto the healthier peripheral retina.5 The SING IMT is inserted through an 8 mm incision into the capsular bag, where three flexible silicone haptic wings unfold to secure the device in situ. Given the telescopic optics, optimal visual performance is highly dependent on optimal device centration; even modest tilt can impair its benefit. This report describes a case of successful surgical repositioning of a severely tilted SING IMT following haptic displacement out of the capsular bag.

Case report

Clinical findings at baseline - screening visit

A 77-year-old Caucasian man was referred to our tertiary eye center for assessment of SING IMT implantation in the context of bilateral late-stage AMD with central geographic atrophy. He had no history of ocular trauma, intra-ocular surgery, glaucoma, or corneal disease, and was otherwise systemically healthy. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy showed clear corneas, deep anterior chambers, and corticonuclear cataracts. Fundoscopy revealed healthy appearing optic nerves, geographic atrophy at the level of the macula, and normal peripheral retinas.

Corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) measured logMAR 0.96 (Snellen 20/200) in the right eye (OD) and logMAR 1.36 (Snellen 20/500) in the left eye (OS). An External Telescope Simulator (ETS) was used to evaluate the potential impact of implanting the device and selecting the responsive eye. With this, acuity improved to logMAR 0.52 in OD and logMAR 0.76 in OS; hence selection of OD for device implantation. A preoperative screening simulation helped the patient understand the expected optical benefits and functional outcomes of the device's implantation, supporting their informed decision to proceed with surgery.

Preoperative measurements of the right eye met all eligibility criteria for implantation: endothelial cell density was 2567 cells/mm2 (minimum requirement of 1600 cells/mm2), axial length measured 22.59 mm (minimum requirement of 21 mm), and anterior chamber depth was 2.86 mm (minimum requirement of 2.5 mm).

SING IMT implantation

The primary procedure was performed under local anesthesia and under strict aseptic conditions. Due to the SING IMT's large dimensions (haptic diameter of 10.8 mm, optic diameter of 3.6 mm, and optic axial length of 4.4 mm), a slightly oversized capsulorhexis (estimated at 6.5 mm in this case) was created, followed by routine phacoemulsification (Fig. 1A). The main wound was then enlarged to 8 mm using a crescent knife (Fig. 1B), after which the Tsert SI preloaded injector was introduced into the anterior chamber under gentle corneal elevation with forceps (Fig. 1C). The SING IMT was slowly injected and all haptics were placed inside the capsular bag. The main incision was closed with 10-0 Nylon sutures, using a central limited running suture supplemented by a single interrupted suture on one side (Fig. 1D). A standard regimen of topical tobramycine 3 mg/1 ml, dexamethasone 1 mg/1 ml (Tobradex; four times daily, tapering with one drop weekly) and indomethacine 1 mg/1 ml (Indocollyre; three times daily for four weeks) was prescribed.

Postoperative follow-up

Postoperative day 1 and week 1 follow-up visits were performed to monitor for acute complications, none of which were observed. In line with other patients, vision remained low during the immediate postoperative period. Usually, this is largely attributed to the highly induced astigmatism by the sutures on the large corneal wound and vision starts to climb up slowly once the sutures are removed and rehabilitation sessions start taking place. At 4 weeks after surgery vision remained lower than baseline (logMAR1.4 – 1.6, though difficult to measure accurately) and a mild device tilt, estimated to be less than 2.5°, was noted on slit-lamp examination. We elected to take a conservative approach and re-evaluate the patient in 2 weeks. At follow-up, the device tilt had progressed and was estimated to exceed 5°. The patient reported that the observed image was not in line with his direction of gaze and also noted monocular diplopia. As such, a dilated examination was done which revealed a dislocation of the inferior haptic out of the capsular bag (Fig. 2). Anterior segment OCT (AS-OCT) (CASIA2, Tomey Corp.) confirmed the device's incorrect position (Fig. 3A). Throughout this period, intraocular pressure remained within normal limits, no signs of intraocular inflammation were observed, and the patient did not report ocular pain.

After consulting with other surgeons regarding management strategies, we confirmed the need for surgical repositioning. However, due to logistical factors related to patient travel, the procedure could only be performed four weeks later (ten weeks after implantation surgery).

SING IMT repositioning

The secondary intervention was performed ten weeks after implantation. A 2.8 mm incision was made superiorly (at the site of the previous main port) and two additional 1.2 mm paracenteses were created at the 3- and 9-o'clock meridians to permit bimanual manipulation. Pharmacologic dilation was achieved by intracameral injection of tropicamide 0.04 mg, phenylephrine 0.62 mg, and lidocaine 2 mg in 0.2 mL (Mydrane). After obtaining proper visualization and confirming the displacement of the inferior haptic out of the capsular bag (Fig. 4A), the anterior chamber was filled with a cohesive ophthalmic viscoelastic device (OVD) (Provisc). After placing a push-pull hook behind the edge of the optic, against the base of the displaced haptic, a controlled superior pull allowed the silicone haptic to snap back into the capsular bag (Fig. 4B). The device was then gently pushed back until complete centralization was achieved and carefully rotated to confirm proper positioning of all haptics (Fig. 4C) The surgical procedure is shown in Video 1.

Residual OVD was removed with irrigation/aspiration. Intracameral cefuroxim (Aprokam) was administered, and all wounds were sealed with stromal hydration. No intra-operative complications occurred. Correct centration was reconfirmed at the slit-lamp a few hours postoperatively.

Post-repositioning follow-up

Follow-up after repositioning showed no complications up to 6 months after implantation surgery. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy showed a device that was centered without any observable tilt, which was confirmed with AS-OCT (Fig. 3B). CDVA improved to logMAR 0.52 (Snellen 20/63), matching the preoperative ETS prediction. The patient's observed image was now equal to his line of sight and there was no monocular diplopia. Endothelial cell density remained stable at 2496 cells/mm2 proving safety of the device's implantation, even when necessitating a secondary intervention.

Discussion

A 6-month follow-up study after SING IMT implantation revealed implant dislocation to occur in only 2.9% of patients (1 out of 35 cases).6 Nonetheless, the large size of the optic, the large 8 mm corneal incision, and the reliance on three large silicone haptics for in-the-bag fixation could introduce specific mechanical vulnerabilities. This case illustrates that clinically significant tilt of a telescopic device caused by the escape of a single haptic from the capsular bag can be corrected safely through a secondary procedure, allowing the patient to fully recover the predicted benefit of the device.

We hypothesize that several factors may contribute to secondary haptic disengagement. First, although intentionally made larger for this device, an anterior capsulorhexis exceeding 6.0 mm may inadequately overlap the optic (as was the case in this patient), reducing haptic stability and increasing the risk of dislocation. Notably, current guidelines recommend creating a capsulorhexis between 5.5 and 6.0 mm to ensure proper optic coverage and device stability. Second, capsular contraction in the postoperative period can exert forces that displace a flexible haptic out of its position, again particularly when the capsulorhexis is large. Lastly, postoperative behaviors, such as eye-rubbing may further amplify these forces. The procedure is inherently more intricate than standard IOL implantation, and subtle haptic misalignment may occasionally go unnoticed at the end of surgery. The lesson from our experience is that even subtle early tilt, abnormal visual complaints, or extremely low vision should prompt close observation because progressive decentration can evolve over a short period of time. Once clinically significant tilt is confirmed, we suggest proceeding with repositioning as soon as it is surgically safe, typically after wound healing has stabilized. Furthermore, the absence of corneal compromise despite secondary intervention supports the growing evidence that the smaller incision and preloaded delivery system (TSERT SI) reduce endothelial harm. Long-term follow-up is, however, still warranted.

Conclusions

In summary, haptic dislocation with consequent tilt represents an infrequent but visually disabling complication after SING IMT implantation. Early diagnosis supported by AS-OCT and prompt repositioning can restore optical alignment and visual acuity without incurring additional (endothelial) harm. Surgeons should maintain a high index of suspicion in the early postoperative period and be prepared to intervene before more complex procedures become necessary.
