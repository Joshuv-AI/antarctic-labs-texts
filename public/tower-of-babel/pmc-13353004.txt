Multi-omics profiling of the diabetic human heart reveals coupled dysregulation in lipid metabolism, mitophagy, and extracellular matrix remodeling
Authors: Qiuhan Lu, Shulin Tang, Sijia Fang, Yuwen Wu, Liang Chen, Mintong Liang, Jiaqi Chen, Pengju Wen, Leigang Jin, Jianshe Yu, Feng Jiao, Yueheng Wu, Guozhi Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) is a major complication of type 2 diabetes whose molecular basis in human hearts remains poorly understood. This study aimed to define the multi-omics landscape of DbCM in the human myocardium.MethodsWe performed integrated transcriptomic, 4D-DIA proteomic, and full-spectrum widely targeted metabolomic analysis on left ventricular tissues from matched Chinese cohorts: DbCM (n = 11), non-diabetic cardiomyopathy (n = 11), and healthy donors (n = 4). Key findings were validated by histological assessment and western blotting of candidate proteins. Background

The hyperglycemia and insulin resistance that characterize type 2 diabetes (T2D) are key drivers of pathological remodeling in the myocardium, adversely affecting its structure and function both independently and in concert with other cardiovascular diseases [1, 2]. The escalating global prevalence of T2D is particularly concerning, given that individuals with T2D exhibit a 2- to 5-fold higher risk of developing heart failure than individuals without diabetes [3, 4]. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which are established pathological drivers in T2D, promote microcirculatory dysfunction and accelerate the progression of cardiovascular disease [1, 5]. Among cardiovascular complications, diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) traditionally defined as myocardial dysfunction in patients with diabetes in the absence of other cardiovascular diseases [6], has been increasingly redefined in recent consensus statements. It is now defined as myocardial structural and/or functional abnormalities occurring in the context of diabetes, where diabetes may act as a contributing factor even in the presence of comorbid cardiovascular conditions, and affects at least 12% of patients with T2D [7–9]. Recent clinical studies further emphasize that DbCM is prevalent yet often underdiagnosed, with early structural and functional abnormalities detectable before overt heart failure develops [10]. Despite these detectable early changes, the insidious onset and progression of DbCM often lead to delayed diagnoses and interventions, thus further aggravating the clinical burden of the disease [5, 11, 12].

The prevention and management of cardiac dysfunction in T2D remains a major clinical challenge, prompting the emergence of diabetocardiology [2, 13]. Despite its growing recognition, the molecular mechanisms underlying diabetes-associated myocardial injury remain poorly understood, largely because of the limited availability of human heart tissue for detailed molecular study. Recent investigations underscore these challenges. For example, Hahn et al. integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses in a heart failure cohort, revealing widespread impairments in myocardial metabolic substrate utilization, particularly in patients with obesity and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [14, 15]. Recently, Shu et al. reported that diabetes accelerates heart failure progression and identified the accumulation of long-chain acyl-coenzyme A as a metabolic hallmark linked to more rapid progression of diabetic cardiomyopathy [16]. Complementary studies further implicate immune dysregulation, altered fuel metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, fibrosis, and vascular dysfunction in DbCM pathogenesis [17–20]. Nevertheless, these efforts have not yet yielded an integrated multi-omics perspective of the human DbCM heart, leaving its comprehensive molecular landscape largely unexplored. Likewise, biomarker discovery efforts based on transcriptomic analyses or peripheral blood data remain limited in their ability to capture tissue-level molecular complexity [21].

Although rodent models are experimentally accessible and have provided important mechanistic insights, including roles for oxidative stress, metabolic signaling, and mitochondrial regulation [22–24], they often fail to fully recapitulate the complexity of human cardiac pathophysiology, limiting translational relevance. Similarly, blood-based omics, while enabling convenient longitudinal monitoring, lacks the structural resolution to directly reflect tissue-level changes. Given the multifactorial pathogenesis of DbCM, integrating multi-omics data from human heart tissue is essential for a comprehensive understanding of disease mechanisms, molecular interactions, and temporal progression.

To this end, we conducted an integrated multi-omics analysis of left ventricular tissues from a large Chinese human heart transplantation cohort, including matched patients with DbCM, non-diabetic cardiomyopathy (CM), and healthy donors. By leveraging transcriptomic, 4D-DIA proteomic, and full-spectrum widely targeted metabolomic profiling, this study seeks to establish a comprehensive molecular map of DbCM, thereby yielding critical insights into the mechanisms of diabetes-driven cardiac dysfunction.

Methods

Human heart tissue collection

Left ventricular tissues were obtained from a large human heart transplantation cohort comprising 210 Chinese patients diagnosed with cardiomyopathy who received cardiac transplantation at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Guangdong, China), from August 2017 to April 2022 [25]. Patients with a confirmed diagnosis of T2D were classified as having diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM group, n = 11), whereas patients without diabetes were assigned to the cardiomyopathy without diabetes group (non-diabetic CM group, n = 11). The two groups were matched for age (± 4 years) and sex in a case-control study. In addition, healthy myocardial tissues (healthy group, n = 4) were obtained from donor hearts. All excised heart tissues were immediately flash-frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen until further analysis. This study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (GDREC2016255H). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (Informed consent form No. 20160628).

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from frozen heart tissues using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen) following the protocol of the manufacturer. RNA quality and integrity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). RNA sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, E7420) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Clean reads were generated by removing adapter sequences, poly-N stretches, and low-quality reads. High-quality paired-end reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38, release 109) using Hisat2 (v2.0.5), achieving mapping rates exceeding 96%. Gene expression was quantified using StringTie (v1.3.3b), and genes absent in more than one-third of the samples were excluded. Detailed processing steps were provided in Additional file 1: Appendix S1, and the corresponding transcriptomic dataset is publicly available through NGDC [26].

4D-DIA proteomics processing

Quantitative proteomic analysis was performed using 4D data-independent acquisition (4D-DIA). Raw mass spectrometry data were processed with DIA-NN (v1.8.1) in a library-free mode. A spectral library was generated using the Homo sapiens SwissProt database (20,376 entries) via deep neural network prediction, and refined using match-between-runs strategies. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at < 1% at both the protein and precursor ion levels. Protein intensities were median-normalized across samples. Proteins missing in more than one-third of the samples were excluded, and missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) method. Full processing details were provided in Additional file 1: Appendix S1 [27].

Metabolite extraction and full-spectrum widely targeted metabolomics data processing

Both hydrophilic and hydrophobic metabolites from frozen myocardial tissue were extracted to maximize the coverage. The raw data were processed using MultiQuant (v3.0) for chromatographic peak integration and calibration. Metabolite intensities were quantified based on peak areas. Metabolites missing in more than one-third of the samples were excluded, and missing values were imputed using the KNN method. Quality control was performed using pooled quality control samples spiked with internal standards. Metabolite identification was based on public and in-house databases, including the Human Metabolite Database and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). Further details were provided in Additional file 1: Appendix S1 [27].

Omics data processing and differential analysis

The transcriptomic read counts were normalized to the number of transcripts per million. The metabolomic and proteomic intensity matrices were log2-transformed prior to analysis. Principal component analysis and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were used to visualize group separation across the datasets.

Differential expression thresholds for statistical significance and effect size were adapted based on biological priors, platform-specific sensitivities, and inherent molecular properties [28, 29]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with FDR adjustments using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. DEGs were defined as follows: (1) DbCM vs. healthy and non-diabetic CM vs. healthy, FDR < 0.1 and |log2 fold change| > 0.5; (2) DbCM vs. non-diabetic CM, |log2 fold change| > 0.5 and P < 0.05, adjusted for age, sex, and cardiomyopathy type. Limma was used to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) across the groups. For DbCM vs. healthy or non-diabetic CM vs. healthy, proteins and metabolites with P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 0.5 were classified as upregulated or downregulated DEPs/DEMs, respectively. For DbCM vs. non-diabetic CM, proteins with P < 0.05, adjusted for age, sex, and type of cardiomyopathy, and metabolites with |log2 fold change| > 0.5 and P < 0.05, adjusted for age, sex, and type of cardiomyopathy, were considered DEPs and DEMs for subsequent analysis.

Bidirectional orthogonal partial least squares analysis was used to assess inter-omics associations among DEGs, DEPs, and DEMs [30]. Multi-omics integration was performed using multi-omics factor analysis across omics profiles of samples [31]. To account for differences in sensitivity and dynamic range across omics platforms, analyses were first performed independently within each dataset following appropriate normalization. Cross-omics integration was conducted using standardized data at the pathway or latent factor level (e.g., O2PLS and MOFA), avoiding direct comparison of absolute molecular abundances.

Functional and pathway analysis

To investigate mitochondrial function, MitoCarta3.0 was used to identify mitochondrial-related DEGs (MitoDEGs) and DEPs (MitoDEPs) [32]. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed on the transcriptome using KEGG pathway annotations and the ClusterProfiler package. Proteomic functional analysis was conducted using Metascape [33]. Joint pathway enrichment of metabolomics with proteomic data was analyzed using MetaboAnalyst [34]. Two-dimensional annotation analysis using Perseus was employed to compare the consistency of KEGG pathway changes in the transcriptome and proteome [35]. Immune and stromal cell infiltration was quantified using CIBERSORT (based on the LM22 leukocyte signature matrix derived from 22 purified human immune cell subsets using cell type–specific gene expression profiles) and xCell [36, 37], in line with a previous study [38]. Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed based on STRING database predictions. The Mouse Genome Informatics database was used to investigate the phenotypic associations of candidate genes with cardiovascular or metabolic traits [39]. The discriminatory performance of key molecules distinguishing DbCM from non-diabetic CM was evaluated using the area under the curve.

External validation of transcriptomic findings

External validation was performed by analyzing the publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus dataset GSE26887, which included transcriptomic profiles of the left ventricular myocardium from 19 patients with heart failure (seven with diabetes, 12 without) and five controls without heart failure [40]. Functional and immune cell infiltration analyses were repeated for cross-validation. The analysis pipeline applied to the external dataset was consistent with that used in the primary transcriptomic analysis. Validations were performed by comparing differentially expressed genes and conducting gene set enrichment analysis, respectively. Immune and stromal cell infiltration analyses were repeated using the same deconvolution methods for cross-validation.

Histological assessments

Left ventricular tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. Following fixation, tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the adjacent sections were stained with Masson’s trichrome to visualize collagen deposition associated with fibrosis. Images were captured using an Eclipse E100 microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Fibrotic areas were quantified using Fiji software by calculating the ratio of the blue-stained (collagen) area to the total tissue area.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted by centrifuging tissue lysates at 1,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (~ 30 µg) were separated via 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto 0.45-µm nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 for 2 h at room temperature. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the indicated proteins, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (GE Healthcare) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Densitometric quantification of the western blot bands was performed using ImageJ software. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-ACSL1 (4047; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-BNIP3L (12396; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-FASN (3180; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-LC3A/B (12741; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-MMP2 (ab92536; Abcam), anti-MMP9 (ab76003; Abcam), and anti-COL5A1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-133162). Full-length, uncropped blots are shown in Additional file 1:figure S5.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.2), and analysis code is available online [41]. Clinical characteristics were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A two-sided significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied.

Results

Overview of patient clinical and multi-omics characteristics

Left ventricular tissue samples were collected from 22 patients (11 with DbCM and 11 with matched non-diabetic CM; 81.8% males) and four healthy donors. The mean ages of patients in the DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups were 57.2 ± 10.2 years and 56.2 ± 8.9 years, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S1). Compared with non-diabetic CM patients, DbCM patients had a higher percentage of oral anti-hyperglycemia drugs or insulin use. The two groups of patients had similar age, body mass index (BMI), levels of blood pressure and left ventricular ejection fraction (Additional file 1: Table S1).

After quality control and filtration, 15,788 genes, 6,459 proteins, and 4,116 metabolites were retained for multi-omics analysis to characterize the molecular compositions of the human heart tissues. One sample from the non-diabetic CM group was identified as an outlier in the principal component analysis and was subsequently excluded (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) revealed discriminatory multi-omics signatures that distinguished the three groups at the transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic levels (Fig. 1A-C).

Transcriptome landscape

Compared with the healthy group, 457 (182 up, 275 down) and 590 (279 up, 311 down) genes were dysregulated in the DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups, respectively, whereas 390 (295 up, 95 down) DEGs were identified between the two disease groups (Fig. 1D, E). Gene set enrichment analysis based on the KEGG database revealed activation of the Wnt signaling pathway and protein digestion and absorption in both disease groups compared to healthy controls, whereas pathways associated with immune regulation (e.g. B cell receptor signaling pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway) and inflammation (e.g. TNF signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway) were downregulated, suggesting a shared dysregulation of biological processes in DbCM and non-diabetic CM (Fig. 1F, Discovery part). Further comparison between the two disease groups revealed the activation of pathways related to the immune response (e.g. NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, B cell receptor signaling pathway), inflammation (e.g. TNF signaling pathway, nuclear factor-κB signaling pathway), and lipid metabolism (e.g. Lipid and atherosclerosis) in the DbCM group (Fig. 1F, Discovery part). Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) analysis revealed a significantly elevated proportion of M1 macrophages in the DbCM group compared to the non-diabetic CM group, while both disease groups showed a decreasing trend relative to healthy controls (Fig. 1G). In line with this, xCell analysis demonstrated a consistent reduction in M1 macrophages in the disease groups, corroborating the CIBERSORT findings (Fig. 1H).

With adjustment for age, sex, and type of cardiomyopathy, interleukin 1 beta (IL1B) (log2FC = 1.91, P = 0.003), interleukin 6 (IL6) (log2FC = 1.87, P = 0.013), and TNF (log2FC = 1.06, P = 0.046) were significantly upregulated in the transcriptomes of patients with DbCM compared to those with non-diabetic CM (Fig. 1I-K). Notably, tryptophan levels were significantly reduced in both DbCM (log2FC = -1.21, P = 0.006) and non-diabetic CM groups (log2FC = -1.04, P = 0.018) compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1L). Furthermore, kynurenine levels were elevated in DbCM versus non-diabetic CM (log2FC = 0.75, P = 0.002) (Fig. 1M), resulting in a higher tryptophan-to-kynurenine ratio in DbCM (Fig. 1N). These changes thereby support the finding that inflammation-related pathways are more activated in DbCM hearts.

External validation of the transcriptome landscape

External validation using data from Greco et al. [40] confirmed the downregulation of immune response and inflammation pathways in both disease groups, including B cell receptor signaling pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 1F, Validation part). Moreover, the validation data specifically linked diabetes to the activation of transforming growth factor (TGF)-β signaling, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling, and nuclear factor-κB signaling pathways in the DbCM hearts (Fig. 1F, Validation part). The xCell analysis further confirmed a consistent reduction in M1 macrophages in both disease groups compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1O).

Proteome profiling

Transcript expression showed a significant correlation with protein levels (r = 0.334, P < 0.001). Analysis of DEPs revealed substantial proteomic alterations in both disease groups compared to healthy controls. Specifically, 236 (181 upregulated, 55 downregulated) and 273 (209 upregulated, 64 downregulated) DEPs were identified in DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups, respectively (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, 176 proteins (86 upregulated, 90 downregulated) were differentially expressed between the two disease groups after adjusting for age, sex, and cardiomyopathy (Fig. 2A, B). The upregulated DEPs in both the DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups were involved in the extracellular matrix (ECM) and fiber organization, as well as in the activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) (Fig. 2C). In contrast, DEPs distinguishing DbCM from non-diabetic CM showed enrichment in pathways related to acylglycerol metabolism and lipid catabolism, while lipid biosynthetic processes were downregulated (Fig. 2D), indicating marked disruptions in lipid metabolic regulation. Of note, STEAP3 metalloreductase was downregulated in patients with DbCM relative to healthy controls, with substantial reductions in both mRNA (log2FC = -0.87, P = 0.003; Fig. 2E) and protein levels (log2FC = -0.51, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2F).

Cardiac fibrosis remodeling

Myocardial fibrosis is a hallmark of cardiomyopathy. Two key alterations were identified in the disease groups compared to healthy controls: activation of fibrosis initiation pathways (Wnt and TGF-β; Fig. 1F) and upregulation of pathways linked to ECM deposition and MMP activation (Fig. 2C). ECM-receptor interactions were consistently upregulated at the protein level in both disease groups compared to healthy controls (Fig. 2G, Additional file 1: Table S2, S3), suggesting active fibrotic remodeling. Notably, the gelatinases MMP2 and MMP9, the primary proteases responsible for degrading ECM proteins, were significantly elevated in both disease groups compared to healthy controls. Specifically, the levels of MMP2 and MMP9 were increased in the DbCM (log2FC = 0.58, P = 0.027 for MMP2; log2FC = 1.24, P = 0.034 for MMP9; Fig. 2H, I) and non-diabetic CM groups (log2FC = 0.56, P = 0.014 for MMP2; log2FC = 1.17, P = 0.047 for MMP9; Fig. 2H, I). Furthermore, the tissue inhibitors of MMP2 (TIMP2) were also upregulated in both disease groups (log2FC = 0.51, P = 0.036 for DbCM; log2FC = 0.59, P = 0.017 for non-diabetic CM; Fig. 2J).

Interestingly, a less pronounced increase in collagen levels was observed in patients with DbCM compared to those with non-diabetic CM (Fig. 2K). Moreover, two-dimensional annotation analysis of the transcriptomic and proteomic differences between the two disease groups revealed a reduction in ECM-receptor interactions in patients with DbCM (Fig. 2L). The protein–protein interaction network of DEPs between DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups revealed strong interactions among collagen type V alpha 1 chain (COL5A1) (log2FC = -0.62, P = 0.043), collagen type V alpha 2 chain (COL5A2) (log2FC = -0.69, P = 0.042), and fibrillin 1 (FBN1) (log2FC = -0.32, P = 0.030), all of which were downregulated in DbCM (Fig. 3A-D). To validate the fibrosis-associated proteomic alterations, western blot analysis of heart tissues was performed. The levels of MMP2, MMP9, and COL5A1 were significantly decreased in patients with DbCM compared to those with non-diabetic CM (Fig. 3E). The extent of fibrosis, as quantified by histomorphometry, presented a lower trend in DbCM heart tissues relative to those from non-diabetic CM patients (Fig. 3F, G). In summary, diabetes is associated with impaired matrix repair capacity in patients with cardiomyopathy, and this impairment has been observed in conjunction with decreased cardiac compliance.

Metabolic alteration landscape

Metabolite levels were significantly altered, with 381 (267 upregulated, 114 downregulated) and 536 (407 upregulated, 129 downregulated) DEMs identified in the DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups compared to healthy controls, respectively (Fig. 4A, B). The primary classes of DEMs were glycerophospholipids, amino acids, and their metabolites (Fig. 4C). Two-dimensional annotation analysis revealed consistent upregulation of protein digestion and absorption pathways in both disease groups compared to the healthy controls in protein alterations (Fig. 2G), with the observed metabolic changes aligned with this finding. In the comparison between the DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups, 571 DEMs (300 upregulated, 271 downregulated) were identified after adjusting for age, sex, and type of cardiomyopathy (Fig. 3A). Most downregulated metabolites were triglycerides, whereas a considerable proportion of upregulated DEMs belonged to glycerophospholipids and fatty acyls (DbCM vs. non-diabetic CM in Fig. 4C).

Lipid accumulation patterns in DbCM group were significantly different from in non-diabetic CM and healthy groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). In patients with DbCM, intermediates of fatty acid oxidation (acylcarnitines; acyl-CARs) were upregulated, whereas triglyceride levels showed no significant changes (Fig. 4D). In contrast, non-diabetic CM group exhibited elevated triglycerides and oxidized lipids, alongside reduced levels of acyl-CARs (Fig. 4E). A comparison between DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups revealed that all triglycerides were significantly downregulated in the DbCM, whereas acyl-CARs, monoglycerides, and diglycerides were upregulated (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, glycerophospholipid metabolism was significantly enriched in the joint enrichment analysis of proteins and metabolites between the disease groups (Fig. 4G). When compared to the non-diabetic CM group, elevated levels of phosphatidylglycerophosphate synthase (PGS1) and lysophospholipase patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing 7 (PNPLA7) were also observed in the DbCM (Fig. 4H, I). Moreover, in the DbCM group, the DEMs of phosphatidylcholines, lysophosphatidylcholines, phosphatidylethanolamines, lysophosphatidylethanolamines, and phosphatidylglycerols were upregulated (Fig. 4J).

Increased utilization of triglyceride-derived fatty acids by the heart in DbCM

A healthy adult heart exhibits high metabolic adaptability and flexibility, allowing for efficient switching between different energy substrates to maintain energy supply. Metabolomic analysis revealed that fatty acid oxidation was reduced in patients with non-diabetic CM but increased in DbCM, as indicated by contrasting alterations in triglycerides, the source of fatty acids, and the oxidation intermediate acyl-CARs between the two groups (Fig. 4D, E). However, no significant differences were observed in the levels of proteins involved in fatty acid transport, such as CD36 and fatty acid-binding proteins, between the patient groups (data not shown). DEPs between the DbCM and non-diabetic CM groups were upregulated in acylglycerol metabolism, but downregulated in lipid biosynthesis (Fig. 2D). Fatty acid synthase (FASN), a key enzyme in the de novo synthesis of fatty acids, was significantly downregulated in patients with DbCM compared to that in patients with non-diabetic CM (log2FC = -0.60, P = 0.047) (Fig. 5A), suggesting that triglyceride breakdown is associated with the primary source of fatty acids for myocardial fatty acid oxidation in DbCM. Furthermore, acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 1 (ACSL1) levels were elevated in the DbCM group (log2FC = 0.32, P = 0.010; Fig. 5B), indicating increased oxidative utilization of fatty acids. In summary, elevated levels of fatty acids and acyl-CARs, coupled with reduced triglyceride levels in patients with DbCM compared to those with non-diabetic CM, indicate enhanced fatty acid oxidation in DbCM (Fig. 5C). The widespread decrease in triglyceride levels suggests an increased utilization of triglyceride in the hearts of patients with DbCM.

Mitophagy was impaired and related to fatty acid oxidation

The mitochondrial functions of DEGs and DEPs between the two disease groups were further examined, and 16 MitoDEGs and 25 MitoDEPs were identified in the MitoCarta3.0 dataset (Fig. 5D). Most of them (41% [n = 17]) were linked to metabolism (Fig. 4E), primarily lipid and amino acid metabolism. Additionally, 22% (n = 8) were involved in oxidative phosphorylation, and 15% (n = 6) were associated with mitochondrial dynamics and surveillance, including mitophagy (Fig. 5E). In the correlation network of MitoDEGs and MitoDEPs (Fig. 5F), the levels of B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2)-interacting protein 3-like (BNIP3L, also known as NIX) in patients showed a significant negative correlation with ACSL1 (rho = -0.708, P < 0.001; Fig. 5G) and a positive correlation with FASN (rho = 0.478, P = 0.030; Fig. 5H). The protein BNIP3L, a key mitophagy inducer, was significantly reduced in patients with DbCM compared to those with non-diabetic CM (log2FC = -0.30, P = 0.018; Fig. 5I). Mitophagy dysregulation is a key mechanism in various metabolic and cardiovascular diseases. When patients were stratified by median ACSL1 protein expression, those with high ACSL1 expression had significantly lower levels of BNIP3L (Fig. 5J). Metabolic correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between ACSL1 protein levels and acyl-CARs, whereas BNIP3L levels showed negative correlations with these lipids (Fig. 5K, Additional file 1: Table S4). Protein quantifications validated that the levels of ACSL1 were significantly increased in DbCM compared to the non-diabetic CM group (Fig. 5L). Additionally, protein levels of BNIP3L and microtubule-associated protein 1-light chain 3 (LC3A/B), which plays a central role in autophagosome formation, were significantly decreased in DbCM (Fig. 5L). These results suggest an association between BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy abnormalities and the presence of DbCM, which was significantly correlated with multiple aspects of fatty acid metabolic dysregulation, in contrast to patients with non-diabetic CM.

Multi-omics integration

A comprehensive, unbiased analysis of variability in multi-omics data is crucial for identifying diabetes-associated alterations and advancing our mechanistic understanding. Bidirectional orthogonal partial least squares models revealed cross-omics correlations, suggesting that the dysregulation of oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolic processes, and the cytoskeleton of muscle cells was significantly influenced by multiple omics factors (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A-E). By integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data from 21 patients via multi-omics factor analysis, we derived a five-factor model to explore clinical relevance. The model captured 48.2%, 13.0%, and 65.7% of the variance in each respective omics layer. (Fig. 6A). Factors 2 and factor 3, which were composed primarily of lipid-related features, exhibited a significant positive correlation with diabetes (rho = 0.457, P = 0.037 for Factor 2; rho = 0.535, P = 0.012 for Factor 3; Fig. 6B, C). Of note, ACSL1 was identified as the top-weighted feature in the protein profile of Factor 3 (Fig. 6C). Enrichment analysis of the top 20 proteins and genes, based on the absolute values of their weights, revealed that immune-related (e.g. positive regulation of immune response, Modulators of TCR signaling and T cell activation) and ECM-related (e.g. NABA CORE MATRISOME) pathways and lipid metabolism (e.g. long−chain fatty acid metabolic process) were strongly associated with DbCM (Fig. 6D).

Mouse mutation models for key proteins

Given the lack of direct in vivo validation, we analyzed genetically modified mouse models from the Mouse Genome Informatics database to investigate the physiological roles of the seven key proteins, including ACSL1, FASN, BNIP3L, COL5A1, COL5A2, FBN1, and STEAP3. To this end, we explored whether genetic alterations in these candidates are associated with DbCM phenotypes. Notably, transgenic models with ACSL1 overexpression exhibited relevant phenotypes, including cardiomyopathy, elevated cardiac muscle triglyceride levels, abnormal myocardial fiber morphology, and reduced heart ventricle muscle contractility (Additional file 1: Table S8). Knockout models for other genes displayed relevant phenotypes such as impaired mitophagy, altered left ventricular morphology, cardiac fibrosis, disrupted collagen fibril structure, and impaired glucose tolerance (Additional file 1: Table S8). Importantly, these proteins also served as effective potential therapeutic targets in distinguishing DbCM from non-diabetic CM in human patients. A classification model based on a panel of five proteins achieved an AUC of 0.927 (Additional file 1: Fig. S4).

Discussion

DbCM is a major cardiovascular complication in patients with diabetes. In this multi-omics study, we analyzed cardiac tissues from Chinese patients with cardiomyopathy (with and without diabetes) and healthy donors, to delineate the diabetes-associated pathophysiological alterations in the human heart. Our investigation yielded several principal findings. First, we identified distinct perturbations in lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function in patients with DbCM compared to those with non-diabetic CM. Second, hearts from DbCM patients exhibited enhanced utilization of triglyceride-derived fatty acids for energy production. Third, BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy appeared to be more severely impaired in DbCM, and this defect was correlated with dysregulated fatty acid oxidation, as evidenced by the inverse relationship between BNIP3L and key lipid-metabolic proteins such as ACSL1 and FASN. Finally, although myocardial fibrosis was evident in both disease groups, DbCM hearts exhibited a compromised matrix repair capacity, reflected by the specific downregulation of ECM components including COL5A1 protein. Collectively, these findings provide novel mechanistic insights into diabetes-associated cardiac dysfunction and highlight potential targets for future therapeutic strategies.

In cardiac conditions like cardiomyopathy and heart failure, the myocardium often reduces or maintains its reliance on fatty acid oxidation, given its relative inefficiency as an energy source [42]. However, patients with obesity or diabetes often exhibit increased fatty acid oxidation, driven by a hyperglycemic environment and insulin resistance [43]. Our study revealed a divergent myocardial metabolic profile in DbCM compared to non-diabetic CM, characterized by elevated fatty acid oxidation. Furthermore, DbCM hearts exhibited downregulated de novo fatty acid synthesis and a significant depletion of triglyceride reserves. Despite the upregulation of fatty acids, especially acyl-CARs, and acyl-CoA synthesis in DbCM hearts, no significant differences were observed in free fatty acid transporters. This suggests that the free fatty acids utilized in DbCM were primarily sourced from triglyceride breakdown. This process concomitantly produced monoglycerides and diglycerides, lipid species linked to lipotoxicity and impaired insulin signaling [5, 42]. DbCM is distinguished from non-diabetic CM by accelerated yet incomplete fatty acid oxidation. This phenotype is driven by upregulated ACSL1, which correlates with metabolic alterations in human DbCM hearts and is associated with loss of regenerative capacity [15, 44, 45]. Furthermore, acyl-CARs, which are the intermediates of incomplete fatty acid oxidation, serve as direct markers of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation dysfunction in DbCM [15, 29]. In summary, the DbCM heart is characterized by a metabolic shift defined by a heightened dependence on triglyceride breakdown for fatty acids and an associated increase in fatty acid oxidation for energy. This shift, however, occurs at the cost of generating lipotoxic intermediates and incomplete fatty acid oxidation products, which contribute to the pathogenesis of cellular dysfunction.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key characteristic of DbCM, leading to the mitophagy of damaged organelles. This process, which degrades structurally compromised mitochondria, occurs primarily via the PINK1-PRKN/PARKIN pathway and receptor-mediated pathways involving proteins such as BNIP3, BNIP3L, and FUNDC1 [11, 46, 47]. The accumulation of damaged mitochondria and lipids resulting from impaired mitophagy exacerbates DbCM [48]. Corroborating this, we observed a significant reduction in BNIP3L protein levels in DbCM patients compared to those with non-diabetic CM. A similar downregulation of BNIP3L has been documented in heart failure patients with ischemic or dilated cardiomyopathy compared to healthy controls, and inhibition of BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy in adult cardiac progenitor cells leads to mitochondrial fission and the formation of damaged mitochondria [49, 50]. Furthermore, the activation of mitophagy protects against high-fat diet-induced DbCM, whereas specific BNIP3L activation can shield retinal pigment epithelial cells from high glucose-induced injury [51, 52]. These findings suggest that diabetes is associated with exacerbated mitophagy defects and suppressed BNIP3L pathway, which are in turn correlated with worsened mitochondrial dysfunction and more rapid DbCM progression. Moreover, the significant correlations we observed between BNIP3L and fatty acid metabolism-related proteins (ACSL1, FASN, and acyl-CARs), reveal a critical link between fatty acid metabolism and receptor-mediated mitophagy. This synergy between increased fatty acid oxidation and defective mitophagy may represent an important component of the pathological processes underlying DbCM [1, 5]. Although establishing a definitive causal relationship remains challenging, a potential positive feedback relationship between impaired mitophagy and metabolic dysfunction may exist. For instance, BNIP3L deletion leads to the accumulation of hypoxia-inducible factors, which in turn suppress FASN expression and disrupt long-chain fatty acid oxidation. This sequence culminates in metabolic defects and compromised adenosine-triphosphate synthesis [53]. In summary, BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy is indispensable for preserving mitochondrial homeostasis and metabolic regulation in hearts with DbCM. Given its profound influence on fatty acid metabolism and oxidative balance, BNIP3L emerges as a promising therapeutic target for DbCM.

ECM remodeling is fundamental to DbCM pathogenesis. Yet, specific effects of diabetes on cardiac matrix repair remain poorly explored. ECM is essential to provide structural support and regulate critical cellular functions [54]. DbCM patients exhibited a distinct ECM profile characterized by the downregulation of COL5A1/2 and FBN1, alongside impaired ECM-receptor interactions. This profile is clinically significant, as collagen V deficiency, reflected by reduced COL5A1/2, is a known driver of aberrant repair, promoting larger scars and impaired healing after acute heart injury [55]. This pathological mechanism is mirrored in COL5A1 knockout mice, which exhibit reduced scar stiffness and compromised contractility, attributed to integrin-driven dysregulation of myofibroblast differentiation [55]. Deficiency in collagen V has been linked to increased scar size following acute myocardial injury, whereas a decrease in FBN1, a pathogenic gene associated with Marfan syndrome, may impair elastic fiber formation and reduce tissue elasticity [55, 56]. In DbCM, levels of FBN1, a key microfibrillar protein for structural integrity, were reduced, impairing elastic fiber formation and promoting tissue laxity [56]. Its expression in the heart is suppressed under diabetic conditions, potentially through H3 epigenetic modifications [57]. In contrast, studies in diabetic foot ulcers show that elevated FBN1 promotes fibroblast proliferation, migration, and wound healing [58], underscoring a critical divergence in its function across tissues. Furthermore, we identified STEAP3 as a novel potential target in DbCM, with its expression specifically downregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in DbCM patients. This decrease in STEAP3 may hinder wound recovery by disrupting iron homeostasis in diabetic murine wounds, thereby impairing proper ECM deposition and remodeling [59, 60]. In summary, cardiac remodeling in DbCM follows a distinct path compared to non-diabetic CM. Diabetes appears to impair the inherent capacity for matrix repair, thereby reducing cardiovascular compliance. Consequently, antifibrotic therapies should be applied judiciously; premature or non-specific interventions that further compromise this repair capacity may be associated with adverse outcomes [61]. Insights from wound healing models, such as diabetic foot ulcers, could inform novel strategies to preserve and enhance matrix repair in DbCM.

Our study revealed a shared feature of downregulated immune and inflammatory regulation in patients with both DbCM and non-diabetic CM compared to healthy controls, a finding robustly confirmed in validation analyses. This may account for the broadly disappointing results of anti-inflammatory therapies in heart failure clinical trials, highlighting the significant heterogeneity in inflammatory profiles of patients with heart failure [62–64]. Additionally, this downregulation may reflect immune exhaustion in end-stage heart failure or the effects of preoperatively administered immunosuppressive therapies. Moreover, because our myocardial samples were obtained from patients with end-stage heart failure undergoing transplantation, some of the observed molecular signatures may be influenced by the end-stage disease context. This represents a potential source of confounding and is an inherent limitation of omics analyses based on explanted human heart tissues. These findings illuminate the complex role of immune alterations in driving heart failure progression. Furthermore, recent integrative transcriptomic and proteomic analyses in individuals with diabetes but without overt heart failure [65], indicate that diabetes-associated myocardial molecular alterations may already be present prior to the clinical onset of heart failure, highlighting a temporal distinction between early disease changes and the end-stage remodeling captured in our study. Notably, despite partial differences in cardiomyopathy subtype composition, the external validation dataset demonstrated broadly consistent immune-related patterns, supporting the robustness of these observations while also suggesting potential context-dependent variation across cohorts.

While this study offers the most comprehensive multi-omics profile of DbCM in the human heart to date, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. First, the sample size, though substantial for this field, remains modest and may limit the statistical power for detecting subtle molecular differences. It should also be noted that, given the limited sample size, the absence of statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics does not necessarily indicate true clinical equivalence between groups. Instead, the baseline characteristics are presented primarily to describe the demographic and clinical features of the study population. Nevertheless, the robust and coherent multi-omics signatures we identified across transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic layers underscore the profound nature of diabetes-associated remodeling and establish a foundational dataset for the research community. Second, the novel mechanistic links proposed, such as the correlation between BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy deficiency and dysregulated lipid metabolism, are derived from multi-omics correlations and require direct functional validation through in vitro or in vivo experiments. Precisely for this reason, our study serves as a hypothesis-generating resource, offering a clear and prioritized roadmap for future investigations into key targets like ACSL1, BNIP3L, and COL5A1. Third, the interpretability of our external validation is somewhat constrained because the publicly available transcriptomic cohorts do not perfectly align with the clinical characteristics of our deeply phenotyped study population. Importantly, the partial concordance observed highlights that our study captures both universal and cohort-specific aspects of DbCM, thereby enriching the understanding of its heterogeneity and emphasizing the unique value of our comprehensive, multi-omics approach. Fourth, our analyses did not address post-translational modifications or isoform-specific protein expression. These regulatory layers may influence protein function and are not captured in the current study, warranting further investigation. While these findings support diabetes-associated molecular remodeling in failing human hearts, they should be interpreted in the context of etiologic heterogeneity and shared end-stage remodeling, which may represent potential sources of confounding. In addition, incomplete medication histories and transplant-related factors may have introduced residual confounding. Although sensitivity analyses adjusting for available comorbidities and medications supported the robustness of the major inflammatory signals, potential effects of treatment exposure and donor-transplant context cannot be fully excluded. Likewise, etiologic heterogeneity in cardiomyopathy subtypes may limit attribution of observed signals to diabetes alone, although adjustment for subtype composition supported the robustness of the principal findings. Addressing these limitations in future investigations will be crucial to refining the mechanistic understanding and therapeutic strategies for DbCM.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study delivers a comprehensive multi-omics characterization of human left ventricular tissues in DbCM, revealing pathophysiological distinctions from non-diabetic CM. We identified profound alterations in lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and ECM remodeling in the hearts of patients with DbCM. Our findings also highlighted a potential mechanistic link between BNIP3L-mediated mitophagy deficiency and dysregulated fatty acid metabolism during DbCM progression. These insights advance our understanding of the molecular insights of diabetes-associated cardiac dysfunction and may inform the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.
