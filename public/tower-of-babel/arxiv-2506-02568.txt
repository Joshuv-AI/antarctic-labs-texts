ML a GA : Multimodal Large Language and Graph Assistant
Dongzhe Fan, Yi Fang, Jiajin Liu, Djellel Difallah, Qiaoyu Tan

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated substantial efficacy in advancing graph-structured data analysis. Prevailing LLM-based graph methods excel in adapting LLMs to text-rich graphs, wherein node attributes are text descriptions. However, their applications to multimodal graphs–where nodes are associated with diverse attribute types, such as texts and images–remain underexplored, despite their ubiquity in real-world scenarios. To bridge the gap, we introduce the M ultimodal Large L anguage a nd G raph A ssistant ( MLaGA ), an innovative model that adeptly extends LLM capabilities to facilitate reasoning over complex graph structures and multimodal attributes. We first design a structure-aware multimodal encoder to align textual and visual attributes within a unified space through a joint graph pre-training objective. Subsequently, we implement a multimodal instruction-tuning approach to seamlessly integrate multimodal features and graph structures into the LLM through lightweight projectors. Extensive experiments across multiple datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of MLaGA compared to leading baseline methods, achieving superior performance in diverse graph learning tasks under both supervised and transfer learning scenarios.

Introduction

Graphs are ubiquitous and versatile data structures that serve as powerful tools for modeling intricate relationships and interactions among entities in various domains, including social networks
(
Wu et al. 2020
)
, molecular graphs
(
Zhou et al. 2020
)
, and recommendation systems
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
. Graph neural networks (GNNs)
(
Veličković et al. 2018
;
Hamilton et al. 2018
)
are extensively utilized to integrate information from node attributes and graph structures through message passing and aggregation mechanisms. Despite their widespread application, GNNs are limited to processing numerical features as inputs
(
Chen et al. 2024b
)
, which constrains their ability to generalize across diverse graphs and tasks
(
Jin et al. 2024
)
.

As a remedy, recent research has shifted from GNNs to graph LLMs
(
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Fang et al. 2024a
;
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2023
)
, which exploit LLMs’ powerful generalization abilities for graph learning tasks by processing nodes’ raw textual attributes
(
He et al. 2023
)
. However, integrating graph structures into queries remains a key hurdle. Current methods tackle this either by heuristically converting graphs into node sequences
(
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Ye et al. 2023
)
or by embedding graph structure into hidden tokens through auxiliary GNNs
(
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
He & Hooi 2024
)
, which are then inserted into query templates for reasoning. Thanks to the strong text comprehension of LLMs, these methods exhibit superior generalization via in-context learning
(
Dong et al. 2022
)
compared to conventional GNNs.

Despite recent advances in graph LLMs, their capabilities remain largely confined to text-based graphs, making it difficult to handle multimodal graphs (MMGs), where node attributes span multiple modalities, such as text and images. Yet, MMGs are increasingly common in real-world applications
(
Epstein et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2022
)
. For instance, in e-commerce platforms, each product node is typically associated with both image and textual descriptions, including user reviews
(
Zhu et al. 2024
)
. Integrating these diverse data modalities offers clear benefits for product profiling and user interest modeling. Therefore, it is imperative to push the boundaries of graph LLMs beyond textual attributes.

However, building an effective graph LLM framework for multimodal graphs presents several non-trivial challenges.
➀
Effective Integration of Multiple Modalities
. Unlike purely textual graphs, MMGs incorporate image, text, and potentially other signals (e.g., audio or sensor data). This variety complicates the alignment of multimodal attributes, which is essential for LLM comprehension. Moreover, the quality of multimodal data can be another stumbling block. As shown in
(
Zhu et al. 2024
)
, a node’s textual and image attributes are often not well matched, especially compared to those in Computer Vision(CV) fields
(
Radford et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
)
. How to leverage nodes’ rich and multimodal attributes in graph learning is a challenging yet rewarding question.
➁
Structure-aware Multimodal Instruction Tuning
. Standard multimodal LLMs, such as LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
, inject image patch sequences into query templates for visual instruction tuning. However, incorporating graph structures into these templates is difficult due to limited context windows and the heterogeneous nature of node attributes. While previous studies
(
Chen et al. 2024a
;
He & Hooi 2024
)
employed sampling strategies to extract the most relevant neighbor information, these approaches do not explicitly address the complexities of multimodal node attributes. Then, accounting for the diverse characteristics of node attributes is thus a necessary and beneficial step to fully harness the power of graph LLMs beyond text.

To answer the aforementioned questions, we propose a novel
M
ultimodal Large
L
anguage
a
nd
G
raph
A
ssistant (
MLaGA
) for MMGs. MLaGA comprises two core innovations: the
structure-aware multimodal aligner
and the
multimodal graph instruction tuning
mechanism. The former adapts and integrates each node’s multimodal attributes—textual and visual—into a shared latent space, preserving common and highly informative signals through a cross-graph pre-training objective. The latter unifies nodes’ multimodal attributes and neighborhood information by training modality-aware projectors to map the learned multimodal embeddings into semantic tokens compatible with LLMs. By combining these strategies, MLaGA significantly enhances a graph LLM’s reasoning capabilities beyond textual descriptions, enabling holistic multimodal analysis.
Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

\star

We investigate how to harness the reasoning capabilities of LLMs within multimodal graphs and present MLaGA. To our best knowledge, MLaGA is the first approach that endows LLMs with multimodal graph reasoning capabilities while preserving their general-purpose nature.

\star

We devise a two-stage training strategy to achieve MLaGA: (1)
Structure-aware multimodal aligner
that unifies node’s textual and visual attributes via cross-graph pre-training. (2)
Multimodal graph instruction tuning
scheme that incorporates these aligned representations into LLMs through modality-aware, versatile projectors, thus endowing the model with strong generalization capabilities for multimodal graph reasoning.

\star

We conduct extensive experiments across multiple graph benchmark datasets from diverse domains, under both supervised and transfer evaluation settings. Empirical results reveal that MLaGA not only excels on datasets and tasks encountered during training, but also demonstrates robust generalization to previously unseen datasets and tasks without any additional tuning.

2
Related Work

Our work is closely related to the following two research directions.

Graph LLMs
.
Large Language Models (LLMs) have catalyzed significant advancements in the field of graph learning, enabling enhanced capabilities in processing and reasoning over complex, structured data. In terms of the exceptional generative capabilities of LLMs, models like GaugLLM
(
Fang et al. 2024b
)
, advance graph self-supervised learning through augmented node feature by a MoE module. TAPE
(
He et al. 2023
)
leverages LLMs to further explain the node text attributes, which serve as new inputs to Graph Neural Networks.

Other than the graph node feature augmentation paradigm, graph LLMs also explore effective frameworks to encode graph structural information into LLMs’ semantic space.
GraphPrompter
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
encodes graph structures into embeddings, serving as soft prompts to guide LLMs in graph-related tasks. LLaGA
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
converts graph nodes into structure-aware sequences and maps them into the LLM embedding space through a trainable projector. Recently, GraphICL
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
proposes novel prompt templates to enable general-purpose LLMs to perform graph reasoning tasks without additional training.

To learn a foundation model for Text-Attributed Graphs (TAGs), GraphGPT
(
Tang et al. 2024
)
incorporates graph structural knowledge into LLMs embedding space through a dual-stage instruction tuning process and Unigraph
(
He & Hooi 2024
)
integrates LLMs with GNNs, leveraging masked graph modeling and instruction tuning to enhance cross-domain generalization.

However, although these graph LLMs leverage LLMs’ understanding capabilities for graph-based tasks, their focus primarily remains on TAGs, leaving the integration of LLM and MMGs largely unexplored.

Multimodal LLMs
.
With the remarkable advancements in LLMs, research efforts are increasingly focusing on developing Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) to enhance understanding and reasoning across multiple modalities, extending beyond text alone. CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
learns transferable visual representations by aligning images and natural language through contrastive pretraining, enabling zero-shot image-text retrieval and classification. Expanding on this, BLIP-2
(
Li et al. 2023
)
introduces the Q-Former, a lightweight transformer that extracts vision features and maps them into an LLM-aligned space, facilitating more efficient multimodal pretraining and fine-tuning.

Building upon these vision-language models, recent studies have explored more effective architectures to enhance multimodal alignment and reasoning. LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
integrates a vision encoder with LLaMA via a projection layer and fine-tunes it on multimodal instruction-following tasks, improving adaptability to diverse vision-language interactions. Qwen-VL
(
Bai et al. 2023
)
also integrates a high-performance vision encoder with an LLM and applies instruction tuning for complex visual and textual inputs.

Beyond textual-visual alignment, some studies have explored generative multimodal learning. DreamLLM
(
Dong et al. 2024
)
integrates an LLM with a diffusion model for prompt-based image synthesis, while Emu2
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
scales this to 37B parameters, enhancing in-context learning and real-time visual reasoning. Chameleon
(
Team 2024
)
unifies early-fusion multimodal learning with stable training for improved multimodal alignment and generation.

Despite recent advancements, existing MLLMs primarily focus on text-image synthesis, overlooking relational dependencies and limiting their potential in MMGs and structured multimodal reasoning.

Problem Formulation

Definition 3.1
.

(Multimodal Graphs (MMGs))
A multimodal graph is defined as
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{Y})
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the set of nodes,
\mathcal{E}
the set of edges,
\mathcal{I}
the collection of images,
\mathcal{T}
the set of documents, and
\mathcal{Y}
the label space. Each node
v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
is associated with an image attribute
\mathcal{I}_{v_{i}}\in\mathcal{I}
and a textual attribute
\mathcal{T}_{v_{i}}\in\mathcal{T}
, and may have a label
\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}\in\mathcal{Y}
for the classification task. If node
v_{i}
is connected to node
v_{j}
, then
(v_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}
.

An example of an MMG is the Amazon co-purchase network (
\mathcal{G}
), where each node (
v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
) represents a product sold on Amazon. Each node is associated with a product image (
\mathcal{I}_{v_{i}}\in\mathcal{I}
) and a short textual description (e.g., product details or user reviews) (
\mathcal{T}_{v_{i}}\in\mathcal{T}
) related to this product.

Problem Definition
. Given a multimodal graph
\mathcal{G}
, the objective is to learn a predictive function
f
that leverages the node-level multimodal attributes(
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T}
) and the underlying graph structure in
\mathcal{E}
. The trained model should accurately perform tasks such as node classification—predicting a node’s label—and link prediction—estimating connectivity between any two nodes—while also demonstrating strong generalization to previously unseen graphs without requiring additional fine-tuning.

The overview of MLaGA framework. MLaGA involves a two-stage pre-training framework. (1) MLaGA trains a unified structure-aware multimodal aligner across multiple MMGs using a contrastive graph pre-training objective. (2) MLaGA trains a versatile projector to map multimodal tokens into LLM’s token space, alongside task-wise demonstration templates to enhance the in-context learning and multimodal graph reasoning capabilities of LLMs.

4
Methodology

In this section, we introduce our MLaGA framework, a novel approach that extends general-purpose LLMs for multimodal graph reasoning tasks. We begin by exploring how to adapt multimodal LLMs to these problems in Section
4.1
. Then, in Section
4.2
, we present a structure-aware multimodal aligner that tackles the heterogeneity of multiple node attributes within the graph connections. Finally, we propose multimodal graph instruction tuning with modality-aware projectors, designed to strengthen LLM reasoning in MMGs in Section
4.3
.

4.1
Multimodal LLMs for MMs (Intuitive Solution)

Inspired by the success of multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), such as LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
and ChatGPT-4
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
, a natural approach is to adapt these models for multimodal graph reasoning tasks through in-context learning. Taking node classification as a representative example, let
\phi
denote an image encoder (e.g., ViT
(
Dosovitskiy 2020
)
). The query template in LLaVA for node classification could be approximately as follows:

<\text{img}:\phi(\mathcal{I}_{v_{i}})>+<\text{txt}:\mathcal{T}_{v_{i}}>+<\text{task}>.

(1)

Here,
<\text{img}>
represents the image patch sequence obtained via
\text{MLP}(\phi(\mathcal{I}_{v_{i}}))
. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) projector is often employed to align the image space with text tokens. The
<\text{txt}>
token captures the textual attribute
\mathcal{T}_{v_{i}}
, and the
<\text{task}>
token denotes the node classification prompt, encompassing both the potential label space and the specific question query. The template in Eq.
1
is sufficiently general to allow direct queries to general-purpose MLLMs or to adapt a general-purpose MLLM to MMGs via supervised instruction tuning.

Remark
. Although these MLLMs have been demonstrated to be effective in CV tasks, their performance on MMGs is hampered by two primary challenges:
C1: Increased multimodal complexity
. In contrast to the typical computer vision setting—where an image is well-aligned with a textual description—node images, textual attributes, and graph structures in MMGs are rarely aligned in practice; rather, they are complementary, capturing different facets of the same node. For instance, in an Amazon co-purchase network, a product’s image primarily reflects its visual features (e.g., color, shape), whereas the textual attributes (e.g., user reviews, functional descriptions) capture user preferences or functional details. This discrepancy is exacerbated when node connectivity is considered, leading to performance degradation of MLLMs (see Table
1
).
C2: Lack of effective instruction tuning for MMGs
. Although graph instruction tuning
(
Tang et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024a
)
and visual instruction tuning
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Bai et al. 2023
)
have been explored separately in their respective domains, the challenge of unifying them for MMG reasoning remains under-explored. The heterogeneity of node attributes and graph structures introduces additional complexities for LLM adaptation in MMGs. To address these challenges, we propose two novel strategies with our MLaGA to overcome these limitations, which we detail in the following sections.

4.2
Structure-Aware Multimodal Aligner

To tackle
C1
, we propose a structure-aware modality alignment encoder that integrates textual and visual attributes using the underlying graph structure. Our goal is to resolve the misalignment inherent in multiple node modalities (images, text) and provide robust representations that capitalizes on LLM reasoning capabilities. Specifically, as depicted in Figure
1
, our multimodal alignment module comprises the following three major components.

4.2.1
Shared Token-level Self-Attention

Given an anchor node
v_{i}
and its image attribute
\mathcal{I}_{v_{i}}
and text attribute
\mathcal{T}_{v_{i}}
, we first employ a well-established multimodal alignment method (e.g., CLIP) to encode the raw image and text data, facilitating initial alignment and yielding image patch sequence
\mathbf{H}_{\text{img},v_{i}}=\phi(I_{v_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}\times d_{i}}
and text token sequence
\mathbf{H}_{\text{txt},v_{i}}=g(T_{v_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times d_{t}}
, respectively. Here,
n_{v}
and
n_{t}
respectively denote the image patch and text token length, and
d_{i}
and
d_{t}
indicate the hidden dimension of image and text encoders. Based on these fine-grained representations, we then employ a shared self-attention layer to process textual and visual tokens within their respective modalities:

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{\text{txt},v_{i}}=\text{ShareAttn}(q,k,v=\mathbf{H}_{\text{txt},v_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times d}\\
\mathbf{H}_{\text{img},v_{i}}=\text{ShareAttn}(q,k,v=\mathbf{H}_{\text{img},v_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}\times d}.\end{split}

(2)

\text{ShareAttn}(\cdot)
is a standard self-attention layer with multiple heads, which allows each modality to exploit its internal context to emphasize the most informative tokens.

4.2.2
Cross-Modal Fusion with Q-Queries

In addition to highlighting the most important tokens in each modality, we also aim to capture common patterns across different modalities. To this end, we adopt another cross-modal fusion layer upon the shared self-attention module. Inspired by
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, we randomly initialize
n_{q}
learnable queries
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}\times d}
in each cross-modal layer to adaptively aggregate the most informative tokens in image and text modalities, formally expressed as:

\mathbf{Q}_{v_{i}}=\text{CrossAttn}(q=\mathbf{Q};k,v=\mathbf{H}_{\text{img},v_{i}}||\mathbf{H}_{\text{txt},v_{i}}),

(3)

where
||
denotes the concatenation operation.
\text{CrossAttn}(\cdot)
is another attention layer that concatenates the hidden sequences of image and text as key and vector arguments.
\mathbf{Q}_{v_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}\times d}
is the fused multimodal representations of node
v_{i}
. The advantages of this design are multi-fold: (1) Eq.
3
acts as a token compression function that adaptively combines the most informative multimodal tokens into a fixed-size token sequence, significantly reducing the input token size for follow-up multimodal instruction tuning; (2) the trainable nature of these query vectors offers node-level personalization allowing different nodes to attend to diverse token patterns depending on the input node attributes—a crucial aspect for graph learning tasks.

4.2.3
Structural Graph Pre-training

Given that
\mathbf{Q}_{v_{i}}
is the final multimodal representations of node
v_{i}
after stacking multiple layers, the next question is how to align the node’s multimodal embeddings with the structural information. Inspired by the homophily assumption, a straightforward solution is to reconstruct the graph structure via auto-encoder
(
Kipf & Welling 2016b
)
. However, this approach has been empirically shown to be suboptimal since the learned model opts to overfit the connected links
(
Veličković et al. 2018
)
. To this end, we adopt a contrastive graph pre-training objective to enhance the robustness of the multimodal aligner, expressed as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{v_{i}}=-\sum_{v_{u}\in\mathcal{N}(v_{i})}\log\frac{\exp\left({\text{sim}(\text{pl}(\mathbf{Q}_{v_{i}}),\text{pl}(\mathbf{Q}_{v_{u}}}))/{\tau}\right)}{\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{B}}\exp\left({\text{sim}(\text{pl}(\mathbf{Q}_{v_{i}}),\text{pl}(\mathbf{Q}_{v_{k}})})/{\tau}\right)}.\end{split}

(4)

Here,
\mathcal{N}(v_{i})
is a set of highly similar neighbors of
v_{i}
, randomly sampled from its local neighborhood.
\text{pl}(\cdot)
represents a pooling function, such as mean pooling, which is used to summarize
n_{q}
queries into a unified representation.
\mathcal{B}
denotes samples in the current mini batch. By optimizing Eq.
4
, the query vectors are trained to generate a unified representation that is important across textual, visual, and structural modalities.

Remark
. The proposed structure-aware multimodal aligner effectively addresses the heterogeneity of node’s multimodal attributes by leveraging graph structure as guidance. For instance, if image and text attributes are poorly matched, e.g., the image is blurred, the learning objective in Eq.
4
will adaptively encourage the query vectors in Eq.
3
to rely more on textual information. Moreover, this multimodal alignment strategy is expressive and scalable: unlike standard GNN pre-training
(
You et al. 2020
)
, it does not require ingesting the entire graph structure. Empirically, we find that these properties facilitate training a universal multimodal graph encoder across different graphs, serving as an embedding foundation model.

4.3
Multimodal Graph Instruction Tuning

Addressing
C2
requires bridging the gap between standard graph instruction tuning and visual instruction tuning for MMGs. We achieve this through a multimodal graph instruction tuning mechanism, which integrates neighborhood-aware demonstration templates and a modality-aligned projector to effectively adapt LLMs for MMG reasoning. As illustrated in Figure
1
, we explicitly incorporate graph structure into the LLM prompt template by reformulating them into effective demonstrations that involve label signals for various graph tasks, thus enhancing in-context learning on multimodal graph reasoning.

4.3.1
Task-wise Demonstration Selection

It is suboptimal to directly take all neighboring nodes or edges as demonstrations in practice, given the limited input window of LLMs and the noise in original graph structures. Thus, we propose several useful practices to construct these demonstrations for two representative graph learning tasks, namely, node classification (NC) and link prediction (LP); which have been demonstrated to be effective in our experiments.

NC demonstration
.
For node classification, a straightforward approach is to select demonstration nodes
\mathcal{D}(v_{i})
that are similar to the anchor node
v_{i}
. However, simply comparing multimodal embeddings often ignores structural context. To include graph structure, we leverage the Personalized PageRank (PPR) scores
\mathbf{S}_{i,j}
between node
v_{i}
and candidate node
v_{j}
. Specifically, a higher PPR score indicates node
v_{j}
is highly relevant or “similar” to node
v_{i}
. The PPR matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is formally computed as:

S=\alpha\mathbf{p}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{r}

(5)

where
\alpha
indicates the teleport probability which controls the balance between personalization and graph structure,
\mathbf{A}
is the normalized adjacency matrix. Therefore, PPR value provides a good trade-off between local and global node connections. Consequently, for each anchor node
v_{i}
, we select the top-
k
samples according their PPR values to construct
v_{i}
’s demonstration set
\mathcal{D}(v_{i})
, ensuring that demonstration samples carry both topological and semantic relevance to
v_{i}
.

LP demonstration
.
While it is reasonable to utilize PPR-based scores for NC demonstration selection; sampling demonstrations for the link prediction task is non-trivial, as it depends on two end nodes. In light of this, we adopt a neighborhood relation selection strategy. Specifically, for an anchor edge
(v_{i},v_{j})
, we randomly choose demonstration edges
\mathcal{E}_{\text{demo}}^{v_{i},v_{j}}
among the shared or intersecting neighborhoods of
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{\text{demo}}^{v_{i},v_{j}}=\{(u,v);u\in\mathcal{N}(v_{i}),u\in\mathcal{N}(v_{j})\,or\,u\in\mathcal{N}(v_{j}),u\in\mathcal{N}(v_{i})\}
. Because these neighbors appear in close proximity to both endpoints, the resulting demonstration edges exemplify similar structural contexts.

4.3.2
Multimodal Alignment Tuning

Projector for Unified Semantic Space
. To align multimodal graph tokens with the LLM’s semantic space, we introduce a versatile projector
p(\cdot)
. Formally, we map the fused multimodal representation
v_{i}
from
\mathbf{Q}_{v_{i}}
to
\mathbf{Q}_{v_{i}}^{\prime}
as follows:

Q_{v_{i}}^{\prime}=p(Q_{v_{i}})

(6)

Because each MMG may have domain-specific feature distributions, we can jointly train
p(\cdot)
across multiple MMGs to map multimodal tokens into a unified latent space that is compatible with LLM token embeddings. In practice,
p(\cdot)
is a lightweight MLP, and we freeze the LLM parameters while only learning the projector parameters
\theta_{p}
, making our model scalable and energy-efficient to train.

Training objective
. As shown in Figure
1
, we feed the learned multimodal representations
\mathbf{Q}_{v_{i}}
, the raw text attributes
\mathcal{T}_{v_{i}}
, and the image patch sequence
\mathbf{H}_{\text{img},v_{i}}
into the LLM. We then organize this input and the corresponding answers in a dialogue-like format for instruction tuning. Specifically, let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}
denote the demonstration template with task instructions. The auto-regressive training objective is:

\mathcal{L}_{v_{i}}=-\sum\log P(\mathcal{T}_{\text{ans},v_{i}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{v_{i}},\mathbf{H}_{\text{img},v_{i}},\textbf{Q}_{v_{i}},\mathcal{D}_{demo}).

(7)

Here,
\mathcal{T}_{\text{ans},v_{i}}
denotes the ground-truth answers to
v_{i}
’s multimodal graph reasoning query, guiding the LLM to produce accurate responses for diverse tasks within a unified multimodal, graph-structured prompt. By training on multiple datasets and graph tasks, MLaGA demonstrates robust generalization to both previously seen and unseen MMGs.

In summary, MLaGA integrates a structure-aware multimodal aligner (Section
4.2
) and multimodal graph instruction tuning (Section
4.3
) to explicitly tackle both
C1
increased multimodal complexity) and
C2
(lack of effective instruction tuning) for MMGs. As a result, MLaGA extends standard graph LLMs beyond text-only attributes, unleashing powerful reasoning capabilities for graphs with diverse multimodal data.

Experiments

We conduct extensive experiments to address the following research questions
RQs
:

•

RQ1:
Does MLaGA outperform leading baseline models on MMGs across various graph learning tasks?

•

RQ2:
Does MLaGA also exhibit superior data and task generalization capabilities?

•

RQ3:
Can MLaGA effectively transfer knowledge in typical transfer learning scenarios?

•

Implementation Details.

For the implementation of the structure-aware multimodal encoder, we jointly pre-train the encoder across all datasets, setting the learning rate to 1e-5. We sample 1-hop neighbors for each anchor node and randomly select five neighbor nodes. For the textual and visual encoders, we employ CLIP ViT-L/14. To align the dimensionality between textual tokens and visual tokens, we use a linear projection layer to map the textual tokens into the dimensional space of the visual tokens. For the multimodal instruction tuning part, we utilize Vicuna-7B-v1.5-16K as MLaGA’s LLM backbone, setting the learning rate to 2e-5. We adopt a 2-layer MLP projector for both MLaGA and LLaGA. For NC demonstrations, we set k to 3. For LP demonstrations, we sample 2-hop neighborhoods
\mathcal{N}(v_{i})
,
\mathcal{N}(v_{j})
of
v_{i}
,
v_{j}
and randomly select one edge from the neighborhood as the demonstration edge. For GNN-based models, we simply concatenate the textual tokens and visual tokens generated by CLIP as input. Since graph LLM baselines excel in TAGs but lack the ability to align different modalities, we use the textual tokens generated by CLIP as input.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose MLaGA, a novel framework that unifies multiple modalities and complex graph structures to enhance LLM reasoning on multimodal graphs (MMGs). While existing Graph LLMs primarily focus on text-attributed graphs, adaptation to MMGs remains largely underexplored. By leveraging a structure-aware multimodal aligner, MLaGA effectively encodes different data modalities into a shared latent space. Through multimodal instruction tuning with demonstration templates, MLaGA exhibits strong generalization and in-context learning capabilities. We hope that MLaGA and our empirical findings will inspire further research on leveraging LLMs for MMGs. In future work, we plan to extend MLaGA to a broader range of real-world tasks, such as traffic prediction and artwork modeling and comprehension.

Impact Statement

This paper introduces a innovative frame work MLaGA which enables multimodal graph reasoning with a two-stage training strategy. The broader impact of MLaGA is extending LLM’s reasoning capability from unimodal graphs, like TAGs to more complex MMGs. This universal framework empowers complex real-world tasks like medical imaging-text joint analysis and robotic multisensory scene comprehension, while advancing graph-centric multimodal deep learning research.

Appendix C
Baselines

To conduct a comprehensive evaluation, we include three different categories of methods for assessment. Here is brief introduction to all the baselines involved.

GNN-based Models:

The first category consists of GNNs for representation learning. Specifically, we evaluate:

•

MLP
: A Multi-Layer Perceptron that serves as a non-graph-based baseline. We set it as one single linear layer.

•

GCN
: A Graph Convolutional Network that captures neighborhood information using spectral convolutions. We sample neighbor nodes of the target node from two hops, selecting 15 neighbors from each node at every hop.

•

GraphSAGE
: A framework that learns node embeddings by sampling and aggregating neighboring features. The neighbor sampling policy keeps the same with the GCN method.

Vision-Language Models:

The second category includes the state-of-the-art MLLM that integrates textual and visual representations. Specifically, we evaluate:

•

LLaVA-1.5-7B
: A vision-language model designed for multimodal reasoning and understanding. We conduct zero-shot evaluation of LLaVA using vLLM, following the implementations from
https://github.com/haotian-liu/LLaVA
and
https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm
.

GraphLLMs:

The final category includes concurrent GraphLLMs, which integrate graph structures with large language models to enhance reasoning capabilities. Specifically, we evaluate:

•

LLaGA
: A model that encodes graph structures into an LLM-friendly format for effective reasoning. We follow the implementation from
https://github.com/VITA-Group/LLaGA
in our experiments.

•

GraphPrompter
: A framework that transforms graph structures into soft prompts to guide LLMs in graph-related tasks. Our experiments are based on the implementation from
https://github.com/franciscoliu/graphprompter
.
