Powerfree integers and Fourier bounds
Sebastián Carrillo Santana

Abstract
. We develop a general approach for showing when a set of integers 𝒜 \mathscr{A} has infinitely many k t ​ h k^{th} powerfree numbers without relying on equidistribution estimates for 𝒜 \mathscr{A} . In particular, we show that if the Fourier transform of 𝒜 \mathscr{A} satisfies certain L ∞ L^{\infty} and L 1 L^{1} bounds, and is also “decreasing” in some sense, then 𝒜 \mathscr{A} contains infinitely many k t ​ h k^{th} powerfree numbers. We then use this method to show that there are infinitely many cubefree palindromes in base b ⩾ 1100 b\geqslant 1100 , and in the process we obtain new L 1 L^{1} bounds for the Fourier transform of the set of palindromes. We also show that there are infinitely many squarefree integers such that its reverse is also squarefree in any base b ⩾ 2 b\geqslant 2 . Moreover, we show that there are infinitely many squarefree integers with a missing digit in base b ⩾ 5 b\geqslant 5 , and infinitely many such cubefree integers in base b ⩾ 3 b\geqslant 3 .

1.
Introduction

In the past few decades, there has been significant interest in sets of integers defined by arithmetic properties of their digits. We mention for example the work of Mauduit and Rivat
[
14
]
on the sum of digits of primes, the work of Bourgain
[
5
,
6
]
and Swaenepoel
[
18
]
on primes with prescribed digits, the work of Maynard
[
15
,
16
]
on primes with missing digits, and more recently the work of Dartyge, Martin, Rivat, Shparlinkski, and Swaenepoel
[
9
]
on reversible primes.

The purpose of this article is to develop a general approach for showing when a set of integers has infinitely many
k^{th}
powerfree numbers. This method is primarily based on obtaining certain bounds for the Fourier transform of a set of integers. This approach applies to many sets of integers defined by arithmetic properties of their digits, because in most of such cases, the Fourier transform has a nice multiplicative structure.

Given a set of integers
\mathscr{A}
and a positive
x\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathscr{A}(x)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathscr{A}\cap[1,x]
. We define the Fourier transform of
\mathscr{A}(x)
as the function
S_{x}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

S_{x}(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{n\in\mathscr{A}(x)}\me(nt),

where
\me(z)=\me^{2\pi iz}
. It is also convenient to define a normalized version:

F_{x}(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{1}{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}|S_{x}(t)|.

Of course
F_{x}(t)
depends on the set
\mathscr{A}
, but for ease of notation we avoid writing such dependence; from the context it should be clear what
F_{x}(t)
means, depending on the set
\mathscr{A}
we are interested in. We are ready to state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
\mathscr{A}
is a set of integers such that its normalized Fourier transform
F_{x}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

An
L^{\infty}
type estimate of the form

(1.1)

F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{d}\Big)\ll\exp{\Big(-c\frac{\log{x}}{\log{d}}\Big)}

for some absolute constant
c>0
and any integers
a,d
with
0<a<d
and
2\leqslant d\leqslant(\log{x})^{B}
for some
B>0
.

(2)

L^{1}
bounds of the form

(1.2)

\int_{0}^{1}F_{x}(t)\dif t\ll\frac{1}{x^{\frac{1}{k}+\delta}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}|F_{x}^{\prime}(t)|\dif t\ll x^{1-\frac{1}{k}-\delta}

for some positive integer
k\geqslant 2
and some
\delta>0
.

(3)

There is some integer
b\geqslant 2
such that for any
x,y\in\{b^{m}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
with
x\leqslant y
, we have

(1.3)

F_{y}(t)\ll F_{x}(t).

Then
\mathscr{A}
contains infinitely many
k^{th}
powerfree integers and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}(x)\\
n\text{ is }k^{\text{th}}\text{ powerfree}\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}{\zeta(k)}

as
x\to\infty
.

Remark
.

Observe that if
\mathscr{A}(x)\asymp x^{\theta}
for some
\theta\in(0,1]
, then by Parseval’s identity,

\int_{0}^{1}|S_{x}(t)|^{2}\dif t=\sum_{n\in\mathscr{A}(x)}1\ll x^{\theta}.

Therefore, by the Cauchy Schwartz inequality,

\int_{0}^{1}|F_{x}(t)|\dif t\ll\frac{1}{x^{\frac{\theta}{2}}},

and we have the same bound for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F_{x}^{\prime}\right\rVert}
but multiplied by
x
. This shows that (
1.2
) always holds for some
k
as long as
\mathscr{A}(x)\asymp x^{\theta}
. Of course, if the Fourier transform of
\mathscr{A}
has considerable cancellation, in some cases, we expect to have a much stronger bound of the form

\int_{0}^{1}|F_{x}(t)|\dif t\ll\frac{1}{x^{\theta-\varepsilon}}

for any
\varepsilon>0
, but this is usually difficult to show. The reason for expecting this is because, by the work of McGehee, Pigno, and Smith
[
17
]
, and independently by Konyagin
[
13
]
, we know that

(1.4)

\frac{\log{x}}{x^{\theta}}\asymp\frac{\log{x}}{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}\ll\int_{0}^{1}|F_{x}(t)|\dif t.

In particular, if
\theta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, our methods are not strong enough to prove the existence of infinitely many squarefree integers because (
1.2
) would be impossible.

The main philosophy of Theorem
1.1
is that, if we have good enough
L^{1}
and
L^{\infty}
bounds, and if we have some kind of decreasing property for
F_{x}(t)
, then we can show that our set has infinitely many
k^{th}
powerfree integers for some
k
that depends on how strong our
L^{1}
estimates are. There are many scenarios in which the Fourier transform
F_{x}(t)
does not exactly satisfy the same properties as in Theorem
1.1
, but it nevertheless satisfies some analogous properties. Consequently, we should think of Theorem
1.1
more as a general method rather than a standalone theorem. One of the main features of this framework is that we don’t rely on equidistribution estimates. In the following subsections, we state some applications of this method.

1.1.
Cubefree palindromes

Banks, Hart, and Sakata
[
1
]
showed that almost all palindromes are composite. Afterwards, Col
[
8
]
improved upon these results by obtaining an upper bound of the right order of magnitude for the number of palindromes less than or equal to
x
. The author did this by obtaining equidistribution estimates for palindromes in arithmetic progressions. Recently, Tuxanidy and Panario
[
19
]
improved upon Col’s results by extending the level of distribution of palindromes in arithmetic progressions to moduli up to
x^{\frac{1}{5}-\delta}
.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{b}
denote the set of nonnegative palindromes in base
b\geqslant 2
. Using equidistribution results for square moduli for palindromes from Tuxanidy and Panario, Chourasiya and Johnston
[
7
]
proved that there are infinitely many
4^{th}
powerfree palindromes in any base
b\geqslant 2
, and they gave the following asymptotic:

Theorem 1.2

(
[
7
]
,Theorem 1.6)
.

Let

\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;(n,b^{3}-b)=1\}.

Then,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)\\
n\text{ is }4^{th}\text{ powerfree}\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{\#\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)}{\zeta(4)}\prod_{p|b^{3}-b}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{4}}\Big)^{-1}.

By obtaining new
L^{1}
estimates for the Fourier transform of the set of palindromes, we showed independently and almost simultaneously, that for
b\geqslant 1100
,
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}
contains infinitely many cubefree integers:

Theorem 1.3
.

For
b\geqslant 1100
,
\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}
contains infinitely many cubefree palindromes, and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)\\
n\text{ is cubefree}\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{\#\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}(x)}{\zeta(3)}\prod_{p|b^{3}-b}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{3}}\Big)^{-1}.

Remark
.

Of course the result also holds if we replace the word cubefree by
k^{th}
powerfree for
k\geqslant 3
, but we work with cubefree for the sake of simplicity.

Subsequently, Chourasiya and Johnston
[
7
]
obtained Theorem
1.3
for all bases
b\geqslant 2
, again using equidistribution estimates from Tuxanidy and Panario
[
19
]
, and a Brun–Titchmarsh style result due to Banks
and Shparlinski
[
2
]
. In this paper, we give a proof of Theorem
1.3
.

1.2.
Squarefree reversible integers

Given an integer
n
written in base
b\geqslant 2
as

n=\sum_{j=0}^{k}n_{j}b^{j}

for some
n_{j}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}
, we define the
reverse
of
n
in base
b
as

\overleftarrow{n}_{b}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}n_{j}b^{k-1-j}.

Recently, Dartyge, Martin, Rivat, Shparlinkski, and Swaenepoel
[
9
]
showed that there are infinitely many squarefree integers
n
such that
\overleftarrow{n}_{b}
is squarefree in base
b=2
:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let

\mathscr{D}_{l}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;2^{l-1}\leqslant n<2^{l}\mbox{ and }n\mbox{ is odd}\},

and let

Q(l)=\#\{n\in\mathscr{D}_{l}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;\mu^{2}(n)=\mu^{2}(\overleftarrow{n}_{2})=1\}.

Then,

Q(l)\sim\frac{66}{\pi^{4}}\#\mathscr{D}_{l}.

Using results for the Fourier transform associated to reversible integers from Bhowmik and Suzuki
[
3
]
, recently improved by Dartyge, Rivat, and Swaenepoel
[
10
]
, and independently by Bhowmik and Suzuki
[
4
]
, we generalize Theorem
1.4
for all
b\geqslant 2
:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
b\geqslant 2
, and let

\mathscr{H}_{b}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;(n,b^{3}-b)=1,\;(\overleftarrow{n}_{b},b^{3}-b)=1\}.

Then,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{H}_{b}(x)\\
n,\overleftarrow{n}_{b}\text{ are squarefree}\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{\#\mathscr{H}_{b}(x)}{\zeta(2)^{2}}\prod_{p|b^{3}-b}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big)^{-2}.

1.3.
Powerfree integers with missing digits

Filaseta and Konyagin
[
12
]
showed that there are infinitely many squarefree integers in base
b=3,4,5
consisting only of the digits
0
and
1
. Afterwards, using equidistribution estimates, Erdős, Mauduit, and Sárközy
[
11
]
obtained an asymptotic for the number of
k^{th}
powerfree integers in base
b
with
q
excluded digits:

Theorem 1.6

(
[
11
]
, Theorem 4)
.

Let
b\geqslant 3
, and
2\leqslant q\leqslant b-1
be integers, let
\mathscr{Q}\subseteq\{0,\ldots,b-1\}
with
\#\mathscr{Q}=q
, and let

\mathscr{W}_{b}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Big\{\sum_{j=0}^{m}n_{j}b^{j}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;n_{j}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}\setminus\mathscr{Q},\,m\geqslant 0\Big\}.

Assume further that for some positive integer
k
,

k>\frac{\log{b}}{\log{(b-q)}}.

Then,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{W}_{b}(x)\\
n\text{ is }k^{th}\text{ powerfree}\\
(n,b^{2}-b)=1\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{\#\mathscr{W}_{b}(x)}{\zeta(k)}\prod_{p|b^{2}-b}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{k}}\Big)^{-1}.

Observe that the above result proves Filaseta and Konyagin’s result for
b=3
. However, it is still an open question to show whether or not for
b\geqslant 6
there are infinitely many squarefree integers such that every digit in base
b
is 0 or 1.

In the particular case where we exclude one digit, say
\mathscr{Q}=\{a_{0}\}
, Maynard
[
16
]
showed that for bases
b\geqslant 10
, the set
\mathscr{W}_{b}
contains infinitely many primes:

Theorem 1.7

(
[
16
]
, Theorem 1.1)
.

Given an integer
b\geqslant 10
, and some
a_{0}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}
, let

\mathscr{M}_{b}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Big\{\sum_{j=0}^{k}n_{j}b^{j}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;n_{j}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}\setminus\{a_{0}\},\,k\geqslant 0\Big\}

be the set of integers in base
b
without
a_{0}
in the digit expansion. Then,
\mathscr{M}_{b}
contains infinitely many primes and

\#\{p\in\mathscr{M}_{b}(x)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;p\mbox{ is prime}\}\asymp\frac{\#\mathscr{M}_{b}}{\log{x}}.

As a final application of our method, we use the
L^{1}
and
L^{\infty}
bounds from Maynard
[
15
,
16
]
to prove a weaker version of Theorem
1.6
that does not rely on equidistribution estimates:

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathscr{M}_{b}
be as in Theorem
1.7
, and let
\mathscr{M}_{b}^{*}=\{a\in\mathscr{M}_{b}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;(a,b)=1\}
. Then, for
b\geqslant 5
or
b=4
with
a_{0}=0,3
,
\mathscr{M}_{b}^{*}
contains infinitely many squarefree integers, and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{M}_{b}^{*}(x)\\
n\text{ is squarefree}\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{\#\mathscr{M}_{b}^{*}(x)}{\zeta(2)}\prod_{p|b}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big)^{-1}.

If
b=3
or
b=4
with
a_{0}=1,2
, then
\mathscr{M}_{b}^{*}
contains infinitely many cubefree integers, and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{M}_{b}^{*}(x)\\
n\text{ is cubefree}\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{\#\mathscr{M}_{b}^{*}(x)}{\zeta(3)}\prod_{p|b}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{3}}\Big)^{-1}.

Remark
.

In base
2
, if the missing digit is
1
, then
\mathscr{M}_{2}=\{0\}
, so trivially
\mathscr{M}_{2}
does not contain infinitely many squarefree integers. If the missing digit is
0
, then
\mathscr{M}_{2}=\{2^{n}-1\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
, so that
\mathscr{M}_{2}(x)\asymp\log{x}
. In this case, our methods are not strong enough to deal with
\mathscr{M}_{2}
because by (
1.4
), it is not possible to have a bound of the form

\int_{0}^{1}|F_{x}(t)|\dif t\ll\frac{1}{x^{\frac{1}{k}+\delta}}

for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, we don’t expect that an exponential sum type approach would work for this, because

\Big|\sum_{n\leqslant\sqrt{x}}\me((2^{n}-1)x)\Big|=\Big|\sum_{n\leqslant\sqrt{x}}\me(2^{n}x)\Big|;

this essentially shows that the Fourier transform can’t differentiate between the sets
\{2^{n}-1\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and
\{2^{n}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. It is still an open problem to show whether or not there are infinitely many squarefree integers of the form
2^{n}-1
.

2.
Proof of the main result

2.1.
Technical Lemmas

By the orthogonality relations, note that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}(x)\\
d|n\end{subarray}}1={}

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant a<d}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n\in\mathscr{A}(x)}\me\Big(\frac{an}{d}\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant a<d}\frac{1}{d}S_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{d}\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}{d}+\sum_{0<a<d}\frac{1}{d}S_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{d}\Big).

Therefore, by the triangle inequality and the definition of
F_{x}(t)
, we have

(2.1)

\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}(x)\\
d|n\end{subarray}}1-\frac{1}{d}\#\mathscr{A}(x)\Big|\leqslant\frac{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}{d}\sum_{0<a<d}F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{d}\Big).

What this equation tells us is that in order to understand
\mathscr{A}
in arithmetic progressions, it suffices to study its Fourier transform. In other words, type I information of a set is interlaced with its Fourier transform. Now, we are interested in counting the number of integers in
\mathscr{A}
that are
k^{th}
powerfree for some
k\geqslant 2
. We then have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}(x)\\
n\text{ is }k^{\text{th}}\text{ powerfree}\end{subarray}}1={}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathscr{A}(x)}\sum_{q^{k}|n}\mu(q)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\mu(q)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}(x)\\
q^{k}|n\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\#\mathscr{A}(x)\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\frac{\mu(q)}{q^{k}}+\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\mu(q)\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}(x)\\
q^{k}|n\end{subarray}}1-\frac{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}{q^{k}}\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}{\zeta(k)}+o\Big(\frac{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}{x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\Big)+O\Big(\#\mathscr{A}(x)\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\Big),

where the last equality follows from (
2.1
) with
d=q^{k}
and the classical result
\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\mu(n)}{n^k}$}}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{\zeta(k)}$}}
. This immediately proves the following result:

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\mathscr{A}
is a set of integers such that its normalized Fourier transform
F_{x}
satisfies

\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)=o(1)

as
x\to\infty
for some positive integer
k\geqslant 2
. Then
\mathscr{A}
contains infinitely many
k^{th}
powerfree integers and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}(x)\\
n\text{ is }k^{\text{th}}\text{ powerfree}\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{\#\mathscr{A}(x)}{\zeta(k)}

as
x\to\infty
.

In order to bound this double sum associated to the Fourier transform, we mainly follow the ideas of Maynard
[
16
]
from his work on primes with missing digits. We start with the following easy lemma:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
be a periodic function with period
1
. Then, for any integer
k\geqslant 1
,

\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}\bigg|f\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\bigg|\ll q^{k}\int_{0}^{1}|f(t)|\dif t+\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime}(t)|\dif t.

Proof.

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus,

-f\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)=-f(t)+\int_{\frac{a}{q^{k}}}^{t}f^{\prime}(x)\dif x,

so that, by the triangle inequality, we have

(2.2)

\bigg|f\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\bigg|\leqslant|f(t)|+\int_{\frac{a}{q^{k}}}^{t}|f^{\prime}(x)|\dif x.

Now, let
\delta>0
. Then, integrating (
2.2
) with respect to
t
over the interval

I\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{a}{q^{k}}+\frac{\delta}{2}\Big),

shows that

\delta\bigg|f\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\bigg|\leqslant\int_{I}|f(t)|\dif t+\int_{I}\int_{\frac{a}{q^{k}}}^{t}|f^{\prime}(x)|\dif x\dif t,

and since
x\in\Big(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{a}{q^k}$}},t\Big)
, then
x\in I
, so that

\displaystyle\delta\bigg|f\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\bigg|\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\int_{I}|f(t)|\dif t+\int_{I}\int_{I}|f^{\prime}(x)|\dif x\dif t

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{I}|f(t)|\dif t+\delta\int_{I}|f^{\prime}(x)|\dif x.

Dividing by
\delta
allows us to obtain

(2.3)

\bigg|f\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{I}|f(t)|\dif t+\int_{I}|f^{\prime}(t)|\dif t.

Now, let
\delta\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=q^{-k}
, and observe that with this choice of
\delta
, the intervals
I
don’t overlap for
0<a<q^{k}
. Moreover, these
I
are contained in the interval
(0,1)
, and so summing (
2.3
) over
0<a<q^{k}
shows that

\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}\bigg|f\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\bigg|\leqslant q^{k}\int_{0}^{1}|f(t)|\dif t+\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime}(t)|\dif t.\qed

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

For convenience to the reader we restate the theorem here:
See
1.1

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, it suffices to show that

\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)=o(1)

as
x\to\infty
. Moreover, without loss of generality, it suffices to prove the result for
x=b^{m}
as
m\to\infty
. The main idea is to write

\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)=S_{1}+S_{2},

where

S_{1}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{q\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{B}{k}}}\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big),

and

S_{2}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{(\log{x})^{\frac{B}{k}}<q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big).

In order to bound
S_{1}
, we employ the
L^{\infty}
estimate, and for
S_{2}
, using a large sieve type estimate, we show that the
L^{1}
estimate is sufficient to obtain the required bound. With these ideas in mind, we begin by bounding
S_{1}
: using (
1.1
), we obtain for some absolute constant
c

S_{1}\ll\sum_{q\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{B}{k}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{c}{k}\frac{\log{x}}{\log{q}}\Big)\ll(\log{x})^{\frac{B}{k}}\exp\Big(-\frac{c}{B}\frac{\log{x}}{\log{\log{x}}}\Big)=o(1).

To bound
S_{2}
, we employ Lemma
2.2
to see that for any
u
,

\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{u}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\ll\int_{0}^{1}F_{u}(t)\dif t+\frac{1}{q^{k}}\int_{0}^{1}|F_{u}^{\prime}(t)|\dif t.

This together with (
1.2
) shows that

\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{u}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\ll\frac{1}{u^{\frac{1}{k}+\delta}}+\frac{u^{1-\frac{1}{k}-\delta}}{q^{k}}.

We now choose
u=b^{l}
maximally subject to
u\leqslant x
and
u\leqslant q^{k}
, so that by (
1.3
), we have

\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{0<a<q^{k}}F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{q^{k}}\Big)\ll\frac{1}{x^{\frac{1}{k}+\delta}}+\frac{1}{q^{1+k\delta}}.

Finally, summing over
(\log{x})^{\frac{B}{k}}<q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}
shows that

\displaystyle S_{2}\ll{}

\displaystyle\sum_{(\log{x})^{\frac{B}{k}}<q\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\Big(\frac{1}{x^{\frac{1}{k}+\delta}}+\frac{1}{q^{1+k\delta}}\Big)

\displaystyle\ll{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x^{\delta}}+\int_{(\log{x})^{\frac{B}{k}}}^{x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\frac{\dif t}{t^{1+k\delta}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x^{\delta}}+\frac{1}{k\delta}\Big(\frac{1}{(\log{x})^{B\delta}}-\frac{1}{x^{\delta}}\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle o(1).\qed

Example 2.3
.

To illustrate Theorem
1.1
, let us consider the simplest example when
\mathscr{A}=\mathbb{Z}^{+}\cup\{0\}
. In this case,

F_{x}(t)=\frac{1}{x}\Big|\sum_{n=0}^{x}\me(nt)\Big|=\frac{1}{x}\Big|\frac{\me(xt)-1}{\me(t)-1}\Big|=\frac{1}{x}\Big|\frac{\sin(\pi xt)}{\sin(\pi t)}\Big|

is essentially the normalized
Dirichlet kernel
. We now show that
F_{x}(t)
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
:

(1)

Let
a,d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
a<d
and
d\geqslant 2
. Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
denote the distance to the nearest integer function. Observe that for any
u\in\mathbb{R}
, since
|\sin(\pi u)|\geqslant 2\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}
, then

F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{d}\Big)\leqslant\frac{1}{2x\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{a}{d}\right\rVert}}\leqslant\frac{d}{x}.

Now, since
\log{x}\ll x^{\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
, it is clear that if we assume that
d\leqslant\log{x}
, we have

F_{x}\Big(\frac{a}{d}\Big)\leqslant\frac{\log{x}}{x}\ll\frac{x^{1-\tfrac{1}{2\log{2}}}}{x}\leqslant\frac{x^{1-\tfrac{1}{2\log{d}}}}{x}=\exp{\Big(-\frac{\log{x}}{2\log{d}}\Big)}.

(2)

By symmetry, observe that

(2.4)

\int_{0}^{1}F_{x}(t)\dif t=2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}F_{x}(t)\dif t.

Now, again using the inequality
|\sin(\pi x)|\geqslant 2\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}
, it is clear that

F_{x}(t)\leqslant\min\Big\{1,\frac{1}{2x\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}}\Big\}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}F_{x}(t)\dif t={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{x}}F_{x}(t)\dif t+\int_{\frac{1}{x}}^{\frac{1}{2}}F_{x}(t)\dif t

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{x}}\dif t+\int_{\frac{1}{x}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{2xt}\dif t

\displaystyle\ll{}

\displaystyle\frac{\log{x}}{x}.

This together with (
2.4
) shows that

\int_{0}^{1}F_{x}(t)\dif t\ll\frac{1}{x^{1-\varepsilon}}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. A completely analogous argument allows us to obtain the same bound for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F_{x}^{\prime}(t)\right\rVert}_{1}
, but multiplied by
x
, showing that
F_{x}(t)
satisfies (
1.2
) for any
k\geqslant 2
.

(3)

Observe that for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we have

|\sin(2^{m+1}\pi t)|=2|\sin(2^{m}\pi t)\cos(2^{m}\pi t)|\leqslant 2|\sin(2^{m}\pi t)|.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}\Big|\frac{\sin(2^{m+1}\pi t)}{\sin(\pi t)}\Big|\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{m}}\Big|\frac{\sin(2^{m}\pi t)}{\sin(\pi t)}\Big|.

Applying this inequality inductively, shows that for any
x,y
powers of
2
with
x\leqslant y
, we have

F_{y}(t)\ll F_{x}(t).

This shows that
F_{x}(t)
satisfies all the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
for any
k\geqslant 2
, and we conclude that the set of positive integers contains infinitely many
k^{th}
powerfree integers, and we recover the classic result

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant x\\
n\text{ is }k^{\text{th}}\text{ powerfree}\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{x}{\zeta(k)}.

3.
Cubefree palindromes

Let
\mathscr{P}_{b}
denote the set of non negative palindromes in base
b\geqslant 2
. It will be convenient to consider the set of palindromes with exactly
l
digits. For
l\geqslant 2
, we define

\mathscr{B}_{l}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;b^{l-1}\leqslant n<b^{l}\}.

For convenience, we define
\mathscr{B}_{1}=\{0,\ldots,b-1\}
. We also define a Fourier transform on
\mathscr{B}_{l}
:

f_{l}(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{n\in\mathscr{B}_{l}}\me(nt).

First, let’s see how we can explicitly compute
f_{2l}(t)
: note that
n\in\mathcal{B}_{2l}
if and only if
n=n_{0}+n_{1}b+\ldots+n_{2l-1}b^{2l-1}
for some
n_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}
with
n_{i}=n_{2l-i}
for
i=0,\ldots,l-1
and
n_{0}\geqslant 1
. Then,

\displaystyle f_{2l}(t)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{0},\ldots,n_{2l-1}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}\\
n_{0}\geqslant 1\\
n_{i}=n_{2l-1-i},\;0\leqslant i\leqslant 2l-1\end{subarray}}\me\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{2l-1}n_{i}b^{i}\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{0}=1}^{b-1}\me((1+b^{2l-1})n_{o}t)\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}\sum_{n_{i}=0}^{b-1}\me((b^{i}+b^{2l-1-i})n_{i}t)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(f_{1}((1+b^{2l-1})t)-1)\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}f_{1}((b^{i}+b^{2l-1-i})t).

Similarly,

(3.1)

f_{2l+1}(t)=(f_{1}((1+b^{2l})t)-1)f_{1}(b^{l}t)\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}f_{1}((b^{i}+b^{2l-i})t).

This shows that the expressions for
f_{j}(t)
are very explicit, because

f_{1}(t)=\sum_{0\leqslant n<b}\me(nt)=\frac{\me(bt)-1}{\me(t)-1}.

Now, note that since
f_{1}(0)=b
, then the product formula
(
3.1
)
shows that

\mathscr{B}_{2l+1}=f_{2l+1}(0)=(b-1)b^{l}.

Since the expression for
f_{l}(t)
differs according to the parity of
l
, for convenience we will work only with palindromes with an odd number of digits, so we let

\mathscr{A}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;n\mbox{ has an odd number of digits}\}=\bigcup_{l=0}^{\infty}\mathscr{B}_{2l+1}.

Then, if
S_{x}(t)=\sum_{n\in\mathscr{A}(x)}\me(nt)
denotes the Fourier transform of
\mathscr{A}(x)
, and if
x=b^{2L+1}
, we then have

S_{x}(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{L}f_{2l+1}(t).

Following
[
19
]
, we define

\Phi_{l}(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}f_{1}((b^{i}+b^{2l-i})t),

and we observe that

(3.2)

|S_{x}(t)|\leqslant\sum_{l=0}^{L}|f_{2l+1}(t)|\leqslant b^{2}\sum_{l=0}^{L}|\Phi_{l}(t)|,

where we define
\Phi_{0}(t)=\Phi_{1}(t)=1
. Hence, in order to study the Fourier transform of the palindromes, it essentially suffices to study
\Phi_{l}(t)
. Moreover, since it is convenient to work with a normalized version, we define

\tilde{\Phi}_{l}(t)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\Phi_{l}(t)}{\#\mathscr{B}_{2l+1}}=\frac{\Phi_{l}(t)}{(b-1)b^{l}}.

We will see that
\tilde{\Phi}_{l}(t)
will play the role of
F_{x}(t)
from the previous sections, because it will satisfy very similar properties to the ones in the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
. It will also later become clear why we work with
\Phi_{l}(t)
instead of
f_{2l+1}(t)
. We start with the
L^{\infty}
bound (the analogous of (
1.1
)) first proved by Col
[
8
]
, and then improved by Tuxanidy and Panario
[
19
]
:

Lemma 3.1

(
[
19
]
, Proposition 6.2)
.

Let
a,d,m,l
be integers with
d\geqslant 2
,
(d,a(b^{3}-b))=1
. Then,

\Big|\tilde{\Phi}_{l}\Big(\frac{a}{d}+\frac{m}{b^{3}-b}\Big)\Big|\ll_{b}\exp\Big(-c_{b}\frac{l}{\log{d}}\Big),

where
c_{b}>0
is some absolute constant depending only on
b
.

This shows that
\tilde{\Phi}_{l}(a/d)
satisfies a slight extension of (
1.1
), but with the more restrictive condition that
d
is relativity prime to both
a
and
b^{3}-b
. This leads us to consider the more restrictive set (just as in
[
19
]
and
[
7
]
)

\mathscr{P}_{b}^{*}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{n\in\mathscr{P}_{b}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;(n,b^{3}-b)=1\}.

Now we prove the analogue of (
1.3
):

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
l_{1},l_{2},d
be positive integers such that
l_{2}\leqslant l_{1}
and
(d,b^{3}-b)=1
. Then,

(3.3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a<d\\
(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big|\tilde{\Phi}_{l_{1}}\Big(\frac{a}{d}+\frac{m}{b^{3}-b}\Big)\Big|\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a<d\\
(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}\max_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big|\tilde{\Phi}_{l_{2}}\Big(\frac{a}{d}+\frac{m}{b^{3}-b}\Big)\Big|

Proof.

From the definition of
\Phi_{l}(t)
we observe that

\Phi_{l}(t)=f_{1}((b+b^{2l-1})t)\Phi_{l-1}(bt).

Hence, using the trivial bound
|f(\theta)|\leqslant b
for any
\theta
, we have

|\Phi_{l}(t)|\leqslant b|\Phi_{l-1}(bt)|.

This shows that

(3.4)

|\tilde{\Phi}_{l}(t)|=\frac{|\Phi_{l}(t)|}{(b-1)b^{l}}\leqslant\frac{|\Phi_{l}(bt)|}{(b-1)b^{l-1}}=|\tilde{\Phi}_{l-1}(bt)|.

Using this equation inductively we see that if
l_{2}\leqslant l_{1}
, then

|\tilde{\Phi}_{l_{1}}(t)|\leqslant|\tilde{\Phi}_{l_{2}}(b^{l_{1}-l_{2}}t)|.

Now, letting
t=\frac{a}{d}+\frac{m}{b^{3}-b}
, and summing over
0<a<d
with
(a,d)=1
completes the proof upon observing that since
(d,b^{3}-b)=1
and
(a,d)=1
, then multiplication by a power of
b
keeps the sum on the right hand side of (
3.3
) invariant.
∎

Remark
.

The main reason we work with
\Phi_{l}(t)
instead of
f_{2l+1}(t)
is because the later does not satisfy a condition as nice as (
3.3
). In fact, a recursive formula for
f_{2l+1}(t)
involves all the functions
f_{2j+1}(t)
for
j=0,\ldots,l-1
:

f_{2l+1}(t)=(f_{1}((1+b^{2l})t)-1)\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}f_{2j+1}(b^{l-j}t).

Now we are interested in
L^{1}
bounds for
\Phi_{l}
and
\Phi_{l}^{\prime}
. Note that by Parseval’s identity,

\int_{0}^{1}|f_{2l+1}(t)|^{2}\dif t=\sum_{n\in\mathscr{B}_{2l+1}}1^{2}=(b-1)b^{l}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}|f_{2l+1}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\dif t\ll b^{5l}

so that by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality we have

\int_{0}^{1}|f_{2l+1}(t)|\dif t\ll b^{l/2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}|f_{2l+1}^{\prime}(t)|\dif t\ll b^{5l/2}.

We expect that
\Phi_{l}(t)
satisfies the same bounds as above. However, this is not as straightforward to show, because when expanding the product as

|\Phi_{l}(t)|^{2}=|\Phi_{l}(t)\Phi_{l}(-t)|=b^{l-1}+\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant N}a_{n}\me(\alpha_{n}t)

for some
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and some sequences
a_{n},\alpha_{n}
, it is not trivial to show that
\alpha_{n}\neq 0
. Moreover, even if this holds, we would only get a “trivial” bound for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi_{l}\right\rVert}_{1}
. Therefore, we use a different approach, using similar ideas to the ones from Maynard
[
15
]
to show that for sufficiently large base
b
, we can do better.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

\alpha_{b}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\log{b}-\log(4+\log(\frac{b}{2}-1))}{2\log{b}}.

Then, for
x=b^{2l}
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}|\tilde{\Phi}_{l}(t)|\dif t\ll\frac{1}{x^{\alpha_{b}}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}|\tilde{\Phi}_{l}^{\prime}(t)|\dif t\ll lx^{1-\alpha_{b}}.

In particular, for every
b\geqslant 1100
, there is some
\delta_{b}>0
such that

\int_{0}^{1}|\tilde{\Phi}_{l}(t)|\dif t\ll\frac{1}{x^{\frac{1}{3}+\delta_{b}}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}|\tilde{\Phi}_{l}^{\prime}(t)|\dif t\ll x^{\frac{2}{3}-\delta_{b}}.

Proof.

We begin by noting that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\Phi_{l}(t)|\dif t={}

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant a<b^{2l}}\int_{\frac{a}{b^{2l}}}^{\frac{a}{b^{2l}}+\frac{1}{b^{2l}}}|\Phi_{l}(t)|\dif t

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant a<b^{2l}}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{b^{2l}}}\Big|\Phi_{l}\Big(\frac{a}{b^{2l}}+\eta\Big)\Big|{\rm d}\eta

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{2l}}\sum_{0\leqslant a<b^{2l}}\sup_{\eta\in[0,\frac{1}{b^{2l}})}\Big|\Phi_{l}\Big(\frac{a}{b^{2l}}+\eta\Big)\Big|

(3.5)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{2l}}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{2l}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}}\sup_{\eta\in[0,\frac{1}{b^{2l}})}\Big|\Phi_{l}\Big(\frac{a_{1}}{b}+\frac{a_{2}}{b^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{a_{2l}}{b^{2l}}+\eta\Big)\Big|.

Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
denote the distance to the nearest integer function. Note that for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
, since
|\sin(\pi y)|\geqslant 2\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}
, then

|f_{1}(y)|=\Big|\frac{\me(by)-1}{\me(y)-1}\Big|=\Big|\frac{\sin(\pi by)}{\sin(\pi y)}\Big|\leqslant\min\Big\{b,\frac{1}{2\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y\right\rVert}}\Big\}.

This together with the definition of
\Phi_{l}(t)
shows that

\displaystyle|\Phi_{l}(t)|={}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}\Big|\frac{\sin(\pi(b^{i+1}+b^{2l+1-i})t)}{\sin(\pi(b^{i}+b^{2l-i})t)}\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}\min\Big\{b,\frac{1}{2\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(b^{i}+b^{2l-i})t\right\rVert}}\Big\}

(3.6)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle b^{l-1}\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}\min\Big\{1,\frac{1}{2b\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(b^{i}+b^{2l-i})t\right\rVert}}\Big\}.

Now, for
t\in[0,1)
, by writing

t=\frac{t_{1}}{b}+\frac{t_{2}}{b^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{t_{i}}{b^{i}}+\cdots+\frac{t_{2l-i}}{b^{2l-i}}+\cdots,

we see that

b^{i}t=\mbox{Integer}+\frac{t_{i+1}}{b}+u_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad b^{2l-i}t=\mbox{Integer}+\frac{t_{2l+1-i}}{b}+v_{i}

for some
u_{i},v_{i}\in[0,1/b)
, showing that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(b^{i}+b^{2l-i})t\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{t_{i+1}}{b}+\frac{t_{2l+1-i}}{b}+w_{i}\right\rVert}

for some
w_{i}\in[0,2/b)
. This together with (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) shows that

\int_{0}^{1}|\Phi_{l}(t)|\dif t\ll\frac{1}{b^{l}}\sum_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{2l-1}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}}\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}\sup_{\beta\in[0,\frac{2}{b})}\min\Big\{1,\frac{1}{2b\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{a_{i}}{b}+\frac{a_{2l-i}}{b}+\beta\right\rVert}}\Big\}.

Given digits
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}
, we let

G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sup_{\beta\in[0,\frac{2}{b})}\min\Big\{1,\frac{1}{2b\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{\theta_{1}}{b}+\frac{\theta_{2}}{b}+\beta\right\rVert}}\Big\}.

Then,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\Phi_{l}(t)|\dif t\ll{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{l}}\sum_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{2l-1}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}}G(a_{1},a_{2l-1})G(a_{2},a_{2l-2})\cdots G(a_{l-1},a_{l+1})

(3.7)

\displaystyle\ll{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{l}}\Big(\sum_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}}G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\Big)^{l}.

In order to understand
G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
we consider the
b\times b
matrix

M_{b}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=[G(i,j)]_{i,j\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}},

and we observe that
M_{b}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

M_{b}
is symmetric because
G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=G(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})
.

(2)

M_{b}
is a Hankel matrix (i.e., a matrix in which each ascending skew-diagonal from left to right is constant) because

G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=G(\theta_{1}+1,\theta_{2}-1).

(3)

For any
\theta_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,b-2\}
, we have
G(0,\theta_{2})=G(0,b-2-\theta_{2})
.

For example, in base
b=10
, the matrix looks like this:

M_{10}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}&1&1\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}&1&1&1\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}&1&1&1&\frac{1}{2}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}&1&1&1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}&1&1&1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}&1&1&1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}&1&1&1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}&1&1&1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}\\[5.0pt]
1&1&1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}\\[5.0pt]
1&1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{8}&\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{2}&1\end{bmatrix}

From the above properties, it is clear that

(3.8)

\sum_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}}G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=b\sum_{0\leqslant\theta_{2}\leqslant b-1}G(0,\theta_{2}).

Firsts let’s assume that
b
is even. Then, for
\theta_{2}\leqslant\frac{b}{2}-2
,
\frac{\theta_{2}}{b}+\beta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, so that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{\theta_{2}}{b}+\beta\right\rVert}=\frac{\theta_{2}}{b}+\beta\geqslant\frac{\theta_{2}}{b},

showing that for
\theta_{2}\neq 0
,

G(0,\theta_{2})\leqslant\frac{1}{2\theta_{2}}.

Now, it is easy to see that
G(0,\frac{b}{2}-1)=\frac{1}{b-2}
, and also
G(0,0)=G(0,b-1)=G(0,b-2)=1
. Combining this with the identity
G(0,\theta_{2})=G(0,b-2-\theta_{2})
gives us the estimate

\sum_{0\leqslant\theta_{2}\leqslant b-1}G(0,\theta_{2})\leqslant 3+\sum_{1\leqslant\theta_{2}\leqslant\frac{b}{2}-1}\frac{1}{\theta_{2}}\leqslant 4+\log\Big(\frac{b}{2}-1\Big).

A similar argument shows that the same bound holds when
b
is odd. Combining this with (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) gives us

\int_{0}^{1}|\Phi_{l}(t)|\dif t\ll\Big(4+\log\Big(\frac{b}{2}-1\Big)\Big)^{l}.

After normalizing, we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}|\tilde{\Phi}_{l}(t)|\dif t\ll\Big(\frac{4+\log\Big(\frac{b}{2}-1\Big)}{b}\Big)^{l}=\frac{1}{x^{\alpha_{b}}},

upon recalling that
x=b^{2l}
