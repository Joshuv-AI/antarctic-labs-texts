Comparative evaluation of parasitological, serological, and molecular methods for the diagnosis of cutaneous leishmaniasis in the Garmian area, Kurdistan region of Iraq
Authors: Hassan Mohammad Tawfeeq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) is a neglected tropical disease and remains a significant public health issue in many endemic regions. Accurate diagnosis is often challenging due to the broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, which can resemble other dermatological conditions. Therefore, highly sensitive and specific diagnostic methods are essential to ensure proper identification and management of the disease.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2019 and March 2020 at Kalar General Hospital in the Garmian area, Kurdistan Region of Iraq, and enrolled 85 patients with clinically suspected CL. Diagnosis was based on dermatological examination or ongoing antileishmanial treatment. Specimens were collected from cutaneous lesions using fine needle aspiration for direct microscopic examination and molecular analysis. Additionally, venous blood samples were obtained for serological testing using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Diagnostic performance was assessed for four methods: direct microscopy, ELISA, conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and nested PCR. Nested PCR served as the comparative reference method rather than an independent gold standard; agreement of the other assays with nested PCR was expressed as positive percent agreement (PPA) with 95% confidence intervals and Cohen's κ.ResultsAmong the 85 patients, CL was confirmed in 84 (98.82%) cases by nested PCR, 71 (83.53%) by conventional PCR, 64 (75.29%) by direct microscopy, and 45 (52.94%) by ELISA. Relative to nested PCR, the positive percent agreement was 84.5% for conventional PCR, 76.2% for direct microscopy, and 53.6% for ELISA. Because only one case was nested-PCR-negative, specificity could not be reliably estimated and is not reported.ConclusionsNested PCR showed the highest detection rate and is a valuable tool for CL diagnosis, particularly in cases with low parasite load. Because nested PCR served as the reference method, formal diagnostic accuracy could not be established, and larger studies employing an independent reference standard (culture, sequencing, or a composite standard) are warranted. In contrast, ELISA showed poor diagnostic performance and should not be relied upon as a standalone test for CL. Incorporating molecular techniques, especially nested PCR, into routine diagnostic protocols is recommended to improve early detection and effective management of cutaneous leishmaniasis.

Introduction

Leishmaniasis is a vector-borne disease caused by obligate intracellular protozoan parasites of the genus Leishmania (Akhoundi et al., 2016), typically transmitted through the bite of infected Phlebotomine sandflies harboring the promastigote stage of the parasite (Burza et al., 2018). The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies leishmaniasis as a neglected tropical disease (NTD), underscoring its substantial impact not only on public health but also on social and economic systems due to its high associated burden. The disease is endemic in approximately 98 countries and three territories across five continents (Alvar et al., 2012). Globally, an estimated 600,000 to 1,000,000 new cases of cutaneous leishmaniasis occur each year, and approximately 1 billion people live in areas at risk of infection. However, the true incidence is likely underreported in many regions, as cases often go unrecognized and disease notification is not mandatory (McGwire and Satoskar, 2014; WHO Expert Committee, 2010).

Leishmaniasis is considered an endemic disease in Iraq, where both cutaneous leishmaniasis (commonly referred to as “Baghdad boil”) and visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar) are present (Al-Hayali and Al-Kattan, 2021). Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) is predominantly concentrated in central Iraq, particularly around Baghdad. However, since the Gulf War, the disease has expanded into previously non-endemic regions, including Maysan, Thi-Qar, Diyala, Al-Anbar, Basrah, Al-Qadisiyyah, and virtually all Iraqi provinces (Alvar et al., 2012; Awad and Jarallah, 2006). The highest incidence rates were recorded during the initial years following the war and large-scale population displacement, peaking at 45.5 cases per 100,000 population in 1992 (Salam et al., 2014). Transmission of CL in Iraq is primarily mediated by Phlebotomus papatasi and Phlebotomus sergenti sandfly species, with Leishmania major and Leishmania tropica identified as the principal causative agents (Salam et al., 2014).

The diagnosis of cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) is challenging due to its wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, which often resemble other dermatological conditions such as skin cancer, fungal infections, leprosy, and staphylococcal infections (WHO Expert Committee, 2010). Consequently, accurate differentiation of CL from other dermal diseases necessitates the use of highly sensitive and specific diagnostic techniques. Several reliable laboratory methods are available, including direct smear microscopy, culture, immunological assays, and molecular techniques.

The conventional diagnosis of CL typically relies on the microscopic detection of amastigotes in Giemsa-stained smears obtained from lesion aspirates, as well as in vitro cultivation of the parasite (Bensoussan et al., 2006; Faber et al., 2003). However, these traditional methods are limited chiefly by low and operator-dependent sensitivity; direct smear microscopy nonetheless retains high specificity when amastigotes are clearly visualised by an experienced microscopist. Serological tests also present limitations, primarily due to potential cross-reactivity with antibodies against other infections such as trypanosomiasis, tuberculosis, and toxoplasmosis (Sundar and Rai, 2002). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) offers a highly sensitive alternative, particularly effective in detecting low parasite loads in chronic lesions. Amplification targets are either genomic (nuclear) DNA—such as the SSU rRNA (18S rRNA) gene (van Eys et al., 1992), ITS1, mini-exon regions, the tubulin gene (Luis et al., 1998), and HSP70 (Garcia et al., 2004)—or extrachromosomal DNA, such as repetitive kDNA minicircles and cytochrome b (Belli et al., 1998).

The present study aimed to compare the diagnostic performance—expressed as positive percent agreement and inter-test agreement—of molecular methods (conventional and nested PCR) with parasitological (direct microscopy) and serological (ELISA) techniques for cutaneous leishmaniasis in the Garmian region.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study was designed as a cross-sectional descriptive investigation and employed a convenience sampling method. It was conducted between December 2019 and March 2020 at Kalar General Hospital, located in Kalar, Garmian area, Kurdistan Region, Iraq. A convenience sampling approach was used, in which all eligible patients presenting during the study period were enrolled; no formal a priori sample-size calculation was performed. A total of 85 patients with clinically suspected cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) were enrolled, including 39 males (mean age: 26.90 ± 14.00 years) and 46 females (mean age: 30.25 ± 16.52 years). All patients presented with skin lesions and originated from various locations within the Garmian area. The diagnosis of CL was established using parasitological, immunological, and molecular techniques.

Patient data sheets

Data from each referred patient were collected in a questionnaire, filled in by direct interviewing the patients. The questionnaire information was included; gender, age, living place either urban or rural, types of lesion and others. The lesion classification into “wet” and “dry” forms was based on routine clinical dermatological assessment commonly used in endemic settings. Specifically, wet lesions were defined as ulcerative and/or exudative lesions with active inflammation, whereas dry lesions were defined as non-exudative, papular or nodular lesions with minimal surface ulceration.

Specimen collection

Samples were obtained from the active infiltrated border of the most representative cutaneous lesion. After the lesion was cleaned with 70% ethyl alcohol and allowed to dry, 0.1–0.2 mL of sterile normal saline was injected intradermally into the active border using a 25-gauge needle, and tissue fluid was gently aspirated. One portion of the aspirate was smeared onto glass slides for direct microscopy; the remaining aspirate was preserved in 96% ethanol, transported to the molecular biology laboratory of the University of Garmian, and stored at 4 °C until DNA extraction. In addition, 2–4 mL of venous blood was collected into clot-activator tubes for serological testing; serum was separated by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 5 min) and stored at −80 °C.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients of any age who attended Kalar General Hospital between December 2019 and March 2020 with a clinically suspected cutaneous leishmaniasis lesion, and who consented to participate, were eligible for inclusion. A subset of patients who had already commenced early antileishmanial therapy was also included. Individuals without observable skin lesions and those who declined to participate were excluded. Prior antileishmanial treatment was not used as an exclusion criterion; because such treatment can reduce parasite load, this is addressed as a study limitation.

Parasitological examination

Direct microscopic examination was conducted by spreading the aspirated fluid from skin lesions onto clean glass slides, which were then air-dried. The smears were fixed with methanol and stained with Giemsa stain. Microscopic evaluation was performed under an oil immersion lens at 100× magnification (de Almeida et al., 2021). A smear was considered positive if at least one amastigote was identified. If no amastigotes were observed after 15 min of examination, the smear was classified as negative.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serological analysis was performed using the Leishmania ELISA IgG + IgM Kit (Vircell, Spain), following the manufacturer's protocol. Optical density values were measured with an ELISA microplate reader. Cut-off values were calculated as the manufacturer specified, using the provided calibrator and control sera. Results were classified as positive, negative, or borderline based on the criteria defined by the kit. Borderline results were considered indeterminate and were excluded from the diagnostic accuracy analysis. Each assay run included the positive and negative controls that came with the kit. Results were accepted only when control values fell within the specified ranges.

Molecular diagnosis

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from the aspirated lesion fluid using the EasyPure® Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TransGen Biotech Ltd., Beijing, China), following the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed by measuring the optical density ratio at 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The DNA samples were stored at −20 °C until further analysis by polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Amplification of SSUrRNA

The genomic DNA of reference strain and local isolated with specific primers (R221-F and R332-R) was used for the amplification of the SSUrRNA. Conventional PCR assays were performed in a 20μl final reaction volume, which consisted of 10μl 2xEasyTaq PCR superMix (TransGene Biotech; contained EasyTaq DNA polymerase, dNTPs, and optimised buffer), 3μl of DNA template, 0.8μl of each forward (R221-F) and reverse (R332-R) primer. The volume of the reaction was completed with the addition of nuclease free water. PCR mixtures were spun down briefly (5–10 s), then placed in a thermal cycler (TCY, Crealcon, NL) and subjected to the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min, 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 40 s, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 8 min. The amplified DNA fragments were visualised using 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis and the amplicon size was determined by comparison with a 100-bp DNA ladder (TransGene Biotech).

Nested PCR

Nested PCR was conducted for all specimens, using the products of the first-round PCR (from both first-round-positive and first-round-negative samples) as template for the second round with the internal primers HMT-F/HMT-R. The nested PCR (second round) was conducted in a final volume of 20 μl containing 10μl of 2xEasyTaq PCR SuperMix, 0.7μl of each forward (HMT-F) and reverse (HMT-R) internal primer, 0.8 μl of first-round PCR product as the template, and 0.5μl of DMSO. The reaction solution was made up to volume with nuclease-free water. The reactions were cycled under the following conditions: 94 °C for 4 min, 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 40 s followed by 72 °C for 7 min. The PCR products were visualised by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

To minimize the risk of contamination inherent to nested PCR assays, strict contamination-control measures were applied throughout the procedure. Pre-PCR steps (DNA extraction and reaction setup) and post-PCR steps (amplification and gel electrophoresis) were performed in physically separated laboratory areas using dedicated equipment and consumables. Aerosol-resistant filter tips were used for all pipetting steps, and work surfaces and instruments were routinely decontaminated before and after use.

Species identification (ITS1–RFLP and sequencing)

For species identification, the internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) region was amplified from the clinical isolates and the two reference strains (L. major and L. tropica) using the primers LITSR/L5.8S (approximately 350 bp). ITS1 amplicons were digested with the restriction endonuclease HaeIII, and the resulting restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) patterns were compared with those of the reference strains. In addition, partial SSU rRNA (343 bp) and ITS1 products from 21 (25%) positive samples were sequenced; consensus sequences were verified by BLAST against GenBank and deposited under accession numbers MZ520144–MZ520157 (SSU rRNA) and MZ502957–MZ502970 (ITS1). This molecular characterization provided an independent, if partial, confirmation of Leishmania identity in the study cohort.

Leishmania reference strain

Two standard isolated DNA references for L. major and L. tropica were provided by the Pasteur Institute of Iran and examined together with the clinical isolates as a control positive.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of the College of Medicine, University of Sulaimani (Approval No. 1–15/10/2019). The principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for ethical conduct in medical research involving human subjects were strictly followed. Verbal and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. For participants under the age of 18, consent was obtained from their legal guardians.

Diagnostic reference standard

In the present study, nested polymerase chain reaction (nested PCR) was used as a comparative reference method due to its high sensitivity for detecting Leishmania DNA. However, it was not considered a definitive gold standard, as independent confirmatory methods such as parasite culture, DNA sequencing, or a composite reference standard were not available.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Associations were assessed using Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. Lesion counts were analysed using the Chi-square goodness-of-fit test. Sensitivity and specificity (95% CI) were calculated using nested PCR as reference, and agreement was assessed using Cohen's Kappa. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism v10 (p < 0.05).

Results

Discussion

In endemic CL areas, clinicians make a CL diagnosis based on the suggestive skin lesions and a history of exposure to sand fly bites. However, the diagnosis of CL can sometimes be challenging because the lesion mimics both infectious and malignant conditions.

Direct smear examination of CL

The use of light microscopy to visualize the amastigote form of the parasite in Giemsa stained smear taken from the edge of the lesion, remains the method of choice for laboratory diagnosis of CL. The edge of the lesion contains amastigotes, whereas the centre consists of necrotic debris, crust, and slough; this is the reason the lesion edge is selected for the preparation of the direct skin smear (Arcari et al., 1990). According to current finding, amastigotes form of the parasite detected in 64 (75.29%) of direct smear and the sensitivity of microscope was 76.19%. The direct microscopic technique, employed for the diagnosis of CL have modest to low rates of sensitivity, may be due to insufficient numbers of parasite in samples (Marfurt et al., 2003). Furthermore, the specificity of microscopic technique can be accepted to be 100%, but its sensitivity can change according to the people taking samples from the lesion, the people checking the smear, and amount of parasites in the lesion aspirate. Sensitivity of the microscopic examination has been reported to be ranging from 17% to 83% (Bensoussan et al., 2006). Therefore, the diagnosis can be difficult in the regions where CL is not endemic and with the cases that are lack of experienced staff (Çulha et al., 2020).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Different serologic assays such as ELISA, IFAT, Direct Agglutination Test (DAT), and immunoblotting have been evaluated and used for routine diagnosis of visceral leishmaniasis (Hatam et al., 2009; Mikaeili et al., 2007). The use of serological tests for the diagnosis of CL has also been evaluated in a few of studies (Jensen et al., 1996; Romero et al., 2004).

The principal objective of the current study was to evaluate the performance of ELISA in diagnosis of CL in Garmian area. Findings of the study demonstrated the sensitivity and specificity of ELISA technique showed a positive percent agreement of 53.6% with nested PCR, while specificity could not be reliably estimated in this design. Previous studies which evaluated the efficacy of serological methods in diagnosis of CL have generated variable results. The reported sensitivity for CL is ranging from 23% to 95% (Castellano et al., 2010; Szargiki et al., 2009; Zeyrek et al., 2007).

The main line of defense against infection in CL is the cellular immune response, which is responsible for the clinical spectrum of leishmaniasis, which ranges from localized CL to its progressive form, diffuse CL (Tapia et al., 1994). The humoral immune response occurs only during the active phase of infection, with the appearance of low titers of antibodies that disappear some months after the end of treatment, representing a temporary response (Masuda et al., 1989; Behin and Jacques, 1984).

Zeyrek et al. (2007) reported a sensitivity of 78.4% and specificity of 69.3% for an ELISA system for diagnosis of CL in Turkey. These findings are contradicting with the findings of current study. Also, the present results are inconsistent with several studies such as: Romero et al. (2004) reported a sensitivity of 89% for an ELISA system with antigen of L. mexicana and sensitivity of 71% with antigen of L. braziliensis. Jensen et al. (1996) reported an ELISA test with a sensitivity of 67% in 33 L. major infected CL patients, by using a sequence specific peptide antigen. Sarkari et al. (2014) reported a sensitivity and specificity of IgG and IgM were of 83.6% and 84.7% and specificity of 62.7% and 54.6%, respectively by ELISA technique.

Furthermore, Leishmania infantum ELISA (Vircell, Spain) is an indirect assay detecting anti-Leishmania IgG and IgM antibodies. Its limited sensitivity in localized cutaneous leishmaniasis and the possibility of cross-reactivity have now been discussed. We emphasize that ELISA results should be interpreted alongside clinical, parasitological, and molecular findings. Serological testing was included in this comparative evaluation not because it is recommended for localized CL, but to document empirically—within this endemic population—the poor diagnostic performance of serology, thereby cautioning clinicians against reliance on ELISA as a standalone test.

Molecular diagnosis

The use of a highly sensitive molecular technique to diagnose CL is important for evaluating disease and prescribing an effective treatment as well as for helping to prevent the spread of the disease. As CL, especially chronic CL, might be confused with a number of granulomatous skin diseases, differential diagnosis is necessary to exclude skin disease such as basal cell carcinomas (Çulha et al., 2020). The conventional method is unreliable and not sufficiently sensitive and is also unable to distinguish the parasites at a species level. Consequently, a technique with greater sensitivity for detection of the disease is essential. This sensitive technique would also be valuable for epidemiological studies (Blum et al., 2004; Schönian et al., 2003).

The conventional PCR (CPCR) method using primers R332 and R221 to amplifies a region of about 602 bp in ssurRNA of Leishmania genome, has previously been evaluated in the diagnosis of leishmaniasis and it has been demonstrated that it can amplify DNA of all Leishmania species (Mathis and Deplazes, 1995; van Eys et al., 1992); speciation, which may be important for treatment considerations, cannot be made here. This assay was confirmed in the present work by performing CPCR with DNA extracted from clinical samples. When using clinical samples, CPCR showed a higher sensitivity than that of classical method. Indeed, the positivity rate of PCR in present study was 83.53%, compared with 75.29% for microscopic examination. In addition, 7 (33.33%) of 21 suspected cases, not confirmed by classical methods, were found positive by CPCR.

The results obtained here, in particular with regard to sensitivity, compare well with previous evaluations. Although the sensitivity observed by different authors may vary from 60% (Mathis and Deplazes, 1995) to 100% (Belli et al., 1998), PCR has been found to have a great impact in the diagnosis of CL, with a sensitivity higher than classical methods including microscopic examination (Rodriguez et al., 2002; Lopez et al., 1993).

CPCR with the mentioned primers can amplify Leishmania species, Leptomonas and Crithidia species (Cruz et al., 2013). This is a major disadvantage of the CPCR since Leptomonas and Crithidia can co-exist with Leishmania and leads to misdiagnosis even if they are non-pathogenic to humans (Ahuja et al., 2015). On other hand, the quality and quantity of DNA in some clinical samples may not be sufficient to generate enough amplicon in first round PCR. These problems were mitigated by using the internal primer pair HMT-F and HMT-R (Tawfeeq and Ali, 2022a), which amplify a region nested between the R221 and R332 sites and were used as the inner primers of the nested PCR (NPCR) method. The region amplified by HMT-F and HMT-R is specific only to Leishmania genus and therefore it excludes the amplification of Leptomonas and Crithidia species according to the BLAST search carried out on published GenBank data. For that reason, the NPCR are 100% specific for detection of Leishmania parasites.

The current results revealed thatof the 21 microscopy-negative cases, 20of these cases, together with conventional-PCR-negative cases, were reclassified as positive by nested PCR (nPCR): specifically, 20 of the 21 microscopy-negative cases and 13 of the 14 conventional-PCR-negative cases were nPCR-positive, the single remaining negative in each comparison corresponding to the one nested-PCR-negative patient in the cohort. These findings demonstrate that nested PCR is a highly sensitive and accurate technique for detecting Leishmania in clinical isolates. This observation is consistent with the findings reported by Tawfeeq and Ali (2022a). However, this conclusion should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively small sample size, and further studies involving larger cohorts are required to confirm these results.

Assessment and comparison of diagnostic assays are extremely important in order to determine the best investigation method. Nowadays, microscopic detection and culture of parasites are still the key diagnostic technique used in many areas where leishmaniasis is endemic (Pourmohammadi et al., 2010). While these techniques are specific for detecting leishmaniasis, they are not sensitive (Bensoussan et al., 2006). Therefore, DNA-based techniques has become the method of choice and it considered valuable criteria for differentiating specifically between various species of Leishmania (Mirahmadi et al., 2018).

Several meta-analyses report that PCR has consistently high diagnostic accuracy compared with conventional methods, with pooled sensitivity often above 90% and high specificity, supporting the robustness of molecular approaches in clinical settings. For example, a systematic review and meta-analysis of molecular and immunological diagnostic tests for human leishmaniasis found that PCR-based assays demonstrated excellent sensitivity and specificity for tegumentary forms of the disease, confirming that molecular methods outperform traditional serological tests in many contexts (Candia-Puma et al., 2025).

Another meta-analysis focusing specifically on cutaneous leishmaniasis reported that PCR assays generally exhibit very high pooled sensitivity (∼0.95) and specificity (∼0.91), highlighting their reliability as diagnostic tools across different specimen types (Mesa et al., 2020).

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. A major methodological limitation is the use of nested PCR as a comparative reference method rather than a definitive gold standard. Although nested PCR is highly sensitive, the absence of independent confirmatory techniques such as parasite culture, sequencing, or a composite reference standard limits the ability to determine true diagnostic accuracy. In addition, the presence of only one nested PCR-negative case resulted in an extremely small reference-negative group, thereby compromising the reliability of specificity estimates and leading to statistically unstable and likely overestimated specificity values (100%) for microscopy and conventional PCR. Furthermore, the study was limited by incomplete clinical characterization of lesions. While anatomical location was documented and included in the analysis, other important clinical parameters such as lesion duration, size, and the presence of secondary infection or inflammation were not consistently recorded. This may affect the validity of analyses involving lesion type and number; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. A further limitation is that a subset of enrolled patients had already commenced antileishmanial therapy at sampling; because the treatment agent and duration were not systematically recorded, this potential confounder could not be quantified. Prior therapy can reduce parasite load and thereby lower the yield of direct microscopy and conventional PCR, which may partly explain their lower agreement with nested PCR.

Conclusion

Finally, microscopic examination is insufficiently sensitive for detection of Leishmania. ELISA showed the lowest agreement with nested PCR and is disappointingly low and unreliable for CL diagnosis. Molecular methods are reliable and powerful methods to detect and identify Leishmania species from clinical samples. nPCR is a sensitive and accurate method for diagnosing Leishmania infection, especially in cases with few parasites or chronic lesions.
