Incidence and risk factors for postpartum infections: A Norwegian cohort study, 2012–2020
Authors: Kjerstine Røe, Ingvil Krarup Sørbye, Cecilie Fredvik Torkildsen, Anders Engeland, Hilde Marie Engjom, Roy Miodini Nilsen, Kari Klungsøyr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFew epidemiological studies have examined postpartum infections in the Nordic countries. This study aimed to assess the incidence and risk factors for postpartum infections in Norway.Material and MethodsWe conducted a historical cohort study including all deliveries in Norway from 2012 to 2020, using linked nationwide data from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway, the Norwegian Patient Registry, and Statistics Norway. We defined two outcomes: endometritis/puerperal sepsis (O85, International Classification of Diseases 10th version (ICD‐10); the code does not differentiate between endometritis and puerperal sepsis, and endometritis predominates) and other postpartum infections (O86, ICD‐10). Associations were estimated as relative risks (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using log‐binomial regression.ResultsAmong 516 874 deliveries, we identified 5942 cases (1.15%) of endometritis/puerperal sepsis (O85) and 8833 (1.71%) of other postpartum infections (O86). The incidence of endometritis/puerperal sepsis increased over time from 0.93% in 2012 to 1.38% in 2020 (test for trend, p < 0.001). After adjusting for all covariates, delivery‐related factors demonstrated the strongest associations with both outcomes, especially emergency cesarean section (aRRO85 2.8; 95% CI 2.6–3.0/aRRO86 5.1; 95% CI 4.8–5.5) and postpartum hemorrhage (aRRO85 3.2; 2.9–3.4/aRRO86 2.0; 1.9–2.2). Risk factors not directly related to delivery, such as multifetal gestation, obesity, nulliparity and maternal single status, were also associated with both outcomes (aRRs in the range of 1.2–2.5). Chorioamnionitis lies on the same infectious continuum as postpartum endometritis and is treated with intrapartum antibiotics, usually discontinued after delivery unless persisting signs of infection. We found a strong association between chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection and postpartum infections (aRRO85 5.7; 5.1–6.4/aRRO86 3.2; 2.9–3.6). Alongside the strong associations with postpartum infections, we also observed an increasing incidence over time in chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection (p < 0.001) and postpartum hemorrhage (p < 0.001).ConclusionsPostpartum infections are common, and the incidence of endometritis/puerperal sepsis is increasing. For all postpartum infections, delivery‐related factors demonstrate the strongest associations. Postpartum hemorrhage and chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection may represent key targets for prevention/prevention of progression. Revision of the coding system is necessary to enable differentiation between puerperal sepsis and endometritis for better surveillance.

INTRODUCTION

Maternal sepsis is an important cause of maternal mortality and severe morbidity worldwide. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 With early identification and timely treatment, maternal death and severe morbidity are largely preventable. 4 , 7 Approximately two‐thirds of maternal sepsis deaths occur postpartum. 8 Postpartum sepsis, also termed puerperal sepsis, most commonly arises from endometritis, surgical site infections after cesarean section (CS), perineal infections, and urinary tract infections. 9 , 10 In the Nordic countries, epidemiological data on such postpartum infections are limited, and effective surveillance is challenged by heterogeneous definitions, insufficient coding systems, and the absence of dedicated monitoring programs. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 A key challenge is the lack of differentiation between puerperal sepsis and endometritis without sepsis in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding system, where both conditions are classified under the same code (O85).

Understanding risk factors is essential for preventing postpartum infections, which may serve as precursors to puerperal sepsis. However, isolating the impact of individual risk factors is challenging due to complex clinical interactions. Various factors, including sociodemographic characteristics, pregestational and gestational diabetes, obesity, nulliparity, multifetal gestation, CS, instrumental delivery, and prelabor rupture of membranes (PROM), have been associated with postpartum infections. 16 , 17 , 18 Evidence on induction of labor and prolonged labor is limited, partly due to overlapping intrapartum risk factors. 19 , 20 Retained pregnancy products and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) have been associated with postpartum infections in several studies 16 , 21 , 22 ; however, current evidence has been considered insufficient to support routine antibiotic prophylaxis in clinical guidelines. 23 , 24 , 25

Chorioamnionitis lies on the same infectious continuum as postpartum endometritis, although intrapartum fever may also have non‐infectious causes. Distinguishing between non‐infectious fever and clinical infection is challenging, and evidence supporting diagnostic criteria for chorioamnionitis and antibiotic treatment protocols is limited. 26 Persistent intrapartum fever may signal ascending Group B streptococcal (GBS) infection with risk of severe neonatal sepsis, and intrapartum antibiotics may serve both therapeutic and prophylactic purposes. 27 Throughout the study period, Norwegian guidelines recommended intrapartum antibiotics for chorioamnionitis, persistent fever, and as prevention in GBS risk, emergency CS, planned CS with risk factors, and fourth‐degree obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI) repair. GBS prevention follows a risk‐based strategy where risk is identified in women with GBS urinary tract infection and/or bacteriuria during pregnancy, or with a history of neonatal GBS disease. However, without universal screening, many GBS carriers may remain unidentified. In cases of PROM, rapid real‐time intrapartum GBS testing is not routine, and antibiotics are only indicated with GBS risk or clinical infection. Antibiotics are usually discontinued after delivery unless clinical signs of maternal infection persist. 28 , 29

During the last decade, the rates of induction of labor and PPH have increased. 19 , 30 It is thus vital to strengthen our understanding of risk factors associated with postpartum infections to inform and update clinical guidelines on prevention and treatment. The aim of this study was to investigate the incidence and risk factors for postpartum infections, including puerperal sepsis, using data on all births in Norway, 2012–2020.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Data source and study population

This population‐based national historical cohort study used data from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway (MBRN), the Norwegian Patient Registry (NPR), and Statistics Norway. MBRN collects data on all live births, stillbirths, and late abortions (from 12 gestational weeks) in Norway through mandatory notifications, reported at discharge after delivery using a structured notification form with predefined checkboxes and free‐text fields. Free text is coded at the registry using ICD. The NPR contains codes for diagnoses (ICD) and procedures (Nomesco Classification of Surgical and Medical Procedures, NCMP/NCSP) from hospital stays and outpatient clinics in specialist care. Statistics Norway is responsible for collecting, analyzing, and publishing official statistics. Additional information on the registries is available in S1Text.

From MBRN, we extracted data on sociodemographic factors, pre‐pregnant maternal health, and pregnancy, labor, and postpartum factors. From NPR, we collected relevant codes for obstetric infections and procedures related to delivery. We did not have information on infections treated in primary care. From Statistics Norway, we collected data on maternal education level and travel time from home to the nearest obstetric institution.

The study population included all women giving birth in Norway, 2012–2020.

Definition of outcome

We defined two postpartum infection outcomes (Table S1 and Figure S1). The first outcome was “endometritis/puerperal sepsis”, defined by the registered ICD‐10 code O85 (including endometritis, peritonitis, and puerperal sepsis) in the NPR and MBRN, and by two variables from the MBRN‐notification form: a checkbox for sepsis and a checkbox for persistent fever >38.5°C after delivery. Puerperal sepsis could not be assessed separately, as the ICD‐10 does not differentiate puerperal sepsis from endometritis without sepsis; the code O85 is used for both. Based on a validation study from a Norwegian hospital cohort, in which approximately 20% of O85 cases were classified as puerperal sepsis, we assume that most cases coded with O85 represent endometritis. 11

The second outcome was “other postpartum infections” defined by the ICD‐10 code O86 in NPR and MBRN. This code includes surgical site infections after CS, perineal and urinary tract infections, pyrexia without known cause, and other postpartum infections after delivery. Although O86 subcategories are defined by four‐character codes, only the three‐character code was accessible in our data, and we could therefore not differentiate specific conditions within this group. Figure S2 presents statistics from the NPR, showing the distribution within this diagnostic group for our study period. More than 50% were surgical wound infections.

For incidence estimates, cases registered with both O85 and O86 were included in both categories. For regression analyses, they were classified as O85 only, ensuring that each case contributed to only one outcome group (Figures 1 and S1).

Risk factors

Potential risk factors studied: Sociodemographic factors; maternal age at delivery, highest attained education level, employment status, partnership status, and travel time from the woman's home to the nearest obstetric unit. Pre‐pregnancy maternal health: chronic kidney disease, rheumatoid arthritis, asthma, chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, and body mass index (BMI). Pregnancy factors: parity, smoking in pregnancy, multifetal gestation, gestational diabetes, and hypertensive pregnancy disorders. Delivery‐related factors: induction of labor, PROM, prolonged labor, mode of delivery, severe PPH (estimated blood loss >1500 mL and/or blood transfusion within 24 h after delivery), retained pregnancy products, and perineal trauma such as episiotomy and OASI.

Administration of antibiotics during labor is not specifically reported to the registries. We therefore created a proxy variable for intrapartum infection and/or antibiotics based on national guidelines for routine administration of antibiotics during labor, 28 , 29 combined with available registry data (Table S1). The factors CS and OASI were excluded from the proxy variable, as they were already included as separate covariates. Our final proxy variable “intrapartum infection and/or antibiotics” had three categories: (1) persistent fever and/or GBS risk (recommended treatment with narrow‐spectrum antibiotics, preferably penicillin), (2) chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection (recommended treatment with broader‐spectrum antibiotics, preferably penicillin and gentamicin combined), and (3) no indication for intrapartum antibiotic treatment (reference category).

All factors were treated as categorical variables with further details described in Table S1.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis on intrapartum infection and/or antibiotics during labor, both including and excluding this variable from the fully adjusted Model 2. This was done for two reasons: (1) intrapartum infection overlaps with the outcome postpartum infections, and (2) the variable was based on imperfect proxy data.

Missing data

The gradual implementation of the revised electronic MBRN notification from 2007, which included maternal height and weight, led to substantial missing BMI data, decreasing from 50% in 2012 to 5% in 2020. Missing data on maternal smoking (15%), BMI (overall 22%), and working status (16%) were handled using multiple imputation with 30 datasets. Smoking and working status were binary variables and were imputed using logistic regression models. BMI was treated as a categorical variable with six categories (Table 1) and was imputed using multinomial logistic regression. All covariates and outcome variables as well as birth weight were included in the imputation models.

Statistical analysis

We estimated the incidence of puerperal infections for the entire study period and by calendar year to assess temporal trends. Temporal trends were evaluated using log‐binomial regression models with birth year (from 2012) as an ordered categorical term with each year as a category, testing for trends by Wald test. Associations between risk factors and outcomes were estimated as adjusted relative risks (aRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using generalized linear models with log link. To account for correlations in data when mothers had more than one pregnancy during the study period, we used clustered robust standard errors with the mother's ID as the cluster.

We assessed risk factors for postpartum infections through stepwise building of regression models: Starting with crude estimates for each factor alone, we then adjusted for time period (year of birth) and sociodemographic factors before adding pre‐pregnancy factors, and finally pregnancy factors. Recognizing the close clinical interactions between delivery‐related factors, we employed two models for their analyses: Model 1 gave RR estimates representing the total effect of each specific delivery‐related factor as the exposure, adjusted for year of birth, sociodemographic, pre‐pregnancy and pregnancy factors as potential confounders. In Model 2, we also added all delivery‐related factors into a fully adjusted model to explore whether any indicators act as independent predictors when considered jointly. RR estimates from Model 2 are therefore adjusted for both confounders and intermediate variables to a varying extent depending on each factor's position along the causal pathway (Figure S3), indicating the controlled direct effect accounting for relationships between factors. Due to the clinical interactions between the delivery‐related factors, we assessed multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor and Spearman correlation matrix and found no indication of problematic multicollinearity in our data. To evaluate associations in vaginal deliveries, we excluded all CS and repeated analyses. For the O86 group, this would eliminate abdominal wound infections and thus reduce the heterogeneity in this group.

Finally, to understand the temporal trends in the outcomes, we evaluated temporal trends (2012–2020) for all delivery‐related factors using log‐binomial regression models as described above.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Visualizations were created in Microsoft Excel and R (version 4.3.2).

RESULTS

Among a total of 516 874 deliveries to 357 004 unique mothers between 2012 and 2020, endometritis/sepsis (O85) was identified after 5942 deliveries (1.15%) and other postpartum infections (O86) after 8833 deliveries (1.71%). The incidence of endometritis/sepsis increased from 0.93% in 2012 to 1.38% in 2020 (p < 0.001, Figure 1). Other postpartum infections showed stable incidence. Stratifying the full cohort by mode of delivery, the highest incidence of both outcomes was observed among births following emergency CS, with 3.1% for endometritis/sepsis and 5.8% for other postpartum infections (Figure 1). There were 1349 cases registered with both O85 and O86. After estimating incidence with temporal trends, these were kept in the O85 category, leaving 7484 women in the final O86 category (Figure S1).

Characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Postpartum infections were more common among younger, single, less educated, and non‐employed mothers. Affected mothers were more often nulliparous, obese, and had a slightly higher prevalence of chronic conditions such as hypertension, asthma, and pregestational diabetes. We examined travel time to the nearest delivery unit as a potential risk factor, but found no association with either outcome and excluded it from further analyses.

Table 2 presents risk estimates for sociodemographic, pre‐pregnancy and pregnancy factors. Multifetal gestation (aRRO85 1.87; aRRO86 2.04), nulliparity (aRRO85 1.60; aRRO86 1.89) and obesity (BMI >40: aRRO85 1.41; aRRO86 2.45) had the strongest associations with both outcomes. The risk linked to obesity was greater for other postpartum infections than for endometritis/sepsis and increased progressively with increasing BMI. Smoking during pregnancy was not associated with either outcome.

DISCUSSION

In this large national population‐based historical cohort study, the incidence of endometritis/sepsis was 1.15% with increasing rates over time, while the incidence of other postpartum infections was stable at 1.71%. Delivery‐related factors showed the strongest associations with both outcomes, particularly chorioamnionitis, PPH, and CS. Maternal sociodemographic, pre‐pregnancy, and pregnancy‐related factors, such as nulliparity, multifetal gestation, and obesity, were also associated.

There are few recent epidemiologic studies regarding the incidence of postpartum infections in the Nordic countries. A Swedish registry‐based cohort study reported a low prevalence of postpartum sepsis at 2.4 per 10 000 women. 31 Another Swedish study, based on self‐reported patient data, showed a higher prevalence of endometritis (2.0%), urinary tract infections (3.0%) and perineal wound infections (1.8%); however, cases were not confirmed by other sources such as medical records. 13 A Norwegian single hospital‐based cohort study of 5182 deliveries found an incidence of 1.3% of endometritis/sepsis and 1.5% of other postpartum infections (including postpartum urinary tract infections and perineal or abdominal wound infections), 11 findings similar to our study. In that cohort, 20% of the O85‐cases were evaluated as puerperal sepsis, 5% as peritonitis, and the remaining as endometritis. It is reasonable to assume a similar distribution of conditions within our O85‐category. At the same hospital, the occurence of surgical site infections was 0.4% of planned and 5.4% of emergency CS. 32 Another Norwegian study reported 8.3% surgical site infections after any CS; however, this included self‐reported infections. 12 Despite differing definitions, periods, and study designs, findings largely align with our estimates of postpartum infection incidence.

Due to clinical interactions between risk factors, isolating the effect of individual exposures is challenging. We present both crude and adjusted estimates to describe associations. For delivery‐related factors, Model 1 adjusts for prelabor factors to highlight the total effect of each delivery factor while adjusting for potential confounders, while Model 2 additionally includes all delivery‐related factors, offering insight into the most predictive factors when considered together. Some of the delivery factors will be mediators of other delivery factors (Figure S3), with the risk of introducing collider bias when adjusting for the mediator. However, since all the delivery factors are very interrelated and occur within a limited time window, the risk of collider bias is likely small.

Pre‐pregnancy obesity and emergency CS were strong risk factors for both outcomes, in line with former studies. 31 , 32 , 33 One Norwegian study found that women with pregestational obesity had nearly threefold higher odds of developing surgical site infection after cesarean delivery. 32 The results from our study indicate that obesity poses a greater risk for other postpartum infections than for endometritis/sepsis, with risk increasing progressively with higher BMI. CS is also a potential mediator, as obese women are at greater risk of emergency CS (in our data, the risk of emergency CS was more than doubled in women with BMI >35 relative to women with BMI 19–24). We expected stronger associations between diabetes and infection. However, we combined all diabetes types to increase statistical power, and gestational diabetes comprised the majority, while previous research only found increased risk with type 1 diabetes. 31

For induction of labor, we only found a slightly elevated risk of both outcomes, consistent with the results from a recent Danish cohort study reporting an adjusted odds ratio of 1.25 (95% CI 1.21–1.27) for any postpartum infection, but no association with postpartum sepsis. 19 Prolonged labor was associated with both outcomes in Model 1, but not when adjusting for all delivery‐related factors (Model 2). Emergency CS could be an important mediator here. However, the association also disappeared in Model 2 when analyzing vaginal deliveries only, indicating interaction with other delivery‐related factors too.

The association between PPH and postpartum infections is supported by several studies; however, without consistent finding. 21 , 22 , 32 CS represents one pathway linking PPH and infection, as it is a known risk factor for both conditions; however, the risk associated with PPH remained elevated in Model 2, where we adjusted for CS. Other potential mechanisms include hemodynamic instability, tissue hypoperfusion, coagulation disorders, immunological dysregulation, and invasive procedures, such as manual removal of the placenta, surgical revision, and uterine tamponade, introducing vaginal and intestinal bacterial flora into the uterus. Severity and management of PPH with invasive procedures, blood transfusion, and anemia are suggested to be important contributing factors, 11 , 22 , 31 and prophylactic antibiotics might be beneficial; for example, if invasive treatment for PPH is needed. 22 One study reported no significant association between PPH and uncomplicated sepsis but a more than fourfold increase in odds of severe sepsis/septic shock. 21 Another study found no significant association between PPH and postpartum sepsis. 34 Discrepancies may be explained by variations in study design, definitions of PPH, population characteristics, and clinical management. This underscores the need for further research to clarify the association between PPH and postpartum infections, causal mediators, and the potential role of prophylactic antibiotics.

Evidence from systematic reviews indicates that there is insufficient support for routine continuation of antibiotic therapy after delivery in women with clinical chorioamnionitis, highlighting that the current evidence base is limited and that high‐quality trials are needed to define optimal treatment protocols. 26 , 27 Accordingly, Norwegian guidelines recommend discontinuing antibiotics after delivery unless clinical signs of infection persist. 28 However, our study shows a sixfold increased risk of endometritis/sepsis in women with chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection, indicating that extended antibiotic treatment could be beneficial. Our results also demonstrate an association between chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection and other postpartum infections. We suggest that future studies evaluate the association between chorioamnionitis and any postpartum infection including potential benefit from increased duration of antibiotic treatment.

It is relevant to consider whether the observed increase in endometritis/sepsis over time may partly reflect shifts in other delivery‐related factors. CS remains a strong risk factor, yet rates have been stable. In contrast, we noted an increasing prevalence of factors closely linked to endometritis/sepsis such as chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection, PPH, and retained pregnancy products. Temporal shifts in incidence might also be due to changes in diagnostics and/or reporting to the registries. However, PPH has shown good validity in the MBRN 35 and an internal report on chorioamnionitis registered in the NPR has also shown good validity. This suggests that the increasing incidence of PPH and chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection is real and may contribute to higher postpartum infection rates, representing potential targets for prophylactic interventions.

The main strength of this study is the large cohort with information based on mandatory reporting to national registries on all deliveries in Norway, minimizing selection bias and providing statistical power. The quality of registered data in the MBRN has been evaluated and found satisfactory for several pregnancy‐ and delivery‐related factors, 36 , 37 , 38 and studies on non‐obstetric factors indicate generally high‐quality data in NPR. 39 , 40 A report describing the validity of severe pregnancy and delivery complications in the registries, reports generally high PPV but low sensitivity in MBRN, while generally high sensitivity but lower PPVs in NPR. 35

However, registry‐based studies are subject to data quality limitations, including coding errors, differences in documentation practices and reporting routines, with potential for misclassification. A hospital‐based validation of the use of the code O85 showed high sensitivity (91%) and acceptable PPV (81%) for postpartum endometritis, but markedly lower PPV for puerperal sepsis (20.3%), 11 likely reflecting the predominance of endometritis coded by O85. The lack of differentiation between endometritis and puerperal sepsis in the ICD‐10 made separate analysis on these two conditions impossible, emphasizing a need for revision of the coding system. There was also potential overlap between the outcomes. For example, a surgical site infection (O86) may progress to sepsis (O85), or conditions may coexist, such as endometritis (O85) alongside urinary tract infection (O86). Women diagnosed with both O85 and O86 were, in our study, classified under O85, which indicates the most severe condition. Summarized, there may be some misclassification in our study. This is likely non‐differential since the data are collected prospectively and the risk factors are registered before the outcome and mostly notified by different clinicians. Any misclassification will therefore likely attenuate the observed effect estimates.

The exposure variable for intrapartum clinical infection and/or antibiotics was created as a proxy variable and not validated, since actual antibiotic administration and treatment details were lacking. This exposure overlaps with the outcome (postpartum infections). When studying PPH as a risk factor, reversed causality is possible since severe hemorrhage may result from intrapartum sepsis. To address this, we conducted sensitivity analyses without the proxy variable included in the models, and results for the other delivery‐related factors, including PPH, remained consistent. Codes for surgical revision of the uterus were included under retained pregnancy products, although such procedures may relate to both PPH treatment and postpartum infection. To minimize misclassification, we excluded NPR‐recorded revisions likely reflecting readmissions due to infection and only included suspected retained products and revisions reported to the MBRN during delivery or postpartum until discharge. The timing supports the classification of surgical revision as PPH‐related, as intrauterine infections postpartum in need of surgical revision typically develop after some days.

Finally, results from registry‐based studies can suggest causal relationships but cannot be interpreted as direct evidence for clinical management. Further studies with broader and more detailed evidence are needed for clinical decision‐making.

CONCLUSION

Postpartum infections were relatively common, and the incidence of endometritis/puerperal sepsis increased over time. Delivery‐related factors were the main risk factors for postpartum infections, particularly CS and PPH. Chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection was strongly associated with endometritis/puerperal sepsis. Increasing rates of chorioamnionitis/intrapartum infection and PPH may partly explain the rising incidence of endometritis/puerperal sepsis and represent potential targets for prevention, including prevention of progression. Further research is needed to clarify recommendations regarding antibiotic prophylaxis and treatment in complications like chorioamnionitis and PPH. To enable separate estimation of puerperal sepsis incidence, revision of the coding system (ICD) is necessary.
