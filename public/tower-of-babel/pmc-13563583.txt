Treatment-resistant Parkinson's disease psychosis responding to xanomeline-trospium: A case report
Authors: Andrew Farah, Karen Timbrook-Dillow, Heather Garrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report the case of an 83-year-old male with Parkinsonism and severe psychosis characterized by persistent daytime and nighttime hallucinations associated with marked fear and distress. His psychosis failed to respond to therapeutic trials of quetiapine, brexpiprazole, and pimavanserin. He was referred specifically for a trial of clozapine therapy, which the patient and caregiver declined due to its risk profile. Treatment with xanomeline–trospium was initiated at 50/20 mg twice daily and resulted in substantial improvement, including resolution of nighttime hallucinations within two weeks. He further achieved a near complete resolution of psychotic symptoms within three weeks of a dose escalation to 100/20 mg twice daily. To our knowledge, this is the first published case of Parkinson's disease psychosis successfully treated with xanomeline–trospium, a novel M1/M4 muscarinic receptor agonist-based therapy. The patient's psychosis proved refractory to three pharmacologically distinct therapies—quetiapine, a low-affinity, rapidly dissociating dopamine D2 receptor antagonist; brexpiprazole, a dopamine D2 partial agonist; and pimavanserin, a selective 5-HT2A receptor inverse agonist—before improving with xanomeline–trospium, whose antipsychotic effects are mediated through central M1/M4 muscarinic receptor agonism. This case supports the further investigation of muscarinic receptor agonism as a therapeutic strategy for Parkinson's disease psychosis.

Case

An 83-year-old man with a ten-year history of Parkinson's disease was referred for treatment of severe Parkinson's-associated psychosis characterized by persistent visual hallucinations of a violent and persecutory content. Hallucinations occurred throughout both daytime and nighttime hours and were frequently associated with intense fear and agitation. The patient repeatedly reported that crowds of individuals were attacking him and shooting at him. These experiences were perceived as real and resulted in marked distress, impaired quality of life, and substantial caregiver burden.

Cognitive assessment revealed no evidence of dementia. The patient remained fully oriented and demonstrated preserved memory, attention, and executive functioning on clinical examination. Although performance on the Mini-Mental State Examination was compromised by inability to complete the written items and his limited articulation, these deficits were attributable to Parkinsonian motor impairment rather than cognitive dysfunction. Thus, no major neurocognitive disorder was clinically evident. This distinction is relevant because psychotic symptoms in Parkinson's disease are frequently discussed in the context of Parkinson's dementia or Lewy body disease; however, our patient exhibited Parkinson's disease psychosis in the absence of cognitive impairment.

Medication trials initiated by his previous providers had failed to provide any benefit. Quetiapine had been gradually titrated to 350 mg/day without a reduction in hallucination frequency or severity. Subsequent treatment with brexpiprazole was initiated and sequentially increased to 1 mg/day, 2 mg/day, and ultimately 3 mg/day, without any noticeable improvement. Treatment with pimavanserin, 34 mg/day, likewise failed to produce any clinically meaningful reduction in psychotic symptoms after 3 months of compliance.

The patient was thus referred to psychiatry specifically for the initiation of clozapine therapy, given its established efficacy in treatment-refractory Parkinson's psychosis. However, a trial of clozapine was declined after risks and side effects were discussed. The patient and caregiver were particularly concerned about the possibilities of agranulocytosis and orthostasis, which prompted consideration of alternative treatment options.

After discussing the risks, benefits, and, to that date, the lack of experience and evidence supporting its use in Parkinson's disease psychosis, informed consent was obtained from the patient and caregiver for an off-label trial of xanomeline-trospium.

Xanomeline-trospium was initiated at a dose of 50 mg/20 mg twice daily. Within two weeks of treatment initiation, the patient experienced a complete resolution of nighttime hallucinations, and was “finally able to sleep,” according to his caregiver. Residual hallucinations continued to occur during day, but they were noted by the caregiver as “non-threatening,” and were reduced in frequency and intensity. Continued treatment resulted in modest improvement, but not full resolution.

Xanomeline-trospium was escalated to 100 mg/20 mg twice daily, and by day 19 at this dose, the patient reported a near-complete resolution of both daytime and nighttime hallucinations. The effect has been sustained for over six months at the time of this submission. He reports occasionally (a few times a week) “thinking he sees someone in the room,” but that they “go away fairly soon…”.

The medication remains well tolerated. The caregiver was aware of the peripheral anticholinergic effects of the agent and is mindful of the possibility of constipation. This has been a concern before xanomeline-trospium therapy and managed well at baseline. The patient did report some blurry vision, which was addressed and resolved by instructing him to take the medication with food, rather than on an empty stomach. This served to lower the anticholinergic burden, by markedly decreases trospium exposure (an expected decrease in AUC by 85–90%) while increasing xanomeline exposure (by approximately 30%), particularly if after a high-fat meal [1].

Otherwise, no clinically significant adverse effects were reported, and no worsening of parkinsonian motor symptoms has been observed to date.

Discussion

At just over six months of xanomeline-trospium at 100 mg/20 mg twice daily, the patient remains essentially free of severe psychotic symptoms, reporting only “stray figures” in his visual field on occasion. The marked clinical response was particularly notable given the prior lack of benefit from quetiapine, brexpiprazole, and pimavanserin and the initial referral for clozapine treatment.

Psychosis affects up to 60% of patients with Parkinson's disease during the course of illness and in our patient, these symptoms were clearly distressing, and the most disabling nonmotor complications of his disease. Visual hallucinations are the most common manifestation and are associated with increased caregiver burden, nursing home placement, and mortality [2].

Clozapine remains the treatment with the strongest evidence in Parkinson's disease psychosis and is recommended by multiple treatment guidelines [3], [4], [5]. However, the risks of orthostasis and blood dyscrasias may be of particular concern in the elderly, and in some circumstances, the requirement for ongoing hematologic monitoring may limit its practical use among patients with mobility limitations and caregiver dependence [6].

Quetiapine is frequently utilized because it lacks mandatory blood monitoring requirements and generally has minimal impact upon motor symptoms, although controlled studies have produced findings of inconsistent efficacy in Parkinson's psychosis [3], [4]. Pimavanserin, a selective 5-HT2A inverse agonist, is the only medication approved specifically for Parkinson's related psychosis and has demonstrated efficacy in randomized controlled trials [7]. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of patients continue to experience persistent psychotic symptoms despite treatment [8]. Brexpiprazole is specifically FDA-approved to treat agitation associated with Alzheimer's dementia, but is not approved for dementia-related psychosis. One case report described a 71-year-old patient whose psychotic symptoms from probable Lewy body dementia benefited from brexpiprazole (at 4 mg per day) [9].

Xanomeline is a preferential M1/M4 muscarinic receptor agonist originally investigated for Alzheimer's disease, and subsequently combined with trospium to reduce peripheral cholinergic adverse effects [10], [11]. The xanomeline-trospium combination demonstrated significant antipsychotic efficacy in the EMERGENT clinical trial program and was approved for the treatment of schizophrenia in 2024 [12], [13], [14]. Its mechanism of action involves activation of central M1 and M4 receptors, which modulate striatal dopaminergic signaling, and thus provides antipsychotic effects without direct dopamine receptor blockade. This is clearly a theoretical advantage in Parkinson's disease, where worsening of parkinsonian motor symptoms limits the use of dopamine receptor-blocking antipsychotics [15].

To our knowledge, this is the first published report describing successful treatment of refractory Parkinson's disease psychosis with xanomeline-trospium. Our patient failed adequate trials of quetiapine, brexpiprazole, and pimavanserin, and was then referred for clozapine therapy. Because clozapine monitoring requirements were impractical, an off-label trial of xanomeline-trospium was undertaken. Treatment was associated with a near-complete remission of hallucinations that has persisted for more than six months without any worsening of motor symptoms.

Conclusion

Although a single case cannot establish efficacy, the sustained, near-complete resolution of psychosis—with only rare, transient, non-distressing residual hallucinations—following failure of three pharmacologically distinct therapies, without apparent worsening of motor symptoms, provides a rationale for further investigation of muscarinic receptor agonism as a therapeutic strategy for Parkinson's disease psychosis.

Ethics statement

This manuscript describes a single patient treated during the course of routine clinical care. The patient received off-label treatment after discussion of the risks, benefits, and the limited available evidence, and written informed consent was obtained from the patient and caregiver for both treatment and publication of this case report.
