Direct synthesis of α-functionalized amides via heteroatom–hydrogen insertion reactions using amide-sulfoxonium ylides
Authors: Haiting Wu, Yougen Xu, An Lin, Zhi Wang, Huanjun Chen, Xinwei Zhu, Yadong Gao, Lebin Su
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
α-Functionalized Si-, Ge-, B-, Se-, and S-amide moieties are present in many medicinally active molecules, but their synthesis remains challenging. Here, we demonstrate a high-throughput synthesis using amide-sulfoxonium ylides as carbene precursors in a Si–H, Ge–H, B–H, Se–H, and S–H insertion reactions to target a wide range of α-silyl, α-geryl, α-boryl, α-selenyl, and α-sulfur (hetero)amides. The process is featured as simple operation, mild conditions, broad substrate scope, high functional group compatibility, and excellent chemoselectivity. Both experimental and computational studies are conducted to explore the mechanisms underlying the formation of C–Si/Ge/B/Se/S bond. This research highlights the use of highly selective X–H insertion reactions with amide-sulfoxonium ylide-derived carbenes, paving the way for the preparation of diverse functional organosilane, organogermane, organoboron, organoselenium, and organosulfur compounds from accessible and bench-stable precursors.

Introduction

Organosilicons are extensively utilized in organic synthesis, pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, organic optoelectronic materials, and other fields1–7. Of particular interest, α-silyl carbonyl compounds have recently demonstrated versatility as synthons in various reactions8–11, such as Peterson olefination for alkene production, β-keto silane and silyl ketene acetal synthesis, and use for α-silyl ester initiators in group transfer polymerization. However, general and reliable approaches for the preparation of α-silyl carbonyl compounds are scarce. Traditionally, α-silyl carbonyl compounds are synthesized via C-silylation of lithium ester enolates/α-bromo esters with silyl chlorides12,13 (Fig. 1a), as well as the Lewis acid-catalyzed reactions of silyl ketenes14. These approaches often require strong bases or acids, involve multistep reaction steps, and/or harsh reaction conditions, thereby limiting their applicability. In the past decade, transition metal-catalyzed Si–H insertion reactions of α-diazo carbonyls have emerged as powerful synthetic tools for accessing α-silyl carbonyl compounds15–22 (Fig. 1b). Despite these advances, the carbene precursors applied in these Si–H bond insertion reactions are typically limited to α-diazo esters, which are air and moisture sensitive, in some cases, toxic, explosive, and difficult to prepare23,24. Consequently, the resulting organosilicon products often contain ester substitutions rather than more useful functional groups like amides, N-heterocycles, or ketones. Therefore, the development of efficient methods for the synthesis of functionalized α-silyl carbonyl compounds is highly desirable. Moreover, there have been successful examples of synthesizing functionalized organosilicons, such as heterocycle-substituted silanes and allyl silanes, through carbene insertion reactions using functionalized alkynes or cyclopropenes as precursors25–31. Sulfoxonium ylides are versatile synthons in organic synthesis32–35. In particular, sulfoxonium ylides are valuable carbene precursors utilized in a variety of reactions, including Johnson–Corey–Chaykovsky cyclopropanation, X–H bond insertions (X = N, O, S, B, halogen), cycloaddition, and intramolecular C–H bond insertions36–42. Compared to diazo compounds, sulfoxonium ylides are safer, easier to synthesize, less-toxic, and can create carbenes with greater structural diversity due to the absence of specific stabilization requirements. Recently, α-keto and vinyl sulfoxonium ylides have been widely utilized for various X–H (X = N, O, B, S, C) insertion reactions and cyclizations under iridium catalysis43–50. Despite considerable progress in the chemistry of α-keto and vinyl sulfoxonium ylides, the potential of amide-sulfoxonium ylides remains largely unexplored51–53. Like their α-keto counterparts, amide-sulfoxonium ylides are thermal stability but offer enhanced reactivity, likely due to the electron-donating nature of the nitrogen atom54. Notable advancements in the activation and functionalization of amide bonds have been achieved by the Szostak group and other chemists55–58. Considering the importance of α-silyl carbonyl compounds and our ongoing interest in amide and sulfoxonium ylide transformations59–61, herein, we report an efficient method for the highly selective Si–H, Ge–H, B–H, Se–H, and S–H bonds insertion reactions of amide-sulfoxonium ylides for general and modular synthesis of α‑silyl, α‑geryl, α-boryl, α-selenyl, and α-sulfur (hetero)amides in good to excellent yields (Fig. 1c). To the best of our knowledge, this method offers a straightforward and effective protocol for Si–H, Ge–H, and Se–H bonds insertion reactions involving sulfoxonium ylides. The process employs readily available starting materials, tolerates a broad array of functional groups, including halogen, trifluoromethyl, ketone, cyano, amino, phenol, azo, thioether, alkene, and a variety of heterocycles, proceeds operationally simple under mild conditions, and shows excellent selectivity. Significantly, this reaction is amenable to automated parallel medicinal chemistry (PMC) approaches, expanding synthetic routes for organosilanes and organogermanes. Additionally, we demonstrate its applicability in the preparation of complex molecules.

Results

Optimization of reaction conditions

We commenced our studies using amide-sulfoxonium ylide (1a) and triethylsilane (2a) as model substrates to validate the desired Si–H bond insertion reaction. Inspired by sulfoxonium ylide chemistry and considering carbene insertion reaction into X–H bonds, a series of different metal catalysts and solvents were screened through automated high-throughput experimentation (HTE) platform60 (Fig. 2, see Supplementary Table 1 for details of the HTE-optimization). To our delight, [Ir(COD)Cl]2 was found to be uniquely effective for this Si–H bond insertion reaction, affording the desired product 3 in 84% yield with excellent selectivity in 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE) under air at 30 °C. [Ir(COD)OMe]2 and Ir(COD)2BF4 also displayed good catalytic activity, while Ir(acac)3 was ineffective in this reaction. A range of other metal catalysts, such as Rh, Ru, Pd, Ag, Co, Cu, and Fe, were examined, some other unknown reactions were detected, but the desired Si–H bond insertion product 3 were not observed. Moreover, scale-up of the reaction from 5 μmol (microscale-HTE experiment) to 0.1 mmol scale was demonstrated in a similar 86% yield (Table 1, entry 1). Notably, both intramolecular carbene C–H insertion reaction (3’) and self-dimerization (3”) were almost suppressed. Further attempts to test different reaction temperatures revealed that 30 °C was the most efficient (Table 1, entries 2 and 3). In the absence of iridium catalyst, the reaction did not occur (Table 1, entry 4). Substrate scope

With the optimal reaction conditions in hand, the scope of this iridium(I)-catalyzed selective Si–H bond insertion reaction was then investigated (Fig. 3). In general, symmetric and asymmetric substituted diarylamide-sulfoxonium ylides were both well tolerated under the reaction conditions. Various electron-donating and -withdrawing functional groups, such as Me, OMe, NMe2, F, Cl, Br, CF3, and azo group, were compatible with the standard reaction conditions and gave the corresponding products (4–14) in 60–91% yields. Amide-sulfoxonium ylides bearing Me at meta or ortho positions of the phenyl ring, giving the desired products (15–17) in high yields, respectively. Moreover, the disubstituted arylamide-sulfoxonium ylide could also react smoothly to furnish the corresponding product (18) in 76% yield. Delightfully, a variety of substituted cycle or S- and O-heterocycle amide-sulfoxonium ylides (21–29), including alkyl (21 and 22), methyl (23), alkenyl (24), chloride (26), cyano (27), and ketone (28) were all compatible with this reaction, with the electron-poor aryl groups showing better reactivity. Furthermore, quinolone, benzo[b]azepine, benzo[b]azepin-5-one, benzothiazine, and benzo[1,4]oxazine-derived amide-sulfoxonium ylides were also suitable substrates, providing the desired products (30–34) in good yields. Interestingly, methyl-, isopropyl-, benzyl-, cyclohexyl-, cyano-, and thiophene-group substituted amide-sulfoxonium ylides also worked well in this protocol, furnishing the Si–H bond insertion products (35–42) in moderate to good yields with high chemoselectivities. NH-Amide-sulfoxonium ylides, such as aniline bearing various functional groups were also well tolerated and thus made this protocol feasible for aniline-containing natural products. For example, 43–49 were afforded in moderate to good yields. Importantly, halogen groups, F, Cl, Br, and I, were all well tolerated, thus providing opportunities for additional modification of the products (46–49). It was noteworthy that the reaction performed using alkenyl substituted amide-sulfoxonium ylides (50) resulted in the formation of the desired product in 82% yield with excellent selectivity. In addition, 2-aminofluorene and 4-aminodibenzofuran also gave the corresponding Si–H insertion products (51 and 52) in 67% and 42% yields, respectively. Notably, coumarin 120 afforded the desired product (53) in 40% yield. The utility of the reaction was further examined on aryl and heteroaryl α-keto sulfoxonium ylides with increased reaction temperature, generating the desired α‑silyl ketones (54–56) in moderate yields.

Next, various silanes were examined for this iridium(I)-catalyzed Si–H bond insertion reaction. Trialkylsilanes exhibited excellent selectivity in the Si–H bond insertion reaction, furnishing the corresponding silicon-incorporated amide products (57–60) in good yields. Cyclohexyl and benzyl-substituted silanes were also demonstrated to be suitable substrates, delivering the desired products (61 and 62) in 70% and 60% yields, respectively. In contrast, the bulky tribenzylsilane gave low yield (63). The reaction with readily available dimethyl aryl silanes were amenable to form α‑silyl amide products (64–67). Notably, an alkenyl-substituted arylsilane also reacted smoothly and was converted to the desired product (65), and no cyclopropanation byproduct was observed in this transformation. Moreover, the steric hindrance effect affected the reactivity. For example, those with greater steric hindrance diphenylmethylsilane and triphenylsilane resulted in significantly diminishing yields (68 and 69). Unfortunately, the reactions using disubstituted silane H2SiPh2 and monoalkoxylsilane HSiMe2OEt as reaction substrates to produce the insertion products (70 and 71) were failed (for details, please see Supplementary Table 4).

Organogermane compounds possess high hydrophobicity and low toxicity and more recently served as alternative and orthogonal coupling reagents in synthetic chemistry62–64. Thus, the Ge–H bond insertion reaction for an efficient, selective installation of Ge functionality was important but challenging through transition-metal catalysis. To our delight, the iridium(I)-catalyzed reaction of amide-sulfoxonium ylide (1a) with triethylgermane afforded the corresponding product (72) in a 93% yield (Fig. 4). Various amide-sulfonium ylides bearing bromide, alkene, sulfur-heterocycle, benzyl, and cyano groups are well-tolerated under the present reaction conditions, resulting in the formation of valuable α-geryl carbonyl compounds (73–77) with high yields. Furthermore, triphenylgermane was also suitable substrate for Ge incorporation (78). Organoborons are widely used in organic synthesis, materials science, and the pharmaceutical industry65–68. Recently, the transition metal-catalyzed insertion of carbenes into B–H bonds have become a powerful strategy for C–B bond construction17,67–71. Although significant progress has been made in the synthesis of α-boryl ketones using diazo compounds, alkynes, and sulfoxonium ylides as carbene precursors24,48,72,73, reliable methods for the synthesis of α-boryl amides remain underexplored. Gratifyingly, a diverse range of amide-sulfoxonium ylides, featuring aryl, heterocycle, or alkyl substituents, proved effective in the iridium(I)-catalyzed B–H bond insertion reactions, giving the desired α-boryl amides in moderate to excellent yields (79–94). The trisubstituted NH-amide-sulfoxonium ylide smoothly underwent the insertion reaction with amine-borane adduct, furnishing the desired product 92 in 79% yield. The reaction tolerated a variety of functional groups, including methyl (80 and 87), halides (81–83, and 89), alkenyl (84 and 90), S-heterocycle (85 and 93), O-heterocycle (86 and 94), and alkynyl (91). The choice of the Lewis base in the borane adducts proved critical for the outcome of the B–H insertion. Triethylamine-borane adduct (95), 4-methylmorpholine-borane adduct (96), pyridine-borane adduct (98), and N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC)-borane adduct (99) have been found effective under optimal conditions. However, the use of pyridine-borane adduct sometimes results in a lower yield (97). Moreover, to showcase potential late-stage functionalization, amide-sulfoxonium ylides derived from complex bioactive compounds (e.g. acetaminophen, vortioxetine, and amoxapine) were introduced into this Ir-catalyzed B–H bond insertion reaction, affording 100, 101, and 102 in 71%, 83%, and 86% yields, respectively. The structure of product 80 was confirmed by X-ray diffraction.

In addition to the Si–H, Ge–H, and B–H insertion reactions, amide-sulfoxonium ylides have also proven to be efficient for insertion into Se–H bond. Phenylselenol reacted with various sulfoxonium ylides under catalyst-free conditions to afford 103–117 in 53–90% yields, thereby establishing the versatility of Se–H insertion74,75. The reaction tolerates a wide range of valuable functional groups, such as ether, alkene, amino, bromide, phenothiazine, pyridine, pyrrole, pyrazole, carbazole, piperidine, indole, and 1,3-benzodioxole. Furthermore, thiol could also be used as an efficient substrate react with amide-sulfoxonium ylides76,77, affording the S–H insertion products (118–121) in high yields. Notably, other nucleophilic groups, such as hydroxyl (122) and amino (123) remained intact in the reaction system, demonstrating the excellent chemoselectivity in this carbene insertion reaction (vide infra). Possibly due to the acidity of S–H bond (p-toluenethiol, pKa = 6.52) and Se–H bond (phenylselenol, pKa = 4.73), the carbene insertion into S–H and Se–H bonds are easier (S–H vs Se–H, please see the Supplementary Information on page 20). In contrast, the Si–H and Ge–H bonds are more inert63, requiring the addition of metal catalyst in this transformation.

Synthetic applications

To demonstrate the practicality of the approach developed in this work, we carried out scale-up experiment and a series of product transformations. Firstly, we synthesized the amide-sulfoxonium ylide (1a, 2.0 g, 69% yield) from commercially available diphenylamine in two steps (Fig. 5a). Then, a gram-scale (5 mmol) synthesis of 64 was carried out, and 1.296 g of product was obtained in 75% yield (Fig. 5b). Interestingly, the reaction of the 1,4-bis(dimethylsilyl)benzene 124 with amide-sulfoxonium ylide 125 could lead to the corresponding double Si–H bond insertions product (126) in 61% yield with high selectivity (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, the amide moiety of 64 could be reduced with SmI2 to generate the corresponding tertiary amine 127 in 45% yield78. Compound 64 could also be smoothly transformed to α-iodide substituted amide 128 by means of an electrophilic substitution reaction79 (Fig. 5d). As shown in Fig. 5e, the amide group in the compound 79 could be reduced with BH3·THF. By following condensation with pinacol, we obtained the target β-borate amine 129 in 51% yield73. In addition, 79 was easily oxidized to α-hydroxy amide 130 in good yield. To our surprise, the reaction of 79 with an excess of N-chlorosuccinimide yielded a stable dichloro borane product (131)80. The reaction of 103 with N-chlorosuccinimide or m-chloroperoxybenzoic gave the corresponding α-disubstituted amides (132 and 133) in 81% and 85% yields, respectively81,82 (Fig. 5f). By making slight adjustments to the reaction conditions, the α-sulfur-substituted amide 118 could be readily converted to either the sulfone (134) or the sulfoxide (135) through oxidation using m-chloroperoxybenzoic acid83. Mechanistic studies

Mechanistic experiments were conducted to elucidate the mechanism of these reactions. Firstly, the experiment for differentiating the reactivity of triethylsilane and triethylgermane was performed, interestingly, delivering the sole Ge–H bond insertion product 72 in an 80% yield with excellent selectivity (Fig. 5h). A competitive experiment between triethylsilane and trimethylamine-borane adduct was conducted, resulting in the Si–H bond insertion product (3) with a 72% yield and high selectivity. In contrast, the B–H bond insertion product (79) was obtained with a comparatively lower yield (Fig. 5i). Besides, the method also showed excellent selectivity between different heteroatom–hydrogen bond insertion in one-pot reaction, such as Si–H bond vs O/N–H bond (for details, see Supplementary Figs. 3–5). Subsequently, the use of deuterated silane led to the incorporation of deuterium into the α-carbon position of the product 64-D (D content: 95%) under the optimal reaction conditions, indicating an iridium(I)-catalyzed direct Si–H bond insertion mechanism (Fig. 5j). We carried out a competitive kinetic isotopic study and observed a kinetic isotope effect (kH/kD = 1.62), the result suggests that Si–H bond insertion process is most probably the rate-determining step (Fig. 5k). In addition, we also employed the disubstituted amide diazo 137 instead of the amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a for the Si–H bond insertion reactions under Rh and Ir catalysis. Despite the ineffectiveness of Si–H insertion, the intramolecular carbene C–H insertion by-product 3’ was obtained in high yield (Fig. 5l). Therefore, the protocol provides concise and efficient access to diverse functionalized organosilanes.

To better understand the reaction mechanism, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations (for computational details, see Supplementary Table 6). As shown in Fig. 6, the iridium catalyst dimer dissociates into monomer and then reacts with amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a to generate Ir-carbene species Int B via a transition state TS-A with an energy barrier of 12.9 kcal/mol. The further insertion reaction of the electron-rich Si–H bond of triethylsilane 2a via the transition state TS-C with an energy barrier of 12.2 kcal/mol, giving the desired product TM-Si and regenerating the iridium catalyst. In a similar way, the insertion reaction of Ge–H bond via the transition state TS-Ge with an energy barrier of 10.6 kcal/mol, giving the desired product TM-Ge and regenerating the iridium catalyst, and the insertion reaction of B–H bond via the transition state TS-B with an energy barrier of 14.3 kcal/mol, giving the desired product TM-B. It is worth noting that the significant differences in transition state energy difference between B–H insertion, Si–H insertion, and Ge–H insertion (2.1 kcal/mol for B–H and Si–H, 1.6 kcal/mol for Si–H and Ge–H), result in excellent chemoselectivity for the Si–H vs B–H insertion and Ge–H vs Si–H insertion reactions, respectively. It is noteworthy that the intramolecular carbene C–H insertion reaction and homo-dimerization reaction could both proceed smoothly under standard conditions via the transition state TS-CH and TS-dimer with an energy barrier of 14.0 kcal/mol and 13.1 kcal/mol, respectively. However, due to the fact that intramolecular C–H insertion and homo-dimerization products are more thermodynamically unstable, selective X–H insertion reactions can be achieved. DFT calculation can also support the reaction mechanism of Se–H insertion and S–H insertion reactions under catalyst-free conditions. The insertion reaction of amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a with Se–H substrate (i.e. phenylselenol) and S–H substrate (i.e. p-toluenethiol) via the transition state TS-Se and TS-S with an energy barrier of 7.8 kcal/mol and 5.2 kcal/mol, respectively.Fig. 6Free energy changes of the possible reaction pathways.Calculations were performed at the M06/def2-TZVP/SMD(1,2-dichloroethane)//B3LYP/6-31G(d)-SDD level of theory.

Having demonstrated a robust substrate scope for this methodology, we envisaged that this protocol would provide a unified set of conditions for the preparation of α‑silyl and α‑geryl (hetero)amides and thus be well-suited for parallel synthesis with a diverse set of amide-sulfoxonium ylides, respectively. Recently, PMC has become an effective way to explore the scope and space of reaction in synthetic chemistry85,86. Meanwhile, the rapid construction of compound libraries could also accelerate high-throughput screening of active drug molecules. Therefore, it is highly desirable to conduct chemical library synthesis for newly developed organic methodologies under simple and convenient procedure. We explored the breadth and utility of the Si–H and Ge–H bonds insertion with drug-containing amide-sulfoxonium ylides using an automated HTE platform. In order to validate this approach in an automated mode, we devised a chemical library of 24 new products using various drug-containing amide-sulfoxonium ylides combined with cyclohexyldimethylsilane and triethylgermane, respectively (Fig. 8). Various amide-sulfoxonium ylides, derived from drug fragments or natural products, including vortioxetine, amoxapine, desloratadine, papaverine, cytisine, terbinafine fragment, nortriptyline, maprotiline, fluoxetine, duloxetine, tryptamine, and coumarin 120. Operationally, we set up 24 reactions on a 50 μmol scale in 900 μL-glass tubes using a HTE platform60. This modular automated workflow included liquid dispensing, heating, agitation, reaction workup, and sample purification and analysis (for details, please see the Supplementary Information on page 30). Notably, all reactions were subjected to an automated MS-triggered Auto-P purification step, accomplishing the workflow and affording the corresponding compounds in high purity (Fig. 8, Auto purification). Discussion

In summary, using HTE we have achieved a highly selective C–Si, C–Ge, C–B, C–Se, and C–S bonds formation employing amide-sulfoxonium ylides as carbene precursors for Si–H, Ge–H, B–H, Se–H, and S–H bond insertion reactions. The method provides a general and efficient access to various polyfunctional α-silyl, α-geryl, α-boryl, α-selenyl, and α-sulfur (hetero)amide compounds (126 examples), many of which are difficult to obtain. This reaction is characterized by mild conditions, a broad substrate scope, high functional group compatibility, and excellent selectivity. Mechanistic studies, incorporating competition experiments, deuterium-labelling experiments, kinetic isotope effect studies, and DFT calculations, yield insights into the reaction pathway, supporting the intermediacy of Ir-carbene and concerted Si–H insertion. Moreover, this work has been implemented in an automated parallel synthesis mode for the accelerated synthesis of a library of complex drug fragments or natural products containing relevant Si- and Ge-incorporation, highlighting the usefulness of this approach to medicinal and material chemists.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of α‑silyl, α‑geryl, and α-boryl amides

An oven-dried glass tube of 4 mL equipped with a magnetic stir bar and was charged with [Ir(COD)Cl]2 (2.7 mg, 0.004 mmol, 4 mol%), amide-sulfoxonium ylide (0.10 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) in DCE (1.0 mL); then silane, germane, or borane adduct (0.20 mmol, 2.0 equiv.) was added. The reaction mixture was sealed and stirred (820 r/min in IKA RCT basic) at 30 °C for 4~24 h under air. After completion, the reaction mixture was recovered to room temperature. The crude product was extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 3.0 mL), and the solution was washed with saturated solution of NH4Cl (3.0 mL), and the organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel (eluent: petroleum ether/ethyl acetate) to give the corresponding products.

General procedure for the synthesis of α-selenyl and α-sulfur amides

An oven dried glass tube of 4 mL equipped with a magnetic stir bar and was charged with amide-sulfoxonium ylide (0.10 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) in DCE (1.0 mL); then phenylselenol or thiol (0.12 mmol, 1.2 equiv.) was added. The reaction mixture was sealed and stirred (820 r/min in IKA RCT basic) at 30 °C for 24 h under air. After completion, the reaction mixture was recovered to room temperature. The crude product was extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 3.0 mL), and the solution was washed with saturated solution of NH4Cl (3.0 mL), and the organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel (eluent: petroleum ether/ethyl acetate) to give the corresponding products.
