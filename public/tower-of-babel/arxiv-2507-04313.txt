Factorization of Basic Hypergeometric Series This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Basic Hypergeometric Series Associated with Root Systems and Applications in honor of Stephen C. Milne’s 75th birthday. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Milne.html

Abstract
The general problem of the factorization of a basic hypergeometric series is presented and discussed. The case of the general ψ 2 2 {}_{2}\psi_{2} series is examined in detail. Connections are found with the theory of basic hypergeometric series on root systems. Alternative proofs of several well-known summation and transformation formulae, including Gustafson’s generalization of Ramanujan’s ψ 1 1 {}_{1}\psi_{1} summation, are obtained incidentally.

Introduction

Throughout this paper,
q
is taken to denote a complex number satisfying
0<|q|<1
. A basic hypergeometric series is any sum
\sum_{n=M}^{N}u_{n}
for which the ratio
u_{n+1}/u_{n}
is a rational function of
q^{n}
. Here,
M
and
N
may take any integer values subject to the condition that
{M\leq N}
, and one may also take
M=-\infty
, or
N=\infty
, or both. It is conventional to write the summand
u_{n}
in terms of a ratio of products of finite
q
-Pochhammer symbols,
(x)_{n}
, defined such as to take the value
1
when
{n=0}
and extended to all integer subscripts
n
by the recursion
{(x)_{n+1}=(1-xq^{n})(x)_{n}}
. The infinite
q
-Pochhammer symbol,
(x)_{\infty}
, is the infinite product given by the limit
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(x)_{n}
. The abbreviation
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m})_{n}
is used for the product
{(x_{1})_{n}(x_{2})_{n}\cdots(x_{m})_{n}}
. The notation
{\theta(x)=(x,q/x,q)_{\infty}}
is used for the Jacobian theta function.

The question of whether a given basic hypergeometric series may be written explicitly as an infinite product has a long history. In the 1840s, both Jacobi
[
18

and Heine
[
15

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{(a,b)_{n}\over(q,abx)_{n}}x^{n}={(ax,bx)_{\infty}\over(x,abx)_{\infty}},

(1.1)

valid for

{}_{2}F_{1}

[
10

[
11

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{(a)_{n}\over(b)_{n}}x^{n}={\bigl({b\over a}\bigr)_{\infty}\theta(ax)\over\bigl(x,{b\over ax},b,{q\over a}\bigr)_{\infty}},

(1.2)

\displaystyle\qquad={\bigl({q\over a_{1}a_{2}},{q\over a_{1}a_{3}},{q\over a_{1}a_{4}},{q\over a_{2}a_{3}},{q\over a_{2}a_{4}},{q\over a_{3}a_{4}}\bigr)_{\infty}\theta\bigl(y^{2}\bigr)\over\bigl({q\over a_{1}y},{q\over a_{2}y},{q\over a_{3}y},{q\over a_{4}y},{qy\over a_{1}},{qy\over a_{2}},{qy\over a_{3}},{qy\over a_{4}},{q\over a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}}\bigr)_{\infty}},

The purpose of this paper is to examine a different sense in which every basic hypergeometric series may be said to admit a factorization—one which involves only finitely many unknowns. There is no loss of generality in restricting our attention to bilateral series, since, given any finite or unilateral series, it is always possible to produce a bilateral series reducible to it by a suitable specialization of variables. We may, for example, obtain (
1.5
) from the series
{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n-1)/2}x^{n}(a)_{n}/(b)_{n}}
by first setting

q

(0)_{n}
in the denominator of any such series.

2
Applications

Aside from an obvious desire to generalize the classical summation formulae, there is another reason why investigating the general problem of factorization of basic hypergeometric series is particularly worthwhile: It provides some insight into, and in many cases simpler proofs of, a number of well-known identities. The following example is taken from the author’s thesis
[
8
, Section 3.6]
. By Theorem
1.1
, we know that the numerator,
{}_{2}\psi_{2}^{\ast}
, of the general
{}_{2}\psi_{2}

1.11
) for some unknown functions
A
and
\rho
. The relation between these two functions provided by Theorem
1.2
need not concern us here. Since the function
\theta
satisfies the elementary identity
\theta(x)=\theta(q/x)
, it follows at once from (
1.11
) that

{}_{2}\psi_{2}^{\ast}(x,y)={}_{2}\psi_{2}^{\ast}(x,q/a_{1}a_{2}xy)
.
This is the second of a pair of
{}_{2}\psi_{2}
transformations found by Bailey
[
7
, equations (2.3) and (2.4)]
. His other
{}_{2}\psi_{2}
transformation does not follow from (
1.11
) alone; rather, it follows from a symmetry of the functions
A
and
\rho
which is not at all obvious: regarded as (multi-valued) functions of the variables
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
,
x
, the functions
A
and
\rho
in (

) are both invariant under the substitution
(a_{2},b_{2},x)\mapsto(a_{1}a_{2}x/b_{2},a_{1}x,b_{2}/a_{1})
.

In the previous section, it was observed that Ramanujan’s
{}_{1}\psi_{1}
summation (
1.2
) follows immediately from Theorem
1.1
and the
q
-binomial theorem. In much the same way, Bailey’s

summation (
1.3
) may be obtained from Theorem
1.3
and the
{}_{6}\phi_{5}
summation (for which see
[
12
, equation (2.7.1)]
). Consider that the special case
r=6
of Theorem
1.3
asserts that
{{}_{6}W_{6}^{\ast}(y)=A\theta\bigl(y^{2}\bigr)}
for some
A
which is independent of
y
. The value of

may then be determined by setting
y=a_{1}
, since the value of
{}_{6}W_{6}^{\ast}(a_{1})
is readily obtained from the
{}_{6}\phi_{5}
summation formula.

A further application of the ideas of this paper is to the derivation of Slater’s general transformation formulae for bilateral basic hypergeometric series
[

]
, which may be written in the form

_{r}\psi_{r}^{\ast}(x,y)={1\over\theta(a_{1}z_{1}\cdots a_{r}z_{r}x)}\sum_{j=1}^{r}{}_{r}\psi_{r}^{\ast}(x,z_{j})\theta\left({a_{1}z_{1}\cdots a_{r}z_{r}xy\over z_{j}}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{r}{\theta\bigl({y\over z_{k}}\bigr)\over\theta\bigl({z_{j}\over z_{k}}\bigr)}

(2.1)

and, for even

{{}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(y)\over\theta\bigl(y^{2}\bigr)}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-6\over 2}{{}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(z_{j})\over\theta(z_{j}^{2})}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{r-6\over 2}{\theta\bigl({y\over z_{k}}\bigr)\theta(qz_{k}y)\over\theta\bigl({z_{j}\over z_{k}}\bigr)\theta(qz_{j}z_{k})},

(2.2)

while for odd

{\theta(y\sqrt{q}){}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(y)\over\theta\bigl(y^{2}\bigr)}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-5\over 2}{\theta(z_{j}\sqrt{q}){}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(z_{j})\over\theta\bigl(z_{j}^{2}\bigr)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{r-5\over 2}{\theta\bigl({y\over z_{k}}\bigr)\theta(qz_{k}y)\over\theta\bigl({z_{j}\over z_{k}}\bigr)\theta(qz_{j}z_{k})}.

As explained in Remarks

3
Theta functions

This section contains several lemmas which are needed in Section
4
. With the possible exception of Lemmas
3.17
and
3.18
, all of the results are classical. Lemma
3.5
, for example, may be found in the book of Briot and Bouquet
[
9
, p. 239]
, albeit in a rather different notation. A more modern statement of it appears in
[
21
, Lemma 3.2]
; functions belonging to the space
\Theta_{n}(c)
defined below are there called
A_{n-1}
theta functions. A special case of Lemma
3.16
was given by Appell
[
5
, p. 140]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
n
be any integer and let
c
be any non-zero complex number. Let
\Theta_{n}(c)
denote the space of analytic functions
f\colon\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
which satisfy identically the relation

f(qx)={(-1)^{n}f(x)\over cx^{n}}.

(3.1)

Lemma 3.2

(classical)
.

Let
n
be an integer and let
c
be any non-zero complex number. If
n
is negative, then
\Theta_{n}(c)=\{0\}
. If
n
is positive, then
\dim\Theta_{n}(c)=n
. If
n=0
, then there are two possibilities:

\Theta_{0}(c)=\begin{cases}\big\{f\mid f(x)=Ax^{-k}\text{ for some }A\in\mathbb{C}\big\}&\text{if $c=q^{k}$ for some integer $k$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 3.3
.

In particular, Lemma
3.2
asserts that
\Theta_{0}(1)
consists only of constant functions, i.e., that any solution of
f(qx)=f(x)
which is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
must be constant. This special case is well known and widely used in proofs of
q
-series identities.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n
be any integer, let
c
be any non-zero complex number, and let
f\in\Theta_{n}(c)
. If
\rho
is a non-zero complex number such that
f(\rho)=0
, then
f(x)=\theta(x/\rho)g(x)
for some
g\in\Theta_{n-1}(c\rho)
.

Proof.

From the relation (
3.1
), it follows that
f(\rho)=0
implies
f\bigl(\rho q^{k}\bigr)=0
for every integer
k
. Consequently the function
g
defined by
g(x)=f(x)/\theta(x/\rho)
is analytic throughout
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. Since

g(qx)={(-1)^{n}f(x)/cx^{n}\over(-\rho/x)\theta(x/\rho)}={(-1)^{n-1}g(x)\over c\rho x^{n-1}},

it follows that
g\in\Theta_{n-1}(c\rho)
.
∎

Lemma 3.5

(classical)
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and let
c
be any non-zero complex number. Then
f\in\Theta_{n}(c)
if and only if

f(x)=A\hskip 0.5pt\theta(\alpha_{1}x)\theta(\alpha_{2}x)\cdots\theta(\alpha_{n}x)

for some
A\in\mathbb{C}
and some
{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}}
such that
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}=c
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Lemma
3.5
, it suffices to verify that, regarded as a function of the variable
y
,
{}_{r}\psi_{r}^{\ast}(x,y)
belongs to the space
\Theta_{r}(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{r}x)
. Since this function is analytic on
{\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}}
, it suffices to check that it satisfies the relation

{}_{r}\psi_{r}^{\ast}(x,qy)={(-1)^{r}{}_{r}\psi_{r}^{\ast}(x,y)\over a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{r}xy^{r}}.

This is easily verified by changing
n\mapsto n+1
in the series in (
1.6
).
∎

Remark 3.6
.

If
f\in\Theta_{2}(c)
, then
f
satisfies identically the relation
f(x)=f(q/cx)
. The reason for this is that, by Lemma
3.5
, any such function
f
may be written in the form
f(x)=A\theta(\alpha x)\theta(cx/\alpha)
.

Remark 3.7
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and
c
any non-zero complex number. Given any
z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
, for
1\leq j\leq n
let

\vartheta_{j}(x)={\theta\bigl({cz_{1}\cdots z_{n}x\over z_{j}}\bigr)\over\theta(cz_{1}\cdots z_{n})}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}{\theta\bigl({x\over z_{k}}\bigr)\over\theta\bigl({z_{j}\over z_{k}}\bigr)}.

Provided that the numbers
z_{j}
are such that the denominators of these functions are not zero, the relation
\vartheta_{j}(z_{k})=\delta_{j,k}
holds for all pairs of integers
j
,
k
in the interval
[1,n]
. These
n
functions are therefore linearly independent of one another and so form a basis for
\Theta_{n}(c)
. Consequently, any given
f\in\Theta_{n}(c)
may be expanded in the form

f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(z_{j})\vartheta_{j}(x).

(3.2)

This expansion is equivalent to a special case of a formula given by Appell
[
5
, equations (8) and (
8^{\prime}
)]
. As is commented upon in
[
8
, p. 88]
and in
[
17
, Section 5.1]
, Slater’s identity (
2.1
) is an instance of (
3.2
).

The results stated in Lemmas
3.9
,
3.11
, and
3.12
relate to a particular subspace of
\Theta_{2n}(c^{n})
, introduced in Definition
3.8
below. These results may be found in
[
17
, Lemma 4.3]
and
[
21
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let

be a positive integer and let

be any non-zero complex number. Let
\Omega_{2n}(c)
denote the subset of

consisting of functions

with each

. It is convenient also to define

to be the space of constant functions (i.e.,

), and further to define

for every

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
n
be a non-negative integer and
c
any non-zero complex number. Then the set
\Omega_{2n}(c)
is in fact a subspace of
\Theta_{2n}(c^{n})
, and its dimension equals

.

Remark 3.10
.

(i)

Although

for every

, the inclusion

is strict for every

(ii)

For

if and only if there is some

such that

for some

. Another characterization of the space

is given in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
n
be any integer, let
c
be a non-zero complex number, and let

. Then
f\in\Omega_{2n}(c)
if and only if the relation

f(x)=f(q/cx)

(3.3)

holds identically.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
n
be any integer, let
c
be a non-zero complex number, and let
f\in\Theta_{2n}(c^{n})
. Then
f
satisfies identically the relation

f(x)=-f(q/cx)

(3.4)

if and only if
f(x)=x\theta\big(cx^{2}\big)g(x)
for some
g\in\Omega_{2n-4}(c)
.

Remark 3.13
.

The special case

of Lemma

asserts that any function

satisfying the relation (

) must be identically zero. This is apparent from the fact that (

.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The function
{}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}
defined by (

) satisfies the two relations

{}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(qy)={(-1)^{r}{}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(y)\over q^{r-4\over 2}y^{r-2}},\qquad{}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(1/y)=-{{}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(y)\over y^{2}}.

These are easily obtained by changing
n\mapsto n+1
and
n\mapsto-n
in the series in (
1.15
). For the remainder of this proof, let us put
f(y)={}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(y)/y
. Then the first equation shows that
f
belongs to the space
\Theta_{r-2}\bigl(q^{(r-2)/2}\bigr)
, while the second, which we may rewrite as

, allows us to infer that

belongs in fact to a subspace thereof which we may determine.

Consider first the case in which
r
is even. Then, by Lemma
3.12
, it must be that
f(y)=y\theta\bigl(qy^{2}\bigr)g(y)
for some

. If

, then this space contains only the function which is identically zero, so it follows that
{}_{4}W_{4}^{\ast}(y)=0
for all

. If, on the other hand,

, then, per Definition

, we may write
g(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{(r-6)/2}g_{j}(y)
, where each of the functions

belongs to the space
\Theta_{2}(q)
and therefore, by Lemma

, admits a factorization of the form
{g_{j}(y)=A_{j}\theta(y/\rho_{j})\theta(q\rho_{j}y)}
. Hence

{{}_{r}W_{r}^{\ast}(y)\over y}=y\theta\bigl(qy^{2}\bigr)\prod_{j=1}^{r-6\over 2}A_{j}\theta\left({y\over\rho_{j}}\right)\theta(q\rho_{j}y).

This is exactly the form of the factorization specified in (
1.18
), the constant
A
there being equal to

in the notation used here.

Consider next the case in which

is odd, and put

. Then

and, since

is invariant under

for some

is identically zero and, for odd

In view of the identity (

Remark 3.14
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and let

be a non-zero complex number. Given any

, for

let

Then, provided that the

are such that the denominators are non-zero,
\omega_{j}\in\Omega_{2n}(c)
for each

. Moreover, these functions
\omega_{j}
are linearly independent since
\omega_{j}(z_{k})=\delta_{j,k}
for all integers

,

in the interval
[0,n]
. By Lemma

. Hence any

may be expressed in the form

f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f(z_{j})\omega_{j}(x).

(3.5)

In the proof of Theorem

, it has been shown that, when

is even, the function

belongs to the space
\Omega_{r-6}(q)
, and that, when

just described; doing so yields Slater’s identities (

valid for every positive integer

.

Definition 3.15
.

For each positive integer

, let
\Delta_{n}\colon(\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

\Delta_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}x_{k}\theta\left({x_{j}\over x_{k}}\right),

with the convention that the function
\Delta_{1}
(defined by an empty product) is identically equal to

.

The following result can be found in

. Its proof is straightforward: since

is anti-symmetric, the ratio of the two sides of (

) is a symmetric function of

; moreover, it follows easily from Lemmas

that this ratio is independent of

.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
n
be a positive integer,
c
any non-zero complex number, and let

. Then there is some constant

\det_{1\leq i,j\leq n}(f_{i}(x_{j}))=A\Delta_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\theta(cx_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n})

(3.6)

holds for all

.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
n

(i)

;

(ii)

satisfies identically the relation

if and only if

for some

Proof.

First, observe that the product in (

The purpose of this section is to demonstrate, in outline, how the result of Theorem

1.1
for any positive integer

Let

x\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1-a_{j}y)-\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1-b_{j}y/q)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\lambda_{j}(x)y^{j}.

Then from the elementary identity

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{(a_{1}y,\dots,a_{r}y)_{n}\over(b_{1}y,\dots,b_{r}y)_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\bigl(1-b_{j}yq^{n-1}\bigr)x^{n}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{(a_{1}y,\dots,a_{r}y)_{n}\over(b_{1}y,\dots,b_{r}y)_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1-a_{j}yq^{n})x^{n+1},

which is the obvious generalization of (
4.1
), it is apparent that

\sum_{j=0}^{r}y^{j}\lambda_{j}(x){}_{r}\psi_{r}\bigl(xq^{j},y\bigr)=0,

or equivalently,

\sum_{j=0}^{r}{y^{j}\lambda_{j}(x)(x)_{j}\over\bigl({b_{1}\cdots b_{r}\over a_{1}\cdots a_{r}xq^{j}}\bigr)_{j}}{}_{r}\psi_{r}^{\ast}\big(xq^{j},y\big)=0.

(5.1)

Hence, for any given non-zero complex numbers
