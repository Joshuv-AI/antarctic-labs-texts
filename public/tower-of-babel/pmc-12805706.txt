University students’ perspectives on delivering health education interventions to underprivileged older adults in the community: a qualitative study of a large-scale community-based research project
Authors: Siu Long Chau, Wanjia He, Sophia Siu Chee Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of underprivileged older adults (≥ 65 years) has been increasing in the context of population ageing. In light of the global primary healthcare reform and development, healthcare professional students would require knowledge and skills to work with community older adults in addition to clinical skills. We established a large-scale community-based research project (Generations Connect) to train healthcare professional students to provide health assessments and interventions to older adults at their homes. This study explores students’ perspectives on providing community interventions to older adults in Hong Kong.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted on 20 Hong Kong healthcare university students recruited using purposive sampling. Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted from May to September 2024. Interview questions focused on their experience and barriers to communicating with older adults during the home visit. Each interview lasted around an hour. All interviews were audiotaped and transcribed verbatim. Two researchers independently analyzed the transcripts using thematic analysis with an additional investigator to verify the consistency of the coding.ResultsFour themes emerged from the analysis: (1) perceived barriers to delivering health education due to functional limitations of the older adults, (2) attitudes of older adults towards receiving health education, (3) perceived factors that moderate older adults’ health literacy, and (4) strategies to enhance health literacy and effectiveness of health education in older adults.ConclusionsOlder adult’s functional limitations and negative generalization of ageing may have prevented them from actively seeking health information and health-promoting behaviors. To enhance the intervention’s effectiveness, the health education contents should be customized based on their health needs, functional capabilities, and readiness to change health behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06880-y.Keywords: Underprivileged older adults, Health education, Community settings, Home visits

Background

Population ageing is a global phenomenon. There are about 700 million older adults (65 years or above) globally, and the number will double to 1.5 billion in 2050 [1]. The proportion of older adults in Asia increased from 6% to 11% in two decades [2], especially in Hong Kong, where is undergoing a demographic shift with a significant increase in the number of older adults in the population [3]. The number is estimated to increase from 1.3 million in 2018 to 2.5 million in 2039 (30% of the population) [3]. Currently, more than 580,000 older adults live under the poverty line (defined as the minimum amount of income a person needs to fulfill basic needs) [4], and the number increased by 43% in 2019 [4].

In line with the global primary healthcare reform, person-centered integrated care and communication are the keys to addressing the holistic needs of older adults [5]. The central element included empowering and engaging individuals and family members in health decision-making and managing their health [6]. Older adults with multi-morbidities and functional limitations need to understand and follow complex health instructions to maintain and promote their health [7]. As health information is conveyed through education delivered by healthcare professionals, further understanding of communication between older adults and health educators is imperative to assist older adults in performing better in health [8]. Although the government has publicized person-centered integrated care as one of the goals of the primary healthcare reform in Hong Kong [9], the perceived barriers and attitudes of health educators toward delivering health education to older adults have not been thoroughly studied as an essential factor in providing the best care in community settings [10].

The healthcare professional students are young and vibrant. They can contribute to improving communication and promoting the health of underprivileged older adults. The University of Hong Kong has established a large-scale community-based research project- Generations Connect- to engage young healthcare professional students in enhancing the health literacy and physical and psychosocial well-being of over 6,000 older adults in Hong Kong through home visiting [11]. This community-based project is training over 1,000 university students to enhance their self-efficacy and competence in delivering health education to and communicating with older adults [11]. Ultimately, it is crucial to understand healthcare students’ perspectives in educating older adults, as they will be at the forefront of providing care in the future with the increasing ageing population. Investigating their initial experiences, perceived barriers, and proposed strategies provides unique pedagogical insights that are distinct from patient-reported outcomes or the views of seasoned professionals. These insights are essential for refining university curricula and training programs to better equip the next generation of healthcare providers for the complexities of community-based geriatric care.

A comprehensive literature search conducted in PubMed, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Embase revealed that while quantitative studies on the effectiveness of health education on older adults are common [12], there remains limited qualitative evidence exploring the specific barriers and facilitators of communicating with older adults as perceived by student health educators during their formative community-based experiences. Aligning with the global and local primary healthcare reform, this qualitative study aimed to explore healthcare university students’ acceptability, perceived challenges, and factors that facilitate the health education of underprivileged older adults to promote better person-centered preventive care and healthy ageing in Hong Kong.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative research approach. This paradigm provided a comprehensive summary and rich description of experience from participants’ perspectives, which aligns with our goal of exploring students’ in-depth views on delivering health interventions to underprivileged older adults. This qualitative study was guided by the following research questions:

What are the perceived barriers and challenges for healthcare university students when delivering health education to underprivileged older adults in a home-visit setting?

What are the perceived barriers and challenges for healthcare university students when delivering health education to underprivileged older adults in a home-visit setting?

What factors do students perceive as facilitating effective communication and health education with this specific population?

What strategies do students identify or suggest to enhance the effectiveness of health education interventions for older adults in the community?”

What strategies do students identify or suggest to enhance the effectiveness of health education interventions for older adults in the community?”

This is an ongoing large-scale community-based research project (The Generations Connect) that is training over 1,000 university students to provide health assessment and health intervention (face-to-face and digital) during a home visit, incorporating demonstration and behavioral coaching to underprivileged older adults with lower socio-economic status in Hong Kong [11]. We conducted semi-structured qualitative interviews with 20 healthcare university students who had completed home visits to explore their acceptability, barriers, and facilitators in delivering health interventions to the older adults in the community to improve the intervention delivery for this ongoing community-based research project.

The health intervention covered four themes: (1) eHealth literacy, (2) non-communicable disease prevention, (3) psychosocial well-being, and (4) COVID-19 prevention. Coaching on eHealth literacy included instruction on using instant messaging apps (WhatsApp or WeChat), using the internet to acquire health information from government websites, and downloading and using medical health service apps. Other coaching contents included advice on a healthy diet, smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, utilization of District Health Centres (A new Government community health centres in the primary healthcare reform to provide primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention services to the general population), and COVID-19 prevention.

We recruited healthcare university students who participated in the Generations Connect project using purposive sampling. The eligibility criteria included (1) being a Hong Kong resident aged 18 or above, (2) studying a healthcare-related degree in the university, (3) being able to communicate in Cantonese, and (4) having completed at least one older adult home visit in the past three months. We recruited potential participants through universities’ mass emails, posters, and social media (e.g., Instagram and Facebook) with an embedded quick response (QR) code. The QR code directed the students to the eligibility questionnaire. Eligible participants were asked to fill out the baseline questionnaire and to participate in the individual interview. To increase the heterogeneity, we selected participants of different sexes, years of study, and academic degrees at baseline. Recruitment ceased after information redundancy was achieved (no new information emerged).

Training of students before home visits

All recruited students were from the Faculty of Medicine, the University of Hong Kong, and they received the same home visit training before the health education journey, which was conducted by the same research and clinical faculty members from the University of Hong Kong School of Nursing. The training covered the four research themes, including (a) the ageing population and Primary Health Care New Journey in Hong Kong, (b) effective communication techniques with older adults, (c) assistance with digital devices and mobile-based eHealth services, (d) knowledge of non-communicable disease prevention by promoting healthy lifestyles, (e) strategies for building positive mental health and well-being, and (f) home visit protocol and general safety practice guidelines.

Details of the training contents on effective communication with older adults were reported elsewhere [11]. In brief, the training contents focused on 1) conversational skills (e.g., appropriate speed and tone, maintaining a positive attitude); 2) the use of clear language (e.g., simple and concrete words); 3) and the use of non-verbal communication (e.g., maintaining eye contact and using gestures). Students also learned the skills to compensate for the physiological and cognitive limitations of older adults in the training session [11]. The training comprised a single 4-hour interactive workshop. We acknowledge that a 4-hour session is brief, but it was designed as an intensive, foundational orientation focused on essential skills for initial engagement, not as a comprehensive course in geriatric care. Competency was assessed through two methods: a short multiple-choice quiz covering key concepts of the four research themes, and a role-playing exercise where students practiced communication techniques with a simulated older adult patient, evaluated by a clinical faculty member using a standardized checklist. While this single session cannot replace longitudinal clinical experience, participants’ positive feedback from the post-training evaluation indicated that the 4-hour interactive workshop provided a good foundation to bridge the gap between theoretical preparation and practical application when engaging with community-dwelling older adults. The challenges reported by students in this study provide valuable data for refining and potentially extending this training in the future.”

Theoretical framework to guide the interview questions

The development of the semi-structure interview questions was guided by the Kallio’s theoretical framework on the five phases of developing semi-structured interview guide [13]. The five phases included (1) identifying the prerequisites for using semi-structured interviews; (2) retrieving and using previous knowledge; (3) formulating a preliminary interview guide; (4) pilot testing the guide; and (5) presenting the complete interview guide [13].

In the first phase, we evaluated the appropriateness of the semi-structured interview as a data collection method in relation to our research question [13]. In the second phase, we conducted literature reviews and critical appraisals of existing literature related to the research question. This phase provided a conceptual basis for the interview questions [13]. In the third phase, we formulated the preliminary semi-structured interview guide [13]. In the fourth phase, we conducted the internal testing of the interview guide to remove ambiguities and inappropriate leading questions [13]. In the final phase, we presented the complete semi-structured interview guide in this qualitative paper [13].

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (IRB/REC Reference No.: UW 22–693). Several ethical issues specific to this project were addressed. For example, the pre-visit training for students included a dedicated module on professional boundaries, managing difficult situations, and protocols for escalating concerns if they identified signs of older adults’ acute distress. To protect the vulnerable older adults, all home visits were conducted in pairs with an experienced faculty member. To manage potential student stress, debriefing sessions were made available with faculty members after the visits. Individual interviews were conducted in a quiet and confidential area. We obtained all participants’ verbal and written informed consent before the interview. The IRB also allowed secondary analysis without additional consent. This study followed the COREQ guidelines strictly [14].

Data collection

We interviewed 20 healthcare university students from 4 May 2024 to 8 September 2024. In accordance with our eligibility criteria, all interviews were conducted within three months of the participant’s last home visit to minimize recall bias and ensure reflections were based on recent experiences. Each interview lasted around 60 min (SD ± 15 min). All interviews were conducted in a meeting room at the University of Hong Kong. Participants completed an online baseline questionnaire on their socio-demographics before being invited for the interview.

A trained researcher (SLC) conducted the individual interviews using a semi-structured guide. Only the researcher and interviewee were present throughout the interview. The discussion began with the interviewer asking the participants to describe their experience of home visits and any barriers or facilitators to communicating with older adults as opening questions. A semi-structured interview guide was developed for this qualitative study (available as a supplement). Following the interview guide, the interviewer asked open-ended questions (e.g., “Based on your observation during the home visits, could you share some of the insights of what needed to be strengthened to promote healthy ageing in older adults?”). All interviews were conducted in Cantonese and audiotaped with participants’ consent. A cash coupon of HK $100 (HK $7.8 ≈ US $1) was given to each participant as a study participation incentive. No repeated interviews were carried out.

Data analysis

The audiotaped interviews were transcribed verbatim. Interim analyses were performed after each interview to refine the questions for in-depth understanding. The data analysis of this qualitative study was guided by the Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis [15]. We employed an inductive approach to thematic analysis, allowing the themes to be generated from the data without being constrained by a pre-existing coding frame [15]. Two researchers (SLC & WH) familiarized themselves with the data by reading and re-reading the transcripts to generate initial ideas and potential patterns. The researchers then systematically worked through the data to generate initial codes. This coding process involved analysis at both a semantic level, by identifying the explicit content of participants’ descriptions, and a latent level, by interpreting the underlying assumptions, ideas, and meanings shaping their perspectives [15]. Codes that shared similar meanings were grouped into themes. Themes were reviewed for coherence within and distinction between themes. Researchers revisited the data to ensure an accurate representation. Each theme was refined and described how it related to the research questions. Finally, disagreements in coding were resolved by discussion with an additional researcher (SCC), who further reviewed and verified the consistency of the coding [15]. Interview data and coding were managed using NVivo12. The coding and initial theme development were conducted on the original Cantonese transcripts to ensure the analysis remained grounded in the participants’ native language and cultural context. For reporting purposes, selected interview excerpts that best represented the themes were translated into English. This translation was performed by the first author (SLC), who is bilingual in Cantonese and English. To ensure the accuracy and conceptual equivalence of the translation, a rigorous back-translation process was employed. An independent researcher, a bilingual speaker with a background in public health who was not involved in the analysis, translated the selected English excerpts back into Cantonese. The original and back-translated Cantonese versions were then compared by the research team (SLC, WH, SCC) to identify and resolve any discrepancies in meaning, ensuring that the nuance and intent of the participants’ original statements were preserved in the final English manuscript.

Information redundancy for data analysis was achieved at 20 participants as no new information was identified. Thematic saturation, defined as the point where no new significant codes or themes emerged from the data [15], was determined to be reached after data saturation was observed at approximately 16 interviews, with the final four interviews confirming that data collection had captured a comprehensive range of perspectives from the participants relevant to the research questions. While our analysis did not formally seek theoretical saturation in the grounded theory, we ensured that all identified themes were well-developed and robustly supported by the data through data triangulation, peer debriefing, and constant comparison of new data against existing themes [15].

Rigor

Credibility was ensured by building trust and rapport with participants in the interviews, allowing researchers to gain more insights into their experiences, behaviors, and beliefs [16]. We also adopted triangulation (cross-verify findings from different sources) to reduce the biases from a single data source [16]. As part of the theme refinement process, we also systematically searched for and analyzed negative or deviant cases, where participant experiences may contradict the dominant patterns in the data. This process helped to refine the boundaries of our themes and ensure our final analysis accounted for the full range of participants’ experiences. To ensure the dependability of the study, we documented each step of the research process to ensure transparency and maintained records of data collection and analysis processes [16]. Detailed descriptions of participants’ characteristics (e.g., socio-demographic characteristics) and the use of quotes from participants were provided to increase the transferability [16]. Confirmability was assured by peer debriefing (weekly meeting with research team members to seek feedback from peers) and the use of the reflective journal to track researchers’ evolving thoughts and reflections throughout the interviews [16]. Furthermore, we engaged in critical reflexivity regarding our positionality. The research team included faculty members from the same institution that trained the student participants. We recognized this could create a power dynamic, potentially leading to social desirability bias where students might provide responses they perceive as favourable to the program or faculty. To mitigate this, the interviewer (SLC), who was not directly involved in the students’ formal academic assessment, took several steps. At the start of each interview, the interviewer explicitly stated that the goal of the research was to improve the program for future students, that all feedback was extremely valuable, and that all responses would be kept confidential and anonymized, with no impact on their academic standing. This framing was intended to foster an environment of trust and encourage candid responses. Our reflective journals were also used to document and challenge our own assumptions as educators and researchers, ensuring that our interpretation remained grounded in the participants’ expressed experiences rather than our institutional perspectives.

This study showed that participants perceived multiple conditions among the older adults that might have hindered effective health education, lowered health literacy, and decreased self-care practices. The analysis revealed four major themes. To provide a clear analytical structure, we conceptually distinguished between the immediate, practical barriers students faced during the educational exchange itself (Theme 1: Perceived barriers due to functional limitations) and the broader, pre-existing socio-environmental factors that influence an older adult’s capacity to understand and act on health information (Theme 3: Perceived factors that moderate health literacy). This distinction separates the challenges of delivering information from the challenges related to an older adult’s fundamental receptiveness to that information.

Theme 1: the perceived barriers to delivering health education due to functional limitations of older adults

Theme 1 focused on the immediate challenges of the educational interaction. Participants frequently described how older adults’ functional limitations, such as memory and sensory decline, directly hindered communication and information transfer. Although the participants spent more time educating the older adults, there could have been misinterpretation of the instructions, which was rather common.

I remembered a case where I educated an older adult for 2 h on using the WhatsApp video call to communicate with family members. He thanked me at the end with a smile. Later, his daughter approached me and said her father had asked her how to make the WhatsApp video call again. I told his daughter that I might not have explained well enough to him and apologized. I feel like I’m not doing my job and get frustrated. [#8, female, year 1]

Poor eyesight of older adults is another common problem that hinders effective health education. Participants suggested that educational materials prepared for the general population might not be suitable for older adults in terms of font sizes and the range of information included. Customizing these materials for older adults is essential to facilitate the sustainability of using this health information after the visit to maintain and enhance health-promoting behaviors.

Because the font size of educational materials is fixed, I need to use extra time to re-write the information in larger letters so that older adults can easily read the information later. [#13, female, year 2]

Some of the older adults I visited said that the font size of the leaflet was too small for them to read. I suggested using more pictures to illustrate the content so they can understand the health information easily. [#12, male, year 1]

Theme 2: attitudes of older adults towards receiving health education

Some participants stated that educating older adults was difficult sometimes as they held certain attitudes that might have prevented them from receiving health information. These attitudes included lacking interest in managing their own health, refusal to change current health behaviors, and self-perceived inability to adapt to new health behaviors. Participants felt that it was difficult to determine if the elders understood the health information and their extent of comprehension.

I remember there were some cases when I taught them how to achieve healthy and balanced diets, and they replied, “I am not going to live for too long, so is it necessary?”, “I live alone, and no one cares about me. I don’t understand the point of following these instructions”. I am not sure how much they understood the information I gave them and whether they would follow the instructions later. [#19, male, year 2]

Some older adults always told me they had difficulty learning new information and resisted listening when I tried to give them health information. I needed to persuade them with patience over and over again so they could understand. [#15, male, year 3]

Some participants stated that most older adults showed a more proactive attitude in engaging in conversation and demonstration with them when family members were present and involved in the education process.

I observed significant attitude changes in older adults when their family members joined the health education. They were more patient in listening to the health information I gave and active in asking questions when they didn’t understand. [#12, male, year 1]

Theme 3: perceived factors that moderate health literacy in older adults

Theme 3 focused on broader contextual factors that participants believed shaped older adults’ foundational health literacy. These factors, including socio-economic status and social isolation, were perceived as underlying conditions that affected older adults’ ability to comprehend and engage with health information. More efforts and detailed explanations were needed when conveying the health information to this vulnerable group.

After the home visits, I found that older adults with low health literacy were usually those who were socially isolated. Some concepts that seem “common sense” were not known by those with low health literacy. For example, older adults who were less educated and lived alone did not know that consuming too much salt might lead to hypertension. I felt we needed to provide this vulnerable group with more care and social support. [#3, female, year 3]

One of the concerns I found is that some of the older adults believed that drinking alcohol is beneficial to their health. When I asked where they got the source of information, they said they had watched the “health” video sent by their friends and advertisements on television. Older adults have a limited capability to judge the quality of information received, which might ruin their health. [#1, female, year 2]

Theme 4: strategies to enhance health literacy and effectiveness of health education in older adults

Participants perceived that changing one’s health behaviors is complex and requires multiple health decision-making processes rather than just providing a simple solution. Some participants suggested that the health information given should be personalized to each older adult’s health need, such as adjusting the depth and length of information based on their comprehension level and capacity to follow the instructions in their daily lives.

I think it is important to assess how much older adults can understand health information before, during, and after the delivery of education. If they show a low level of understanding and limited capability to follow, then I would emphasize the fundamental points. If he/she has high compliance and can understand, I would set a specific target for them to achieve so they can manage their health more effectively. [#19, male, year 2]

Other strategies suggested by the participants included repeating explanations, writing to-do lists at the end of each visit, and drawing pictures to illustrate health information.

If the older adults have difficulty remembering what I said, I will try to use drawings and ask them to tape the drawings on the wall so they do not forget them. [#20, female, year 2]

Most older adults I visited cannot memorize the information I told them. In this case, I will give them a to-do list at the end of the visit and summarize what to do and not to do. For example, I will remind them on the list to take medications and measure their blood pressure regularly if he or she has hypertension. [#14, female, year 2]

Participants stated that some of the older adults they visited were illiterate, and the written materials were not suitable for them. Engaging with their family members and caregivers in providing health education is crucial as they can help to gatekeep the older adults’ health.

Some of the older adults I visited were living with their adult children. When they need to make a health decision, they discuss it with their children first. I need to provide sufficient explanations to their children as well when providing health education. [#10, male, year 1]

Discussion

This study explored the perspectives of healthcare university students on providing health education to underprivileged older adults. The findings suggested multiple factors might hinder health education delivery to this group. The declining functional abilities and social isolation of older adults can be barriers to receiving health education and complying with health-promoting behaviors. The less satisfactory socio-environmental factors and exposure to online health misinformation can further worsen the motivation to change. Participants with first-hand experience communicating with older adults provided strategies to enhance health literacy and the effectiveness of health education.

While our findings are rooted in the specific cultural and healthcare context of Hong Kong, they hold potential transferability to other settings. The core challenges identified, including communicating across generational and digital divides, managing attitudes shaped by cultural beliefs about ageing, and adapting educational materials for older adults with functional limitations, are not unique to Hong Kong. Healthcare systems globally are grappling with an ageing population and the need to train a workforce capable of providing person-centered community care [1]. The strategies suggested by our participants, such as personalizing health information, involving family members, and using simple, repetitive communication, represent principles of effective health education with older adults. Therefore, while there are cultural differences, the themes of our study can inform the development of student training programs in other urban, multicultural, and rapidly ageing societies.

Our findings highlighted that students perceived older adults’ functional limitations, such as cognitive decline and poor eyesight, as significant barriers to effective health education. This aligns with the literature, which demonstrates that age-related physiological changes necessitate adapted communication strategies [17]. Rather than simply being a source of frustration for novice educators, these challenges underscore a critical gap in standard healthcare curricula. The experience of our participants suggested that training must go beyond theoretical knowledge to include practical skills in creating and using customized educational tools, such as large-print materials and simplified language, to ensure health information is accessible. The education sessions should be brief, taught slowly, and customized based on each older adult’s cognitive and functional capabilities.

Participants reported that older adults’ resistance to receiving health education was one of the issues encountered during home visits. The resistant attitude might reflect older adults’ negative impression of ageing in society and low self-efficacy for managing own’s health [18–20]. Some participants observed that this group of older adults became more proactive when their family members were involved in the education process. These findings suggested that engaging family members in health education might be key to empowering and motivating them to adopt health-promoting behaviors [21]. Since older adults with multi-morbidities tend to rely on their family caregivers [21], health educators must involve family members in the self-care planning process and gear towards person-centered care rather than disease-centered care.

Participants observed that some external factors might influence the health literacy of underprivileged older adults, including low socio-economic status, lacking social supportive systems, and access to media misinformation. These findings emphasized the important role of the health hub in bridging the health communication and support in the population. The Hong Kong government is currently taking forward the primary healthcare reform with the paradigm shift from treatment-oriented to prevention-focused care. Territory-wide District Health Centres (DHCs) were set up to cover 18 geographical districts in Hong Kong [9]. The reform aims to enhance the health of the population and alleviate pressure on the public healthcare system by providing primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention services, which include health education and assessments, chronic disease screening and management, and community rehabilitation for the public [9]. With these comprehensive and sustainable infrastructures (e.g., DHC), healthcare providers and health educators should engage older adults to join and fully utilize these community healthcare services to enhance their health [9]. More importantly, these DHCs provided older adults with lower socio-economic status equal access to preventive health information and affordable health services that meet their life course needs.

Strategies suggested by the participants resonate with the skills recommended for individuals with limited health literacy, including repeating explanations, writing to-do lists, and drawing pictures to illustrate health information [22]. These strategies involved confirmation of comprehension from older adults and were limited to asking questions rather than using return demonstration [22]. It is necessary to evaluate the effectiveness of these teaching methods from the older adults’ perspectives and to test their comprehension. In addition, participants perceived that changing an individual’s behavior is complex and requires multiple decision-making processes, suggesting that health education intervention should be customized for older adults based on their readiness to make changes [23, 24]. First and foremost, healthcare providers or educators should assess older adults’ stage of change and tailor the intervention to change their health behaviors more effectively.

It is noteworthy that 80% of our participants had no prior primary healthcare training before our pre-visit training. Therefore, the challenges they described, such as frustration with communication barriers and difficulty managing older adults’ resistant attitudes, should be interpreted as the initial hurdles faced by novices. It is plausible that as these first-year students advance in their studies and gain more clinical exposure, their perspectives may evolve from focusing on immediate communication hurdles to a more nuanced understanding of the systemic and psychosocial complexities of geriatric care. This finding underscores the critical need for early and targeted training that prepares students not only with theoretical knowledge but also with the emotional resilience and practical communication strategies required for real-world community engagement with older adults.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the views expressed on the acceptability, barriers, and facilitators in delivering health interventions were limited to healthcare university students, and we did not triangulate this data with the perspectives of the older adults who received the intervention. Future studies should investigate these perceptions among non-healthcare university students and older adults to provide a more comprehensive view. Second, our sample was characterized by a degree of homogeneity, with a majority of participants being first-year students (65%) and nursing students (45%). This composition may limit the transferability of our findings. Perspectives from students in later years of study or from other healthcare disciplines might have revealed different challenges or strategies. Future research should aim to recruit a more balanced sample across disciplines and years of study to capture a broader spectrum of experiences. Third, a significant limitation is that member checking was not performed, which would have involved asking participants to review the findings to ensure they accurately reflect their experiences [25]. This was primarily due to logistical and funding constraints. We employed other strategies to ensure rigor and enhance trustworthiness, including triangulation, peer debriefing, and the use of reflective journals to document thoughts on data [25]. Future studies should incorporate member checking or transcript review as a standard procedure. Fourth, this study only captured students’ views at a single point in time. Longitudinal interviews to track how their perspectives evolve with further training and experience would provide deeper insights. Finally, social desirability bias may have influenced participant responses, as students were interviewed by researchers from their own institution. Although the interviewer was not involved in their academic assessment, students may have still felt inclined to present their experiences in a more positive light. To mitigate this bias, we emphasized to the interviewees that all responses would be kept confidential and anonymized and encouraged candid feedback.

Conclusions

This study provided insights into improving the delivery of health education to underprivileged older adults to increase their motivation to engage in health-promoting behaviors and to enhance its effectiveness. In the context of the rapidly ageing population, we will be facing more older adults with cognitive and functional limitations, and their negative generalization of ageing might pose challenges in engaging in health-promoting behaviors. It is crucial to include health-promoting strategies in the healthcare professionals’ training curriculum and develop a guideline to deliver these health education interventions to older adults more effectively. As behavioral change requires multiple decision-making processes, health education should be personalized and stage-matched based on older adults’ readiness to change. Ultimately, with the global and local primary healthcare reform and the set-up of the territory-wide DHCs in Hong Kong, these infrastructures should be fully utilized as they could provide preventive services and social support that meet the life course needs of the population. The project’s name, “Generations Connect”, points to the intergenerational dynamics at play. While not a primary focus of this study, future research should investigate these intergenerational dynamics more explicitly, perhaps through dyadic interviews with both students and older adults, to understand how age-related perceptions influence the educational partnership.

Implication of study

Based on our findings, we propose the following actionable recommendations to improve intervention delivery for the ongoing Generations Connect project:

For curriculum development: Integrate mandatory modules in the healthcare professionals’ training curriculum on geriatric communication that include simulations with trained actors portraying older adults with functional and cognitive limitations. These modules should focus on teaching patience, empathy, and strategies for adapting communication in real-time.
