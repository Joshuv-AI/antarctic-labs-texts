Error Exponents for DNA Storage Codes with a Variable Number of Reads
Yan Hao Ling, Nir Weinberger, Jonathan Scarlett

Abstract
In this paper, we study error exponents for a concatataned coding based class of DNA storage codes in which the number of reads performed can be variable. That is, the decoder can sequentially perform reads and choose whether to output the final decision or take more reads, and we are interested in minimizing the average number of reads performed rather than a fixed pre-specified value. We show that this flexibility leads to a considerable reduction in the error probability compared to a fixed number of reads, not only in terms of constants in the error exponent but also in the scaling laws. This is shown via an achievability result for a suitably-designed protocol, and in certain parameter regimes we additionally establish a matching converse that holds for all protocols within a broader concatenated coding based class.



I
Introduction

In recent years, significant research attention has been paid to characterizing the capacity of DNA storage systems; see
[
1
]
for a recent overview. While capacity is perhaps the most fundamental notion of a performance limit, it is limited by its lack of consideration of
how quickly
the error probability approaches zero as the problem size increases. This limitation is addressed by studies of
error exponents
, which characterize the exponential decay rate of the error probability at rates below capacity. Error exponents have long been studied for channel coding problems
[
2
,
3
]
, and were recently studied for DNA storage in
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

In more detail,
[
4
]
studied error exponents of random codes, whereas
[
5
,
6
]
focused on a class of
concatenated codes
in which an an “inner code” for the sequencing channel is used in a black-box manner for individual molecules, and an “outer code” is used to handle the entire set of molecules. This class of codes is practically well-motivated, since the inner code can make use of well-established techniques from point-to-point channel coding. Moreover, in
[
5
,
6
]
the sequencing channel need not be a discrete memoryless channel as was assumed in
[
4
]
; for example, insertions and deletions can be considered.

While the results of
[
6
]
(building on
[
5
]
) provide exact (i.e., optimal) error exponents for a class of concatenated codes under a standard setup, they turn out to leave significant room for improvement under a minor modification of the setup. In particular, the error exponent is dominated by the event that there are unusually few distinct molecules sampled, and correspondingly, their converse result holds
even when the sequencing error probability is zero
. This suggests that the performance could be significantly improved by allowing a
variable number of reads
, so that more reads can be performed when needed, without increasing the
average
number of reads too much.

In this paper, we make this intuition precise by studying error exponents of concatenated codes under a variable number of reads, where we now consider the
average
number of reads instead of a pre-specified number. We show that this setup permits a considerably smaller error probability, and we attain matching achievability and converse results in certain regimes of interest.

[

,

]
, while allowing a variable number of reads. For completness, we provide the full description.

II-A

Basic Notation

We adopt a standard information-theoretic model of DNA storage, with the building blocks being as follows:

•

The goal is to reliably store and recover a
message

m
drawn uniformly at random from a finite set of messages.

•

A
molecule
is a length-
L
string whose symbols come from some finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
(e.g.,
\mathcal{X}=\{A,C,G,T\}
).

•

Each message is associated with an unordered collection of
M
input molecules that are stored. This collection is referred to as the (outer)
codeword
for
m
.

•

At the decoder,
N

reads
are performed. For each read, (i) one of the
M
input molecules is
sampled
uniformly at random; and (ii) the molecule in
\mathcal{X}^{L}
is passed through a
sequencing channel
(independently of all other reads) and the decoder sees the output, whose alphabet we denote by
\mathcal{Y}^{(L)}
. For example, the channel might be a discrete memoryless channel, but we do not require this to be the case, so
\mathcal{Y}^{(L)}
need not be an
L
-fold product.

•

In the fixed-length setup, the number of reads
N
is pre-specified, whereas in the variable-length setup, the decoder may choose when to stop performing reads and thus
N
is a random variable.

•

The decoder uses the reads to produce an estimate
\hat{m}
of the message.

We proceed to provide the full details and introduce aspects that are more specific to our setup.

II-B

Concatenated Coding Based Class of Protocols

We now describe the concatenated coding based class of DNA storage codes that we consider. The following definition summarizes the relevant notions of inner codes.

Definition 1
.

We define the following:

1.

An
inner code

(X_{\rm in},D_{\rm in})
with parameters
(R_{\mathrm{in}},L)
is given by the following:

•

A sequence of
\exp(R_{\mathrm{in}}L)
distinct molecules, each of length
L
, denoted by
X_{\rm in}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{\exp(R_{\mathrm{in}}L)})
.

•

An inner decoding function operating on the sequencing channel output,
D_{\rm in}:\mathcal{Y}^{(L)}\rightarrow\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{\exp(R_{\mathrm{in}}L)}\}
.

2.

Given the inner codebook
(X_{\rm in},D_{\rm in})
, for each
x\in X_{\rm in}
, the (inner)
error probability
for
x
is the probability that
D_{\rm in}(y)\neq x
, where
y
is distributed according to the sequencing channel with input
x
. The highest (among all
x\in X_{\rm in}
) of these error probabilities gives the
maximal error probability
of the inner code.

3.

A sequence of inner codebooks
(X_{\rm in}^{(L)},D_{\rm in}^{(L)})_{L=1}^{\infty}
achieves a rate
R_{\mathrm{in}}
if each
(X_{\rm in}^{(L)},D_{\rm in}^{(L)})
has parameters
(R_{\mathrm{in}},L)
, and the maximal error probability of
(X_{\rm in}^{(L)},D_{\rm in}^{(L)})
approaches zero as
L\to\infty
. Such a rate is said to be
achievable
for the sequencing channel.

We highlight that we use the inner code in a “black-box” manner, only assuming that it has maximal error probability approaching zero as
L
increases, in accordance with the above notion of achievable (inner) rate.

The class of concatenated codes that we consider is then defined as follows; when specialized to the case of a fixed number of reads, this recovers the class studied in
[
5
,
6
]
.

Definition 2
.

Given an inner code
(X_{\rm in},D_{\rm in})
, the protocol is said to perform
separate inner and outer coding
if it satisfies the following two properties:

•

For any message
m
at the encoder, the resulting input (i.e., the
outer codeword
) is a multiset
A_{m}
of size
M
whose elements are chosen from the inner codebook
X_{\rm in}
.

•

After performing some number
\tilde{N}
of reads and observing the outputs
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{\tilde{N}}
, any decision made by the decoder (i.e., whether to continue performing reads or not, and if not, what to output as the estimate) depends only on
D_{\rm in}(y_{1}),D_{\rm in}(y_{2}),\ldots,D_{\rm in}(y_{\tilde{N}})
.

In this paper, we further impose the requirement that the code is
index-based
according to the following definition (which was also adopted in
[
5
,
6
]
).

Definition 3
.

A codebook is
index-based
if there exist
M
disjoint sets of molecules
(B_{i})_{i=1}^{M}
of equal size, such that every outer codeword
A_{m}
contains exactly one molecule from each
B_{i}
.

Index-based codes are often considered to be favorable for keeping the encoder and decoder simple; see
[
1
, Sec. 4.3]
and
[
5
]
for further discussion. We note that Definition
3
ensures that every
A_{m}
contains
M
distinct molecules, meaning that the multiset in Definition
2
is a set.

II-C

Sampling, Sequencing, and Decoding

As noted in
[
6
]
, under separate inner and outer coding it is useful to think of sequencing and inner decoding as a single step; every time a read is performed, the following occurs:

•

One of the
M
(distinct) input molecules
x\in A_{m}
is chosen uniformly at random;

•

The molecule is passed through the sequencing channel;

•

The inner code’s decoder is applied to the channel output to produce
\widetilde{x}
.

For a fixed inner code, the second and third steps can be viewed as inducing an overall distribution
P(\widetilde{x}|x)
, where it we have
P(x|x)=1-o(1)
as
L\to\infty
when the inner code achieves rate
R_{\mathrm{in}}
.
However, we will not use this probabilistic approach; rather, in accordance with our goal of using the inner code in a “black-box” manner, we will instead adopt a
worst-case
(adversarial) model of what happens when the inner code fails. Specifically:

•

For each read performed, a
sequencing error
occurs with some probability
p=o(1)
, independently of all other reads. (Hence, we have
\widetilde{x}=x
with probability
1-p=1-o(1)
.)

•

When a sequencing error occurs, we assume that
\widetilde{x}
may equal
any
element of the inner code. (We allow this element to be
x
itself, so strictly speaking
p
is an
upper bound
on the error probability of the inner code.)

In the second dot point, we will view
\widetilde{x}
as being chosen by an adversary; see Definitions
4
,
5
, and
6
below.

Our focus in this paper is on the case where the number of reads is variable. Thus, at each time step, having performed some number of reads, the decoder may choose to perform another one, or may choose to stop and output the estimate of the message. Thus, the number of reads
N
is a random variable (even if the decoder is deterministic, these decisions depend on the random message and noise). We are interested in keeping the
average
number of reads small, and accordingly write

\overline{N}=\mathbb{E}[N]

(1)

to denote this average. We assume that
\overline{N}<\infty
, meaning that the decoder stop in finite time with probability one.

For a uniformly random message
m
and its estimate
\hat{m}
, we define the
error probability

P_{e}=\mathbb{P}(\hat{m}\neq m).

(2)

We re-iterate that we are not only interested in attaining
P_{e}\to 0
, but also in the speed of convergence to zero.

II-D

Scaling of parameters

While in principle there any many possibilities for how
(L,M,\overline{N})
scale with respect to one another, we will focus our attention on scaling regimes that are the most widely-adopted in information-theoretic studies (e.g.,
[
5
,
6
,
4
,
12
]
), and are practically well-motivated (e.g.,
L=\Theta(\log M)
corresponding to relatively short reads).
Specifically, we focus on the case that the following quantities are constant as
M\to\infty
:

•

c=\overline{N}/M
, representing the (average) coverage depth;

•

\beta=\frac{L}{\log M}
, characterizing the molecule length;

•

R_{\mathrm{in}}
, which is the inner codebook rate as per Definition
1
;

•

R
, which we write for the outer code rate such that there are
\exp(RML)
messages.

Observe that the inner code contains
\exp(R_{\mathrm{in}}L)=M^{\beta R_{\mathrm{in}}}
molecules, and accordingly we define

\alpha={\beta R_{\mathrm{in}}}

(3)

so that this simplifies to
M^{\alpha}
. We assume that
\alpha>1
, since the use of index-based codes (Definition
3
) requires having at least
M
inner codewords.

1

Moreover, for
\alpha\leq 1
the capacity, as defined in the next paragraph, is zero even when the code need not be index-based.

The

is defined as the supremum of

for which there exists a sequence of codes (indexed by
M
) attaining
P_{e}\to 0
.
Under a simpler model in which the
M

R_{\mathrm{in}}-\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{\alpha-1}{\beta}.

Following

II-E

Discussion on Capacity with a Fixed vs. Variable Number of Reads

We briefly pause to discuss the overall storage capacity (for general codes that need not be concatenated nor index-based). This capacity can be defined under a fixed number of reads with parameter
c=N/M
, or under a variable number of reads with parameter
c=\overline{N}/M
, and it is not obvious whether these two capacity values (with all other parameters being identical) are always the same. Clearly, allowing a variable number of reads cannot decrease capacity.

Building on ideas from variable-rate channel coding
[
13
, Thm. 10]
, we can also provide a partial statement in the opposite direction by showing that any achievable rate in the variable-
N
setting implies a certain positive result for the fixed-
N
setting. Consider any variable-
N
code performing
\overline{N}
reads on average and satisfying
P_{e}\to 0
. By Markov’s inequality, we have
N\leq\overline{N}(1+\delta)
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{1+\delta}=\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}
. Then, we can form a fixed-length code that performs
\overline{N}(1+\delta)
reads, produces the same output as the variable-
N
decoder whenever the latter stops by time
\overline{N}(1+\delta)
, and declares an error otherwise. This code performs a fixed number
\overline{N}(1+\delta)
of reads, and succeeds with probability at least
\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}-o(1)
.

Since
\delta
is arbitrarily small, this argument implies that the following conditions would suffice for the fixed-
N
and variable-
N
capacities to be identical:

(i)

The
strong converse
holds in the fixed-
N
setting;
3
3

3

That is, the
\epsilon
-capacity
defined with respect to the requirement
P_{e}\leq\epsilon+o(1)
(rather than the usual
P_{e}\to 0
) is identical for all fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

(ii)

The capacity is continuous with respect to the parameter
c=N/M
(in the fixed-
N
setting) or
c=\overline{N}/M
(in the variable-
N
setting).

These conditions seem reasonable to expect, but formally establishing them may be difficult. Condition (i) is complicated by the fact that existing upper bounds on capacity typically rely on Fano’s inequality (e.g., see
[
1
,
4
]
), which only gives a weak converse.
4
4

4

An alternative to Condition (i) would be a condition that
N
is
sufficiently concentrated
around its mean for the optimal variable-
N
code, so that the above-mentioned success probability bound
\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}-o(1)
becomes
1-o(1)
. However, such a concentration result also seems difficult to prove in general.

Condition (ii) is partially supported by existing capacity
bounds
being continuous in
c
(again see
[
1
,
4
]
), but in general we do not have a single-letter expression for the capacity.

Perhaps unsurprisingly given the above discussion, the
achievable rates
in our work (i.e., the rates under which we attain
P_{e}\to 0
) will be identical to those attained in the fixed-
N
regime with index-based concatenated codes in
[
5
,
6
]
. (See (
7
) below.)

II-F

Discussion on Random vs. Adversarial Sequencing Errors

There are several possible models for what happens when a sequencing error occurs, with the main distinction being random vs. adversarial (both of which are explored in
[
6
]
). For example, a simple randomized model would be that in which each sequencing error leads to a uniformly random incorrect molecule. In this paper, we focus entirely on adversarial models for the sequencing errors; compared to a randomized model, this amounts to a strength in the achievability results, but a limitation in the converse results. We believe that adversarial models are partially justified by the preference for using the inner code in a “black-box” manner without needing to know its inner workings, which points to “worst-case” thinking regarding what happens when errors occur.

In the setting of a fixed number of reads, random and adversarial sequencing error models give identical error exponents
[
6
]
, at least under the standard scaling laws adopted in our work. Intuitively, this is because sequencing errors are not dominant, and instead the dominant error event is failing to sample enough distinct molecules. With that no longer being the case under a variable number of reads, it may be that random and adversarial models behave more differently; this direction is left for future work.

III
Main Results

Here we present and discuss our main results, deferring the proofs to Sections
IV
and
V
.

III-A

Strong and Weak Adversaries

Recall that for each molecule sampled, a sequencing error occurs with probability
p
, and when this is the case, the decoded molecule may be arbitrary. We consider two adversarial models for how this molecule may be chosen, one corresponding to a stronger adversary, and one corresponding to a weaker one. Both our achievability and converse results will ultimately hold under both adversaries, but it will be convenient to first prove the converse for the stronger adversary.

We first state some basic assumptions that hold under both adversaries.

Assumption 4
.

(Basic Assumptions) We adopt the following assumptions:

•

The message
m
is chosen uniformly at random, and is revealed to the adversary.

•

The protocol uses separate inner and outer coding (Definition
2
), and the code is index-based (Definition
3
).

•

The adversary knows the inner and outer codebooks (and thus the encoder).

•

The decoder is deterministic.

•

Each read has a sequencing error with probability
p
independently of all other reads.

•

Whenever a sequencing error occurs, the molecule is replaced by some molecule in the inner codebook (possibly including the correct one).

We believe that the first two and last two assumptions are natural in an adversarial setting. The restriction to concatenated codes is justified by the fact although they can be suboptimal in terms of capacity (let alone error exponent), they are often favorable in terms of complexity. The restriction to
index-based
codes is also partly justified by the goal of reducing complexity, though ideally a converse could be proved (in future work) without needing this assumption, and similarly for the assumption of deterministic decoding.
Our current converse proofs rely on both of these assumptions holding.

Next, we define the additional assumptions for our stronger adversary.

Assumption 5
.

(Strong Adversary) For our strong adversary, we adopt the following additional assumptions:

•

The adversary knows the (deterministic) decoder.

•

The adversary knows the infinite-length sequence of molecules that would be sampled (before sequencing errors) if the decoder were to continue performing reads.

•

The adversary also knows the infinite-length sequence of which reads will be subject to sequencing errors.

While the first of these assumptions is fairly natural, the second and third may be less so, and may appear to “give the adversary too much power”. This amounts to a strength for an achievability result, but a weakness for a converse result. Fortunately, we will also establish our converse under the following relaxed assumptions.

Assumption 6
.

(Weak Adversary) For our weak adversary, we relax the assumption stated in Assumption
5
as follows:

•

The adversary does not know the decoder.

•

If a sequencing error occurs at the
i
-th read, then the adversary’s choice of replaced molecule is only allowed to depend on what was observed in previous reads (i.e., those indexed by
1,\dotsc,i-1
).

•

The adversary does not know which future reads will be subject to sequencing errors.

We now proceed to state our main results.

III-B

Statement of Main Results

Our main achievability result is stated as follows, and holds even under Assumptions
4
and
5
(which, for achievability, is a stronger result than one using Assumptions
4
and
6
).

Theorem 7
.

Let
c=\log\frac{1}{1-R_{0}-\delta}
for any
\delta\in(0,1-R_{0})
. Under Assumptions
4
and
5
, there exists a protocol with error probability
P_{e}\leq p^{(\delta+o(1))M}
and an average number of reads
\overline{N}\leq(c+o(1))\cdot M
when the message is chosen uniformly at random.
5
5

5

In fact, our proof shows the same for the worst-case message, not only a random message.

Next, we state our converse result under the strong adversary, and then under the weak adversary. The latter result of course implies the former, but we find it useful to prove the former one first.

Theorem 8
.

Let
c=\log\frac{1}{1-R_{0}-\delta}
with
\delta
being sufficiently small such that
\delta<ce^{-c}
. Under Assumptions
4
and
5
(strong adversary), any decoder with error probability satisfying
P_{e}\leq o(p^{\delta M+1})
must require at least
\overline{N}\geq(c-o(1))M
reads in expectation when the message is chosen uniformly at random.

Theorem 9
.

Let
c=\log\frac{1}{1-R_{0}-\delta}
with
\delta
being sufficiently small such that
\delta<ce^{-c}
. Under Assumptions
4
and
6
(weak adversary), any decoder with error probability satisfying
P_{e}\leq o(2^{-M}p^{\delta M+1})
must require at least
\overline{N}\geq(c-o(1))M
reads in expectation when the message is chosen uniformly at random.

We now proceed to compare the achievability and converse.

III-C

Discussion and Numerical Evaluation

We first note that the statements
P_{e}\leq p^{(\delta+o(1))M}
,
P_{e}\leq o(p^{\delta M+1})
, and
P_{e}\leq o(2^{-M}p^{\delta M+1})
, while slightly different, all have right-hand sides behaving as
p^{(\delta+o(1))M}=\exp\big(-\big(\delta M\log\frac{1}{p}\big)(1+o(1))\big)
when
p=o(1)
, and thus all amount to having the same error exponent (i.e., the same value of
\lim_{M\to\infty}-\frac{1}{M\log(1/p))}\log P_{e}
, which in this case equals
\delta
).

Recall that error exponents for concatenated codes with a fixed number of reads were studied in
[
5
,
6
]
, with the achievability parts using index-based codes and only assuming that
p=o(1)
.
One might be tempted to compare those exponents with Theorem
7
, but in fact, we can make a stronger claim than a higher exponent: We also improve the
scaling law
in the exponent, reducing the error probability from
e^{-\Theta(M)}
to
e^{-\Theta(M\log(1/p))}
. Thus, using a variable number of reads can provably allow considerably smaller error probability. As discussed in the introduction, the intuition is that being variable-length allows us to avoid certain (dominant) “bad events” where too few distinct molecules are sampled.

Next, we compare our achievability and converse bounds. As discussed above, the error exponents themselves are identical, but with the important difference that the converse is restricted to
\delta<ce^{-c}
. This means that we have matching achievability and converse bounds, but only for a limited range of parameters. As we see in Figure
1
, the range where the converse holds is limited but still reasonable, and corresponds to regimes where the average number of reads is not too high.

Finally, observe that
P_{e}\leq p^{(\delta+o(1))M}
implies
P_{e}\to 0
whenever
\delta>0
. Thus, we attain a positive error exponent in Theorem
7
whenever
c>\log\frac{1}{1-R_{0}}
, or equivalently
R_{0}<1-e^{-c}
. Combining this with the definition of
R_{0}
in (
5
), and choosing
R_{\rm in}
to be arbitrarily close to the sequencing channel capacity
C_{\rm in}
, we find that we attain a positive error exponent for all rates satisfying

R<(1-e^{-c})\Big(C_{\rm in}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Big).

(7)

This matches the set of rates with a positive error exponent in the related prior works studying the fixed-
N
setting
[
5
,
6
]
, so our improvements are only in the decay rate of
P_{e}
and not in the set of rates for which
P_{e}\to 0
.

IV
Achievability Proof

In this section, we prove Theorem
7
. We first describe the codebook choice and the decoding rule, the latter of which makes use of the parameter
\delta\in(0,1-R_{0})
from the theorem statement:

•

Codebook
: We choose an index-based codebook
\{A_{m}\}
such that, for any
\epsilon>0
, when
M
is sufficiently large, it holds that

|A_{i}\cap A_{j}|<(R_{0}+\epsilon)M,\quad\forall i\neq j,

(8)

with
R_{0}
being as in (
5
). The existence of such a codebook was proved in
[
6
, Lemma 7]
using a simple greedy construction analogous to the classical Gilbert-Varshamov construction (e.g., see
[
2
, Ex. 5.19]
).

•

Decoding
: We say that a codeword
A_{i}
is
\delta
-consistent
with the decoded molecules if there are at most
\delta M
decoded molecules not in
A_{i}
. The decoding rule is then as follows:
Continue sampling until exactly one codeword
A_{i}
is
\delta
-consistent, and declare that the message is
i
.
(If a point is ever reached where none of the
A_{i}
are
\delta
-consistent, the decoder stops and declares failure.)

The following lemma characterizes the average number of samples taken.

Lemma 10
.

Under the preceding codebook and decoding rule, with sequencing error probability
p=o(1)
, the expected number of reads is at most
(\log\frac{1}{1-R_{0}-\delta}+o(1))\cdot M
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrarily small constant
\epsilon>0
and suppose that
M
is large enough such that (
8
) holds.
We first observe that once we see at least
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
decoded molecules in
A_{i}
, the decoding process stops and outputs
i
. This is because once this is true, we have from (
8
) that all of the other messages
j\neq i
must have more than
\delta M
decoded molecules outside
A_{j}
.

Letting
m
be the true message, it follows that the average stopping time is upper bounded by the average time to seeing
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
decoded molecules in
A_{m}
. To characterize this, note that after seeing
k
distinct decoded molecules from
A_{m}
, every new sample gives a probability at least
(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{M})
of seeing the
(k+1)
-th one. The average time for this to occur is at most
\frac{1}{(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{M})}
, since
{\rm Geometric}(q)
has mean
\frac{1}{q}
.

The expected time taken to see
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
molecules from
A_{m}
is therefore upper bounded by

\sum_{k=0}^{(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M}\frac{1}{(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{M})}=\frac{1}{1-p}M\bigg(\log\frac{1}{1-R_{0}-\epsilon-\delta}+o(1)\bigg).

(9)

Since
\epsilon
is arbitrarily small and
p=o(1)
, the conclusion follows.
∎

Next, we study the error probability.

Lemma 11
.

Under the preceding codebook and decoding rule, and with sequencing error probability
p=o(1)
, the error probability behaves as
P_{e}\leq p^{(\delta+o(1))M}
.

Proof.

Let
m
be the true message. In order for an error to occur upon stopping, it must be that
m
itself is no longer
\delta
-consistent, which is only possible if there are at least
\delta M
sequencing errors. Moreover, as noted in the proof of Lemma
10
, once we see at least
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
decoded molecules in
A_{m}
, the decoding process stops and outputs
m
. Combining these two observations gives:
If we see at least
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
distinct molecules without sequencing errors before incurring
\delta M
sequencing errors, then the decoding decision is correct.

Suppose that at a certain point we have seen
k
distinct decoded molecules (some of which may be from sequencing errors). The probability that the next molecule will be a new molecule without sequencing error is
(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{M})
, while the probability that the next sample will incur a sequencing error is
p
. Hence, the probability of incurring a sequencing error before seeing a new molecule without a sequencing error is given by
\frac{p}{p+(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{M})}
. Observe that for
k\leq(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
, this probability is upper bounded as follows:

\frac{p}{p+(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{M})}\leq\frac{p}{(1-p)(1-\frac{k}{M})}\leq\frac{p}{(1-p)(1-R_{0}-\epsilon-\delta)}.

(10)

To understand the implication of this finding on the error probability, we imagine a binary string being formed as we sample:

•

Begin with the empty string.

•

When we see a sequencing error, append a ‘0’ to the string.

•

When we see a new decoded molecule (i.e., one different from any seen before) without a sequencing error, append a ‘1’ to the string.

•

The remaining case is that we see an already-seen molecule without a sequencing error, in which case we do not extend the string.

•

Stop when the string contains
\delta M
0s or
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
1s.

Note that by construction, the stopping rule corresponds to having
\delta M
sequencing errors, or having
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
distinct molecules without sequencing errors.

By (
10
), the probability of seeing any specific string with
\delta M
0s is upper bounded by

\left(\frac{p}{(1-p)(1-R_{0}-\epsilon-\delta)}\right)^{\delta M}.

(11)

Moreover, there are at most
2^{\delta M+(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M}
strings to consider, as any binary string with length exceeding
\delta M+(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
must either contain at least
\delta M
0s or
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
1s.
Hence, by the union bound, the probability that
\delta M
0s appear before
(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M
1s is at most

2^{\delta M+(R_{0}+\epsilon+\delta)M}\cdot\left(\frac{p}{(1-p)(1-R_{0}-\epsilon-\delta)}\right)^{\delta M}.

(12)

This simplifies to
p^{(\delta+o(1))M}
due to
\epsilon
being arbitrarily small and
p=o(1)
.
∎

Our analysis will center around the sequence of molecules that have been sampled (before sequencing errors) and observed (after sequencing errors) when the decoder stops and outputs a decision. Accordingly, we provide the following definition.

We define the following sequences with (random) length

whose entries are drawn i.i.d. via

conditioned on the message being

We additionally introduce the following definition specific to index-based codes (see Definition

).

Definition 13

The (random)

indicates that the

(as specified in Definition

denote a (fixed) realization of such a sequence.

Importantly, the sequence

for index-based codes. That is, the entries of

are drawn i.i.d. from

.
We also mention that while this definition is taken with respect to the molecules before sequencing errors, we will consider an adversary that never changes the index, and hence (for our adversary) the index sequence remains identical after sequencing errors.

Observe also that conditioned on any index sequence

and any message

, the infinite-length sequence
(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\dotsc)
is uniquely determined, since the

-th molecule is determined by the

-th index and the unique molecule of codeword

The following definition provides a set of index sequences

which will prove to be amenable to lower bounding the error probability (because

Definition 14

Fix

\delta\in(0,1-R_{0})
from the statements of Theorems

(i)

;

(ii)

.

For each message

be the decoding region of message

6
6

6

Hence, if some

Proof of Theorem

(Converse Under a Strong Adversary)

Recall the set

Given any

such that the index sequence

with probability

.

Lemma 16
.

There exists an adversary satisfying Assumptions
4
and
5
such that the following holds:
Suppose that the message

be an index sequence in

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m}|f)\geq p^{\delta M+1}\Big(\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|f)-p-\exp(-(R_{0}-R^{\prime})(\alpha-1)M\log M)\Big).

Note that here and subsequently, we write “

We defer the proofs of these lemmas to subsequent subsections, and first show how they imply Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

Consider a strategy that samples at most

\overline{N}=\mathbb{E}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|)\leq c^{\prime}M

Moreover, we have the following lower bound on the error probability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m}\land F\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime}))

(19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(f)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m}|f)

(20)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(f)\cdot p^{\delta M+1}\Big(\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|f)-p-\exp((R^{\prime}-R_{0})(\alpha-1)M\log M)\Big)

, and

Moreover, Lemma

, and combining this with (

\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M\land F\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime}))\geq\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M)-\mathbb{P}(F\notin S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime}))\geq\frac{c^{\prime}}{c^{\prime\prime}}-o(1).

Substituting into (

p^{\delta M+1}\Big(\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M\land F\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime}))-o(1)\Big)\geq p^{\delta M+1}(c^{\prime}/c^{\prime\prime}-o(1))=\Omega(p^{\delta M+1}).

(\lambda^{(m)})_{j_{1}}=(\lambda^{(m)})_{j_{2}}
. Therefore,
\lambda^{(m)}|_{T_{2}}
can be uniquely determined by a function
f^{\prime}\,:\,T_{2}\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,M^{\alpha-1}\}
specifying each molecule within
B_{i}
(see Definition
3
) when the index is
i=f(j)
(with
j\in T_{2}
). Since there are at most
R^{\prime}M
distinct values in
\{f(j)\}_{j\in T_{2}}
(see Definition
14
) and each
B_{i}
has size
\frac{M^{\alpha}}{M}=M^{\alpha-1}
, there are at most
(M^{\alpha-1})^{R^{\prime}M}=\exp(R^{\prime}M(\alpha-1)\log M)
such choices of
f^{\prime}
, and thus at most
\exp(R^{\prime}M(\alpha-1)\log M)
possible values of
\lambda^{(m)}|_{T_{2}}
.
∎

Next, define
V\subseteq U
as follows:

V=\{m\in U_{1}\,:\,\lambda^{(m)}|_{T_{2}}\text{ is unique within }U_{1}\}.

(40)

By Lemma
18
,
|V|\leq\exp(R^{\prime}M(\alpha-1)\log M)
, so with a uniformly randomly message
m
over
\exp(RML)=\exp(RM(\alpha-1)\log M)
values (see (
6
)), we have

\mathbb{P}(m\in V)\leq\frac{\exp(R^{\prime}M(\alpha-1)\log M)}{\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)}=\exp(-(R_{0}-R^{\prime})M(\alpha-1)\log M).

(41)

This property will be used shortly.

Using Lemma
39
, we have for all
m\in U_{1}\setminus V
(i.e., messages in
U_{1}
with non-unique
\lambda^{(m)}|_{T_{2}}
) that

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m}\land\Psi=1|m)\geq p^{\delta M+1},

(42)

which implies

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m}\land\Psi=1)\geq\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{1}\setminus V)p^{\delta M+1}.

(43)

We can also observe a useful fact about messages
m\notin U
: The adversary sets
\Psi=0
(and is thus inactive) with probability at least
1-p
, and when this happens, we have
|\widetilde{\Lambda}|>c^{\prime\prime}M
by the definition of
U
in (
36
). Hence, we have for
m\notin U
that

\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|m)\leq p,

(44)

which implies

\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M)\leq p\mathbb{P}(m\notin U)+\mathbb{P}(m\in U)\leq p+\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{1})+\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2}).

(45)

Re-arranging gives
\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{1})\geq\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M)-p-\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2})
, which we combine with (
43
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m}\land\Psi=1)

(46)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{1}\setminus V)p^{\delta M+1}

(47)

\displaystyle\geq(\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{1})-\mathbb{P}(m\in V))p^{\delta M+1}

(48)

\displaystyle\geq p^{\delta M+1}\Big(\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M)-p-\mathbb{P}(m\in V)-\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2})\Big).

(49)

Observe that if
m\in U_{2}
and
\Psi=0
, then the adversary does nothing and the decoder outputs something other than
m
, so an error occurs. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m}\land\Psi=0)\geq(1-p)\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2}).

(50)

The overall error probability is then lower bounded by the sum of (
49
) and (
50
). Note that
1-p>p^{\delta M+1}
, which means the resulting coefficient of
\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2})
is positive, and lower bounding this term by zero gives

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m})\geq p^{\delta M+1}\Big(\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M)-p-\mathbb{P}(m\in V)\Big).

(51)

Lemma
17
then follows by substituting the bound on
\mathbb{P}(m\in V)
from (
41
).

Proof of Theorem
9
(Converse Under a Weak Adversary)

Under our weak adversary setup (Assumption
6
), the adversary can no longer look into the future in order to identify which
R^{\prime}M
(or more) molecules to leave unchanged and which
\delta M
(or fewer) to change. Accordingly, we adopt an adversary that
randomly
chooses
R^{\prime}M
indices of molecules to be unchanged, and attempts to change certain molecules in the remaining indices to cause an error (if possible). While this makes it less likely that the adversary will succeed in its goal, it turns out to suffice for attaining a converse having the same error exponent.

Formally, the adversary is described as follows:

•

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a uniformly randomly chosen set of
R^{\prime}M
indices in
\{1,\dotsc,M\}
, meaning there are
\binom{M}{R^{\prime}M}
possible choices of
\mathcal{I}
.

•

In accordance with Assumption
4
, the adversary knows true message
m
; recall that
A_{m}
is the corresponding (outer) codeword.

•

If there exists
m^{\prime}\neq m
such that
A_{m}|_{\mathcal{I}}=A_{m^{\prime}}|_{\mathcal{I}}
, the adversary will choose such an
m^{\prime}
uniformly randomly. Otherwise, we say that
m^{\prime}
is unspecified.

•

As in Section
V-D
, the adversary additionally generates
\Psi\sim{\rm Bernoulli}(p)
(independent of everything else). If
m^{\prime}
is specified and
\Psi=1
then we say that the adversary is
active
, and otherwise the adversary is
inactive
and leaves all molecules unchanged.

•

When active, the adversary attempts to change every molecule to match the corresponding molecules of
A_{m^{\prime}}
. The adversary will only achieve this goal if all of the required sequencing errors occur.

Let
t_{m}(f)
be the stopping time given index sequence
f
and message
m
when there are no sequencing errors, and define

U(f)=\{m:t_{m}(f)\leq c^{\prime\prime}M\}.

(52)

Analogous to (
37
)–(
38
), let
\Phi(f)
be the set of messages such that the decoder outputs
m
when the index sequence is
f
and there are no sequencing errors, and partition
U(f)
as follows:

\displaystyle U_{1}(f)=U(f)\cap\Phi(f),

(53)

\displaystyle U_{2}(f)=U(f)\setminus\Phi(f)=U(f)\setminus U_{1}(f).

(54)

Recall also the definition of
S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})
in Definition
14
, and for each
f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})
, let
T_{1}(f)
and
T_{2}(f)
be the sets meeting the conditions of Definition
14
, i.e.,
|T_{1}(f)|\leq\delta M
, and there are at most
R^{\prime}M
distinct values of
\{f(j)\}_{j\in T_{2}(f)}
.

The following lemma follows easily from the preceding definitions.

Lemma 19
.

If the message
m
and the index sequence
f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})
satisfy all of the following conditions, then a decoding error occurs:

•

The adversary’s choice of
m^{\prime}
satisfies
m^{\prime}\in U_{1}(f)
(if no
m^{\prime}
satisfies
A_{m}|_{\mathcal{I}}=A_{m^{\prime}}|_{\mathcal{I}}
, then
m^{\prime}
is unspecified and this condition is false);

•

T_{2}(f)\subseteq\mathcal{I}
;

•

For each index in
T_{1}(f)
, a sequencing error occurs at that index;

•

The adversary’s Bernoulli decision returns
\Psi=1
(and thus the adversary is active).

Proof.

Observe that under the above conditions, the adversary can modify all molecules to match the message
m^{\prime}
, and since
m^{\prime}\in U_{1}(f)
, the decoder stops before seeing
c^{\prime\prime}M
molecules and declares that the message is
m^{\prime}
.
∎

Next, we give a useful characterization of the probability of each possible
m^{\prime}
occurring.

Lemma 20
.

Let
m_{0}
be any message such that
A_{m_{0}}|_{\mathcal{I}}
is not unique. Then the adversary’s (random) choice of
m^{\prime}
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(m^{\prime}=m_{0})=\exp(-R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)
. (Note that we do not condition on the message
m
here, but rather this probability is averaged over all possible values of
m
).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(m_{0},\mathcal{I})
be the set of all messages that share the same
A_{m_{0}}|_{\mathcal{I}}
.
For each
m\in\mathcal{M}
with
m\neq m_{0}
, the probability of the adversary choosing
m^{\prime}=m_{0}
conditioned on the true message being
m
is exactly
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|-1}
. Recalling from (
6
) that there are
\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)
messages in total, we also have
\mathbb{P}(m\in\mathcal{M}\land m\neq m_{0})=\frac{|\mathcal{M}|-1}{\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)}
. Combining the preceding two observations, we obtain
\mathbb{P}(m^{\prime}=m_{0})=\exp(-R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)
as desired.
∎

We can now proceed to compute the probability that the conditions of Lemma
19
hold, initially conditioning on fixed
f
and
\mathcal{I}
.
Define the set

V(\mathcal{I})=\{m:A_{m}|_{\mathcal{I}}\text{ is unique}\}.

(55)

By Lemma
20
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(m^{\prime}\in U_{1}(f)|f,\mathcal{I})\geq\frac{|U_{1}(f)\setminus V(\mathcal{I})|}{\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)}.

(56)

Note that if
m\notin U(f)
, then recalling that with probability
1-p
the adversary generates
\Psi=0
and does nothing, we have
\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|m,f,\mathcal{I})\leq p
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|f,\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(m\in U(f)|f,\mathcal{I})+\sum_{m\notin U(f)}\mathbb{P}(m)\cdot p

(57)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{1}(f)\setminus V(\mathcal{I})|f,\mathcal{I})+\mathbb{P}(m\in V(\mathcal{I})|f,\mathcal{I})+\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2}(f)|f,\mathcal{I})+p.

(58)

We evaluate the first term as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{1}(f)\setminus V(\mathcal{I})|f,\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{1}(f)\setminus V(\mathcal{I}))

(59)

\displaystyle=\frac{|U_{1}(f)\setminus V(\mathcal{I})|}{\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)},

(60)

where the first step uses that
F
and
\mathcal{I}
are independent of
m
, and the second step uses the fact that
m
is uniform over
\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)
messages. Substituting into (
58
) and re-arranging, we obtain

\frac{|U_{1}(f)\setminus V(\mathcal{I})|}{\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)}\geq\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|f,\mathcal{I})-\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2}(f)|f,\mathcal{I})-\mathbb{P}(m\in V(\mathcal{I})|f,\mathcal{I})-p,

(61)

meaning the right-hand side lower bounds
\mathbb{P}(m^{\prime}\in U_{1}(f)|f,\mathcal{I})
via (
56
).

Next, observe that each
A_{m}|_{\mathcal{I}}
can be described by a function from
\mathcal{I}
to the molecule in
A_{m}
with the corresponding index in
\mathcal{I}
, so that there are at most
(M^{\alpha-1})^{R^{\prime}M}
such choices (similar to the proof of Lemma
18
). Therefore,

|V(\mathcal{I})|\leq(\text{number of possible $A_{m}|_{\mathcal{I}}$})\leq(M^{\alpha-1})^{R^{\prime}M}=\exp(R^{\prime}M(\alpha-1)\log M),

(62)

which implies

\mathbb{P}(m\in V(\mathcal{I})|f,\mathcal{I})\leq\frac{\exp(R^{\prime}M(\alpha-1)\log M)}{\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M}.

(63)

We now check the conditions of Lemma
19
:

•

By (
56
), the first condition of Lemma
19
holds with probability (conditioned on
f,\mathcal{I}
) at least

\frac{|U_{1}(f)\setminus V(\mathcal{I})|}{\exp(R_{0}M(\alpha-1)\log M)}\geq\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|f,\mathcal{I})-\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2}(f)|f,\mathcal{I})-o(1),

(64)

where the inequality follows by applying (
61
) and noting that the last two terms therein are
o(1)
due to (
63
) (with
R^{\prime}<R_{0}
) and
p=o(1)
.

•

Conditioned on any
f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})
, the second condition occurs with probability at least
2^{-M}
; this is because at least one such
\mathcal{I}
exists, and the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{I}
is over at most
2^{M}
elements.

•

Conditioned on any
f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})
, the third condition occurs with probability at least
p^{\delta M}
, since
|T_{1}(f)|\leq\delta M
.

•

Lastly, the adversary sets
\Psi=1
with probability
p
.

Combining these and averaging over
\mathcal{I}
, the error probability conditioned on any fixed
f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})
is lower bounded as follows:

\mathbb{P}(\text{error}\wedge\Psi=1|f)\geq(\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|f)-\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2}(f)|f)-o(1))\cdot 2^{-M}\cdot p^{\delta M}\cdot p,

(65)

where we used the fact that the relevant sources of randomness (for
m^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{I}
, sequencing errors, and
\Psi
) are independent.

If the adversary is not active, then an error will occur whenever
m\in U_{2}(f)
by the definition of
U_{2}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(\text{error}\wedge\Psi=0|f)\geq\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2}(f)|f)(1-p).

(66)

Adding together the terms for
\Psi=0
and
\Psi=1
, we see that the overall coefficient to
\mathbb{P}(m\in U_{2}(f)|f)
is positive, and lower bounding this term by zero gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{error}|f)\geq(\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|f)-o(1))\cdot 2^{-M}\cdot p^{\delta M}.

(67)

Averaging (
67
) over
f
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{error})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(f)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\Lambda}\in E_{m}|f)

(68)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{f\in S(c^{\prime\prime},R^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(f)(\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{\Lambda}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}M|f)-o(1))\cdot 2^{-M}\cdot p^{\delta M+1}.

(69)

This result matches (
22
) from the proof of Theorem
8
, but with an extra
2^{-M}
factor. The remainder of the proof is identical to that of Theorem
8
but with this extra factor incorporated, and thus the final scaling in the theorem statement is
o(2^{-M}p^{\delta M+1})
instad of
o(p^{\delta M+1})
.

VI
Conclusion

We have established achievablity and converse results for error exponents of index-based concatenated codes under a variable number of reads. Our achievability result illustrates a significant reduction in error probability compared to the fixed-reads setting (from
e^{-\Theta(M)}
to
e^{-\Theta(M\log\frac{1}{p})}
), and our converse shows result that our achievability result is tight in certain regimes where a relatively small number of reads is performed.

Perhaps the most immediate direction for future research is to establish converse bounds and/or improved achievability bounds that are suited to a higher average number of reads. As exemplified in Figure
1
, we do not provide a converse in such regimes, and our achievable exponent approaches a finite limit no matter how many reads are done. (This should not be unavoidable, since with infinitely many reads the error probability is zero, corresponding to an infinite error exponent.) Another interesting direction would be to study different models of sequencing errors, particularly random instead of adversarial.
