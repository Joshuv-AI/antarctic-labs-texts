Well-posedness of kinetic McKean-Vlasov equations
Andrea Pascucci, Alessio Rondelli

Abstract
We consider the McKean-Vlasov equation d ​ X t = b ⁡ ( t , X t , [ X t ] ) ​ d ​ t + σ ⁡ ( t , X t , [ X t ] ) ​ d ​ W t dX_{t}=b(t,X_{t},[X_{t}])dt+{\sigma}(t,X_{t},[X_{t}])dW_{t} where [ X t ] [X_{t}] is the law of X t X_{t} . We specifically consider the kinetic case , where the equation is degenerate because the dimension of the Brownian motion W W is strictly smaller than that of the solution X X , as commonly required in classical models of collisional kinetic theory. Assuming Hölder continuous coefficients and a weak Hörmander condition, we prove the well-posedness of the equation. This result advances the existing literature by filling a crucial gap: it addresses the previously unexplored case where the diffusion coefficient σ \sigma depends on the law [ X t ] [X_{t}] . Notably, our proof employs a simplified and direct argument eliminating the need for PDEs involving derivatives with respect to the measure argument. A critical ingredient is the sub-Riemannian metric structure induced by the corresponding Fokker-Planck operator.

1
Introduction

Let
Z_{t}=(X_{t},V_{t})
be a kinetic particle in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
X_{t}
and
V_{t}

denote, respectively, its position and velocity. Its evolution is typically described by Newton’s
law of motion and, in a random environment, by a McKean-Vlasov (MKV) SDE of the form

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}V_{t}=b(t,Z_{t},[Z_{t}])\mathrm{d}t+{\sigma}(t,Z_{t},[Z_{t}])\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=V_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
W
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and
[Z_{t}]
denotes the law of
Z_{t}
. Equation
(
1.1
) is commonly referred to as a
second-order
(or
inhomogeneous
) system, in
contrast with
first-order
(or
homogeneous
) systems, where only the velocity variable
is retained. In the latter setting, the position component is ignored and the problem falls within
the classical framework of non-degenerate, uniformly parabolic SDEs, for which a complete and
well-developed theory is available. The present paper is concerned with the
kinetic
case,
where the equation is degenerate, since the Brownian motion acts only on the velocity component
and therefore has dimension strictly smaller than that of the full state variable
Z
.

Whenever it exists, a solution to (
1.1
) is also called a MKV diffusion, or a
non-linear Markov process
[
19
]
. In the deterministic case
{\sigma}=0
, (
1.1
) is
related to the classical Vlasov equation
[
18
]
, one of the basic models in plasma
physics and celestial mechanics. The interest in stochastic kinetic particle systems, however,
goes well beyond their intrinsic mathematical relevance and is largely motivated by applications.
In models of collective behaviour, for instance, the stochastic Cucker-Smale system

[
3
]
plays a prominent role in the study of flocking and self-organization. In
mathematical finance, equations of the form (
1.1
) arise in the modelling of path-dependent
contingent claims, such as Asian options, and have more recently appeared in the analysis of local
stochastic volatility models; see, for example,
[
30
,
21
]
. This framework is also
motivated by recent applications in energy systems: for instance,
[
11
]
considers a
stochastic optimal control problem arising from the operation of a large ensemble of residential
storage devices coordinated by an energy aggregator. Further motivations, together with a detailed
discussion of the related literature, are given in Section
1.2
.
For a survey of MKV equations, with a focus on kinetic models, see

[
28
]
.

Despite their importance, only partial well-posedness results for the kinetic MKV equation
(
1.1
) are currently available; see
[
16
]
,
[
15
]
,

[
34
]
and
[
17
]
. In particular,
[
17
]

considers a constant diffusion coefficient and allows for a drift that may be discontinuous with
respect to the measure argument. A major common limitation of these works is that the diffusion
coefficient
\sigma
is assumed to be independent of the measure argument.

The aim of this paper is precisely to remove this restriction. To the best of our knowledge,
Theorem
1.3
below provides the first well-posedness result for kinetic MKV equations with
diffusion coefficient depending on the law, in a genuinely degenerate regime where the drift is
essential for the propagation of noise; see Assumption
1.2
. Allowing the diffusion to depend
on the distribution is important not only from a structural viewpoint, but also for applications,
since such a dependence naturally appears in several models. For instance, it is used in

[
9
]
to describe grazing collisions of particles, and related considerations also
underlie the models studied in
[
12
]
.

A further motivation comes from the work of Lacker
[
20
]
,
who established quantitative propagation-of-chaos estimates for a broad class of mean field
diffusions. As explained in Remarks 2.11 and 4.5 therein, his method also applies to kinetic
systems with constant diffusion. In this setting, our weak well-posedness result for the limiting
MKV equation can be combined with his estimates to obtain quantitative propagation
of chaos, provided the remaining assumptions of
[
20
]
hold. As pointed out in
Remark 2.12 of the same paper, however, the key entropy estimates break down when the diffusion
coefficient involves interactions.

Let us finally explain the analytical difficulty behind the problem and the strategy of the proof.
In the degenerate kinetic setting, the transition density of the solution does not enjoy the
regularity familiar from the uniformly parabolic case. In particular, one cannot in general expect
Euclidean differentiability in the position variables, not even at first order. This makes it
essential to work in a functional framework adapted to the geometry of the operator, rather than
in standard isotropic spaces. The relevant geometry is the sub-Riemannian one associated with the
kinetic Fokker-Planck operator, and a central ingredient of our analysis is the use of the
corresponding intrinsic regularity theory. This allows us to exploit the smoothing effects
generated by the transport-diffusion structure even in directions where no direct noise is
present.

This intrinsic viewpoint fits naturally with the duality method introduced in
[
33
]
, which
yields a new and direct proof of well-posedness for MKV equations, different in spirit from the
existing approaches. In the present kinetic framework, the combination of intrinsic regularity and
duality is particularly effective: it avoids the use of PDEs involving derivatives with respect to
the measure variable and, at the same time, is flexible enough to treat diffusion coefficients
with genuine measure dependence.

1.1
Assumptions and main results

Let
T>0
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
denote the space of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.
For
t\in[0,T]
, we consider the MKV system

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X^{0}_{t}=\left(B^{0}X_{t}+b(t,X_{t},[X_{t}])\right)\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma(t,X_{t},[X_{t}])\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}X^{1}_{t}=B^{1}X_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
W
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion (
1\leq d\leq N
) defined on a filtered probability
space
({\Omega},\mathcal{F},\mathsf{P},\mathcal{F}_{t})
, and
[X_{t}]
denotes the law of
X_{t}=(X^{0}_{t},X^{1}_{t})
. In
(
1.2
),
B^{0}
,
B^{1}
are constant matrices of dimension
d\times N
and
(N-d)\times N

respectively, and the coefficients are measurable functions

b:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\qquad\Sigma:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}.

(1.3)

The prototype of (
1.2
), with
N=2d
, is the kinetic particle system (
1.1
).

For a fixed flow of distributions
{\mu}=({\mu}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, we consider
the associated SDE with frozen measure flow

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X^{0}_{t}=\left(B^{0}X_{t}+b(t,X_{t},{\mu}_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma(t,X_{t},{\mu}_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}X^{1}_{t}=B^{1}X_{t}\mathrm{d}t.\end{cases}

(1.4)

Setting

b^{\mu}(t,x):=b(t,x,\mu_{t}),\qquad\Sigma^{\mu}(t,x):=\Sigma(t,x,\mu_{t}),\qquad\mathcal{C}^{\mu}:=\Sigma^{{\mu}}(\Sigma^{{\mu}})^{\ast},\qquad B:=\begin{pmatrix}B^{0}\\
B^{1}\end{pmatrix},

(1.5)

the characteristic operator of (
1.4
) is
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{t,x}+Y
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{t,x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\mu}(t,x)\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}^{\mu}(t,x)\partial_{x_{i}}\hskip 30.0pt\text{(diffusion)}

(1.6)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}+\left\langle Bx,\nabla\right\rangle=\partial_{t}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}B_{ij}x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}.\hskip 40.0pt\text{(drift)}

(1.7)

We now introduce the main structural conditions.

Assumption 1.1

(
Coercitivity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
).

The
d\times d
matrix

\mathcal{C}:=\Sigma\Sigma^{\ast}
is uniformly positive definite, that is

\langle\mathcal{C}(t,x,{\mu}){\eta},{\eta}\rangle\geq\lambda|{\eta}|^{2},\qquad(t,x,{\mu})\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\ {\eta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(1.8)

for some positive constant
\lambda
.

Note that Assumption
1.8
is a non-degeneracy condition in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, while the
system’s dimension
N
can be strictly greater than
d
. Propagation of diffusion is ensured by the following hypoellipticity
condition.

Assumption 1.2

(
Weak Hörmander’s condition).

The vector fields
\partial_{x_{1}},\cdots,\partial_{x_{d}}
and
Y
satisfy the Hörmander’s
condition

\text{rank Lie}(\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}},Y)=N+1.

In Section
2
, we recall that Assumption
1.2
induces a natural
anisotropic

distance on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
associated with problem (
1.2
). The coefficients
b
and
\Sigma
are assumed to be Hölder continuous
in the spatial variable with respect to this anisotropic distance and Lipschitz continuous
in the measure variable with respect to suitable dual metrics introduced in Section
2
.
The precise conditions are stated in Assumption
2.3
.

Our main result is the following

Theorem 1.3

(
Weak well-posedness).

Under Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
and
2.3
there exists a unique weak solution of (
1.2
)
with initial distribution
[X_{0}]=\bar{{\mu}}_{0}
. The solution is a strong Markov and Feller
process, and admits a transition density
p
satisfying the two-sided Gaussian estimate

C_{-}{\Gamma}^{\lambda_{-}}(t,x;s,y)\leq p(t,x;s,y)\leq C_{+}{\Gamma}^{\lambda_{+}}(t,x;s,y),\qquad 0\leq t<s\leq T,\ x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

(1.9)

for some positive constants
C_{\pm},\lambda_{\pm}
, where
{\Gamma}^{\lambda}
is the Gaussian kernel in
(
3.4
).

Remark 1.4
.

As a direct consequence of Theorem
1.3
, if under Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
and
2.3
,
for any fixed flow of distributions
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
the frozen SDE (
1.4
)
admits a unique strong solution (see, for instance,
[
8
]
), then the MKV equation
(
1.2
) is strongly well-posed.

1.2
Motivations and related literature

MKV equations significantly extend the scope of classical SDEs by incorporating the
effects of interactions between particles or agents subject to the same dynamics. The initial
formulations of MKV equations are attributed to McKean
[
26
]
, in the context of
nonlinear PDEs, and independently to Vlasov
[
38
]
, in the study of plasma dynamics.
The central idea is that in a multi-agent system of many interacting particles, the effect of the
other particles on any single particle can be averaged out in favor of a mean field; this process
has subsequently been termed propagation of chaos.

MKV equations have garnered significant attention across various fields of applied mathematics,
including mathematical finance, demography, statistical mechanics, and mean-field games (see,
e.g.,
[
2
]
). More specifically, MKV equations offer a natural framework for explaining
how aggregative phenomena emerge from microscopic descriptions. Notable examples include their
application in fluid dynamics, biology and population dynamics (see
[
4
]
,

[
37
]
).

A substantial body of literature addresses the well-posedness problem for
non-degenerate
MKV
equations (i.e., with
N=d
). Funaki
[
13
]
studied the martingale problem under
Lyapunov-type conditions and global Lipschitz assumptions. Subsequently, Shiga and Tanaka

[
36
]
proved that strong well-posedness holds when the diffusion is the identity matrix
and the coefficients are bounded and Lipschitz continuous with respect to the total variation
norm. More recently, Mishura and Veretennikov
[
27
]
established weak existence under
relatively minimal hypotheses and strong well-posedness under more restrictive assumptions using
Girsanov’s theorem (which requires independence of the diffusion with respect to the law of the
solution). Independently, Chaudru de Raynal and Frikha
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
, and we

[
33
]
proved well-posedness for MKV equations with Hölder coefficients. Röckner and
Zhang
[
35
]
(for a measure-independent diffusion coefficient) and Zhao
[
39
]
(for
the general case) obtained remarkably comprehensive well-posedness results, even in the presence
of a singular drift term but under the assumptions of uniform non-degeneracy (i.e., with
N=d
).
Finally, quantitative estimates for propagation of chaos are established in
[
6
]
.

As previously mentioned, in the degenerate case, weak existence and strong well-posedness have
been studied in
[
14
]
,
[
17
]
, and
[
34
]
, even for measurable
coefficients and/or singular drift. However, these results are only valid under the assumption
that
the diffusion coefficient is measure-independent
, which excludes the crucial case of
interactions mediated by a collision kernel.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the
sub-Riemannian metric structure associated the the kinetic system. In Section
3
, we
prove a crucial duality result, which expresses the push-forward operator in terms of pull-back
operators and allows us to formulate a fixed-point problem in the space of continuous flows of
marginals. Section
4
is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

2
Anisotropic Hölder spaces

As already mentioned, the regularity properties induced by the infinitesimal generator of a
kinetic SDE are drastically different from those in the uniformly non-degenerate case. Within this
framework, a vast literature has been developed by numerous authors, starting from Lanconelli and
Polidoro
[
22
]
, and Lunardi
[
25
]
; here, we recall only a few
fundamental results, such as the intrinsic Taylor formula and the Sobolev embedding theorems,
which were recently established in
[
29
]
and
[
32
]
.

According to
[
22
]
, Assumption
1.2
is equivalent to
B^{1}
having
the form

B^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}B^{1}_{1}&*&\cdots&*&*\\
0&B^{1}_{2}&\cdots&*&*\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&B^{1}_{q}&*\end{pmatrix}

(2.1)

where the
*
-blocks are arbitrary and
B^{1}_{j}
is a
(d_{j-1}\times d_{j})
-matrix of rank
d_{j}

with

d=d_{0}\geq d_{1}\geq\dots\geq d_{q}\geq 1,\qquad\sum_{i=0}^{q}d_{i}=N.

For example, in the Langevin equation (
1.1
), we simply have
B^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}
where
I_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix.

The structure of
B^{1}
in (
2.1
) induces a natural definition of anisotropic norm on

{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, along with related notions of moments and Hölder continuity.

Definition 2.1

(
Anisotropic norm).

For any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
let

|x|_{B}=\sum_{j=0}^{q}\sum_{i=\bar{d}_{j-1}+1}^{\bar{d}_{j}}|x_{i}|^{\frac{1}{2j+1}},\qquad\bar{d}_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}d_{k},\qquad{\bar{d}_{-1}=0.}

(2.2)

Definition 2.2

(
Anisotropic spaces).

Let
\alpha\in]0,1]
:

i)

\mathcal{P}^{\alpha}_{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
denotes the space of distributions
{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
such
that

[{\mu}]_{\mathcal{P}^{\alpha}_{B}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}|x|^{\alpha}_{B}{\mu}(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty.

(2.3)

ii)

for functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the seminorm and norm

[f]_{C^{\alpha}_{B}}:=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}_{B}},\qquad\|f\|_{bC^{\alpha}_{B}}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}|f(x)|+[f]_{C^{\alpha}_{B}};

(2.4)

for a function
f:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times\mathcal{D}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\mathcal{D}

is the domain of the measure variable, we also use the notation

[f]_{C^{\alpha}_{B}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[0,T]\\

In cases i) and ii) of Assumption
2.3
, we take
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
and

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{P}^{\alpha}_{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
, respectively.

We now introduce our last main assumption.

Assumption 2.3

(

.

[

]

3
A duality result

For a fixed flow of marginals
{\mu}=({\mu}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
and
an initial distribution
\bar{{\mu}}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we consider the frozen SDE
(
1.4
) with
[X_{0}]=\bar{{\mu}}_{0}
and the corresponding characteristic operator

\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{t,x}+Y
in (
1.6
)-(
1.7
). Note that the integral curve, starting from

(t,x)
, of the drift
Y
is the map
s\longrightarrow(s,e^{(s-t)B}x)
.

Since in degenerate problems there are some directions that are more natural than others to
consider and most importantly
Euclidean derivatives only exist in some special directions
,
we need to properly define the notion of solution to

\mathcal{A}^{\mu}_{t,x}u+Yu=f.

(3.1)

Definition 3.1

(
Strong Lie solution).

We say that
u:\,]0,T[\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a.e. differentiable along
Y
if there
exists
