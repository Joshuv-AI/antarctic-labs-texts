Genomic heterogeneity drives distinct infiltration patterns in glioblastoma
Authors: Dylan Scott Lykke Harwood, Sara Blaabjerg Artzi, Vilde Pedersen, Alessio Locallo, Maya Jeje Schuang Lü, David Scheie, Dorte Schou Nørøxe, Nadine Margaretha Hammouda, Ulrik Lassen, Joachim Weischenfeldt, Bjarne Winther Kristensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntertumoral heterogeneity in glioblastoma—driven by both genomic and transcriptomic variation—complicates our understanding of how different tumor cell populations contribute to disease progression. Infiltrating tumor cells, which invade surrounding brain tissue and evade surgical resection, are thought to play a central role in recurrence. To address this, we aimed to characterize the gene expression profiles and cellular states of infiltrative tumor cells in glioblastoma. We performed high-plex spatial transcriptomics using the CosMx Spatial Molecular Imager (NanoString) on tumor tissue from eight glioblastoma patients. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples were selected to capture both the tumor core and invasive margin. A targeted panel of 1,000 genes enabled spatially resolved gene expression profiling at single-cell resolution, allowing precise identification and localization of malignant and non-malignant cell states. We show that malignant cells can be distinguished from non-malignant populations by using patient-specific clustering. Based on this annotation, we identified several known malignant states—including AC-, OPC-, NPC-, and MES-like cells—as well as a recently characterized glial-progenitor (GPC)-like state. This population co-expressed genes associated with both astrocytic and oligodendrocyte progenitor lineages and was found to be more proliferative than the traditional AC-like state. The GPC-like state was most enriched in the classical glioblastoma subtype and was strongly associated with EGFR amplification or mutation. Spatial analyses investigating malignant differences between tumor and infiltrated tissue showed heterogeneous infiltration patterns across patients. In the most extreme case, the dominant GPC-like population in the tumor core gave way to increased proportions of AC-like cells in infiltrated regions. Our study highlights diverging infiltration patterns across glioblastoma tumors, with indications of a GPC-like to AC-like transition occurring in classical-subtyped tumors. This shift is associated with a decrease in cell proliferation and may have implications for clinical treatment.Keywords: Glioblastoma, Infiltration, Spatial transcriptomics

Introduction

Glioblastoma is the most lethal brain tumor, with a median survival of approximately 15 months [1, 2]. The first-line treatment involves maximal surgical resection, followed by radiotherapy with concomitant and adjuvant temozolomide chemotherapy [3]. However, surgery rarely eliminates all tumor cells; those that infiltrate the surrounding brain parenchyma remain and are the targets of unsuccessful treatment. Understanding the biology of these residual infiltrative cells is essential for developing effective strategies to prevent recurrence. These infiltrative cells exhibit diverse behaviors. Some display high proliferative potential while invading brain tissue, consistent with the “go-and-grow” model, while others reduce proliferation to facilitate migration, representing the “go-or-grow” model [4, 5]. While debated, the effects of chemotherapy are widely believed to target more proliferative cells [6] and low proliferation of infiltrating cells may be associated with treatment resistance. However, regardless of their proliferative status, these cells drive recurrence, making their characterization especially relevant.

Early efforts to classify the molecular subtypes of glioblastoma tumors using bulk gene expression profiling identified four subtypes, namely the mesenchymal, classical, neural, and proneural [7]. The neural subtype was later excluded due to its association with lower tumor purity and contaminating adjacent brain tissue [8]. The remaining glioblastoma subtypes have been linked to specific genomic alterations. EGFR amplifications are characteristic of the classical subtype, CDK4 and PDGFRA amplifications are associated with the proneural subtype, and NF1 deletions with the mesenchymal subtype. Subsequent studies using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) further revealed these subtypes to contain different compositions of four main malignant cell states in glioblastoma: (1) neural progenitor-like (NPC-like), (2) oligodendrocyte progenitor-like (OPC-like), (3) astrocyte-like (AC-like), and (4) mesenchymal-like (MES-like) [9]. More refined classification of these states led to the addition of glial progenitor-like (GPC), ciliated-like and neuronal-like (NEU) [10, 11]. We previously reported that glioblastoma tumors harboring TP53 mutations show an upregulation of Notch signaling and enrichment of NPC-like signatures in infiltrated tissue [12]. Notably, TP53 mutations are more common in proneural tumors [8] and may not be representative of all tumor subtypes.

To explore the composition and invasive properties of TP53 wild-type (TP53-wt) glioblastoma tumors, we selected eight cases with matched tumor core and infiltrated brain tissue. We performed spatially resolved single-cell profiling using a targeted panel of 990 genes, including a customized panel of 30 genes, to distinguish between malignant and non-malignant brain cells. Here, we report that malignant cells in TP53-wt glioblastoma exhibit heterogeneous infiltration patterns that are partly shaped by underlying malignant cell states and their associated genomic alterations.

Methods

Patient tissue selection

The glioblastoma periphery cohort was established as previously described [12]. We retrieved archived histology slides from newly diagnosed glioblastoma patients between January 1, 2015, and June 30, 2021, at Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen University Hospital, Copenhagen. The slides underwent evaluation with a BX51 microscope (Olympus Denmark A/S, Ballerup, Denmark) and additional review by a neuropathologist (BWK or DS). Patients selected for the CosMx experiments met the following inclusion criteria: (I) Presence of tissue from the tumor core and infiltrated brain tissue, including both grey and white matter, on the same slide; and (II) adequate tissue availability for analysis. Patients of each gender were included in the study. All adult glioblastoma patients were eligible for inclusion regardless of age.

Histology stains

As previously described [12], the chosen formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples were cut into 5 μm sections with a microtome and placed onto glass slides. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the tissue was first deparaffinized and rehydrated, then stained with hematoxylin for 4 min followed by eosin 0.1% for 5 min. Slides were subsequently dehydrated and covered with a coverslip.

CosMx RNA slide preparation

A 5 μm section from each of eight glioblastoma samples was profiled using the CosMx spatial molecular imager as described in He et al. [13]. In brief, each sample was mounted on a histological slide, and a flow cell for reagent administration was affixed to the slide. The panel consisted of the 1000-plex Universal Cell panel and a 30-gene custom panel designed to characterize malignant states. The panel was imaged with stains for histone (blue channel), 18S rRNA (yellow channel), GFAP (red channel), and DAPI (UV). Reagents were flowed across the slide by the CosMx instrument to cyclically image each target in the panel, which was decoded based on its assigned barcode sequence and spatial localization.

CosMx counts normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell annotation

Cell segmentation results from Baysor [14] were pooled across samples and used to create cell metadata and a count matrix. To ensure that cells retained for downstream analysis were of high quality, we removed cells containing fewer than 80 RNA counts. The resulting count matrix and metadata were used to create a Seurat object [15], in which we normalized the data by RNA size factors, followed by dimensionality reduction (RunPCA, RunUMAP), neighbor graph construction (FindNeighbors), and clustering (FindClusters).

NMF rank determination and clustering resolution

To determine the optimal number of factors for non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), we performed cross-validation using the RcppML package. After determining the optimal rank for our analysis to be 15, based on comparisons of the loadings of NMF eigenvectors to known cell types and states, we ran the NMF algorithm 50 times with random starts to ensure that our signatures were less affected by the random component of the algorithm. Hierarchical clustering of 750 signatures showed multiple clusters of eigenvectors were highly similar to each other, and we therefore decreased the number of clusters to 15 using Ward’s method (ward.D).

Cell neighborhood identification

The dbscan R package was utilized to identify the 30 nearest neighbors for each cell, and distances were converted into weights for each neighbor. Gene expression vectors for variably expressed genes were retained, weighted by neighbor proximity, and aggregated into a matrix. This matrix was normalized using quantile normalization and subsequently scaled for dimensional reduction and clustering. To identify tumor versus infiltrated tissue for differential expression analysis, we calculated the proportion of malignant to resident brain cells in each region and performed hierarchical clustering (k = 2) to classify tumor and infiltrated tissue.

Differential expression analysis

Differential marker expression analysis was performed using the Seurat function FindMarkers. Differential expression between tumor and infiltrated tissue was performed using a pseudo-bulk approach and the limma R package.

TCGA data preprocessing and annotation

Using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) glioma dataset [8, 16], we initially removed samples from a group characterized by high epithelial expression and a lack of correlation between inferred copy number variations (CNVs) and CNV data provided by TCGA, suggesting potential sample misclassification. We retained the remaining samples, which were annotated as isocitrate dehydrogenase wild-type (IDH-wt), for downstream analysis.

Statistics

Multiple statistical methods are described above. p-values < 0.05 were considered significant when testing for differential expression, and p values were adjusted using false-discovery rate (FDR) correction. Investigators were not blinded to the analysis. No power calculation was performed prior to data collection.

Data availability

The CosMx data generated in this study will be deposited in the Zenodo database under accession number 10.5281/zenodo.16957940. Any additional information is available from the corresponding authors upon request.

Results

Spatial profiling of glioblastoma tumors and infiltrated regions at single-molecule resolution

We performed spatial transcriptomic profiling using the CosMx Spatial Molecular Imager (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA, USA) on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue from eight primary glioblastoma tumors (Fig. 1A). Fields of view (FOVs) were selected based on pathological assessment of adjacent H&E-stained sections, with each FOV measuring 0.51 mm × 0.51 mm. A commercial panel of 960 genes was used to characterize biological pathways, immune cells, and stromal cells, supplemented with a custom 30-gene panel designed to distinguish malignant states from non-malignant resident brain cells. Each sample contained tumor regions as well as areas with malignant cell infiltration into either white or grey matter, verified by a neuropathologist (BWK). Cell segmentation and mRNA transcript assignment were performed using CellPose 2.0 [13, 17] and Baysor [14], as previously described [12].

Tumor cores and infiltrated grey- or white-matter regions were identified by neuropathological review. A 990-gene panel (960 commercial + 30 custom) enabled distinction of malignant, immune, and stromal cell types. We generated molecular neighborhoods by mapping each transcript to its nearest neighbors, enabling visualization of spatially distinct compartments. These molecular maps revealed characteristic patterns associated with key histopathological features, including tumor core regions, grey- and white-matter infiltration, microvascular proliferation, and hypoxic areas (Fig. 1B). This approach served as a visual aid for downstream analysis and highlighted the spatial heterogeneity of cell states and microenvironments within the tumor and across infiltrated regions.

Patient heterogeneity allows the separation of resident brain cells from malignant cells

Distinguishing malignant from non-malignant resident glial cells is challenging in spatial transcriptomics, as some glioblastoma cell states retain transcriptional features of their glial or progenitor origins. Astrocyte-like tumor cells share gene expression profiles with non-malignant astrocytes, while OPC- and NPC-like states overlap with oligodendrocyte lineage cells. These transcriptional similarities can blur the distinction between malignant and non-malignant populations, particularly in panel-based datasets with limited gene coverage. However, malignant cells often exhibit patient-specific clustering due to underlying genomic alterations, whereas non-malignant cells tend to cluster by cell type across patients, a feature that can be leveraged to resolve ambiguous populations.

To address this, we implemented a two-step strategy: (1) identifying gene expression programs across all cells, and (2) reanalyzing states where malignant and non-malignant populations are difficult to distinguish. We chose non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), as it enables the discovery of both known and unknown signatures [15]. To reduce sparsity and stabilize gene expression signals, we aggregated gene counts from highly similar cells within each sample to create meta-cell-like profiles for NMF analysis (Fig. 2A). After identifying the optimal rank of the matrix (n = 15), we applied cNMF to identify robust NMF signatures (Fig. 2B), followed by hierarchical clustering on the correlation matrix of all NMF vectors (Fig. 2C). This revealed signatures associated with cell types, states, and pathways (Fig. 2C-D).

Some programs reflected non-malignant populations, including endothelial cells (FLT1, KLF2, VWF), pericytes (COL4A1, FN1), fibroblasts (DCN, COL1A1), microglia (CSF2RA, CX3CR1), and macrophages (CD163, CD68), along with a myeloid inflammatory program enriched for CCL4, IL1B, and FOS. We also detected a distinct interferon-response program (MX1, IFI27, IFITM1) and two neuronal-like programs: one enriched for CHGA, CALM1, and MEG3, and another enriched for SST, NRIP3, and FGF13.

Among malignant programs, we identified signatures reflecting cell cycling (e.g., TOP2A, HMGB2, UBE2C, MKI67), OPC-like (e.g., PDGFRA, COL9A1, GPR17, SOX10), and NPC-like states (e.g., DLL3, HES6, SOX4). Notably, we identified two distinct mesenchymal-like (MES-like) programs, suggesting further heterogeneity within this cell state. The first MES-like signature (MES-like 1) included SOD2, SERPINA3, CD44, and TGFβ2, indicative of stress response and TGFβ signaling, while the second (MES-like 2) included VEGFA, LGALS3, and VIM, pointing to a vascular and hypoxic response. Finally, we identified astrocyte-like (AC-like) and glial progenitor-like (GPC-like) signatures previously described [9]. The AC-like program featured ETNPPL, AQP4, AQP1, and NTRK2, genes also expressed in non-malignant astrocytes, while the GPC-like program included HOPX, ETV4, PDGFA, FGFR3, and LGALS3, associated with progenitor-like cell types and receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling. While these programs were enriched in malignant cells, a small subset of non-malignant astrocytes and oligodendrocytes also scored within them—astrocytes within the AC-like, GPC-like, and MES-like 1 programs, and oligodendrocytes within the OPC- and NPC-like programs—likely reflecting their close transcriptional resemblance to tumor cells in these states (Fig. 2E). While the newest edition of malignant states has recently been published [10, 11], our gene panel was curated toward the previous state signatures from Neftel et al. [9]. Therefore, we only included the addition of the GPC-like state in this study, and adapted the remaining malignant programs to fit with the newest classification when possible.

To better distinguish malignant from non-malignant cells within these shared programs, we focused specifically on two groups: (1) cells scoring highest for the astrocyte-like programs, and (2) cells enriched for OPC-like or NPC-like states. This allowed us to capture variation associated with patient-specific genomic alterations. In the AC-like group (Fig. 2F–I), clusters 7, 11, and 12 had high entropy of patient composition, indicating transcriptional similarity across patients. Cluster 12 exhibited elevated expression of immune-related genes, likely reflecting contamination from neighboring immune cells due to imperfect segmentation. These cells were removed from downstream analysis. Therefore, clusters 7 and 11 were annotated as non-malignant astrocytes. In the OPC-like group (Fig. 2J-M), cluster 8 exhibited high entropy and strong expression of OPC markers, including SOX10, APOD, and PDGFRA, consistent with a shared, less altered progenitor-like phenotype. Clusters 5 and 7, in contrast, showed high expression of immune-related genes, as seen previously, and were also removed from downstream analysis. We then aggregated gene expression counts for each annotated cell state and measured transcriptional distances between them (Fig. 2N). While non-malignant OPCs were highly distinct from OPC-like malignant cells, astrocytes shared more transcriptional similarity with AC-like and MES-like 1 states. To identify genes conserved in malignant cells that hijack the phenotype of resident brain cells, we compared the expression of AC- and MES-like 1 cells to astrocytes and simultaneously compared all three to the remaining cells (Fig. 2O). This analysis revealed genes such as CHI3L1, NTRK2, AQP4, and CD44 to be highly expressed across all three populations, while astrocytes retained higher expression of known astrocytic genes such as ETNPPL. NTRK2 has been shown to promote malignant progression through interaction with neurons [18]. Conversely, AC- and MES-like 1 cells had increased expression of ID1 and TGFB2. Applying the same approach to non-malignant OPCs and OPC-like malignant cells, we found shared expression of PDGFRA, MMP16, and GPR17, while DLL3, CLOCK, and SEC61G were more highly expressed in OPC-like cells (Fig. 2P). In both comparisons, malignant cells exhibited increased expression of EGFR and SEC61G, two genes commonly amplified in GBM.

Cellular landscape across patient samples

After classifying malignant and non-malignant cells, we refined the annotation of non-malignant astrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursor cells and performed dimensionality reduction separately for malignant and non-malignant compartments (Fig. 3A–B). This further underscored the trend that malignant cells clustered by patient of origin, reflecting inter-patient genomic variability, while non-malignant cells grouped primarily by cell type across samples, as expected for cell types not shaped by tumor-specific genomic alterations.

We then tested for differential marker expression across cell states (Fig. 3C). As expected, AC-like malignant cells showed substantial transcriptional overlap with non-malignant astrocytes. However, key differences included elevated expression of BAG3 in astrocytes, a gene located on chromosome 10 and frequently lost in glioblastoma cells. Conversely, EGFR was highly expressed in malignant cells. Notably, astrocytes also shared CHI3L1 and CD44 expression with MES-like 1 malignant cells, suggesting that a subset of astrocytes may exist in a reactive state.

Cycling cells exhibited expression of markers associated with multiple lineages, including METTL7B and HOPX from the GPC-like state, as well as HES6 and SOX4 from the NPC-like state. In contrast, FGFR3—enriched in both AC-like and GPC-like populations—was expressed at low levels in cycling cells. Given prior evidence linking FGFR3 to glioma cell infiltration [19], this observation suggests that the GPC-like state may possess greater proliferative capacity than the AC-like state. To further examine which malignant states were represented among cycling cells, we assigned them to their strongest non-cycling gene program. This revealed patient-specific variation in the composition of cycling cells and showed that the NPC-like and GPC-like states contained the highest fractions of cycling cells overall (Fig. 3D-E).

To assess variation in cell type and state composition across and within patient tumors, we quantified the proportions of each cell type within the profiled tissue regions—referred to as fields of view (FOVs)—and performed hierarchical clustering based on cell type proportions per FOV, grouped by patient (Fig. 3F). We observed pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity in cellular composition, with some regions showing marked increases in either MES-like 2 cells or resident brain cell populations. Notably, the abundance of GPC-like and NPC-like malignant cells varied considerably across patients; for instance, some tumors, such as P1 and P8, largely lacked one of these populations, pointing to distinct cellular programs across glioblastoma subtypes. These differences appeared to reflect spatial variation across tumor regions. To further explore these patterns, we computed pairwise correlations between cell type proportions across all FOVs (Fig. 3G). Resident brain cell types consistently showed strong correlations with one another, further supporting our ability to distinguish them from their malignant counterparts. NPC-like and OPC-like cells showed the strongest correlations with cycling cell proportions, as previously reported [9]. Endothelial clusters 1 and 2 were correlated with either resident brain cells or tumor cells and pericytes, respectively. IGF2, a paralog of IGF1 which has recently been found to increase GBM proliferation [20] was more highly expressed in endothelial cluster 1, suggesting that IGF2 is increased in the normal vasculature of the brain but is decreased within the tumor microenvironment.

Brain infiltration patterns vary across patient samples

To identify genes and cellular programs associated with malignant infiltration across tumors, we first aimed to (1) define regions of infiltration and (2) compare gene expression profiles between malignant cells located in infiltrated tissue versus the tumor core. To distinguish these regions, we clustered FOVs by the composition of the microenvironment to identify whether tumor cells were primarily surrounded by tumor-associated cell types (e.g., macrophages), grey matter (e.g., neurons and astrocytes), or white matter (e.g., oligodendrocytes and astrocytes) (Fig. 4A-B). Each FOV, along with all cells located within it, was annotated according to its location and compared with the pathological assessment of the adjacent tissue section, thereby validating the approach (Fig. 4C).

To identify gene programs overexpressed in infiltrating malignant cells in grey and white matter, we performed differential expression analysis between malignant cells located in infiltrated tissue and those within the tumor (Fig. 4D-G). However, we removed cells of the hypoxic state, as this would likely overrepresent all other states in the infiltrated tissue due to the spatial localization of the hypoxic signature within the tumor. In malignant cells infiltrating the grey matter, this identified multiple genes previously found to be associated with malignant infiltration (ATP1A2, FGFR3) [19]. We next performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using our NMF-derived signatures and found that AC-, NPC-, and OPC-like programs were enriched in infiltrated tissue, whereas MES-like, hypoxic, and cycling states were significantly enriched in tumor core regions (Fig. 4E). Notably, the GPC-like state was more enriched in the grey matter tissue but did not reach statistical significance. We repeated this analysis on white matter vs. tumor core and found that no genes were differentially expressed (Fig. 4F), although similar patterns were found in the GSEA analysis. Importantly, the GPC-like state was no longer enriched in the infiltrated white matter as compared to grey matter, and the NPC-like state was no longer significantly enriched (Fig. 4G). To further investigate the abundance of cell states found across areas, we calculated the fractions of each state in each patient sample with over 100 cells in a given area, and visualized the proportion of cells in each area. This showed that the AC-like and GPC-like state had the highest proportions across samples, with the AC-like state increasing its proportion almost consistently in the infiltrated tissue compared with the tumor. However, patients displayed heterogeneous patterns, and no comparison was significant apart from a tumor-white matter increase in AC-like cells.

To assess the local cellular environment surrounding each malignant state, we identified the ten nearest neighbors for each malignant cell and computed the odds ratio of each neighboring cell type (Fig. 4I). This analysis confirmed expected spatial associations: malignant states in infiltrated tissue were more frequently surrounded by resident brain cells, while the MES-like 2 state, characterized by hypoxic expression, showed the largest spatial enrichment with same-annotation neighbors. Malignant cells were also significantly enriched for neighboring cells of the same state, suggesting spatial clustering by cell identity and aligning with previous findings [21]. However, this was most prominent in the tumor core, whereas these results were less evident in the infiltrated tissue. Interestingly, T cells were found to be in closer proximity to malignant cells in the white matter, but not in the tumor or grey matter, suggesting an increased interaction between T cells and malignant cells in the infiltrated white matter.

These results display heterogeneous patterns of infiltration and likely reflect a combination of tumor-intrinsic biology and microenvironmental differences between the tumor, white matter, and grey matter. However, an increase in the AC-like state was observed in multiple tumors in both white and grey-matter, indicating that the AC-like state is the most abundant cell state in the area remaining after surgery.

GPC-like states are enriched in the tumor core of classical-subtyped tumors

To investigate how malignant cellular states vary across a broader cohort, we analyzed transcriptomic data from 528 glioma tumors in the TCGA dataset [16]. After excluding non-tumor controls and samples with unknown IDH status, we scored each tumor for expression of our NMF-derived cell state signatures and performed linear discriminant analysis (LDA) using established tumor subtypes (Fig. 5A–C). LDA was used to identify which cell state programs best separate tumors based on their molecular subtype and showed the GPC-like signature to be the signature contributing most to the separation of classical tumors. Further inspection of signature expression across tumors showed stark differences between proneural and classical subtypes, with the main differences associated with the GPC- and OPC-like states (Fig. 5D). Conversely, MES-like state signatures showed overlapping expression patterns with both subtypes.

In contrast to our earlier results, which showed that TP53-mutated tumors often exhibit NPC-like infiltration, our current spatial analysis showed that no consistent pattern was found across tumors, especially in those enriched for the GPC-like state. To further explore the relationship between classical tumors and the GPC-like state, we tested whether the abundance of the GPC-like state correlated with key genomic alterations (Fig. 5E–G). Wild-type TP53, EGFR missense mutations, and EGFR amplification were all significantly associated with increased expression of the GPC-like signature, with EGFR-amplified tumors exhibiting the strongest enrichment.

Given prior reports suggesting that the neural subtype may reflect neuronal contamination rather than a distinct tumor lineage, the transcriptional programs from these tumors are more likely a mix of tumor and infiltrated tissue. Therefore, we reclassified neural-subtyped tumors into core GBM subtypes using a k-nearest neighbors algorithm, assigning compound labels such as “Classical_neural” and “Mesenchymal_neural” (Fig. 5H). After rescoring tumors, we compared the selected signatures between each tumor subtype and its neural component (Fig. 5I). We found that the GPC-like program was significantly reduced in Classical_neural tumors compared to canonical classical tumors, while AC-like expression remained highest in the Classical_neural group, though not significantly higher than in classical tumors. Interestingly, the MES-like 1 signature did not differ between tumor and neural components of each subtype, although the MES-like 2 signature decreased in the neural component of classical and mesenchymal tumors. Increases in the abundance of neurons were significant for classical and mesenchymal neural components, but not for the proneural subtype.

Together, these findings suggest that the GPC-like transcriptional program is most strongly associated with classical-subtyped glioblastomas, particularly those harboring EGFR amplification. These results indicate that genomic alterations such as EGFR amplification may influence the cellular trajectory of malignant cells toward a GPC-like expression profile. While bulk-level analysis across tumor subtypes and their neural counterparts showed a significant decrease in GPC-like abundance in reclassified neural tumors, no corresponding change was observed for the proliferative or AC-like programs. Notably, AC-like gene expression remained highest in the Classical_neural group.

Discussion

Malignant cells at the tumor margin often escape surgical resection, and understanding how they infiltrate surrounding brain tissue is key to addressing glioblastoma recurrence. Characterizing the cellular and transcriptional states involved in this infiltration is critical for identifying the malignant populations that persist beyond the resection cavity. In this study, we performed single-cell spatial transcriptomics to profile eight TP53-wildtype primary glioblastomas and found marked variation in infiltration patterns across patients and between white- and grey-matter regions.

We first demonstrated that patient-specific clusters of cells displayed malignant expression patterns, whereas differentiated brain cells clustered independently of the sample of origin. This likely reflects the genomic instability and tumor-specific expression profiles that make malignant cells from the same patient more similar to each other than to cells from other patients, an effect readily captured by dimensionality reduction techniques. In samples with variable tumor purity due to the presence of resident brain cells, this separation is essential for minimizing misclassification between transcriptionally similar populations.

Using this annotation, we identified several previously described malignant states - including AC-, OPC-, NPC-, and MES-like cells - and also further defined associations with the newly characterized glial progenitor cell-like (GPC-like) state [10, 11]. This population co-expressed genes associated with both astrocytic and oligodendrocyte progenitor lineages and was enriched among cycling cells, indicating that it is intrinsically more proliferative than the more infiltrative AC-like state.

Notably, we found that the GPC-like state was most enriched in classical glioblastoma tumors and strongly associated with EGFR amplification, EGFR mutation, and TP53-wild-type status. Spatial analyses showed that in some GPC-like-enriched tumors, these cells were concentrated in the tumor core, whereas the infiltrating edge was predominantly composed of AC-like cells. In line with these findings, the GPC-like state was reduced in neural-subtyped tumors that otherwise resembled classical-subtyped tumors. In contrast, the AC-like state signature was highest in the neural component of classical tumors, although this was not statistically significant. Differences in tumor purity may partly explain these subtype-specific patterns, highlighting a key limitation of bulk transcriptomic analyses. While single-cell resolution provides a more accurate view of infiltrating cell states, larger cohorts are needed to capture the full spectrum of infiltration patterns. Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that glioblastoma does not follow a uniform infiltration program but instead exhibits multiple, possibly subtype-dependent, modes of invasion. Future studies should aim to (1) identify infiltration patterns in grey and white matter and (2) determine how genomic alterations shape the transcriptional states of infiltrating tumor cells.

In our previous study of TP53-mutated glioblastomas [12], we found that proneural- and AC-related gene modules were upregulated in infiltrating tumor regions, while mesenchymal-like (MES-like) modules were more prominent in the tumor cores. Because TP53 mutation allowed for segmentation and ensured the identification of malignant cells, the study was inherently biased toward proneural tumors, which typically exhibit NPC-, OPC-, and AC-like infiltration patterns. Multiple studies have demonstrated that TP53 mutations and EGFR amplifications are mutually exclusive [16, 19, 20, 22], and a recent paper described a noncanonical regulatory axis in which EGFR signaling reduces the DNA-binding activity of wild-type p53 [23]. These findings provide a potential explanation for the differing infiltration profiles observed between our current study, which focuses on EGFR-amplified tumors, and our previous TP53-mutated cohort. Together, the evidence suggests that different genomic alterations may drive distinct infiltration patterns in glioblastoma.

Our work builds upon a strong foundation of single-cell and spatial studies that have modeled the cellular composition and spatial organization of glioblastoma tumors [7, 9, 21, 24, 25], including a recent proposal of a five-layered structure extending from the hypoxic core to the infiltrated brain tissue [21]. However, our findings suggest that this spatial architecture may differ by subtype, and that separate models may be needed for proneural and classical tumors. Using single-cell spatial transcriptomics, we show that classical tumors can exhibit a GPC- to AC-like transition, consistent with a “go-or-grow” phenotype in which cells alternate between proliferation and infiltration. TP53-mutated tumors, enriched for NPC- and OPC-like cells, differed less between the non-hypoxic tumor core and infiltration and displayed similar levels of proliferation, thereby displaying a “go-and-grow” phenotype.

These differences may have clinical relevance, particularly for the treatment of EGFR-amplified glioblastomas. In classical tumors, the AC-like cells dominating the infiltrating edge appear less proliferative, which could render them less sensitive to standard treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy that primarily target rapidly dividing cells. It is possible that these cells persist in a quiescent state during treatment and subsequently revert to the more proliferative GPC-like phenotype once therapy ends. While current standard-of-care does not account for the genomic status of the tumor, our findings suggest that stratifying patients by TP53 or EGFR status could inform treatment decisions, as infiltrating cells from these tumors differ in both their transcriptional profiles and proliferative capacity. Further research is needed to better define these subtype-specific infiltration patterns and understand how they influence therapeutic response.

Limitations

Despite these insights, our study has several limitations. The gold standard for identifying malignant cells remains the inference of large-scale chromosomal alterations (e.g., using InferCNV), which is not possible with targeted gene panels. While patient-specific clustering and expression patterns provide a reasonable proxy, as shown by us and others [10–12], future studies would benefit from expanded gene panels or full-transcriptome platforms to improve cell classification.

In addition, the resolution of spatial transcriptomic data can be affected by imperfect cell segmentation, particularly in areas where astrocytes and malignant cells are intermingled. This may contribute to inflated AC-like expression in certain regions. However, the concordance between our spatial data and single-cell RNA-seq – which does not rely on segmentation – suggests that this is not the sole explanation.

Finally, even after restricting our cohort to TP53-wildtype tumors, we observed significant heterogeneity in infiltration patterns across patients. While the GPC-to-AC shift was common among classical tumors, the relative abundance and spatial distribution of malignant states varied, likely reflecting differences in lineage potential, tumor microenvironment, and regional interactions with resident brain cells. Therefore, increasing the number of tumors and sampled regions will be critical for identifying the heterogeneous infiltration programs of glioblastoma subtypes.

Conclusion

This study provides new insight into the cellular dynamics driving glioblastoma infiltration, with a particular focus on the classical subtype. Using single-cell spatial transcriptomics, we further characterized the functional properties of a recently described GPC-like state that co-expresses astrocytic and oligodendrocyte progenitor markers and is enriched among proliferating malignant cells in the tumor core. This state was highly associated with EGFR amplification and the classical subtype. Spatial analyses revealed a consistent transition from GPC-like to AC-like phenotypes as malignant cells infiltrate the surrounding brain tissue, defining a potential GPC-to-AC continuum that characterizes brain infiltration in classical glioblastoma.

Importantly, our findings indicate that the mechanisms of infiltration differ by genomic subtype, with classical tumors displaying a distinct infiltration program compared to proneural, TP53-mutant tumors. The reduced proliferation observed in infiltrating AC-like cells suggests that these cells may be less susceptible to therapies targeting dividing cells, potentially enabling recurrence once treatment ends. These results emphasize the importance of incorporating genomic context – such as EGFR amplification or TP53 mutation – into models of glioblastoma infiltration and recurrence. Future treatment strategies may benefit from targeting subtype-specific infiltration programs to more effectively eliminate residual malignant cells and prevent recurrence.
