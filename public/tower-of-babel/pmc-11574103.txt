Bundle structures inside the deep cervical lymphatic vessels of mice
Authors: Joonyoung Shin, Sungchul Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The lymphatic system plays a crucial role in immune function and the removal of cellular waste. Recent studies have highlighted the presence of primo vessels (PVs) inside lymphatic vessels, distinct from conventional lymphatic tissues, yet their structural and functional characteristics remain poorly understood, particularly PVs inside deep cervical lymphatic vessels (dcLVs) connected to the meningeal lymphatics. We used a combination of Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), Alcian Blue (AB) and H&E staining techniques to identify and characterize PVs inside the dcLVs of mice. PVs were identified inside the dcLVs, revealing distinct bundle structures composed of 3 to 5 primo subvessels (sub-PV), with diameters of 5.4 ± 2.4 μm. AFM analyses confirmed the presence of nm-sized pores on the sub-PVs, which may facilitate the absorption and retention of nanoparticles. Rod-shaped nuclei were also observed, further distinguishing PVs from other vascular structures. Our study provides new insights into the structural characteristics of PVs inside dcLVs, suggesting their potential role in targeted drug delivery. However, further research is required to explore the functional implications of these structures, especially their therapeutic applications and roles in various lymphatic regions, including the meningeal lymphatics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80155-1.

Introduction

The lymphatic system, an integral part of the immune framework, consists of an extensive network of lymph vessels, nodes, and associated organs. It plays a pivotal role in transporting lymph fluid throughout the body, facilitating the removal of toxins, cellular debris, and waste. Additionally, this system is instrumental in recognizing and combating infections, thus serving as a fundamental defense mechanism against various diseases by managing fluid levels and coordinating immune responses1,2. The discovery of meningeal lymphatics, initially identified in earlier studies but prominently documented in 2015, has significantly advanced our understanding of their vital role in regulating immune functions and modulating inflammation within the central nervous system (CNS)3–10. Notably, the deep cervical lymphatic nodes and vessels are directly connected with the meningeal lymphatics, functioning as primary drainage pathways for macromolecules, cellular waste, toxic substances, and immune cells. Moreover, deep cervical lymphatic vessels (dcLVs), which are comparatively less susceptible to the aging process than meningeal lymphatics, act as efficient conduits for the drainage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), enhancing neuroprotective mechanisms11. Despite these insights, the intricate structure and specific characteristics of the deep cervical lymphatics remain poorly understood. Furthermore, the exact interplay between meningeal lymphatics and the development of various neuropathological conditions has yet to be fully elucidated, underscoring the need for further research in this area.

In addition to the well-established components of the lymphatic system, recent studies have brought attention to an intriguing feature inside lymphatic vessels (LVs) known as primo vessels (PVs). These microscopic structures, distinct from the conventional lymphatic tissues, have been identified in various animal models, including rabbits12, rats13, and mice14. PVs are primarily found running inside LVs12–14 and blood vessels15, as well as within other organ surfaces16. Their morphology is characterized by a bundle structures, rod-shaped nuclei, thread-like appearance, often transparent or semi-transparent, making them difficult to detect without specific staining techniques17.

This study extended the existing knowledge on PVs by pioneering a morphological analysis of these structures inside the dcLVs, which are instrumental in meningeal lymphatics. Through advanced imaging techniques and histological analysis, we have documented the unique features and configurations of these PVs, which may offer new insights into their potential roles in drug delivery pathways.

Results

PVs inside dcLVs

Approximately 12 min after the injection of 0.1% Alcian Blue (AB) solution into the left deep cervical lymph node (dcLN), thin stained PVs were observed, appearing thread-like and having bundled structures (Fig. 1). The samples containing the dcLN were carefully collected, and to facilitate the observation of PVs inside the dcLVs, they were transferred to a glass slide. The total length of the sample was measured as 1.9 mm (Fig. 1b). The PVs near the dcLN were predominantly located in the central part of the dcLVs (Fig. 1c). In the central portion of the sample, the primo node (PN), which is one of the components of the primo vascular system (PVS), was observed (Fig. 1d). The PVs at the ends of the sample were observed laterally inside the dcLVs, crossing over to the opposite side. At the very ends of the sample, the PVs were observed curled inside the dcLVs due to the collection process (Fig. 1e). The diameters of each structure, especially the morphological analysis of the primo subvessels (sub-PVs) forming the bundle structure of PVs were expressed as the average ± S.D (Table 1). The diameters (µm) were as follows: dcLVs, 122.1 ± 25.6; PVs, 24.3 ± 6.5; sub-PVs, 5.4 ± 2.4; and the number of sub-PVs was 3.7 ± 0.8. Correlation analyses were performed to assess the relationships between the diameters of the dcLVs, PVs, and sub-PVs (Fig. 2). While there was no significant correlation between the diameters of dcLVs and PVs (r2 = 0.2052, p = 0.374, Fig. 2a), significant correlations were found between the diameters of dcLVs and sub-PVs (r2 = 0.6146, p = 0.0369, Fig. 2b), and between PVs and sub-PVs (r2 = 0.6100, p = 0.0381, Fig. 2c).

Bundle structure of PVs revealed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

In the Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis, the bundle structure of PVs was clearly observed inside the dcLVs (Fig. 3). Small, densely packed honeycomb structures were noted on the surface of the dcLN (Fig. 3a). The sub-PVs, which constitute the bundle structure of PVs running along the inside dcLVs, were composed of a total of four in this sample. The uppermost sub-PVs bifurcated into two branches, while the middle sub-PVs had the thinnest diameter. Additionally, the lower sub-PVs constituted the largest portion of the PVs, and a protruding structure was observed (Fig. 3b).

Rod-shaped nuclei of PVs inside dcLVs

Although the dcLVs were partially adhered, PVs were separated from the dcLVs to enable clear observation of the nuclei (Fig. 4). In the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained samples, rod-shaped nuclei inside the PVs were observed, which displayed a distinct difference compared to the round-shaped nuclei of the adjacent dcLVs (Fig. 4a). Similarly, in the 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) stained samples, clear rod-shaped nuclei were identified, and their arrangement corresponded to a linear pattern within certain areas, aligning with the sub-PVs (Fig. 4b).

Pores on PVs by atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis

In the 15 × 15 μm region in the PVs, Sub-PVs and their interstitial spaces were observed (Fig. 5). A total of three Sub-PVs were identified in this area, with an average diameter of 3.2 ± 0.9 μm, and the distance between the interstitial spaces was 1.7 ± 0.4 μm. The histogram analysis revealed that the height of data points on the Sub-PVs surface was 129.17 nm, and the number of data points with the same height value was 1951.615 pxl/div (Fig. 5b). Additionally, fine elliptical pores were observed on the outer walls of each Sub-PV, with long axis diameters of 520.5 ± 51.9 nm, short axis diameters of 412 ± 210 nm, and circumferences of 2.8 ± 1.3 μm (Fig. 5c). By drawing a red slanted line in Fig. 5b, the maximum heights of the Sub-PV regions were determined to be 246.1, 541.9, and 153.4 nm from left to right (Fig. 5d). The 2D Power Spectral Density (PSD) analysis showed a peak at a spatial frequency of 0.247 cycles/µm with a PSD value of 0.108 μm2. In the X-axis PSD, a peak was observed at 0.240 cycles/µm with a value of 0.12 μm2, while the Y-axis PSD exhibited a peak at 0.247 cycles/µm with a value of 0.119 μm³. These findings indicate the presence of dominant structural patterns with periodicities of approximately 4.05 to 4.17 μm in the PVs. Therefore, these specific patterns likely represent the distribution regions of the Sub-PVs (Fig. 5e).

Discussion

We used various approaches to confirm the presence of PVs inside dcLVs, as this had not been explored before. The discovery of PVs inside LVs has previously been primarily studied in rabbits12,18–21, rats13,22–25, and mice14,26,27. These studies have focused on relatively large body regions, such as near the inferior vena cava12,14,18–20,23−27, inguinal13,21,26, axillary13,26, and thoracic ducts22,24. The diameter of PVs inside LVs varies slightly between species and locations, typically ranging from 20 to 50 μm. Several staining techniques have been employed, including Phalloidin, DiI, Hematoxylin, EMP-3, and Von Willebrand factor (vWF)22. However, the simplest and most reliable method is AB staining. AB selectively binds to hyaluronic acid, which is abundant in the extracellular matrix of PVs25, enhancing their visibility by highlighting the mucopolysaccharides content. Other dyes, such as Trypan blue and Toluidine blue, have shown limited effectiveness for staining PVs inside lymphatic vessels, as Typan blue is quickly cleared and Toluidine blue primarily stains surrounding tissues26. For this reason, AB was selected for consistent PV visualization inside the dcLVs. We focused on PVs inside LVs in the neck, rather than in the body, to explore their role in the deep cervical lymphatics. The PVs inside dcLVs were stained with AB and had a diameter of 24.3 ± 6.5 μm, which falls on the smaller end of the previously reported size range for PVs. This smaller size is likely due to the narrower space in the neck region compared to the body. In this study, we confirmed the presence of PVs on the caudal side of the dcLN, where lymphatic drainage flows downward toward the body, rather than toward the brain. This region is the final pathway for lymphatic fluid entering the venous circulation28, suggesting that PVs may play a critical role in regulating lymph flow or selectively transporting substances. This discovery raises the possibility that PVs serve as a crucial functional link between the meningeal lymphatics and deep cervical lymphatics. Future research should focus on elucidating the specific mechanisms by which PVs influence these processes.

PVs, along with PNs, are the key components that constitute the PVS. Over the past 25 years, numerous studies have confirmed various aspects of the structure and function of the PVS. PVS tissue has been identified on the surfaces of abdominal organs, inside blood vessels and LVs, in the central nervous system, and in most internal organs29. Notably, a common feature observed across different PVS tissues is the bundle structure, which is one of the most reliable characteristics17. The gross morphology of a typical PVs is described as a single vessel covered by an outer membrane, containing 1 to 20 sub-PVs ranging from 5 to 15 μm in diameter29–31. In our study, we found that the PVs were composed of 3 to 5 sub-PV bundles with a diameter of 5.4 ± 2.4 μm, consistent with previous findings. Additionally, we observed the rod-shaped nuclei, another distinct feature of PVS tissue. This finding indicates that PVs are distinct from blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and other tissues29–31. The identification of these bundle structures and rod-shaped nuclei inside the dcLVs suggests that the consistent morphological characteristics of PVs extend to this region, reinforcing the idea that PVs may play a universal role in various anatomical sites. However, further experiments are necessary to fully understand the functional implications of these findings and to determine whether similar structures exist in other lymphatic regions, such as the meningeal lymphatics.

PVS is composed of various types of cells, including mast cells, histiocytes, and immune cells such as granulocytes (neutrophils and eosinophils) and lymphocytes25,32. In addition, PVS contains various kinds of stem cells, including primo microcell, putative hematopoietic stem cells, and cancer stem cells in cancer patients25,27,33–39. PVS has also been proposed to be a stem cell niche containing extracellular matrix, mesenchymal, hematopoietic, endothelial stem cells, and megakaryocyte lineages40–42. In particular, PVs contain basophilic granules, and nucleus-like bodies32,33. The basophilic granules are DNA granules, considered to be either extracellular DNA microvesicles (eDNA)43 or very small cells known as primo microcells36,44,45. Primo microcells are 1 to 2 μm in size and express pluripotent stem cell markers (Oct4, Nanog, Sox2, Stella, Rex1, Klf4)46,47. They migrate through the PVS, accumulate in damaged tissues or organs, and contribute to their regeneration, a function well-known in resident stem cells27,34,46.

The outer wall of PVs contains pores ranging from 1 to 5 μm48–50. The sub-PVs have a double membrane structure, with the inner membrane composed of endothelial cells and the outer membrane made up of epithelial cells. Small pores or openings, ranging from 0.2 to 3 μm, were observed between these cells48,51–53. According to Bong-Han Kim, the interstitial space of sub-PVs has a fibrous structure and is filled with amorphous substances54. Therefore, if particles of an appropriate size are applied to the PVs from outside, they are expected to get into the PVs via the pores and be retained in the interstitial space. Through AFM analysis, we confirmed the presence of nm-sized pores and interstitial spaces in sub-PVs, consistent with previous reports48,51–53. This finding suggests that when nanoparticles smaller than 1 μm are introduced into the lymphatic system, PVs, unlike lymphatic vessels, are more likely to absorb and retain these nanoparticles through their outer wall pores. Once inside, these nanoparticles could be retained in the sub-PVs pores and interstitial spaces, where they would interact with various stem cells and immune cells within the PVs. These interactions activate primo microcells, which can then travel through PVs and accumulate in damaged tissues or organs, suggesting that PVs may be a promising route for targeted drug delivery. Interestingly, a recent study identified PVs in the meningeal vascular network, suggesting a potential role in CSF transport. While the detailed morphology and function of PVs were not the primary focus of the study, their presence in this region indicates a possible involvement in CSF regulation55. Additionally, several studies are demonstrating retrograde lymphatic flow from the dcLNs to the brain, indicating that substances can move against typical lymphatic flow patterns56–58. The absence of intraluminal valves in PVs, unlike lymphatic vessels, suggests that PVs may facilitate this retrograde movement. This characteristic could enable PVs to contribute to the transport of substances, including nanoparticles, between the periphery and the brain. These possibilities hold important implications for fluid dynamics, drug delivery, and neuroimmune interactions. However, further research is needed to confirm the functional role of PVs and explore their potential therapeutic applications.

This study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. Due to the natural direction of lymph flow, our focus was on the body-proximal lymphatic vessels, limiting observations to regions further from the brain. Future research should explore alternative techniques, such as fluid pressure control and intracerebroventricular or subcutaneous injections around the dcLN, to better visualize PVs in the brain-proximal dcLVs. Second, this study was limited to the left side of the deep cervical lymphatics and included only female mice. This choice was based on the higher incidence of Alzheimer’s disease in females and the dominance of left-sided lymphatic drainage. While this approach provided targeted insights, future studies should include male mice and examine both sides to enhance generalizability. Investigating sex-specific and side-specific differences in PV morphology and function could further clarify their role in neurodegenerative diseases and drug delivery. Additionally, while structural characteristics were identified, the functional implications of PVs, particularly their role in drug delivery and immune interactions, were not directly assessed, highlighting a gap that future studies should address. Lastly, the lack of comparative analysis across different lymphatic regions, such as the meningeal lymphatics, limits the generalizability of the findings, underscoring the need for further comparative studies. While this study focused on identifying PVs inside the dcLVs of mice, future studies are planned to explore whether similar structures exist in humans. Investigations using human cadaver specimens will help determine if PVs are present in human lymphatic systems and whether they play comparable functional roles.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we identified and characterized PVs inside the dcLVs of mice. Through various imaging techniques, including SEM and AFM, we observed distinctive morphological features of PVs, such as bundle structures, rod-shaped nuclei, and nm-sized pores. These findings suggest that PVs may play a significant role in the lymphatic system, particularly in drug delivery and tissue regeneration. While our study provides new insights into the structure of PVs in the dcLVs, further research is needed to explore the functional significance of these structures, especially their potential therapeutic applications and roles in different lymphatic regions, including the meningeal lymphatics.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (7 weeks old, female, n = 7) were purchased from Samtaco BIO (Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). Mice were group-housed with 3 to 4 animals per cage and had free access to food and water at room temperature, with a constant 12:12 h light–dark cycle. All experimental methods were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Wonkwang University Animal Experiment Ethics Committee (WKU23-48). Anesthesia was induced via intramuscular injection into the hindlimb using an anesthetic cocktail containing Zoletil (20 mg/kg, Virbac, France) and xylazine (5 mg/kg, Bayer, Germany). Following the completion of sampling, the mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation.

Sampling

All observation procedures for sampling were performed using a stereomicroscope (SZX16, Olympus, Japan). Following the confirmation of deep anesthesia, the legs of mice were secured using tape to stabilize positioning during surgery. The hair on the neck was meticulously shaved using a razor blade to ensure a clean working area. Subsequently, a skin incision was made along the midline of the neck. The left submandibular gland was gently retracted laterally to expose the underlying structures. The sternocleidomastoid muscle was then incised to reveal the dcLNs and the dcLVs.

The 0.1% AB solution was prepared by dissolving AB 8GX powder (A5268, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco, USA) and subsequently filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane filter (Sartorius, Germany) using a syringe (3 ml, Koreavaccine, Republic of Korea). The injection syringe (1 ml, Koreavaccine, Republic of Korea) was modified by replacing its standard needle with a 34G nanoneedle (AND0201-04, Japan Bio Products, Republic of Korea) to facilitate precise injections. The AB solution was preheated to 37 °C in a water bath, and 10 to 15 µl was slowly injected into the dcLNs to ensure stable delivery. Approximately 10 to 12 min after injection, blue-stained PVs inside the dcLVs were identified. The dcLVs containing the PVs were then carefully harvested and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) at 4 °C for 24 h. The precise morphological analysis of all samples was conducted using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Histological analysis and Immunofluorescence

To examine the structure of the PVs inside the dcLVs, the paraffin-embedded samples were cross-sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm using a microtome (RM2135, Leica, Germany). H&E staining was performed following previously reported protocols16. The stained samples were investigated using a phase contrast microscope (BX51, Olympus, Japan).

For nuclear staining in the PVs inside the dcLVs, we used the DAPI reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following previously reported methods59. The samples were stained with DAPI for one hour, ensuring thorough coverage. Following the staining period, the samples were washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye and reduce background fluorescence. The samples were examined under a fluorescent microscope (BX51, Olympus, Japan).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM, S-4800, Hitachi, Japan) analysis was carried out at the Biomedical Materials Analysis & Imaging Support Center of Wonkwang University. The fixed samples were further processed as described in a previously established protocol60. For the imaging conditions used in Fig. 3b1, the SEM was configured with the following settings: Data Size of 1280 × 960 pixels, Pixel Size of 39.6875, Accelerating Voltage of 10,000 volts, Magnification set to 2500×, Working Distance maintained at 8900 μm, and an Emission Current of 9400 nA.

Atomic force microscope (AFM)

Atomic Force Microscope (AFM, XE-100, Park Systems, Republic of Korea) analysis was also conducted at the Biomedical Materials Analysis & Imaging Support Center of Wonkwang University. Fixed samples were initially rinsed three times with distilled water to remove any residual fixatives. The samples were then dehydrated using a series of ethanol and carefully placed on a cover glass (18 × 18 mm). After allowing the samples to air-dry, they were secured at both ends to the cover glass using transparent-colored manicure to ensure stability during analysis. The prepared cover glass with the attached samples was then mounted on an AFM specimen disk for analysis. The analysis was performed using a non-contact mode (NC-AFM) with a high XY and Z range mode, utilizing a nchr cantilever. The topography scans were conducted on a 15 × 15 μm area at a scan rate of 0.5 Hz. The scan was characterized by an X scan offset of − 4.04 μm and a Y scan offset of − 8.5 μm, with no rotation or tilt applied to the head, ensuring a straightforward and stable measurement environment.
