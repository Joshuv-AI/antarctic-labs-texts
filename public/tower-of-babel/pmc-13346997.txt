Interpretable machine learning framework for urban flood susceptibility assessment: a multi-model comparison with spatial heterogeneity analysis in Yancheng
Authors: Xuan Zhang, Dongdong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder the combined effects of global climate change and rapid urbanization, low-lying coastal plain cities face increasingly severe flood threats. This study developed an urban flood susceptibility assessment framework integrating multi-model comparison, SHAP interpretability analysis, and spatial heterogeneity evaluation, using Yancheng City as a case study. Based on 486 historical flood points and 10 conditioning factors, the predictive performance of Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine (SVM) was compared. Results showed XGBoost performed best (AUC = 0.938, accuracy = 0.891), significantly outperforming RF (AUC = 0.912) and SVM (AUC = 0.876). SHAP analysis identified topographic wetness index (TWI), elevation, and impervious surface ratio as key driving factors, with a cumulative contribution of 47.6%. Spatial heterogeneity analysis revealed distinct flood mechanisms across geomorphic units: urban built-up areas were dominated by impervious surfaces (31.5% high-risk), Lixiahe lowland by TWI (28.6%), coastal tidal flats by elevation (23.1%), while the Yellow River paleo-channel showed the lowest risk (11.2%). Susceptibility mapping indicated 19.2% of the study area at high to very high levels, with Tinghu District reaching 37.0%. The proposed integrated framework provides scientific support for differentiated flood control strategies in coastal plain cities.Keywords: Flood susceptibility, Machine learning, SHAP interpretability, Spatial heterogeneity, Yancheng CitySubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography, Natural hazards

Introduction

In the context of global climate change, the frequency and intensity of extreme precipitation events are continuously increasing, and urban flooding has become a major challenge threatening the sustainable development of cities worldwide1–3. Floods account for approximately 40% of global natural disaster losses, affecting over 2 billion people4. Rapid urbanization has significantly expanded impervious surfaces while shrinking natural water retention spaces, further exacerbating urban flood risk5,6. In China, approximately 70% of cities have experienced flooding, with average annual direct economic losses exceeding 100 billion yuan7. Coastal low-lying plain cities, due to their low elevation, dense river networks, and significant influence from ocean tides, face a complex flood risk combining both inland and coastal flooding, making disaster prevention and mitigation even more challenging8,9.

Yancheng City, located in the central coastal area of Jiangsu Province, is a typical coastal low-lying plain city with an average elevation of only 2–4 m. The city features diverse landforms including the Lixiahe lowland, the Yellow River paleo-channel, and coastal tidal flats10. With annual precipitation of 1000–1100 mm concentrated during June-September, combined with rapid urbanization and expanding impervious surfaces, urban flooding has become increasingly severe11. Therefore, assessing flood susceptibility and revealing the driving mechanisms are of significant importance for improving flood control capabilities in Yancheng.

Flood susceptibility assessment methods have evolved from traditional statistical approaches to physical models and machine learning methods12. Traditional methods such as AHP and fuzzy comprehensive evaluation rely on expert experience, resulting in strong subjectivity and limited accuracy13. Physical models like SWMM and MIKE, though mechanistically clear, require extensive data and complex calculations, limiting large-scale application14. Machine learning methods, with powerful nonlinear fitting capabilities, have been widely applied in flood susceptibility assessment15. Ensemble models including RF, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost have become mainstream methods due to their high accuracy and generalization ability, achieving good results in cities such as the Greater Bay Area, Chengdu, Shenzhen, and Beijing16,17.

However, existing research has several shortcomings. The “black box” nature of machine learning models limits mechanistic interpretation and practical application in decision-making18,19. SHAP method, based on game theory, provides a solution by quantifying each feature’s marginal contribution, and has been applied to reveal factor importance in flood analysis10,20. Yet systematic multi-model comparisons with interpretability analysis for coastal low-lying cities remain lacking21. Moreover, existing studies mostly adopt global perspectives, neglecting spatial heterogeneity across different geomorphic units7. For cities like Yancheng with diverse landforms, flood mechanisms may differ significantly across regions, requiring detailed spatial differentiation studies9. Additionally, non-flooded point selection methods significantly affect model performance, but scientifically sound criteria are still lacking22.

This study constructs an interpretable machine learning framework for urban flood susceptibility assessment in Yancheng City. The main contents include: selecting non-flooded points using the 3/4 quantile distance method; comparing RF, XGBoost, and SVM models; applying SHAP to reveal factor importance and nonlinear effects; conducting spatial heterogeneity analysis across geomorphic units; and generating five-level susceptibility maps. The innovations lie in: establishing a “multi-model comparison - SHAP interpretability - spatial heterogeneity” integrated framework; revealing spatial differentiation patterns of flood driving mechanisms in coastal plain cities; and providing a transferable methodology for similar coastal cities in eastern China.

Study area overview and data sources

Study area overview

Yancheng City is located in the central coastal area of Jiangsu Province (32°34′−34°28′N, 119°27′−121°00′E), bordered by the Yellow Sea to the east, Huai’an to the west, Taizhou and Nantong to the south, and Lianyungang to the north (Fig. 1). The city administers three districts (Tinghu District, Yandu District, and Dafeng District), one county-level city (Dongtai City), and five counties (Jianhu County, Sheyang County, Funing County, Binhai County, and Xiangshui County). Its total land area is 16,931 square kilometers, making it the largest prefecture-level city in Jiangsu Province.

Geographical location and geomorphic zoning map of Yancheng City.

Yancheng is located in the eastern Jianghuai Plain, a typical coastal low-lying plain with an average elevation of only 2–4 m. Based on geomorphological characteristics, the city can be divided into five geomorphic units: coastal tidal flat area (3,847 km²), distributed along the coast of Dafeng, Sheyang, and Binhai, with low terrain significantly affected by ocean tides; the Lixiahe low-lying area (4,562 km²), distributed in Jianhu, Funing, and western Yandu, with the lowest elevation and poor drainage, historically known as the “pot-bottom depression”; the Yellow River paleo-channel area (2,918 km²), distributed in northern Binhai and Xiangshui, with relatively higher elevation due to Yellow River deposition; urban built-up area (1,284 km²), centered in Tinghu and Yandu Districts, with high urbanization and large proportion of impervious surfaces; and general agricultural area (4,320 km²), distributed in Dongtai and southern Xiangshui, mainly consisting of farmland.

Yancheng has a subtropical monsoon climate with average annual precipitation of 1000–1100 mm, approximately 60% concentrated during the flood season from June to September. The region has a dense river network, with north-south rivers such as the Chuanchang River and Tongyu River traversing the city, and east-west rivers such as the Sheyang River flowing into the Yellow Sea. In recent years, the frequency of extreme precipitation events has increased, and coupled with rapid urbanization, the risk of urban flooding continues to rise.

The flood inventory data integrates historical disaster records from the Yancheng City Emergency Management Bureau (2015–2023), local news reports, and social media (Sina Weibo). Through place name matching, coordinate correction, and duplicate removal, 486 valid flood point samples were obtained. It is acknowledged that event documentation from news reports and social media is inherently subject to reporting bias, with urban and commercially significant areas likely overrepresented relative to rural zones. To reduce this bias, all social media records were cross-verified against official Emergency Management Bureau records where available, and duplicate events from multiple sources describing the same flood were consolidated into single sample points. While this procedure cannot eliminate the possibility of unrecorded flood occurrences at selected non-flooded locations, the spatial constraint imposed by the 3/4 quantile buffer substantially reduces the risk of mislabeling proximal transitional areas.

Non-flooded points were selected through a spatially constrained sampling procedure termed the 3/4 quantile distance method. The pairwise Euclidean distances between all 486 recorded flood locations were first computed in ArcGIS using nearest-neighbor analysis. The resulting distance distribution was summarized, and its 75th percentile — approximately 2.3 km — was adopted as the exclusion buffer radius. All grid cells falling within 2.3 km of any flood record were excluded from candidate non-flooded locations, thereby preventing spatial proximity between positive and negative samples from introducing autocorrelation bias into model training. Non-flooded points were then randomly selected from the remaining spatial domain at a 1:1 ratio with flooded samples (486 points each), with final locations verified to occupy land cover classes inconsistent with documented flood occurrence, such as elevated terrain and Yellow River paleo-channel ridges. This constraint ensures that the non-flooded class genuinely reflects conditions associated with flood absence rather than merely unreported events. Comparison with random negative sample selection — conducted using an identical 70/30 stratified split, fixed random seed, and identical model architecture — showed that the 3/4 quantile method improved the test-set AUC by approximately 4.2% points (from 0.896 to 0.938). Since the test set composition was held constant across both experiments, this improvement reflects a genuine enhancement in training data quality — specifically, reduced spatial autocorrelation between training samples — rather than any change in test set difficulty or spatial separation from the evaluation set23.

Data preprocessing included: projecting all data to WGS_1984_UTM_Zone_50N coordinate system; clipping data using Yancheng City administrative boundary; resampling all continuous raster layers (DEM, slope, TWI, flow accumulation, and mean annual precipitation) to a uniform 30 m × 30 m resolution using bilinear interpolation, and resampling categorical layers (land use/land cover) using nearest-neighbor assignment to preserve class label integrity; and standardizing all conditional factors to 0–1 range using Min-Max normalization. For the ESA WorldCover LULC layer (native resolution 10 m), resampling to 30 m was performed using majority assignment, whereby each output cell received the land cover class occupying the largest areal proportion within its footprint. Thematic agreement between the 10 m source classification and the 30 m resampled product, assessed through a stratified random sample of 1,500 cells, reached 94.3% overall, with slightly lower agreement in heterogeneous urban fringe zones (89.7%), confirming that the resampling introduced negligible classification error at the spatial scale of this analysis. Multicollinearity testing using Pearson correlation coefficients and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) showed that VIF values of all 10 factors were less than 5 (range 1.23–4.67), and maximum Pearson correlation was 0.72 (TWI and flow accumulation), neither exceeding the exclusion threshold of 0.8, confirming no serious multicollinearity problems.

High TWI values indicate that water easily accumulates, meaning there is a large upstream drainage area and a gentle slope, making it difficult for water to drain; low TWI values indicate that the terrain is conducive to drainage. The flow accumulation represents the degree of water convergence from upstream, and is calculated based on the DEM using the D8 algorithm.

Hydrological factors characterize the influence of river systems on flooding. The closer the distance to a river, the greater the impact of river overflow; river network density reflects the regional drainage capacity, and a dense river network may become a flood channel during the flood season. Land use factors reflect the changes to the underlying surface caused by human activities. The higher the proportion of impervious surfaces, the weaker the surface infiltration capacity and the larger the runoff coefficient. The impervious surface ratio was computed as the proportion of cells classified as “Built-up” in the ESA WorldCover dataset within a 300 m circular neighborhood around each grid cell, with the assumption that the WorldCover “Built-up” category serves as a spatially consistent proxy for impervious coverage; fine-scale impermeability variations within built-up zones, such as permeable paving or internal green spaces, are not captured at this resolution; areas with high vegetation cover have better soil and water conservation and water retention functions. NDVI was derived from Landsat 8 OLI surface reflectance imagery (30 m) using flood-season median composites (June–September, 2015–2023) generated in Google Earth Engine to minimize cloud contamination and seasonal variation. Among meteorological factors, average annual precipitation reflects the spatial differences in regional precipitation input.

Research methods

Research framework

This study constructs a three-stage interpretable machine learning research framework: “Data Preparation - Model Development - Interpretation and Mapping” (Fig. 2). The first stage is the data preparation stage, which mainly involves collecting flood inventory data, selecting non-flooded points, extracting conditional factors, and conducting multicollinearity testing. The second stage is the model development stage, where three machine learning models—RF, XGBoost, and SVM—are compared, and the optimal model is selected through hyperparameter optimization and 5-fold cross-validation. The third stage is the interpretation and mapping stage, where SHAP interpretability analysis and spatial heterogeneity assessment are conducted based on the optimal model, generating a flood susceptibility classification map and proposing policy recommendations6.

Technical roadmap.

Machine learning models

Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning method based on the Bagging concept. This method generates multiple training subsets through Bootstrap sampling, independently constructs decision trees on each subset, and finally performs classification prediction through a voting mechanism20. The advantages of RF include its ability to handle high-dimensional data, strong robustness to noise, and resistance to overfitting. The main hyperparameters include the number of decision trees (n_estimators), the maximum depth of the trees (max_depth), and the minimum number of samples required to split a node (min_samples_split). Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is an ensemble learning algorithm based on the gradient boosting framework17. Unlike RF, which builds decision trees in parallel, XGBoost iteratively adds weak learners in a sequential manner. Each new model in each round fits the residuals of the previous round, optimizing the loss function through gradient descent. XGBoost introduces L1 and L2 regularization terms, effectively controlling model complexity and preventing overfitting, and performs particularly well in handling structured tabular data. The main hyperparameters include the learning rate (learning_rate), the maximum depth of the trees (max_depth), the number of iterations (n_estimators), and the subsampling ratio (subsample).

Support Vector Machine (SVM) is based on the principle of structural risk minimization, achieving sample classification by finding the optimal classification hyperplane7. For non-linearly separable problems, SVM uses kernel functions to map the original feature space to a high-dimensional space, and constructs a linear classification surface in the high-dimensional space. SVM performs robustly in small-sample, high-dimensional data classification, but has high computational complexity for large datasets. The main hyperparameters include the penalty coefficient (C), the kernel function parameter (gamma), and the kernel function type (kernel). This study uses the radial basis function (RBF) kernel, with optimal parameters determined through GridSearchCV and summarized in Table 3.

All three models were trained and evaluated using a consistent experimental protocol. The dataset (972 samples) was partitioned into a training set (680 samples, 70%) and a test set (292 samples, 30%) through stratified random sampling to preserve the 1:1 flood-to-non-flood ratio, with a fixed random seed (random_state = 42) to ensure reproducibility. Hyperparameter optimization employed GridSearchCV combined with 5-fold cross-validation on the training set, with the mean AUC across folds serving as the optimization criterion; the resulting optimal combinations for each model are summarized in Table 3. Model classification performance on the held-out test set was assessed via accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUC, and Kappa coefficient, with statistical significance of AUC differences between models evaluated using the DeLong test24,25. The probability threshold used to convert predicted flood probabilities into binary class labels for computing Precision, Recall, and F1-score was determined independently for each model by maximizing the Youden Index (J = TPR − FPR) on the test set. The resulting optimal thresholds were 0.487 for XGBoost, 0.501 for RF, and 0.512 for SVM — values close to 0.5, consistent with the balanced class distribution in the dataset. All modeling and spatial analyses were implemented in Python 3.9.13. Machine learning models were constructed using scikit-learn 1.2.0 (for RF and SVM) and XGBoost 1.7.3; SHAP interpretability analysis was performed using the shap 0.41.0 library with the TreeExplainer interface; and spatial raster operations were conducted in ArcGIS Pro 3.1 and supplemented by the rasterio 1.3.4 and geopandas 0.13.2 libraries.

SHAP interpretability analysis

Although machine learning models offer high prediction accuracy, their “black box” nature limits our understanding of the decision-making process. The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) method, based on the Shapley value principle from cooperative game theory, decomposes the model’s predicted value into the sum of the marginal contributions of each feature, thus transforming the model from a “black box” to a “transparent box"19,26,27.

For a model with features, the Shapley value of feature is defined as its average marginal contribution across all possible feature combinations:

In the formula, represents the complete feature set, is an arbitrary feature subset that does not contain feature , denotes the number of features in subset , is the model prediction function, and represents the change in predicted value after adding feature to the feature subset . Formula (2) traverses the marginal contributions of feature across all possible feature combinations, using the probability of occurrence as weights for a weighted average. Shapley values satisfy four axiomatic properties that collectively guarantee the rationality and uniqueness of feature attribution. Local accuracy ensures that the sum of all SHAP values equals the model’s prediction for each sample, linking local explanations to global model output. Missingness guarantees that features absent from a sample receive zero attribution. Consistency ensures that if a feature’s marginal contribution increases across model versions, its Shapley value does not decrease. Additivity permits decomposition of complex multi-feature predictions into independent, summable contributions. In the context of flood susceptibility modeling, these properties enable physically meaningful interpretation: a positive SHAP value for TWI at a given grid cell indicates that the local topographic configuration actively promotes flood probability relative to the baseline prediction, while a negative SHAP value for elevation reflects the risk-reducing effect of terrain height at that location. This direct correspondence between SHAP attribution and hydrological process variables transforms the model from a predictive black box into a quantitative tool for understanding spatial flood drivers.

This study employs the TreeSHAP algorithm to efficiently compute SHAP values for tree-based models. TreeSHAP leverages the structural properties of trees to reduce computational complexity from in traditional methods to , making SHAP analysis feasible for large-scale samples. Based on SHAP values, this study conducts interpretability analysis at three levels:

Global importance analysis quantifies the overall contribution of each factor to flood susceptibility by calculating the mean of the absolute SHAP values for each feature. The SHAP Summary Plot displays the feature ranking in order of average |SHAP| values, and visually presents the correspondence between feature values and SHAP values through scatter plot color mapping.

Dependency analysis uses the SHAP Dependence Plot to show the functional relationship between a single feature value and its SHAP value. This plot can identify non-linear effects (such as thresholds, saturation, and U-shaped relationships) and feature interactions (through color mapping of the second feature), revealing the specific mechanisms by which driving factors influence flood risk.

Local explanation analysis decomposes the prediction results of individual samples using the SHAP Force Plot, identifying the key factor combinations that promote or inhibit flood risk in that sample, achieving attribution explanation at the case level28.

Spatial heterogeneity analysis

Global SHAP analysis assumes that the driving factors act homogeneously within the study area, but Yancheng has diverse geomorphology, and the flood causation mechanisms may differ significantly in different regions. To reveal the spatial differentiation patterns of flood driving mechanisms, this study conducts spatial heterogeneity analysis based on five types of geomorphic units29,30.

For each geomorphic unit, the statistical characteristics of SHAP values (mean, median, interquartile range) are calculated for the samples within that unit. Box plots are used to compare the differences in the distribution of SHAP values of each factor among different units. The Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric test is used to assess the statistical significance of the differences in dominant factors between geomorphic units (significance level α = 0.05).

The local SHAP values of each grid cell are spatially visualized by first computing SHAP attributions at all 972 sample locations using the TreeSHAP algorithm, and then applying inverse distance weighting (IDW) interpolation to extend these point-wise attributions to a continuous 30 m raster surface covering the full study area. Direct per-pixel SHAP computation across the approximately 18.8 million grid cells of the study domain was computationally infeasible within the available resources; IDW was therefore adopted as a computationally tractable spatial generalization method. The interpolation parameters — power exponent p = 2, variable search radius with 12 nearest neighbors — were selected through leave-one-out cross-validation of the sample-point SHAP values to minimize mean absolute interpolation error. The IDW interpolation parameters are set as follows: power exponent p = 2, search radius is a variable radius mode, the number of nearest neighbors is 12, and the output grid resolution is consistent with the conditional factors (30 m×30 m). By identifying areas with high SHAP values, the spatial hotspots of each factor’s influence are determined, providing a basis for differentiated flood control strategies in different regions31.

Flood susceptibility mapping

Based on the trained XGBoost model, spatial prediction was performed across all 30 m × 30 m grid cells covering Yancheng City’s 16,931 km² extent. The ten conditioning factor rasters were co-registered to a common spatial reference (WGS_1984_UTM_Zone_50N) and stacked into a pixel-wise feature array using the rasterio library in Python 3.9. Each row of this array, representing the conditioning factor values of one grid cell, was passed to the trained XGBoost classifier, and the model output a flood probability estimate between 0 and 1 for each cell. The resulting probability surface was written back to a GeoTIFF raster and imported into ArcGIS 10.8 for visualization. This pixel-by-pixel prediction approach ensured that the full spatial heterogeneity of the conditioning factors was captured without spatial smoothing, yielding a continuous flood susceptibility index across the study area. The natural breaks (Jenks) method was used to divide the continuous index into five levels: very low (0–0.18.18), low (0.18–0.35), medium (0.35–0.52), high (0.52–0.71), and very high (0.71–1.0.71.0). The natural breaks method maximizes inter-class variance and minimizes intra-class variance, ensuring that the classification boundaries are located at natural “breaks” in the data distribution, and the classification results conform to the intrinsic structure of the data.

The area and proportion of each susceptibility level were statistically analyzed to understand the spatial distribution pattern of susceptibility. The proportion of high-risk areas was summarized by county/district to identify key prevention and control areas, providing spatial decision support for flood risk management32.

To assess potential overfitting, training-set performance metrics were additionally computed for all three models. The AUC values on the training set were 0.963, 0.933, and 0.895 for XGBoost, RF, and SVM, respectively, yielding training-to-test AUC gaps of 0.025, 0.021, and 0.019. These modest discrepancies, all below the 0.05 threshold conventionally indicative of overfitting, demonstrate that the models generalize well to unseen data. The narrow standard deviations from 5-fold cross-validation (SD = 0.009, 0.010, and 0.018 for XGBoost, RF, and SVM, respectively) provide further confirmation of model stability across different subsets of the training data.

The ROC curve comparison is shown in Fig. 3. All three curves are above the diagonal reference line, indicating good classification capabilities. The XGBoost curve is closest to the upper left corner with the largest AUC, demonstrating the best classification performance. The Youden index (J) of XGBoost (0.779) is higher than RF (0.735) and SVM (0.671), further confirming the superiority of XGBoost. DeLong’s test results show that the difference in AUC between XGBoost and RF is statistically significant (p = 0.023), and the difference between XGBoost and SVM is highly significant (p < 0.001)34.

ROC curve comparison.

The 5-fold cross-validation results show that the average AUC of XGBoost is 0.938 (95% CI: 0.927–0.949, SD = 0.009), RF is 0.911 (95% CI: 0.898–0.924, SD = 0.010), and SVM is 0.875 (95% CI: 0.853–0.897, SD = 0.018). XGBoost not only has the highest mean but also the smallest standard deviation, indicating high prediction accuracy and good stability. Therefore, subsequent SHAP analysis and susceptibility mapping were based on the XGBoost model.

The differences in model performance can be attributed to algorithmic characteristics. XGBoost uses a gradient boosting framework, iteratively fitting residuals and introducing regularization to control model complexity, performing excellently in handling flood data with complex nonlinear relationships. RF, though also an ensemble method, trains each base learner independently without considering residual information, resulting in slightly lower accuracy. SVM is sensitive to hyperparameters, and with large sample sizes and complex feature relationships, kernel function selection and parameter tuning become difficult, limiting its performance35,36.

SHAP global feature importance analysis

SHAP values were computed across all 972 samples (the complete dataset of flood and non-flooded points) using the final XGBoost model trained on the 680-sample training partition, ensuring that the global feature importance rankings reflect the model’s behavior across the full range of conditioning factor values present in the study area. The Topographic Wetness Index (TWI) ranks first, with a mean |SHAP| value of 0.185 (SD = 0.092), indicating both its greatest overall contribution and considerable spatial variability in its role across the study area. Elevation (DEM) ranks second with mean |SHAP| of 0.142 (SD = 0.071), followed by Impervious Surface Ratio at 0.098 (SD = 0.063). The standard deviations reflect the heterogeneity of each factor’s influence across geomorphically diverse locations — consistent with the spatial heterogeneity results reported in Sect. 4.4. The moderate correlation between TWI and Flow Accumulation (Pearson r = 0.72), the highest pairwise correlation in the dataset, may contribute to some shared attribution between these two features; however, as both VIF values remain below 5, multicollinearity is unlikely to substantially distort the SHAP ranking37,38.

The cumulative contribution of these three factors reaches 47.6%, constituting the core driving factors for flooding in Yancheng. Distance to rivers ranks fourth (average |SHAP|=0.081), showing a negative contribution, indicating higher flood risk near rivers. River network density ranked fifth (average |SHAP|=0.067), reflecting that dense river networks may become flood channels during flood season. Key factor dependency analysis

SHAP dependency analysis revealed nonlinear response characteristics between key factors and flood susceptibility (Fig. 5). TWI showed a significant positive correlation with SHAP values (ρ = 0.986, R²=0.67), with an approximately linear increasing pattern, indicating that TWI’s promoting effect on flood risk is relatively uniform across the entire value range. This is consistent with Yancheng Plain’s hydrological characteristics, where flat terrain makes natural drainage difficult. Notably, samples at higher elevations have lower SHAP values at the same TWI value, indicating that elevation weakens the TWI effect; even with high TWI, higher elevation can reduce risk through gravity drainage. Elevation showed a significant negative correlation with SHAP values (ρ = 0.980, R²=0.72), with clear threshold characteristics: below 10 m, SHAP values and risk increased rapidly; above 20 m, SHAP values stabilized at low levels.

SHAP Dependence Plot of Key Factors (Top 4 Factors).

Human activities and hydrological factors also showed significant nonlinear characteristics. Although the correlation between impervious surface ratio and SHAP values (ρ = 0.376) was weaker than topographic factors, a more prominent threshold effect was observed: when impervious surface exceeded approximately 60%, SHAP values increased sharply, indicating significantly increased flood risk regardless of other conditions. This finding reveals the “amplifier” effect of urbanization on flooding and provides scientific basis for setting impervious surface control red lines in sponge city construction. Distance from rivers showed an exponential decay pattern (τ = 1.8 km), with 0–1.5 km being a high-risk buffer zone and risk stabilizing at low levels beyond 5 km. This spatial decay is consistent with the physical mechanism of river flood influence: areas near rivers are exposed to overflow risk, while distant areas are mainly affected by rainfall-induced waterlogging.

Spatial heterogeneity analysis based on geomorphic units

The spatial heterogeneity analysis based on five geomorphic units is shown in Fig. 6. All high-risk proportion statistics reported for each unit are calculated relative to the total number of 30 m grid cells with valid conditioning factor data within that unit’s boundary; given that data preprocessing produced no missing-value cells within the Yancheng study area, these proportions are equivalent to area-based percentages computed over the full spatial extent of each geomorphic zone. The box plots reveal significant spatial differentiation of flood driving mechanisms across geomorphic units. In natural topography-controlled areas, the Lixiahe low-lying area is most typical, with TWI SHAP values significantly higher than other units (Kruskal-Wallis test: H = 67.34, df = 4, p < 0.001). With strong water accumulation and difficult drainage, high-risk areas account for 28.6%, confirming the historical experience of “flooding whenever it rains.” The coastal tidal flat area is primarily controlled by elevation, related to its low-lying position and significant influence from ocean tides and storm surges, with high-risk areas accounting for 23.1%. The general agricultural area shows joint dominance of TWI and elevation, with high-risk areas at 14.3%39,40.

In stark contrast, the urban built-up area (1,284 km²) is dominated by impervious surface ratio, with an average SHAP value of 0.267, far higher than other units. Surface hardening causes rapid transformation of rainwater into runoff, with high-risk areas reaching 31.5%, indicating that human modification has become the core driving force in urban areas, exceeding natural factors. The Yellow River paleo-channel area (2,918 km²) exhibits unique low-risk characteristics, with high-risk areas only 11.2%. This stems from relatively high elevation due to historical sediment deposition and good vegetation cover, making it a potential spatial carrier for ecological conservation and flood regulation.

Discussion

Model performance and applicability analysis

This study compared the performance of three machine learning models—RF, XGBoost, and SVM—in assessing flood susceptibility in Yancheng. XGBoost achieved the best performance with an AUC of 0.938, consistent with previous findings in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area and Yangtze River Delta24. The superiority of XGBoost can be attributed to its gradient boosting mechanism and effective overfitting control through regularization, well-suited to the numerous driving factors and complex non-linear relationships in Yancheng. RF, proven robust in many studies, achieved an AUC of 0.912, demonstrating value as an alternative to XGBoost. SVM performed poorly, possibly due to the large sample size (n = 486 × 2) and difficulty in kernel function selection and parameter tuning43.

It is acknowledged that the AUC difference between XGBoost and RF (ΔAUC = 0.026) is statistically significant but modest in absolute terms. At the spatial scale of Yancheng City, this performance gap corresponds to differences in the classification of approximately 440km² of land area (2.6% of the 16,931 km² study extent), which carries practical relevance for flood risk zoning and resource allocation. However, the revised discussion recognizes that RF, with its AUC of 0.912 and comparable interpretability, constitutes a methodologically sound and operationally practical alternative when computational efficiency or model transparency is paramount. The choice of XGBoost as the primary model in this study is therefore justified by its superior performance under the DeLong test and its smaller cross-validation standard deviation (SD = 0.009 vs. 0.010 for RF), while the operational comparability of the two ensemble models is acknowledged as a limitation on the uniqueness of the XGBoost recommendation.

The 3/4 quantile distance method for selecting non-flooded points is crucial for improving model performance. This method ensures that non-flooded points maintain a reasonable distance from flooded points (approximately 2.3 km) through spatial constraints, effectively avoiding sample overlap and spatial autocorrelation problems23. Comparative experiments showed that using the 3/4 quantile method improved AUC from 0.896 to 0.938 (approximately 4.2% points), verifying its effectiveness. Notably, the selection was based solely on spatial distance constraints, without introducing conditional factors such as elevation, to avoid artificially creating spurious correlations between features and labels.

To further verify model robustness, sensitivity analysis was conducted. Using three training/testing split ratios (6:4, 7:3, 8:2), XGBoost’s AUC values were 0.931, 0.938, and 0.942 respectively, with a fluctuation range of only 1.1% points, indicating insensitivity to data split ratio. After adding 5% Gaussian noise to each conditional factor, AUC decreased by only 0.8–1.5% points, indicating good noise resistance. Bootstrap resampling (n = 1,000) yielded a 95% confidence interval of [0.929, 0.947] for XGBoost’s AUC, further verifying the reliability of prediction results. To assess the degree to which random data splitting may overestimate model generalizability, a complementary spatial holdout analysis was performed, withholding all samples from Dongtai City as an independent geographic test zone while training the model on samples from the remaining administrative units. The XGBoost model achieved an AUC of 0.921 on the Dongtai holdout set, compared to 0.938 under random splitting, indicating a modest but measurable reduction in performance when the test zone is spatially separated from the training domain. This 0.017-point gap is consistent with spatial autocorrelation effects documented in comparable machine learning flood susceptibility studies, and suggests that the reported AUC of 0.938 represents a slight upper-bound estimate of the model’s true spatial generalization ability.

Analysis of flood driving mechanisms

SHAP analysis revealed that TWI, elevation, and impervious surface ratio are the three key driving factors of flooding in Yancheng, with a cumulative contribution of 47.6%, reflecting the dual control of natural topography and human activities on flood risk. TWI, as the primary factor (18.47%), highlights the controlling effect of flat terrain on hydrological processes. With average elevation of only 2–4 m and slopes generally less than 1°, water drainage is difficult, requiring artificial drainage. The average TWI in the Lixiahe low-lying area reaches 12.3, far higher than the city’s average of 9.7, consistent with the dominant effect of TWI revealed by SHAP analysis.

Elevation shows a significant negative contribution (16.23%), indicating strong correlation between low-altitude areas and higher flood risk. The SHAP dependence plot shows that the 10-meter contour is an important risk boundary. This threshold has guiding significance for urban planning: major infrastructure should be prioritized in areas above 10 m. The impervious surface ratio exhibits a threshold effect at approximately 60%. Once impervious surface exceeds 60%, surface infiltration capacity is almost completely lost, leading to urban flooding when rainfall exceeds design standards. Currently, the impervious surface ratio in Tinghu District’s core area has exceeded 75%, urgently requiring increased permeable space through sponge city construction.

Policy implications of spatial heterogeneity

Spatial heterogeneity analysis reveals significant differences in flood driving mechanisms across geomorphic units, providing a scientific basis for differentiated flood control strategies. Urban built-up areas should focus on source reduction and drainage network upgrades. For the flooding mechanism dominated by impervious surfaces, strict control of new impervious surfaces should be implemented, and low-impact development technologies such as permeable paving, green roofs, and rain gardens should be promoted; simultaneously, renovation of old drainage networks should be accelerated with standards upgraded to a 30-year return period.

The Lixiahe low-lying area should focus on improving polder drainage systems. For the waterlogging mechanism dominated by TWI, dike layout should be optimized, drainage pumping stations added, and a hierarchical drainage model of “high water high drainage, low water low drainage” promoted; meanwhile, farmland water conservancy facilities should be strengthened to improve agricultural disaster resistance. Coastal tidal flat areas should coordinate seawall protection and wetland conservation. For the tidal disaster mechanism dominated by elevation, seawall projects should be reinforced and storm surge warning systems improved; coastal wetlands should be protected to utilize their wave dissipation function. The Yellow River paleo-channel area has a lower risk baseline and can serve as ecological conservation and water storage space for regional flood regulation.

It should be noted that the susceptibility framework developed in this study assesses static landscape predisposition to flooding from any source without mechanistically distinguishing between inland pluvial events and coastal tidal or storm surge inundation. In the coastal tidal flat unit, the primacy of elevation as identified by SHAP analysis serves as an indirect proxy for chronic tidal exposure — areas at lower elevations are more frequently inundated by regular tidal cycles as well as episodic storm surges. However, dynamic coastal processes such as typhoon-induced storm surges, sea level variability, and tidal phasing are not captured by any of the ten static conditioning factors, meaning that the susceptibility map for coastal areas should be interpreted as reflecting long-term landscape vulnerability rather than event-specific inundation probability under tidal forcing. Integration of coastal hydrodynamic variables — tidal range, storm surge return period, coastal barrier crest elevation — would substantially improve the model’s representation of coastal flood mechanisms and constitutes a priority direction for future work. The importance of differentiating between compound flood mechanisms — including the interaction of storm surge and precipitation — in coastal urban risk assessment has been demonstrated by Wahl et al. (2015)42.

Research limitations and prospects

This study has the following limitations. In model validation, random split training/testing set strategy was adopted without rigorous temporal extrapolation validation (e.g., training with 2015–2020 data, validating with 2021–2023 data) or spatial extrapolation validation (e.g., reserving a specific county as independent validation area), potentially overestimating model generalization ability. Regarding data quality, flood inventory data compiled from emergency management records and news reports may carry spatial biases toward urban areas and positional uncertainties due to imprecise location descriptions. A spatial sensitivity analysis was performed by applying a random positional perturbation of up to 90 m (three raster cells) to all flood sample coordinates and re-running the full modeling pipeline. The resulting AUC of 0.935 confirms that the model outputs are robust to plausible levels of coordinate uncertainty in the flood inventory. Nevertheless, systematic location errors that are correlated with land use type — for instance, consistently reporting locations at road intersections rather than the actual inundation centroid — cannot be fully assessed without field validation data, and this remains an acknowledged limitation of the study; conditional factors lacked detailed infrastructure data such as drainage network density and pump station locations; the study was a static assessment without considering dynamic impacts of climate change and urbanization. The ESA WorldCover LULC layer used in this study represents land cover conditions for the reference period 2020–2021. Given that the flood inventory spans 2015–2023, earlier flood records may have been associated with lower impervious surface coverage than captured in the static LULC layer, particularly in rapidly urbanizing districts. Future studies should consider incorporating multi-temporal LULC data or land-use change trajectories to better align the conditioning factor values with the temporal context of each flood event.

A further limitation concerns the spatial structure of the non-flooded point sample. The 3/4 quantile buffer of 2.3 km, while reducing autocorrelation and sample contamination, creates a spatial separation between positive and negative training samples that may simplify the classification boundary learned by the model. This separation could lead to an optimistic AUC estimate relative to scenarios where near-boundary discrimination is required. The buffer threshold was derived objectively from the empirical distance distribution of flood points and validated through comparative experiments, but future studies should investigate the sensitivity of model performance to alternative buffer distances and consider incorporating near-boundary negative samples in a stratified design to better capture transitional risk zones.

Future research directions include: integrating multi-source data such as social media and satellite remote sensing to improve flood sample representativeness; incorporating infrastructure factors to build a more complete conditional factor system; coupling climate models and land-use change scenarios for dynamic flood risk prediction; and integrating social vulnerability to upgrade from susceptibility assessment to comprehensive risk assessment.

Conclusion

This study constructed an interpretable machine learning framework for urban flood susceptibility assessment in Yancheng City, integrating multi-model comparison, SHAP interpretability analysis, and spatial heterogeneity assessment.

The XGBoost model performed best with an AUC of 0.938 and accuracy of 0.891, significantly outperforming RF and SVM models. SHAP analysis identified TWI, elevation, and impervious surface ratio as core driving factors with a cumulative contribution of 47.6%. TWI contributed most (18.47%), reflecting topographic control on water accumulation; elevation showed negative contribution (16.23%); and impervious surface ratio exhibited a threshold effect at 60%. Spatial heterogeneity analysis revealed distinct dominant factors across geomorphic units: impervious surfaces dominated urban built-up areas (high-risk proportion 31.5%), TWI dominated the Lixiahe lowland (28.6%), elevation dominated coastal tidal flats (23.1%), while the Yellow River paleo-channel showed lowest risk (11.2%). Overall, 19.2% of the city area was classified as high-to-very high susceptibility, exhibiting a “one core, two belts” pattern, with Tinghu District highest (37.0%) and Dongtai City lowest (9.7%).

This study’s integrated “multi-model comparison- SHAP interpretability- spatial heterogeneity” analysis framework achieved an organic unity of prediction accuracy and mechanism explanation, revealing the spatial differentiation patterns of flood driving mechanisms in coastal plain cities. This provides a scientific basis and methodological reference for developing differentiated flood control strategies for Yancheng and similar coastal cities in eastern China.
