Abstract
. We provide a new proof of the analogue of the Artin–Springer theorem for groups of type 𝖣 \mathsf{D} that can be represented by similitudes over an algebra of Schur index 2 2 : an anisotropic generalized quadratic form over a quaternion algebra Q Q remains anisotropic after generic splitting of Q Q , hence also under odd-degree field extensions of the base field. Our proof is characteristic-free and does not use the excellence property.

. In particular,

,

Lemma 1.2
.

The following square of isomorphisms is commutative:

\textstyle{(V\otimes_{Q}I)\otimes_{k}(V\otimes_{Q}I)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Phi_{b_{a*s}}}
\scriptstyle{\Theta^{\prime}}
\textstyle{\End_{k}(V\otimes_{Q}I)}
\textstyle{V\otimes_{Q}\overline{V}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Phi_{h_{s}}}
\textstyle{\End_{Q}(V)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Theta}

Moreover, for all
v
,
w\in V
, we have

\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}\bigl(\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\otimes\overline{w})\bigr)=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(h_{s}(w,v)\bigr).

Remark
.

The second assertion also holds when
Q
is a division algebra
by
[
8
, (5.1)]
.

Proof.

For
v_{1}
,
v_{2}\in V
and
\xi_{1}
,
\xi_{2}\in I
, the endomorphism

(\Theta\circ\Phi_{h_{s}}\circ\Theta^{\prime})\bigl((v_{1}\otimes\xi_{1})\otimes(v_{2}\otimes\xi_{2})\bigr)

maps

to

\bigl(v_{1}\cdot(\xi_{1}\otimes a(\xi_{2},\mbox{\textvisiblespace}))h_{s}(v_{2},w)\bigr)\otimes\eta=v_{1}\otimes\bigl((\xi_{1}\otimes a(\xi_{2},\mbox{\textvisiblespace}))h_{s}(v_{2},w)\cdot\eta\bigr).

Now,
(\xi_{1}\otimes a(\xi_{2},\mbox{\textvisiblespace}))h_{s}(v_{2},w)\cdot\eta=\xi_{1}\otimes a(\xi_{2},h_{s}(v_{2},w)\eta)=\xi_{1}\otimes(a*h_{s})(v_{2}\otimes\xi_{2},w\otimes\eta)
. Since

,
it follows that

\bigl(v_{1}\cdot(\xi_{1}\otimes a(\xi_{2},\mbox{\textvisiblespace}))h_{s}(v_{2},w)\bigr)\otimes\eta=(v_{1}\otimes\xi_{1})\otimes b_{a*s}(v_{2}\otimes\xi_{2},w\otimes\eta).

This proves the first assertion of the lemma.

Since the reduced trace is linear and
\Theta^{\prime}
is an isomorphism, it is enough to prove the second assertion for the image of a symbol
(v_{1}\otimes\xi_{1})\otimes(v_{2}\otimes\xi_{2})
. So,
we let

and

so that
v\otimes\overline{w}=\Theta^{\prime}\bigl((v_{1}\otimes\xi_{1})\otimes(v_{2}\otimes\xi_{2})\bigr).
By uniqueness of the reduced trace, we have
\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}=\Trd_{\End_{k}(V\otimes_{Q}I)}\circ\Theta
. Therefore, the commutative diagram above shows that

\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}(\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\otimes\overline{w}))=\Trd_{\End_{k}(V\otimes_{Q}I)}\bigl(\Phi_{b_{a*s}}((v_{1}\otimes\xi_{1})\otimes(v_{2}\otimes\xi_{2}))\bigr).

By
[

, this reduced trace is equal to
b_{a*s}(v_{2}\otimes\xi_{2},v_{1}\otimes\xi_{1})=a(\xi_{2},h_{s}(v_{2},v_{1})\xi_{1})
.
On the other hand, we have

h_{s}(w,v)=h_{s}(v_{2},v_{1}\cdot\bigl(\xi_{1}\otimes a(\xi_{2},\mbox{\textvisiblespace})\bigr)=h_{s}(v_{2},v_{1})\bigl(\xi_{1}\otimes a(\xi_{2},\mbox{\textvisiblespace})\bigr)=\bigl(h_{s}(v_{2},v_{1})\cdot\xi_{1}\bigl)\otimes a(\xi_{2},\mbox{\textvisiblespace}).

The lemma follows, since under the identifications
I\otimes_{k}I^{\sharp}=\End_{k}(I)=Q
,

(7)

\Trd_{Q}(\xi\otimes\psi)=\psi(\xi)\qquad\text{for $\xi\in I$ and
$\psi\in I^{\sharp}$.}

1.4.
Quadratic pairs

Recall from
[
8
, § 5.B]
that a quadratic pair on a central
simple algebra
A
of even degree
n
over an arbitrary field
k
is a
pair
(\sigma,f)
consisting of a
k
-linear involution
\sigma
and a
linear map
f\colon\Sym(\sigma)\to k
, where
\Sym(\sigma)=\{x\in A\mid\sigma(x)=x\}
, subject to the following conditions:

(i)

\dim_{k}\Sym(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)
and
\Trd_{A}(x)=0
for all
x\in A
such that
\sigma(x)=-x
;

(ii)

f\bigl(x+\sigma(x)\bigr)=\Trd_{A}(x)
for all
x\in A
.

The map
f
is called the
semitrace
of the quadratic pair

(\sigma,f)
, because (ii) implies
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Trd_{A}(x)
for all

x\in\Sym(\sigma)
if
\charac k\neq 2
.

When
A=\End_{k}(W)
for some
k
-vector space of even dimension, it is
shown in
[
8
, (5.11)]
how to attach a quadratic pair

(\sigma_{b},f_{q})
to each nonsingular quadratic form
q\colon W\to k

with polar bilinear form
b
using the isomorphism (
5
).
The involution
\sigma_{b}
is adjoint to
b
, and is given by the
switch map on
W\otimes_{k}W
:

\sigma_{b}\circ\Phi_{b}(v\otimes w)=\Phi_{b}(w\otimes v)\qquad\text{for $v$, $w\in W$.}

Therefore,
\Sym(\sigma)
is spanned by elements of the form

\Phi_{b}(w\otimes w)
with
w\in W
.
The semitrace
f_{q}
is the unique linear map such that

f_{q}\bigl(\Phi_{b}(w\otimes w)\bigr)=q(w)
for all
w\in W
. Moreover,
every quadratic pair on
A
is
attached to a nonsingular quadratic form on
W
, uniquely determined
up to a factor in
k^{\times}
.

Our goal in this subsection is to show how to associate a quadratic
pair on
\End_{Q}(V)
to every generalized quadratic space
(V,[s])
on

Q
. We also show that when
Q=\End_{k}(I)
the quadratic
pair associated to
[s]
coincides with the quadratic pair attached to

(V\otimes_{Q}I,q_{a*s})
under the canonical isomorphism

\Theta\colon\End_{Q}(V)\xrightarrow{\sim}\End_{k}(V\otimes_{Q}I)

of (
6
). Thus, the Morita equivalence discussed
in §
1.2
has no effect on quadratic pairs.

Let
(V,[s])
be a generalized quadratic space over
Q
and let
\Phi_{h_{s}}
be the isomorphism defined in (
4
).
Write
\sw
for the switch map on
V\otimes_{Q}\overline{V}
, which
carries
v\otimes\overline{w}
to
w\otimes\overline{v}
for
v
,
w\in V
. The involution
\sigma_{h_{s}}
adjoint to
h_{s}
satisfies

\sigma_{h_{s}}\circ\Phi_{h_{s}}=-\Phi_{h_{s}}\circ\sw
because
h_{s}
is
skew-hermitian, hence the space
\Skew(\sw)=\{u\in V\otimes_{Q}\overline{V}\mid\sw(u)=-u\}
is mapped bijectively to
\Sym(\sigma_{h_{s}})
by

\Phi_{h_{s}}
:

\Sym(\sigma_{h_{s}})=\Phi_{h_{s}}\bigl(\Skew(\sw)\bigr).

For every
t\in\Sesq(V)
, define a
k
-linear functional

T_{t}\colon V\otimes_{Q}\overline{V}\to k\qquad\text{by}\quad T_{t}(v\otimes\overline{w})=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(t(w,v)\bigr)\quad\text{for $v$, $w\in V$.}

Then
T_{t^{\dagger}}=T_{t}\circ\sw
for all
t\in\Sesq(V)
, hence

T_{t+t^{\dagger}}

vanishes on
\Skew(\sw)
and the restriction of
T_{s}
to
\Skew(\sw)

depends only on
[s]\in\Quad(V)
. Let
f_{[s]}\colon\Sym(\sigma_{h_{s}})\to k
be defined by

f_{[s]}\bigl(\Phi_{h_{s}}(u)\bigr)=T_{s}(u)\qquad\text{for $u\in\Skew(\sw)$.}

Proposition 1.3
.

The pair
(\sigma_{h_{s}},f_{[s]})
is a quadratic pair on
\End_{Q}(V)

attached to
[s]\in\Quad(V)
. Every quadratic pair on
\End_{Q}(V)
is
attached to a nonsingular generalized quadratic form on
V
, which
is uniquely determined up to a factor in
k^{\times}
.

Proof.

Since
h_{s}
is an even skew-hermitian form on
V
, the adjoint
involution
\sigma_{h_{s}}
satisfies condition (i) in the definition
of a quadratic pair by
[
8
, (2.6) & (4.2)]
. To check condition (ii),
observe that for
v
,
w\in V
we have

f_{[s]}\bigl(\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\otimes\overline{w})+\sigma_{h_{s}}(\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\otimes\overline{w}))\bigr)=T_{s}(v\otimes\overline{w})-T_{s}(w\otimes\overline{v})\\
=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(s(w,v)\bigr)-\Trd_{Q}\bigl(s(v,w)\bigr)=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(h_{s}(w,v)\bigr).

Using
[
8
, (5.1)]
and Lemma
1.2
, we see that for
v
,
w\in V

f_{[s]}\bigl(\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\otimes\overline{w})+\sigma_{h_{s}}(\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\otimes\overline{w}))\bigr)=\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}\bigl(\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\otimes\overline{w})\bigr),

hence
(\sigma_{h_{s}},f_{[s]})
is a quadratic pair on
\End_{Q}(V)
.

Now, let
(\sigma,f)
be a quadratic pair on

\End_{Q}(V)
. By
[
8
, (4.2)]
, the involution
\sigma
is adjoint
to some nonsingular skew-hermitian form
h
on
V
.
To prove the result, we need to construct a sesquilinear form
s
on
V
such that
f=f_{[s]}
and
h=h_{s}
.
Consider the

k
-vector space isomorphism
\Phi_{h}\colon V\otimes_{Q}\overline{V}\to\End_{Q}(V)
defined as in (
4
), and recall
from
[
8
, (5.7)]
that there exists
\ell\in\End_{Q}(V)
such that

(8)

f\bigl(\Phi_{h}(u)\bigr)=\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}\bigl(\ell\,\Phi_{h}(u)\bigr)\qquad\text{for all $u\in\Skew(\sw)$.}

To every
v
,
w\in V
we attach the
k
-linear functional

\tau_{v,w}\colon Q\to k
defined by

\tau_{v,w}(x)=\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}\bigl(\ell\,\Phi_{h}(v\,x\otimes\overline{w})\bigr).

Since the bilinear form
(X,Y)\mapsto\Trd_{Q}(XY)
on
Q
is
nonsingular, there exists a unique element
s(w,v)\in Q
such that

\tau_{v,w}(x)=\Trd_{Q}(s(w,v)x)\qquad\text{for all $x\in Q$.}

Consider the map
s\colon V\times V\to Q
that carries
(w,v)\in V\times V
to
s(w,v)
.
It is readily verified that
s\in\Sesq(V)
, and by definition

\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}\bigl(\ell\,\Phi_{h}(v\otimes\overline{w})\bigr)=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(s(w,v)\bigr)=T_{s}(v\otimes\overline{w})\qquad\text{for
$v$, $w\in V$,}

hence
\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}\bigl(\ell\,\Phi_{h}(u)\bigr)=T_{s}(u)
for all

u\in V\otimes_{Q}\overline{V}
.
Comparing with (
8
), we see
that
f\circ\Phi_{h}
coincides with
T_{s}
on
\Skew(\sw)
, and it only remains to prove that
h=h_{s}
.
Using condition (ii) in the definition of a quadratic pair, together with
[
8
, (5.1)]
and Lemma
1.2
, we obtain for
v
,
w\in V

f\bigl(\Phi_{h}(v\otimes\overline{w})+\sigma(\Phi_{h}(v\otimes\overline{w}))\bigr)=\Trd_{\End_{Q}(V)}\bigl(\Phi_{h}(v\otimes\overline{w})\bigr)=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(h(w,v)\bigr).

But
\sigma\bigl(\Phi_{h}(v\otimes\overline{w})\bigr)=-\Phi_{h}(w\otimes\overline{v})
and
f\circ\Phi_{h}=T_{s}
on

\Skew(\sw)
, hence we also have for
v
,
w\in V

f\bigl(\Phi_{h}(v\otimes\overline{w})+\sigma(\Phi_{h}(v\otimes\overline{w}))\bigr)=T_{s}(v\otimes\overline{w})-T_{s}(w\otimes\overline{v})=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(h_{s}(w,v)\bigr).

Therefore,
\Trd_{Q}\bigl(h(w,v)\bigr)=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(h_{s}(w,v)\bigr)
for
all
v
,
w\in V
and also, since
h
and
h_{s}
are sesquilinear,

\Trd_{Q}\bigl(h(w,v)x\bigr)=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(h_{s}(w,v)x\bigr)
for all

x\in Q
. It follows that
h(w,v)=h_{s}(w,v)
for
v
,
w\in V
because
the bilinear form
(X,Y)\mapsto\Trd_{Q}(XY)
on
Q
is
nonsingular. Therefore,
(\sigma,f)=(\sigma_{h_{s}},f_{[s]})
.

It remains to prove
[s]
is unique up to a scalar factor. Consider
another sesquilinear form
s^{\prime}\in\Sesq(V)
and assume it gives rise to
the same quadratic pair. In particular, the skew-hermitian forms

h_{s}=s-s^{\dagger}
and
h_{s^{\prime}}=s^{\prime}-s^{\prime{\dagger}}
induce the same adjoint
involutions. Hence they are equal up to a scalar factor. Scaling
s^{\prime}

if necessary, we may thus assume
h_{s}=h_{s^{\prime}}
. It follows that
s
and

s^{\prime}
differ by a hermitian form
g
, and it only remains to prove that

g
is actually even-hermitian. By definition of the corresponding
semitraces,
T_{s}
and
T_{s^{\prime}}
coincide on
\Skew(\sw)
, therefore

T_{g}
vanishes on this subset. In particular, for all
v\in V
and

x\in\Skew(Q,\overline{\rule{7.11317pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{4.0pt}})
, we have

T_{g}(vx\otimes\overline{v})=\Trd_{Q}(g(v,v)x)=0.

By
[
8
, (2.3)]
, we get that
g(v,v)

is a symmetrized element of
(Q,\overline{\rule{7.11317pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{4.0pt}})
for all
v\in V
and the
result follows by
[
9
, I(3.1.1)]
.
∎

Recall from
[
8
, (6.5)]
that a quadratic pair
(\sigma,f)
on a
central simple
k
-algebra
A
is said to be
isotropic
if there
exists a nonzero right ideal
I\subset A
subject to the following
conditions:

(i)

\sigma(x)y=0
for all
x
,
y\in I
;

(ii)

f(x)=0
for all
x\in I\cap\Sym(\sigma)
.

It is shown in
[
8
, (6.6)]
that the quadratic pair

(\sigma_{b},f_{q})
on
\End_{k}(W)
attached to a nonsingular quadratic
form
q
on a
k
-vector space
W
is isotropic if and only if
q
is
isotropic. We prove the same property for generalized quadratic
forms.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
(V,[s])
be a generalized quadratic space over
Q
and

(\sigma_{h_{s}},f_{[s]})
the attached quadratic pair on

\End_{Q}(V)
. The form
[s]
is isotropic if and only if the pair

(\sigma_{h_{s}},f_{[s]})
is isotropic.

Proof.

Every right ideal in
\End_{Q}(V)
has the form
\Hom_{Q}(V,U)
for some

Q
-submodule
U\subset V
. Using the isomorphism
\Phi_{h_{s}}

of (
4
), we may write
\Hom_{Q}(V,U)=\Phi_{h_{s}}(U\otimes_{Q}\overline{V})
. Suppose
\Hom_{Q}(V,U)
is a nonzero ideal
satisfying (i) and (ii), and let
u\in U
be nonzero. For all
x\in Q
such that
\overline{x}=-x
, we have
\Phi_{h_{s}}(u\,x\otimes\overline{u})\in\Hom_{Q}(V,U)\cap\Sym(\sigma_{h_{s}})
and

(9)

\Trd_{Q}(s(u,u)x)=f_{[s]}\bigl(\Phi_{h_{s}}(u\,x\otimes\overline{u})\bigr)=0.

Therefore,
s(u,u)\in k
and
u
is isotropic in
(V,[s])
.

Conversely, let
u\in V
be isotropic, and consider the nonzero
right ideal

\Hom_{Q}(V,uQ)=\Phi_{h_{s}}(uQ\otimes_{Q}\overline{V}).

It satisfies (i), because
for all
v
,
w\in V

\sigma_{h_{s}}\bigl(\Phi_{h_{s}}(u\otimes\overline{v})\bigr)\circ\Phi_{h_{s}}(u\otimes\overline{w})=-\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\otimes\overline{u})\circ\Phi_{h_{s}}(u\otimes\overline{w})=-\Phi_{h_{s}}(v\,h_{s}(u,u)\otimes\overline{w})

and
h_{s}(u,u)=s(u,u)-\overline{s(u,u)}=0
.

To prove (ii), we need to show that
T_{s}
vanishes on

(uQ\otimes_{Q}\overline{V})\cap\Skew(\sw)
since
f_{[s]}\circ\Phi_{h_{s}}=T_{s}
by definition of
f_{[s]}
.
We first observe that
(uQ\otimes_{Q}\overline{V})\cap\Skew(\sw)
has
a nice description.
Since
\widehat{h}_{s}
is an isomorphism of right
Q
-modules, it
follows that for all
x\in Q
the equations
ux=0
and

\widehat{h}_{s}(u)x=0
are equivalent. Therefore, the right ideal

A(u)=\{x\in Q\mid ux=0\}
can be alternatively described as

A(u)=\{x\in Q\mid\overline{x}h_{s}(u,w)=0\text{ for all $w\in V$}\},

which means that the left ideal
\overline{A(u)}
image of
A(u)

under the canonical map
\overline{\rule{7.11317pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{4.0pt}}
is the annihilator of the right ideal

h_{s}(u,V)\subset Q
:

\overline{A(u)}=\{y\in Q\mid yh_{s}(u,V)=0\}.

By
[
8
, (1.14)]
, it follows that
h_{s}(u,V)
is the annihilator
of the left ideal
\overline{A(u)}
:

h_{s}(u,V)=\{x\in Q\mid yx=0\text{ for all $y\in\overline{A(u)}$}\}.

If
e\in Q
is an idempotent such that
A(u)=eQ
, then
y(1-\overline{e})=0
for all
y\in\overline{A(u)}
, hence there exists
w\in V
such
that
h_{s}(u,w)=1-\overline{e}
.

Now, let
v\in V
be such that
u\otimes\overline{v}=-v\otimes\overline{u}
, so that
u\otimes\overline{v}\in(uQ\otimes_{Q}\overline{V})\cap\Skew(\sw)
.
Using
\Phi_{h_{s}}
, we see that this equation implies
that
uh_{s}(v,w)=-vh_{s}(u,w)
, hence
v(1-\overline{e})=-uh_{s}(v,w)
. But since
ue=0
,

v(1-\overline{e})\otimes\overline{u}=v\otimes\overline{u}-v\otimes\overline{ue}=v\otimes\overline{u}.

Therefore,
v\otimes\overline{u}=-uh_{s}(v,w)\otimes\overline{u}
. We
have thus proved

(uQ\otimes_{Q}\overline{V})\cap\Skew(\sw)\subset\{ux\otimes\overline{u}\mid x\in Q\}.

Note that
u=u(1-e)
since
ue=0
, hence

\{ux\otimes\overline{u}\mid x\in Q\}=\{u(1-e)x(1-\overline{e})\otimes\overline{u}\mid x\in Q\}=\{ux\otimes\overline{u}\mid x\in(1-e)Q(1-\overline{e})\}.

Moreover,
ux\otimes\overline{u}=0
implies
uxh_{s}(u,w)=0
, i.e.,

ux(1-\overline{e})=0
. It follows that
ux=0
if
x\in(1-e)Q(1-\overline{e})
. But then
x\in A(u)=eQ
, hence
x=0
if
x\in(1-e)Q(1-\overline{e})
. Thus, every element in
\{ux\otimes\overline{e}\mid x\in Q\}
has a unique expression of the form
ux\otimes\overline{u}
with
x\in(1-e)Q(1-\overline{e})
. Therefore,

(uQ\otimes_{Q}\overline{V})\cap\Skew(\sw)=\{ux\otimes\overline{u}\mid x\in(1-e)Q(1-\overline{e})\text{ and }\overline{x}=-x\}.

Since
u
is isotropic, we have
s(u,u)\in k
, hence for all
x\in Q

such that
\overline{x}=-x
,

T_{s}(ux\otimes\overline{u})=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(s(u,ux))=s(u,u)\Trd_{Q}(x)=0.

Therefore,
T_{s}
vanishes on
(uQ\otimes_{Q}\overline{V})\cap\Skew(\sw)
,
which means that
\Hom_{Q}(V,uQ)
satisfies condition (ii) and completes
the proof that it is an isotropic ideal, as required.
∎

is split, and use the

and

.
In particular,

is the isomorphism

of (

) and

is the ordinary quadratic form
on

associated to
[s]
.

Proposition 1.5
.

The canonical isomorphism
\Theta
carries the quadratic pair

(\sigma_{h_{s}},f_{[s]})
attached to
[s]
on
\End_{Q}(V)
to the
quadratic pair
(\sigma_{b_{a*s}},f_{q_{a*s}})
attached to

q_{a*s}
on
\End_{k}(V\otimes_{Q}I)
.

Proof.

The adjoint involution
\sigma_{h_{s}}
is defined by the equation

h_{s}(\rho(v),w)=h_{s}\bigl(v,\sigma_{h_{s}}(\rho)(w)\bigr)\qquad\text{for $\rho\in\End_{Q}(V)$ and $v$, $w\in V$.}

Since
b_{a*s}=a*h_{s}
, this equation implies that for

\rho\in\End_{Q}(V)
,
v
,
w\in V
and
\xi
,
\eta\in I
,

b_{a*s}(\rho(v)\otimes\xi,w\otimes\eta)=a(\xi,\,h_{s}(\rho(v),w)\eta)=a(\xi,\,h_{s}(v,\sigma_{h_{s}}(\rho)(w))\eta)=b_{a*s}(v\otimes\xi,\sigma_{h_{s}}(\rho)(w)\otimes\eta).

Therefore,
\sigma_{b_{a*s}}\circ\Theta=\Theta\circ\sigma_{h_{s}}
,
hence
\Theta
maps
\Sym(\sigma_{h_{s}})
to

\Sym(\sigma_{b_{a*s}})
. It remains to show that

for
\rho\in\Sym(\sigma_{h_{s}})
.

Recall that
\Sym(\sigma_{b_{a*s}})
is spanned by the elements

\Phi_{b_{a*s}}\bigl((v\otimes\xi)\otimes(v\otimes\xi)\bigr)
,
with

and
\xi\in I
. Moreover,

maps such an element to

.
Therefore, by the commutative diagram given in Lemma

,
we need to prove that for

and

Since

, letting

, we have by definition of

f_{[s]}

f_{[s]}\bigl((\Phi_{h_{s}}\circ\Theta^{\prime})(v\otimes\xi,v\otimes\xi)\bigr)=T_{s}(v\,x\otimes\overline{v})=\Trd_{Q}\bigl(s(v,v\,x)\bigr).

Since

, the equation

\Trd_{Q}\bigl(s(v,v\,x)\bigr)=a(\xi,s(v,v)\xi)
follows
from (

).
∎

Remark

2.
Generic splitting of quaternion algebras and the degree map

Throughout this section,
Q
is a quaternion division algebra
over an arbitrary field
k
. We fix a separable quadratic extension

K
of
k
in
Q
and write
Q
as a crossed product

Q=(K,\iota,b)=K\oplus Kj

where
\iota
is the nontrivial
k
-automorphism of
K
and
j\in Q

satisfies

j^{2}=b\in k^{\times}\qquad\text{and}\qquad jx=\iota(x)j\quad\text{for
$x\in K$.}

Let
t
be an indeterminate over
K
. Extend
\iota
to an
automorphism of the rational function field
K(t)
by setting

\iota(t)=bt^{-1}.

The function field
F
of the Severi–Brauer conic of
Q
is a
generic splitting field of
Q
; recall from
[
12
, Remark A.8]
that
it can be viewed as the subfield of
K(t)
fixed under
\iota
:

F=K(t)^{\iota}.

Every
k
-base
(1,\ell)
of
K
is also an
F
-base of
K(t)
,
since every
f\in K(t)
can be written as

(10)

\ f=f_{0}+\ell f_{1}\quad\text{with}\quad f_{0}=\frac{\iota(\ell)f-\ell\iota(f)}{\iota(\ell)-\ell}\in F\quad\text{and}\quad f_{1}=\frac{\iota(f)-f}{\iota(\ell)-\ell}\in F.

Therefore,
K\otimes_{k}F=K(t)
, and the inclusion
F\subset K(t)

yields an identification

Q\otimes_{k}F=K(t)\oplus K(t)j=(K(t),\iota,b).

We write
Q_{F}
for the
F
-algebra
Q\otimes_{k}F
.
It is split, as expected, since
j^{2}=b=\iota(t)t
. Note that
t
does not belong to
F
, hence it is not a central element in
Q_{F}
.
Consider

\varepsilon=1+jt^{-1}=1+b^{-1}tj\in Q_{F}\qquad\text{and}\qquad I=Q_{F}\cdot\varepsilon\subset Q_{F}.

The following equations will be used repeatedly:

(11)

j\varepsilon=bt^{-1}+j=bt^{-1}\varepsilon=\iota(t)\varepsilon.

Proposition 2.1
.

The left ideal
I
in
Q_{F}
satisfies
I=K(t)\varepsilon=K\otimes_{k}F\varepsilon
, and the expression of an element in
I

as
f\varepsilon
with
f\in K(t)
is unique. Moreover, identifying
each
x\in Q_{F}
with multiplication on the left by
x
yields
an identification
Q_{F}=\End_{F}(I)
.

Proof.

The equations (
11
) show that
K(t)j\varepsilon=K(t)\varepsilon
, hence

I=K(t)\varepsilon
. Moreover,
K(t)=K\otimes_{k}F
, hence

K(t)\varepsilon=K\otimes_{k}F\varepsilon
. Multiplication by

\varepsilon
is an injective map
K(t)\to I

since
K(t)
is a field, hence every element in
I
has a unique
expression as
f\varepsilon
with
f\in K(t)
, and it follows that

\dim_{F}I=2
. The left
Q_{F}
-module structure on
I
yields a
map
Q_{F}\to\End_{F}(I)
, which is injective because
Q_{F}
is a
simple ring, hence surjective by dimension count.
∎

In preparation for the Morita equivalence between generalized
quadratic forms over
Q_{F}
and ordinary quadratic forms over
F
in
the next section, we now define a nonzero alternating form on
I
.
We use the unique expression of elements of
I
as
f\varepsilon
with

f\in K(t)
, and the choice of an element
u\in K^{\times}
such that

\iota(u)=-u
. Note that
u
is
uniquely determined up to a factor in
k^{\times}
. If
\charac k=2
we may take
u=1
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The map
a\colon I\times I\to F
defined by

a(f\varepsilon,g\varepsilon)=u(\iota(f)g-f\iota(g))\qquad\text{for $f$, $g\in K(t)$}

is a nonzero alternating bilinear form. For
x\in Q
and
\xi\in I
,
the equation

a(\xi,x\xi)=0
holds if and only if
x\in k
or
\xi=0
.

Proof.

It is clear from the definition that
a
is an alternating bilinear
form. If
(1,\ell)
is a
k
-base of
K
, then
a(\varepsilon,\ell\varepsilon)=u(\ell-\iota(\ell))\neq 0
, hence
a
is
nonzero.

If
\xi=0
or
x\in k
, then
a(\xi,x\xi)=0
because
a
is

k
-linear and alternating.
Now, suppose
\xi\in I
is nonzero, and write
\xi=f\varepsilon

with
f\in K(t)^{\times}
. For
x=x_{0}+x_{1}j
, with
x_{0},x_{1}\in K
,
using (
11
) we obtain
x\xi=(x_{0}f+x_{1}\iota(ft))\varepsilon
, and the definition of
a

yields

\displaystyle a(\xi,x\xi)

\displaystyle=u\bigl(\iota(f)(x_{0}f+x_{1}\iota(ft))-f\iota(x_{0}f+x_{1}\iota(ft))\bigr)

\displaystyle=uf\iota(f)(x_{0}-\iota(x_{0})+x_{1}\iota(ft)f^{-1}-\iota(x_{1})ft\iota(f)^{-1}).

Therefore, letting
g=\iota(ft)f^{-1}\in K(t)^{\times}
, the equation

a(\xi,x\xi)=0
implies

\bigl(x_{0}-\iota(x_{0})\bigr)+x_{1}g-\iota(x_{1}g)=0.

Note that
g\iota(g)=b
, hence
g\notin K
because
Q
is a division
algebra. It follows that there exists a valuation
v
on
K(t)
,
trivial on
K
, such that
v(g)>0
. Then
v\bigl(\iota(g)\bigr)<0

since
g\iota(g)=b
and
v(b)=0
, hence
the three terms on the left side of the last displayed equation
have different valuations if they are nonzero. Their sum vanishes
only if they are all zero, which means that
x_{0}\in k
and
x_{1}=0
.
∎

The main tool for the proof of the main theorem is a degree map on a
subring of
Q_{F}
, which we now introduce. Consider the subring

\mathcal{F}
of
K[t,t^{-1}]
fixed under
\iota
,

\mathcal{F}=K[t,t^{-1}]^{\iota}\subset F,\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q_{\mathcal{F}}=Q\otimes_{k}\mathcal{F}\subset Q_{F}.

Proposition 2.3
.

The field of fractions of
\mathcal{F}
is
F
, and

K\otimes_{k}\mathcal{F}=K[t,t^{-1}],\qquad Q_{\mathcal{F}}=K[t,t^{-1}]\oplus K[t,t^{-1}]j.

Every element in
Q_{\mathcal{F}}
has a unique expression of the form

\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{z}t^{z}
with
x_{z}\in Q
for all
z\in\mathbb{Z}
and

x_{z}\neq 0
for only a finite number of
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Since
K(t)
is the field of fractions of
K[t,t^{-1}]
,
every element in
F
can be written as
gh^{-1}=g\iota(h)\cdot(h\iota(h))^{-1}
with
g
,
h\in K[t,t^{-1}]
and
g\iota(h)
,
h\iota(h)\in\mathcal{F}
. Hence
F

is the field of fractions of
\mathcal{F}
. Using (
10
), we
see that
K\otimes_{k}\mathcal{F}=K[t,t^{-1}]
, so
Q_{\mathcal{F}}=K[t,t^{-1}]\oplus K[t,t^{-1}]j
. Since
tj=bjt^{-1}
, we have
K[t,t^{-1}]j=jK[t,t^{-1}]
, and it follows that every element in
Q_{\mathcal{F}}
can be
uniquely written in the stated form.
∎

The proposition readily shows that
\varepsilon\in Q_{\mathcal{F}}
. We may
therefore consider the left ideal in
Q_{\mathcal{F}}
generated by

\varepsilon
,

\mathcal{I}=Q_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\varepsilon\subset Q_{\mathcal{F}}.

Using Proposition
2.3
and (
11
), which shows
that
j\varepsilon\in K[t,t^{-1}]\varepsilon
, we see that

\mathcal{I}=K[t,t^{-1}]\varepsilon=K\otimes_{k}\mathcal{F}\varepsilon.

A degree map on
Q_{\mathcal{F}}
is defined from the expression of each

\mathfrak{X}\in Q_{\mathcal{F}}
as

\mathfrak{X}=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{z}t^{z}

with
x_{z}\in Q
for all
z\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
x_{z}\neq 0
for only a finite
number of
z\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
\mathfrak{X}\neq 0
, we define its degree
in
\mathbb{N}

by comparing the absolute values of the exponents:

\deg(\mathfrak{X})=\max\{\lvert z\rvert\mid x_{z}\neq 0\}.

We also let
\deg(0)=-\infty
, with the usual convention that

-\infty<z
for all
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark
.

The restriction of the degree to
K[t,t^{-1}]
is related to the

t
-adic and the
t^{-1}
-adic valuations on
K(t)
by

\deg(f)=-\min\{v_{t}(f),v_{t^{-1}}(f)\}=-\min\{v_{t}(f),\,v_{t}\bigl(\iota(f)\bigr)\}\qquad\text{for $f\in K[t,t^{-1}]$.}

The valuations
v_{t}
and
v_{t^{-1}}
restrict to the same valuation

v_{\infty}
on
F
; they are the extensions of
v_{\infty}
to

K(t)
, see
[
12
, Remark A.8]
. (This result is not used in the
sequel.)

The degree map induces a filtration of
\mathcal{I}
, for which the next proposition provides two equivalent descriptions.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}=\{\xi\in\mathcal{I}\mid\deg(\xi)\leq d\}
. Then

\mathcal{I}_{\leq 0}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}=\Bigl\{\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}x_{i}t^{i}\varepsilon\mid x_{i}\in Q\Bigr\}=\Bigl\{\sum_{z=-d}^{d-1}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon\mid y_{z}\in K\Bigr\}\qquad\text{for $d\geq 1$.}

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{I}=K[t,t^{-1}]\varepsilon
, every element in
\mathcal{I}
has
the
form
\sum_{z=-m}^{n}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon
for some
y_{-m}
, …,

y_{n}\in K
and
m
,
n\geq 0
.
If
y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon\neq 0
, then expanding
\varepsilon
and using

t^{z}j=j\iota(t^{z})=jb^{z}t^{-z}
,
we find

y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon=y_{z}t^{z}+y_{z}t^{z}jt^{-1}=y_{z}t^{z}+b^{z}y_{z}jt^{-z-1},

hence

\deg(y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon)=\max\{\lvert z\rvert,\;\lvert-z-1\rvert\}=\begin{cases}z+1&\text{if $z\geq 0$,}\\
-z&\text{if $z<0$.}\end{cases}

This shows that
\sum_{z=-d}^{d-1}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon\in\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}
for
y_{-d}
, …,
y_{d-1}\in K
.

Now, suppose
y_{-m}
, …,
y_{n}\in K
for some
m
,
n\geq 0
,
and

\deg\bigl(\sum_{z=-m}^{n}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon\bigr)\leq d.

Since the degree of a sum of terms of different degrees is the
maximum of the degrees of the terms, we must have

(y_{n}t^{n}+y_{-n-1}t^{-n-1})\varepsilon=0
if
n\geq d
, hence
y_{n}=y_{-n-1}=0
because by
Proposition
2.1
the expression of an element in
I
as

f\varepsilon
with
f\in K(t)
is unique. Therefore,
n\leq d-1

if
y_{n}\neq 0
and
m\leq d

if
y_{-m}\neq 0
. Hence

\mathcal{I}_{\leq 0}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}=\Bigl\{\sum_{z=-d}^{d-1}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon\mid y_{z}\in K\Bigr\}.

To complete the proof, observe that
jt^{-z-1}\varepsilon=b^{-z-1}t^{z+1}j\varepsilon=b^{-z}t^{z}\varepsilon
for all

z\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, for
y_{z}\in K
,

y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon=b^{z}y_{z}jt^{-z-1}\varepsilon.

Using the substitution
i=-z-1
, it follows that
\sum_{z=-d}^{-1}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}b^{-i-1}y_{-i-1}jt^{i}\varepsilon
, hence

(12)

\sum_{z=-d}^{d-1}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(y_{i}+b^{-i-1}y_{-i-1}j)t^{i}\varepsilon.

On the other hand, for
x_{0}
, …,
x_{d-1}\in Q
we may find

y_{-d}
, …,
y_{d-1}\in K
such that
x_{i}=y_{i}+b^{-i-1}y_{-i-1}j

for
i=0
, …,
d-1
; then (
12
) yields

\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}x_{i}t^{i}\varepsilon=\sum_{z=-d}^{d-1}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon.

Therefore,
\bigl\{\sum_{z=-d}^{d-1}y_{z}t^{z}\varepsilon\mid y_{z}\in K\bigr\}=\bigl\{\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}x_{i}t^{i}\varepsilon\mid x_{i}\in Q\bigr\}

and the proof is complete.
∎

The following degree computation related to the alternating form
a

of Proposition

is needed in Lemma

below.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\xi
,
\eta\in\mathcal{I}
, we have

. Moreover,
for
d
,

and

,

\deg a(t^{d}\varepsilon,xt^{e}\varepsilon)\leq d+e+1.

If
\deg a(t^{d}\varepsilon,xt^{e}\varepsilon)<d+e+1
, then

.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{I}=K\otimes_{k}\mathcal{F}\varepsilon
, it is clear from the

for
\xi
,
\eta\in\mathcal{I}
.

Let

with
x_{0}
,

. Using (

),
we obtain

xt^{e}\varepsilon=(x_{0}t^{e}+x_{1}b^{e+1}t^{-e-1})\varepsilon,

hence the definition of

yields

\displaystyle a(t^{d}\varepsilon,xt^{e}\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=u\bigl(\iota(t^{d})(x_{0}t^{e}+x_{1}b^{e+1}t^{-e-1})-t^{d}\iota(x_{0}t^{e}+x_{1}b^{e+1}t^{-e-1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=u\bigl(x_{0}b^{d}t^{e-d}-\iota(x_{0})b^{e}t^{d-e}+x_{1}b^{d+e+1}t^{-d-e-1}-\iota(x_{1})t^{d+e+1}\bigr).

Since

,

. Moreover, the

.
∎

3.
Proof of the theorem

The argument is given at the end of this section. The core of the
proof lies in Lemma
3.3
, which is a crucial tool to reduce
the degree of a given isotropic vector. We start with some technical
preparation, which mostly consists of some degree computations.

Throughout this section we fix a generalized quadratic space

(V,[s])
over
Q
and an element

such that

\iota(u)=-u
, which yields a nonzero alternating bilinear form

as in Proposition
2.2
. After

, the form

is used to define a quadratic
form

on the
F
-vector space

by the Morita
equivalence of §
1.2
. We write simply

for

and

for its polar form:

q\colon V\otimes_{Q}I\to F,\qquad q(v\otimes\xi)=a(\xi,s(v,v)\xi)\qquad\text{for $v\in V$ and $\xi\in I$,}

and, for
v
,

and
\xi
,

,

Proposition
1.1
shows that

is isotropic if and only
if the generalized quadratic form on the

-module

obtained from
[s]
by scalar extension from

to

is
isotropic. Our goal is to show that
[s]
is isotropic if

is
isotropic.

Since
F
is the field of fractions of
\mathcal{F}
by

2.3
, it follows that

, hence for every

there exists
\lambda\in F^{\times}
such that
v\lambda\in V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}
. As

q(v\lambda)=0
if and only if
q(v)=0
, it suffices to consider
isotropic vectors in
V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}
. Note that the restrictions of

and

to

take values in
\mathcal{F}
, because

: see Lemma

. The degree
filtration on

yields a filtration on

: each

lies in

for
some

.

Lemma 3.1
.

For

,

V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}=\Bigl\{\sum_{i=0}^{d}v_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon\mid v_{0},\,\ldots,\,v_{d}\in V\Bigr\}.

Let

,

. If

v=(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)+v^{\prime}\in V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad w=(w_{e}\otimes t^{e}\varepsilon)+w^{\prime}\in V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq e+1}

with

,

and

,

, then

\deg b_{q}(v,w)\leq d+e+1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\deg q(v)\leq 2d+1.

Moreover, if
\deg b_{q}(v,w)<d+e+1
, then

. If
\deg q(v)<2d+1
, then

.

Proof.

If
(e_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
is a
Q
-base of
V
, vectors in

can be written as
\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\otimes\xi_{j}
with
\xi_{1}
, …,
\xi_{n}\in\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}
.
Proposition
2.4
shows that every
\xi_{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}
has an expression
\xi_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}x_{ji}t^{i}\varepsilon
for some

. Then

\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\otimes\xi_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}x_{ji}\bigr)\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon,

hence every vector in
V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}
can be
written in the form

\sum_{i=0}^{d}v_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon

for some
v_{0}
, …,
v_{d}\in V
.

Now, let
v\in V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}
and

be as in the statement.
We first prove the part concerning
b_{q}(v,w)
, by induction on

. For this, we expand

(13)

b_{q}(v,w)=b_{q}(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon,w_{e}\otimes t^{e}\varepsilon)+b_{q}(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon,w^{\prime})+b_{q}(v^{\prime},w_{e}\otimes t^{e}\varepsilon)+b_{q}(v^{\prime},w^{\prime}).

The first term on the right side is

b_{q}(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon,w_{e}\otimes t^{e}\varepsilon)=a(t^{d}\varepsilon,h_{s}(v_{d},w_{e})t^{e}\varepsilon).

Lemma
2.5
shows that
the degree of this term is at most
d+e+1
, and that
h_{s}(v_{d},w_{e})\in K

when its degree is strictly less than
d+e+1
. The last three terms
on the right side
of (
13
) vanish if
d=e=0
, for then
v^{\prime}=w^{\prime}=0
, and if

d+e\geq 1
their degree
is at most
d+e
by the induction hypothesis. The part of the
lemma related to
b_{q}(v,w)
is thus proved.

The other part is proved similarly, by induction on
d
: we have

(14)

q(v)=q(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)+b_{q}(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon,v^{\prime})+q(v^{\prime}).

The first term on the right is

q(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)=a(t^{d}\varepsilon,s(v_{d},v_{d})t^{d}\varepsilon).

Its degree is at most
2d+1
by
Lemma
2.5
; and if it is strictly less than

2d+1
, then
s(v_{d},v_{d})\in K
. The other terms on the right
side of (
14
) vanish if

d=0
. If
d\geq 1
, the degree of the second term is at most
2d

by the first part of the proof, and the degree of the third term is
at most
2d-1
by the induction hypothesis. Therefore,

\deg q(v)\leq 2d+1
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
v=(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)+v^{\prime}\in V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}
, with
v_{d}\in V
and
v^{\prime}\in V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}
, be an isotropic vector of
q
.
If

s(v_{d},v_{d})\notin k
, then
h_{s}(v_{d},v_{d})\neq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose
v_{d}\neq 0
and
h_{s}(v_{d},v_{d})=0
. We have to show
that
s(v_{d},v_{d})\in k
. By definition,
h_{s}(v_{d},v_{d})=s(v_{d},v_{d})-\overline{s(v_{d},v_{d})}
, hence

s(v_{d},v_{d})=\overline{s(v_{d},v_{d})}
. If
s(v_{d},v_{d})\notin k
,
then
s(v_{d},v_{d})\notin K
since
k=\{x\in K\mid\overline{x}=x\}
. Lemma
3.1
then yields

\deg q(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)=2d+1.

Now, from
q(v)=0
it follows that

q(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)=-b_{q}(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon,v^{\prime})-q(v^{\prime}),

but Lemma
3.1
yields
\deg b_{q}(v_{d}\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon,v^{\prime})\leq 2d
and
\deg q(v^{\prime})\leq 2d-1
, so this equation is impossible.
∎

The degree reduction argument in the proof of the theorem
relies on the following key result:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
v\in V
and
\xi\in\mathcal{I}
be such that
\deg(\xi)=d+1\geq 2
. If

\deg q(v\otimes\xi)\leq 2d-1
, then

q(v\otimes\xi)=q(v\otimes\eta)
for some

\eta\in\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}
.

Proof.

If
q(v\otimes\xi)=0
, the lemma holds with
\eta=0
. For the rest
of the proof, we assume
q(v\otimes\xi)\neq 0
.
By Proposition
2.4
we may write

\xi=(x_{d}t^{d}+\cdots+x_{0})\varepsilon
with
x_{d}
,
…,
x_{0}\in Q
and
x_{d}\neq 0
. Substituting
vx_{d}
for
v
,
we may (and will) assume
x_{d}=1
. Lemma
3.1
then yields

s(v,v)\in K
. If
s(v,v)\in k
, then

q(v\otimes\xi)=a(\xi,s(v,v)\xi)=s(v,v)a(\xi,\xi)=0.

This case is excluded since

q(v\otimes\xi)\neq 0
, hence
s(v,v)\in K\setminus k
.

By Proposition
2.4
we
have

(x_{d-2}t^{d-2}+\cdots+x_{0})\varepsilon=(y_{d-2}t^{d-2}+\cdots+y_{-d+1}t^{-d+1})\varepsilon

for some
y_{d-2}
, …,
y_{-d+1}\in K
. We prove below:

Claim:

x_{d-1}
lies in
K
.

Assuming the claim, we complete the proof as follows: we write

\xi=z\varepsilon\qquad\text{with}\qquad z=t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1}+y_{d-2}t^{d-2}+\cdots+y_{-d+1}t^{-d+1}\in K[t,t^{-1}].

Since
s(v,v)\in K
, we get, by definition of
q
and
a
,

(15)

q(v\otimes\xi)=a(\xi,s(v,v)\xi)=u\bigl(\iota(z)s(v,v)z-z\iota(s(v,v)z)\bigr)=uz\iota(z)\bigl(s(v,v)-\iota(s(v,v))\bigr).

The rightmost expression does not change when we change
z
into

\iota(z)
,
hence
q(v\otimes\xi)=q(v\otimes\iota(z)\varepsilon)
.
The lemma will follow because

\iota(z)\varepsilon=(b^{d}t^{-d}+b^{d-1}\iota(x_{d-1})t^{1-d}+b^{d-2}\iota(y_{d-2})t^{2-d}+\cdots+b^{-d+1}\iota(y_{-d+1})t^{d-1})\varepsilon\in\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}.

To prove the claim,
let
\xi_{0}=(x_{d-2}t^{d-2}+\cdots+x_{0})\varepsilon\in\mathcal{I}_{\leq d-1}
, so
\xi=(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1})\varepsilon+\xi_{0}
and

q(v\otimes\xi)=q(v\otimes(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1})\varepsilon)+b_{q}(v\otimes(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1})\varepsilon,v\otimes\xi_{0})+q(v\otimes\xi_{0}).

Lemma
3.1
shows that the middle
term on the right side has degree at most
2d-1
and the rightmost
term has degree at most
2d-3
. Therefore, the hypothesis that
\deg q(v\otimes\xi)\leq 2d-1
implies

(16)

\deg q(v\otimes(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1})\varepsilon)\leq 2d-1.

Now, we have

q(v\otimes(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1})\varepsilon)=q(v\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)+b_{q}(v\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon,v\otimes x_{d-1}t^{d-1}\varepsilon)+q(v\otimes x_{d-1}t^{d-1}\varepsilon)

and Lemma
3.1
shows that the rightmost term has degree at
most
2d-1
. Moreover, since
s(v,v)\in K\setminus k
, the same computation as for (
15
) yields

q(v\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)=ub^{d}\bigl(s(v,v)-\iota(s(v,v))\bigr)\in K^{\times},

hence
\deg q(v\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon)=0
. Therefore,
(
16
) implies

\deg b_{q}(v\otimes t^{d}\varepsilon,v\otimes x_{d-1}t^{d-1}\varepsilon)\leq 2d-1,\qquad\text{i.e.,}\quad\deg a(t^{d}\varepsilon,h_{s}(v,vx_{d-1})t^{d-1}\varepsilon)\leq 2d-1,

hence
h_{s}(v,\,vx_{d-1})\in K
by
Lemma
3.1
. But

h_{s}(v,\,vx_{d-1})=h_{s}(v,\,v)x_{d-1}=\bigl(s(v,v)-\overline{s(v,v)}\bigr)x_{d-1}

and
s(v,v)-\overline{s(v,v)}\in K^{\times}
because
s(v,v)\in K\setminus k
, hence
x_{d-1}\in K
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of the theorem.

Suppose
q
is isotropic. As observed at the beginning of this
section, by clearing denominators we may find an isotropic vector in

V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}
. We will
show that the minimal
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that

V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}

contains a
q
-isotropic vector is
0
; it will quickly follow
that
s(v,v)\in k
for some nonzero
v\in V
, which means that
[s]

is isotropic and proves the theorem.

Suppose that
V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d+1}
contains an isotropic
vector
for some
d\geq 1
. This vector has the form

\sum_{i=0}^{d}v_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon
for some
v_{0}
, …,

v_{d}\in V
. It is already in
V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}
if
v_{d}=0
, so we need to consider only the case where

v_{d}\neq 0
. We may also assume
s(v_{d},v_{d})\notin k
,
otherwise we readily get the
q
-isotropic vector

v_{d}\otimes\varepsilon\in V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq 1}
.
Now, Corollary
3.2
shows that
h_{s}(v_{d},v_{d})\neq 0
.
We may then project each
v_{i}
on
v_{d}
: letting

x_{i}=h_{s}(v_{d},v_{d})^{-1}h(v_{d},v_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad v^{\prime}_{i}=v_{i}-v_{d}x_{i}\quad\text{for $i=0$, \ldots, $d-1$,}

we get
h_{s}(v_{d},v^{\prime}_{i})=0
for
i=0
, …,
d-1
, which implies
that
b_{q}(v_{d}\otimes\xi,v^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\xi^{\prime})=0
for all
\xi
,

\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
. Rewrite the
given
q
-isotropic vector

(17)

\sum_{i=0}^{d}v_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon=v_{d}\otimes(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1}+\cdots+x_{0})\varepsilon+\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}v^{\prime}_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon\Bigr).

Since this vector is isotropic and
h_{s}(v_{d},v^{\prime}_{i})=0
for all
i
,
it follows that

q(v_{d}\otimes(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1}+\cdots+x_{0})\varepsilon)=-q\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}v^{\prime}_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon\Bigr).

Lemma
3.1
shows that the right side has degree

\leq 2d-1
, hence Lemma
3.3
yields
\eta\in\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}
such that

q(v_{d}\otimes\eta)=q(v_{d}\otimes(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1}+\cdots+x_{0})\varepsilon).

Then

q\Bigl((v_{d}\otimes\eta)+\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}v^{\prime}_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon\Bigl)=0\qquad\text{with}\qquad(v_{d}\otimes\eta)+\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}v^{\prime}_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon\in V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}.

It remains to prove that this vector in nonzero, or equivalently, in view of (
17
), that

\sum_{i=0}^{d}v_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon\not=v_{d}\otimes\xi\qquad\text{where}\qquad\xi=(t^{d}+x_{d-1}t^{d-1}+\cdots+x_{0})\varepsilon-\eta.

To prove this, we use Proposition
2.2
. Since

\eta\in\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}
, the element
\xi
is nonzero. Moreover, we
have
s(v_{d},v_{d})\not\in k
. Therefore,

a(\xi,s(v_{d},v_{d})\xi)=q(v_{d}\otimes\xi)\not=0
, and it follows

v_{d}\otimes\xi
is not equal to the isotropic vector

\sum_{i=0}^{d}v_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon
.

Therefore,
(v_{d}\otimes\eta)+\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}v^{\prime}_{i}\otimes t^{i}\varepsilon
is an isotropic vector
in
V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq d}
,
and we may repeat the argument if
d\geq 2
until we find a

q
-isotropic vector in
V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq 1}
.

We have thus shown that
V\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{I}_{\leq 1}
contains a

q
-isotropic vector. This vector has the form

v\otimes\varepsilon
for some nonzero
v\in V
, and

a(\varepsilon,\,s(v,v)\varepsilon)=q(v\otimes\varepsilon)=0
.
By Proposition
2.2
, this implies that
s(v,v)\in k
,
hence
[s]
is isotropic.
∎
