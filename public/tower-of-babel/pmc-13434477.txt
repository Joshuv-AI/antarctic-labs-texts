Large and regional-scale processes influencing Heat Waves: Insights from Qatar
Authors: Buri Vinodhkumar, Sripathi Gollapalli, U. N. Athira, Safi Ullah, Krishna Kishore Osuri, M. Rami Alfarra, Tareq Al-Ansari, Sami G. Al-Ghamdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Qatar faces rising temperatures and more frequent extreme heat waves (HWs), highlighting its vulnerability to climate change. This study presents a comprehensive assessment of HW characteristics over Qatar, associated mechanisms, and land–atmosphere feedback during 2003–2024. HWs are identified using the 95th percentile of daily maximum temperature lasting at least three consecutive days. A total of 32 HW events are detected at Al Udeid station, mostly between May and September, with a gradual increase of 0.1 events per year. HWs occur under cloud-free conditions (OLR anomalies 0–8 W/m²) and weak surface pressure anomalies (~–1.25 hPa) relative to the 2003–2024 climatology, forming a strong vertically coupled heat dome from the surface to 900 hPa. This structure is sustained by upper-level subsidence, a mid-tropospheric inversion, and weak surface updrafts. Land–atmosphere interactions further intensify HWs through enhanced sensible heat flux, reduced latent heat flux, and low soil moisture, limiting evaporative cooling. Suppressed moisture transport, dry boundary-layer conditions, and widespread subsidence create a thermally stratified and dynamically stable atmosphere, reinforced by anticyclonic vorticity anomalies. These insights enhance understanding of extreme heat dynamics in arid regions and offer valuable inputs for improving HW prediction and climate resilience strategies in Qatar, specifically, and the Arabian Peninsula in general.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54666-y.

Introduction

Heat waves (HWs) are among the most hazardous climate extremes and have increased in frequency, intensity, and duration due to human-induced climate change1,2. Their impacts are particularly severe in the hot and arid Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, where further intensification poses significant risks to human health3–5, agriculture6, the water and energy nexus7,8, and infrastructure, thereby affecting the socio-economic condition at regional and global scales9. The Arabian Peninsula has experienced pronounced warming in recent decades, with significant increases in the frequency and intensity of heat extremes10–15. A large number of mild HWs are observed all over Saudi Arabia, while extreme HWs are dominant in the northwestern region16. Moderate and severe HWs are observed less in both the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf coastal regions16. Summers over the Arabian Peninsula are the hottest globally17,18, and marked by extremely high daytime temperatures and warm nights11. A warming trend of ~ 0.5–0.6 °C per decade is observed in annual mean air temperatures across the Persian Gulf and Saudi Arabia19,20. Similarly, a noticeable rise in both the number and severity of hot days has been reported, with nighttime temperature extremes rising more rapidly than daytime extremes over Qatar21.

Recent extreme events highlight the severity of HWs in the region. For instance, in July 2016, parts of the Middle East recorded temperatures as high as 54 °C, among the highest observed in Asia22. Similarly, extreme heat events have significantly impacted infrastructure, including power system failures in Saudi Arabia during the summer of 2010 23. These regional events are consistent with global trends, such as the 2003 European HW, the 2010 Russian HW, and the 2015 South Asian HW, which resulted in substantial mortality and economic losses24–26. Despite these impacts, the physical drivers of HWs differ across regions. Midlatitude HWs (e.g., Europe and Russia) are typically associated with atmospheric blocking and Rossby wave amplification, whereas HWs over the Middle East are primarily governed by subtropical dynamics. In this region, large-scale circulation features such as the subtropical jet stream and the South Asian High act as waveguides for Rossby waves, modulating subsidence, atmospheric stability, and heat advection27–30. These processes play a crucial role in shaping the development and persistence of HWs over the Arabian Peninsula.

Qatar, located in the eastern Arabian Peninsula, represents a unique arid environment characterized by extremely low soil moisture and intense summer heating10,11,31. However, despite increasing evidence of rising temperature extremes, the relative roles of large-scale circulation and regional processes in driving HWs over Qatar remain insufficiently understood17,32,33. In this context, the present study aims to investigate the linkage between large-scale atmospheric circulation and regional processes in modulating heat-wave characteristics over Qatar. Specifically, we examine how large-scale dynamical features (e.g., upper-level circulation and subsidence) interact with regional thermodynamic conditions to influence the intensity, persistence, and structure of HWs. Using high-resolution European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) fifth-generation atmospheric reanalysis (ERA5) and ERA5-Land data, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of the multi-scale drivers of extreme heat, offering insights to improve the understanding and prediction of HWs in Qatar and similar arid regions.

Data and methods

Qatar is geographically small, and significant spatial temperature gradients can occur between inland and coastal regions34. The daily maximum temperature (Tmax) data of summer months (May to September) from 2003 to 2024 were obtained from two stations located at the Al Udeid Airport. The Al Udeid station (ID: QIR; 25.1167°N, 51.3167°E) and the Doha station (ID: OTBH; 25.1173°N, 51.315°E) are located very close to each other within the airport area. Al Udeid provides data from May 2003 to May 2016. From May 2016 onwards, we used the Doha station, which has continuous data up to September 2024. The Tmax time series was constructed by merging continuous observations from the Al Udeid and Doha stations; no homogenization or bias adjustment was applied. Both stations follow standardized meteorological observation protocols, and the measurements are considered highly consistent. These datasets were obtained from the IOWA State University, United States (https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu). Additionally, the Tmax, sensible heat flux (SHF), latent heat flux (LHF), soil moisture (SM), and soil temperature (ST) data at 0.1° × 0.1° resolution are obtained from the ERA5–Land, which is part of the fifth-generation ECMWF reanalysis products. The outgoing long wave radiation (OLR), mean sea level pressure (MSLP), geopotential height (GPH), U-wind, V-wind, specific humidity, temperature, vertical velocity, divergence, potential vorticity, and relative vorticity at 0.25° × 0.25° resolution and different levels are obtained from ERA5. The ERA5 data have been validated and widely used for various atmospheric studies over the Arabian Peninsula11,13,35. The World Meteorological Organization states that there is no uniform definition of HWs. Researchers are using different criteria to define HW. Here, we used a percentile-based threshold to define HW events. A HW event was defined as a period of at least 3 consecutive days during which the daily Tmax equals or exceeds the 95th percentile threshold calculated using the calendar-day. The percentile method is widely used to detect HWs across the globe, including the Arabian Peninsula15. Table 1 provides a comprehensive list of HW events observed in Al Udeid during the period 2003–2024, identified based on station Tmax data. A total of 32 distinct HW events were identified during the period 2003–2024. The duration of these events varied, ranging from 3 to 7 days.

Results and discussions

To examine the characteristics of Tmax during HW and non-HW days in Qatar during the summer months (May to September) of the study period, the probability density function (PDF) of daily Tmax is plotted (Fig. 1a). The results show notably higher Tmax values during HW days, ranging from approximately 46–49 °C, whereas non-HW days exhibit a distribution between 37 and 45 °C. This difference highlights the extremity of thermal conditions during HW events and the thermal anomaly associated with HWs. Moreover, Fig. 1b shows the frequency of HW events in Qatar during the summer months of the study period. The results suggest that HW events predominantly occur during the entire summer season, with a noticeable peak in July and September (Fig. 1b). This seasonal pattern reflects a late-summer intensification of HW activity in the study region.

Figure 1c illustrates the interannual variability of Tmax and the frequency of HW events during the period 2003–2024. The temporal pattern of Tmax shows an overall increasing trend of approximately + 0.028 °C per year, indicating a marginally significant (90%; p-value of 0.055) warming trend over the region. While year-to-year variations are present, a gradual intensification of summer Tmax is evident, particularly after 2014. Simultaneously, the frequency of HW events displays a statistically significant (95%; p-value of 0.015) increasing trend of about + 0.1 events per year. The later part of the study period, particularly 2014 and onward, is characterized by a significant increase in the frequency of HW occurrences. Notably, 2024 has emerged as the year with the maximum number of HW occurrences and the highest Tmax recorded over the entire study period, followed by the years 2012 and 2023. To objectively identify the dominant spatial patterns of temperature variability associated with HWs over Qatar, we applied Empirical Orthogonal Function (EOF) analysis. This method helps to segregate the major modes of variability from high-dimensional Tmax anomaly fields and clarifies whether HWs in Qatar are embedded within broader regional patterns across the Arabian Peninsula. The EOF analysis for daily Tmax anomalies over the Arabian Peninsula is performed and is presented in Fig. 2. The three leading EOF modes provide insight into the dominant modes of temperature variability in the region. The first EOF mode shows large variability (28.36%) covering the northern parts of the Arabian Peninsula, indicating a major dominant HW region (Fig. 2a). This suggests that HWs in the Arabian Peninsula predominantly happen with maximum intensity in the northern and central areas (Fig. 2a). The second mode (EOF2) depicts a dipole structure, which explains 24.61% of the total variance. This pattern indicates a second type of HW events occurred over southern regions, covering the United Arab Emirates, Yemen, and other countries (Fig. 2b). The third mode (EOF3) shows less variance of 8.99% with a more localized pattern, suggesting a more complex and localized temperature variability near Qatar and the central parts of Saudi Arabia (Fig. 2c).Fig. 2Empirical Orthogonal Function (EOF) analysis of maximum temperature anomalies for May to September over the Arabian Peninsula (a) first mode, (b) second mode, and (c) third mode. Surface features of HW conditions over the Arabian peninsula

Figure 3 displays the composite anomalies of land surface variables, such as SHF, LHF, ST, and SM during HWs across the Arabian Peninsula from 2003 to 2024. SHF shows a pronounced increase across much of the Arabian Peninsula, particularly over Qatar and surrounding regions. This increase in sensible heating (Fig. 3a) is often linked to warmer surface conditions and reduced evaporative cooling over the land. The negative LHF anomalies across most of the region also imply suppressed evaporative fluxes, which typically coincide with moisture-limited conditions; thus, the dominance of SHF in the surface energy balance due to excess surface heating (Fig. 3b).

The same can be seen in terms of the widespread positive anomalies of ST over central and northeastern parts of the Peninsula (Fig. 3c). The HW days have witnessed reduced SM (negative anomalies) over most parts of the study region (Fig. 3d). These elevated ST and reduced SM are consistent and can cause a prolonged heat exposure40. This dry surface reinforces surface warming through positive feedback, where reduced evaporation and increased SHF further increase ST and air temperatures. Figure 4 illustrates the composite anomalies of OLR and MSLP during HW episodes. Notably, enhanced positive OLR anomalies are evident over Qatar and much of the Arabian Peninsula (Fig. 4a), indicating reduced cloud cover and increased radiation at the surface. These features reflect suppressed convective activity, which is a common characteristic during HWs41. In contrast, negative OLR anomalies appear over the northern parts of the peninsula, suggesting local cloud presence.

Concurrently, negative MSLP anomalies are observed over the northern and eastern Arabian Peninsula, including Qatar (Fig. 4b), indicating the development of an anomalous thermal low associated with intense heating. While such thermal lows reflect strong surface warming, the dry and stable atmospheric conditions during HW events are more appropriately linked to large-scale subsidence associated with mid- and upper-tropospheric anticyclonic circulation41. Together, the presence of strong positive OLR anomalies and accompanying low-pressure conditions to an atmospheric setup favorable for extreme heat events. A recent study demonstrated a similar pattern of HW conditions, suggesting that HWs are associated with persistent dry conditions, high solar insolation, and strong subsidence under subtropical high-pressure systems41. These findings suggest that during these periods, the Arabian Peninsula, particularly Qatar, experienced conditions that supported the onset and persistence of intense HW events.Fig. 4Composite anomalies of outgoing longwave radiation (OLR) and mean sea level pressure (MSLP) during HW events over the period 2003–2024. Large-scale circulations associated with HWs

To understand the large-scale underlying drivers of these HWs over Qatar, we further analyzed large-scale atmospheric conditions, including both upper- and lower-level circulations. Figure 5 presents the composite anomalies of GPH, specific humidity, and winds at 200 hPa, 500 hPa, and 850 hPa levels during the Qatar HW days over the Arabian Peninsula. The anomalies exhibit a vertically coherent structure, highlighting the interplay between dynamical and thermo-dynamical processes that favor HW development. At the upper troposphere (200 hPa; Fig. 5a), negative GPH anomalies are observed over northern Saudi Arabia, Iraq, and the eastern Mediterranean, in contrast to positive anomalies over the southern Arabian Peninsula. This dipole pattern indicates a ridge–trough configuration, which enhances subsidence over Qatar and the surrounding regions17,27.

The associated wind vectors align with this pattern, showing divergence in regions of positive GPH anomalies (indicating outflow aloft) and convergence where negative anomalies prevail (indicating inflow toward the center), further supporting the subsidence and stable atmospheric conditions conducive to HW formation. The specific humidity anomalies (Fig. 5b) at this level are generally weak and slightly negative, consistent with dry upper-level conditions that inhibit cloud formation and support increased solar insolation, enhancing surface warming.

At the mid-troposphere (500 hPa), positive GPH anomalies dominate over Qatar and Saudi Arabia (Fig. 5c), indicating the presence of a strong and persistent mid-level anticyclone. The wind vectors at this level display a circulation pattern similar to that at 200 hPa, reinforcing the presence of a vertically aligned anticyclonic system. This mid-level structure further suppresses convective activity and vertical mixing, promoting sustained surface heating. This feature suppresses vertical ascent, limiting convective mixing and thereby supporting sustained surface heating. This level appears to be drier over the study region (Fig. 5d). Conversely, the central Arabian Peninsula shows positive anomalies, suggesting enhanced moisture availability, which may contribute to elevated thermal discomfort through higher apparent temperatures, although the impact on actual HW intensity may vary depending on cloud cover and nighttime cooling efficiency42.

At the lower troposphere (850 hPa), weak negative GPH anomalies (Fig. 5e) are seen across the central Arabian Peninsula along with weak and disorganized wind vectors. The specific humidity anomalies (Fig. 5f) show a distinct drying over Qatar and the southern Gulf region at this level, with relatively wetter conditions over the west coast of Saudi Arabia and Yemen. This dry anomaly near the surface during HW events suggests the advection of hot, dry continental air masses, which reduces SM and evaporative cooling, reinforcing the surface heat. This mid-tropospheric divergence may be associated with a Rossby wave train pattern, which can modulate the intensity of HW 27. This low-level convergence due to thermal low, when combined with subsidence and warm air advection, enhances surface heating43.

Qatar’s climate is strongly influenced by land surface processes, particularly anomalously high air temperatures (Figure S1a) at the surface, which supports high SHF (Fig. 3a), indicating strong upward heat transport. Further, the intensified heat is extended to 850 hPa (Figure S1b), particularly over the Qatar region. At the same time, advection of heat from surrounding warm regions (Fig. 5e and Figure S1) under dry conditions helps to attain higher temperatures over that region. The combination of intense surface heating and heat advection to that region drives the development of a deeper and more vertically coherent boundary layer, allowing thermal anomalies to penetrate the lower and mid-troposphere. Vertical velocity anomalies show significant subsidence (negative values, dashed contours) between 600 and 900 hPa, indicating large-scale downward motion and reinforcing near-surface warming through adiabatic compression. This vertical structure, marked by strong low-level warming, mid-tropospheric stability, and upper-level divergence, is characteristic of dynamically driven HWs.

The above pattern aligns with the enhancement of SHF (Fig. 3a) over dry soil with suppressed lower-level moisture availability, exacerbating further in terms of intensification and persistence of HWs42,45. The observed IVT deficit is consistent with the negative specific humidity anomalies at lower tropospheric levels (see Fig. 5f), highlighting the role of atmospheric drying in sustaining HW conditions. Previous studies have identified such reductions in IVT and associated moisture convergence as key contributors to HW amplification over semi-arid regions, including the MENA46.

The composite anomalies of potential vorticity (PV) and relative vorticity (RV) at 200 hPa, 500 hPa, and 850 hPa during HW events also show strong negative PV and RV anomalies over Qatar and the Arabian Peninsula, accompanied by positive anomalies to the north over Iraq and Iran (Fig. 8). This suggests the presence of a strong upper-level anticyclone, indicative of enhanced subsidence, consistent with the earlier discussion. The PV minimum reflects reduced instability, supporting prolonged clear-sky and radiative heating conditions. At the mid-troposphere, negative PV and strong negative RV anomalies dominate over central Arabia, signifying suppressed baroclinic activity and weakened vertical motions. These dynamical conditions reduce vertical air movement and help maintain warm conditions.

Overall, the results demonstrate that HW conditions over Qatar arise from the combined but distinct roles of surface processes and synoptic-scale dynamics. Surface warming is primarily driven by land-atmosphere feedback, as evidenced by enhanced SHF, reduced LHF, increased ST, and depleted SM (Fig. 3), which together amplify near-surface heat under moisture-limited conditions. This warmer surface can strengthen the upward transport of heat. As a HW progresses, the accumulation of heat in the lower atmosphere stagnates for a longer period due to the inhibition of vertical heat transport by strong descending motion. In contrast, the large-scale dynamical forcing is characterized by an upper-level anticyclonic circulation (Fig. 5) associated with negative potential vorticity and relative vorticity anomalies (Fig. 8), indicating subsidence that suppresses vertical mixing and maintains clear-sky conditions. The vertical temperature structure (Fig. 6) shows that warming is mainly confined to the lower and mid-troposphere (~ 1000 − 500 hPa), with maximum anomalies near the surface. This indicates that the observed feature is not a deep tropospheric heat dome, but rather a relatively shallow, vertically coupled warm structure. In this framework, surface fluxes control the buildup of heat near the ground, while synoptic-scale subsidence acts to trap heat and sustain high near-surface temperatures41,47, leading to the persistence of HW conditions. Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive assessment of HW characteristics, their underlying mechanisms, and feedback processes over Qatar and the Arabian Peninsula during the period 2003–2024. HWs over Qatar predominantly occur between May and September, with a clear intensification in late summer. A marginally significant warming trend in Tmax (+ 0.028 °C/year; p = 0.055) and a statistically significant increase in HW frequency (+ 0.1 events/year; p = 0.015) are evident, particularly after 2014. Notably, 2024 recorded the highest Tmax and number of HW events, highlighting an increasing trend of extreme heat in the region.

The EOF analysis identifies three dominant modes of temperature variability. The first mode indicates spatially uniform warming across the peninsula, the second exhibits a north-south dipole with warming in the north and relative cooling in the south, and the third mode highlights localized variability centered around Qatar. Land–atmosphere interactions emerge as key drivers of HW persistence. During HW periods, increased SHF and decreased LHF reflect reduced evaporative cooling due to dry soils. Negative SM and elevated ST anomalies confirm this shift, where low SM enhances SHF, promoting further surface warming. This positive feedback loop reinforces HW intensity, demonstrating the critical role of land surface drying in sustaining extreme heat.

Atmospheric conditions during HW events are marked by strong positive OLR anomalies, indicating reduced cloud cover and enhanced radiative heating, and negative MSLP anomalies, signaling the formation of thermal lows due to surface heating. Composite analyses reveal pronounced subsidence and anticyclonic circulation in the upper troposphere (200–500 hPa), along with weak low-level updrafts (850–900 hPa), forming a heat dome structure that traps heat near the surface. IVT and atmospheric moisture analyses further suggest that Qatar is primarily affected by dry HWs. These events are associated with intense near-surface warming (1000–850 hPa), weak inversion near 500 hPa, and upper-level divergence indicating a thermally stratified and dynamically stable atmosphere. Analysis of potential vorticity and relative vorticity confirms a coherent anticyclonic structure that enhances atmospheric stability and inhibits vertical mixing. These findings collectively emphasize that HW persistence in this region is a result of strong feedback between (warmer) land surface and (stable) atmospheric circulation patterns. Such insights are crucial in understanding the predictive capabilities of numerical models for HWs in arid environments such as Qatar and the Arabian Peninsula.
