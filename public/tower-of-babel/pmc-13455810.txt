Rational Design and Optimization of MCh-AMP1: A Stable α-Helical Antifungal Peptide with Enhanced Activity Against Candida albicans
Authors: Kamran Pooshang Bagheri, Zahra Salehi, Mohammad Mahdi Arablou, Mehdi Goudarzi, Zahra Jahanshiri, Behnam Hasannejad-Asl, Mehdi Razzaghi-Abyaneh, Sima Sadat Seyedjavadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCandida infections, particularly those caused by Candida albicans, are a growing threat to global health, exacerbated by increasing antifungal resistance. Traditional antifungal treatments are becoming increasingly ineffective due to resistance mechanisms, highlighting the need for innovative therapeutic solutions.MethodsIn this study, we engineered MCh-AMP1-A7, a derivative of the natural antimicrobial peptide MCh-AMP1, through rational design to enhance its antifungal activity, stability, and selectivity. By modifying the alpha-helical structure, amphipathy, and cationicity of the peptide, we significantly improved its antimicrobial potency.ResultsMCh-AMP1-A7 showed a marked reduction in minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) compared with the parent peptide. Against C. albicans ATCC 10231, the MIC decreased from 16 μg/mL for MCh-AMP1 to 8 μg/mL for MCh-AMP1-A7. Similarly, for clinical isolates of C. glabrata and the reference strain C. krusei DSM 70079, MCh-AMP1-A7 showed a 2- to 4-fold reduction in MIC values, indicating increased potency. This peptide also showed remarkable stability over a wide range of temperature (10–70°C) and pH conditions (pH 2–12), and retained significant antifungal activity even at very high pH values, where the parent peptide would lose much of its potency. Furthermore, MCh-AMP1-A7 displayed an improved in vitro selectivity profile, with a higher lower-bound therapeutic index (>14.5) compared with MCh-AMP1 (>5.9), together with reduced hemolytic activity and lower cytotoxicity against human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293). Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations showed that MCh-AMP1-A7 interacts effectively with fungal membranes, with high affinity (ΔG = −8.8 kcal/mol), supporting a membrane-associated mechanism of action.DiscussionThis study provides compelling evidence that MCh-AMP1-A7 is a promising next-generation antifungal agent capable of overcoming the limitations of conventional therapies. The potency, stability, and improved safety profile of the engineered peptide make it a promising candidate for further antifungal development.Keywords: antimicrobial peptides, MCh-AMP1-A7, rational design, Candida spp, antifungal activity, stability, molecular dynamics simulation

Introduction

Candidiasis, caused by Candida spp. is one of the most prevalent and clinically challenging fungal infections worldwide.1 C. albicans remains the predominant etiological agent responsible for superficial and invasive diseases, but the prevalence and clinical importance of infections caused by non-albicans species is increasing. Systemic candidiasis, especially among immunocompromised populations, including transplant recipients, cancer patients, and individuals with implanted medical devices, exhibits high morbidity and mortality rates.2 Despite advances in antifungal therapy, the global burden of candidiasis continues to increase, due in part to an aging population, widespread use of immunosuppressive therapies, and the pervasive challenge of antifungal drug resistance.3

The current antifungal drug portfolio is limited to a handful of drug classes, including azoles, echinocandins, and polyenes. Although these agents have improved clinical outcomes over historical therapies, their efficacy has been increasingly compromised by the emergence of resistant strains, concerns about toxicity, and suboptimal activity against biofilm-associated infections.4 Azole resistance, mediated by upregulation of the efflux pump and target enzyme mutations, has been widely documented; Echinocandin resistance, initially rare, is now more commonly observed due to mutations in key glucan synthase genes,5 and polyenes such as amphotericin B are limited due to significant nephrotoxicity.6 These limitations are exacerbated in biofilm contexts, where fungal cells embedded in extracellular matrices exhibit greatly reduced drug susceptibility and the potential for persistent infection.7

In response to the growing crisis of antifungal resistance and the shortage of new chemical classes, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have gained significant attention as potential next-generation antifungal agents. AMPs are typically short (10–50 amino acids), cationic, and amphipathic molecules that act through mechanisms distinct from conventional small molecule drugs, often involving direct disruption of pathogen membranes or interference with essential cellular processes.8 Their multimodal modes of action provide rapid antimicrobial effects and a lower likelihood of developing resistance compared to classical drugs. Furthermore, antifungal peptides (AFPs) can be extracted from a wide range of natural sources—including plants, animals, and microbes—providing a rich source of bioactive sequences for therapeutic development.9 Despite their inherent advantages, natural antifungal peptides face significant limitations that have so far hindered their clinical application. Among the most important of these are susceptibility to proteolytic degradation in biological environments, structural flexibility that undermines sustained antimicrobial efficacy, and variable selectivity that can lead to off-target cytotoxicity toward mammalian cells. These challenges necessitate rational peptide engineering strategies to generate analogs with enhanced stability, well-defined structures such as stable alpha-helices, and optimized cellular specificity.10

Rational design strategies for optimizing antifungal peptides commonly involve systematic modulation of key physicochemical and structural properties, including net charge, charge distribution, hydrophobicity, amphipathicity, and α-helical propensity. In addition, the incorporation of structural constraints, non proteinogenic residues, or sequence substitutions can enhance peptide stability and improve antifungal selectivity. These approaches have enabled the development of engineered antifungal peptide libraries with improved potency, reduced hemolytic activity, and lower potential for resistance development in C. albicans models.11,12

Among these design parameters, stabilization of an amphipathic α-helical secondary structure is particularly important for membrane-active antifungal peptides. A well-defined α-helical conformation can promote selective interaction with fungal membranes, facilitate peptide insertion into the lipid bilayer, enhance membrane disruption or intracellular target engagement, and improve overall antimicrobial potency. However, many natural or plant-derived antifungal peptides show only moderate biological activity and limited structural stability under physiological conditions, which restricts their potential therapeutic application. Therefore, improving α-helical affinity in fungal membrane environments, increasing structural stability, and enhancing antifungal efficacy while maintaining low mammalian cytotoxicity remain major challenges in antifungal peptide optimization.13,14

MCh-AMP1, a plant-derived antimicrobial peptide originally isolated from Matricaria chamomilla, represents a promising scaffold with inherent antifungal potential.15–17 However, the native peptide exhibits limitations related to structural flexibility, suboptimal α-helical stability, and moderate activity against Candida spp., particularly C. albicans. These limitations indicate the need for rational optimization to improve its biological performance. In the present study, we designed and optimized MCh-AMP1 to generate the modified peptide MCh-AMP1-A7. The optimization strategy was directed toward strengthening the predicted amphipathic α-helical structure, improving peptide–membrane interactions, enhancing antifungal activity against Candida spp., and improving physicochemical stability while minimizing cytotoxicity to mammalian cells. To address these aims, the optimized peptide was evaluated through an integrated experimental and computational workflow, including physicochemical and structural assessment, molecular docking, membrane-interaction analysis, molecular dynamics simulation, antifungal activity assays, stability evaluation, and cytotoxicity assessment.

Accordingly, we applied a rational design framework that integrated computational predictions with targeted amino acid substitutions to improve helical stability, optimize amphipathicity, and enhance fungal membrane selectivity. The redesigned peptide was then evaluated for antifungal activity using minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays, physicochemical stability under different temperature and pH conditions, and cellular specificity through mammalian cytotoxicity assays. By addressing key limitations of natural peptides through targeted sequence optimization, this study aims to develop an antifungal peptide candidate with improved therapeutic potential, offering a promising alternative strategy to conventional antifungal agents in the context of increasing antifungal resistance.

Materials and Methods

Study Timeframe

This experimental peptide-design study was conducted over an approximately one-year period. The study was performed in sequential phases, beginning with computational peptide redesign and in silico analyses, followed by peptide synthesis and experimental validation. Data collection included MIC assays, stability evaluation under different pH and temperature conditions, hemolytic activity assessment, and mammalian cytotoxicity testing. All experimental data reported in this manuscript were collected during the stated study period.

Materials

3-(4,5-Dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) was purchased from Sigma (Saint Louis, MO, USA). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco, Life Technologies (Grand Island, NY, USA). All other chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), unless otherwise specified. Human Embryo Kidney (HEK293, NCBI code C497) cells were procured from the Pasteur Institute of Iran (Tehran, Iran).

Microorganisms

Candida albicans ATCC 10231 and clinical isolates of Candida spp. were sourced from the Pathogenic Fungi Culture Collection at the Pasteur Institute of Iran (https://fa.pasteur.ac.ir/MBankResult.aspx). Fresh fungal cultures for all experiments were prepared by subculturing the strains on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) plates and incubated at 35 °C for 24 h prior to use.

Peptide Design and in silico Optimization

The 23-residue natural antimicrobial peptide, MCh-AMP1 (LSVKAFTGIQLRGVCGIEVKARG), previously isolated from Matricaria chamomilla with a random coil structure,15 was used as a template for rational optimization. To enhance structural stability, amphipathicity, and antifungal activity, a rational design strategy was employed by combining residue substitutions to form an alpha helix, optimize hydrophobic torque, and enhance cationic charge distribution, while minimizing predicted mammalian cytotoxicity. To improve peptide stability, the cysteine residue at position 15 was substituted to eliminate the potential for disulfide bond formation and oxidative dimerization. Helix-disrupting residues, including glycine and threonine, were replaced with alanine to enhance α-helix formation. In addition, selected neutral polar residues were replaced with positively charged residues (arginine or lysine) to increase cationicity and enhance electrostatic interactions with negatively charged fungal membranes. The physicochemical properties of the designed analogs were primarily analyzed using ProtParam programs (ExPASy Proteomics Server: https://www.expasy.org/tools/protparam.html)18 and the 3D structure was predicted by I-TASSER (https://zhanglab.ccmb.med.umich.edu/I-TASSER/).19 The helical wheel image was calculated using the online program NetWheels: Peptides Helical Wheel and Net Projections Maker (https://lbqp.unb.br/NetWheels/).20 To screen the most promising candidates for antimicrobial activity of these analogs, the CAMPR4 server (https://www.camp.bicnirrh.res.in/), including all 3 algorithms21 were used. Furthermore, to ensure safety and selectivity, the hemolytic toxicity of the analogs on human red blood cells was predicted using the HemoPI server (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/hemopi/).22

Molecular Docking Analysis

To investigate the initial binding orientation and relative affinity of the peptide analogs toward the fungal membrane surface, molecular docking analysis was performed using a yeast-mimetic lipid bilayer model. The membrane model was constructed using POPC, POPE, POPI, and ergosterol. The outer/inner leaflet distributions of POPC, POPE, POPI, and ergosterol were 40/35, 30/30, 10/10, and 20/25%, respectively. The bilayer and peptide–bilayer systems were prepared using the CHARMM-GUI web server (https://www.charmm-gui.org).23 The membrane surface dimensions along the X and Y axes were approximately 79 Å.

The three-dimensional structure of each peptide analog was predicted using the trRosetta web server (https://yanglab.qd.sdu.edu.cn/trRosetta/).24 The quality of the predicted peptide structures was assessed using SAVES v6.0 (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/). The peptide molecules were then prepared for docking using UCSF Chimera version 1.17.1.25 Hydrogen atoms and Gasteiger charges were added, nonpolar hydrogens were merged, and the peptide structures were energy-minimized using the AMBER force field for 1000 steps. The prepared peptide structures were saved in PDBQT format.

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina version 1.2.1.26 The yeast-mimetic lipid bilayer was defined as the receptor, and each peptide analog was used as the ligand.

The best-ranked docking pose was selected based on the lowest predicted binding energy and visual inspection of peptide orientation relative to the membrane surface.

The most promising analog was selected for further molecular dynamics simulation based on a combined assessment of predicted antimicrobial score, amphipathicity, predicted toxicity, docking affinity, and membrane-binding orientation.

Peptide‑Membrane Molecular Dynamics Simulation

The peptide was positioned 30 Å above the upper leaflet of the previously constructed yeast-mimetic lipid bilayer. The simulation box was solvated with TIP3P water molecules and neutralized with Na⁺ and Cl− ions to achieve a final ion concentration of approximately 0.15 M. Initial energy minimization and equilibration were performed according to the CHARMM-GUI protocol. Briefly, the system was subjected to energy minimization followed by six equilibration steps. The initial NVT equilibration was performed for 50 ps at 310.15 K using the velocity-rescaling thermostat. Subsequently, NPT equilibration was performed at 310.15 K and 1 bar using the Nosé–Hoover thermostat27 and the Parrinello–Rahman barostat.28

All-atom steered molecular dynamics (SMD) simulations were then performed using GROMACS 2023.29 Prior to SMD, the system underwent an additional energy minimization step using 5000 steps of the steepest descent algorithm, followed by 10 ns of NVT equilibration at 310.15 K using the Nosé–Hoover thermostat and 10 ns of NPT equilibration at 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat. During the SMD simulation, the peptide was pulled along the membrane-normal direction, corresponding to the z-axis, for 6 ns using a pulling force constant of 500 kJ mol−1 nm−2.

Bond constraints were applied using the LINCS algorithm.30 Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald method with a cutoff of 1.2 nm. Van der Waals and Coulomb interactions were also treated using a cutoff distance of 1.2 nm. The simulation trajectories were analyzed using GROMACS tools, and plots were generated using XMGrace.31

Peptide Synthesis

Peptide synthesis was carried out by GenScript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). The peptide was purified to 95% purity using Reverse Phase-High Performance Liquid Chromatography (RP-HPLC). The molecular weight of the peptide was further confirmed by mass spectrometry.

Antifungal Susceptibility

The Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Fungicidal Concentration (MFC) of the peptide were determined using the modified broth microdilution method, following the CLSI M27-A4 guidelines.32 Briefly, fungal cells were diluted in Sabouraud dextrose broth to a concentration of 5×105 cells/mL and incubated with various peptide concentrations at 35°C for 24 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that inhibited fungal growth. To determine the MFC, 50 μL of the culture at a concentration equal to or greater than the MIC was plated onto Sabouraud dextrose agar plates for colony-forming unit (CFU) counting. After incubation at 35°C for 24 h, the MFC was identified as the lowest concentration that killed at least 99.9% of the initial inoculum.

Peptide Stability

The functional stability of the peptide under diverse physicochemical conditions was carefully evaluated, as previously described by Chan et al33 To assess thermal stability, peptide samples were incubated in a temperature gradient from 10 to 100°C. Unheated peptide solution was used as a control reference. pH stability was investigated by exposing the peptide to a wide pH range (pH 2.0–12.0) for 1 h at 25°C using appropriate buffer systems. After incubation, the solutions were neutralized to pH 7.2 and the residual antimicrobial activity against C. albicans ATCC 10231 was measured using the radial diffusion method (RDA). All stability assays were performed in three independent replicates.

Cytotoxicity Assay

The cytotoxic potential of the engineered peptide against the human embryonic kidney cell line (HEK293) was evaluated using the MTT colorimetric assay.15 HEK293 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% (v/v) FBS and maintained in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37°C. For this assay, cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of approximately 5000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then treated with increasing concentrations of the peptide (ranging from 2 to 128 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following the treatment, 50 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h to allow for formazan crystal formation. The plates were subsequently centrifuged for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and 150 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve the crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. All measurements were performed in triplicate across three independent experiments.

Hemolysis Assay

Hemolytic activity was evaluated using fresh human red blood cells (RBCs) following the protocol outlined by Liu et al34 The experimental procedure was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committees of the Pasteur Institute of Iran (IR.PII.REC.1401.035). Human red blood cells were obtained from fresh peripheral blood samples collected from healthy adult volunteers. All blood donors provided informed consent prior to blood sample collection, and the study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.35 In brief, heparinized human whole blood was centrifuged for 5 min at 4°C to separate the RBCs. The RBCs were then washed three times with PBS and resuspended in the same buffer. Serial dilutions of the peptide (ranging from 2 to 128 μg/mL) were mixed with the RBC suspension and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged, and the amount of hemoglobin released into the supernatant was measured by determining the absorbance at 567 nm. PBS and 1% Triton X-100 were used as negative (0% hemolysis) and positive (100% hemolysis) controls, respectively. The experiment was performed in triplicate. Therapeutic Index

The therapeutic index (TI) was calculated to estimate the cellular selectivity of the peptides. TI was defined as the ratio of HC10 to the geometric mean (GM) of the MIC values. HC10 was defined as the lowest peptide concentration required to induce 10% hemolysis of human red blood cells. The GM was calculated using the MIC values obtained against all Candida spp. strains tested in this study. Because 10% hemolysis was not reached within the tested concentration range of 2–128 µg/mL, HC10 was reported as greater than the highest tested concentration (>128 µg/mL). Accordingly, TI values were expressed as lower-bound estimates using the formula TI > 128/GM. A higher TI value indicates greater selectivity toward fungal cells over mammalian red blood cells.36

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For temperature stability, pH stability, cytotoxicity, and hemolytic activity assays, differences between MCh-AMP1 and MCh-AMP1-A7 across different experimental conditions were analyzed using ordinary two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA), followed by Šídák’s multiple comparisons test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels were defined as follows: ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; and ****P < 0.0001. Statistical analysis of the experimental data was conducted using GraphPad Prism 5 statistical software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Peptide Design and Bioinformatic Studies

The goal of the design process was to overcome the inherent structural and functional limitations of the parent peptide, MCh-AMP1 (LSVKAFTGIQLRGVCGIEVKARG). Despite its moderate net positive charge (+3) and hydrophobicity (47%), this peptide adopts a predominantly random coil conformation and contains a cysteine residue at position 15, which makes it susceptible to uncontrolled oxidation and dimerization. Therefore, a structure-based rational design strategy was employed to generate a library of optimized analogs by systematically modifying key physicochemical factors. To improve structural stability, the cysteine residue was substituted to prevent disulfide bond formation and keep the peptide in the monomeric state. To enhance secondary structure formation, helix-breaking residues, including glycine and threonine, were replaced with alanine to facilitate alpha-helical folding. In parallel, neutral polar residues were replaced with lysine or arginine to increase the overall net cationicity of the peptides. As shown in Table 1, a total of seven analogs were designed, and comparative analysis of the designed analogs identified MCh-AMP1-A7 as the most favorable candidate based on multiparameter evaluation of hydrophobicity, net charge, and predicted secondary structure. MCh-AMP1-A7 exhibited a hydrophobicity of 68%, a range associated with efficient membrane interaction while maintaining water solubility. Furthermore, the net positive charge of MCh-AMP1-A7 increased to +6 relative to the parent peptide, indicating increased electrostatic compatibility with the negatively charged fungal cell membrane. Antimicrobial activity was evaluated using the CAMPR4 server with SVM, RF, and ANN models (Table 1). The parent peptide MCh-AMP1 showed moderate predicted activity with SVM and RF values of 0.75 and 0.99, respectively. In contrast, MCh-AMP1-A7 consistently showed high antimicrobial prediction scores, with SVM and RF values of 0.96 and 0.99, respectively, representing an improvement of approximately 28% over the parent peptide. Hemolytic potential was assessed using the HemoPI server (Table 1). HemoPI results showed that the scores of MCh-AMP1 and MCh-AMP1-A7 were 0.62 and 0.5, respectively, and MCh-AMP1-A7 maintained a high safety profile (0.5) despite increased hydrophobicity and net positive charge.

Sequence and Biophysical Properties of MCh-AMP1 and Its Analogs

Three-dimensional (3D) structure and helical wheel analysis of MCh-AMP1 and its designed analogs are shown in Figure 1a and b. To investigate the structural basis of the enhanced antimicrobial potential of MCh-AMP1-A7, helical wheel designs were generated using NetWheels to assess residue distribution and amphipathicity. As shown in Figure 1b, the parent peptide MCh-AMP1 exhibits a disordered distribution of hydrophobic and charged residues, without any distinct hydrophobic or polar features. This sparse arrangement is consistent with its largely random helical structure. In contrast, the helical wheel image of MCh-AMP1-A7 revealed a prominent amphipathic alpha-helical organization. Hydrophobic residues (including phenylalanine, isoleucine, valine, and alanine) are clustered on one side of the helix, forming a continuous hydrophobic face, while positively charged residues (arginine and lysine) are aligned on the opposite side, creating a distinct cationic face. This clear spatial separation indicates a high hydrophobic moment and a structurally optimized amphipathic topology.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking analyses were performed to characterize the initial binding mode of the designed analogs in the fungal membrane. As shown in Figure 2a, distinct interaction patterns were observed between the peptide analogs. PyMOL stereo 3D software was used to visualize the peptide-bilayer membrane interaction. Examination of the snapshot obtained from the peptide-membrane interaction shows that, except for MCh-AMP1-A3 and MCh-AMP1-A5, all analogs completely crossed the hydrophilic membrane barrier and entered the hydrophobic and intermediate regions of the bilayer membrane. A large portion of MCh-AMP1-A3 and MCh-AMP1-A5 also penetrated the hydrophobic region, but very small portions of them remained in the hydrophilic barrier and even outside the membrane, according to snapshots.

Peptide‑Membrane MD Simulation

Steered molecular dynamics (SMD) simulations using GROMACS provided insights into peptide transport across the yeast bilayer. The total area of the leaflet was 6241 Å2, which exceeded the dimensions of MCh-AMP1-A7 and allowed for complete encapsulation. The dissolved system contained 3617 water molecules, which were neutralized by 292 SOD⁺ ions and 178 CLA− ions. The positive charges of MCh-AMP1-A7 increased its affinity for anionic membrane lipids, which was further enhanced by hydrophobic residues that facilitated penetration into the hydrophobic core. Density profiles obtained from the SMD simulation showed the relative distribution of the peptide with respect to the lipid headgroup regions and the hydrophobic core of the yeast-mimetic bilayer (Figure 3a). The peptide density overlapped with the membrane density profile during translocation, indicating progressive peptide insertion and passage through the lipid bilayer. The pulling force profile showed a gradual increase in force as the peptide approached and penetrated the membrane interior, reaching a maximum resistance of approximately 5000 kJ mol−1 nm−1, followed by a marked decrease after traversal (Figure 3b). Representative molecular snapshots further suggested the sequential stages of peptide–membrane interaction, including initial contact with the upper leaflet, partial insertion, embedding within the bilayer core, and passage toward the lower leaflet accompanied by local membrane deformation (Figure 4). These findings support the membrane-interactive behavior of MCh-AMP1-A7 and its ability to penetrate the yeast-mimetic lipid bilayer during steered translocation.

Antifungal Activity

The antifungal activity of MCh-AMP1 and MCh-AMP1-A7 against standard and clinical isolates of Candida spp. was evaluated by determining the MIC and MFC. The antifungal potency of both peptides was compared to assess their relative effectiveness.

As summarized in Table 3, MCh-AMP1-A7 showed a marked improvement in inhibitory potency compared to MCh-AMP1. Against C. albicans ATCC 10231, MCh-AMP1-A7 reduced the MIC from 16 μg/mL for the parent peptide to 8 μg/mL. Similar reductions were observed for C. glabrata and C. krusei, where MCh-AMP1-A7 showed lower MIC values, indicating greater potency against these strains. For some clinical isolates of Candida spp., MCh-AMP1 required concentrations up to 64 μg/mL to inhibit growth, whereas MCh-AMP1-A7 remained effective at much lower concentrations, from 8 μg/mL to 32 μg/mL. In clinical isolates of C. glabrata and C. krusei, MIC values for MCh-AMP1 ranged from 32 μg/mL to 64 μg/mL, whereas MCh-AMP1-A7 showed stronger activity, with MIC values ranging from 8 μg/mL to 32 μg/mL.

Stability

As shown in Figure 5a, MCh-AMP1-A7 exhibited greater thermal stability than the parent peptide MCh-AMP1. The engineered peptide maintained high residual activity between 10°C and 50°C, with relative activity values ranging from 94% to 99%, and still retained considerable activity at 60°C and 70°C. Although its activity decreased at higher temperatures, MCh-AMP1-A7 retained approximately 25% residual activity at 80°C. In contrast, MCh-AMP1 showed a narrower temperature-dependent activity profile, with maximal activity between 30°C and 50°C, followed by a marked decrease at 60°C and 70°C and complete loss of activity at 80°C and above. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of peptide type, temperature, and their interaction on relative activity (P < 0.0001). Post-hoc Šídák’s multiple comparison test showed that MCh-AMP1-A7 had significantly higher activity than MCh-AMP1 at 10°C, 20°C, 60°C, 70°C, and 80°C (****P < 0.0001), whereas no significant difference was observed at 30°C, 40°C, 50°C, 90°C, or 100°C.

As shown in Figure 5b, the antifungal activity of both peptides was strongly influenced by pH. MCh-AMP1-A7 exhibited a broader pH tolerance than the parent peptide MCh-AMP1. The engineered peptide retained higher activity under mildly acidic conditions and also showed improved residual activity under alkaline conditions. Specifically, MCh-AMP1-A7 displayed significantly higher activity than MCh-AMP1 at pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, and 12, whereas no significant difference was observed at pH 2, 3, 8, or 9. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of peptide type, pH, and their interaction on relative activity (P < 0.0001).

Cytotoxicity Assay

As shown in Figure 5c, both peptides showed concentration-dependent cytotoxicity. However, MCh-AMP1-A7 induced significantly lower cell death than the parent peptide MCh-AMP1 at all tested concentrations from 2 to 128 µg/mL (P < 0.0001). At the highest concentration, MCh-AMP1 caused approximately 36% cell death, whereas MCh-AMP1-A7 induced only about 21% cell death. No significant difference was observed between the two peptides in the untreated control group.

Hemolysis Assay

As shown in Figure 5d, both peptides displayed concentration-dependent hemolytic activity. Nevertheless, MCh-AMP1-A7 consistently caused lower hemolysis than MCh-AMP1, especially at higher concentrations. At 128 µg/mL, hemolysis induced by MCh-AMP1 reached approximately 9.3%, whereas MCh-AMP1-A7 caused about 6.2% hemolysis. Statistical analysis showed significantly lower hemolysis for MCh-AMP1-A7 at 16, 32, 64, and 128 µg/mL, while no significant difference was observed at lower concentrations.

Therapeutic Index (TI)

The therapeutic index was calculated using HC10 and the geometric mean of MIC values obtained against all Candida spp. strains tested. Based on the MIC data, the GM value decreased from 21.5 µg/mL for MCh-AMP1 to 8.8 µg/mL for MCh-AMP1-A7, indicating improved antifungal potency of the engineered peptide. In the hemolysis assay, 10% hemolysis was not reached for either peptide within the tested concentration range. Therefore, HC10 was reported as >128 µg/mL for both peptides, and TI values were expressed as lower-bound estimates. The calculated TI values were >5.9 for MCh-AMP1 and >14.5 for MCh-AMP1-A7, indicating that MCh-AMP1-A7 exhibited a broader in vitro selectivity window than the parent peptide (Table 4).

Discussion

The increasing prevalence of Candida infections and the rise of antifungal resistance highlight the urgent need for new therapeutic strategies. This study successfully engineered MCh-AMP1-A7, a derivative of the parent peptide MCh-AMP1, through rational design to improve its antifungal activity, stability, and selectivity. The improved properties of MCh-AMP1-A7 provide insights into how molecular-level modifications overcome the inherent limitations of natural AMPs.

One of the key advances in this study was the conversion of the MCh-AMP1 peptide into a more stable and potent antimicrobial agent by enhancing its predicted α-helical propensity.13 This structural change is crucial for AMP function, as it allows for better interaction with microbial membranes. Because the design included residue substitutions to enhance alpha-helix formation and increase cationicity, these structural optimizations resulted in a more stable peptide with increased membrane affinity.37 The increased electrostatic interactions between the peptide and the negatively charged fungal membrane likely contribute to its superior antifungal properties.

This structural modification not only increased the potency of MCh-AMP1-A7 against Candida spp., but also demonstrated that rational design strategies can lead to peptides with targeted improvements in function. Similar studies have shown that creating a stable helical structure in antimicrobial peptides enhances their interaction with microbial membranes, thereby improving antimicrobial potency while minimizing off-target effects.13,38 This suggests that structure-based peptide engineering could be a suitable approach for optimizing natural peptides for therapeutic use.

One of the challenges of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) in therapeutic applications is their stability under physiological conditions.39 MCh-AMP1-A7 also showed improved stability under thermal and pH stress conditions compared with the parent peptide. This enhanced tolerance to temperature and pH variation may be advantageous for peptide handling, formulation, storage, and processing, where exposure to non-physiological conditions can reduce peptide activity. The broader pH stability profile of MCh-AMP1-A7 may also support its potential application in environments with variable local pH, such as mucosal or topical infection sites. However, these in vitro stability findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of prolonged in vivo persistence. Further studies, including serum stability, protease resistance, pharmacokinetic evaluation, and in vivo efficacy assays, are required to determine whether the improved physicochemical stability translates into enhanced therapeutic performance.40

An important point in the development of therapeutic peptides is their selectivity for pathogens over host cells.41 The engineered peptide MCh-AMP1-A7 showed an improved in vitro safety profile, with reduced hemolytic activity and cytotoxicity compared to the original peptide. This is very important to minimize potential side effects when used in vivo. While both peptides exhibited concentration-dependent hemolytic activity at higher concentrations, MCh-AMP1-A7 showed lower hemolysis than the parent peptide and displayed a higher lower-bound therapeutic index, suggesting improved in vitro selectivity toward fungal cells over mammalian red blood cells. This finding is consistent with other studies in which engineered peptides with enhanced amphipathicity have shown better selectivity for negatively charged microbial membranes while sparing human cells.42,43 A balance between antimicrobial potency and minimal cytotoxicity is essential to ensure the clinical utility of the peptide, especially in immunocompromised patients who are at higher risk for infections and side effects from treatments.

Molecular docking and SMD simulations provided further insight into the mechanisms underlying the enhanced activity of MCh-AMP1-A7. In silico analyses showed that MCh-AMP1-A7 exhibited the most favorable predicted binding profile among the designed analogs, with the lowest predicted docking energy and the highest number of hydrogen-bond and hydrophobic interactions. These findings suggest that the structural modifications introduced into MCh-AMP1-A7, particularly the incorporation of helix-stabilizing residues and increased amphipathicity, contribute to more efficient membrane entry and disruption.43

These molecular dynamics simulations suggest that MCh-AMP1-A7 was able to cross the lipid bilayer, which is consistent with its enhanced membrane-disruptive potential. The SMD simulations demonstrated peptide translocation across the bilayer and provided insight into the force profile associated with membrane traversal. Charge-induced adsorption, hydrophobic interactions, and predicted peptide helical stability may contribute to the membrane-interactive behavior of MCh-AMP1-A7. These findings highlight molecular dynamics simulations as a predictive tool for peptide membrane dynamics.

Although MCh-AMP1-A7 shows promising antifungal potential, several aspects require further investigation before therapeutic application can be considered. In vivo studies using appropriate fungal infection models are needed to evaluate its antifungal efficacy, systemic effects, bioavailability, pharmacokinetic behavior, and long-term safety. In addition, the development of an effective drug delivery system may be necessary to protect the peptide from degradation and ensure targeted release at the site of infection, thereby improving its therapeutic applicability.

Furthermore, combination therapies involving MCh-AMP1-A7 and conventional antifungal agents, such as azoles or echinocandins, should be investigated to determine possible synergistic effects. Given the increasing problem of multidrug resistance, combining engineered antifungal peptides with existing antifungal therapies may provide complementary mechanisms of action, reduce the likelihood of resistance development, and improve overall therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the rational optimization of MCh-AMP1 to generate MCh-AMP1-A7, an engineered antifungal peptide with improved antifungal activity, stability, and cellular selectivity compared with its parent peptide, MCh-AMP1. Through rational design, the α-helical structure, amphipathicity, cationic charge distribution, and membrane-interaction properties of the peptide were optimized, resulting in reduced MIC and MFC values against Candida spp. In addition, MCh-AMP1-A7 showed improved stability under different pH and temperature conditions, along with reduced mammalian cytotoxicity and hemolytic activity, suggesting a broader in vitro therapeutic window.

Overall, these findings highlight the potential of rationally engineered antifungal peptides as promising alternatives or adjuncts to conventional antifungal therapies, particularly in the context of multidrug-resistant Candida infections. However, further in vivo validation, pharmacological evaluation, and formulation development are required to support the preclinical advancement of MCh-AMP1-A7 as a potential antifungal candidate.
