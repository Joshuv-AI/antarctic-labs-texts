Insight into drilling performance parameters for in-situ detection of lithological transition
Authors: Kazumasa Tanimoto, Yohei Hamada, Ikuo Katayama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In order to examine the rock dependence on drilling performance parameters, such as torque and rate of penetration (ROP), we conducted laboratory drilling experiments using mafic and ultramafic rocks from the Oman ophiolite and the Horoman Complex. Serpentinized peridotite shows approximately twice the ROP (dry conditions: 2.95 ~ 3.85, wet conditions: 8.25 ~ 10.3 × 10−5 m/min) compared to that of peridotite and mafic samples (dry conditions: 4.75 ~ 6.90, wet conditions: 14.5 ~ 18.3 × 10−5 m/min) under both dry and wet conditions. This result is likely due to the reduction in fracture strength associated with serpentinization. We compared the laboratory results of drilling parameters with in situ data obtained from IODP Expedition 360 drilling of gabbro and Expedition 399 drilling of serpentinized peridotite, using the equivalent strength method to normalize ROP to the given rotation rate and torque. The calculation results show that the normalized ROP for the serpentinized peridotite formation was systematically higher than that for the gabbro layer, which is in agreement with our experimental results. We propose that the in situ drilling performance parameters can be used to detect the lithological transitions, such as serpentinite.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80740-4.

Introduction

The oceanic crust is formed at mid-ocean ridges and sinks into the mantle at the trenches with deep material circulation (e.g., Morgan1; Macdonald2). To understand the evolution of the oceanic crust, international scientific ocean drilling projects such as ODP and IODP have operated holes more than 2,000 m below the sea floor, penetrating the sheeted dike and upper gabbro in the oceanic crust (e.g., Ildefonce3; Michibayashi et al.4) to recover core samples and measure several physical properties, such as seismic velocity, electrical resistivity, and gamma-ray, among others. Drilling parameters are also mechanical data obtained during drilling, which include weight on bit (WOB), rotations per minute (RPM), torque, and rate of penetration (ROP). In contrast to the logging and coring operations, these drilling parameters can be obtained continuously from the simple drilling operations, which have been applied to estimate the relative rock strength (e.g., Wolcott and Bordelon5; Karasawa et al.6; Hamada et al.7). Thus, the depth profile of rock strength derived from drilling parameters could be used to distinguish the lithological transition.

Generally, the Moho is identified from the seismic velocity discontinuity, which is inferred to be the transition from mafic to ultramafic rocks at the crust-mantle boundary (e.g., Christensen8; Christensen and Salisbury9; Mengel and Kern10). However, due to the marked reduction in seismic velocity for serpentinized mantle (e.g., Christensen et al.11; Christensen12), an alternative petrological model has been proposed in which the seismic boundary is the alteration front of fresh and serpentinized ultramafic rocks (e.g., Clague and Straley13; Muller et al.14; Dick et al.15). As illustrated by this complex lithological model around the Moho, differentiating between serpentinite and gabbro layers is challenging when relying solely on seismic velocity. However, drilling parameters offer valuable insights that can facilitate the accurate detection of serpentinite layers. Furthermore, drilling parameters possess a notable advantage in challenging drilling areas where obtaining data on physical properties through logging is difficult. However, limited research has applied drilling parameters to interpret the lithological transition of the oceanic plate due to the lack of experimental investigation. In this study, we conducted laboratory drilling experiments using mafic and ultramafic rocks from the Oman ophiolite and the Horoman Complex. We demonstrate the rock dependence of the drilling parameters by comparison with those obtained from IODP expeditions and discuss their applicability for detecting the in situ lithological transition.

The serpentinized peridotite and gabbro (SG samples) were collected from the Wadi Haymiliyah area in the Oman ophiolite, while the YA samples, which include mafic granulite and peridotite, were obtained from the equigranular zone in the Horoman Peridotite Complex, Japan (see, Sawaguchi16). The experimental samples were collected from outcropping formations. Grain density and porosity, calculated from the solid volume measured by gas pycnometer and weights between wet and dry conditions, were 2.97–3.09 g/cm3 and 0.14–0.71% for the mafic samples, 3.16–3.19 g/cm3 and 0.09–0.18% for the peridotite samples, and 2.80–2.89 g/cm3 and 0.44–0.96% for the serpentinized peridotite samples. Grain density indicated that the Horoman peridotites are mainly composed of olivine, while the ultramafic samples from the Oman ophiolite were highly serpentinized (Christensen12). Seismic velocity (VP, Vs) was measured using the pulse transmission method at atmospheric pressure (see Akamatsu et al.17). Travel times were determined using a double-picking technique, where the first arrival was estimated from the first peak and a quarter of the period. The experimental samples were cored and prepared as cylindrical specimens (25 mm diameter and ~ 20 mm long) with flat end faces that were ground parallel to a precision of ± 0.01 mm.

Experimental procedure

Drilling experiments were conducted under dry and wet conditions using the pressurized high-velocity (PHV) rotary shear apparatus located at the Kochi Institute for Core Sample Research, JAMSTEC, Japan (e.g., Tanikawa et al.18). The schematic experimental assembly, and photographs of the experiments under wet conditions are shown in Fig. 1. The experimental specimens and tubular diamond drill bit with inner and outer diameters of 5 mm and 9.6 mm were inserted into the hollow cylindrical holder. The drill bit was adjusted to maintain a rotation axis deviation of less than 40 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}m, and the rotation direction was consistent across all experiments. Wet condition experiments were performed with water filling the space around the dry samples. To facilitate experiments under wet conditions, a water tank was attached to the lower holder with O-rings and Teflon tape for filling with water. A vinyl tube was inserted into the drill bit, and water was injected at 40 cc/min before any change in WOB, to eliminate the cuttings in the drill hole. In this study, the vertical load applied to the tip of the drill bit (weight on bit: WOB) and the rotational speed (rotations per minute: RPM) were controlled, while torque applied to the bit and vertical displacement (distance of bit penetration) were recorded at 1 Hz. The input parameters and recorded data were the same as those used in actual geotechnical drilling. The WOB was changed to 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9 kN, and the RPM was altered to 1.5, 1.2, 0.9, 0.6, 0.3, and 0.1/min. The interval between changes in WOB and RPM was more than 3 min after each stepwise adjustment. ROP (m/min) was calculated by dividing the bit penetration by the duration of 2 min. The amount of bit penetration was determined by taking the difference between the vertical displacement data just before the RPM and WOB were changed and the data from 2 min prior. All experiments were conducted using the same drill bit. After each experiment, the bit was visually inspected for damage or variations in roughness, but no changes were observed. In addition, there were no systematic changes in the drilling data throughout the experiments.Fig. 1(a) Schematic diagram of the experimental system with drilling parameters. We calculated the rate of penetration (ROP) from the vertical displacements divided by the duration of 2 min under constant WOB and RPM conditions (see Table 2). Figure 3 shows the results of ROP under dry and wet conditions, indicating a positive correlation with increasing WOB and RPM. ROP shows an increase proportional to RPM, but the trend shows a slight increase with WOB. This correlation between ROP and WOB or RPM is consistent with the equivalent strength model as shown for sandstone and limestone (e.g., Deng et al.19). Serpentinized peridotite shows approximately twice the ROP of peridotite and mafic samples under both dry and wet conditions, indicating the possibility that the difference between these rock types can be identified by ROP contrast. The drilling process involves two stages of deformation: bit indentation into the rock surface by thrust force and cutting by bit rotation (Kalantari et al.20; Li et al.21). Previous deformation experiments showed that serpentinized peridotite is characterized by almost half the fracture strength compared to fresh, non-altered peridotite (e.g., Escartín et al.22; Diamantis et al.23). Therefore, the high ROP during drilling experiments on serpentinized peridotite is likely caused by its weak fracture strength. Another mechanism that may reduce the fracture strength is the variation in porosity. As is commonly known, fracture strength reduces with increasing porosity, so pre-existing cracks in high porosity samples such as SG65 may cause a higher ROP. Meanwhile, serpentinization is also characterized by the development of cracks during the hydration reaction (MacDonald and Fyfe,24), so this effect of porosity on fracture strength may contribute to the positive feedback for higher ROP of serpentinite. As shown in Fig. 3a,b, the ROP under wet conditions is approximately twice that under dry conditions in each lithology. The uniaxial compressive strength of crystalline rocks is less sensitive to the presence of water, which is limited to approximately a 10% decrease for water-saturated strength (e.g., Wong et al.25). In contrast, the crack growth velocity increases rapidly in wet environments due to stress corrosion occurring at the crack tip (e.g., Atkinson26; Michalske and Freiman27). Discussion

Our experimental results indicate the markedly different drilling parameters, such as ROP in serpentinized peridotite. Figure 4 shows the calculation results of ROP normalized by that of the peridotite sample (YA21) at a WOB of 0.9 kN, using our experimental results at a RPM of 1.5/min under wet conditions. The dotted lines show the linear regressions of normalized ROP for each rock type. The relative ROP is high for each WOB condition in serpentine; thus, relative ROP can serve as an indicator to distinguish serpentine from other mafic rocks at a constant WOB. In other words, it may be applicable for the in situ detection of serpentinite layers in oceanic plates by focusing on the relative value of ROP. Conversely, our experimental data were obtained under ambient temperature and pressure conditions. However, the in situ drilling parameters obtained from the drilling of oceanic plates must take into account several factors, including the drilling environment, pressure, and temperature. Consequently, an attempt was made to ascertain the feasibility of differentiating between rock types through the comparison of these drilling parameters, utilising data obtained from actual ocean drilling operations.Fig. 4Plots of ROP/ROPYA21 at WOB = 0.9 kN versus WOB from our experimental results at RPM = 1.5/min under wet conditions. We analyzed the drilling parameters obtained from the International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP), to examine the applicability of the aforementioned ROP-based lithological identification and to investigate the rock dependence of the drilling performance parameters during in-situ drilling of the oceanic plate. IODP Expedition 360 has drilled on the summit of Atlantis Bank, which features an 11–13 million-year-old elevated oceanic core complex massif adjacent to the Atlantis II Transform on the Southwest Indian Ridge (e.g., Dick et al.28). Hole U1473A was drilled to a depth of about 800 m and was primarily composed of olivine gabbro. IODP Expedition 399 drilled Hole U1601C on the southern wall of the Atlantis Massif oceanic core complex in the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, which is dominated by the serpentinized peridotite (e.g., Boschi et al.29; McCaig et al.30). The locations of drilling sites Hole U1473 and U1601 are shown in Fig. 5. Both expeditions were conducted by the research vessel JOIDES Resolution (USA), which is one of the drilling vessels used for IODP expeditions. U1473A and U1601C were drilled at similar water depths (700–850 m) using the roller cone bits with the same bit diameter (see MacLeod et al.31), so the obtained drilling parameters likely represent the differences in lithology. Figure 6 shows the result of drilling parameters reported from the IODP Exp. 360 and 399 to a depth of 800 m. Drilling parameters from IODP expeditions were used as raw data without statistical analysis due to the limited number of data points and the inconsistent interval of data recording. These data indicate significantly higher ROP and lower torque in the serpentinized peridotite of Exp.399 compared to those in the gabbro of Exp. 360.Fig. 5The map shows the locations of the drilling sites Hole U1473 and Hole U1601. https://iodp.tamu.edu/scienceops/maps.html.Fig. 6Results of drilling parameters collected during IODP Expeditions 360 and 399 and normalized ROP to reference of torque = 200 and RPM = 55.

Since the input parameters such as WOB and RPM were not exactly the same in these expeditions, corrections are required to compare the drilling performance parameters at each site. We applied the equivalent strength (EST) method to normalize the drilling parameters (e.g., Hamada et al.7) as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ROP= \frac{2\times Torque}{EST/RPM\boldsymbol{ }\times \boldsymbol{ }{\left(R/2\right)}^{2}}$$\end{document}Where R is the diameter of the drill bit. In this equation, EST represents the slope of the relationship between the measured torque energy per minute and the volume rate of penetration; therefore, we normalized ROP to the reference values at Torque = 200 and RMP = 55. The normalized ROP for the serpentinized peridotite formation at Exp. 399 varies from 5 to 7 m/hr, while that for the gabbro at Exp. 360 varies from 2 to 4 m/hr (Fig. 6e).

These in-situ drilling data show significantly higher ROP and normalized ROP for the serpentinized peridotite than that for the gabbro, consistent with our laboratory experiments. The normalized ROP at Hole U1473A (blue line) tends to decrease at depths greater than 500 m, while characteristic high ROP peaks are observed at shallow depths, such as 50 m and 400–500 m depth. These variations in ROP with depth may be influenced by the presence of fractures, as relatively high fracture density has been reported at the shallow depths (MacLeod et al.31). In Hole U1473A, clay vein density is most common at depths of 100–250 m, and carbonate vein density increases at depths of 350–450 m (e.g., Dick et al.28), but the peaks of vein density do not correspond to the normalized ROP. These results are likely due to the lower resolution of ROP caused by the long recording interval (~ 10 m) of drilling parameters and the presence of centimetre-scale veins, which do not reflect the contrast in fracture strength during drilling. Hamada et al.32 investigated drilling parameters obtained at the Nankai Trough subduction zone in IODP Exp. 370, but the contrast in fracture strength was not represented in the drilling parameters around the plate boundary fault zone, where the localized slip zone is usually at the sub-metre scale. Therefore, drilling parameters may reflect the differences in fracture strength at the tens of metre scale, rather than at a smaller scale.

The decrease in ROP with depth can be attributed to the “torque loss” due to the long drill strings required for deeper drilling down. The torsion of the lengthening drill pipe increases as drilling progresses, and the actual weight or torque applied to the bit tip decreases. This can cause the ROP to decrease and the apparent formation strength to increase, even though the drilling parameters recorded on the rig floor remain constant. It is important to note that this background effect was not considered in this study. Although the equivalent strength method could estimate the relative rock strength for drilling in the formation, the effect of bit type—such as roller cone, PDC, and diamond—on the equivalent strength has not been verified, which implies that the equivalent strength method may vary with bit type. In addition, it should also be noted that this method, which uses only the drilling parameters, provides only a strength contrast. It is not possible to determine whether the strength variation is related to fractures, intrusions, or lithology when using only this parameter alone. In this study, the drilling parameters from IODP expeditions were compared with the descriptions of the drilling cores, however, understanding the subsurface/undersea conditions to some extent in advance through cuttings, seismic reflection sections, and logging may provide an effective way to utilise the drilling parameters. In any case, the ROPs of the oceanic crust compared under nearly identical conditions suggest that drilling parameters may be used to distinguish serpentine from other lithologies. Additionally, gabbro is consistently harder in ROP than serpentine even in the presence of structural heterogeneities such as veins and fractures, and drilling at this lithological boundary is expected to manifest as a distinct unit boundary on the ROP profile. Nevertheless, while the methodologies employed in this research may provide an indicative measure of average rock strength, they are not sufficiently robust to determine lithology or elucidate structural characteristics in isolation. The integration of these methodologies with seismic reflection surveys and well logging will facilitate a more comprehensive and effective determination of lithology and structure.

Our drilling experiments revealed that the lithology dependence of drilling parameters is sensitive in ROP at a given WOB and RPM, whereas the torque results were roughly the same. This lithology dependence of drilling parameters indicates that the relative ROP can serve as an indicator to distinguish serpentine from other mafic rocks at constant WOB and RPM. Therefore, we applied this lithology distinction using drilling parameters to the ROP data from IODP expeditions, which were normalized to the reference values for a given WOB and RPM. Our experiments were conducted in both dry conditions and wet conditions, with water filling around dry samples; thus, our experiments could not fully replicate the in-situ ocean drilling environment, where the formation rocks are saturated and drilling fluid circulates in the borehole. These limitations of the experimental setup may not reproduce the rock physics under the in-situ ocean drilling environments (e.g., stress corrosion), which implies that the characteristics of ROP shown in our experimental results may not fully correspond to the IODP data. Therefore, numerous laboratory drilling experiments replicating the in-situ ocean drilling environment may contribute to improving the applicability of drilling parameters for detecting the serpentinite layer.

Conclusion

In this study, we conducted drilling experiments in mafic and ultramafic rocks and showed that drilling the ROP in serpentinite is significantly higher than in unaltered peridotite and mafic rocks due to the differences in fracture strength, which is consistent with in-situ drilling in gabbro and serpentinized peridotite during IODP expeditions in the oceanic plate. The results indicate that it is possible to identify ultramafic rocks to some extent using drilling parameters, even in in-situ drilling. In contrast to the logging and coring operations, drilling parameters have the advantage of being obtained continuously from the simple drilling operations. Although it is difficult to determine the serpentine front from seismic velocity alone, combining drilling performance parameters provides a clue for detecting the lithological transition.
