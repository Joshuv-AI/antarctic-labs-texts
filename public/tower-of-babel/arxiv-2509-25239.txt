A Formal Comparison Between Chain-of-Thought and Latent Thought
Kevin Xu, Issei Sato

Abstract
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) elicits reasoning in large language models by explicitly generating intermediate steps in natural language. In contrast, Latent Thought in looped models operates directly in the continuous latent space, enabling computation beyond discrete linguistic representations. While both approaches exploit iterative computation, their comparative capabilities remain underexplored. In this work, we present a formal analysis showing that Latent Thought in Looped Transformers enables parallel computation, which is more efficient than the inherently sequential process of CoT. In contrast, CoT leverages stochastic decoding to approximate solutions to problems where exact computation is intractable. These separations suggest the tasks for which depth-driven recursion is more suitable, thereby offering practical guidance for choosing between reasoning paradigms. Code is available at https://github.com/kevin671/cot-vs-loop .

Introduction

Transformer
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
-based large language models (LLMs) have shown strong performance across diverse tasks and have recently been extended to complex reasoning.
Rather than directly predicting final answers, prompting LLMs to generate intermediate reasoning steps, known as
Chain-of-Thought (CoT)

(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, has been shown to enhance reasoning capabilities.
This naturally raises the question:
why is CoT effective for complex tasks?

Recent studies have approached this question by framing reasoning as a computational problem and analyzing its complexity
Feng et al. 2023
;
Merrill & Sabharwal 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Nowak et al. 2024
, showing that CoT improves performance by increasing the model’s
effective depth
through iterative computation, thereby enabling the solution of problems that would otherwise be infeasible.

Recent studies have proposed to enhance the expressive power of Transformer through
Latent Thought reasoning
, which operates in the hidden state space rather than the linguistic space, with the potential to achieve more expressive and efficient reasoning
(
Hao et al. 2025
;
Saunshi et al. 2025
)
. One prominent approach is
Looped Transformer (Looped TF)
, where Transformer blocks are applied recurrently by feeding outputs back as inputs
(
Dehghani et al. 2019
;
Lan et al. 2020
;
Giannou et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Csordás et al. 2024
;
Bae et al. 2025
;
Fan et al. 2025
;
Saunshi et al. 2025
;
Snell et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
. Such iterative structures have been shown to empower Transformers: they are Turing complete
(
Giannou et al. 2023
)
and universal approximators
(
Xu & Sato 2025
)
. In addition, they achieve competitive performance with fewer parameters
(
Lan et al. 2020
;
Csordás et al. 2024
;
Bae et al. 2025
)
, improve performance on complex reasoning tasks
(
Saunshi et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
.

The existence of two distinct approaches raises a fundamental question:

What is the separation between Chain-of-Thought and Latent Thought (Looped Transformer)?

This question has motivated comparisons of their expressivity under an equal number of steps or loops.

Saunshi et al. 2025
showed an inclusion of deterministic CoT within Looped TF, and
Merrill & Sabharwal 2025a
showed a separation where Looped TF is more expressive in the logarithmic regime.
While these results suggest a superiority of looped models over CoT, several important questions remain unanswered:
Does separation exist beyond the logarithmic regime?

Are Looped TFs universally more expressive than CoT?

In this paper, we address these questions by revealing their distinct strengths, illustrated in
Figure

4
and summarized as follows.

•

Efficient Parallel Computation by Looped TF.
We establish a separation in favor of Looped TFs in the polylogarithmic regime for deterministic settings, through asymptotic analysis of the number of steps or loops and model size required to evaluate directed acyclic graphs. That is, Looped TFs enable
efficient parallel computation
, whereas CoT is inherently sequential.

•

Randomized Approximation by CoT.
CoT has strengths in probabilistic settings, enabling
randomized approximation
to efficiently address problems where exact computation is infeasible by producing approximate solutions. This yields a counterpart separation: CoT supports approximate sampling of complex combinatorial structures, a capability absent in Looped TFs.

Preliminaries and Related Work

2.1
Models of Computation

We study a model in which a Transformer
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
is applied iteratively (formal definitions are given in
Appendix

B
).
Informally, the CoT model generates intermediate reasoning steps explicitly as tokens in an autoregressive manner before producing the final answer.

Definition 2.1

(CoT)
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be a vocabulary, and let
f_{\mathrm{dec}}:{\mathcal{V}}^{*}\to{\mathcal{V}}
denote an
autoregressive
, decoder-only Transformer that generates the next token either deterministically or stochastically.
Given an input sequence
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathcal{V}}^{n}
, define the intermediate outputs recursively by

f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{0}(x)\coloneq x,\quad f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{k+1}(x)\coloneq f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{k}(x)\cdot f_{\mathrm{dec}}(f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{k}(x)),

where
\cdot
denotes concatenation.
We define the output to be the last
L(n)
tokens of
f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{T(n)}(x)\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\,n+T(n)}
, where
L(n)
denotes the output length, i.e.,
f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{T(n)}(x)_{\,n+T(n)-L(n)+1:\,n+T(n)}
.

In contrast to CoT, Looped TF iteratively refines internal embeddings without generating tokens.

Definition 2.2

(Looped Transformer)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{F}^{d\times n}\to\mathbb{F}^{d\times n}
denote a Transformer block, where
\mathbb{F}
denotes finite-precision floating-point set and
d
is the embedding dimension.
Given an input sequence
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathcal{V}}^{n}
, define the sequence of hidden states recursively by

f_{\mathrm{loop}}^{0}(x)\coloneq(e(x_{1}),\dots,e(x_{n})),\quad f_{\mathrm{loop}}^{k+1}(x)\coloneq f(f_{\mathrm{loop}}^{k}(x)),

where
e:{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
denotes an embedding function.
After
T(n)
loop iterations, the output is defined as the last
L(n)\leq n
tokens

\mathrm{Dec}(f_{\mathrm{loop}}^{T(n)}(x))_{\,n-L(n)+1:\,n},

where
\mathrm{Dec}
denotes the decoding function.

(
Giannou et al. 2023
)
, capable of simulating iterative algorithms
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
and graph algorithms
(
de Luca & Fountoulakis 2024
)
, whereas recent work
(
Liang et al. 2024
)
indicates that looped ReLU networks already yield strong expressive capacity.

In comparison with CoT,
Saunshi et al. 2025

Merrill & Sabharwal 2025a

(

\mathsf{TC}^{k}

3
Latent Thought Enables Efficient Parallel Solutions

This section analyzes the separation between CoT and Looped TF by formalizing deterministic computations as the evaluation of graphs.
In
Section

3.2
, we illustrate how CoT and Looped TF differ in the same computation time (i.e., number of steps or loops), highlighting their respective strengths.
Finally,
Section

3.3
shows a computational separation between them in polylogarithmic time.

3.1
Problem Setting

Figure 1
:
Comparison of strategies for evaluating DAG. (a) A computation graph
G_{n}
. (b) A Looped TF simulates the graph in a number of loops proportional to the depth of the graph
O(\mathrm{depth}(G_{n}))
. (c) A CoT simulates in a number of steps proportional to the size of the graph
O(\mathrm{size}(G_{n}))
.

We formalize deterministic computations as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), since straight-line programs can be represented in this form
(
Aho & Ullman 1972
)
. Example in Fig.
1
(a).

Definition 3.1

(Computation graph)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet, and let
\mathcal{F}
denote a finite set of functions

f:\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma
. A
computation graph
is a directed acyclic graph
G_{n}=(V_{n},E_{n})
that defines a function
F_{G_{n}}:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{m(n)}
, where
m(n)
denotes the output length.
Here
V_{n}
denotes the set of nodes, consisting of (i)
n
input nodes with in-degree
0
, (ii) function nodes labeled by
f\in\mathcal{F}
, which take as arguments the predecessor nodes specified by their incoming edges in
E_{n}
, and (iii)
m(n)
output nodes with out-degree
0
.
The overall function is obtained by evaluating the graph in topological order.
The
size
of the graph is
|V_{n}|
, denoted by
\mathrm{size}(G_{n})
, and its
depth
is the length of the longest path from an input to an output node, denoted by
\mathrm{depth}(G_{n})

Assumptions on models.

Our goal is to evaluate the computational efficiency of each model through an asymptotic analysis of how the number of loops or steps scales with the input size
n
.
In addition to this
time complexity
, we also consider the
space complexity
, such as the embedding size in Transformers, which also scales with the input size, analogous to the number of processors in classical parallel computation models.
To this end, we adopt a
non-uniform
model, allowing a different model for each input size.
This setting is consistent with prior work in the context of circuit complexity and parallel computation models
(
Sanford et al. 2024b
;
Li et al. 2024
)
.

3.2
CoT Suffices with Size-scaled Steps and Looped TF Suffices with Depth-scaled Loops

We show how each model can evaluate DAGs, which provides a lower bound on their expressivity. Before presenting our main result, we first state the underlying assumptions.

Definition 3.2

(
Merrill & Sabharwal 2023
)
.

Informally, a model is
log-precision
if each scalar is stored with
O(\log n)
bits and every arithmetic operation is rounded to that precision.

Assumption 3.3

(Polynomial-size graph)
.

\mathrm{size}(G_{n})\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
.

Assumption 3.4

(Polynomially-efficient approximation, cf.
(
Feng et al. 2023
)
)
.

Informally, each node function of
G_{n}
can be approximated by a log-precision feedforward network whose parameter size is polynomial in the input length and the approximation error. We denote by
\mathrm{ff\_param}(G_{n})
an upper bound such that every
f\in\mathcal{F}
admits such a network with at most
\mathrm{ff\_param}(G_{n})
parameters.

We first consider CoT.
Under Assumptions
3.3
,
3.4
, a CoT model can simulate the computation by sequentially decoding node by node.
Intermediate tokens act as scratchpad memory to store partial results, allowing the model to evaluate any node once all its predecessors are available.

Theorem 3.5

(CoT for DAGs)
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of computation graphs that satisfy Assumptions
3.3
and
3.4
.
Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a log-precision CoT with parameter size bounded by
O(\mathrm{ff\_param}(G_{n}))
, such that for every input
x\in\Sigma^{n}
, the model outputs
F_{G_{n}}(x)
in a number of steps proportional to the “size” of the graph,
\mathrm{size}(G_{n})
.

Proof sketch.

The construction generalizes mechanisms in
(
Feng et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
)
: a CoT model can evaluate a graph by processing its nodes in topological order.
At each step, the attention mechanism retrieves the outputs of the predecessor nodes, which are available as previously generated tokens.
A feed-forward network is then applied to compute the output of the current node, and this value is generated as the next token. The iterative process yields the final result in
O(|V_{n}|)
steps.
∎

Unlike CoT, Looped TF can simulate the computation in parallel, layer by layer, where all nodes at the same depth are computed simultaneously, provided the embedding size is sufficiently large.

Assumption 3.6

(Output bound)
.

The computation graph contains at most
n
output nodes.

Theorem 3.7

(Looped TF for DAGs)
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of computation graphs that satisfy Assumptions
3.3
,
3.4
, and
3.6
.
Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a log-precision Looped TF with parameter size
O(\mathrm{ff\_param}(G_{n})\cdot\mathrm{size}(G_{n}))
, such that for every input
x\in\Sigma^{n}
, it computes
F_{G_{n}}(x)
in a number of loops proportional to the “depth” of the graph
G_{n}
, ,
\mathrm{depth}(G_{n})
.

Proof sketch.

The role assignment of each layer is based on
Li et al. 2024
, as illustrated in
Figure

5
.
A uniform attention layer aggregates all inputs from the entire sequence into a single position. The hidden state is partitioned into dedicated blocks, each block corresponding to a node in the computation graph, and the looped feedforward layer simultaneously computes the local function of every node on its respective block.
By iterating this process, all nodes at the same depth of the graph are evaluated in parallel, and successive iterations propagate the computation layer by layer.
∎

Remark.

Formal proofs are given in
Appendix

C
, with illustrations in Fig.
1
.
These results show complementary strengths: CoT can employ intermediate steps as scratchpad memory, supporting general computation without constraints. In contrast, Looped TF can exploit structural parallelism to achieve greater efficiency, but requires additional resources.

3.3
Separation in Polylogarithmic Time

We now analyze the complexity classes of the models to establish the separation. At the beginning, we define the classes
\mathsf{CoT}
and
\mathsf{Loop}
, following the definition of
\mathsf{CoT}
in
Li et al. 2024
.

Definition 3.8

(
\mathsf{CoT}
and
\mathsf{Loop}
)
.

Let
\mathsf{CoT}[T(n),d(n),s(n)]
(resp.
\mathsf{Loop}[T(n),d(n),s(n)]
) denote the set of languages
{\mathcal{L}}:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}
for which there exists a
deterministic
CoT (resp. Looped TF)
M_{n}
for each input size
n
, with embedding size
O(d(n))
and
O(s(n))
bits of precision, such that for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, the final output token, after
O(T(n))
decode steps (resp. loops), is
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
.

To connect these models with classical complexity-theoretic notions, we employ Boolean circuits.

Definition 3.9

(Informal: Boolean Circuits)
.

A
Boolean circuit
is a DAG over the alphabet
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
, where each internal node (gate) computes a Boolean function such as AND, OR, or NOT.

While CoT has been characterized in terms of circuit
size
, we show that Looped TF corresponds to the
depth
.
Here,
\mathsf{SIZE}[s(n)]
(resp.
\mathsf{DEPTH}[d(n)]
) denotes the class of languages decidable by (non-uniform) circuit families
\{C_{n}\}
with size at most
O(s(n))
(resp. depth at most
O(d(n))
).

Theorem 3.10

(
Li et al. 2024
)
.

\forall T(n)\in\mathrm{poly}(n),\,\mathsf{SIZE}[T(n)]\subseteq\mathsf{CoT}[T(n),\log{n},1].

Theorem 3.11
.

\forall T(n)\in\mathrm{poly}(n),\,\mathsf{DEPTH}[T(n)]\subseteq\mathsf{Loop}[T(n),\mathrm{size}(C_{n}),1].

Since a Boolean circuit is a DAG, the claim follows from
Theorem

3.7
.
To establish a separation, we next focus on classes of problems efficiently solvable in
parallel computation
.

Definition 3.12

(
\mathsf{NC}^{k}
,
\mathsf{AC}^{k}
, and
\mathsf{TC}^{k}
)
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the classes
\mathsf{NC}^{k}
,
\mathsf{AC}^{k}
, and
\mathsf{TC}^{k}
consist of languages decidable by non-uniform circuit families of size
\mathsf{poly}(n)
and depth
O(\log^{k}n)
, using bounded-fanin Boolean gates, unbounded-fanin
\mathsf{AND}
/
\mathsf{OR}
gates, and threshold gates, respectively.
It holds that
\mathsf{NC}^{k}\subseteq\mathsf{AC}^{k}\subseteq\mathsf{TC}^{k}\subseteq\mathsf{NC}^{k+1}

(
Allender et al. 1999
)
.

Parallel computation corresponds to polylogarithmic time using a polynomial number of processors (gates)
(
Stockmeyer & Vishkin 1984
)
.
Thus, we also assume polylogarithmic steps (for CoT) or loops (for Looped TF) and a polynomial embedding size.
We now provide the exact power of Looped TFs in this regime.

Theorem 3.13
.

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\,\mathsf{Loop}[\log^{k}n,\,\mathsf{poly}(n),\,1\ \textup{(resp.\ }\log n)]=\mathsf{AC}^{k}\ \textup{(resp.\ }\mathsf{TC}^{k}).

Proof sketch.

The inclusion from circuits to Looped TFs follows from
Theorem

3.7
.
For the converse inclusion, we build on the arguments of
Li et al. 2024
, which showed that under finite precision,

\mathsf{CoT}[0,\,\mathsf{poly}(n),\,1\ \textup{(resp.\ }\log n)]\subseteq\mathsf{AC}^{k}\ \textup{(resp.\ }\mathsf{TC}^{k})
.
We extended this to the looped setting, yielding the desired inclusion.
The formal proof is provided in
Section

C.6
.
∎

Conversely, we establish an upper bound on CoT with a polylogarithmic number of steps.

Lemma 3.14
.

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\,\mathsf{CoT}[\log^{k}{n},\mathsf{poly}(n),\log{n}]\subseteq\mathsf{TC}^{k-1}.

Proof.

The total
\log^{k}n
steps can be divided into
\log^{k-1}n
blocks, each consisting of
\log n
steps.
Since
\mathsf{CoT}[\log n,\mathsf{poly}(n),\log n]\subseteq\mathsf{TC}^{0}

(
Li et al. 2024
)
, each block with the previous block’s outputs fed as inputs to the next block can be simulated in
\mathsf{TC}^{0}
; iterating this over
\log^{k-1}n
layers yields a depth
O(\log^{k-1}n)
threshold circuit, i.e., in
\mathsf{TC}^{k-1}
.
∎

This leads to the separation in their expressive power under standard complexity assumptions.

Theorem 3.15
.

\mathsf{TC}^{k-1}\subsetneq\mathsf{NC}^{k}\Rightarrow\mathsf{CoT}[\log^{k}{n},\mathsf{poly}(n),\log{n}]\subsetneq\mathsf{Loop}[\log^{k}{n},\mathsf{poly}(n),1].

Theorem 3.16
.

\mathsf{TC}^{k-1}\subsetneq\mathsf{TC}^{k}\Rightarrow\mathsf{CoT}[\log^{k}{n},\mathsf{poly}(n),\log{n}]\subsetneq\mathsf{Loop}[\log^{k}{n},\mathsf{poly}(n),\log{n}].

Remark.

The claims follow directly from
Theorem

3.13
and
Lemma

3.14
. These results show that, under the same polylogarithmic number of steps or loops, Looped TF can simulate parallel computations more efficiently than CoT.
This highlights the inherent parallelism of looped models.

4
Chain-of-Thought Enables Randomized Approximation

While the previous section established the efficiency of Looped TF, this section reveals the converse situation: CoT is indispensable for approximating computationally hard problems.

Section

4.1
presents the preliminaries,
Section

4.2
formalizes the problem setting of approximate sampling, and
Section

4.3
proves a conditional separation result highlighting the superiority of CoT.

4.1
Preliminaries: Randomized Approximation Scheme for Hard Problems

While the previous section focused on problems solvable efficiently, some important problems are computationally hard and intractable in polynomial time unless
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
.
When exact computation of a function
f:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
is intractable, approximation schemes are employed
(
Hochba 1997
)
.

Definition 4.1

(FPTAS)
.

An algorithm is a

fully polynomial-time approximation scheme (FPTAS)
for
f
if, for any input
x
and error parameter
\varepsilon>0
, it outputs an estimate
\hat{f}(x)
satisfying

(1-\varepsilon)f(x)\leq\hat{f}(x)\leq(1+\varepsilon)f(x),

and runs in time polynomial in
|x|
and
1/\varepsilon
.

However, for some problems, even such an approximation remains intractable.
An important development was the introduction of randomized algorithms
(
Karp & Luby 1983
)
, which enable tractable solutions by allowing a small probability of error specified by a confidence parameter.

Definition 4.2

(FPRAS)
.

An algorithm is a
fully polynomial-time randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS)
for
f
if, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
, it outputs an estimate
\hat{f}(x)

\textit{s}.\textit{t}.

\Pr\left[(1-\varepsilon)f(x)\leq\hat{f}(x)\leq(1+\varepsilon)f(x)\right]\geq 1-\delta,

and runs in time polynomial in
|x|
,
1/\varepsilon
, and
\log(1/\delta)
.

For example, the knapsack problem admits an FPTAS using a dynamic programming algorithm
(
Vazirani 2001
)
, and the DNF counting problem admits an FPRAS, where randomization through Monte Carlo methods is essential to achieve an efficient approximation
(
Karp et al. 1989
)
.

Assumptions on complexity.

Although it has been conjectured that randomized computation offers no advantage over deterministic computation, i.e.,
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{BPP}

(
Nisan & Wigderson 1994
)
, randomization can nonetheless reduce the complexity of certain approximation algorithms
(
Stockmeyer 1983
)
. Moreover, there exist canonical problems for which only an FPRAS is currently known
(
Jerrum et al. 2004
)
. We therefore present our results under the assumption that
\mathsf{FPTAS}\subsetneq\mathsf{FPRAS}
, where each denotes the set of problems that admit an FPTAS and an FPRAS, respectively.

4.2
Problem Setting

We study the problem of sampling, with a particular focus on the
uniform generation of combinatorial structures
.
Formally, let
R\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}
be a relation. For a problem instance
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, the corresponding solution set is
R(x)=\{\,y\in\Sigma^{*}:(x,y)\in R\,\}.
The task of uniform generation is then to sample
y
uniformly from
R(x)
.
Building on prior work on the exact representation of probability distributions over strings
(
Nowak et al. 2024
)
, we consider a distribution whose combinatorial structure makes exact sampling intractable, motivating the study of
approximate sampling
.

Definition 4.3

(FPAUS)
.

A
fully polynomial almost uniform sampler (FPAUS)
is a randomized algorithm that, given any input
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and error tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, runs in time polynomial in
|x|
and
\log(1/\varepsilon)
, and outputs a sample from a distribution
q(\cdot|x)
supported on
\Sigma^{*}
such that

\bigl\|q(\cdot\mid x)-U(R(x))\bigr\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\;\leq\;\varepsilon,

where
U(R(x))
denotes the uniform distribution over
R(x)
,
extended to
\Sigma^{*}
by assigning zero probability mass outside
R(x)
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
denotes the total variation distance.

We focus on problems with a compositional structure, formalized as
self-reducibility

(
Schnorr 1976
)
, where solutions can be constructed recursively from smaller subproblems.

Definition 4.4

(Informal: Self-reducibility
(
Schnorr 1976
)
)
.

A relation
R
is
self-reducible
if there exists a polynomial-time procedure that, given any input
x
and prefix
y_{1:k}
(with respect to a fixed output order), produces a sub-instance
\psi(x,y_{1:k})
such that every solution
z
of
\psi(x,y_{1:k})
extends
y_{1:k}
to a solution of
R(x)
(and conversely), i.e.,

\mathrm{solution}\bigl(\psi(x,y_{1:k})\bigr)=\{z\mid\ \mathrm{concat}(y_{1:k},z)\in R(x)\,\}.

For computational models, we define CoT and Looped TF in the setting of random generation of strings
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\in\Sigma^{m}
via next-token prediction.
Specifically, CoT stochastically generates intermediate reasoning steps before producing the target symbol
y_{i}
.
In contrast, Looped TF performs additional iterations in the latent space and samples the target symbol
y_{i}
after the final loop iteration, as in practice
(
Csordás et al. 2024
;
Snell et al. 2025
;
Bae et al. 2025
)
. Definitions are in
Section

D.1
.

4.3
Separation in Approximate Sampling

We first analyze the expressiveness of CoT.
While algorithms such as FPAUS (and FPRAS) regard the accuracy parameter
\varepsilon
as part of the input, this contrasts with the behavior of practical LLMs: once a base model has been pretrained to sufficiently low error, it can meet the required accuracy
\varepsilon
at inference time.
To formalize this, we first formally define well-pretrained models.

Definition 4.5

(Pretraining convergence)
.

Let
p
be a target distribution supported on
\Sigma^{*}
, let
\alpha(n)\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
, and let
\gamma\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.
A stochastic model
\pi_{\omega}
, indexed by the internal randomness
\omega
for each inference run, is said to be an
(\alpha,\gamma)
-weak probable approximation
to
p
if,
for every position
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, every input
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, and every prefix
y_{<i}\coloneq(y_{1},\ldots,y_{i-1})
, it holds that

\Pr_{\omega}\left[(1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha(|x|)})p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;\pi_{\omega}(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;(1+\tfrac{1}{\alpha(|x|)})p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\right]\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma.

Intuitively, the model does not guarantee a small approximation error in every run, but achieves with probability strictly greater than random chance. We begin by showing the expressiveness of a pretrained CoT, augmented with inference-time algorithms, such as majority voting.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
R
be a relation. Suppose the CoT model
\pi
is an
(\alpha,\gamma)
-weak probable approximation to the uniform distribution
U(R(x))
for a sufficiently large
\alpha
. Then there exists a polynomial-time algorithm, with access to
\pi
, that constitutes an FPAUS.

We now establish a conditional separation between CoT and Looped TFs. Building on the result of
Nowak et al. 2024
, we show that CoT can represent any FPRAS, and therefore can realize convergence during pretraining, and then constitute an FPAUS as above. In contrast, we show that there exist instances where a Looped TF fails to achieve comparable approximation guarantees.

Theorem 4.7
.

Assume
\mathsf{FPTAS}\subsetneq\mathsf{FPRAS}
for self-reducible relations
R
, with respect to the counting problem
|R(x)|
.
Then there exists a function
\alpha(n)\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
and constant
\gamma>0
such that a CoT model with polynomial steps satisfies the
(\alpha,\gamma)
-weak probable approximation to the uniform distribution
U(R(x))
, whereas no Looped TF with polynomial loops can achieve the same approximation, nor realize an FPAUS for
U(R(x))
.

Proof sketch.

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that for every polynomial function, there exists a Looped TF satisfying the weak probable approximation.
Since a Looped TF introduces randomness only at the decoding stage, its logits are computed deterministically, and the stochasticity can thus be derandomized. This would yield an FPTAS, contradicting the assumption that
\mathsf{FPTAS}\subsetneq\mathsf{FPRAS}
.
∎

Remark.

Formal proofs are given in
Appendix

D
. This result suggests that looped models may not achieve the same expressive power as CoT in approximation under the assumption, uncovering an advantage of CoT and the benefit of stochastic decoding for enabling randomized approximation.

5
Experiments

In this section, we experimentally validate our theoretical results. For parallelizable tasks, Looped TF achieves more efficient solutions than CoT. In contrast, for computationally hard tasks, CoT leverages stochastic sampling to obtain approximate solutions, whereas Looped TF cannot achieve the same.

5.1
Experimental Setting

This section provides the task descriptions and training methodology for both tasks.

5.1.1
Parallelizable Tasks

Task Description

We use four problems. (1) Word problems for finite non-solvable groups: given a sequence of generators, the task is to evaluate their composition. This problem is
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
-complete
(
Barrington 1986
)
, also studied for Looped TF by
Merrill & Sabharwal 2025a

(2)
s
–

G=(V,E)
and two vertices
s,t\in V

t
is reachable from
s

\mathsf{TC}^{1}

(
Gibbons & Rytter 1989
)
.
(3) Arithmetic expression evaluation: given a formula consisting of

operations on integers, the task is to evaluate it. This problem is
\mathsf{TC}^{0}
-reducible to Boolean formula evaluation
(
Feng et al. 2023
)
, which is
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
-complete
(
Buss 1987
)
.
(4) Edit distance: given two strings
x
and

x
into
y

\mathsf{TC}^{1}

(
Apostolico et al. 1990
)
. Details are in
Section

E.1.1
.

Training Methodology

For both CoT and Looped, the same Transformer block is adopted to ensure comparable parameter counts.
For CoT models, training is performed with supervision of sequential algorithms. For instance, in dynamic programming tasks, the model is supervised to emit DP table entries. For inference, only the input is given. To control the number of CoT steps, intermediate positions are uniformly sampled, while preserving structure, along the full solution trajectory.
All algorithms for each task are provided in
Section

E.1.2
.
We found that, just as CoT benefits from intermediate supervision, Looped similarly benefits from curriculum learning for certain tasks, as also discussed by
Merrill & Sabharwal 2025a
. Details of configuration are in
Section

E.1.3
.

5.1.2
Approximation Task

Task Description

Since approximate counting and approximate sampling are equivalent for self-reducible relations, we employ approximate counting tasks for experimental evaluation, specifically the DNF counting task. The input is a propositional formula
F
, expressed as the disjunction of
m
clauses
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}
, where each clause is a conjunction of literals over
n
Boolean variables. The output is the number of truth assignments over the
n
variables that satisfy
F
, denoted by
|F|
. The DNF counting problem is
\#\mathrm{P}
-complete; thus, if
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}
, no polynomial-time algorithm exists for exact computation. However, a FPRAS has been established
(
Karp et al. 1989
)
. In our experiments, we use

Training Methodology

For Looped TF, we trained the models end-to-end to directly map inputs to counts. In contrast, CoT models were trained with intermediate steps simulating a single trial of the randomized counting algorithm of
Karp & Luby 1983
. Performance is evaluated using relative error. At inference time, CoT executes multiple Monte Carlo samples and aggregates the results through post-processing. The total number of CoT steps is defined as the product of the reasoning steps per sample and the number of Monte Carlo samples. Further details are provided in
Section

E.2
.

6
Conclusion

We have conducted a formal comparison between Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Latent Thought in Looped Transformers, elucidating their respective strengths: Latent Thought enables efficient parallel computation, whereas CoT enables randomized approximation. This study characterizes these distinct computational paradigms of Transformer architectures from the perspective of representational capacity. These findings are further substantiated by experimental evidence. Although this study compared loop counts and step counts under equivalent settings, it did not explicitly address computational cost. An important direction for future work is to examine inference efficiency on hardware and to assess whether practical acceleration methods, such as parallelization, speculative decoding, and distillation, can be applied to reduce the required number of loops or steps.

B.1
Transformer Block

We define the computational components of a Transformer using the notation of
Merrill & Sabharwal 2025a
. Let
\mathbb{F}_{s}
denote the set of
s
-bit fixed-point numbers with truncated arithmetic.

Definition B.1

(Transformer)
.

A Transformer consists of the following components:

1.

A word embedding function
\mathrm{WE}:{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}
, where
{\mathcal{V}}
denotes the vocabulary set.

2.

A positional embedding function
\mathrm{PE}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}
.

3.

A saturated multi-head self-attention layer
\mathbf{SA}:\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m\times N}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m\times N}
for arbitrary sequence length
N
, parameterized by a matrix
{\bm{O}}:\mathbb{F}_{s}^{s\times H}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}
and, for each head
h\in[H]
with head size
s
, matrices
{\bm{Q}}_{h},{\bm{K}}_{h},{\bm{V}}_{h}:\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{s}
. Given an input
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}
for each position
i\in[N]
, it computes the query
\mathbf{q}_{i,h}={\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{x}}_{i}
, key
{\bm{k}}_{i,h}={\bm{K}}_{h}{\bm{x}}_{i}
, and value
\mathbf{v}_{i,h}={\bm{V}}_{h}{\bm{x}}_{i}
, and outputs
{\bm{O}}\cdot({\bm{a}}_{i,1},\ldots,{\bm{a}}_{i,H}),

where each attention output
{\bm{a}}_{i,h}
is defined as:

{\bm{a}}_{i,h}=\sum_{j=1}^{c(i)}\frac{\exp(\mathbf{q}_{i,h}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{j,h})}{Z_{i,h}}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{j,h},\quad Z_{i,h}=\sum_{j=1}^{c(i)}\exp(\mathbf{q}_{i,h}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{j,h}),

with
c(i)=i
for causal attention and
c(i)=n
for full attention.

4.

A feedforward layer
\mathrm{FF}:\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}
with parameter
{\bm{W}}_{1}:\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{w}
,
{\bm{W}}_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{s}^{w}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}
, and
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}
, where

{\bm{W}}_{2}\mathrm{ReLU}({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}_{i}+{\bm{b}})
, where
\mathrm{ReLU}({\bm{x}})=(\max\{0,{\bm{x}}_{1}\},\ldots,\max\{0,{\bm{x}}_{m}\})^{\top}
.

5.

An output function
\mathbf{OUT}:\mathbb{F}_{s}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{s}^{|{\mathcal{V}}|}

B.2
Chain-of-Thought

Definition B.2

(CoT)
.

Let the Transformer be defined as the composition:

f_{\mathrm{dec}}\coloneq\mathbf{OUT}\circ(\id+\mathbf{FF}_{L})\circ(\id+\mathbf{SA}_{L})\circ\cdots\circ(\id+\mathbf{FF}_{1})\circ(\id+\mathbf{SA}_{1})\circ(\mathbf{WE}+\mathbf{PE}),

where
\mathbf{SA}_{\ell}
and
\mathbf{FF}_{\ell}
denote the causal attention and the feedforward layers at depth
\ell\in[L]
, respectively, and
\id
denotes the identity function. The input tokens are first embedded via the word embedding function
\mathbf{WE}
and the positional encoding
\mathbf{PE}
, and the final output is produced by a linear projection
\mathbf{OUT}
.
Given an input sequence
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathcal{V}}^{n}
, we define the initial sequence as:
f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{0}(x)\coloneq x.

Then, the
CoT
computes recursively as:

f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{k+1}(x)\coloneq f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{k}(x)\cdot\mathrm{Dec}(f_{\mathrm{dec}}(f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{k}(x))),

where
\cdot
denotes concatenation of sequences, and
\mathrm{Dec}(\cdot)
is a decoding function that maps the output logits to a token in
{\mathcal{V}}
: in the
deterministic
model,
\mathrm{Dec}(z)\coloneq\arg\max_{i\in[|{\mathcal{V}}|]}z_{i}
; in the
stochastic
model,
\mathrm{Dec}(z)\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}({z_{i}}/{\sum_{j}z_{j}})
, assuming
z_{i}>0
for all
i
.
The final output of the CoT model after
T(n)
steps is defined as the last output length
m
tokens of
f_{\mathrm{cot}}^{T(n)}(x)
.

B.3
Looped Transformer

Definition B.3

(Looped TF)
.

Let the Transformer block be defined as the composition:

f\coloneq(\id+\mathbf{FF}_{L})\circ(\id+\mathbf{SA}_{L})\circ\cdots\circ(\id+\mathbf{FF}_{1})\circ(\id+\mathbf{SA}_{1}),

where
\mathbf{SA}_{\ell}
and
\mathbf{FF}_{\ell}
denote the (non-causal) self-attention and feedforward layers at depth
\ell\in[L]
.

Given an input token sequence
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathcal{V}}^{n}
, the initial hidden state is:

f_{\mathrm{loop}}^{0}(x)\coloneq\mathbf{WE}(x).

At each loop iteration

, the hidden state is updated by:

f_{\mathrm{loop}}^{k+1}(x)\coloneq f\left(f_{\mathrm{loop}}^{k}(x)\right).

The final outputs after
T(n)
loop iterations are decoded as
\mathrm{Dec}\circ\mathbf{OUT}\circ f_{\mathrm{loop}}^{T(n)}(x),
and the model’s prediction is defined as the last output length
m\leq n
tokens of this projected sequence.

x_{i}\in\Sigma
at position
i\in[n]
is embedded as
{\bm{h}}^{(0)}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{2n+(1+|\Sigma|)N+|\Sigma|}
:

{\bm{h}}^{(0)}_{i}=\bigl(\,{\bm{e}}_{i},\;{\bm{e}}_{o_{i}},\;\bm{e}(x_{i}),\;0,\;\bm{e}(x_{i}),\;0,\;\ldots,\;\bm{e}(x_{i}),\;0,\;{\bm{0}}_{(1+|\Sigma|)(N-n)+|\Sigma|}\bigr).

(53)

The first attention layer is an identity map, while the first feedforward layers compute

{\bm{h}}^{(1)}_{i}\;=\;\bigl(\,{\bm{e}}_{i},\;{\bm{e}}_{o_{i}},\;({\bm{e}}_{i})_{1}\cdot\bm{e}(x_{i}),\;0,\;({\bm{e}}_{i})_{2}\cdot\bm{e}(x_{i}),\;0,\;\ldots,\;({\bm{e}}_{i})_{n}\cdot\bm{e}(x_{i}),\;0,\;{\bm{0}}_{(1+|\Sigma|)(N-n)+|\Sigma|}\,\bigr).

(54)

The second attention layer uniformly gathers all positions and appends a constant
1
to each block:

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}^{(1.5)}_{i}

\displaystyle\;=\;\bigl(\,{\bm{e}}_{i},\;{\bm{e}}_{o_{i}},\;\bm{e}(x_{1}),\;1,\;\bm{e}(x_{2}),\;1,\;\ldots,\;\bm{e}(x_{n}),\;1,\;{\bm{0}}_{(1+|\Sigma|)(N-n)+|\Sigma|}\,\bigr)

(55)

\displaystyle\;=\;\bigl(\,{\bm{e}}_{i},\;{\bm{e}}_{o_{i}},\;{\bm{h}}(0),\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Sigma|}\,\bigr).

(56)

We now proceed by induction.
Assume that at the
k
-th iteration, the output after the second attention layer at position
i
is

{\bm{h}}^{(1.5)}_{k,i}\;=\;\bigl(\,{\bm{e}}_{i},\;{\bm{e}}_{o_{i}},\;{\bm{h}}(k),\;t_{o_{i},k-1}\cdot\bm{e}(v_{o_{i}}(x))\bigr),

(57)

where

t_{o_{i},k-1}\coloneq\bigl({\bm{h}}^{(1.5)}_{k-1,i}\bigr)_{\,2n+(1+|\Sigma|)(o_{i}-1)}

denotes the indicator flag showing whether the
o_{i}
-th node has been reached,
i.e., whether it lies at depth
k-1
.

After passing through the second feedforward layer, the third attention layer with all weights set to zero, and the third feedforward layer, the hidden state updates to

{\bm{h}}^{(3)}_{k,i}\;=\;\bigl(\,{\bm{e}}_{i},\;{\bm{e}}_{o_{i}},\;{\bm{h}}(k+1),\;t_{o_{i},k-1}\cdot\bm{e}(v_{o_{i}}(x))\,\bigr).

(58)

After the fourth feedforward layer, the hidden state becomes

{\bm{h}}^{(4)}_{k,i}\;=\;\bigl(\,{\bm{e}}_{i},\;{\bm{e}}_{o_{i}},\;{\bm{h}}(k+1),\;t_{o_{i},k}\cdot\bm{e}(v_{o_{i}}(x))\bigr).

(59)

By induction, after the final iteration of depth
\mathrm{depth}(G_{n})
of the computation graph
G_{n}
, we obtain

{\bm{h}}^{(4)}_{\mathrm{depth}(G_{n}),i}\;=\;\bigl(\,*,\;t_{o_{i},\mathrm{depth}(G_{n})}\cdot\bm{e}(v_{o_{i}}(x))\bigr)\;=\;\bigl(\,*,\;\bm{e}(v_{o_{i}}(x))\bigr).

(60)

The final output is given by

z_{i}=\mathbf{OUT}({\bm{h}}^{(4)}_{\mathrm{depth}(G_{n}),i})=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{0}}&{\bm{I}}_{|\Sigma|}\end{bmatrix}({\bm{h}}^{(4)}_{\mathrm{depth}(G_{n}),i})=\bm{e}(v_{o_{i}}(x)).

(61)

The decoding function then selects the symbol corresponding to the maximum score:

y_{i}\;=\;\mathrm{Dec}(z_{i})=\arg\max_{j\in[|\Sigma|]}\,\bigl(\bm{e}(v_{o_{i}}(x))\bigr)_{j}=v_{o_{i}}(x).

(62)

The parameter size grows proportionally with the input dimension
\mathrm{size}(G_{n})
,
since the function must be approximated along each dimension.
Therefore, it can be bounded by

O\!\left(\mathrm{ff\_param}(G_{n})\cdot\mathrm{size}(G_{n})\right).

∎

Discussion:
One might object that our proof, compressing the entire length
n
input to a single position and then applying an arbitrary feed-forward computation, deviates from standard Transformer architecture.
An alternative is to distribute computation across positions, as shown by
Sanford et al. 2024b
, which can reduce the embedding dimension. We nevertheless adopt the single-position construction to isolate the essence of looped models: attention serves solely to aggregate information, a single layer is sufficient, while the feed-forward network performs the computation in latent space, which is more expressive than computations in the language space. This perspective aligns with expressivity results for looped ReLU networks
(
Liang et al. 2024
)
.

D.1.1
Models of Computation

To analyze the distinction between CoT and latent thought in the context of next-token prediction, we formalize CoT as a process that, given the prefix

y_{<i}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{i-1})
,
generates the next token
y_{i}
via a stochastic reasoning step, as follows.

Definition D.1

(CoT with stochastic decoding)
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be a vocabulary.
Given an input
x\in{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
, CoT stochastically generates a sequence of output blocks of the form

\Big(r_{1},\,e,\,y_{1},\,e^{\prime},\;r_{2},\,e,\,y_{2},\,e^{\prime},\;\cdots\;r_{m},\,e,\,y_{m},\,e^{\prime}\Big)

where each
r_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
is a reasoning path and
e,e^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{V}}
are special delimiter tokens,
yielding the final output string
y_{1}\dots y_{m}
.
Generation proceeds
autoregressively
via
next-token prediction
: for each
i\in[m]
,
the model generates a reasoning step
r_{i}
followed by an output token
y_{i}
,
conditioned on the input
x
, previous outputs
y_{<i}
, and prior reasoning steps
r_{<i}
.

For looped models, we use the last output of Looped TF as the next token.

Definition D.2

(Looped TF with stochastic decoding)
.

Let
f
be a Looped TF with vocabulary
\Sigma
and loop iteration bound
T(n)\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
.
Given an input
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and prefix
y_{<k}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k-1})
, its hidden representation at the last position after
T(|x|)
iterations is

h(x,y_{\leq k-1})\coloneq f^{T(|x|)}(x,y_{\leq k-1})_{n+k-1},

A stochastic decoding function

\mathrm{Dec}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\Delta(\Sigma)

maps the hidden state at each position to a probability distribution over tokens.
The autoregressive generation distribution of the model is then

\Pr(y\mid x)\;\coloneq\;\prod_{k=1}^{m}\mathrm{Dec}\bigl(h(x,y_{<k})\bigr)(y_{k}),

for every

.

D.1.2
Self-Consistency Enhances Reliability

The lemma shows that the success probability can be improved by repeating the trial multiple times.

Lemma D.3

(Error reduction)
.

Suppose there is a randomized estimator
A
for a nonnegative function
f
such that for every input
x
, every accuracy parameter
\varepsilon>0
, and some constant
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
,

\Pr\!\left[(1-\varepsilon)f(x)\ \leq\ A(x,\varepsilon)\ \leq\ (1+\varepsilon)f(x)\right]\ \geq\ \tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma,

(63)

and the running time of
A
is polynomial in
|x|
and
1/\varepsilon
.
Then, for every confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, there is an FPRAS for
f
outputs
\widehat{f}(x)
satisfying

\Pr\!\left[(1-\varepsilon)f(x)\ \leq\ \widehat{f}(x)\ \leq\ (1+\varepsilon)f(x)\right]\ \geq\ 1-\delta.

(64)

Proof.

Fix
x,\varepsilon,\delta
. Run
A
independently
k
times with fresh randomness and the same
(x,\varepsilon)
, obtaining estimates
\widehat{f}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{f}^{(k)}
. Output the median

\widehat{f}_{\mathrm{med}}\;\coloneqq\;\mathrm{median}\!\left\{\widehat{f}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{f}^{(k)}\right\}.

(65)

For each run
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, define the Bernoulli indicator

Y_{i}\;\coloneqq\;\mathbf{1}\!\left[(1-\varepsilon)f(x)\ \leq\ \widehat{f}^{(i)}\ \leq\ (1+\varepsilon)f(x)\right]\in\{0,1\},

(66)

so that
p\coloneqq\Pr[Y_{i}=1]\geq\tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma
by assumption, and let
S\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y_{i}
, so
\mathbb{E}[S]=pk
.
If at least half the runs are successful (i.e.,
S\geq\lceil k/2\rceil
), then at least
\lceil k/2\rceil
samples lie inside the interval
\mathcal{I}=[(1-\varepsilon)f(x),(1+\varepsilon)f(x)]
, hence the median must also lie in
\mathcal{I}
. Therefore

\Pr\!\left[\widehat{f}_{\mathrm{med}}\notin\mathcal{I}\right]\;\leq\;\Pr\!\left[S<\frac{k}{2}\right].

(67)

Since the
Y_{i}
are independent in
[0,1]
, Hoeffding’s inequality gives

\Pr\left[S\leq\frac{k}{2}\right]\;=\;\Pr\left[\frac{S}{k}-p\leq-\Bigl(p-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\right]\;\leq\;\exp\bigl(-2k\,(p-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\bigr)\;\leq\;\exp\bigl(-2k\,\gamma^{2}\bigr).

(68)

Choose
k\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\,\ln\!\frac{1}{\delta},

then
\Pr[\widehat{f}_{\mathrm{med}}\notin\mathcal{I}]\leq\delta
.
We invoke
A
independently
k=O\bigl(\gamma^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\bigr)
times and take a median, Hence the total running time is polynomial in
|x|
,
1/\varepsilon
, and
\log(1/\delta)
. ∎

Lemma D.4
.

For
m\geq 1
and
-m\leq\varepsilon\leq\sqrt{m}
, it holds that

e^{\,\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}}\;\leq\;\Bigl(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{m}\Bigr)^{m}\;\leq\;e^{\,\varepsilon}.

(69)

Proof.

For
u>-1
, the elementary bounds

\ln(1+u)\leq u
and
\ln(1+u)\geq u-\tfrac{u^{2}}{2}
hold.
Substituting
u=\varepsilon/m
and multiplying by
m
gives

m\ln\Bigl(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{m}\Bigr)\leq\varepsilon,\qquad m\ln\Bigl(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{m}\Bigr)\geq\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2m}.

(70)

This yields, respectively,

(1+\varepsilon/m)^{m}\leq e^{\varepsilon},\qquad(1+\varepsilon/m)^{m}\geq\exp\bigl(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}/(2m)\bigr).

(71)

If
\varepsilon^{2}\leq m

\varepsilon^{2}/(2m)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, thus we have

D.1.3
Self-Reducibility

Definition D.5

(
Schnorr 1976
)
.

A relation
R\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}
is
self-reducible
if:

1.

There exists a polynomial-time computable function
g\in\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
s.t.,
(x,y)\in R\Rightarrow|y|=g(x);

2.

There exists a polynomial-time Turing machine that decides membership in
R
.

3.

There exist polynomial-time computable functions
\psi\in\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
s.t.

\displaystyle\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=O(\log|x|),

(72)

\displaystyle g(x)>0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sigma(x)>0\quad\forall x\in\Sigma^{*},

(73)

\displaystyle|\psi(x,w)|

\displaystyle\leq|x|\quad\forall x,w\in\Sigma^{*},

(74)

and such that, for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,
y=y_{1}\dots y_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
,

\langle x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\rangle\in R\iff\langle\psi(x,y_{1}\dots y_{\sigma(x)}),y_{\sigma(x)+1},\dots,y_{n}\rangle\in R.

(75)

For example, SAT is self-reducible: by fixing a prefix of variables and applying the reduction map
\psi
, the problem is simplified to a smaller instance whose solutions extend the chosen prefix.
Consider the Boolean formula

F=(x_{1}\vee x_{2})\ \wedge\ (\neg x_{1}\vee x_{3})\ \wedge\ (\neg x_{2}\vee\neg x_{3}),

and suppose we fix the first variable to
x_{1}=1
.
The residual instance is obtained by applying
\psi(F,(1))
, which substitutes
x_{1}=1
and simplifies the formula by deleting satisfied clauses and removing falsified literals:

\psi(F,(1))\;=\;(x_{3})\ \wedge\ (\neg x_{2}\vee\neg x_{3}).

The unit clause
(x_{3})
forces
x_{3}=1
, which in turn simplifies
(\neg x_{2}\vee\neg x_{3})
to
\neg x_{2}
, yielding
x_{2}=0
.
Hence the unique residual assignment is
(x_{2},x_{3})=(0,1)
, and together with the prefix
x_{1}=1
, we obtain the satisfying assignment

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(1,0,1).

For self-reducible relations, the existence of an FPRAS and an FPAUS is equivalent.

Theorem D.6

(
Jerrum et al. 1986
)
.

Let
R
be a self-reducible relation. There exists an FPRAS for approximating
|R(x)|
if and only if there exists an FPAUS for sampling uniformly from
R(x)
.

D.2
Proof for Proposition
4.6

The basic strategy follows the reduction from almost uniform generation to approximate counting
(
Jerrum et al. 1986
)
.

Proof.

Fix target parameters
\varepsilon,\delta>0
for the FPAUS.
Let
m\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
be such that for any
y\in R(x)
we have
|y|\leq m(|x|)
.
By
Lemma

D.3
and the assumption of CoT model
\pi
, for every position
i\in[m(|x|)]
, every input
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, and every prefix
y_{<i}
, it holds that

\Pr_{\omega}\!\left[(1-\tfrac{1}{2m(|x|)})\,p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;\pi_{\omega}(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;(1+\tfrac{1}{m(|x|)})\,p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\right]\;\geq\;1-\frac{\delta}{3m(|x|)}\,,

(76)

where
\pi_{\omega}
denotes the distribution obtained by taking the median over multiple trials,
and
\omega
represents the underlying randomness of these trials.

Hence the autoregressive sampling of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m(|x|)}
succeeds at all steps with probability at least

1-m(|x|)\cdot\tfrac{\delta}{3m(|x|)}\;=\;1-\tfrac{\delta}{3}\,,

(77)

by the union bound. Condition on this “good” event. Let
p(y\mid x)=1/|R(x)|
denote the uniform target on
R(x)
.
Multiplying the stepwise bounds and using
Lemma

D.4
, namely
\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{2m}\bigr)^{m}\leq\mathrm{e}^{1/2}
, we obtain

\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}\;\leq\;\frac{\pi_{\omega}(y\mid x)}{p(y\mid x)}\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{1/2}\qquad\text{for all }y\in R(x).

(78)

To obtain exact uniformity, we apply rejection sampling. Run one preliminary draw
z\sim\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(\cdot\mid x)
and set

\varphi_{0}\;\coloneq\;\mathrm{e}^{-1}\,\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(z\mid x),

and define the acceptance probability, denoted by
a(y)
, as

a(y)\;\coloneq\;\min\Bigl\{1,\,\frac{\varphi_{0}}{\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr\}.

(79)

By equation
78
applied to both
z
and any
y\in R(x)
,

\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(z\mid x)\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{1/2}\,p(z\mid x)\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1/2}}{|R(x)|}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(y\mid x)\;\geq\;\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}\,p(y\mid x)\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}}{|R(x)|}.

(80)

Therefore

\varphi_{0}\;=\;\mathrm{e}^{-1}\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(z\mid x)\;\leq\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}}{|R(x)|}\;\leq\;\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(y\mid x)\qquad\text{for all }y\in R(x).

(81)

Thus
a(y)
is well-defined, and the output probabilities of the rejection sampling procedure satisfy

\Pr[y]\;\propto\;\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(y\mid x)\,a(y)\;=\;\min\{\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(y\mid x),\,\varphi_{0}\}.

(82)

which is constant in
y
. Hence, conditional on the good event, the output distribution is
exactly
uniform over
R(x)
.
Still, on the good event, the acceptance probability in one trial is

\Pr[\mathrm{accept}]\;=\;\sum_{y\in R(x)}\pi_{\bm{\omega}}(y\mid x)\,a(y)\;=\;|R(x)|\,\varphi_{0}\;\geq\;|R(x)|\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-1}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}}{|R(x)|}\;=\;\mathrm{e}^{-3/2}.

(83)

Hence, the per-trial rejection probability is at most
q\coloneq 1-\mathrm{e}^{-3/2}
.
Repeating independently
k
times, the probability of rejecting in all trials is at most
q^{k}
.
To make this at most
\delta/3
, it suffices to take

k\;\geq\;\log(3/\delta)/\log\bigl(1/q\bigr)\;=\;O(\log(1/\delta)).

(84)

There are three failure modes: (i) stepwise approximation fails (probability
\leq\delta/3
);
(ii) the preliminary draw does not satisfy equation
78
(already covered by (i), so no extra cost);
(iii) all
k
rejection-sampling trials reject (
\leq\delta/3
).
By the union bound, the overall failure probability is at most
\delta
.
On success, the output law is uniform, so its total variation distance to the uniform target is also at most
\delta
.
The running time is polynomial in
|x|
and
\log(1/\delta)
, so the procedure is an FPAUS.
∎

chooses uniformly at random between
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}

Each transition
\delta_{i}(q,a)=(q^{\prime},b,\sigma,D)
is interpreted as: writing

on the work tape, writing
\sigma\in\Sigma
on the output tape, where
\varepsilon
means that nothing is written, and moving the tape head in direction

L
= left,
R
= right, and

= stay. The output of

is defined to be the string remaining on the output tape when the machine halts.

Our result builds on

from simulating deterministic Turing machines with attention to incorporating probabilistic decoding, thereby capturing the probabilistic transitions of PTMs. Their proof, however, relies on hard attention, whereas our setting uses softmax attention. In addition, the definition of the output format of CoT models considered here differs from theirs. We therefore establish the corresponding statements in our setting.

Let

be a PTM with input and output alphabet
\Sigma

\Pr\bigl[\mathsf{CoT}_{M}(x)=y\bigr]\;=\;\Pr\bigl[M(x)=y\bigr].

We argue by induction on the number of machine steps and construct a CoT whose token sequence mirrors the PTM’s execution.
Let

be the state set of

\Gamma
the work-tape alphabet, and
\Sigma
the input/output alphabet.

is the current state,

is the symbol written to the output tape (or
\varepsilon

Let the output string be

map each output index

to the index of step

in which

writes
y_{i}
to the output tape.

The precision of CoT is

s(n)\;=\;C\cdot\bigl\lceil\log_{2}n\bigr\rceil,

where

exceeds the maximum polynomial step bound under consideration.
For input

\Bigl(x,\;{\bm{r}}_{1:r(1)},\;e,\;y_{1},\;e^{\prime},\;{\bm{r}}_{r(1)}^{\prime},\;{\bm{r}}_{r(1)+1:r(2)},\;e,\;y_{2},\;e^{\prime},\;{\bm{r}}_{r(2)}^{\prime},\;\ldots,\;{\bm{r}}_{r(m-1)+1:r(m)},\;e,\;y_{m},\;e^{\prime}\Bigr),

where blocks of configuration tokens

{\mathcal{V}}\;\coloneq\;Q\times\Gamma\times\bigl(\Sigma\cup\{\varepsilon\}\bigr)\times\{-1,0,1\}\times\{r,r^{\prime}\}\;\;\cup\;\;\bigl(\Sigma\cup\{\varepsilon\}\bigr)\;\;\cup\;\;\{e,e^{\prime}\}.

\bm{e}_{Q}:Q\to\{0,1\}^{|Q|}

\bm{e}_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\to\{0,1\}^{|\Gamma|}
, and
\bm{e}_{\Sigma}:\Sigma\to\{0,1\}^{|\Sigma|}
denote one-hot encodings, and let
{\bm{p}}(\cdot)=\mathsf{sbin}_{s(n)}(\cdot)
be the positional code. We write
{\bm{0}}
for a zero block of appropriate size.
The embedding of an input token
x_{i}
is

\bigl(\bm{e}_{Q}(q^{(0)}),\;\bm{e}_{\Gamma}(x_{i}),\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Sigma|},\;0,\;{\bm{p}}(i),\;{\bm{0}}\bigr).

(87)

For a configuration token
{\bm{r}}_{i}=(q^{(i)},v^{(i)},w^{(i)},m^{(i)})
at position
n+j
, the embedding is

\bigl(\bm{e}_{Q}(q^{(i)}),\;\bm{e}_{\Gamma}(v^{(i)}),\;\bm{e}_{\Sigma}(w^{(i)}),\;m^{(i)},\;{\bm{p}}(j),\;{\bm{0}}\bigr).

(88)

For delimiters
d\in\{e,e^{\prime}\}
at position
n+j
, we use

\bigl({\bm{0}}_{|Q|},\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Gamma|},\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Sigma|},\;0,\;{\bm{p}}(j),\;{\bm{p}}(j-1),\;{\bm{p}}(j-3)\;{\bm{0}},\;{\bm{1}}[d=e],\;0,\;{\bm{1}}[d=e^{\prime}],\;{\bm{0}}_{|{\mathcal{V}}|}\bigr).

(89)

Finally, for an output token
y_{i}\in\Sigma
at position
n+j
, the embedding is

\bigl({\bm{0}}_{|Q|},\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Gamma|},\;\bm{e}_{\Sigma}(y_{i}),\;0,\;{\bm{p}}(j),\;{\bm{0}},\;0,\;1,\;0,\;{\bm{0}}_{|{\mathcal{V}}|}\bigr).

(90)

First layer

For each input position
i
, the model computes the head position of the Turing machine both at and immediately after the
i
-th step. This construction follows the approach of
Pérez et al. 2021
;
Merrill & Sabharwal 2024
;
Nowak et al. 2024
.
The next head position is obtained by summing the head movement directions
m^{(j)}\in\{-1,0,1\}
up to step
i
:

c^{(i+1)}\;=\;m^{(0)}+m^{(1)}+\cdots+m^{(i)}.

(91)

To realize this with single-head attention, we define the query, key, and value vectors at position
j
as

\mathbf{q}_{j}\;=\;(1),\quad\mathbf{k}_{j}\;=\;(1),\quad\mathbf{v}_{j}\;=\;(m^{(j)}).

(92)

Since the attention scores are uniform across all positions, the attention layer computes the cumulative sum of head movements.
A subsequent feed-forward layer then yields

c^{(i)}\;=\;c^{(i+1)}-m^{(i)}.

Thus, at token
{\bm{r}}_{k}
at position
n+i
, the representation is

\Bigl(\,\bm{e}(q^{(k)}),\;\bm{e}(v^{(k)}),\;\bm{e}(w^{(k)}),\;m^{(k)},\;{\bm{p}}(i),\;c^{(k)}\;{\bm{0}}\Bigr).

(93)

Second layer

For configuration
{\bm{r}}_{k}
, the model retrieves the symbol located under the head, denoted by
s^{(k)}
.
To accomplish this, we define an attention mechanism that filters positions
j\leq k
such that
c^{(j)}=c^{(k)}
, and selects the largest such index
j
.
Formally,

s^{(k)}\;=\;v^{(j^{\star})},\quad\text{where}\quad j^{\star}=\max\{\,j\leq k:c^{(j)}=c^{(k)}\,\}.

To implement this retrieval, we set the attention vectors at position
j
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{j}

\displaystyle=\Bigl({\mathsf{sbin}_{s(n)}(j)}^{\frown}{1_{s(n)}},\;T(n)\cdot j\Bigr),

(94)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{j}

\displaystyle=2^{s(n)+1}\cdot\Bigl({\mathsf{sbin}_{s(n)}(j)}^{\frown}{-1_{s(n)}},\;T(n)\cdot j\Bigr),\quad\mathbf{v}_{j}=\Bigl(\bm{e}_{\Gamma}(v^{(j)}),\;{\bm{0}}\Bigr).

(95)

Here, the construction guarantees that there are only
T(n)\cdot O(\log n)
distinct scores.
Although the resulting attention is not a perfect one-hot vector, the ordering of scores is preserved, so the maximum weight still corresponds to the correct index.
Consequently, the model can reliably retrieve a value that approximates
\bm{e}_{\Gamma}(s^{(k)})
.
The subsequent feed-forward layer computes the two transition functions
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
in parallel:

z^{(k+1)}_{j}\;=\;\bm{e}\big(\delta_{j}\bigl(q^{(k)},\,s^{(k)}\bigr)\big)\;\coloneq\;\bigl(\bm{e}_{Q}(q^{(k+1)}_{j}),\,\bm{e}_{\Gamma}(v^{(k+1)}_{j}),\,\bm{e}_{\Sigma}(w^{(k+1)}_{j}),\,m^{(k+1)}_{j}\bigr),\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

(96)

Finally, the overall output at token
{\bm{r}}_{k}
at position
n+i
is

\Bigl(\,\bm{e}(q^{(k)}),\;\bm{e}(v^{(k)}),\;\bm{e}(w^{(k)}),\;m^{(k)},\;{\bm{p}}(i),\,c^{(k)},\,s^{(k)},\,z^{(k+1)}_{1},\,z^{(k+1)}_{2},\,{\bm{0}}\Bigr),

(97)

where
z^{(k+1)}_{1}
and
z^{(k+1)}_{2}
represent the encodings of the two candidate transitions.

Third layer

This layer handles the delimiters.
When the last input token is
e
, the model copies the most recently written output symbol
y_{i}
from the immediately preceding position.
When the last input token is
e^{\prime}
, the model copies the most recent configuration
{\textnormal{v}}_{i}
from three positions earlier.
We implement this mechanism using two attention heads:

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{i,1}

\displaystyle\;=\;{\mathsf{sbin}_{s(n)}(i-1)}^{\frown}{1_{s(n)}},

(98)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{i,2}

\displaystyle\;=\;{\mathsf{sbin}_{s(n)}(i-3)}^{\frown}{1_{s(n)}},

(99)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{i,1}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbf{k}_{i,2}\;=\;2^{s(n)+1}\cdot{\mathsf{sbin}_{s(n)}(i)}^{\frown}{(-1_{s(n)})},

(100)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{i,1}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbf{v}_{i,2}\;=\;\bigl(\bm{e}(q^{(i)}),\,\bm{e}(v^{(i)}),\,\bm{e}(w^{(i)}),\,m^{(i)},\,{\bm{0}}\bigr).

(101)

The first head is configured to retrieve the symbol from one position earlier, while the second head retrieves the configuration from three positions earlier.
The following feed-forward map, conditioned on the one-hot flags indicating whether the input token is one of
\{{\bm{r}}_{i},\,e,\,y_{i},\,e^{\prime}\}
, outputs

\bigl(\,*,\;{\bm{z}}\bigr),

where
{\bm{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{|{\mathcal{V}}|}
is defined casewise as

{\bm{z}}\;=\;\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\bm{e}\bigl(z^{(k+1)}_{1}\bigr)+\bm{e}\bigl(z^{(k+1)}_{2}\bigr),\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Sigma|+4}\Bigr),&\text{if the input is }{\bm{r}}_{i}\text{ and }i\neq r(t)\ \forall t,\\
\Bigl({\bm{0}}_{|Q|\times|\Gamma|\times(|\Sigma|+1)\times 6},\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Sigma|+1},\;(1,0,0)\Bigr),&\text{if the input is }{\bm{r}}_{i}\text{ and }i=r(t)\ \text{for some }t,\\
\Bigl({\bm{0}}_{|Q|\times|\Gamma|\times(|\Sigma|+1)\times 6},\;\bm{e}_{\Sigma}(y_{i}),\;{\bm{0}}_{3}\Bigr),&\text{if the input is }e,\\
\Bigl({\bm{0}}_{|Q|\times|\Gamma|\times(|\Sigma|+1)\times 6},\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Sigma|+1},\;(0,0,1)\Bigr),&\text{if the input is }y_{i},\\
\Bigl(\bm{e}({\bm{r}}_{r(i)}^{\prime}),\;{\bm{0}}_{|\Sigma|+4}\Bigr),&\text{if the input is }e^{\prime}.\end{cases}

(102)

Here
\bm{e}(\cdot)
denotes one-hot encodings aligned with the corresponding slots of
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Output layer

The final output layer is

\mathbf{OUT}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{0}}&{\bm{I}}_{|{\mathcal{V}}|}\end{bmatrix}.

The decoding function generates tokens in two modes:
when the current token is
{\bm{r}}_{i}
, it stochastically samples from the two transitions
z^{(k+1)}_{1}
and
z^{(k+1)}_{2}
;
otherwise, it deterministically outputs
y_{i}
for
e
,
e^{\prime}
for
y_{i}
, and
{\bm{r}}_{r(i)}
for
e^{\prime}
.

∎

Proof for
Theorem

4.7
.

Under the assumption
\mathsf{FPTAS}\subsetneq\mathsf{FPRAS}
for self-reducible relations, let
R
denote a self-reducible relation whose counting problem
|R(x)|
admits an FPRAS but not an FPTAS.

Proof of the CoT Part

Define the extension counting function

\operatorname{EXT}_{R}:\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N},\qquad\operatorname{EXT}_{R}(x,w)\;=\;\bigl|\{\,z\in\Sigma^{*}:\langle x,wz\rangle\in R\,\}\bigr|.

First, we briefly show that if there exists an FPRAS for
|R(x)|
, then there also exists an FPRAS for
\operatorname{EXT}_{R}
, as shown by
(
Jerrum et al. 1986
)
.
Let
\mathcal{A}(x,\varepsilon,\delta)
denote an FPRAS for the counting problem

|R(x)|
, i.e., for any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
,

\Pr\!\left[(1-\varepsilon)\,|R(x)|\;\leq\;\mathcal{A}(x,\varepsilon,\delta)\;\leq\;(1+\varepsilon)\,|R(x)|\right]\;\geq\;1-\delta.

(103)

By self-reducibility, given
(x,w)
we proceed as follows:
while
|w|>\sigma(x)
, decompose
w=uv
with
|u|=\sigma(x)
, set

x\leftarrow\psi(x,u)
and
w\leftarrow v
. When the loop terminates, define

S:=\Sigma^{\sigma(x)-|w|}
. Then

\operatorname{EXT}_{R}(x,w)\;=\;\sum_{u\in S}|R(\psi(x,wu))|.

An
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-relative estimate of
\operatorname{EXT}_{R}(x,w)
is obtained by
replacing each summand with
\mathcal{A}\!\bigl(\psi(x,wu),\varepsilon,\delta/|S|\bigr)

and summing. A union bound guarantees overall relative error within

(1\pm\varepsilon)
with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Let
\widehat{\operatorname{EXT}}_{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
be these
(1\pm\varepsilon)
estimates, and define

\pi(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;:=\;\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{EXT}}_{R}(x,y_{<i}y_{i})}{\sum_{u\in\Sigma}\widehat{\operatorname{EXT}}_{R}(x,y_{<i}u)}\qquad(y_{i}\in\Sigma).

(104)

The target (uniform) conditional distribution is

p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;:=\;\frac{\operatorname{EXT}_{R}(x,y_{<i}y_{i})}{\sum_{u\in\Sigma}\operatorname{EXT}_{R}(x,y_{<i}u)}\qquad(y_{i}\in\Sigma).

(105)

On the event that all estimates in equation
104
are

(1\pm\varepsilon)
-accurate, we obtain

\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\,p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;\pi(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\,p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\qquad\text{for all }y_{i}\in\Sigma.

(106)

Choosing
\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{2\alpha(|x|)+1}
for any polynomial

\alpha(n)\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
yields

\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\alpha(|x|)},\qquad\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\geq 1-\frac{1}{\alpha(|x|)},

thus, we have an approximation to the true conditional distribution.

Finally, let
m(n)\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
be such that
|y|\leq m(|x|)
for all
y\in R(x)
.
Applying a union bound over the
m(n)
calls in
Equation

104
, if each call
uses failure probability
\delta\leq\tfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma}{m(|x|)}
for any fixed

\gamma\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, then the joint success event holds with probability at least

1-(|\Sigma|+1)\delta\geq\tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma
.
Thus there exists an FPRAS ensuring

\Pr\left[(1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha(|x|)})p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;\pi(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;(1+\tfrac{1}{\alpha(|x|)})p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\right]\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma.

(107)

By
Lemma

D.8
, any PTM that realizes an FPRAS can be simulated by a log-precision CoT model with stochastic decoding, whose output distribution coincides exactly with that of the PTM.
Hence, there exists a CoT model with polynomially many reasoning steps that achieves the
(\alpha,\gamma)
-weak probable approximation to the uniform distribution
U(R(x))
.

Proof of the Looped TF Part

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a Looped TF
\pi
with polynomial loops, which, for every input
x
, achieves an
(\alpha,\gamma)
-weak probable approximation to the uniform distribution
U(R(x))
with

\alpha(|x|)\;\geq\;\frac{\max\{\log(1+\varepsilon),\;\log(1-\varepsilon)+\tfrac{1}{2}\}}{m(|x|)},

(108)

where
m(n)\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
satisfies
|y|\leq m(|x|)
for any
y\in R(x)
, with
\varepsilon>0
and constant
\gamma<1/2
.

By definition of the model, all randomness is injected only during decoding. The logits, and thus the conditional distributions
\pi(\cdot\mid x,y_{<i})
, are deterministically computable in time
\mathsf{poly}(|x|)
. Hence, the guarantee reduces to a deterministic bound: for every
i
and prefix
y_{<i}
,

(1-1/\alpha(|x|))\,p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;\pi(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})\;\leq\;(1+1/\alpha(|x|))\,p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i}).

(109)

For a fixed string
\hat{y}\in R(x)
, define the polynomial-time computable quantity

\widehat{Z}(x)\;\coloneq\;\Biggl(\prod_{i=1}^{|\hat{y}|}\pi(\hat{y}_{i}\mid x,\hat{y}_{<i})\Biggr)^{-1}.

(110)

Since
p(y\mid x)=1/|R(x)|=\prod_{i}p(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i})
, we have

(1-1/\alpha(|x|))^{m}\;\leq\;\frac{\widehat{Z}(x)}{|R(x)|}\;\leq\;(1+1/\alpha(|x|))^{m}.

(111)

By
Lemma

D.4
, whenever
\varepsilon^{2}\leq m
,

e^{\,\varepsilon-\tfrac{1}{2}}\;\leq\;\Bigl(1+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{m}\Bigr)^{m}\;\leq\;e^{\,\varepsilon}.

(112)

Substituting
\varepsilon=\max\{\log(1+\varepsilon),\;\log(1-\varepsilon)+\tfrac{1}{2}\}
into the inequality, we have

(1-\varepsilon)\,|R(x)|\;\leq\;\widehat{Z}(x)\;\leq\;(1+\varepsilon)\,|R(x)|,

(113)

i.e., a deterministic
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-approximation to
|R(x)|
computable in time
\mathsf{poly}(|x|,1/\varepsilon)
.
This constitutes an
\mathsf{FPTAS}
, contradicting the assumption that
\mathsf{FPTAS}\subsetneq\mathsf{FPRAS}
.

From the preceding argument, there exist a polynomial function
\alpha(n)\in\mathsf{poly}(n)
and a constant
\gamma>0
such that, for any input
x
, any Looped TF with polynomial loops has a first-step conditional
\pi(\cdot\mid x)
that deviates from the target
p(\cdot\mid x)=U(R(x))
on a set of symbols of non-negligible mass.
Formally, define

A\;\coloneq\;\Bigl\{\,a\in\Sigma\;\Bigm|\;\frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{p(a\mid x)}\leq 1-\frac{1}{\alpha(|x|)}\;\;\text{or}\;\;\frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{p(a\mid x)}\geq 1+\frac{1}{\alpha(|x|)}\Bigr\},

(114)

and assume
p(A\mid x)\geq\gamma
. Let
q(y\mid x)\coloneq\prod_{k=1}^{m}\pi(y_{k}\mid x,y_{<k})
be the model’s full distribution over strings.
By data processing (marginalization cannot increase total variation),

\|q(\cdot\mid x)-p(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\;\geq\;\|\pi(\cdot\mid x)-p(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\bigl|\pi(a\mid x)-p(a\mid x)\bigr|.

(115)

For every
a\in A
we have

\bigl|\pi(a\mid x)-p(a\mid x)\bigr|=p(a\mid x)\,\bigl|\tfrac{\pi(a\mid x)}{p(a\mid x)}-1\bigr|\geq\tfrac{1}{\alpha(|x|)}\,p(a\mid x)
.
Hence

\|q(\cdot\mid x)-p(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in A}\frac{1}{\alpha(|x|)}\,p(a\mid x)\;\geq\;\frac{\gamma}{2\,\alpha(|x|)}.

(116)

Therefore, the total variation distance from
U(R(x))
is bounded below by
\gamma/(2\alpha(|x|))
and thus cannot be made arbitrarily small. In particular, such a model cannot realize an FPAUS for
U(R(x))
.
∎

Appendix E
Experimental Details

E.1
Parallelizable Tasks

E.1.1
Task Settings

Word Problem

We define a sequence prediction task based on finite groups such as the symmetric group
S_{5}
.
Given a sequence of group elements of length
k
, the model is required to output the cumulative products obtained by scanning the sequence from left to right.
Formally, for an input sequence
(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{k})
, the target sequence is

(g_{1},\;g_{1}g_{2},\;g_{1}g_{2}g_{3},\;\ldots,\;g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{k}).

We follow the setting of
Merrill & Sabharwal 2025b
.

Connectivity

To ensure the dataset has balanced labels, we generate undirected graphs according to the Erdős–Rényi model
(
Erdos & Renyi 1959
)

G(n,p)
, where
n
is the number of vertices and each possible edge is included independently with probability
p
. In the supercritical regime (
pn=c>1
), a single “giant” connected component emerges, occupying a fraction
s\in(0,1)
of the vertices, which satisfies
s=1-e^{-cs}.

Consequently, the probability that two uniformly random vertices are both in this component—and hence mutually reachable—is approximately
s^{2}
. To target a reachability probability of
1/2
, we set

s\approx\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}}\approx 0.707,\qquad c\approx\tfrac{-\ln(1-s)}{s}\approx 1.74,

and thus

p=\tfrac{c}{n}\approx\tfrac{1.7}{n}.

In practice, for each graph of size
n
we fix
p=1.7/n
, which empirically yields
\Pr[\text{reachable}]\approx 50\%
for
n\in[50,100]
, up to small fluctuations. Other random models (e.g., stochastic block models) can also be tuned to achieve such a balance, but the Erdős–Rényi choice offers the simplest parametrization.
We follow the encoding scheme of
Sanford et al. 2024a
.
The input to the model is serialized as a flat token sequence consisting of three parts:

\underbrace{v_{0}\,v_{1}\,\cdots\,v_{n-1}}_{\text{vertices}}\ \underbrace{e_{1}\,e_{2}\,\cdots\,e_{m}}_{\text{edges}}\ \underbrace{s,t}_{\text{query}},

where each vertex is denoted by a token
v_{i}
, each edge is represented as a pair “
u,v
” with
u<v
, and the final token “
s,t
” specifies the source–target pair for the reachability query.

Arithmetic Expression Evaluation

Following
Feng et al. 2023
, we generate expressions over integers modulo
r
using the four operations
{+,-,\times,\div}
, where multiplication and division are defined via precomputed modular tables. To guarantee that each expression evaluates to a specific target value, we grow expressions
backwards
: starting from a sampled number, we iteratively replace it with a binary sub-expression that preserves its value under modular arithmetic. Different from
Feng et al. 2023
, we fix the modulus to
r=3
, as our focus lies in evaluating the reasoning over expressions rather than exploring the properties of each modular arithmetic system.

Edit Distance

The Edit Distance task requires computing the minimum number of edit operations needed to transform one string into another. The allowed operations are insertion, deletion, and replacement of a single character, and the objective is to predict the total edit distance given two input strings.
To build the dataset, we follow
Feng et al. 2023
. We first generate two strings over a randomly sampled alphabet.
The first string has a fixed length, while the second string is produced in two possible ways: with probability
0.4
, it is drawn as a random string of nearly the same length (within
\pm 3
characters), and with probability
0.6
, it is derived from the first string by applying a given number of random edit operations.
Each edit operation is chosen uniformly among deletion, replacement, and insertion.
To avoid trivial cases, string pairs that are identical or whose lengths differ excessively are rejected and resampled.

E.1.2
Sequential Algorithms for CoT

(1) Word problem:
for this task, the CoT algorithm proceeds by sequentially scanning the token sequence and
producing at each prefix the evaluation result of the expression step by step. Thus, the overall length of the CoT sequence matches the input length.

(2) Graph connectivity:
Following
Bavandpour et al. 2025
, the algorithm sequence is simply the trace of a breadth-first search (BFS) starting from the source
s
. At each step, the model emits the incident edges of the currently expanded node in the order they are visited. The sequence terminates as soon as the target
t
is discovered. To implement this algorithm, we maintain a list (“scratchpad”) initialized
with a dummy marker and the source,
(\texttt{N},s)
. We iterate through this
list from left to right (i.e., queue order). Whenever the current node
u
is
expanded, we append to the end of the list all incident edges
(u,v)
for
neighbors
v
, optionally followed by a separator token
(u,\texttt{N})
.

(3) Arithmetic expression evaluation:
Following
Feng et al. 2023
, the CoT takes the fully expanded expression and repeatedly evaluates one innermost subexpression, writing down the simplified expression at each step until only a single numeral remains. For example,

2*(0+1)/2\to 2*1/2\to 2/2\to 1.

The overall CoT sequence has quadratic length.

(4) Edit distance:
Following
Feng et al. 2023
, the CoT algorithm outputs the DP table entries in the same order they are computed, i.e., row by row from top-left to bottom-right (topological order). This yields a quadratic-length CoT sequence.

E.1.3
Training Configuration

We trained all models using the AdamW optimizer with a linear learning rate schedule.
The initial learning rate was set to
1\times 10^{-4}
with a weight decay of
0.01
,
and a batch size of
256
. Training was continued until the training loss plateaued.
For Looped TFs, curriculum learning was applied to all tasks except edit distance: the input size was increased by
2
for the word problem task, and by
4
for the connectivity and arithmetic evaluation tasks.
The model architecture was based on standard Transformers with an embedding dimension of
256
.
We used
4
attention heads, and varied the number of Transformer layers depending on the task:
two layers for word problems, a single layer for connectivity,
and time-modulated
(
Xu & Sato 2025
)
model with a single layer for Looped TF on both arithmetic evaluation and the edit distance task. For CoT, we use the same configuration of the Transformer block.

E.2
Approximation Task

To generate the dataset, we first construct a DNF formula
F
by sampling
m
clauses,
each consisting of
w
distinct literals over
n
Boolean variables.
Each literal is independently assigned to be either positive or negated.
The formula is then serialized into a token sequence in which each clause is represented by its index together with variable–value pairs such as “
2=

+1
” or “
4=

-1
”.
For Looped TFs, we prepare
100{,}000
training samples and
1{,}000
test samples.
For CoT models, we instead generate an online dataset with the following structure.
To train the CoT models, we simulate a single trial of the randomized counting algorithm of
Karp & Luby 1983
as follows:

1.

A clause
C_{1}
is sampled with probability proportional to
2^{-|C_{j}|}
(uniform in our experiments).

2.

An assignment consistent with
C_{1}
is generated by fixing the variables in the clause
C_{1}
and randomly setting the remaining variables.

3.

A clause
C_{2}
is then selected uniformly at random, and the assignment is checked against it.

4.

If the assignment satisfies
C_{2}
, the outcome token is set to
Success
; otherwise,
Fail
.

The sequence concatenates the serialized formula, the sampled clause, the full assignment, and the verification outcome, separated by
<sep>
tokens and terminated by
<eos>
. The CoT model is trained in an autoregressive manner, where prediction targets are defined by shifting the token sequence while masking out the formula description.
We trained the models using the AdamW optimizer with a linear learning rate schedule.
The initial learning rate was set to
1\times 10^{-4}
, with a weight decay of
0.01
.
We used a batch size of
256
(reduced to
32
for the
1000
-loop setting)
and trained for
10{,}000
iterations.
