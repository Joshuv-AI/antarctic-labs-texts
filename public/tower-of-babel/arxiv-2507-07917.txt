Convergence rates for regularized unbalanced optimal transport: the discrete case
Luca Nenna, Paul Pegon and Louis Tocquec

Abstract
Unbalanced optimal transport (UOT) is a natural extension of optimal transport (OT) allowing comparison between measures of different masses. It arises naturally in machine learning by offering a robustness against outliers. The aim of this work is to provide convergence rates of the regularized transport cost and plans towards their original solution when both measures are weighted sums of Dirac masses.



Keywords.
optimal transport, unbalanced optimal transport, entropic regularization, convex analysis, Csiszàr divergence, asymptotic analysis.

1
Introduction

Given two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on compact domains
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a cost function
c\!:\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problem consists in solving

\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)

(1.1)

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of all probability measures on the product space
\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}
having
\mu
and
\nu
as marginals.
A popular way to solve numerically this problem consists in adding an entropy regularizing term, tuned via a nonnegative parameter
t
, which leads to the following
entropic optimal transport
problem

\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}t\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\right)+\entropy(\gamma\lvert\mu\!\otimes\!\nu),

(1.2)

where
\entropy
is the relative entropy. In the literature, one often finds a parameter
\varepsilon\geq 0
placed in front of the entropy rather than in front of the cost function. In this case, the focus is on the limit as
\varepsilon
vanishes. These two formulations are equivalent as long as the parameters remain positive, corresponding in our case to
t^{-1}=\varepsilon
and letting
t\to+\infty
.

Over the last decade this kind of approach has witnessed an impressive increasing interest since it has proved to be an efficient way to approximate OT problems. From the computational point of view,
1.2
can be solved by an iterative projections method, which turns out to correspond to the celebrated Sinkhorn’s algorithm
[
Sin67
,
Sin64
]
, successfully developed in the pioneering works
[
Cut13
,
GS09
,
Ben+15
]

The simplicity of the implementation and the good convergence guarantees
[
FL89
,
MG20
]
determined the success of the entropic optimal transport for a wide range of applications, see for instance
[
PC+19
]
and the references therein.
Notice now if we look at
1.2
as a perturbation of
1.1
, then it is quite natural to study the behavior of both optimal values and minimizers, when the regularization parameter varies, and in particular when
t\to+\infty
.
According to this let us mention some related works
[
Mik90
,
Léo12
,
Léo14
,
Car+17
]
, which established
\Gamma
-convergence results of
1.2
to
1.1
,
[
Pal19
,
CT21
,
ANS22
,
EN22
,
CPT23
,
MS23
,
NP24
]
, which provided asymptotic expansion for the cost. Here we want to focus on the convergence rate of the primal and dual variable in the spirit of
[
CM94
,
Wee18
]
in the discrete setting for a generalized OT problem: unbalanced OT.

By definition, classical optimal transport requires the two given measures to have the same total mass, which can be a limitation for certain practical applications, particularly in machine learning, or imaging. An extension of the classical framework, known as unbalanced optimal transport, addresses this limitation by relaxing the marginal constraints and incorporating a divergence term into the objective function to penalise the marginals. It was simultaneously introduced by
[
Chi+18a
,
LMS18
,
KMV16
]
and may be expressed in the following form,

\boxed{\inf\limits_{\gamma\in\mathscr{M}^{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)+\mathrm{D}_{\phi}(\gamma_{1}\lvert\mu)+\mathrm{D}_{\phi}(\gamma_{2}\lvert\nu)},

(1.3)

where
\gamma_{i}
is the
i
-th marginal of the coupling
\gamma
and
\mathrm{D}_{\phi}
is a
\phi
-divergence, also known as Csiszàr divergence (see
Definition

2.4
).

As in the usual optimal transport problem, one can introduce an entropy regularized version of the unbalanced optimal transport problem, as follows

\boxed{\inf_{\gamma\in\mathscr{M}^{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}t\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}c(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)+\mathrm{D}_{\phi}(\gamma_{1}\lvert\mu)+\mathrm{D}_{\phi}(\gamma_{2}\lvert\nu)\right)+\entropy(\gamma|\mu\!\otimes\!\nu)},

(1.4)

This paper focuses on this problem when both measures have finite support. The aim is to establish convergence rates of the regularized optimal variables, for the primal and dual problems, towards the original ones. The main contributions can be summarized in the following two theorems.

Theorem A
.

(Simplified statement of
Theorem

4.11
)

For positive
t
, let
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
be the curve of optimal solutions to the regularized unbalanced optimal transport problem
1.4
.
Under the assumption that
\mu
and
\nu
are atomic measures, and given certain conditions, the trajectory
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
converges towards an optimizer
\gamma^{*}
of
1.3
at a rate of at least
1/\sqrt{t}
, i.e.

\lVert\gamma^{*}-\gamma(t)\rVert\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}.

Theorem B
.

(Simplified statement of
Theorem

4.9
)

For positive
t
, let
t\mapsto\xi(t)
be the curve of optimal solutions to the regularized dual problem to
1.4
.
Under the assumption that
\mu
and
\nu
are atomic measures, and given certain conditions, the dual trajectory
t\mapsto\xi(t)
converges towards the dual optimizer
\xi^{*}
at a rate of at least
1/t

\lVert\xi^{*}-\xi(t)\rVert\lesssim\frac{1}{t}.

2
Discrete unbalanced optimal transport

2.1
The setting

Since we study the case of measures
\mu,\nu
with finite support, we assume from now on that
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are finite sets. Notice that measures
\mu\in\mathscr{M}^{+}(\mathcal{X})
,
\nu\in\mathscr{M}^{+}(\mathcal{Y})
and
\gamma\in\mathscr{M}^{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
can now be written as

\mu=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{x}\delta_{x},\quad\nu=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\nu_{y}\delta_{y},\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma=\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}\!\gamma_{x,y}\delta_{(x,y)},

and can thus be identified with their respective weight vectors
(\mu_{x})_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
,
(\nu_{y})_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
and
(\gamma_{x,y})_{(x,y)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
. In the same way, the cost
c
can be identified with the vector
(c(x,y))_{(x,y)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
. By introducing the linear map
A\!:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
defined by

A\gamma=\left(\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\gamma_{x,y}\right)_{x\in\mathcal{X}},\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{x,y}\right)_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\right),

one can re-formulated the constraint
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
as
A\gamma=(\mu,\nu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}q
and the optimal transport problem and its regularized formulation can then be expressed as finite-dimensional problems as follows,

\inf_{\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{\langle c|\gamma\rangle\>|\>A\gamma=q\right\},

(
OT
)

and for any non-negative
t
,

\inf_{\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{\mathcal{X}}}\left\{t\langle c|\gamma\rangle+\entropy(\gamma)\>|\>A\gamma=q\right\},

(
\text{EOT}_{t}
)

where
\langle c|\gamma\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}c(x,y)\gamma_{x,y}
denotes the canonical inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}
and
\entropy\!:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}
is the discrete entropy, i.e. the entropy relative with respect to the counting measure
\mathcal{H}^{0}
defined by

\entropy(\gamma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\entropy(\gamma|\mathcal{H}^{0})=\begin{cases}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\gamma_{x,y}(\log\gamma_{x,y}-1)&if $\gamma_{x,y}\geq 0\;\text{for every }(x,y)$\\
+\infty&otherwise,\end{cases}

with a slight abuse of notation.

Remark 2.1
.

We take the convention that
0\log 0=0
providing a continuous extension of
t\mapsto t\log t
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Notice that, in the case where
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X)}
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y)}
, the entropy relative with respect to the product measure
\mu\otimes\!\nu
and the discrete entropy satisfy the following relation:

\inf\lx@cref{refnum}{DEOT_t}=\inf\lx@cref{refnum}{intro:entropic}-\entropy(\mu)-\entropy(\nu)-2.

One can also express problems
OT
and

EOT
t

respectively as follows,

\inf_{\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{\langle c|\gamma\rangle\;|\;A\gamma=q\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{t\langle c|\gamma\rangle+\entropy(\gamma)\;|\;A\gamma=q\right\}.

Remark 2.2
.

Assuming that
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are finite subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, say
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\cdots,y_{M}\}
, the linear map
A
can be associated with its matrix
A_{mat}
in the bases
\mathcal{B}=(e_{x_{1},y_{1}},e_{x_{1},y_{2}},\cdots,e_{x_{n},y_{M}})
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=(e_{x_{1}},\cdots,e_{x_{n}},e_{y_{1}},\cdots e_{y_{M}})
defined as

A_{mat}=\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}_{M}&0_{M}&\cdots&0_{M}&\quad\textnormal{I}_{M}\quad\\
0_{M}&\mathbb{1}_{M}&\cdots&0_{M}&\textnormal{I}_{M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\quad\vdots\quad\\
0_{M}&0_{M}&\cdots&\mathbb{1}_{M}&\quad\textnormal{I}_{M}\quad\\
\end{pmatrix}\end{array}^{T}.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
t>0
,

EOT
t

and its dual admit a unique solution
\gamma_{t}
and
\xi_{t}
(up to the kernel of
A^{*}
, which has dimension
1
, for
\xi_{t}
), respectively. Moreover, the following relation holds

\gamma_{t}=e^{t(A^{*}\xi_{t}-c)}.

We will not detail the proof since it is well known in the literature (for instance it is a by-product of the result in
[
PC+19
]
).

The unbalanced optimal transport problem is a natural extension of classical optimal transport problem where one can consider measures of different masses by relaxing the mass conservation constraint, for more details we refer the reader to the seminal works
[
Chi+18a
,
Chi+18
,
LMS18
,
KMV16
]
. This approach, in particular, allows the creation or destruction of mass. To formalize this problem, we introduce a function
\F\!:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
which is used as a penalization on the marginals of the transport coupling. The unbalanced optimal transport problem then reads as:

\boxed{\inf_{\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{\langle c|\gamma\rangle+\F(A\gamma)\right\}}.

(
UOT
)

In the same way as before, we consider a regularized version of this optimization problem which is given, for any non-negative
t
, by

\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{t\langle c|\gamma\rangle+t\F(A\gamma)+\entropy(\gamma)\right\}.

For practical reasons, it will often be more convenient to work with its re-scaled version, defined for any positive
t
as

\boxed{\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{\langle c|\gamma\rangle+\F(A\gamma)+\frac{1}{t}\entropy(\gamma)\right\}}.

(
\text{UOT}_{t}
)

For any positive
t
, we denote by
\mathscr{C}_{t}
the functional of the regularized unbalanced optimal transport problem, i.e.,

\mathscr{C}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\langle c|\gamma\rangle+\F(A\gamma)+\frac{1}{t}\entropy(\gamma).

Throughout all this paper we will use the following assumptions for
\F
:

1.

\F
is a proper convex and lower semi-continuous function;

2.

There exists
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{\mathcal{Y}}
such that
\F((\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}))<+\infty
.

In the literature, for instance
[
SVP22
]
,
\F
is often taken to be a Csiszàr
\phi
-divergence defined as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

(Discrete Csiszàr
\phi
-divergence)

Let
p,\,q\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{N}
be two non-negative vectors and
\phi\!:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
an entropy function meaning that it is a convex and lower semi-continuous function such that
\phi(1)=0
. The discrete Csiszàr
\phi
-divergence is defined as

\mathrm{D}_{\phi}(p\>|\>q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i\,:\,q_{i}>0}q_{i}\phi\bigg(\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\bigg)+\phi^{\prime}_{\infty}\sum_{i\,:\,q_{i}=0}p_{i},

where
\phi_{\infty}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi(t)/t
is the recession constant.

Remark 2.5
.

The choice of considering a general function
\F
instead of the Csiszàr divergence is motivated by the fact that one can also consider variational problems given by the sum of an optimal transport term and a congestion on the marginal. For this reason, in the following, we are going to make assumptions only on the function
\F
that one can easily translate as assumptions on the entropy function
\phi
when
\F(\cdot)=\mathrm{D}_{\phi}(\cdot|q)
.

Remark 2.6
.

In the case where
\F(\cdot)=\mathrm{D}_{\phi}(\cdot|q)
with fixed
q
, assuming that
\phi
is a proper, convex, and lower semi-continuous function is sufficient to ensure
item

1
. The existence of some
\alpha>0
such that
\phi(\alpha)<+\infty
guarantees
item

2
. Indeed, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, define

\gamma_{1}^{x}=\begin{cases}\mu_{x}\alpha&\text{if }\mu_{x}>0,\\
\alpha&\text{if }\mu_{x}=0,\end{cases}

and build
\gamma_{2}
similarly. This ensures that
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{\mathcal{X}}\oplus(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{\mathcal{Y}}
and that
\F((\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}))<+\infty
. Moreover, if
\phi
is also superlinear (see
definition

3.1
), which implies that
\phi^{\prime}_{\infty}=+\infty
, then it is necessary that
q\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{\mathcal{X}}\oplus(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{\mathcal{Y}}
.

2.2
Properties of the regularized unbalanced optimal transport

Before studying in more details the properties of the solutions let us give some results concerning the primal and dual unbalanced problems. We introduce
A^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}
as the adjoint operator of
A
defined for any
\xi=(\varphi,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
as

A^{*}\xi=\varphi\oplus\psi=(\varphi_{x}+\psi_{y})_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}.

We start our analysis by introducing the dual formulations of
UOT
and

UOT
t

.

Proposition 2.7
.

Under assumptions
item

1
and
item

2
, the dual of
UOT
is given by

\min_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{\F^{*}\!\left(-\xi\right)\>|\>A^{*}\xi\preceq c\right\}.

(
Dual
)

Moreover, if
\F
is assumed to be coercive then strong duality holds, that is

\min_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{\F^{*}\!\left(-\xi\right)\>|\>A^{*}\xi\preceq c\right\}=-\min_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}}\langle c|\gamma\rangle+F(A\gamma).

Proof 2.8
.

We begin by introducing the function
G:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

G(\gamma)=\langle c,\gamma\rangle+\iota_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}}(\gamma),

where
\iota_{(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}}
denotes the indicator function of the non-negative orthant in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
. With this definition, the unbalanced optimal transport
UOT
problem can be written as

\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}}G(\gamma)+\F(A\gamma).

By the Fenchel–Rockafellar duality theorem, strong duality holds, and there exists a solution to the dual problem, which is given by

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}-G^{*}(A^{*}\xi)-\F^{*}(-\xi)=\max_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}-G^{*}(A^{*}\xi)-\F^{*}(-\xi),

where
G^{*}
and
\F^{*}
are the Legendre–Fenchel transforms of
G
and
\F
, respectively.
Now, assume that
\F
is coercive. Consider a sequence
(\gamma^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
such that
\|\gamma^{n}\|\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
. Since
c\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
, we have

\langle c,\gamma^{n}\rangle+\F(A\gamma^{n})\geq\F(A\gamma^{n}).

Moreover, because
A
is injective (i.e.,
\ker A=\{0\}
), it follows that
\|A\gamma^{n}\|\to+\infty
, and hence the coercivity of
\F
implies

\F(A\gamma^{n})\to+\infty.

Therefore, the objective functional is coercive, and it follows that the primal problem admits a solution.

Definition 2.9
.

Take
\varphi
and
\psi
solution to
Dual
then we denote by
I_{0}
the set of saturated constraint,

I_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{(x,y)\!\in\!\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}\>|\>\varphi_{x}^{*}+\psi_{y}^{*}=c(x,y)\}.

The slack variable
\kappa
is the gap between
A^{*}\xi^{*}
and

. For any

\kappa_{x,y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}c(x,y)-\varphi_{x}^{*}-\psi_{y}^{*}.

Proposition 2.10
.

For any positive
t
, and under assumptions
item

1
and
item

2
, the problem

UOT
t

admits a unique solution. Moreover, strong duality holds, and the corresponding dual problem is given by

\min_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\,\F^{*}(-\xi)+\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{t}\exp\left(t\langle A^{*}\xi-c|e_{x,y}\rangle\right),

(
\text{Dual}_{t}
)

where
(e_{x,y})_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
. The dual problem admits a solution, and if
\F^{*}
is strictly convex, then it is unique.

Proof 2.11
.

Let
t>0
. Since
\F
is convex, there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
such that for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
, we have

\F(\xi)\geq\langle a,\xi\rangle+b.

This implies that

\mathscr{C}_{t}(\gamma)=\langle c,\gamma\rangle+\F(A\gamma)+\frac{1}{t}\,\entropy(\gamma)\geq\langle c+A^{*}a,\gamma\rangle+\frac{1}{t}\,\entropy(\gamma)+b.

Using the superlinearity of the entropy functional
\entropy
, we deduce that
\mathscr{C}_{t}
is coercive. Therefore, the rescaled regularized unbalanced optimal transport problem

UOT
t

admits at least one solution. Moreover, since
\entropy
is strictly convex, it follows that
\mathscr{C}_{t}
is strictly convex as well, implying that the solution is unique.
Now, let
\gamma_{t}
be the unique minimizer of

UOT
t

. Then, by first-order optimality conditions,

0\in\partial\mathscr{C}_{t}(\gamma_{t}).

Define the functional
G_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}
by

G_{t}(\gamma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\langle c,\gamma\rangle+\frac{1}{t}\,\entropy(\gamma).

Then the optimality condition becomes

0\in A^{*}\,\partial\F(A\gamma_{t})+\partial G_{t}(\gamma_{t}),

where we used assumption
item

2
(see
[
Roc70
]
[Theorem 23.8]). Therefore, there exists
u\in\partial\F(A\gamma_{t})
and
v\in\partial G_{t}(\gamma_{t})
such that

0=A^{*}u+v.

By the Fenchel–Young equality, we have

\F(A\gamma_{t})+\F^{*}(u)=\langle u,A\gamma_{t}\rangle,\quad\text{and}\quad G_{t}(\gamma_{t})+G_{t}^{*}(v)=\langle\gamma_{t},v\rangle=\langle\gamma_{t},-A^{*}u\rangle=-\langle u,A\gamma_{t}\rangle.

Summing the two equalities yields:

\F(A\gamma_{t})+G_{t}(\gamma_{t})+\F^{*}(u)+G_{t}^{*}(-A^{*}u)=0,

from which we get

\F(A\gamma_{t})+G_{t}(\gamma_{t})=-\F^{*}(u)-G_{t}^{*}(-A^{*}u),

and this concludes the proof.

3.

Proposition 3.4

Proof 3.5
.

Let

\F^{*}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle v|\xi\rangle-\F(v)\leq\max\left\{\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\domain\F\\

Lemma 3.9

(Uniform coercivity of

)
.

Under assumptions

and

Proof 3.10
.

For any

\displaystyle\inf_{t>0}\mathscr{K}_{t}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\F^{*}\left(-\xi\right)+\inf_{t>0}\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}\!\frac{1}{t}e^{t(\varphi_{x}+\psi_{y}-c(x,y))}

\displaystyle\geq\F^{*}\left(-\xi\right)+\inf_{t>0}\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{t}e^{t\left(\varphi_{x}+\psi_{y}-c(x,y)\right)_{+}}

\displaystyle\geq\F^{*}\left(-\xi\right)+\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}\left(\varphi_{x}+\psi_{y}-c(x,y)\right)_{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}V(\xi).

Let us show that

is coercive. Let
(\xi^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=((\varphi^{n},\psi^{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in

such that
\|\xi^{n}\|\to+\infty
as

. On one hand, if there exists

such that, after taking a subsequence,

then we have, using

4

such that

. It leads to

On the other hand, if that is not the case, then for any

is bounded from below by some constant

. Thus there exists

d(t)/t=\xi(t)-\xi^{*}\to 0
and
-\xi^{*}\in\domain\F
. Thus, by
5
, for some
r>\varepsilon
and all
t\geq r
, we have

\F^{*}\left(-\frac{1}{t}d(t)-\xi^{*}\right)\geq\F^{*}\left(-\xi^{*}\right)-\frac{1}{t}\langle D\!\F^{*}\left(-\xi^{*}\right)|d(t)\rangle+\frac{\lambda_{0}(-\xi^{*},r)}{2t^{2}}\lVert d(t)\rVert^{2},

(4.2)

where
\lambda_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\lambda_{0}(-\xi^{*},r)>0
, and by
6
and Taylor’s expansion, we have

\F^{*}\left(-\frac{1}{t}d^{(0)}(t)-\xi^{*}\right)\leq\F^{*}\left(-\xi^{*}\right)-\frac{1}{t}\langle D\F^{*}\left(-\xi^{*}\right)|d^{(0)}(t)\rangle+\frac{C}{t^{2}}

(4.3)

for some
C>0
and every
t\geq r
. By
Lemma

4.1
, we have

\langle D\!\F^{*}\!\left(-\xi^{*}\right)|d(t)\rangle=\langle D\!\F^{*}\!\left(-\xi^{*}\right)|d^{(0)}(t)\rangle,

and combining this with
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
, we obtain

\lambda_{0}\lVert d(t)\rVert^{2}\leq\sum_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}2te^{\langle A^{*}d^{(0)}(t)-t\kappa|e_{x,y}\rangle}+C.

We have seen that
d^{(0)}(t)
is bounded, and since
\kappa_{x,y}>0
for every
(x,y)\not\in I_{0}
, the right-hand side is bounded, thus
d(t)
is bounded as well for
t\geq r\geq\varepsilon
. We obtain the result since
d
is bounded on any compact interval included in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, by regularity of
t\mapsto\xi(t)
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Under assumptions
\textnormal{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{H1}}-\textnormal{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{H6}}
, the trajectory
t\mapsto d(t)
converges towards a finite quantity denoted
d^{*}
.

Before going into the proof, we introduce the following useful lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
E^{\prime}
be a normed vector space,
f\!:E^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
a strictly convex function and
L\!:E\to E^{\prime}
a linear operator and
F
an affine subspace of direction
\vec{F}
. If the set of solutions
\mathcal{U}
of the problem

\inf_{x\in F}f(Lx)

is nonempty, then for any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
, one has

\mathcal{U}=x_{0}+\left(\vec{F}\cap\ker L\right).

Proof 4.7
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
. The affine subspace
F
can be expressed as
F=x_{0}+\vec{F}
. Then, any
x_{1}\in\mathcal{U}
can be expressed as
x_{1}=x_{0}+y
, with
y\in\vec{F}
. Assuming that
y\notin\ker L
,
Lx_{1}\leq Lx_{2}
and by strict convexity of
f
we have

f(Lx_{0})=\frac{1}{2}f(Lx_{0})+\frac{1}{2}f(Lx_{1})>f(Lx_{2}),

where
x_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(x_{0}+x_{1})/2=x_{0}+\frac{1}{2}y
.
Since
x_{2}\in F
, this contradicts the optimality of
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
. Therefore
y\in\ker L
.

Conversely, let
x_{1}\in F
defined as
x_{1}=x_{0}+y
, with
y\in\vec{F}\cap\ker L
.
Then, since
f(Lx_{1})=f(Lx_{0})
, it follows that
x_{1}\in\mathcal{U}
.

Proof 4.8

(Proof of
Proposition

4.5
)
.

Let
d^{*}
be an accumulation point of the curve
t\mapsto d(t)
at
+\infty
, meaning that
d(t_{n})\to d^{*}
as
n\to+\infty
for some sequence
t_{n}\to+\infty
. We know that there exists such an accumulation point thanks to
Proposition

4.3
. Since
\xi(t_{n})
is a minimizer of

Dual
t

for
t=t_{n}
, we obtain the following equation:

D\!\F^{*}\!\left(-\frac{1}{t_{n}}d(t_{n})-\xi^{*}\right)+\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}}e^{\langle A^{*}d(t_{n})-t_{n}\kappa|e_{x,y}\rangle}Ae_{x,y}=0.

Taking the limit in this expression leads to

D\!\F^{*}(-\xi^{*})+\sum_{(x,y)\in I_{0}}e^{\langle A^{*}d^{*}|e_{x,y}\rangle}Ae_{x,y}=0.

We recognize that is the first order optimality condition of the following differentiable convex function

\mathrm{G}:z\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\mapsto\langle D\!\F^{*}(-\xi^{*})|z\rangle+\sum_{(x,y)\in I_{0}}e^{\langle A^{*}z|e_{x,y}\rangle},

so that
d^{*}
is a minimizer of
\mathrm{G}
. Notice that by
Lemma

4.1
,
\langle D\!\F^{*}(-\xi^{*})|z\rangle
is a linear function of the quantities
\langle A^{*}z\,|\,\mathopen{}e_{x,y}\rangle
for
(x,y)\in I_{0}
, and since the exponential is strictly convex, we may apply
Lemma

4.6
to assert that the set of optimal solutions
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathrm{G}
is given by

\mathcal{U}=d^{*}+\bigcap\limits_{(x,y)\in I_{0}}\ker Ae_{x,y}.

Now, let us introduce
d_{1}^{*}\in\mathcal{U}
such that
d_{1}^{*}\neq d^{*}
. We define
d_{1}(t)
by

d_{1}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}d(t)+d_{1}^{*}-d^{*}

which satisfies
d_{1}(t_{n})\rightarrow d_{1}^{*}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

We know that
d(t)
is a minimizer solution of the functional
\tilde{\mathscr{K}}_{t}
, thus

\tilde{\mathscr{K}}(d(t_{n}))\leq\tilde{\mathscr{K}}(d_{1}(t_{n})),

or equivalently

\F^{*}\left(-\frac{1}{t_{n}}d(t_{n})-\xi^{*}\right)+\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\!\frac{1}{t_{n}}e^{\langle A^{*}d(t_{n})|e_{x,y}\rangle}\\
\leq\F^{*}\left(-\frac{1}{t_{n}}d_{1}(t_{n})-\xi^{*}\right)+\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{t_{n}}e^{\langle A^{*}d_{1}(t_{n})|e_{x,y}\rangle}.

Since
d(t_{n})
and
d_{1}(t_{n})
are bounded, we can write the second order Taylor expansion of
F^{*}
at
-\xi^{*}
to get

\frac{\lVert d(t_{n})\rVert_{D^{2}\F^{*}(-\xi^{*})}^{2}}{2}+\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\!t_{n}e^{\langle A^{*}d(t_{n})-t_{n}\kappa|e_{x,y}\rangle}\\
\leq\frac{\lVert d_{1}(t_{n})\rVert_{D^{2}\F^{*}(-\xi^{*})}^{2}}{2}+\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}t_{n}e^{\langle A^{*}d_{1}(t_{n})-t_{n}\kappa|e_{x,y}\rangle}+o(1),

noticing that the first-order term cancelled thanks to
Lemma

4.1
. Besides, thanks to the same lemma and the fact that
d(t_{n})-d_{1}(t_{n})\in\bigcap_{(x,y)\in I_{0}}\ker Ae_{x,y}
, the terms corresponding to
(x,y)\in I_{0}
in the sum are equal on both sides. By non-negativity of the exponential, we forget the terms corresponding to
(x,y)\not\in I_{0}
in the left-hand side, and we get:

\|d(t_{n})\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi^{*})}^{2}\leq\|d_{1}(t_{n})\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi^{*})}^{2}+\!\sum\limits_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\!\frac{2}{t}e^{\langle Ad_{1}(t_{n})-t\kappa|e_{x,y}\rangle}+o(1).

Passing to the limit, we obtain

\|d^{*}\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi^{*})}^{2}\leq\|d_{1}^{*}\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi^{*})}^{2}.

Thus, we have shown that
d^{*}
is the unique solution of the strictly convex (by
5
) problem:

\inf\limits_{z\in\mathcal{U}}\>\|z\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi^{*})}^{2}.

Since every accumulation point of the trajectory is also a solution, it follows that there is an unique accumulation point
d^{*}
.

Now, one shall compute the derivative of
d(t)
with respect to
t
. A simple computation gives us that
\dot{d}(t)=\frac{1}{t}d(t)+t\dot{\xi}(t)
. Notice that this quantity consists of two parts:
d(t)
, which converges by the previous proposition, and
\dot{\xi}(t)
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Under assumptions
\textnormal{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{H1}}-\textnormal{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{H6}}
, the convergence rate of the curve
t\mapsto\xi(t)
is at least of order
O(t^{-1})
, i.e. for any
t
sufficiently large, one has

\|\xi(t)-\xi^{*}\|\lesssim\frac{1}{t}.

Proof 4.10
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
t\geq\varepsilon
. We define the functional
\Lambda_{t}
by

\Lambda_{t}(\xi)=\|A^{*}\xi+A^{*}\xi^{*}-c\|_{\diagonal(\gamma(t))}^{2}+\frac{1}{t}\|\xi-\xi(t)+\xi^{*}\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))}^{2}.

Note that
\dot{d}(t)
is optimal for
\Lambda_{t}
, since the latter is convex and

\displaystyle D\Lambda_{t}(\xi)

\displaystyle=2A\diagonal(\gamma(t))(A^{*}\xi+A^{*}\xi^{*}-c)+\frac{2}{t}D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))(\xi-\xi(t)+\xi^{*})

and thus

\displaystyle D\Lambda_{t}(\dot{d}(t))

\displaystyle=2t\left(A\diagonal(\gamma(t))A^{*}\dot{\xi}(t)+\frac{1}{t}D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))\dot{\xi}(t)+\frac{1}{t^{2}}A\diagonal(\gamma(t))\log\gamma(t)\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Using this property, we get the following inequality

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}(\dot{d}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda_{t}(0)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\>\>\,\frac{1}{t}\|t\dot{\xi}(t)\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)\langle A^{*}\xi^{*}-c|e_{x,y}\rangle^{2}+\frac{1}{t}\|\xi(t)-\xi^{*}\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\>\>\>\|\dot{\xi}(t)\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)\langle A^{*}\xi^{*}-c|e_{x,y}\rangle^{2}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\|\xi(t)-\xi^{*}\|_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\>\>\>\|\dot{\xi}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\frac{C_{1}}{\lambda_{\min}(t)}\max_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)+\frac{1}{t^{4}}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(t)}{\lambda_{\min}(t)}\|d(t)\|^{2},

where
C_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\kappa_{x,y}^{2}
and
\lambda_{\min}(t)>0
and
\lambda_{\max}(t)<+\infty
are respectively the smallest and the largest eigenvalues of
D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))
. We have already seen that the trajectory
t\mapsto d(t)
is bounded for
t\geq\varepsilon
which implies that there exists
C_{2}>0
such that, for any
(x,y)\notin I_{0}
, one has

\gamma_{x,y}(t)=e^{t\langle A^{*}\xi(t)-c|e_{x,y}\rangle}=e^{\langle A^{*}d(t)|e_{x,y}\rangle}e^{-t\kappa_{x,y}}\leq C_{2}e^{-t\kappa^{*}},

where
\kappa^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\min\limits_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\kappa_{x,y}
. One obtains

\|\dot{\xi}(t)\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{t}Ce^{-t\kappa^{*}}+\frac{1}{t^{4}}K,

where

C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{t>\varepsilon}\frac{C_{1}C_{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(t)}<+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{t>\varepsilon}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(t)}{\lambda_{\min}(t)}\|d(t)\|^{2}<+\infty.

Moreover,
t\mapsto t^{-1}e^{-t\kappa^{*}}
converges exponentially fast towards
0
and thus
t^{-1}e^{-t\kappa^{*}}\lesssim t^{-4}
when
t
is sufficiently large. Consequently, we have as
t
goes to
+\infty

\|\dot{\xi}(t)\|\lesssim t^{-2},

which leads to

\|\xi^{*}-\xi(t)\|\leq\int_{t}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\xi}(\omega)\|\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\lesssim\int_{t}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\lesssim\frac{1}{t}.

4.2
Asymptotic analysis of the regularized transport plan

Theorem 4.11
.

Under assumptions
\textnormal{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{H1}}-\textnormal{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{H6}}
, the convergence rate of the curve
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
is at least of order
O(t^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, i.e. for any
t
sufficiently large, one has

\|\gamma(t)-\gamma^{*}\|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}.

Proof 4.12
.

Let
t>0
sufficiently large in order to have
\|\xi(t)-\xi^{*}\|\lesssim t^{-1}
as shown in
Theorem

4.9
. We have seen in
Proposition

3.13
that
\dot{\xi}
solves
ODE
, which can be recognized as the first-order optimality condition of the following convex optimization problem

\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\Psi_{t}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\inf\limits_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\!\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\|A^{*}\xi+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\log\gamma(t)\|^{2}_{\diagonal(\gamma(t))}+\frac{1}{t}\|\xi\|^{2}_{D^{2}\!\F^{*}\!(-\xi(t))}.

We compare
\dot{\xi}(t)
with the competitor
h(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\xi^{*}-\xi(t)}{t}
to obtain

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(\dot{\xi}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\Psi_{t}(h(t))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)\left(\langle A^{*}h(t)\,|\,\mathopen{}e_{x,y}\rangle+\frac{\log\gamma_{x,y}(t)}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{t}\|h(t)\|^{2}_{D^{2}\mathrm{F}^{*}(-\xi(t))}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)\langle A^{*}\xi^{*}-A^{*}\xi(t)+A^{*}\xi(t)-c|e_{x,y}\rangle^{2}+\frac{1}{t^{3}}\|\xi^{*}-\xi(t)\|^{2}_{D^{2}\mathrm{F}^{*}(-\xi(t))}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)\langle A^{*}\xi^{*}-c|e_{x,y}\rangle^{2}+\frac{1}{t^{3}}\lambda_{\max}(t)\|\xi^{*}-\xi(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{2}}\max\limits_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)+\frac{1}{t^{5}},

where the last inequality is obtained using that
\sum_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\langle A^{*}\xi^{*}\!-\!c|e_{x,y}\rangle^{2}
and
\lambda_{\max}(t)
, the largest eigenvalue of
D^{2}\mathrm{F}^{*}\!(-\xi(t))
, are bounded quantities. From this, for any
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\!\times\!\mathcal{Y}
, one can obtain

\gamma_{x,y}(t)\langle A^{*}\dot{\xi}(t)+\frac{\log\gamma(t)}{t^{2}}|e_{x,y}\rangle^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{2}}\max_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)+\frac{1}{t^{5}}.

Moreover, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{\textit{d}}{\textit{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{t}\log\gamma(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\!-\frac{1}{t^{2}}\log\gamma(t)+\frac{1}{t}\frac{\dot{\gamma}(t)}{\gamma(t)}

\displaystyle=\!-\frac{1}{t}(A^{*}\xi(t)-c)\!+\!\frac{1}{t}\frac{(tA\dot{\xi}(t)+A\xi(t)-c)\gamma(t)}{\gamma(t)}

\displaystyle=\!A^{*}\dot{\xi}(t),

and in particular
A^{*}\dot{\xi}(t)+\frac{\log\gamma(t)}{t^{2}}=\frac{1}{t}\frac{\dot{\gamma}(t)}{\gamma(t)}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{x,y}(t)\left(\frac{1}{t}\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t)}{\gamma_{x,y}(t)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{2}}\max_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)+\frac{1}{t^{5}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t)^{2}}{\gamma_{x,y}(t)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)+\frac{1}{t^{3}}

\displaystyle\implies\>\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\gamma_{x,y}(t)+\frac{1}{t^{3}},

where the last inequality uses that
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
is bounded when
t
is sufficiently large. Since
d(t)
is bounded for

t
large enough and recalling that the transport coupling can be expressed in terms of the dual variable as

\gamma(t)=e^{t(A^{*}\xi(t)-c)}=e^{tA^{*}(\xi(t)-\xi^{*})-t\kappa}=e^{A^{*}d(t)-t\kappa},

it follows that there exists
C>0
such that

\forall(x,y)\notin I_{0},\quad\gamma_{x,y}(t)\leq Ce^{-t\kappa^{*}},

with
\kappa^{*}=\min_{(x,y)\notin I_{0}}\kappa_{x,y}
.
It allows to conclude that for any
(x,y)\notin I_{0}
,
\gamma_{x,y}(t)
converges exponentially fast towards
0
. Consequently, we obtain the asymptotic estimate

\lvert\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t)\rvert\lesssim t^{-\frac{3}{2}}.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\|\gamma^{*}-\gamma(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t}^{+\infty}\|\dot{\gamma}(\omega)\|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\lesssim\int_{t}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\omega^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\omega\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}.

which allows to obtain

\|\gamma(t)-\gamma^{*}\|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}.

We consider two finite sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

N
and
M

\mathrm{F}(\cdot)=\mathrm{D}_{\phi}(\cdot|q)

q
represents the marginals and
\phi
is an entropy function.

For each divergence
\mathrm{F}
considered in this section, we provide numerical illustrations of the expected upper bounds on the convergence rates for both the primal and dual problem.
We consider two different datasets.
The first dataset consists of two point clouds
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{Y}
contains outliers—points that are significantly distant from the others. The measures supported on these sets have different total masses, with
|\mu|=13
and

The second dataset is composed of two discretized Gaussian measures on a regular 1-dimensional grid, also with unequal masses:
|\mu|=11
and

.

The convergence rate plots are presented on a logarithmic scale and are compared to their theoretically expected rates.

5.1
Kullback-Leibler divergence

The most widely used case corresponds to the function
\phi
defined as
\phi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}x(\log x-1)
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
+\infty
otherwise. It gives the so called Kullback-Leibler divergence, defined for any
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\!+\!M}
by

\mathrm{F}(\psi)=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{N\!+\!M}\psi_{i}\left(\log\frac{\psi_{i}}{q_{i}}-1\right)&if $\psi_{i}\geq 0\;(\forall i)$\\
+\infty&otherwise.\end{cases}

The Legendre transform of
\phi
is defined for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
by

\phi^{*}(y)=e^{y}.

It follows that
\phi^{*}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
that is strongly convex at every point of its domain.

In the same framework as previously defined, another divergence function found in the literature is based on the Euclidean norm distance to
