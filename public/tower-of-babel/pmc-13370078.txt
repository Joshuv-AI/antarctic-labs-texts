Fibroblastic and myofibroblastic differentiation of cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas in rabbits
Authors: Hinata NIIMI, Seika ISOGAI, Miyuu TANAKA, Ryokyo YAMAZAKI, Nobuhiro IMANISHI, Hiroshi SASAI, Kohji NOMURA, Mitsuru KUWAMURA, Takeshi IZAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous and subcutaneous soft tissue sarcomas of undetermined differentiation are common in pet rabbits and are often diagnosed as sarcoma not otherwise specified (sarcoma NOS). Such sarcomas with marked cellular pleomorphism and presence of multinucleated giant cells are also referred to as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, anaplastic sarcoma, or giant cell sarcoma. However, these terminologies do not define any cellular differentiation. In this study, we aimed to identify the line of differentiation of cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas in rabbits. Histopathological, histochemical, and immunohistochemical analyses were performed on 48 cutaneous masses in rabbits, previously diagnosed as sarcoma NOS. Histologically, the tumors were characterized by proliferation of pleomorphic mesenchymal cells, primarily spindle cells, with frequent presence of multinucleated giant cells (58% of the cases). Sirius red staining revealed fine collagen fibers directly extending from the neoplastic cells in all cases. Some cases showed immunoreactivity for calponin (35%), α-smooth muscle actin (31%), and pan-muscle actin (25%). Almost all the case were negative for SOX10, ionized calcium binding adapter molecule-1 and CD204. Based on the findings above, rabbit cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas are considered to differentiate towards fibroblasts and myofibroblasts. In addition, our follow-up data revealed a high recurrence rate and short survival times, indicating aggressive biological behavior of rabbit cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas.Keywords: fibroblast, giant cell sarcoma, myofibroblast, pleomorphic sarcoma, rabbit

INTRODUCTION

Cutaneous and subcutaneous soft tissue sarcomas are one of the most common neoplastic diseases in rabbits, following uterine adenocarcinomas and trichoblastomas [1, 11, 12, 14]. The rabbit sarcomas consist of highly pleomorphic neoplastic cells often accompanied by atypical multinucleated giant cells. The neoplastic cells usually show no specific lineage of differentiation on histological sections. Therefore, they are often diagnosed using umbrella terms such as “undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma”, “anaplastic sarcoma with giant cells”, and “giant cell tumor of soft tissue” [1, 2, 8, 10]. Actually, a previous etiological study in Japan described that sarcoma not otherwise specified (sarcoma NOS) is the most common diagnosis for soft tissue sarcomas in rabbits [12]. Sarcoma NOS refers to a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm for which no specific cellular lineage can be identified morphologically [5, 15]. Despite their high prevalence, histopathological and immunohistochemical features of rabbit soft tissue sarcomas have not been well characterized.

Bertram et al. defined rabbit pleomorphic sarcomas with giant cells, most commonly arising from the skin or subcutis, as “anaplastic sarcoma” and showed that the neoplastic cells often have immunoreactivity for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and/or desmin, suggesting possible differentiation into smooth muscle, myofibroblastic, or pericytic lineage [2]. However, their differentiation has not been investigated in detail.

The aim of this study is to specify the differentiation of pleomorphic sarcoma in rabbits. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 48 cutaneous and subcutaneous masses diagnosed as sarcoma NOS. Histopathological examination, histochemical examination, and immunohistochemistry were performed to characterize neoplastic cell differentiation. We also investigated the relationship between their pathological characteristics and clinical outcomes, including local recurrence and distant metastasis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Case selection

Biopsy specimens, submitted to Osaka Metropolitan University or MLT Co., Ltd. (Osaka, Japan) between 2004 and 2026, were included in this study. Among these specimens, 48 cutaneous or subcutaneous masses diagnosed as sarcoma NOS, were selected. Small specimens such as Tru-Cut biopsy were excluded as they may not accurately reflect the histological characteristics of the entire mass. Sarcoma cases, which fulfilled histopathological and immunohistochemical criteria for the diagnosis of leiomyosarcoma, liposarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, hemangiosarcoma, or osteosarcoma, were also excluded. Submitted tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 3–7 days, routinely processed, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were cut at a thickness of 3 µm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Evaluation of surgical margins was done in 12 of the 48 cases.

Sirius red staining

After dewaxing, sections were stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for 8 min. Then, sections were incubated in Sirius red solution (Sirius Red; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) for 30 min to visualize collagen fibers in red. Sections were then washed in 0.5% acetic acid, dehydrated and coverslipped.

Alcian blue staining

After dewaxing, sections were immersed in 3% acetic acid for 5 min. The sections were then stained with Alcian blue solution (pH 2.5) (Muto Pure Chemicals Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for 30 min at room temperature to visualize acidic mucins in blue. Sections were then washed three times in 3% acetic acid for 3 min, dehydrated and coverslipped.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed using primary antibodies for calponin, α-SMA, pan-muscle actin, SOX10, ionized calcium binding adapter molecule-1 (Iba-1), CD204, with the conditions listed in Table 1. After dewax and antigen retrieval treatment, sections were processed using HistostainerTM(Nichirei Biosciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Briefly, sections were treated with 5% skimmed milk for 15 min, followed by incubation with 3% H2O2/0.065% sodium azide in phosphate-buffered saline for 15 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Sections were incubated with each primary antibody for 60 min, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Histofine Simple Stain MAX PO; Nichirei Biosciences) for 10 min. Signals were visualized with 3, 3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Nichirei Biosciences) and counterstained lightly with hematoxylin.

Antibody information with staining conditions for immunohistochemistry

Results of immunohistochemistry were semi-quantitatively scored as follows: −, negative; +, 1–30% positive cells; ++, 31–60% positive cells; +++, >60% positive cells. For Iba-1 immunohistochemistry, stained slides were compared with negative control, in which the primary antibody was replaced with normal rabbit IgG, to differentiate specific Iba-1 immunoreaction from the secondary antibody-derived reactions with endogenous IgG.

Statistical analysis

Survival analysis was performed using the log-rank test for 13 cases with follow-up data. Survival times were compared between groups with and without the presence of neoplastic multinucleated giant cells. The significance level was set at 5%.

RESULTS

Data on signalment, histology, histochemistry, immunohistochemistry, and clinical outcome (survival time, frequency of recurrence, and presence or absence of metastasis) for each case are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Signalment and location of the neoplasm

Of the 48 cases examined in this study, 34 were male, 13 were female, and 1 was of unknown sex. The ages ranged from 3 to 12 years, with a median age of 8 years. Breed information was available from 27 cases: 9 Netherland dwarf, 4 Mini rabbit, 4 lop-eared, 3 Holland lop, 1 Lionhead, 1 Jersey Wooly, 1 Mini rex, 1 Rex, and 3 mixed breed. Masses were located predominantly in the subcutis, occasionally involving the dermis. The anatomical distribution of the masses was as follows: abdomen (14/48; 29%), forelimb (12/48; 25%), head and neck (10/48; 21%), hindlimb (8/48; 17%), dorsum (3/48; 6%), and scrotum (1/48; 2%).

Gross findings

Large masses were often formed in the shoulder region of the forelimb (Fig. 1a) or the abdomen. Some masses exhibited invasive growth into the surrounding tissues (Fig. 1b). Most masses were relatively well-demarcated, solitary or occasionally multinodular, elastic to firm, and white in color (Fig. 1c and 1d).

Histopathological and histochemical findings

The masses were usually expansile or partially invasive and were unencapsulated or incompletely encapsulated, despite their relatively well-demarcated gross appearance. The morphology and growth patterns of the neoplastic cells were highly variable between the cases (Fig. 2). The masses were composed of spindle, polygonal, ovoid to stellate shaped mesenchymal cells arranged in an interlacing, fascicular, diffuse, storiform, and whorl patterns. The neoplastic cells had single to multiple, and oval to irregularly shaped nuclei. Based on these characteristic histological features, six distinct histological patterns were identified among the examined cases. The most common histological characteristic patten was a mixed spindle cell-giant cell pattern (Fig. 2a) followed by a giant cell-rich (Fig. 2b), spindle cell-rich (Fig. 2c), solid (Fig. 2d), and small-cell (Fig. 2e) patterns. As a rare histological pattern, case 32 showed proliferation of spindle cells arranged in storiform and perivascular whirling patterns without multinucleated giant cells (Fig. 2f), which closely resembles canine perivascular wall tumor (formally canine hemangiopericytoma) [8]. Multinucleated giant neoplastic cells were characteristically observed in more than half of the cases (28/48; 58%) (Table 2). Production of mucinous matrix by the neoplastic cells was present in some cases (14/48; 29%). Cellular and nuclear pleomorphism and atypia were moderate to severe. Mitotic count ranged from 0 to 124 per 10 high-power fields, equal to 2.37 mm2, with a median value of 5.5. Capsular invasion and necrosis were occasionally observed. A variable amount of fine collagen fibers, visualized with Sirius red staining (Fig. 3a and 3b), were characteristically observed in the neoplastic tissue of all the 48 cases. Collagen fibers were often shown to be continuous with the cytoplasm of the neoplastic cells, indicative of in situ production of collagens by the neoplastic cells. The mucous matrix observed on HE-stained sections stained light blue with Alcian blue. (Fig. 3c and 3d). Infiltrate of inflammatory cells, particularly macrophages and lymphocytes, were variably present within the neoplastic tissue. Surgical margins were often infiltrated by the neoplastic cells (8/12; 67%).

Immunohistochemical findings

Twenty-four cases among 48 cases did not show specific differentiation, as they were negative for all markers used in this study (Supplementary Table 1). A subset of the neoplastic cells in some cases, including multinucleated giant cells, exhibited immunoreactivity for the myogenic markers calponin (17/48, 35%), α-SMA (15/48, 31%), and pan-muscle actin (12/48, 25%) (Fig. 4a, 4b, and 4c). Neoplastic cells in almost all cases were negative for SOX10 (Fig. 4d); a small population of the neoplastic cells were positive for SOX10 in case 1 (1/48, 2%). Numerous Iba-1- and CD204-positive macrophages were usually present within the neoplastic tissues whereas the neoplastic cells were negative for these histiocyte markers in all cases (Fig. 4e and 4f).

Clinical outcome

Follow-up data was obtained from 20 of the 48 cases (Supplementary Table 1). Fifteen rabbits died shortly after surgery, mostly within 1 year. The shortest and median survival times were 0 days and 143 days, respectively. Local recurrence was observed in 11 of the 19 cases (58%). Metastasis was identified in 4 of the 16 cases (25%), involving the lungs, thoracic and abdominal cavity, and liver, based on X-ray and computed tomography findings. Among the 13 cases with follow-up data, no significant difference in survival was observed between cases with and without neoplastic multinucleated giant cells.

DISCUSSION

Considering the presence of in situ collagen production and the lack of consistent immunoreactivity for histiocytic and peripheral nerve markers, rabbit cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas are considered to differentiate toward fibroblasts. In addition, these neoplastic cells occasionally express smooth muscle cytoskeletal proteins calponin, α-SMA, and pan-muscle actin. Given the fibroblastic differentiation of the neoplastic cells, this immunohistochemical results suggest partial differentiation of the neoplastic cells toward myofibroblasts, rather than smooth muscles [2, 6, 10].

Fibrosarcoma is defined as a neoplasm composed of atypical mesenchymal cells producing collagens and its diagnosis is supported by the absence of other specific differentiation by immunohistochemical panel [8, 10]. Collagen staining would be most useful for the characterization of rabbit sarcomas as no fibroblast-specific antibodies are currently available for the veterinary diagnostics. The direct continuity of Sirius red-positive collagen fibers with the neoplastic cells, can differentiate neoplastic collagen production from stromal collagen production. Also, collagen fibers produced by neoplastic cells tend to be immature and appear as thin fibers compared with mature collagen bundles produced by stromal fibroblasts. The presence of mucus production in approximately 30% of the cases is compatible with fibroblast differentiation as myxoid pattern is characteristics of myxosarcoma, a histological subtype of fibrosarcoma [8]. However, immunohistochemistry using a panel of antibodies would also be needed for differential diagnosis. Based on our findings, the rabbit cutaneous pleomorphic sarcoma may be classified into a pleomorphic subtype of fibrosarcoma.

The cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas in this study showed a male predisposition, particularly in rabbits older than 8 years, which is consistent with the previous study [12]. Common anatomical locations include abdomen, forelimb, head and neck, and hindlimbs, whereas dorsal lesions were relatively uncommon. Netherland Dwarf rabbits were the most frequently affected breed in this study; however, this finding may reflect breed popularity in Japan rather than true breed predisposition.

The primary differential diagnosis of the rabbit cutaneous fibrosarcoma would be histiocytic sarcoma. Although histiocytic sarcoma in rabbits most commonly arises in the lungs, it may also occur in the skin [2, 7, 9]. The solid type of the fibrosarcoma, composed of plump polygonal cells with multinucleated giant cells, resembles histological features of histiocytic sarcoma. In addition, some histiocytic sarcomas may have spindle cell morphology [9, 10, 13]. Thus, it may be difficult to differentiate fibrosarcoma from histiocytic sarcoma in rabbits on routine HE slides. Immunohistochemistry using histiocyte markers such as CD18, CD204, and Iba-1 [2, 4, 9, 10], is useful for the differential diagnosis between histiocytic sarcoma and fibrosarcoma. In this study, focal SOX10 expression was observed in a case, suggesting that the neoplasm may represent either a fibrosarcoma with partial peripheral nerve differentiation or a peripheral nerve sheath tumor, given its high degree of pleomorphism. However, SOX10 is not an effective diagnostic marker for rabbit cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas as its expression is infrequent (2% of the cases) and partial (less than 30% of the neoplastic cells).

In the human WHO classification, undifferentiated sarcoma of soft tissue (ICD-O coding 8805/3) is defined as sarcoma that shows no identifiable line of differentiation with presently available technology [3]. It is histologically divided into pleomorphic, spindle cell, round cell, and epithelioid subsets, with frequent bizarre multinucleated neoplastic giant cells, sharing histological features with the rabbit cutaneous pleomorphic sarcoma. Human undifferentiated sarcomas typically have extensive genomic rearrangements and substantial copy-number heterogeneity compared with other sarcoma subtypes [3]. Thus, cytogenetical study would provide new insight into the molecular carcinogenesis of rabbit cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas.

Interpretation of clinical follow-up data in this study is limited due to the limited number of cases. Nevertheless, our results indicate a high recurrence rate and an aggressive biological behavior of rabbit cutaneous pleomorphic sarcomas, as most cases died within 1 year after surgery. Recurrence was frequent even in cases with longer survival times. The high recurrence rate may be attributable to the infiltrative nature of the neoplasms. In addition, invasion into the bone and muscle is frequently observed at surgery, which causes the technical complexity or difficulty of the surgical excision. Although there is no established consensus regarding the treatment strategy for rabbit cutaneous sarcomas, surgical excision is currently considered the first-line treatment, based on clinical experience. No clear association was identified between the presence of multinucleated giant cells and clinical outcome in this study, indicating the difficulty to interpret the pathobiological significance of the giant cells. Multi-center and large-scale study is required to better understand the biological behavior of rabbit pleomorphic sarcomas and to establish the optimal treatment strategy.

In conclusion, rabbit pleomorphic sarcomas, which are routinely diagnosed as sarcoma NOS, pleomorphic sarcoma, or anaplastic sarcoma, mostly differentiate toward fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, despite their histomorphological variations. Special stain is most useful for diagnosis to identify the fibers. Immunohistochemistry for histiocytic markers is also helpful to exclude histiocytic sarcoma. Accumulation of further cases are required to better characterize the pathology of cutaneous pleomorphic sarcoma in rabbits.
