Distinct pathogenic mechanisms underlying two protein C variants (p.Arg211Gln and p.Val367Met) in a thrombophilic family: integrated functional and structural analyses
Authors: Huayang Zhang, Chong Wang, Huiqin Jiang, Ying Zhao, Li Zhang, Meixiu Gu, Baishen Pan, Zhenyu Shi, Beili Wang, Wei Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHereditary protein C deficiency (PCD) increases thrombotic risk, but the molecular mechanisms of distinct missense variants remain incompletely defined. Two siblings from a family with recurrent deep vein thrombosis and a strong family history of thrombotic events were found to carry compound heterozygous PROC variants.ObjectivesTo elucidate the biosynthetic and functional consequences of two PROC missense variants, c.632G > A (p.Arg211Gln) and c.1099G > A (p.Val367Met), and their combined impact on anticoagulant capacity.MethodsTwo siblings with recurrent deep vein thrombosis were analyzed. Family segregation analysis was performed on multiple heterozygous carriers to support genotype-phenotype correlations. Recombinant protein C variants were expressed in HEK293T cells to assess expression efficiency and intracellular accumulation. Zymogen activation by thrombin-thrombomodulin and APC anticoagulant activity were evaluated using FVa degradation, clotting, and thrombin generation assays. Structural modeling and Na⁺-dependent enzymatic assays were performed to explore variant-specific mechanistic defects.ResultsBoth variants exhibited partial expression defects, with p.Val367Met showing significantly increased intracellular accumulation and reduced secretion efficiency. p.Arg211Gln selectively impaired zymogen activation without affecting APC function (type IIa deficiency), whereas p.Val367Met preserved activation but reduced Na⁺-dependent catalytic efficiency (type IIb deficiency). Structural modeling revealed that Arg211 substitution disrupts activation peptide conformation, while Val367Met perturbs the Na⁺-binding loop adjacent to the S1 pocket. Co-inheritance of both resulted in synergistic anticoagulant impairment, leading to markedly elevated thrombin generation.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that PCD pathophysiology can arise from combined biosynthetic and functional defects, with distinct structural perturbations differentially affecting zymogen processing and protease activity. Integrated genetic, biochemical, and structural analyses, in the context of family history and clinical phenotype, are essential for accurate variant interpretation. Understanding the specific molecular mechanisms of PROC variants can inform clinical decision-making, guide personalized anticoagulant therapy, and help prevent thrombotic events in affected individuals. Therapeutic strategies targeting zymogen activation or proteostasis may offer potential avenues to mitigate thrombotic risk in hereditary PCD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00879-7.Keywords: Protein C deficiency, PROC variants, Thrombophilia, Functional assays, Structural modeling

Introduction

Protein C (PC) is a vitamin K-dependent plasma glycoprotein synthesized in the liver and secreted as a single‑chain zymogen [1]. On endothelial surfaces, it is converted to activated protein C (APC) by the thrombin-thrombomodulin (TM) complex [2]. APC exerts potent anticoagulant effects by proteolytically inactivating factors Va [3] and VIIIa [4], thereby establishing a negative feedback loop essential for maintaining hemostatic balance. Reduced APC activity leads to a hypercoagulable state and is a well-established risk factor for venous thromboembolism (VTE) [5].

Hereditary protein C deficiency (PCD) is an inherited thrombophilia, affecting 0.2–0.4% of the general population [6]. Heterozygous carriers display a markedly increased risk of first-episode VTE, with a hazard ratio of 7.5 compared to unaffected individuals [7]. In contrast, severe forms of PCD, caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in the PROC gene, are exceedingly rare, with an estimated incidence of ~ 1 per 4 million live births [8]. The PROC gene locus on chromosome 2q13-q14 comprises nine exons, and more than 500 variants have been described [9], the majority being missense variants spanning multiple functional domains [10]. Laboratory diagnosis of PCD integrates clotting‑based and chromogenic activity assays with antigen quantification [11]. While type I deficiency is defined by concordant reductions in both activity and antigen [12], type II deficiency reflects qualitative dysfunction with preserved antigen levels, indicating a qualitative defect in protein C function [13]. Subtypes of type II can be subclassified into type IIa, characterized by defective zymogen activation or catalytic activity, and type IIb, characterized by normal amidolytic but reduced clotting activity, indicating disrupted cofactor interactions. Such subclassification is clinically relevant, as it aids variant interpretation and supports functional validation.

The prevalence and mutational spectrum of PROC variants vary among populations. In Western cohorts, factor V Leiden and prothrombin G20210A dominate [14], while deficiencies of PC and protein S are relatively more frequent in Asian cohorts [15]. Nevertheless, genotype-phenotype correlations of rare PROC variants remain incompletely understood, especially in compound heterozygous contexts where multiple functional impairments may converge. A more detailed characterization of such cases is essential for refining diagnostic algorithms and improving risk stratification.

Here, we describe two adolescent sisters presenting with lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis who were identified as compound heterozygous carriers of missense variants c.632G >A (p.Arg211Gln) [16] and c.1099G >A (p.Val367Met) in PROC [17]. Both variants map to functionally critical regions of the protein C, with Arg211 residing within the activation peptide adjacent to the thrombin cleavage site required for TM-dependent activation [18, 19], and Val367 localizing within the serine protease domain near the sodium-binding pocket critical for catalytic efficiency [17]. Although both variants have been reported in Asian populations, their precise molecular consequences and classification into type IIa or IIb deficiency have not been systematically characterized.

To address this gap, we performed family-based segregation analysis, recombinant protein expression, functional coagulation and thrombin generation assays, alongside in silico structural modeling. Our results demonstrate that p.Arg211Gln primarily impairs thrombin-TM-mediated activation while preserving normal APC function (type IIa phenotype), whereas p.Val367Met disrupts Na⁺-dependent catalytic regulation with preserved amidolytic activity (type IIb phenotype). Together, these findings provide mechanistic insight into two rare PROC variants, illustrate the cumulative functional effect of compound heterozygosity on clinical expression, and highlight the value of integrating molecular genetics with functional assays in the evaluation of early-onset hereditary PCD.

Materials and methods

Patient

Two female siblings (aged 13 and 19 years) presenting with recurrent lower extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT) were referred to Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. Peripheral blood samples were collected for genomic DNA extraction and variant analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University.

Hemostatic analysis

Peripheral blood was drawn into 3.2% sodium citrate anticoagulation tubes. Platelet-poor plasma (PPP) was prepared by double centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at room temperature. Normal pooled plasma (NPP) was obtained from 30 healthy volunteers without personal or family history of thrombosis or bleeding disorders.

PC activity was measured using both a chromogenic and a clotting assay (Cat. No.:CK031K, HEMOCLOT™ Protein C, Hyphen BioMed, France). The chromogenic assay measures the generation of activated protein C (APC) by thrombin-thrombomodulin and the subsequent cleavage of a chromogenic substrate (S-2366) (p-Glu-Pro-Arg-pNa, synthesized in-house by Shanghai Shenggong, China), thus reflecting the combined efficiency of PC activation and APC catalytic function. The clotting-based assay quantifies the ability of plasma PC to prolong the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) of test plasma, providing an integrated measure of the overall anticoagulant capacity of circulating PC.

PC antigen (PC: Ag) levels were determined using an ELISA kit (Cat. No.:PC-EIA, Enzyme Research Laboratories). Protein S (PS) and antithrombin (AT) activities were measured using commercial kits on an automated coagulation analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Japan).

Whole exome sequencing and variants validation

Genomic DNA was extracted using the MagPure Fast Blood DNA Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China). Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed using the Exome Plus Panel V2.0 (Nantong ZhongKe Co., Ltd., China), covering ~ 46.7 Mb of coding regions. Paired-end 150 bp reads were generated on the MGISEQ-2000 platform.

Bioinformatic analysis was conducted using standard pipelines. Variant annotation was based on public databases (ClinVar, 1000 Genomes, dbSNP, gnomAD, OMIM). Candidate variants were validated by Sanger sequencing using the ABI 3700 DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA).

Analysis of thrombin generation in plasma

Thrombin generation (TG) was assessed using the Calibrated Automated Thrombogram (CAT) system (Stago) [20]. Briefly, PPP (80 µL) was incubated with or without 5 nM soluble recombinant thrombomodulin (sTM, Novoprotein Scientific). TG was triggered by 5 pM tissue factor and 4 µM phospholipids, and measured by monitoring the hydrolysis of a fluorogenic substrate (FluCa, Diagnostica Stago). Parameters including lag time, peak thrombin, endogenous thrombin potential (ETP), and time to peak were analyzed using the Thrombinoscope software.

Recombinant protein C expression

The full-length human PROC cDNA was cloned into pcDNA3.1(+) with a C-terminal 10 × His tag. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using restriction enzyme digestion and homologous recombination with synthetic fragments (BGI, Shenzhen, China). Constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Stable transfection for protein purification: Expression plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells using linear polyethylenimine (PEI, MW 4000; Yeasen, China). Stable lines were selected with 0.8 mg/mL G418 (Cat. No.:ST081-5 g, Beyotime). Recombinant PC was harvested from serum-free, vitamin K1-supplemented medium after 72 h, and purified using HisTrap Excel and Q Sepharose™ High Performance columns (Cytiva). Purified PC variants were then activated by incubation with 5 nM α-thrombin and 20 nM soluble thrombomodulin (sTM), followed by purification of APC using a HiTrap™ Q HP anion-exchange chromatography column and elution with a NaCl gradient (0.1-1.0 M), yielding fully activated APC free of residual thrombin and sTM. Protein purity and molecular integrity were evaluated by SDS-PAGE (10%).

Transient transfection for expression and functional assays: For experiments involving protein expression, ELISA, Western blot, or immunofluorescence, plasmids were transiently transfected into HEK293T cells using the same PEI protocol. Cells and culture supernatants were collected 48–72 h post-transfection for downstream analyses. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence staining of HEK293T cells expressing PC

HEK293T cells were seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips and transfected with either wild-type or variant PROC expression vectors. After 24 hours, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 30 minutes and then blocked with 1 × PBS containing 5% goat serum and 0.3% Triton™ X-100 for 60 minutes. Cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with Anti-Human Protein C, Clone 1 Monoclonal Antibody to detect recombinant PC, and with Calreticulin (D3E6) XP® Rabbit mAb (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:400) to stain the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Following primary antibody incubation, cells were incubated with Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) to visualize PC and with Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), F(ab’)2 Fragment (Alexa Fluor® 555, 1:500) to visualize the ER. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted and imaged using a Leica STELLARIS 5 high-resolution white-light confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Images were acquired under identical settings at 1000 × magnification to allow qualitative comparison of intracellular signal intensity between WT and variant constructs.

Protein C activation by thrombin

Recombinant protein C (1 µM) was activated in Tris-buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.4) containing 2.5 mM CaCl₂, 1 mg/mL BSA, and 0.1% PEG-8000. For downstream functional assays, purified PC (1 µM) was incubated with 5 nM human α-thrombin (Cat. No.:EZ006K, Hyphen BioMed) and 20 nM soluble thrombomodulin (sTM) (Cat. No.:CU42, Novoprotein Scientific Inc) at 4 °C overnight to ensure complete activation. This 4 °C overnight activation produced fully cleaved and functionally equivalent APC compared with 37 °C (Figure S1) and was therefore used for all subsequent experiments to maximize recovery, especially for variants with impaired activation efficiency.

To evaluate activation efficiency, parallel activation reactions were performed at 37 °C and sampled at the indicated time points (0, 30, and 120 min). Reaction aliquots were analyzed by Western blot using polyclonal anti-PC antibodies (detecting antibody in the ELISA kit) capable of detecting both zymogen PC and activated APC.

Anticoagulant assays

The anticoagulant activity of APC (WT, p.Arg211Gln, p.Val367Met) was evaluated using FVa degradation and plasma clotting assays. In the FVa assay, 5 nM human FVa was incubated with increasing concentrations of APC in TBS containing 2.5 mM CaCl₂ and 50 µM PC/PS vesicles (Cat. No.: PCPS-02, Prolytix) for 20 min at room temperature. Residual FVa activity was quantified by a prothrombinase assay using 5 nM FXa and 1 µM prothrombin (2 min). For protein S-dependent enhancement, reactions included 100 nM protein S, with the APC incubation reduced to 2 min.

Plasma-based aPTT assays were performed by adding varying APC concentrations to either normal pooled plasma or protein S-deficient plasma. Clotting times were measured at 37 °C using a semi-automated coagulometer.

Thrombin generation assays in purification system

TG was evaluated using both zymogen- and APC-based setups. For zymogen assays, protein C-deficient plasma was reconstituted with 50 nM recombinant PC (wild-type or variant) in the presence or absence of 5 nM sTM. For APC-based assays, 5 or 10 nM of activated PC (WT or variant) was added to normal pooled plasma. Reactions were initiated by PPP-Reagent (5 pM tissue factor and 4 µM phospholipids) (Cat. No.:86193, Diagnostica Stago, France) and monitored in real time using a fluorogenic substrate (FluCa) (Cat. No.:86197, Diagnostica Stago, France) on the CAT system. TG parameters, including lag time, peak thrombin, ETP, and time to peak, were calculated using Thrombinoscope software.

Computational prediction of protein impact

The functional consequences of missense variants were evaluated using in silico prediction tools. OpenCRAVAT was used to evaluate the pathogenicity of missense variants based on integrated variant annotation pipelines [21]. To investigate potential structural alterations, AlphaFold 3 [22] was employed to model three-dimensional conformations. Full-length human protein C was used for PROC models, while the activation peptide was removed for APC models; single-point substitutions (p.Arg211Gln or p.Val367Met) were introduced to generate variant structures.

Interaction of APC derivatives with Na⁺

The sodium-dependent amidolytic activity of APC variants was measured using the chromogenic substrate S-2366 in Tris-HCl buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4) containing 2.5 mM CaCl₂, or in the absence of Ca²⁺, 1 mg/mL BSA, and 0.1% PEG-8000. APC-WT (5 nM), APC-p.Arg211Gln (5 nM), and APC-p.Val367Met (5 nM) were incubated with increasing concentrations of NaCl (0-260 mM). Total ionic strength was kept constant at 0.3 M by adjusting the Tris-HCl (0.5 M) concentration. Substrate cleavage was monitored at 405 nm in a 96-well plate using a microplate reader.

The initial reaction rates were plotted as a function of Na⁺ concentration and fitted by nonlinear regression using the one-site specific binding model in GraphPad Prism 10 to determine the apparent Na⁺ affinity (Kd(app)). Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical comparisons among groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed t tests.

Results

Case presentation

Two adolescent sisters, aged 13 (III‑2) and 19 years (III‑1), presented with recurrent episodes of lower‑extremity DVT. Both had experienced multiple thrombotic events and had previously received anticoagulation therapy with low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH) or rivaroxaban, which was discontinued for at least two weeks before laboratory testing. Routine coagulation screening (PT, APTT, fibrinogen, and thrombin time) was within reference ranges (Table S1). Both siblings showed elevated D‑dimer and fibrin/fibrinogen degradation product (FDP) levels, consistent with active thrombosis.

Family investigation revealed a pattern suggestive of hereditary protein C deficiency. Routine screening of relatives showed variably decreased protein C activity, with the probands’ grandfather (I-1), grandmother (I-4), and mother (II-2) all exhibiting reduced activity to different degrees, although none had a history of thrombosis. These findings supported a familial component and prompted molecular and functional analyses to determine the underlying cause.

Genetic findings

Whole‑exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous PROC missense variants, c.632G > A (p.Arg211Gln) and c.1099G > A (p.Val367Met), inherited paternally and maternally, respectively (Fig. 1A). Segregation analysis within the family showed that p.Arg211Gln was present in the grandfather (I-1), while p.Val367Met was present in the mother (II-2) and grandmother (I-4). Mild reductions in protein C activity and antigen levels were observed in these heterozygous relatives, although none had a history of thrombosis, indicating that the compound heterozygous state underlies the severe thrombotic phenotype in the probands. No pathogenic variants were detected in other thrombosis-related genes, supporting that these PROC variants are the primary genetic contributors to the observed phenotype.

Formal ACMG/AMP classification was performed for both variants, integrating population frequency, functional data, literature reports, and segregation analysis (Table S2). Notably, p.Arg211Gln is reported as Pathogenic/Likely pathogenic in ClinVar and extremely rare in gnomAD (allele frequency 0.000004), while p.Val367Met is classified as Likely pathogenic in ClinVar and similarly rare in gnomAD (allele frequency 0. 000077) (Table S3). For p.Arg211Gln, the pathogenicity supporting evidence includes functional studies showing reduced protein C activity (PS3_Moderate), extremely rare allele frequency in population databases (PM2_Supporting), at least three previously reported patients carrying the variant (PS4_Supporting + PP4), and a different pathogenic variant affecting the same residue (PM5). For p.Val367Met, pathogenicity supporting evidence includes moderate reduction in protein C activity in reported patients (PS3_Moderate), rare population frequency (PM2_Supporting), multiple reported carriers (PS4_Supporting + PP4), and observation in trans with another potentially pathogenic variant (PM3_Supporting). Taken together, both variants are classified as likely pathogenic/variants of uncertain significance (VUS) according to ACMG guidelines (Table S3).

Plasma-based functional assessment

Both probands, who were compound heterozygotes for PROC variants, exhibited markedly reduced protein C activity in plasma. Clotting‑based activity was 3.3% and 3.2% of normal, while chromogenic activity was 10.7% and 6.8%, accompanied by moderately decreased antigen levels (36.5% and 41.7%) (Fig. 1B). Among heterozygous family members, p.Arg211Gln carriers showed reduced PC antigen levels together with severe decreases in both clotting‑based and chromogenic activities, suggesting combined quantitative and qualitative defects. In contrast, p.Val367Met carriers had normal antigen and chromogenic activity but reduced clotting‑based activity, consistent with a type IIb-like functional pattern implicating defective cofactor-dependent anticoagulant function.

Thrombin generation assays in the presence of sTM (5 nM) further demonstrated a markedly impaired anticoagulant response in plasma from the compound heterozygous proband, characterized by persistently elevated thrombin peak and ETP (Fig. 1C), with corresponding quantitative data are summarized in Table 1. In contrast, single heterozygous carriers of either variant exhibited partial impairment (Fig. 1D and E). Collectively, these data indicate that both variants independently impair PC function, while the compound heterozygous state leads to near‑complete loss of sTM‑dependent anticoagulant activity, providing a coherent explanation for the severe thrombotic phenotype in the sisters.

PC variants exhibit defective expression consistent with a type I deficiency pattern

Multiple in silico prediction tools consistently classified p.Arg211Gln as pathogenic, with the affected residue showing high evolutionary conservation across vertebrates. In contrast, p.Val367Met was predicted to have milder functional impact, with moderate conservation at this position (Fig. 2A and B). To test these predictions, we transiently expressed recombinant wild-type (WT) or variant PC in HEK293T cells. ELISA of culture supernatant revealed mildly reduced PC antigen levels for both variants (~ 82% of WT for p.Arg211Gln, p = 0.0121; ~60% of WT for p.Val367Met, p = 0.0003), with a more pronounced expression defect for the p.Val367Met variant (Fig. 2C). Functional assays of the culture supernatants measuring PC amidolytic activity (chromogenic substrate S-2366) and PC anticoagulant activity (clotting-based assay) showed that p.Arg211Gln exhibited markedly reduced activity (~ 2% of WT; chromogenic, p < 0.0001; clotting, p < 0.0001). In contrast, p.Val367Met partial amidolytic activity (~ 74% of WT; p = 0.0017) but displayed substantially reduced clotting activity (~ 4.5% of WT; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C). Western blot analysis of intracellular (lysate) and secreted (supernatant) fractions (Fig. 2D) revealed a clear increase in intracellular protein levels for p.Val367Met, whereas p.Arg211Gln displayed only a mild deviation from WT. Lysate signals were normalized to GAPDH, and equal total protein was loaded for supernatants. Based on these normalized band intensities, we subsequently calculated the Secretion Efficiency Ratio (S/I) (Figure S2). This quantitative analysis confirmed a markedly reduced secretion efficiency for p.Val367Met (p = 0.002), while p.Arg211Gln showed no statistically significant difference from WT (p = 0.0846).

Immunofluorescence studies showed that the p.Val367Met variant exhibited stronger intracellular fluorescence intensity compared with WT and p.Arg211Gln (Fig. 2E), indicating increased intracellular accumulation. The fluorescence signal of p.Arg211Gln was comparable to that of WT, consistent with its mild expression defect. These findings suggest that p.Val367Met impairs the release of protein C into the extracellular space, resulting in reduced secretion efficiency. Overall, p.Arg211Gln shows only a mild expression impairment, whereas p.Val367Met exhibits a more pronounced intracellular accumulation while preserving amidolytic activity but markedly reducing coagulant function. In compound heterozygosity, these distinct molecular effects act synergistically to produce a profound loss of anticoagulant activity.

Expression, purification, and functional assessment of PC variants affecting activation and enzymatic activity

Recombinant WT and variant PC constructs were expressed in HEK293T cells and purified by affinity and anion-exchange chromatography. SDS-PAGE under reducing and non-reducing conditions confirmed comparable purity and post-translational modifications across all proteins (Fig. 3A).

Kinetic assays of PC activation by thrombin demonstrated distinct effects of the variants on thrombin-dependent activation. In the absence of sTM, the mean activation rate (slope, expressed as nM APC·min⁻¹·(nM Th)⁻¹) for WT PC was 0.297 ± 0.008, comparable to that of p.Val367Met (0.303 ± 0.016), whereas p.Arg211Gln showed a markedly reduced rate (0.068 ± 0.005) (Fig. 3B). In the presence of sTM, activation of WT and p.Val367Met was strongly enhanced (WT: 2.60 ± 0.02; p.Val367Met: 2.55 ± 0.21), corresponding to a ~ 9-fold increase compared with reactions without sTM. In contrast, p.Arg211Gln exhibited minimal improvement (0.314 ± 0.033), remaining > 8-fold lower than WT (Fig. 3C).

Activation kinetics in the presence of sTM, measured with increasing PC concentrations (Fig. 3D), and functional assays using the chromogenic substrate S-2366 (Fig. 3E), performed under conditions that allow PC conversion to APC, reflect the combined outcome of PC activation and APC catalytic activity. These measurements showed that p.Val367Met retained near-normal overall activity, indicating that both its activation to APC and the catalytic function of APC are preserved. In contrast, p.Arg211Gln exhibited markedly reduced overall activity; however, these experiments cannot distinguish whether this deficit arises from impaired activation of PC or intrinsic catalytic defects of APC. These results highlight the need for subsequent experiments using fully activated APC to clarify the specific contribution of each step.

Functional characterization of APC variants in anticoagulant activity

Prior to assessing the anticoagulant function of purified APC variants, we monitored the activation of recombinant PC to APC by Western blot using a polyclonal anti-PC antibody capable of detecting both zymogen and activated forms (Figure S3). At 30 min, APC generated from WT and p.Val367Met PC was readily detectable, whereas APC from p.Arg211Gln remained largely undetectable. By 120 min, WT and p.Val367Met PCs were fully converted to APC, while only partial conversion was observed for p.Arg211Gln. These results confirm the markedly impaired activation of p.Arg211Gln and provide the rationale for using fully activated APC preparations in subsequent FVa inactivation and clotting assays.

The anticoagulant function of APC variants was evaluated using FVa inactivation assays, both in the absence and presence of protein S. APC-WT and APC- p.Arg211Gln efficiently inactivated FVa without protein S (Fig. 4A). In contrast, APC-p.Val367Met showed markedly impaired FVa inactivation in the absence of protein S, and although its activity was partially restored by protein S, its anticoagulant activity remained substantially reduced compared to APC-WT (Fig. 4A and B).

These findings were corroborated by APTT-based clotting assays using both normal and protein S-deficient plasma. APC-p.Val367Met displayed a reduced ability to prolong clotting time compared with APC-WT, whereas APC-p.Arg211Gln exhibited anticoagulant activity similar to wild-type in both plasma types (Fig. 4C and D).

Thrombin generation assays were performed in PC-deficient plasma supplemented with 50 nM wild-type or variant PC (p.Arg211Gln and p.Val367Met) in the presence of 5 nM sTM. Both variants exhibited impaired suppression of thrombin generation relative to wild-type PC, with p.Arg211Gln displaying a more severe impairment, as reflected by increased peak thrombin and endogenous thrombin potential (Fig. 5A and B). Structural modeling of protein C variants

To investigate the structural basis of the functional defects in the two PROC variants, in silico structural modeling was performed using AlphaFold3. Modeling of wild-type PC in complex with thrombin-TM suggested that the catalytic triad of thrombin (His57, Asp102, and Ser195; red stick) recognizes and cleaves at the Arg211 residue (magenta stick), facilitating removal of the activation peptide (AP) domain (Fig. 6A). In the p.Arg211Gln variant, substitution of Arg211 appears to alter the orientation of its side chain within the cleavage pocket, which could reduce accessibility to the catalytic triad and thereby impair activation by the thrombin-TM complex (Fig. 6B).

For the p.Val367Met variant, AlphaFold3 modeling of APC with PS, activated coagulation factor V (FVa), Ca²⁺, and Na⁺ indicated that the Na⁺-binding pocket within the serine protease (SP) domain remains intact in wild-type APC, coordinating Na⁺ (purple sphere) to stabilize the active conformation (Fig. 6C). The p.Val367Met substitution may locally perturb the pocket geometry and potentially weaken Na⁺ coordination (Fig. 6D), a structural alteration that likely underlies the reduced anticoagulant activity observed in functional assays. It should be noted that AlphaFold3 does not directly compute ion-binding energetics; thus, these models provide plausible structural hypotheses rather than definitive conclusions, aligning with the biochemical evidence observed in this study.

Reduced Na⁺ binding affinity of the APC-p.Val367Met Variant

The Val367 residue in APC is located on a loop adjacent to the S1 specificity pocket, a structural region critical for Na⁺ binding and stabilization of the protease domain. To assess whether the p.Val367Met substitution affects Na⁺-binding properties, amidolytic activity was measured across a range of Na⁺ concentrations in the absence (Fig. 6E) and presence (Fig. 6F) of Ca²⁺. In both conditions, APC-p.Val367Met exhibited a markedly attenuated response to Na⁺ compared with wild-type APC, whereas APC-p.Arg211Gln remained comparable to WT. Nonlinear regression analysis of the saturation curves yielded apparent Na⁺ affinity constants (Kd(app), n = 3): with Ca²⁺, WT = 2.639 ± 0.086 mM, p.Arg211Gln = 2.345 ± 0.333 mM (P > 0.05 vs. WT), and p.Val367Met = 6.689 ± 0.584 mM (P < 0.05 vs. WT); without Ca²⁺, WT = 25.293 ± 1.321 mM, p.Arg211Gln = 21.880 ± 1.291 mM (P > 0.05 vs. WT), and p.Val367Met = 50.587 ± 8.143 mM (P < 0.05 vs. WT). These results indicate that the p.Val367Met substitution significantly weakens Na⁺ binding under both Ca²⁺-dependent and Ca²⁺-independent conditions. The impaired Na⁺-dependent stabilization of the protease domain likely contributes to the reduced catalytic and anticoagulant activity of APC-p.Val367Met, consistent with alteration of the Na⁺-binding pocket geometry.

Discussion

In this study, we functionally characterized two rare PROC missense variants, p.Arg211Gln and p.Val367Met, identified in a thrombophilic family with severe early-onset venous events. The variants were inherited on separate alleles and co-segregated in affected siblings, suggesting a compound heterozygous basis for the phenotype. To elucidate the molecular basis of the observed anticoagulant defect, each variant was expressed independently and characterized using an array of coagulation assays, thrombin generation tests, and structural modeling. In our family, the p.Arg211Gln and p.Val367Met variants impair protein C anticoagulant capacity through distinct molecular mechanisms. This observation highlights the mechanistic diversity of hereditary protein C deficiency and underscores the value of integrating genetic and biochemical evidence to improve diagnostic precision and risk stratification. Given the limited sample size and the family-based nature of this study, conclusions regarding variant classification and clinical penetrance should be interpreted with caution. Further validation in larger cohorts or additional families will be important to confirm these genotype-phenotype correlations.

Both p.Arg211Gln and p.Val367Met variants demonstrated reduced expression efficiency, approximately 80% and 60% of wild-type, respectively, with p.Val367Met exhibiting markedly increased intracellular accumulation and reduced secretion efficiency. Such biosynthetic defects likely reflect perturbations in protein folding or conformational maturation, impairing trafficking to the Golgi apparatus. Similar intracellular accumulation due to missense variants has been well documented in other vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors, including factor VII [23] and factor IX [24], where missense substitutions disrupt the native folding, compromise expression efficiency, and contribute to quantitative deficiencies in plasma [25, 26]. Although HEK293T cells are not hepatocyte-derived, they are widely used for heterologous expression of vitamin K-dependent serine proteases, including protein C [27, 28], FIX [29], and prothrombin [30], due to their robust transfection efficiency and capacity for correct post-translational modification. The higher expression percentages observed in HEK293T supernatants compared with patient plasma likely reflect cell type-specific differences in protein quality control and the absence of physiological clearance mechanisms. Nonetheless, the relative expression trend parallels the plasma antigen data, supporting the validity of the cellular findings as a model of variant-induced expression impairment. Notably, the co-occurrence of impaired expression with distinct functional defects in our variants underscores the cumulative contribution of multiple pathogenic mechanisms to thrombotic risk. These observations support the notion that both quantitative (secretion defect) and qualitative (functional) impairments may act synergistically to exacerbate the clinical phenotype, particularly in compound heterozygous contexts.

The p.Arg211Gln variant resides at the C-terminal end of the activation peptide, a region essential for proteolytic cleavage by the thrombin-TM complex [2, 31]. Clinically, this variant has been variably reported as either a type I [32] or type II [33] deficiency, with heterozygous carriers typically showing moderately reduced PC activity and antigen levels, whereas no homozygous cases have yet been described. This ambiguity has left the underlying pathogenic mechanism unresolved. Residues near the cleavage site, particularly Asp209, Pro210, and Arg211, are highly sensitive to substitution, and prior studies suggest that alterations in this region perturb Ca²⁺-dependent activation efficiency [18, 19, 34]. Our functional data clarify this uncertainty by demonstrating that p.Arg211Gln selectively impairs proteolytic activation of PC by the thrombin-TM complex. Importantly, once activated, the resulting APC retains near-normal anticoagulant function, establishing that the primary defect resides at the level of zymogen processing rather than catalytic activity.

Structural modeling provided a plausible mechanistic explanation for the functional defect caused by p.Arg211Gln. The substitution of Arg211 with Gln appears to alter the orientation of its side chain within the thrombin catalytic pocket, which could introduce steric interference and reduce recognition of the scissile bond, thereby impairing cleavage at Arg211. This modeled structural alteration is consistent with the experimental observation that the thrombin-TM complex fails to efficiently activate the zymogen, whereas once activated, APC remains functionally intact. These findings underscore the structural precision required for zymogen recognition and highlight the activation peptide as a mutational hotspot in PROC. Clinically, these insights are critical. Because p.Arg211Gln resides in the activation peptide, distant from the catalytic site, conventional chromogenic assays, which measure the combined effect of zymogen activation and APC catalytic activity, may not fully capture the specific impairment in activation, as they cannot distinguish whether reduced activity arises from defective activation or intrinsic catalytic defects. This highlights a diagnostic blind spot and emphasizes the need for comprehensive assays that interrogate both zymogen activation and APC anticoagulant function, in order to avoid misclassification of this variant as benign or low risk.

In contrast, the p.Val367Met variant has historically been classified as a type I deficiency in previous reports [35], primarily based on chromogenic amidolytic assays and limited antigen measurements. However, our comprehensive functional analyses reveal a distinct type IIb profile. Specifically, zymogen activation and antigen levels are largely preserved, while APC anticoagulant activity is selectively impaired, as demonstrated by thrombin generation assays. Structural modeling suggests that Val367 resides within a loop adjacent to the S1 specificity pocket of the serine protease domain [36], a region critical for Na⁺ coordination [37]. Sodium ions stabilize the active conformation of serine proteases such as thrombin [38] and factor Xa [39], enhancing substrate recognition and catalytic efficiency [40, 41]. In wild-type APC, this Na⁺-binding pocket geometry supports catalytic activity, whereas substitution of the smaller hydrophobic valine with a bulkier methionine may perturb local packing and weaken Na⁺ coordination. Although AlphaFold3 modeling does not directly compute ion-binding energetics, the predicted structural perturbation is consistent with the experimentally observed reduction in Na⁺-dependent catalytic activity. It should be noted that AlphaFold3 modeling does not directly compute binding energetics or ion affinities, and therefore cannot quantitatively assess Na⁺ binding or substrate interactions. The current models should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and consistent with the biochemical data rather than as definitive structural evidence. Additional computational analyses, such as short molecular dynamics (MD) simulations or energy-minimized docking, could be pursued in future work to further substantiate these mechanistic interpretations. These findings reconcile the discrepancy between previous classifications and our experimental data, establishing p.Val367Met as a true type IIb variant. Structural modeling indicates that Val367Met may subtly perturb the loop near the S1 pocket, slightly reducing local folding efficiency. Although the overall protease fold is preserved, this local instability likely triggers intracellular accumulation, consistent with immunofluorescence and Western blot showing intracellular accumulation. Glycosylation assays (Endo H/PNGase F) could further validate protein maturation and trafficking, but they were not performed in this study. Nevertheless, quantitative Western blot and immunofluorescence provide sufficient evidence for intracellular accumulation and impaired expression. Future studies may incorporate glycosylation analysis to substantiate these findings. Thus, p.Val367Met impairs catalytic activity and exhibits intracellular accumulation with reduced secretion efficiency, providing a dual mechanism for the observed phenotype.

Amidolytic assays across a range of Na⁺ concentrations, with or without Ca²⁺, confirmed this structural prediction: APC-p.Val367Met exhibited an approximately 2-3-fold reduction in Na⁺ affinity, resulting in reduced catalytic efficiency. These findings establish Na⁺ binding as a crucial determinant of APC function and demonstrate how subtle alterations in the Na⁺-binding loop can compromise anticoagulant activity without affecting zymogen activation [42]. Clinically, the implications are profound. Routine antigen or chromogenic assays may fail to detect Na⁺-dependent catalytic defects, risking underestimation of thrombotic risk in carriers [43].

The coexistence of p.Arg211Gln and p.Val367Met in compound heterozygous individuals provides a compelling illustration of how distinct molecular defects can act synergistically to exacerbate disease severity. Each variant individually causes partial functional impairment: p.Arg211Gln compromises zymogen activation with only mild expression impairment, while p.Val367Met impairs the catalytic efficiency of APC and shows significantly increased intracellular accumulation. When combined, these defects collectively reduce circulating PC levels, limit APC generation, and impair APC activity once formed. This “double-hit” mechanism explains the early-onset and recurrent thrombotic events observed in affected family members, despite each variant alone being insufficient to produce such severe phenotypes. Importantly, this demonstrates that family-based genetic analyses are essential, as single-variant assessment may underestimate thrombotic risk in individuals with compound heterozygous or digenic inheritance.

The findings of this study have several direct clinical implications. (i) They underscore the importance of comprehensive functional testing. Sole reliance on antigen or chromogenic assays risks misclassification of patients [12], particularly those with variants like p.Arg211Gln or p.Val367Met, whose pathogenicity manifests in noncanonical ways. Incorporating thrombin-TM activation assays, Na⁺-sensitivity assays, and thrombin generation assays into diagnostic workflows would allow more accurate phenotypic characterization. (ii) Our results inform risk stratification. Carriers of type IIa or type IIb variants may present with different thrombotic risks, and compound heterozygotes are at substantially higher risk than either variant alone would predict [44]. Recognizing such patterns could guide clinical decisions regarding long-term anticoagulation, prophylaxis during high-risk periods (e.g., surgery, pregnancy), and family screening. (iii) These findings open potential avenues for therapeutic innovation [45]. For p.Arg211Gln, strategies aimed at enhancing PC activation, such as pharmacological modulation of thrombin-TM interactions or supplementation with recombinant activated PC, could be explored. For p.Val367Met, interventions that stabilize the Na⁺-binding loop or mimic Na⁺ effects on catalytic conformation may restore partial function. While speculative, these concepts exemplify how mechanistic insights can inspire targeted therapeutic development.

In summary, we provide a mechanistic characterization of two PROC variants, p.Arg211Gln and p.Val367Met, which impair anticoagulant capacity through distinct mechanisms. p.Arg211Gln disrupts zymogen activation by the thrombin-TM complex without affecting APC function once activated, consistent with a type IIa deficiency, whereas p.Val367Met selectively reduces Na⁺ binding, diminishing APC catalytic efficiency while preserving activation, consistent with a type IIb deficiency. p.Val367Met variant also exhibits a pronounced expression defect, leading to reduced circulating protein C levels, whereas p.Arg211Gln variant shows only a mild reduction. When both variants are co-inherited, the combined functional and quantitative impairments may exacerbate thrombotic risk. These findings advance understanding of PCD pathophysiology, highlight the importance of integrated genetic, functional, and structural evaluation for accurate diagnosis, and provide a framework for personalized risk assessment and therapeutic strategies. Future studies should validate Na⁺-dependent catalytic assays in clinical settings, explore modulators of PC activation or ion binding, and further investigate cellular mechanisms affecting protein C expression and maturation to better inform therapeutic approaches.
