Introduction

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare but rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft-tissue infection characterized by widespread necrosis of the fascia and subcutaneous tissue, often sparing the skin and underlying muscle until late stages. It is associated with severe systemic toxicity, high morbidity, and mortality rates exceeding 30% even with prompt treatment [1], [2]. NF typically arises from polymicrobial synergy, with Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, and gram-negative aerobes (e.g., Acinetobacter, Klebsiella) being common pathogens [3]. While NF most frequently affects the extremities, perineum (Fournier's gangrene), and abdominal wall, involvement of the thoracic wall and retroperitoneum is exceptionally rare and portends a dire prognosis due to anatomic complexity and delayed diagnosis [4], [5].

Penetrating trauma is a well-documented but uncommon precursor to NF, as breaches in the skin barrier facilitate bacterial invasion into deeper tissues [6]. The infection's rapid progression—often advancing at 2–3 cm/h—can lead to septic shock, multiorgan failure, and death without emergent surgical intervention [7]. Diagnostic challenges arise from nonspecific early symptoms (e.g., pain disproportionate to exam, erythema) and the absence of classic crepitus or gas on imaging in non-clostridial cases [8]. Scoring systems like the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) and imaging (CT/MRI) aid diagnosis, but clinical suspicion remains paramount [9].

Management hinges on aggressive resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and radical surgical debridement, often requiring multiple operations and reconstructive procedures [10]. Despite advances in critical care, mortality escalates with delays in surgery, comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, immunosuppression), and infections involving truncal regions due to their vascular and anatomic complexity [11].

Here, we present a catastrophic case of extensive NF involving the thoracic wall and retroperitoneum following a seemingly minor penetrating trauma. The patient developed fulminant septic shock with metabolic acidosis, leukocytosis (WBC 54,000), and necrotic spread from the thoracic cavity to the pelvic floor, necessitating multistage debridement, rib exposure, and eventual reconstruction with flaps and skin grafts. This case underscores the lethality of truncal NF, the challenges of managing retroperitoneal involvement, and the importance of early, radical intervention in averting fatal outcomes.

Case presentation

A previously healthy adult male presented to an outside healthcare facility following a penetrating trauma to the thoracic wall. The wound was primarily repaired, and the patient was discharged. However, within days, he returned to our tertiary care center in extremis, manifesting signs of severe septic shock: hemodynamic collapse with a blood pressure of 50–60 mmHg, thready pulses, and altered mental status (Glasgow Coma Scale score of 8). Systemic signs of inflammation were profound, including high-grade fever (39.5 °C), marked leukocytosis (WBC count of 54,000/μL), and severe lactic acidosis (pH 7.1, lactate 8.2 mmol/L). Locally, the trauma site had become grossly infected, with foul-smelling, necrotic tissue, crepitus, and cutaneous erythema extending across the posterior thorax and upper abdomen.

Diagnostic workup

Urgent imaging via computed tomography (CT) revealed a gas-forming infection tracking along the thoracic fascia and extending into the retroperitoneal space. These findings were consistent with necrotizing fasciitis (NF). The Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) score was 10, indicating a high probability of NF. Intraoperative tissue cultures isolated Acinetobacter baumannii, which was sensitive to meropenem and colistin.

Surgical management

Initial management focused on aggressive fluid resuscitation and hemodynamic support with norepinephrine, along with empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics including meropenem, vancomycin, and clindamycin. The patient underwent immediate surgical exploration. Thoracic debridement revealed extensive necrosis, requiring complete excision of the pectoralis major, serratus anterior, and latissimus dorsi muscles, exposing ribs 4 through 9. Partial resection of intercostal muscles was also necessary.

A concurrent exploratory laparotomy demonstrated retroperitoneal necrosis extending from the diaphragm to the pelvic floor, involving the fascia around the left kidney. Extensive fascial debridement and abscess drainage were performed. A chest tube was placed for a concurrent tension pneumothorax, and three surgical drains were inserted into the thoracic cavity and retroperitoneum.

Staged debridements

Over the next 14 days, the patient underwent four additional staged debridements to address residual necrotic tissue. These surgeries ultimately resulted in full exposure of thoracic ribs and resolution of all infected and non-viable tissue.

Postoperative course

The patient remained in the intensive care unit for 21 days. Sepsis gradually resolved, with vasopressors discontinued by day 10. Wound management included daily dressing changes and negative-pressure wound therapy to promote granulation. Once a healthy wound bed was achieved, reconstructive surgery was performed. A pedicled flap was used to cover the thoracic defect, and split-thickness skin grafts were applied to remaining open areas.

Outcome

Despite a predicted mortality risk exceeding 80% for thoracic and retroperitoneal necrotizing fasciitis, the patient survived. At his 12-month follow-up, he had regained full independence and returned to his baseline physical activities. Clinical examination confirmed the stability of the pedicled flap and skin grafts, with no signs of infection recurrence, late-onset incisional hernias, or functional limitations (Video 1).

Discussion

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) of the thoracic wall with retroperitoneal extension is an exceptionally rare and devastating clinical entity. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic complexities associated with truncal NF, particularly when triggered by minor penetrating trauma. The patient's initial presentation with delayed signs of infection underscores a major diagnostic challenge—early symptoms of NF are often nonspecific, and the absence of overt crepitus or radiographic gas can mislead clinicians. Despite initial wound repair, rapid microbial proliferation—here caused by Acinetobacter baumannii—led to fulminant septic shock within days.

Thoracic and retroperitoneal NF are associated with particularly poor outcomes due to the anatomical depth, proximity to vital organs, and the difficulty in achieving early, comprehensive debridement. In this case, successful management hinged on the prompt recognition of systemic toxicity, high clinical suspicion for NF (supported by a LRINEC score of 10), and immediate surgical intervention. Radical thoracic muscle excision, retroperitoneal debridement, and staged operations allowed for containment of the infection and preservation of life despite extensive tissue loss.

The use of negative-pressure wound therapy and aggressive ICU support played a pivotal role in controlling sepsis and facilitating subsequent reconstructive efforts. Multidisciplinary coordination among surgeons, intensivists, and infectious disease specialists was essential, especially in managing a multidrug-resistant organism. Ultimately, early surgical aggression, even in the face of extensive tissue loss and multiorgan dysfunction, proved life-saving.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the high lethality and rapid progression of necrotizing fasciitis involving the thoracic wall and retroperitoneum, particularly when precipitated by penetrating trauma. Early recognition, aggressive surgical debridement, and coordinated critical care can significantly alter what is otherwise a grim prognosis. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for NF in any patient with rapid clinical deterioration and deep soft tissue involvement, regardless of the initial severity of trauma. Prompt, radical intervention remains the cornerstone of survival in truncal necrotizing fasciitis.
