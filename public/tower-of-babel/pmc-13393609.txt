Prevalence and associated factors of opioid-related disorders among patients with chronic pain: a single-center retrospective study in a Chinese tertiary hospital
Authors: Zhiyan Xu, Cheng Si, Yinling Dong, Hang Zhang, Jinyuan Zhang, Weihong Ge, Xiaoyan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpioid-related disorders (ORD) among patients with chronic pain represents a significant clinical challenge, yet surveillance data within Chinese healthcare settings remain limited. This study explored the feasibility of utilizing hospital information system (HIS) data to quantify indicators of ORD and analyze associated factors in a tertiary care setting.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. Patients with chronic pain prescribed opioids for ≥ 90 days between 2022 and 2023 were included. Indicators of ORD were defined by the presence of an ICD-10 diagnosis of opioid use disorder (OUD), documented subjective symptoms, or prescription overlap accounting for ≥ 25% of the total treatment duration. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent associated factors for ORD.ResultsAmong 751 eligible patients, 126 (16.8%) met the criteria for ORD. Multivariate analysis revealed that a history of cancer (OR: 2.47; 95% CI: 1.26–4.86), obtaining prescriptions from multiple departments (OR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.16–1.71), and the initial use of sustained-release (SR) formulations (OR: 1.97; 95% CI: 1.18–3.29) were significant independent associated factors. Furthermore, every 50 mg increment in the maximum daily morphine milligram equivalent (MME) was significantly associated with an increased factor of ORD (OR: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.18–1.37).ConclusionIatrogenic ORD is a notable concern in this population, driven by specific prescribing patterns such as the early initiation of SR formulations and multi-source prescribing. These findings highlight the urgency of implementing active HIS-based surveillance algorithms and establishing regional inter-hospital data sharing to mitigate the risks of long-term dependency and OUD.Keywords: Chronic pain, Opioid-related disorders, Associated factors, Hospital information system, Pharmacovigilance

Introduction

The clinical spectrum of opioid-related disorders (ORD) is broad, encompassing both opioid misuse and opioid use disorder (OUD). Opioid misuse refers to the non-prescribed use of opioids, while OUD specifically denotes a pattern of opioid use that results in clinically significant impairment or distress, primarily manifested through two severe consequences: opioid dependence and opioid overdose [1]. The burden of ORD represents a critical global public health challenge, with the 2024 United Nations World Drug Report and the 2023 Global Burden of Disease Study estimating that over 60 million people use opioids globally, contributing to approximately 17 million prevalent cases of OUD [2–4]. In China, while strict regulations have historically controlled opioid availability, the rising prevalence of cancer and chronic non-cancer pain has led to increased clinical utilization of prescription opioids [5, 6]. Consequently, the poly-drug use and misuse of prescription opioids, such as tramadol and oxycodone, have emerged as a growing concern among Chinese patients, contributing to the rising prevalence of ORD [7]. For many individuals, healthcare settings serve as the primary source of initial prescription opioid exposure [8]. In clinical practice, patients with chronic pain are typically initiated on non-opioid analgesics. When these conventional treatments prove insufficient or pain severity escalates, oral opioids may be prescribed. However, as tolerance to opioids develops or analgesic needs further increase, some patients may resort to dose escalation or non-standard administration methods, significantly heightening the risk of developing ORD [9].

In the context of chronic pain management, patients can legitimately experience severe pain while simultaneously engaging in opioid misuse or suffering from OUD; these conditions are not mutually exclusive [10]. Therefore, the clinical challenge lies not in verifying the pain, but rather in distinguishing between patients who strictly adhere to prescribed opioid regimens and those who exhibit signs of ORD [11]. Currently, these disorders are assessed through distinct clinical frameworks: OUD is definitively diagnosed using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria [12], while opioid misuse is often identified using validated screening tools like the Current Opioid Misuse Measure (COMM) [13]. However, the development of ORD is frequently preceded or exacerbated by inappropriate opioid prescription (IOP) practices [14]. IOP represents prescriber- or system-level issues that deviate from evidence-based guidelines, serving as significant associated factor for patient misuse and subsequent OUD. Prescription Drug Monitoring Programs (PDMPs) and electronic health records offer a data-driven approach to detect these IOP indicators and aberrant patient behaviors. For instance, studies analyzing prescription records consistently identify overlapping opioid prescriptions accounting for ≥ 25% of total treatment days, or obtaining prescriptions from multiple prescribers (“doctor shopping”), as strong indicators of IOP and potential ORD [15–19].

Beyond overlapping prescriptions, the literature has identified several other IOP indicators and associated factor associated with ORD, including specific opioid regimens, high dosages, and concurrent use of sedative medications [20–22]. The inappropriate initiation of opioid therapy with sustained-release (SR) formulations is a recognized IOP indicator associated with a higher risk of subsequent misuse and OUD [23–25]. Furthermore, clinical guidelines establish clear dosage thresholds to minimize harm. Maximum recommended daily limits range from 90 morphine milligram equivalents (MME) in the United States and Canada to 120 MME in Germany [26–29]. Notably, the 2022 CDC Clinical Practice Guideline warns that daily dosages reaching ≥ 50 MME significantly increase specific risks, including severe physical dependence and ORD, explicitly advising against initiating treatment with SR formulations [26]. Particularly, extreme daily doses (> 200 MME) are not only linked to central sleep apnea [30], but also drastically increase the risk of life-threatening complications, including severe respiratory depression, fatal overdose, and death [31–33].

In China, the Expert Consensus on the Construction of Pharmacovigilance Systems in Medical Institutions (2022), jointly issued by the Center for Drug Evaluation of the National Medical Products Administration and the Chinese Pharmacists Association, has formally incorporated the monitoring of ORD into hospital pharmacovigilance frameworks. This consensus recommends establishing comprehensive workflows for risk monitoring, conducting specialized staff training, and formulating watchlists for high-risk drugs to ensure medication safety without compromising necessary treatment [34]. Despite these regulatory advancements, empirical data on the surveillance of ORD and its specific prevalence within Chinese medical institutions remain scarce [35, 36].

To address the data gap in Chinese healthcare settings, this study reviewed opioid utilization among patients with chronic pain at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. We aimed to explore the feasibility of mining hospital information system data to identify indicators of opioid misuse and OUD, and to analyze the relationship between key clinical characteristics and ORD.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This single-center retrospective study analyzed opioid use among chronic pain patients receiving long-term opioid therapy at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital by extracting demographic information, electronic medical records, treatment details, and laboratory test results from the hospital information system (HIS). It explored the relationship between associated factors and indicators of ORD. We explicitly acknowledge ORD is not a standard psychiatric diagnosis but rather an operationally defined composite metric designed for retrospective data mining in this study. The research protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School (Approval ID: 2024-097-01). Given the retrospective design of the study and the use of de-identified data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Study population, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and drug selection

We reviewed data for patients receiving long-term opioid analgesia between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a documented diagnosis or clinical record indicating chronic pain or chronic intractable pain; (3) continuous use of prescribed opioids covering every consecutive day within a 90-day window during the study period. Patients receiving hospice care or those with incomplete data were excluded.

A total of 45,036 patients who received opioid prescriptions at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023, were initially identified. Following the application of inclusion criteria, 776 patients aged ≥ 18 years with chronic pain treated with opioids for ≥ 90 days were selected for further evaluation. Twenty-five patients were subsequently excluded (15 due to enrollment in hospice care and 10 due to missing EMR demographic data). Given that the overall missingness rate was minimal (1.3%, 10/776), we adopted a complete case analysis approach for the remaining cohort. Ultimately, 751 patients met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. Based on the established screening criteria for opioid misuse and OUD, 126 patients were classified into the ORD group, while the remaining 625 patients comprised the non-ORD group. Patient screening flowchart

The study included oral and topical opioids listed in the Catalogue of Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Drugs (October 2023 edition) currently used at the hospital. These included tramadol (acetaminophen tablets and SR hydrochloride tablets), morphine (sulfate SR tablets and hydrochloride tablets), oxycodone hydrochloride SR tablets, and fentanyl transdermal patches. Buprenorphine transdermal patches were excluded from the primary opioid assessment due to their distinct safety profile and the lack of a standardized MME conversion factor [37].

MME calculation and dosage categorization

To standardize dosages, all opioid prescriptions were converted to daily MME using conversion factors derived from the 2022 CDC Clinical Practice Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Pain. Specific conversion factors utilized were: morphine (1.0), oxycodone (1.5), fentanyl transdermal patches (2.4), and tramadol conversion factor varies (0.1–0.2) by formulation. Specifically, conversion factors of 0.1 and 0.2 were applied to tramadol SR hydrochloride tablets and tramadol acetaminophen tablets, respectively. Fentanyl patches (mcg/h) and other opioids (mg/day) were converted accordingly. Based on guidelines and relevant literature, daily dosages were stratified into six levels: extremely low (< 30 MME), low (30–50 MME), medium (50–90 MME), higher (90–120 MME), high (120–200 MME), and extremely high (> 200 MME).

Definition of ORD and data collection

Based on evidence-based practice and references, ORD, encompassing both OUD and opioid misuse, were identified by the presence of at least one of the following criteria in HIS records (identified through keyword queries followed by manual validation of clinical notes by two independent investigators): (1) Clinical diagnosis: A diagnosis of mental and behavioral disorders due to the use of opioids (ICD-10 code F11); (2) Documented clinical symptoms: Explicit documentation in the EMR (captured via keyword searches such as “craving”, “unauthorized dose escalation”, “withdrawal symptoms”, or “inability to reduce dose”) meeting at least two of the 11 diagnostic criteria for OUD as defined by the DSM-5; (3) Abnormal prescribing patterns: Overlapping opioid prescriptions accounting for ≥ 25% of the total duration of drug use. The 25% overlap was adapted from regulatory frameworks (e.g., the North Carolina Administrative Code) which utilize a 25% threshold to identify anomalous prescribing patterns [38], and is supported by broader literature evaluating high-risk prescription opioid misuse [18, 39]. When applying the ≥ 25% prescription overlap criterion, investigators manually reviewed clinical notes to exclude overlaps caused by legitimate clinical needs, including early planned refills, drug substitutions, increased disease severity, or physiological tolerance. Only overlaps strongly indicative of craving or euphoria-seeking behaviors were classified as ORD.

The patient characteristic information collected in this study includes demographics (age, gender, no fixed income), healthcare utilization (self-payment for opioids, the number of prescribing departments), clinical history (history of tumors, surgery, liver disease, kidney disease, psychiatric treatment), medication factors(duration of opioid use, maximum daily MME, initial use of SR formulations, MME of the initial prescription), and concomitant medications (concurrent use of benzodiazepines for ≥ 1 week, buprenorphine transdermal patches for ≥ 1 week). In this study, the first dose is defined as the very first opioid prescription the patient received at our hospital within the study window. Similarly, the initial SR formulation refers strictly to the first sustained-release formulation prescribed during this period.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) and MSTATA (www.mstata.com). Numerical variables are expressed as means ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) or medians with interquartile range (25th -75th percentiles) based on normality, and occurrence is expressed as frequencies (%).

Patients were classified into an experimental (ORD) and control (non-ORD) groups. Associated factor for the development of ORD were assessed using univariate and multivariate analyses. Differences in normal numerical variables between groups were assessed in a univariate analysis using independent sample t-tests or analysis of variance. Differences in abnormal numerical variables and in frequency data between groups were analyzed using two independent sample rank sum tests, and chi-square tests, respectively. Variables demonstrating statistical significance (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were selected for the multivariate logistic regression model. With 126 events and 9 independent variables, the events per variable (EPV) ratio was 14. Given this robust EPV, all univariate-significant candidates were included without further stepwise reduction. Prior to regression, Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) diagnostics were performed, with all VIF values < 2.0, indicating no severe multicollinearity among predictors.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 751 included patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 67 years (IQR: 58–75), with 59.7% of patients aged > 65 years. Males accounted for 55.2% of the population, and 31.4% lacked a fixed income. Regarding medical history, malignancy was the most prevalent comorbidity (49.8%), followed by liver disease (17.2%), kidney disease (17.0%), history of surgery (11.1%), and psychiatric treatment (2.9%). In terms of prescription and utilization patterns, patients obtained opioids from a median of 2 (IQR: 1–3) departments. The median duration of opioid therapy was 145 days (IQR: 108–219), and the median maximum daily dosage was 80 MME (IQR: 40–120). Approximately one-quarter of patients (26.2%) purchased opioids via self-payment. Substance use history was less common, with smoking and alcohol consumption reported in 4.0% and 2.4% of patients, respectively. Regarding concomitant medications, concurrent use of benzodiazepines for ≥ 1 week was observed in 18.5% of patients, while 2.5% utilized buprenorphine transdermal patches concurrently for ≥ 1 week.

Opioid utilization and ORD patterns

Opioid-related disorders

Based on the established screening criteria, 126 patients (16.8%) of the total cohort were identified as having ORD. Among the 126 patients identified with ORD, 9 (7.1%) met the ICD-10 diagnostic criteria, 46 (36.5%) were identified through documented DSM-5 clinical symptoms, and 99 (78.6%) met the ≥ 25% prescription overlap criterion. Specifically, regarding single criteria, 75 patients were identified solely by prescription overlap, 19 by DSM-5 symptoms, and 5 by ICD-10 diagnosis. Regarding concurrent criteria, 23 patients met both DSM-5 and prescription overlap, 3 met both ICD-10 and DSM-5, and 1 patient met all three criteria simultaneously. A detailed visualization of this distribution is provided in Fig. 2. Specific analysis of the implicated substances among these ORD cases revealed that oxycodone and tramadol were the most frequently implicated agents, accounting for 8.5% (n = 64) and 7.6% (n = 57) of the total cohort, respectively. Fentanyl (3.1%, n = 23) and morphine (0.3%, n = 2) were less commonly misused. Notably, polysubstance involvement was identified in 18 of these patients (2.4% of the total cohort), involving at least two types of opioids.

Distribution of ORD Patients

Chronic pain

The primary etiologies of chronic pain were analyzed based on outpatient diagnoses and inpatient discharge summaries. Malignant tumors represented the most prevalent etiology, affecting nearly half of the study population (49.8%, n = 374). Other major causes included neuralgia (47.0%, n = 353) and musculoskeletal pain (27.2%, n = 204), while postoperative pain (11.1%, n = 83) and visceral colic (4.7%, n = 35) were less frequent.

Opioid prescriptions

A total of 13,304 opioid prescriptions were issued to the 751 patients, comprising 9,562 outpatient and 3,742 inpatient prescriptions. The overall median daily dosage was 40 MME (IQR: 30–80), indicating a predominantly low-dose usage pattern. Regarding specific agents, tramadol was the most frequently prescribed opioid (54.1%), followed by oxycodone (37.2%), with fentanyl and morphine accounting for a minor proportion of prescriptions. Stratification by dosage intensity revealed that tramadol acetaminophen, tramadol hydrochloride SR, and morphine hydrochloride tablets were typically associated with low daily dosages (< 50 MME); oxycodone hydrochloride SR tablets corresponded to medium dosages (50–90 MME); while fentanyl transdermal patches and morphine sulfate SR tablets were associated with high dosages (≥ 120 MME). These prescription patterns are visualized in Fig. 3, a Sankey diagram that illustrates the distribution flows of patients across age groups, sex, formulation types, and daily MME categories, with flow widths proportional to patient volume.

Prescription patterns

Initial opioid regimens

We analyzed the characteristics of the initial opioid prescriptions issued to patients at the hospital (Table 2). Regarding the choice of analgesic agent, tramadol was the predominant drug for treatment initiation (79.6%), followed by oxycodone (15.3%), while morphine (1.3%) and fentanyl (3.7%) were prescribed significantly less frequently. In terms of formulation type, immediate-release preparations accounted for 53.3% of initial prescriptions, whereas sustained-release formulations were utilized in 46.7% of cases. It should be noted that the high implication rates of oxycodone and tramadol likely reflect their overall high prescribing frequency in our institution rather than inherently higher abuse liability.

Multivariate logistic regression

The statistically significant variables were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent factors associated with ORD (Table 4; Fig. 4). The regression analysis demonstrated that the number of prescribing departments, analyzed as a continuous variable, was a significant associated factors; specifically, each additional department from which a patient received opioid prescriptions increased the risk of ORD by 41% (OR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.16–1.71; P < 0.001). Similarly, every 50 mg increment in the maximum daily MME was significantly associated with a 27% increased risk of ORD (OR: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.18–1.37; P < 0.001). Furthermore, clinical characteristics played a critical role; patients with a history of cancer exhibited a more than two-fold increase in the odds of developing ORD (OR: 2.47, 95% CI: 1.26–4.86; P = 0.009), and those initiated on sustained-release formulations were also at significantly higher risk (OR: 1.97, 95% CI: 1.18–3.29; P = 0.009).

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the prevalence and associated factors of ORD among chronic pain patients in a Chinese tertiary hospital. Our analysis revealed an overall ORD prevalence of 16.8% in our cohort. Furthermore, we identified four independent factors significantly associated with the development of ORD: a history of malignancy, obtaining prescriptions from multiple departments, the initiation of therapy with SR formulations, and high daily opioid dosages.

In terms of overall utilization, the median daily opioid dosage among patients with chronic pain at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital was 40 MME, representing a relatively safe, low-dose profile. The prescribing pattern demonstrated a rational preference for tramadol, a weak opioid, over stronger alternatives. Prescriptions involving high or extremely high doses—exceeding maximum guideline recommendations—were primarily associated with potent SR formulations, such as fentanyl transdermal patches, morphine sulfate SR tablets, and oxycodone hydrochloride SR tablets. Regarding treatment initiation, the selection of analgesic agents appeared largely rational. The vast majority of patients (79.6%) were initiated on tramadol, while potent opioids like fentanyl patches or morphine sulfate SR tablets were rarely selected as first-line therapy. Similarly, dosage selection was generally conservative; most patients commenced therapy at low-to-medium doses, with fewer than 10% initiating treatment at ≥ 90 MME. However, the choice of formulation at the onset of treatment warrants closer attention. The proportion of patients initiated on immediate-release preparations was comparable to that of patients initiated on SR formulations. While the optimal formulation for the dose-titration phase remains a subject of debate in the literature, major clinical guidelines—including those from the CDC—explicitly advise against initiating treatment with SR preparations [26]. Crucially, our study corroborated this recommendation by identifying the initial use of SR formulations as a significant independent associated factor for the development of ORD. This association warrants a nuanced interpretation and may be partially driven by “confounding by indication”, where clinicians preferentially prescribe SR formulations to patients with more severe baseline pain profiles or complex clinical presentations, who are inherently at higher risk for developing ORD [40]. Furthermore, SR formulations typically contain higher aggregate loads of opioids per tablet to allow for prolonged release. If the release mechanism is compromised (e.g., by chewing or crushing), the immediate absorption of a large dose can induce profound euphoria, thereby reinforcing the progression to OUD. This pharmacological characteristic renders SR formulations particularly attractive for opioid misuse, despite their intended stable profile [41]. Consequently, patients initiating therapy with SR formulations require enhanced pharmaceutical surveillance and rigorous clinical monitoring to mitigate the risk of adverse outcomes.

ORD is a comprehensive concept encompassing multiple definitions of opioid abuse, including opioid misuse and opioid use disorder. Furthermore, the conceptual overlap between “opioid misuse” and “opioid use disorder” often leads to inconsistent classification, resulting in widely fluctuating prevalence rates across studies [1]. In the present study, we identified 126 patients exhibiting indicators of ORD, representing a prevalence rate of 16.8%, which is notably lower than rates reported in many international studies. This discrepancy may be attributed to two primary factors. First, China maintains stringent drug control policies and a rigorous investigative system, making it difficult for illicit drug users to impersonate patients to obtain narcotics, patients typically initiate opioid therapy for legitimate medical purposes but subsequently develop misuse behaviors and progress to OUD due to inadequate pharmaceutical surveillance [42]. Second, the study’s design imposed inherent limitations. As a single-center retrospective analysis, we could only monitor prescription behaviors within Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. Currently, China lacks a regional PDMP or an information-sharing platform among medical institutions, making it impossible to detect “doctor shopping” behaviors across different hospitals. Additionally, the retrospective nature of the study relied solely on electronic medical records (EMR) for screening, precluding the use of validated scales (such as the COMM) to assess the risk of ORD, and we excluded the effects of buprenorphine on ORD, which likely led to an underestimation of the true prevalence. Furthermore, utilizing a 25% prescription overlap as a criterion for ORD transposes a regulatory metric originally designed for prescriber-level anomalies onto individual patient behavior. This transposition may affect the prevalence of ORD, particularly in a healthcare system where patients legitimately obtain prescriptions from multiple departments for complex care coordination. Importantly, our study period partially overlapped with strict COVID-19 pandemic restrictions in China. Patients unable or afraid to attend regular hospital appointments may have actively sought early refills or stockpile medications out of necessity rather than drug-seeking behavior. This pandemic-driven behavior likely contributed to an overestimation of the ORD prevalence identified through the ≥ 25% prescription overlap metric. In light of these limitations, we strongly advocate for the establishment of a centralized prescription information-sharing mechanism that integrates hospital information systems with public security databases and necessitates robust ethical firewalls to protect legitimate patients, thereby achieving real-time identity verification, which would enable the early identification of high-risk individuals. Such a system would effectively prevent patients from obtaining opioids from multiple institutions and mitigate the risk of diversion into the community.

As highlighted in our initial summary, our univariate and multivariate regression analyses identified four independent associated factors for the development of ORD: a history of malignancy, obtaining prescriptions from multiple departments, the initiation of therapy with SR formulations, and high daily opioid dosages. These findings are largely consistent with established evidence in the current literature. However, several well-documented associated factor reported in previous studies—specifically sex, history of psychiatric treatment, income status, and concomitant benzodiazepine use—did not reach statistical significance in our analysis [43–45]. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the limited sample size of this single-center study, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect these associations. Moreover, owing to the limitations of the retrospective data extraction from the HIS, we were unable to include baseline pain intensity scores (e.g., NRS or visual analog scale (VAS)) as a covariate in the regression analysis. Since higher pain intensity is a known driver for both higher dosages and SR formulation use, the lack of adjustment for pain severity represents a limitation that may confound the observed associations. Therefore, we recommend that future multi-center studies with larger cohorts continue to investigate these potential variables. Furthermore, our data highlighted a subset of patients with overlapping prescription durations. This pattern indicates that patients frequently requested early refills before their previous supply was theoretically exhausted, objectively resulting in the accumulation of excess medication. Such behavior serves as a critical indicator of potential risk for ORD and warrants closer clinical monitoring. It is vital to clarify that the strong association between cancer history and ORD (OR: 2.47) does not imply that oncology patients inherently possess addictive tendencies. Rather, this association likely reflects that cancer patients face a heavier pain burden, longer and more aggressive treatment courses, a greater likelihood of requiring SR formulations, and profound psychosocial distress.

Particular attention must be directed toward the issue of ORD among patients with chronic non-cancer pain (CNCP) [46]. Unlike cancer-related pain, the evidence base supporting the long-term efficacy of opioids for CNCP is significantly less robust [47]. The clinical trajectories of these two conditions differ markedly: while cancer pain typically intensifies with disease progression, CNCP symptoms often stabilize or even improve following appropriate diagnosis and interventional management [48, 49]. Given the inherent subjectivity of pain assessment, there is a heightened potential for discordance between a patient’s reported pain levels and actual physiological requirements. This discrepancy significantly increases the susceptibility to ORD, as medication consumption may persist despite a reduction in pathological pain [50].

Significant inter-individual variability in analgesic efficacy is common among patients with chronic pain [51]. Inadequate pain control often drives patients to aggressively seek additional medication—a behavior that is clinically distinct from OUD [52]. Furthermore, the development of pharmacological tolerance and physical dependence are expected physiological adaptations to chronic opioid therapy, rather than definitive indicators of ORD [26]. However, misconceptions regarding these physiological processes often result in under-reporting. Healthcare providers may harbor a fear of professional liability, erroneously perceiving indicators of ORD as failures in their own clinical management, which leads to a reluctance to document or report actual incidents [53]. Consequently, the risk of ORD is frequently underestimated, and proactive surveillance remains insufficient. Addressing these challenges necessitates the optimization of opioid stewardship programs by adopting active surveillance strategies with an expanded scope, specifically targeting high-risk behaviors such as “doctor shopping” and dangerous polypharmacy combinations [16, 19]. We strongly advocate for empowering clinical pharmacists to assume a central role in this management process. Leveraging their specialized expertise in pharmacokinetics and medication reviews, pharmacists are uniquely positioned to conduct continuous follow-ups, establish clear tapering schedules, and bridge the communication gap between prescribers and patients, thereby effectively mitigating the risks of long-term dependency [54–56].

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, the investigation was conducted at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, a prominent Tertiary Grade A medical center in China. As a major regional referral hub, the hospital serves a high volume of patients from diverse geographical backgrounds across the country, which enhances the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to the broader Chinese chronic pain population. Second, the study utilized a rigorous screening process derived from a large initial dataset of 45,036 patients. This comprehensive sampling strategy allowed for the identification of a robust cohort of 751 patients receiving long-term opioid therapy, providing a substantial evidentiary basis for analyzing ORD patterns and associated factor in a clinical setting.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design precludes causal interpretations; variables such as dose escalation and SR formulation initiation may either precede ORD or result from emerging drug-seeking behaviors. Second, our reliance on EMR data depends heavily on clinician documentation, potentially underestimating the true prevalence compared to prospective studies using validated tools like the COMM scale. Third, we lacked baseline pain severity data (e.g., VAS scores), meaning we cannot definitively exclude confounding by indication. Fourth, the absence of a unified regional PDMP in China prevented the capture of cross-institutional ‘doctor shopping’. Finally, regarding outcome measurements, relying on a composite operational definition introduces potential misclassification bias and outcome heterogeneity. Specifically, transposing a prescriber-level metric (the 25% overlap) onto individual patient-level behavior risks further misclassification, especially in complex care settings. Furthermore, the lack of formal inter-rater reliability metrics (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) during manual chart review limits strict reproducibility. Distinguishing true misuse from legitimate logistical stockpiling also remains challenging retrospectively, particularly as our study period overlapped with COVID-19 pandemic restrictions. Future multi-center, prospective investigations with integrated data sharing are warranted to validate these findings and provide a more comprehensive overview of opioid safety.

Conclusion

Our study elucidates the prevalence and specific risk profile of ORD among patients with chronic pain in a tertiary Chinese healthcare setting. We identified an overall ORD prevalence of 16.8% and four independent associated factors for its development: a history of malignancy, multi-source prescribing, high daily opioid dosages, and the initiation of therapy with SR formulations. The latter finding is particularly significant, as clinicians have traditionally favored SR formulations for treatment initiation under the assumption that their stable, prolonged release profiles render them inherently less susceptible to opioid misuse. However, our empirical evidence challenges this conventional assumption and aligns with major international protocols, specifically the CDC Clinical Practice Guideline, which explicitly advises against initiating opioid therapy with SR preparations [26]. These results suggest that the prevention of ORD requires a paradigm shift from passive regulation to active, data-driven stewardship. It is imperative to establish regional prescription information-sharing mechanisms to detect cross-institutional “doctor shopping”, and integrate clinical pharmacists more deeply into the pain management team based on recommendations in the literature. By leveraging HIS for real-time surveillance and optimizing initial prescribing strategies, healthcare institutions can better balance the imperative of effective analgesia with the critical need to minimize long-term dependency and OUD.
