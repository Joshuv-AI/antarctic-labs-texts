Implementation of electronic medication management systems in hospital settings: a theory-informed systematic review protocol of mechanisms influencing patient safety, operational efficiency and staff experience
Authors: Chisom Victor Ukaegbu, Deborah Kamsi Asiegbu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionElectronic medication management systems (EMMS) are central to hospital digital transformation and are intended to improve patient safety, operational efficiency and staff experience, but evidence of their impact remains fragmented, inconsistent and context-dependent. Consequently, there is an urgent need to understand how local organisational structures and complex sociotechnical factors drive successful long-term integration.Methods and analysisThis systematic review will search MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus, ABI/INFORM Collection and Business Source Premier for empirical hospital studies published from 2010 to 2026 that centrally apply a theoretical or conceptual framework. Study quality will be appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) Version 2018, and a theory-informed thematic synthesis will identify implementation mechanisms and organise them into higher-order domains. This protocol has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261283974).Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required because only published data will be used. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and doctoral outputs.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420261283974

Introduction

Electronic medication management systems (EMMS) are central to hospital digital transformation and are extensively advocated to enhance safety, efficiency and broader organisational performance.1–4 By replacing paper-based medication processes, including prescribing, administration and more, EMMS intends to standardise medicines management, improve information access and support clinical decision-making.2 3 5 6 These systems should reduce medicine prescribing and administration errors, strengthen medicines governance and improve workflow coordination.1 5 However, evidence regarding their implementation impact on patient safety, operational efficiency and staff experience remains fragmented and inconsistent across settings and stages of rollout.1 4 5

EMMS deployment offers clinical and organisational benefits but also introduces important sociotechnical challenges, including usability concerns, interoperability limitations, alert burden, infrastructure constraints and the need for tailored training and support.1–3 6 7 These factors influence whether EMMS are used as intended, how well they are embedded into routine practice and whether the anticipated benefits are realised.1 3 5 7 8 The impact of EMMS depends not only on the technology itself but also on the clinical and organisational setting.4 5 8 Poor integration with existing workflows may disrupt established routines, elevate workarounds, modify communication patterns and generate new avenues for error.2–6 Implementation should therefore be understood as a sociotechnical process rather than a purely technical endeavour, with results arising from interactions between people, tasks, technology and organisational structures.4 5 8

A range of theoretical and conceptual frameworks has been employed to examine EMMS implementation. Some studies use acceptance-orientated frameworks, including the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), which emphasise perceived usefulness, ease of use and behavioural intention.9 10 Others apply human factors and organisational approaches, such as Actor Network Theory (ANT), Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and Normalisation Process Theory (NPT), to explore aspects of EMMS implementation, including integration into daily practices, redistribution of work and temporal adaptation over time.11–13 However, these frameworks have not been integrated to clarify which implementation processes are most consistently associated with better or worse outcomes.

Despite the growing body of EMMS implementation research, no systematic review has yet synthesised the evidence through a mechanism-based lens that cuts across individual theoretical frameworks. Many studies have examined specific EMMS systems or individual outcomes (eg, medication errors), but none has integrated findings across heterogeneous frameworks to identify which implementation mechanisms recur across studies and settings and how these mechanisms influence the interrelated outcomes of patient safety, operational efficiency and staff experience. This review addresses that gap by moving beyond framework-by-framework comparison towards a cross-cutting synthesis of implementation mechanisms, thereby generating findings that are more transferable across hospital contexts and more directly useful for implementation planning. The primary objective of this systematic review is to synthesise empirical research on the implementation of EMMS in hospital settings that apply a theoretical or conceptual framework, to explain how implementation mechanisms influence patient safety, operational efficiency and staff experience.

Methods and analysis

This protocol has been prepared in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 checklist (see attached online supplemental file A),14 and the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome and Studies)15 frameworks were employed to formulate the search strategy and establish a structure for the review. The systematic review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420261283974) on 05 May 2026. The current version is 1.2 (01 July 2026), which reflects refinements to the Scopus search strategy in addition to the minor refinements in outcome descriptors in Version 1.1. This is an original protocol. Any amendments will be documented and tracked through PROSPERO version updates and reported in the final review manuscript. The search strategy was developed in consultation with an information specialist (librarian) at Swansea University Library Services, with expertise in health informatics and systematic review methodology. The librarian contributed to the development of the search terms, database selection and the adaptation of the strategy across databases. The search strategy is structured around four concept sets: Set 1 (electronic medication management systems and related digital health technologies), Set 2 (hospital and clinical care settings), Set 3 (implementation, adoption, and quality improvement processes) and Set 4 (theoretical and conceptual frameworks), combined using Boolean operators and adapted for each database’s native syntax. The search strategy was designed to prioritise sensitivity because terminology in the EMMS literature is fragmented. Set 1 intentionally uses broad terminology to avoid excluding relevant studies, while specificity is introduced through the Boolean intersection with Sets 2, 3 and 4. Pilot searches were used to refine the strategy and confirm that the combined sets captured the relevant literature without over-narrowing retrieval. The sample Embase (Ovid) search in table 2 below yielded approximately 2087 records, and final retrieval numbers across all databases will be reported in the PRISMA flow diagram. Searches will be conducted in Embase (Ovid), MEDLINE (Ovid), PsycINFO (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), ABI/INFORM Collection (ProQuest), Business Source Premier (EBSCOhost) and Scopus (Elsevier) for studies published between 2010 and 2025 (see attached supplemental file B). An updated search covering January 2026 to June 2026 will be undertaken prior to review completion to identify any additional eligible studies.

Studies will be eligible for inclusion if they examine the implementation, adoption, integration or sustainability of EMMS within hospital settings. Eligible systems include electronic prescription and medicines administration (EPMA), computerised provider order entry (CPOE), electronic health records (EHR), clinical decision support systems (CDSS) and similar systems that support prescribing and/or medication administration and are embedded within broader medication management processes. For the purposes of this review, broader medication management processes are defined as the interconnected stages of medication use in hospital settings, including prescribing, verification, dispensing, administration and monitoring, supported by integrated electronic systems and workflows. Relevant systems may also incorporate functionalities such as clinical decision support, interoperability, or workflow integration across clinical processes. Studies must report original empirical findings and incorporate a theoretical or conceptual framework as a central component of the study design, data collection, analysis, or interpretation. Qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods studies conducted in real-world hospital settings will be included. Studies published between 2010 and June 2026 will be considered, with no language restrictions applied. The 2010 start date reflects the period when EMMS moved from early pilots to broader institutional deployment and when framework-informed implementation research became more prevalent in the literature.

Studies will be excluded if they are conducted outside hospital or focus exclusively on outpatient or primary care contexts. Studies focusing solely on standalone or single-function systems that are not integrated into these broader medication management processes will be excluded. Non-empirical publications will be excluded. Studies that do not apply a theoretical or conceptual framework as a central component of the study will also be excluded. Studies published prior to 2010 will not be considered. All records identified through the search will be imported into Rayyan, a reference management system, and duplicates removed. Two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Full texts of selected articles will then be assessed against the inclusion criteria. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted if necessary. Where required, non-English studies will be translated using appropriate translation tools such as Google Translate or DeepL with interpretation checked where necessary to ensure accuracy. The study selection process will be reported using a PRISMA flow diagram. A screening decision aid with worked examples of the 'centrally applied’ criterion will be developed as an online supplement to support consistent application during full-text screening.

Two independent reviewers will extract data from the included studies based on a predetermined data extraction form (see table 3). Disputes in extraction will be addressed through dialogue among reviewers or consultation with a third reviewer if disputes are not resolved. The methodological quality of the included studies will be assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) Version 2018, a validated instrument specifically developed for systematic reviews of mixed studies, facilitating uniform evaluation across qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods designs.16 MMAT ratings will be reported in a summary table for each included study and used to describe the overall quality and key methodological limitations of the evidence base; they will not be used to weight studies, determine inclusion or exclude findings from the narrative, theory-informed thematic synthesis.

A theory-informed thematic synthesis will be undertaken, consistent with established approaches from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for mixed-methods systematic reviews.17 The primary focus of synthesis will be the identification of recurring implementation mechanisms related to patient safety, operational efficiency and staff experience in EMMS implementation. These three outcome measures will function as higher-order consequence domains through which the influence of these mechanisms is interpreted rather than as standardised outcome measures requiring meta-analytic comparability. Reported concepts, constructs, themes and determinants from each included study will first be extracted and coded. Related findings will then be interpreted to identify recurring implementation mechanisms through which EMMS influences outcomes. These mechanisms will subsequently be organised into higher-order domains according to their function and level of influence. The identification of recurring implementation mechanisms will proceed through three analytical steps. First, descriptive themes will be generated inductively from the findings of each included study. Second, these themes will be compared across studies and grouped into analytic themes based on conceptual similarity, regardless of the specific theoretical framework used in individual studies. Third, each analytic theme will be assessed for its role as a potential implementation mechanism: that is, a recurring process through which EMMS influences outcomes. Disagreements in the identification or categorisation of mechanisms will be resolved through discussion between the two reviewers. Where consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted to adjudicate. A record of all coding decisions, including borderline cases and the rationale for inclusion or exclusion as a mechanism, will be maintained in a study audit trail to ensure transparency.

This approach reflects a convergent integrated synthesis whereby qualitative and quantitative findings are analysed together using a common analytical framework.17 The synthesis will be predominantly narrative and will focus on explaining how implementation processes influence patient safety, operational efficiency and staff experience. Theoretical frameworks will be used to contextualise findings rather than serve as the primary unit of analysis. Results will be presented in summary tables and visual models where appropriate. Given the anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, outcomes and theoretical frameworks across included studies, a quantitative meta-analysis is not planned. The synthesis will be narrative and thematic, following the JBI methodology for mixed-methods systematic reviews.17
