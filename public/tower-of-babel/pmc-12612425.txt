Development of the rationale of a personalized cancer vaccine based on the in situ vaccine effect of radiotherapy: a mechanistic study of the POLARSTAR trial
Authors: Kai Pang, Peilin Sun, Xinzhi Liu, Dan Yang, Pengfei Zhao, Yongjie Huang, Shun Cao, Yang Gao, Guangyong Chen, Huajing Yu, Liping Duan, Yingchi Yang, Zhongtao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRadiotherapy induces multiple forms of tumor cell death, including immunogenic cell death (ICD) like GSDME-mediated pyroptosis and MLKL-mediated necroptosis; and ICD has been increasingly accepted as a crucial element leading to enhanced anti-tumor adaptive immunity. We aim to clarify whether a vaccine-like effect is intrinsic for radiation-induced tumor cell death, and to explore potential applications.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was performed to validate the synergism between clinical radiotherapy and PD1 blockade in patients with resectable rectal cancer. Colorectal cancer cell lines and patient-derived organoids were used to study the impact of ionizing radiation on tumor cells. Syngeneic mouse tumor models were used to study the microenvironment modulating effect of localized irradiation, as well as the potential vaccine-like effect of the in vitro irradiated autologous tumor cells.ResultsFor patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, adding immune checkpoint blockade to radiotherapy significantly increases tumor response rate. On cellular level, ionizing radiation induces GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis of colorectal cancer cells and increased the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells into tumor microenvironment. On syngeneic, immune-competent mouse tumor models, autologous tumor cells irradiated in vitro at a sterilization dosage work as a therapeutical vaccine to inhibit the growth of inoculated tumors.ConclusionsRadiation-elicited tumor cell death exhibited an in situ vaccine effect to potentiate immune checkpoint blockade, and autologous tumor cells irradiated in vitro at a sterilization level may serve as a candidate source of immunogen for personalized therapeutical cancer vaccine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04229-3.Keywords: Radiation, Immunotherapy, Checkpoint blockade, Synergism, Vaccine

Introduction

Currently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) are widely used in late-stage cancers of mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability high (MSI-H) subtype, as is supported by evidences from a series of milestone trials [1–4]. And mismatch repair/microsatellite status is ubiquitously accepted as a primary prognosis biomarker for ICI treatment [5, 6]. Now, the focus of ICI-related trials has shifted to early-stage cancers. In resectable colon cancers, two cycles of αPD1 + αCTLA4 dual therapy before surgery resulted in a pathological complete response (pCR) rate of 60% for dMMR tumors, versus 0% for mismatch repair proficient (pMMR) ones [7]. The most direct evidence explaining this difference is that dMMR/MSI-H tumors generally have denser CD8+ T cell infiltration in the microenvironment, serving as the primary effector of ICI [7, 8]. However, dMMR/MSI-H tumors take up merely 0–16% of all cases depending on cancer type, and less than 2% in most cancer types [9–11]. Worldwide researchers have thus started exploring ways to artificially turn cold tumors to hot ones using existing therapeutical options.

Current evidences suggest that radiotherapy is one of the most efficient ways to boost ICI efficacy [12]. And evidences from the renowned NICHE trial and our recently published POLARSTAR trial confirmed that radiotherapy plus ICI lead to more robust tumor regression than either radiotherapy alone or ICI alone for non-metastatic rectal cancer [7, 13]. Mechanistically, radiation induces immunogenic tumor cell death and impacts the immune microenvironment through the DNA damage sensor pathways and the pro-inflammatory cytokine pathways [12, 14]. In mouse tumor models, increased cytotoxic T cell infiltration alone, in response to immunogenic tumor cell death induced by artificially over-expressing gasdermin (a pyroptosis effector protein), is sufficient to significantly suppress tumor growth [15]. And pyroptosis of less than 15% of the tumor cell population within a bulk tumor on mouse model can lead to complete clearance of entire tumor, via the cytotoxic effect of T lymphocytes [16], confirming a therapeutical vaccine effect of in situ pyroptosis of cancer cells.

As radiotherapy exerts direct cytotoxic effect and renders multiple forms of tumor cell death [12, 17], this translational research aims to characterize the radiation-induced tumor cell deaths and ensuing microenvironment changes, and explore potential applications.

Methods

Post hoc analysis of the POLARSTAR trial

The multi-institutional POLARSTAR trial enrolled patients with locally advanced rectal cancer and randomized them into three treatment groups: neoadjuvant long-course radiotherapy plus concurrent PD1 blockade (experiment group 1), neoadjuvant long-course radiotherapy plus sequential PD1 blockade (experiment group 2), and neoadjuvant long-course radiotherapy alone (control group) [18]. In this post hoc analysis, the two experiment groups were combined into a single experiment group based on their similarity regarding the primary and secondary endpoints [13]. And for both the experiment group and the control group, only patients who have completed both neoadjuvant treatment and subsequent tumor resection were included in this analysis. The two primary outcomes for this post hoc analysis were objective response and pathological response. An objective response is defined as complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) as per the RECIST1.1 criteria, based on MRI re-evaluation. A pathological response is defined as TRG0, meaning no residue tumor cells detected in the primary lesion after treatment, as per the AJCC 4-tier system that stratifies pathological tumor regression.

MRI image acquisition and surgical/pathological procedures

Details regarding the radiological assessment of patients’ rectal tumors can be found online in our previous publications [13, 18]. As the multi-institutional POLARSTAR trial was conducted in 8 different colorectal cancer centers, the exact parameters and sequences used for the rectal MRI scan varied from institution to institution. But for any specific patient, his/her pre-treatment MRI and post-treatment MRI were conducted in the same institution (hospital) using the same MRI machinery and the same scanning parameters and sequences, which is therefore ideal for pair-wise pre-treatment and post-treatment comparisons. All MRI images were downloaded directly from the hospital information system (HIS).

Patients included in this post hoc analysis all underwent surgeries with curative intent, after the designated neoadjuvant treatments. However, specific surgical techniques (local resection, trans-anal total mesorectal excision, low anterior resection, abdominoperineal resection, etc.) were determined by attending surgeons. Resected specimens were subjected to routine pathological processing, as has previously been described by other teams [19, 20].

Immunohistochemical staining and image analysis

Resected tumors were fixed with formalin and embedded with paraffin, then sectioned into 4 μm thick slices. Deparaffinization was performed with xylene. And hydration was performed with ethanol at the concentrations of 95%, 85%, and 70%, sequentially. After antigen retrieval, slides were blocked by incubation with 3% H2O2 and 1% BSA. Subsequently, slides were incubated with primary antibodies (diluted according to manufacture protocol) at 4 °C overnight. After multiple washing with PBS, slides were incubated with secondary antibodies (Fuzhou Maixin Biotech, catalog# KIT-5005) at room temperature for 1 h. DAB staining was performed with a DAB kit (Fuzhou Maixin Biotech, catalog# DAB-1031). Slides were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, mounted with coverslips and scanned with an Olympus SLIDEVIEW VS200 slide scanner. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-cytokeratin (ZSGB-BIO, catalog# ZM-0069), anti-CD8 (CST, catalog# 98941S).

For quantification of CD8-positive stained cells, three random 500 μm × 500 μm fields were captured to represent each slide, and the positive stained cells in captured images were automatically detected and counted with QuPath software (version 0.3.2) [21].

Multiplex immunohistochemical staining and image analysis

An AlphaTSA Automation Multiplex IHC Kit (Alpha X Bio, catalog# AXT37100041) was used. Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue slides were prepared (4 μm thick slices) and were subjected to deparaffinization and hydration, as is the routine for conventional immunohistochemical staining. After antigen retrieval and blocking, slides were incubated with a primary antibody at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by multiple TBST washing and incubation with a corresponding secondary antibody at 37 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, slides were washed with TBST, incubated with fluorescent dye (with a color pre-designated for this primary antibody) and washed with TBST again. For all 6 different primary antibodies, the previous procedures were repeated (from antigen retrieval to incubation with fluorescent dye). Then, slides were washed with TBST and incubated with DAPI staining buffer. After washing with distilled water, slides were added with anti-fading mounting media (Phygene, catalog# PH0429) and mounted with coverslips. Slides were scanned with a Zeiss Axioscan 7 Microscope Slide Scanner. Cell types of interest were quantified with HALO software (version 3.5).

Antigen retrieval buffer, blocking buffer, antibody dilution buffer, DAPI staining buffer, secondary antibodies, fluorescent dye, and tyramide signal amplification buffer were all provided in the Kit. The following primary antibodies were used (in this particular sequence): FOXP3 (Abcam, catalog# ab20034, dilution 1:100), CD3 (Alpha X Bio, catalog# AXB1014, dilution none), CD45RO (ZSGB Biotech, catalog# ZM0055, dilution 1:200), GZMB (Abcam, catalog# ab134933, dilution 1:100), CD8 (ZSGB Biotech, catalog# ZA0508, dilution 1:200), PANCK (Alpha X Bio, catalog# AXB3012, dilution none).

Syngeneic mouse tumor models

To study the in situ vaccine effect of localized clinical-dose X-ray irradiation, as well as the vaccine effect of autologous tumor cells irradiated at sterilization level, two syngeneic mouse tumor models were established, respectively. For the former purpose, immune-competent, female C57BL/6 mice at 6–8 weeks old were subcutaneously inoculated with MC38 cells (5 × 105 cells per mouse) on the back adjacent to right hind leg. And for the latter purpose, immune-competent, female BALB/c mice at 6–8 weeks old were subcutaneously inoculated with CT26 cells (5 × 105 cells per mouse) on the right flank. All subcutaneous inoculations of tumor cells were performed with tumor cells suspended in 150 μL PBS for each injection.

In vivo therapeutical assay of mouse tumors

When the inoculated tumors reached approximately 100–200 mm3 in volume, C57BL/6 mice bearing MC38 tumors in the treatment group were subjected to tumor-localized irradiation, while those in the control group received no irradiation. For the localized X-ray irradiation, anesthetized mice were fixed on a platform within a RS-2000 PRO X-ray irradiator and a beam-defining device was used, where the irradiation was performed at a dose rate of 1 Gy/min. Irradiation for each mouse in the treatment group consisted of two doses: 5 Gy each dose/per day, for 2 consecutive days. Tumors were resected on the 5th day after irradiation for downstream analysis.

For intra-tumor injections of the vaccine composed of 2000 Gy-irradiated CT26 cells (5 × 105 cells per mouse), injections of the vaccines for experiment group (and PBS for control group) into subcutaneous CT26 tumors grown on BALB/c mice were performed when the inoculated tumors reached approximately 50–100 mm3 (instead of 100–200 mm3) in volume, simulating a scenario of using autologous tumor cells for vaccination under the condition of a relatively small tumor burden. Subsequently, anti-PD1 monoclonal antibodies (Bio X Cell, catalog# BE0146) were injected through tail veins at a dosage of 5 mg/kg for mice in both groups, on the 5th day since the injection of vaccine (experiment) or PBS (control). Tumor growth in the experiment and control group was then recorded.

As a tumor measuring 50–100 mm3 in volume (i.e., approximately 5 mm in diameter) grown on a BALB/c mice (with a body-length of merely 6–7 cm, not including tail) might still be too much of a tumor burden (considering the regular tumor/body proportion of most cancer patients), potentially exceeding the maximum handling capacity of systematic anti-tumor adaptive immunity of mice and thus overwhelming the efficacy of the studied vaccine, hence we endeavored to further clarify the efficacy of the studied vaccine under the condition of an even smaller tumor burden, where 5.0 × 105 unirradiated CT26 cells were used for subcutaneous tumor inoculation on DAY 0, and 2000 Gy-irradiated CT26 cells at an amount of 1.7 × 105 per mouse (for experiment group) and PBS (for control group) were subcutaneously injected on DAY 4, a time point when the inoculated tumors were hardly palpable rather than when the inoculated tumors reached approximately 50–100 mm3. The impact of the vaccine and control on subsequent tumor growth was recorded. All tumor volumes were measured with a vernier caliper and calculated with the formula of Volume = (length × width2)/2.

GEO data analysis

Two sets of RNA-seq data from patients with locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) were downloaded from GEO database (GSE94104, GSE93375) for analysis of immune infiltration before and after radiotherapy, using an online deconvolution computation tool. Dataset GSE94104 consists of matched pre-radiotherapy and post-radiotherapy rectal cancer samples of 40 patients; and dataset GSE93375 consists of specially microdissected rectal cancer epithelium samples (pre-radiotherapy n = 13, post-radiotherapy n = 9) and rectal cancer stroma samples (pre-radiotherapy n = 14, post-radiotherapy n = 6) of 15 patients. When analyzing gene expression data, duplicate genes were merged by calculation of mean values of expression level. Cell type fractions of infiltrated immune cells were calculated for all samples with CIBERSORTx, an online deconvolution computation tool (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) developed by Newman et al. [22] to estimate the abundances of different immune cell types in a mixed cell population using bulk RNA-seq data, for GSE94104 and GSE93375, respectively.

Cell lines

Colorectal cancer cell lines of mouse and human were purchased from Wuhan Sunncell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (MC38, catalog# SNL-505) and Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (CT26, catalog# CL-0071; HCT116, catalog# CL-0096). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling. MC38 was cultured in DMEM (Gibco, catalog# 11,965,092) with 10% FBS (Gibco, catalog# A5670801), CT26 was cultured in RPMI1640 (Gibco, catalog# 11875500BT) with 10% FBS, and HCT116 was cultured in McCoy’s 5A (Gibco, catalog# 16,600,082) with 10% FBS. Cell lines were periodically authenticated through morphological observation and examined to preclude mycoplasma contamination. Cell culture was maintained in 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Patient-derived rectal cancer organoids

Tumor tissues were biopsied from treatment-naive patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, in preparation for organoids. Organoids were established by Accurate International Biotechnology Co., Ltd. as previously described [23]. In brief, 5–6 pieces of tumor tissue fragments (approximately 1 mm3 each) were acquired for each case. Tumor tissues were then subjected to washing (with cold Hank’s balanced salt solution plus antibiotics), further fragmentation, and incubation with DMEM/F12 medium (supplemented with DNase, collagenase/dispase, and antibiotics). Then the tissue suspension was filtered and the resulting cell suspension was subjected to centrifugation (120 g), red blood cells lysis and further centrifugation (120 g), after which the cell pellet was resuspended with 100 μL specialized culture medium. Subsequently, 200 μL Matrigel was added to the suspension for establishing organoids with gel drops in 35 mm dishes. After solidification of the Matrigel, 2 ml of the specialized culture medium was added into dish to submerge the gel domes. Culture medium was refreshed every 3–5 days, and organoids were passaged approximately once every two weeks. All organoids used for experiment were in their 1st or 2nd passage.

Irradiation of cell lines and PDOs

For clinical-level irradiation, cell lines cultured in 6 cm or 10 cm dishes and PDOs cultured in 3.5 cm dishes were irradiated with a Rad Source RS-2000 PRO X-ray irradiator at a dose rate of 1 Gy/min, mimicking the dose rate of clinical X-ray radiotherapy. For the 5 Gy, 10 Gy, and 25 Gy treatments, the duration of irradiation was 5 min, 10 min, and 25 min, respectively.

For sterilization-level irradiation, cell suspensions preloaded in 1.5 mL EP tubes were irradiated at Beijing Hongyisifang Radiation Disinfection Center with an industrial 60Co γ-ray irradiator, at a dose rate of 30 Gy/min. For the 2000 Gy treatment, the duration of irradiation is approximately 65 ~ 70 min.

DNA damage detection of cultured cells

A DNA Damage Assay Kit by γ-H2AX Immunofluorescence (Beyotime, catalog# C2036S) was used to assess radiation-induced DNA damage in HCT116 cell line, as per the manufacture protocol. In brief, cells were grown in 24-well glass-bottom plates (NEST, catalog# 801,006) and were subjected to 25 Gy X-ray irradiation (treatment) or no irradiation (control). At 2 h post-irradiation, all cells (treatment and control) were washed by PBS and then fixed with fixing buffer for 15 min. Cells were then treated with blocking buffer for 20 min at room temperature, then incubated with anti- γ-H2AX antibody (rabbit) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, cells were incubated with anti-rabbit secondary antibodies for 1 h, and then incubated with DAPI staining buffer for 5 min, both at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were washed before being observed with a KEYENCE All-in-One Fluorescence Microscope. All buffers and antibodies were supplied in the Kit.

Colony formation

Irradiated and unirradiated HCT116 cell lines were subjected to colony formation assay. At 2 h after irradiation, irradiated cells in 60 mm dishes were trypsinized, counted, and relocated into 6-well plates at an amount of 1 × 103 cells per well. And unirradiated cells were processed simultaneously as control. 14 days later, cells were washed with PBS, and then fixed with absolute methanol for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio, catalog# G1063) for 30 min. Cells were then washed carefully with slow-running tap water. Whole-well and whole-plate pictures were obtained using a digital camera with the assistance of a white-light luminous board.

Cell viability assay

Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, catalog# C0038) was used to assess the impact of X-ray irradiation on the viability of colorectal cancer cell lines, according to manufacture protocol. Irradiated cancer cells were trypsinized, counted, and relocated into 96-well plates at 2 h after irradiation, at an amount of 2000 cells per well. Unirradiated cells were processed simultaneously as control. Cell viability was assessed on Day 0, 1, 2, and 3 post-irradiation.

Flow cytometry cell death analysis

For irradiated and unirradiated colorectal cancer cell lines, membrane-rupturing cells were detected by flow cytometry, using a Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, catalog# C1062L) and a BD LSRFortessa™ Cell Analyzer, according to manufacture protocols. Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining of cancer cells started at 36 h post-irradiation for the irradiated cells, and simultaneously for the unirradiated cells. 106 cells were analyzed for each sample. Data analysis was performed with FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

A TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, catalog# 9767) was used for total RNA extraction (spin-column-approach) from irradiated and unirradiated, nitrogen-frozen mouse tumor tissues, according to manufacture protocol. Two of the six samples in each group (irradiated group vs. unirradiated group) with the lowest RNA quality, as measured by nucleic acid concentration, OD260/280 and OD260/230 on a Shimadzu BioSpec-nano spectrophotometer, were discarded. Subsequently, cDNAs were synthesized with a PrimeScript™ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, catalog# 6110A) and an Applied Biosystems Veriti Dx 96-well Thermal Cycler, according to manufacture protocol. For real-time quantitative PCR, a Death Screening PCR Array kit (WcGene Biotech, catalog# WC-MRNA0358-M) was used. The PCR plate in the kit has 96 wells, of which 90 wells were preloaded with qPCR primers of different cell death-related genes, 4 wells were preloaded with qPCR primers of 4 commonly used reference genes, and the remaining 2 wells were designed to serve as negative controls; each plate is designed for one cDNA sample. Diluted cDNA samples were added to the plates according to manufacture protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed using a PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher, catalog# A25742) and a Thermo Fisher QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System. Levels of expression were normalized according to the reference gene Actb (β-actin). Fold changes were calculated with the 2−ΔΔCt approach.

Protein extraction and western blot

Cells lines were washed with cold PBS, collected with a scraper, suspended with M-PER Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher, catalog# 78,501), and then relocated into 1.5 mL EP tubes, where a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Abcam, catalog# 201,119) was added to the mixture. The tubes were then put on a roller in a 4 °C refrigerator for 30 min. For protein extraction of PDOs cultured in 35 mm dishes, Matrigel drops were firstly blown to fragments by pipetting 1 mL trypLE (Thermo Fisher, catalog# 12,604,013) in each dish. Dishes were placed in a 37 °C incubator for 5 min for the Matrigel to dissolve. Subsequently, the cell-trypLE suspension was placed into 2 mL EP tubes and was subjected to centrifugation at 160 g for 3 min. The precipitates were collected and lyzed in the same way as cell lines. Cell lysates in EP tubes were subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min, after which the supernatants were collected and protein samples were quantified using an Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, catalog# P0010).

The protein samples were mixed with loading buffer (Beyotime, catalog# P0015L) and boiled at 100 °C for 15 min. Then 30 μg (for cell line samples) or 10 μg (for PDO samples, due to relatively low protein yields) total proteins were loaded on SDS-PAGE gel for electrophoresis, which were subsequently transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes. After blocking with QuickBlock Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, catalog# P0231) for 10 min at room temperature, membranes were submerged into primary antibody solutions for incubation at 4 °C overnight. The next day, membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with HRP-linked secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed again, and the blots were detected with an ECL kit (Beyotime, catalog# P0018S) on a Bio-Rad Gel Doc XR + System. The following antibodies were used (dilution ratio as per manufacture protocol): GSDMD and GSDMD-N (CST, catalog# 39754S), β-actin (CST, catalog# 4970S), secondary antibodies (CST, catalog# 7074P2).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, unless otherwise specified. Inter-group comparisons of mean values were performed with unpaired or paired t tests, as appropriate. Clinical response rates presented as proportions were compared between groups using chi-square tests. Statistical significance for p values was evaluated at the 0.05 threshold. For multiple t tests in the analysis of GEO data, q (or adjusted p) values were calculated by two-stage step-up Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli FDR correction, and statistical significance was defined as q < 0.05. All analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4.1) or SPSS software (version 27).

Study approval

Both the clinical trial and animal experiments were approved and monitored by the ethical committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital. All patients participating the randomized POLARSTAR trial provided written informed consents.

Results

Adding PD1 blockade to preoperative radiotherapy significantly enhances tumor regression

To validate the synergistic effect between radiotherapy and αPD1, our team conducted a randomized clinical trial (the POLARSTAR trial, NCT05245474), where the extent of tumor regression brought by radiotherapy plus αPD1 (experiment) was compared with that by radiotherapy alone (control). Schema of the protocol treatments of the trial is briefly illustrated in Fig. 1a. The workflow of this post hoc analysis is demonstrated in Fig. S1.

An objective response was defined as complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) as per the RECIST1.1 criteria, based on MRI re-evaluation. A pathological response was defined as TRG0 as per the AJCC 4-tier system that stratifies pathological tumor regression. c Comparison in the pMMR subgroup (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, chi-square test). d Representative images of objective responses in the experiment group (left) versus the control group (right). e Representative images of pathological response in the experiment group (upper) versus the control group (lower). Post-treatment samples were surgically acquired, and the mean radiotherapy-surgery interval was 12.6 weeks for the experiment group and 11.1 weeks for the control group

In this post hoc analysis of the POLARSTAR trial, the proportion of objective response (substantial radiological regression) and the proportion of pathological response (complete regression of primary lesion) were compared between the experiment group and the control group. When analyzed in all-comer patients (i.e., pMMR, dMMR, and MMR unknown patients combined; Fig. 1b), the proportion of objective response was significantly higher in the experiment group than in the control group (49.5% vs 21.7%, p = 0.001, chi-square test), and the proportion of pathological response was also significantly higher in the experiment group than in the control group (34.9% vs 17.4%, p = 0.030, chi-square test); and similar results were obtained when the two endpoints were analyzed in the pMMR subgroup (i.e., excluding patients with dMMR and MMR unknown; Fig. 1c). Representative images of objective response and pathological response for the two groups are displayed in Fig. 1d and e, where no substantial differences were observed between experiment and control regarding the patterns of radiological or pathological tumor regression.

Additionally, we randomly selected 3 patients with pathological response from the experiment group and the control group each. And the surgically resected post-treatment FFPE specimens of these 6 patients were acquired for multiplex immunohistochemical staining, in an attempt to compare the immune microenvironments of the tumor bed between the two groups (even though tumors had completely regressed), or in other words, to check if there were differences between pathological responses resulting from the two different treatments regarding localized immune infiltration. As is demonstrated in Fig. S2, significantly higher proportion of GZMB+CD8+ T cells was observed in the scar tissue (tumor bed) of patients from the experiment group than those from the control group (p = 0.0249, t test).

Radiotherapy + αPD1 is more efficient in establishing cancer-hostile microenvironment than radiotherapy alone

To gain a deeper understanding on the difference between the two preoperative treatment modalities (radiotherapy + αPD1 versus radiotherapy alone), we compared the two treatments on their efficiency in inducing changes to the immune microenvironment. Briefly, 3 patients with moderate pathological response (i.e., TRG1/2 as per the AJCC 4-tier classification system) were randomly selected from the experiment group and the control group, respectively. And the matched pre-treatment and post-treatment FFPE tissues samples of the 6 patients were acquired and subjected to multiplex immunohistochemical staining.

Representative multi-stained images are presented in Fig. 2a to provide an intuitive look on the immune infiltration in tumors undergoing the two treatments. Upon statistical analysis with paired t tests, radiotherapy plus PD1 blockade significantly increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells (p = 0.0396), meanwhile significantly decreased the proportions of GZMB+CD8+ T cells (p = 0.0449) and FOXP3+CD8− T cells (p = 0.0293). In comparison, radiotherapy alone significantly decreased the proportion of FOXP3+CD8− T cells (p = 0.0247), without significantly impacting the proportion of CD8+ T cells and GZMB+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2b, the left column).

Radiotherapy plus PD1 blockade is more efficient in establishing a tumor-hostile immune microenvironment than radiotherapy alone. a Representative multiplex immunohistochemical images demonstrating changes of tumor immune microenvironment resulted from radiotherapy plus αPD1 (upper) versus radiotherapy alone (lower). Post-treatment samples were surgically acquired, and the mean radiotherapy-surgery interval was 12.6 weeks for the experiment group and 11.1 weeks for the control group. b Changes of abundance of tumor-infiltrated T cell subsets after treatment with radiotherapy plus αPD1 (upper) versus radiotherapy alone (lower)

Furthermore, we divided the multi-stained images into areas of tumor epithelium and areas of tumor stroma, using artificial intelligence (AI) function of the HALO software, and re-analyzed the abundance of different subsets of T cells. Representative examples of AI-performed epithelium/stroma demarcation are displayed in Fig. S3. In areas of tumor epithelium, radiotherapy plus PD1 blockade significantly increased the proportions of CD8+ T cells (p = 0.0172) and CD8+CD45RO+ T cells (p = 0.0030), and similarly, radiotherapy alone significantly increased the proportions of CD8+ T cells (p = 0.0435) and CD8+CD45RO+ T cells (p = 0.0388) as well (Fig. 2b, the middle column). In stroma areas, radiotherapy plus PD1 blockade significantly increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells (p = 0.0485), meanwhile significantly decreased the proportion of FOXP3+CD8− T cells (p = 0.0405), whereas radiotherapy alone merely decreased the proportion of FOXP3+CD8− T cells (p = 0.0201), without significantly impacting the proportion of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2b, the right column).

Localized irradiation modulates the immune microenvironment of mouse and human tumors alike

Syngeneic mouse tumor models were used to evaluate the influence of localized irradiation on tumor immune microenvironment (Fig. 3a). Immunohistochemistry of the resected tumor tissues demonstrated significantly increased CD8-positive staining cells in irradiated tumors, as compared to unirradiated controls (p = 0.0017, unpaired t test; Fig. 3b–d). We then extracted RNA out of the tumor tissues and measured the expression level of a series of cell death-related genes, where the mRNA expression of majority of genes associated with the PANoptosis (i.e., pyroptosis, apoptosis and necroptosis) pathway was found to be upregulated in irradiated mouse tumor tissues, as compared to unirradiated controls (Fig. S4).

Clinical-dose, localized irradiation modulates tumor immune microenvironment. a Schema showing the localized irradiation procedures for C57BL/6 syngeneic mouse tumor models. b Volumes of resected mouse tumors after different treatments. c Representative immunohistochemical images demonstrating the CD8+ T cell infiltration in the mouse tumor tissues after different treatments. d CD8+ T cell density in the irradiated mouse tumors was significantly higher than that in the unirradiated (ctrl) tumors (**p < 0.01, t test). e Workflow of bioinformatic analysis with public bulk RNA sequencing data of rectal cancer patients. f Relative abundance of major immune effector cells in rectal cancer tissues of 40 patients before and after radiotherapy (q or adjusted p values were calculated by two-stage step-up Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli FDR correction of multiple t tests; *q < 0.05, ***q < 0.001, ****q < 0.0001). g Relative abundance of major immune effector cells in specially microdissected epithelium and stroma sections of rectal cancer tissues of 15 patients before and after radiotherapy (q or adjusted p values were calculated by two-stage step-up Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli FDR correction of multiple t tests; *q < 0.05, **q < 0.01, ***q < 0.001)

Considering the above evidence showing that localized X-ray irradiation increased CD8+ T cell infiltration into in vivo mouse tumors, as well as the results displayed in Fig. 2, we further validated such microenvironment modulating effect of ionizing radiation using human rectal cancer samples from GEO database and CIBERSORTx [22], an online analytical tool that can provide an estimation of the abundances of different cell types based on bulk RNA-seq data (Fig. 3e). In dataset GSE94104 comprising of 40 pairs of matched pre-radiotherapy and post-radiotherapy rectal cancer samples, the calculated cell type fractions significantly increased from pre-radiotherapy to post-radiotherapy for plasma cells, CD8+ T cells, and activated NK cells (q or adjusted p = 0.0015, < 0.0001, 0.0106, respectively; multiple paired t tests; Fig. 3f). In dataset GSE93375 comprising of specially dissected rectal cancer epithelium samples (pre-radiotherapy n = 13, post-radiotherapy n = 9) and rectal cancer stroma samples (pre-radiotherapy n = 14, post-radiotherapy n = 6) of a cohort of 15 rectal cancer patients, the calculated cell type fractions significantly increased from pre-radiotherapy epithelium to post-radiotherapy epithelium for plasma cells and CD8+ T cells, but not for activated NK cells (q or adjusted p = 0.0358, 0.0082, 0.1918, respectively; multiple unpaired t tests; Fig. 3g left), and also significantly increased from pre-radiotherapy stoma to post-radiotherapy stroma for activated NK cells, but not plasma cells and CD8+ T cells (q or adjusted p = 0.0007, 0.6107, 0.4384, respectively; multiple unpaired t tests; Fig. 3g right).

Radiation substantially impacts tumor cells by causing DNA damages and inducing membrane-rupturing deaths, including GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis

To look for the trigger on the cellular level that initiated the microenvironment modulation, we started by evaluating the DNA damage of tumor cells caused by irradiation. Briefly, irradiated and unirradiated HCT116 cell lines were subjected to immunofluorescence assays to detect the expression of γ-H2AX (phosphorylated histone H2AX), a common indicator of nuclear DNA damage. As a result, clinical-dose X-ray radiation was found to induce substantially increased expression of γ-H2AX at 2 h post-irradiation (Fig. 4a). Observations on cell morphology indicated that HCT116 cells swelled substantially after irradiation (Fig. S5). Furthermore, the colony-forming capabilities of HCT116 cells were substantially suppressed by both 5 Gy and 25 Gy irradiation (Fig. 4b). To assess the influence of varying radiation dosages on the growth rate of tumor cells, cell viability assays were performed on three colorectal cell lines (MC38, CT26, and HCT116), results of which indicated that single-dose 25 Gy irradiation, rather than single-dose 5 Gy irradiation, significantly inhibited the growth of all three cell lines (MC38, p < 0.0001; CT26, p = 0.0015; HCT116, p = 0.0189; unpaired t test; Fig. 4c).

Clinical-dose ionizing radiation damages nuclear DNA, inhibits growth and induces membrane-rupturing deaths of tumor cells. a The amount of phosphorylated H2AX (γ-H2AX, an indicator of nuclear DNA damage) increased in HCT116 cells at 2 h after 25 Gy X-ray irradiation. b Colony formation of HCT116 cells were substantially inhibited by X-ray irradiation. c Cell growth was inhibited by X-ray irradiation for both mouse and human cell lines. d and e The proportions of membrane-rupturing cells (PI positive staining) were significantly higher in 25 Gy-irradiated tumor cells, as compared to unirradiated ones (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001, unpaired t test). Annexin V-FITC and PI staining of cancer cells started at 36 h post-irradiation for the irradiated cells, and simultaneously for the unirradiated cells. f Representative western blot images showing the cleavage of GSDMD in both mouse and human cell lines after 25 Gy X-ray irradiation. Ctrl: unirradiated cells, 25 Gy: cells having received 25 Gy X-ray irradiation, with proteins extracted 36 h post-irradiation

As we suspected that membrane rupture (a common feature of immunogenic cell deaths such as pyroptosis and necroptosis) might be an important factor responsible for the generation of immunogenicity of radiation-induced tumor cell death, we detected and compared the proportions of membrane-rupturing cells in irradiated versus unirradiated tumor cell lines using flow cytometry Annexin V-FITC and PI staining. As is demonstrated in Fig. 4d and e, proportion of membrane-rupturing cells was significantly higher in 25 Gy-irradiated cell lines, as compared to unirradiated controls (MC38, p < 0.0001; CT26, p = 0.0461; HCT116, p = 0.0065; unpaired t test).

Based on results displayed in Fig. S4, as well as existing evidences reported by other teams [17], we suspected that pyroptosis might be one of the primary forms of membrane-rupturing cell death induced by radiation. And this turned out to be confirmed by our western blot results, where substantial increase of GSDMD cleavage upon irradiation was observed in all three colorectal cell lines (Fig. 4f). Moreover, increased cleavage of GSDMD upon irradiation was also observed in organoids derived from rectal cancer patients (Fig. S6).

Irradiated autologous tumor cells exhibit therapeutical vaccine effect to inhibit tumor growth

Considering the immune stimulating effect of tumor-localized irradiation, we hypothesized that irradiating autologous tumor cells in vitro and then injecting them back into the original microenvironment might also lead to immune-mediated tumor-suppression. For animal experiments aiming to validate this hypothesis, we increased the irradiation dosage on the cancer cells from clinical level (1–100 Gy) to sterilization level (≥ 2000 Gy), in an attempt to ensure absolute oncological safety for the mice to be tested on. In this context, we found that 2000 Gy-irradiated CT26 cells completely lost the capacity to proliferate, whereas 25 Gy-irradiated ones can still grow despite an initial loss of cell population (Fig. S7). And western blot results suggested that 2000 Gy irradiation was also efficient in inducing pyroptosis of tumor cells (Fig. S8).

Subsequently, we established a syngeneic mouse tumor model by inoculating CT26 cells into immune-competent BALB/c mice (Fig. 5a). When the subcutaneously inoculated tumors reached 50–100 mm3 in volume, 2000 Gy-irradiated CT26 cells were intra-tumorly injected for mice in the experiment group, whereas PBS was injected for mice in the control group (Day 0). On Day 4, significant difference was observed regarding tumor volume between experiment and control (Fig. 5b). Then on Day 5, mice in both experiment group and control group received tail vein injection of anti-PD1 antibody, at a dosage of 5 mg/kg. On Day 9, all tumors were resected (Fig. 5c, d). Notably, the tumor on one of the mice in the experiment group completely regressed (Fig. 5d), despite the fact that the difference on tumor weight between the two groups did not reach statistical significance.

Autologous tumor cells irradiated at sterilization level (2000 Gy) serve as a therapeutic vaccine to inhibit tumor growth. a Schema of treatments to the syngeneic mouse tumor models. b Growth curve of the subcutaneous tumors since intra-tumor injection of vaccine and control. Notably, intra-tumor injection was performed when the inoculated subcutaneous tumors reached approximately 50–100 mm3. c and d Subcutaneous tumors before and after resection (resection performed on the 9th day since intra-tumor injection). One tumor in the vaccine group completely regressed. e Treatment schema of a repeat experiment, where tumor burden was substantially lowered to further validate the therapeutical efficacy of the studied vaccine. On Day 0, CT26 tumor cells were inoculated at an amount of 5 × 105 cells per mouse subcutaneously on the right flank to establish syngeneic tumor model. On DAY 4, mice in the experiment group received subcutaneous injection of irradiated CT26 cells (at an amount of 1.7 × 105 cells/per mouse) whereas mice in the control group received subcutaneous injection of PBS. f Growth curve of subcutaneous tumors (***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, t test). g and h Subcutaneous tumors before and after resection. i Inter-group comparison of tumor weight (***p < 0.001, t test)

Regarding the partial failure of the above animal experiment, we suspected that existing tumor burden might be one important factor for the sustained efficacy of the studied therapeutical tumor vaccine. We therefore substantially lowered the tumor burden in a repeat experiment, where we injected on BALB/c mice subcutaneously with CT26 cells (5 × 105 cells/per mouse) for establishment of syngeneic tumor models on DAY 0. And vaccines composed of 2000 Gy-irradiated CT26 cells (1.7 × 105 cells/per mouse) for experiment group, or PBS for control group, were subcutaneously injected on DAY 4 (Fig. 5e) when the inoculated tumors were hardly palpable (rather than when they reached 50–100 mm3 in volume). As a result, the inoculated tumors grew significantly slower on mice in the experiment group, as compared with mice in the control group (Fig. 5f). Subsequently, tumors were resected on the 12th day since subcutaneous inoculation (Fig. 5g, h), and tumor weight was significantly lower in the experiment group than in the control group (p = 0.0005; t test; Fig. 5i).

Discussion

This translational research confirmed the synergism between clinical radiotherapy and PD1 blockade in human rectal cancer, described the impact of ionizing radiation on tumor microenvironment and the behaviors of tumor cells, and discovered in mouse models the potential of irradiated autologous tumor cells to serve as immunogens for therapeutical cancer vaccines.

Currently, prominent evidences are suggesting that clinical radiotherapy suppresses tumor growth not only by direct cytotoxic effect, but also by enhanced adaptive immunity resulting from radiation-induced immunogenic tumor cell death [17, 24]. Pyroptosis and necroptosis, as the two most commonly known forms of immunogenic cell death, are both characterized by membrane rupture and release of immunogenic contents, including damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), assorted immunostimulatory cytokines, and most importantly, tumor antigens [25–27]. Membrane rupture of dying tumor cells is highly suspected to be one of the most relevant events leading to the generation of immunogenicity [26]. Yet, in the context of radiation-induced tumor cell death, it is unlikely to obtain concrete experimental evidences to compare the immunogenicity between pure lytic versus pure non-lytic fashions of cell death, as cell populations always die in a mixture of fashions. In this regard, our results demonstrated that tumor cells undergoing membrane-rupturing deaths increased significantly after clinical-dose irradiation (Fig. 4e), and the cleavage of GSDMD was substantially enhanced in the irradiated tumor cells (Fig. 4f, S6). Reportedly, pyroptosis of a fraction of tumor cells within a bulk tumor induces significantly enhanced anti-tumor immunity that is characterized with significantly increased infiltration of cytotoxic T cells into the tumor microenvironment, and this led to suppression or even complete regression of existing tumors in mouse models [15, 16]; Accordingly, our results demonstrated that CD8+ T cell infiltration was significantly enhanced after localized irradiation in both mouse models (Fig. 3d) and humans (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, as previous studies have elaborated the important roles of GSDME-mediated pyroptosis [17] and MLKL-mediated necroptosis [28] of tumor cells in radiation-induced enhanced adaptive immunity, our results suggested that GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis is also important in the biological process in response to irradiation. Therefore, radiotherapy-enhanced immune infiltration might actually be initiated by tumor cell PANoptosis (i.e., deaths mediated by a mixture of different and inter-related molecular pathways).

In rectal cancer, over 93% of all cases are pMMR [29, 30], which necessitates an efficient approach to transform cold microenvironments to hot ones in order to achieve improved efficacy with immunotherapy and better long-term oncological outcome. With this motivation in mind, we previously designed and conducted the randomized POLARSTAR trial [13, 21]. As is demonstrated by this post hoc analysis, adding PD1 blockade to neoadjuvant radiotherapy significantly increased the proportion of pathological complete response of rectal lesion from 17.4 to 34.9% in all-comer patients, and from 15.4% to 32.0% in pMMR patients (Fig. 1b, c), confirming the clinical benefit of the novel combination treatment. Interestingly, the renowned NICHE trial previously reported that stand-alone immune checkpoint blockade (with no radiotherapy to prime the microenvironment beforehand) for resectable pMMR colorectal cancer patients resulted in a corresponding proportion of 6.7% (1/15) [7], which serves as an ideal external reference for our trial and naturally eliminated the necessity for us to set another treatment group where patients received PD1 blockade alone without the priming of radiotherapy. Another reason we did not set such an extra treatment group when designing the trial is the difficulty to obtain ethical clearance, as pMMR patients are the contraindication for stand-alone immunotherapy, especially considering the existing results of the NICHE trial. All considered, it is now evident that there is a substantial and clinically meaningful synergism between radiotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade, and the primary mechanism is that radiotherapy enhances the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells into tumors and induces an in situ vaccine effect. Nevertheless, a question still remains: will the enhanced tumor regression translate into superior long-term survival. In this regard, the PACIFIC trial reported long-term survivals for patients with stage III, unresectable lung cancer receiving radiotherapy with or without durvalumab (PD-L1 inhibitor), where 5-year overall survival rate and 5-year progression-free survival rate of the experiment group increased, respectively, from 33.4 to 42.9% and from 19.0 to 33.1%, compared to the control group [31]. Considering the PACIFIC trial and the fact that our results demonstrated a superior efficiency for radiotherapy plus αPD1 in creating cancer-hostile microenvironment as compared to radiotherapy alone (Fig. 2b), we are optimistic about the long-term outcomes of our POLARSTAR trial.

As radiotherapy induces immunogenic tumor cell death and modulate immune microenvironment (Figs. 3, 4), we were intrigued by the possibility that autologous tumor cells irradiated in vitro might also exert a vaccine effect when re-injected into the original tumor microenvironment. When validating this hypothesis, two factors were found to require special considerations, i.e., irradiation dosage and existing tumor burden. At first, we found in cellular experiments that clinical-dose irradiation (5 Gy, 25 Gy) was incapable of ensuring 100% deaths for all tumor cells, especially when the cultured tumor cells were at a relatively high density (Fig. S7). We therefore adopted a sterilization dosage (i.e., 2000 Gy) to irradiate tumor cells for the production of the vaccine. In theory, sterilization-level irradiation could guarantee the destruction of all DNA structures and the initiation of dying process for all tumor cells with no exception, which means perfect oncological safety. And this is supported by our preliminary observations on cell morphology (Fig. S7). Furthermore, we found that the majority of irradiated cells get to stay intact (with no visible membrane rupture when observed through bright field microscopy) for at least 12 h post-irradiation (Fig. S7), which could serve as a perfect and practical window for the timely injection of these antigens-releasing cells into the in vivo tumor microenvironment. Timely injection after irradiation ensures that the dying process of tumor cells takes place primarily in vivo, mimicking clinical radiotherapy and contributing to optimal antigen presentation. On the other hand, existing tumor burden is also a crucial factor for the efficacy of the vaccine. When injecting the vaccines into mouse tumors with volumes of 50-100mm3, it exhibits a visible but transient suppressing effect on tumor growth, which, however, did not warrant sustained tumor regression, even with the addition of PD1 blockade (Fig. 5b). Subsequently, we administered the vaccine to mice with an even smaller tumor burden, where the vaccine achieved significant and enduring suppressing effect on tumor growth, even without the addition of PD1 blockade (Fig. 5f). In these two batches of animal experiments, the tumors cells used for the establishment of syngeneic mouse tumor models and the preparation of vaccines were both CT26 cell lines, so as to simulate the effect of using autologous tumor cells for vaccination.

Traditional tumor vaccines commonly utilize peptides, full-length proteins, or open-reading-frame RNA or cDNA as candidate tumor-associated antigens (TAA), all requiring characterization of TAAs for individuals, where accuracy and comprehensiveness are essential for inducing effective adaptive immune response [32]. Whole tumor cell vaccines avoid these troubles altogether, as antigens derived from whole tumor cells cover the entire spectrum of TAAs (both characterized and uncharacterized). These antigens provide epitopes for both cytotoxic and helper T cells, and the parallel presentation of both MHC-I and MHC-II restricted antigens may lead to stronger anti-tumor response and immune memory; moreover, the full-spectrum antigens derived from whole tumor cells could, in theory, substantially reduce the possibility of tumor escape as opposed to single-epitope or oligo-epitope vaccines [32]. So far, a number of basic researches have explored the notion of whole tumor cell vaccines [33–40], where the tumor cells used for vaccination, whether gene edited [33–35] or not [36–40], are unanimously subjected to treatment with irradiation (1–100 Gy, clinical dosage) [33–35, 37, 40] or chemotherapeutic agents [39] or both [36, 38], before being injected into mouse models (via subcutaneous injection [33–37], intra-peritoneal injection [39, 40], or peri-tumoral injection [38]). Furthermore, three whole tumor cell-based vaccines (GVAX vaccine [41–46], VIGIL vaccine [47, 48], and CANVAXIN vaccine [49], all of which are preprocessed with clinical-dose irradiation before subcutaneous injection) have entered clinical trials, achieving somewhat visible clinical benefits that are, however, neither satisfactory nor statistically significant. In this context, it is worth mentioning that chemotherapeutic agents and clinical-dose irradiation can hardly guarantee 100% loss of proliferation ability of tumor cells, which is also the reason why we adopted sterilization-level irradiation to ensure absolute oncological safety, while at a pretty low cost as no additional gene-editing or epitope prediction is needed. The excellent safety profiles potentially allow for all approaches of administration, including intra-tumor injection to mimic the in situ vaccine effect of radiotherapy. Also, the tumor cells needed to prepare vaccines might ideally come from each individual cancer patient, so that each patient’s antigens are used to optimally stimulate his/her own immune system, constituting a typical scenario of personalized medicine.

Overall, this study added to the evidences that radiation synergizes with PD1 blockade significantly on the cellular level. As a translational research, one major limitation of the study was a heavy emphasis on potential clinical application and lack of rescue experiments on molecular pathways.

Conclusions

In conclusion, radiotherapy elicits immunogenic tumor cell death, which exhibits an in situ vaccine effect to potentiate immune checkpoint blockade. And autologous tumor cells irradiated in vitro at a sterilization level may serve as a potential source of immunogen for personalized cancer vaccine.
