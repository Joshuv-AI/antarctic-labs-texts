Fast integral methods for the Neumann Green’s function: applications to capture and signaling problems in two dimensions.
Sanchita Chakraborty, Jeremy Hoskins, Alan E. Lindsay

Abstract
We present a high order numerical method for the solution of the Neumann Green’s function in two dimensions. For a general closed planar curve, our computational method resolves both the interior and exterior Green’s functions with the source placed either in the bulk or on the surface – yielding four distinct functions. Our method exactly represents the singular nature of the Green’s function by decomposing the singular and regular components. In the case of the interior function, we exactly prescribe an integral constraint which is necessary to obtain a unique solution given the arbitrary constant solution associated with Neumann boundary conditions. Our implementation is based on a fast integral method for the regular part of the Green’s function which allows for a rapid and high order discretization for general domains. We demonstrate the accuracy of our method for simple geometries such as disks and ellipses where closed form solutions are available. To exhibit the usefulness of these new routines, we demonstrate several applications to open problems in the capture of Brownian particles, specifically, how the small traps or boundary windows should be configured to maximize the capture rate of Brownian particles.

Introduction

The aim of this paper is to present a fast integral method for numerical solution of the Neumann Green’s function in general two dimensional geometries. Consider a closed region
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with an oriented boundary
\partial\Omega
and outward facing normal vector
n
(see Fig.
1

G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})
for
\textbf{x}\in\Omega

\displaystyle\Delta G^{\textrm{int}}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}-\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}),\quad\textbf{x}\in\Omega

(1a)

\displaystyle\qquad\partial_{\textbf{n}}G^{\textrm{int}}\equiv\nabla G^{\textrm{int}}\cdot\textbf{n}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega;

(1b)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})d\textbf{x}=0,\qquad\textbf{y}\in\Omega;

(1c)

\displaystyle G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\to G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})+R^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{y};\textbf{y})\,,\quad\mbox{as}\quad\textbf{x}\to\textbf{y}.

(1d)

When the source term

1

surface
Green’s function, henceforth labeled
G^{\textrm{int}}_{s}

\textbf{y}\in\Omega

Green’s function and is henceforth labeled
G^{\textrm{int}}_{b}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})
. When written as
G^{\textrm{int}}
without subscript, we mean both the surface or bulk function
G^{\textrm{int}}_{s,b}

is the free space Green’s function which is singular as
\textbf{x}\to\textbf{y}
and given by

G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}|,&\textbf{y}\in\Omega,\\[10.0pt]
-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}|,&\textbf{y}\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(2)

The function
R^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})
is the regular part of the interior Green’s function and is bounded as
\textbf{x}\to\textbf{y}
. In equation (
1a

When the problem is posed in the exterior of the domain, we solve for the corresponding Green’s function
G^{\textrm{ext}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\Delta G^{\textrm{ext}}=-\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}),\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega;

(3a)

\displaystyle\qquad\partial_{\textbf{n}}G^{\textrm{ext}}\equiv\nabla G^{\textrm{ext}}\cdot\textbf{n}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega;

(3b)

\displaystyle G^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\to G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})+R^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{y};\textbf{y})\,,\quad\mbox{as}\quad\textbf{x}\to\textbf{y}.

(3c)

The approach presented here is based on a boundary integral method and exactly reproduces the singularity induced by the Dirac source. In the case of the interior Green’s function, our method exactly satisfies the zero mean condition (
1c
).
We will demonstrate that our method approximates
R^{\textrm{int},\textrm{ext}}_{s,b}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})
(and hence

) to relative errors of around
\mathcal{O}(10^{-12})

For the interior problem, a long standing optimization problem asks: how should a collection of small internal traps or small boundary windows be arranged in order to minimize the expected capture time of a diffusing particle? The mean first passage time (MFPT) of a diffusing particle to a small target is a fundamental quantity in stochastic processes, with wide-ranging applications in cellular biology , physics, and material science

,

,

The MFPT

gives the average time taken for a particle of diffusivity

\textbf{x}\in\Omega

\displaystyle D\Delta T+1=0,\qquad\textbf{x}\in\Omega;\qquad\partial_{\textbf{n}}T=0,\qquad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega;

(4a)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\varepsilon}\partial_{\textbf{n}}T+\kappa_{j}T=0,\qquad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}}\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.

(4b)

In the context of the NCP, the set
\partial\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}}=\cup_{j=1}^{N}\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}}
is a collection of
N
small interior traps of extent
\mathcal{O}({\displaystyle\varepsilon})
centered at
\textbf{x}_{j}
, such that
\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}}\to\textbf{x}_{j}
as
{\displaystyle\varepsilon}\to 0
for

. The boundary condition (

for the absorber. When
\kappa_{j}\to\infty

\partial\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}}
reacts rapidly. Conversely for
\kappa_{j}\ll 1
, the trap is weakly reactive so that particles reaching
\partial\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}}
are mostly reflected
[

,
10
,
13
,
30
]

A specific quantity that determines the overall capture rate for a domain
\Omega
with traps centered at
\{\textbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{x}_{N}\}
is the global mean first passage time (GMFPT). This is a scalar quantity determined by

\tau=\tau(\textbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{x}_{N})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}T(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x},

(5)

corresponding to an MFPT averaged over a uniform distribution of starting locations.

Matched asymptotic analysis performed in the limit as
{\displaystyle\varepsilon}\to 0
is a powerful methodology that allows for the systematic reduction of the continuous problem (
4
) to a discrete one. The method constructs solutions of (
4
) by considering “local” problems in the vicinity of each trap where
|\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{j}|=\mathcal{O}({\displaystyle\varepsilon})
and a global or “outer” problem valid away from the traps where all the local behaviors are integrated. The details of this process vary in different dimensions and depending on whether the absorbers are centered interior to the domain (
\textbf{x}_{j}\in\Omega
) or on its boundary on (
\textbf{x}_{j}\in\partial\Omega
). Across these geometric settings, a commonality is that the influence between traps is encoded in a discrete energy of form

q(\textbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{x}_{N})=\textbf{c}^{T}\mathcal{G}\textbf{c},\qquad\mathcal{G}_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}R^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x}_{i};\textbf{x}_{i}),&i=j;\\[4.0pt]
G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x}_{i};\textbf{x}_{j}),&i\neq j,\end{array}\right.

(6)

where
\textbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a fixed vector that encodes variations in trap shapes and size. Hence in the limit of small traps, the crucial information regarding the optimal configuration of traps is determined by the Green’s function
G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})
and its regular part
R^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})
which satisfy equation (
1
). Furthermore, if the traps are identical, the optimization problem reduces to minimizing the functional

p(\textbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{x}_{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[R^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x}_{i};\textbf{x}_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x}_{j};\textbf{x}_{i})\Big].

(7)

In more general applications to singular solutions of nonlinear PDEs, it is long known that the critical points of (
7
) establish the limiting singular behavior for a wide class of nonlinear PDEs, see for example
[
14
,
22
,
23
,
39
,

]
.

The Green’s function (
1
) encodes global information about the shape of the domain and hence closed form solutions for the Green’s function are very valuable for determining optimizing configurations. Unfortunately, they are relatively scarce. In the two dimensional unit disk setting, the bulk regular part
R^{\textrm{int}}_{b}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})
was derived (cf.
[
38

R^{\textrm{int}}_{b}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{1}{2}\log(1+|\textbf{x}|^{2}|\textbf{y}|^{2}-2\textbf{x}\cdot\textbf{y})-\frac{1}{2}(|\textbf{x}|^{2}+|\textbf{y}|^{2})+\frac{3}{4}\right].

(8)

This expression was utilized together with (
7
) to enable the identification of optimal annular ring configurations of traps with the possibility of a center trap for numbers
N=1,\ldots,25

[
38
]
.

In the case where diffusion is occurring exterior to a two dimensional geometry, the MFPT is not finite. In this setting, a more appropriate static quantity is the so-called “splitting probabilities” or steady-state fluxes. For a collection of boundary windows
\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}}
, the splitting probabilities

give the probability that a particle initially at
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega

window. These quantities satisfy

,

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{k}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega;\qquad\phi_{k}\quad\text{bounded as }|\textbf{x}|\to\infty;

(9a)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}=\delta_{jk},\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}},\quad j=1,\ldots,N;\qquad\partial_{\textbf{n}}\phi_{k}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial{\Omega}\setminus\partial\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}}.

(9b)

The quantities
\{\phi_{k}(\textbf{x})\}_{k=1}^{N}
have been utilized in several recent studies
[
25
,
24
,
44
,
4
,
6
,
49
]
of directional sensing, the cellular process whereby cells detect the directionality of chemical cues from the noisy arrival of diffusing signaling molecules at membrane receptors. The problem (
9
) is posed exterior to the domain
\Omega
with absorbing windows placed on
\partial\Omega
. As we discuss in Sec.
2.3
, the asymptotic solution of this problem as
{\displaystyle\varepsilon}\to 0
is derived in terms of the exterior surface Green’s function
G^{\textrm{ext}}_{s}
solving (
3
).

For elliptical and rectangular geometries, rapidly convergent series based on re-summation of the Fourier series solution
[
36
,
28
]
have recently been developed which provide the necessary accuracy to employ finite differencing for approximation of the gradient and Hessian of the objective function. This new analysis identified GMFPT minimizing configurations and bifurcations of co-linear configurations
[
19
]
. In contrast to the exact solution (
8
) for the disk, we cannot easily use these rapidly convergent series to find exact bifurcation values or closed forms for optimizing arrangements. Hence our high accuracy numerical algorithm to approximate solutions to (
1
) and (
3
) has utility comparable to series solutions for the investigation of optimal trap arrangements, with the advantage of being applicable to general geometries.

In a recent survey article
[
19
]
, the authors mention the paucity of high fidelity solutions to (
1
) as a major impediment to further study of this central problem in stochastic processes, a bottleneck that has hindered the broader application of asymptotic–numerical methods for trap optimization in realistic biological and physical settings.

In the present work, we fill this gap by developing a boundary integral formulation that is able to provide accurate solutions to (
1
) and (
3
) for general two dimensional geometries
\Omega
with both bulk and surface sources (cf. Fig.
1
). Our method is based on two steps; 1) a separation of the solution into a prescribed singular component and a regular component that our method resolves for, and 2) an exact implementation of the average constraint
\int_{\Omega}G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})d\textbf{x}=0
for the interior problem. Our approach combines a boundary integral representation for the regular part with fast multipole acceleration and high order quadrature to achieve both accuracy and computational efficiency. Moreover, the explicit nature of the boundary integral solution ansatz allows us to calculate the gradient and Hessian of the solution to similar precision, without any need for differencing.

We validate our approach against known analytical benchmarks (disk and ellipse geometries) and demonstrate its performance on deformed and non-convex domains. We demonstrate several examples of the optimization of trap configurations in general geometries. Beyond MFPT analysis, our framework provides a foundation for tackling related questions in eigenvalue optimization, homogenization theory, and biophysical modeling of diffusive search.

2.1
Two Dimensional analysis

For the case

\displaystyle D\Delta T_{0}=-1,\quad\textbf{x}\in\Omega;\qquad\partial_{\textbf{n}}T_{0}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega;

(10a)

\displaystyle T_{0}\sim A_{j}\nu_{j}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{j}|+A_{j},\qquad\textbf{x}\to\textbf{x}_{j},\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.

(10b)

Here

\displaystyle\Delta v_{c}=0;\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{c};\qquad\partial_{\textbf{n}}v_{c}+\kappa_{c}v_{c}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial\mathcal{A}_{c}

(11a)

\displaystyle v_{c}=\log|\textbf{x}|-\log d_{c},\qquad|\textbf{x}|\to\infty.

(11b)

A process for determining the functional relationship between the logarithmic capacitance and the reactivity parameter,
d_{c}=d_{c}(\kappa_{c})
, was recently deployed in

30
]
. In terms of the Green’s function (
1
), the solution of (

(12)

The value of

, known as the global MFPT, is determined as

\tau_{0}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}T_{0}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}

and gives a broad measure of capture statistics averaged uniformly over all starting locations. From matching the solution (

), a linear system for the unknowns is

\Big[I+2\pi\mathcal{G}\mathcal{V}]\textbf{A}=\tau_{0}\textbf{e},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nu_{j}A_{j}=\frac{|\Omega|}{2\pi D}.

(14)

where
\mathcal{V}
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N})
along the main diagonal. In terms of the Green’s matrix
\mathcal{G}
defined in (

\textbf{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{N})
and the GMFPT
\tau_{0}
. However, we can take the approximation one step further by applying the asymptotic inverse
[1+2\pi\mathcal{G}\mathcal{V}]^{-1}\sim[1-2\pi\mathcal{G}\mathcal{V}]
which yields the following solution

\tau_{0}=\tilde{\tau}_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\nu_{M}^{2}),\qquad\tilde{\tau}_{0}=\frac{|\Omega|}{2\pi D\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}N}\left[1+\frac{2\pi}{N\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{T}\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\nu}\right],

(15)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nu_{j}
and
\nu_{M}=\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\nu_{j}
. If we specialize to the case of identical traps, such that
\nu_{j}=\nu
for

\tilde{\tau}_{0}=\frac{|\Omega|}{2\pi D\nu N}\left[1+\frac{2\pi\nu}{N}p(\textbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{x}_{N})\right]\qquad p(\textbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{x}_{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{G}_{i,j}.

(16)

Previous optimization studies

considered minimizers of both

derived in (

2.3
Splitting probabilities and directional sensing.

In recent applications of asymptotic theory to chemical signaling, several authors have investigated how a cell can infer the source of a diffusive signal from localized receptor activity
[
25
,
24
,
44
,
49
]
. Specifically, let us define
\phi_{k}(\textbf{x})
to be the probability that a signaling molecule originally at
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega
first arrives at the
k^{th}
surface receptor. We can consider the steady state flux into each receptor, also known as the
splitting probabilities
. The splitting probabilities satisfy the exterior Laplace mixed value problem

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{k}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega;\qquad\phi_{k}\quad\text{bounded as }|\textbf{x}|\to\infty;

(19a)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}=\delta_{jk},\quad\partial\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}},\quad j=1,\ldots,N;\qquad\partial_{\textbf{n}}\phi_{k}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial{\Omega}\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{N}\partial\Omega_{{\displaystyle\varepsilon}_{j}}

(19b)

The process of developing an asymptotic solution to (
19
) as
{\displaystyle\varepsilon}\to 0
is similar and described in details in many works. As is commonly performed in other analysis, the key step is to replace each receptor by a local singularity condition. Specifically, we expand the solution of (
19
) in a “sum-of-all logs” expansion
\phi_{k}(\textbf{x})=\phi_{k}^{\ast}(\textbf{x};{\boldsymbol{\nu}})+o(1)
where

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{k}^{\ast}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega;\qquad\phi_{k}^{\ast}\quad\text{bounded as }|\textbf{x}|\to\infty;

(20a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\textbf{n}}\phi_{k}^{\ast}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial{\Omega}\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{N}\{\textbf{x}_{j}\}

(20b)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{\ast}\sim\delta_{jk}+A_{jk}\nu_{j}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{j}|+A_{jk},\quad\mbox{as}\quad\textbf{x}\to\textbf{x}_{j}\,\quad j=1,\ldots,N;\qquad

(20c)

The solution of this problem is expressed as

\phi_{k}^{\ast}(\textbf{x})=-\pi\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{jk}\nu_{j}G^{\textrm{ext}}_{s}(\textbf{x};\textbf{x}_{j})+\bar{\phi}_{k},

(21)

where
\bar{\phi}_{k}=\lim_{|\textbf{x}|\to\infty}\phi_{k}^{\ast}
is a constant arising form the homogeneous solution which describes the splitting probabilities averaged over all initial locations. The matching procedure as
\textbf{x}\to\textbf{x}_{j}
, together with the solvability condition
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nu_{j}A_{jk}=0
yields a system of equations for the unknowns
(A_{1k},\ldots,A_{Nk},\bar{\phi}_{k})
. The system can be compactly represented as

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{I}+\pi\,\mathcal{G}_{s}\,\mathcal{V}&-\textbf{e}^{T}\\[5.0pt]
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{A}_{k}\\[5.0pt]
\bar{\phi}_{k}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{e}_{k}\\[5.0pt]
0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{A}_{k}=(A_{1k},\ldots,A_{Nk}),\\[5.0pt]
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N}),\end{array}

(22a)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{e}=(1,1,\ldots,1),\\[5.0pt]
\textbf{e}_{k}=(0,\ldots\underbrace{1}_{k^{\textrm{th}}}\ldots,0),\end{array}\quad[\mathcal{G}_{s}]_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}R^{\textrm{ext}}_{s}(\textbf{x}_{i})&i=j\\[5.0pt]
G^{\textrm{ext}}_{s}(\textbf{x}_{i};\textbf{x}_{j})&i\neq j\end{array}\right.\quad[\mathcal{V}]_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\nu_{i}&i=j\\[5.0pt]
0&i\neq j\end{array}\right.

and
\mathcal{I}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix. As we have seen in other related problems, the key step is to determine the Green’s matrix
\mathcal{G}_{s}
. This will be performed with out numerical method.

3.1
Boundary integral methods.

We first reduce the problem to a boundary integral equation, an approach which is commonplace in potential theory (cf.
[
40
,
56
,
1
,
26
]
). Our presentation focuses on the interior Green’s function (
1
) which, compared to the counterpart exterior function
G^{\textrm{ext}}
, has two additional features to be accounted for. First the source term
1/|\Omega|
in (
1a
) and second, the integral constraint (
1c
). To begin our process of reduction to an integral equation, we introduce for
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
the particular solution

v(\textbf{x})=\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}{4},\qquad\Delta v=1.

(23)

Following this, we separate the solution of (
1
) into singular, particular and homogeneous components. The homogeneous solution is defined as

w(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})=G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}v(\textbf{x})-G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y}).

(24)

The equation for the homogeneous component
w(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta w(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})=0,\quad&\textbf{x}\in\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\textbf{n}}w(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})-\partial_{\textbf{n}}G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y}),\quad&\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big[w(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})+\frac{1}{|\Omega|}v(\textbf{x})+G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\,{\rm}\Big]d\textbf{x}=0.\end{cases}

(25)

The solution ansatz for the problem (
25
) is given by

w(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|\,\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})+\alpha,

(26)

for an unknown density
\sigma
and constant
\alpha(\textbf{y}),
depending on
y
but independent of
x
. Taking the limit as
\textbf{x}\to\partial\Omega
, we obtain the following integral equation for the unknown density
\sigma

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{(\textbf{x}-\textbf{z})\cdot\textbf{n}}{|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|^{2}}\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})=-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})-\partial_{\textbf{n}}G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y}).

(27)

Here
n
denotes the normal derivative of
\partial\Omega
at
\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega
. We note that the left-hand side has a one-dimensional nullspace
[
40
]
. By the divergence theorem, the right-hand side integrates to zero which guarantees solvability. We are then free to impose the additional constraint
\int_{\partial\Omega}\sigma(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}\textbf{x}=\alpha(\textbf{y}).

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{(\textbf{x}-\textbf{z})\cdot\textbf{n}}{|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|^{2}}\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})+\int_{\partial\Omega}\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}\textbf{z}-\alpha(\textbf{y})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})-\partial_{\textbf{n}}G_{0}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y}).

(28)

We refer the reader to
[
61
,
40
]
and the references therein for related treatments of this nullspace.

Implementation of the integral constraint.

Turning to the integral constraint on
G^{\textrm{int}},
defined in (
1c
). We recall from (
23
) that
\Delta v(\textbf{x})\equiv 1,
and apply Green’s second identity to obtain that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}\textbf{x}=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta v(\textbf{x}))\,G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}\textbf{x},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}v(\textbf{x})\,{\rm d}\textbf{x}-v(\textbf{y})+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})\,G^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x})

(29)

In the above expression, the term
v(\textbf{y})
arises from integration against either a bulk or surface Green’s function. In the end, we wish for all calculations to be performed on
\partial\Omega
, hence we note from the divergence theorem that

\int_{\Omega}v(\textbf{x})\,{\rm d}\textbf{x}=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})d\textbf{x}=\frac{1}{12}\int_{\partial\Omega}\textbf{n}\cdot(x_{1}^{3},x_{2}^{3})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x}).

Let
\mu(\textbf{y})
be defined by

\displaystyle\mu(\textbf{y})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}v(\textbf{x})\,{\rm d}\textbf{x}-v(\textbf{y})+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})\,\left(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}v(\textbf{x})+G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\right)\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x}).

(30)

Then, the integral constraint becomes
\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})\,w(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x})=-\mu(\textbf{y}).

Expanding, gives

-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})\int_{\partial\Omega}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x})+\alpha(\textbf{y})\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x})=-\mu(\textbf{y}).

We note that

\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\textbf{x}\cdot\textbf{n}\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x})=|\Omega|,

and hence

\alpha(\textbf{y})=\frac{1}{2\pi|\Omega|}\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})\int_{\partial\Omega}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{x})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\mu(\textbf{y}).

(31)

Inserting this identity into (
3.1
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{(\textbf{x}-\textbf{z})\cdot\textbf{n}}{|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|^{2}}\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})+\int_{\partial\Omega}\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}\textbf{z}

(32)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi|\Omega|}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{r})\log|\textbf{r}-\textbf{z}|\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{r})\right]\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})=-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})-\partial_{\textbf{n}}G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\mu(\textbf{y}).

If the integral
\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{r})\log|\textbf{r}-\textbf{z}|\,\sigma_{*}(\textbf{r})\,{\rm d}\textbf{r}
does not vanish for any non-zero
\sigma_{*}
in the kernel of the left-hand side of (
27
) then we can set
\alpha\equiv 0
in our representation of
w
(
26
). One then obtains the integral equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{(\textbf{x}-\textbf{z})\cdot\textbf{n}}{|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|^{2}}\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{r})\log|\textbf{r}-\textbf{z}|{\rm d}S(\textbf{r})\right]\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})

(33)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\partial_{\textbf{n}}v(\textbf{x})-\partial_{\textbf{n}}G_{0}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})-\mu(\textbf{y}).

Once the linear system (
32
) has been solved, the final solution for the regular part is then

R^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})=\alpha(\textbf{y})+\frac{|\textbf{x}|^{2}}{4|\Omega|}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|\,\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y}){\rm d}S(\textbf{z}).

(34)

For evaluation off surface
(\textbf{x}\in\Omega)
, explicit differentiation yields the gradient and Hessians as

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}R^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{i}}{2|\Omega|}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{x_{i}-z_{i}}{|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|^{2}}\,\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y}){\rm d}S(\textbf{z})

(35a)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}R^{\textrm{int}}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{2|\Omega|}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[\frac{\delta_{ij}}{|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|^{2}}-\frac{2(x_{i}-z_{i})(x_{j}-z_{j})}{|\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}|^{4}}\right]\,\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y}){\rm d}S(\textbf{z}),

(35b)

and the above integrals are smooth. For determining derivatives on surface
\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega
, a more involved process of applying the normal derivative (
27
) together with spectral differentiation to compute tangential derivatives can be used to find these quantities.

Discretization of the integral equation

In two dimensions we discretize the integral equation (
32
) using a standard patch-based collocation method, in which the unknowns of the system are values of the unknown density at a set of discretization nodes. For ease of exposition, we write the integral equation (
32
) as
\frac{1}{2}\sigma+\mathcal{K}[\sigma]=f,
where
\mathcal{K}_{\textbf{y}}
is a compact weakly-singular integral operator. Let
K_{\textbf{y}}
denote its kernel. In two dimensions, the boundary
\partial\Omega
is divided into panels, each of which is discretized using a
k^{th}
order Gauss-Legendre rule. Let
\{\textbf{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
denote the collection of discretization nodes on
\partial\Omega.
We then enforce the integral equation at the discretization nodes, yielding a set of
N
equations

\displaystyle\sigma(\textbf{x}_{j};\textbf{y})+\int_{\partial\Omega}K_{\textbf{y}}(\textbf{x}_{j};\textbf{z})\sigma(\textbf{z};\textbf{y})\,{\rm d}S(\textbf{z})=f(\textbf{x}_{j}),\quad j=1,\ldots,N.

(36)

In order to fully discretize the system we require suitable quadrature nodes
w_{j,k}
for computing the integrals on the left-hand side of (
36
). For points which are far away, we can choose
w_{j,k}
independent of
j
. Typically these quadrature weights
w_{k}
are chosen so that the quadrature rule with nodes
\textbf{x}_{j}
and weights
w_{j}
accurately integrate sufficiently smooth functions on
\partial\Omega.
For
\textbf{x}_{j}
near
\textbf{x}_{k},
custom quadrature formulae based on a mixture of adaptive integration and
generalized Gaussian quadratures
are used. Let
\mathcal{S}_{j}
denote the set of
k
such that
w_{j,k}
requires such a correction. We note that if patches are approximately uniform in size, that
\mathcal{S}_{j}
will typically be
\mathcal{O}(1)
for large
N
. Let
W_{j,k}
denote the corresponding corrections. We can then replace (
36
) by the approximation

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}_{j}}W_{j,k}\sigma_{k}+\sum_{k\notin\mathcal{S}_{j}}K_{\textbf{y}}(\textbf{x}_{j};\textbf{x}_{k})w_{k}=f_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,N,

(37)

where
\sigma_{j}\approx\sigma(\textbf{x}_{j};\textbf{y})
and
f_{j}=f(\textbf{x}_{j}).
Moreover, we note that the first term in (
37
) is sparse and can be applied in
\mathcal{O}(N)
operations. The second term can be computed using many standard fast algorithm techniques, including the fast multipole method
[
2
]
which reduces the cost of applying the discretized integral operators from
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
to
\mathcal{O}(N)
, and fast direct methods
[
32
]
which construct a highly-compressed factorization of the inverse of the system matrix. The implementation of these quadratures, together with geometric calculations, are performed using the integral equation package

chunkIE
2
2

2

available at
https://chunkie.readthedocs.io

[
3
]
.

3.3
Optimization routines.

In this section, we describe our approach to the numerical minimization of the discrete energy
p(\textbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{x}_{N})
described in (
7
). The global minimization of this class of discrete energies is a long known computation challenge problem due to the large number of critical points, which grows in complexity as the dimensionality increases. In light of this, a number of optimization strategies combine local (e.g. Newton methods) and global search methods (e.g. particle swarm optimization)
[
19
,
28
,
36
]
. In the present work, we are primarily interested in demonstrating the effectiveness of our numerical method when interfaced with standard optimization methods.

Our numerical method is implemented in
Matlab
and utilizes the boundary integral method package
chunkIE
. This package handles geometry, implements high order quadrature routines and handles fast multipole method acceleration. Owing to this integration, the results we present here are obtained with the
Matlab
optimization routine
fmincon
. We utilize the
interior-point
algorithm and where possible specify the gradient of the objective function. In the case where we are optimizing over the centers of interior traps, the optimization routines have a tendency to take points outside the domain. To counteract this, we set a large value of the objective function when the points are outside the domain using the
chunkerinterior
function which returns a boolean for whether
\textbf{x}\in\Omega
. In an effort to conduct a global search for minima, we initialize each routine with a random array of points in the domain and take the minima from many independent realizations of the optimization process. Other details can be viewed in our available code
3
3

3

.

4.3
Geometric shielding effects in source detection.

Ratiometric sensing is a proposed mechanism for inferring the directionality of a signaling external sources through a comparison of receptor activity over the cellular surface. The underlying concept is that heightened receptor activity in a particular section of the cellular surface indicates the directionality of the source
[
31
,
35
,
42
,
65
]
. In this example, we explore how cellular geometry can sharpen or modulate this mechanism. We consider the class of
cassini
domains given parametrically for
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
by

\big((x_{1}-a)^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\big)\big((x_{1}+a)^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\big)=b^{4}.

(43)

This choice of domain, which varies over the two parameters
(a,b)
, allows us to consider a range of shapes which can reflect non-circularity observed in cellular shapes from ovals to rods like shapes and also dumbbell geometries.

If we fix the area
|\Omega|=b^{2}E(a^{4}/b^{4})
of these curves, we can reduce to a one-parameter family of curves for
k=a/b\in(0,1)
where

a=kb,\qquad b^{2}=\frac{|\Omega|}{E(k^{4})}.

(44)

Here

is the complete elliptic integral of the second kind. As
k\to 0
, the curve becomes circular, while
k\to 1
represents a dumbbell shaped domain whose neck pinches at
k=1
. The case
k=1/\sqrt{2}
represents a “pill” shaped geometry typical of bacteria (see Fig.
12

In order to explore how geometry can influence the mechanism of ratiometric sensing, we place a source at position
\textbf{x}=[R,0]
and consider the
differential splitting probabilities
defined (see
[

\Xi[\textbf{x}]=\phi^{\ast}_{1}(\textbf{x})-\phi^{\ast}_{2}(\textbf{x})\in(-1,1),

(45)

where
\phi^{\ast}_{1}(\textbf{x})
is the splitting probability to the right receptor and
\phi^{\ast}_{2}(\textbf{x})
the splitting probability to the left receptor from
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega
. The splitting probabilities are described in Sec.
2.3
and a matched asymptotic solution in the small receptor limit in given by (
21
). In this setting, values of
\Xi\approx 1
are associated with a strong directional signal to the right,
\Xi\approx-1
with a strong directional signal to the left and when
\Xi\approx 0
, there is little directional information to be inferred from the splitting probabilities.

5
Discussion

In this work we have presented and validated a high order numerical method for the planar Green’s function, a central quantity in narrow capture and escape problems and applications to chemical signaling. Previously available solutions were an exact formula in the case of a disk domain and rapidly convergent series for rectangles and ellipses. By interfacing with standard optimization routines, we have shown GMFPT minimizing arrangements of traps, either placed interior to the domain or on the boundary as escape windows.

There are several avenues for extension of this work which warrant further study. One is extension to other operators such as the modified Helmholtz operator. For the interior problem in
\textbf{x}\in\Omega
, this equation satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta G_{h}-sG_{h}=-\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}),\qquad\textbf{x}\in\Omega;

(49)

\displaystyle\partial_{\textbf{n}}G_{h}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\qquad G_{h}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y},s)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}|+R_{h}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y},s).

(50)

The equation (
49
) arises when the Laplace Transform is applied to the heat equation. This approach is one route
[
49
,
6
,
15
,
16
,
47
,
21
]
to reproduce the full time-dependent statistics
[
29
]
of arrivals at absorbing sites, i.e. determining not just the mean statistics determined by (
4
). These quantities are essential to determine extreme statistics for first passage time problems
[
51
,
45
,
52
]
.

In our presentation, we have reproduced existing optimization results for the minimizing locations of traps in a disk (cf. Fig.
6
) and in an ellipse (cf. Fig.
7
). However, we expect that more sophisticated global routines may be able to identify lower energy configurations of (
7
). Hence a priority for future studies should be to integrate this new numerical method with more sophisticated optimization strategies
[
28
,
58
,
59
]
.

In future work, we will also address numerical algorithms for the corresponding three dimensional Green’s function. In the case where
N
small traps are located inside a bounded three dimensional region
\Omega
, similar asymptotic analysis have been performed in the limit as
{\displaystyle\varepsilon}\to 0

[
17
,
5
]
. In the case that each trap has a different size or shape, the GMFPT is given (see Principal Result 3.1 of
[
17
]
) by

\tau_{0}=\frac{|\Omega|}{D}\left[\frac{1}{4\pi{\displaystyle\varepsilon}N\bar{c}}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\frac{\textbf{c}^{T}\mathcal{G}\textbf{c}}{\bar{c}^{2}}\right],\qquad\mathcal{G}_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}R^{\textrm{int}}_{b}(\textbf{x}_{i};\textbf{x}_{i}),&i=j;\\[5.0pt]
G^{\textrm{int}}_{b}(\textbf{x}_{i};\textbf{x}_{j}),&i\neq j.\end{array}\right.

(51)

The entries of the vector
\textbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N})
are the capacitance of each trap and
\bar{c}=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}
. The capacitance of each trap is determined by their shape through the solution of the electrostatic problem

\displaystyle\Delta v_{d}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega_{k};\qquad v_{d}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega_{k};

(52a)

\displaystyle v_{d}=\frac{c_{d}}{|\textbf{x}|}+\mathcal{O}(|\textbf{x}|^{-2})\quad\mbox{as}\quad|\textbf{x}|\to\infty.

(52b)

The corresponding Green’s function for the interior bulk problem is given by

\displaystyle\Delta G^{\textrm{int}}_{b}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}-\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}),\quad\textbf{x}\in\Omega;\qquad\partial_{\textbf{n}}G^{\textrm{int}}_{b}\equiv\nabla G^{\textrm{int}}_{b}\cdot\textbf{n}=0,\quad\textbf{x}\in\partial\Omega;

(53a)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}G^{\textrm{int}}_{b}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})d\textbf{x}=0,\qquad\textbf{y}\in\Omega;\qquad G^{\textrm{int}}_{b}(\textbf{x};\textbf{y})\to\frac{1}{4\pi|\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}|}+R^{\textrm{int}}_{b}(\textbf{y};\textbf{y})\,,\quad\mbox{as}\quad\textbf{x}\to\textbf{y}.

(53b)

The singularity behavior and average constraint in (
53b
) for the interior problem are relatively easy to accommodate using the strategy discussed in Sec.
3
.

The scenario where the source is located on the surface arises in many contexts
[
34
,
5
,
6

44
,
4

48

53

\textbf{x}\to\textbf{y}

[
