Glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1-altered mesenchymal tumour of the upper oesophageal sphincter
Authors: Sage Hebert, Mallory Peters, Areli Cuevas-Ocampo, Karuna Dewan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlioma-associated oncogene homologue 1-altered mesenchymal tumours (GLI1-AMT) are rare, recently characterised soft tissue neoplasms driven by GLI1 fusions or amplifications. They predominantly arise in the head and neck, though their full clinicopathological presentation remains variable.A female in her 30s with a year-long history of progressive dysphagia was found to have a GLI1-AMT of the upper oesophageal sphincter, the first known case at this site. She underwent laser excision of the mass with adjuvant radiotherapy with no disease recurrence to date.Literature review identified 42 additional cases in the head and neck, most commonly affecting the tongue. Genetic alterations include GLI1 amplifications, frequently co-amplified with CDK4 and MDM2, and fusions, typically with ACTB, PTCH1 or MALAT1. Immunohistochemical staining patterns are inconsistent, though CD56, S100 and MDM2 are frequently positive.Classification of GLI1-AMT has evolved over recent years, and research is needed to refine diagnostic criteria and elucidate the behaviour of this spectrum of neoplasms.Keywords: Ear, nose and throat/otolaryngology; Immunohistochemistry; Pathology; Otolaryngology / ENT; Head and neck cancer

Background

Glioma-associated oncogene homologue 1-altered mesenchymal tumours (GLI1-AMT) are a rare and newly emerging classification of soft tissue tumours characterised by fusions or amplifications of GLI1, a transcription factor involved in the regulation of the Sonic Hedgehog signalling pathway.1 They can arise at any site throughout the body, though they have a predilection for the head and neck, particularly the tongue.2 They affect a wide age range (1–88 years) and show no gender preference.2 The clinical presentation is highly variable based on anatomic location, and these neoplasms can display a broad spectrum of morphological and immunohistochemical (IHC) features.1 Though initially thought to be benign, these tumours have proven their potential for recurrence and metastasis, particularly in tumours with high mitotic activity or infiltrative growth patterns. Presented herein is the first known case of a GLI1-AMT of the upper oesophageal sphincter (UES), found on imaging during evaluation for a presumed neurologic event in the patient.

Case presentation

A female in her early 30s with no significant past medical history presented to clinic with complaints of dysphagia to solids and liquids of all consistencies for 8 months. Initial symptoms began as dysphagia and globus sensation, prompting an evaluation in the emergency department with concern for a stroke-like episode. Workup at this time revealed a post-cricoid mass on CT, and flexible laryngoscopy showed a well-circumscribed cystic mass ball-valving in and out of the UES, effacing the arytenoid though not obstructing the airway. On discharge from the emergency department she was lost to follow-up, and her symptoms gradually progressed over time to frequent choking on food, water and saliva, leading the patient to seek care in clinic.

On presentation to clinic, in addition to her dysphagia she reported recent unintentional weight loss, but denied wheezing, stridor, odynophagia or voice changes. Flexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing showed a grossly normal swallow without evidence of pharyngeal residue, penetration, aspiration or regurgitation; the post-cricoid mass was observed to have smooth edges and no ulcerations. Laryngostroboscopy demonstrated a ball-valving motion of the mass based on the pharyngeal surface of the right arytenoid (figure 1A,B). She underwent laser excision of the mass for biopsy without complications.

Investigations

Histopathological examination showed a stromal-based mesenchymal neoplasm composed of a multinodular spindle cell proliferation arranged in loose fascicles (figure 2A). The neoplastic cells exhibited eosinophilic cytoplasm and oval to spindled nuclei with mild pleomorphism, while the tumour cells were mixed with variable myxoid to hyalinised stroma. Numerous capillaries and dilated blood vessels were noted in the background, and abundant sprinkling of lymphocytes was noted throughout the tumour (figure 2B). Mitotic figures were easily identifiable, reaching up to 8 mitoses per 10 high-power fields. No necrosis was noted. An extensive IHC panel was performed, the findings are outlined below (table 1, figure 2C–G). Ki67 was also found to be moderately elevated, estimated to be 10%–15%. Tumour mutational burden was low (3.9 mutations/Mb) and no microsatellite instability was observed.

Differential diagnosis

The final diagnosis was determined to be GLI-1 altered spindle cell mesenchymal tumour of the UES. Molecular pathology results showed 12q13-15 amplification including CDK4, GLI-1 and MDM2 amplification.

Outcome and follow-up

Following excision of the lesion, the case was reviewed at multidisciplinary tumour board. Although post-operative CT showed no evidence of residual disease, emerging literature suggested these lesions should be regarded at least as low-grade sarcoma with 20% rate of local recurrence, regional or distant metastases, especially when associated with worrisome histologic features. Given the elevated mitotic activity and anticipated morbidity of recurrence in this location, the recommendation was made for adjuvant radiotherapy extrapolating from management principles of other soft tissue neoplasms with uncertain biologic potential. The patient was treated with intensity modulated radiation therapy at 66 Gy to the right pyriform sinus and bilateral neck, in 33 fractions over 6.5 weeks. On completion of radiation therapy, positron emission tomography (PET) scan showed a hypermetabolic focus at the left base of tongue, but did not show any findings suggestive of recurrence or metastatic malignancy, and this focus was not present on subsequent PET scans. She is now 1.5 years out from completing radiation and has been compliant with regular follow-ups and surveillance imaging, which has revealed no evidence of malignancy thus far.

Other than a single congenital lesion reported by Papke et al,3 GLI1-AMT of the head and neck were diagnosed predominantly in adults with a median age of 34.5 (mean age 35.2, range 1–84) though 13 were paediatric cases. While this spectrum of neoplasms shows an equal sex distribution overall, a strong male predominance was shown for the head and neck, with 69.8% (n=30) male patients. The most common primary site in this region was by far the tongue, with 26 cases (60.5%) occurring at the tongue or tongue base. Tumours were also found in the neck, submandibular, submental soft tissues (n=8), floor of mouth (n=3), tonsil (n=1), oropharynx (n=1), mandibular gingiva (n=1), soft palate (n=1), hard palate (n=1) and UES (n=1). The tumours ranged from 0.8 to 6.0 cm in size, with an average of 2.9 cm. GLI1 amplification was seen in 14 cases. Of these, co-amplification with CDK4 (12/14), MDM2 (12/14), DDIT3 (4/14), STAT6 (1/14) and HMGA2 (1/14) was also seen. Gene fusion occurred in 26 cases, with the fusion gene partners being ACTB (19/26), PTCH1 (5/26) and MALAT1 (2/26). The remaining 3 cases had unspecified GLI1 rearrangements. All cases used some combination of FISH, RNA sequencing and/or DNA sequencing for diagnosis.

Pathological characteristics were variable among cases. Mitoses per 10 hpfs ranged from 0 to >25, with the mean being 5.4 and median being 4. Necrosis was only seen in four tumours, however three of those four involved metastatic disease. As is expected, IHC studies performed varied across cases. Though clinical outcome was not reported for some cases, only one patient was reported to have died of disease. This patient showed nodal recurrence and bilateral lung metastasis before succumbing to disease after almost 7 years of follow-up.4 Five cases showed local recurrence and/or distant metastasis. One patient had local recurrence at 27 and 40 months, respectively, and their clinical outcome is unknown.5 Another case had recurrence after 6 months, and they were reported to be alive with disease after 36 months of follow-up.6 Two patients showed distant metastases to the lungs, and both patients were alive with disease at 26 and 80 months respectively.7 8 The final patient showed nodal recurrence along with a metastatic lesion in the sacrum, 27 months after initial diagnosis. Following neck dissection, wide resection of the sacral mass, and chemotherapy, this patient showed no evidence of disease after 36 months of follow-up.9 Two of the cases were recurrent tumours, and of note both patients had PTCH1::GLI1 fusions.1 7 Twenty patients in total had no evidence of disease at last follow-up, though follow-up times ranged widely from 0.5 to 120 months.

Discussion

GLI1-AMT are increasingly recognised soft tissue neoplasms defined by GLI1 fusion or amplification events. Despite growing recognition, their biologic behaviour remains incompletely understood, particularly in the head and neck where anatomic constraints may significantly influence clinical presentation and management. Diagnosis of GLI1-AMT remains challenging because histologic and immunophenotypic findings are variable and overlap with other similar soft tissue neoplasms. Similar to other reports, the current tumour demonstrated diffuse CD56 expression with focal staining for S100, SMA and CD34, while lacking in keratin and SOX10 expression. Definitive classification ultimately required molecular identification of GLI1 amplification with co-amplification of CDK4 and MDM2.

This case is notable as it represents the first reported GLI1-AMT arising in the UES/post-cricoid region, an area more commonly associated with epithelial neoplasms like squamous cell carcinoma. Despite the patient’s young age and lack of tobacco exposure, diagnostic and management considerations were influenced by the aggressive behaviour typically exhibited by malignancies arising in this region. Aside from being an unusual pathology, this tumour was also unique in that the risk for progressive swallowing dysfunction and potential airway compromise was heightened by its dynamic ball-valving motion.

Although many reported GLI1-AMTs have followed an indolent course, accumulating evidence supports malignant potential. Local recurrence and distant metastases have been reported months to years after diagnosis among head and neck cases, particularly in cases with elevated mitotic activity, tumour necrosis or GLI1 amplification.4 6–9 The increased mitotic activity in the current case, along with the functional risks of recurrence at this location, ultimately led to the recommendation for adjuvant radiation therapy.

This case expands on the anatomic spectrum and diagnostic complexity of GLI1-AMT. Given their uncertain biologic behaviour, continued reporting of site-specific presentations and clinicopathologic features will be important for refining risk stratification and management strategies for these neoplasms.
