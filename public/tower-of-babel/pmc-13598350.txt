Chemical Profiling, Box–Behnken Optimisation, Antimicrobial Activity, and Molecular Docking Study of Z‐Ligustilide Rich Essential Oil From Himalayan Angelica glauca Edgew.
Authors: Balam Singh Bisht, Mahesh Chandra Vishwakarma, Prakash Chandra Mathpal, Jyoti Niwas Pant, Hemant Kumar Joshi, Himanshu Rawat, Chandra Shekhar Mathela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Angelica glauca Edgew., a high‐altitude Himalayan medicinal plant, is an important source of bioactive essential oil rich in phthalide derivatives. The present study investigated the influence of environmental conditions and extraction parameters on essential oil yield and chemical composition, with particular emphasis on Z‐ligustilide. Essential oils obtained from roots collected across different altitudes revealed marked variations in yield and phytochemical profile, with plants growing at 3500–3800 m producing the highest concentration of Z‐ligustilide (75.2%). The optimised essential oil exhibited broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activity against both Gram‐negative bacteria (Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa) and Candida species. Molecular docking studies further supported its therapeutic potential by demonstrating strong interactions with microbial target proteins, with binding affinities reaching approximately −6.9 kcal/mol. The predicted mechanisms of antimicrobial action include membrane disruption, interference with ATP production, and inhibition of biofilm formation, indicating a multi‐target mode of action. Overall, these findings demonstrate that altitude‐driven phytochemical variation combined with optimised extraction significantly enhances the quality and biological efficacy of A. glauca root oil, highlighting its potential as a natural antimicrobial agent for pharmaceutical and nutraceutical applications.

Introduction

The Himalayan region, covering parts of India, Nepal, Bhutan and Tibet, is a biodiversity hotspot with a unique climate that fosters the growth of rich aromatic and medicinal herbs and spices featuring distinct phytochemical properties. These species are not only integral to culinary traditions but also hold significant medicinal potential and economic value. The high‐altitude environment, coupled with organic farming practices, enhances the bioactive compound content, thereby adding to their quality and therapeutic efficacy. Himalayan spices are widely used in traditional medicines like Ayurveda, Unani and Tibetan systems for their antimicrobial, antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties [1]. Their essential oils and bioactive compounds have been explored for use in pharmaceuticals and food additives [2, 3]. With increasing global interest in organic and medicinal spices, the sustainable cultivation and conservation of Himalayan spices have gained significant attention. Among these, Angelica glauca Edgew. (Apiaceae), commonly known as ‘Gandrayan’, is highly valued for its root essential oils used in traditional spice, medicine, perfumery and pharmaceuticals. Endemic to the high‐altitude zones of the Himalayas, this species exhibits remarkable adaptability to varying ecological niches [4]. The essential oil of A. glauca has been reported to possess antimicrobial, anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties, attributed to its diverse phytochemicals [5, 6, 7, 8]. One of its constituent Z‐ligustilide, is a bioactive phthalide primarily found in medicinal plants of the Apiaceae family, such as Angelica sinensis, A. glauca and Ligusticum chuanxiong. It is well known for its diverse pharmacological activities, including neuroprotective, anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticancer and cardiovascular benefits [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Earlier reports show drastic qualitative and quantitative variations with respect to mono‐ and sesquiterpenoids, bioactive ligustilide, phthalides and their oxygenated derivatives as constituents of A. glauca root oil. Additionally, the influence of environmental and geographical factors on its chemical profile with reference to bioactive constituents is lacking [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. We report here variations in phytochemical and antimicrobial activities among root oils collected from six geographical regions to assess the suitability of the materials and their commercial potential. Previous studies have reported considerable variation in the chemical composition of A. glauca essential oil due to differences in geographical location, altitude and environmental conditions. Such variability influences the plant's biological activities, including its reported antimicrobial and therapeutic potential. Therefore, the present study focuses on GC–MS chemical profiling, Box–Behnken design (BBD) optimisation and molecular docking study to evaluate the effect of extraction parameters and altitude on the accumulation of phthalide‐rich essential oil and its bioactive potential.

Chemical Composition

A total of 64 different chemical compounds were identified, with their respective retention indices (LRI) and percentage compositions (FID %; Figures S1–S6) across different samples (AG1–AG6). Z‐Ligustilide has the highest percentage across samples, particularly at 75.2% in the sample collected from Seepu village (AG1), while also showing significant percentages in other samples. E‐Ligustilide is comparatively lower than Z‐ligustilide but still notable; this compound ranges around 3.99%–6.98% across different samples. Senkyunolide H shows variability, with a high of 7.11% in a sample collected from Dantu village (AG2) and lower percentages in other samples. Compounds like α‐phellandrene, limonene and β‐caryophyllene appear, with varying concentrations in each sample. The percentage composition of compounds, such as butyl phthalide and other constituents, varies significantly between samples, reflecting differences in chemical profiles based on source or preparation. Notably, the sample collected from Namik village (AG3) was found to contain maximum amount of Z‐butylidenephthalide (45.39%) while other samples having range 1.85%–13.11%. The total percentages of identified compounds in the samples are AG1: 92.64%, AG2: 95.06%, AG3: 98.97%, AG4: 94.15%, AG5: 97.42%, and AG6: 91.48% (Table 2). Multivariate analysis revealed strong correlations between essential oil composition and environmental factors. Altitude positively influenced the concentration of monoterpenes such as Z‐ligustilide and Z‐butylidenephthalide (Figure 1). This is the first time we are reporting the maximum percentage of Z‐ligustilide 75.2% in a sample collected from Seepu village (AG1). Z‐Ligustilide is a bioactive phthalide compound primarily found in medicinal plants of the Apiaceae family, such as A. sinensis, A. glauca and L. chuanxiong. It is well known for its diverse pharmacological activities, including neuroprotective, anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticancer and cardiovascular benefits [9, 25, 26, 27]. Z‐Ligustilide has demonstrated antibacterial and antifungal properties, particularly against Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans [12]. It also exhibits antiviral potential by inhibiting viral replication in infections such as influenza and herpes simplex virus (HSV). Studies suggest Z‐ligustilide induces apoptosis (programmed cell death) in various cancer cell lines, including lung, breast and colorectal cancer [24]. It improves blood circulation and prevents ischemia‐reperfusion injury, making it beneficial in conditions like stroke, hypertension and atherosclerosis [13]. It acts as a vasodilator, reducing high blood pressure and promoting vascular relaxation. Traditionally, it has been used to relieve menstrual cramps and muscle spasms by relaxing smooth muscles in the uterus and gastrointestinal tract. It reduces pain and inflammation, making it useful for treating headaches and migraines [10]. Thus, the sample collected from Darma village (AG1) is highly valuable due to its maximum concentration of Z‐ligustilide.

Response surface analysis of ZOI was performed using a Box–Behnken design incorporating four quantitative factors (Z‐butylidenephthalide, Z‐ligustilide, temperature, time) and one categorical factor (pathogen, five levels). The fitted model was not statistically significant (F = 1.57, p = 0.3598), indicating that within the tested experimental space the selected model terms did not account for zone of inhibition (ZOI) variability beyond what would be expected from random error (Figure 2). Although the coefficient of determination was high (R 2 = 0.9106), the substantial divergence from the adjusted R 2 (0.3298) points to model over‐fitting relative to the available residual degrees of freedom (4), a consequence of the categorical factor's five levels combined with multiple two‐factor interactions saturating the design. Several higher‐order and interaction terms (AD, BC, BD, CD and all quadratic terms) were aliased and could not be independently estimated, and variance inflation factors for terms A, B, C, AB and AC were extremely elevated (103–105), reflecting severe multicollinearity among these predictors. The predicted R 2 could not be computed, and Adeq Precision (4.456) only marginally exceeded the minimum threshold of 4, together indicating limited predictive reliability of the current model. On this basis, the present dataset does not support a validated quantitative RSM optimization of ZOI as a function of the tested factors; the results should be interpreted as preliminary/exploratory, and individual factor trends discussed qualitatively rather than as statistically confirmed drivers of antimicrobial activity (Tables 3, 4, 5, 6).

Effect of Z‐Butylidenephthalide–Z‐Ligustilide Interaction on Antimicrobial Activity (Figures 4, 5, 6, 7, 8)

The response‐surface models describing the combined effect of Z‐butylidenephthalide (A) and Z‐ligustilide (B) revealed a pronounced curvature in the fitted surfaces across all five test organisms, indicating a statistically meaningful interaction between the two phthalides rather than a simple additive effect. For C. albicans (Figure 4) and Candida parapsilosis (Figure 5), the saddle‐shaped surfaces show that the ZOI is not maximised by either compound alone at its highest tested level, but by a specific combined ratio of A and B—visible as the innermost contour band on the corresponding 2D plots. This pattern is consistent with a synergistic interaction, where the two phthalides together produce a greater inhibitory effect than would be predicted from their individual contributions.

Three‐dimensional response surface and contour plot showing the interaction effect of A: Z‐butylidenephthalide and B: Z‐ligustilide on Candida parapsilosis.

A similar interaction shape is observed for Candida tropicalis (Figure 6), suggesting that susceptibility to the combined phthalide treatment is conserved across multiple Candida species, which is notable given that these species differ in their intrinsic azole‐resistance profiles and cell‐wall composition. This consistency strengthens the case that the mechanism of inhibition is related to a shared structural or membrane‐level target rather than a species‐specific susceptibility.

Three‐dimensional response surface and contour plot showing the interaction effect of A: Z‐butylidenephthalide and B: Z‐ligustilide on Candida tropicalis.

In contrast, the surfaces for the Gram‐negative bacteria—Escherichia coli (Figure 7) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Figure 8)—while following the same general curvature, show a comparatively narrower high‐response region, implying that the effective combination window for meaningful inhibition is tighter in bacteria than in the yeasts tested. This is consistent with the generally lower reported susceptibility of Gram‐negative bacteria to phthalide/terpenoid‐type compounds, likely attributable to the additional outer‐membrane permeability barrier that limits compound uptake compared with fungal cell walls.

Effect of Temperature on Z‐Butylidenephthalide Activity

When temperature (C) was substituted for Z‐Ligustilide as the second factor, the response surfaces for C. albicans (Figure 9), C . parapsilosis (Figure 10), and C . tropicalis (Figures 11 and 12) showed a marked transition from a low‐response (blue) region to a high‐response (red) region as temperature increased across the tested range, with the sharpest transition occurring in the mid‐range of tested temperatures rather than at the extremes. This indicates that Z‐butylidenephthalide's antimicrobial potency is temperature‐dependent, which is an important practical consideration if the compound were to be formulated for storage or applied under variable ambient conditions.

Antimicrobial Activity

The antimicrobial activity of the tested samples (AG1–AG6) was evaluated against five microorganisms: P. aeruginosa, E. coli, C. albicans, C. tropicalis, and C. parapsilosis. The inhibition zones were measured in millimetres (mm) with standard deviation values. Root essential oil exhibited strongest activity against some of the pathogens with the highest inhibition observed in the positive control across all microorganisms, confirming the validity of the experiment. AG2 showed the highest activity against C. albicans (9.00 ± 0.00 mm). AG5 exhibited significant inhibition against C. parapsilosis (8.67 ± 1.15 mm). AG1 demonstrated strong activity against E. coli (7.67 ± 0.57 mm). Most samples exhibited moderate inhibition against P. aeruginosa, with values ranging from 7.00 mm to 9.00 mm. Candida tropicalis showed moderate susceptibility, with AG4 having the highest inhibition (8.00 ± 0.00 mm). The lowest inhibition was observed against C. albicans in AG1 (5.00 ± 0.00 mm). Some samples (AG3 and AG6) showed lower inhibition against E. coli (6.00 ± 0.00 mm) (Table 7 and Figure 14). The presence of Z‐ligustilide and Z‐butylidenephthalide can attribute the significant activity of different samples.

Comparative account for the antimicrobial activity of Himalayan Angelica glauca root oil.

The antimicrobial activity of A. glauca root essential oil is attributed to its bioactive terpenoids, which act through multiple mechanisms to inhibit microbial growth. Major components like Z‐ligustilide and Z‐butylidenephthalide along with other constituents may interact with the lipid bilayer of microbial cell membranes. This interaction alters membrane permeability, causing leakage of cellular contents such as ions, ATP, and proteins, ultimately leading to cell death. Compounds such as Z‐ligustilide and Z‐butylidenephthalide may also interfere with microbial ATP synthesis, affecting energy production. Inhibition of key metabolic enzymes disrupts the electron transport chain, leading to bacterial cell dysfunction and death. Angelica glauca oil compounds interfere with microbial DNA replication and transcription. Coumarins can bind to DNA, preventing bacterial division, while terpenoids interfere with RNA polymerase activity, inhibiting protein synthesis. The oil prevents microbial communication (quorum sensing), which is essential for biofilm formation. This makes bacteria more susceptible to antimicrobial agents and prevents the establishment of persistent infections. This study highlights the significant variation in essential oil composition of A. glauca across different locations in the Uttarakhand Himalaya, driven by environmental factors. The findings underscore the potential for site‐specific cultivation strategies to optimize the yield and quality of essential oils. The antimicrobial activity of A. glauca root oil is due to its membrane‐disrupting, enzyme‐inhibiting, oxidative stress‐inducing, and DNA‐damaging effects. Its ability to prevent biofilm formation further enhances its potential as a natural antimicrobial agent against bacteria and fungi. Its principal constituent Z‐ligustilide is a promising phytochemical with broad medicinal applications, particularly in neuroprotection, inflammation control, antimicrobial defence, cancer treatment, and cardiovascular health. Ongoing research is exploring its potential for drug development, herbal formulations, and nano‐based delivery systems for enhanced therapeutic efficacy. The antimicrobial results suggest that AG2 and AG5 are the most effective samples against fungal pathogens, while AG1 showed better antibacterial potential against E. coli. Further studies, including mechanism analysis and MIC determination, may provide deeper insights into their antimicrobial properties. Hierarchical cluster analysis effectively categorised AG samples based on their chemical composition, highlighting similarities and differences that could be significant for subsequent research. The clustering results provide valuable insights into potential applications in green synthesis, pharmaceutical formulations and quality assurance. The distinct nature of AG4 suggests unique chemical properties, warranting further investigation. The results demonstrate the utility of hierarchical clustering in understanding complex chemical datasets and guiding future experimental designs. The present study provides data on phytochemical diversity and antimicrobial properties of A. glauca roots collected from six distinct regions of the Uttarakhand Himalayas. The findings reveal significant variations in bioactive compounds. The insights gained from this study can aid in the sustainable utilisation of A. glauca while promoting its application in pharmaceutical and nutraceutical industries.

Hierarchical Cluster Analysis and Interpretation of Cluster

Hierarchical clustering was performed to analyse the similarity among six A. glauca samples (AG1–AG6) based on their chemical composition, specifically phthalide derivative percentages and total composition percentages. The dataset was structured into a feature matrix where rows represented the samples and columns contained the two chemical parameters. The Euclidean distance metric was employed to measure similarity, and Ward's linkage method was applied for clustering due to its ability to minimize variance within clusters. The clustering process involved iteratively merging the most similar clusters until all samples formed a hierarchical tree. Molecular Docking

Molecular docking analysis was carried out to evaluate the binding affinity of the Z‐ligustilide with selected microbial target proteins using the CB‐Dock2 platform employing the AutoDock Vina scoring function. The docking results, including predicted binding cavities, Vina binding scores, cavity volumes, and docking grid parameters, are summarised in Table 8. The Z‐ligustilide exhibited favourable binding interactions with all selected microbial proteins, as indicated by the negative Vina binding energies. Among the tested microorganisms, the strongest binding affinity was observed with P. aeruginosa, showing a Vina score of −6.9 kcal/mol in cavity C1 with a cavity volume of 4268 Å3 (Figures 17 and 18). Similarly, docking with E. coli resulted in a binding score of −6.3 kcal/mol in cavity C3 with a cavity volume of 1150 Å3, indicating moderate binding stability (Figures 19 and 20). The Z‐ligustilide also demonstrated good binding affinity towards C. albicans, with a Vina score of −6.6 kcal/mol in cavity C1, characterised by a large binding pocket volume of 11 100 Å3, indicating sufficient space for Z‐ligustilide accommodation and stabilisation within the active site (Figures 21 and 22). This suggests a stable interaction between the Z‐ligustilide and the active site residues of the protein, indicating potential inhibitory activity. Similarly, the docking analysis with C. parapsilosis revealed a relatively strong interaction with a binding score of −6.7 kcal/mol in cavity C1, which corresponds to a cavity volume of 4942 Å3 (Figures 23 and 24). In the case of C. tropicalis, the Z‐ligustilide showed a binding energy of −6.4 kcal/mol in cavity C2 with a cavity volume of 468 Å3. Although the cavity size was smaller compared to other targets, the Z‐ligustilide was still able to interact effectively with the active site residues (Figures 25 and 26). The docking results demonstrate that the Z‐ligustilide interacts effectively with proteins from both bacterial and fungal pathogens. The relatively low binding energies suggest stable protein–Z‐ligustilide complexes, highlighting the potential antimicrobial activity of the Z‐ligustilide against the studied microorganisms. These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular basis of Z‐ligustilide protein interactions and supports experimental investigations to validates its antimicrobial potential.

Conclusions

This study highlights significant variations in the essential oil yield, composition, and antimicrobial activity of A. glauca roots collected from six locations in the Uttarakhand Himalaya. The essential oil yield ranged from 0.82% to 1.45% (w/w), with Z‐ligustilide identified as the predominant bioactive constituent, reaching a maximum of 75.2% in the Seepu village sample (AG1), representing a notable concentration. Response surface methodology based on a Box–Behnken design demonstrated that extraction temperature, extraction time, and altitude significantly influence the accumulation of Z‐ligustilide and total phthalide derivatives. The optimised conditions predicted by the model were approximately 60°C extraction temperature, about 3 h extraction time, and plant material collected from altitudes around 3500–3800 m, which resulted in enhanced enrichment of ligustilide‐rich essential oil. The essential oils exhibited preliminary to moderate antibacterial and antifungal activities, particularly in AG2, AG5 and AG1, which can be attributed mainly to the presence of Z‐ligustilide and Z‐butylidenephthalide. Although the essential oil exhibited detectable antimicrobial activity against the tested microorganisms, the absence of MIC, MBC and MFC determinations limits the quantitative assessment of its antimicrobial potency. Future investigations incorporating standardised MIC, MBC and MFC assays are necessary to validate and comprehensively characterise its antimicrobial potential. Hierarchical cluster analysis further revealed compositional similarities among samples, indicating a strong correlation between phytochemical composition and environmental factors such as altitude and geographical conditions. In addition, molecular docking analysis demonstrated favourable binding interactions of Z‐ligustilide with selected microbial target proteins, with binding energies ranging from −6.3 kcal mol− 1 to −6.9 kcal mol− 1, indicating stable protein–ligand complexes within the predicted binding cavities. The strongest interaction was observed with P. aeruginosa, followed by C. parapsilosis and C. albicans, suggesting a higher binding affinity of Z‐ligustilide towards these microbial targets. The ligand was effectively accommodated within the active site pockets, forming stabilising interactions with key amino acid residues. Overall, the integration of BBD‐based optimisation, GC–MS phytochemical profiling, antimicrobial evaluation, hierarchical clustering, and molecular docking analysis provides comprehensive insight into the phytochemical diversity and biological potential of A. glauca essential oil. These findings highlight Z‐ligustilide as a promising natural antimicrobial compound and support further investigations into its pharmacological mechanisms, sustainable utilisation, and formulation development for pharmaceutical and nutraceutical applications.

Extraction of Oils

The roots of A. glauca (300 g of each) were steam distilled using Clevenger apparatus with 2.5 L water for 5 h. The oils were dried with anhydrous Na2SO4 and stored in sealed vials in refrigerator until further analysis [17].

Gas Chromatography (GC‐FID) and Gas Chromatography‐Mass Spectrometry (GC‐MS) Analysis

Gas chromatography‐mass spectrometry (GC‐MS) analysis was conducted at A.I.R.F. (J.N.U), New Delhi, India, using a GCMS‐QP 2010 Ultra system equipped with a DB‐5 and Rtx‐5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 µm). Helium served as the carrier gas, maintaining a flow rate of 1.21 mL/min with a split ratio of 10.0. The GC oven temperature program started at 50°C, increasing at a rate of 3°C/min until reaching 210°C, where it was held for 2 min, followed by a rise of 6°C/min up to 280°C. The identification of essential oil components was achieved by comparing mass spectral fragmentation patterns and relative retention index (RI) values against established MS libraries (NIST14.lib, FFNSC2.lib, WILEY8.LIB) and corroborating the results with existing literature.

Identification of Major Constituents

The identification of compounds was based on the Linear Retention Index (LRI), determined using a homologous series of n‐alkanes (C9–C24, Polyscience Corp., Niles, IL) under identical experimental conditions. Additional confirmation was achieved through co‐injection with standards (Sigma), mass spectral library searches (NIST and WILEY), comparison with published MS data [18, 19], and NMR analysis (1H and 13C NMR) of the major isolates. The relative quantification of individual components was performed by calculating the GC peak area (FID response) without applying a correction factor [17].

Box–Behnken Design Optimisation

Response surface methodology based on a five‐factor Box–Behnken design was applied to evaluate the antimicrobial activity. The two natural phthalide constituents evaluated in this study, Z‐butylidenephthalide (Factor A) and Z‐ligustilide (Factor B), were obtained from five essential oil samples extracted in 60 min to 120 min (Factor C). The extraction temperature range was 50°C–65°C (Factor D). This essential oil sample was evaluated against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, C. albicans and C. tropicalis pathogens (Factor E) in the experimental design. All assays were performed in triplicate, and mean ZOI values were used in the statistical analysis. A five‐factor Box–Behnken design was constructed using Design‐Expert software (Stat‐Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) to evaluate the individual and interactive effects of four numeric factors—Z‐butylidenephthalide concentration (A), Z‐ligustilide concentration (B), incubation temperature (C), and incubation time (D)—together with one categorical factor, test pathogen (E, five levels), on the zone of inhibition (mm) as the measured response. The numeric factors were varied over three coded levels (−1, 0, +1); the corresponding actual factor ranges are given in Table 1. The design generated a total of 30 experimental runs, which were carried out in a randomised order to minimise systematic error.

The data were fitted to a quadratic polynomial equation to evaluate the interaction between variables (Table 10).

Microbial Culture

The in vitro antibacterial activity of root essential oil was evaluated against a total of two Gram‐negative bacteria, P. aeruginosa (MTCC‐ 3541) and E. coli (MTCC‐ 452). The antifungal activity of the samples was performed against C. parapsilosis (MTCC 998), C. tropicalis (MTCC 1000) and C. albicans (MTCC 854). The test strains were purchased from the Institute of Microbial Technology (IMTECH), Chandigarh, India. MTCC (Microbial Technology Culture Collection) numbers represent the standard strain numbers assigned to these microorganisms. The bacteria were maintained on nutrient broth (NB) at 37°C, and the fungus was maintained on Malt yeast agar at 28°C. The gram‐negative bacteria (P. aeruginosa, E. coli) were pre‐cultured in nutrient broth overnight in a rotary shaker at 37°C, centrifuged, and the pellet was suspended in double‐distilled water, while the fungus strains were pre‐cultured in malt yeast broth at 28°C [17, 20].

Determination of Zone of Inhibition

The antimicrobial activity of the essential oils was investigated by the disc diffusion method. The disc diffusion method is a widely used technique to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of plant extracts. In this method, a standardised microbial culture (0.5 McFarland) is evenly spread onto Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) for bacteria or Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) for fungi. Sterile filter paper discs (6 mm) impregnated with the test sample are placed on the agar surface alongside a positive control (standard antibiotic) and a negative control (DMSO). The plates are incubated at 37°C for 24 h (bacteria) or 25°C–28°C for 48 h (fungi). After incubation, the diameter of the inhibition zone around each disc is measured to assess antimicrobial potency. Larger inhibition zones indicate stronger antimicrobial activity, and results are typically expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mm inhibition zone). Ciprofloxacin disc (10 µg) and amphotericin B disc (50 µg) were taken as positive controls for bacteria and fungi, respectively. This method follows the standardised protocol [20, 21, 22, 23].

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking analysis was performed to investigate the interaction between the major bioactive compound Z‐ligustilide and selected microbial target proteins. The three‐dimensional structure of Z‐ligustilide was obtained from the PubChem database and converted into an appropriate docking format after energy minimisation. The three‐dimensional structures of the microbial proteins of P. aeruginosa, E. coli, C. albicans, C. tropicalis, and C. parapsilosis were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Prior to docking, the protein structures were prepared by removing water molecules and co‐crystallised ligands, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms. Molecular docking simulations were carried out using the CB‐Dock2 platform, which employs the AutoDock Vina scoring function. The CB‐Dock2 server automatically detected potential binding cavities in the protein structures and defined the docking grid box based on the predicted binding pockets. Docking calculations were then performed to evaluate the binding affinity and possible binding modes of Z‐ligustilide with the target proteins. The docking results were ranked according to the predicted binding affinity (kcal/mol), and the best binding poses were selected based on the lowest binding energy values. The protein–ligand interaction complexes were further visualised and analysed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio to identify key amino acid residues involved in hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions [30, 31].

Statistical Analysis

The experimental data were fitted to a quadratic polynomial model relating ZOI to the linear, interaction, and quadratic terms of the numeric factors and their interactions with the categorical pathogen factor. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess the significance of the model and of individual terms, and model adequacy was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R 2), adjusted R 2, predicted R 2, coefficient of variation (C.V.%), and adequate precision. A term or model was considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Diagnostic plots (predicted vs. actual values) and three‐dimensional response surface plots were generated to visualise the effect of each factor and factor interaction on ZOI for each test pathogen. The experimental data obtained from the BBD experiments were analysed using response surface methodology (RSM) using Design Expert 13. The significance of the model and the interaction between variables were evaluated using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Three‐dimensional response surface plots and contour plots were generated to visualise the relationship between the independent variables and the responses. The optimum extraction conditions were determined based on the maximum desirability value predicted by the model. Samples were analysed individually in triplicate for their antimicrobial activities, and data were analysed by ANOVA statistical software. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation of triplicate measurements (p < 0.001).
