Zirconium particles in toothpaste: a novel approach to whitening and its impact on enamel wear
Authors: Besir Salihu, Miranda Stavileci, Teuta Pustina, Xhevahir Bajrami, Afrim Gjelaj, Freskim Rrustemaj, Jacques Veronneau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbrasive agents in toothpaste play a crucial role in plaque removal and stain reduction; however, excessive abrasivity may compromise enamel integrity. Zirconium oxide has recently been proposed as a polishing additive, but its optimal concentration remains unclear. This in vitro study aimed to identify the optimal concentration of zirconium oxide particles in toothpaste for effective cleaning through abrasion of natural dental tissues. The study evaluated enamel substance loss (wear) and surface alteration (roughness) across different zirconium oxide concentrations, highlighting both the advantages and potential risks associated with their use in dental care.Materials and methodsFifty caries-free third molars with intact crowns were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 10). Four groups were brushed with toothpaste containing zirconium oxide particles at concentrations of 0.5%, 1%, 2%, and 5%, while the control group was brushed with distilled water. Tooth wear and surface roughness were measured before and after brushing using profilometry and intraoral scanning. Statistical analysis included the paired t-test, Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test, Kruskal–Wallis test, and Dunn’s multiple comparisons test.ResultsAll zirconium oxide concentrations caused significant tooth wear. Wear analysis revealed significant differences between the 0.5% and 5% groups (p < 0.001) and between the 0.5% and 2% groups (p < 0.05), with no other significant intergroup differences. The 5% concentration produced the greatest increase in surface roughness, with an average rise of 91% (p = 0.002). Conversely, the 0.5% concentration resulted in a significant reduction in roughness (p = 0.002). No significant changes were observed for the 1% (p = 0.375) or 2% (p = 0.105) concentrations.ConclusionToothpaste containing 5% zirconium oxide particles markedly increases enamel wear and surface roughness, whereas lower concentrations (0.5–2%) provide a polishing effect with minimal damage. Optimizing the abrasive properties of toothpaste is crucial to achieve effective cleaning without compromising enamel integrity. Further research is warranted to establish the long-term effects of zirconium oxide-based formulations.

Introduction

Tooth color plays a crucial role in both aesthetics and dental health. Natural tooth shade varies among individuals, influenced by factors such as diet, oral hygiene habits, and genetics [1]. Tooth color is determined by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors [2, 3]. Intrinsic characteristics are primarily defined by the way enamel and dentin reflect and absorb light, whereas extrinsic factors involve the deposition of pigments from dietary sources and other external agents on the tooth surface, leading to discoloration [4, 5]. The surface appearance of teeth, particularly when affected by extrinsic discoloration, is often managed through whitening toothpastes, which have demonstrated significant efficacy in removing surface stains, as confirmed by systematic reviews [6, 7].

Whitening toothpastes have transformed oral care by providing an affordable and convenient means to achieve a brighter smile. These formulations typically contain solid abrasives, humectants, thickening agents, surfactants, fluoride, opacifying agents, and buffering agents, which act synergistically to clean and maintain oral health [8]. Compared to conventional toothpastes, whitening formulations generally contain higher levels of abrasives, including hydrated silica, calcium carbonate, dicalcium phosphate dihydrate, calcium pyrophosphate, alumina, perlite, and sodium bicarbonate [9]. These abrasives physically remove surface stains, while bleaching agents chemically break down deeper stains, enhancing the whitening effect [3, 4]. Despite their effectiveness, concerns remain about the long-term impact of whitening toothpastes on enamel. Excessive abrasivity can cause enamel erosion, exposing dentin and increasing tooth sensitivity [5], while prolonged use of bleaching agents such as hydrogen peroxide may further weaken enamel, rendering teeth more vulnerable to damage [6]. Thus, although whitening toothpastes provide aesthetic benefits, their extended use raises concerns about potential risks to enamel integrity.

Tooth wear has increasingly been recognized as a significant oral health concern, exacerbated by the growing use of abrasive toothpastes. Tooth wear may result from normal function (e.g., mastication), parafunction (e.g., bruxism), or environmental factors (e.g., dietary acids) [10]. Erosive wear, in particular, is attributed to chemical processes involving acidic foods, beverages, or gastric reflux [11]. The abrasive action of whitening toothpastes can aggravate this erosion, accelerating enamel loss. Excessive tooth wear negatively affects aesthetics, tooth structure, and even facial morphology, and may lead to increased sensitivity, a higher risk of caries, and potential speech impairments [12].

Striking a balance between aesthetic improvement and enamel preservation is therefore essential [13]. A novel toothpaste containing zirconia particles has recently attracted attention for its potential role in whitening and polishing. This formulation, developed by Dermaflore Inc. (St-Denis-sur-Richelieu, Quebec, Canada), incorporates zirconium compounds as the primary abrasive [14]. Unlike traditional abrasives, zirconia may provide a gentler polishing effect. Its chemical inertness also reduces the likelihood of interactions with oral tissues or salivary enzymes, potentially lowering the risk of irritation or adverse effects, as suggested by previous studies on zirconia as a biomaterial [15].

The primary aim of this in vitro study is to evaluate the effectiveness of different concentrations of zirconium oxide in whitening toothpaste on natural tooth enamel. Specifically, the study investigates enamel substance loss (wear) and changes in surface roughness associated with zirconium oxide at varying concentrations. The null hypothesis (H₀) states that there is no significant difference in enamel wear or surface roughness between toothpastes containing different zirconium oxide concentrations.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

Approval to carry out this research was granted by the Ethical Committee (297/2) of the Dental University Clinical Center of Kosovo.

Preparation of samples

Before initiating the study, a power analysis was performed to determine the minimum sample size required to detect statistically significant differences in enamel wear and surface roughness among the experimental groups. With a statistical power of 80% and a significance level of α = 0.05, the analysis indicated that at least 10 samples per group were sufficient, resulting in a total of 50 teeth for the experimental design. Accordingly, 50 human teeth were selected for this study, specifically caries-free third molars with intact crowns. These teeth were extracted for periodontal reasons from patients at the Dental University Clinical Center of Kosovo and collected during routine dental procedures with explicit patient consent, in full accordance with ethical guidelines. Extractions were carried out by qualified dental professionals, and all teeth were stored in distilled water under standardized conditions until further use to preserve their integrity. For experimental preparation, the crowns were separated from the roots at the cemento-enamel junction and mounted onto plastic model jaws (ANA-4; Frasaco GmbH, Tettnang, Germany) to simulate a clinical scenario.

Tooth wear simulation

Following a predefined and previously validated protocol, five groups of teeth, each comprising ten specimens, were evenly distributed across the dental models described above. The teeth underwent manual grinding to simulate occlusal wear at two levels of severity, corresponding to approximately 1 mm and 2 mm of vertical loss. The grinding process employed both symmetric and asymmetric techniques using a diamond round bur (DiamondCo Scientific Instruments, USA) to replicate various natural wear patterns. Achieving approximately 1 mm of vertical loss required 100–120 strokes, whereas 2 mm of loss necessitated 200–250 strokes. To ensure precision, vertical loss measurements were verified using digital 3D surface scanning before and after the grinding procedure. This approach allowed for accurate quantification of enamel reduction and ensured consistency across all specimens. The grinding was confined to the enamel layer, with ongoing inspection under 2.5× magnification dental loupes to monitor changes in color and texture indicative of potential dentin exposure. No discoloration or structural alterations suggestive of dentin breach were observed. Unaltered teeth, specifically the second premolars and molars, along with surrounding anatomical structures, served as reference areas for superimposition. These untouched regions provided the gold standard for measurements, representing true baseline values.

Experimental procedure

After fixing the natural tooth, the plastic model was scanned at baseline t0 using an intraoral scanner (Helios-500; Changzhou Sifary Medical Technology Co.Ltd. 213000. China), with a step size of 0.01 mm (10 μm) and a scanning resolution of 200 steps along the X-axis, 200 steps along the Y-axis, and 100 steps along the Z-axis. After all scans at t0 were accomplished, wear areas were manually simulated using a diamond-bur (Ø1.0 mm, grit size 42 μm).

Overall, wear areas were sequentially generated on the occlusal surfaces of the natural tooth representing tooth 46 in the plastic jaw model (Fig. 1). Subsequently, the models underwent another round of scanning (t1) using the aforementioned scanner. The total sample of teeth exhibiting simulated tooth wear was divided into five groups, each comprising 10 teeth. Four groups underwent brushing sessions using a fixed brush attached to a micromotor, and the paste containing Zirconia particles at four different concentrations (0.5, 1, 2, and 5%). A toothpaste slurry was evenly applied to each sample. The fifth group served as a control group, undergoing brushing under the same conditions but using only distilled water. Each group underwent 5,000 brushing cycles, corresponding to approximately 5 min of timed brushing, using medium-bristle brushes to simulate consistent manual brushing pressure. The tooth-brushing simulation was performed with a fixed medium-bristle toothbrush mounted on a micromotor (Strong 204, Saeshin Precision Co., Ltd., Daegu, South Korea) operating at a constant rotational speed of 300 rpm. To maintain uniform brushing efficiency, the brushes were replaced after every five samples.

After the brushing sessions, the models underwent the final round of scanning (t2). At every phase, the model underwent three consecutive scans, all conducted by the same skilled operator. After the scanning was completed, the average of the three scans was taken and the samples were removed from the plastic jaw model and fixed on the resin cylinders, and were subjected once again to triple profilometric measurements.

Tooth wear analysis

The tooth wear analysis was performed using (3Shape TRIOS® Patient Monitoring, version 2.1.1.0, 3Shape A/S, Denmark) and exocad software (DentalCAD, exocad GmbH, Germany) The scanned teeth before and after brushing were aligned using the software’s precise matching algorithms. The software facilitated the superimposition of the scanned data at t0, t1 and t2 to assess the extent of tooth wear accurately. The unaltered reference areas (central occlusal fissure mesially and distally, and foramen caecum molare) were utilized as fixed points to ensure the alignment accuracy.

After alignment, a color map tool allowed for the visualization of differences between the baseline model and the models obtained at various time points (Fig. 2). The software set a threshold of 50 μm for surface changes to be visible on the color map. The initial visual changes exceeding this threshold were observed for each semi-arch.

Statistics

Data processing was performed using InStat free version. The statistical parameters calculated included the arithmetic mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values. The comparison of data between two groups (before and after application) was conducted using the Paired T-test or the Wilcoxon Matched Pairs Signed-rank test, depending on the data distribution. Differences between groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn’s Multiple Comparison test. A difference was considered significant if P < 0.05.

The mean difference in tooth wear for each concentration was calculated, and the Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a statistically significant overall difference (p < 0.0001). In contrast, the control group, brushed with distilled water, exhibited negligible enamel wear, with initial and final values remaining virtually unchanged. This finding emphasizes that mechanical brushing alone did not induce significant wear, highlighting the contribution of the toothpaste’s active ingredients to enamel abrasion.

Analysis using the 3Shape TRIOS® Patient Monitoring software (version 2.1.1.0, Denmark) revealed a concentration-dependent pattern of enamel surface changes. At 0.5%, the observed enamel loss was minimal and uniformly distributed, with surface changes mostly below the detection threshold, suggesting a polishing effect that may help in smoothing minor surface irregularities. In contrast, higher concentrations (particularly 5%) showed more intense and irregular colour changes, consistent with excessive enamel removal and increased surface irregularities (Fig. 2 (C–F)).

Regarding surface roughness, a significant decrease was observed after brushing with the 0.5% toothpaste (P = 0.002), further supporting its polishing effect. The 1% and 2% concentrations did not produce statistically significant changes in roughness (P = 0.375 and P = 0.105, respectively), suggesting a neutral effect. However, the 5% concentration caused a significant increase in surface roughness (P = 0.002), indicating abrasive action beyond safe thresholds. Discussion

This study evaluated the effects of whitening toothpaste containing different concentrations of zirconium oxide particles on enamel wear and surface roughness. As shown in Table 2 and Chart 1, all tested concentrations caused statistically significant enamel loss, with wear progressively increasing at higher concentrations. This observation reflects the expected abrasive potential associated with higher particulate loads and suggests that zirconia’s action may involve both mechanical and chemical effects. The results indicate that lower concentrations of zirconia toothpaste (0.5%–2%) can contribute to surface polishing without causing substantial enamel loss or surface damage, potentially offering both aesthetic and preventive benefits by reducing plaque accumulation. In contrast, the 5% concentration appears excessively abrasive, increasing the risk of enamel wear, surface roughening, and potential long-term dental complications, including heightened sensitivity, increased susceptibility to cavities, and enamel erosion.

Based on these findings, the null hypothesis (H₀), which proposed no significant differences in enamel wear or surface roughness among the various zirconium oxide concentrations, is rejected. The observed differences demonstrate that zirconium oxide concentration plays a significant role in determining the abrasiveness and polishing efficacy of whitening toothpaste.

When selecting toothpaste, considerations extend beyond efficacy for specific dental concerns to include overall oral health impact. Modern toothpaste formulations have been greatly improved through the inclusion of safe active ingredients, fluoride synergistic agents, and carefully selected, highly effective abrasives [11, 12]. These products address a range of oral health issues, including cavities, gingivitis, tooth sensitivity, and halitosis, while meeting consumer expectations for performance and safety. Although comprehensive studies on all combined ingredients are limited, numerous investigations have examined the effects of individual active or inactive components commonly found in toothpaste [12–21].

As zirconium-containing whitening toothpastes are not yet popular on the market, direct comparisons with other studies on their effects on natural enamel are limited. However, similar investigations on dentin have been conducted. Stavileci M. et al. reported that increasing zirconium content in whitening toothpaste leads to greater abrasivity on dentin, which supports the results observed in our study [14].

While zirconia particles present an interesting development, another notable abrasive used in some whitening toothpastes is diamond particles. According to Hamza B. et al., the extreme hardness of diamond particles may lead to greater enamel wear compared to conventional abrasives [22]. These findings are consistent with those of Enax J. et al., who reported that toothpastes containing larger and more abrasive particles resulted in increased dentin loss compared to formulations with finer particles [23]. While our study focused on identifying an optimal concentration of zirconium particles that remains within physiological limits for enamel wear, Fischer M. et al. emphasized the role of particle type in influencing toothpaste efficacy against erosion and wear. Their findings suggest that the type of abrasive particles significantly affects performance, even in the absence of mechanical forces. This is supported by the observation that NaF toothpastes with the same fluoride concentration can have varying efficacies, possibly due to the particle fraction contained and not to the concentration [24]. In addition to abrasive composition and concentration, the inclusion of protective agents can significantly influence a toothpaste’s effectiveness against tooth wear. For instance, TiF₄ combined with chitosan has been shown to effectively minimize erosive tooth wear, offering a protective benefit beyond standard formulations [25]. As for the other toothpastes and their effect on enamel surface roughness, Alpan et al. found that among six whitening toothpastes evaluated, Splat Special Blackwood and Colgate Optic White effectively reduced enamel roughness [26]. Additionally, Shamel et al. demonstrated that whitening toothpastes with blue covarine caused less surface roughness than other whitening toothpastes [27]. Concerning microhardness, two studies indicated that whitening toothpastes had no effect [28, 29], whereas two other studies observed a decrease in microhardness [30, 31].

During the realization of this research, a key challenge was adopting modern methodologies to achieve accurate results. While profilometry is still considered the gold standard for analyzing surface roughness [32–34], recent research has explored the use of intraoral scanners for evaluating tooth wear. While we used both methods in our study, several studies have evaluated the effectiveness of intraoral scanners (IOS) in measuring tooth wear, highlighting their potential and limitations. Kühne et al. [35] examined three different scanners such as Cerec Omnicam AC, Trios 3, and True Definition, using a zirconia cast. Their findings suggest that digital impression systems are promising for wear measurement, though inaccuracies of approximately ± 20 μm per partial arch impression should be considered. Similarly, Witecy et al. [36] assessed the ability of Trios 3 and Carestream CS3600 scanners to track erosive tooth wear in human molar slices. They concluded that both internal and external measurement software allowed effective monitoring of tissue loss, making IOS a valuable tool for detecting even minimal wear progression. Gkantidis et al. [37] utilized a laboratory scanner to analyze dental plaster models and found that this method enables precise visualization of tooth wear across all spatial dimensions. This level of accuracy suggests that such techniques could be highly informative for future research. Lastly, Charalambous et al. [38] investigated a clinical intraoral scanner (TrueDefinition) on unpolished human enamel samples. Their study demonstrated that the scanner could reliably detect surface changes as small as 73 μm, indicating a level of precision suitable for both clinical observation and research purposes. Overall, these studies collectively suggest that intraoral scanning technology is a reliable and promising method for measuring and monitoring tooth wear, though variations in accuracy and software capabilities should be acknowledged.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, it was conducted in vitro, which may not fully replicate the complex oral environment, including factors such as saliva flow, pH fluctuations, and mechanical forces from mastication. Second, the duration of exposure and brushing cycles may not reflect long-term, real-world use. Additionally, the sample size and substrate type may influence the generalizability of the findings. Third, the roughness values of the control group were unexpectedly higher compared to some test groups. While this may result from intrinsic surface variation among natural enamel samples, it could also have influenced the baseline values for comparison. All specimens were randomly distributed and prepared under standardized conditions, but future studies should consider initial surface preparation and polishing to eliminate such inconsistencies. Fourth, in some cases, wear values appeared to decrease after brushing. This counterintuitive result is explained by the surface-smoothing or polishing effect of lower-concentration zirconia formulations, which may reduce the profilometric measurement without indicating any gain in enamel volume. Such effects are known in profilometric studies and highlight the need for complementary surface characterization techniques.

A further important limitation is the lack of high-resolution surface characterization techniques, such as Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), or confocal laser scanning microscopy. These methods would have allowed for more detailed visualization and analysis of microstructural changes and enamel surface morphology, providing insights beyond what volumetric analysis alone can reveal.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, tooth wear occurs at the 5% concentration of Zirconia particles in the toothpaste, as indicated by the increase in surface roughness. Lower concentrations (0.5%, 1%, 2%) likely produce a polishing effect, as evidenced by the decrease in surface roughness.

It is crucial to balance the abrasive properties of toothpaste to ensure effective cleaning without causing damage to the teeth. Abrasives should be sufficient to remove stains and plaque but not so aggressive as to cause wear. To confirm these findings, additional tests with concentrations around the 5% mark (e.g., 4.5%, 5.5%) are necessary to pinpoint the threshold more accurately. Furthermore, examining the long- term in-vivo effects of these concentrations on enamel integrity will provide more comprehensive insights.
