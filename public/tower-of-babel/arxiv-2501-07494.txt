Strengthened upper bound on the third eigenvalue of graphs
Sida Li

Abstract
Let G G be a graph on n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 vertices, whose adjacency matrix has eigenvalues λ 1 ≥ λ 2 ≥ ⋯ ≥ λ n \lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n} . The problem of bounding λ k \lambda_{k} in terms of n n was first proposed by Hong and was studied by Nikiforov, who demonstrated strong upper and lower bounds for arbitrary k k . Nikiforov also claimed a strengthened upper bound for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , namely that λ k n < 1 2 ​ k − 1 − ε k \frac{\lambda_{k}}{n}<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}}-\varepsilon_{k} for some positive ε k \varepsilon_{k} , but omitted the proof due to its length. In this paper, we give a proof of this bound for k = 3 k=3 . We achieve this by instead looking at λ n − 1 + λ n \lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n} and introducing a new graph operation which provides structure to minimising graphs, including ω ≤ 3 \omega\leq 3 and χ ≤ 4 \chi\leq 4 . Then we reduce the hypothetical worst case to a graph that is n / 2 n/2 -regular and invariant under said operation. By considering a series of inequalities on the restricted eigenvector components, we prove that a sequence of graphs with λ n − 1 + λ n n \frac{\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}}{n} converging to − 2 2 -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} cannot exist.



1
Introduction

For a graph
G
on
n\geq 3
vertices, let the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of
G
be
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}
. Denote the complement of
G
by
\overline{G}
, with eigenvalues
\overline{\lambda_{1}}\geq\dots\geq\overline{\lambda_{n}}
.

The problem of bounding the
k
-th eigenvalue of a graph
G
of order
n
was first proposed by Hong
[
7
]
. In accordance with Nikiforov
[
14
]
, let
c_{k}=\sup\{\lambda_{k}(G)/n:|V(G)|=n\geq k\}
and
c_{-k}=\sup\{|\lambda_{n-k+1}(G)|/n:|V(G)|=n\geq k\}
. Nikiforov proved that for all
k\geq 2
,

c_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}}.

A natural lower bound is provided by the construction
kK_{\frac{n}{k}}
which gives
\lambda_{k}=\frac{n}{k}-1
, hence
c_{k}\geq\frac{1}{k}
. For
k=1,2
, we indeed have
c_{1}=1
and
c_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
, but Nikiforov showed that for
k\geq 5
, we have an improvement
c_{k}\geq\frac{1}{k-1/2}
. Recently Linz
[
10
]
demonstrated a construction for
c_{4}\geq\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{12}\approx 0.26967>\frac{1}{4}
.

Nikiforov claimed that for each
k\geq 3
, there exists
\varepsilon_{k}>0
such that

c_{k}<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}}-\varepsilon_{k},

though the proof was never published. In this paper, we prove the claim for
k=3
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{3}>0
such that

c_{3}<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}-\varepsilon_{3}.

In comparison to Nikiforov’s proof outline, our proof doesn’t rely on the Removal Lemma of Alon et al.
[
1
]
nor analytic graph theory. Nonetheless, the final step is motivated by considering a sequence of worst-case graphs and observing averages that do converge, since graph convergence is not guaranteed. We also provide possible alternative proof ideas that delve into the theory of graphons.

Instead, we shift our focus to the related but untouched problem of minimising
\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}
over all graphs of order
n
. This is motivated by observing the inequalities used to derive the original
c_{3}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}
bound by Nikiforov
[
14
]
and determining conditions for which they are tight. Indeed, we first have
\lambda_{1}=\frac{2e(G)}{n}
which corresponds to regular
G
, and in the last step we require
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
, which Leonida and Li
[
9
]
also provided strong evidence for. In this case, minimal
\overline{\lambda_{n-1}}+\overline{\lambda_{n}}
is indeed equivalent to maximal
\lambda_{3}
.

We prove the following theorem, which strengthens the current bound of
\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}\geq-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}n
, given by AM-QM on
\lambda_{n-1}^{2}+\lambda_{n}^{2}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4}
. The infimum exists by Nikiforov
[
12
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\inf\left\{\frac{\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}}{n}:|V(G)|=n\geq 3\right\}>-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}+\varepsilon.

Theorem
1.2
and
\lambda_{n-1}\geq\lambda_{n}
immediately give:

Corollary 1.3
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{3}>0
such that

c_{-2}<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}-\varepsilon_{3}.

Then Theorem
1.1
follows immediately from Weyl’s inequalities, which gives
\lambda_{3}+\overline{\lambda_{n-1}}\leq\lambda_{2}(K_{n})=-1
.

The structure of the rest of the paper, dedicated to proving Theorem
1.2
, is as follows. In Section
2
, we first introduce a new graph operation which restricts the structure of graphs minimising
\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}
, forcing them to look not dissimilar to
Pi_{n}
as introduced by Leonida and Li
[
9
]
. For example, we prove the following, where
\omega
and
\chi
denote the clique and chromatic numbers respectively.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every
n\geq 3
, there exists
G
of order
n
with minimal
\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}
such that
\omega(G)\leq 3
and
\chi(G)\leq 4
.

This operation was inspired by the analogous operation for
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
, proposed by Ebrahimi et al.
[
6
]
. We then briefly describe a generalisation, though not involved in the proof.

Then in Section
3
we further impose the graph to be
n/2
-regular and prove the theorem for this family of constrained invariant graphs. We do this by deriving a series of inequalities from eigenvector components, where we consider two different patterns of eigenvectors that each impose distinct structural restrictions on the graph. Then, given a sequence of graphs with
\frac{\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}}{n}
converging to
-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
, we take local averages of degrees and eigenvector components to prove that these simultaneous restrictions yield a contradiction.

We conclude in Section
4
by reducing the hypothetical ‘worst-case’ graph to this family by showing that we can obtain the same required structure with
o(n^{2})
edge additions/removals, which doesn’t affect the overall growth of the spectrum.

2
Structure of minimising graphs

We introduce a new operation similar to the one used to bound
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
by Ebrahimi et al.
[
6
]
. Since we are focussing on the last two eigenvalues, intuitively this operation ought to increase the third eigenvalue proportion of
\overline{G}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Define
Operation
∗
on a graph
G
as follows. Let
G^{*}
be any graph such that
V(G^{*})=V(G)
and there exists an orthonormal pair of eigenvectors
\{x,y\}
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{n-1}(G),\lambda_{n}(G)\}
of
G
such that in
G^{*}
, we have
i\sim j\iff x_{i}x_{j}+y_{i}y_{j}<0
.

The motivation for this operation is as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

For any
G^{*}
, we have
\lambda_{n-1}(G^{*})+\lambda_{n}(G^{*})\leq\lambda_{n-1}(G)+\lambda_{n}(G)
.

Proof.

As
A(G),A(G^{*})
are Hermitian matrices, we have:

\displaystyle\lambda_{n-1}(G^{*})+\lambda_{n}(G^{*})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}|z|=|w|=1\\
z\perp w\end{subarray}}\langle z,A(G^{*})z\rangle+\langle w,A(G^{*})w\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle x,A(G^{*})x\rangle+\langle y,A(G^{*})y\rangle

\displaystyle=2\cdot\sum_{x_{i}x_{j}+y_{i}y_{j}<0}(x_{i}x_{j}+y_{i}y_{j})

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\sum_{ij\in E(G)}(x_{i}x_{j}+y_{i}y_{j})

\displaystyle=\langle x,A(G)x\rangle+\langle y,A(G)y\rangle=\lambda_{n-1}(G)+\lambda_{n}(G).\qed

Corollary 2.3
.

For every
n\geq 3
, there exists
G
of order
n
with minimal
\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}
such that
G=G^{*}
.

Proof.

Since there are finitely many graphs of order
n
, pick
G
with minimal
\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}
and furthermore with minimal number of edges, then we must have
\lambda_{n-1}(G^{*})+\lambda_{n}(G^{*})=\lambda_{n-1}(G)+\lambda_{n}(G)
. Hence
G^{*}
can only possibly differ from
G
by the removal of edges where
x_{i}x_{j}+y_{i}y_{j}=0
. Yet
G
was selected with minimal number of edges hence no edges were removed, so
G=G^{*}
.
∎

To each vertex of
G
, associate a
\mathbb{R}^{2}
vector
v_{i}=\binom{x_{i}}{y_{i}}
. Then the adjacency condition becomes:
i\sim j\iff v_{i}\cdot v_{j}<0\iff v_{i},v_{j}\text{ are non-zero and }\angle(v_{i},v_{j})>\pi/2
.

Note that
G^{*}
is not necessarily unique if the geometric multiplicity of the last eigenvalue is at least 3 or the geometric multiplicity of the second-last eigenvalue is at least 2, due to the choice in orthonormal vectors. If the geometric multiplicity of the last is exactly 2, then rotating our orthonormal pair corresponds to rotating the
v_{i}
, hence adjacencies are invariant and
G^{*}
is unique.
G^{*}
is immediately unique if the geometric multiplicity of the last and second-last eigenvalues are both 1.

We now demonstrate that the output graphs of this operation have a rich structure, with traces of pivalous graphs as introduced by Leonida and Li
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose
G=H^{*}
for some graph
H
. Then there exists a labelling of the vertices
[1,2,\dots,n]
such that the neighbours of vertex
i
are
\{a_{i},a_{i}+1,\dots,b_{i}\}
mod
n
, and that the
(a_{i}),(b_{i})
are increasing.

Proof.

Plot all the vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
and re-label them in anti-clockwise order, with WLOG (without loss of generality) all zero vectors first. Since the neighbours of
i
are exactly the vectors at an obtuse angle away from
v_{i}
, or empty if
v_{i}=0
, this forms a consecutive run taken mod
n
where necessary. It also satisfies the fact that the start and end-points of the run increase as we rotate anti-clockwise around the origin.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
G=H^{*}
for some graph
H
. Then
\omega(G)\leq 3
.

Proof.

Suppose FTSOC (for the sake of contradiction) there exists a
K_{4}
, say non-zero
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}
such that
w_{i}\cdot w_{j}<0
for all pairs
\{i,j\}\subset\{1,2,3,4\}
. Draw the four vectors on the plane. A pair of consecutive vectors must have an acute angle between them, otherwise the sum of the angles is
>2\pi
. Between these two,
w_{i}\cdot w_{j}\geq 0
, contradiction.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose
G=H^{*}
for some graph
H
. Then
\chi(G)\leq 4
.

Proof.

Consider the partition of the vertices into quadrants of the usual Cartesian plane, i.e. by sign of
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
with WLOG zero included into the positives. Then vectors within a quadrant differ by an acute or right angle, hence form an independent set.
∎

Combining Corollary
2.3
with Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
immediately yields Theorem
1.4
. We explore some specific sub-cases of output graphs which admit additional structure.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose
G=H^{*}
for some graph
H
. If there exist two anti-clockwise consecutive neighbours in
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
which differ by an obtuse angle, then
\chi(G)\leq 3
.

Proof.

Rotate the Cartesian plane to force at most three quadrants to contain a vector, with the last quadrant contained inside this obtuse angle gap. Thus, we get at most three independent sets.

Alternatively, one can notice that
\overline{G}
is a chordal graph, hence perfect. By the perfect graph theorem,
G
is perfect hence has
\chi(G)=\omega(G)\leq 3
. Both results are corollaries of the strong perfect graph theorem of Chudnovsky et al.
[
4
]
.
∎

Recall that a closed blow-up by
k
of a graph is constructed by replacing each vertex with a
K_{k}
and each edge with a complete bipartite graph between the endpoint cliques.

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose
G=H^{*}
for some graph
H
. If
G
is regular and each
v_{i}
occurs exactly
k
times for some positive integer
k
, then
\overline{G}
is the closed blow-up by
k
of a circulant graph.

Proof.

Consider
\overline{G}
where
i\sim j\iff v_{i}\cdot v_{j}\geq 0
. Note that if
v_{i}=v_{j}
, then they have the exact same neighbourhood, hence
\overline{G}
is a closed blow-up by
k
of say
G^{\prime}
.

Now suppose
G^{\prime}
has regularity
d
. We have
\frac{n}{k}
distinct vectors now, say
(v_{i}^{\prime})
, where
\lambda v_{i}^{\prime}
is the sum of a run of
d
consecutive vectors. Yet each such run is determined uniquely by the first vector, hence there are
\frac{n}{k}
such runs and they biject with the vectors. Given Theorem
2.4
, it must necessarily be circulant.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

Suppose
G=H^{*}
for some graph
H
. If
G
is regular and each
v_{i}
occurs exactly
k
times for some positive integer
k
, then
\frac{\lambda_{3}(G)}{|V(G)|}\leq\frac{1}{3}
.

Proof.

Leonida and Li
[
9
]
proved the result for abelian Cayley graphs, and circulant graphs are Cayley graphs over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Note that the closed blow-up of a circulant graph is still circulant, hence the result follows from Theorem
2.8
.
∎

2.1
Examples of invariant families

In alignment with the intuition that decreasing
\overline{\lambda_{n-1}}+\overline{\lambda_{n}}
ought to correspond to increasing
\lambda_{3}
, we prove that many families of graphs with large third eigenvalue proportion are invariant under the joint operation of
G\to\overline{G}\to\overline{G}^{*}\to\overline{\overline{G}^{*}}
. Alternatively,
\overline{G}
with significantly negative
\lambda_{n-1}
proportion is invariant under
∗
.

Following Leonida and Li
[
9
]
, define
H_{a,b}
to be the closed vertex multiplication of
C_{6}
by
[a,b,a,b,a,b]
and
Pi_{n}
the pivalous graph on
n
vertices. They proved that as
n\to\infty
(or
3(a+b)\to\infty
), we have
\frac{\lambda_{3}(H_{a,b})}{3(a+b)}\to\frac{1}{3}
and
\frac{\lambda_{3}(Pi_{n})}{n}\to\frac{1}{\pi}
.

:
Invariant families.

on the left,
\overline{Pi_{21}}
on the right.

Theorem 2.10
.

H_{a,b}
is invariant under
G\to\overline{G}\to\overline{G}^{*}\to\overline{\overline{G}^{*}}
.

Proof.

We claim that for
\omega=\exp(\pi i/3)
,

z=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3(a+b)}}(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{a},\underbrace{\omega,\dots,\omega}_{b},\underbrace{\omega^{2},\dots,\omega^{2}}_{a},\underbrace{\omega^{3},\dots,\omega^{3}}_{b},\underbrace{\omega^{4},\dots,\omega^{4}}_{a},\underbrace{\omega^{5},\dots,\omega^{5}}_{b})

is an eigenvector of
\overline{H_{a,b}}
of eigenvalue
-(a+b)
. Indeed, note that
\omega^{k}
is adjacent to
a\cdot\omega^{k+2}+b\cdot\omega^{k+3}+a\cdot\omega^{k+4}
or vice versa swapping
a,b
. But nonetheless we get
\omega^{k}\omega^{3}(a(\omega+\omega^{-1})+b)=-\omega^{k}(a+b)
, hence
z
is an eigenvector of eigenvalue
-(a+b)
.

Let the real and imaginary parts of
z
be
\xi,\eta
. We have
z=\xi+i\eta
, hence
z^{T}z=(|\xi|^{2}-|\eta|^{2})+2i\xi\cdot\eta
and
z^{T}\overline{z}=|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}
. Now
z^{T}z=\frac{2}{3(a+b)}(a(1+\omega^{4}+\omega^{8})+b(\omega^{2}+\omega^{6}+\omega^{10}))=0
, hence
|\xi|^{2}=|\eta|^{2}
and
\xi\cdot\eta=0
. Then
z^{T}\overline{z}=\frac{2}{3(a+b)}(a(1+1+1)+b(1+1+1))=2
. Thus,
\xi,\eta
are orthonormal, and must be the last two eigenvectors since
|\lambda_{n-2}|\leq\frac{n}{2\sqrt{3}}<\frac{n}{3}
.

We get that the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
vectors are the (scaled)
6
th roots of unity in order, clustered with multiplicities
[a,b,a,b,a,b]
, and they indeed already satisfy
i\sim j\iff\angle(v_{i},v_{j})>\pi/2
.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

Pi_{n}
is invariant under
G\to\overline{G}\to\overline{G}^{*}\to\overline{\overline{G}^{*}}
.

Proof.

Identically to above, the real and imaginary parts of
\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}(1,\omega,\dots,\omega^{n-1})
give the desired last two eigenvectors of
\overline{Pi_{n}}
where
\omega=\exp(2\pi i/n)
. The
\mathbb{R}^{2}
vectors are therefore the (scaled)
n
-th roots of unity in order and already satisfy
i\sim j\iff\angle(v_{i},v_{j})>\pi/2
.
∎

2.2
Generalisation to more eigenvalues

Although not immediately relevant to our proof of Theorem
1.2
, we quickly note a generalisation of Operation
∗
to more eigenvalues.

Definition 2.12
.

For positive integer
k
, define
Operation
∗k
on graph
G
of order
n\geq k
as follows. Let
G^{*k}
be any graph such that
V(G^{*k})=V(G)
and there exists an orthonormal set of eigenvectors
\{x_{n-k+1},x_{n-k+2},\dots,x_{n}\}
corresponding to eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{n-k+1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}
respectively of
G
such that in
G^{*k}
, we have
i\sim j\iff\sum_{r=1}^{k}(x_{n-r+1})_{i}(x_{n-r+1})_{j}<0
.

In particular,
G^{*}=G^{*2}
. We can prove identically to Theorem
2.2
and Corollary
2.3
the analogous results for
∗k
. Indeed, we still have

\displaystyle\lambda_{n-k+1}(G^{*k})+\dots+\lambda_{n}(G^{*k})

\displaystyle=\inf_{U^{T}U=I}Tr(U^{T}A(G^{*k})U)

\displaystyle=\inf_{u_{i}^{T}u_{j}=\delta_{ij}}\sum_{r=1}^{k}\langle u_{n-r+1},A(G^{*k})u_{n-r+1}\rangle,

and as before, we pick
G
with minimal number of edges.

Theorem 2.13
.

For any
G^{*k}
, we have

\sum_{r=1}^{k}\lambda_{n-r+1}(G^{*k})\leq\sum_{r=1}^{k}\lambda_{n-r+1}(G).

Corollary 2.14
.

For every
n\geq k
, there exists
G
of order
n
with minimal
\lambda_{n-k+1}+\dots+\lambda_{n}
such that
G=G^{*k}
.

Analogous to above, to each vertex associate a
\mathbb{R}^{k}
vector

v_{i}=((x_{n-k+1})_{i},(x_{n-k+2})_{i},\dots,(x_{n})_{i})^{T}.

The adjacency condition is again
i\sim j\iff v_{i}\cdot v_{j}<0
. We prove the generalisations of Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
.

Theorem 2.15
.

Suppose
G=H^{*k}
for some graph
H
. Then
\omega(G)\leq k+1
.

Proof.

Rankin proved that we cannot have
w_{1},\dots,w_{k+2}
such that
w_{i}\cdot w_{j}<0
for all pairs in Lemma 8 of
[
15
]
. Thus, there is no
K_{k+2}
sub-graph.
∎

Theorem 2.16
.

Suppose
G=H^{*k}
for some graph
H
. Then
\chi(G)\leq 2^{k}
.

Proof.

Again partition
\mathbb{R}^{k}
by sign of each component, then for
v_{i},v_{j}
in the same part, we have
(v_{i})_{r}(v_{j})_{r}\geq 0
for all
r=1,\dots,k
. Thus
v_{i}\cdot v_{j}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}(v_{i})_{r}(v_{j})_{r}\geq 0
, hence this forms an independent set. There are
2^{k}
such parts formed by a choice of two signs for each component.
∎

3
Imposing
n/2
-regularity

We first analyse
G
for which
G=G^{*}
and
G
is
n/2
-regular. This represents a hypothetical worst-case graph in the sense of Nikiforov’s bounds in
[
14
]
and in light of Corollary
2.3
. In this section we prove the following theorem, and in the next section, seek to formalise this intuition by extending it to the worst-case constraints.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\inf\left\{\frac{\lambda_{n-1}(G)+\lambda_{n}(G)}{n}:G=G^{*},G\textrm{ is $n/2$-regular}\right\}>-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}+\varepsilon.

We simplify the problem to that of a symmetric matrix with entries in
\{-1,1\}
with incredibly rich structure. A perfect elimination ordering is an ordering of the vertices such that each vertex forms a clique with its subsequent neighbours in the order.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
G=G^{*}
and
G
is
n/2
-regular. Then for any rotation of the labelling in Theorem
2.4
, we have

A(G)=\begin{pmatrix}Q&J_{n/2}-Q\\
J_{n/2}-Q&Q\end{pmatrix}

where the complement of
Q
is in a perfect elimination ordering.

Proof.

Given
k\leq n/2
, if vertex
k
has neighbours
\{j,j+1,\dots,n/2\}\subset\{1,2,\dots,n/2\}
, then its neighbours in
\{n/2+1,n/2+2,\dots,n\}
are necessarily
\{n/2+1,\dots,n/2+j-1\}
by Theorem
2.4
and the degree being
n/2
. Identically, if
k
has neighbours
\{1,2,\dots,j\}
for
j<k\leq n/2
, then its other neighbours must be
\{n/2+j+1,\dots,n\}
. Thus, if the top-left
\frac{n}{2}\times\frac{n}{2}
sub-matrix is
Q
, the top-right must be
J-Q
.
Q
is symmetric hence so is
J-Q
, and by
n/2
-regularity we can then fill out the rest of
A(G)
as shown.

By virtue of Theorem
2.4
, the complement of
Q
has a perfect elimination ordering.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose
G=G^{*}
and
G
is
n/2
-regular. Then for
Q
constructed as in Theorem
3.2
, the spectrum of
G
is the union of the spectra of
J
and
2Q-J
.

Proof.

We have

\det(A-\lambda I)=\det\begin{pmatrix}Q-\lambda I&J-Q\\
J-Q&Q-\lambda I\end{pmatrix}=\det((Q-\lambda I)+(J-Q))\det((Q-\lambda I)-(J-Q)),

which reduces to
\det(J-\lambda I)\det((2Q-J)-\lambda I)
.
∎

We now fix the choice of rotation for further control over
Q
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose
G=G^{*}
and
G
is
n/2
-regular. Then there exists a
canonical
rotation of the labelling such that
Q
is the adjacency matrix for a graph on
n/2
vertices which satisfies the following property. The vertices of
Q
can be partitioned into
front
, non-empty
middle
,
back
where there are only edges between the front and back, and the middle consists of isolated vertices.

Proof.

By regularity we have
\sum v_{i}=\sum\binom{x_{i}}{y_{i}}=0
since
x,y
are orthogonal to
j
. Noting that the lower half of
A(G)
is the complement of the upper half, for
i\leq n/2
we have
\lambda x_{i}=\sum_{j\sim i}x_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}-\sum_{j\not\sim i}x_{j}=0-\sum_{j\sim(i+n/2)}x_{j}=-\lambda x_{i+n/2}
, hence
x_{i}=-x_{i+n/2}
and identically
y_{i}=-y_{i+n/2}
, so
v_{i}=-v_{i+n/2}
.

Each vector has a corresponding reflection in the origin, which means that for every angle
\theta
, taking all vectors between
[\theta,\theta+\pi)
gives exactly
n/2
vectors. Note that if
v_{i}=v_{j}
, then they lie in the same neighbourhoods and non-neighbourhoods. Thus, supposing that the
n
vectors yield exactly
k
distinct vectors, there are exactly
k
possible sets of
n/2

Then the

In addition to restrictions on the structure of the graph, we also find order within the eigenvector components, which we split into two types.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose
G=G^{*}
and
G
is
n/2
-regular. Let

are of two different types:

1.

Type 1
: The front entries are increasing up to the middle and decreasing after, with all entries non-negative.

2.

Type 2
: The entries are always decreasing, and positive in the front, non-positive in the back.

Proof.

For
i\leq n/2
,

\lambda_{n-1}x_{i}=\sum_{j\sim i}x_{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\sim i\\
j\leq n/2\end{subarray}}x_{j}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\sim i\\
j>n/2\end{subarray}}x_{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\sim i\\
j\leq n/2\end{subarray}}x_{j}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\not\sim i\\
j\leq n/2\end{subarray}}x_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}(2Q-J)_{ij}x_{j}.

In particular, the top half of entries of
x,y
are still eigenvectors of
2Q-J
with corresponding eigenvalues.

Let
v_{k+1}
lie in the middle. We claim that
x
is Type 2 and
y
is Type 1. First we prove the signs. As all vectors have argument

we have all

non-negative. Suppose FTSOC there exists a back vector with

positive. Then we claim the neighbourhoods of
v_{i}
and
v_{k+1}
are the same, contradiction. Indeed, both are non-adjacent to
v_{1}
since they lie in the same quadrant of the Cartesian plane. But

is non-adjacent to
v_{n}
and
v_{i}
is anti-clockwise of

positive, where zero can’t occur since the

Now we consider the piecewise monotonicity. Consider going anti-clockwise from a vector
v_{i}
to the next vector
v_{i+1}
in the original space of
n

In particular, we get that
\lambda_{n-1}x_{i+1}-\lambda_{n-1}x_{i}=2mx_{j}

\lambda_{n}y_{i+1}-\lambda_{n}y_{i}=2my_{j}

∎

We make most of the progress through the following theorem. Although we already knew the presented bound, we determine the equality case and later prove that it cannot occur even as a limit.

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose
G=G^{*}
and
G
is
n/2
-regular. Let

Due to the length of the full proof, we introduce our notation (which we continue to adopt in the subsequent proof of Theorem

The stages proceed as follows.

1.

In Stage 1, we set up the algebra, with elements inspired by Csikvári
[
5

. In particular, we consider the algebraic formulation of the statement and extract equations relating the front, middle and back to the eigenvalues.

2.

In Stage 2, we use these equations alongside Corollary
3.5
and Theorem

to form inequalities that restrict the feasible region of the eigenvalues, which will depend on a set of parameters and two variables.

3.

In Stage 3, to analyse the minimum values of the eigenvalues, we consider the boundaries of the feasible regions, which correspond to cases of equality in the inequalities.

4.

In Stage 4, we bound the minimum of the feasible region when it occurs at an intersection of two boundaries. Furthermore, we find that in order to obtain

5.

In Stage 5, we identify all the families of Type 1 feasible regions that can occur as we vary a given parameter, which motivates the casework in Stage 4. We also consider a Type 2 feasible region to aid the casework in the final stage.

6.

In the final stage, we use the above framework to deal with each case in order.

Consider an intersection of an equation of the form

5
Concluding remarks

Having established a strengthened upper bound on
c_{3}
and in light of Theorem
A.1
, this makes it more plausible that we indeed have
c_{3}=\frac{1}{3}
. In fact, we have the following conjecture as a strengthened version of the original, which we’ve verified for all graphs of order at most 9. Though there are possible variations, we believe this is the most natural formulation.

Conjecture 5.1
.

Given a graph
G
of order
n
, we have
\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}\geq-\frac{2n}{3}
.

We were not able to make use of the additional structure in Section
2
for a general
G=G^{*}
. Our attempts revolved around reducing to the pivalous graph, with intuition from Theorem
2.8
. Yet, for example, it is not true that replacing two copies of the same row with one copy always decreases
\frac{\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}}{n}
, nor that
Pi_{k}^{[t]}
is minimal over all
G=G^{*}
of order
kt
and
k
distinct rows.

Our methods in Section
3
leave room for improvement in Theorem
3.1
with the lack of interaction between Type 1 and Type 2 bounds, leading to the
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
situation when realistically only one equality can hold at once. However, this cannot then be adapted to improve Theorem
1.1
by virtue of
n/2
-regularity up to
o(n)
being forced by the algebraically worst case scenario. Furthermore, this also means that our proof doesn’t provide any actual bounds on
c_{3}
and instead eliminates the
c_{3}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}
case.

In addition, we comment on alternative proof directions for Theorem
3.7
and outline possible sketches, with analytic ideas. Firstly, it seems plausible that one can reduce the structure above to ensure feasibility but leave just enough for only one structure to be permissible. Then the sequence of worst-case graphs converges in cut metric, and as demonstrated by Borgs et al.
[
2
]
, the spectrum of the limit graphon is
\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}},0,0,\dots
, but must have trace
\leq 1
, contradiction. However, we found that demonstrating the structure cannot exist is simpler than fixing it down.

Alternatively, having deduced the structure of
Q
in Theorem
3.2
, one may also be able to adapt analytic techniques to provide intuition for the block construction yielding minimal
\lambda_{n-1}+\lambda_{n}
. This has been explored by Terpai
[
16
]
, Breen, Riasanovsky, Tait, Urschel
[
3
]
, Liu
[
11
]
and more recently by Kumar, Liu, Monterde, Pragada and Tait
[
8
]
.

Indeed, both operations of stretching and averaging components in an interval fix Type 1 and Type 2 properties. Then we can run the same arguments with instead
x_{0}\in(0,X+Y)
and an interval neighbourhood
R=(x_{1},x_{2})
around
x_{0}
where the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
average of eigenfunction
f
on
R
is
f(x_{0})
. Letting
\tilde{f}
be
f
except
\tilde{f}(x)=f(x_{0})
on
R
, we can similarly prove that the change in the integral is
\tilde{I}-I=-(l(R)f(x_{0})-\int_{R}f(x)dx)^{2}\leq 0
. Since both Type 1 and Type 2 are monotone on
(0,X+Y)
, we have that
W
must be a step with at most two steps on
(0,X+Y)
, and identically for
(X,1)
.

The primary roadblock here is the lack of orthogonality. In fact, we have that if
f_{1}(x_{0})
and
f_{2}(x_{0})
are both
\mathcal{L}^{2}
averages on
R
, then by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle f_{1}(x_{0})f_{2}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{l(R)}\int_{R}f_{1}(x)^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{l(R)}\int_{R}f_{2}(x)^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{l(R)}\int_{R}f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\int_{R}\tilde{f_{1}}(x)\tilde{f_{2}}(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{R}f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x)dx

with equality iff
f_{1},f_{2}
were linearly dependent on
R
. Yet this cannot happen in the front where Type 1 and Type 2 are oppositely oriented.

Finally, we haven’t yet explored into the generalised operation in Subsection
2.2
, but we hope it inspires some further research into the unveiled structure. Indeed, the strikingly different growth rates of the naive bounds for
\omega
and
\chi
may be of interest.

Note that with
k\geq 3
dimensions, there is no natural selection of the vectors ‘in order’, hence we envisage it will be difficult to pursue the same approach without the ability to restrict eigenvectors to controlled Types.

Theorem A.1
.

Suppose
G
is the closed vertex multiplication of
C_{6}
by
[a,b,c,a,b,c]
. Then
\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}\leq\frac{2n}{3}-2
, with equality iff
a=b=c
.

Proof.

Add a self-loop at each vertex and take the divisor matrix for the equitable partition.

\begin{pmatrix}a&b&0&0&0&c\\
a&b&c&0&0&0\\
0&b&c&a&0&0\\
0&0&c&a&b&0\\
0&0&0&a&b&c\\
a&0&0&0&b&c\end{pmatrix}.

This has characteristic polynomial
x^{2}(x-(a+b+c))P(x)
where

P(x)=x^{3}-(a+b+c)x^{2}+4abc.

Thus, the eigenvalues of
G
are 0,
a+b+c
which corresponds to the regularity, and the roots of
P(x)=0
. Normalising
a+b+c=\frac{1}{2}
, we get
x^{3}-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+4\alpha
where
0\leq\alpha\leq\frac{1}{216}
due to AM-GM. Note that
P(1/3)=-\frac{1}{54}+4\alpha\leq 0
, hence it either lies between the first two roots or before the last root. Yet
P(0)=4\alpha\geq 0
hence the last root is non-positive.

If
\frac{1}{3}+t
is a root for
P
with
0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{3}
, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{27}+\frac{1}{3}t+t^{2}+t^{3}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{9}+\frac{2}{3}t+t^{2}\right)-4\alpha

\displaystyle\Rightarrow-\frac{2}{3}t-2t^{3}+\frac{2}{3}t

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{27}-\frac{1}{3}t+t^{2}-t^{3}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{9}-\frac{2}{3}t+t^{2}\right)-4\alpha

\displaystyle\Rightarrow P\left(\frac{1}{3}-t\right)

\displaystyle=-2t^{3}\leq 0.

Thus,
\frac{1}{3}-t
lies between the first two roots. In particular,
\frac{1}{3}+t
must be the first root, and the sum of the first two roots is at most
\frac{2}{3}
, with equality iff
t=0
iff
\frac{1}{3}
is a root. This happens at
abc=\frac{1}{216}
and hence
a=b=c=\frac{1}{6}
as the equality case for AM-GM.

Since we obtained the eigenvalues of
A(G)+I
from
P
, we have
(\lambda_{2}+1)+(\lambda_{3}+1)\leq\frac{2n}{3}
with equality iff
a=b=c
.
∎
