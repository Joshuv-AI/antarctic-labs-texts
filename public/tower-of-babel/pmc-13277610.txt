Characterizing proprioceptive dysfunction as an early sensory indicator of mild Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy
Authors: Karlen Ka Pui Law, Kenney Ki Lee Lau, Graham Ka Hon Shea, Kenneth Man Chee Cheung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Over 90% degenerative cervical myelopathy individuals experience dorsal column damage.•Proprioception deficit was the most common in early degenerative cervical myelopathy.•Sensory loss progresses: dorsal column first, antero-lateral column later.•Proprioception deficit strongly correlated with the severity of myelopathy.•Lower limbs are the most commonly affected in early degenerative cervical myelopathy.Keywords: Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy, Somatosensory deficits, Dorsal column, Proprioceptive deficit, Early indicator, Diagnosis

Introduction

Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is an age-related degenerative condition characterized by spinal cord compression, typically resulting from bony osteophytes, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, intervertebral disc protrusion, or ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament [1]. The diagnosis of DCM is challenging due to its highly variable symptomatology, often leading to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis [2].

To date, the diagnosis of DCM is frequently delayed by more than a year, resulting in substantial neurological impairment, despite patients typically seeking medical consultation within 3.3 months of initial symptom onset [3,4]. Early identification of DCM is critical, as timely surgical intervention can halt neurological deterioration and preserve functional independence [5]. Current clinical practice relies heavily on radiological evidence of spinal cord compression, particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) [6]. However, these imaging modalities may be limited in outpatient settings due to restricted availability, high cost, and prolonged waiting times [7,8].

Hand clumsiness, gait disturbance, and impaired balance are commonly observed in individuals with DCM [9]. Motor performance depends on the integration of somatosensory and motor functions, with tactile sensation and proprioception playing critical roles in this coordination [10,11]. Accordingly, our research-based performance assessment tool, the Hong Kong Myelopathy Criteria (HKMC), quantifies functional deficits in balance and upper- and lower-limb coordination associated with severe radiological stenosis on MRI [12]. However, further validation is required before it can be adopted as a diagnostic criterion. In the context of global population aging and the need to prevent severe disability, there is a pressing need to develop accurate diagnostic tools that facilitate the timely identification of DCM in clinical settings [13,14].

Somatosensory function is a fundamental component of accurate perception and motor coordination. It is recognized as a key domain in various patient-reported outcome measures, including the modified Japanese Orthopedic Association score (mJOA), Cervical Myelopathy Severity Index (CMSI), and Japanese Orthopedic Association Cervical Myelopathy Evaluation Questionnaire (JOACMEQ) [[15], [16], [17]]. Sensory information is transmitted through the spinal cord, and the integrity of ascending pathways—the dorsal column, anterior spinothalamic tract, and lateral spinothalamic tract—is essential for safe interaction with the environment, which is critical for preserving independence and quality of life [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Disruption of these pathways due to cervical spinal cord compression may lead to neurological deficits, including gait disturbance, hand numbness, and clumsiness, progressively impairing an individual’s ability to perform daily activities [22]. Consequently, preserving somatosensory function is essential not only for neurological health but also for maintaining autonomy and functional capacity in individuals with cervical spinal pathology [23,24].

Somatosensory deficits often precede overt motor pathway abnormalities; however, early motor signs are typically subtle and nonspecific, contributing to delayed diagnosis [25]. Previous studies suggested that up to 62% of patients with DCM experience progressive neurological deterioration in both sensory and motor functions beyond that expected with normal ageing [[26], [27], [28]]. Longer delays before surgical treatment are associated with greater disability and may result in permanent neurological and functional deficits [29,30]. To facilitate the early identification of patients with DCM who present with subtle or nonspecific symptoms, or who are asymptomatic, this study aimed to investigate somatosensory deficits in the early stages of the disease.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the University of Hong Kong and the Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (UW 20-746). It was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05183971). All subjects were recruited voluntarily from 2 tertiary referral centers from March 2022 to November 2025. Written consent was obtained from participants before the assessment.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Chinese individuals aged ≥45 years, irrespective of sex, were recruited for this study. Patients with DCM were diagnosed by experienced orthopedic surgeons based on typical symptomatology (i.e., bilateral upper limb numbness, clumsiness, and gait disturbance), supported by physical examination findings (i.e., brisk reflexes, a positive Romberg test, and myelopathic hand signs, including Hoffmann’s sign, Inverted Supinator Reflex, and Finger Escape Test. Cervical spinal cord compression was confirmed with axial T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as the current gold standard of diagnosis of DCM.

All participants were independent ambulators with a Frankel grade of D or higher, a Nurick grade of ≤3, and a score above the 16th percentile on the Hong Kong–Montreal Cognitive Assessment (HK-MoCA) [31]. Individuals with extrapyramidal symptoms, vestibular deficits, or active thoracic or lumbar stenosis were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included congenital or acquired joint deformities, gigantism or dwarfism, impaired vision or hearing, and other neurological disorders that could affect somatosensory function.

Assessment protocol

The Modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association Scoring System for Cervical Myelopathy (mJOA)

This grading scale is widely used to evaluate the severity of cervical myelopathy. It assesses 5 domains: upper-limb function, deltoid or biceps muscle strength, lower-limb function, sensory disturbance, and bladder function (Fig. 1). Scores range from 0 to 17, with higher scores indicating milder impairment. Severity is classified as mild for scores ≥15, moderate for scores 12 to <15, and severe for scores <12 [32].

The modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association Scoring System for Cervical Myelopathy (mJOA)

Somatosensory assessment

Somatosensory function was assessed while participants were blindfolded.

Superficial pain was assessed by measuring the mechanical pain threshold of the skin using the Neuropen (Owen Mumford Ltd., Oxford, UK), a device sensitive for evaluating nerve function (sensitivity 91.8%) [33]. It is equipped with a Neurotip™ for assessing small-fiber sensory perception related to superficial pain (Fig. 2A). Failure to detect the stimulus from the Neurotip™ suggests impaired superficial pain sensation [34].

Somatosensory Assessment Protocol for Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy. (A) Superficial Pain (B) Thermal Sensation (C) Discriminative Touch (D) Vibration (E) Proprioception

Thermal sense was evaluated using a hot-and-cold discriminator kit, with temperatures set at 40–45°C for warm and 5–10°C for cold stimuli (Fig. 2B). Thermal thresholds were assessed separately at proximal and distal sites of the limbs and along dermatomal regions of the trunk. Inability to distinguish between warm and cold stimuli indicated impaired thermal sensation [35].

Discriminative touch was assessed using the validated Semmes–Weinstein Monofilament Test. Individual filaments, ranging from 1.65 to 6.65 on the perceived intensity scale, were applied perpendicularly to the pulp of the fingers and toes for 1 second (Fig. 2C). Sensibility was defined by the lightest detectable filament, with scores ranging from 0 to 5. A score of 5 indicated normal sensation, whereas a score of 0 indicated loss of protective sensation [36]. Discriminative touch was considered impaired if the patient scored less than 5 on a filament size greater than 2.83 [37].

Vibratory sense was evaluated using the Rydel–Seiffer tuning fork (RSTF), a validated quantitative clinical tool for assessing vibratory perception [38]. The RSTF consists of a 128-Hz tuning fork with add-on clamps on each prong; the dampers have a pair of black-and-white arrows on the front surface, with a number scale ranging from 2 to 8 (Fig. 2D) [39]. The dorsum of the interphalangeal joints of the index finger and the great toe was tested bilaterally. The vibratory perception threshold was defined as the point at which perception ceased, with higher values indicating greater sensitivity. Values below 5.5 at the index finger or below 3.5 at the hallux were considered indicative of impaired vibratory perception [38,40].

Proprioceptive sense was assessed using the Distal Proprioceptive Test (DPT), which evaluates joint position sense by detecting movement at the distal interphalangeal joints of each finger and toe (Fig. 2E) [41]. The examiner passively moved each joint slowly while gently stabilizing the adjacent proximal joint at the same anchor points throughout the test, thereby avoiding proximal tactile cues. Inability to perceive the direction of movement indicated a proprioceptive deficit (PD) [42].

Data analysis

Participant demographics were analyzed using the chi-squared test, Fisher’s exact test, independent-samples t-test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The association between mJOA score and the presence of somatosensory deficits was then analyzed by modality and site of involvement (upper and lower limbs) according to DCM severity. The prevalence of somatosensory deficits across disease severity, using a mJOA cut-off of 15 to define mild DCM, was assessed using the chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test, whereas Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the strength of association, expressed as the point-biserial correlation coefficient (rpb). The point of maximal manifestation along the mJOA scale was predicted using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and the area under the curve (AUC), with values greater than 0.5 indicating discriminatory ability. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 30.0, with statistical significance set at p<.05.

Results

This study included 436 individuals with DCM, comprising 221 males and 215 females, with a mean age of 63.7±10.0 years. Participants were classified into 3 groups based on mJOA score: 165 with mild DCM (mJOA 15–17), including 83 females; 149 with moderate DCM (mJOA 12 to <15), including 63 females; and 122 with severe DCM (mJOA <12), including 49 females. The mean mJOA score was 14.7±2.5. Of these, 87 participants had no noticeable symptoms or functional deficits, scored full marks on the mJOA, and were considered asymptomatic DCM patients. More than 80% of the symptomatic DCM group presented with hand numbness (n=287), followed by gait disturbance and hand clumsiness. Combined upper- and lower-limb incoordination was observed in 20% of the symptomatic group (n=69) (Table 1).

Among the cohort, 37% had no somatosensory deficits, whereas 275 individuals with DCM, including 27 asymptomatic and 248 symptomatic cases, showed impairment in 1 or more sensory modalities. Dorsal column dysfunction was the most prevalent finding, affecting more than 90% of DCM subjects with somatosensory deficits, including 225 symptomatic and 12 asymptomatic individuals. Of these, 148 subjects had isolated PD, 9 had isolated vibratory deficits (Fig. 3A), and 118 had combined somatosensory deficits, which mainly involved the dorsal column (69%). In contrast, deficits in superficial pain and thermal sensation, which reflect involvement of the anterolateral pathway, were rare, occurring in only 6% of subjects with combined deficits. PD predominantly affected the lower limbs (80%), whereas 13% had PD in both the upper and lower limbs, and 7% had PD only in the upper limbs (Fig. 3B).

Presentation and location of somatosensory deficits in DCM. (A) Presentation of Somatosensory Deficits in DCM (B) Location of Proprioceptive Deficit (PD) in DCM

Somatosensory dysfunction in the dorsal column, including proprioception, vibration, and discriminative touch, was the most commonly affected sensory modality across all stages of DCM severity, affecting 50% of patients with mild DCM, 88% with moderate DCM, and 97% with severe DCM, as shown in Fig. 4. In contrast, superficial pain and thermal sensation, which reflect anterior and lateral column function, respectively, became more prominent and approached the frequency of dorsal column deficits only at later stages of the disease. Isolated anterior or lateral column deficits were rare and were typically accompanied by concomitant dorsal column involvement (Fig. 4).

ANOVA showed a significant difference in the prevalence of somatosensory deficits across DCM severity subgroups. A significant association with disease severity was observed only for proprioceptive and vibratory deficits, with chi-square values of 39.3 (p=.002, OR=7.0) and 10.9 (p=.003, OR=4.8), respectively. PD showed the strongest correlation with mJOA score (rpb=−0.51, p<.001), whereas thermal deficits showed the weakest correlation (rpb=−0.28, p<.001) (Table 2).

Somatosensory deficits did not differ by sex or age (p=.18 and p=.09, respectively). Among individuals with mild DCM (mJOA 15–17), those with somatosensory deficits had a significantly higher risk of functional impairment, defined as an mJOA score below 17 (OR=6.5, 95% CI 3.4–12.6). Lower-limb involvement was predominant (OR=3.7, 95% CI 1.8–7.7, p<.001). In the mildest DCM subgroup (mJOA 16–17; n=84), PD was common among symptomatic patients. Twenty-three patients had isolated PD, predominantly in the lower limbs (n=18), and 8 had isolated vibratory deficits, also predominantly in the lower limbs. The remaining patients had no somatosensory deficits (Fig. 5).

Prevalence of somatosensory deficits in mild DCM with mJOA 16–17.

The presence of PD was evidenced at a higher mean mJOA score of 14.7±1.4. Discriminative touch and vibratory deficits occurred at lower mean mJOA scores of 12.9±1.0, whereas superficial pain and thermal deficits were observed at even lower scores of 11.0±1.6 and 10.5±1.8, respectively (Fig. 6). These findings support the higher likelihood of PD (OR=7.0, p=.002) and vibratory deficits (OR=4.8, p=.003) at better functional status during DCM progression, with PD appearing at a milder disease stage (Table 2).

The prevalence of somatosensory deficits in relation to disease progression.

The occurrence of PD was associated with DCM severity; ROC analysis identified an mJOA cutoff of 15.3 (AUC=0.84, sensitivity=0.83, specificity=0.69). Vibrational deficits emerged at a lower mJOA cutoff of 14.3 (AUC=0.72), followed by discriminative touch deficits at 12.8 (AUC=0.70). Superficial pain and thermal impairments appeared later, at mJOA scores of 11.3 and 10.8, respectively. These findings suggested that dorsal column-related somatosensory deficits occur earlier than those involving the anterolateral column (Table 3).

Discussion

This study confirmed that PD was the most prevalent clinical symptom in individuals with mild and moderate DCM, even among those who were asymptomatic, having a mJOA score at full marks without any sign of motor incoordination. Additionally, vibration sense emerged as the second most commonly affected somatosensory function. Both PD and vibratory deficits showed a significant association with disease progression, as indicated by the worsening of mJOA.

A solitary deficit in PD was indicative of a milder form of DCM and was generally associated with additional somatosensory deficits in more severely affected individuals. Furthermore, 45% of the subgroup with severe DCM demonstrated a combination of somatosensory deficits, which likely resulted from extensive circumferential compression of the spinal cord tracts [25,43].

Vibration sense and discriminative touch deficits were observed only in individuals with moderate-to-severe DCM who also had PD. Conversely, superficial pain and thermal deficits appeared only in severe cases. Notably, deficits in superficial pain, thermal, and discriminative touch rarely occurred alone across all DCM severity levels.

Previous studies indicate that degenerative changes in DCM primarily occur in the anteroposterior direction, thereby impairing neural tissue perfusion in the spinal cord. As a result, the dorsal column experiences ischemic neuronal changes that originate from axonal demyelination and degeneration [44,45]. This could account for the early posterior column sign, which includes incoordination and impairments in proprioception, vibration, and discriminative touch sensation [46,47].

Furthermore, the small fibers in the dorsal column were more susceptible to mechanical compression compared to the combination of small and larger fibers in the lateral and anterior columns [48,49]. Our findings provide new insights into the clinical signs of DCM and highlight the value of proprioceptive and vibration testing for early detection of the often “nonspecific and subtle” symptomatic DCM in outpatient care [1,50].

The early signs of PD in individuals with mild DCM were evident in a cohort with an average mJOA score of 14.7, surpassing other somatosensory symptoms. This indicated early dorsal column disturbances and questioned the common view that the anterolateral column is more susceptible to cervical cord compression [51].

Until now, the natural timeline of somatosensory deterioration in DCM has been unclear [52]. Our findings indicate that disruption of somatosensory function is more evident in the dorsal column during the early, mild stage of the disease, while other tracts become involved only in moderate-to-severe cases. It's important to note that the presence of multimodal deficits (such as dorsal column and anterior/lateral column involvement) likely indicates that patients have already reached a critical point for considering surgery, as evidenced by a reduced mJOA score [52].

Lower-limb symptoms have long been considered an early indicator of DCM. Munro et al. (2023) identified ``heavy legs” as the only significant symptom linked to early DCM diagnosis [53]. Furthermore, Makino et al. (2023) showed that in the early stage of compressive cervical myelopathy, disruptions in lower limb proprioception lead to reductions in stride length, swing phase, and gait speed [54].

In our study, PD primarily affected the lower limbs, occurring in 80% of the cohort, while 13% exhibited involvement of both the upper and lower limbs. A small fraction (7%) had deficits limited to the upper limbs. Notably, lower-limb PD was observed in 80% of individuals with the mildest DCM, with mJOA scores between 16 and 17. Furthermore, over 30% of asymptomatic DCM patients also showed signs of lower limb PD.

The initial somatosensory deficit aligned with Chen et al. (2022), who reported that the neurodegenerative distance affecting the Corticospinal Tract—essential for motor control—was considerably longer than within the somatosensory pathway of the Dorsal Column [55]. Additionally, larger axonal fibers in the lateral part of the spinal cord, where the Corticospinal tract is situated, showed greater resistance to long-term mechanical compression [48,49].

These findings may clarify how sensory disturbances occur before gait abnormalities initially develop. The results are consistent with previous research indicating that PD could be an early sign or a clinical diagnostic criterion for DCM [2,14]. Clinicians should consider adding proprioceptive testing to standard assessments for individuals suspected of DCM. To build stronger evidence, more longitudinal studies are necessary to confirm early somatosensory deficits in asymptomatic people and to explore whether PD can predict clinical outcomes. These efforts are vital for incorporating routine proprioceptive evaluation into the diagnosis and treatment strategies for DCM.

Conclusion

This study is the first comprehensive investigation to confirm somatosensory deficits in mild and even asymptomatic DCM using reliable sensory testing. The results revealed a new pattern of somatosensory involvement in DCM, progressing from isolated PD to combined deficits as disease severity increases. These findings suggest that dorsal column dysfunction may evolve from isolated PD to more complex somatosensory impairments.

Importantly, PD was the most prevalent somatosensory symptom across all levels of DCM severity and can be easily evaluated using the Distal Proprioceptive Test, a straightforward clinical tool that could be valuable in regular outpatient assessments. These findings highlight the significance of proprioceptive testing, especially of the lower limbs, in patients with suspected DCM to facilitate early detection and prevent delays in diagnosis and treatment.
