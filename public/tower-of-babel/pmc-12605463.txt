A New Variant in CTLA4 Highlights the Heterogeneous Phenotype of CTLA4 Haploinsufficiency
Authors: Jonathan Sormani, Alexandre Belot, Raphaele Nove-Josserand, Capucine Picard, Jérémie Rosain, Marine Villard, Sebastien Viel, Marie Ouachee-Chardin, Emma Mercier, Catherine Giannoli, Philippe Moskovtchenko, Maud Rabeyrin, Brigitte Balme, Isabelle Durieu, Anne-Laure Mathieu, Quitterie Reynaud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHaploinsufficiency of cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA4), a known cause of inborn errors of immunity, can lead to autoimmunity, inflammation, neoplasia and infections. A previously undescribed CTLA4 variant was identified in a patient who presented with life-threatening cutaneous infection caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, severe VZV infection, and Evans syndrome. Our aim was to assess the pathogenicity of the previously undescribed c.379T > G variant in the CTLA4 gene and explore its phenotypic presentation in relatives. We employed genetic and protein-based in silico analyses to evaluate the potential role of the c.379T > G variant in the CTLA4 gene. We subsequently studied CTLA4 expression ex vivo, analyzed the clinical presentation of affected carriers and compared the biological status across affected individuals, healthy carriers and noncarriers. In silico analyses revealed that the c.379T > G mutation, which is located within the ligand binding area of CTLA4, is highly pathogenic. Its clinical manifestations, which are sometimes fatal, include multiple infections, autoimmunity, inflammation of the central nervous system, lymphoproliferation and thymoma with Good’s syndrome. Compared with its expression in healthy donors, the expression of CTLA4 following stimulation in memory regulatory T cells was decreased in affected individuals. Immunophenotyping revealed an increased proportion of immature and double-negative B cells in affected patients compared with their nonmutated relatives. Additionally, a reduction in naive T cells and an increase in CD4 + CD25-Foxp3 + cells were observed in carriers compared with controls. The c.379T > G variant of CTLA4, resulting in a p.Tyr127Asp substitution, is pathogenic and contributes to the development of a CTLA4 haploinsufficiency phenotype. This finding highlights the heterogeneous presentations of the disease, including oncological and neurological manifestations.Keywords: Cytotoxic t-lymphocyte antigen 4, Abatacept, Autoimmunity, Common variable immunodeficiency, Hypogammaglobulinemia, Immune dysregulation, Primary immunodeficiency, Genetics, Malignancy

Introduction

Inborn errors of immunity include a wide range of diseases characterized by an impaired immune system due to genetic abnormalities [1]. Among them, haploinsufficiency of cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA4), first described in 2014, can lead to autoimmunity, autoinflammation, neurological involvement and neoplasia [2–4]. CTLA4 is a crucial regulatory immune checkpoint protein that competes with the costimulatory molecule CD28. Thus, the permanent activation of T cells is prevented by the transendocytosis of CD80 and CD86 from the surface of antigen-presenting cells after their interaction with CTLA4. This molecule is also constitutively expressed in regulatory T cells (Tregs) and participates in their suppressive functions [2]. To date, more than 60 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), indels and copy number variants have been described as pathogenic in CTLA4, and knowledge of the clinical presentations and outcomes of these variants has progressively improved thanks to large clinical cohort studies [5–7]. Herein, we present a new key mutation and highlight the heterogeneous and not yet described clinical presentations of CTLA4 haploinsufficiency.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or a first-degree relative and/or nominated person. This study was conducted in accordance with French legislation, Good Clinical Practice, and the Declaration of Helsinki.

PCR Sequencing and DNA/Proteomic Analysis

DNA was extracted from whole blood samples from all patients, except for one deceased patient (II.1), in whom DNA was extracted from a frozen skin biopsy. PCR sequencing was performed on the genomic DNA of the patients. PCR sequencing was performed using the Twist HID panel library of the Study Center of Immune Deficiency (CEDI) or using CTLA4 mutation-specific primers (forward, GCTTTGTGTGTGAGTATG; reverse, CTGTGAGGAGAAAGCTATAA), and sequencing was performed using the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform.

Variant pathogeny was assessed using the in silico programs CADD (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu), Polyphen-2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), and AlphaMissense (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) [8–10]. The population allele frequency was obtained from the gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org) database, and a description of previous mutations was established using HGMD and ClinVar [11–13]. The variant was confirmed using Sanger sequencing and was studied in a relative when DNA was available. The variant was annotated on the CTLA4 NM_005214.4 transcript.

CTLA4 Expression Analysis

Healthy donors were obtained from the French blood establishment (EFS) facility. Thawed PBMCs isolated from whole blood collected in EDTA were stimulated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 beads (Invitrogen) at a ratio of 0.8 beads to 1 PBMC for 16 h.

For surface staining, the cells were incubated with CD7 BV510 (clone M-T701; Becton Dickinson), CD4 PE-CF594 (clone RPA-T4; Becton Dickinson), and CD45RA APC-Vio 770 (clone REA1047; Miltenyi) at room temperature for 20 min. The cells were fixed and permeabilized with Foxp3 staining buffer (eBioscience) and incubated with Foxp3 APC (clone PCH101; eBioscience) and CTLA4 PE (clone 14D3; eBioscience) for 30 min at 4 °C. The cells were acquired on a BD LSRII cytometer, and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software (TreeStar).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are reported as the medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for nonparametric distributions. To compare continuous data, the nonparametric Wilcoxon test was employed. For the multiple test analysis, the Kruskal‒Wallis rank sum test was used. When the results were significant, post hoc analyses were performed using the pairwise Wilcoxon test. Categorical variables are reported as absolute numbers and were compared using Fisher’s exact test. For all analyses conducted, p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical corrections were assumed using the false discovery rate correction for multiple testing and were performed for all analyses except those described in Table 2. All analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org).

Results

Index Case

An 18-year-old female patient was referred to our internal medicine department following severe necrotic dermohypodermitis of the left leg caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The severity of the lesion required intensive care unit hospitalization and invasive surgery. Notably, she had been diagnosed with immune thrombopenia (ITP) at 14 years of age, followed by autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), together presenting as Evans syndrome. In terms of infection, she experienced early-onset herpes zoster and chronic Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) viremia. A genetic assessment for inborn errors of immunity was performed due to the presence of atypical manifestations, including hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy and hypogammaglobulinemia. A previously unreported c.379T >G variant in CTLA4 was thereby identified by targeted high-throughput sequencing. These findings motivated the initiation of abatacept, a recombinant CTLA4 protein developed to treat rheumatoid or psoriatic arthritis that has also been used as a treatment for CTLA4 haploinsufficiency [14]. Treatment prevented relapses or new manifestations of the disease for more than 4 years.

Interestingly, both deceased individuals presented previously undescribed and/or rare manifestations of CTLA4 haploinsufficiency.

Patient II.1 had a past history of unilateral uveitis. At the age of 60 years, the patient was diagnosed with thymic carcinoma, which was discovered following the onset of rapid and severe erythroderma (Fig. 2.A and B) and alopecia. These clinical features, combined with hypogammaglobulinemia, were consistent with a diagnosis of Good’s syndrome [15]. However, the patient presented with rapid progression of pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 2.C and D), leading to intubation, ventilation difficulties and ultimately death.

Patient III.3 had a medical history starting at the age of 7 years, including multiple relapses of AIHA, severe chickenpox and viral meningoencephalitis. He subsequently developed hypogammaglobulinemia and kidney failure, leading to renal transplantation. Kidney histopathological analysis revealed interstitial fibrosis and benign, nonclonal lymphoproliferation with EBV replication. Later, the patient experienced graft loss due to recurrent lymphocytic infiltration of the kidney. His clinical history was further complicated by the onset of flaccid paraparesis in the lower limb. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed two inflammatory lesions in the spinal cord, which were consistent with transverse myelitis (Fig. 2.E). At the age of 30, he developed acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), which was diagnosed after facial paralysis (Fig. 2.F and G). His condition rapidly improved after the intensification of immunosuppressive therapy. However, the patient died one year later due to several relapses of severe Clostridum difficile colitis, leading to malnutrition and multiple organ failure.

In Silico Analyses and Familial Genetic Segregation

The c.379T >G variant is absent from public population databases such as gnomAD v4.1 and occurs in the ligand-binding exon of the gene (Fig. 1.B). The combined annotation-dependent depletion score was high, and an evaluation of the impact of the variant at the protein level using Polyphen-2 and AlphaMissense predicted pathogenicity (Fig. 1.B). The variant induces a p.Tyr127Asp substitution in a highly conserved region of the CTLA4 protein across species, which is also present in its paralog protein CD28 and in abatacept or ipilimumab. Conversely, ipilimumab is an anti-CTLA4 antibody and an immune checkpoint inhibitor widely used as an antineoplastic drug [16] (Fig. 1.C).

The pedigrees of the family members were assessed together with familial segregation of the variants. The genetic status of the two deceased patients was evaluated using previously extracted gDNA for human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genotyping before performing the renal graft of relative III.3 and from a skin biopsy obtained during the last hospitalization of patient II.1 (Fig. 1.A).

Familial segregation revealed autosomal dominant inheritance with an incomplete penetrance of 58.3% (7 affected carriers out of 12 carriers without including the index case).

Functional Assessment of CTLA4 Expression

To assess the pathogenic role of the CTLA4 c.379T >G variant at the cellular level, we collected whole blood samples from two relatives, III.7 and II.3. As previously reported by Hou et al., we measured the expression of CTLA4 in memory Tregs (mTreg) and in conventional T cells (nTconv) before (Fig. 3.A) and after (Fig. 3.B) stimulation [17]. Stimulation did not alter the ratio of CTLA4 expression between the mTreg and nTconv, in contrast to findings in the patients. These findings support the conclusion that partial loss of CTLA4 expression occurs in individuals with the c.379T >G mutation. Compared with that in healthy donors, Foxp3 + expression in CTLA4-mutated samples was unchanged (Fig. 3. C), and the results were consistent with the patient immunophenotyping results.

A Different Immune Phenotype

The biological status of symptomatic and asymptomatic carriers as well as that of noncarriers is summarized in Table 2. No significant differences in inflammatory markers, immunoglobulins, complement levels, or standard blood counts were detected between the groups. Immunophenotyping revealed differences among the three subgroups concerning immature CD19 + CD21- B cells (p value = 0.023), naive CD4 + CD45RA + T cells (p value = 0.047) and double-negative CD19 + CD27-IgD- B cells (p value = 0.015).

An analysis of immature B cells revealed a higher percentage in symptomatic carriers than in noncarriers (median 13% vs. 4%, respectively; p value = 0.029). An increase in the proportion of double-negative B cells was also observed in symptomatic carriers compared with noncarriers (median 6% vs. 2.55%, respectively; p value = 0.043). We hypothesized that the small population could explain the lack of differences between asymptomatic carriers and noncarriers. To address this hypothesis, we clustered relatives into carriers (symptomatic and asymptomatic) and noncarriers. Thus, when immature CD19 + CD21- and double-negative B cells were compared between the two groups, the differences were significant (median 10% vs. 4%, p value = 0.034; and 5.50% vs. 2.55%, p value = 0.008, respectively). The same significance was found between carriers and noncarriers for naive CD4 + CD45RA + T cells (median 16% vs. 34%, p value = 0.034).

Finally, we compared the proportions of naive CD4 + T cells, CD4 + CD25-Foxp3 + Tregs, CD4 + Foxp3 + T cells, and immature and double-negative B cells to the normal ranges. Only the proportions of naive CD4 + CD45RA + T cells and CD4 + CD25-Foxp3 + Treg cells were significantly different, with a lower proportion of naive T cells and a higher proportion of CD4 + CD25-Foxp3 + Treg cells (p values = 0.023 and 0.004, respectively).

Discussion

We reported a previously undescribed variant of CTLA4 in a family with a clinical penetrance of approximately 60%, which is consistent with previous reports [5, 6]. In terms of clinical manifestations, ocular inflammation has been reported in 3–6% of patients in some studies [4, 6, 18]. Interestingly, uveitis can appear after treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors, including with anti-CTLA4 antibodies, mimicking CTLA4 haploinsufficiency [19, 20].

Several oncological manifestations, including lymphomas, gastric cancers, multiple myeloma and metastatic melanoma, have been previously related to CTLA4 haploinsufficiency in large cohorts, often with significant EBV involvement [3]. To our knowledge, this is the first report of Good’s syndrome in patients with CTLA4 haploinsufficiency. However, the patient died from pulmonary interstitial disease, which occurs in 20% of patients with this disease [2, 6].

Neurological manifestations are also present in 20% of CTLA4 haploinsufficient patients and can sometimes result in fatal outcomes [4, 5]. While myelitis has been described as a presenting neurological feature, ADEM has not been previously reported. Common presentations include autoimmune encephalitis, multiple sclerosis-like lesions, perivascular or cerebral lymphocytic infiltration, and ischemic or hemorrhagic lesions caused by AIHA or ITP, respectively [4, 5].

Among the family evaluated in this study, we highlighted the efficacy of abatacept treatment in two patients [6, 21–23].

In the variant, the substitution is located near the MYPPPY motif of CTLA4, starting at amino acid 134, which interacts closely with CD28 and has already been associated with pathogenic mutations [24, 25]. Finally, a single-nucleotide variant located on the same amino acid but leading to another missense change was reported in a family with CTLA4 deficiency (c.380 A >G, p.Tyr127Cys), further supporting the pathogenicity of the p.Tyr128Asp variant [26].

The relative CTLA4 expression was shown to decrease in the patients after stimulation, which is consistent with a previous report [17]. However, likely due to the small sample size, the decreases in the relative expression of CTLA4 before stimulation and the frequency of FoxP3 + cells in stimulated conditions did not differ significantly compared with controls.

Finally, the immunophenotyping results regarding the clinical status of the family members are consistent with previous reports in CTLA4 haploinsufficient patients, where the proportion of CD19 + CD21- B cells is increased and the proportion of naive CD4 + T cells is decreased [5, 6]. The increase in CD4 + CD25-Foxp3 + Tregs is not fully understood, but this subset likely plays a role in autoimmune diseases under the control of TGF-β and requires CTLA4 coexpression to induce anergy in reactive T cells [27, 28]. Additionally, double-negative B cells have been implicated in autoimmune diseases, which may explain the increased levels of this subset in symptomatic patients [29].

In conclusion, we report a novel c.379T > G variant in CTLA4, leading to previously undescribed or rare manifestations such as Good’s syndrome, ADEM or uveitis, thus broadening the spectrum of the disease. Immunophenotyping revealed a decreased proportion of naive and CD4 + CD25-Foxp3 + T cells and increased numbers of immature and double-negative B cells and could be helpful for clinical diagnosis.
