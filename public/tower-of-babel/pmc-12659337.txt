War exposure and mental health in Sudanese medical students: the roles of psychological flexibility and self-esteem
Authors: Sohaib Mohammed Mokhtar Ahmed, Isra Abdelgafar Hassan Eltaeib, Yasir Abdelrafi Dafalla Fadlelmoula, Hebatallah Fadhl, Mohamed Salah Abdulrazeg Abdulrahman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe ongoing conflict in Sudan has disrupted medical education and affected the mental well-being of students. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of war exposure on psychological inflexibility (the inability to adaptively respond to distressing internal experiences), self-esteem, and mental health outcomes, specifically depression and anxiety.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in March 2025 among 148 medical students at Gadarif University, categorized as war-exposed (42.6%) or non-exposed (57.4%). Validated Arabic questionnaires assessed psychological inflexibility (AAQ-II), self-esteem (RSE), depression (PHQ-9), and anxiety (GAD-7). Mann-Whitney U tests and linear regression were used to assess group differences and predictors.ResultsNo significant differences were found between groups in psychological inflexibility (p = 0.451) or self-esteem (p = 0.327). However, war-exposed students had higher depression (p = 0.031) and anxiety (p = 0.025). In regression models, war exposure was associated with increased depression (β = -2.371, p = 0.011) and anxiety (β = -2.529, p = 0.005). Furthermore, in both groups, higher psychological inflexibility predicted worse depression (β = 0.126, p < 0.001) and anxiety (β = 0.150, p < 0.001), while lower self-esteem predicted higher depression (β = -0.295, p = 0.009). A 1-point increase in inflexibility corresponded to a 0.13-point increase in depression symptoms.ConclusionWar exposure was associated with increased depression and anxiety, though it did not significantly alter psychological inflexibility or self-esteem. These psychological traits were independently associated with mental health outcomes, suggesting their role as key vulnerability factors. Strengthening resilience and self-worth—through interventions that enhance emotional regulation and coping—may reduce the psychological burden of conflict. Limitations include the cross-sectional design and sampling from a single institution.Keywords: Medical students, Depression, Anxiety, Self-esteem, Psychological flexibility

Introduction

Education is the backbone of a country’s progress [1]. In Sudan, medical education focuses on building strong academic knowledge and practical skills to train future healthcare professionals [2]. However, this intense training comes with significant challenges, including academic pressure, competition, and long study hours, which contribute to high stress among medical students, impacting their mental well-being [3]. Untreated mental distress can cause serious issues, including poor academic performance, declining mental health, low self-esteem, and reduced educational success [4, 5].

Sudanese medical students face even greater challenges after the ongoing war that began on April 15, 2023, in Khartoum, Sudan [6]. The ongoing violence has displaced 9 million people [7] and led to military attacks on over 34 universities [8]. The situation is increasingly difficult and burdensome for both universities and students due to the displacement and scattering of students across various countries.

Given these disruptions, medical students have faced serious setbacks in both their education and well-being. Consequently, access to essential services such as employment, healthcare, and education has been severely disrupted. One of the most significant disruptions has been in education, leading to curriculum interruptions, a decline in clinical training quality in hospitals, and a lack of internet access, which has hindered online learning [9].

Beyond these physical and academic challenges, the mental health of medical students is also impacted. Mental well-being is a fundamental aspect of overall health, essential for both personal and societal functioning [10]. Mental health means feeling well enough to handle stress, know your strengths, learn effectively, work well, and help your community [11]. Mental health disorders have emerged as a significant global public health concern, particularly among medical students [12, 13].

These mental health challenges are compounded by external pressures, particularly the intense expectations placed on students by their families and society [14, 15]. In Sudan, a recent study found that 61.4% of students reported psychological distress linked to the conflict, while nearly half (49.1%) showed signs of depression and over half (51.8%) struggled with anxiety [2].

Students in medicine face many challenges, and psychological flexibility (PF) is important for coping with them. Psychological flexibility (PF) is the ability to pursue significant goals when stressors and/or emotional challenges are present. This is an important component for one’s well-being [16, 17]. It reduces distress and fosters positive outcomes, such as improved relationships and overall mental health [17]. Recently, psychological flexibility has gained attention in understanding how individuals cope with adversity.

Another crucial factor is self-esteem, which refers to an individual’s sense of self-worth, acceptance, and confidence [18]. Having balanced self-esteem is highly important for healthcare professionals, including medical students [19], because high self-esteem influences a person’s initiative, motivation, and success in academic and professional activities [20]. Low self-esteem leads to psychosocial weakness and lack of self-confidence, creating problems and risky behaviors [21], and low self-esteem is considered one of the risk factors for depression and anxiety, eating disorders, violence and educational exclusion, and drug abuse [22].

Given the challenging environment and the devastating impact of war on mental health and medical education, it is crucial to investigate the psychological factors that shape well-being in conflict-affected students. This study is the first to examine the association between psychological inflexibility and self-esteem with mental health outcomes—mainly depression and anxiety—within a conflict zone like Sudan. Additionally, there is a scarcity of direct comparative studies assessing differences between students exposed to war and those who are not. Furthermore, limited research has explored the link between psychological inflexibility, self-esteem, and mental health outcomes specifically in medical students. By comparing medical students with and without war exposure, this study aims to uncover how reduced self-esteem and increased psychological inflexibility contribute to elevated depression and anxiety in those affected by war. Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that war exposure would be associated with higher psychological inflexibility—grounded in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) theory, which views inflexibility as a core contributor to psychopathology—lower self-esteem, and increased depression and anxiety. Addressing this gap will provide critical insights into mental health disparities in conflict settings and inform strategies to support medical students facing extreme adversity.

Methodology

Study design

This cross-sectional comparative study was conducted at Gadarif University in March 2025 to assess the impact of war exposure on psychological flexibility, self-esteem, and mental health among medical students. It compared two groups: those exposed to war and those not.

Study sitting

The Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences at Gadarif University, established in 1997, is located in Gadarif State, eastern Sudan. The faculty offers a ten-semester Bachelor of Medicine and Surgery (MBBS) program following a semester-based system. Currently, it has approximately 840 students enrolled across six academic batches (batches 21 through 26). While Gadarif State itself has not been directly involved in the ongoing war, many of its students come from conflict-affected regions, making it a suitable setting to study the psychological impact of war exposure on medical students. The university serves as a key institution for medical education in the region, contributing to the training of future healthcare professionals.

Study population

This study included undergraduate medical students enrolled at Gadarif University who are at least 18 years old. Participants were divided into two groups based on their exposure to war. Students from other healthcare programs such as nursing, pharmacy, laboratory sciences, or dentistry will be excluded. Additionally, those with a documented psychiatric disorder diagnosed before the war, those currently receiving psychiatric treatment or counseling, and individuals with significant personal trauma unrelated to war exposure was excluded to minimize confounding factors.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software, targeting an independent samples t-test to compare war-exposed and non-exposed students. As prior studies in Sudanese medical students are lacking, we used a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), with α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80, and a two-tailed test. These parameters align with standard guidelines for power analysis in psychosocial research [23, 24]. The minimum required sample was 128 (64 per group). Accounting for 20% potential nonresponse or data loss, we targeted 154 students. The final analyzed sample consisted of 148 students (63 war-exposed, 85 non-exposed) exceeding the minimal required sample size.

Sampling method

A systematic random sampling approach was employed. The study recruited participants from the WhatsApp groups of medical students in batches 21 through 26, which collectively include approximately 90% of the total student population, making these groups a reasonable proxy for the full student body.

Given the lack of a university email system, centralized student database, or any official contact registry, these WhatsApp groups represent the most reliable and comprehensive means of reaching students. The groups are widely used by students for academic communication, and their high membership rate ensures that the selected sample closely reflects the actual student population. In the absence of alternative contact methods, any other approach—such as on-campus recruitment—would risk lower participation rates and increased selection bias, particularly in the context of ongoing war-related disruptions that may limit students’ physical presence at the university.

Within each batch, 30 students were systematically selected using a fixed-interval method. Specifically, after randomly determining a starting point, the first student in the sequence was chosen, followed by every third student (i.e., selecting the 1st, 4th, 7th, and so on), thereby skipping two students between selections. Students WhatsApp group member lists served as the sampling frame. This systematic process ensured a fair random selection of participants from each batch and yields a final sample of 180 students (30 from each of the six batches).

Selected individuals received a private invitation via WhatsApp, which includes a survey link and an explanation of the study’s purpose, ethical considerations, and the voluntary nature of participation. To maximize the response rate, a structured reminder schedule was implemented: if a student does not respond within three days, a first reminder was sent, followed by a second reminder after an additional three days, and a final reminder three days later. The final response rate was 82.2%.

Upon agreeing to participate, students completed an online version of the questionnaire. Participants were not pre-selected into war-exposed or non-exposed groups. Instead, the questionnaire included a screening item at the beginning to classify exposure status. Students were asked whether they had experienced any of the following war-related events: witnessing violence (e.g., bombings or clashes), forced displacement, prolonged loss of basic services (e.g., food, water, healthcare), the death of a loved one due to conflict, or trauma directly linked to the ongoing war. These indicators were adapted from validated trauma exposure checklists commonly used in conflict research [25], war exposure was coded as binary (exposed vs. not exposed) based on these self-reported experiences.

Study instrument

Data were collected using standardized and validated self-reported questionnaires to assess psychological inflexibility, self-esteem, and mental health outcomes. Psychological inflexibility was measured using the Arabic version of the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II (AAQ-II) [26], a 7-item scale scored on a 7-point Likert scale (Score range 7–49), with higher scores indicating greater psychological inflexibility. Self-esteem was assessed using the Arabic version of the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES) [27], a 10-item scale rated on a 4-point Likert scale (Score range 0–30), where higher scores reflect higher self-esteem, scores below 15 were classified as low self-esteem, while scores ≥ 15 as normal self-esteem [28]. Mental health outcomes were evaluated using the Arabic versions of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depression and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) for anxiety, both consisting of 9 and 7 items, respectively, rated on a 4-point Likert scale, Consistent with established clinical thresholds, PHQ-9 scores ≥ 10 [29] and GAD-7 scores ≥ 9 [30] were used to identify probable cases of depression and anxiety, respectively. The questionnaire included a socio-demographic section covering age, gender, marital status, academic batch, state of origin, type of residence (with family, alone, etc.), and self-reported economic status. The overall Cronbach’s alpha across all scales used in this study was 0.71, suggesting acceptable internal consistency.

Data management

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 [31]. Demographic characteristics were summarized using means (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (%) for categorical variables. Comparative analysis was conducted using independent samples t-tests or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data normality, to assess differences in psychological inflexibility, self-esteem, and mental health outcomes between war-exposed and non-exposed students. Correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between psychological inflexibility, self-esteem, and mental health outcomes. Additionally, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to determine mental health outcomes predictors and whether psychological inflexibility and self-esteem are significant predictors of depression and anxiety in both groups. Although PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores were not perfectly normally distributed, linear regression was chosen due to the continuous nature of the outcomes and because residual plots confirmed acceptable model assumptions. Linear regression also provides greater sensitivity for detecting linear relationships in symptom severity across the full scale, which aligns with the study’s objectives. A significance level of ≤ 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Results

The study included a total of 148 participants, with demographic and psychological characteristics summarized in Tables 1 and 2. The sample had a mean age of 22.9 years (SD = 2.6), ranging from 18 to 36 years, and consisted of 61.5% females and 38.5% males. The majority of participants were single (95.3%), while a small proportion were married (3.4%), divorced (0.7%), or widowed (0.7%). Geographically, most participants originated from Gadarif State (70.3%), followed by Gezira (13.5%) and Khartoum States (9.5%). In terms of residence, 74.3% lived with family, 19.6% resided in university accommodations, and smaller proportions lived with friends (4.1%) or a spouse (2.0%). Economic status was predominantly reported as average (62.2%), with fewer participants identifying as above average (24.3%), high (9.5%), below average (2.0%), or low (2.0%).

Psychologically, the mean AAQ-II score, reflecting psychological inflexibility, was 24.2 (SD = 11.9), while the mean RSE score for self-esteem was 20.5 (SD = 4.2), with 8.1% of participants classified as having low self-esteem. Regression analyses (Table 5) identified several predictors of depression and anxiety. War exposure significantly predicted higher scores for both PHQ-9 (β = -2.371, p = 0.011) and GAD-7 (β = -2.529, p = 0.005). Psychological inflexibility (High AAQ-II score) was a positive predictor of both outcomes (p < 0.001), while low self-esteem (low RSE score) predicted depression (β = -0.295, p = 0.009). Sex also emerged as a significant predictor, with males reporting lower depression scores than females (β = -2.752, p = 0.002). Although psychological inflexibility and self-esteem did not significantly differ between war-exposed and non-exposed groups, these constructs still independently predicted mental health outcomes. This indicates that, regardless of war exposure, individuals with higher psychological inflexibility or lower self-esteem were more likely to experience depression and anxiety symptoms.

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of war exposure on psychological flexibility, self-esteem, and mental health outcomes among medical students at Gadarif University. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between self-esteem, psychological flexibility, and mental health in medical students within a conflict setting. Contrary to our hypothesis, war exposure did not significantly reduce psychological flexibility (AAQ-II) or self-esteem (RSE). However, students affected by war reported significantly higher anxiety (GAD-7) and depression (PHQ-9) scores, with prevalence rates of 34.5% for depression and 21.6% for anxiety. Notably, psychological inflexibility strongly correlated with lower self-esteem and heightened depression and anxiety. Regression analyses further identified war exposure, psychological inflexibility, and low self-esteem as key predictors of poor mental health outcomes, these findings clarify that while psychological inflexibility and self-esteem did not differ significantly between war-exposed and non-exposed students, they still played an important role in shaping mental health outcomes. This suggests that these traits function as transdiagnostic risk factors that impact psychological well-being across both groups, regardless of exposure status. The implication is that interventions targeting psychological inflexibility and self-esteem may be broadly effective for improving student mental health, not only in trauma-exposed populations.

Our findings indicate that 34.5% of students exhibited depression and 21.6% exhibited anxiety. These rates are lower than those reported in other conflict-affected regions, such as Syria and Yemen [32, 33], but are comparable to a prior study conducted among 6,641 Sudanese medical students five months after the war onset [34]. Furthermore, our results suggest that medical students exposed to war experience significantly higher levels of depression and anxiety compared to their non-exposed peers. While direct comparative studies are scarce, prior research has consistently demonstrated that medical students in conflict zones face significantly elevated mental health burdens. A global meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of depression among medical students at 27.2%, with anxiety rates ranging from 7.7% to 65.5%, depending on the study population and assessment tools [35, 36]. Studies in war-affected regions, such as Sudan and Syria, have reported even higher prevalence rates. For instance, research conducted at the University of Khartoum found heightened psychological distress among students residing in conflict-affected areas [37]. Similarly, a systematic review on children exposed to armed conflict reported significantly higher anxiety (43%) and depression (47%) rates compared to non-conflict settings [38]. These findings highlight the detrimental psychological impact of war and the urgent need for targeted mental health interventions in conflict-affected educational settings.

Interestingly, our study found no significant differences in psychological flexibility and self-esteem between war-exposed and non-exposed students. This could be attributed to the relatively stable academic environment at Gadarif University, which may provide a degree of psychological insulation. Additionally, cultural factors in Sudan—including extended family networks, collectivist social norms, and strong religious faith—may help sustain core psychological traits under stress [39]. For example, communal solidarity and shared suffering in displaced or conflict-affected communities can foster emotional validation and belonging, mitigating the erosion of self-worth. Furthermore, Islamic faith practices such as prayer, acceptance of divine will (qadar), and religious reframing of adversity are commonly used coping mechanisms that reinforce emotional regulation and resilience [40]. These culturally embedded supports may help explain why psychological inflexibility and self-esteem remained relatively stable, even among war-exposed students.

However, psychological inflexibility and low self-esteem emerged as significant predictors of both depression and anxiety. This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that psychological inflexibility and diminished self-esteem contribute to poorer mental health outcomes [41, 42]. Psychological inflexibility, characterized by difficulty adapting to change and avoidance of negative thoughts and emotions, has been robustly linked to increased depressive and anxiety symptoms. A meta-analytic review during the COVID-19 pandemic reported strong associations between psychological inflexibility and mental health issues, including depression and anxiety [43]. Self-esteem, defined as an individual’s overall sense of self-worth, plays a crucial role in stress management. Studies indicate that high self-esteem serves as a protective factor against stress, mitigating its adverse effects on mental health [44]. Conversely, low self-esteem may impair stress-coping abilities, increasing susceptibility to psychological distress. Addressing these factors through targeted interventions—such as Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) to enhance psychological flexibility and self-esteem-focused programs—could be effective in alleviating depression and anxiety symptoms. Implementing such strategies may improve overall mental health outcomes and reduce the prevalence of these disorders in conflict-affected populations.

Strengths of the study

This study provides critical evidence from a war-affected population of medical students in Sudan—an underrepresented group in global mental health research. It is the first to explore psychological inflexibility and self-esteem as predictors of depression and anxiety in this context. The use of validated Arabic instruments, systematic sampling, and both comparative and predictive analyses strengthens the study’s internal validity. Despite operational challenges due to the conflict, the response rate was high and data were collected uniformly.

Recommendations

The findings of this study have significant implications for medical education and mental health interventions in conflict-affected regions. The high prevalence of depression (34.5%) and anxiety (21.6%) among medical students—particularly those exposed to war—emphasizes the urgent need for institutional mental health support, including accessible counseling services and stress-management programs. ACT-based group workshops can be tailored to medical students through culturally adapted metaphors and mindfulness exercises. Self-esteem programs might include peer-mentorship circles, faculty-led encouragement strategies, and reflective journaling assignments embedded in the curriculum [41, 44]. Additionally, the preserved psychological flexibility and self-esteem among war-exposed students suggest that these traits may serve as protective factors, warranting further research into cultural and contextual resilience mechanisms (e.g., communal support, religious coping). Universities and policymakers should prioritize creating stable academic environments and addressing structural barriers (e.g., disrupted education, economic instability) to mitigate the mental health burdens associated with war exposure.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationship between war exposure and psychological outcomes. Second, reliance on self-reported measures may introduce bias, such as underreporting due to stigma or social desirability. Third, students without internet access or smartphones—potentially those most vulnerable due to economic hardship or displacement—may have been excluded, possibly underestimating the psychological burden of war exposure. Future studies should incorporate offline methods to capture this underrepresented group. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single university in a relatively stable region (Gadarif state), limiting generalizability to students in active conflict zones like Khartoum. Finally, the use of general psychological measures (AAQ-II, RSE) may not capture war-specific impacts, suggesting the need for trauma-sensitive instruments in future research.

Conclusion

This study provides critical insights into the psychological toll of war on Sudanese medical students, revealing elevated depression and anxiety among war-affected individuals despite preserved psychological flexibility and self-esteem. The significant role of psychological inflexibility and low self-esteem as predictors of poor mental health underscores the importance of targeted interventions to foster adaptive coping strategies. While the stable academic environment and cultural resilience factors may buffer against severe psychological deterioration, the high prevalence of distress calls for urgent institutional and policy responses. Future longitudinal studies should explore long-term mental health trajectories and the efficacy of interventions like ACT in conflict settings. Addressing these challenges is essential to safeguarding the well-being of future healthcare professionals in Sudan and similar conflict-affected regions.
