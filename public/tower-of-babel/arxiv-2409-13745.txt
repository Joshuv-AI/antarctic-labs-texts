Context-Aware Membership Inference Attacks against Pre-trained Large Language Models
Hongyan Chang, Ali Shahin Shamsabadi, Kleomenis Katevas, Hamed Haddadi, Reza Shokri

Abstract
Prior Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) on pre-trained Large Language Models (LLMs), adapted from classification model attacks, fail due to ignoring the generative process of LLMs across token sequences. In this paper, we present a novel attack that adapts MIA statistical tests to the perplexity dynamics of subsequences within a data point. Our method significantly outperforms prior loss-based approaches, revealing context-dependent memorization patterns in pre-trained LLMs.



1
Introduction

To assess memorization and information leakage in models, Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) aim to determine if a data point was part of a model’s training set
[
1
]
. However, MIAs designed for pre-trained Large Language Models (LLMs) have been largely ineffective
[
2
,
3
]
.

This is primarily because these MIAs, originally developed for classification models, fail to account for the sequential nature of LLMs. Unlike classification models, which produce a single prediction, LLMs generate text token-by-token, adjusting each prediction based on the
context
of preceding tokens (i.e., prefix). Prior MIAs overlook token-level loss dynamics and the influence of prefixes on next-token predictability, which contributes to memorization. This simplification misses critical LLM behaviors, notably
context-dependent memorization
, making these attacks ineffective at identifying training set members.

Additionally, state-of-the-art MIA tests
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
rely on reference models trained similarly to the target model but on distinct, similarly distributed data. Achieving this is extremely costly and often impractical for pre-trained LLMs. Also, arbitrarily using other available pre-trained models as reference models leads to inaccurate attacks due to significant differences in their training processes and model architectures (as reasoned analytically
[
8
]
, and shown empirically in this paper and the prior work
[
2
]
).

To design a strong MIA against LLMs, we need to fully understand how and why memorization may occur during the training. Any piece of text is modeled as a sequence of tokens, and LLMs are trained to optimize parameters that maximize the conditional probabilities of generating each token based on the preceding context. This process is progressive, as the model adjusts its predictions with each new token, refining its understanding of the sequence.

A key point we highlight in this paper is that memorization can occur at any stage of sub-sequence generation, particularly when the model encounters prefixes (contexts) that are ambiguous or complex. When the model faces high perplexity—indicating uncertainty about the next token—it may rely more heavily on memorization of training data to resolve the ambiguity and generate accurate predictions. This dynamic suggests that the
model’s tendency to memorize specific sequences is context-dependent
and influenced by how challenging the prefix is in guiding the generation of subsequent tokens. This can lead to subtle patterns of memorization which the model might repeatedly fall back on when facing high uncertainty. This is why building MIAs solely based on the loss of (sub-)sequences, as done in prior work, without adapting the test to the context (prefix), is very likely to fail. It is essential to design context-dependent MIAs for LLMs that account for the influence of the prefix on the model’s predictions and potential memorization. This is what we propose in this paper.

We design
CAMIA
(Context-Aware Membership Inference Attack), a composite MIA framework tailored for LLMs, which extracts calibrated membership information from the sequence of next-token prediction losses in a given text. Our approach leverages several key observations: the length of the prefix, the diversity of tokens in the prefix, and other factors influencing the perplexity of the prefix all impact memorization.
We calibrate our test based on these contextual factors
. Additionally, we account for changes in next-token prediction loss across the sequence, incorporating critical indicators such as the slope of the loss, variance (fluctuations), and the presence of outlier values. These factors enable us to effectively distinguish between members and non-members. As a result, our attack decision is not uniform across all sequences (i.e., not relying on a fixed threshold for loss values); instead, it is highly adapted and calibrated to the specific context (the prefix) and the dynamic behavior of the loss throughout the text. This significantly increases the true-positive-rate of our attack, notably for low false-positive-rates.

We comprehensively evaluate the performance of our attack using
9
pre-trained LLMs from the Pythia
[
9
]
and GPT-Neo
[
10
]
suites, comparing it with all prior MIAs (which are also evaluated on the MIMIR benchmark
[
2
]
). Our approach,
CAMIA
, consistently outperforms all prior MIAs on pre-trained LLMs. For instance, on member/non-member data sampled from the arXiv domain,
CAMIA
successfully identifies three times more members than prior baselines while maintaining a
1\%
false-positive error rate. The membership signals used in our work complement each other and help capture different subsets of members compared to prior context-independent loss-based approaches. We highlight the effectiveness of the individual signals in
CAMIA
in our experimental results.

When attacking the
70
MB Pythia model,
CAMIA
achieves a
3.35
x higher True Positive Rate (TPR) at
1
% False Positive Rate (FPR) compared to the Loss attack (from
6.69
% to
23.37
%). Against the Min-K%
[
11
]
(which in some scenarios performs better than a simple loss attack), our attack doubles the TPR (
23.37
% versus
12.23
%). For the
125
MB GPT-Neo model,
CAMIA
improves the TPR from
9.4
% to
28
%, which is a nearly
20
% increase at
1
% FPR. This improvement is consistent across GPT-Neo models of various sizes. Our attacks consistently achieve the highest TPR at low FPR across
6
data domains and
9
different models.

In summary, the main contribution of our paper is the introduction of context-dependent MIAs tailored for LLMs, which capture memorization patterns throughout a text sequence. The high TPR achieved by our attack sets a significantly stronger baseline for analyzing information leakage in pre-trained LLMs.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x\in\mathbf{V}}\delta(x-x_{t})\log P(x|\mathbf{x}_{<t};\mathcal{M})=-\log P(x_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{<t};\mathcal{M}).

(1)

\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X}_{i};\mathcal{M})=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P(x_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{<t};\mathcal{M})

(2)

Threat model

We consider a
service provider
(e.g., a company or a hospital) that wants to (pre-)train the Large Language Model (LLM)
\mathcal{M}
on their unlabeled private data
[
2
]
,
PrivSet
(as described above) and provides LLM-based services to their users. Providing such services may risk the privacy of
PrivSet

\mathcal{M}
unintentionally leaks information about the training data due to the data memorization
[

. Analyzing this privacy risk can be studied with various attacks
[

13

. In our work, we focus on
Membership Inference Attacks
(MIAs). MIAs aim to determine whether a target input text,
\mathbf{X}
, was present in the private training set of the service provider by having only

to
\mathcal{M}
. In this black-box setting, the adversary can query the model on any prefixes
\mathbf{x}_{<t}
and obtain the loss on predicting a specific token
x_{t}
. However, the adversary does not have access to the model architecture and the model parameters.

MIA is the de-facto threat model for discussing model privacy. Therefore, MIAs can help the service provider assess privacy risks associated with providing LLM-based services to comply with privacy regulations
[
16
]
. MIAs are also beneficial from a copyright perspective as publishers might want to verify if their protected content was used by the service provider to train their LLMs without their consent. [
17
]
.

2.3
MIAs against pre-trained LLMs

MIAs
[
13
,
11
,
18
,
14
,
14
]
define
membership scoring function

f(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})
and compare its outputted score to a threshold
\tau
to determine the target input’s membership. Loss-based attack
[
13
]
(LOSS) defines the membership scoring function based on the LLM’s computed loss over all tokens,
f_{\text{LOSS}}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})
. Then, LOSS determines that
\mathbf{X}
is a member if the LLM’s computed loss is below the threshold,
f_{\text{LOSS}}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})<\tau
. Recently, a few MIAs
[
11
,
18
,
19
,
14
]
have been proposed in the literature to tailor the LOSS membership scoring function to pre-trained LLMs using calibration based on different techniques quantifying text complexities.

Min-K% attack
[
11
]
uses only a subset of per-token loss
\mathcal{L}_{t}
based on the hypothesis that a non-member input text is most likely to have a few outlier tokens with high loss, while a member input text is less likely to have tokens with such high losses. Min-k% attack defines the membership scoring function based on the top k% of tokens with the lowest likelihoods as
{f_{\text{Min-K\%}}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{|\text{min-k}(\mathbf{X})|}\sum_{x_{t}\in\text{min-k}(\mathbf{X})}\mathcal{L}_{t}}
. Min-K%++ attack
[
18
]
normalizes the loss for each token by the expectation
\mu_{<t}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{<t}
of the next token’s
x_{t}
log probability over the LLM’s vocabulary
\mathbf{V}
given its prefix
\mathbf{x}_{<t}
as
{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}(x_{t})=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})-\mu_{<t}}{\sigma_{<t}}}
. Min-K%++ membership scoring function is
{f_{\text{Min-K\%++}}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{|\text{min-K}(\mathbf{X})|}\sum_{x_{t}\in\text{min-K}(\mathbf{X})}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}(x_{t})}
.

Reference-based attack, Neighborhood attack and Zlib attack calibrate LOSS with respect to the intrinsic complexity of the input text using additional (reference) LLMs
[
14
]
, additional queries to the target LLM
[
19
]
and entropy of the target input.
Zlib attack
[
14
]
normalizes the LLM’s computed loss with the zlib entropy of the target input
[
20
]
,
{f_{\text{Zlib}}(\mathbf{X};M)=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})/\text{zlib}(\mathbf{X})}
.
Reference-based attack
[
14
]
defines the membership scoring function relative to a reference model as
{f_{\text{Ref}}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M}_{ref})}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{ref}
is the reference LLM that is trained on data that is same-distribution but largely disjoint from
PrivSet
. Therefore,
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M}_{ref})
calibrates the target LLM’s loss with respect to the
intrinsic complexity
of the input text, which reflects the probability that this text appears in any LLMs.
Neighborhood attack
[
19
]
approximates the intrinsic complexity of the input text based on its
neighbors
. Specifically, the adversary first constructs a set of “neighboring” points
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})
around the input text
\mathbf{X}
and calibrate the loss of
\mathbf{X}
to the average loss for its neighbors, i.e.,
{f_{\text{Nei}}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})|}\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X})}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}};\mathcal{M})}
.

2.4
Goal

MIMIR
[
2
]
, which was presented at the 2024 Conference on Language Modeling, evaluated the performance of MIAs on pre-trained LLMs and demonstrated that “
the performance across most
MIAs and target domains is near-random
”. This MIMIR benchmark highlights a need for designing better MIAs on pre-trained LLMs. Our goal is to design effective MIAs in this challenging setting by exploiting:

•

token-level complexities relative to its prefix. Prior MIAs compute input-level complexities failing to capture the fact that the complexity of predicting a specific token can vary a lot depending on its index (i.e., location within the input text) and the characteristics of its prefix.

•

per-token LLM’s computed loss sequences. Prior MIAs use only LLM’s computed loss in aggregation over all (or a subset) of tokens. This overlook of the loss dynamic misses the membership information encoded in the per-token loss sequences over time.

•

combined LLM’s output and input text’s semantics. Prior MIAs focus on each of these in isolation.

To achieve this goal, we design an MIA framework, called
CAMIA
. As shown in Figure
1
,
CAMIA
1) computes next-token loss sequences of the target input
\mathbf{X}
based on black-box access to
\mathcal{M}
; 2) extracts calibrated membership information; 3) compose the MIA tests based on calibrated membership information; and 4) decide whether
\mathbf{X}
is a member or non-member based on the composed tests outcome.

Section
3
introduces our proposed calibration technique, characterization of next-token loss dynamic followed by extracted membership information from the sequence of next-token losses. Section
4
describes ways to compose membership tests.

3
Our Calibration and Membership Information

3.1
Inherent text complexity

Prior MIAs for classification ML models exploit the model’s tendency to overfit the training data. However, these loss-based approaches fail when applied to pre-trained LLMs due to a significant overlap in the LLM’s computed loss histogram of members and non-members. A member sample may exhibit high loss if it is inherently complex (e.g., a piece of text containing multiple languages). This issue is more severe in pre-trained LLMs which are typically trained with only near-one epoch but on a massive amount of training data
[
2
]
. A non-member sample may have a very low loss simply because it is simple, containing repetitive and inherently predictable tokens.

Therefore, it is important to calibrate LLM’s computed loss based on input difficulties. Existing difficulty calibration techniques are not suitable for pre-trained LLMs. Calibration based on a reference model
[
14
]
requires i) access to data that is same-distribution but largely disjoint from the training data of the LLM under attack; and ii) pre-training a reference LLM. Finding such data is challenging (if not impossible) as information about pre-training data of the target model is often not accessible. In addition to this, pre-training the reference model is extremely expensive due to the large scale of pre-training data and models. Calibration based on neighborhood samples
[
19
]
requires querying the target model hundreds of times with perturbed inputs, making them limited to fine-tuning tasks and small models (which differ significantly from pre-training tasks and pre-trained LLMs).

We propose a lightweight calibration technique based on token diversity that we define as follows.

Definition 1

(Token diversity)
.

The token diversity of a target input text
\mathbf{X}
is the number of unique tokens in the input text divided by the total number of tokens:

\displaystyle d_{\mathbf{X}}\triangleq\frac{|Dedup(\mathbf{X})|}{|X|},

(3)

where
|\cdot|
represents the length of a vector (i.e., number of tokens) and the operation
Dedup(\cdot)
removes duplicated tokens.

We then calibrate the LLM’s computed loss on
\mathbf{X}
with its token diversity
d_{\mathbf{X}}
as
{f_{\text{Cal}}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})}{d_{\mathbf{X}}}}
. Figure
2
demonstrates the benefit of our calibration: calibration decreases the overlap between the histogram of members versus the histogram of non-members. After calibration, the lowest membership scores belong mostly to members, enabling high TPR at a very low FPR. At 1% FPR, calibration significantly improves TPR from
45.9\%
(Figure
2
, left hand side) to
69.78\%
(Figure
2
, right hand side).

Figure 2:
The impact of our token diversity calibration on loss histograms using the Pythia-deduped model (2.8B) on the GitHub domain. Token diversity calibration improves the distinguishability between member loss histograms versus non-member histograms, resulting in more effective MIAs (measured by the TPR at a low FPR).

3.2
Next-token loss sequence

Recall from Section
2
that the input text
\mathbf{X}=[x_{1}x_{2}....x_{T}]
is a sequence of tokens and the model
\mathcal{M}
is trained to minimize each per-token loss given its prefix. To account for this loss dynamic, we define the next-token loss sequence of
\mathbf{X}
with respect to model
\mathcal{M}
as the sequence of loss on each token, i.e.,
{\text{Seq}(\mathbf{X})=\{\mathcal{L}_{1}(x_{1}),\mathcal{L}_{2}(x_{2}),\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{T}(x_{T})\}}
. Then, we extract the membership information from the key characteristics of the loss sequence: the period of interest (which tokens should be considered?); the convergence speed (how fast the per-token loss
\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})
decreases as
t
increases?); and the fluctuation rate (how regular is the per-token loss over time?).

3.2.1
Filtering the loss sequence

Not all per-token losses are useful for MIAs. We aim to filter out the non-informative portion of the next-token loss sequence. Figure
3
(left) shows a separation between the members and non-members, based on the losses computed at the first
100
indices–members have smaller losses on average at each index whereas non-members have higher losses. Figure
3
(right) shows high overlaps between next-token losses of members and non-members at the last
100
indices (both members and non-members have low losses on average).

Figure 3:
Average next-token loss for members and non-members as a function of the token index from the beginning and end. The average losses for tokens located at the first
100
indices are separable for members and non-members whereas the separation is less obvious at the last
100
indices.

Therefore, we propose to filter the loss sequence up to some
T^{\prime}
.

\text{Filtered-Seq}(\mathbf{X})=\{\mathcal{L}_{1}(x_{1}),\mathcal{L}_{2}(x_{2}),\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{T^{\prime}})\}.

(4)

We refer to the loss averaged over the filtered loss sequence as cut-off loss, i.e.,
f_{\text{Cut}}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{T^{\prime}}\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})
, where
T^{\prime}
is the cut-off time.

Figure 4:
We fit linear functions to the next-token loss sequences of the Pythia model (
2.8B
) for a member and a non-member sampled from the Pile-CC domain. The rate of change in loss for the member (i.e.,
0.016
) is much larger than for the non-member (i.e.,
0.001
).

3.2.2
Decreasing rate of the loss sequence

LLMs tend to have a lower loss for tokens at the end (i.e.,
x_{t}
’s with larger
t
’s), compared with tokens at the front (i.e.,
x_{t}
’s with smaller
t
’s)
[
21
]
.
Figure
4
demonstrates that the decreasing rates for the loss sequence of members and non-members are different. In particular, the rate of a member is significantly higher than a non-member. The intuitive explanation is that as the model continues to predict the next tokens, the member (i.e., a sample from the training data) would seem more and more familiar to the model whereas for non-members, the model’s uncertainty in next-token prediction remains. We quantify this decreasing rate by fitting a linear function on the loss sequence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})\simeq a\cdot t+b,\text{ for }t=1,2,\ldots,T^{\prime}.

(5)

Here
a
represents the decreasing rate of the linear function, which is the signal of our interest. We refer to this signal as the slope signal. Given the filtered sequence for the target input, i.e.,
(\mathcal{L}_{1}(x_{1}),\mathcal{L}_{2}(x_{2}),\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{T^{\prime}}))
, the slope signal is computed as follows.

\displaystyle f_{\text{Slope}}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{T^{\prime}\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}t\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}t\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})}{T^{\prime}\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}t^{2}-\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}t\right)^{2}}.

(6)

3.2.3
Number of low losses in the loss sequence

In general ML models, we expect members to have lower losses than non-members, as the model is trained to minimize the loss on members. When it comes to LLMs, however, simply computing the average loss over the loss sequence (see equation
2
) is not a good option. For instance, if a member text contains multiple languages, the loss can spike significantly at the points where the language switches. As shown in Figure
5
, the loss is extremely high when changing the language from English to Bulgarian. The loss at that point is
484.2
, which dominates the sum of losses for all other tokens, resulting in an average loss of
9.33
.

Figure 5:
Sample next-token loss sequence where the multiple languages are used in one member text. We notice a loss spike when the language is switched from English to Bulgarian.

To mitigate this issue, we propose to count the number of losses in the loss sequence that are below a certain threshold, which can be seen as a more robust (quantized) measurement of average losses.

Count below a constant threshold.

We compute the frequency of losses that are lower than a constant threshold
\tau
.

\displaystyle f_{\text{CB}}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{T^{\prime}}\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}\mathbb{1}[\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})\leq\tau].

(7)

Count below the mean.

Alternatively, we can set
\tau
to be data-dependent–we adopt the average loss of the target input over all the tokens as the threshold (denoted as
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{X}}
), and compute

\displaystyle f_{\text{CBM}}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{T^{\prime}}\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}\mathbb{1}[\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})\leq\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{X}}],

(8)

Count below the previous mean.

Finally,
\tau
could vary at different indices–we use the average loss in the sequence up to the current index as the threshold and compute

\displaystyle f_{\text{CBPM}}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{T^{\prime}}\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}}\mathbb{1}[\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})\leq\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{X}_{<t}})],

(9)

where
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{X}_{<t}}
is the average loss over the first
t-1
tokens in
\mathbf{X}
. We can also obtain variations to the above signals by choosing different constants (for the first signal) and also applying different cut-off threshold
T^{\prime}
.

3.2.4
Fluctuations in the loss sequence

Figure
4
also demonstrates that the member and non-member have different levels of fluctuations in the next-token loss sequence. For tokens nearby each other, the loss can differ drastically for the non-member, compared to the member. Since the model is trained to minimize the loss on
each
token, it is expected to perform more consistently across all tokens for members. In contrast, for non-members, the model’s performance may vary significantly across different tokens. Therefore, the fluctuations of the next-token loss sequence differ between members and non-members. To capture this difference, we propose to adopt two commonly applied measurements in time-series analysis as our membership signals: Approximate entropy
[
22
]
and Lempel–Ziv complexity
[
23
]
.
At a high level, the
approximate entropy
quantifies the frequency of repeating patterns (i.e., segments in the sequence) in the next-token loss sequence. The
Lempel–Ziv complexity
measures the “diversity” of the sequence segments. We refer readers to Appendix
A
for detailed definitions.

3.3
Amplifying signals from the loss sequence

LLMs tend to perform better on repeated strings. Namely, when we create concatenated repetitions of the target
\mathbf{X}
, the loss on the latter repetitions will be lower. Similar to the slope signal in Section
3.2.2
, the loss decreasing rate on such concatenated copies may differ for members and non-members. To exploit such membership information, we propose to use the difference of signals computed at different repetitions as the membership signal, formalized as follows.

We use
f_{\text{Rep}}^{1}(\mathbf{X})
and
f_{\text{Rep}}^{2}(\mathbf{X})
to denote the signal difference obtained from repeating once and twice, respectively.

\displaystyle f_{\text{Rep}}^{1}(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})-f(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{X}))

\displaystyle f_{\text{Rep}}^{2}(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M})-f(\mathbf{X};\mathcal{M}([\mathbf{X},\text{`` ''},\mathbf{X}])),

where
f
can be the signal we defined above, e.g., calibrated loss, slope, and count below a constant threshold.
Here

\mathbf{X}

4
MIA Test Compositions

In this section, we combine our proposed membership signals,
\mathcal{F}
(see Section
3
). We consider two common settings based on the adversary’s knowledge of the underlying data distributions, following the MIA literature
[
24
,
6
,
4
,
1
,
25
,
5
]
.

4.1
Compose multiple MIA tests

In the first setting, the adversary has access to a non-member dataset sampled from the same underlying distribution as the training dataset of the target model.
The overall idea is as follows. First, based on each signal
f
from the signal set
\mathcal{F}
, we perform a hypothesis testing for MIA on
\mathbf{X}
and compute the corresponding confidence that
\mathbf{X}
is a member using this signal. Next, we combine the confidence of all hypothesis tests and get the predicted membership for
\mathbf{X}
. Next, we go through our framework in more detail.

Hypothesis testing.

We focus on one particular signal
f
and formalize MIA under the hypothesis testing framework
[
26
]
. Under this framework, the null hypothesis corresponds to the target input text
\mathbf{X}
being sampled from the underlying data distribution (i.e., not a member); the alternative hypothesis corresponds to the target data point being sampled from the training dataset (i.e., a member). Using the non-member dataset
D_{\text{non-mem}}
, we compute the signal
f(\mathbf{X}_{\text{non-mem}})
for every
\mathbf{X}_{\text{non-mem}}\in D_{\text{non-mem}}
.
We assume, without loss of generality, that when the target data point
\mathbf{X}
is a member,
f(\mathbf{X})
should generally be smaller than most of
f(\mathbf{X}_{\text{non-mem}})
; and vice versa (e.g., consider the loss signal where members have smaller losses than non-members), since otherwise the attacker can always flip the sign of the signal and obtains the same membership prediction.

Prediction confidence.

Based on the above hypothesis testing framework, we then compute the
p
-value associated with
\mathbf{X}
. A smaller value indicates that
\mathbf{X}
is more likely to be a member.
Following prior work
[
27
]
, we see observations of signal
f
on the non-member dataset
D_{\text{non-mem}}
as samples from some distribution
\mathcal{D}_{f}
(the distribution for the signals computed on the underlying data distribution). Based on the samples, we can then compute the
p
-value, which equals the cumulative distribution function of
\mathcal{D}_{f}
evaluated on the target data point’s signal
f(\mathbf{X})
, written as
CDF_{\mathcal{D}_{f}}(f(\mathbf{X}))
.
We denote this
p
-value
f
as
p_{f}(\mathbf{X})
, which is a value between
0
and
1
. For example, when
f(\mathbf{X})
is smaller than or equal to
80
percent of the signal computed on samples from
\mathcal{D}_{f}
(i.e., the value of
p_{f}(\mathbf{X})
is
0.2
), then we are
80
percent confident that
\mathbf{X}
is a member.

Combining all
p
-values

. After we have computed the
p
-values corresponding to all signals in
\mathcal{F}
, what is left to do is combining them to get an overall
p
-value that leads to membership prediction. This problem of combining
p
-values of multiple tests is well studied in the statistics literature, and there are various ways to do it, e.g., Edgington’s method
[
28
]
, Fisher’s method
[
29
]
, and George’s method
[
30
]
.
We adopt Edgington’s method, which computes the sum of
p
-values corresponding to all signals in
\mathcal{F}
, written as

{p_{\text{combined}}(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}p_{f}(\mathbf{X})}
.
Finally, based on
p_{\text{combined}}(\mathbf{X})
, we predict the target
\mathbf{X}
as a member if this value is lower than a certain threshold. In our experiments, we demonstrate the attack performance using different combination methods.

4.2
Learning to compose

In the second setting, the adversary has access to a non-member dataset as well as a member dataset of the target model. We refer to the union of these two datasets as the
attack dataset
(denoted as
D_{\text{attack}}
)
[
24
]
. This dataset helps the adversary to
learn
how to combine all the membership signals, with the help of auxiliary machine learning models (unlike the above Edgington’s method where the combination rule is fixed beforehand).

Formalization.

To formalize the combination of signals as a learning problem, we first need to specify the input and output of the model. Given the signal set
\mathcal{F}
and target data
\mathbf{X}
and target model
\mathcal{M}
, we construct the input features as
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{F}}=(f_{1}(\mathbf{X}),f_{2}(\mathbf{X}),\ldots)
, a
|\mathcal{F}|
-dimensional vector where each dimension
i
corresponds to the signal
f_{i}
computed on
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathcal{M}
. The output of this model is the probability that
\mathbf{X}
is a member. The learning objective is to make the model correctly predict the membership of all samples from
D_{\text{attack}}
.

Choice of model.

We choose to use the logistic regression model as the attack model due to its simplicity and explainability. In particular, each input feature
f_{i}(\mathbf{X})
) is associated with a weight
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. Based on the weighted combination of the features, the model outputs either
0
(i.e., non-member) or
1
(i.e., member) with probability as follows

\displaystyle P(Y=1\mid\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{F}})=\sigma(\langle X_{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{w}\rangle).

(10)

Here
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
is the sigmoid function and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product of two vectors.
w
is a
(|\mathcal{F}|+1)
-dimensional vector written as
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{|\mathcal{F}|})
. We optimize the weight vector
w
to minimize the following loss computed over
D_{\text{attack}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{(x,y)\in D_{\text{attack}}}\frac{y\log(\hat{y})+(1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})}{|D_{\text{attack}}|}

(11)

where
\hat{y}=\sigma(x_{\mathcal{F}}^{T}\textbf{w})
is the predicted membership label of
x
and
y
is the true label. Finally, on any new input target data point
\mathbf{X}
, we first obtain the feature vector using the signal set
\mathcal{F}
and then feed it into the logistic regression model with the trained weights
\mathbf{w}
, obtaining the membership prediction.

Dimensionality reduction.

For each signal we introduced in Section
3
, there are multiple variations of it.
Take the slope signal as an example, we can vary the cut-off time and obtain many variations, each of which corresponds to one feature
3.2.2
. We refer to all such variations of the same signal as a signal group. Since different variations may have different attack performances (and we do not know which one is the best beforehand), we may want to include all of them in our attack framework at first sight. However, as the number of features increases within a signal group, model redundancy may become an issue. To strike a balance between model redundancy and attack performance, we propose another component on top of the above logistic regression approach. In particular, we apply the Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
[
31
]
to compress the features computed from the same signal group, by computing the projection of the feature vector (composed of features from the same signal group) onto the principal subspace of the feature vector.

5
Experiments

Next, we evaluate the performance of
CAMIA
. Our evaluation is focused on the following two dimensions.

1.

How effective is
CAMIA
in inferring membership information? We show that
CAMIA
outperforms all prior solutions in terms of both AUC and TPR in low FPR regimes by a large margin.

2.

How effective is each individual signal in revealing the membership information? We find that
(i)
: the effectiveness of different signals vary, and
(ii)
: the effectiveness of the same signal also varies across different textual domains. Both findings in turn highlight the contribution of our proposed
CAMIA
, a holistic MIA framework that carefully combines all membership signals.

5.1
Setup

LLMs under MIA.

We conduct membership inference attacks on three types of models, all of which are provided with ground-truth membership labels. (1) Pythia Suite
[
9
]
contains models trained on the Pile dataset
[
32
]
with parameter sizes of 70M, 160M, 1.4B, 2.8B, 6.9B, and 12B. (2) Pythia-deduped contains models trained on the Pile dataset with the same parameter configurations as in Pythia Suite, after the training data is deduplicated.
(3) GPT-Neo Suite
[
10
]
contains models trained on the same dataset as Pythia Suite, with parameter sizes of 125M, 1.3B, and 2.7B.

Data.

We consider seven data domains from the Pile dataset, Pile-CC (general web), Wikipedia (knowledge sources), PubMed Central (academic papers), Arxiv (academic papers), HackerNews (dialogues), DM Mathematics (specialized-domain math dataset), and Github (specialized-domain code dataset).
For each domain, MIMIR benchmark
[
2
]
provides different levels of distinguishability between members and non-members. In most of our evaluations, we use the
7_gram_0.2
data split. Under this split, a non-member is selected for evaluation if less than
20
percent of its 7-gram substrings is a substring of some training data point of the target model. As highlighted in
[
2
]
, due to the nature of the LM training, a high n-gram overlap may indicate that substrings of non-members may be used for training. Therefore, when there is a large overlap for a non-member, the definition of its membership is ambiguous.

Baselines.

[

[

We also include a blind baseline where the adversary infers the membership directly from the content, without any information from the model
[
3

[

We note that this blind attack is not designed to attack the model. Rather, its purpose is to evaluate how distributional shifts between member and non-member data affect the attack’s performance. For non-trivial attacks, the performance should exceed that of the blind attack.

Data access for

As mentioned in Section

For each signal from Section

\exp(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X}))

Metrics.

As mentioned in Section
2
, the adversary compares the computed membership score against a threshold
\tau
, to make a binary decision–determining whether the input text was used for training or not. Following the literature
[
5
,
6
,
2
]
, we evaluate the effectiveness of MIAs using the following metrics: Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC), the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC) and the True Positive Rate (TPR) at low False Positive Rates (FPR). The ROC curve plots the TPR against the FPR at various threshold settings. The AUC-ROC is a single scalar value that summarizes the performance of attacks over all the threshold values. The TPR at a low FPR focuses on the attacker’s ability to correctly identify training set members while keeping the rate of false positives (incorrectly identified non-members) very low.

The value of
\tau
determines the trade-off between the TPR, the fraction of members that are correctly identified, and the FPR, the fraction of non-members that are misclassified as members. Intuitively, setting the threshold to an extremely large value results in both the TPR and FPR dropping to zero–while the attack avoids misclassifying any non-members as members, it also fails to predict any data point as member.

To validate the necessity of combining different signals together under the
CAMIA
framework compared with using one signal only, we examine the overlapping among the members identified by different signals. To do this, Specifically, we first gather the sets of identified members when the false positive rate (FPR) is below 1% for each signal. For each signal
f

\mathcal{C}_{f}

•

Fraction of newly identified members

1-\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{f}\bigcup\mathcal{C}_{\text{Loss}}|}{|\mathcal{C}_{f}|}

f
compared to the average loss.

1-\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{f}\bigcup\mathcal{C}_{\text{Loss}}|}{|\mathcal{C}_{\text{Loss}}|}

f

represents the set of members correctly identified by the average loss signal. From Figure

These findings suggest that different signals complement one another by identifying distinct sets of members, underscoring the importance of combining multiple signals for a more comprehensive identification.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we present
CAMIA
, a novel MIA framework specifically designed for pre-trained LLMs. Unlike traditional MIAs, our approach recognizes the unique, sequential nature of LLMs and the critical role that contextual factors play in their memorization tendencies. By accounting for token-level loss sequences, prefix complexity, and changes in next-token prediction loss across sequences,
CAMIA
is able to effectively capture context-dependent memorization patterns that previous methods overlook.
Our comprehensive evaluation demonstrates that
CAMIA
significantly outperforms existing MIAs, particularly at low false-positive rates. These results underline the importance of adapting MIAs to the structure of LLMs which generate predictions based on token sequences rather than single outputs.

Appendix A
Measurement of fluctuations

The
Approximate entropy
signal quantifies the amount of regularity and the unpredictability of fluctuations over the loss trajectory by comparing the frequency of repeating patterns. It does this by counting similar patterns of a certain length, denoted by
m
and then seeing how often these patterns remain similar when the length is increased by one, where the similarity tolerance is denoted by
r
. Formally, we define consecutive loss sequence of length
m
starting from
t
as
{u_{t}^{m}=(\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t}),\mathcal{L}_{t+1}(x_{t+1}),\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{t+m-1}(x_{t+m-1}))}
. The distance between two vectors
u_{t}^{m}
and
u_{t^{\prime}}^{m}
is defined as the maximum difference in their corresponding components, as follows.

\displaystyle d(u_{t}^{m},u_{t^{\prime}}^{m})=\max_{k=1,\ldots,m}\left|\mathcal{L}_{t+k-1}(x_{t+k-1})-\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}+k-1}(x_{t^{\prime}+k-1})\right|.

For each vector
u_{t}^{m}
, calculate the proportion of vectors
u_{t^{\prime}}^{m}
that are within a certain distance
r
:

\displaystyle C_{t}^{m}(r)=\frac{\text{Number of }t^{\prime}\text{ such that }d(u_{t}^{m},u_{t^{\prime}}^{m})\leq r}{T^{\prime}-m+1}.

Compute the logarithmic average of these measures over all
t
.

\displaystyle\Phi^{m}(r)=\frac{1}{T^{\prime}-m+1}\sum_{t=1}^{T^{\prime}-m+1}\ln C_{t}^{m}(r).

Approximate entropy is then defined as the difference between the logarithmic averages for sequences of length
m+1
and
m
:

\displaystyle f_{\text{ApEn}}(\mathbf{X})=\Phi^{m}(r)-\Phi^{m+1}(r).

In our evaluation, we set
m=8
and
r=0.8
, which has the best performance in attack when using the signal individually.

The high-level idea of
Lempel–Ziv complexity
is to measure how “diverse” the patterns that are present in the loss trajectory signal.
It quantifies the complexity in the next-token loss trajectory based on the Lempel-Ziv compression algorithm. It measures the complexity of a finite sequence by parsing it into the smallest number of distinct phrases not seen before in the sequence. We first map the loss trajectory into discrete bins and then compute the complexity of the binned loss trajectory. Specifically, the signal is defined as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\text{LZ}}(\mathbf{X})=\text{LZW}(\{B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{T^{\prime}}\}),

where
B_{t}
is the bin index corresponding to
\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t})
and LZW returns the total number of distinct phrases needed to describe the entire sequence
\{B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{T^{\prime}}\}
.

: we compute the ppl which takes the exponential of the cut-off loss. The cut-off time is the same as previous signals. We also include the signal difference obtained from repetition.

•

PPL
f_{\text{Cal, PPL}}
: we compute the calibrated ppl, which divide the
f_{\text{PPL}}
with the token diversity. The cut-off time is the same as previous signals. We also include the signal difference obtained from repetition.

•

Count below a constant threshold
f_{\text{CB}}
(defined in Section
3.2.3
): we set the threshold
\tau
to be
1,2,3
. The signal is also computed from the first
200
tokens. We also include the signal difference obtained from repetition.

•

Count below mean
f_{\text{CBM}}
(defined in Section
3.2.3
): we set the cut-off time to be the same as previous signals. We also include the signal difference obtained from repetition.

•

LZ complexity
f_{\text{LZ}}
(defined in Section
3.2.4
and formally defined in Appendix
A
): we map the loss into 3, 4, and 5 bins to compute the LZ complexity. The complexity is computed with respect to the first 200 tokens. We also include the signal difference obtained from repetition.

•

Count below the previous mean
f_{\text{CBPM}}
(defined in Section
3.2.3
): we set the cut-off time to be the same as previous signals.

•

Slope
f_{\text{Slope}}
(defined in Section
3.2.2
): we compute the slope with respect to the first
600
,
800
, and
1000
tokens. When the input text length is shorter than the threshold, we repeat the input text to construct a longer loss sequence.

•

Approximate entropy
f_{ApEn}
(defined in Section
3.2.4
and formally defined in Appendix
A
): we compute the approximate entropy based on the cut-off loss for the first
600
,
800
, and
1000
tokens. Similar to the computation of slope signal, we repeat the original text to when the input text length is lower than the cut-off time.

From the results on all signals, we observe that there is no signal that universally outperforms other signals. This highlights the importance of combining different signals.

Effectiveness of the attacks on other splits.

We provide the results on the other splits in the MIMIR benchmark dataset. Table
10
and Table
11
shows the performance on the
13_gram_0.2
split and
13_gram_0.2
split respectively. As noted in previous work
[
2
]
, as the overlap between members and non-members increases, membership ambiguity also grows. This means that some substrings of non-members may have been encountered by the model during training. As a result, distinguishing between members and non-members becomes more challenging for the attack. We observe that in scenarios where the attack performance is significantly better than random guessing (i.e., TPR at 1% FPR is greater than 1%),
CAMIA
consistently outperforms the existing baselines.
