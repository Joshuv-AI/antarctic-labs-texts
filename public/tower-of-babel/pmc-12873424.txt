ScRNA profiling of peripheral blood in kidney transplant recipients across rejection responses and treatments
Authors: Dan Wu, Jia You, Hongxia Liu, Kaile Zheng, Yan Zhu, Na Zeng, Hong Sun, Xuebin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudies have reported diverse functions of immune cells in response against the transplanted kidney, highlighting their significant roles in immunosuppressive response. Peripheral blood analyses showed differences in immune cell composition between kidney transplant recipients and healthy donors, however, the differences in peripheral blood immune landscape between patients with different rejection types and treatment phases remain unclear. Here, we present a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset of peripheral blood cells derived from five participants after kidney transplantation. The dataset comprises 98,595 quality-control passed cells along with clinical metadata, enabling a comparative analysis of immune cell populations before and after immunosuppression treatment as well as between different rejection types. We preliminarily identified major cell types including immune and non-immune cells, and further annotated the immune cell subsets. This transcriptomic data will serve as a resource to explore new cellular biomarkers and microenvironmental changes in peripheral blood of kidney recipients with different reaction types, and to deepen our insights into the role of immunosuppression treatment on graft rejection.Subject terms: Kidney diseases, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Background & Summary

For patients suffering from end-stage renal disease, which is rapidly rising in the ranked order of fatal diseases1,2, kidney transplantation has become a critical intervention to improve survival and quality of life than dialysis3,4. However, the success of transplantation is often hampered by the challenge of allograft rejection, an immune-mediated response against the transplanted organ. Rejection of a transplanted kidney can manifest in various forms, primarily T cell-mediated rejection (TCMR) characterized by the activation of T lymphocytes against transplanted organs5 and antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR) facilitated by the development of donor-specific antibodies6. Regarding TCMR in kidney transplantation, specific T cell subsets have been correlated with transplant rejection types7,8. Particularly noteworthy is the communication between regulatory cells in suppressing the cytotoxic function of T cells9, and protection from senescent macrophages on renal allograft rejection by suppressing CD8 + T cells was validated in mouse models8. As for ABMR in kidney transplantation, the frequency of specific B cell subsets, notably resting memory B cells, is associated with early ABMR in kidney transplant recipients, these findings underscore the importance of profiling B cell populations as a potential pre-transplant diagnostic tool for assessing rejection risk10–12. Previous study on preclinical mouse data demonstrated that follicular regulatory T cells (Tregs) restrained kidney allograft rejection by limiting alloreactive B cell responses13. These findings highlight the potential for more immunosuppressive approaches for kidney transplant recipients to avoid inappropriate immunosuppression.

Allograft rejection poses a considerable challenge in the management of kidney transplantation. Tacrolimus is a cornerstone immunosuppressant widely used in organ transplantation and autoimmune diseases to prevent graft rejection and manage immune responses. Tacrolimus demonstrates efficacy by reducing T cell-mediated responses, maintaining a favorable balance of Tregs in the transplant setting which is critical for long-term allograft survival14,15. The management of both ABMR and TCMR generally includes corticosteroids as first-line treatments. Methylprednisolone sodium succinate (MPS) is a frequently used corticosteroid in renal conditions for its immunosuppressive effects, yet individualizing dosing strategies based on pharmacokinetic assessments should be considered to optimize therapeutic outcomes16. Prednisolone acetate has demonstrated immunosuppressive effects beneficial for kidney treatments, particularly in renal transplant scenarios. Notably, the effectiveness of these treatments has been marked by variability, underscoring the necessity for personalized immunosuppressive strategies based on rejection types17,18.Immune cell responses to renal allograft under immunosuppression drugs have been investigated at the single-cell level by mass cytometry (CyTOF)19 and through in vitro T cell culture system20, nevertheless, the development of new therapeutic strategies to treat rejection of transplanted kidney is hindered by a limited understanding of the various immune cells and their communications in the immune microenvironment. Continued research is vital for addressing the complexities in therapeutic influence on immune cell biology in different transplant contexts.

Transcriptional signatures were indentified in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of kidney recipients with acute rejection21. Traditional biopsy methods often involve invasive procedures that may pose risks to patients, and recent advancements in the field of liquid biopsies utilizing peripheral blood samples offer a less invasive alternative for diagnosing and monitoring disease progression. The procedure of peripheral blood puncture is essential in various clinical settings for diagnostic testing, administering therapies, and monitoring patient health22,23. Elevated levels of biomarkers were shown to correlate with disease severity, demonstrating the clinical relevance of peripheral blood analyses24,25. Studies showed that immune cell composition in peripheral blood mononuclear cells differs between kidney transplant recipients and healthy donors26, and also differs after tacrolimus discontinuity in kidney transplant recipients27, highlighting the potential of peripheral blood puncture for the prediction of responses to kidney transplant and treatments. However, these aforementioned researches are often studied using bulk approaches, which ignore the variability between individual cells, likely masking the presence of distinct cell subpopulations in different scenarios. Despite that, a dataset is still absent that explore the differences in immune cells biology between different patient reactions after a kidney transplantation, and between before and after medical treatment.

Here, we present a single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of eight peripheral blood samples collected from five kidney transplant recipients: one with TCMR, two with ABMR and two with normal renal function and the peripheral blood samples were obtained from these patients before and after medical treatments (Fig. 1a). Sequencing data from all the samples were uniformly processed to eliminate potential batch effects (Fig. 1b). After initially performing quality control and dimensionality reduction, we clustered cell populations based on their gene expression profiles, and identified the major cell types including immune and non-immune cells, and further annotated the immune cell subsets according to the marker genes derived from previous published literatures or databases. Cell-type compositional analysis revealed distinct profiles of immune cell compositions across the different scenarios of kidney transplantation. Our dataset provides detailed coverage of the gene expression profiles of individual PBMCs of patients with different reactions after a kidney transplantation, and PBMCs of samples before and after medical treatment, thereby providing new insights into the various immune cells and their communications in the specific immune microenvironment.

Methods

Sample collection and ethical compliance

Our study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Changhai Hospital (Approval No. CHEC2021-133) and the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children’s Hospital (Approval No. 2025R171-E01). Patients were taken into consideration if they had a minimum follow-up period of 6 months post-transplantation, treated with a tacrolimus-based immunosuppressive regimen and/or showed rejection phenotypes (i.e. TCMR or ABMR) according to the Banff classification criteria28. Patients were excluded if they had pharmacologic kidney injury, or had multi-organ transplantation, or died during the study period. Potential participants were approached individually and were explained the purpose of the study, data collection methods, participant information sheet including confidentiality and their right to withdraw from the study. In addition, we removed all the personally identifiable information and used the rest for historical and research purposes. After being fully informed, participants signed written consent forms stating that their peripheral blood specimens could be used exclusively for research purposes related to this study.

The study included five human participants after at least six months of kidney transplantation: one recipient with TCMR, two recipients with ABMR and two recipients with normal renal function (Normal). Patients received maintenance immunosuppressive therapy after kidney transplantation. Peripheral blood samples were collected from the participants before and after treatment with targeted immunosuppressive regimens in case rejection (Table 1).

Single-cell suspension preparation

PBMCs were isolated using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation. Freshly prepared suspensions were diluted to a concentration of 700–1200 cells/μL and processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using the 10 × Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits v3.1 (Cat. No. 1000268).

Single-cell RNA library construction and sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the 10xGenomics Chromium platform. Single cells with barcoded gel beads were encapsulated into nanoliter-scale oil droplets (GEMs) via microfluidics. Following cell lysis, released mRNA hybridized to oligonucleotides bearing cell barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). Reverse transcription was performed within droplets, and the barcoded cDNAs were subsequently purified and amplified after droplet disruption. Libraries were constructed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (10 × Genomics, Cat. No. 1000268) per manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 150 bp). Single-cell suspensions (800–1200 cells/μL; viability 90–95%) were loaded onto Chromium chips targeting ~10,000 cells per sample. Cellular barcoding enabled tracing on parallelly massive cells at single-cell resolution, and sample indexing allowed high fidelity multiplexing.

Bioinformatic analysis

Preprocessing and quality control

The raw reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38-3.0.0) using Cell Ranger (version 7.0.1) for gene expression quantification and to generate a preliminary cell–gene expression matrix and the output matrix data were analyzed with the Seurat toolkit29 (version 5.1.0). To exclude low-quality cells, we filtered out cells with fewer than 200 or more than 10,000 genes detected. Erythrocyte cells were separated based on the expression of three marker genes (HBA1, HBA2 and HBB). The PercentageFeatureSet function was applied to calculate the percentage of mitochondrial and erythrocyte genome expression in individual cells, and cells with a percentage higher than 10% of mitochondrial or erythrocyte gene content were filtered out. The gene expression data were log-normalized. The normalized data were scaled and adjusted for sequencing depth variations. DoubletFinder30 was used to identify doublets from the top 10 principal components (PCs) with parameters pN = 0.25 and pK = 0.09, and the identified doublets were removed. Only singlet cells were retained for downstream analyses. The quality control and filtering results were summarized in Table 2.

Data integration and clustering

After quality control, the filtered expression matrix was processed using Seurat (v5.1.0). FindVariableFeatures was applied to identify highly variable genes that capture key transcriptional heterogeneity. Gene expression values were standardized across cells using ScaleData. RunPCA was used to reduce dimensionality. RunHarmony function in Harmony31 (version 1.2.0) was applied to correct batch effects based on sample identity in the top 50 PCs. A shared nearest neighbor graph was built with the corrected principal components using FindNeighbors and unsupervised clustering was performed on the data using the Louvain algorithm with FindClusters at a resolution of 0.3. RunUMAP was used for nonlinear dimensionality reduction and for visualization of the identified cell clusters.

Annotation of cell types

We identified cluster-specific marker genes using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat with a log fold-change threshold of 0.25 and a minimum expression percentage of 25%. To assign cell identities, we employed SingleR32 which compared the transcriptomic profiles of each cluster against multiple reference datasets, including the Human Primary Cell Atlas, the Novershtern hematopoietic dataset and immune-specific references. To validate and refine the annotations, we used scMayoMap33 based on a blood-specific reference in the built-in scMayoMapDatabase. Cell-type labels were manually curated and assigned, especially for those cell types in closely related lineages (e.g., T cells, B cells, NK cells) by sub-clustering and by examining the expression of canonical marker genes.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Intercellular communication networks were inferred using CellChat34 (version 1.6.1). Seurat objects were stratified by the sample groups and were transformed into CellChat to infer intercellular communications between cell types. Further processing involved identifying over-expressed genes, setting the ligand-receptor interaction database, computing communication probability, aggregating pathways and computing network centrality. To assess and compare interactions across different conditions, CellChat objects were merged using the “mergeCellChat” function. Differences in intercellular communications were depicted using functions such as compareInteractions, netVisual_diffInteraction, netVisual_heatmap and rankNet.

Data Records

All the raw scRNA-seq data have been deposited on the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa-human) under accession number HRA01287435. These data include sequencing paired fastq files named according to the convention, i.e. SampleID.R1.fq.gz and SampleID.R2.fq.gz (read 1 and read 2). The processed data (Cell Ranger outputs) are available at the Open Archive for Miscellaneous Data (Omix) database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix/) under accession number OMIX01234736. These data include sparse expression matrix, cell barcode list and annotated gene features for each sample.

Technical Validation

Data quality

Eight peripheral blood samples were collected from five kidney transplant recipients suffering from different rejection types, i.e. one recipient with TCMR, two recipients with ABMR and two recipients with normal renal function (Table 1). The single-cell libraries were constructed and subsequently sequenced. Raw sequencing quality metrics for each scRNA-seq sample were detailed in Table 2. More than 89.8% of bases in all samples achieved a Q30 score or higher, which indicates a very low sequencing error rate in general. After quality control and raw data filtering, the genome mapping rates for these libraries were over 92.4%, indicating adequate sequencing quality for further data analysis. Each sample contained more than 9,600 captured cells. Most of the cells had small proportion of mitochondrial and hemoglobin-derived transcripts over the total transcripts, indicating that the majority of cells were of high quality with low contamination (Fig. 2a). We observed a weak negative correlation between mitochondrial transcript fraction and the number of detected genes per cell and a strong positive correlation between mRNA content and the total number of reads (Fig. 2b). Quality control of the eight libraries demonstrated no significant batch-specific differences among them (Fig. 2c,d). Altogether, the above quality-control measures highlighted the robustness of the sequencing analysis and the high-quality of our scRNA-seq dataset.

Cell type compositions

After quality control, a total of 98,595 high-quality cells were obtained. We successfully identified ten distinct cell clusters with individual clusters containing between 159 and 69,326 cells (Fig. 3a), including 69,326 T cells, 16,317 monocytes, 4,493 B cells, 3,845 NK cells, 1,636 neutrophils, 1,108 platelets/megakaryocytes, 1,010 dendritic cells, 437 plasma cells, 264 basophils and159 hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). The expression levels of genes that distinguish these clusters were visualized in a DotPlot (Fig. 3b). Subsequently, we successfully identified 20 distinct cell subpopulations (Fig. 3c).

To elucidate cell layer dynamics, we analyzed the proportions of cells for different kidney rejection type at the assayed treatment stages. We observed a remarkable difference in the cellular compositions between the five sample groups classified by rejection type and treatment phase (Fig. 4a). T cells were relatively more abundant in peripheral blood of recipients with TCMR, either before or after treatment, which is consistent with the characterization of TCMR outlined by the activation of T lymphocytes against transplanted organs5. We identified differentially expressed genes between all cells from a condition versus all cells from all other conditions using the FindMarkers function, and we found that the LYZ gene, encoding lysozyme associated with activity of myeloid cells37, was highly expressed specifically in peripheral blood of recipients with ABMR (Fig. 4b). It has been reported that use of the Rho kinase inhibitor Y27632, formulated with lysozyme, reduced macrophage accumulation and lymphangiogenesis in experimental models of acute renal rejection38. These results align with known biological features of kidney transplant rejection, reinforcing the reliability of the data in capturing distinct transcriptional landscapes of peripheral blood of kidney recipients with different rejection types.

Cell-cell communication

The cellular microenvironment plays a pivotal role in regulating cellular behavior, influencing disease progression and treatment responses in the context of various diseases and conditions, including kidney transplant rejection. We, therefore, employed CellChat to explore the impact of the cellular microenvironment on kidney transplant rejection.

We inferred intercellular communications for the five sample groups classified by rejection type and treatment phase, and constructed cell–cell communication networks. Elevated numbers and strength of intercellular interactions were detected in the recipients after presence of rejection, either ABMR or TCMR, and attractively, remarkable reduced numbers and strength of intercellular interactions were observed in recipients with ABMR before treatment (Fig. 4c). The circle plots (Fig. 4d) summarized the intercellular communications between cell types in the five sample groups classified by rejection types and treatment conditions. Balanced interactions were more frequently observed in Normal and TCMR samples, while ABMR, especially ABMR_post, exhibited enhanced cell–cell communications centered around B cells, monocytes, and plasma cells (Fig. 4d). We deduced the transformation of cell population roles based on changes in out-degree and in-degree within the communication network (Fig. 4e). Among all cell types, dendritic cells exhibited the highest incoming interactions in Normal patients, whereas NK cells displayed the highest incoming interactions in groups with graft rejection; B cells possessed highest outgoing interactions in recipients with ABMR before treatment while the highest outgoing interaction was seen in Dendritic cells in recipients with ABMR after treatment.

We identified enriched signaling pathways based on intercellular interaction information. We further compared the information flow for each signaling pathway between patients at different stages of graft rejection. The information flow for a given signaling pathway is defined by the sum of communication probability among all pairs of cell groups in the inferred network. We found that some pathways, including CD45, CD22, ADGRE5 in ABMR and ADGRE5, CD2, TIGHT in TCMR, maintained similar information flow before and after treatment (Fig. 4f). In contrast, some pathways prominently changed their information flow at time after treatment as compared to before treatment (Fig. 4f). The activity of some pathways, which are important for B cells’ functionality, changed in response to ABMR: information flows in some pathways significantly decreased after treatment, including MHC-I39, CD4540, MIF41, ADGRE542 and RESISTIN, while information flows in some other pathways increased after ABMR such as TGFβ43, CD2344, PECAM145 and MHC-II46. In addition, the activity of some pathways, which are pivotal in shaping T cell function, varied in response to TCMR: information flows in some pathways significantly decreased after treatment, e.g. SELL, JAM, CD2247, IL148, SEMA349 and FLT350, while information flows in some other pathways increased such as MHC-II51, CD8652, PECAM153, BAFF54, CXCL55 and ANGPT56. Notably, some pathways exhibited distinct patterns of information flow between ABMR and TCMR, indicating rejection type-specific signaling dynamics.

Taken together, these preliminary processing and analyses not only demonstrated the high quality of the dataset, but also revealed the potential for exploring new cellular biomarkers and microenvironmental changes in peripheral blood samples collected from kidney transplant recipients with different reaction types, especially changes between patients before and after a rejection treatment.
