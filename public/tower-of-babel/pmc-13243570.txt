Machine-learning emergent constraints on surface albedo feedback over Arctic land regions
Authors: Linfei Yu, Guoyong Leng, Lei Yao, Qiuhong Tang, Manfred Wendisch, Jiali Qiu, Shengzhi Huang, Xiaoyong Liao, Jian Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface albedo feedback (SAF) amplifies warming in northern high latitudes, affecting the Arctic climate system, ecosystems, infrastructure, and global trade routes. However, Earth system models (ESMs) exhibit large uncertainties in SAF projections, complicating future Arctic warming estimates. Here, we develop a machine-learning method based on emergent constraints (ECs) and use in-situ observations to constrain SAF projections over Arctic land regions. Our approach leverages a physical relationship between historical albedo-temperature dynamics (1985–2014) and future SAF (2070–2099) across ESM ensembles. The constrained SAF is reduced by 0.29–0.52 W m-2 K-1 across emission scenarios, with uncertainties decreased by 45–55% compared to unconstrained projections. These findings enhance confidence in regional climate projections, offering more precise insights for climate adaptation and policy in vulnerable high-latitude communities.Subject terms: Climate change, Projection and prediction

Introduction

The Arctic is undergoing warming at rates more than twice the global average. This phenomenon is referred to as Arctic amplification; it is reshaping ecosystems, economies, and geopolitics with unprecedented speed1–4. As a result, there is widespread thawing permafrost that destabilizes critical infrastructure—roads, pipelines, and buildings—causing ground subsidence threatening indigenous communities and incurring economic losses projected to reach tens of billions of dollars by 20505,6. Although the dramatic Arctic sea ice retreat observed since the 1970s has seemingly come to a halt since around 2005, the larger open water regions of the Arctic Ocean provide new opportunities for maritime pathways, such as the Northern Sea Route, potentially slashing transit times between Europe and Asia but raising risks of oil spills, ecosystem disruption, and increased greenhouse gas emissions from shipping2,3. These cascading changes underscore the urgency of understanding the drivers of Arctic warming to improve predictive capabilities to inform adaptation and mitigation strategies.

Arctic warming is driven by several interconnected mechanisms, including surface albedo feedback (SAF), ocean heat transport, lapse rate feedback, cloud feedback and other processes7–9. Central to Arctic amplification is the SAF, which is a critical mechanism amplifying warming over both land and ocean10–13. As global temperatures rise, melting snow and ice reduce surface reflectivity (albedo), allowing darker surfaces—open water or bare soil—to absorb more solar radiation, which further accelerates warming in a self-reinforcing loop12–17. Over northern high-latitude land areas, this feedback is driven by shrinking snow cover and vegetation changes, such as the greening of tundra into shrublands, which darken the surface and enhance absorption of solar radiation increasing the surface temperature18–21. SAF plays a critical factor in projections of Arctic land warming, yet its representation in Earth system models (ESMs) remains fraught with uncertainty20. ESMs struggle to accurately simulate complex processes and feedback mechanisms like snow metamorphosis, cloud-surface interactions, and vegetation dynamics, often overestimating or underestimating SAF’s strength due to sparse observational data and simplified parameterizations18. These uncertainties translate into divergent warming projections, undermining confidence in model results and complicating policy decisions for vulnerable Arctic regions, where even small temperature changes can trigger tipping points in permafrost stability or sea ice loss.

Emergent constraints (ECs) have proven to be a promising technique for reducing ESM uncertainties by exploiting physically based relationships between observable climate variables and future projections across model ensembles22,23. By anchoring projections to real-world data, ECs can refine estimates of climate processes like SAF, offering a path to more reliable projections22,24. However, traditional EC approaches often assume linear relationships between predictors and predictands,22–24, failing to capture the nonlinear complexities of the Arctic climate system. Moreover, many of these EC methods rely on gridded observational datasets (i.e., satellite or reanalysis products), which suffer from inconsistencies in remote regions like the Arctic due to limited sensor coverage and data interpolation errors9,25, particularly for land surface albedo26–28. As such, a low coefficient of determination (R2) less than 0.5 is often reported by previous ECs23,29–37. Lack of out-of-sample tests and verifications also cast doubt on the confidence of the constraints24,38–40. These limitations reduce the robustness and effectiveness of conventional ECs, leaving significant gaps in our ability to constrain SAF and predict accurate warming trajectories.

In this study, we address these challenges by developing a machine-learning method based on emergent constraints (ECs) to constrain SAF projections over Arctic land areas (defined by latitudes larger than 60°N). We identify physical and robust relationships between historical surface albedo-temperature dynamics and future SAF across ESMs from Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), based on which future SAF is constrained by leveraging high-quality in-situ observations of surface albedo and near-surface air temperature. Our approach overcomes the shortcomings of linear ECs and gridded observational datasets by using machine-learning to capture complex interactions and by basing our constraints on direct measurements from flux networks, which avoid the uncertainties related to satellite retrievals or reanalysis products. The revealed emergent relationships are carefully confirmed via out-of-sample tests, leave-one-out tests and verified against in-situ observations. By significantly reducing model uncertainty, we provide more precise estimates of SAF projections, which has great implications for climate impact assessment and adaptation planning for policymakers, indigenous communities, and stakeholders in northern high-latitude regions.

Results

Projected changes and uncertainties in surface albedo feedback (SAF)

SAF is driven by two key components: the sensitivity of planetary albedo αp to land surface albedo αs changes (∂αp/∂αs), and the response of αs to air temperature Ta changes (∂αs/∂Ta)10,18. We analyzed these components across four emission scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, SSP5-8.5) using a 30-year moving window from 35 CMIP6 ESMs (Supplementary Table 1). The strength of the SAF fluctuates around 1 W m−2 K−1, with statistically significant differences in averages between scenarios (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Fig. 1). In the low-emission scenario (i.e., SSP1-2.6), SAF weakens significantly (p < 0.05) by the late 21st century due to declines in both ∂αp/∂α and ∂αs/∂Ta (Fig. 1b, c). Conversely, under SSP2-4.5, a decrease in ∂αs/∂Ta dominantly drives SAF (Fig. 1c). For high-emission scenarios (SSP3-7.0, SSP5-8.5), SAF variations are primarily governed by ∂αs/∂Ta, reflecting changes in snow cover and vegetation dynamics (Fig. 1a–c; Supplementary Fig. 2). These differences arise from scenario-specific greenhouse gas concentrations affecting temperature and albedo dynamics after 2035 (Supplementary Figs. 3–5).

Spatially, SAF exhibits positive signals across the region, peaking in Alaska, the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, central and eastern Russia (>1.4 W m−2 K−1 under SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-4.5; Fig. 1d, e). Under SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5, SAF weakens in these areas but intensifies in northwestern Siberia, driven by enhanced vegetation greening and snowmelt (Fig. 1f, g; Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). Similar patterns of SAF were found for the near future period 2040 − 2069 and projections from Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 5 (CMIP5) (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). However, ESM uncertainties are substantial, with inter-model standard deviations of 0.46–0.74 W m−2 K−1 (43–81% of ensemble means), particularly in Alaska, eastern Russia, and Greenland (Fig. 1d–g). These uncertainties highlight the need for observational constraints to refine SAF projections.

Emergent constraints on future SAF and the resulting warming rate

To address uncertainties in ESM projections, we developed an emergent constraints (ECs) framework leveraging in-situ observations to refine future SAF estimates over Arctic land areas. We identified a robust negative relationship between historical (1985–2014) response of land surface albedo (αs) to air temperature changes (∂αs/∂Ta) and future (2070–2099) SAF across the CMIP6 ESM ensemble. Linear regression (LR) yields R² values of 0.28–0.48 (p < 0.05), indicating a moderate correlation, whereas random forest regression (RF) achieves R2 of 0.70–0.88 (p < 0.05), uncovering a stronger, nonlinear connection (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 10a–c) across different emission scenarios. This suggests that models simulating a higher ∂αs/∂Ta in the history—reflecting greater albedo sensitivity to past air temperature changes—tend to project lower SAF in the future, likely due to stabilizing vegetation dynamics or snow cover limits. This relationship’s strength across SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 scenarios underscores its robustness, offering a pathway to constrain future projections using in-situ observations of αs and air temperature.

The representativeness of in-situ observations is critical for effective constraints. We validated that local ∂αs/∂Ta, derived from flux network sites41–43, correlates strongly with regional ∂αs/∂Ta (R² = 0.59 for LR, 0.85 for RF; Fig. 2b), confirming that these sites capture regional albedo-temperature dynamics44,45. This correlation holds across diverse land cover types, from tundra to boreal forests, enhancing confidence in their applicability. Furthermore, historical ∂αs/∂Ta from flux network sites exhibits a significant (p < 0.05) relationship with future SAF, consistent across emission scenarios (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Figs. 10d–f). The RF model effectively captures sharp transitions in surface albedo, reflecting nonlinear interactions among snow cover, vegetation dynamics, and radiative forcing (r = 0.63 − 0.83, p < 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 11–13). These transitions are physically plausible, aligning with substantial inter-model spread in temporal variability and thresholds for snowmelt and vegetation greening, where vegetation fluctuations and vegetation–snow interactions emerge as dominant drivers (Supplementary Note 1 and Fig. 14). Subgrouping analysis confirms that removing a small subset of “sharp transition” models would not substantially alter our major conclusions (Supplementary Note 2 and Figs. 15−17). By basing our ECs on high-quality in-situ measurements, we avoid uncertainties inherent in gridded satellite or reanalysis datasets. These findings establish a reliable bridge between past observations and future SAF projections.

Our machine-learning-based ECs using random forest (ECRF) transforms SAF estimates and the probability density functions (PDFs) of constrained SAF are statistically different from the raw projections (Kolmogorov−Smirnov test, Supplementary Note 3). Raw CMIP6 models project future SAF at 1.06–1.21 W m−2 K−1 by 2070–2099 across the four scenarios. After applying ECRF, it decreases to 0.60–0.92 W m−2 K−1, a reduction of 24–46%, revealing systematic ESM overestimation (Fig. 3a). This correction aligns with model biases in snowmelt and vegetation parameterizations (Supplementary Fig. 18), which amplify SAF in unconstrained models. ECRF also slashes uncertainty by 45–55%, far surpassing linear EC (14–25%) methods (ECLR) (Fig. 3b), due to RF’s ability to capture nonlinear interactions. Leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity tests confirm that neither the emergent relationships nor the constrained SAF estimates are heavily influenced by any individual model, demonstrating strong robustness to model selection (Supplementary Note 4 and Figs. 19 − 21). These adjustments narrow the range of expected temperature increases32,46–49, offering a clearer picture of future Arctic climate.

Physical mechanisms underlying the emergent constraints

To elucidate the physical basis of the ECs, we applied a piecewise structural equation model (pSEM) based on land surface energy balances (see Methods and Supplementary Note 5). In pSEM, a positive standardized path coefficient (ρ) indicates that an increase in the independent variable drives an increase in the dependent variable, and vice versa. During the historical period, rising greenhouse gas concentrations (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O) significantly increased surface net (downward minus upward) irradiances (Rn, comprising net surface shortwave radiation; ρ = 0.89, p < 0.05), amplifying near-surface air temperature (ρ = 0.93, p < 0.05; Fig. 4a). This warming altered land surface properties, boosting vegetation (leaf area index, LAI; ρ = 0.96, p < 0.05) and reducing snow cover fraction (SCF; ρ = -0.96, p < 0.05).

R2 represents the coefficient of determination for each path; “*” represents statistical significance with p < 0.05.

These changes drove SAF via αs. Decreased snow cover strongly lowered αs (ρ = 0.80, p < 0.05), while increased vegetation had a weaker effect (ρ = −0.23, p > 0.05), trapping more energy (Rn sensitivity to αs: -1.79 W m−2 %−1) and further warming the surface, forming a positive feedback loop (Fig. 4a). By 2070–2099, this loop persists across SSP1-2.6 to SSP5-8.5 scenarios (Supplementary Fig. 22), with vegetation’s influence on αs strengthening (ρ = −0.33, p < 0.05) and snow’s weakening (ρ = 0.69, p < 0.05; Fig. 4b). Thus, historical and future SAF share consistent physical drivers—snowmelt and greening—explaining the robust EC relationship across diverse ESM parameterizations (Fig. 4c, d).

Out-of-sample tests, alternative periods (1975−2004, 2040–2069), choice of flux stations and CMIP5 ensembles confirm EC’s robustness (Supplementary Note 6, Figs. 23–26, Table 2). By capturing these mechanisms, ECRF corrects future SAF overestimations by 24–46%, enhancing Arctic warming projection accuracy.

Verification of the emergent constraints

Debates on EC’s robustness have intensified due to limited validation24,38–40, since there are no observations for the future climate. To address this, we conducted a two-stage cross-validation, training ECs on ESMs output during 1985–1999 and verifying their performance against in-situ observations during 2000–2014. We compared our machine-learning-based EC (ECRF) with the traditional linear EC (ECLR), which assumes a linear predictor-predictand relationship as previously developed22,31. Both ECRF (R² = 0.80) and ECLR (R² = 0.39) show strong predictive skill for 2000–2014, and the constrained local albedo−temperature sensitivity (∂αs/∂Ta) aligns more closely with in-situ measurements (Fig. 5a, b). ECRF tends to surpass ECLR’s performance in terms of uncertainty reductions. To further validate the robustness of our methodology and results, we conducted a hindcast experiment spanning 2000–2014, benchmarking our approach against ERA5 reanalysis data. The results show that the ECRF-constrained SAF estimates align more closely with ERA5 than both the unconstrained and linearly constrained variants (Supplementary Fig. 27). This demonstrates the robustness of our proposed ECs for constraining future SAF projections using historical observations.

When the ECs trained on 1985–2014 data are applied to future projections, both ECRF and ECLR lead to smaller SAF estimates with reduced uncertainty. ECRF lowers the mean SAF estimates by 0.29–0.52 W m−2 K−1 and reduces the uncertainty by 45–55%, which more than double that by ECLR (Fig. 5c, d). These results validate ECRF’s effectiveness, enhancing confidence in future SAF projections across SSP1-2.6 to SSP5-8.5 scenarios. By leveraging nonlinear random forest regression, ECRF better captures complex albedo dynamics, offering a superior approach to constrain Arctic warming projections.

Discussion

Arctic warming is driven by a complex interplay of feedbacks (e.g., SAF, lapse rate feedback, cloud feedbacks) and forcings (e.g., ocean heat transport)7–9. Our analyses reveal SAF as a key contributor to Arctic land warming trend, exhibiting strong seasonal variability (Fig. 1e; Supplementary Note 7; Supplementary Figs. 28, 29). In spring, accelerated snowmelt exposes darker underlying surfaces, thereby increasing shortwave radiation absorption and triggering further warming18,20. This amplification persists into summer through interactions with residual snow cover and vegetation phenology19,25. SAF plays a more modest role in autumn, mediated by delayed snow onset and prolonged vegetation greenness, while its influence in winter is negligible owing to minimal insolation and the overriding effects of ocean heat transport48. Annually, SAF accounts for approximately 37 − 56% of the Arctic land warming rate, but substantial uncertainties in ESMs hinder precise projections of future Arctic climate change (Supplementary Fig. 29). We address this critical issue by leveraging flux observations and machine learning to enhance confidence in Arctic climate projections.

By developing a machine-learning-based emergent constraint (ECRF), we reduce SAF by 24–46% and its uncertainty by 45–55%, leveraging high-quality in-situ observations to deliver more precise projections critical for adaptation and mitigation planning. The root of ESM overestimation lies in biased representations of snow and vegetation dynamics across high latitudes, the core components of SAF50–52. Many models simulate overly rapid snowmelt, projecting linear albedo declines that fail to account for physical constraints (Supplementary Figs. 18, 30), such as persistent snowpack in Greenland, high-altitude regions, or late-season snow cover in northern Canada51–53. These assumptions amplify SAF by overestimating the exposure of darker surfaces. Similarly, ESMs often predict excessive tundra greening (Supplementary Figs. 18, 30), anticipating widespread shrub expansion that disregards ecological limits like nutrient scarcity, water availability, or permafrost stability, which slow vegetation transitions in reality52. For instance, studies highlight poor model representation of vegetation controls on snow-albedo feedbacks and the invariance of LAI across diverse canopy structures54,55. Furthermore, disturbance processes such as wildfires, which are often inadequately captured in current ESMs, represent a significant additional source of uncertainty. Wildfires can profoundly reshape vegetation structure, potentially increasing post-fire albedo through exposure of mineral soils or snow, thereby modulating SAF in boreal and Arctic ecosystems56. Collectively, these limitations underscore the urgent need for enhanced ESM representations of vegetation dynamics and disturbance regimes. Our ECRF addresses these flaws by employing random forest regression to capture nonlinear interactions between snow cover, vegetation shifts, and albedo responses53,57, achieving R² of 0.70–0.88 compared to 0.28–0.48 for linear ECs (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 10). In-situ data, drawn from flux sites across Alaska, Russia, and Canada, outperform gridded datasets, which suffer from sparse coverage and interpolation errors in Arctic regions. By grounding projections in direct measurements, ECRF reveals nuanced albedo dynamics—such as gradual greening rates—that correct ESM tendencies to overstate SAF’s warming contribution.

To ensure ECRF’s reliability, we conducted extensive sensitivity tests, confirming its robustness across multiple dimensions. The emergent relationship between historical αs sensitivity to air temperature (∂αs/∂Ta) and future SAF holds for all four SSP scenarios, demonstrating consistency under diverse greenhouse gas trajectories. We restrict the historical period to start from 1985, as the influence of internal climate variability is relatively small and in-situ observations are readily available58. Temporal tests further validate ECRF across different historical (1975–2004, 1985–2014) and future (2040–2069, 2070–2099) periods, showing stable predictive power despite varying climate states (Supplementary Fig. 24). We also conducted robustness tests by applying ECRF to CMIP5 ensembles (Supplementary Fig. 25). This confirms that the constraint remains effective across earlier model generations, supporting its generalizability. In addition, based on ensemble means of models from the same modeling center, yielded consistent results—indicating that SAF is also overestimated in CMIP6 models (Supplementary Fig. 31). The ECRF-constrained SAF estimates exhibit remarkable stability across all subsets of flux stations, with inter-subset variations below 5% and consistent uncertainty reductions of 43–50%, underscoring the robust representativeness of the available station network (Supplementary Fig. 26). Moreover, hindcast experiments demonstrate that the ECRF method consistently outperforms the linear EC approach, achieving superior predictive skill and closer alignment with both in-situ observations and ERA5 reanalysis data (Supplementary Fig. 27). Together, these tests underscore ECRF’s robustness and reliability, making it a powerful tool for refining SAF projections under a wide range of conditions.

Despite these advances, our approach has limitations that warrant further exploration. In-situ flux sites, while highly accurate, are unevenly distributed, with sparse coverage in remote areas like eastern Siberia, the Canadian High Arctic, or Greenland’s interior, potentially underrepresenting localized surface albedo dynamics driven by patchy snow or variable vegetation. This spatial bias could affect ECRF’s precision in capturing fine-scale processes, such as abrupt snowmelt in coastal regions or shrub encroachment in drier uplands. Future studies could address this by integrating emerging satellite technologies, such as hyperspectral surface albedo measurements, which offer near-global coverage at higher resolutions. Expanding flux tower networks to underrepresented regions would also enhance observational density, strengthening EC applications. Although our study centers on Arctic land areas due to limited in-situ ocean observations, the oceanic component of SAF—especially related to seasonal sea ice dynamics and albedo changes—remains critical yet underexplored. Future work could extend EC-based methods to incorporate satellite-derived sea ice concentration and radiative fluxes, enabling data-driven constraints on oceanic SAF and bridging land-ocean feedbacks for a more comprehensive view of Arctic climate change. Moreover, we implement ECs to SAF only, without constraining other related feedbacks like atmospheric heat transport, permafrost carbon release, or cloud radiative effects, which regulate Arctic warming in complex ways53,59. For instance, although sea-ice retreat boosts local shortwave absorption, it often triggers a compensatory reduction in poleward atmospheric heat transport60–62. Comparable offsets occur vertically: heightened humidity and low-cloud formation enhance longwave trapping, while increased ocean heat uptake tempers the lapse-rate feedback, redistributing SAF-induced heat anomalies across seasons and altitudes63,64. SAF’s induced warming can also be partially offset by cooling contributions from turbulent sensible and latent heat-flux feedbacks from a surface-energy-balance perspective or by atmospheric heat transport from a top-of-atmosphere perspective60,61,65,66. Such interdependencies highlight the challenges in mechanistic attribution and accurate projection of Arctic warming. Developing multi-feedback ECs could provide a more holistic view of Arctic climate, though this would require sophisticated modeling to disentangle overlapping signals. Nevertheless, ECRF’s robustness, validated through our sensitivity tests, ensures its immediate utility for SAF-focused projections.

The implications of our findings are transformative for Arctic policy, adaptation, and global climate science. Overpredicted warming rates in raw ESMs risk misallocating resources for critical adaptation measures, such as reinforcing infrastructure against permafrost thaw, protecting Indigenous communities’ traditional livelihoods, or conserving fragile Arctic ecosystems (Supplementary Fig. 32). Although permafrost thaw and its associated impacts are already underway—and certain thresholds may be crossed irrespective of SAF magnitude—mitigated future SAF and attendant potential reductions in warming rates over northern Russia and Alaska could delay disruptions to transport networks and traditional hunting grounds. These refined projections enable policymakers to prioritize targeted strategies, such as enhanced land-use planning to preserve permafrost or restoration projects to bolster ecosystem resilience. Scientifically, ECRF’s nonlinear framework and in-situ foundation establish a blueprint for constraining other climate feedbacks, from tropical cloud dynamics to methane emissions in wetlands. By significantly reducing uncertainty in SAF, our study equips stakeholders with actionable insights to safeguard communities, infrastructure, and biodiversity, while advancing the precision of global climate models for a warming world.

Methods

Observations and CMIP6 simulations

We analyzed the outputs of CMIP6 Earth system model (ESM) ensemble (35 models; Supplementary Table 1), covering SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 scenarios from 1985–2099. Monthly outputs included downward shortwave radiation, upward shortwave radiation, shortwave radiation incoming at the top of the atmosphere, shortwave radiation outgoing at the top of the atmosphere, near-surface air temperature (Ta), snow cover fraction (SCF), and leaf area index (LAI). Greenhouse gas concentrations (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O), cloud area fraction, convective/stratiform cloud optical depth were sourced from scenario forcings. The first realization (r1i1p1f1) of each model is used and an equal weight is assigned to each model in the multi-model analysis. Simulations from ESMs are commonly interpolated to a 1°×1° resolution using the bilinear interpolation method.

In-situ observations of daily mean air temperature, incoming shortwave (solar) radiation and outgoing shortwave radiation from 38 flux network sites (Alaska, Canada, Russia; Supplementary Table 3) are obtained from the FLUXNET 2015, AmeriFlux and ICOS (Integrated Carbon Observation System) (Supplementary Fig. 33a)41–43. We focus on the historical period from 1985 onward, when reliable and continuous observational data become readily available. In-situ measurements were quality-controlled, and missing values are filled using over 10,000 meteorological stations including Global Historical Climatology Network-Daily (GHCNd)67 (Supplementary Fig. 33b), satellite-based (GLASS and CLARA-A3)68,69 and reanalysis products (ERA5 and MERRA-2)70,71 (Supplementary Table 4). Missing air temperature records were filled using the nearest neighbor method, while αs is constructed via a regression model. All the simulations and observations are aggregated to the annual scale to be consistent with SAF constraint. Details on the data filling process can be found in Supplementary Note 8 and Figs. 34 and 35.

Emergent constraint framework

To constrain future SAF (2070–2099), we developed an emergent constraint (EC) framework linking historical (1985–2014) ∂αs/∂Ta (predictor) to future SAF (predictand). Like previous studies12,22,23,29, the ECs are used to adjust SAF projections post-hoc rather than modifying model parameterizations during simulations. Two EC methods were applied: a linear EC (ECLR), assuming a linear predictor-predictand relationship like previous studies21,23,32, and a machine-learning-based EC (ECRF) using random forest regression to capture nonlinearities. For ECRF, we trained random forests on CMIP6 outputs of ∂αs/∂Ta and SAF. The 10-Fold cross-validation is adopted to test the potential overfitting issue of RF (Supplementary Note 9). ECLR was fitted using ordinary least squares (Supplementary Note 10). Both methods were evaluated with R² and p-values (p < 0.05 threshold).

Attribution analysis

To explore SAF’s physical drivers, we constructed a piecewise structural equation model (pSEM)74, linking greenhouse gases, Rn, Ta, LAI, SCF, and αs (Supplementary Note 5). Path coefficients (ρ) were estimated using standardized regressions, with significance at p < 0.05. A positive ρ suggests that an increase in the independent variable results in a rise of the dependent variable, while a negative ρ suggests that an increase in the independent variable leads to a decrease in the dependent variable. The direct effect refers to the immediate impact of one variable on another without involving intermediaries, while the indirect effect indicates the influence mediated through one or more intermediate variables. The pSEM has been widely adopted for decomposition of complex causal relationships74. Here, it was fitted using the R package ‘piecewiseSEM’ (v2.3.0.1).

Sensitivity tests

The robustness of our proposed emergent constraints (ECs) was evaluated across multiple dimensions. We applied both ECRF and ECLR to all SSP scenarios, alternative historical (1985–2014) and future (2040–2069) periods, and CMIP5 ensembles (Supplementary Table 2). In addition, we conducted model community-based evaluations using ensemble means from models developed by the same institutions, assessing both the strength of fit and the consistency of SAF estimates (Supplementary Fig. 24). Out-of-sample tests with randomly selected 20% holdout sets confirmed the stability of the constraints, demonstrating that the RF-based emergent relationship is robust and outperforms the LR model (Supplementary Note 6 and Fig. 23). Leave-one-out sensitivity tests were used to assess whether the constrained estimates are disproportionately influenced by any individual CMIP6 model or flux station (Supplementary Note 4 and Figs. 15, 17, 19 − 21, 26). Collectively, these evaluations confirm the applicability and reliability of the ECs under a wide range of conditions.
