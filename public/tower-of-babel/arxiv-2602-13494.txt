Quantum Speedups for Group Relaxations of Integer Linear Programs
Brandon Augustino, Dylan Herman, Guneykan Ozgul, Jacob Watkins, Atithi Acharya, Enrico Fontana, Junhyung Lyle Kim, Shouvanik Chakrabarti

Abstract
Integer Linear Programs (ILPs) are a flexible and ubiquitous model for discrete optimization problems. Solving ILPs is NP-Hard yet of great practical importance. Super-quadratic quantum speedups for ILPs have been difficult to obtain because classical algorithms for many-constraint ILPs are global and exhaustive, whereas quantum frameworks that offer super-quadratic speedup exploit local structure of the objective and feasible set. We address this via quantum algorithms for Gomory’s group relaxation. The group relaxation of an ILP is obtained by dropping nonnegativity on variables that are positive in the optimal solution of the linear programming (LP) relaxation, while retaining integrality of the decision variables. We present a competitive feasibility-preserving classical local-search algorithm for the group relaxation, and a corresponding quantum algorithm that, under reasonable technical conditions, achieves a super-quadratic speedup. When the group relaxation satisfies a nondegeneracy condition analogous to, but stronger than, LP nondegeneracy, our approach yields the optimal solution to the original ILP. Otherwise, the group relaxation tightens bounds on the optimal objective value of the ILP, and can improve downstream branch-and-cut by reducing the integrality gap; we numerically observe this on several practically relevant ILPs. To achieve these results, we derive efficiently constructible constraint-preserving mixers for the group relaxation with favorable spectral properties, which are of independent interest.

1.2
Relaxations of ILPs

Modern integer-programming solvers—commercial (Gurobi

The canonical choice is the LP relaxation, obtained by dropping integrality:

\textsf{OPT}_{\text{LP}}\coloneqq\min\left\{c^{\top}x:x\in{\cal P}_{\text{LP}}\right\},

where

{\cal P}_{\text{LP}}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}:Ax=b\}.

The upshot of working with the LP relaxation is that it can be solved efficiently using
interior point methods

[
Wri97
]

\textup{{OPT}}_{\text{LP}}\leq\textup{{OPT}}

It is immediate that

Contributions

The main contributions in this paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

We give a competitive classical algorithm for solving Gomory’s group relaxation via a feasibility–preserving local search over a finite abelian group.

2.

We design a quantum algorithm within the generalized short–path framework

]

.

3.

We construct

over the group relaxation’s feasible set—(i) a product–of–cycles with explicit log–Sobolev bounds and (ii) an expander with constant spectral gap. We also show that the ground states of the corresponding mixing Hamiltonians can be prepared efficiently. Our approach therefore provides feasibility‑preserving, good‑gap primitives for short‑path/QAOA/QAA on group relaxations; and these are of independent interest.

We construct a quantum algorithm that, with high probability, finds an optimal solution

\mathcal{O}\left(\left(|\overline{{\cal P}}_{{\cal B}}|/|\overline{{\cal P}}_{{\cal B}}^{\star}|\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha(n)}\;\poly(n,L)\right),

Solving the group relaxation with null space walks

As discussed in our motivations, quantum algorithms for discrete optimization can struggle to handle constraints in a natural way. For example, a large class of methods are based on quantum Hamiltonians, which encode the cost function in a diagonal Hermitian operator and perform “mixing” operations, via a non-diagonal Hamiltonian, to transform from some initial configuration to the optimal solution, i.e. a minimal energy configuration of the diagonal Hamiltonian. To handle constraints, a penalty term is often added to the objective, or, in situations where the constraint set is simple (e.g., a single Hamming weight constraint) a constraint-preserving mixer is used. This typically has detrimental effects on performance, however, since either approach often disrupts the spectral properties of the relevant Hamiltonians which determine the efficiency of such approaches. This is connected to a similar difficulty for
classical
algorithms, where most algorithms that handle constraints are global and exhaustive. Local algorithms that explore the feasible space directly, either resort to penalty terms or are limited to constrained spaces that can be efficiently “enumerated.” Since the enumeration of the feasible space of an ILP is
NP-complete
in general, this is only applicable to very specific ILPs.

The first ingredient of our quantum algorithm for the group relaxation is therefore a corresponding classical algorithm that is only based on a local search.
Classical methods for solving the group relaxation are rooted in Gomory’s original work, and run in time
{\cal O}(n\;|\det A_{{\cal B}}|)
, see, e.g.,
[
AWW02
, Proposition 4.4]
. These involve searching for the shortest path between two elements (the identity and an element determined by the right-hand side vector
b
) in a directed Cayley graph associated with a particular finite abelian group
G
, where the presented runtime uses the upper bound
\lvert G\rvert\leq|\det A_{{\cal B}}|
. The group
G
is related to the integer span of the constraint matrix
A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}A_{{\cal N}}
. This algorithm does not directly explore the feasible space locally; it actually always first encounters infeasible points before finding the solution. Nevertheless, the algorithm does use more local information than an exhaustive search method. There is also no current quantum algorithmic framework that facilitates a path to super-quadratic speedups for this shortest-path approach, and we leave this as an interesting potential direction for future work.

To ameliorate these difficulties, we move the search to the null space of the constraint matrix,
A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}A_{{\cal N}}
. Specifically, we reduce (

ILP
ℬ

) to a problem with a domain
Z
that is a finite abelian group. The feasible set for this finite problem is a coset
\overline{{\cal P}}_{{\cal B}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}+K\subseteq Z
, where
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in Z
is any feasible residue vector and
K\leq Z
is the kernel of the map
\mathbf{z}\mapsto A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}A_{\mathcal{N}}\mathbf{z}\pmod{\mathbb{Z}^{m}}
,
\mathbf{z}\in Z
. Note that
|\hat{\mathbf{x}}+K|=|K|
. In Section
3

It remains to argue that the local search algorithm is comparable to preexisting classical methods. Particularly, our algorithm is competitive with Gomory’s shortest-path approach when

is comparable to

In Section
3.5

These include popular heuristics such as the QAOA and quantum annealing, as well as algorithms such as the quantum adiabatic algorithm whose runtime analysis diverges from that considered here.

It is clear that our result satisfies most of the criterion laid out in Question
1
. Specifically, the group relaxation can be applied to general ILPs with many constraints, the new classical algorithm is based on local search, and a super-quadratic quantum speedup can be obtained.

The only remaining question is that of
usefulness
. After all, Theorem
1
only offers us a way to solve a relaxation of the ILP, and not the ILP itself, and it is not immediately clear whether there is an impact for the latter problem.
Solving the group relaxation can be helpful for solving the ILP in two ways. Firstly, when the group relaxation is nondegenerate (see, Definition
7
), the group relaxation solves the ILP directly. In this case, the new classical algorithm that we are accelerating is in fact solving the ILP itself. Whether the quantum algorithm achieves a quantum speedup over
state-of-the-art
classical algorithms for such ILPs depends not only on the size of the feasible set
|\overline{{\cal P}}_{{\cal B}}|
, but on the performance of state-of-art solvers for these problems (it could be the case, for example that ILPs with nondegenerate group relaxations are easier for these solvers).

A different setting is when the group relaxation is used as the bounding procedure for an outer Branch-and-Bound algorithm. It is known that the performance of Branch-and-Bound algorithm is heavily influenced by the strength of the bounding procedure that is used at each node, and a stronger bounding procedure can substantially reduce the number of nodes that must be explored by the branching procedure. A quantum speedup for the group relaxation can therefore indirectly accelerate a branch-and-bound solver if the resulting optimality gap is lesser than that obtained from the continuous LP relaxation. In Section
5
, we test this hypothesis on a mixture of benchmark and synthetically generated integer problems. We find that the group relaxation indeed substantially reduces the gap compared to the LP relaxation. Furthermore, we observe that for a substantial fraction of tested problems, solving the group relaxation produces a solution for the original ILP.

Beyond pure ILPs, the group relaxation machinery extends naturally to
mixed‑integer linear programs
(MILPs), which are problems of the form:

\textsf{OPT}_{\text{MILP}}\coloneqq\min\left\{c^{\top}x:Ax=b,\;x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\,n-p}\right\}.

(MILP)

MILPs blend integer and continuous decisions and thus enable one to model a wide range of challenging applications. Wolsey
[
Wol71
]
showed that, after clearing denominators in the continuous block by choosing

\mathbf{D}\coloneqq\mathrm{lcm}\big\{|\det A_{{\cal B}}|:{\cal B}\in\mathscr{B}\cap\{p{+}1,\dots,n\}\big\}

and substituting
z_{j}=\mathbf{D}\,x_{j}
for the continuous variables, every optimal solution to (
MILP
) is obtained by solving the resulting ILP. This reduction allows the group relaxation techniques developed here to apply verbatim. That said, computing a practically useful
\mathbf{D}
(and the requisite bases) at scale is nontrivial; devising scalable strategies for this step is beyond the scope of this work and left to future investigation.

Integer linear programming

We give a high-level overview of algorithmic development for ILP. For a thorough treatment of the field, we refer the reader to the classic texts

A significant breakthrough was made by Lenstra

shortly after

generalize Lenstra-type algorithms by decomposing the feasible region along lattice shifts of a linear subspace.

Building on these ideas, Dadush

]
developed an improved Kannan-type algorithm for the more general problem of

, where the task is to decide whether a convex set

. In a recent breakthrough, Reis and Rothvoss
[

]
gave a constructive resolution of the

, thereby enabling Dadush’s algorithm to attain this improved bound.

The running time of the algorithm described in

cannot be reduced in general, and whether there exists a single exponential time algorithm for exact ILP remains open.

Quantum algorithms for integer and combinatorial optimization

By now quantum proposals for combinatorial optimization have been investigated for over two decades. The Quantum adiabatic algorithm

2.1
Abelian groups and lattices

For the basic definitions and properties of groups, we refer the reader to any abstract algebra textbook such as
[
Lan12
]
. Here we provide some key results and definitions pertinent to this work.

A group
G
is called
abelian
if its group binary operation is commutative. In the abelian setting, it is customary to represent the group operation using the plus symbol, its identity using
0
, its inverse using a minus sign, etc. All groups considered in this work are abelian. For an integer
k
and
g\in G
, let
kg
denote repeated addition of
g
or its inverse
k
times, depending on the sign of
k
. Let
\cong
denote group isomorphism. We will use the notation
S\leq G
to denote subgroups
F=S
of
G
, and
G/S=\{g+S:g\in G\}
to denote the quotient group, whose elements are cosets
g+S=\{g+s:s\in S\}
. Given
a,b\in G
, we write
a=b\pmod{S}
if
a\in b+S
. To maintain brevity, we frequently use
a
to refer to the coset
a+S
, and context should make clear when the quotient space is being discussed.

Given a subset
S\subseteq G
, let
\langle S\rangle
denote the smallest subgroup of
G
containing
S
. We say that
S

generates

G
if
\langle S\rangle=G
. Equivalently,
S
generates
G
if every
g\in G
can be obtained from finite products and inverses of elements in
S
. A group
G
is called

if it has a single generator
g

G=\langle g\rangle
. The

of a group

is simply its cardinality as a set

. We also refer to the order of an element

to mean the order of
\langle g\rangle

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{r}
denote the additive group of integers modulo

. The
fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups
states that every nontrivial finite abelian group

is isomorphic to a direct sum

\displaystyle G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{r_{1}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{r_{2}}\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{r_{k}},

for integers
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\geq 2
. There are several canonical choices for the integers
r_{j}
; here we typically consider the
invariant factor
decomposition, whereby
r_{j}|r_{j+1}
(i.e.,
r_{j}
divides
r_{j+1}
), for all
j\in[k-1]
. In this case, the

are called invariant factors, and the decomposition is unique.

A

\left\|\cdot\right\|:G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
over an abelian group

is a real-valued function that is positive definite, subadditive, and inverse symmetric (
\left\|-g\right\|=\left\|g\right\|
). Of interest in this work is a group norm defined below.

Definition 1

(Cyclic metric)
.

For
v\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{a_{i}}
and nonzero

, define

\left\|v\right\|_{\mathrm{cyc},1,w}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}|w_{i}|\,\max\!\big\{v_{i},\,a_{i}-v_{i}\big\}.

For our purposes, a

is a discrete abelian subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by

\Lambda=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}B_{i}:z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

where
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are linearly independent. See
[
Cas96
]
for a comprehensive overview. If the
B_{i}
also form a basis (

), the lattice is
full rank
. We also denote
\Lambda
by
B\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, where
B
has columns
B_{i}
. When
\ker(B)\neq 0
, i.e., the columns are linearly dependent,
B\mathbb{Z}^{n}
may not form a lattice but will in certain cases, such as if
B
has rational entries. A square integer matrix
U
is
unimodular
if
\det U=\pm 1
. Such a matrix always has unimodular inverse, and two full-rank lattices
A\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
B\mathbb{Z}^{n}
are equal iff
A=BU
for some unimodular
U
. We can think of

as a change of basis matrix for a lattice, changing the fundamental cell while preserving the volume of that cell.

Any integer matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
can be expressed in
Smith normal form
(SNF)
A=URV
, where
U,V
are unimodular with dimension
m,n
respectively, and
R=\diag(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})
is a diagonal integer matrix with
r_{i}|r_{i+1}
for all
i\in[k-1]
. The rightmost
r_{i}
may be zero. The SNF can be computed in time polynomial in the dimension and log of the matrix norm
[
Ili89
]
, with improved performance in sparse instances. Given a full-rank lattice
A\mathbb{Z}^{m}
with square matrix

, the quotient group
Q=\mathbb{Z}^{m}/A\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is finite and

\left|Q\right|=\left|\det A\right|.

In fact, the SNF of

fully determines the group structure in the sense that

\mathbb{Z}^{m}/A\mathbb{Z}^{m}\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{r_{j}}

is the invariant factor decomposition. Specifically,

has independent generators which are the columns

of

(as cosets), where each

generates the cyclic group

.

2.2
Basics of finite Markov chains

Let
{\cal X}
be a finite set of size
N
. A
Markov chain
on
{\cal X}
is a random process that moves between elements of
{\cal X}
according to an
N\times N
transition matrix
P
, where
P(x,y)
is the probability of moving from
x
to
y
. The matrix
P
is stochastic:
\sum_{y\in{\cal X}}P(x,y)=1
for all
x\in{\cal X}
. Probability distributions
\rho
on
{\cal X}
evolve according to
\rho^{(t+1)}=\rho^{(t)}P
. After
t
steps the state is
\rho^{(t)}=\rho^{(0)}P^{\top}
,
\rho^{(0)}=\rho
. A distribution
\pi
is
stationary
if
\pi P=\pi
. For any function
f:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(Pf)(x)=\sum_{y\in{\cal X}}P(x,y)f(y).

The
reversed Markov chain

\mathcal{M}^{\star}=({\cal X},P^{\star},\pi)
shares the stationary distribution
\pi
and has transition matrix
P^{\star}
defined by

\pi(x)P(x,y)=\pi(y)P^{\star}(y,x).

The chain is
reversible
if
P^{\star}=P
, and the chain is
symmetric
if
P=P^{\top}
.

For reversible chains, there exists an inner product space over which
P
is diagonalizable, with eigenvalues

1=\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq-1,

where
\lambda_{1}=1
and
\lambda_{2}<1
. The
spectral gap
is

\delta=1-\max\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\{\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n}\},\lambda\neq\pm 1\}.

The chain is
aperiodic
if the set

\mathcal{T}(x)=\{t\geq 1:P^{\top}(x,x)>0\}
satisfies
\gcd(\mathcal{T}(x))=1,\forall x\in{\cal X}
. This can always be assured by making the chain
lazy
:

\displaystyle P_{\text{lazy}}=\frac{I+P}{2},

which only halves the spectral gap but preserves the stationary distribution.

The
mixing time
of a Markov chain is the amount of time it takes for the distance to stationarity to be small:

t_{\text{mix}}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\min\left\{t:d(t)\leq\varepsilon\right\},

where
d(t)\coloneqq\sup_{\mu}\textup{TV}(\mu P^{\top},\pi)
, and
TV
is the
total variation distance

\textup{TV}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|\mu-\nu\|_{1}.

The relationship between mixing time and the spectral gap for a reversible Markov chain can be expressed as

t_{\text{mix}}(\varepsilon)={\cal O}\left(\delta^{-1}\cdot\text{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

We also introduce the concept of log-Sobolev inequalities.

Definition 2

(Log-Sobolev inequality)
.

A Markov chain
\mathcal{M}=({\cal X},P,\pi)
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality if for all
f:{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi}\mathbb{E}_{y\underset{P}{\sim}x}\left[(f(x)-f(y))^{2}\right]\geq\omega\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi}[f(x)\ln f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi}[f(x)]\ln\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi}[f(x)]\right).

The largest such
\omega
is called the log-Sobolev constant, and is related to the spectral gap via
\omega\leq\delta
.

The
discriminant matrix
is a useful operator for analyzing random walks:

Definition 3

(Discriminant matrix)
.

For
\mathcal{M}=({\cal X},P,\pi)
, the discriminant matrix is

D(P)_{ji}=\sqrt{P_{ij}P^{\star}_{ji}}=\left(\operatorname{diag}(\pi)^{1/2}P\operatorname{diag}(\pi)^{-1/2}\right)_{ij}.

Lastly, we also define the concept of pseudo-Lipschitzness.

Definition 4

(
P
-pseudo Lipschitz norm)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=({\cal X},P,\pi)
be a Markov chain. The
P
-pseudo Lipschitz norm of
f:{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined to be

\lVert f\rVert_{P}\coloneqq\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{y\underset{P}{\sim}x}[(f(x)-f(y))^{2}].

2.3
Background on linear programming

The
linear optimization
problem and its dual can be written in standard form as

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{c^{\top}x:Ax=b,\,x\geq 0\right\},\quad\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{b^{\top}y:A^{\top}y\leq c\right\},

where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
define a collection of
m
linear constraints. Here and throughout, we assume
A
is full rank. For our purposes, a
basis

{\cal B}\subseteq[n]
is a subset of
m
indices such that the vectors
\{A_{j}\}_{j\in{\cal B}}
form a basis over
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in the usual sense. A vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
feasible
if
x
satisfies the constraints, i.e.,
Ax=b
and
x\geq 0
. Moreover, a
basic feasible solution

x=(x_{\cal B},x_{{\cal N}})
is a feasible solution such that its nonzero entries are indexed by
{\cal B}
. Note that the values of
x_{{\cal B}}
are uniquely determined by the system
A_{{\cal B}}x_{{\cal B}}=b
. The associated basic dual solution is
y_{{\cal B}}=(A^{\top}_{{\cal B}})^{-1}c_{{\cal B}}
.

Let
\mathscr{B}
denote the collection of all feasible bases of the LP relaxation. For any basis
{\cal B}\in\mathscr{B}
, we define the complement
\mathcal{N}\coloneqq[n]\setminus{\cal B}
, and the complementary parts
A_{{\cal N}},c_{\cal N}
of the constraints and the objective function, respectively. We will assume that
m=\Theta(n)
. Associated to any basis
{\cal B}
, the
reduced costs
(or dual slacks) are given by
\bar{c}\coloneqq c-A^{\top}_{{\cal N}}y_{{\cal B}}
. The reduced cost
\bar{c}_{j}
for
j\in[n]
quantifies the rate of improvement in the objective function per unit increase in
x_{j}
, subject to feasibility.

Let
(x^{\star},y^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote a primal-dual solution corresponding to basis
{\cal B}\in\mathscr{B}
. We say that
{\cal B}
is an
optimal
basis if, in addition to primal-dual feasibility,
complementarity slackness
is satisfied:

x^{\star}\odot\left(c-A^{\top}y^{\star}\right)=\mathbf{0}_{n}

where
\odot
denotes the element-wise product. When the LP is nondegenerate,
{\cal B}=\supp(x^{\star})
and
{\cal N}=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}^{\star}=0\}
.

When the problem data
(A,b,c)
is integer, we let
L
denote the binary input length:

L\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[1+\left\lfloor\log_{2}|A_{ij}|\right\rfloor\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[1+\left\lfloor\log_{2}|b_{i}|\right\rfloor\right]+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[1+\left\lfloor\log_{2}|c_{j}|\right\rfloor\right].

The optimal partition
({\cal B},\mathcal{N})
can be found in time
{\cal O}\left(n^{3}L\right)
using interior point methods
[
Wri97
]
.

2.4
The generalized short path algorithm

Short path (SP) algorithms, first proposed by Hastings
[
Has18
]
and later refined by Dalzell et al.
[
DPCB23
]
, represent a methodological advancement over the QAA
[
FGGS00
]
. Below, we outline the core concepts.

While quantum computers cannot efficiently prepare the ground state for general Hamiltonians, certain Hamiltonians have ground states that are easy to construct. The QAA leverages this by starting with an initial Hamiltonian
H_{0}
whose ground state is simple to prepare—commonly, the transverse-field operator
H_{0}=-\sum_{i\in[n]}\sigma^{x}_{i}
, where
\sigma^{x}_{i}
is the Pauli
X
operator on qubit
i\in[n]
. The algorithm then defines a time-dependent Hamiltonian

H_{\text{QAA}}(t)\coloneqq(1-\tau(t))H_{0}+\tau(t)H,

where
\tau:[0,T]\to[0,1]
is a smooth, monotonic function satisfying
\tau(0)=0
and
\tau(T)=1
so that
H_{\text{QAA}}(0)=H_{0}
and
H_{\text{QAA}}(T)=H
. According to the
adiabatic theorem

[
Mes58
]
, simulating the Schrödinger equation

i\frac{\text{d}}{\text{dt}}\mathinner{|{x(t)}\rangle}=H_{\text{QAA}}(t)\mathinner{|{x(t)}\rangle}

for sufficiently large
T
prepares the ground state of
H
, provided that the minimum gap
\delta_{\mathrm{min}}
between the lowest two eigenvalues is nonzero. Here

\delta_{\mathrm{min}}:=\min_{t\in[0,T]}\{\lambda_{2}(H_{\text{QAA}}(t))-\lambda_{1}(H_{\text{QAA}}(t))\},

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are the smallest and second smallest eigenvalues, respectively.

The challenge is that
\delta_{\mathrm{min}}
tends to shrink rapidly with system size, necessitating prohibitive simulation times
T
. Roughly,
T
scales polynomially with the inverse gap. Obtaining analytical bounds on the spectral gap is notoriously difficult
[
HS12
]
, but more importantly, the QAA is hampered by “localization” phenomena—quantum analogues of a classical particle getting trapped in a local minimum. Localization can induce (super) exponentially small gaps, resulting in run times that may be exponentially slower than classical brute-force search
[
AKR10
]
.

SP algorithms avoid first-order phase transitions via two key modifications:

1.

Instead of using the standard adiabatic Hamiltonian
H_{\text{QAA}}
, the algorithm uses a slightly different interpolation between the mixer Hamiltonian
H_{0}
and the final Hamiltonian
H
which encodes a truncated and normalized cost function.

2.

The algorithm does not follow the adiabatic path, but performs two successive jumps called short jump and long jump to avoid the points where the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian decays (super) exponentially.

The
generalized
quantum SP algorithm, as developed in
[
CHO+24
]
, further provides a principled framework for achieving super-quadratic quantum speedups over not just over unstructured search, but also over classical Markov Chain search in combinatorial optimization. The key ingredient is a generalized mixer Hamiltonian over the Markov Chain graph of interest. Below, we review the algorithm and conditions for super-quadratic speedups.

2.4.1
Short path Hamiltonian and algorithmic steps

Let
{\cal M}=({\cal X},P,\pi)
be a reversible, aperiodic Markov chain over a finite set
{\cal X}
, where
\pi
is the stationary distribution of
P
.
The generalized SP Hamiltonian
H_{\mu}
is defined as in (
2
). This construction generalizes the original SP framework to arbitrary reversible Markov chains and cost functions, as formalized in
[
CHO+24
]
. We define
E^{\star}\coloneqq\min_{z\in{\cal X}}H(z)
, and subtract a scalar from the cost function to ensure
E^{\star}<0
.

We outline the generalized SP algorithm in Algorithm
1

3
Solving group relaxations through local search

In this section, we first review important details regarding the group relaxation in order to derive a formulation of this problem amenable to local search. We then show how to solve the reformulated problem using
Markov chain search
, and analyze the complexity of the resulting scheme.

3.1
The group relaxation

Let
{\cal B}
denote an optimal basis for the LP relaxation of (
ILP
). Recall the definition of the group relaxation induced by this basis

\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}\coloneqq\min\left\{c^{\top}x:x\in{\cal P}_{{\cal B}}\right\},

(
\text{ILP}_{{\cal B}}
)

where
{\cal P}_{{\cal B}}:=\{(x_{{\cal B}},x_{{\cal N}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n-m}_{\geq 0}:Ax=b\}
.

Since any
x=(x_{{\cal B}},x_{{\cal N}})\in{\cal P}_{{\cal B}}
satisfies
Ax=b
, we may write

\displaystyle c^{\top}x=c_{\cal B}^{\top}x_{\cal B}+c_{\mathcal{N}}^{\top}x_{\mathcal{N}}

\displaystyle=c_{\cal B}^{\top}A_{\cal B}^{-1}(b-A_{\mathcal{N}}x_{\mathcal{N}})+c_{\mathcal{N}}^{\top}x_{\mathcal{N}}=c_{\cal B}^{\top}A_{\cal B}^{-1}b+\bar{c}_{{\cal N}}^{\top}x_{\mathcal{N}}.

Given that
A_{{\cal B}}
is invertible, we can also reformulate the system
A_{{\cal B}}x_{{\cal B}}+A_{{\cal N}}x_{{\cal N}}=b
as

\displaystyle A^{-1}_{{\cal B}}A_{{\cal N}}x_{{\cal N}}+x_{{\cal B}}=A^{-1}_{{\cal B}}b\implies x_{{\cal B}}=A^{-1}_{{\cal B}}(b-A_{{\cal N}}x_{{\cal N}}).

Thus, once
x_{{\cal N}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-m}_{\geq 0}
is fixed,
x_{{\cal B}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is uniquely determined. Accordingly, (

ILP
ℬ

) is equivalent to

\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}=\min_{x_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n-m}}\left\{c_{{\cal B}}^{\top}A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}b+\bar{c}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\top}x_{\mathcal{N}}:A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}A_{{\cal N}}x_{{\cal N}}=A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}b\pmod{\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\right\}.

(5)

Since
{\cal B}
is optimal for (
LP
), the nonbasic part of the reduced cost is nonnegative,

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{{\cal N}}^{\top}\coloneqq c_{\mathcal{N}}^{\top}-c_{\cal B}^{\top}A_{\cal B}^{-1}A_{\mathcal{N}}\geq 0,

and
c_{{\cal B}}^{\top}A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}b=\textup{{OPT}}_{\text{LP}}
, so we have
\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}\geq\textup{{OPT}}_{\text{LP}}
.

To further refine this formulation, consider the Smith normal form
A_{{\cal B}}=URV
, where
U
and
V
are unimodular matrices, and
R=\textup{diag}(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})
with

\left|\det A_{\cal B}\right|=\prod_{j=1}^{m}r_{j},\quad r_{1}~|~r_{2}~|~\cdots~|~r_{m}.

Each
r_{j}>0
, as
A_{\cal B}
is full-rank. Multiplying both sides of the constraint in (
5
) by
RV
, we obtain

\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}=\textup{{OPT}}_{\text{LP}}+\min_{x_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n-m}}\left\{\bar{c}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\top}x_{\mathcal{N}}:U^{-1}A_{{\cal N}}x_{{\cal N}}=U^{-1}b\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\right\}.

Labeling the data
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=U^{-1}A_{{\cal N}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times(n-m)}
,
\mathbf{b}=U^{-1}b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
\mathbf{c}=\bar{c}_{\mathcal{N}}
, we arrive at

\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}=\textup{{OPT}}_{\text{LP}}+\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n-m}}\left\{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}:\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\right\}.

(6)

Note it may be the case that some columns of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
are already in
R\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. For those columns, we can simply set
x_{j}=0
, as this process never increases the objective value, nor changes the LHS of the constraints modulo
R\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. Denoting by
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times d}
, with
d\leq n-m
, the matrix obtained upon dropping these columns, problem (
6
) simplifies to

\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}=\textup{{OPT}}_{\text{LP}}+\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\left\{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}:\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\right\}.

(
\widetilde{\text{ILP}}_{{\cal B}}
)

We thus describe an instance of a group relaxation via the tuple
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c},\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m})
.

Gomory originally showed that, under certain conditions, the group relaxation is
asymptotically tight
—that is, it solves the original ILP exactly when the right-hand side is sufficiently large.

Theorem 4

(Theorem 5.6 in
[
WN99
]
)
.

Let
{\cal B}
be any feasible basis for the
LP
relaxation of the
ILP
problem. The group relaxation defined by
{\cal B}
solves the
ILP
problem to optimality if

A^{-1}_{{\cal B}}b\geq\max_{i,j}\left\{|(A^{-1}_{{\cal B}}A_{\mathcal{N}})_{ij}|\right\}\cdot|\det A_{{\cal B}}|\mathbf{1}_{n-m}.

Lasserre later established a
nondegeneracy condition
:

Definition 7

(Nondegeneracy condition for group relaxations
[
Las04
]
)
.

Denote the set of all optimal bases to the LP relaxation of (
ILP
) by
\mathscr{B}
. Let
{\cal B}\in\mathscr{B}
be an optimal LP basis. If there exists another basis
{\cal B}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}
such that
\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}=\textup{{OPT}}_{{\cal B}^{\prime}}
then the problem is degenerate. Otherwise, the group relaxation defined by
{\cal B}
is nondegenerate, and solves (
ILP
) to optimality.

This nondegeneracy condition can be viewed as a similar statement to the usual notion of nondegeneracy in linear programming, in the following sense: the situation in which the group relaxation only provides a lower bound on
OPT
arises, necessarily, when there is another LP basis
{\cal B}^{\prime}
whose group relaxation yields the same lower bound. We emphasize that group nondegeneracy is strictly stronger than LP nondegeneracy. Moreover, while LP nondegeneracy can often be certified a posteriori (e.g., via reduced costs and basic variable signs), no obvious efficient a posteriori certificate is available for the group case. That said, there is a simple
sufficient
a posteriori check for exactness: if a group‑optimal solution is ILP‑feasible (i.e., satisfies the dropped nonnegativity), then
\textup{{OPT}}_{{\cal B}}=\textup{{OPT}}
; conversely, failure of this check does not certify degeneracy, since there may be another basis which induces a group relaxation that solves the ILP.

3.2
Null space formulation

Recall from (

~
ILP

ℬ

) of the prior section that the feasible set to the group relaxation is

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}:\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\}.

This set is infinite, provided it is nonempty. However, note that the constraints are linear equations over the finite group
{\cal R}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}^{m}/R\mathbb{Z}^{m}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{r_{i}}
. Thus, the order of
\mathbf{A}_{j}
is finite, and necessarily divides
r_{m}
. Reducing each component modulo
r_{m}
never increases the objective value
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
, since
\mathbf{c}\geq 0
. We can thus take the feasible set to be a subset of
Z\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}_{r_{m}}^{d}
while preserving optimality and feasibility. We note that the ambient space of
\mathbf{x}
can be even further reduced with additional refinements, and explore this approach in Appendix
A
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}
is solvable, with some solution
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in Z
. Then, the set of solutions can be written as the coset
\hat{\mathbf{x}}+K
. Here,
K
is the kernel of
\mathbf{A}
as a homomorphism from
Z
to
{\cal R}

\displaystyle K=\{\mathbf{x}\in Z:\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\}.

(7)

Thus, as typical with more familiar linear systems over vector spaces, one can parameterize the feasible set of the group relaxation using any feasible solution together with the kernel of some group homomorphism. We shall see, however, that characterizing the kernel is somewhat more challenging than the vector space setting.

Supposing the group problem is feasible, we can formulate the objective function in terms of the kernel subgroup as follows

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\hat{\mathbf{x}}+K}\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+c_{\cal B}^{\top}A_{\cal B}^{-1}b=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in K}\mathbf{c}^{\top}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{x}\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}})+c_{{\cal B}}^{\top}A_{\cal B}^{-1}b=:\min_{\mathbf{x}\in K}f(\mathbf{x}).

(8)

The constant shift
c_{{\cal B}}^{\top}A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}b
ensures that
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in K}f(\mathbf{x})=\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}
.

3.3
Computing the null space

Our formulation (
8
) is only useful algorithmically provided there are efficient methods for

(a)

producing a feasible solution
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in Z
to
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}
; and

(b)

accessing elements
\mathbf{x}\in K
.

We shall show how to accomplish both of these tasks in classical polynomial time using tools from computational number theory
[
Coh00
]
. We remark that one can formulate (
8
) as an instance of the abelian Hidden Subgroup Problem by considering the task of finding the kernel of
K
as a hidden subgroup of
Z
. This is a problem for which quantum computers are known to be effective. However, because
\mathbf{A}
is stored in classical memory rather than provided via an oracle, the setting is somewhat distinct, and in any case, an efficient classical algorithm which produces a set of generators is preferable.

Solving
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}
is complicated by the fact that
{\cal R}
is rectangular, as opposed to square: we would rather work in a space of the form
a\mathbb{Z}^{m}
for some positive integer
a
because it remains invariant under unimodular transformations. This can be accomplished with some preconditioning. Note that
\mathbf{x}\in Z
satisfies
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}
, if and only if

\displaystyle B\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=B\mathbf{b}\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{m}},

where
{B}=\textup{diag}(r_{m}/r_{1},\dots,r_{m}/r_{m-1},1)
. We shall presently see how this aides us.

Given
B\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times d}
, we first compute the Smith normal form
B\mathbf{A}=UTV
for
U\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times m}
,
V\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}
unimodular and
T=\textup{diag}(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{k})
consisting of invariant factors, with
k=\min(m,d)
. Multiplying by
U^{-1}
, the system reduces to

TV\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{m}},

(9)

where
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\coloneqq U^{-1}B\mathbf{b}
. To solve this system, observe that by unimodularity, the operation
\mathbf{y}\mapsto V^{-1}\mathbf{y}\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
defines an automorphism on
Z
. Thus, any solution
\mathbf{x}\in Z
to (
9
) can be obtained uniquely from some
\mathbf{y}\in Z
that solves

T\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{m}}.

(10)

The problem has thus been reduced to
m
independent linear equations modulo
r_{m}

t_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\pmod{r_{m}}

whose solutions are well known from number theory. A solution exists if and only if

\gcd(r_{m},t_{i})\;\big|\;\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{i}

(11)

for each
i\in[k]
, and in this case a particular solution
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
can be found through the extended Euclidean algorithm. Thus,
V^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{y}}\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{m}}
is a feasible solution in
\mathbb{Z}_{r_{m}}^{d}
.

It remains to determine the kernel
K
, and this can be done by setting
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}=0
. In this case, the
i
th equation in (
11
) has
\gcd(r_{m},t_{i})
solutions: the multiples of
r_{m}/\gcd(r_{m},t_{i})
. The number of solutions is thus
\prod_{i\in[k]}\gcd(r_{m},t_{i})
. The solutions
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r_{m}}^{d}
to (
9
) are obtained as

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}h_{i},\quad n_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\gcd(r_{m},t_{i})-1\}.

(12)

where
h_{i}=V_{i}^{-1}\frac{r_{m}}{\gcd(r_{m},t_{i})}\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
and all addition is entrywise modulo
r_{m}
. To summarize,

K\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}_{\gcd(r_{m},t_{i})}

(13)

with corresponding cyclic generators
{\cal S}=(h_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
.

The procedure outlined above computes a generating set for
K
, and shows that transforming the problem in (

~
ILP

ℬ

) to (
8
) is classically efficient, enabling us to efficiently compute a generating set for

. We summarize the overall procedure in Algorithm

.

3.4
Markov-chain search algorithms for the group relaxation

In this section, we design a random-walk-based approach for sampling optimal solutions of the group relaxation.
We will show how to construct a random walk over a coset corresponding to the set of feasible solutions, enabling one to run Markov chain search to solve the group relaxation. The motivation for considering Markov-chain search is primarily for analysis: it is easier to demonstrate quantum speedup in this framework compared with a classical algorithm that directly samples uniformly over the feasible coset.

As done in the previous section, by exploiting the algebraic structure of the group relaxation, one can efficiently generate a basis for the kernel of the linear map defined by the coefficient matrix with the range mapped to
{\cal R}

3.5
Comparison with the shortest path on the Cayley graph

The classical approach for solving (

~
ILP

ℬ

) leverages the nonnegativity of
\mathbf{c}
to reduce the group relaxation to finding the shortest path in a Cayley directed graph (digraph)
[
Gom65
,
Sha68
]
. Unlike MCS, this algorithm does not explicitly depend on
\lvert K\rvert
. It instead depends on
\lvert G\rvert
, where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} G&\coloneqq(\mathbf{A}\mathbb{Z}^{d}+R\mathbb{Z}^{m})/R\mathbb{Z}^{m}\leq\mathbb{Z}^{m}/R\mathbb{Z}^{m}.\end{aligned}

(15)

In words, the group
G
is the range of the map
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\mapsto\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}
. In this section, we recall the shortest-path approach of Gomory and present a setting under which
\lvert K\rvert=\lvert G\rvert
, making the shortest-path and MCS approaches comparable. Hence, we want to identify settings in which our quantum algorithm has a super-quadratic speedup over Gomory’s shortest-path approach.

Let
{\cal G}
be the corresponding Cayley digraph over
G
generated by

\displaystyle{\cal A}=\{\mathbf{A}_{1}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}},\cdots,\mathbf{A}_{d}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\},

where an edge incident on
\mathbf{y}\in G
corresponding to
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\left[\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{A}_{j}\right]\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}
has a weight of
\mathbf{c}_{j}\geq 0
. Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\prime}=\left(\mathbf{b}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\right)\in G.

Feasible solutions correspond to directed paths from
\mathbf{0}
to
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathbf{c}
is nonnegative, the optimal solution is the feasible solution with smallest
\ell_{1}
-norm, i.e., the shortest path from
\mathbf{0}
to
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}
.

This approach has complexity dependent on
\lvert G\rvert
, as there are
\mathcal{O}(\lvert{\cal A}\rvert\lvert G\rvert)
edges in
{\cal G}
. Thus, shortest-path algorithms can solve the problem with
\mathcal{O}\left(n\lvert G\rvert\right)
time. In contrast, the null space approach depends on
\lvert K\rvert
. In general, either
\lvert K\rvert
or
\lvert G\rvert
may be larger. More precisely, (
7
) and the first isomorphism theorem says that
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{r_{m}}^{d}/K
, and in particular
r_{m}^{d}=\left|K\right|\left|G\right|
. As discussed in Appendix
A
, choosing
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{s_{i}}
, where
s_{i}\coloneqq\left|\langle\mathbf{A}_{i}\rangle\right|
, as the group relaxation domain can result in a smaller kernel
K^{\prime}
. Then the relation is
\prod_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}=\left|K^{\prime}\right|\left|G\right|
. As expected, the range and kernel sizes are inversely related for fixed domain, but in general either could be larger than the other.

In fact, it appears one can construct families of instances where either the range space or kernel space algorithms can be asymptotically faster than the other. The goal of the following result is to show there at least exists a seemingly generic family of ILPs, where
\lvert G\rvert=\lvert K\rvert
.

Theorem 6
.

There is a constructable family of integer linear programming problems, parameterized by
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, whose group relaxations satisfy
G\cong K
(in particular,
\left|G\right|=\left|K\right|
).

Proof.

Consider arbitrary positive integers
t,m
. Let
n=2m
,
r=t^{2}
,
R=rI_{m}
, and
T=tI_{m}
. Pick unimodular matrices
U,V,U^{\prime},V^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times m}
. We then let the ILP constraint matrix be
A=[URV|U^{\prime}TV^{\prime}]=[A_{{\cal B}}|A_{{\cal N}}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
. Additionally, we require
U,V
are chosen such that
A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}
has all non-negative entries.

We want the right-hand side vector
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{\geq 0}
to be such that the original ILP is feasible. To do this, we first look at the group relaxation constraint of

\displaystyle U^{-1}A_{{\cal N}}\mathbf{x}=U^{-1}b\pmod{r\mathbb{Z}^{m}}.

The feasibility of the group relaxation hinges on

\displaystyle T\mathbf{z}=b\pmod{r\mathbb{Z}^{m}}

having a solution. By a discussion in Section
3.3
, this is true if and only if
t~|~b_{j}
,
\forall j\in[m]
. Choosing
b
as such and keeping all entries non-negative, one solution is then
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(V^{\prime})^{-1}\left(\frac{b}{t}\right)\pmod{r\mathbb{Z}^{n}}
. From Section
3.1
, ILP feasibility then hinges on

\displaystyle A^{-1}_{{\cal B}}(b-A_{{\cal N}}\hat{\mathbf{x}})\geq 0,

which holds by our assumptions on
A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}
.

We also want to be able to plant our choice of basis
{\cal B}
as an optimal one. To perform the planting, we only need to ensure primal and dual feasibility of the corresponding LP basic feasible solution (BFS). The primal BFS is
x_{\textup{LP}}=(x_{{\cal B}},x_{{\cal N}})=(A_{{\cal B}}^{-1}b,\mathbf{0}_{n-m})
and the associated dual solution is
y_{\textup{LP}}=(A_{\cal B}^{\top})^{-1}c_{{\cal B}}
. By the earlier paragraph, we already have
x_{{\cal B}}\geq 0
, ensuring primal feasibility. Dual feasibility requires the reduced costs to be nonnegative:

c_{\mathcal{N}}^{\top}-c_{\cal B}^{\top}A_{\cal B}^{-1}A_{\mathcal{N}}\geq 0,

which we can ensure by choosing
c
.

From equation (
13
), the null space
K
has cardinality

\displaystyle\lvert K\rvert=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\gcd(r,t)=t^{m}.

Meanwhile, the cardinality of
G
satisfies

\displaystyle\lvert G\rvert=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{r_{m}}{\gcd(r_{m},t_{i})}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{t^{2}}{t}=\left|K\right|

Hence for each
m
we have a collection of ILPs corresponding to the set of all possible choices for
t,U,V,U^{\prime},V^{\prime}
, where
\lvert K\rvert=\lvert G\rvert
for all of them.
∎

To summarize, the only difference between the construction in Theorem
6
and the set of all ILPs with
n=2m
is the requirement on the invariant factors of
A_{{\cal B}}
and
A_{{\cal N}}
, i.e. the invariant factors of
A_{{\cal B}}
are all equal and each factor is exactly the square of a factor of
A_{{\cal N}}
.

Note from (
15
),
G
satisfies
\lvert G\rvert\leq\left|\det A_{{\cal B}}\right|
and
\lvert{\cal A}\rvert\leq n
, which is why the shortest-path approach for the group relaxation is usually presented with an
\mathcal{O}(n|\det A_{{\cal B}}|)
runtime. Here we present a set of sufficient conditions on an ILP under which
\lvert K\rvert
in the null space formulation is not significantly larger, i.e. in
\mathcal{O}(\textup{poly}(n,m)|\det A_{{\cal B}}|)
. Unlike the previous theorem, we relax some of the constraints on
n
and the invariant factors of
A_{{\cal B}}
. There are no conditions on
A_{{\cal N}}
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n},A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
define an integer linear program, and
{\cal B}
be an optimal LP basis. Let
R=\diag(r_{j})_{j}^{m}=UA_{{\cal B}}V\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times m}
be the SNF of
A_{{\cal B}}
. Suppose
n\leq 2m+\mathcal{O}(\log(m))
and that there exists an integer

such that for all integers

, we have
r_{j}=r_{m}
. Then, we can construct a null space formulation of the ILP group relaxation where
\lvert K\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\textup{poly}(m,n)\left|\det A_{{\cal B}}\right|)

Proof.

We follow the procedure outlined in Section
3.2
with the domain
\mathbb{Z}_{r_{m}}^{d}
. Without loss of generality, we take
d=n-m
here. By construction,
n-m\leq m+\mathcal{O}(\log(m))
. Note trivially
\lvert K\rvert\leq\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{r_{m}}^{d}\rvert
. Also
\lvert\det A_{{\cal B}}\rvert=\prod_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}=\mathcal{O}(\textup{poly}(m)r_{m}^{m-j})
, which one can easily see is the same order of
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{r_{m}}^{d}\rvert=r_{m}^{d}
.
∎

The main point is that
\lvert K\rvert
tends to not be much larger than
|\det A_{{\cal B}}|
for problems with around twice as many variables as constraints and with an
A_{{\cal B}}
that has the majority of its invariant factors being equal.

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}}{\lVert\mathbf{c}\rVert_{\infty}}=\Theta\!\big(r_{m}\log\left(\lvert K\rvert/\lvert K^{\star}\rvert\right)\big).

Suppose

2.

One will note that most of these conditions are light, except for the condition on the sparsity of the generators.

The following result shows that there does exist a family of ILPs whose group relaxations satisfy the conditions for super-quadratic speedups using the product-cycle walk. The following ILPs turn out to have a specific structure that enables them to be solved easily. However, the goal is simply to use this construction to show that our conditions are not vacuous.

Theorem 10
.

There exists a family of integer linear programming problems whose group relaxations satisfy conditions (
16a
) and (

) in Theorem

.

Proof.

The family is constructed using the procedure outlined in the proof of Theorem
6
, with some additional assumptions on the unimodular matrix
V^{\prime}
chosen in the construction therein. To recall for the reader, the construction considers arbitrary positive integers
t,m
, with

and

The conditions for super-quadratic speedup are

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[k]}\,\left\|h_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{cyc},1,\mathbf{c}}=\Theta\!\left(\frac{|{\textup{{OPT}}}_{\cal B}|}{\log\left(|K|/\lvert K^{\star}\rvert\right)}\right),\qquad\max_{j\in[k]}\lvert\langle h_{j}\rangle\rvert^{2}=\Theta\left(\frac{\log\left(|K|/\lvert K^{\star}\rvert\right)}{k}\right).

The group relaxation constraint is

\displaystyle U^{-1}A_{{\cal N}}\mathbf{x}=U^{-1}b\pmod{r\mathbb{Z}^{m}}.

Then let us follow the steps of Algorithm
2

U^{-1}U^{\prime}TV^{\prime}

\displaystyle\left\{\ell t:\ell\in[t]\right\}^{\times m}.

We then transform the generating set

via
(V^{\prime})^{-1}
and mod by
r\mathbb{Z}^{m}

Hence,
\max_{j\in[k]}\,\left\|h_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{cyc},1,\mathbf{c}}=\Theta_{m}(1)

\displaystyle\log(\lvert K\rvert/\lvert K^{\star}\rvert)=\Theta(m),\qquad|\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B}|=\Theta(m).

From the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle\ell+\sum_{j}tn_{j}\mathbf{e}_{j}\pmod{t^{2}\mathbb{Z}^{n}},\quad n_{j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,t-1\}.

This construction ensures that there is only one optimal solution, i.e. the element of the coset with smallest entries. We can pick

4.3
Expander walk approach

We can convert the product-of-cycles walk into an expander graph, with high probability, by randomly sampling additional elements from
K
. Specifically, if we use the minimal generating set
{\cal S}
found using Algorithm
2

C\log\lvert K\rvert
(for some constant

\widetilde{{\cal S}}
will generate
K
and the Cayley graph spanned by
\widetilde{{\cal S}}
is an expander (Algorithm
5

Alon–Roichman theorem

[
AR94
]
, which asserts that the spectral gap is
\delta\geq\epsilon
for a constant
\epsilon>0
. Since the Cayley walk that we generate is with high probability a spectral expander, we refer to this approach as the
Expander Walk

Appendix A
Compressing the search space

This appendix discusses an approach to shrink the kernel group
K
by compressing the ambient space. Recall from equation (
7
) that
K
is a subgroup of
Z=\mathbb{Z}_{r_{m}}^{d}
. Though this set is finite, it is clearly not the minimal choice. To see this, let
s_{j}=\left|\langle\mathbf{A}_{j}\rangle\right|
be the order of
\mathbf{A}_{j}
. Then, for any
\mathbf{x}\in K
the coordinate
\mathbf{x}_{j}
can always be reduced modulo
s_{j}\coloneqq\left|\langle\mathbf{A}_{j}\rangle\right|
while remaining in constraint. Explicitly, the orders are given by

\displaystyle s_{j}=\textup{lcm}\left(\frac{r_{1}}{\gcd(r_{1},\mathbf{A}_{1j})},\dots,\frac{r_{m}}{\gcd(r_{m},\mathbf{A}_{mj})}\right).

To see this, observe that any solution to
s_{i}\mathbf{A}_{ij}=0\pmod{r_{i}}
is a multiple of
\gcd(r_{i},\mathbf{A}_{ij})
. Any solution to these equations simultaneously must therefore be a common multiple. By definition of order, the smallest
s_{j}
satisfying these equations is therefore the
least
common multiple.

Note that
r_{m}
is a common multiple of the
\gcd(r_{i},A_{ij})
, hence
s_{j}|r_{m}
. This implies a natural homomorphism

Z\rightarrow Z^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{s_{i}}

obtained by reducing each component modulo
s_{i}
. The kernel
K
can be reduced under this map to some
K^{\prime}\leq Z^{\prime}
. In this way, we obtain a new, generally smaller kernel space containing the original optimal solutions. This procedure provides a benefit when

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\ll r_{m}^{d}.

A set of generators for
K^{\prime}
can be obtained from the generators
(h_{i})
of
K
by
h_{i}^{\prime}=h_{i}\pmod{Z^{\prime}}
. However these generators do not necessarily provide a direct sum decomposition of
K
. Such a decomposition is desirable for several reasons. At a high level, it allows for efficient search over the group elements without repetitions. More specifically to our purposes, we will find the cyclic decomposition useful for considering Markov chain approaches: the Cayley graph becomes a product of cycles, which has provably nice mixing properties.

To obtain cyclic generators for
K^{\prime}
, we utilize Algorithm 4.1.10 of Cohen
[
Coh00
]
for computing
K^{\prime}
. The set up is a follows: let
\varphi(x)=x\pmod{Z^{\prime}}
be the homomorphism mapping
Z
to
Z^{\prime}
(and thus
K
to
K^{\prime}
). We note that
\varphi
is “effective,” meaning images and pre-image elements are trivial to compute. The domain space
Z
is already in SNF (as a direct sum of cyclic groups, each order dividing the previous) , while the codomain
Z^{\prime}
can be put in SNF efficiently (especially given that the corresponding group relation matrix
\diag(s_{j})_{j}
is diagonal). Let
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{q})
be the cyclic generators of
Z
with invariant factors
z_{1},\ldots z_{q}
,
q\leq d
. We note that
\varphi
is still effective with respect to the new generators
(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{q}
.

The last ingredient is an effective description of
K
as a subgroup of
Z
. In particular, by Prop. 4.1.6 of
[
Coh00
]
,
any subgroup
A
of
B
is in 1-1 correspondence with integral matrices
H_{A}
in
Hermite Normal Form
(HNF). Such a matrix can be found in time polynomial in
d
, and typically faster than SNF. For our present case,
H_{K}
is the matrix obtained from an HNF algorithm on the block matrix

(h_{1}h_{2}\ldots h_{\ell}|r_{m}I_{d\times d}).

(19)

We thus assume access to the approriate descriptor
H_{K}
of
K
in
Z
.

With
H_{K}
in hand, Algorithm 4.1.10 outputs another HNF integer matrix
L
such that

(\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{q})L=(h_{1}^{\prime}\ldots h_{p}^{\prime})

(20)

where
(h_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{p}
form the cyclic generators of
K^{\prime}
and
p\leq\ell
. The runtime for this Algorithm is dominated by HNF calculations, which are again polynomial in
d
and the size of the invariant factors.

Here we also present an alternative and self-contained approach (Algorithm

K^{\prime}
.

(Runtime of

Suppose one has data

Recall

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\pmod{R\mathbb{Z}^{m}}

Let

. We then want to solve

\displaystyle D\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\pmod{S\mathbb{Z}^{d}},

which is to find the kernel of the map
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{y}\pmod{S\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
in
\langle h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\rangle\leq Z

. Let

. We then compute a generating set for the solution set to

\displaystyle BD\mathbf{x}=0\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}},

i.e. run algorithm
2
getting generators
\widetilde{m}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{m}_{t}\in Z
. We then know that the set of
m_{t}=D\widetilde{m}_{t}\pmod{r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
span the kernel of the map
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{y}\pmod{S\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
in
\langle h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\rangle\leq Z

Let us now work in the coset space
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cong Z
: we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}:=\langle h_{1}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle h_{k}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle\leq\langle\mathbf{e}_{1}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle\oplus\cdots\langle\mathbf{e}_{d}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle=\mathbb{Z}^{d}/r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

We also have that

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}:=\langle m_{1}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle m_{t}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle

forms the kernel of
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{y}\pmod{S\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
in
\langle h_{1}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle h_{k}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=[m_{1},\dots,m_{t}|r_{m}e_{1},\dots r_{m}e_{d}].

We then compute the SNF of
\mathbf{C}
to get
\mathbf{C}=UMV
. By unimodularity of

we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}\leq\langle u_{1}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle u_{d}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rangle=\mathbb{Z}^{d}/r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d},

We can also drop

is not in
\widetilde{Z}
. To see this: first, note that
M_{jj}=0
and the columns of

are linearly independent, so we have that for any integer

. The only option then is either
u_{j}\notin\widetilde{K}
or
\langle u_{j}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}\rangle\in\widetilde{Z}

\widetilde{Z}

Suppose the later. Then we know that if we delete the all-zero columns from

to get

then

. However, the columns of

are linearly independent and form a basis over

This implies

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}/\widetilde{K}=\langle(u_{1}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d})+\widetilde{K}\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle(u_{v}+r_{m}\mathbb{Z}^{d})+\widetilde{K}\rangle.

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}/\widetilde{K}\cong\langle u_{1}\pmod{S\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\rangle\oplus\cdots\oplus\langle u_{v}\pmod{S\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\rangle=K^{\prime}\leq\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{s_{j}},

Appendix B
A Gibbs sampling approach for quantum speedup

In this section, we present a Gibbs sampler that comes from applying a Metropolis filter (i.e. Metropolis-Hastings algorithm) to either of the Cayley walks described in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
.

While Gibbs sampling for inverse-temperature
\beta>0
will outperform the uniform sampling approaches, the complexity of classical Gibbs sampling from the null space
K
for
\beta>0
is unclear. The reason we still mention the Gibbs sampler, is that if one shows that classical Gibbs sampling at some
\beta>0
can be done classically in
\mathcal{O}(\textup{poly}(n))
time and the conditions we present are met, then there is a super-quadratic speedup over the Gibbs sampler.

Beyond uniform sampling, one can also introduce a Metropolis filter (run the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
[
LP17
]
) to perform Gibbs sampling over the feasible coset
\hat{\mathbf{x}}+K
using a walk with the corresponding Gibbs measure as its stationary distribution. The Gibbs distribution over
\hat{\mathbf{x}}+K
at inverse-temperature
\beta
is

\displaystyle\pi_{\beta}(x)\propto\exp\left(\beta\langle\mathbf{c},x\rangle\right),x\in\hat{\mathbf{x}}+K,

for
\beta>0
. The Metropolis filter performs an accept-reject step on top of another walk, referred to as the
base chain
. Specifically, we can use either the expander or product cycle walk. One step of the Metropolis-adjusted chain is as follows: starting from
x
, accept a move to
y\neq x
proposed by the base chain with probability
\min\left(1,\exp\left(\beta\langle\mathbf{c},y-x\rangle\right)\right)
.

For
\beta>0
, the Gibbs sampler will provide a strict improvement over uniform sampling. Unfortunately, it appears there is not much known about the complexity of sampling for
\beta>0
, implying that the current best theoretical result only applies to
\beta=0
.

The below theorem shows that if there is a
\beta>0
such that the classical Metropolis chain satisfies a LSI with sufficiently large constant, then SP can provide a super-quadratic speedup over the Gibbs sampler. The next result follows from Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
.

Theorem 12

(Conditional Super-Quadratic Speedup Over Gibbs Sampling)
.

Suppose there exists a
\beta>0
such that a Metropolis-adjusted Cayley Walk with stationary distribution
\pi_{\beta}
has LS constant lower bounded by
\omega=\Omega\left([\log(1/\pi_{\beta}(\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B})]^{-1})\right)
. Then, under condition (
16a
) from Theorem
9
, there exists a generalized quantum SP algorithm that runs in time

\mathcal{O}\left([\pi_{\beta}(\textup{{OPT}}_{\cal B})]^{-(1/2-\alpha)}\poly(n,L)\right),

(21)

for
\alpha>0
.

Note that the proof is effectively the same as Theorem
9
, as adding the Metropolis filter can only decrease
\Delta_{P}
, so the choices made for
\Delta_{P}
in the proof of Theorem
9
remain valid. In addition, the Metropolis-adjusted Cayley walk can be performed with a constant number of queries to the base chain and cost function operators using the approach of
[
LHP+20
]
combined with the Cayley-walk implementation previously mentioned and Section
4.1
. The above theorem implies that if there exists a
\beta>0
that satisfies the specified conditions, the runtime of both classical and quantum algorithms proposed in the main text can be improved as the distribution is tilted towards the optimal solutions. Unfortunately, for most general Gibbs measures, the distribution starts forming clusters and there is no local Markov chain that can efficiently sample from such a measure due to long range correlations.
