Prognostic value of plasma biomarkers in early Alzheimer’s disease: a longitudinal clinical and neuroimaging study
Authors: Frederikke Kragh Clemmensen, Mathias Holsey Gramkow, Fernando Gonzalez-Ortiz, Andréa Lessa Benedet, Kübra Tan, Wiebke Traichel, Ulrich Lindberg, Otto Mølby Henriksen, Henrik Zetterberg, Kaj Blennow, Ian Law, Anja Hviid Simonsen, Kristian Steen Frederiksen, Steen Gregers Hasselbalch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile blood biomarkers for AD have proven useful in identifying AD pathology from other neurodegenerative conditions, their prognostic value in real-world clinical settings, such as memory clinics, remains unexplored. We aimed to examine the prognostic value of AD plasma biomarkers and their short-term changes within 4 months, for predicting clinical and neuroradiological progression over two years.MethodWe performed a prospective observational longitudinal cohort study of patients with early stages of AD and followed them for up to 2 years. Plasma was analysed for neurofilament light (NfL), brain-derived tau (BD-tau), phosphorylated tau 217 (p-tau217), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) on single molecule array. The outcomes were annual changes in clinical dementia rating scale, sum of boxes (CDR-SB) and changes in [18F]-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography ([18F]FDG-PET) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain scans. We performed linear regression models with baseline and short-term plasma biomarker changes as predictors, adjusted for age, sex, and creatinine.ResultsIn total, 94 patients with MCI or dementia due to AD had baseline and one-year follow-up, with 85 patients completing two-year follow-up. We found that baseline plasma NfL was significantly associated with an increase in CDR-SB at one- and two-year follow-up. Neither plasma BD-tau, p-tau217 or GFAP were associated with future clinical progression. Short-term changes of plasma NfL were associated with reduced glucose metabolism in the hippocampus and temporal cortex on [18F]FDG-PET. Short-term changes in plasma p-tau217 were significantly associated with reduced hippocampal glucose metabolism, and further, short-term changes in the p-tau217/BD-tau ratio were significantly associated with reduced metabolism in hippocampal and isthmus cingulate cortex on [18F]FDG-PET. We found no association between plasma biomarkers and MRI volumetric changes after multiple testing correction.ConclusionOur findings indicate that plasma NfL can serve as a predictor for subsequent clinical progression in early AD. Short-term changes in AD-related plasma biomarkers may reflect underlying disease processes, however, several limitations affect their interpretability. Therefore, short-term changes should be evaluated carefully and always in the context of baseline levels and clinical characteristics.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov (NCT05175664), date of registration 2021–12-01.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-025-01892-7.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by the accumulation of beta-amyloid plagues, neurofibrillary tangles, and brain atrophy, causing a decline in cognitive functions consequentially leading to death. These pathological alterations start decades before symptom onset [1, 2]. Progression of AD varies considerably across individuals making disease prognosis a significant challenge. Development of prognostic tools is important to help guide clinical decisions, particular in patients in the early stages of AD when disease modifying therapies, i.e., anti-amyloid drugs, are presumed to be most beneficial. Blood biomarkers for AD have emerged as promising in reflecting various aspects of AD pathology including neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation, and should ideally work to identify individuals at risk of disease, detect disease pathology, monitor effects of interventions, and predict disease progression [3, 4].

Blood biomarkers reflecting neurodegeneration, such as neurofilament light (NfL), have been proposed as a potential prognostic neurodegenerative marker in AD [5–8]. Brain-derived tau (BD-tau) has recently been suggested as a neurodegenerative marker in plasma, selectively measuring tau proteins derived from the brain [9, 10] and has been shown to predict AD progression [11]. Phosphorylated tau at amino acid 217 (p-tau217) has been associated with risk of cognitive decline in cognitively unimpaired individual [12] and different p-tau isoforms have been found to be elevated depending on disease stage [13, 14]. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), released by activated astrocytes, has been suggested as a marker of neuroinflammation and elevated in the early stages of AD [15]. Further NfL, GFAP, and p-tau217 have been used as outcome measures in clinical trials with anti-amyloid drugs [16, 17]. These four markers each reflect different aspects of AD neuropathology and, thus, may contribute differently to our understanding of the AD continuum.

The prognostic value of AD biomarkers has been studied in preclinical cohorts with a high predictive value for progression in amyloid-positive vs. -negative individuals [7, 18]. Most of these studies are retrospective and aim to predict the risk of progression from cognitively unimpaired to MCI or dementia [14, 19–23]. In contrast, relatively few studies have explored the ability of plasma biomarkers to predict progression within the symptomatic AD spectrum—from early stages to more advanced dementia stages. These studies have reported that baseline plasma NfL was associated with cognitive decline [5, 6, 24], and further predicted MRI-detected brain volume loss in AD patients [6]. A recent review of blood biomarkers and MR modalities further supports these findings, reporting associations between plasma NfL, GFAP, p-tau181, and p-tau217 with various AD-specific regional brain atrophy patterns [25].

Biomarker levels can be affected by protein production, release and clearance, as well as pre-analytical and analytical procedures, causing variability in biomarker measures [26]. Factors such as sex, age, kidney function [27], BMI and fasting status [28], along with comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, peripheral nervous systems diseases, stroke history), significantly influence biomarker levels and must be considered when interpreting biomarker levels [29]. Using the plasma p-tau217 to non-phosphorylated tau ratio (%p-tau217) has been shown to mitigate the influence of peripheral confounders [30, 31] while the p-tau217 to BD-tau ratio has shown reduced variability [32], which reduces the possible risk of misclassification and misinterpretation of blood biomarkers levels and changes. However, whether changes in blood biomarkers over a few months only reflect intra-individual or assay variability, or whether it may reflect disease activity that entails prognostic value is not well-understood.

In this prospective longitudinal observational cohort study, we aimed to examine the predictive value of plasma biomarker levels and their short-term changes in memory clinic patients with early AD. Specifically, we examined if plasma NfL, p-tau217, BD-tau or GFAP levels could predict clinical progression in up to two years. Further, we assessed the prognostic value of the same markers when predicting imaging progression as assessed by volumetric MRI and semi-quantitative 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography ([18F]FDG-PET).

Methods

Study design

Prospective longitudinal cohort study.

Participants

From January 2022 to June 2023, we included patients with MCI or mild to moderate dementia due to AD from a university memory clinic at Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark. Patients were included at the time of their diagnosis and followed for up to two years, see study flow chart in Fig. 1. Participants were referred to the Memory clinic (a tertiary centre) due to cognitive complaints. The diagnostic standard work-up program includes assessment of medical history and physical examination by an experienced physician, cognitive testing by Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [33] and Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (ACE) [34], a structural brain scan (magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT), and routine blood examination (kidney function, D-vitamin, and B12-vitamin status). If necessary, additional examinations such as cerebrospinal fluid examination, [18F]FDG-PET, amyloid PET, or dopamine active transporter scans, are carried out. At a multidisciplinary consensus conference, the diagnosis is given based on the criteria for MCI or dementia due to AD [35, 36]. For further information on the diagnostic evaluation in the memory clinic Rigshospitalet, see [37].

Inclusion criteria for this study were a diagnosis of MCI or mild to moderate dementia due to AD, a MMSE > 19, a baseline diagnostic [18F]FDG-PET/MRI or [18F]FDG-PET/CT scan, presence of a caregiver to serve as an informant, and the patient’s ability to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were current alcohol or substance abuse, other major neurologic or psychiatric disorder, or participation in intervention trials. The study was approved by the Regional Scientific Ethics Committee (H-21040317) and was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05175664). The study followed the tenets of the 1975 and 2024 Declaration of Helsinki.

Baseline clinical, demographic and biochemical data

Baseline cognitive test results, which included MMSE, ACE, and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) were stored in an electronic database (REDCap, Vancouver). Demographic and medical data were extracted from the medical records and included age, sex, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), years of education, medication status and comorbidities. Biochemical data of relevance in this study included glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). For patients with an eGFR > 90, the numeric value was calculated as Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CDK-EPI) mL/min/1.73m2 based on creatinine level, age and sex. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from patients who underwent cerebrospinal fluid examination as part of their diagnostic evaluation was analysed at the Clinical Biochemistry Laboratory at Rigshospitalet. CSF from patients enrolled before June 30th, 2022, was analysed with the Innotest enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) (Fujirebio, Ghent) (n = 8) and CSF collected after July 1 st, 2022, was analysed with the Elecsys sandwich electrochemiluminescence-immunoassay (Roche, Cobas 8000) [38, 39].

Longitudinal follow up visits

At each study visit patients were asked to bring a primary caregiver. Study visits were scheduled 3–5 months, 12 months (denoted 1Y), and 18–24 (denoted 2Y) months after baseline visit. At each study visit a blood sample was collected, and a MMSE was administered. At 1 and 2Y, additionally cognitive testing included ACE and the semi-structured interview, the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) was carried out with the caregiver and patient [40]. CDR rates cognition and function by assessing six domains (Memory, Orientation, Judgement and Problem solving, Community affairs, Home and Hobbies, and Personal care) on a scale ranging from 0 to 18 (higher the score indicating greater impairment), termed the Sum-of-Boxes (SB).

A follow-up hybrid [18F]FDG-PET/MRI brain scan was carried out at the Department of Clinical Physiology and Nuclear Medicine, Rigshospitalet, approximately two weeks prior to the 1Y FU.

Finally, the study investigator carried out a routine medical assessment at each study visit, including evaluation of disease severity stage, blood pressure, weight, and medicine, including antidementia medication drugs, which were modified when clinically indicated.

Blood sampling and analyses

Baseline plasma was collected as part of the patient’s diagnostic work up program in the clinic. If, at baseline, a blood sample was collected more than 3 months earlier, a new blood sample was collected. At each study visit a blood sample was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)–plasma tubes. Within 30 to 120 min after collection, the tubes were centrifuged at 2,000 g, 4 °C for 10 min, then redistributed into 250μl aliquots and, stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Plasma was analyzed at the Department of Psychiatry and Neurochemistry, the Sahlgrenska Academy, University of Gothenburg, in December 2024 and January 2025. Using the single molecule array (Simoa) technology by Quanterix HD-X [29]. Plasma NfL and GFAP were measured by the Simoa Neurology 2-PLEX B assay, p-tau217 by the Simoa singleplex assay ALZpath p-tau217, and plasma BD-tau was measured with the Gothenburg University previously validated method [9]. Analyses were carried out according to Simoa instructions [29] with samples run in singlicates, and in one round of experiment. Samples from each participant were placed next to each other on the same plate. NfL and GFAP analysis were diluted fourfold, and p-tau217 threefold—results were compensated for the dilution. Concentrations lower than the calibration curve were labelled under limits of quantification (< LOQ).

Biomarker changes between baseline and 4M were calculated as the relative change (%), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\frac{4M-baseline}{baseline}\bullet 100)$$\end{document}, denoted short-term changes. We also included the p-tau217/BD-tau ratio as this has previously been found to reduce biomarker variability [32].

Clinical progression

To assess clinical progression, our primary outcome was changes in score on the CDR-SB. We defined changes as a continuous variable, hence the differences in points between baseline and 1- or 2-year FU, normalized to represent annual changes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\frac{\text{follow up}-\text{baseline}}{\text{time between baseline and follow up}})$$\end{document}. As secondary clinical outcomes, we used changes in disease severity stages, which were based on the full clinical assessment at each visit (CDR-SB, MMSE, ACE, patient and caregiver information) and assessed by the trained study investigator at each study visit in accordance with the NIA-AA [36] and the WHO ICD-11 classification [41]. We categorized patients into four groups based on: MCI stable (MCI patients who remained MCI), MCI converter (MCI patients who progressed to dementia), dementia stable (patients with dementia who remained at the same severity stage (mild, moderate, severe), and dementia progression (dementia patients who advanced to more severe stages), assessed at one- and two-year FU. To improve statistical power given the limited sample size, these four groups were further merged into two categories: stable and progression.

Imaging progression—[18F]FDG-PET/MRI

All patients who had an [18F]FDG-PET (n = 67) and MRI (n = 70) both at baseline and at one year FU were included for analysis. The scans were performed on a hybrid 3 T PET/MRI scanner (Biograph mMR, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany), except for 14 individuals who received separate 1.5T MRI and PET scan at baseline. PET imaging was performed according to current guidelines [42]. To assess potential impact of scanner differences, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding these 14 participants. Simultaneous PET and MRI was commenced 40 min after i.v. administration of approx. 200 MBq [18F]FDG. The MRI protocol included 3D-T1-weighted (T1), T2-weighted (T2), T2 fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and T2*-weighted imaging. The T1-weighted MRI images together with FDG-PET data were analysed using the volumetric longitudinal pipeline and PETSurfer (FreeSurfer, ver. 7.4.1) [43]. Volumetric quantifications were extracted for predefined AD specific regions of interest (ROIs) that included total grey matter, cortex, hippocampal, and lateral ventricle volumes, normalised to the intra-cranial volume (ICV). Selection of regions was based on previous findings of changes in MRI regions in AD [7, 44]. From [18F]FDG-PET scans, regional Standardised Uptake Value ratios (SUVr) was calculated by normalizing ROI SUV to uptake in the reference region (cerebellum). Based on previous findings, predefined AD specific regional SUVr measures of interest were hippocampal, isthmus cingulate, temporal, and parietal cortex [45].

Annual changes in [18F]FDG-PET and MRI measures were defined as the difference between baseline and FU volume/SUVr, adjusted for baseline volume/SUVr and time between baseline and 1Y \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\frac{\frac{1Y-baseline}{baseline}}{time between baseline and 1Y}*100)$$\end{document}.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics summarized baseline cohort characteristics. Normality of distribution was assessed by visual inspection of histograms and quantile–quantile plots. Continuous variables are presented as mean (+ -standard deviation (SD)) and median and interquartile range (IQR) depending on data distribution. Categorical variables are summarised as frequence and percentage. Outliers were assessed for each biomarker by the interquartile range (IQR) method. One observation of baseline plasma NfL above the 95% percentile, was identified. The patient had a baseline NfL value above > 100mg/mL with levels that decreased by −50% at follow-up visits. The abnormal level was, based on the medical record, determined to be caused by a mild head trauma prior to the baseline blood sampling, and the patient was therefore excluded from analyses with NfL. Group differences were assessed by the two-sample t-test or chi-square test. To assess group differences in baseline plasma levels, these were log-transformed for normal distribution and examined by linear regression models, adjusted for age, sex, and creatinine.

Linear regression models were used to assess the relationship between annual clinical and imaging changes by the explanatory variables, baseline and short-term changes in plasma biomarker levels. Outcome measures were annual changes in CDR-SB, annual changes in MRI region of interest (ROI) volumes, and annual changes in [18F]FDG-PET, regional SUVr. We evaluated two models 1) baseline biomarker, adjusted for age, sex, and creatinine (see correlation coefficients in Additional file 10, Fig. 3), and 2) short-term biomarker changes, additionally adjusted for baseline biomarker values. As there was a significant negative association between baseline biomarker levels and short-term changes, with a tendency of lower baseline levels leading to greater short-term changes (see Additional file 9, Fig. 2), all linear regression models were examined with an interaction term (baseline*short-term changes), to adjust for regression toward the mean. If a significant effect, the interaction term was included in the model. If not, the models were adjusted for baseline biomarker level.

As a secondary clinical outcome, we assessed the biomarkers performance to discriminate between stable and progression, by fitting two logistic regression models: 1) baseline biomarker, adjusted for age, sex, and creatinine and 2) short-term biomarker changes, additionally adjusted for baseline biomarker values. The dataset was split into two: 80% training and a 20% hold out test set. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). To quantify uncertainty, 95% confidence intervals for AUCs were computed using bootstrap resampling with 2,000 replicates.

To better address how both intra-individual and assay-specific variability influence short-term changes of biomarkers, we included biomarker specific reference change values (RCV, %) from previously published data on RCVs of plasma NfL, GFAP, and p-tau217 in AD patients [26]. RCV represents the minimum change between two measurements needed to exceed intra-individual variability and assay variation, and thus is important to ensure clinically relevant interpretation of changes in biomarker levels. For plasma BD-tau, we included data from a previously published study and calculated the RCV [32]. We created a three-level categorical variable with short-term changes grouped as within, above or below the markers’ RCV, and used it in model 2.

Model assumptions were checked and met. To account for multiple comparisons across imaging regions, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05. β-estimates, 95% confidence intervals and p-values (both raw and multivariable correction) are reported. All statistical analyses were performed using the R Statistical Software, version 4.5.0 and statistical significance was set to alpha = 0.05.

Results

Participants

A total of 94 patients had baseline and 1 year FU. Out of these, 85 also completed 2-year follow-up. Nine patients discontinued participation after the one-year follow-up, either because they changed their mind or felt that the testing burden was too high. Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1, grouped as stable (n = 54) vs. progression (n = 40). In general, the two groups were comparable, however the progression group was significantly older, had higher levels of plasma creatinine, and showed a higher proportion of patients treated with antithrombotic medication (45% vs. 17%; Table 1). We found no differences in various comorbidities (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, or depression) or treatment with cholinesterase inhibitors (data not shown) between groups. Plasmatic levels of NfL were higher in the progression group at baseline, but no other differences in biomarker concentrations were found. Discussion

In this prospective longitudinal study of patients with early stages of AD, we investigated whether baseline and short-term changes in plasma NfL, BD-tau, p-tau217, and GFAP could predict clinical progression. We further investigated whether these were supported by changes in brain metabolism and structural changes measured by [18F]FDG-PET and MRI, respectively.

Our findings demonstrate that plasma NfL was significantly associated with annual clinical progression up to two years after blood sampling. None of the other biomarkers were associated with clinical progression. We found associations between annual decreased relative regional brain glucose metabolism and short-term changes in plasma NfL, p-tau217, and the p-tau217/BD-tau ratio. Regarding MRI, the association with short-term changes in BD-tau and cortical and grey matter atrophy, was not significant after multiple testing correction.

Our finding that elevated plasma NfL levels were associated with clinical progression is consistent with previous studies linking plasma NfL levels to cognitive progression [5–7, 24, 46]. The association between plasma NfL and reduced relative regional glucose uptake on [18F]FDG-PET, is also consistent with previous studies [45, 47–49]. In contrast, we found no association between plasma NfL and MRI brain volume loss, which has been suggested previously in several studies [7, 6, 44, 49–51], and recently summarised in a comprehensive review on AD blood biomarkers and MRI measures in AD [25]. Many of these studies on NfL and MRI had longer follow up time which may explain the discrepancies [7, 44, 49, 50]. Our finding of a relationship between short-term NfL changes and glucose metabolism, but not with MRI atrophy, may reflect more subtle underlying neuropathological changes, that are not yet associated with structural changes detectable by MRI.

In contrast, we found that short-term BD-tau changes were associated with MRI atrophy, including cortical and total grey matter volume, consistent with previous findings [11]. This implies that plasma BD-tau could be sensitive to widespread MRI-detected atrophy and may be more robust in reflecting structural brain changes than NfL.

P-tau217 is suggested as a core blood biomarker for detection of amyloid pathology [52, 53] and has been proposed to also hold prognostic abilities across the AD continuum [54]. In this study we did not find any association with clinical progression, in contrast to previous studies reporting that baseline plasma p-tau217 could predict cognitive decline in cognitive unimpaired older individuals [12, 55]. This supports plasma p-tau217 as a valuable marker for predicting conversion from cognitively unimpaired to MCI or dementia, while suggesting that p-tau217 may provide different information within the clinical AD continuum. We also considered if the p-tau217 to BD-tau ratio, could be a more sensitive prognostic marker as the ratio may be less influenced by peripheral confounders [31] and lead to less intra-individual variability [32]. Though we found a negative association with one-year CDR-SB, the estimate was considered of limited clinical importance. We did find an association with relative brain glucose metabolism across several AD specific regions (hippocampus and isthmus cingulate cortex). Thus, we cannot exclude that the ratio may offer better insight to the underlying neuropathological changes by reducing the effect of peripheral confounders.

Lastly, we found no association between plasma GFAP and either clinical or imaging progression. Plasma GFAP has been suggested as a marker that could predict clinical AD incidence several years prior to diagnosis [56] but may also entail considerable concerns regarding its discrepancy with CSF GFAP and expression in other non-brain tissue complicating its disease specificity as a brain biomarker [57].

In general, we observed that most short-term changes were within the expected range defined by the marker’s RCV, suggesting that these fluctuations may be largely attributed to intra-individual and assay variability rather than true pathological change. While Simoa is highly sensitive, alternative platforms such as the Fujirebio Lumipulse platform or the NUcleic acid-Linked Immuno-Sorbent Assay developed by Alamar Biosciences could offer improved precision, which is important for detecting subtle short-term pathological changes. Furthermore, we found that short-term changes in biomarker levels were negatively associated with baseline, likely reflecting the statistical phenomenon of regression toward the mean. Thus, we had to adjust for baseline levels to ensure unbiased models. Therefore, we suggest that for clinical interpretation of biomarker changes, one should be careful not to oversimplify biomarker trajectories without considering their interaction with baseline status. However, we acknowledge that the relationship between short-term biomarker changes and imaging findings is exploratory and that these results should be interpreted with the caveat of the above-mentioned. Future work on biomarker changes should continue to consider statistical controls for this phenomenon and, where possible, take RCVs into account. We suggest that future studies should investigate short-term biomarker changes in diverse cohorts as indicators of disease susceptibility and risk of progression, while concurrently monitoring external factors such as infection or inflammation (e.g., C-reactive protein, leukocytes, neutrophils), physical activity, and fasting status, which could further improve the interpretation of these biomarkers’ variabilities.

This study has some limitations. The modest sample size and dropouts after one-year follow-up may compromise statistical power and generalisability. The study was not designed to account for pre-analytical factors such as fasting status that may influence plasma biomarker levels [28]. Further, in this study we did not include markers of common AD co-pathology, such as vascular changes or alpha-synuclein, that may contribute to clinical symptoms and neurodegeneration, potentially masking associations between AD specific biomarkers and disease progression [58]. The restriction of imaging analyses to a predefined set of regions warrants some limitations. While our selection was based on methodological considerations, including the risk of type I error, other regions may also be highly informative in early disease stages. Future studies with larger cohorts should extend the regional analyses to capture a more complete picture of disease progression. Finally, previous studies reporting associations between baseline plasma markers and MRI changes used follow-up periods of 2–4 years [7, 44], suggesting that longer follow-up may be needed to detect structural changes that biomarkers could potentially predict. Because some patients had their baseline scan performed on a 1.5T MRI scanner, we re-evaluated the MRI analysis after excluding these individuals. This exclusion, however, did not alter our results after correction for multiple testing, see Additional file 8, Fig. 1. Finally, clinically observed progression in AD is a complex interplay between neuropathological changes, comorbidities and psychosocial factors and remains a challenge to measure objectively. Although this study included well-validated objective measures of clinical progression, the authors acknowledge that establishing a relationship between biological markers and clinical disease progression is a complex task. Future studies should further validate plasma biomarkers in larger and more diverse populations to support their clinical implementation as AD specific prognostic biomarkers.

We consider that our use of a prospective study design in a real-life memory clinic cohort of patients with early AD provides an important addition to studies investigating retrospective cohorts measuring disease progression from cognitively unimpaired to MCI or AD dementia [14, 19–23]. Despite our limited sample size, this cohort is a unique collection of well-characterised patients including information on several potential confounders and comorbidities. We found a significant effect of several confounding factors, including age, sex, and creatinine, suggesting that these may confound the relationship between biomarker levels and progression risk. Finally, the study design allowed novel investigations of short-term changes in AD plasma biomarkers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings indicate that plasma NfL may serve as a predictor of disease progression in early stages of AD. Baseline plasma NfL was associated with subsequent clinical progression, while short-term changes in NfL were linked to changes in AD-specific regional brain hypometabolism. However, none of the other markers were associated with clinical progression. Although some of the biomarkers' short-term changes may reflect underlying disease processes, there are several caveats to this interpretation, such as intra-individual variability and comorbidities. Therefore, short-term biomarker changes should be carefully evaluated and always in the context of baseline values.
