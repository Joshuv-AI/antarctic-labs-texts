Intracardiac Thrombi Mimicking Cardiac Metastases in a Patient With Lung Adenocarcinoma

Case Presentation

A 50-year-old man presented with extensive left lower extremity deep venous thrombosis and was subsequently diagnosed with ROS1-positive lung adenocarcinoma. Anticoagulation was initiated with fondaparinux (7.5 mg daily) and later with rivaroxaban (20 mg daily).

The patient subsequently developed worsening dyspnea, prompting transthoracic echocardiography, which revealed 2 mobile intracardiac masses—a 20 × 34 mm mass within the right atrium and an 18 × 25 mm mass within the right ventricle—entangled in the tricuspid subvalvular apparatus. Biventricular systolic function was preserved. Laboratory testing showed markedly elevated D-dimer levels, hypofibrinogenemia, and elevated lactate dehydrogenase, consistent with severe hypercoagulability.

The patient was admitted to the cardiological intensive care unit, where repeat imaging demonstrated persistent venous thrombosis and distal pulmonary embolism despite anticoagulation. Intravenous unfractionated heparin (target activated partial thromboplastin time: 2.0-2.5× baseline) and targeted therapy with entrectinib were initiated, but serial echocardiography over 10 days showed no significant reduction in the intracardiac masses.

Given the persistence of the masses, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) was performed. Both lesions demonstrated low T1 signal intensity, mildly increased T2 signal, elevated T2 mapping values, and heterogeneous late gadolinium enhancement, raising concern for metastases given overlapping imaging features.

Because of the diagnostic uncertainty and the perceived risk of embolization, a multidisciplinary heart team recommended surgical intervention.

The right atrial mass was successfully excised. The ventricular lesion was left in place because of its deep involvement within the tricuspid subvalvular apparatus.

Histopathologic examination of the atrial mass demonstrated organizing fibrin with inflammatory cells and cellular debris. Immunohistochemical staining was negative for markers of lung adenocarcinoma, establishing the diagnosis of thrombus.

Following surgery, anticoagulation was changed to high-dose enoxaparin (8,000 IU twice daily). One month later, follow-up echocardiography demonstrated complete resolution of the remaining right ventricular mass, supporting a thrombotic etiology.

Discussion

Differentiating intracardiac thrombi from cardiac metastases is particularly challenging in patients with active malignancy, in whom hypercoagulability and metastatic disease may coexist.1,2 Although echocardiography is the first-line imaging modality for its wide availability and excellent temporal resolution, CMR is the preferred noninvasive technique for evaluation of intracardiac masses, as it provides superior tissue characterization. Thrombi are typically avascular, and they demonstrate absent or minimal late gadolinium enhancement, whereas malignant lesions commonly exhibit contrast enhancement and increased T2 signal intensity.2 The strengths and limitations of different imaging modalities for intracardiac masses is summarized in Supplemental Table 1.

In the present case, CMR findings demonstrated heterogeneous late gadolinium enhancement and elevated T2 signal, raising concern for malignancy. However, histopathologic examination identified an organizing thrombus, and subsequent resolution of the untreated ventricular lesion with therapeutic low–molecular weight heparin supported a thrombotic etiology.

The atypical CMR appearance likely reflected the fibrin-rich and inflammatory composition of a cancer-associated thrombus resulting from malignancy-associated hypercoagulability.3 Organized thrombi may occasionally exhibit contrast enhancement on CMR, limiting its specificity for distinguishing thrombus from tumor.2 In patients with lung adenocarcinoma, where cardiac metastases are relatively common, this overlap can create a significant diagnostic dilemma.1

This case demonstrates that imaging findings should always be interpreted within the overall clinical context. When noninvasive imaging and clinical suspicion are discordant, histopathologic confirmation may be required to establish the correct diagnosis and guide management.

(A) Transthoracic echocardiography. Apical 4-chamber view focused on the right chambers demonstrated 2 mobile right-sided intracardiac masses. One mass was attached to the right atrial free wall, whereas the second was located within the right ventricle and entangled in the tricuspid subvalvular apparatus. The lesions measured 20 × 34 mm and 18 × 25 mm, respectively. (B to I) Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. T1-weighted 4-chamber (B) and right ventricular outflow tract (C) views demonstrated low signal intensity of both masses. T1 mapping (D) showed reduced signal within the right atrial lesion. T2-weighted 4-chamber (E) and right ventricular outflow tract (F) views demonstrated mildly increased signal intensity. T2 mapping (G) showed elevated values within the right atrial mass. Late gadolinium enhancement images demonstrated heterogeneous enhancement of the right atrial (H) and right ventricular (I) masses. (J and K) Surgical removal of the right atrial mass.
