Seronegative Hypophysitis Presenting as Unilateral Lateral Rectus Palsy
Authors: Edwin G Pullan, Mathew Abraham, Ramzeen N Hameed
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Hypophysitis is an uncommon inflammatory condition of the pituitary gland that may be primary or secondary, often associated with autoimmune disorders, systemic inflammation, or immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy.[12] Clinically, it presents with headache, visual disturbances, hypopituitarism, or symptoms related to pituitary mass effect.[34] Ophthalmoplegia due to cranial nerve involvement is rare and usually results from cavernous sinus extension of the inflammatory process.[123]

Unilateral lateral rectus palsy, resulting from dysfunction of the abducens (sixth) cranial nerve, most commonly arises from microvascular ischemia, increased intracranial pressure, or compressive lesions.[5] Its occurrence as an initial manifestation of hypophysitis is extremely rare.[12] Recognizing this unusual presentation is important, as timely diagnosis and treatment can prevent permanent neurological and endocrine deficits.[67]

A 39-year-old male with no prior comorbidities presented with one month of diplopia and left eye pain, preceded by headache. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain performed at an outside center was reviewed, and it showed that the pituitarygland was diffusely enlarged with symmetric expansion of the sella [Figure 1]. There was compression of the optic chiasma and enhancement of the left cavernous sinus [Figure 2]. The pituitary stalk appeared thickened (>3 mm) [Figure 3] and demonstrated uniform enhancement, findings strongly suggestive of hypophysitits. No dural tail sign was observed.

On admission to our center six months earlier, examination revealed left eye proptosis with isolated left lateral rectus palsy, whereas the rest of the neurological examination was normal. Given the atypical presentation with isolated sixth nerve involvement, further evaluation was undertaken. The vasculitis workup (antinuclear antibody [ANA], cytoplasmic antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody [C-ANCA], and perinuclear ANCA [P-ANCA]) was negative. Serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels were also negative, and in the absence of other clinical features, sarcoidosis was ruled out. Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) subclass 4 was also negative. In addition, serum myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein IgG antibody and aquaporin 4 IgG antibody were negative. A lumbar puncture was performed and showed a total cell count of 5 cells with lymphocyte predominance. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology was negative for malignancy. CSF IgG levels were within normal limits. Neurosurgical and endocrinology opinions favored conservative management. Ophthalmology evaluation, including diplopia charting, confirmed left lateral rectus palsy.

A working diagnosis of atypical seronegative autoimmune hypophysitis was made. Pituitary biopsy was advised to establish a definitive diagnosis, but the patient was unwilling to undergo the procedure. The patient was started on high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 5 days). He showed clinical improvement, with resolution of pain and improvement in ocular movements. He was discharged on tapering dose oral steroids (tablet prednisolone 50 mg once daily for 10 days, followed by tablet wysolone 40 mg once daily for 10 days). At 10-day outpatient follow-up, he reported marked improvement of symptoms. However, he could not continue to follow up with us as he was working in another part of the country. He was therefore advised to continue further follow-up at a center of his preference.

Hypophysitis may present with headache, visual disturbance, hypopituitarism, or features of a sellar mass.[78] Cranial neuropathies, particularly involving the abducens nerve, are extremely uncommon and usually occur due to extension of inflammation into the cavernous sinus.[29] Several reports describe similar presentations of abducens nerve palsy associated with hypophysitis, often mimicking pituitary adenoma or other infiltrative disorders.[35]

The differential diagnoses considered included pituitary adenoma, infiltrative disorders such as sarcoidosis and IgG4-related disease, demyelinating disease, and paraneoplastic etiologies.[34] Extensive investigations, including hormonal assays, autoimmune markers, CSF analysis, and imaging studies, are essential to exclude these possibilities.[78] In our patient, the extensive workup for these causes was negative.[610] MRI of the brain demonstrated the characteristic finding of thickening of the pituitary stalk, which was strongly suggestive of hypophysitis. As the patient was unwilling to undergo a pituitary biopsy, he was managed as a case of seronegative hypophysitis.

This case underscores the importance of recognizing atypical presentations of hypophysitis. Isolated cranial nerve palsies should raise suspicion for pituitary or parasellar pathology, when associated with pituitary enlargement on imaging.[29] Early multidisciplinary involvement and escalation of immunosuppressive therapy can lead to favorable outcomes.[4610]

Hypophysitis can rarely present with isolated cranial neuropathy, such as unilateral abducens nerve palsy.[129] Thorough evaluation is essential to exclude alternative causes.[345] High-dose corticosteroid therapy may result in significant clinical improvement.[6710] This case highlights the need to consider hypophysitis in the differential diagnosis of atypical cranial nerve palsies to ensure timely diagnosis and management.[78]
