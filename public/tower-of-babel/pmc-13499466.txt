Intracranial solitary fibrous tumor with multiloculated cystic change: A case report
Authors: Yasuyuki Kojita, Tomonori Kanda, Shiho Yokoo, Tomoka Kanai, Hiroaki Sanada, Mari Idei, Hiroaki Nagashima, Naoe Jimbo, Takeaki Ishihara, Kazunari Ishii, Takamichi Murakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracranial solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is a mesenchymal neoplasm that typically appears as a solid mass, whereas cases with predominantly cystic change are extremely uncommon. We report a case of intracranial SFT presenting as a cystic lesion. A 67-year-old woman presented with progressive memory impairment and gait instability over a 6-month period. CT and MRI revealed a mass located along the falx cerebri, composed predominantly of cystic components. The patient underwent surgical tumor resection, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of SFT, CNS WHO grade 2. Postoperative radiotherapy was administered, and no evidence of recurrence was observed during a 2-year follow-up period. Because SFT is an aggressive tumor with a high risk of recurrence and distant metastasis, accurate diagnosis is essential for appropriate treatment planning and postoperative surveillance. Therefore, SFT should be included in the differential diagnosis of intracranial tumors with cystic morphology.Keywords: Solitary fibrous tumor, Intracranial tumor, Cystic tumor, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is a mesenchymal neoplasm of fibroblastic origin characterized by the presence of the NAB2–STAT6 fusion gene and can arise in virtually any anatomical location, including the pleura [1,2]. Occurrence in the central nervous system is extremely rare, accounting for less than 1% of all intracranial tumors [3]. Tumors previously classified as hemangiopericytoma (HPC) were integrated into the SFT category in the 2016 WHO classification of tumors of the central nervous system, and the term “HPC” was subsequently eliminated in the 2021 fifth edition of the WHO classification. Currently, SFTs are classified into CNS WHO grades 1-3 based on histopathological features [[4], [5], [6]].

Although recent advances in immunohistochemical and molecular genetic techniques have improved the diagnostic accuracy of SFT, preoperative radiological diagnosis remains challenging because intracranial SFTs can closely resemble other dural-based tumors, such as meningiomas. Diagnosis is particularly difficult when SFTs show cystic change, as their appearance may differ from that of typical solid SFTs [[7], [8], [9]]. Nevertheless, preoperative recognition of SFT is clinically important because these tumors have a high risk of local recurrence and may occasionally develop extracranial metastases, with implications for treatment planning and long-term postoperative follow-up [10]. Therefore, further accumulation of atypical cases and detailed analyses of their imaging characteristics are needed to better understand the diagnostic spectrum of SFT.

We report a case of intracranial SFT with multiloculated cystic change that was difficult to differentiate from other cystic intracranial tumors on preoperative imaging.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old woman presented with a 6-month history of progressive memory impairment and gait instability, followed by worsening left-sided upper and lower limb weakness over the preceding month. She had no history of malignancy, intracranial tumor, cranial irradiation, or neurosurgical procedures. Her family history was unremarkable.

On neurological examination, she was alert and had no disorientation. Manual muscle testing showed grade 3/5 weakness in the left upper and lower extremities. She dragged her left leg while walking, and left hemispatial neglect was also observed. Preoperative laboratory tests showed no clinically significant abnormalities. Serum tumor marker levels were not elevated.

Noncontrast CT performed at presentation is shown in Fig. 1. A low-attenuation mass measuring 6.0 × 4.8 × 5.9 cm was identified on the right side of the falx cerebri with internal septations. A small solid component with attenuation similar to that of gray matter was observed adjacent to the falx, extending across the midline to the contralateral side. No intratumoral calcification was detected.

MRI findings are shown in Fig. 2. The majority of the lesion consisted of multiloculated cystic components showing hypointensity on T1-weighted images and hyperintensity on T2-weighted images. The small solid component adjacent to the falx cerebri was isointense to gray matter on T1-weighted images. On fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging, the cystic components demonstrated slightly higher signal intensity than cerebrospinal fluid. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging demonstrated enhancement along the cyst wall and in the small solid component adjacent to the falx. The solid component extended across the falx and exhibited restricted diffusion, with a mean apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) value of 0.63 × 10−3 mm²/s. No intratumoral hemorrhage was identified. Mild peritumoral edema was observed.

Based on the imaging findings, cystic meningioma and metastatic brain tumor were considered in the differential diagnosis. Because of progressive neurological deterioration, surgical tumor resection was performed 1 week after MRI examination. The tumor was firmly adherent to the falx cerebri just beneath the superior sagittal sinus, and subtotal resection was achieved.

Histopathological findings are shown in Fig. 3. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed densely proliferating short spindle-shaped cells with oval nuclei, accompanied by thin-walled, dilated, branching vessels with a staghorn appearance. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positivity for STAT6 and focal positivity for CD34, while EMA, CK CAM5.2, CK AE1/AE3, TTF-1, and PAX8 were negative. The Ki-67 labeling index was approximately 10%. Based on these findings, the tumor was diagnosed as SFT, and CNS WHO grade 2 was assigned according to the mitotic count.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining at low (A) and high (B) magnification demonstrates densely proliferating short spindle-shaped cells with oval nuclei, accompanied by thin-walled, dilated, branching vessels with a staghorn appearance. (C) Immunohistochemical staining shows strong nuclear STAT6 expression. (D) CD34 shows focal positivity. The Ki-67 labeling index was approximately 10% (not shown).

As adjuvant therapy, intensity-modulated radiation therapy was administered at a total dose of 60.0 Gy in 30 fractions of 2.0 Gy per day. Follow-up MRI performed 2 years after treatment showed no evidence of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

We report a case of intracranial SFT presenting with multiloculated cystic change that was difficult to differentiate from other cystic intracranial tumors on preoperative imaging. Most intracranial SFTs appear as well-circumscribed, solid, dural-based masses with strong contrast enhancement [[11], [12], [13]]. Although cystic degeneration has been reported in approximately 17%-42% of SFTs [11,12], cases in which cystic components predominate and occupy most of the tumor, as observed in the present case, are extremely rare. SFTs with cystic change often resemble cystic meningiomas or metastatic brain tumors on imaging, making accurate preoperative diagnosis challenging [9,14].

Recent studies have described several MRI features that may help differentiate intracranial SFT from meningioma. Regarding T1-weighted imaging, Cho et al. reported that high signal intensity relative to gray matter on T1-weighted images was more frequently observed in intracranial SFTs than in meningiomas, suggesting that T1 hyperintensity may serve as a supportive finding for the diagnosis of SFT [15]. Regarding ADC values, Ran et al. compared grade II/III intracranial SFTs with meningiomas and reported significantly lower ADC values in SFTs, supporting the utility of ADC values in differential diagnosis [16]. Other ADC-based studies have also reported that the solid components of SFTs tend to show low ADC values [[17], [18], [19]]. In the present case, the small solid component adjacent to the falx cerebri was isointense to gray matter on T1-weighted images. In contrast, the same solid component showed restricted diffusion, with a mean ADC value of 0.63 × 10−3 mm²/s, which was consistent with previous reports. Although most of the lesion consisted of multiloculated cystic components, evaluation of the solid component helped identify imaging features compatible with SFT.

Histopathologically, SFTs are characterized by proliferation of spindle-shaped cells and abundant collagenous stroma, and they frequently show positivity for CD34 on immunohistochemical staining [8,13]. Recently, the NAB2–STAT6 fusion gene has been identified as a characteristic molecular abnormality in nearly all SFTs, and nuclear expression of STAT6 has become a highly reliable diagnostic marker [1,8]. In the present case, tumor cells demonstrated nuclear positivity for STAT6, supporting the diagnosis of intracranial SFT, CNS WHO grade 2.

Surgical resection is the first-line treatment for intracranial SFT, and gross total resection (GTR) is recommended whenever feasible [3]. However, when tumors extend into deep brain structures or involve venous sinuses, subtotal resection (STR) may be unavoidable to ensure patient safety. In such cases, postoperative radiotherapy is recommended as adjuvant therapy, particularly in STR cases and high-grade tumors, and has been reported to improve local tumor control [[20], [21], [22]]. Compared with meningiomas, intracranial SFTs have higher rates of recurrence and distant metastasis, necessitating long-term, and careful postoperative surveillance [3,[20], [21], [22]]. In the present case, no evidence of recurrence has been observed during a 2-year follow-up period, and imaging surveillance is ongoing. Further accumulation of cases may help clarify the imaging characteristics of SFTs with cystic change and improve preoperative diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusion

We present a rare case of intracranial SFT with multiloculated cystic change. SFT is an aggressive tumor with a high risk of recurrence and distant metastasis, and accurate diagnosis is essential for appropriate treatment strategies and postoperative surveillance. Although predominantly cystic intracranial tumors may mimic cystic meningiomas or metastatic brain tumors, careful evaluation of the solid component may provide useful imaging clues for considering SFT in the differential diagnosis. Further accumulation of similar cases may help clarify the radiological characteristics of SFTs with cystic change and improve preoperative diagnostic accuracy.
