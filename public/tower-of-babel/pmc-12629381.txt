Outcomes after infected humeral shaft nonunion surgery
Authors: Patrick Pallitto, Nathaniel Koutlas, Noah Harrison, Elsa Rodriguez, William Obremskey, Alexander Hysong, Samuel L. Posey, Joseph R. Hsu, Roman M. Natoli, Rachel B. Seymour, Andrew T. Chen, the Evidence-Based Musculoskeletal Injury & Trauma Collaborative (EMIT)
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:This study looks to report overall union rate after surgical treatment of infected humeral shaft nonunions.Design:Retrospective.Setting:Eight Level 1 trauma centers.Patients/Participants:Twenty-six patients with infected humeral shaft nonunions and ≥180-day follow-up. A nonunion was considered infected in the presence of a draining sinus or positive intraoperative cultures.Intervention:Surgical treatment of infected humeral nonunion.Main Outcome Measurements:The primary outcome was union rate, and subanalysis was performed comparing planned single stage versus staged surgery. Secondary outcomes included overall postoperative complications.Results:The mean follow-up time was 20.7 months; median age was 39.5 years. The initial fixation construct in most patients was plate and screws (81%). After revision surgery, only 50% (n = 13) of these went on to union. Preplanned staged treatment does not appear to be associated with successful union. Postoperative complications were high including persistent nonunion and infection. Tobacco use, diabetes, BMI, and bone grafting did not affect union.Conclusions:This study supports our hypothesis that infected humeral nonunions are challenging to treat and are at high risk for recalcitrant nonunion (50%). Further research is warranted to determine whether staged treatment is associated with a higher rate of union.Level of Evidence:III.

1. Introduction

Fractures of the humerus comprise approximately 3% of all fractures.1 Although nonoperative management of humerus fractures has been shown to be effective, operative management must sometimes be employed. In his original series, Sarmiento showed a high rate of healing with functional bracing, with a nonunion rate of 1.5% for closed injuries.2,3 However, more recent data on functional bracing demonstrates a failure rate of near 29% with nearly 20% being attributed to nonunion.4 Although both nonoperative and operative management ultimately have low rates of failure, humeral nonunions cause significant morbidity and are at continued risk for recalcitrant nonunion.

Although the treatment of both fractures and nonunions of the humerus has evolved over time, the results of humeral nonunion surgery have not similarly improved. Treatments are usually individually directed, but good outcomes have been demonstrated with external or circular frame fixation, locking compression plates utilized as an external fixator, and either single or multiplanar plating.5–7 Humeral intramedullary nails in the setting of nonunion have shown less reliable results.8 The use of adjuvants including autologous or allograft bone has also been shown to be effective in the treatment of humeral nonunions.9 A more recent technique described by Brennan et al10 utilizes a shortening osteotomy of the humerus with compression plating and demonstrated a 100% union rate with intermittent use of adjuvant graft. Although the above literature all demonstrated a very high union rate, more recent data out of our group demonstrate a union rate of 76% whose initial fracture was treated operatively, which is similar to the 77% union rate described in this subgroup by Wiss in his single surgeon series.11 In both cohorts, an infection after nonunion surgery was an independent risk factor for recalcitrant nonunion. In a small study specifically examining infected nonunions of the humerus, Haidukewych demonstrated a union rate of less than 50%.12

Based on currently available literature regarding infected long bone nonunions, the cornerstone of treatment is irrigation, debridement, and systemic antibiotics, coupled with dead space management and soft tissue coverage as needed. Historically, infected long bone nonunions have been treated with staged protocols; however, recently, there has been an increased interest in single stage procedures for long bone nonunions.13,14 In the setting of positive intraoperative cultures, the rate of secondary surgery for recalcitrant nonunion was nearly 30%, compared to just 6.4% in those without positive cultures. This demonstrates that, although single staged protocols may demonstrate some promise, a significant amount of caution should be employed in the presence of infection.

In the present study, a database of long bone nonunions collected from 9 level-one academic trauma centers was queried to report overall union rate after surgical treatment of infected humeral shaft nonunions. Secondary aims included describing any differences in outcomes between a single stage versus staged treatment protocol.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Institutional Review Board

This retrospective case series was reviewed and approved by each site's Institutional Review Board (IRB) in accordance with the Declaration of the World Medical Association. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the Wake Forest University Health Sciences IRB approved a waiver of informed consent (IRB00082256, PI Joseph Hsu).

2.2. Study Design

A database of long bone nonunions was established with the participation of 8 Level 1 trauma centers by querying the electronic medical records at each participating site. Using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 24430 and 24435, which correspond to operative treatment of a humerus nonunion, we identified all surgically treated humeral shaft nonunions within this database. After application of strict inclusion/exclusion criteria, the cohort was narrowed down to those with infected nonunions undergoing surgery (Fig. 1).

Infected humeral nonunion inclusion.

Infected nonunions were identified by either presence of a draining sinus tract or 2 positive intraoperative cultures, in concordance with the fracture-related infection (FRI) definition.13 Final inclusion criteria were defined as age 18 years or older, humeral shaft nonunion undergoing surgical repair, and an associated FRI. Patients were excluded from the analysis if <18 years of age, the initial fracture was pathologic in nature or the patient had an underlying skeletal dyscrasia, there was insufficient documentation present in the EMR, the patient did not have adequate follow-up of a minimum of 180 days, lacked radiographic follow-up, or if the infected nonunion was not treated operatively. Demographic details, comorbidities, operative and culture details from treatment, postoperative complications, and ultimate outcome of bony union were gathered. One patient with a proximal humerus nonunion and 4 patients with distal humerus nonunions were excluded due to small numbers and inherent differences in intra-articular fracture nonunions. Four patients were excluded secondary to inadequate follow-up leaving a final cohort of 26 infected humeral nonunions. The number of patients included from the 8 centers ranged from 1 to 6.

The primary outcome of this study was successful union after nonunion repair. Radiographic union was defined as evidence of bridging of at least 3 of 4 cortices on orthogonal radiographic views with a stable implant. Union was verified and independently reviewed with each center's senior author. Nonunions that united before the minimum 180 days of follow-up were included. Failure was defined as persistent nonunion with a minimum follow-up of 180 days, if the patient suffered a failure of fixation, or if the patient required further surgical intervention for nonunion regardless of the 180-day window. Patients were considered lost to follow-up if their surgery could not be classified as a success or failure and their follow-up was less than 180 days. Staged treatment or single treatment was left at the discretion of the treating surgeon.

2.3. Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to present the demographics of patients with an infected humeral nonunion. χ2 test, Fisher exact test, and nonparametric tests were used to determine the significance of associations between union rate and preconditions, single or staged treatment, and complications. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to identify risk factors for persistent nonunion. P-value less than 0.05 was used for significance threshold. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1, Copyright 2014 SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

4. Discussion

To date, this is the largest study examining the treatment of infected humeral nonunions. The work here corroborates that infected nonunions remain difficult to treat and are at high risk for recalcitrant nonunion. Through a multicenter, retrospective design, only half of the patients achieved successful union after revision surgery.

Despite a large cache of literature regarding aseptic humeral nonunions, there is a paucity of literature regarding the treatment and outcomes of infected humeral nonunions. It appeared that there may be increased union in younger patient populations (35.5 vs. 55.5 years) potentially representing a healthier population. Risk factors such as BMI, tobacco use, and diabetes mellitus were not found to be independently associated with continued nonunion. Biological augmentation was used in about 73% of patients with no final impact on union rates. Seven of the patients (27%) continued to have a persistent infection after their initial nonunion surgery.

The previous largest series on infected humeral nonunions was 14 patients, but only 2 of these involved the humeral shaft making direct comparisons difficult.12 All patients underwent surgical debridement and antibiotic therapy. Only 7 patients achieved union (50%) although only 10/14 patients underwent attempts of achieving bony union (external fixation, plating, and bone grafting). Despite modern advances in surgical technique, our union rate of 46.4% remained similarly low to this study that was performed 2 decades prior. Outside of this case series, there are only independent case reports exploring atypical surgical techniques such as induced membrane with nail/plate construct, Ilizarov technique, or a locked plate as an external fixator.6,14,15

The standard treatment of an infected nonunion utilizes irrigation and debridement, antibiotic therapy, and revision fixation. This can occur in a single stage or staged fashion. A single stage surgery was employed 27% of the time in our study of infected humeral shaft nonunions. Although tibial and femoral shaft long bone nonunions have been successfully treated via a one-stage approach, similar success in this cohort of infected humeral shaft nonunions was unable to be reproduced.16,17 Although our study is highly limited due to only 26 patients, we did not note any differences in union rates with a staged procedure. Although a preplanned single stage treatment of humeral nonunion may be reasonable for an aseptic nonunion, if there are signs of infection, it is unclear whether or a staged or single stage procedure is better. If staged surgery is performed, typically the first stage would consist of a thorough debridement and excision of infected and/or dead bone with possible placement of an antibiotic spacer. The patient would then be treated with antibiotics and then return for staged treatment with definitive fixation. In a single stage surgery, perhaps a thorough debridement with concomitant fixation is sufficient and avoids the necessity of an additional surgery.

The current study was not without limitations. The multicenter nature of the study lead to a variability in treatment protocols. The lack of detailed microbiology data may affect union rates, rate of cure of infection, and the success of or failure of single staged treatment. The design of the study necessitated that the choice of single versus staged treatment of infected humeral nonunion was not controlled, but instead was at the discretion of the treating surgeon. This can be for a number of reasons including a presumed aseptic nonunion with an unexpected intraoperative positive culture or perhaps the decision to not risk injury to the radial nerve with additional surgery. Thus, selection bias likely played a role in how patients were allocated to their assigned treatment. It is possible that this affected the observed difference between the groups. Due to the rarity of this injury, an additional limitation of this study was the small number of patients in this study. A prospective trial is warranted to randomize patients with an infected humerus nonunion to single or staged treatment to help confirm these findings. Last, we do not have data regarding treatment for potential vitamin D deficiency or any other metabolic abnormalities, which may affect bony healing of nonunions.

5. Conclusions

This study reported on the outcomes after treatment of known infected humerus nonunions and is the largest series to date. It corroborated the existing knowledge that infection at time of nonunion repair is a risk factor for recalcitrant nonunion. The results of our small series demonstrated that treatment of an infected humeral nonunion has an exceptionally high risk of persistent nonunion (50%) and no differences between planned staged surgery or single stage surgery. Further research into the optimal treatment strategies for infected humeral shaft nonunions is warranted.
