Regression-based model for predicting preterm birth using vaginal lactobacilli and routine clinical data
Authors: Lihua Kang, Yuan Su, Shu Li, Tao Zhang, Shuang Li, Chunli Li, Wei Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreterm birth (PTB) is a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality. Although increasing evidence links vaginal microbiota to preterm birth, clinically practical models integrating microbial and clinical indicators are lacking. We aimed to develop a model that combines vaginal microbiota with routine clinical data for prenatal PTB risk assessment.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-centre cohort study including 4,558 pregnant women who delivered between 2019 and 2023 at the Women’s and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Clinical variables included blood counts, biochemical tests, and vaginal microbiota. After excluding incomplete and non-hospital cases, we excluded clinical variables with missing rates greater than 50% to improve data quality and the accuracy of model predictions. For variables with less than 50% missingness, we applied the MissForest algorithm for non-parametric imputation, 1,228 participants were included in the final analysis for model development. Logistic, LASSO, and Poisson regression models were internally validated, with no external validation performed.ResultsReduced vaginal Lactobacillus, vaginal fluid leakage, abnormal blood glucose, and elevated Cystatin C were significantly associated with an increased risk of preterm birth (Lactobacillus reduction OR = 1.723; PROM OR = 3.297; abnormal glucose OR = 1.401; Cystatin C OR = 4.298; all P < 0.05). When the logistic prediction model combining indicators reflecting vaginal microecological imbalance (such as reduced Lactobacillus, vaginal cleanliness, microbial density, fungi, hydrogen peroxide, and N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase) with clinical variables including cervical cerclage (performed for CL ≤ 25 mm), vaginal fluid leakage, blood glucose, uric acid, and Cystatin C, the model demonstrated moderate predictive performance for preterm birth (AUC = 0.753), suggesting that integrating vaginal microbiota with routine clinical parameters may improve the accuracy of prenatal risk assessment for preterm birth.ConclusionsThis study constructed a prediction model for preterm birth integrating routine prenatal examination indicators and vaginal microbiota information. The model provides a preliminary, feasible, and low-cost framework approach for prenatal risk stratification in pregnancy. However, at this stage, it should be regarded as an exploratory research prototype that requires further multicenter validation across diverse clinical settings and populations before potential clinical application.

Introduction

Preterm birth (PTB), defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation, remains one of the major global public-health challenges and the leading cause of mortality among children under five years of age [1–3]. Each year, approximately 15 million infants are born prematurely, accounting for around 10% of live births, with the highest burden observed in low- and middle-income countries [4]. In addition to high mortality, survivors of PTB frequently experience long-term complications involving neurodevelopmental, respiratory, and cardiovascular systems [5].

Current preventive strategies primarily rely on transvaginal ultrasound measurement of cervical length to identify women at high risk of PTB. However, its routine use in asymptomatic populations remains controversial, and standardisation is limited by inter-operator variability [6]. Although multi-omics biomarkers derived from the vaginal microbiome have shown promise for predicting PTB risk [7–10], their high cost and technical complexity restrict clinical implementation. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a predictive approach that is both practical and affordable, and can be readily integrated into existing healthcare systems.

Routine clinical laboratory tests—including complete blood counts, biochemical parameters, and vaginal microbiota assessments—are widely used in prenatal care and generate large amounts of accessible clinical data. However, clinicians often interpret these parameters individually, potentially overlooking combined patterns that may reflect underlying risk of PTB [11–13]. With the increasing digitalisation of electronic medical records, these routinely available data offer new opportunities for statistical modelling and data-driven approaches to improve prenatal risk stratification and prediction of PTB [14–16].

Unlike previous studies relying on complex omics analyses or single biomarkers, this study integrates vaginal microbiota findings with routinely collected prenatal examination data from women in late pregnancy to explore the feasibility of a research framework for PTB risk prediction. By utilizing information already available from standard clinical tests, the study provides a preliminary assessment of predictive potential without imposing additional testing burden.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-centre retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Women and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. The workflow is shown in Fig. 1. Pregnant women who underwent routine antenatal care and delivered at the hospital between January 2019 and December 2023 were included.

Fig. 1Flowchart of prediction model development process

Flowchart of prediction model development process

Preterm birth was defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation [11]. Eligibility screening was performed by obstetricians before data extraction based on predefined exclusion criteria: (1) major fetal structural or chromosomal abnormalities; (2) HIV infection; (3) history of organ transplantation; and (4) severe psychiatric disorders affecting informed consent or compliance.

After exporting data from the Hospital Information System (HIS), participants with missing rates greater than 50% in key clinical variables or who did not deliver at the study hospital were further excluded (n = 3,330). A total of 1,228 of the initially identified 4,558 women were included in the final analysis. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University Women and Children’s Hospital (Approval No. 2024-LS-B053) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the anonymised and retrospective design.

Data collection

All variables were derived from standard prenatal examinations routinely performed at the hospital, without preselection of specific indicators. According to the Expert Consensus on the Clinical Application of Vaginal Microecology Evaluation published by the Chinese Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology in 2016 [17]. Perform vaginal microbiome evaluation. All clinical parameters and vaginal microbiota assessments were obtained from routine antenatal visits performed during the mid-to-late stages of pregnancy, when vaginal microecological testing is routinely recommended in obstetric practice in China. Grading standard: Grade I (+): The average number of bacteria in each field of view observed by oil scope (magnification 10 × 100x) is 1 ~ 9; Level II. (++): The average number of bacteria in each field of view is 10 ~ 99; Class III. (+++): The average number of bacteria in each field of view is 100 or more; Under the light microscope, the field of bacteria is full; Grade IV (++++): Observed under oil microscopy, bacteria clump together or densely cover mucosal epithelial cells. Vaginal flora diversity: the number of all bacterial species in the smear. Normal Lactobacillus group: The dominant bacteria are mainly lactic acid bacteria, and the bacterial density class is II-III; Lactobacillus reduction group: the dominant bacteria are not predominantly lactic acid bacteria, or although lactobacilli are dominant, the density level of lactobacilli is 0 or 1+.

To ensure consistency and minimize observer bias, microscopic evaluations were independently performed by two qualified laboratory specialists with intermediate professional titles. In cases of discrepancy, a senior microbiology expert reviewed the slides to reach a consensus.

To improve data quality and accuracy of model predictions, clinical variables with missing rates greater than 50% were excluded. For variables with less than 50% missingness, we applied the MissForest algorithm for non-parametric imputation. Eventually, the X feature from the complete blood count and the Y feature from the biochemical test were retained for modeling analysis (see Table 1).

A total of 1,228 women who met the inclusion criteria were selected to build prediction model. The data was randomly divided into a training set (80%) and an internal validation set (20%).

Modeling methods and statistical analysis

Logistic Regression (LR) is a generalized linear regression model widely used in the fields of automatic disease diagnosis and data mining. Logistic regression models a given set of independent variables to estimate the probability of an event occurring. Since the output is probability, the dependent variable is limited to a value between 0 and 1. In order to ensure the rigor of the analysis and illustrate the advantages of various algorithms in feature optimization, LASSO regression and Poisson regression were also used for multiple validations. During the study, univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify important variables. Variables significant in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and variables with VIF ≥ 10 were excluded. Factors retaining significance in the final model were deemed independent predictors of the outcome. The area under the curve (AUC) was used as the primary metric to evaluate the performance of the model, which underwent internal validation only without external validation. All statistical analyses in our study were performed using R software (version 4.2.3).

Results

Association of vaginal lactobacillus reduction with pregnancy

We first assessed the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes in the context of Lactobacillus reduction among all 4,558 participants (Table 1). Women with reduced Lactobacillus had significantly higher rates of PROM and preterm birth. Notably, among women with PROM, the rate of preterm birth was significantly higher in those with Lactobacillus reduction compared to those with normal Lactobacillus levels (Table 2) (50.8% vs. 34.7%, p < 0.001). We then compared baseline characteristics between the preterm and term groups in 1,228 women with complete data (Table 3). There were no significant differences in maternal age or infection-related indicators between groups. Notably, the prevalence of Lactobacillus reduction remained significantly higher in the preterm group. Consistent with previous studies, Lactobacillus reduction was associated with an increased risk of preterm birth, further supporting its potential value as a predictive indicator. In addition, adverse outcomes were generally more frequent among women with Lactobacillus reduction: the rates of preterm birth (56.1% vs. 43.3%; p < 0.001), PROM (51.5% vs. 33.3%; p < 0.001), and placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) disorders (10.2% vs. 6.6%; p < 0.001) were all significantly increased. Taken together, these findings indicate that vaginal microbiota imbalance—particularly Lactobacillus deficiency—is strongly associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes of both spontaneous and medically indicated origin.

Lactobacillus reduction combined with multifactorial as an independent predictor of preterm birth

As shown in Fig. 2, multivariate logistic regression analysis identified elevated cystatin C, elevated uric acid, abnormal oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results, reduced Lactobacillus, and abnormal vaginal secretions as independent risk factors for preterm birth.

Fig. 2Multivariate analysis of preterm birth risk factors: forest plot

Multivariate analysis of preterm birth risk factors: forest plot

Elevated cystatin C (OR = 4.29, 95% CI: 2.58–7.17, P < 0.001) was the strongest independent risk factor for preterm birth, indicating that women with elevated levels had more than fourfold higher odds of preterm delivery compared with those with normal values. This finding suggests that cystatin C may reflect underlying renal dysfunction or systemic inflammatory activation during pregnancy, both of which could contribute to impaired placental function and early parturition. Similarly, women with reduced vaginal Lactobacillus had approximately twice the odds of preterm birth (OR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.10–4.08, P = 0.024), suggesting that vaginal microecological imbalance may promote preterm birth by enhancing inflammation or increasing susceptibility to infection.

Combined predictors improved clinical utility

The ROC curve (Fig. 3) shows that the multivariate logistic regression model is significantly better than the univariate model using lactobacilli alone in predicting preterm birth. The area under the curve (AUC) of the multivariate model was 0.7695, suggesting that it had moderate but clinically meaningful discrimination ability. The AUC of the Lactobacillus single-index model was 0.5475, which had limited predictive performance. The above results further indicate that the combination of a variety of clinical indicators of prenatal examination and vaginal microecological characteristics has higher clinical application value in the risk identification of high-risk groups of preterm birth.

Optimize the variable selection strategy to improve the performance and practicability of the model: Strategy 3 achieves the best balance

To develop an optimized and clinically implementable model for preterm birth prediction, we compared four variable selection strategies according to their discriminative performance and clinical interpretability. Strategy 1 included all variables that were statistically significant in univariate analysis (AUC = 0.790, AIC = 1427.07, BIC = 1597.17). Although it achieved the highest discrimination, its complexity and large number of variables limited its clinical feasibility. Strategy 2 retained only variables that remained significant in multivariate analysis (AUC = 0.755, AIC = 1447.26, BIC = 1483.34), simplifying the model but slightly reducing its predictive ability. Strategy 3 incorporated variables from Strategy 2, supplemented by clinically important indicators identified by expert consensus, including vaginal cleanliness, hydrogen peroxide, platelet count, fungal infection, and N-acetylglucosaminidase (AUC = 0.767, AIC = 1444.59, BIC = 1527.06). This strategy achieved a comparable level of discrimination to Strategy 1 while markedly improving simplicity and interpretability. Strategy 4, derived from stepwise regression, exhibited similar performance (AUC = 0.761, AIC = 1441.38, BIC = 1492.93) but was purely data-driven and less clinically interpretable. Considering the trade-off between predictive performance, interpretability, and feasibility, Strategy 3 was ultimately selected as the final model. The importance of variables in strategy 3 is shown in Fig. 4. This model maintained good discrimination with fewer variables and incorporated clinically accessible predictors, enhancing its potential for real-world application. After bootstrap resampling (1 000 iterations), the final logistic model yielded an internally validated AUC of 0.752. Compared with the LASSO and Poisson models, the logistic model demonstrated superior reclassification: continuous NRI = 0.3832 (p < 0.001) and IDI = 0.0087 (p < 0.001) versus LASSO; continuous NRI = 0.3262 (p < 0.001) and IDI = 0.0101 (p < 0.001) versus Poisson, indicating improved discrimination across risk strata. The calibration curve showed close alignment with the ideal line, indicating that the model’s predicted risks accurately reflected the observed probabilities. Through the confusion matrix, we get an overall accuracy of 0.712, sensitivity of 0.835, and specificity of 0.518, suggesting reliable detection of high-risk pregnancies (Fig. 5). Decision-curve analysis showed that the model provided greater net benefit than either the “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies across a broad range of probability thresholds, demonstrating potential clinical utility for individualized antenatal risk assessment (Fig. 6).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis and variable importance evaluation

Among the 26 candidate predictors, vaginal fluid leakage and cervical cerclage (performed for CL ≤ 25 mm) were the most influential factors for preterm birth (Fig. 7). Cystatin C and uric acid also showed strong predictive value. Vaginal microbiota-related features, including Lactobacillus reduction, fungal infection, cleanliness score and hydrogen peroxide status ranked highly in variable importance. In the final multivariate logistic regression model, 15 variables were retained. Cystatin C (OR = 4.298, 95% CI: 2.578–7.165, P < 0.001), vaginal fluid leakage (OR = 3.297, 95% CI: 2.494–4.357, P < 0.001), and Lactobacillus reduction (OR = 1.723, 95% CI: 1.087–2.729, P = 0.021) were independent risk factors.

The final logistic regression model demonstrated stable and reliable performance in internal validation and after 1,000 bootstrap resampling iterations, and was ultimately selected as the optimal model for this study.

To demonstrate the model’s visualization and operability, a web-based prototype tool was developed, allowing users to input maternal information and generate estimated preterm birth risk. This prototype is intended solely for research demonstration and should be regarded as exploratory, supporting future external validation and methodological refinement. Discussion

Preterm birth remains one of the most serious obstetric complications globally, posing substantial health and economic burdens, particularly in resource-limited setting [18]. Effective risk stratification and early intervention among high-risk pregnant populations are essential for improving maternal and neonatal outcomes [19]. Most existing prediction tools depend on complex technologies, limiting their clinical utility. Thus, an accurate and practical model for preterm birth risk remains a pressing need in obstetrics.

Several recent studies have developed prediction models for preterm birth based on high-throughput sequencing or metabolomics. Some of these models, particularly in high-quality studies, have demonstrated good discriminatory performance, with areas under the curve (AUCs) ranging from 0.75 to 0.88 [9, 20, 21]. Nonetheless, their clinical utility is hindered by the dependence on advanced bioinformatics pipelines and costly laboratory infrastructure, which constrains their implementation in routine obstetric care. The model proposed in our study, although demonstrating moderate predictive ability (AUC = 0.753), it falls within the lower range of AUCs (0.75–0.80) reported by Fettweis et al. [9]. We used only clinically available data to enhance real-world practicality.

In addition, other studies have investigated the predictive potential of clinically accessible vaginal microbiota indicators—such as pH level, Lactobacillus dominance, and aerobic vaginitis—and similarly identified Lactobacillus depletion as a significant risk factor for preterm birth [22]. Compared with these studies, our model incorporates a larger real-world cohort (n = 1,228) and integrates key antenatal risk factors (including cervical cerclage performed for CL ≤ 25 mm, gestational glucose intolerance, and renal function abnormalities) alongside vaginal microecological features. Unlike studies that rely on high-throughput or advanced experimental platforms to investigate the relationship between the vaginal microbiota and preterm birth, this study was conducted in a real-world clinical setting using a large hospital-based cohort, providing preliminary validation of the feasibility and potential clinical value of combining routinely available vaginal microbiota indicators with standard antenatal parameters.

This study was based on a five-year real-world retrospective cohort from the Women’s and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. It utilized routinely available prenatal clinical data, including hematological parameters, biochemical profiles, and vaginal microbiota indicators, to construct a prediction model for PTB risk. Key variables selected included cervical cerclage (performed for CL ≤ 25 mm) on ultrasound, OGTT results during pregnancy, reduced vaginal lactobacilli, elevated cystatin C levels, and abnormal vaginal cleanliness. This finding is consistent with prior studies.

Prior research has demonstrated that cervical length measured during the mid-trimester (23–28 weeks) is significantly associated with an increased risk of PTB [23]. Additionally, the HAPO (Hyperglycemia and Adverse Pregnancy Outcome) study found that even mild elevations in maternal blood glucose that do not meet the diagnostic threshold for gestational diabetes may increase the risk of PTB [24], highlighting the clinical significance of dynamic glucose monitoring.

Alterations in the vaginal microecology, such as a reduction in lactobacilli and an increase in facultative anaerobes, may compromise the vaginal barrier and amplify inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to the occurrence of PTB [25, 26]. Cystatin C, a sensitive marker of renal function, has also been implicated in PTB risk even when serum creatinine levels are within normal ranges, particularly in the context of severe preeclampsia [27]. Moreover, vaginal cleanliness, though a routine clinical indicator, can reflect local microecological status to some extent and may indirectly indicate microbial imbalance and inflammation [28].

While previous studies have independently investigated the associations between vaginal microbiota, biochemical markers, and pregnancy complications with PTB, few have systematically integrated these multidimensional clinical variables into a unified prediction model. Thus, the present study aims to explore a prediction strategy that combines diverse types of indicators, offering a clinically feasible and integrative tool for PTB risk assessment.

While previous studies have focused on specific vaginal metabolites or pathogens, this study suggesting the value of routine vaginal microbiota metrics, such as cleanliness levels, leukocyte esterase, and hydrogen peroxide, in the prediction of preterm birth. These parameters are commonly used in clinical screening for bacterial vaginosis and other infections and are effective in reflecting the imbalance of the vaginal microbiota associated with PTB. Our findings suggest that these routine indicators may provide preliminary insights into a feasible complement to molecular approaches for predicting preterm birth, particularly in resource-limited settings, but further validation across multiple centers and populations is required.

Additionally, our approach considers both spontaneous and iatrogenic mechanisms of preterm birth, aiming to enhance the clinical applicability and etiological coverage of the model. In addition, we incorporated pregnancy-related comorbidities such as OGTT results, which are closely associated with medically indicated preterm births in clinical practice. By integrating diverse risk factors, the model may contribute to potential improvements in comprehensiveness and clinical relevance. Moreover, certain predictors, such as vaginal fluid leakage and cervical cerclage, were intentionally retained in the model to reflect real-world clinical decision-making scenarios, although we acknowledge that this may introduce causal complexity. Nevertheless, their inclusion enhances the model’s clinical interpretability and relevance to routine obstetric practice. Recent research has emphasized that such multidimensional modeling approaches are key to improving the scalability and predictive performance of obstetric risk assessment tools [29–33].

However, caution is still needed when evaluating the generalization ability of the developed model. Evidence from the Human Microbiome Project and multiple international cohort studies suggests that the composition and stability of the vaginal microbiota vary significantly among ethnicities and geographic populations [9, 34–36]. Therefore, external validation in a multi-center, multi-ethnic population is necessary to ensure that the prediction model has broader applicability and robustness.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Second, inflammatory status was evaluated using individual hematologic parameters only, and composite indices (e.g., NLR, PLR, SII) derived from complete blood counts were not included. Third, the assessment of vaginal microbiota was based on microscopic examination, which has relatively low sensitivity compared with molecular methods such as sequencing, and therefore may have underestimated subtle microbial variations. Finally, the absence of external validation limits the immediate clinical applicability of the model. To address these limitations, future research should incorporate larger sample sizes and adopt prospective, well-designed study frameworks to validate and extend our findings. Given the relative homogeneity of the present cohort, subsequent studies are warranted across diverse geographic regions and healthcare settings, with real-time data acquisition and careful adjustment for potential confounders—including ethnicity, obstetric history, and socioeconomic background—to minimize bias and more precisely characterize the predictive performance and generalizability of the model. Furthermore, a web-based prototype tool was developed to illustrate the model’s feasibility and promote future external validation. At this stage, it serves as an exploratory research platform that may inform subsequent refinement and potential clinical translation.

Conclusion

This single-center retrospective cohort study investigated a potential clinically pragmatic model for preterm birth prediction using routinely collected examinations, without additional testing. The final logistic regression model showed moderate discrimination (AUC = 0.753) and may support risk stratification in antenatal care. Given the retrospective design and absence of external validation, these findings should be regarded as preliminary, and multicenter validation is warranted to determine their generalizability before potential clinical implementation.
