Recent Advances in Artificial Intelligence for Endoscopic and Multimodal Assessment of Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A Review
Authors: Xiangling Lv, Chen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a group of chronic inflammatory bowel disorders characterized by complex etiology and significant clinical heterogeneity. With the evolution of the “treat-to-target” (T2T) concept, progressive endpoints such as endoscopic mucosal remission, histological remission, and deep remission have become key outcomes in the management of IBD. Traditional endoscopic assessment of IBD suffers from issues such as high subjectivity, lack of consistency, limited quantitative capabilities, and reliance on specialist experience; artificial intelligence (AI) is driving the evolution of endoscopic assessment toward standardization, objectivity, and real-time analysis. AI has made significant progress in areas such as UC activity scoring, CD ulcer identification, small bowel capsule endoscopy image analysis, relapse prediction, and tumor monitoring; in selected datasets or experimental settings, some of its performance metrics approach expert levels. Research trends are gradually shifting from single-image analysis toward multimodal decision-support systems that integrate endoscopic, pathological, biomarker, and clinical information, which are expected to enhance the objectivity of IBD diagnosis, treatment, and efficacy evaluation. However, current challenges include data heterogeneity, inconsistent standards, insufficient external validation, limited interpretability, and inadequate ethical and regulatory frameworks. This article reviews the progress, technical approaches, clinical value, and future directions of AI applications in IBD endoscopy, focusing on the transition from subjective scoring to AI-based digital inflammation phenotyping.Keywords: artificial intelligence, inflammatory bowel disease, ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease, endoscopy, deep learning

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which primarily includes ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is a group of chronic, recurrent, and progressive inflammatory bowel disorders. With its incidence continuing to rise worldwide, IBD has become a major public health issue in the field of gastroenterology. Endoscopy plays a central role in the diagnosis, classification, assessment of disease activity, evaluation of treatment response, monitoring of relapse, and surveillance for neoplasms in IBD. For UC, the Mayo Endoscopic Score (MES) and the Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Severity Index (UCEIS) are the most commonly used assessment tools in clinical practice and research; for CD, the Simplified Endoscopic Score (SES-CD), the Rutgeerts score, and small bowel capsule endoscopy-related evaluation systems are frequently used to assess disease burden and the risk of postoperative recurrence. However, these scoring systems are generally characterized by high subjectivity, significant inter-rater variability, a strong reliance on operator experience, and difficulty in accurately quantifying spatial heterogeneity of lesions in routine clinical practice.

As treatment goals for IBD have shifted from simple clinical remission to endoscopic mucosal remission, histological remission, deep remission, and even barrier and molecular remission, there is an increasingly urgent clinical need for more objective, stable, and high-resolution assessment tools. Artificial intelligence, particularly computer vision and machine learning technologies, offers new possibilities for addressing these challenges. Specifically, it includes CNN, Vision Transformer, multi-task learning, semi-supervised learning, multimodal fusion and explainable AI, etc. Unlike AI applications for other gastrointestinal diseases, IBD has a relatively greater need for multimodal data fusion. This is because the diagnosis, management, and assessment of disease activity in IBD all require the integration of data from multiple sources—including endoscopy, histology, imaging, and clinical data—and involve subjective variations; AI enables objective, automated quantitative analysis. In contrast, the assessment of other gastrointestinal diseases (such as peptic ulcers and gastroesophageal reflux disease) requires only single-modality data. On the one hand, AI can automatically identify key findings—such as inflammation, erosion, ulcers, bleeding, and loss of vascular patterns—based on static images or videos, and generate standardized scores. For example, CNN models, such as those used in UC, can automatically identify inflammatory lesions and provide differential diagnoses. Combined with image enhancement techniques, they generate virtually stained images that help physicians assess the microvasculature of the intestinal mucosa. On the other hand, by analyzing image features that are difficult to discern with the naked eye, AI can establish links between endoscopic findings and histology, recurrence risk, treatment response, and long-term outcomes, thereby transforming endoscopy from an observation tool into a predictive tool and a decision-making tool.

In recent years, numerous reviews and consensus statements have highly praised the potential applications of AI in IBD. Crăciun et al1 noted that AI holds promise for improving the consistency and reproducibility of imaging tools such as endoscopy and intestinal ultrasound, serving as a key driver for the precise assessment of IBD. Testoni et al2 and Gregorio et al3 argued that the development of AI in IBD endoscopy has progressed from early lesion identification to more complex disease phenotype analysis, treatment response prediction, and tumor monitoring. European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation (ECCO) literature further emphasizes that the clinical translation of AI in IBD must be grounded in high-quality data annotation, rigorous external validation, methodological transparency, interpretability, and regulatory compliance.4–7 At the same time, model reliability in AI research is also affected by label noise, expert discrepancies, and inconsistent image quality in the real world. Therefore, a systematic review of the current status of AI applications in IBD endoscopy (Figure 1) not only helps summarize existing achievements but also aids in identifying future research priorities. Automatic Severity Assessment in UC Endoscopy

In the field of AI-assisted endoscopy for IBD, ulcerative colitis (UC) currently represents the area with the most robust evidence and the most mature technological development. This is partly due to the fact that UC lesions primarily affect the colon and rectum, allowing for relatively standardized endoscopic data collection; it is also attributed to the well-established system of clinical trial endpoints and widely adopted scoring criteria for UC. Current research primarily focuses on the automated interpretation of MES and UCEIS, the identification of mucosal healing, real-time analysis of endoscopic video, and the prediction of disease recurrence risk based on endoscopic images.

In terms of image-level classification, Lee et al8 proposed a multi-task deep learning model that provides a multidimensional and accurate assessment of UC. This model is particularly well-suited for assessing disease activity and can reduce the risk of underestimating the severity of the condition. Vezakis et al9 found that properly trained basic convolutional neural networks can achieve severity assessment performance comparable to that of advanced neural models. This suggests that clinical applicability does not depend entirely on model performance, but rather on high-quality data, reasonable labeling, and clear task definitions. In addition to traditional CNN architectures, Transformers and their variants have also been introduced into UC endoscopic assessment. Ahmed et al10 integrated multimodal learning, meta-learning, and Transformers for UC severity classification. The model is capable of effectively integrating global image information, particularly regarding diffuse and continuous lesions in UC. Furthermore, Lin et al11 employed subclass-aware contrastive semi-supervised learning under limited annotation constraints, which improved performance on the UC evaluation, reduced reliance on high-quality labels, and can lower the cost of developing large-scale models.

The clinical significance of video-level analysis is more direct. Chang et al12 developed a visual Transformer model based on endoscopic video data, which performs on par with experienced physicians in predicting UC activity. Compared to still images, videos also contain temporal and spatial information, which helps avoid selection bias and provides a more accurate representation of the actual condition of the disease. A prospective study by Ogata et al13 showed that AI can not only be used for MES stratification but also to predict the risk of recurrence and improve the diagnostic performance of junior physicians. Therefore, AI is not limited to serving as an “automatic scoring tool”; it can also narrow the gap in interpretation among physicians of varying experience levels and raise the overall standard of endoscopic assessment.

Some studies have focused on real-time decision-making applications. Lo et al14 proposed a clinical model of “AI-triggered second opinions,” in which the system provides additional prompts to the operator when it detects interpretation uncertainty or a potential risk of misclassification. This approach is more readily accepted by clinicians than fully automated interpretation because it preserves the physician’s leading role in the process while leveraging AI’s strengths in identifying borderline cases. The model can improve the interpretation performance of physicians who are not IBD specialists, suggesting that AI is likely to be integrated into clinical workflows in an advisory capacity.

AI is driving a shift in the endoscopic assessment of UC from traditional grading systems toward intelligent continuous quantification and spatial phenotyping. The UC-SCALE system, developed by Gutierrez-Becker et al15 can automatically assess the severity of inflammation, the extent of lesions, and the distribution of local lesions, and generate a topological representation of the lesions. Compared to traditional MES, which provides only a rough classification, this system can more comprehensively reflect the spatial distribution characteristics of the disease and more accurately capture the true biological heterogeneity of UC. For disease monitoring, this system may not only provide a more sensitive reflection of treatment response but also reveal the relationship between different patterns of lesion distribution and the risk of recurrence, hospitalization, or even surgery.

The consistency between AI-based endoscopic scores and patient clinical outcomes is also gaining increasing attention. A study by Stidham et al16 in a Phase III clinical trial for ulcerative colitis demonstrated that AI-generated endoscopic quantitative measures showed better consistency with symptom relief and improvements in quality of life than traditional MES. This finding is significant because, although traditional scores are widely used as trial endpoints, they do not always fully align with patients’ actual benefits. If AI scores can more consistently reflect patients’ disease burden and treatment benefits, they are expected to become more sensitive and objective endpoint assessment tools in future clinical trials.

However, UC endoscopy AI currently faces several challenges. Different studies employ varying classification systems—some use the MES, others the UCEIS, while still others rely on a binary classification of mucosal healing or custom criteria—making cross-study comparisons difficult. Many models are built on the basis of single-center data or carefully selected high-quality images, and thus do not adequately account for real-world scenarios such as poor bowel preparation, limited field of view, variations in lighting, and differences in endoscopic instruments. Although UC endoscopy AI demonstrates impressive technical performance, there remain few studies with endpoints focused on prospective, real-time intervention, and improved patient outcomes. A systematic review by Lee et al17 clearly states that AI for assessing the severity of UC endoscopy has high potential for clinical translation but requires more rigorous external validation and standardized study designs.

Automatic Lesion Recognition in CD Endoscopy

Compared with UC, CD is more complex in terms of pathophysiology and endoscopic phenotype; lesions can affect the entire gastrointestinal tract and exhibit diverse characteristics such as segmental distribution, deep ulcers, strictures, fistulas, and postoperative recurrence, with a higher incidence of small bowel involvement. These characteristics significantly increase the difficulty of developing endoscopic AI for CD and result in a general scarcity of relevant evidence compared to UC. However, precisely because imaging assessment for CD is more complex and the burden of manual interpretation is heavier, the potential value of AI in this field is equally significant.

There has been relatively extensive research on the application of traditional endoscopy and small-bore endoscopy, primarily for the identification, grading, and quantification of CD ulcers. Liu et al18 developed an explainable AI system for the identification and severity assessment of CD ulcers in double-balloon enteroscopy images; the results showed that the model performed at a level close to that of experts and outperformed general endoscopists. Notably, this study emphasized the importance of interpretability; by presenting the model’s reasoning through heatmaps or regions of interest, it enhances physicians’ understanding of and trust in AI results. For CD, where lesions often exhibit complex morphology and diverse background mucosal changes, interpretability is more conducive to driving clinical adoption than high accuracy alone. Another key breakthrough of AI in CD is the continuous quantification of lesion severity. Cai et al19 proposed an AI method for quantitatively assessing the severity of endoscopic mucosal ulcers in CD, shifting ulcer evaluation from subjective grading to objective, continuous numerical descriptions. Although the traditional SES-CD scoring system is relatively systematic, it still relies primarily on observers’ subjective estimates of ulcer size, affected area, and degree of stricture, and its reproducibility is limited. If AI can automatically calculate ulcer area proportion, lesion density, or depth-related features, it may not only improve assessment consistency but also enhance sensitivity to detecting subtle changes, making it more suitable for longitudinal follow-up and monitoring of treatment response.

Small bowel capsule endoscopy is one of the most representative applications of AI in the assessment of Crohn’s disease (CD). Because CD often affects the deep small intestine, and capsule endoscopy can provide extensive information on the small intestinal mucosa in a non-invasive manner, it plays a crucial role in the diagnosis of suspected small bowel CD, the determination of disease extent, and the evaluation of treatment efficacy. However, capsule endoscopy often generates tens of thousands of images. Manual image review is time-consuming, carries a high risk of missed diagnoses, and relies heavily on experience—issues that are ideally suited for resolution through AI. In a multicenter validation study, Andrade et al20 found that AI-assisted small bowel capsule endoscopy outperformed standard manual review in detecting CD ulcers and erosions, while significantly reducing review time. Such studies clearly demonstrate the dual value of AI in both “improving accuracy” and “enhancing efficiency.” The INTELCAPE system developed by Fan et al21 has further advanced high-performance automated diagnosis in CD capsule endoscopy, suggesting that future capsule endoscopy interpretation may shift from manual frame-by-frame review to a new model of AI prescreening + physician confirmation. Gregorio et al3 noted that capsule endoscopy AI is particularly well-suited for diseases like CD, where lesions are scattered, involve the deep small intestine, and pose a significant burden for manual interpretation. Lee et al17 also observed that while the overall evidence base for CD remains less robust than that for UC, small bowel imaging and ulcer recognition have demonstrated considerable potential for development. This suggests that future breakthroughs in endoscopic AI for CD are likely to emerge first in small bowel assessment, rather than in simple severity grading via traditional colonoscopy.

The challenges of AI in CD endoscopy lie not only in lesion recognition but also in the complexity of labeling caused by disease heterogeneity. CD lesions vary significantly depending on the anatomical location, disease stage, and treatment history. For example, active deep ulcers may overlap visually with fibrotic strictures; postoperative anastomotic changes may resemble recurrent inflammation; and superficial erosions may resemble drug-induced mucosal changes. Most current studies still focus on relatively straightforward tasks such as “ulcer/non-ulcer” or “lesion present/absent,” while research on the automation of stenosis, fibrosis, penetrating lesions, and postoperative Rutgeerts scores remains insufficient. Testoni et al2 and Lodola et al22 have both pointed out that this imbalance in the evidence base represents a key difference between CD endoscopic AI and UC endoscopic AI. Furthermore, follow-up for CD patients often requires the integration of colonoscopy, small bowel endoscopy, capsule endoscopy, MR enterography, intestinal ultrasound, and laboratory parameters; therefore, single-modality AI struggles to fully reflect the disease status. A study by Ma et al23 using intestinal ultrasound images to predict mucosal healing in CD demonstrated that non-invasive imaging AI can provide monitoring information that complements endoscopy in CD. This also suggests that, in the future, endoscopic AI for CD is more likely to be used as part of a multimodal management system rather than in isolation.

Overall, AI for CD endoscopy has demonstrated clear advantages in identifying small bowel lesions and automatically interpreting capsule endoscopy images; however, it remains in the developmental stage with regard to analyzing complex phenotypes, predicting long-term outcomes, and establishing standardized scoring systems. Endoscopic-Histological Fusion: from the Mucosal Surface to Deep Inflammatory Phenotypes

As approaches to IBD management continue to evolve, relying solely on endoscopic mucosal findings is no longer sufficient to meet the demands of precision medicine. A growing body of research indicates that even when patients achieve endoscopic remission, persistent histological inflammation may still be present, and residual histological activity is closely associated with the risk of relapse, corticosteroid use, hospitalization, and surgery. Therefore, developing more efficient and standardized methods to assess histological inflammation and establishing a mapping relationship between endoscopic and histological findings has become a key area of development for AI in the field of IBD.

Research on automated histological assessment is relatively well-established. The AI tool for UC histology developed by Rubin et al24 showed a high degree of agreement with pathologists’ assessments based on the Nancy Index. This finding demonstrates that AI can not only replicate human visual judgment at the endoscopic level but also perform standardized analysis of features such as inflammatory cell infiltration, changes in crypt architecture, and mucosal damage at the histopathological level. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Puga-Tejada et al25 further demonstrated that AI-assisted histological assessment is as accurate as pathologists in determining histological remission in UC. From a clinical practice perspective, this implies that AI has the potential to reduce subjective variability in pathological evaluation, making it particularly suitable for standardized interpretation in multicenter clinical trials and high-workload settings. Another key area of histology AI is the quantification of inflammatory cells, particularly the automatic identification and counting of neutrophils. Neutrophil infiltration is considered a key marker of active inflammation and plays a central role in UC histological scoring systems. Ohara et al26 established a system for automatic neutrophil counting and histological score estimation, demonstrating that it cannot only be used to assess histological activity but also has potential associations with clinical prognosis stratification. Iacucci et al27 further proposed an AI-driven model for neutrophil detection, localization, and quantification, highlighting its application potential in assessing disease activity and evaluating early treatment responses. Compared to traditional grade-based scoring, cell quantification methods provide continuous variables that more closely reflect the essence of pathobiology, offering the potential to serve as a key bridge linking microscopic inflammation to clinical outcomes.

The application of AI in predicting the correlation between endoscopic and histological findings has garnered increasing attention. Research by Chang et al12 on video Transformers has demonstrated that histological activity can be accurately predicted using endoscopic video alone, suggesting that endoscopic images contain a wealth of latent histological information that is difficult for the human eye to extract directly. An extension of this approach is the development of a true endoscopic-histological fusion model. Iacucci et al28 proposed the Endo-Histo foundational fusion model, which jointly models endoscopic images and histological features for the assessment of histological remission and treatment response in UC clinical trials. This approach breaks away from the traditional parallel evaluation model of “endoscopic scoring followed by pathological scoring,” shifting instead toward integrating both types of information to jointly define disease status, thereby providing a technical foundation for constructing more precise composite endpoints.

The integration of endoscopy and histology is evolving toward a broader, multimodal inflammatory phenotype. Iacucci et al29 integrated advanced endoscopic imaging, mucosal barrier integrity assessment, and molecular omics features for early outcome prediction. This strategy demonstrates that AI can not only capture morphological abnormalities but also link them to deeper-level barrier damage and molecular inflammatory pathways, thereby explaining why patients who appear to be in remission may still be at high risk at the biological level. Reinisch et al30 therefore proposed that future endpoint assessment in IBD should not be limited to the single dimensions of traditional endoscopy or pathology, but should gradually transition to a composite endpoint system that integrates molecular biomarkers with AI-derived imaging information. From a methodological perspective, endoscopy-histology fusion also presents new technical challenges. First, there is a spatial and temporal mismatch between endoscopy and biopsy: the biopsy site may not fully correspond to the most prominent lesion on the image, and different histological sections may reflect local biases. Second, histological scoring systems are not standardized; for example, the Nancy Index, Geboes score, and other classification criteria differ in their definitions of remission, which affects the consistency of labels used in model training. Furthermore, multimodal models often require more complex data acquisition workflows, stricter data registration, and higher computational costs. Nevertheless, reviews by Santacroce et al31 and Iacucci et al32 both highlight that histology AI and endoscopy-pathology fusion represent one of the most promising directions in the precision management of IBD, as they directly address the clinical needs for deep remission and beyond mucosal healing.

Consequently, two significant shifts are currently taking place in this field: first, a transition from AI-based replication of histopathological scoring to AI-based quantification of inflammatory microstructures; and second, a shift from separate interpretation by endoscopy and pathology to multimodal, unified definition of disease status. These shifts are not only likely to improve the accuracy of clinical assessments but also have the potential to transform the design of endpoints and criteria for evaluating efficacy in future IBD drug trials.

Multimodal Endoscopic Enhancement, Differential Diagnosis, and Special Clinical Scenarios

The application of artificial intelligence in IBD endoscopy is no longer limited to grading inflammation in white-light endoscopic images. With the rapid development of image enhancement technologies, advanced endoscopic modalities, and cross-modal data integration, AI is now being applied to more complex clinical tasks, including predicting the risk of relapse, differential diagnosis of diseases, identifying infectious complications, and monitoring IBD-related tumors. Although these applications lack the extensive body of evidence found in UC severity scoring, their clinical value is often more targeted and better demonstrates AI’s potential to go beyond traditional scoring.

In the field of multimodal endoscopic enhancement, Kuroki et al33 proposed a method that sequentially combines white light imaging (WLI) and narrow-band imaging (NBI) models to stratify the risk of UC recurrence. The results demonstrated that the combined dual-model approach outperformed single-model approaches, suggesting that the information provided by different imaging modalities is complementary. WLI better reflects macroscopic mucosal morphology and the extent of widespread inflammation, while NBI is more effective at revealing microvascular structures and subtle mucosal abnormalities. For IBD—a disease characterized by complex pathological layers and ever-evolving remission criteria—a single imaging modality may fail to fully capture the risk profile, giving multimodal AI a natural advantage. The “AI-switching” model proposed by Iacucci et al34 further breaks through traditional analytical paradigms, enabling the system to automatically switch between different endoscopic modes and generate more diagnostically valuable multimodal images to optimize inflammation assessment and outcome prediction. From a technical perspective, this marks a gradual shift in AI from passive image recognition to active optimization of information presentation, representing an early form of generative intelligence.

AI has also demonstrated significant value in differential diagnosis. In some cases, IBD—particularly CD—must be distinguished from intestinal tuberculosis, yet there is considerable overlap between the two conditions at the clinical, endoscopic, and even pathological levels; misdiagnosis can lead to serious consequences. The colonoscopy AI model developed by Park et al35 can assist in distinguishing CD from intestinal tuberculosis, and its benefits are particularly evident for less experienced physicians. Such studies suggest that AI may be more clinically relevant in rare but high-risk differential diagnosis tasks than in routine severity scoring, as it can help primary care or non-specialist centers compensate for a lack of experience. A study by Huang et al36 also noted that machine learning models combining endoscopic and fecal biomarkers show significant potential in distinguishing UC from CD, suggesting that future IBD classification may no longer rely on a single information source but instead utilize multimodal algorithms to integrate endoscopic, laboratory, and clinical features. The application of AI in specific infectious contexts is another emerging area worthy of attention. UC patients, particularly during periods of severe disease activity or while receiving immunosuppressive therapy, often require differentiation from cytomegalovirus (CMV) colitis, as CMV infection can significantly impact treatment strategies. Liang et al37 developed a multimodal model integrating clinical and endoscopic data to identify UC complicated by CMV colitis, thereby improving the efficiency of early diagnosis. Kim et al38 developed a deep learning ensemble model to distinguish severe UC from CMV infection based solely on endoscopic images, demonstrating high accuracy and recall rates. Although most of these studies have limited sample sizes, their clinical value cannot be overlooked: on the one hand, such models facilitate risk warning while awaiting pathological or virological results; on the other hand, they reflect the trend of AI expanding from assessing disease activity to inferring the underlying cause.

In addition to inflammation, monitoring IBD-related tumors and dysplasia is another important area of application for endoscopic AI. Long-term chronic inflammation places patients with UC and some with CD at increased risk for colorectal cancer, and IBD-associated dysplasia often exhibits atypical morphology and a complex mucosal background, making it difficult to identify during routine surveillance endoscopy. Urquhart39 et al noted that the potential of AI in endoscopic monitoring of IBD-associated neoplasms lies primarily in lesion identification, risk stratification, and standardized follow-up. Johnson et al40 utilized AI-based automated phenotypic analysis to predict the future risk of high-grade neoplasia in patients with colitis-associated low-grade dysplasia. This advancement is particularly significant because it demonstrates that the value of AI in IBD tumor surveillance extends beyond merely detecting lesions to predicting progression. If future approaches can integrate lesion morphology, local inflammatory context, disease duration, and molecular characteristics, it may be possible to establish more precise, individualized monitoring protocols.

Multimodal endoscopic enhancement and its application in specialized scenarios reveal a developmental trajectory for AI in IBD endoscopy: initially, AI was primarily used to improve the efficiency of traditional tasks, but now an increasing number of studies are beginning to leverage AI to tackle tasks that are inherently difficult for humans to perform, such as the extraction of microstructural risk signals, complex differential diagnosis, prediction of tumor progression, and identification of infectious complications. This shift implies that AI is no longer merely a “digital assistant” but may gradually evolve into a tool for discovering novel imaging biomarkers. Of course, these applications also face certain limitations. First, specific clinical scenarios often involve a small number of cases, which can easily lead to model overfitting. Second, there is a particular lack of multicenter external validation, as disease spectra and clinical pathways vary significantly across regions. For example, the baseline prevalence of intestinal tuberculosis differs among countries and regions, which directly affects the model’s positive predictive value. These tasks typically involve multidimensional clinical, pathological, and laboratory information; models relying solely on endoscopic images may be limited in terms of transferability and stability. Optimizing multimodal modeling may involve incorporating longitudinal time-series modeling, as the management of IBD relies on dynamic predictions of disease trajectories, recurrence risk, and treatment response, rather than on assessments based on a single time point. Therefore, future research in this area should place greater emphasis on multimodal joint modeling, prospective clinical validation, and real-world application evaluation.

Beyond Endoscopy: Ultrasound, Digital Biomarkers, and Multimodal Precision Management

IBD is a chronic disease that spans time, organs, and modalities; no single examination can fully capture its true state. Therefore, the future development of endoscopic AI increasingly depends on the synergistic integration of intestinal ultrasound, pathology, molecular biomarkers, stool testing, patient-reported outcomes, and even lifestyle data (Figure 2).

Although this article focuses on the application of artificial intelligence in IBD endoscopy, recent research trends have clearly demonstrated that AI relying solely on endoscopic images is unlikely to meet the complex and dynamic clinical needs of long-term IBD management. IBD is a chronic disease that spans time, organs, and modalities; no single examination can fully capture its true state. Therefore, the future development of endoscopic AI increasingly depends on the synergistic integration of intestinal ultrasound, pathology, molecular biomarkers, stool testing, patient-reported outcomes, and even lifestyle data. Against this backdrop, endoscopic AI is no longer an isolated recognition technology but rather a core visual component within a multimodal precision management system.

Crăciun et al1 notes that endoscopy and intestinal ultrasound are currently the two most important tools for the imaging assessment of IBD, and that AI can improve the standardization and reproducibility of both. Compared to endoscopy, intestinal ultrasound offers advantages such as non-invasiveness, reproducibility, and ease of bedside implementation, making it particularly suitable for long-term follow-up and frequent monitoring; endoscopy, however, remains irreplaceable for direct visualization of the mucosa, biopsy, and tumor surveillance. Therefore, from a disease management perspective, the two are not in competition but are more likely to complement each other with the support of AI. Ma et al23 developed a deep learning model based on intestinal ultrasound images to predict mucosal healing in CD, and the results showed that ultrasound AI can reflect endoscopic treatment response to a certain extent. Kumaralingam et al41 achieved automated measurement of intestinal wall thickness in pediatric IBD, offering a new approach to reducing the frequency of invasive examinations for pediatric patients. This suggests that in the future, endoscopic AI may not only be used for direct scoring but also serve as a reference standard or training target for other imaging modalities.

In addition to traditional imaging, digital biomarkers are becoming an important complement to AI research in IBD. Lee et al42 used stool photographs to predict endoscopic inflammation in UC, and the results showed that their performance was comparable to that of fecal calprotectin. Although this study may seem distant from endoscopy, its value lies in demonstrating that data generated by patients in a home setting can also reflect the status of intestinal inflammation, thereby establishing a more continuous disease monitoring chain. If such non-invasive digital signals are combined with endoscopic AI results, a continuous management pathway of “home screening—outpatient evaluation—endoscopic confirmation—long-term follow-up” may be realized in the future. De Deo et al43 Tariq et al44 and Labarile et al45 have all noted that future IBD management will progressively integrate endoscopy, pathology, ultrasound, clinical indicators, biomarkers, and patient-generated data to form a dynamically updated digital disease profile.

From a technical perspective, multimodal integration offers two key benefits for endoscopic AI. First, it enhances predictive capabilities. When relying solely on endoscopic images to predict recurrence or treatment efficacy, predictions are often limited by visible morphological information; however, by incorporating laboratory parameters, pathological features, or ultrasound findings, the model can provide a more comprehensive description of disease activity and the depth of inflammation. Second, it enhances clinical interpretability. When model predictions do not align with a single endoscopic finding, auxiliary modalities can help explain this discrepancy. For example, even if a patient appears to be in remission based on endoscopy, ultrasound findings of intestinal wall thickening and elevated fecal markers may indicate the presence of persistent deep-seated inflammation.

Of course, precise multimodal management does not mean that more data is always better, each type of data has its own unique clinical value (Table 2). In actual clinical practice, for multimodal models to be truly implemented, issues such as synchronized data collection, handling of missing values, inconsistent time windows, differences in equipment across centers, and standardized interfaces must be addressed. IBD patients often undergo endoscopy, ultrasound, pathology, and laboratory tests at different times, and this information is not naturally aligned. How to construct models that can both leverage complex information and adapt to the reality of missing and incomplete clinical data is a key challenge that future research must address. Discussions by Kopylov et al7 and Noor et al4 regarding the ECCO framework both emphasize that AI model design should fully account for clinically available data rather than merely pursuing the highest theoretical performance. At the microscopic level, AI-based phenotypic identification can be used to accurately screen for IBD subtypes characterized by oxidative stress, inflammatory imbalance, and dysbiosis, thereby providing a basis for stratified regulation of the inflammation-redox network. This “phenotypic identification—targeted intervention” model facilitates more precise personalized treatment.46,47

Furthermore, multimodal precision management will also transform how endoscopic AI is evaluated. While past studies have primarily used AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity as key endpoints, the more important questions in the future may be: Can integrated multimodal endoscopic AI systems reduce unnecessary endoscopies, identify high-risk patients earlier, improve the timing of treatment adjustments, lower hospitalization rates, enhance quality of life, or achieving more precise treatment by linking AI-driven digital phenotyping with targeted therapy platforms48,49? In other words, the success of endoscopic AI will no longer depend solely on how accurately it interprets images, but rather on its ability to generate substantial clinical value within the broader clinical pathway.

Clinical Implications and Challenges

The significance of artificial intelligence in IBD endoscopy extends beyond routine clinical practice to its potential to transform clinical trials, drug development, regulatory science, and real-world healthcare systems. Because IBD clinical trials rely heavily on endoscopic and histological endpoints, and traditional manual scoring suffers from issues such as inter-center variability, high costs associated with central review, limited reproducibility, and inefficiency, AI is considered one of the key tools for optimizing the assessment of trial endpoints.

A study by Stidham et al16 in a Phase III clinical trial for ulcerative colitis has demonstrated that AI-based endoscopic analysis correlates better with symptom remission and improved quality of life in patients than conventional MES. This suggests that AI may not only reduce scoring bias but also provide alternative endpoints that more accurately reflect patients’ actual benefits. Stidham50 et al further noted that AI can enhance the consistency of disease activity assessment, automate the central reading process, and reduce trial noise caused by subjective differences among readers. This is particularly critical for IBD drug development, as inconsistencies in endpoint determination directly impact sample size requirements, trial costs, and the reliability of efficacy assessments. Sedano et al51 Le Berre52 et al, and Silverman53 et al also agree that AI is transforming multiple aspects of IBD clinical trials, including patient screening, baseline stratification, activity confirmation, efficacy assessment, and long-term response prediction. For example, during the trial enrollment phase, AI can assist in identifying patients who truly meet the criteria for moderate-to-severe disease activity, thereby reducing heterogeneity caused by human error; during the follow-up phase, AI can capture subtle improvements through more continuous quantitative metrics, thereby enhancing endpoint sensitivity; and in the central image review process, AI can serve as a preliminary screener or consistency-correction tool, reducing labor costs and time delays. Iacucci et al28‘s Endo-Histo fusion model further suggests that in the future, AI may be able to directly participate in the automatic definition of composite endpoints, unifying endoscopic and histological responses within a single analytical framework.

There remains a significant gap between laboratory and experimental settings and real-world clinical practice. Lodola et al22 critically point out that the primary issue with current AI for IBD endoscopy is not whether high-performance models can be developed, but rather whether these models can operate reliably in the complex, noisy, and non-standardized real-world environment. This involves challenges on multiple levels.

First, there are issues at the data level. Most existing studies are based on high-quality static images, curated video clips, or expert-annotated datasets, whereas real-world endoscopy often involves poor bowel preparation, blurred views, bleeding that obscures the image, camera shake, and variations in device parameters across different brands. The fact that a model performs well in a controlled environment does not necessarily mean it will be equally reliable in primary care hospitals or non-specialized centers. Lee et al17 noted that AI shows high translational potential for UC severity scoring, but data transparency and external validation remain insufficient. Second is the issue of labels and gold standards. In IBD endoscopy AI research, the most common ground truth comes from expert scoring; however, discrepancies among experts may exist, particularly in cases such as MES 1 versus 2, borderline mucosal healing, mild erosions, and microvascular changes. If the training labels themselves are inconsistent, even if the model is accurate, it may have learned only the subjective preferences of a specific group of experts rather than truly stable disease criteria. Therefore, future research must place greater emphasis on multi-expert consensus annotation, blinded repeated scoring, and endpoint designs that integrate clinical outcomes. In addition, we need to consider model bias and generalizability, as there may be differences in patients’ conditions across different regions—that is, there may be discrepancies between the labels and the actual cases. Third is the issue of model interpretability and regulatory compliance. For medical AI systems that directly influence treatment decisions, both clinicians and regulatory authorities need to understand why the model makes a particular judgment. Particularly in high-risk scenarios such as the identification of complex lesions in CD, tumor risk stratification, and infection differentiation, simply outputting a score or classification label is insufficient to meet clinical needs. Research by Okpete et al54 on explainable AI notes that transparency and explainability are essential prerequisites for achieving personalized management of IBD. The explainable model by Liu et al18 for CD small bowel endoscopy and the AI second opinion model by Lo et al14 both reflect a more realistic path to clinical translation: AI provides evidence-based auxiliary judgments rather than completely replacing physicians. Fourth are issues of ethics, privacy, and continuous updates. ECCO-related literature by Noor et al4 Mookhoek et al5 and Kopylov et al7 repeatedly emphasizes that the clinical deployment of AI in IBD must consider data privacy, security, algorithmic bias, device compatibility, and performance drift following model updates Endoscopic AI models often require continuous optimization using cross-center data, but this raises issues regarding the sharing of patient imaging data and governance structures. At the same time, models may experience performance degradation when applied to new devices, new patient populations, or new treatment contexts. Establishing mechanisms for monitoring, retraining, and oversight is a critical issue that must be resolved before AI can be integrated into routine clinical practice. Finally, and most importantly, evidence of clinical value remains insufficient. Most current studies use algorithmic performance metrics as endpoints, whereas the truly critical questions are: Can AI improve physician decision-making? Can it reduce misdiagnoses? Can it shorten examination time? Can it optimize treatment adjustments? Can it improve long-term patient outcomes? Is it cost-effective? These questions currently lack support from large-scale prospective studies. In other words, the next phase of research on endoscopic AI for IBD should shift its focus from model development to assessment of clinical impact.

Therefore, the true value of AI in IBD endoscopy lies not only in its ability to achieve high AUC scores, but also in its capacity to be safely, reliably, and transparently integrated into clinical and research workflows, thereby serving as a sustainable tool for improving the quality of diagnosis and treatment and optimizing resource allocation. This represents a crucial step in the field’s transition from technological innovation to clinical translation.

Discussion

The application of AI in IBD is expanding from simple image recognition to disease assessment, prognosis prediction, treatment decision-making, and clinical research management. Its core value lies in enhancing the objectivity, reproducibility, and efficiency of assessments, as demonstrated in the analysis of endoscopic, histological, and multimodal data. Representative AI Models and Clinical Tasks

Endoscopic assessment is the area with the strongest current evidence. AI can accurately identify mucosal inflammation in UC and grade its severity; some models perform at a level close to that of experts and can improve the interpretation skills of non-specialists.12,14 Systems such as UC-SCALE have further enabled the spatial assessment of disease severity and lesion extent, and have established associations with fecal calprotectin, CRP, patient-reported outcomes, and histological scores.15 AI can also be used in CD to identify and grade ulcers and speed up image interpretation using small-bowel capsule endoscopy or double-balloon enteroscopy; however, the overall number of studies and external validation trials remains lower than for UC.18–20

In terms of histology, AI can automatically quantify key inflammatory features such as neutrophils and basal plasma cells, and assist in assessing histological remission.24,26,55 The accuracy of AI in assessing histological remission in UC can approach that of pathology specialists.25 This helps reduce variability in pathological interpretation and improves consistency in the assessment of clinical trial endpoints. In the future, multimodal models that integrate endoscopy, histology, barrier function, and molecular omics may more accurately reflect disease activity and treatment response.28,29,31

Machine learning has also advanced noninvasive monitoring and biomarker discovery. Models based on stool images, complete blood counts, volatile organic compounds in exhaled breath, gut microbiota, and proteomics have all demonstrated some ability to diagnose the disease or predict disease activity.42,56–60 At the same time, several transcriptomic and single-cell studies have identified potential biomarkers such as IRF1, GBP5, PARP9, PLEKHA1, CSF3R, and LCN2.61–64 A comparison revealed that the candidate biomarkers identified in various studies are highly disparate, show limited overlap, and there is no clear correlation regarding their mechanisms of action.63,65–68 We must therefore ask ourselves: Does high diagnostic performance equate to true clinical utility? There are differences among these biomarkers in terms of sample origin, disease stage, treatment status, and analytical workflows, and some studies have limited sample sizes. Machine learning is prone to overfitting in high-dimensional, small-sample settings. In the absence of independent prospective validation and functional experiments, a high AUC alone is insufficient to demonstrate clinical utility.

In treatment decision-making, AI has been used to predict the efficacy of biologics, the development of neutralizing antibodies, mucosal healing, and disease progression. These models can help identify patients who are likely to benefit from or fail to respond to infliximab, adalimumab, usulumab, and vedolizumab.69–72 However, many models show a decline in performance during external validation, indicating that their generalization ability remains limited.69,73 Therefore, prior to clinical application, emphasis should be placed on calibration, decision curves, subgroup performance, and real-world net benefit, rather than relying solely on the AUC.

Most studies still use scoring systems such as MES and UCEIS as training labels or gold standards. For example, many UC severity classification models are still built around the MES classification.8,9,12,14,74 Another set of studies combines data from other dimensions in an effort to achieve a more comprehensive assessment.15,16,33 This means that, on the one hand, AI is often trained under the supervision of traditional scoring systems, while on the other hand, it attempts to transcend those systems. This is the core contradiction in current AI-driven healthcare. Model training requires labels, which are largely derived from existing scoring systems; however, these existing scoring systems themselves suffer from subjectivity, coarse granularity, and information loss. Therefore, if AI merely learns from these scoring systems, it can at best serve as an automated assessment tool for that system; only by integrating multidimensional data can it come closer to accurately reflecting the actual disease state.

Overall, AI is expected to become an important tool for the precise management of IBD, but it still faces challenges such as data heterogeneity, insufficient sample size, inconsistent annotation standards, lack of external validation, and limited model interpretability.1,4–7,17 Table 4 lists strategies for addressing various challenges. Future research should focus on strengthening multicenter, prospective, and cross-population validation, establishing uniform data standards, and integrating the model into real-world clinical workflows. AI should be appropriately positioned to assist physicians in decision-making, rather than to replace clinical judgment.

IBD Endoscopic AI Challenges and Improvement Paths

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is gradually transforming the endoscopic assessment and management of IBD. AI has already reached a relatively mature stage in areas such as UC endoscopic severity scoring, mucosal healing recognition, and real-time video analysis. It has also demonstrated significant potential in lesion identification during small bowel endoscopy and capsule endoscopy for CD, and is gradually expanding into fields such as relapse prediction, differential diagnosis, tumor monitoring, and clinical trial endpoint assessment. Current research trends have shifted from single-image classification to video analysis, lesion quantification, and multimodal fusion, which are expected to establish a more precise digital inflammatory phenotype and enhance the objectivity of IBD diagnosis, treatment, and efficacy evaluation. However, existing evidence remains predominantly based on retrospective and single-center studies, Future areas for optimization include multicenter prospective validation, clinical benefits for patients, integration into clinical workflows, real-time video-level analysis, multimodal longitudinal modeling, interpretable AI that complies with regulatory requirements, and clinical outcome-oriented trials. In the future, AI is expected to evolve from an “automated scoring tool” into an intelligent assistance platform that integrates multimodal information, supports treatment decisions, and improves long-term outcomes. It will enhance the consistency of endoscopic assessments, support clinical decision-making, and play a more important role in precision medicine for IBD.
