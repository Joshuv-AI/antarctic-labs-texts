Dosimetric advantages and clinical feasibility of a novel lateral decubitus position for left breast cancer patients undergoing PORT
Authors: Yingying Zhou, Jinfeng Xu, Yuan Deng, Lisheng Pan, Sufen Deng, Huali Li, Mengqi Yuan, Shaojun Mai, Huanhuan Liu, Bo Chen, Longmei Cai, Lin Yang, Ting Song, Xin Zhen, Hongmei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiation-induced cardiac toxicity remains a significant concern in breast cancer radiotherapy. Our institution developed a novel lateral decubitus position (nLDP) technique for patients unable to maintain deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) during postoperative radiotherapy (PORT). This study quantitatively evaluated the cardiac dosimetry benefits and setup errors of nLDP and compared its heart-sparing effects to DIBH within the low-risk patients (free breathing mean heart dose [FB–MHD] <4 Gy).MethodsPatients with left-sided breast cancer indicated for PORT were enrolled and stratified into three cohorts based on positioning and breathing techniques: Cohort 1 (nLDP in FB, nLDP-FB), Cohort 2 (supine position in FB, SP-FB), and Cohort 3 (SP in DIBH, SP-DIBH). The planning target volume (PTV) encompassed the whole breast/chest wall ± regional lymph nodes, and organs at risk (OARs) were delineated according to institutional protocols. While all patients were clinically treated without internal mammary node irradiation (IMNI), supplementary IMN-inclusive plans were generated specifically for Cohort 1 for dosimetric comparison. All plans utilized 6 MV photon intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). Dosimetric parameters, normal tissue complication probability (NTCP), anatomical heart-PTV distances, and setup errors were assessed. For the low-risk patients, cardiac dose reductions in nLDP were compared with DIBH.ResultsA total of 76 patients were analyzed: Cohort 1 (n = 28, median age 51.5 years, 75% underwent modified radical mastectomy [MRM]), Cohort 2 (n = 28, median age 50.5 years, 61% MRM), and Cohort 3 (n = 20, median age 45 years, 55% MRM). In Cohort 1, nLDP significantly reduced the mean doses and V5 Gy–V30 Gy to the whole heart and cardiac substructures compared to SP, regardless of IMNI inclusion, without compromising PTV coverage. Furthermore, nLDP significantly increased the heart–PTV distance and lowered the NTCP for cardiac mortality and contralateral breast secondary malignancy. The setup errors for nLDP remained below 0.5 cm, with no significant difference compared to SP. In patients with FB-MHD < 4 Gy, nLDP achieved heart dose reductions comparable to DIBH.ConclusionsThe nLDP offers an effective cardioprotective alternative to DIBH by significantly reducing cardiac doses without compromising PTV coverage and setup stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02836-7.

Background

Postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) for breast cancer significantly improves locoregional control and survival; however, the potential risk of radiation-induced heart disease (RIHD) remains a clinical concern [1, 2]. A seminal study in 2013 established a linear correlation between mean heart dose (MHD) and major coronary events, with a 7.4% per Gray (Gy) increase without an apparent safe threshold [3]. However, the 2023 EBCTCG meta-analysis of contemporary trials (conducted 1989–2008) demonstrated that there was no significant increase in non-breast-cancer mortality with regional nodal irradiation [4]. Nevertheless, minimizing cardiac exposure remains a paramount objective to prevent potential late risks, particularly in left-sided breast cancer patients undergoing internal mammary node irradiation (IMNI), where anatomical proximity inherently challenges cardiac sparing [5]. Consequently, minimizing cardiac dose remains a paramount objective in radiation oncology. Current cardiac-sparing strategies encompass deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) [6], prone positioning [7], intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) [8], accelerated partial breast irradiation (APBI) [9], and proton therapy [10]. While DIBH is the most widely implemented, its adoption is constrained by patient compliance (with 20–30% of patients being unsuitable), substantial medical costs [11–13], and infrastructural barriers—61% of U.S. radiation oncologists citing inadequate facility availability as a primary barrier [14]. Cost-effectiveness analyses suggest that DIBH may be impractical in resource-limited settings [15, 16]. Our previous study identified patients with free breathing MHD (FB–MHD) below 4 Gy as a low-risk group, owing to their lower heart dose and RIHD risk. Notably, this group exhibited only modest heart dose reductions with DIBH [17]. Thus, there is a compelling clinical need for cost-effective and universally applicable techniques to reduce cardiac exposure.

The lateral decubitus position (LDP) radiotherapy utilizes gravity to displace the heart away from the radiation field, potentially reducing cardiac exposure. Although prior studies have indicated its dosimetric benefits, detailed descriptions of immobilization methods are lacking, with the majority of literature focusing on patients after breast-conserving surgery (BCS) receiving three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) [18, 19]. The objectives of our study were: 1) to introduce a novel lateral decubitus position (nLDP) technique employing customized styrofoam immobilization and to evaluate its dosimetric advantages and clinical feasibility in left-sided breast cancer patients undergoing IMRT; and 2) to investigate whether nLDP can achieve cardiac dose reductions comparable to DIBH in low-risk patients.

Methods

Patients with pathologically confirmed left-sided breast cancer who received PORT at our institution between July 2021 and February 2025 were screened for enrollment. Those unable to undergo DIBH who consented to treatment in the nLDP were assigned to the nLDP-FB group (Cohort 1). Patients also ineligible for DIBH, but treated prior to nLDP implementation or preferring the supine position (SP), were categorized as the SP-FB group (Cohort 2). Finally, from the population treated with DIBH, those with an FB-MHD ＜ 4 Gy were selected for the SP-DIBH group (Cohort 3). Dosimetric evaluations were performed through both intra-cohort self-control analyses (within Cohort 1 and Cohort 3) and inter-cohort comparisons (between Cohort 1 and Cohort 3). Cohort 2 served primarily as a comparator for setup errors against Cohort 1 and was not included in the dosimetric analyses. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Patient positioning and CT scanning

A standardized 25° lateral tilt was utilized for the nLDP positioning according to our institutional protocol. All patients underwent positional immobilization using styrofoam. For the nLDP setup, a right-angled trapezoidal foam mold, constructed from polystyrene, was designed, with the following dimensions: top surface (200 mm length × 42 mm width); bottom surface (200 mm × 200 mm), height (75 mm) and a smallest angle of approximately 25°. A slot measuring 200 mm in length, 30 mm in width, and 13 mm in depth was positioned 35 mm below the upper edge on the lower surface. To implement the nLDP setup, a waterproof bag was placed on the simulation couch. The trapezoidal foam was inserted into the bag, and the slot was connected to a fixation bar positioned at chest level, thereby securing the foam. The superior edge of the foam was aligned with the patient’s left third rib, the inferior edge with the sixth rib, and the medial edge was positioned ~50 mm to the right of the spinous process. The boundaries of the foam were marked on the waterproof bag, and a foam mixture was poured into the bag. The patient was then positioned, ensuring that the foam aligned with the premarked locations, and the foam was allowed to set into a custom mold (Fig. 2). A patent for the nLDP technique has been approved (Patent number: CN 117815581B; Publication Date: April 5, 2024) [20]. The SP simulation was performed according to our institutional protocol. Delineation and radiotherapy planning

The planning target volume (PTV) and organs at risk (OARs), including cardiac substructures (left anterior descending artery [LAD], left atrium [LA], left ventricle [LV], right atrium [RA], and right ventricle [RV]), were delineated according to the guidelines established by RTOG [21] and Feng et al. [22] using the Varian Eclipse treatment planning system (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The PTV encompassed the whole breast or left chest wall. Regional nodal irradiation (RNI), including the supraclavicular and axillary nodes, was delivered based on individual risk factors; however, IMNI was not performed in clinical practice. To investigate the dosimetric advantage of nLDP for IMNI, additional experimental plans incorporating the IMN were generated specifically for the nLDP patients for dosimetric comparison. All delineations were performed by a senior radiation oncologist (HMW) to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Modified radical mastectomy (MRM) patients received 50 Gy/25 fractions over 5 weeks, while BCS patients received 43.5 Gy/15 fractions over 3 weeks plus an 8.7 Gy boost (excluded from analysis). All treatment plans utilized 6 MV photon IMRT and were developed by an experienced physicist. A standardized beam arrangement was designed for the nLDP technique to optimize target coverage and OARs sparing. For non-IMNI plans, a 7-field static IMRT technique was employed with gantry angles typically set at 280°, 300°, 320°, 90°, 110°, 130°, and 150°. For patients requiring IMNI, an eighth field at 15° was added to ensure adequate coverage of the IMN. Dose calculations were performed using the Anisotropic Analytical Algorithm (AAA, version 15.5) with a 3.0 mm calculation grid. Heterogeneity corrections were applied throughout the planning process to account for tissue density variations.

Geometric parameters

Cardiac-to-chest Euclidean distance (CCED), LAD-to-chest Euclidean distance (LCED), and left ventricle-to-chest Euclidean distance (LVCED) were measured on SP and nLDP CT images in Cohort 1, as described previously [17]. These parameters represent the shortest distances from the forementioned structures to the chest wall.

NTCP

Normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) models were used to evaluate the risk of normal tissue toxicity induced by radiotherapy. In this study, the assessed endpoints included long-term cardiac mortality following radiotherapy and the risk of radiation-induced secondary breast malignancy. The latter was quantified using the excess absolute risk (EAR), expressed per 10,000 person-years. Detailed descriptions and parameters of the NTCP models are provided in Supplementary Table 1 [23–28].

Setup errors

Electronic portal imaging device (EPID) scans were performed for all patients before treatment during the first three fractions and then weekly to verify patient positioning. The setup errors in the X (lateral), Y (longitudinal), and Z (ventral) directions were calculated by comparing the pretreatment EPID images with the simulation CT images.

Timing measurements

We recorded the immobilization time and simulation CT time for both the nLDP and SP. Immobilization time was defined as the duration from initial positioning and the completion of the customized mold setting in the immobilization room. Simulation CT time was defined as the interval from the patient lying down on the simulation CT couch to stepping off after scan was completed.

Statistical analysis

For intra-cohort comparisons of dosimetric parameters, geometric parameters, and NTCP, paired t-test or paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. For inter-cohort comparisons involving the dosimetric parameters, setup errors, and procedural timing, independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test were employed. Patient characteristics were compared using either Pearson chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Differences were considered significant when the bilateral P value was below 0.05. All the statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Software 27.0 (Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Basic characteristics

A total of 76 patients were included: 28 in the nLDP-FB group (Cohort 1), 28 in the SP-FB group (Cohort 2), and 20 in the SP-DIBH group (Cohort 3). Among 76 patients (median age 47 years, interquartile range [IQR] 39–54), most tumors were located in the upper outer quadrant, and most cases were early-stage (T1-2N0-1M0). 64% underwent MRM and 36% underwent BCS. Nearly all patients received adjuvant chemotherapy (95%), with 78% undergoing adjuvant radiotherapy to the left chest wall/breast and regional lymph nodes (Supplementary Table 2). All patients successfully completed the full course of radiotherapy as planned, with no interruptions due to positioning discomfort or breath-holding difficulties.

Dosimetric comparison

As demonstrated in Fig. 3A–B, a representative patient demonstrated that the heart irradiation was smaller in the nLDP than in the SP. As shown in Table 1, compared with the SP, the volumes of the whole heart and its substructures in the nLDP had no significant differences (p > 0.05), except for the LA (p = 0.048). Except for the RV V30 Gy, the V5–V30 Gy for the whole heart and ventricles were lower in the nLDP (all p < 0.05). Similarly, with IMNI, the nLDP showed significantly lower doses to both the whole heart and its substructures compared with the SP (Fig. 4). Compared with non-IMNI, both the SP and nLDP showed significantly greater radiation dose to the whole heart and its substructures when IMNI was performed (Supplementary Table 3). NTCP

Compared with the SP, the nLDP significantly reduced the heart and contralateral breast doses while also lowering the risk of radiation-induced adverse events. With non-IMNI, the nLDP showed significantly lower long-term cardiac mortality risk after radiotherapy (0.09 ± 0.09% vs 0.25 ± 0.20%, p < 0.001) and contralateral breast secondary malignancy risk (9.77 ± 4.13 vs 14.98 ± 5.07, p < 0.001). With IMNI, the nLDP maintained significantly reduced cardiac mortality (0.31 ± 0.42% vs 0.55 ± 0.48%, p = 0.001) and secondary malignancy risk (12.42 ± 4.33 vs 18.11 ± 6.11, p < 0.001).

Setup errors and timing measurements

Analysis of the setup errors revealed the vertical, lateral, and longitudinal displacements of 0.01 ± 0.19 cm, −0.03 ± 0.29 cm, and 0.01 ± 0.26 cm for the SP, and 0.05 ± 0.23 cm, 0.06 ± 0.40 cm, and 0.03 ± 0.31 cm for the nLDP. Meanwhile, the simulation CT times were 10.02 ± 2.35 min for the nLDP and 10.17 ± 1.53 min for the SP (p = 0.781).

Discussion

Various techniques have been explored to minimize heart dose for left-sided breast cancer, including IMRT, APBI, and proton therapy (plan design/equipment-based), as well as DIBH and prone positioning (anatomy position change-based). However, many patients cannot use these methods due to equipment, technical or compliance limitations. Our study introduces the nLDP technique, which increases the heart-to-target distance compared with the SP. With non-IMNI, the nLDP significantly reduced MHD by 90.52 cGy (22.52%), LAD Dmean by 625.17 cGy (27.46%), LV Dmean by 105.08 cGy (18.98%), and RV Dmean by 82.84 cGy (20.38%). Doses to the contralateral lung and breast were also significantly reduced without compromising target coverage. With IMNI, dose reductions were even greater. These reductions could significantly lower the risk of RIHD and secondary malignancies. The nLDP did not increase setup errors compared with the SP. Importantly, the nLDP achieved cardiac dose reductions comparable to DIBH in low-risk patients.

The LDP for cardiac sparing has been explored for decades through techniques such as the reverse semi-decubitus (RSD) and semi-lateral decubitus (SLD) positions [18, 19]. Prior studies, primarily utilizing 3D-CRT and focusing on BCS patients, consistently demonstrated the cardioprotective benefits of these positions. For instance, Niglas et al. [19] reported that the RSD significantly reduced MHD compared with the SP, while Helal et al. [18] achieved a marked MHD decrease from 300 cGy to 169 cGy (p < 0.001). However, as IMRT has become increasingly prevalent due to its superior dose conformity and OARs sparing, questions remain regarding whether these benefits persist under such advanced planning techniques. A recent 2024 study by Emam et al. [29] expanded this evidence to IMRT, reporting that the LDP yields a significantly lower MHD than the SP in BCS patients (448 cGy vs 476 cGy, p < 0.04). However, they found no significant difference in the LAD Dmean or lung dose between the two positions. Our nLDP technique confirms these previously reported benefits while extending the evidence base in several critical areas. While earlier reports on LAD and contralateral OARs sparing were inconsistent [19, 29, 30], our data show definitive dose reductions across all four cardiac chambers, the LAD, and contralateral breast and lung, regardless of IMNI. By evaluating both MRM and BCS patients within an IMRT framework, our study confirms that nLDP is a highly generalizable and effective cardioprotective technique.

DIBH remains the most widely used cardiac-sparing technique but is resource-intensive. In this study, we evaluated nLDP as a streamlined, accessible strategy and found that it achieved comparable cardiac-sparing effects in low-risk patients. Although nLDP was slightly inferior in specific dosimetric parameters such as LA V5 Gy, this may be due to already low baseline doses in the SP-FB, leaving limited room for further reduction. The nLDP was less effective than DIBH in reducing RV dose, possibly due to differences in heart displacement: DIBH displaces the heart rightward and posteriorly, while nLDP may cause both displacement and rotation. MHD was used to define low-risk patients based on prior studies and its widely use [17, 31, 32]. However, some studies suggest MHD may not best represent heart radiation exposure due to non-uniform dose distribution [33, 34]. Other studies have proposed dose thresholds for predicting RIHD, such as LV V25 Gy < 4% [35], RV Dmean < 7 Gy [36], and LAD Dmean < 3.0 Gy [12]. These thresholds were not fully applicable to our cohort: 22/28 nLDP patients met LV V25 Gy < 4%, all met RV Dmean < 7 Gy, and all exceeded LAD Dmean > 3.0 Gy. We are further investigating cardiac substructures dose metrics to better define high-risk and low-risk patients and establish cardiac dose limits. A cost-effectiveness analysis comparing DIBH and nLDP is also underway to guide future cardiac-sparing strategies. Ultimately, for low-risk patients, the cardiac-sparing effect of nLDP is comparable to DIBH, offering a valuable alternative for centers or patients unable to utilize breath-hold techniques.

Patient comfort and setup stability are essential for the clinical adoption of nLDP. Unlike vacuum bags or breast board-based RSD setups, our nLDP employs a customized styrofoam immobilization device that provides stable support while requiring minimal manual adjustments. We selected a 25° incline as the optimal balance between cardiac sparing and positioning stability; preliminary tests showed that a steeper angle (30°) increased the cardiac dose benefits but also increased the risk of patient sliding, while a shallower angle (20°) offered limited dosimetric benefits. Although the 25° incline device serves as a standardized foundation, the final position is individually tailored to account for variations in musculature and soft tissue thickness. This inherent flexibility accommodates patient-specific anatomy without compromising cardioprotective efficacy, as evidenced by our minimal setup errors. Furthermore, the efficiency of this workflow—supported by our comparable simulation CT times to SP—ensures that nLDP can be seamlessly integrated into busy radiotherapy departments without the need for specialized respiratory gating hardware or the complex technical overhead inherent to breath-hold techniques.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was limited. However, our cohort used to explore differences in the nLDP and SP (N = 28) was comparable to previous studies on ILD radiotherapy (e.g., Niglas et al. 2015 [19]: N = 12; Helal et al. 2021 [18]: N = 35). Future multicenter collaborations are needed for validation. Second, although scanning each patient in all three setups (SP-FB, nLDP-FB, and SP-DIBH) would ideally minimize anatomical bias, acquiring reproducible DIBH scans was largely unfeasible for the nLDP cohort due to poor breath-hold tolerance. Additionally, such a protocol would subject patients to additional radiation exposure. Therefore, we relied on an inter-cohort comparison with tightly matched baseline characteristics to assess the relative dosimetric benefits. Third, while the 25° incline for nLDP was chosen to balance positional stability and dosimetric benefits based on preliminary trials, systematic testing at finer angular increments was not performed due to logistical constraints. Fourth, our assessment of patient comfort relied on clinical observation rather than validated tools. We intend to incorporate objective questionnaires in future work to better evaluate patient tolerance. Fifth, the dosimetric advantages demonstrated here are limited to the treatment planning phase. Long-term follow-up is essential to determine whether these dosimetric improvements translate into a measurable reduction in clinical cardiac events. Finally, as a single-institution study, our findings may be influenced by site-specific practices (e.g., IMRT field configuration protocols). External validation with heterogeneous populations and immobilization systems is necessary before wide adoption.

Conclusion

Our study introduces the nLDP technique, a practical positioning technique using 25° left-sided elevation with styrofoam immobilization. It effectively reduces radiation doses to the whole heart, its substructures, contralateral lung and breast, without compromising target coverage. For centers unable to implement DIBH, the labor-efficient nLDP offers a valuable alternative. Future multicenter randomized trials are warranted to validate these findings and explore combining nLDP with advanced techniques like proton therapy to optimize OARs sparing.
