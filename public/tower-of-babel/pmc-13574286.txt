Cardiometabolic medication exposures and cognitive outcomes in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Yihan Wang, Benjamin Goudey, Colin L Masters, Liang Jin, Yijun Pan, The Health and Aging Brain Study (HABS-HD) Study Team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a major public health challenge, accounting for 60%–70% of dementia cases worldwide [1, 2]. People with AD are more likely to experience polypharmacy than cognitively healthy older adults. Alghamdi et al. [3] reported 1.25-fold higher odds of polypharmacy (≥5 medications) before AD diagnosis, resulting in greater cumulative medication exposure.

Cardiometabolic medications are widely prescribed in AD because hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia and cardiovascular disease (CVD) frequently coexist [4–8]. These comorbidities are associated with cognitive decline and may accelerate AD through inflammatory, vascular and metabolic mechanisms [8–10]. Consequently, medications used to treat these conditions, including antihypertensives, metformin and statins, may also influence AD progression. However, evidence for their effects on cognitive decline remains limited and inconsistent [11].

Large-scale longitudinal cohorts enable reproducible and well-powered dementia research. However, differences in medication coding, follow-up schedules and clinical characterisation hinder cross-cohort investigations. Because cardiometabolic medication use is closely linked to comorbidities, analyses must account for clinical confounding. Harmonised multicohort analyses are needed to clarify these relationships inAD.

In this study, we harmonised medication use for seven commonly prescribed cardiometabolic drugs across three longitudinal ageing AD cohorts, and examined associations with cognitive decline, measured using the CDR sum of boxes (CDR-SB). The classes included angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), β-blockers, calcium-channel blockers (CCBs), diuretics, statins and metformin. Data from National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC) [12], the Australian Imaging, Biomarkers and Lifestyle study (AIBL) [13] and the Health and Aging Brain Study: Health Disparities (HABS-HD) [14] were harmonised using consistent medication vocabularies and time variables.

The primary aim was to determine whether cardiometabolic medication exposure was associated with longitudinal CDR-SB changes across cohorts. Associations were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models with medication-by-time interactions, adjusting for cardiovascular, endocrine and psychiatric comorbidities to reduce confounding by indication and disease burden. Subgroup analyses were performed by sex, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, baseline age group and longitudinal progression groups [i.e. non-progression, cognitively unimpaired (CU)-to-mild cognitive impairment (MCI), CU/MCI-to-Alzheimer’s dementia (ADem) progression and ADem-stable group]. This harmonised framework enabled multicohort evaluation of associations between cardiometabolic medication exposure and cognitive trajectories across the AD continuum.

Methods

Study design

This secondary data analysis drew on three independent longitudinal observational studies —the NACC [12], the AIBL [13], and the HABS-HD [14]. The primary objective was to examine associations between cardiometabolic medication use and longitudinal cognitive decline measured by the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) [15], selected for its availability across cohorts.

Seven cardiometabolic drug classes (medication exposomes) were examined as primary exposures of interest, each modelled with a time-interaction term to assess association with longitudinal CDR-SB trajectory: ACEi, ARBs, β-blockers, CCBs, diuretics, statins and metformin. Drug exposure was identified using generic and brand names (Supplementary eTable S1), via Levenshtein-based fuzzy string matching (90/100 threshold; Python fuzzywuzzy library [16]); details are provided in the Supplementary Materials—eMethods (Medication data preprocessing). The models were adjusted for baseline cardiovascular (C; hypertension, myocardial infarction, stroke), endocrine (E; diabetes, thyroid disease, high cholesterol) and psychiatric (P; anxiety, depression) comorbidity domains, collectively referred to as cardiovascular, endocrine, and psychiatric (CEP) comorbidities. CEP-free indicated absence of all three domains.

Ethics and participants

Institutional human ethics approval for this secondary analysis was granted by Monash University (Project ID: 48297), in accordance with the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research 2025, and all participants provided written informed consent.

The NACC, established in 1999, maintains a harmonised relational database of clinical and neuropathological data from US Alzheimer’s Disease Centers (ADCs). Each ADC had an Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, and written informed consent was obtained from participants or, when appropriate, legally authorised representatives. Data for this study were obtained via the NACC portal (https://naccdata.org/). The AIBL study is a longitudinal observational cohort with assessments conducted approximately every 12–18 months; ethics approval was granted by the St Vincent’s Health Melbourne HREC (028/06), and dataset access was authorised by the AIBL scientific committee. HABS-HD is a longitudinal observational study of normal cognitive ageing and preclinical AD conducted at Massachusetts General Hospital; approval was obtained from the Partners Human Research Committee, and data were accessed via the HABS-HD website: https://apps.unthsc.edu/itr/our. All participants across the three cohorts provided written informed consent.

Participants were included if they had at least two visits with recorded CDR-SB scores, available time or age information for harmonised follow-up, and available comorbidity and cardiometabolic medication data or values recoverable through imputation. Participant-visit records with missingness exceeding 15% across the fixed set of analysis variables were excluded. Remaining missing values for comorbidity and medication variables were imputed using the missRanger algorithm [17], a random-forest-based chained imputation approach that preserves multivariate relationships across predictors. The eligible sample included 28,044 NACC, 1708 AIBL and 1425 HABS-HD participants. The corresponding variable names for each cohort are provided in Supplementary eTable S2.

Descriptive analysis

Participants were denoted as ‘users’ if medication use was documented at baseline or at any follow-up visit. Harmonisation enabled cross-cohort comparisons of medication use. Medication combinations were visualised using UpSet plots [18] (Figure 1a; Supplementary eFigure S1), comorbidity overlap using Venn diagrams (Figure 1b), medication–comorbidity relationships using Sankey diagrams (Figure 1c–e) and age-specific medication prevalence using binomial-smoothing splines [19] (Supplementary eFigure S2).

Statistical models

Longitudinal CDR-SB trajectories were analysed using cohort-specific linear mixed-effects models [20] with participant-specific random intercepts and time slopes. Fixed effects comprised time since baseline (years), baseline age, sex (reference = male), education (years, z-scored), APOE ε4 carrier status (reference = non-carrier) and seven time-varying binary drug classes. Models were adjusted for baseline comorbidity domains (CVD, endocrine and psychiatric conditions). Drug × time interactions estimated differences in CDR-SB slopes between users and non-users. Point estimates with standard errors, and P-values were reported. Statistical significance was defined at α = 0.05; P < .05 was considered significant.

To determine if medication effects varied across different clinical trajectories and demographic backgrounds, we executed two distinct stratified modelling approaches. First, participants were stratified into four mutually exclusive clinical progression subgroups (Figure 2): (1) non-progression (CU/MCI stage throughout follow-up, including MCI-to-CU reversion); (2) CU-to-MCI progression (baseline CU progressing to MCI without reaching dementia); (3) CU/MCI-to-ADem progression (baseline CU/MCI and progressed to ADem during follow-up); and (4) ADem-stable (ADem at baseline and throughout follow-up). Baseline and follow-up diagnoses were determined by expert clinicians and/or a consensus panel; cohort-specific criteria and adjudication procedures are detailed in the Supplementary Methods—eMethods (Diagnostic Criteria for Mild Cognitive Impairment and Alzheimer's Dementia). Corresponding results for baseline diagnosis subgroups (CU, MCI and ADem) are presented in Supplementary eFigure S3. Second, to evaluate the moderating effects of systemic and genetic vulnerabilities, models were fully stratified by sex (female vs. male), APOE ε4 status (carrier vs. non-carrier) and age category (<75 vs. ≥75 years at baseline) (Figure 3).

Results

Participant characteristics

A statistical summary is provided in Table 1. Overall, 31 177 participants were included in the analysis (NACC: 28 044; AIBL: 1708; HABS-HD: 1425). At baseline, the mean age was 71.7 (SD = 9.9) years in NACC, 72.1 (SD = 7.3) years in AIBL and 65.7 (SD = 8.4) years in HABS-HD. The proportion of female participants was 57.1% (NACC), 55.5% (AIBL), and 63.8% (HABS-HD). Follow-up duration varied across cohort, with a mean of 56.0 months (SD = 44.3) in NACC, 78.1 months (SD = 50.4) in AIBL and 41.2 months (SD = 19.4) in HABS-HD.

Medication and comorbidity characteristics

Medication exposure and comorbidity-to-medication relationships were summarised for seven classes across cohorts (Figure 1 and Supplementary eFigures S1 and S2). Statin monotherapy was the most frequent single-drug regimen (NACC: 3478; AIBL: 244; HABS-HD: 131; Supplementary eFigure S1), and statin combinations with β-blockers (n = 1097) and ACEi (n = 853) were the most common dual therapies. Polypharmacy was common: 16 of the 30 most frequent combinations involving ≥3 drug classes (Figure 1a). Comorbidity overlap was substantial (Figure 1b), with endocrine-only (n = 4552), CVD-only (n = 3249), and psychiatric-only (n = 2599) being the most common groups, while 2846 participants had all three conditions.

Prescribing patterns were broadly consistent yet cohort-specific. Overall medication use was higher in NACC and HABS-HD than in AIBL, with non-user proportions of 24.9%, 25.8% and 51.6%, respectively (Figure 1c–e). In NACC and HABS-HD, comorbidity flows were distributed more evenly across multiple drug classes, whereas AIBL flows were predominantly driven by statins. Participants with CVD in AIBL were also more likely to be non-users than those in NACC or HABS-HD. Medication-age trajectories showed steeper age-related prevalence increases in NACC and HABS-HD than in AIBL (Supplementary eFigure S2). HABS-HD participants were younger overall, whereas CVD and endocrine groups were older than psychiatric or comorbidity-free groups in NACC and AIBL (Supplementary eTable S3).

Longitudinal cognitive trajectories across clinical progression stages

Pooled meta-analyses across showed heterogenous medication associations with longitudinal CDR-SB change (Figure 2).

ACEi exposure was significantly associated with attenuated cognitive worsening in the full cohort (βmeta = −0.019 [95% CI: −0.029, −0.010], P < .001), with the strongest association observed in the CU/MCI-to-ADem subgroup (βmeta = −0.130 [−0.248, −0.012], P < .05). Metformin was similarly associated with a slower rate of CDR-SB increase in the full cohort (βmeta = −0.019 [−0.032, −0.005], P < .01) and in the non-progression subgroup (βmeta = −0.007 [−0.012, −0.003], P < .01), with non-significant inverse estimates in the remaining progression subgroups.

ARBs showed no overall association, except for a modest positive association in the non-progression subgroup (βmeta = 0.004 [0.001, 0.007], P < .01), whereas no significant meta-analytic association was detected in the remaining subgroups. A cohort-specific inverse association was observed in HABS-HD within the CU/MCI-to-ADem subgroup but was not replicated in the pooled meta-analysis. CCBs likewise showed no overall association, despite divergent cohort-specific estimates (positive in NACC and negative in AIBL). Across progression subgroups, only the CU-to-MCI subgroup showed a significant positive association (βmeta = 0.017 [0.007, 0.026], P < .001).

Diuretics were not associated with overall CDR-SB change, although NACC showed a cohort-specific inverse association. Within the CU/MCI-to-ADem progression subgroup, diuretic exposure was significantly associated with slower progression (βmeta = −0.063 [−0.092, −0.034], P < .001). Statins similarly showed no overall association, despite a positive association in NACC. Stage-specific analyses demonstrated slower cognitive decline in the CU/MCI-to-ADem subgroup (βmeta = −0.119 [−0.208, −0.030], P < .01), but faster decline in the ADem-stable subgroup (βmeta = 0.118 [0.080, 0.156], P < .001).

Longitudinal cognitive trajectories stratified by demographic and genetic subgroups

Stratification analyses showed heterogeneous associations by sex, APOE ε4 status and baseline age (Figure 3).

Across sex strata, most medication classes showed similar associations. CCB exposure was associated with a faster rate of CDR-SB progression among females at the meta-analytic level (βmeta = 0.011 [0.001, 0.022], P < .05). Although cohort-specific signals were observed in ACEi, metformin, β-blockers and statins, none reached meta-analytic significance.

ACEi exposure was significantly associated with a slower rate of CDR-SB change in both APOE ε4 carriers (βmeta = −0.026 [−0.045, −0.007], P < .01) and non-carriers (βmeta = −0.016 [−0.027, −0.006], P < .01). Diuretic exposure showed a more gradual rate of cognitive worsening restricted to APOE ε4 carriers (βmeta = −0.033 [−0.051, −0.015], P < .001). CCB, metformin and statin exposure demonstrated cohort-specific associations within APOE ε4 strata, but these were not supported by the pooled meta-analysis.

Age-stratified analyses showed that ACEi exposure was associated with slower cognitive decline in both age groups, with a stronger association among participants aged ≥75 years (βmeta = −0.039 [−0.060, −0.019], P < .001) than those aged <75 years (βmeta = −0.012 [−0.022, −0.001], P < .05). β-blocker, CCBs, diuretics and statins showed cohort-specific associations across age groups, but no additional significant meta-analytic associations were observed.

Discussion

Several studies have investigated whether non-Alzheimer’s medications influence disease progression [12, 22]. Given the high prevalence of cardiometabolic medication use in older adults [22], we conducted a multicohort medication-exposome study to evaluate associations between major cardiometabolic drug classes and longitudinal cognitive trajectories. Prior work has been limited by small samples (frequently <1000) [23, 24], single-cohort designs [23, 25–29] or randomised trials restricted to a single class [30–32]. In contrast, our study included 31 177 participants from three international cohorts and evaluated seven cardiometabolic medication classes (ACEi, ARBs, β-blockers, CCBs, diuretics, statins and metformin), spanning >70 generic compounds and >100 brand names (Supplementary eTable S1).

Overall, cardiometabolic medications were not uniformly associated with cognitive trajectories. Instead, associations varied by medication class, disease-progression stage, APOE ε4 carrier status, biological sex, age and cohort context. ACEi and metformin showed the most consistent associations with attenuated cognitive worsening, whereas ARBs and β-blockers were largely neutral in the meta-analysis. Diuretics, CCBs and statins demonstrated more heterogeneous patterns, with associations that appeared restricted to specific clinical or biological-risk subgroups.

Metformin, a medication to control type 2 diabetes mellitus, showed a modest inverse association with cognitive decline in the full-cohort meta-analysis, consistent with prior reports linking metformin use to a lower risk of cognitive decline [25, 33, 34]. Associations were broadly similar across most stratified groups and cohorts, although the strength of association varied across progression strata. The strongest association was observed in the non-progression subgroup, with additional cohort-specific inverse associations, including males in HABS-HD and APOE ε4 non-carriers in AIBL and HABS-HD. In 2025, the ongoing Metformin in Alzheimer’s Dementia Prevention study (NCT04098666), an 18-month double-blind placebo-controlled trial of extended-release metformin up to 2000 mg/day, is further evaluating whether metformin is associated with slower cognitive decline and AD-related biomarker changes in at-risk individuals [35].

Among renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system medications, ACEi exposure was associated with slower cognitive decline in the full-cohort meta-analysis and showed the strongest association in participants transitioning from CU/MCI to ADem. This finding supports the hypothesis that modulation of vascular tone, neuroinflammation or angiotensin-mediated pathways may be relevant during later stages of AD clinical progression [36, 37]. ACEi associations were also evident across APOE ε4 carrier and non-carrier strata and were stronger among participants aged ≥75 years. Stronger associations in participants aged ≥75 years may reflect greater vascular burden in older adults [38], making ACEi-related blood pressure and renin–angiotensin system pathways more relevant to cognitive trajectories in this age group. In contrast, ARBs showed no overall association, with only limited cohort- or subgroup-specific signals. Although some previous studies have reported stronger associations with slower cognitive decline for ARBs than ACEi [39–41], our findings do not support a consistent association between ARB exposure and the rate of cognitive decline. Differences in study populations may partly explain this discrepancy, as prevention-focused studies often enrol hypertensive or cognitively normal adults at earlier disease stages, whereas our cohorts span a broader clinical and older age spectrum.

Diuretics showed no significant association in the overall meta-analysis, but subgroup analyses suggested potential benefits in clinically or genetically vulnerable groups. In particular, diuretic exposure was associated with slower cognitive decline in the CU/MCI-to-ADem progression subgroup and among APOE ε4 carriers. This pattern is consistent with prior reports suggesting possible cognitive benefit from antihypertensives in selected populations [23, 42]. The subgroup-specific nature of these findings suggests that diuretic-related associations may depend on clinical stage and genetic risk context rather than reflecting a uniform class-wide pattern.

CCBs, β-blockers and statins showed no overall association with cognitive decline in the full-cohort meta-analysis, although subgroup- and cohort-specific associations were heterogeneous. Previous studies of CCBs have similarly reported both increased [43] and reduced risks (notably on dihydropyridines) [24, 44] of cognitive decline. In our analysis, faster cognitive decline was observed among females, whereas slower decline was observed in the CU-to-MCI progression subgroup, potentially reflecting differences in central nervous system penetration [45] and age-related treatment context [46]. β-blockers also demonstrated inconsistent associations across cohorts, consistent with previous reports [28, 47], possibly owing to differences in lipophilicity, blood–brain barrier penetration and neuropsychiatric effects. Statin exposure was associated with slower cognitive decline in the CU/MCI-to-ADem progression subgroup but faster decline in the ADem-stable subgroup, consistent with the heterogeneous literature reporting inverse [29, 48], null or mixed [11], and positive associations [23] with cognitive decline. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect differences in underlying cardiometabolic comorbidities, treatment indication, disease stage and cohort composition rather than uniform medication-specific associations.

Several design features strengthen this study. Medication exposure was harmonised across three independent cohorts using a standardised fuzzy-matching approach, longitudinal mixed-effects models leveraged repeated cognitive assessments and random-effects meta-analysis accounted for between-cohort heterogeneity. Although the overall patterns were generally consistent, some cohort-specific associations differed in magnitude or direction, likely reflecting variation in sample size, baseline age, follow-up duration, medication prevalence, comorbidity burden and statistical power. NACC contributed the largest sample and greatest medication exposure, increasing power to detect modest associations, whereas AIBL and HABS-HD were smaller cohorts with lower medication prevalence and reduced precision in subgroup analyses. Consequently, cohort-specific findings should be interpreted as evidence of expected clinical heterogeneity rather than contradictory results.

Future studies should incorporate pharmacy-verified medication histories, including dosage, duration, adherence and medication switching, alongside detailed measures of cardiometabolic disease severity. Causal inference methods [49] may further improve estimates under time-varying treatment and confounding, but cannot replace randomised evidence. Future work should also evaluate co-prescribing patterns, drug–drug interactions and cumulative medication burden. Ultimately, prospective clinical trials incorporating standardised longitudinal cognitive assessments are needed to determine whether cardiometabolic medications influence cognitive trajectories or simply reflect underlying health status.
