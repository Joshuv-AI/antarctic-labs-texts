MGS-HCT enables resistance gene discovery and functional sequence design in forest trees
Authors: Xuemei Guan, Zhenguang Wei, Liuyan Wang, Dezhi Zhi, Wenhui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResistance gene discovery in forest trees remains challenging because of their large, repeat-rich genomes and the sparse distribution of functional elements, which restrict the direct application of deep learning approaches developed for crop species. While genomic language models have shown promise in crop species, their application to forest trees remains limited. Here, we present MGS-HCT (Multi-scale Gated Sparse Encoder-Hybrid Convolutional Transformer), a deep learning framework tailored for forest tree genomic sequences. The model integrates multi-scale gated units with structure-aware sparse attention for efficient sequence compression, and combines convolutional modules with transformer layers to capture both local motifs and long-range regulatory dependencies. We show that MGS-HCT supports two key applications in forest tree biotechnology: (1) the conditional generation and prioritization of putative NLR candidates for Alnus glutinosa (common alder) and (2) the de novo generation of synthetic DNA sequences containing promoter-like and splice-site-like elements. These results provide a computational foundation for accelerating disease-resistance gene discovery and genomic-assisted breeding in forest tree species, expanding the scope of deep learning from crop plants to forest genomics. By prioritizing candidates supported by multiple computational criteria for subsequent experimental validation, the framework is intended to narrow the experimental search space and help shorten the long breeding cycles of forest trees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12896-026-01205-2.Keywords: Forest tree genomics, Deep learning, NLR resistance gene, Inverse design, DNA language model

Introduction

Forest trees are integral components of terrestrial ecosystems, playing irreplaceable roles in carbon sequestration, soil and water conservation, and biodiversity maintenance [29, 33, 34]. However, forest tree breeding is confronted with inherent challenges, such as long generation cycles and complex genetic backgrounds; consequently, traditional phenotypic selection often necessitates decades to complete a single breeding cycle [45]. The introduction of Genomic Selection (GS) has provided a new avenue for accelerating genetic improvement in forest trees, yet its efficiency is limited by the depth of our understanding of genomic functional elements [21]. Forest tree genomes possess unique attributes that distinguish them sharply from the herbaceous and crop species on which most genomic deep-learning models have been developed, presenting a threefold challenge for modelling. First, the sequence length is extreme. Foundational model-plant and crop genomes are comparatively compact, measuring approximately 0.125 Gb in Arabidopsis thaliana [1] and 0.4 Gb in rice (Oryza sativa; [18]), respectively. Even the larger staples remain within a few gigabases, such as maize (Zea mays, ≈ 2.3 Gb), which is comparable to the human genome [38], reaching roughly 16 Gb only in hexaploid bread wheat [19]. Conifer genomes, by contrast, routinely exceed 20 Gb: the assemblies of Norway spruce (Picea abies, ≈ 20 Gb) and loblolly pine (Pinus taeda, ≈ 22 Gb) are roughly seven times the size of the human genome [29, 34, 36]. Second, the repetitive content is both high and pervasive. Repeat content varies widely across crops, from approximately 14% to 20% in the compact Arabidopsis genome and ≈ 35% in rice to as much as 85% in maize [38]. In contrast, in forest trees, and conifers in particular, transposable elements consistently account for approximately 70% to 80% of the genome [45]. Critically, the small, repeat-poor crop genomes (Arabidopsis, rice) that underpin most existing models are precisely those least representative of forest trees, which combine very large size with uniformly high repeat density. Third, functional signals are sparse: regulatory elements are thinly distributed across these massive genomes and diluted by vast amounts of redundant sequence, hindering conventional Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) from capturing long-range dependencies [7].

In recent years, DNA language models based on the Transformer architecture have opened a new paradigm for genomic functional analysis. DNABERT first introduced the BERT pre-training framework to DNA sequences, achieving breakthroughs in multiple downstream tasks through a k-mer tokenization strategy [17]. DNABERT-2 further employed Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) to enable cross-species transfer learning [50]. The Nucleotide Transformer, pre-trained on 300 billion base pairs across 2,500 species, represents one of the largest genomic foundation models to date [11]. Enformer significantly enhanced gene expression prediction accuracy by extending the receptive field to 200 kb [2]. However, the quadratic complexity of traditional Transformers presents a severe computational bottleneck when processing ultra-long sequences. To address this issue, researchers have developed various efficient architectures. The Sparse Transformer reduces complexity to through sparse attention patterns [9]; Longformer and BigBird achieve linear complexity by combining local windows with global tokens [4, 47]; and Performer realizes attention computation via random feature approximation [10]. State Space Models (SSMs) offer an alternative path, with Mamba achieving efficient long-sequence modeling through selective state spaces [14]. In the genomic field, HyenaDNA was the first to model 1 million base pairs at single-nucleotide resolution [31]; Evo extended the context length to 131 kb and demonstrated design capabilities from molecular to genomic scales [30]; Evo 2 further covered the whole-genome scale [8]; and Caduceus introduced bi-directional equivariance constraints to better capture the reverse-complement nature of DNA [37]. megaDNA utilized a multi-scale Transformer architecture to decipher bacteriophage genomes, offering significant insights for long-genome modeling through its hierarchical representation learning strategy [39].The development of plant-specific DNA language models has been relatively lagging. AgroNT was pre-trained on 48 edible plant genomes, demonstrating advantages in predicting cis-regulatory elements [25]. PlantCaduceus introduced the Mamba architecture to plant genomes, achieving breakthroughs in variant effect prediction for Arabidopsis thaliana [48]. Recent studies have also demonstrated the capability of deep learning models to decipher the plant cis-regulatory code [5, 35]. Furthermore, generative deep learning has shown immense potential in biological sequence design. ProtGPT2 proved that autoregressive language models can generate proteins with natural folding properties [12]. RFdiffusion introduced diffusion models to protein structure design, achieving the de novo generation of functional proteins [44]. At the DNA level, generative models have been employed to design synthetic promoters and regulatory elements [51]. However, sequence generation models targeting forest tree-specific genes, such as NLRs, remain a gap. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) strategies provide cost-effective solutions for adapting large-scale pre-trained models to downstream tasks. LoRA significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters through low-rank decomposition [16]; Adapter inserts lightweight modules while freezing the backbone network [15]; and LINGO proposed a customized fine-tuning strategy for genomic language models [49]. These methods enable the full utilization of large foundation models under limited computational resources.

Nucleotide-binding Leucine-rich Repeat (NLR) genes serve as core components of the plant innate immune system, mediating the recognition of pathogen effector proteins and triggering defense responses [20, 42]. Typical NLR proteins consist of three domains: CC/TIR-NB-LRR, among which the high variability of the LRR region determines recognition specificity [43]. Recent structural biological studies on the resistosome have revealed the molecular mechanism by which NLRs form calcium-permeable channels to trigger cell death upon activation [6, 23, 24]. Analysis of the Arabidopsis pan-NLRome has identified over 10,000 NLR alleles, showcasing astonishing evolutionary diversity [41]. NLR genes form complex networks within plant genomes, synergistically mediating broad-spectrum resistance against diverse pathogens [46]. For forest trees, their extended life cycles and open ecological niches subject them to continuous pathogen selection pressure, making the diversity and function of the NLR genome particularly critical [27, 28]. Therefore, it is crucial to develop deep learning models capable of efficiently processing ultra-long forest tree genomes, accurately capturing the structural features of NLR genes, and supporting the inverse design of resistance genes. This is of significant importance for the mining of forest resistance gene resources and precise design breeding.

In this study, we propose the MGS-HCT (Multi-scale Gated Sparse encoder – Hybrid Convolutional Transformer) architecture, specifically designed to address the threefold characteristics of forest tree genomes: long sequences, high repetition, and sparse signals. The model aims to decipher the typical CC/TIR-NB-LRR domain organization of NLR genes and support the generation and prioritization of putative resistance-gene candidates. The front-end Multi-scale Gated Sparse (MGS) Encoder draws upon the concept of deep unfolding and recent structural similarity-inspired heuristic methods from the field of image super-resolution [32]. By utilizing Multi-scale Gated Units (MGU) and a Structure-Aware Sparse Attention (SASA) module, the encoder achieves efficient compression of redundant sequences, reducing computational complexity from to approximately. The back-end Hybrid Convolutional Transformer (HCT) backbone is inspired by the multi-token attention mechanism [13]. It introduces Key-Query convolution and Head-Mixing convolution within the compressed feature space to capture both short-range motif patterns of cis-regulatory elements and long-range dependencies. Subsequently, we pre-trained the model on the genomes of 28 forest tree species and employed the LoRA strategy to efficiently fine-tune the model for the NLR gene generation task in Alnus glutinosa. Conditional generation experiments demonstrated that the sequences generated by MGS-HCT showed no significant differences in NLR domain organization compared to natural reference sequences. Multidimensional in silico characterization, including domain annotation, protein structure prediction, and Open Reading Frame (ORF) analysis, supports the biological plausibility of the generated sequences. Forest-tree disease-resistance breeding is constrained by decades-long generation cycles, low genetic-transformation efficiency in most tree species, and limited catalogues of characterized resistance genes, which together make conventional breeding and transgenic approaches slow and resource-intensive. Against this backdrop, MGS-HCT is intended as a computationally driven biotechnology tool that prioritizes high-quality candidate resistance-gene sequences for subsequent experimental validation, narrowing the search space and reducing screening cost. This work thus provides a computational framework, and an accompanying biotechnology workflow, for forest-tree disease-resistance gene design and functional-genomics research.

Data preprocessing

The training dataset comprised 28 forest tree genomes obtained from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and CNGBdb (https://db.cngb.org/) as of December 2024; detailed data sources, assembly statistics, and BUSCO completeness values are provided in Supplementary Table S1. To unify the coding system, all ambiguous bases were first mapped to “N”. Subsequently, sequences with an “N” content greater than 10% or insufficient length were filtered out to construct the final training dataset. For sequence representation, we employed a sliding window strategy (K-mer = 6, stride = 1). The vocabulary was constructed based on the Cartesian product of {A, C, G, T} (4,096 tokens), plus one token for unknown bases, resulting in a total of 4,097 tokens. Each k-mer was mapped to a 256-dimensional dense vector via a learnable embedding layer, and sinusoidal positional encodings were superimposed.

In the Structure-Aware Sparse Attention (SASA) module, the top 3% high-activation positions are first selected according to the gating scores (topk_ratio = 0.03) as sparse anchors; each anchor is then expanded by a 16-token neighbourhood (esam_window = 16), and the resulting expanded sparse set is capped at 512 tokens (max_tokens = 512). The Jaccard threshold governs the selection of homology edges within SASA: a distant fragment is admitted as an attention target for a query when the MinHash Jaccard similarity between the two fragments exceeds the threshold. Each 4101-bp fragment contains 4096 6-mer positions at stride 1, which span essentially the entire 6-mer vocabulary (), so this Jaccard was computed over the expanded sparse set of up to 512 6-mer tokens (max_tokens = 512) rather than over the full fragment, for which the comparison would be uninformative. The non-homologous background was measured empirically on randomly sampled cross-family fragment pairs, giving a mean Jaccard of 0.148 (median 0.119; 95th percentile 0.335); the edge threshold of 0.5 therefore lies above the 95th percentile of this background, so spurious edges are excluded with high confidence. The value 0.5 was selected from a threshold sweep (Supplementary Material S3) as the operating point at which the admitted homology edges are most precise, jointly maximising homology-detection F1 and clustering agreement and minimising held-out validation perplexity on the corpus of 28 genomes. The empirical background, the threshold sweep, and the MinHash estimation error (num_perm = 128) are reported in Supplementary Material S3.

The model was implemented based on PyTorch, consisting of a front-end MGS Encoder (with 6 unfolding stages) cascaded with a back-end HCT Backbone. The AdamW optimizer was selected ( , weight decay =), with a learning rate of , employing linear warmup followed by cosine annealing.

Pre-training and generation masking strategy. MGS-HCT uses a single backbone under two attention regimes. During pre-training, the HCT backbone is bidirectional under a masked language modeling (MLM) objective: 15% of the input 6-mer tokens are selected as targets, of which 80% are replaced with a [MASK] embedding, 10% with a random token, and 10% left unchanged, mitigating the train–inference mismatch from the absent [MASK] token at inference. Because 6-mer tokens generated with stride 1 share five overlapping bases, masking isolated tokens would let a target be reconstructed from its neighbors; we therefore adopt contiguous span masking following DNABERT [17], masking consecutive 6-mers so that the central bases of a masked span cannot be recovered from the overlapping context. No causal constraint is applied: self-attention is fully bidirectional and all convolutional components use symmetric padding, so that each position exploits both upstream and downstream context. This is appropriate for genomic elements such as splice sites and cis-regulatory motifs that are constrained by flanking regions on both sides.

For generation (Section “Inverse design of resistance genes”–“De novo genome generation”), a brief adaptation stage converts the backbone to autoregressive mode through three changes: self-attention masks become causal, restricting each position to preceding ones; every convolution is made causal by replacing symmetric padding with left-only zero padding of size k-1, where k is the kernel size, confining each receptive field to current and past positions; and the parameters are briefly fine-tuned with a next-token objective to realign the bidirectionally learned representations to sequential generation. The adapted model then performs conditional generation with LoRA (Section “Inverse design of resistance genes”) or unconditional generation (Section “De novo genome generation”).

Because our tokenization uses overlapping 6-mers with stride = 1, any two consecutive tokens must share five bases. Given a generated 6-mer (), the only admissible next tokens have the form () with { A,C,G,T }: exactly four of the 4,096 tokens are legal, so each step determines a single new base. We enforce this overlap at two levels and apply the same procedure to both generation tasks: conditional generation under LoRA (Section Inverse design of resistance genes”) and unconditional de novo generation (Section “De novo genome generation”). First, at each decoding step we apply a prefix-constrained mask: the logits of all overlap-violating tokens are set to, the model probabilities defined by Eqs. (14) and (15) are renormalized over the four admissible tokens, and Top-p nucleus sampling is performed within this set. The first token is unconstrained. It is either drawn from the full vocabulary or fixed as a seed for conditional generation, and the constraint applies from the second step onward. Second, we reconstruct the nucleotide sequence by sliding concatenation: the full 6-mer is written for the first token, and each subsequent token contributes only its final base. The two mechanisms are consistent by construction, since the prefix mask guarantees that the five discarded bases of each token are exactly the five already emitted; no information is lost and no contradictory base is produced.

In addition to internal statistical metrics, FIMO and BLASTN toolchains were integrated for external biological validation: the former was used to count the hit rate and enrichment of target motifs in the generated sequences, while the latter was used to evaluate the match degree between generated sequences and known genes or genomic regions through homology alignment. To further assess the structural validity of the generated resistance-gene candidates, candidate ORFs were screened for NB-ARC domains and canonical motifs and compared with species-matched A. glutinosa NLRs and experimentally validated NLRs. Amino-acid composition, ordered NB-ARC motif architecture, and LRR-repeat architecture were evaluated using length-stratified dinucleotide-shuffled controls, with detailed methods and results provided in Supplementary Material S5. For broader composition-matched validation, each of the 362 reference A. glutinosa NLR loci was independently shuffled 10 times using uShuffle v1.1.2 with k = 2, producing 3,620 sequences arranged into 10 replicate sets. Generated versus Shuffled NLR comparisons used two-sided paired permutation tests with Holm adjustment, and the complete results are provided in Supplementary Material S7.

Protein-structure confidence was initially assessed using ESMFold [22], with mean pLDDT calculated across all modelled residues in each translated protein. An independent AlphaFold2 analysis was subsequently performed for the Generated, Natural reference, Random, and experimentally determined NLR benchmark groups. Full-length and domain-level confidence metrics and structural comparisons with experimental PDB chains are described in Supplementary Material S8.

Phylogenetic placement was evaluated using the NB-ARC domains of filtered generated candidates and authentic NLR references. The domains were aligned with MAFFT, trimmed with trimAl, and analysed by maximum likelihood using IQ-TREE with 1,000 SH-aLRT and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. Detailed filtering and tree-construction procedures are provided in Supplementary Material S9.

Memorisation and sequence-novelty assessment

To assess potential sequence memorisation, all LoRA-conditioned and de novo generated sequences were compared against their corresponding training databases using BLASTN. Exact duplicates were defined as sequences with 100% nucleotide identity and 100% query coverage, whereas near duplicates required at least 95% identity and at least 80% query coverage. The analysis included separate comparisons with the MGS-HCT pretraining corpus and the NLR adapter-training set. The complete search parameters, nearest-neighbour statistics, and similarity-category distributions are provided in Supplementary Material S6.

As shown in Fig. 2A, the model converged stably across different parameter scales (5 M, 10 M, 15 M), with increased parameter counts yielding lower validation losses, indicating that expanding model capacity facilitates the capture of complex sequence features. The narrow gap between training and validation losses suggests that the MGU gating mechanism and regularization effectively suppressed overfitting. This observation is consistent with the adaptive sparsification behavior shown in Fig. 2B: in the early stages of training, the proportions of active channels and effective tokens dropped sharply under regularization pressure and subsequently stabilized, reflecting that the model progressively compressed redundant channels and tokens, concentrating representational capacity on a highly informative feature subset and achieving nearly an order-of-magnitude compression in representation.

This compact representation further translates into computational advantages for long sequences (Fig. 2C). We benchmarked MGS-HCT (15.8 M parameters) against five representative DNA language models spanning a wide range of architectures and scales: DNABERT (110 M), DNABERT-2 (117 M), Nucleotide Transformer (500 M), Caduceus (7 M), HyenaDNA (160 M) and a standard Transformer baseline. Despite having substantially fewer parameters than DNABERT, DNABERT-2, and Nucleotide Transformer, MGS-HCT exhibited a much flatter increase in inference latency approaching linear complexity, whereas the standard Transformer displayed quadratic growth and encountered an out-of-memory (OOM) bottleneck at 16,384 bp. Notably, MGS-HCT maintained latency comparable to Caduceus and HyenaDNA—two models specifically designed for efficient long-range modeling—while significantly outperforming the larger Transformer-based models at extended sequence lengths.

Frequency domain analysis confirmed that this efficiency gain did not compromise reconstruction quality (Fig. 2D). The spectral distributions of the original and compressed reconstructed features showed high overlap, particularly in the mid-to-low frequency regions where peak positions and amplitudes remained consistent. Low-frequency components correspond to global GC content distribution and large-scale repetitive elements, while mid-to-high frequency components encode local motif patterns and codon usage bias. The spectral stability constraint effectively preserved these key structural features. In summary, MGS-HCT achieves a favorable balance between long-sequence inference efficiency and reconstruction fidelity through sparse learning.

Ablation studies

Ablation studies were conducted on the forest tree genomic dataset described in Section “Data preprocessing” to verify the contribution of each component module of MGS-HCT. All model variants were evaluated with a sequence length setting of 4,096. Training-related statistics were collected on an RTX 4090 (24 GB) GPU, where latency was defined as the wall-clock time for a single training iteration, and memory usage referred to the peak memory consumption during the training process. To establish a domain-matched architectural control, we first trained a standard Transformer baseline on the same forest tree genomic dataset using the same 6-mer tokenizer, sequence length, data split, batch size, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and training epochs as MGS-HCT. This baseline did not include the proposed MGU, SASA, and MoE modules.

As shown in Table 1, a standard Transformer baseline trained under the same dataset and protocol provides the reference point for assessing each proposed module. It achieves a pseudo-PPL of 18.42, a JSD of 0.187 and an Acc of 72.3% with 15.2 M parameters. Adding the MGU module alone reduces pseudo-PPL to 15.83 and raises Acc to 76.8%, indicating that the gating mechanism suppresses redundant representations and sharpens the characterization of functional signals such as short k-mer motifs.

Validation of core module effectiveness

The SASA module alone gives a comparable improvement (pseudo-PPL 16.21, Acc 75.2%, JSD 0.164). By concentrating attention on high-weight positions through structured sparsity, SASA captures non-local dependencies such as repetitive-element boundaries and distal co-occurring fragments, in a manner consistent with the uneven distribution of functional elements across genomic sequences.

Combining MGU and SASA yields further gains (pseudo-PPL 13.56, Acc 81.4%, JSD 0.128), confirming their complementarity: MGU preserves salient features via gating, while SASA optimizes sparse associations among them, so that local motifs and long-range dependencies are captured within one framework.

The full MGS-HCT, which adds the cross-layer MoE module on top of MGU and SASA, achieves the best performance (pseudo-PPL 11.24, Acc 85.7%, JSD 0.096); compared with the baseline, this represents a 39.0% lower pseudo-PPL, a 48.7% lower JSD, and a 13.4-point higher Acc at a comparable parameter scale (15.8 M vs. 15.2 M). Because all variants share the same data and protocol, these gains are attributable to the proposed architectural components rather than to domain-specific training.

Table 2 compares the pre-training performance of MGS-HCT with established DNA language models on the same forest-tree test set. To remove tokenization as a confound, all models were evaluated under a unified 6-mer protocol: each model was re-tokenized to the common 6-mer vocabulary and scored with the same masked-LM pseudo-perplexity, so that holds for every entry and the per-token losses are placed on one scale. We further report Bits Per Nucleotide (), which normalizes the loss to the individual base pair and is upper-bounded by 2.0 bits/bp (the entropy of a uniform base distribution).

Under this protocol MGS-HCT attains the lowest perplexity (11.24), the lowest BPN (0.582 bits/bp) and the highest accuracy (85.7%) with only 15.8 M parameters. The decisive comparison is against the same-domain standard Transformer baseline (0.701 bits/bp), which shares identical data, tokenization, and optimization and differs only in architecture. Therefore, the drop from 0.701 to 0.582 bits/bp, the 39.0% lower perplexity, and the accuracy gain of 13.4% points are attributable to the proposed MGU, SASA, and MoE modules rather than to domain-matched data. The general-purpose models, evaluated zero-shot, show markedly higher BPN (DNABERT 1.314, DNABERT-2 1.228, Nucleotide Transformer 1.405), reflecting the domain shift onto out-of-distribution plant genomes; notably the 500 M Nucleotide Transformer performs worst, indicating that capacity alone cannot bridge the mammalian–plant gap. Single-nucleotide models (e.g. Caduceus) are excluded from this table because their four-class per-token loss cannot be reconciled to the 6-mer basis without exceeding the 2.0 bits/bp bound; they are instead compared by inference efficiency in Fig. 2C, where latency is tokenization-independent.

Inverse design of disease-resistance genes using MGS-HCT

We aimed to design disease-resistance genes for Alnus glutinosa(GCF_958979055.1) and evaluated the conditional generation performance across three dimensions: sequence statistical characteristics, domain organization, and functional relevance. Under the explicit constraint of conserved motifs, the frozen MGS-HCT utilized autoregressive decoding to generate 531 candidate sequences (Fig. 3A), thereby focusing the generation process on a structured search space more closely aligned with NLR locus characteristics. In terms of global composition, the GC content distribution of the generated set largely overlapped with that of the reference set, albeit with a slight rightward shift (Generated vs. Reference,; KS D=0.252,; Cliff’s). This suggests that while the difference was statistically significant, the effect size was moderate, and the GC values remained within the common range of the target gene family (Fig. 3B). Comparison of length distributions further revealed that the generated sequences covered the typical 2–12 kbp range of NLR loci (Generated median bp, Reference median ≈4,301 bp; KS; Cliff’s), thereby reducing the risk of generating only short fragments that fail to form complete locus structures (Fig. 3C).At the domain organization level, NLR domains were annotated using NLR-Annotator [40]. Based on its default output, sequences were classified into Complete NLR, NB-ARC-like fragments, and Non-NLR, with the first two combined as “NLR-related” for statistical testing. The experiment demonstrated that the proportion of NLR-related sequences in the generated set reached 60%, showing no significant difference from the 58.9% in the reference set (two-sided Fisher’s exact test). Complete NLRs accounted for 35% of the generated set (Reference 38.7%), and NB-ARC-like fragments accounted for 25% (Reference 20.2%). In sharp contrast, random sequences only hit NLR-related domains in 2% of cases ( p<0.001), illustrating that the conditional generation strategy effectively captured the complex structural constraints of the NLR gene family (Fig. 3E). To provide a composition-matched biological negative control, we evaluated dinucleotide-preserving shuffled NLR sequences. Although the shuffled control matched the reference mean GC content (38.1%; KS p = 0.71), it showed lower NLR-related, Complete-NLR, and NB-ARC-like hit rates, functional scores, and mean pLDDT than the Generated group. All five comparisons remained significant after Holm correction (adjusted p = 0.0094–0.0098; Supplementary Table S7.1). Additional conservation analysis showed that the generated NLR-related ORFs retained canonical NB-ARC motifs, including the P-loop, Kinase-2, and GLPL motifs, and their expected order more frequently than the shuffled control (Supplementary Material S5). Amino-acid composition was supportive but not discriminatory, while the lower LRR-repeat counts in the Generated group were consistent with its shorter ORFs. Finally, 27 of the 35 generated Complete-NLR candidates passed the predefined NB-ARC domain and alignment-quality criteria for phylogenetic analysis. These candidates were distributed across several regions of the maximum-likelihood tree, with several placed near natural Alnus glutinosa homologs or experimentally characterised NLRs (Supplementary Figure S9.1 and Supplementary Table S9.1). Together, these analyses support the presence of higher-order NLR-like features that cannot be attributed solely to nucleotide composition. Functional score distributions showed that the generated set remained consistent with the reference set overall (median 0.48 vs. 0.48, two-sided Mann-Whitney U test p=0.623), and both were significantly higher than the random set (random median 0.20, ). Furthermore, when restricting the analysis to the NLR-related subset, the scores of generated sequences remained comparable to those of the reference sequences (median 0.83 vs. 0.82, p=0.86), indicating that conditioned on successfully hitting NLR-related domains, the generated candidates possessed similar values under the computational functional-score definition (Fig. 3D).Finally, Best-of-N analysis revealed that utilizing the generate-and-filter strategy, a qualitative leap in candidate quality could be achieved with only a small sampling size (e.g. N=10) (best score 0.89 vs. random 0.31,) (Fig. 3F). This significant enrichment effect demonstrates that the model can provide a high-confidence candidate pool for disease-resistance gene design, making the subsequent experimental workflow integrating domain analysis and biological validation more targeted and actionable.

De novo generation of genome sequences using MGS-HCT

We jointly evaluated the gene-like plausibility of the conditionally generated sequences for Alnus glutinosa from three dimensions: the recognizability of gene structural signals, ORF integrity, and protein folding confidence. MGS-HCT generated 1,000 de novo candidate sequences, all of which were included in the gene-structure evaluation and the memorisation and sequence-novelty assessment (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Material S6). Zero-shot discrimination revealed that the generated sequences contained clear structural signals (Fig. 4B). The discriminatory performance for coding sequences (CDS) (AUC = 0.775) was significantly higher than that for start and stop sites (AUC 0.68–0.70). This reflects that the model prioritized capturing stable long-range coding region features, while there remains room for optimization in defining local signals that precisely delimit gene boundaries.ORF statistical analysis further verified the significant improvement in fragment integrity within the generated sequences. By analyzing the probability density distribution and cumulative distribution function of ORF lengths (Fig. 4D–E), we found that the proportion of ORFs with lengths bp in the generated sequences was significantly higher than that in random sequences, and their distribution curves aligned highly with natural reference sequences. This indicates that the generated sequences effectively avoided the fragmentation of short ORFs common in random sequences and possessed the potential to encode functional protein domains.To assess whether these ORFs have the potential to form stable protein structures, the DNA sequences of candidate ORFs were first translated into protein sequences using ExPASy, followed by structure prediction and calculation of mean pLDDT for each protein using ESMFold (Fig. 4C). The results showed that the mean pLDDT of the generated sequences () fell between that of the reference () and random () sequences. An independent AlphaFold2 analysis showed the same confidence ordering, with mean full-length pLDDT values of 51 ± 9.6 for the Generated group, 80 ± 4.9 for the Natural reference group, 31 ± 5.7 for the Random control, and 78 ± 5.2 for the experimental benchmarks (Supplementary Table S8.2). The benchmark predictions agreed with their resolved PDB chains, with TM scores from 0.86 to 0.91, Cα RMSD values from 1.4 to 2.3 Å, and alignment coverage from 89% to 96%; domain analysis showed stronger similarity for the Generated NB-ARC region than for the full length architecture (Supplementary Tables S8.1 to S8.3). This suggests that the generated sequences are more likely to produce proteins with a certain degree of folding stability compared to random sequences, although the overall structural confidence remains lower than that of natural reference proteins. Subsequent improvements could be achieved through stronger boundary constraints, splicing consistency filtering, or structure-guided screening to further enhance candidate quality. In the gene-structural-element evaluation shown in Fig. 4F, the generated sequences achieved AUCs of 0.895, 0.908, 0.847, and 0.831 for start codons, stop codons, splice sites, and promoters, respectively. We further extended this analysis to five prespecified endpoints by including CDS recognition and by applying the same classifier and scoring procedure to the Random and dinucleotide-preserving Shuffled NLR controls (Supplementary Table S7.2). Across the five endpoints, the Generated group achieved AUCs of 0.831–0.912, including an AUC of 0.912 for CDS, whereas the Random and Shuffled NLR controls yielded AUCs of 0.468–0.502 and 0.504–0.559, respectively. The near-chance performance of the Shuffled NLR control indicates that preservation of dinucleotide composition alone did not reproduce the higher-order sequence signals detected in the Generated group. Overall, these results demonstrate that the generated Alnus glutinosa sequences have achieved significant convergence from a random background toward the reference distribution at the gene structure level, particularly excelling in coding region stability and ORF length distribution. In a separate novelty assessment, no exact duplicates were identified, and the near-duplicate rates were 8.0% and 2.0% for the LoRA-conditioned and de novo outputs, respectively, providing no evidence of widespread verbatim or near-verbatim memorisation under the predefined criteria (Supplementary Tables S6.1 and S6.2). Moreover, to assess the generalizability of these findings across phylogenetically diverse taxa, we conducted cross-species evaluations on six woody plant genomes (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

In this study, we proposed MGS-HCT, a model that enables efficient representation of forest-tree genomic data and targeted sequence design. Experimental results confirmed its effectiveness: during cross-species pre-training across 28 forest-tree species the model showed superior convergence and computational efficiency, and in application to Alnus glutinosa it achieved both the inverse design of NLR resistance genes with proper domain organization and the de novo generation of sequences with realistic gene structural features.

To further quantify cross-species generalization, we evaluated MGS-HCT on six representative species spanning four angiosperm families and one gymnosperm (Supplementary Table S2). In-distribution species gave PPL values of 11.18–12.75, whereas out-of-distribution species showed the expected degradation (PPL 14.29–15.21), indicating that the model captures transferable genomic representations whose accuracy modulates with phylogenetic distance. This pattern is consistent with the genomic contrast established in the Introduction. The in-distribution angiosperm species share with the training corpus not only closer evolutionary distance but also comparable genome size and repeat architecture, whereas the gymnosperm out-of-distribution case combines greater phylogenetic distance with the largest, most repeat-dense genomes, the regime in which transferable representation is hardest to achieve.

The benchmarking results further support this interpretation. Against a standard Transformer baseline of similar scale trained on the same forest-tree dataset, MGS-HCT achieved lower PPL and JSD and higher masked-token accuracy, indicating that the gain cannot be attributed to domain-specific data alone; the ablation further shows that MGU, SASA and the cross-layer MoE make complementary contributions. Reporting Bits Per Nucleotide (BPN) places these comparisons on a tokenization-normalized scale, though comparisons with externally trained models should be read as evidence of task-specific suitability rather than a universal ranking of architectures.

The core design philosophy of MGS-HCT is to address the specificities of forest tree genomes through sparse representation learning. In forest tree genomes, regulatory elements, conserved sequences, and functional genes are heavily diluted by transposable elements and repetitive sequences, resulting in a highly sparse distribution of functional signals. The gated sparsification mechanism is specifically designed to tackle this characteristic, enabling the model to adaptively concentrate its representational capacity on regions more likely to contain functional information. The observation that the proportion of active channels gradually decreased and stabilized during training (Fig. 2B) indicates that the model learned to distinguish functional sites from the redundant background. The spectral stability constraint ensured that the GC content distribution and repetitive element organization patterns were preserved during compression (Fig. 2D), which is crucial for maintaining biological features such as codon usage bias and gene expression regulatory signals. Unlike standard Transformers, which hit memory bottlenecks when processing long sequences, MGS-HCT maintained near-linear complexity growth through its sparse attention mechanism (Fig. 2C), enabling it to capture distant regulatory relationships common in plant genomes. Ablation studies further revealed the synergistic effects among architectural components (Table 1): the MGU suppressed low-information channels to enhance signal purity, while the SASA reinforced the modeling of long-range non-uniform dependencies. The combination of these two produced a super-additive effect, allowing the model to simultaneously capture local motif patterns (e.g., splice sites, promoter motifs) and larger-scale genomic structural features.

The inverse-design experiment illustrates the model’s potential for functional sequence design. At the level of sequence architecture, the generated sequences reproduced the canonical multi-domain organization of plant intracellular immune receptors, CC/TIR-NB-ARC-LRR (Fig. 3E), and NLR-Annotator assigned them domain proportions not significantly different from those of natural NLR genes. This architecture consists of an N-terminal CC or TIR signalling domain, a central nucleotide-binding NB-ARC module, and a C-terminal LRR recognition region. Because it defines the NLR family and underlies its function in effector recognition and immune activation, its faithful recovery indicates that the model captured the structural grammar of the family rather than merely fitting superficial sequence statistics. At the level of the encoded product, ESMFold assigned the generated sequences a mean pLDDT (48.7 ± 10.8) intermediate between natural NLR proteins (83.5 ± 6.7) and random sequences (30.5 ± 5.2; Fig. 4C), indicating that their translated products are more likely than random sequences to adopt an ordered, foldable conformation, while remaining below the confidence of natural proteins. Together, these analyses provide convergent in silico evidence that the candidates are biologically plausible, although their functionality has not yet been experimentally demonstrated. Building on this plausibility, the Best-of-N sampling strategy (Fig. 3F) provides a basis for prioritizing candidate sequences: for forest-tree species with generation cycles spanning decades, conventional disease-resistance breeding requires repeated hybridization and long-term field screening, whereas this computationally driven exploration can substantially narrow the search space prior to experimental validation, offering a route to accelerate the genetic improvement of disease resistance in forest trees.

The de novo generation experiment for Alnus glutinosa genomic sequences demonstrated that the model is capable of generating sequences with realistic gene structural features. The generated sequences performed well in the identification of functional elements such as coding regions, splice sites, and promoters (Fig. 4F), indicating that the model acquired sequence features—including codon usage bias, splicing signal motifs, and core promoter structures—through pre-training. The trend of ORF length distribution converging toward the reference set (Figs. 4D–E) carries significant biological implications: functional proteins typically require ORFs of sufficient length to encode complete folding domains, and the enrichment of long ORFs in the generated sequences is consistent with an increased potential for protein coding. Protein structure prediction further supported this assessment: the pLDDT scores of the generated sequences fell between those of natural proteins and random sequences (Fig. 4C), suggesting that their encoded products may have a higher potential to form ordered three-dimensional structures than random sequences. The independent AlphaFold2 analysis provided a consistent structural benchmark. The generated sequences showed higher predicted confidence and stronger NLR related structural similarity than the random controls but remained below the natural reference proteins and experimentally determined benchmarks (Supplementary Tables S8.1 to S8.3). These results support the computational structural plausibility of the generated sequences but require experimental validation. Although the shuffled NLR control matched the mean GC content and dinucleotide composition of the reference loci, its performance remained close to that of the random control, supporting the interpretation that the generated sequences captured aspects of higher order sequence organization rather than nucleotide composition alone (Supplementary Tables S7.1 and S7.2).

This study nonetheless has several limitations. First, all evaluations were computational; the generated sequences have not yet undergone biological-activity validation, so the true disease-resistance function of the candidates remains to be confirmed. Second, the current motif-based conditioning achieves only coarse-grained control and does not yet permit precise regulation of domain boundaries and functional sites within NLR genes. To address the first limitation, we envisage a staged validation roadmap that includes (i) prioritization of top-ranked candidates by the in silico criteria reported here, namely domain architecture, predicted foldability, and codon usage, as described in Supplementary Material S4; (ii) heterologous transient expression of selected Alnus glutinosa NLR candidates in Nicotiana benthamiana to assess protein accumulation and the autoactivity of cell-death responses; and (iii) pathogen-challenge assays in N. benthamiana or the native host to test whether the designed receptors confer measurable resistance. Such experiments would move the candidates from in silico plausibility to demonstrated function and, in turn, provide labelled feedback for refining the model. To address the second, we aim to integrate multi-omics data, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and epigenomics, into a multi-level conditional-constraint framework [26], enabling refined generation control from sequence to function.
