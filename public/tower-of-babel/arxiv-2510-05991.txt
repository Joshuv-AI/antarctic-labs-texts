Abstract
This paper develops distribution theory and bootstrap-based inference methods for a broad class of convex pairwise difference estimators. These estimators minimize a kernel-weighted convex-in-parameter function over observation pairs that are similar in terms of certain covariates, where the similarity is governed by a localization (bandwidth) parameter. While classical results establish asymptotic normality under restrictive bandwidth conditions, we show that valid Gaussian and bootstrap-based inference remains possible under substantially weaker assumptions. First, we extend the theory of small bandwidth asymptotics to convex pairwise estimation settings, deriving robust Gaussian approximations even when a smaller than standard bandwidth is used. Second, we employ a debiasing procedure based on generalized jackknifing to enable inference with larger bandwidths, while preserving convexity of the objective function. Third, we construct a novel bootstrap method that adjusts for bandwidth-induced variance distortions, yielding valid inference across a wide range of bandwidth choices. Our proposed inference method enjoys demonstrable more robustness, while retaining the practical appeal of convex pairwise difference estimators.



Keywords:
small bandwidth asymptotics, generalized jackknife, bootstrap, U-process, pairwise comparisons, robust distribution theory.

Introduction

Suppose
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n}
is a random sample from the distribution of a random vector
\mathbf{z}
. This paper studies the large-sample properties of the following
convex
pairwise difference estimator:

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\in\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})K_{h_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{j}),\qquad K_{h}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{h^{d}}K\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}}{h}\right),

(1.1)

where
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a parameter space,
\sum_{i<j}
denotes
\sum_{j=2}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}
,
(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})\mapsto m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})
is a permutation symmetric function,
K
is a symmetric, non-negative kernel,
h_{n}
is a positive bandwidth (or localization) parameter sequence,
\mathbf{w}
is a continuously distributed
d
-dimensional subvector of
\mathbf{z}
, and where
\bm{\theta}\mapsto m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})
is a
convex
function. Pairwise difference estimation, which relies on local comparisons between observation pairs, has been used to address heterogeneity in nonlinear models. See
Powell 1994
,
Honoré and
Powell 2005
, and
Aradillas-Lopez et al. 2007
for overviews, and Section
2
for three motivating examples.

In contrast to classical extremum estimators,
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
is a local
M
-estimator that employs observation pairs
(i,j)
for which
\mathbf{w}_{i}
and
\mathbf{w}_{j}
are similar. The bandwidth
h_{n}
governs the degree of similarity: When
h_{n}\to 0
(as
n\to\infty
), the estimator increasingly focuses on nearly identical-in-
\mathbf{w}
pairs. In turn, focusing on such pairs is natural in settings where identification can be based on the condition
\mathbf{w}_{i}\approx\mathbf{w}_{j}
(combined with smoothness assumptions). The localization introduces a familiar trade-off for estimation and inference: A smaller
h_{n}
reduces bias from dissimilarity between
\mathbf{w}_{i}
and
\mathbf{w}_{j}
, but increases variance due to fewer available usable pairs. As a consequence, the large-sample behavior of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
depends critically on a delicate bias-variance trade-off determined by
h_{n}
. This paper develops novel inference methods for convex pairwise difference estimators that are demonstrably more robust to bandwidth choice than existing methods.

Under regularity conditions and assuming that

nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad nh_{n}^{4}\to 0,

the pairwise difference estimator is asymptotically linear:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\xi}_{0}(\mathbf{z}_{i})+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\rightsquigarrow\mathsf{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbb{E}[\bm{\xi}_{0}(\mathbf{z})\bm{\xi}_{0}(\mathbf{z})^{\prime}]),

(1.2)

where
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is the estimand and
\bm{\xi}_{0}(\cdot)
is the influence function (whose exact form is given below).
The condition
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
lower bounds the level of localization
h_{n}
allowed for, while the condition
nh_{n}^{4}\to 0
upper bounds the level of localization. The purpose of the latter condition is to control a smoothing bias term. The bias condition
nh_{n}^{4}\to 0
could be replaced by the weaker condition
nh_{n}^{2L}\to 0
if a (higher-order) kernel of order
L>2
were used, but a higher-order kernel annihilates the convexity of the objective function because higher-order kernels take negative values.

The main results of this paper are obtained by combining three ideas:

1.

Small Bandwidth Asymptotics
. Utilizing the framework introduced by
Cattaneo
et al. 2014a
, we establish a more robust Gaussian distributional approximation for the pairwise difference estimator that allows for higher levels of localization by remaining valid even when the condition
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
is violated. This generalized distributional approximation shows that, while the localization restriction
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
is necessary for establishing asymptotic linearity, a Gaussian approximation can hold under the substantially weaker condition
n^{2}h_{n}^{d}\to\infty
, albeit with a convergence rate and large sample variance that depends explicitly on the level of localization used.

2.

Debiasing
. Following
Honoré and
Powell 2005
we debias the pairwise difference estimator using the method of
generalized jackknifing
introduced by
Schucany and
Sommers 1977
. Doing so allows for (larger) bandwidths that violate the bias condition
nh_{n}^{4}\to\infty
. This debiasing approach retains the convexity of the objective function, which is attractive for both theoretical (weaker regularity conditions) and practical (faster computation) reasons. The debiasing procedure combines linearly a collection of convex pairwise difference estimators constructed using different levels of localization. The resulting ensembling-based pairwise difference estimator admits a small bandwidth Gaussian approximation with an associated bias condition of the form
nh_{n}^{2L}\to 0
, where
L\geq 2
denotes the order of a certain (equivalent) kernel induced by the debiasing procedure.

3.

Bootstrapping
. Building on insights in
Cattaneo
et al. 2014b
, we develop a valid bootstrap-based distributional approximation for the debiased pairwise difference estimator rescaling the localization parameter. The nonparametric bootstrap distributional approximation exhibits a mismatch in its asymptotic variance under small bandwidth asymptotics. The mismatch is characterized by a known multiplicative factor involving the localization parameter
h_{n}
. As a result, bootstrapping the (debiased) pairwise difference estimator with a different localization parameter (namely,
3^{1/d}h_{n}
rather than
h_{n}
) leads to a valid bootstrap-based inference procedure also under small bandwidth asymptotics.

In combination, these three ideas enable us to offer a novel resampling-based inference method for (convex) pairwise difference estimators that are demonstrably more robust to a wider set of choices of the localization parameter
h_{n}
.

Our theoretical work is carefully developed to retain and leverage convexity of the objective function defining the pairwise difference estimator. This feature not only allows for fast implementation of the estimator and resampling-based methods, but also enables us to proceed under relatively weak conditions when obtaining theoretical results. When developing our theoretical results, we rely heavily on the foundational work of
Hjort and
Pollard 1993
and
Pollard 1991
, which we apply to the case of
U
-processes.

2
Motivating Examples

We use three examples to motivate and illustrate our work. The first example involves an estimator that can be written in closed form (because it has a quadratic-in-
\bm{\theta}
function
m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})
), while the other two examples do not. The second example has a smooth-in-
\bm{\theta}
function
m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})
, while the third example does not. All three examples have convex-in-
\bm{\theta}
functions
m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})
and employ the following notation:
\mathbf{z}_{i}=(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\prime})^{\prime}
, with
y_{i}
a scalar outcome variable,
\mathbf{x}_{i}
a
k
-dimensional covariate, and
\mathbf{w}_{i}
a
d
-dimensional covariate. For more details on the examples, see
Powell 1994
,
Honoré and
Powell 2005
, and
Aradillas-Lopez et al. 2007
.

2.1
Partially Linear Regression Model

The partially linear regression model studied here is of the form

y_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}_{0}+\gamma_{0}(\mathbf{w}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},

where
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is the parameter of interest,
\gamma_{0}(\cdot)
is an unknown function, and where
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{i}]=0
. Defining
\dot{y}_{i,j}=y_{i}-y_{j}
and
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}=\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}
, a pairwise difference estimator of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
can be based on

m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=m_{\mathtt{PLR}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}(\dot{y}_{i,j}-\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta})^{2}.

Setting
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the minimization problem defining the estimator admits a closed form solution (provided that a non-negative kernel function is used), namely

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\left(\sum_{i<j}\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}K_{h_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{j})\right)^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}\dot{y}_{i,j}K_{h_{n}}(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{j}).

2.2
Partially Linear Logit Model

The partially linear logit model studied here is of the form

y_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}_{0}+\gamma_{0}(\mathbf{w}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}\geq 0\},

where
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is the parameter of interest,
\gamma_{0}(\cdot)
is an unknown function, and where

\mathbb{P}\big[\varepsilon_{i}\leq u|\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{i}\big]=\Lambda(u),\qquad\Lambda(u)=\frac{\exp(u)}{1+\exp(u)}.

The parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
can be estimated using a pairwise difference estimator with
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{k}
and

m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=m_{\mathtt{PLL}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=-\mathbbm{1}\{\dot{y}_{i,j}\neq 0\}\left(y_{i}\ln\Lambda(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta})+y_{j}\ln\Lambda(-\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta})\right).

The minimization problem defining the estimator does not admit a closed form solution, but (provided that a non-negative kernel function is used) it is convex because
u\mapsto-\ln\Lambda(u)
is.

2.3
Partially Linear Tobit Model

The partially linear censored regression model studied here is of the form

y_{i}=\max\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}_{0}+\gamma_{0}(\mathbf{w}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},0\},

where
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is the parameter of interest,
\gamma_{0}(\cdot)
is an unknown function,
\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}\varepsilon_{i}|\mathbf{w}_{i}
, and where the conditional distribution of
\varepsilon_{i}
given
\mathbf{w}_{i}
admits a Lebesgue density. A pairwise difference estimator of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
can be obtained by setting
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{k}
and employing

m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=m_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=\tilde{m}_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})-\tilde{m}_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{0}),

where

\tilde{m}_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=\begin{cases}|y_{i}|-\big(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}+y_{j}\big)\sgn(y_{i})&\text{if }\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}\leq-y_{j}\\
\big|\dot{y}_{i,j}-\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}\big|&\text{if }-y_{j}<\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}<y_{i}\\
|y_{j}|+\big(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}-y_{i}\big)\sgn(y_{j})&\text{if }y_{i}\leq\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}\end{cases}.

Because
\tilde{m}_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{0})
does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
, the presence of
\tilde{m}_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{0})
in
m_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})
does not affect the minimization problem defining the estimator. Nevertheless, it is theoretically attractive to work with
m_{\mathtt{PLT}}
rather than
\tilde{m}_{\mathtt{PLT}}
, as doing so allows for weaker regularity conditions for the existence of the expectation of the objective function.

For future reference, we note that
m_{\mathtt{PLT}}
admits the alternative representation

m_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=\begin{cases}\big|\dot{y}_{i,j}-\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}\big|-\big|\dot{y}_{i,j}\big|&\text{ if }y_{i}>0,y_{j}>0\\
\max\{y_{i}-\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta},0\}-y_{i}&\text{ if }y_{i}>0,y_{j}=0\\
\max\{y_{j}+\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta},0\}-y_{j}&\text{ if }y_{i}=0,y_{j}>0\\
0&\text{ if }y_{i}=0,y_{j}=0\end{cases}.

The function
\bm{\theta}\mapsto m_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})
is convex and therefore so is the minimization problem defining the estimator (provided that a non-negative kernel function is used).

3
Distributional Approximation and Bootstrap Inference

As is standard in the literature, we generalize (
1.1
) slightly and define our estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(h_{n})
to be any approximate minimizer of
\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta};h_{n})
, where

\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta};h)=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})K_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{j}).

To be specific, we require

\widehat{M}_{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(h);h)\leq\inf_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta};h)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1}).

The objective function
\widehat{M}_{n}
is a sample counterpart of the function
M
given by

M(\bm{\theta};h)=\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta};h)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})K_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2})\big].

Under regularity conditions, this function approximates, as
h\downarrow 0
, a function
M_{0}
, which (does not depend on
K
and) admits a unique minimizer, namely the parameter of interest
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

For the purposes of analyzing
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
it is convenient to define
\bm{\theta}_{n}=\bm{\theta}(h_{n})
, where

\bm{\theta}(h)\in\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}M(\bm{\theta};h)

is interpretable as a (fixed-
h
) “pseudo” parameter. With the help of
\bm{\theta}_{n}
we can decompose the estimation error
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
into a (non-stochastic) “bias” component
\bm{\theta}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
and a “noise” component
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{n}
. Each component can be analyzed separately and in both cases the analysis will leverage convexity.

3.1
Regularity Conditions

The following assumption guarantees, among other things, that
\bm{\theta}_{n}
is well defined for large
n
and that the bias component
\bm{\theta}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
vanishes asymptotically; for details, see Lemma
1
of Section
4.1
.

Assumption 1
.

1.

The kernel function
K
is a symmetric, bounded probability density.

2.

\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
is convex,
(\mathbf{z},\bar{\mathbf{z}})\mapsto m(\mathbf{z},\bar{\mathbf{z}};\bm{\theta})
is permutation symmetric, and
\bm{\theta}\mapsto m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})
is convex with probability one.

3.

The distribution of
\mathbf{w}
admits a Lebesgue density
f_{\mathbf{w}}
, which is bounded and continuous on its support
\mathcal{W}
.

4.

For each
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
,

\mathbb{E}[m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}[m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{v}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{v})\right]<\infty

and (with probability one)

\lim_{\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{0}}\mathbb{E}[m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u}]=\mathbb{E}[m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}].

5.

On
\Theta
, the function
M_{0}
given by

M_{0}(\bm{\theta})=\int_{\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}[m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w})^{2}d\mathbf{w}

is uniquely minimized at an interior point
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

The next assumption enables us to analyze the asymptotic properties of the noise component
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{n}
. To accommodate examples (such as the partially linear Tobit model) where
\bm{\theta}\mapsto m(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j},\bm{\theta})
is not fully differentiable, we assume the existence of derivative-like functions
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{j};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
such that, for any direction
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the (remainder) terms

r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)=\frac{m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta}+\mathbf{t}\tau)-m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})}{\tau}-\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})^{\prime}\mathbf{t}

and

R_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)|\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}]}{\tau}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t}

are suitably small for
\bm{\theta}
near
\bm{\theta}_{0}
,
\tau>0
near zero, and
\mathbf{w}_{1}\approx\mathbf{w}_{2}
. As further discussed below, functions
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{H}
satisfying the following assumption exist (and are relatively easy to find) in each of our motivating examples.

Assumption 2
.

1.

For each
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, there is some
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tau\in(0,\delta),\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|<\delta,\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{W}}\big|\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)|\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}]\big|f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w}_{2})^{2}\right]<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tau\in(0,\delta),\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|<\delta,\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)^{2}|\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w}_{2})\right]<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tau\in(0,\delta),\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|<\delta,\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{W}}\left|R_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)\right|f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w}_{2})\right]<\infty,

and (with probability one)

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0,(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{u})\to(\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{0})}\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)|\mathbf{z}_{1}=\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u}]=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0,(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{u})\to(\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{0})}\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)^{2}|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u}]=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0,(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{u})\to(\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{0})}\mathbb{E}[R_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u}]=0.

2.

There is some
\delta>0
and some function
b
with

\sup_{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|<\delta}\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})\|\leq b(\mathbf{z}_{1})b(\mathbf{z}_{2}),

such that

\mathbb{E}[b(\mathbf{z})^{4}]+\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}[b(\mathbf{z})^{4}|\mathbf{w}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w})<\infty

and

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|<\delta,\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{W}}\|\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})\|f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w}_{2})\right]<\infty\qquad\text{for each }\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

3.

There exist functions
\mathbf{G}_{0},\bm{\xi}_{0}
, and
\bm{\Xi}_{0}
such that, for each
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
(and with probability one),

\lim_{(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{u})\to(\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{G}_{0}(\mathbf{w}),

\lim_{(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{u})\to(\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{0})}2\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})|\mathbf{z}_{1}=\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w})=\bm{\xi}_{0}(\mathbf{z}),

and

\lim_{(\bm{\theta},\bar{\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{u})\to(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{0})}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bar{\bm{\theta}})^{\prime}|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w})=\bm{\Xi}_{0}(\mathbf{w}).

4.

\bm{\Gamma}_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{0}(\mathbf{w})],\bm{\Sigma}_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\xi}_{0}(\mathbf{z})\bm{\xi}_{0}(\mathbf{z})^{\prime}]
, and
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\Xi}_{0}(\mathbf{w})]
are positive definite.

3.2
Small Bandwidth Asymptotics

Defining

\mathbf{V}_{n}=\mathbf{V}_{n}(h_{n})=\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\left[n^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}+\binom{n}{2}^{-1}h_{n}^{-d}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(K)\right]\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1},\qquad\bm{\Delta}_{0}(K)=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\Xi}_{0}(\mathbf{w})]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K^{2}(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u},

and letting
\Phi_{k}
denote the distribution function of a
k
-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector, we have the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. If
n^{2}h_{n}^{d}\to\infty
and if
h_{n}\to 0
, then

\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbf{V}_{n}^{-1/2}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{n})\leq\mathbf{t}\right]-\Phi_{k}(\mathbf{t})\right|\to 0.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
, the convergence rate of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{n}
equals

\rho_{n}=\sqrt{\min\left(n,\binom{n}{2}h_{n}^{d}\right)},

the magnitude of
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{-1/2}
. Provided that the bias is “small” in the sense that
\|\bm{\theta}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|=o(\rho_{n}^{-1})
, Theorem
1
therefore encompasses the following three distinct large-sample regimes:

•

Asymptotic Linearity
: If
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
, then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})
satisfies (
1.2
). In particular,
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})
convergences in law to a mean-zero Gaussian distribution with asymptotic variance

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathbf{V}_{n}(h_{n})=\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}.

•

Root-
n
Consistency without Asymptotic Linearity
: If
nh_{n}^{d}\to 2c\in(0,\infty)
, then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})
is not asymptotically linear, but convergences in law to a mean-zero Gaussian distribution with asymptotic variance

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathbf{V}_{n}(h_{n})=\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\left[\bm{\Sigma}_{0}+\frac{1}{c}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(K)\right]\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}.

•

Slower than Root-n Consistency
: If
nh_{n}^{d}\to 0
(but
n^{2}h_{n}^{d}\to\infty
), then
\sqrt{n^{2}h^{d}/2}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})
converges weekly to a mean-zero Gaussian distribution with asymptotic variance

\lim_{n\to\infty}\binom{n}{2}h_{n}^{d}\mathbf{V}_{n}(h_{n})=\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(K)\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}.

The small bandwidth component (i.e., the term involving
\bm{\Delta}_{0}(K)
) in
\mathbf{V}_{n}
captures the additional uncertainty generated from increasing the localization of the observations pairs. Incorporating this component in the approximate variance is key to enabling us to replace the condition
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
by the weaker condition
n^{2}h_{n}^{d}\to\infty
when obtaining a Gaussian approximation. As demonstrated by
Cattaneo
et al. 2025a
, incorporating the small bandwidth component can furthermore lead to a higher-order corrected distributional approximation even under asymptotic linearity.

3.3
Debiasing

In Theorem
1
, we centered the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(h_{n})
at
\bm{\theta}_{n}=\bm{\theta}(h_{n})
to circumvent bias issues. This section focuses on the bias term
\bm{\theta}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
and introduces an automatic debiasing approach under the assumption that
\bm{\theta}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
can be expanded in even powers of
h_{n}
. To be specific, we follow
Honoré and
Powell 2005
and dicuss debiasing under the following high-level condition.

Assumption 3
.

For some even
L\geq 0
,
\bm{\theta}(\cdot)
admits
\mathbf{b}_{2l}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
(for
l=1,\dots,L/2
) such that

\bm{\theta}(h)-\bm{\theta}_{0}=\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\mathbf{b}_{2l}h^{2l}+o(h^{L})\qquad\text{as }h\downarrow 0.

The ease with which Assumption
3
can be verified depends on the magnitude of
L
. For instance, Assumption
1
implies that Assumption
3
holds with
L=0
. Under additional smoothness conditions and using symmetry of
K
, the following result gives conditions under which Assumption
3
holds with
L=2
. When stating the result, we employ standard multi-index notation: for
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
,
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a sufficiently smooth-in-
\mathbf{v}
function
f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}),

\partial_{\mathbf{v}}^{\bm{\alpha}}f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})=\frac{\partial^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}{\partial v_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial v_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}),\qquad|\bm{\alpha}|=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
2
hold and that

1.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}K(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}<\infty
, and

2.

With probability one,
\mathbf{v}\mapsto\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta}_{0})|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{v}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{v})
is twice continuously differentiable with
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\|\partial_{\mathbf{v}}^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})\|]<\infty
for all
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
with
|\bm{\alpha}|\leq 2
.

Then
\bm{\theta}(\cdot)
admits a
\mathbf{b}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\bm{\theta}(h)-\bm{\theta}_{0}=\mathbf{b}_{2}h^{2}+o(h^{2})\qquad\text{ as }h\downarrow 0.

The proof of Proposition
1
leverages convexity and may therefore be of independent interest. The convexity argument in question can furthermore be adapted to form the basis of a verification by induction of Assumption
3
with
L>2
. Details are provided in Section
4.4
, which describes the induction step for general
L
and states explicit (smoothness) conditions under which Assumption
3
holds with
L=4
.

To describe the debiasing procedure based on generalized jackknifing, we maintain Assumption
3
, define
c_{0}=1
, and let
\mathbf{c}=(c_{0},\dots,c_{L/2})^{\prime}
be a vector of (distinct) positive constants such that the vector

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{0}(\mathbf{c})\\
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{c})\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{L/2}(\mathbf{c})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\
1&c_{1}^{2}&\dots&c_{L/2}^{2}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
1&c_{1}^{L}&\dots&c_{L/2}^{L}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}

is well defined. The debiased estimator is

\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(\mathbf{c},h_{n})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(h_{n,l}),\qquad h_{n,l}=c_{l}h_{n},

the construction of which involves solving
L/2+1
convex optimization problems. As defined, the debiased estimator is a generalization of the original pairwise difference estimator because if
L=0
, then
\mathbf{c}=1=\lambda_{0}
and therefore
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
.

The next theorem generalizes Theorem
1
by establishing the small bandwidth Gaussian approximation for
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
. To state the theorem, let

\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(\mathbf{c},h_{n})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\bm{\theta}(h_{n,l})

and

\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}=\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}(\mathbf{c},h_{n})=\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\left[n^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}+\binom{n}{2}^{-1}h_{n}^{-d}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})\right]\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1},\qquad\bar{K}(\mathbf{u})=\bar{K}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{c})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})K_{c_{l}}(\mathbf{u}).

As they should, the expressions have the feature that if
L=0
, then
\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\bm{\theta}_{n}
and
\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}=\mathbf{V}_{n}
. Another noteworthy feature of the expressions is that debiasing via generalized jackknifing affects the variance
\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}
only through the kernel shape entering its small bandwidth component.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. If
n^{2}h_{n}^{d}\to\infty
and if
h_{n}\to 0
, then

\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left[\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{-1/2}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\leq\mathbf{t}\right]-\Phi_{k}(\mathbf{t})\right|\to 0.

As a consequence, if also Assumption
3
holds and if
nh_{n}^{2L}\to 0
, then

\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left[\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{-1/2}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})\leq\mathbf{t}\right]-\Phi_{k}(\mathbf{t})\right|\to 0

The magnitude of
\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{-1/2}
is the same as that of
\mathbf{V}_{n}^{-1/2}
. As a consequence, with obvious modifications the discussion of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
following Theorem
1
applies to
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
, the only noteworthy difference being that (by design), the relevant “small bias” condition is different (and typically milder) in the case of
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
.

It is worth noting that the equivalent kernel
\bar{K}
is of higher order, even though the debiased estimator
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
only employs estimators constructed using second-order kernels, hereby retaining the desired convexity for implementation. To be specific, if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{L+2}K(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}<\infty
, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{K}(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{c_{l}}(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})=1

and, for
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{+}
with
0<|\bm{\alpha}|\leq L+1
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{u}^{\bm{\alpha}}\bar{K}(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{u}^{\bm{\alpha}}K_{c_{l}}(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})c_{l}^{|\bm{\alpha}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{v}^{\bm{\alpha}}K(\mathbf{v})d\mathbf{v}=0

where the last equality uses the defining property of
\{\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\}
and symmetry of
K
, and where
\mathbf{u}^{\bm{\alpha}}
denotes
\prod_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
for
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other words,
\bar{K}
is of order
L+2
.

3.4
Bootstrapping

To develop feasible inference procedures that do not require (explicit) estimation of
\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}
, we consider nonparametric bootstrap-based approximations to the distribution of
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
. Since
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
when
L=0
, results for
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
can be extracted by setting
L=0
in what follows.

Letting
\mathbf{z}^{*}_{1,n},\dots,\mathbf{z}^{*}_{n,n}
denote a random sample from the empirical distribution of
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n}
, the defining property of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}(h)
, the nonparametric bootstrap analogue of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(h)
, is the following:

\widehat{M}_{n}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}(h);h)\leq\inf_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\widehat{M}_{n}^{*}(\bm{\theta};h)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1}),

where

\widehat{M}_{n}^{*}(\bm{\theta};h)=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}m(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n};\bm{\theta})K_{h}(\mathbf{w}^{*}_{i,n}-\mathbf{w}^{*}_{j,n}).

Similarly, the nonparametric bootstrap analogue of
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
is

\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}=\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{c})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}(h_{n,l}).

The following theorem characterizes the large sample properties of
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}
, the bootstrap counterpart of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{n}
. In perfect analogy with the results in
Cattaneo
et al. 2014b
, we find that the bootstrap distribution estimator is consistent only when
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
, but otherwise exhibits a variance inflation making the distributional approximation inconsistent. To state the result, let
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}[\cdot]
denote
\mathbb{P}[\cdot|\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n}]
, let
\to_{\mathbb{P}}
denote convergence in probability, and define

\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{*}=\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{c},h_{n})=\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\left[n^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}+3\binom{n}{2}^{-1}h_{n}^{-d}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})\right]\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
2
hold and that, for
\bm{\theta}
near
\bm{\theta}_{0}
,
m(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z};\bm{\theta})=0
and
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z};\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{0}
(with probability one). If
n^{2}h_{n}^{d}\to\infty
and if
h_{n}\to 0
, then

\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left|\mathbb{P}^{*}\left[\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{*-1/2}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})\leq\mathbf{t}\right]-\Phi_{k}(\mathbf{t})\right|\to_{\mathbb{P}}0.

Because
\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{*}\to\mathbf{I}_{k}
if and only if
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
(where
\mathbf{I}_{k}
denotes the
k
-dimensional identity matrix), under the assumptions of Theorem
3

\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left|\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\leq\mathbf{t}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\leq\mathbf{t}\right]\right|\to_{\mathbb{P}}0

if and only if
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
. In particular, if
\liminf_{n\to\infty}nh_{n}^{d}<\infty
, then the nonparametric bootstrap is inconsistent, albeit conservative in the sense that the (approximate) variance under the bootstrap distribution is larger than the (approximate) variance of the asymptotic distribution:
\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}^{*}>\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{n}
in a positive definite sense.

The variance inflation problem associated with the nonparametric bootstrap under the small bandwidth regime can be easily fixed by appropriately rescaling the bandwidth used for the bootstrap implementation of the pairwise estimator: employing

\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}=\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{c},h_{n})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}(3^{1/d}h_{n,l}).

and centering its distribution at

\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(\mathbf{c},h_{n})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(3^{1/d}h_{n,l}).

automatically adjusts the bootstrap variance, leading to a consistent distributional approximation. Indeed, the following result is an immediate consequence of Theorems
2
and
3
.

Corollary 1
.

If the assumptions of Theorem
3
hold, then

\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left|\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}\left[\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}-\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\leq\mathbf{t}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\leq\mathbf{t}\right]\right|\to_{\mathbb{P}}0.

As a consequence, if also Assumption
3
holds and if
nh_{n}^{2L}\to 0
, then

\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left|\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}\left[\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}-\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\leq\mathbf{t}\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\leq\mathbf{t}\right]\right|\to_{\mathbb{P}}0.

The statement of Corollary
1
emphasizes the rate-adaptive nature of the consistency property enjoyed by the bootstrap distributional approximation. The result has immediate implications for robust inference. For example, letting
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a fixed vector, and using the “percentile method”
(
van der
Vaart 1998
, in the terminology of)
, the (nominal) level
1-\alpha
bootstrap confidence interval for
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}_{0}
is

\breve{\mathsf{CI}}^{*}_{n}(1-\alpha)=\left[\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\breve{q}^{*}_{1-\alpha/2,n}~,~\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\breve{q}^{*}_{\alpha/2,n}\right],\qquad\breve{q}^{*}_{t,n}=\inf\left\{q\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}[\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{*}-\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\breve{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\leq q]\geq t\right\}.

If Assumptions
1
-
3
hold and if
n^{2}h_{n}^{d}\to\infty
and
nh_{n}^{2L}\to 0
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\breve{\mathsf{CI}}^{*}_{n}(1-\alpha)\right]=1-\alpha.

Proofs and Other Technical Results

4.1
A Useful Lemma

The following lemma is used in the proofs of Theorems
1
-
3
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds. Then
\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}M(\bm{\theta};h)
is non-empty for
h>0
near zero and

\bm{\theta}(h)-\bm{\theta}_{0}=o(1)\qquad\text{as }h\downarrow 0.

If also Assumption
2
holds, then
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta}(h))K_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2})]=\mathbf{0}
for
h>0
near zero.

Proof.

For every
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle M(\bm{\theta};h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{W}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}h]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w})f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}h)K(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}d\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\to\int_{\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}[m(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w})^{2}d\mathbf{w}=M_{0}(\bm{\theta})\qquad\text{as }h\downarrow 0,

the convergence being uniform on compact subsets of
\Theta
because
\bm{\theta}\mapsto M(\bm{\theta};h)
is convex
(e.g.,
Hjort and
Pollard 1993
, Lemma 1)
.

Take any
\epsilon>0
with
\Theta^{\epsilon}_{0}=\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|\leq\epsilon\}\subseteq\Theta
. By the preceding paragraph,

\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\epsilon}_{0}}|M(\bm{\theta};h)-M_{0}(\bm{\theta})|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\inf_{\bm{\theta}:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|=\epsilon}M_{0}(\bm{\theta})-M_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\right)

for
h>0
near zero. For any such
h
and any
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\setminus\Theta^{\epsilon}_{0}
, we have

\eta=\frac{\epsilon}{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|}\in(0,1),

and therefore, by convexity of
\bm{\theta}\mapsto M(\bm{\theta};h)
,

M(\eta\bm{\theta}+(1-\eta)\bm{\theta}_{0};h)\leq\eta M(\bm{\theta};h)+(1-\eta)M(\bm{\theta}_{0};h),

which rearranges as

M(\bm{\theta};h)-M(\bm{\theta}_{0};h)\geq\frac{1}{\eta}[M(\eta\bm{\theta}+(1-\eta)\bm{\theta}_{0};h)-M(\bm{\theta}_{0};h)]\geq 0.

As a consequence,

\inf_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}M(\bm{\theta};h)=\inf_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\epsilon}_{0}}M(\bm{\theta};h)=\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta^{\epsilon}_{0}}M(\bm{\theta};h),

where the last equality uses continuity of
\bm{\theta}\mapsto M(\bm{\theta};h)
and compactness of
\Theta^{\epsilon}_{0}
.

The above argument shows in particular that
\bm{\theta}(h)\in\Theta^{\epsilon}_{0}
for
h>0
near zero.

If also Assumption
2
holds, then, for
\bm{\theta}
near
\bm{\theta}_{0}
,
h>0
near zero, and for any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{M(\bm{\theta}+\mathbf{t}\tau;h)-M(\bm{\theta};h)}{\tau}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})K_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2})]^{\prime}\mathbf{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)K_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2})]\right|

\displaystyle\to 0\qquad\text{as }\tau\downarrow 0,

implying that for
h>0
near zero,
\bm{\theta}\mapsto M(\bm{\theta};h)
is (directionally) differentiable near
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, the directional derivative
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})K_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2})]^{\prime}\mathbf{t}
being zero when
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}(h)
because
\bm{\theta}(h)
minimizes
M(\bm{\theta};h).

∎

4.2
Proof of Theorems
1
and
2

Theorem
1
can be obtained from Theorem
2
by setting
L=0
in the latter, so it suffices to prove Theorem
2
. To do so, for
l\in\{0,\dots,L/2\}
, let
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n,l}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}(h_{n,l}),\bm{\theta}_{n,l}=\bm{\theta}(h_{n,l}),
and

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\mathbf{s}^{\mu}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j}),\qquad\mathbf{s}^{\mu}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})=\mathbf{s}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2})],

where

\mathbf{s}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})=\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta}_{n,l})K_{h_{n,l}}(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{j}).

By Lemma
1
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bm{\theta}_{n,l}=\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})]=0
for large
n
.

Suppose that

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{n,l}-\bm{\theta}_{n,l}=-\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(\rho_{n}^{-1})\quad\text{for}\quad l\in\{0,\dots,L/2\}.

(4.1)

Then

\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=-\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{n}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(\rho_{n}^{-1}),

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}

satisfies

\left[n^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}+\binom{n}{2}^{-1}h_{n}^{-d}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})\right]^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{n}\leadsto\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{k\times 1},\mathbf{I}_{k})

(4.2)

because, letting
\sum_{i}
denote
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
,

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{n}=\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{n}+\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{n},

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{i}),\qquad\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{i})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}^{\mu}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})|\mathbf{z}_{i}]\qquad(j\neq i),

and

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{n}=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j}),\qquad\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\big[\mathbf{s}^{\mu}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})-\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{i})-\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{j})\big],

satisfy

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sqrt{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{n}\\
\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}h_{n}^{d}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{n}\end{array}\right)\leadsto\mathcal{N}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}_{k\times 1}\\
\mathbf{0}_{k\times 1}\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}&\mathbf{0}_{k\times k}\\
\mathbf{0}_{k\times k}&\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})\end{array}\right]\right),

as can be shown by means of the Cramér-Wold device and the central limit theorem of
Heyde and
Brown 1970
, the latter being applicable because it follows from routine calculations that for every
\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have

\varsigma_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i}\mathbb{V}[g_{i,n}]=\bm{\mu}_{1}^{\prime}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\bm{\mu}_{1}+\bm{\mu}_{2}^{\prime}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})\bm{\mu}_{2}+o(1),

\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[g_{i,n}^{4}]=o(1),

and

\mathbb{V}\left[\sum_{i}\varsigma_{i,n}^{2}-\varsigma_{n}^{2}\right]=o(1),\qquad\varsigma_{i,n}^{2}=\mathbb{V}[g_{i,n}|\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{i-1}],

where

g_{i,n}=g_{i,n}(\bm{\mu})=\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\bm{\mu}_{1}^{\prime}\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{i})+\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}^{-1}h_{n}^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\bm{\mu}_{2}^{\prime}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j}).

The proof of Theorem
2
can therefore be completed by verifying (
4.1
).

To do so, we leverage convexity. For any
l\in\{0,\dots,L/2\}
and any
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, it can be shown that

\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0,h\downarrow 0,\bm{\theta}\to\bm{\theta}_{0}}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)K_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2})|\mathbf{z}_{1}]^{2}\big]=0

and

\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0,h\downarrow 0,\bm{\theta}\to\bm{\theta}_{0}}h^{d}\mathbb{E}[r_{\mathbf{t}}(\bm{\theta},\tau)^{2}K_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{2})^{2}]=0,

and it therefore follows from a Hoeffding decomposition that

\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{2}\Big[\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})-\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l};h_{n,l})\Big]

\displaystyle=\rho_{n}^{2}[M(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})-M(\bm{\theta}_{n,l};h_{n,l})]+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\rho_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Moreover,

\rho_{n}^{2}[M(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})-M(\bm{\theta}_{n,l};h_{n,l})]\to\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\Gamma}_{0}\mathbf{t},

and proceeding as in the proof of (
4.2
) it can be shown that
\rho_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Because
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}
is positive definite and because
\mathbf{t}\mapsto\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})
is convex (almost surely), the corollary following
Hjort and
Pollard 1993
implies that (
4.1
) holds.

4.3
Proof of Theorem
3

The proof of Theorem
3
is a natural bootstrap analog of the proof of Theorem
2
.

For
l\in\{0,\dots,L/2\}
, let
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{n,l}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{n}(h_{n,l})
and

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n,l}=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\mathbf{s}^{\mu,*}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n}),\qquad\mathbf{s}^{\mu,*}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n})=\mathbf{s}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n})-\mathbb{E}^{*}_{n}[\mathbf{s}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{1,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{2,n})],

where
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{n}[\cdot]
denotes
\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n}]
.

It suffices to show that

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{n,l}-\bm{\theta}_{n,l}=-\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n,l}+\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}\big)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(\rho_{n}^{-1})\quad\text{for}\quad l\in\{0,\dots,L/2\}

(4.3)

and that

\left[n^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}+3\binom{n}{2}^{-1}h_{n}^{-d}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(K)\right]^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n}\leadsto_{\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{k\times 1},\mathbf{I}_{k}),

(4.4)

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n,l},

and where
\leadsto_{\mathbb{P}}
denotes weak convergence in probability.

For every
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, using a Hoeffding decomposition and the fact that
m(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z};\bm{\theta}_{n,l})=0
and
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z};\bm{\theta}_{n,l})=\bm{0}
for large
n
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{2}\Big[\widehat{M}^{*}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})-\widehat{M}^{*}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l};h_{n,l})\Big]

\displaystyle=\rho_{n}^{2}(1+o(1))[\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})-\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l};h_{n,l})]+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\rho_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n,l}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where it can be shown that
\rho_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n,l}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and where it follows from the proof of (
4.1
) that

\rho_{n}^{2}\Big[\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})-\widehat{M}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l};h_{n,l})\Big]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\Gamma}_{0}\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\rho_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where
\rho_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. In other words,

\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{2}\Big[\widehat{M}^{*}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})-\widehat{M}^{*}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l};h_{n,l})\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\Gamma}_{0}\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\rho_{n}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n,l}+\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{n,l}\big)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\qquad\text{for every }\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Because
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}
is positive definite and because
\mathbf{t}\mapsto\widehat{M}^{*}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{n,l}+\mathbf{t}\rho_{n}^{-1};h_{n,l})
is convex (almost surely), the corollary following
Hjort and
Pollard 1993
implies that (
4.3
) holds.

To prove (
4.4
), we begin by decomposing
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n}
as

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{n}=\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{n}+\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*}_{n},

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n}),\qquad\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\mathbb{E}^{*}_{n}[\mathbf{s}^{\mu,*}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n})|\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n}]\qquad(j\neq i),

and

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*}_{n}=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n}),\qquad\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n})=\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{c})\big[\mathbf{s}^{\mu,*}_{n,l}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n})-\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n})-\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n})\big].

Defining

\pi_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{nh_{n}^{d}}}{1+\sqrt{nh_{n}^{d}}},

routine calculations can be used to show that for every
\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have

\widehat{\varsigma}_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i}\mathbb{V}^{*}_{n}[g^{*}_{i,n}]=\bm{\mu}_{1}^{\prime}\big[\pi_{n}^{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}+4(1-\pi_{n})^{2}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})\big]\bm{\mu}_{1}+\bm{\mu}_{2}^{\prime}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})\bm{\mu}_{2}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}^{*}_{n}[g^{*4}_{i,n}]=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

and

\mathbb{V}\left[\sum_{i}\widehat{\varsigma}_{i,n}^{2}-\widehat{\varsigma}_{n}^{2}\right]=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),\qquad\widehat{\varsigma}_{i,n}^{2}=\mathbb{V}^{*}_{n}[g^{*}_{i,n}|\mathbf{z}^{*}_{1,n},\dots,\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i-1,n}],

where
\mathbb{V}^{*}_{n}[\cdot]
denotes
\mathbb{V}[\cdot|\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n}]
and where

g^{*}_{i,n}=g^{*}_{i,n}(\bm{\mu})=\frac{\pi_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}2\bm{\mu}_{1}^{\prime}\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n})+\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}^{-1}h_{n}^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\bm{\mu}_{2}^{\prime}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{z}^{*}_{i,n},\mathbf{z}^{*}_{j,n}).

The Cramér-Wold device and the central limit theorem of
Heyde and
Brown 1970
therefore imply that if
\pi_{n}\to\pi_{0}\in[0,1]
, then

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sqrt{n}\pi_{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{n}\\
\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}h_{n}^{d}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{*}_{n}\end{array}\right)\leadsto_{\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{N}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}_{k\times 1}\\
\mathbf{0}_{k\times 1}\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\pi_{0}^{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}+4(1-\pi_{0})^{2}\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})&\mathbf{0}_{k\times k}\\
\mathbf{0}_{k\times k}&\bm{\Delta}_{0}(\bar{K})\end{array}\right]\right).

Whether or not
\pi_{n}
is convergent, the result (
4.4
) can be obtained from the preceding display by arguing along subsequences (if necessary).

4.4
Verifying Assumption
3

It follows from Lemma
1
that if Assumption
1
holds, then so does Assumption
3
with
L=0
. This observation provides the base case for an induction argument. To describe the induction step, suppose that for some even
L\geq 0
, we have

\bm{\theta}(h)-\bm{\theta}_{0}=\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\mathbf{b}_{2l}h^{2l}+o(h^{L})\qquad\text{ as }h\downarrow 0.

Suppose also that, for every
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and some
\bm{\beta}_{L+2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have

\displaystyle h^{-2(L+2)}\left[M\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\mathbf{b}_{2l}h^{2l}+\mathbf{t}h^{L+2};h\right)-M\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\mathbf{b}_{2l}h^{2l};h\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\beta}_{L+2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\Gamma}_{0}\mathbf{t}+o(1)\qquad\text{as }h\downarrow 0.

Then, the corollary following
Hjort and
Pollard 1993
implies that

\displaystyle h^{-(L+2)}\left(\bm{\theta}(h)-\bm{\theta}_{0}-\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\mathbf{b}_{2l}h^{2l}\right)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}M\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\mathbf{b}_{2l}h^{2l}+\mathbf{t}h^{L+2};h\right)

\displaystyle=-\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\bm{\beta}_{L+2}+o(1)\qquad\text{ as }h\downarrow 0;

that is, defining
\mathbf{b}_{L+2}=-\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{-1}\bm{\beta}_{L+2}
, we have

\bm{\theta}(h)-\bm{\theta}_{0}=\sum_{l=1}^{(L+2)/2}\mathbf{b}_{2l}h^{2l}+o(h^{L+2})\qquad\text{ as }h\downarrow 0.

To complete the proof of Proposition
1
, it therefore suffices to note that (for every
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and) under the assumptions of the proposition, we have

h^{-4}\left[M(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{t}h^{2};h)-M(\bm{\theta}_{0};h)\right]=\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\beta}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\Gamma}_{0}\mathbf{t}+o(1)\qquad\text{as }h\downarrow 0,

where

\bm{\beta}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathcal{W}}\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})}{\partial v_{i}^{2}}\Big|_{\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}}f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w})d\mathbf{w}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}u_{i}^{2}K(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}.

Similarly, if in addition to the assumptions of Proposition
1
it is assumed that for every
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and for some
\bm{\beta}_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have

h^{-8}\left[M(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{b}_{2}h^{2}+\mathbf{t}h^{4};h)-M(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{b}_{2}h^{2};h)\right]=\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\beta}_{4}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\bm{\Gamma}_{0}\mathbf{t}+o(1)\qquad\text{as }h\downarrow 0,

then Assumption
3
holds with
L=4
. One set of sufficient conditions for this to occur is that Assumptions
1
-
2
hold and that, for every
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the following are satisfied (with probability one):

1.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{4}K(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}<\infty
.

2.

\mathbf{v}\mapsto\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};\bm{\theta}_{0})|\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{v}]f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{v})
is four times continuously differentiable with
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\|\partial_{\mathbf{v}}^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})\|]<\infty
for all
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
with
|\bm{\alpha}|\leq 4
.

3.

f_{\mathbf{w}}
is twice continuously differentiable and
\mathbf{v}\mapsto\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v};\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{t})
is twice continuously differentiable with
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\|\partial_{\mathbf{v}}^{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v};\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{t})f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{v})\|]<\infty
for all
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
with
|\bm{\alpha}|\leq 2
.

4.

For some function
\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
,
\mathbf{v}\mapsto\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v};\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{t})
is continuous,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\|\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v};\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{t})f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{v})\|\right]<\infty,

\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0,(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{u})\to(\bm{\theta}_{0},\mathbf{0})}\left\|\frac{\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u};\bm{\theta}+\tau\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t})-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})}{\tau}-\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{u};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})\right\|=0,

and, for some
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tau\in(0,\delta),\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|<\delta,\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{W}}\left\|\frac{\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2};\bm{\theta}+\tau\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t})-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})}{\tau}-\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2};\bm{\theta},\mathbf{t})\right\|\right]<\infty.

\bm{\theta}

\mathbf{t}

5.3
Partially Linear Tobit Model

We take

, where

\mathbf{s}_{\mathtt{PLT}}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j};\bm{\theta})=\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}\left(\mathbbm{1}\{y_{j}>\max(y_{i}-\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta},0)\}-\mathbbm{1}\{y_{i}>\max(y_{j}+\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i,j}^{\prime}\bm{\theta},0)\}\right)

Conclusion

This paper has developed bandwidth robust distribution theory and bootstrap-based inference procedures for a broad class of convex pairwise difference estimators. Our theoretical work is based on small bandwidth asymptotics and carefully leverages convexity. The theory is illustrated by means of three prominent examples. In addition to expanding the scope of small bandwidth asymptotics, our results lay the groundwork for several promising avenues of future research. First, our methods could be generalized to develop bandwidth selection based on higher-order stochastic expansions. Second, they could be expanded to allow for pairwise difference estimators based on generated regressors, a class of estimators that sometimes arises in the context of control function and related econometric methods. Third, when the objective function is smooth, plug-in variance estimation could be developed as an alternative to bootstrap inference. Finally, our current results do not cover settings where the objective function is sufficiently non-smooth to result in non-Gaussian distributional approximations. We plan to investigate these research directions in upcoming work.
