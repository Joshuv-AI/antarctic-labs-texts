Polyomaviruses and the risk of oral cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tahoora Mousavi, Fatemeh Shokoohy, Mahmood Moosazadeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOral cancer (OC) is the most common malignant tumor of the head and neck (HN) and ranks 16th among the most frequently diagnosed cancers worldwide. A systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to provide an evidence-based analysis of the relationship between polyomaviruses and oral cancer.MethodsThe global online databases was used to identify relevant studies published between January 2000 and September 2024. The quality of each article was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) checklist. Data analysis was performed using STATA Ver. 11 software, and the standard error was calculated using the binomial distribution formula. The heterogeneity of the study results was assessed using the I-square and Q index, while publication bias was examined using the Begg’s test. In addition, a random effects model was used to determine the risk difference (RD), and a forest plot diagram was used to present the results with 95% confidence intervals. The Trim and Fill test was applied to estimate publication bias, and sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall estimate.ResultsOf the nine studies, the relationship between BK virus (BKV) and the Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCV) and oral cancer was investigated. In five primary studies, and by combining the results of these studies, the risk of oral cancer in MCV and BKV-positive individuals was significantly higher than in the negative groups by 13% (RD: 0.13, 95% CI: 0.00, 0.26) and 2% (RD: 0.02, 95% CI: -0.03, 0.07), respectively. In addition, the association between JC virus (JCV) and oral cancer was investigated in six primary studies. By combining the results of these studies, the risk of oral cancer in JCV-positive individuals was 1% higher than that in JCV-negative individuals (RD: 0.01, 95% CI: -0.02, 0.06).DiscussionThis meta-analysis showed that there was a significant association between MCV and oral cancer, and the risk of oral cancer in MCV- positive individuals was 13% higher than that in MCV- negative individuals. More preliminary studies are needed on the association between BKV and JCV and the risk of oral cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05330-2.Keywords: Polyomavirus, Merkel cell polyomavirus, Mouth Neoplasms, BK Virus, and JC Virus, Simian virus 40, Human polyomavirus 6

Introduction

Oral cancer (OC) is the most common head and neck (HN) malignancy and the sixth most common cancer worldwide [1]. The oral cavity contains the buccal mucosa, gingiva, oral tongue, floor of the mouth, and palate [2]. Each of these locations may present distinct risks when exposed to viral infections, requiring careful consideration of the impact of these infections on specific areas of the oral cavity [3]. The process of oral oncogenesis is complex, involving multiple factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, dietary influences, and genetic predisposition [4].

The family of human polyomaviruses consists of five recognized members. The JCV and the BKV, the first two identified in 1971, are known to infect immunocompromised individuals, resulting in atypical clinical manifestations [5, 6]. The BKV, also known as Human polyomavirus 1, is transmitted primarily by the respiratory or the fecal-oral route during childhood. After the initial infection, it resides in mononuclear cells of the blood and the kidneys of the urinary tract. BKV remains latent and is excreted in the urine, causing asymptomatic infection [7]. JCV, or Human Polyomavirus 2, is associated with a rare fetal disease called progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), which occurs when the immune system is weakened [8].

Polyomaviruses have been identified in a variety of cancers [9]. The causal association of MCV with Merkel cell carcinoma is well documented [10], and several polyomaviruses have also been observed in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). These viruses may act as cofactors in malignant transformation and tumor progression. Specifically, the BKV and JCV have been found in lip and laryngeal carcinomas, respectively [11]. Furthermore, Simian virus 40 (SV40) has been implicated in various malignancies, including osteosarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and mesothelioma [8], and has also been detected in HNSCC [9]. MCV has been identified as the causative agent in most cases of Merkel cell carcinoma, a rare and highly aggressive form of skin cancer. In addition, MCV gene fragments have been found in the upper aerodigestive tract, including the esophagus and oral cavity [12].

Recently, there has been increasing emphasis on the association between polyomaviruses and oral cancer. However, the results of various studies are inconclusive, mainly due to differences in detection techniques and characteristics of the compared groups [13, 14]. These inconsistencies have hindered the comparability of the results obtained. The aim of this study was to conduct a thorough systematic review and meta-analysis of existing studies based on the relationship between polyomaviruses and oral cancer.

Materials and methods

The research was planned and executed by the guidelines of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [15]. The protocol for this study has been recorded on the PROSPERO (CRD42024548068). The main focus of this study was to evaluate the relationship between human polyomavirus and oral cancer.

Search strategy

In the present investigation, two researchers (F.SH and T.M) independently performed the literature search. Published articles were collected through a systematic exploration of literature databases such as Scopus, Science Direct, PubMed, Web of Science, ProQuest, and Google Scholar search engine from September 2000 to 2024. The search terms used were “BK Virus”, “JC Virus”, “Polyomavirus”, “Simian Virus 40”, “Human polyomavirus 6 “, “Merkel Cell Polyomavirus”, “Mouth Neoplasms”, “Tongue Neoplasms “, “Oropharyngeal Neoplasms”, and “Tonsillar Neoplasms” using a combination of “OR” and “AND” Boolean Operators in the Title/Abstract/Keywords field (Table 1). Furthermore, article references were reviewed to identify additional relevant studies and improve the search sensitivity. Subsequently, all collected references were then imported into reference management software (EndNote). The reference lists of all related studies were also reviewed to identify other relevant publications. One of the team’s researchers randomly assessed the search outcomes and confirmed that no relevant studies were missed. The search was limited to original articles/abstracts published in the English language that reported the prevalence of polyomaviruses in oral cancer patients. All these procedures were conducted by two authors (F.SH and T.M), and any discrepancies in article selection were resolved through discussion, with a third author (M.M) available to mediate any disagreements.

Study selection

First, the full-text or abstract of articles, documents, and reports were collected from various databases. After removing duplicate articles, irrelevant articles were excluded from the study. Then, full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and review studies, case reports, and unavailable articles were excluded from the meta-analysis. Finally, data was extracted from full-text articles according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

Two researchers independently reviewed the studies, focusing on the first author’s name, publication year, country of study, virus detection test, and total number of positive cases for each virus.

Quality evaluation

All case-control studies included in our meta-analysis were assessed for quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) checklist. This checklist consists of nine questions that scored based on various criteria, including study design, sample size, research objectives, target population, sampling technique, data analysis approach, proper presentation of results, and the consistency of findings with the study objectives. The checklist is categorized into three sections: selection, comparability, and exposure, with a total score ranging from 0 to 9. Only studies scoring at least 5 were eligible for inclusion in the analysis (Table 2). The maximum possible score for selection and comparability is 2 points, while the maximum possible score for exposure is 3 [16, 17].

Analysis

Data analysis was performed with STATA software Ver. 11 [18]. To estimate the risk difference, the number of individuals positive and negative for MCV, BKV, and JCV was extracted from each primary study, separately from the group of oral cancer sufferers and the control group, using a two-by-two table. Using a random effect model and inverse variance, the risk difference was estimated separately for each hematological index with a 95% confidence interval. The criterion for assessing the significance of the difference in oral cancer risk between individuals with positive and negative MCV, BKV, and JCV did not include zero between the upper and lower confidence intervals of the risk difference. Heterogeneity between primary study results was evaluated using the I-square and Q index and publication bias using Egger and Begg tests. In addition, sensitivity analysis was performed to check the impact of each primary study on the overall estimate.

Results

A total of 1554 full-text or summary articles, documents and reports were extracted from various databases. After removing duplicate articles, 684 records were reviewed and 396 irrelevant articles were excluded from this study. Subsequently, 288 reports were screened for retrieval and 141 of them were omitted. Next, 147 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility and 138 review studies and book chapters were excluded from this meta-analysis. Finally, 9 studies were included in this analysis based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Of the nine studies, the relationship between MCV and oral cancer was investigated in five primary studies (250 cases and 186 controls) (Table 3). In four studies, the risk of oral cancer was higher in MCV-positive individuals than in MCV-negative individuals, and in two primary studies (Salakova et al.‘s study and Hamiter et al.‘s study [19, 20], the observed differences were statistically significant. In these four studies, the risk of oral cancer in MCV-positive subjects in the Dickinson [21], Salakova [19], Hamiter [20], and Herberhold [22] studies was 10% (RD: 0.10, 95% CI: -0.15, 0.35), 24% (RD: 0.24, 95% CI: 0.13, 0.35), 29% (RD: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.03, 0.54), and 11% (RD: 0.11, 95% CI: -0.05, 0.27) respectively. Heterogeneity indices showed high heterogeneity among primary studies (I2 statistics > 50%) (I-square: 60.3%, Q: 10.07, P = 0.039). Combining the results of these five studies using the A random effect model, the risk of oral cancer in MCV- positive individuals was 13% higher than in MCV-negative individuals (RD: 0.13, 95% CI: 0.00, 0.26) (Fig. 2). Publication bias was checked using Egger and Begg tests, which were not significant (P-value for Egger test: 0.464, Begg test: 1.000). Furthermore, based on the results of the sensitivity analysis, the impact of each of the primary studies on the overall estimate was not significant(Table 4).

Of the nine studies, the relationship between BKV and oral cancer was investigated in five primary studies (553 cases and number of 320 controls) (Table 3). In one study (Polz et al.‘s study [23], the risk of oral cancer in BKV-positive individuals was 15% higher than in BKV- negative individuals (95% CI: 0.05, 0.25, RD: 0.15). It should be noted that the difference in oral cancer risk was not significant in the remaining four studies. Heterogeneity indices showed high heterogeneity between primary studies (I2 statistics > 50%) (I-square: 72.8%, Q: 14.68, P = 0.005). It should be noted that this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 3). Discussion

The present meta-analysis aimed to systematically review and summarize the existing literature on the prevalence of polyomaviruses in oral cancer patients. Overall, in our study, the risk of oral cancer in MCV- positive people was significantly 13% higher than in MCV- negative people (RD: 0.13, 95% CI: 0.00, 0.26) and the risk of oral cancer in BKV- positive people are estimated to be 2% higher than BKV- negative people (RD: 0.02, 95% CI: -0.03, 0.07). Also, the risk of oral cancer in JCV- positive people was 1% higher than JCV- negative people (RD: 0.01, 95% CI: -0.02, 0.06).

Ramqvist et al. [27], examined 91 primary tumors, including 12 subtypes and 8 associated metastases, for the presence of human polyomavirus DNA. This assay was performed through a multiplex bead-based method using polymerase chain reaction and Luminex technology. Of the samples, three positive specimens were identified: one adenocarcinoma (unspecified), one adenoid cystic carcinoma, and one mucoepidermoid carcinoma. In Amoli et al. [28] study to determine the prevalence of coinfection with HPV, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and MCV in oral cavity lesions in Iranian patients, 114 samples were analyzed by real-time quantitative PCR. Based on histological features, 32.5% and 28.9% of the cases were oral irritant fibroma and oral squamous cell carcinoma, respectively. Infection with at least two viruses was observed in 21.1% of patients. In this group, coinfection with HPV/EBV was detected in 37.5% of cases, HPV/MCV in 29.2%, EBV/MCV in 12.5%, and HPV/EBV/MCV in 20.8%. There was no statistically significant difference between multiple infections and the anatomical sites of the cancer. The prevalence of triple viral infection (HPV/EBV/MCV) in well-differentiated tumors was higher than that of single EBV or MCV infection. Our results showed a significantly higher risk of developing oral cancer by 13% in those who tested positive for MCV compared with those who tested negative (RD: 0.13, 95% CI: 0.00, 0.26).

Passerini et al. [29], conducted a study that involved detecting and analyzing MCV DNA in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and potentially malignant oral disorders (OPMDs). MCV DNA was found in 3 out of 11 OSCC and 4 out of 12 OPMD samples, with an average viral load of 1.49 × 102 copies/ml. The presence of MCV replication in both OSCC and OPMD indicates the oral cavity is a common site for recurrent MCV infection, emphasizing the active role of this virus in the development of oral lesions. Another case control study, Polz et al. [23] investigated the prevalence of BKV and JCV in patients with OSCC. BKV DNA was detected in 18.5% of patients with OSCC. In the control group, BKV DNA was detected in 3.3%. Their study revealed that the presence of BKV DNA was significantly higher in patients with squamous cell carcinoma in comparison to the control group (p < 0.05). Carpen et al. [11] conducted a study of a total of 158 consecutive patients diagnosed with oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC). DNA was extracted from tumor specimens to identify the presence of EBV, HPV, and several polyomaviruses, including JCV, SV40, and BKV using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Among the samples, 29.1% (94 out of 158) tested positive for BKV DNA, 13.9% for JCV DNA, and 0.6% for SV40 DNA. While polyomaviruses were found in OPSCC cases, they did not appear to affect survival outcomes.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis showed a significant association between MCV and oral cancer and the risk of oral cancer in MCV-positive individuals was 13% higher than that in MCV-negative people. Our study showed that the frequency of BKV and JCV was very low in patients with oral cancer tissue.

Future studies on the mechanism of coinfection and/or superinfection, more sensitive methods, and determination of viral protein expression at different stages of oral cancer development are needed to provide information on the pathogenesis of BKV and JCV and to better define the role of these viruses in oral cancer. Knowledge of these viruses may inform new strategies for the use of antiviral therapy in cancer patients.
