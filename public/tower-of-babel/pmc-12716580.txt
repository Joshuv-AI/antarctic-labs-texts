Laparoscopic management of congenital gastric muscular layer defect and gastric perforation: a case series
Authors: Ying Wu, Xin Guo, Xiaoxia Wu, Baohong Zhao, Yuanyuan Jin, Wenyue Liu, Hongxia Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and efficacy of laparoscopic management in neonates diagnosed with congenital gastric muscular layer defects and gastric perforation. A retrospective analysis was conducted on four male neonates (median gestational age: 36.79 weeks; median birth weight: 2460 g) who underwent laparoscopic repair at Shanxi Provincial Children’s Hospital between January 2022 and October 2024. All neonates presented with symptoms of vomiting, feeding intolerance, and abdominal distension, with preoperative imaging confirming pneumoperitoneum. Laparoscopic surgery was successfully completed in all cases without conversion to open surgery. The median operative time was 102 min, and no surgery-related complications such as anastomotic leakage or hemorrhage were observed. One neonate was transferred to the department of internal medicine due to postoperative feeding difficulties, failure, and persistent oxygen dependence. The median hospital stay was 27.5 days. At the 6-month postoperative follow-up, three neonates exhibited satisfactory growth and development, while one exhibited mild growth delay. Our findings indicate that laparoscopic repair offers significant advantages, including mitigated surgical trauma, quicker recovery, and lower incidence of intestinal adhesion. Provided that surgical indications are strictly assessed and the surgeon possesses proficient laparoscopic skills, it is feasible for specialized surgeons to manage this disease using laparoscopy.Keywords: Colorectal Surgery; Congenital Abnormalities; Surgery, Plastic

Introduction

Congenital gastric muscular layer defect is a rare congenital malformation caused by disrupted development of the gastric muscular layer during the embryonic period. It typically manifests as spontaneous gastric perforation in neonates.1 Once perforation occurs, clinical deterioration may rapidly progress with a mortality rate as high as 30%–64%.2,5 Therefore, neonates with suspected perforation require urgent evaluation. Given that the etiology of pneumoperitoneum is often unknown preoperatively, emergency laparoscopy or laparotomy is typically performed as both the definitive diagnostic and therapeutic intervention. Immediate surgical repair of the defect is imperative on intraoperative confirmation. However, conventional laparotomy carries significant limitations, including substantial surgical trauma and prolonged recovery time, leaving plenty of scope for technical innovation and procedural refinement. This is particularly critical for preterm and low-birth-weight infants, for whom laparotomy significantly increases perioperative risks and the incidence of adverse outcomes.6 In recent years, with the widespread adoption of minimally invasive techniques, laparoscopy has gradually been applied to neonatal acute abdominal emergencies and has shown promising prospects. Nevertheless, inherent challenges in neonates, such as limited abdominal cavity space, obscuration by the liver lobe, and severe abdominal contamination, remain major obstacles to laparoscopic repair.6 Therefore, we present and analyze a case series treated via laparoscopy at our institution. This study aims to explore the feasibility of laparoscopic management for congenital gastric muscular layer defect with gastric perforation, to summarize the technical key points and our preliminary experience, and to provide evidence-based support for optimizing minimally invasive strategies for this disease.

Case summary

Between January 2022 and October 2024, four neonates with gastric perforation caused by congenital gastric muscular layer defect were diagnosed and treated laparoscopically at our institution. All were male, with a median gestational age of 36.79 weeks (range from 32.43 to 39.71 weeks) and a median birth weight of 2460 g (range from 1650 g to 3090 g). Among them, each of the two infants was from a different twin pregnancy. All neonates initially presented with vomiting, feeding intolerance, and progressive abdominal distension. Additionally, two neonates underwent emergency tracheal intubation and positive-pressure ventilation immediately after birth due to respiratory distress and asphyxia. Sepsis with septic shock complicated two cases. All infants presented with signs of peritonitis before surgery, including progressive abdominal distension and muscular rigidity. Additionally, gastrointestinal perforation was indicated at upright abdominal X-rays of all cases. Consequently, emergency laparoscopic exploration and repair of the gastric wall defect were performed.

All four neonates successfully underwent laparoscopic repair of gastric wall defects without conversion to laparotomy. The median age at surgery was 4 days (range from 4 to 7 days). The median operation duration was 102 min (range from 94 to 112 min), with a mean of 102.5 min. Intraoperative exploration revealed gastric muscular layer defect and perforation at the greater curvature in two cases, at the gastric cardia in one case, and involving both the cardia and gastric body in the remaining case. The defect size ranged from 4 cm to 5 cm. Abdominal drainage tubes were inserted in all cases, with the median indwelling time of 11 days (range: 7–13 days). After surgery, all infants received continuous gastrointestinal decompression, fasting, and parenteral nutritional support. To evaluate the condition of the anastomosis and gastric motility, an upper gastrointestinal contrast examination was performed about 1 week postoperatively for all infants. The results demonstrated reduced gastric volume with smooth mucosal lining, patent pyloric transit, and favorable peristalsis. During the follow-up period, no anastomotic leakage, hemorrhage, or other surgery-related complications occurred. Three neonates achieved successful initiation of postoperative feeding. The remaining one was transferred to the department of neonatal medicine due to feeding difficulties and persistent oxygen dependence; he ultimately recovered and was discharged after 2 weeks of treatment. The median hospital stay was 27.5 days (range: 21–40 days), and the average hospital stay was 29 days. All infants were followed up for 6 months after surgery, and their weight, length, and corresponding Z-scores at the age of 6 months were collected. The results indicated that all parameters were within the normal range, except for one infant (case 1) with mild growth delay (online supplemental table).

Surgical procedure

The infants were positioned in reverse Trendelenburg with the surgeon standing at the patient’s feet and the assistant holding the laparoscope adjacent to the surgeon. A 5 mm incision was made at the lower left edge of the umbilical ring, and a trocar was inserted to establish pneumoperitoneum (maintain the pressure at 4–7 mmHg), followed by the insertion of a laparoscope. Under laparoscopic monitoring, two additional 3 mm incisions were made in the left and right mid-abdomen, respectively, for 3 mm trocar placement and insertion of operative instruments. After aspirating peritoneal effusion and thoroughly irrigating the abdominal cavity with warmed saline until clear, a comprehensive exploration was performed with particular attention to difficult-to-visualize areas like the gastric fundus and cardia. The ligamentum teres hepatis and the left edge of the liver were routinely suspended using 2–0 silk sutures to adequately expose the surgical field (figure 1A). An additional trocar was inserted in the right upper quadrant, and an atraumatic grasper was used through this port to elevate the left lobe of the liver, achieving adequate visualization. With laparoscopic assistance, the edges of the gastric perforation and surrounding necrotic tissue were carefully trimmed (figure 1B). Subsequently, continuous inverted suturing of the gastric defect was carried out using 5–0 absorbable sutures (figure 1C). During the suturing process, the initial suture was preplaced transabdominally for the first stitch, then knotted. Furthermore, external traction was applied to the extra-abdominal suture tail for superior exposure. On completion of the initial repair, the needle-suture complex was externalized through the abdominal wall for traction. Finally, the seromuscular layer was reinforced with interrupted 5–0 absorbable sutures to enhance the stability of the repair (figure 1D). After the repair was completed, air was injected through the gastric tube to confirm that there was no leakage at the anastomosis. The abdominal cavity was flushed again with warm saline until the fluid was completely clear. Under the endoscope, no fibrin exudation or residual pus was found in the subphrenic, subhepatic, and intestinal spaces. Finally, a drainage tube was inserted through the right abdominal port site. Once the pneumoperitoneum was released, the port incisions were closed in layers, finalizing the surgical procedure.

Discussion

Congenital gastric muscular layer defect is the primary cause of gastric perforation in neonates. In 1943, Herbut first comprehensively documented neonatal gastric perforation.7 Since the first successful surgical intervention was reported in 1959,8 subsequent studies have consistently advocated for early exploratory laparotomy combined with perioperative management to improve survival. For instance, Tianyou Yang et al. reviewed 68 neonatal gastric perforation cases: 94.1% underwent the repair of gastric wall defect, only three neonates received the additional gastrostomy, and one received gastroduodenal anastomosis combined with gastrostomy due to extensive necrosis. The overall postoperative mortality rate reached 39.4%.4 Feng Yong et al. analyzed 104 cases of neonates with congenital gastric muscular layer defect, all of whom underwent laparotomy exploration and continuous suture repair of the gastric wall.9 The perforations predominantly occurred in the greater curvature, and prognosis was worse in neonates with lower gestational ages. Traditional laparotomy has long been regarded as the standard approach; however, its inherent limitations—significant trauma, poor exposure of the gastric fundus or cardia, high incidence of postoperative intestinal adhesions, and elevated risk of infection—cannot be overlooked, particularly in preterm and low-birth-weight neonates. In 2020, Zhao Baohong et al. first reported two cases of laparoscopic gastric wall repair, highlighting its advantages, such as better visualization of the surgical field, particularly the cardiac region, more precise interventions through the synchronization of diagnosis and treatment, and a lower incidence of severe complications.10 This study contributes four additional neonatal cases of laparoscopic management for congenital gastric muscular layer defect with perforation, aiming to evaluate the feasibility of laparoscopic diagnosis and treatment for this condition.

Based on our experience with four cases of laparoscopic management for congenital gastric muscular layer defect and gastric perforation, we summarize the following technical principles: First, before laparoscopic surgery, several essential procedures are required to create sufficient working space: proper patient positioning, bladder emptying, and establishment of a pneumoperitoneum. Then, the camera and operating ports are strategically placed in the left, right, and mid-abdomen to identify the lesion. If exposure is inadequate, additional trocars can be placed. Meanwhile, traction techniques are crucial for optimal exposure. This can be achieved by suspending the ligamentum teres hepatis and the left lobe of the liver. Alternatively, transabdominal traction methods can be used, in which either the initial suture or the entire suture line is externalized through the abdominal wall to provide traction. Another effective way to expose the surgical field is to place a traction suture through the abdominal wall and normal gastric tissue. During the suturing process, continuous suturing is the preferred approach, particularly for larger defects, followed by mandatory seromuscular imbrication to ensure meticulous repair of muscular defect and perforation, thereby minimizing postoperative complications.

Laparoscopy offers distinct technical benefits for treating gastric muscular layer defect and gastric perforation. First, the wide-angle view provided by laparoscopy allows surgeons to directly identify pathological tissues, which minimizes the risk of unnecessary damage and exploratory procedures. Second, laparoscopy facilitates superior exposure of anatomically complex regions, such as the gastric fundus and greater curvature. It provides particularly improved access to the cardia, an area that is challenging to visualize even during laparotomy due to its deep location and poor exposure. Additionally, the minimally invasive nature of laparoscopy, characterized by small incisions, results in less mechanical irritation to abdominal organs compared with traditional laparotomy. This contributes to a faster recovery of gastrointestinal function and a significant reduction in postoperative complications. This method not only supports the concept of accelerated recovery in neonates but also satisfies families’ desires for safety and aesthetic considerations. Our clinical experience demonstrates that laparoscopic surgery has marked superiority in repair integrity, functional recovery, and long-term prognosis. With ongoing technological advancements, laparoscopy is likely to become the preferred treatment option for repair of congenital gastric muscular layer defects and perforation.

In this study, the oldest infant undergoing surgery was 7 days old (case 2). Although this infant had started with minimal feeding early after birth, the actual milk intake was extremely low. As a result, the defect of the gastric muscular layer lacked sufficient tension stimulus, which delayed the occurrence of perforation. The other infants also had a history of feeding immediately after birth but developed perforation earlier. All infants underwent emergency laparoscopic surgery within 12 hours after pneumoperitoneum was identified. None of them developed anastomotic leakage or secondary infection after surgery, and all had favorable outcomes. Previous studies have indicated that the age of onset is not a critical factor determining prognosis. It was reported that 11.11% of the infants developed gastric perforation on the seventh day after birth, and the median age of onset in the survival group was 4 days (range: 3.18–7.22 days).1 Wan et al. further emphasized that the interval between the onset of symptoms and medical consultation is the core factor influencing prognosis, rather than the age of onset itself.11 We also believe that laparoscopic repair remains safe and feasible even in slightly older patients, provided surgery is performed promptly after perforation and intraoperative management is appropriate.

To further evaluate, we compared our outcomes with previous cases involving open surgery. Rishika et al. reported on 13 neonates who underwent open repair surgery,12 noting a mean hospital stay of 42 days (range: 21–196 days), which was significantly longer than the 29 days (range: 21–40 days) observed in our study. Additionally, Yang et al.2 found that the mortality rate following open surgery was 20%, and that 56% of infants required a second operation. In contrast, none of the four infants in our study experienced surgery-related complications. Although this study is limited by its sample size, the available data preliminarily suggest that laparoscopic surgery may offer advantages in expediting postoperative recovery and minimizing complications. The operative time for the laparoscopic group (102.5 min) was slightly longer than that reported by Zheng Jintao et al. for open surgery (94.89 min).13 This discrepancy can be primarily attributed to two factors: first, the limited number of early cases meant that surgeons were still on the learning curve for laparoscopic surgery; second, the confined operative space within the neonatal abdominal cavity imposes greater demands on procedures such as laparoscopic suturing. Nevertheless, laparoscopy presents distinct advantages, including reduced abdominal wall trauma, enhanced magnification of the surgical field, and decreased risks associated with postoperative intestinal adhesion and incision infection. These benefits are substantial enough to offset any increase in operative time resulting from the initial learning curve.

Regarding postoperative management, an upper gastrointestinal contrast study is typically performed approximately 7 days after surgery. This timing is based on pathophysiological principles and clinical experience: by this point, the gastric anastomosis has achieved initial healing and is generally capable of withstanding the mechanical stress induced by contrast administration, thereby minimizing the risk of iatrogenic anastomotic disruption. Currently, there is a lack of high-level evidence regarding the optimal timing for contrast studies following neonatal gastric muscular layer defect repair. Although earlier imaging may theoretically align better with enhanced recovery principles, we currently favor performing it around 7 days postoperatively based on existing clinical experience and safety considerations. This approach is particularly relevant given that most patients are preterm infants with a history of generalized peritonitis. It is acknowledged that this protocol may require further refinement and intends to conduct larger, systematic follow-up studies to develop more individualized and efficient postoperative assessment strategies in the future.

All four cases of congenital gastric muscular layer defect with perforation in this series were male, aligning with the well-documented male predominance reported in the literature. For instance, studies by Yang et al.4 and Cui et al.14 reported male proportions of 76.5% and 74.8%, respectively, in their cohorts, and similar gender distribution patterns have been consistently observed in other relevant reports.5 The underlying mechanisms contributing to this sex-based disparity remain incompletely understood. Although the small sample size of our study limits definitive conclusions and cannot entirely rule out sampling bias, awareness of this potential epidemiological trend may still provide a valuable clinical reference for evaluating neonates presenting with acute abdomen. Future research will focus on multicenter, large-scale investigations to validate this association and explore its underlying mechanisms.

In conclusion, laparoscopy has demonstrated significant advantages in the management of congenital gastric muscular layer defect and gastric perforation, including minimized surgical trauma, accelerated recovery of gastrointestinal function after surgery, and lower incidence of intestinal adhesion. Provided that surgical indications are strictly assessed and the surgeon possesses proficient laparoscopic skills, it is feasible for specialized surgeons to manage this disease using laparoscopy.
