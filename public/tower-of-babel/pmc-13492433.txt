Introduction

Erdheim-Chester disease (ECD) is a rare non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis characterized by systemic infiltration of foamy CD68+/CD1a− histiocytes.1 It belongs to the L group in the revised 2016 histiocytosis classification.2 More than 1500 cases have been reported.2 Cardiac involvement is seen in approximately 40% of patients, manifesting as atrial pseudotumors, atrioventricular (AV) sulcus infiltration, pericardium (pericarditis and cardiac tamponade), small and large vessel disease (coronary artery infiltration and coated aorta), and conduction disorders.2,3 Here, we report a unique case of ECD presenting as complete AV block, emphasizing the importance of early recognition.

Case report

A 43-year-old woman with hypothyroidism, well controlled on thyroxine, and a history of recurrent supraventricular tachycardia previously terminated with adenosine. She had been treated with bisoprolol, which was discontinued owing to persistent symptoms. 6 months later, she presented with a 3-day history of palpitations, atypical chest pain, and one episode of syncope. The patient reported sudden-onset palpitations, not associated with exertion. She denied any family history of cardiac disease, arrhythmias, or sudden cardiac death.

The patient was alert, oriented, ambulatory, and hemodynamically stable. The general, cardiovascular, and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. Electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated complete AV block with ventricular escape rhythm (Figure 1). The patient was hemodynamically stable at presentation, and no immediate cardiac device was implanted, because the patient refused any intervention. Given the presence of complete AV block in a young patient, a contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) scan was initially performed to evaluate for infiltrative or structural pathology; this demonstrated only mild pulmonary edema.

Owing to persistent concern for cardiac infiltration, a subsequent dedicated ECG-gated contrast-enhanced cardiac CT study was performed, which demonstrated right atrial thickening with a soft-tissue hypodensity encasing the free atrial wall, extending into the right AV groove, and disrupting adjacent structures. In addition, a perivascular soft-tissue density involved the distal aortic arch and descending thoracic aorta with minimal periadventitial infiltration, raising suspicion for an infiltrative disease (Figure 2A).

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed characteristic cardiac involvement in ECD, with infiltrative soft-tissue thickening involving the right atrial free wall and extension of infiltrative tissue along the AV groove. Findings are consistent with the classic right atrial pseudotumor described in ECD and pericardiac histiocytic infiltration frequently observed in cardiac involvement of systemic non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis, as well as pericardial thickening, reflecting fibrotic inflammatory infiltration of the pericardium (Figure 3A). Positron emission tomography − CT demonstrated fluorodeoxyglucose-avid involvement of the right atrium and mural thickening of the aortic arch, with evidence of extracardiac disease in the axial skeleton (Figure 4A and 4B).

Laboratory evaluation was unremarkable, and central nervous system and pituitary investigations were not performed. Hypothyroidism was well controlled on thyroxine, although its relationship to ECD remains uncertain.

A cardiac biopsy was offered. However, she refused and went abroad for a second opinion, where an electrophysiology study showed dual AV physiology with no induced supraventricular tachycardia. No ablation or further workup was done.

A few months later, she consented to tissue sampling. Pericardial biopsy revealed infiltration by nests of pale histiocytes that were CD68+ and CD1a−, consistent with ECD. Further molecular testing demonstrated the presence of a BRAF V600E mutation.

A multidisciplinary team, including cardiology, oncology, and hematology, initiated targeted therapy with vemurafenib (BRAF inhibitor). The patient reported adherence to the prescribed regimen, and follow-up imaging demonstrated a reduction in tumor burden (Figure 4C).

9 months after initiating the treatment, she was admitted to a peripheral hospital with syncope. The rhythm strip showed episodes of bradycardia-induced ventricular fibrillation secondary to complete heart block (Figure 5). She underwent an emergency temporary pacing wire and has remained stable. Given the complete AV block, the patient was transferred to our center, where she underwent an electrophysiology study, which showed a normal bipolar and unipolar right ventricular map. The decision was made to implant an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator.

Discussion

ECD is a rare multisystem histiocytic disorder in which cardiovascular involvement, occurring in nearly 40% of cases, portends a worse prognosis and often mandates more aggressive management.4,5 Cardiac manifestations are diverse, ranging from myocardial infiltration (including pseudotumoral atrial masses) and pericardial involvement (which can progress to pericarditis or tamponade) to aortic infiltration producing the classic “coated aorta” appearance.4,6 Given this heterogeneity, a high index of suspicion is essential, particularly when patients present with unexplained conduction abnormalities such as complete heart block.2 ECD is primarily a clinicoradiological diagnosis, supported by histologic and molecular findings. Histopathologic examination typically shows infiltrates of foamy histiocytes with variable fibrosis and an associated inflammatory infiltrate composed of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and occasional Touton-type giant cells. These features are not specific to ECD and may be observed in other inflammatory or histiocytic conditions; therefore, pathology findings should be interpreted as consistent with ECD rather than diagnostic in isolation.5 In this context, tissue biopsy has a crucial role in enabling molecular characterization, the identification of activating mutations such as BRAF V600E, and in excluding alternative diagnoses. Notably, Langerhans cell histiocytosis does not produce the characteristic multisystem and cardiovascular imaging pattern observed in ECD.

A systematic analysis of cardiac involvement has demonstrated pericardial infiltration in 44% of patients, myocardial involvement in 31%, and occasional right atrial masses; concomitant complications such as valvular dysfunction, heart failure, and myocardial infarction further underscore the clinical severity.7 Moreover, myocardial infiltrates, often identifiable as areas of late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, can disrupt electrical conduction and compromise ventricular function, contributing to a range of ECG abnormalities.8,9

Management of cardiac ECD requires a multidisciplinary approach involving cardiologists, radiologists, oncologists, and pathologists. Therapeutic strategies include interferon alfa, targeted therapies (such as BRAF and MEK inhibitors), corticosteroids, and cytotoxic agents, with treatment tailored to disease severity, BRAF mutational status, and patient comorbidities.10,11

Hypothyroidism observed in this patient may be related to ECD. Endocrine involvement is well recognised in ECD, and hypothyroidism has been reported in substantial proportion of patients, arising either from histiocytic infiltration of hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis or as a consequence of interferon-alpha therapy.12 This reflects the multisystem nature of the disease. In the present case, a direct causal relationship cannot be established; however, the coexistence of hypothyroidism with cardiac involvement underscores the need for systemic endocrine assessment during evaluation and follow up.11

This case highlights the critical importance of considering infiltrative diseases such as ECD in the differential diagnosis of unexplained conduction abnormalities in the young. Early recognition, advanced imaging, histopathologic confirmation, and prompt initiation of targeted therapy are essential to improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion

ECD should be considered in patients with unexplained conduction abnormalities and infiltrative cardiac findings. Early recognition through advanced imaging and biopsy is crucial. Targeted therapies, particularly BRAF inhibitors, can improve clinical outcomes in affected patients.
