The central precocious puberty-associated gene MKRN3 is a tumor suppressor regulating CSDE1 ubiquitination in ovarian cancer
Authors: Fengrui Zhang, Chang Yuan, Yumei Cheng, Chun Chen, Luyao Guan, Xiaofang Wang, Yuxiang Luo, Chunling Zeng, Yangjie Xiong, Haoqi Lan, Yue Dong, Xiaona Jia, Simin Wang, Qi Cao, Yuexiang Wang, Yuan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarlier age at menarche has long been known to be associated with an increased risk of ovarian cancer (OC). However, the underlying mechanisms are not completely understood. Here, we report a paternally inherited imprinted gene, MKRN3, that is associated with central precocious puberty (CPP) and that plays a tumor suppressive role in OC. We found that genomic MKRN3 mutations are significantly more common in OC patients. Genomic mutations in MKRN3 and low MKRN3 expression levels are correlated with poor patient survival. The restoration of MKRN3 in MKRN3-inactivated OC cells significantly suppresses tumor growth and proliferation in vitro and in vivo. Ovary-specific knockout of endogenous MKRN3 accelerates P53 inactivation-induced tumorigenesis in mice. Mechanistically, CSDE1 was identified as a major substrate for MKRN3 in OC via mass spectrometry–based proteomics screens. MKRN3 regulates OC cell proliferation through proteolytic ubiquitination of CSDE1. Our comprehensive data reveal that the CPP-associated gene MKRN3 is a tumor suppressor in human OC. We propose that germline mutations in the MKRN3 gene might partially explain the association between precocious puberty and the risk of OC.Subject terms: Ovarian cancer, Oncogenes

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the eighth most common cancer in women and has a dismal prognosis because it is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage [1]. Each year, 324,398 new cases and 206,839 deaths from OC occur worldwide, and epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is the most common type of OC [1]. With rapid progress in treatments based on surgery and targeted chemotherapy [2], the prognosis has only marginally improved because of relapse and chemotherapy resistance [3, 4]. Thus, a better understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms of EOC is imperative, and new treatment strategies are needed to improve the overall prognosis of EOC patients.

Earlier age at menarche has long been known to be associated with an increased risk of ovarian cancer [5]. The plausible mechanism of this phenomenon is that an earlier age at menarche and a later age at natural menopause increase the ovulatory life of a woman; this results in a longer damage-repair process for ovarian epithelium, which leads to a greater chance of carcinogenesis [6]. However, a Mendelian randomization analysis suggested an underlying connection between genomic mutations and body mass index-unrelated risk for ovarian cancer and age at menarche [7]. The paternally expressed, imprinted gene Makorin ring finger protein 3 (MKRN3) was included in this network.

As an E3 ubiquitin ligase, germline loss-of-function mutations of MKRN3 were shown to be an important cause of central precocious puberty (CPP) [8]. We previously demonstrated that MKRN3 is a bona fide tumor suppressor in non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [9]. It has been reported that cohorts of individuals with CPP have an increased risk of OC [10–12]. Another consideration of the OC risk in individuals with CPP is earlier exposure to sex steroid hormones, especially estrogen, which is also a well-known risk factor for OC [13, 14].

Here, we investigated public data from cancer genomics studies and paraffin sections of high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) and clear cell ovarian cancer (CCOC) samples and revealed a potential tumor suppressor role of MKRN3 in OC. A series of in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed to demonstrate that MKRN3 can suppress OC tumorigenesis and OC cell proliferation largely through ubiquitination of the cold shock domain-containing protein E1 (CSDE1) protein.

Results

Loss-of-function mutations and downregulation of MKRN3 correlate with poor prognosis in patients with ovarian cancer

To investigate the potential role of the CPP-associated gene MKRN3 in OC, we queried The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets. The information from the TCGA database revealed that the MKRN3 gene is occasionally mutated in OC and that most of the mutations are deep genomic deletions, which are loss-of-function alterations (Fig. 1A, Table 1). To further explore the role of MKRN3 in OC, a Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed, and the results indicate that patients with MKRN3 mutations had worse overall survival (OS) than those without MKRN3 mutations (Fig. 1B). In addition to gene mutations, we investigated how the protein expression level of MKRN3 affects patient prognosis. We assessed MKRN3 protein levels in human ovarian cancer tissues from 30 patients with HGSOC and 30 patients with CCOC using IHC staining and found that lower MKRN3 expression was associated with worse OS (Fig. 1C, D, Table S1). Taken together, these findings suggest that MKRN3 inactivation has a potential role in OC.

Functional validation of MKRN3 in suppression of OC

To investigate the biological function of MKRN3 in OC, we further explored the effect of MKRN3 on tumor cell proliferation. We selected two HGSOC cell lines, HEY and CAOV3, which represent type 2 ovarian cancer, and one CCOC cell line, ES-2, with low transcription of MKRN3, which represents type 1 ovarian cancer, as representative MKRN3-inactivated OC cell models (Table S2). Lentivirus-mediated MKRN3 transduction into OC cells induced MKRN3 expression, and MKRN3 overexpression significantly suppressed OC cells’ proliferation (Fig. 2A), which manifests as decreased proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) expression, which is a representative indicator of the malignant phenotype (Fig. 2B). Moreover, MKRN3 overexpression significantly inhibited colony formation ability in an anchorage-dependent manner (Fig. 2C) and in an anchorage-independent manner (Fig. 2D) in these cell lines. To determine whether the inhibition of cell proliferation occurs in vivo, we generated both control, which expresses a truncated MKRN3-c258* that lack of the ubiquitination domain, and MKRN3-restored ES2 and CAOV3 xenografts in nude mice. MKRN3 overexpression markedly attenuated tumor growth (Fig. 2E–G), and MKRN3 overexpression caused S/G2/M arrest in cells (Fig. 2H).

To further explore the potential downstream molecular pathway that MKRN3 affects, total RNA from two independent samples per group was analyzed by RNA sequencing (RNAseq). Significantly (p < 0.05) different expression patterns were detected for 1438 genes between MKRN3 overexpression and control (Fig. S1A, B, Data file 1). Consistent with the former results, GO enrichment analysis indicates that the overexpression of MKRN3 affects a lot of key cellular functions, including “cytoplasm, control of RNA polymerase II, apoptosis, cell population proliferation, transcription, etc. (Fig. S1C)”. And further enrichment analysis with KOG and KEGG database indicates that a series of functions, including “signal transduction mechanisms, transcription, etc. (Fig. S2A)” and pathways including “axon guidance, microRNAs in cancer, TNF signaling pathway, etc. (Fig. S2B)”, which is consistent with the reports that MKRN3 taking part in the regulation of neuronal development, synaptic plasticity and RNA-binding function [15, 16].

Taken together, our results demonstrate that MKRN3 suppresses OC by regulating cell proliferation and affecting a series of key pathways.

Loss of Mkrn3 in the ovarian surface epithelium or the fallopian tube epithelium accelerates tumorigenesis in mice

HGSOC is characterized by gain-of-function mutations in TP53 [17]. To investigate the role of MKRN3 in tumorigenesis, we constructed genetically engineered mouse models of epithelial ovarian cancer with Cre-inducible deletion of Mkrn3 and Trp53, and Trp53 acts as the characteristic silencing mutations in HGSOC (Fig. 3A, Fig. S3a). Since MKRN3 is maternally imprinted, which means that only the paternal allele is expressed [8]. we used the male conditional Mkrn3 and Trp53 knockout mouse model Mkrn3pfl/m+/ Trp53fl/fl mice to intercross with female Trp53fl/fl mice to produce littermate Mkrn3pfl/m+ and Trp53fl/fl mice (MT) and Trp53fl/fl mice (T) (Fig. S3b). A recombinant adenoviral vector expressing Cre recombinase (AdCre) was injected into the bursal cavity to establish an ovarian surface epithelium (OSE)-specific knockout model (Fig. 3B). Monitoring and intermittent abdominal palpation of these mice were performed for one year. The survival rate and neoplasms of these mice revealed that 50% (6/12) of the MT mice developed neoplasms, 66.7% (4/6) of which were ovary-related, and 16.7% (1/6) of which exhibited classical ovarian cancer manifestations, including hemorrhagic ascites and peritoneal metastasis (Fig. 3C, Table 2, Fig. S3c, d). We performed histological examinations and immunohistochemical staining of the tumors and confirmed positive expression of the ovarian cancer markers PAX8 and WT1 and negative expression of our targeted proteins MKRN3 and P53 (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that Mkrn3 deletion accelerates tumorigenesis in Trp53 knockout OSE.

Despite its appellation, the fallopian tube is the more important origin of serous ovarian cancer, especially HGSOC. However, an appropriate method for the specific deletion of genes in the mouse fallopian tube in vivo is not available. Therefore, we constructed FTE organoids, which mainly comprises secretory (PAX8 + ) and ciliated (acetyl-α-tubulin + ) epithelia, from genetically engineered mouse models including those with Cre-inducible deletion of Mkrn3 and Trp53 and Cre-inducible activation of KrasG12D and their combinations, according to Zhang et al. [18]. (Fig. 3A, E). Kras activation acts as characteristic background mutations of type I ovarian tumors, part from HGSOC [19]. We found that the dual Cre-induced FTE organoid exhibited greater tumorigenic behavior, lumen filling and decreased ciliary differentiation, which manifests as altered ciliated cell differentiation, than the single-gene-induced FTE organoid (Fig. 3F). Taken together, these findings indicate that Mkrn3 deletion induces a more tumorigenic phenotype not only in OSE cells but also in FTE cells.

MKRN3 mediates CSDE1 degradation through the ubiquitin proteasome pathway

To further explore the molecular mechanism by which MKRN3 expression controls cell proliferation, we performed mass spectrometry–based proteomics screening of proteins that coimmunoprecipitated with 3×Flag-tagged MKRN3 in ES-2 cells (Fig. 4A). The subsequent data were analyzed, and the protein hits were rated by the number of unique peptide counts in the 3×Flag-MKRN3 group (Fig. 4B, Data file 2). We focused on CSDE1, which is a potential oncogene in ovarian cancer [20]. Our prioritization of CSDE1 was primarily guided by its established clinical relevance in ovarian cancer, as evidenced by multiple studies: Preclinical studies in multiple ovarian cancer cell lines demonstrate that CSDE1 might be an anticancer drug sensitizer [21]. The abundance of studies on CSDE1 enables the rapid clinical translation of our findings. A coimmunoprecipitation assay was conducted to confirm the results of the proteomic screening of ES-2 cells transfected with 3×Flag-tagged MKRN3, and CSDE1 was pulled down with 3×Flag-MKRN3 (Fig. 4C). We found that MKRN3 overexpression decreased CSDE1 protein levels in these cell lines (Fig. 4D). To determine whether MKRN3 expression suppresses CSDE1 production or accelerates its degradation, a CHX assay was performed. We found that the CSDE1 protein in the setting of MKRN3 overexpression had a significantly shorter half-life (Fig. 4E), which indicates that MKRN3 mediates CSDE1 degradation. MKRN3 belongs to a ubiquitin (Ub) E3 ligase family that facilitates the ubiquitination of target proteins [8]. We found that CSDE1 protein levels were robustly increased in OC cells overexpressed MKRN3 after being treated with MG132, a proteasome inhibitor (Fig. 4F). Therefore, we transiently transfected MKRN3 or control plasmids with 3×Flag-tagged CSDE1 and HA-tagged Ub into HEK293T cells, which acts as a tool cell with a better transfection efficiency, and the results revealed that CSDE1 was ubiquitinated by MKRN3 (Fig. 4G). These results indicate that MKRN3 regulates CSDE1 expression through proteasome pathway. Furthermore, we found that MKRN3 modulated CSDE1 ubiquitin in Lys48-linked polyubiquitylation (Fig. 4H). Taken together, these results suggest that MKRN3 mediates CSDE1 degradation through the ubiquitin proteasome pathway.

MKRN3 inhibits OC cell proliferation through degradation of CSDE1

To verify that MKRN3 suppresses OC cell proliferation through the degradation of CSDE1, we conducted a CRISPR-mediated CSDE1 knockout experiment. We found that knockout of CSDE1 recapitulated the inhibitory effect of MKRN3 in ES-2 cells and that a lower expression level of CSDE1 leads to a greater inhibitory effect (Fig. 5A, B). We subsequently performed a restoration experiment to confirm the molecular mechanism of this MKRN3-CSDE1 axis. We found that the restoration of CSDE1 abrogated the inhibitory effect of MKRN3 overexpression in ES-2 cells (Fig. 5C, D). These data suggest that MKRN3 suppresses OC cells’ proliferation largely through CSDE1 ubiquitination (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

While OC is a heterogeneous disease with various histological subtypes, over 90% of cases are of epithelial origin, of which HGSOC and CCOC together account for more than 85% of all EOCs [22–24]. Therapeutic advancements in ovarian cancer (OC) are progressing swiftly, driven by a deeper understanding of DNA repair deficiencies and the identification of novel therapeutic targets [25]. Most OC patients present with advanced-stage disease, and no reliable methods for early detection are currently available. HGSOC in particular tends to respond well to platinum-based chemotherapy, which leads many patients into clinical remission. However, the majority of patients experience relapse after initial treatment and ultimately succumb to the disease, which emphasizes the critical need for innovative therapeutic approaches [6].

The ubiquitin–proteasome system is widely recognized for its critical role in regulating various cellular processes in cancer cells, including gene expression, cell cycle control, proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy [26–28]. In this study, we showed that the E3 ubiquitin ligase MKRN3 suppresses OC tumorigenesis and OC cell proliferation, largely through the canonical ubiquitin‒proteasome system to degrade CSDE1, which is reported to be oncogenic in OC [21]. To our knowledge, MKRN3 is involved in numerous cellular processes and wide occurrence of its ubiquitination targets but displays a high degree of specificity in its molecular action, which indicates a sequence-specific recognition of mRNAs by MKRN3 that remains to be confirmed [16]. Interestingly, CSDE1 was suggested to have a “protein-RNA connector” model that connects with different RNAs and their regulators, which may assists the recognition of MKRN3 with downstream mRNAs, which needs further investigation [20].

In the United States, the lifetime risk of developing ovarian cancer (OC) is 1.3% by the age of 80 [29]. More than 70% of epithelial ovarian cancers are diagnosed at advanced stages (III and IV), which is considered the reason for its high mortality. However, a recent general population screening trial conducted in the United Kingdom, the UK Collaborative Trial of Ovarian Cancer Screening (UKCTOCS), revealed that the reduction in stage III or IV disease incidence achieved by annual multimodal screening was not sufficient to translate into lives saved [30]. Thus, better screening strategies and risk reduction methods are urgently needed for OC.

OC has a strong hereditary component and a significant genetic predisposition in many cases [23, 24]. Familial or hereditary ovarian cancer syndromes involve many tumor suppressor genes, and inherited mutations collectively account for more than 24% of epithelial ovarian cancer cases [23]. Current guidelines advise germline genetic testing for all women diagnosed with ovarian cancer, along with cascade testing for family members and risk-reducing surgeries for individuals carrying actionable mutations [31]. Our results indicate that MKRN3 may be a tumor suppressor gene associated with familial or hereditary cancer syndromes. However, the germline MKRN3 mutation carrier in ovarian cancer does not present with a classical cancer history in female family members because of its paternally expressed imprinted characteristics. This may be the reason that it is recognized only by parent-of-origin-specific association analysis under the paternal model [7]. A germline MKRN3 loss-of-function mutation was reported to have complete penetrance, which means that it would manifest itself by presenting as paternal transmission of a family history of CPP. This approach will be helpful in the early screening of genomic mutations.

It has been reported that cohorts of individuals with CPP have an increased risk of OC [10–12]. Another consideration of the OC risk in individuals with CPP is earlier exposure to sex steroid hormones, especially estrogen, which is also a well-known risk factor for OC [13, 14]. Interestingly, a lack of expression of inherited imprinted genes in the paternally derived Prader‒Willi critical region on chromosome 15q11.2‒q13, which includes MKRN3, was also reported to be associated with a predisposition for gonadal tumors [32]. The mainstay of treatment for CPP is gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogues (GnRHas), genetically modified versions of native GnRH with an extended duration of action and increased potency. In patients, GnRHas suppress the normal GnRH axis until their typical age range for puberty and physical development matches those of their peers [33]. The treatment would theoretically work to lower the patients’ risk of OC by removing their estrogen exposure and ovulation cycle.

A limitation of our study is the selective validation of CSDE1 from a broader set of potential downstream proteins identified via proteomics screening. While our mass spectrometry analysis revealed numerous candidates with enriched peptide counts (e.g., HELZ, DHX30, HELZ2, EIF4G3, ZFR, etc.; Supplementary Table S3), we prioritized CSDE1 based on a dual criterion of clinical relevance and experimental accessibility.

We acknowledge that this prioritization may overlook novel mechanisms mediated by other candidates. For example, DHX30’s role in mitochondrial ribosome biogenesis [34] or EIF4G3’s involvement in translation initiation complex [35] could represent unexplored axes of MKRN3 function. These proteins thus warrant investigation in future studies.

In summary, our data demonstrates that MKRN3 is a bona fide tumor suppressor gene in OC and that MKRN3 inactivation promotes OC tumorigenesis. The functions of this gene are achieved largely through the canonical ubiquitin‒proteasome system, which degrades CSDE1. Further investigations are needed to verify this phenomenon and mechanism in larger patient populations.

Materials and methods

Human cancer datasets

We collected information on the somatic mutations of OC and generated a survival plot using the TCGA data portal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) [36–40].

Tumor samples

Deidentified paraffin-embedded tumor sections were obtained from OC patients at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University (approval no. 2024-168). All samples were collected with institutional review board approval. Informed written consent was obtained from all human participants.

Immunohistochemistry

The sections were deparaffinized in xylene for 15 min and then rehydrated in a graded series of alcohol. We incubated the slides at 95 °C in citric acid buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval after which non-specific protein binding was blocked by incubating the sections in 10% normal goat serum (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at room temperature. Next, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Incubation with the secondary antibody and staining visualization were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for each primary antibody.

Five randomly selected areas of each section were imaged at 400 × magnification, and the mean density of the immunohistochemical staining was acquired via Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (version 6.0.0.206; Media Cybernetics, Inc., Bethesda, MD, USA, RRID:SCR_007369) for subsequent analysis.

The primary antibodies used were as follows: MKRN3 (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# HPA029494, RRID:AB_10603873), KRT7 (ABclonal Cat# A4357, RRID:AB_2863248), PAX8 (Proteintech Cat# 10336-1-AP, RRID:AB_2236705), WT1 (ABclonal Cat# A2446, RRID:AB_3674524), and P53 (ABclonal Cat# A25915, RRID:AB_3674525).

MKRN3 expression and patient survival analysis

To study the possible relationship between MKRN3 expression and survival, we categorized patients into MKRN3-high and MKRN3-low groups according to their MKRN3 expression levels (top 50% versus bottom 50%). Next, we analyzed the difference in survival between these two groups using GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798).

Cell culture

HEK293T (RRID:CVCL_0063) cells and the OC cell lines ES-2 (RRID:CVCL_3509), HEY (RRID:CVCL_0297) and CAOV3 (RRID:CVCL_0201) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HEK293T, ES-2, CAOV3 and HEY cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (HyClone; #SH30027.01) medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific #10099141) and penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific; #15140122). These cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. None of the cell lines in this study appeared in the misidentified cell line list maintained by the International Cell Line Authentication Committee. All cell lines were routinely tested for microbial contamination (including mycoplasma contamination).

Plasmid constructs and lentivirus production

The MKRN3 and CSDE1 expression plasmids were constructed by cloning the corresponding cDNAs into the p3×FLAG-CMVTM-7.1 vector (Sigma‒Aldrich; #E7533). If not noted specifically, all of the experiments concerning MKRN3 overexpression, truncated MKRN3-c258* was used as control. Hemagglutinin (HA)-Ub vectors were generously provided by Dr. Shao-Cong Sun (Department of Immunology, MD Anderson Cancer Center, The University of Texas, Houston, TX). The short guide RNAs (sgRNAs) that target human CSDE1 were designed using the optimized CRISPR design web tool (http://crispr.mit.edu/). And the sgRNA vectors were generated by cloning the guide sequences into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector that comprises Cas9 (Addgene #52961) individually. MKRN3 and CSDE1 lentiviral constructs were generated by cloning the corresponding cDNAs into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro lentiviral expression vector (System Biosciences; #CD510B-1). Lentivirus particles were generated by cotransfecting these lentiviral constructs and the helper virus packaging plasmids pCMVΔR8.9 and pHCMV-VSV-G into HEK293T cells via Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen; #L3000015). Lentiviruses were harvested after 24, 48, and 60 h and frozen at −80 °C in aliquots of appropriate volumes for infection. All cells were infected with lentivirus for 16 h in the supernatant containing 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma #107689) and then treated with puromycin (Millipore #540411) one day after infection.

Soft agar assay and colony formation assay

Six-well plates were first layered with 0.6% bottom agar (Noble agar; BD Difco; #214220) containing RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. ES-2 (20,000 cells per well) and HEY (5000 cells per well) cells were transduced with the MKRN3 lentivirus or the control lentivirus and seeded on 0.35% top agar containing 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were allowed to grow for 2 weeks and were then stained with 1 ml of 1 mg/ml methyl thiazol tetrazolium (Sigma‒Aldrich; #M5655) for 3 h. The colonies were counted via ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, RRID: SCR_003070). Each assay was performed in triplicate, and the entire study was replicated three times. A colony formation assay was conducted by seeding ES-2 (500 cells per well) cells or HEY (500 cells per well) cells transduced with the WT or truncated MKRN3 lentivirus into 6-well plates. The cells were allowed to grow for 2 weeks, after which they were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with crystal violet solution (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co.; #G1014) for 3 h. All samples were tested in triplicate wells, and the entire study was replicated three times.

Cell cycle assays

For cell cycle analysis, the cells were grown to 70% confluence, serum-starved for 12 h and washed with PBS. The cells were harvested and fixed in 70% ethanol for 24 h, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS containing 50 μg/ml propidium iodide (Sigma‒Aldrich; #P4170) and 100 μg/ml RNaseA (TIANGEN; #RT405), after which they were analyzed via a Gallios flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, RRID:SCR_008520).

Western blotting analysis

Whole-cell lysates from cell lines or frozen tissues were prepared in IP lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 1% NP-40, 100 mM sodium fluoride, 2 mM sodium molybdate, 30 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 5 mM EDTA, and 2 mM sodium orthovanadate) containing protease inhibitors (10 μg/ml leupeptin, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride). The lysates were then rocked for 8 h at 4 °C and cleared by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The protein concentrations in the lysates were determined via the use of Quick Start Bradford 1× Dye Reagent (Bio-Rad; #5000205). Electrophoresis and Western blotting were performed according to standard techniques. The hybridization signals were detected via chemiluminescence (Immobilon Western, Millipore Corporation) and captured using an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare; #29083461). The primary antibodies used were as follows: MKRN3 (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# HPA029494, RRID:AB_10603873), CSDE1 (ABclonal Cat# A5941, RRID:AB_2716822), Flag (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# F1804, RRID:AB_262044), HA (Proteintech Cat# 51064-2-AP, RRID:AB_11042321), PCNA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat# sc-56, RRID:AB_628110), and GAPDH (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# G8795, RRID:AB_1078991). For the cycloheximide chase assay, the cells were treated with 10 μM cycloheximide and harvested at the indicated times.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was isolated from cells using the standard Trizol protocol. RNA-seq and bioinformatics analyses were executed by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. Briefly, the sequence data were processed and mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38.112) using HISAT2, followed by reference genome-guided transcriptome assembly and gene expression quantification using StringTie. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by DEseq2 with a cut-off value of log2|fold-change | > 1 and p-adjust < 0.05. The clusterProfiler [was used to perform functional enrichment analysis for the annotated significant GO, KOG and KEGG pathway categories. Terms with p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Mice

The animal experiments were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health Chinese Academy of Science (with approved ID SIBS-2023-WYX-1). All genetically engineered mice were of the C57BL/6 (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) background. Mkrn3pfl/m+ and Trp53fl/fl mice were generated as described for the conditional KO mouse model. K-rasLSL-G12D/+ mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (stock #008179, RRID:IMSR_JAX:008179). Mice were randomly assigned to groups using Excel’s =RAND()function to generate a random sequence. Blinding was not implemented during animal handling or outcome assessment.

Xenograft tumor model

ES-2 (2 × 106 cells) cells and CAOV3 (5 × 106 cells) cells transduced with the MKRN3 WT or control lentivirus were injected subcutaneously into 6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice, and the tumor xenografts were allowed to grow for 4–5 weeks for ES-2 cells and 6–7 weeks for CAOV3 cells. The resulting tumors were measured every 3 d for ES-2 cells and every 7 d for CAOV3 cells. Tumor volume was calculated according to the following formula: tumor volume = length × width × width/2. Once the largest tumor diameter reached the maximal tumor diameter allowed under our institutional protocol, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were collected. The maximal tumor diameter allowed by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee was 2.0 cm.

Conditional knockout mouse model

The protocol by which the mice with a floxed Mkrn3 exon 1 allele on the C57BL/6 background were generated is described below. We prepared a targeting construct including a 5.2 kb 5’ arm, a floxed fragment containing Mkrn3 exon 1 and a neomycin positive selection cassette flanked by flippase recognition target sites, a 6.1 kb 3’ arm, and a thymidine kinase negative selection cassette. JM8A3 C57BL/6 embryonic stem cells were electroporated with the targeting vector and selected via G418 and ganciclovir. Three clones were identified from 144 clones by long-range PCR. Clonally expanded embryonic stem cells were injected into C57BL/6 blastocysts to generate chimeric mice. Trp53fl/fl mice were generated using the same process in which Trp53 exons 5-7 were targeted. Mkrn3pfl/m+ and Trp53fl/fl mice were generated by crossing chimeric mice with Flp recombinase-expressing mice. MT mice were generated by crossing Mkrn3pfl/m+ male mice with Trp53fl/fl female mice. The ovary-specific KO assay was conducted according to the study by Dinulescu DM et al. [41]. Briefly, AdCre was injected into surgically exposed ovaries. The recombinant adenovirus carrying a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter was purchased from OBiO Technology Corp., Ltd., Shanghai. To synchronize ovulation, the animals were injected with 5 U of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (Ningbo Second Hormone Factory, Zhejiang Province, China), and 48 h later, they were injected with 5 U of human chorionic gonadotropin (Ningbo Second Hormone Factory, Zhejiang Province, China). The animals were subsequently given a single intrabursal injection of AdCre (2.5 × 107 plaque-forming units (p.f.u.)) to the right ovary only (the contralateral ovary served as a control) approximately 1.5 days later. The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation 12 months after activation.

Organoid culture and engineering

Fallopian tube epithelium (FTE) organoids from Mkrn3pfl/m+/ Trp53fl/fl / K-rasLSL-G12D/+/ Mkrn3pfl/m+, Trp53fl/fl / Mkrn3pfl/m+, K-rasLSL-G12D/+ mice were generated according to the method described by Ford et al. [42]. Briefly, oviducts from different genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) were surgically removed and minced into small pieces, followed by collagenase digestion. Single Fallopian tube epithelial cells were seeded in 2D cultures and infected with 1 × 105 p.f.u. AdCre. The cell pellets were harvested and seeded into Matrigel to establish 3D organoid cultures.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was performed on frozen tissue sections (5 μm in thickness). The cells were permeabilized in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 and blocked in PBS containing 1% BSA, 3% normal goat serum, and 0.2% Triton X-100. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, after which they were washed in PBS (3×, 10 min each), followed by incubation with DAPI (2 μg/ml) and Alexa 555-conjugated anti-rabbit or Alexa 647-conjugated anti-mouse antibodies, as indicated. After washing, the samples were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade reagent (Life Technology). The primary antibodies used were PAX8 (Proteintech Cat# 10970-1-AP, RRID:AB_10596918, 1:250) and acetylated tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# T7451, RRID:AB_609894, 1:250). The secondary antibodies used were as follows (all at 1:200): goat anti-mouse IgG, Alexa Fluor 647 conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# A28181, RRID:AB_2536165), and goat anti-rabbit IgG, Alexa Fluor 555 conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# A27039, RRID:AB_2536100).

Mouse pathology

Both ovaries were surgically removed and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde, fixed overnight, transferred to 70% ethanol, and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm and stained with H&E for pathological examination.

IP (immunoprecipitation) and mass spectrometry

The cells were lysed in IP buffer containing protease inhibitors (10 μg/ml leupeptin, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride), mixed with 2 μg of anti-Flag antibody and 20 μl of protein G-Sepharose (Thermo Fisher Scientific; #101242), incubated overnight, and eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer. The eluted samples were detected by SDS‒PAGE followed by Coomassie blue staining (Invitrogen; #LC6025; Colloidal Blue Staining Kit). Finally, IP samples were eluted by shaking with 8 M urea and 100 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, and analyzed by mass spectrometry. And the details of data analysis of mass spectrometry was referred to Domon et al. [43].

Coimmunoprecipitation

The cells were lysed with IP buffer containing protease inhibitors (10 μg/ml leupeptin, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride), mixed with 2 μg of anti-Flag antibody and 20 μl of protein G-Sepharose and incubated overnight. The immunoprecipitates were eluted by boiling with SDS loading buffer. IP samples and whole-cell lysates were analyzed by Western blotting.

Ubiquitination assay

293 T cells were transfected with MKRN3-WT or mutants, 3×Flag-CSDE1-WT, HA-Ub-WT, or 20 μM MG132 (Cat# HY-13259; MedChemExpress, USA), and whole-cell lysates were subsequently immunoprecipitated with 2 μg of anti-Flag antibody and analyzed by Western blotting with an anti-HA antibody.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, RRID: SCR_002798). The difference between two groups was analyzed by one-tailed or two-tailed unpaired T test or two-way ANOVA. The differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. The Kaplan–Meier estimation method was used for overall survival analysis, and a log-rank test was used to compare differences. Sample sizes were calculated using G*Power (version 3.1) with α = 0.05, 80% power, and effect sizes derived from preliminary experiments. For in vitro experiments, n = 3–5 biological replicates/group were used. For in vivo studies, n = 5 mice/group were randomized to ensure adequate power (≥80%) to detect a 40% difference in tumor growth.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

In vitro samples were excluded if contaminated (mycoplasma-positive) or showing >20% viability loss. In vivo, animals with tumor ulceration or non-tumor-related mortality were excluded. Criteria were pre-defined in the protocol (IACUC approved ID SIBS-2023-WYX-1).
