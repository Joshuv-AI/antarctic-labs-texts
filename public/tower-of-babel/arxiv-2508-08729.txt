Real-time forecasting of chaotic dynamics from sparse data and autoencoders Journal: Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering
Elise Özalp, Andrea Nóvoa, Luca Magri

Abstract
The real-time prediction of chaotic systems requires a nonlinear-reduced order model (ROM) to forecast the dynamics, and a stream of data from sensors to update the ROM. Data-driven ROMs are typically built with a two-step strategy: data compression in a lower-dimensional latent space, and prediction of the temporal dynamics on it. To achieve real-time prediction, however, there are two challenges to overcome: (i) ROMs of chaotic systems can become numerically unstable; and (ii) sensors’ data are sparse, i.e., partial, and noisy. To overcome these challenges, we propose a three-step strategy: (i) a convolutional autoencoder (CAE) compresses the system’s state onto a lower-dimensional latent space; (ii) a latent ROM (echo state network, ESN), which is formulated as a state-space model, predicts the temporal evolution on the latent space; and (iii) sequential data assimilation based on the Ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) adaptively corrects the latent ROM by assimilating noisy and sparse measurements. This provides a numerically stable method (DA-CAE-ESN), which corrects itself every time that data becomes available from sensors. The DA-CAE-ESN is tested on spatio-temporally chaotic partial differential equations: the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation, and a two-dimensional Navier-Stokes equation (Kolmogorov flow). We show that the method provides accurate and stable forecasts across different levels of noise, sparsity, and sampling rates. As a by-product, the DA-CAE-ESN acts as a localization strategy that mitigates spurious correlations, which arise when applying the EnKF to high-dimensional systems. The DA-CAE-ESN provides a numerically stable method to perform real-time predictions, which opens opportunities for deploying data-driven latent models.

2
Learning latent dynamics

Consider a nonlinear dynamical system evolving the state
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
according to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=\mathcal{F}\left(\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)\right),

(1)

where
\bm{x}
is defined on the domain
\partial D
, and the operator
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a smooth and nonlinear function, which, in fluid mechanics represents the Navier-Stokes operator.
Direct numerical simulation of such systems becomes computationally prohibitive due to the high dimensionality of
N
, which scales with the spatial resolution of
\bm{x}
.
Following prior work on reduced-order modelling of chaotic systems
[
76
,
33
,
37
,
29
]
, we adopt a hybrid architecture that combines a CAE with an ESN. In summary, the CAE compresses the high-dimensional flow data onto a nonlinear latent manifold, capturing key spatial features
[
27
]
, while the ESN models the temporal evolution of the low-dimensional latent variables. The two components are trained separately, as end-to-end optimization has not demonstrated superior forecasting accuracy and incurs higher computational cost
[
33
]
.
The overall structure of the CAE-ESN is illustrated Fig.
2
.
On the one hand,
autoencoders
[
77
,
78
]
are based on neural networks that learn efficient lower-dimensional encodings of high-dimensional input data, capturing the main features of the input
[
79
,
80
]
. In chaotic systems, such as the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation and the Kolmogorov flow, well-trained autoencoders have been shown to preserve key stability characteristics of the original dynamics
[
28
,
29
]
.
In this work, the CAE maps each flow snapshot to a compact latent representation, extracting essential spatial features, as detailed in §
2.1
. This process yields a low-dimensional time series, which is the input to the temporal evolution model.
On the other hand,
ESNs are a class of reservoir computing models suited for modelling chaotic fluid dynamics
[
8
,
81
, e.g.,]
. Their efficiency is enabled by a training procedure based on solving a linear least-squares problem, avoiding back-propagation. ESNs also naturally yield a nonlinear state-space formulation
[
48
]
, which facilitates data assimilation (§
3
) and enables data-driven stability analysis
[
82
,
83
,
29
]
.

,

2.1
Data compression with a convolutional autoencoder

An autoencoder approximates an identity mapping through two networks: an encoder and a decoder. The encoder
\mathcal{E}
maps the physical state
\bm{u}(t_{k})
to a latent state
\bm{y}(t_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{lat}}}
on the manifold, where
N_{\text{lat}}\ll N
. The decoder
\mathcal{D}
reconstructs the physical state from the latent representation by mapping back to the full state,

\displaystyle\bm{y}(t_{k})=\mathcal{E}\left(\bm{u}(t_{k})\right),\;\text{and }\;\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{y}(t_{k})\right)=\bm{\hat{u}}(t_{k})\approx\bm{u}(t_{k}),

(2)

where
\bm{\hat{u}}(t_{k})
is output of the autoencoder.
We use convolutional layers
[
85
]
in both
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{D}
to capture the spatial structure of the input
\bm{u}(t_{k})
, resulting in a CAE.
Each convolutional layer is followed by a
\tanh(\cdot)
activation function, as linear activation functions make the network equivalent to POD
[
86
,
27
]
.
For flow fields, we adopt a multi-scale CAE
[
87
]
, which has proven successful in generating latent representations for flow fields
[
31
,
37
]
.
The multi-scale CAE consists of three parallel encoders and decoders, but with a shared latent manifold. Each encoder-decoder pair is characterised by a different kernel size to learn spatial structures of different sizes, which are characteristic features of flows.
The CAE is trained to minimize the mean squared error (MSE)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{u},\bm{\hat{u}}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{\text{train}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}\|\bm{u}(t_{k})-\bm{\hat{u}}(t_{k})\|_{2}^{2},

(3)

where
N_{\text{train}}
is the number of training snapshots. Once trained, the autoencoder encodes the physical solution
\bm{u}(t_{k})
to the latent representation
\bm{y}(t_{k})
of it, resulting in the latent training data
\{\bm{y}(t_{k})\}^{N_{\text{train}}}_{k=1}
.
Further details on the implementation can be found in
A
, and for a detailed discussion on selecting an appropriate latent space dimension for chaotic systems, we refer to
[
33
,
37
,
29
]
.

2.2
Temporal prediction with an echo state network

Echo state networks (ESNs) are designed to learn the temporal dynamics of sequential data through a hidden high-dimensional reservoir state
\bm{r}(t_{k})
.
The time evolution of the reservoir state can be expressed in the state-space formulation, as explained by
[
48
]

\displaystyle\bm{r}(t_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\tanh\left([\bm{y}_{\text{in}}(t_{k});b_{\text{in}}]^{\top}\bm{\mathsf{W}}_{\text{in}}+\bm{r}^{\top}(t_{k})\bm{\mathsf{W}}\right).

(4)

where

\bm{y}_{\text{in}}
is the input to the network, which is augmented with the input bias
b_{\text{in}}
to break the symmetry of
\tanh
, and
[~;~]
indicates vertical concatenation.
The input is mapped to the reservoir via the input matrix
\bm{\mathsf{W}}_{\text{in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{lat}}+1\times N_{r}}
, which is a dense matrix whose entries are sampled from a uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}[-\sigma_{\text{in}},\sigma_{\text{in}}]
with input scaling
\sigma_{\text{in}}
.
The reservoir state
\bm{r}(t_{k+1})
retains information from the previous reservoir state
\bm{r}(t_{k})
through the state matrix
\bm{\mathsf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}\times N_{r}}
, which is an Erdös-Renyi sparse matrix with connectivity
c
, i.e., each row of
\bm{\mathsf{W}}
has only
c
non-zero elements sampled from
\mathcal{U}[-1,1]
and subsequently rescaled to a set spectral radius
\rho
.
The predicted latent state
\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k+1})
is computed through the linear readout

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\bm{r}^{\top}(t_{k+1})\bm{\mathsf{W}}_{\text{out}},

(5)

where

\bm{\mathsf{W}}_{\text{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}\times N_{\text{lat}}}
is the output matrix, which is the only trainable part of the ESN.
Training
\bm{\mathsf{W}}_{\text{out}}
consists of minimising the MSE between the network prediction
\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k})
and the reference data
\bm{y}(t_{k})
over the training dataset

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{y}_{\text{in}},\bm{\hat{y}}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{\text{train}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}\|\bm{y}_{\text{in}}(t_{k})-\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k})\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

Eq. (
6
) is a quadratic optimisation problem. Instead of backpropagation, the optimal solution is obtained by solving one ridge regression system

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathsf{R}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\top}+\beta\mathbb{I}_{N_{r}})\bm{\mathsf{W}}_{\text{out}}=\bm{\mathsf{R}}{\bm{\mathsf{Y}}}^{\top},

(7)

where

\bm{\mathsf{Y}}=[\bm{y}(t_{2}),\dots,\bm{y}(t_{N_{\text{train}}})]
is the training data,

\bm{\mathsf{R}}=[\bm{r}(t_{2}),\dots,\bm{r}(t_{N_{\text{train}}})]
is computed via Eq. (
4
) using the training data
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}_{\text{in}}=[\bm{y}(t_{1}),\dots,\bm{y}(t_{N_{\text{train}}-1})]
as input,

\mathbb{I}_{N_{r}}
is the identity matrix of size
N_{r}
,
and
\beta
is the Tikhonov regularization parameter. The hyperparameters
\{\beta
,
\rho
,
\sigma_{\text{in}}\}
are optimized with a grid search followed by a Bayesian optimisation to maximize short-term prediction on the different intervals of the validation data (see
A
).

Figure 1
:
Echo state network architecture. (a) Compact representation of the open-loop and closed-loop mode based on the network input and (b) unfolded representation illustrating closed-loop steps.

The reservoir can operate in two configurations: open-loop and closed-loop, which differ in
\bm{y}_{\text{in}}
, as depicted in Fig.
1
. When input data is available, the network propagates in open-loop, such that
\bm{y}_{\text{in}}(t_{k})=\bm{y}(t_{k})
, which is an encoded flow field snapshot.
The network runs in open-loop during training and during the initialization phase, i.e., the washout (see §
4
for details).
Once trained, the ESN runs autonomously in closed-loop, such that the predicted latent state becomes the input to the next step, i.e.,
\bm{y}_{\text{in}}(t_{k})=\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k})
, where
\bm{\hat{y}}
is forecasted via Eq. (
5
). To obtain the full state forecast, the latent prediction is decoded as
\bm{\hat{u}}(t_{k})=\mathcal{D}(\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k}))
.

2.3
Discussion

By construction, there are limitations to the CAE-ESN architecture. The encoder requires the full system snapshot to map the physical state into the latent space.
Once the input has been encoded, the ESN evolves autonomously, and noise is propagated through the autonomous forecast. New information can only be incorporated through the open-loop mode, when new latent inputs are provided.
The CAE-ESN model described in §
2
offers a computationally efficient approach to forecasting high-dimensional dynamical systems and yields accurate and stable short-term predictions across quasi-periodic and chaotic regimes
[
33
,
37
,
29
, e.g.,]
.
Long-term autonomous forecasting, particularly in chaotic regimes, often becomes numerically unstable in the long term
[
33
,
37
,
29
]
, which is exacerbated when data is noisy.
The onset of instabilities appears to depend on a combination of factors, including the network architecture, the network initialization, and the complexity of the system’s attractor. These effects are likely network- and system-specific, as evidenced by observations across different studies in the literature
[
88
,
89
,
44
,
37
]
(see also footnote
1
).
In Section
3
, we propose a solution by introducing the data-assimilated CAE-ESN (DA-CAE-ESN).

3
Real-time modelling via data assimilation

We deploy a sequential data assimilation method to correct the forecast using observations, which may be noisy and sparse. Specifically, we focus on the Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF) because its sequential nature and Monte Carlo approximation make it well-suited for real-time forecasting
[
54
,
61
,
63
]
. The proposed methodology is pictorially illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2
:
Illustration of the proposed DA-CAE-ESN framework, which integrates a convolutional autoencoder, an echo state network and an ensemble Kalman filter with Kolmogorov flow data. The CAE encodes an ensemble of initial snapshots into a latent state
\bm{y}
at a time
t_{1}
via the encoder
\mathcal{E}
.
In the latent space, the ESN forecasts in closed-loop an ensemble of predicted latent states
\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{j})
until observations become available (here, for illustration at
t=t_{3}
). Then, the EnKF combines the partial and noisy observations
\bm{d}
with the ensemble of reservoir states
\bm{r}_{j}^{f}
, resulting in the analysis ensemble
\bm{r}^{a}(t_{j})
. The updated ESN can continue to forecast in closed-loop. The decoder
\mathcal{D}
recovers the prediction of the full flow field
\bm{\hat{u}}(t_{j})
from any
\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{j})
(here, for illustration at
t=t_{4}
).

Sequential data assimilation consists of iteratively repeating the following steps:
(i) the forecast step, in which the model evolves the state forward in time, and
(ii) the analysis step, in which new measurements are assimilated to update the state estimate.
The updated state estimate is the new initial condition for the next forecast step.

We formulate the sequential data assimilation problem from a Bayesian perspective (§
3.1
) and present the ensemble Kalman filter (§
3.2
).
We define a general framework that, albeit demonstrated on the CAE-ESN, applies broadly to other data-driven ROMs with recurrent components. The approach operates directly on both the model output and the recurrent states, making it easily adaptable to other architectures such as the POD-ESN
[
90
]
, autoencoder–LSTM
[
33
]
, or variational autoencoder-transformer
[
39
,
41
]
.

3.1
Data assimilation for reduced order models

In data-driven reduced order modelling, we approximate (
1
) as

\displaystyle\bm{r}(t_{k+1})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\bm{r}(t_{k}))

(8a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{u}}(\bm{x},t_{k+1})

\displaystyle=f_{2}(\bm{r}(t_{k+1}))+\bm{\epsilon}_{f},

(8b)

where

\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}}
is the state vector of the ROM,

f_{1}
is the surrogate model that forecasts the ROM state vector approximating the true system dynamics, and

f_{2}
is the decoding that maps the ROM state to the physical state. The term
\bm{\epsilon}_{f}
represents the error introduced with the ROM approximation (i.e., by
f_{2}
) and by discretizing the system in time
t_{k}
and in space
\bm{x}=[x_{1};\dots;x_{N}]
.
Specifically, for the CAE-ESN model deployed in this work,

\bm{r}
is the ESN reservoir state,

f_{1}
is the reservoir Eq. (
4
), and
the decoding function is
f_{2}\left(\bm{r}\right)=\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{r}^{\top}\bm{\mathsf{W}}_{\text{out}}\right)
from Eqs. (
2
) and (
5
).

As discussed in §
1
, existing methods combining data assimilation and reduced order models either assume access to full-state observations or rely on data preprocessing techniques to encode partial measurements. However, in practice, especially for high-dimensional and chaotic systems, only sparse and spatially limited observations are available.
To address this challenge, we propose a method to correct the latent state directly using an augmented state-space formulation
[
63
,
91
,
92
, e.g.,]
.
Let
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{obs}}}
denote a measurement operator that maps the full state to observable quantities, yielding measurements of the form

\bm{d}(\bm{x},t_{k})=\mathcal{M}\left(\bm{u}(\bm{x},t_{k})\right)+\bm{\epsilon}_{d}.

(9)

where
\bm{\epsilon}_{d}
is measurement noise,
and
N_{\text{obs}}\ll N
reflects the limited number of observation points.
We define the augmented state vector
\bm{\psi}=\left[\bm{r};\mathcal{M}\left(\bm{\hat{u}}\right)\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\psi}=N_{r}+N_{\text{obs}}}
, such that the measurement operator in augmented state-space
\bm{\mathsf{M}}=\left[\bm{\mathsf{0}}_{N_{\text{obs}}\times N_{r}};\mathbb{I}_{N_{\text{obs}}}\right]
is linear and the model prediction of the measurements is

\bm{\mathsf{M}}\bm{\psi}=\mathcal{M}\left(\bm{\hat{u}}\right)
.
This augmented state-space approach effectively facilitates the assimilation of partial measurements through the decoder
[
90
,
92
]
.

Our objective is to exploit the information from the sparse sensors to update the predictions from the ROM in real time.
Here, we update the reservoir state of the ESN as this yields faster convergence and accuracy than updating the ESN’s prediction on the latent state
[
90
]
.
To find the
analysis
state, i.e., the state that best captures the evolution of the system given the surrogate model
\bm{F}=[f_{1};f_{2}]
and the noisy, partial observations
\bm{d}
, we seek a Bayesian maximum
a posteriori
estimator.
First, the state
\bm{\psi}
and observations
\bm{d}
are assumed to be realizations of their corresponding random variables.
The confidence is quantified by a probability density function
\mathcal{P}
.
Second, we assume that our model is Markovian, i.e., our prior on the state follows

\mathcal{P}\left(\bm{\psi}(t_{k})|\bm{\psi}(t_{k-1}),\bm{F}\right)=\mathcal{P}\left(\bm{\psi}(t_{k})|\bm{\psi}\left(t_{1}\right),\dots,\bm{\psi}(t_{k-1}),\bm{F}\right)
.
Third, we assume that the observations
\bm{d}(t_{k})
are conditionally independent given the current state
\bm{\psi}(t_{k})
, meaning that the measurement errors are independent and uncorrelated in time. Thus, our
prior
can be updated using the Bayes’ rule
[
93
]

\mathcal{P}\left(\bm{\psi}(t_{k})\mid\bm{d}(t_{k}),\bm{F}\right)\propto\mathcal{P}\left(\bm{d}(t_{k})\mid\bm{\psi}(t_{k}),\bm{F}\right)\mathcal{P}\left(\bm{\psi}(t_{k})\mid\bm{\psi}(t_{k-1}),\bm{F}\right),

(10)

where
\mathcal{P}\left(\bm{d}(t_{k})\mid\bm{\psi}(t_{k}),\bm{F}\right)
is the likelihood of the observations, i.e., how consistent the predicted state
\bm{\psi}(t_{k})
is with the new observation
\bm{d}(t_{k})
; and
\mathcal{P}\left(\bm{\psi}(t_{k})\mid\bm{d}(t_{k}),\bm{F}\right)
is the resulting
posterior
distribution. Fourth, we assume a Gaussian prior (i.e., the forecast) and likelihood functions with
covariances

\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\psi\psi}^{f}
and
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{dd}
, such that the posterior remains Gaussian and the analysis state can be computed as

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}^{a}=\underset{\bm{\psi}}{\text{arg min}}~{\mathcal{P}\left(\bm{\psi}\mid\bm{d},\bm{F}\right)}=\underset{\bm{\psi}}{\text{arg min}}~{\mathcal{J}\left(\bm{\psi}\right)},

(11)

where the cost function to minimize is

\mathcal{J}(\bm{\psi})=(\bm{\psi}^{f}-\bm{\psi})^{\top}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\psi\psi}^{f})^{-1}(\bm{\psi}^{f}-\bm{\psi})+\left(\bm{d}-\bm{\mathsf{M}}\bm{\psi}\right)^{\top}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{dd}^{-1}\left(\bm{d}-\bm{\mathsf{M}}\bm{\psi}\right).

(12)

For nonlinear
\mathcal{M}
or
f_{2}
, the augmented state formulation ensures the measurement operator remains linear in the
\bm{\psi}
space, preserving the Gaussian update structure.

3.2
The ensemble Kalman Filter

The ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) approximates the
prior
and
posterior
distributions via Monte Carlo sampling, i.e., by generating
N_{\text{ens}}
instances (ensemble members) of the ROM.
The EnKF offers a computationally-cheap alternative to the Kalman filter for nonlinear models
[
54
,
94
]
.
In the ensemble framework, each ensemble member represents a different realization of the nonlinear model, thereby capturing nonlinear error growth propagating the full covariance matrix. This makes the EnKF advantageous for high-dimensional systems.
Unlike other assimilation methods such as the extended Kalman filter or the 4D-Var
[
95
]
, the EnKF effectively handles nonlinearities without computing tangent linear operators or linearizing the forecast model.

The ensemble statistics approximate the expectation and covariance of the model predictions as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{\psi}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\overline{\bm{\psi}}=\dfrac{1}{N_{\text{ens}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{ens}}}\bm{\psi}_{j},

(13)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\psi\psi}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{rr}&\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{r\hat{u}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\hat{u}r}&\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\hat{u}\hat{u}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\approx\dfrac{1}{N_{\text{ens}}-1}{\left(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{f}-\overline{\bm{\psi}}\bm{1}_{N_{\text{ens}}}^{\top}\right)\left(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{f}-\overline{\bm{\psi}}\bm{1}_{N_{\text{ens}}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}},

(14)

where
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=[\bm{\psi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\psi}_{N_{\text{ens}}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\psi}\times N_{\text{ens}}}
denotes the collection of ensemble members and
\bm{1}_{N_{\text{ens}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{ens}}}
is a column vector of ones.
Additionally, under linear-Gaussian assumptions, the ensemble approximation preserves the first two moments exactly, which ensures consistency with the Kalman filter in the limit of infinite ensemble size.
At every assimilation step, each ensemble member
\bm{\psi}_{j}^{f}
is updated by minimizing the cost function (
12
), which has the analytical solution

\bm{\psi}_{j}^{a}=\bm{\psi}_{j}^{f}+\underbrace{\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\psi\psi}^{f}\bm{\mathsf{M}}^{\top}\left(\bm{\mathsf{M}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\psi\psi}^{f}\bm{\mathsf{M}}^{\top}+\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{dd}\right)^{-1}}_{\text{Kalman gain}}\left(\bm{d}_{j}-\bm{\mathsf{M}}\bm{\psi}_{j}^{f}\right),\;\text{for}\;j=1,\dots,N_{\text{ens}}.

(15)

Each ensemble is updated with a different
\bm{d}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{d},\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{dd}\right)
to avoid underestimating the analysis covariance
[
96
]
.
The EnKF (
15
) can written in matrix form as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{r}^{a}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{r}^{f}+\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{r\hat{u}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{dd}+\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\hat{u}\hat{u}}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{\mathsf{D}}-\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{\hat{u}}^{f}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{r}^{f}\left[\mathbb{I}_{m}+\frac{1}{N_{\text{ens}}-1}\left(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{f}_{\hat{u}}\right)^{\top}\left(\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{dd}+\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\hat{u}\hat{u}}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{\mathsf{D}}-\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{f}_{\hat{u}}\right)\right]

(16)

where
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{r}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{\hat{u}}
are the rows of the ensemble
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
containing the reservoir states
\bm{r}_{j}
and the observables
\mathcal{M}\left(\bm{\hat{u}}_{j}\right)
, respectively,
and
\bm{\mathsf{D}}=[\bm{d}_{1},\dots,\bm{d_{N_{\text{ens}}}}]
.
This EnKF formulation avoids the storage of
\mathcal{O}(N_{\psi}^{2})
matrices, reducing the cost to
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{\text{ens}}\times N_{r}\right)
.
For further details on the EnKF and data assimilation, we refer to
[
53
,
97
,
98
]
.

4
DA-CAE-ESN implementation

The DA-CAE-ESN framework is algorithmically implemented as follows.

1.

CAE-ESN set-up

(a)

CAE training:
Given training data
\{\bm{u}(t_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}
, train the CAE to
minimize the MSE loss (Eq. (
2
)). This yields the low-dimensional latent representation of the training dataset
\bm{y}(t_{k})=\mathcal{E}(\bm{u}(t_{k}))\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{lat}}\ll N}
for
k=1,\dots,N_{\text{train}}
.
The physical state is approximated as
\bm{\hat{u}}(t_{k})\approx\mathcal{D}(\bm{y}(t_{k}))\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

(b)

ESN training
: Given the training latent trajectories
\{\bm{y}(t_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}
, train the ESN to learn the temporal mapping
\bm{y}(t_{k})\to\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k+1})
.

(c)

Assimilation-free forecast
:
Initialize
\bm{r}(t_{0})=\bm{0}
and perform
N_{\text{wash}}
open-loop steps using the encoded snapshots as input. Next, run the ESN in closed-loop for a transient time and decode the latent trajectory to obtain the physical state forecast.

2.

Initialization

(a)

Ensemble initialization

\{\bm{\psi}_{j}^{f}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{ens}}}
: In physics-based models, ensemble members are generated by adding uncertainty to the equation-based model state and parameters using physical information
[
99
, e.g.,]
. Here, we construct an ensemble of latent states using perturbed initial conditions or by randomly sampling states from the training set.

(b)

Reservoir washout
: Each reservoir is initialized as
\bm{r}_{j}(t_{0})=\bm{0}
. Then, for each ensemble member, we compute the reservoir for
N_{\text{wash}}
open-loop steps, using the encoded snapshots from the ensemble initialization. The washout phase is not used for forecasting but it drives the reservoir state towards the system’s attractor. This mitigates the influence of the zero initialization and stabilises the subsequent forecast.

3.

Data assimilation cycle
(Repeat until convergence/criteria met)

(a)

Forecast step
: Integrate each ensemble member through the nonlinear model

\bm{\psi}_{j}^{f}(t_{k+1})=\bm{F}(\bm{\psi}_{j}^{a}(t_{k}))
, for
j=1,\dots,N_{\text{ens}}
, until observations
\bm{d}
become available.
This operation can be parallelized for computational efficiency.

(b)

Analysis step
: Sample
\bm{d}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{d},\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{dd}\right)
and obtain the analysis ensemble via the EnKF (
15
). We select
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{dd}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma\bm{u}_{\text{max}})
, where
\bm{u}_{\text{max}}
is the element-wise maximum of
\{\bm{u}(t_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}
.

(c)

Inflation
(applied when necessary):
To mitigate ensemble covariance underestimation (i.e., covariance collapse) due to e.g., finite ensemble size, we apply additive covariance inflation

\bm{\psi}_{j}^{a}=\overline{\bm{\psi}}^{a}+\rho_{I}\left(\bm{\psi}_{j}^{a}-\overline{\bm{\psi}}^{a}\right),\quad\text{for }j=1,\dots,N_{\text{ens}}

where
\rho_{I}\geq 1
is inflation factor
[
53
]
. We use
\rho_{I}=1.1
or
1.05
in selected cases; most cases proceed without inflation, i.e.,
\rho_{I}=1.0
.

(d)

Re-initialization:
The analysis ensemble members
\{\bm{\psi}_{j}^{a}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{ens}}}
are the initial conditions for the next forecast step.

4.

Decoding and post-processing
:
At any time step
t_{k}
of the assimilation cycle, the physical flow field can be decoded from the latent state forecast.

(a)

Mean reservoir state
: The best estimate in the EnKF is the ensemble mean. Thus, we first compute the mean of reservoir states
\overline{\bm{r}}(t_{k})=\frac{1}{N_{\text{ens}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{ens}}}\bm{r}_{j}(t_{k})
.

(b)

Decoding
: To find the physical state prediction from
\overline{\bm{r}}
, we compute the latent state with the readout (
5
) as
\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k})=\bm{\mathsf{W}}_{\text{out}}\overline{\bm{r}}(t_{k})
, and then decode
\bm{\hat{u}}(t_{k})=\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\hat{y}}(t_{k})\right)
.

(c)

Visualization and analysis
: Generate visualizations and statistical metrics to assess the quality of the forecast, which we evaluate with the mean absolute error (MAE) at each time step
t_{k}
, defined as

\displaystyle\text{MAE}_{k}(\bm{u},\bm{\hat{u}})=\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{u}\left(\bm{x},t_{k}\right)-\bm{\hat{u}}\left(\bm{x},t_{k}\right)\|_{1},

(17)

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
refers to the
\ell_{1}
norm. The MAE over a given time interval is obtained by averaging
\text{MAE}_{k}
across the respective time steps.

In sections §
5
and §

, we show the DA-CAE-ESN on the Kuramoto-Shivasinky equation and the 2D Navier-Stokes, respectively.

5
Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation

The Kuramoto-Sivashinsky (KS) equation is a one-dimensional partial differential equation (PDE) that describes diffusion-induced spatio-temporal chaos
[
100
]
. For a range of paratmeters, the dynamics of the KS equation have persistent, chaotic behaviour over space and time, providing a canonical example of spatiotemporal chaos relevant to a wide range of fluid flow phenomena
[
101
]
,
such as flame front propagation
[
102
]
,
instabilities of thin liquid films flowing down an inclined plane
[
103
]
,
or diffusion-induced chaos in reactive systems

[
104
]
.
Writing
\bm{u}=\bm{u}\left(t,\bm{x}\right)
, the KS equation is given by

\bm{u}_{t}+\bm{u}_{xx}+\bm{u}_{xxxx}+\bm{u}\bm{u}_{x}=0,

(18)

with periodic boundary conditions
\bm{u}\left(t,0\right)=\bm{u}\left(t,L\right)
on the domain
[0,L]
. The spatial domain is discretized as
\bm{x}=[x_{1};\dots;x_{N}]
. The KS system is chaotic for
L\geq 22

[
100
,
105
]
. Here, we consider the chaotic KS equation with
L=20\pi
, for which the system has a Lyapunov exponent
\lambda\approx 0.08

[
29
, e.g.,]
. The Lyapunov exponent
\lambda
defines a characteristic timescale of predictability
[
106
]
,
\tau_{\lambda}=\lambda^{-1}\approx 12.5
, which quantifies a scale over which two nearby trajectories diverge. We discretize the system with 128 degrees of freedom and solve it with a fourth-order spectral solver for stiff PDEs
[
107
]
.

We train the CAE-ESN with
N_{\text{lat}}=24
and
N_{r}=10,000
, following the configuration of
[
29
]
.
Next, we initialize an ensemble with
N_{\text{ens}}=100
by selecting random washout windows from the training data. Each washout window is first encoded into the latent space and used for the warm-up phase of the reservoir of the ESN ensemble. From these random initializations on the attractor, the ESN ensemble is evolved in closed-loop within the latent space for one
\tau_{\lambda}
.
Then, we start the data assimilation loop.

6
2D Navier-Stokes

Although the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation captures essential features of spatio-temporal chaos, fluid systems have higher dimensional dynamics.
In this section, we consider the Kolmogorov flow, a model for two-dimensional turbulence. The flow evolves on a doubly periodic domain
[0,L_{x}]\times[0,L_{y}]
and it is forced with an amplitude per unit mass of flow
\chi
.
The Kolmogorov system is governed by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations and is non-dimensionalized using the length scale
L_{y}/2\pi
and the time scale
\sqrt{L_{y}/(2\pi\chi)}
. This rescaling transforms the domain to
[0,2\pi L_{x}/L_{y}]\times[0,2\pi]

[
109
]
.
In this work, we consider the domain
\Omega=[0,2\pi]\times[0,2\pi]
and
\chi=1
in the non-dimensionalization, such that the Reynolds number is
Re=1/\sqrt{\nu}
.
With this, the equations governing the Kolmogorov flow are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}

\displaystyle=-\nabla p+\nu\nabla^{2}\bm{u}+\bm{g},

(19)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{u}

\displaystyle=0.

(20)

Here,
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the velocity field,

p
is the pressure,

\nu
is the kinematic viscosity,
and the external forcing is

\bm{g}\left(x\right)=\sin(4y)\bm{e}_{1}-\alpha\bm{u}
,
where
\bm{e}_{i}
denotes the standard basis of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and

\alpha
is the amplitude of the velocity-dependent drag term
[
110
,
111
]
.
The average energy dissipation
D
and the local dissipation rate
d(\bm{x},t)
are defined as

\displaystyle D(\bm{u},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\Omega}d(\bm{u},\bm{x},t)d\bm{x},\;\text{and}\;\;d(\bm{u},\bm{x},t)=\frac{1}{Re}\|\nabla\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)\|^{2}.

(21)

We test the DA-CAE-ESN framework on two cases in the Kolmogorov flow:
(i) a quasiperiodic solution of the system for
Re=30,\alpha=0.0

[
37
]
(§
6.1
) and
(ii) a 2D chaotic solution of the system at
Re=34,\alpha=0.1
(§
6.2
).
To solve Eq. (
19
), we use JAX-CFD (
google/jax-cfd
), which implements a finite volume scheme with a second-order time integration method
[
111
]
.
The vorticity, defined as
\bm{\omega}=\nabla\times\bm{u}
, is sampled as snapshots on a
48\times 48
grid using a solver time step of
0.01
and subsequently downsampled to
\Delta t=0.1
. Table
1
includes further details on the simulations’ parameters.
Although Lyapunov times can be defined in the Kolmogorov flow, we employ the non-dimensionalised time-units (TUs) because the instantaneous or finite-time Lyapunov exponents have significant fluctuations in chaotic systems, i.e., up to three times the average Lyapunov exponent
[
112
,
113
]
.
As a result, the mean Lyapunov exponent does not necessarily provide a representative scale of the short-term growth rate of perturbations, particularly in regimes for which the dynamics are intermittent.

Conclusion

Real-time forecasting of high-dimensional chaotic systems typically requires reduced-order models (ROMs). A strategy to design ROMs is to employ autoencoders, which nonlinearly compress the state into a lower-dimensional latent space. Autoencoder-based ROMs have proved successful in a large spectrum of applications; however, they have two limitations: (i) they may become numerically unstable in chaotic regimes; and (ii) they do not naturally handle sparse and noisy measurements in real-time (i.e., they are offline methods).
To address this, we develop the data-assimilated convolutional autoencoder echo state network (DA-CAE-ESN, with psuedoalgorithm in Sec.
4
). This method consists of a three-step strategy: (i) a convolutional autoencoder (CAE) to compress the high-dimensional state into a latent space; (ii) an echo state network (ESN) to propagate the dynamics within the latent space; and (iii) an augmented state-space sequential data assimilation framework (specifically, the ensemble Kalman Filter) to update the ESN forecast by assimilating sparse and noisy measurements into the latent space. The DA-CAE-ESN provides accurate short- and long-term forecasts and reconstructs the full system state without full-state information during assimilation.
We test the DA-CAE-ESN framework on two spatio-temporally chaotic systems to compare accuracy, robustness and stability against an assimilation-free CAE-ESN model. First, we validate the framework on the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation, in which predictions from the assimilation-free model diverge from the ground truth. Using as few as 25 measurements, the DA-CAE-ESN achieves a negligible error, which enables long-term forecasts. Robustness is tested across different sampling frequencies, numbers of sensors, and noise levels.
Second, we test the DA-CAE-ESN on the two-dimensional Navier-Stokes equation (Kolmogorov flow) in both quasi-periodic and chaotic regimes. In the quasi-periodic regime, assimilating measurements from 2% of the grid points in the full flow field enables accurate long-term predictions of the vorticity field and dissipation rate. In the chaotic regime, the DA-CAE-ESN provides stable forecasts with correct long-term statistics, whereas the assimilation-free CAE-ESN becomes unstable (Fig.
13
). The robustness of the DA-CAE-ESN is tested against three sensor placement strategies: uniformly random sampling, equidistant placement on a regular grid, and proper orthogonal decomposition. All three sensor placements yield small errors.
The augmented state assimilation acts as a localization strategy, which mitigates spurious correlations that arise in high-dimensional systems. The proposed framework can be employed in other autoencoder-based ROMs, e.g., with transformers and recurrent neural networks, which opens opportunities for real-time prediction of chaotic systems.
