Determinants of depression help-seeking intentions in Xizang, China: leveraging the Andersen model for mental health services utilization improvement
Authors: Ruixue Zhao, Yongzhu Lacuo, Zhaoyu Li, Zhaxi Dawa, Wenhua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression, a highly prevalent mental health disorder, exerts profound global and economic impacts. In China, rapid industrialization, post-COVID-19 challenges, and evolving social structures have collectively increased depression incidence. Despite evidence-based therapies, substantial treatment gaps persist, particularly due to low utilization of mental health services among individuals with depressive symptoms. Notably, research on help-seeking intentions in Xizang Autonomous Region’s unique cultural context remains scarce. This gap hinders understanding of mechanisms underlying mental health service underutilization and the development of targeted interventions. Thus, this study aims to comprehensively explore factors influencing help-seeking intentions among Xizang residents.MethodA cross-sectional study was carried out in October and December 2023. Using systematic random sampling, 1,374 residents from the Shannan, Nyingchi, and Qamdo regions of Xizang were surveyed. Data collection was achieved through face-to-face interviews with structured questionnaires, grounded in the Andersen Behavioral Health Services Use Model. Statistical analyses encompassed descriptive statistics, univariate analyses, and binary logistic regression to discern significant influencing factors.ResultsThe results indicated that 748 (54.4%) of the participants manifested negative help-seeking intentions (i.e., a lower likelihood of seeking professional help for depression problems), while 626 (45.6%) exhibited positive intentions. Notably, among those meeting the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) criteria for moderate-to-severe depression (prevalence = 15.1%), only 34.3% (71/207) demonstrated help-seeking intentions. Multiple factors were significantly associated with help-seeking intentions. Predisposing factors included age (p < 0.05), gender (β = 0.27, p = 0.041), education level (p < 0.05), attitude towards depression (β = 0.13, p < 0.001), and perceived stigma (β=-0.09, p = 0.020). Key enabling/hindering factors were subsistence allowances (β=-0.64, p = 0.008), access to higher-level healthcare facilities (β = 0.49, p = 0.010), received family physician services (β = 0.76, p < 0.001), and geographical location (p < 0.001). For needs factors, both insomnia symptoms (p < 0.05) and depression symptoms (β=-0.06, p < 0.001) were significant. Among these, enabling factors had the most robust association, followed by predisposing and needs factors.ConclusionsThese findings not only provide a comprehensive understanding of the determinants of depression help-seeking intentions among Xizang residents but also underscore the imperative for targeted interventions to surmount barriers to mental health service utilization in Xizang. Overall, this study accentuates the urgent necessity of formulating and implementing targeted interventions to dismantle the obstacles to mental health care access in Xizang, thereby contributing to the improvement of mental health outcomes in this region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02679-w.

Introduction

Background

Depression ranks as a highly prevalent mental health disorder, affecting an estimated 280 million people worldwide [1], approximately one in every 20 individuals [2]. This condition not only undermines the quality of life but also increases vulnerability to other health problems, imposing substantial social and economic costs on both individuals and societies [3–5].

In China, the rapid industrialization, the sluggish economic recovery in the post- COVID era, and the transformation of traditional social structures have significantly contributed to a burgeoning mental health crisis. Among these, depression has emerged as a particularly pressing issue [6]. Depression- related medical expenses account for 14.7% of China’s total personal healthcare spending [7], straining individual finances and detrimentally impacting overall productivity, thus becoming a pivotal concern for public health and economic development [8].

Among different regions in China, Xizang presents unique characteristics in terms of mental health issues. According to [9], an online survey involving 101,392 residents from 31 provinces in mainland China revealed striking differences in the prevalence of depressive symptoms. Nationally, it was 15.8%, while in Xizang, it was alarmingly high at 23.7%. The elevated prevalence of depressive symptoms in Xizang compared to the national average can be attributed to multiple interconnected factors. First, biological factors associated with chronic hypoxia at high altitudes may contribute to depression severity, as chronic low-pressure conditions can lead to changes in dopamine and serotonin generation in the brain, both of which are closely linked to depressive symptoms [10]. Additionally, the harsh natural environment characterized by geographical isolation, extreme UV radiation exposure, and cold, dry climate conditions creates additional stressors for mental health [11]. Furthermore, Xizang faces substantial socioeconomic challenges including poor transportation infrastructure, limited communication systems, and restricted access to medical services, particularly in remote pastoral areas [12]. These compounding biological, environmental, and socioeconomic factors collectively contribute to the disproportionately high burden of depression among Xizang residents.

Despite the well-established availability of effective, evidence-based treatments for depressive disorders [13], the prevalence of individuals actively seeking assistance for these symptoms remains disappointingly low. For example, within a single year, merely one-third of Americans diagnosed with a depressive disorder reported approaching a mental health professional for symptoms [14]. In Germany, despite diverse support services, only around half of those with depressive symptoms initiate contact with professional help, and this low help-seeking behavior persists even in regions with high healthcare resource density [15]. Globally, more than 75.2% of patients in low-and middle - income countries do not have access to treatment for mental disorders, despite the existence of efficacious treatment modalities [1].

In the context of depression management in China, there are additional challenges. For instance, patients often show reluctance to accept a depression diagnosis [16]. This situation is further complicated by cultural factors and social stigma [17–19]. Therefore, to further improve mental health care, a fundamental factor that cannot be overlooked is patients’ intention to seek help [20]. It serves as the cornerstone for effective mental health interventions, and without it, even the most well-equipped facilities and highly-trained medical staff may not be able to fully address the mental health issues of the patients.

Framework

Given the global prevalence of low help-seeking behavior for depression, there is a critical need to investigate the multifaceted determinants influencing individuals’ intentions to seek professional mental health services. Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Service Use (BMHSU) has emerged as a highly relevant and widely applied framework in health service research. The BMHSU offers several distinct advantages over alternative theoretical frameworks for investigating health service utilization. First, its comprehensive tripartite structure systematically integrates individual, contextual, and clinical factors, providing a more holistic understanding than single-domain models. Second, the model’s hierarchical organization allows researchers to assess the relative contribution of different factor categories, thereby identifying which intervention targets may yield the greatest impact on service utilization. Third, unlike purely medical models that focus solely on clinical need, or behavioral models that emphasize only individual attitudes, the BMHSU explicitly incorporates structural barriers (enabling factors) and their interaction with individual characteristics, making it particularly valuable for examining healthcare disparities in resource-limited settings [21, 22]. Finally, the model’s extensive empirical validation across diverse populations and healthcare contexts enhances its generalizability and practical applicability for policy development.

The BMHSU comprises three key dimensions: predisposing factors, enabling factors, and need factors [23]. Predisposing factors encompass individual characteristics such as demographic attributes, social structures, and health beliefs, which collectively influence an individual’s propensity to seek help. Enabling factors refer to the logistical and resource-based facilitators or barriers to service access, including income and the availability of health services. Finally, need factors capture both the perceived and clinically evaluated health needs, such as the severity of depressive symptoms and their impact on daily functioning [24]. By integrating these dimensions, the BMHSU offers a holistic lens through which to analyze the persistent disparity between the prevalence of depressive disorders and the low rates of professional help-seeking behavior.

The BMHSU has been widely applied in mental health research to explore factors influencing service utilization across diverse populations, such as Latinos with fatalismo beliefs [25], men with depression [26], postpartum women [27], and emerging adults from racial/ethnic minority groups [28]. However, despite its extensive use, several limitations persist in the current literature. First, most studies have predominantly focused on developed regions [28, 29], leaving a significant gap in understanding the model’s applicability in unique contexts such as Xizang, where geographic, cultural, and socioeconomic factors may profoundly shape mental health service use. Furthermore, our review of the existing literature reveals a scarcity of policy-relevant investigations focusing on the impact of specific initiatives, such as the family practice physician contracting system, on mental health service accessibility in regions like Xizang. This highlights a critical knowledge gap regarding which policies may be most effective in bridging the region’s healthcare disparities. These limitations highlight the need for context-specific adaptations of the BMHSU to better address the unique challenges faced by populations in understudied and resource-limited settings.

There is a notable scarcity of mental health research within the unique sociocultural and geographical context of the TAR. As documented by Xie et al. (2018) [30], there exists a general lack of literature introducing mental health in Tibet, especially in English, resulting in limited international knowledge about mental health conditions in the region. This knowledge gap presents a significant challenge for public health, as the drivers of mental health service utilization in this population are poorly understood. Without a clear understanding of whether barriers to care are primarily predisposing (e.g., personal beliefs, stigma), enabling (e.g., healthcare access, cost), or related to health needs, it is difficult to formulate effective and culturally-appropriate interventions.

To address this foundational gap, our study applies the Andersen Behavioral Model in a large-scale quantitative analysis of this population. Our primary objective is to answer a critical question: Which dimensions of the BMHSU—predisposing, enabling, or needs factors—are most strongly predictive of the willingness to seek help for depression among Xizang residents? By identifying the most influential domains, our findings will provide an evidence-based foundation for prioritizing and designing targeted policies to enhance the accessibility and uptake of mental health services in Xizang.

Methods

Study design and sampling

A cross-sectional, in-person survey was conducted between October and December 2023 in the Shannan, Nyingchi, and Qamdo regions of the Xizang. The study was designed to focus exclusively on the ethnic Xizang population, who constituted over 90% of the Xizang’s 3.65 million residents in 2021 [31]. To ensure the sample was representative of typical Xizang communities, the highly urbanized capital, Lhasa, and the geographically extreme Ngari prefecture were intentionally excluded. The target population included permanent residents, defined as individuals living in the area for more than six months.

The required sample size was determined to be 1,374 participants. This was calculated based on an estimated 24% prevalence of depressive symptoms from prior research [9], a 95% confidence level, a 3% margin of error, a design effect (DEFF) of 1.5 to account for the complex sampling design, and an adjustment for a 15% non-response rate.

A multi-stage, stratified random sampling method was employed. First, leveraging the sampling frame of the 2023 National Health Service Survey, sample counties, townships, and villages were randomly selected within the three chosen regions [32, 33]. Second, at the village level, a complete list of all permanent resident households was compiled, and households were selected using systematic random sampling. The sampling interval (k) for each village was determined by dividing the total number of households by the required sample size for that village. Following a random start, every k-th household was selected to participate.

This process yielded a final sample of 1,374 individuals, all of whom were of Tibetan ethnicity (Shannan: n = 622; Nyingchi: n = 150; Qamdo: n = 602). Interviews were conducted face-to-face by trained graduates from the Medical College of Xizang University using a computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) system to ensure data quality. All participants were fully briefed on the study’s purpose and provided written informed consent prior to the interview.

Measures and variables

Dependent variable

Participants’ help-seeking intentions regarding depressive symptoms were assessed using the Attitudes Toward Seeking Professional Psychological Help Scale—Short Form (ATSPPH-SF), a widely validated and extensively utilized instrument in primary care settings. This scale comprises two dimensions: openness to seeking professional help for emotional problems and perceived value and need of professional help [34, 35].

Following consultations with Xizang cultural experts and consideration of sociocultural realities specific to the local Xizang population, the measurement focused exclusively on the dimension of openness to seeking professional psychological help for emotional problems. This dimension was operationalized through three items: “If I believed I was having a mental breakdown, my first inclination would be to get professional attention.“, “I would want to get psychological help if I was worried or upset for a long period of time.” and “A person with an emotional problem is not likely to solve it alone; he or she is likely to solve it with professional help.”

Responses were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (disagree) to 3 (agree), yielding a total score between 0 and 9. Higher scores reflected stronger proactive help-seeking intentions. A cut-off score of > 6 was established to differentiate positive help-seeking intentions (scores > 6) from negative intentions (scores ≤ 6), consistent with standard clinical interpretation guidelines for this scale. In the current study, this dimension demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.813).

Independent variable

Guided by the Andersen Behavioral Model, the independent variables were systematically categorized into three principal domains: predisposing factors, enabling/hindering factors, and needs factors.

Predisposing Factors. This domain encompassed both demographic characteristics and Social-psychological variables that may associated with healthcare-seeking behavior. Demographic characteristics included Age, Gender, Marital status, whether lived alone in past 6 months, Education level, Employment status. Social-psychological variables, specifically Cognition and Attitude Toward Depression and Perceived Stigma, were measured using relevant subscales from the Attitudes to Depression and its Treatment (ADepT) questionnaire [36]. The “Cognition and Attitude Toward Depression” construct was evaluated using three items: “People with depression deserve substantial support from friends and family,” “Depression is a medical condition similar to other illnesses,” and “Anyone can experience depression.” The “Perceived Stigma” construct was assessed with two items: “Depression indicates weakness” and “If others knew I had been depressed, they would think less of me.” Responses for both constructs were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree [5] to strongly disagree [1]. In the current study, these scales demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cognition and Attitude Toward Depression: Cronbach’s α = 0.713; Perceived Stigma: Cronbach’s α = 0.674).

Enabling/Hindering Factors. These variables represented structural and resource-related determinants of healthcare access: total household income, subsistence allowance status, type of nearest healthcare facility, distance to nearest facility, family practice physician contracting status, contact with family practice physician services in the past year, regional location, and urban-rural location. Subsistence allowance status was assessed as a binary variable (yes/no), indicating whether the household was a recipient of the national minimum living security allowance as certified by the Department of Civil Affairs.

Needs Factors. This domain captures the perceived and evaluated health needs of the individual. Depressive symptom was assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a validated instrument aligned with DSM-IV criteria [37]. The Chinese version of the PHQ-9 has demonstrated strong psychometric validity [38], and a cutoff score of ≥ 10 is commonly used to define clinically significant depression [39, 40].In the current study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.779). Anxiety symptom was measured using the 2-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-2) scale, a brief and robust screening tool [41]. A cutoff score of ≥ 3 was applied to indicate probable anxiety disorders [41, 42]. The scale also showed good reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.783). Other needs factors included the limitation of daily activities due to health issues over the past six months, the frequency of Insomnia Symptoms experienced in the previous two weeks and self-rated depression or anxiety. Limitation of daily activities due to health was assessed by asking participants to what extent health problems had limited their daily activities over the past 6 months, with the response options: ‘Not limited,’ ‘Limited, but not severe,’ and ‘Severely limited.’

Statistical analysis

Prior to the main analysis, the internal consistency reliability of the key psychometric scales used in the study was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, were calculated to summarize the characteristics of the 1,374 respondents. The final sample consisted of 1,374 participants, and a completeness check confirmed that there was no missing information for the variables included in the analyses.

Univariate analyses were conducted to examine the distribution of help-seeking intentions across different groups based on participants’ characteristics. Specifically, independent sample t-tests were performed for continuous variables, and χ² tests were utilized for categorical variables to investigate differences in help-seeking intentions among various subgroups.

To identify the determinants of help-seeking intentions regarding depression, binary logistic regression analysis was utilized, as it is appropriate for dichotomous outcome variables [43].

Four Logit models were constructed for analysis:

Model I: Logit (Yi) = predisposing factors + enabling variables.

Model II: Logit (Yi) = predisposing factors + needs factors.

Model III: Logit (Yi) = enabling variables + needs factors.

Model IV: Logit (Yi) = predisposing factors + enabling variables + needs factors.

Model performance was evaluated using key indices: -2 Log Likelihood (-2LL), Cox & Snell R², and Nagelkerke R². Change values of these indices relative to Model IV were calculated. This approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of different variable combinations’ contributions to the model, aiding in selecting the most suitable one for further analysis.

Prior to model fitting, we assessed multicollinearity among all predictors using variance inflation factors (VIFs). All VIF values were below the conservative threshold of 5 (mean VIF = 1.73; range: 1.06–3.61), indicating that multicollinearity did not unduly influence the estimates. To ensure the robustness of our variable selection and to mitigate any potential bias, we supplemented our theoretical approach with a statistical method. A backward stepwise regression (with a variable removal criterion of p > 0.05) was performed on the complete set of candidate variables from all three domains (predisposing, enabling, and needs). All data analyses were conducted using STATA 17.0 software, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the respondents

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for the 1,374 respondents. The age of the respondents ranged from 12 to 89 years, with the majority falling between 36 and 59 years old (60.8%, n = 835). The gender distribution was nearly equal, with 49.3% identifying as men and 50.73% as women. A significant proportion of respondents (83.7%, n = 1,15) were married, and the majority (86.8%) reported not living alone in the past six months. Additionally, 45.1% of respondents were illiterate, and 83.1% were employed. The average score for cognition and attitude toward depression was 9.37 (SD = 2.13), with a range of 3 to 15. The average score for perceived stigma associated with depression was 5.696 (SD = 1.62), with a range of 2 to 10.

The largest income group consisted of households earning less than Renminbi (RMB) 30,000 annually, accounting for 47.2% of respondents. A significant proportion of respondents (91.9%) did not receive subsistence allowances. Most respondents (86.9%) lived in close proximity to primary care facilities, with only 13.0% residing near secondary or tertiary hospitals. Regarding the distance to the nearest healthcare facility, 39.6% of respondents lived within one kilometer. In terms of family practice physician contracting services, the majority of respondents (70.7%) had registered for a family practice physician. Furthermore, 64.2% of respondents reported having contacted or received family practice physician services in the past year. Participants were primarily from Shannan (45.3%), Nyingchi (10.9%), and Qamdo (43.8%), with the sample distribution reflecting the population density of these regions.

Regarding limitations in daily activities due to health, 81.3% of respondents reported no limitations. Additionally, 77.9% had no insomnia symptoms in the past two weeks. Self-rated depression or anxiety, had an average score of 1.114 (SD = 0.37); the average PHQ-9 score was 4.041 (SD = 4.67), with a range of 0 to 24. The average GAD-2 score was 0.583 (SD = 1.20), with a range of 0 to 6.

Univariate analysis results

Table 1 presents the univariate analysis of variables associated with Help-Seeking Intentions Regarding Depression. A total of 1,374 respondents participated in the analysis, with 748 (54.4%) showing negative help-seeking intentions and 626 (45.6%) showing positive intentions. According to the PHQ-9 criteria for moderate to severe depression (score ≥ 10), the prevalence of depressive symptoms among respondents was 15.1%. Notably, among individuals with depressive symptoms, only 71 displayed positive help-seeking intentions. This finding suggests that merely one-third of participants with depressive symptoms reported positive help-seeking intentions.

Predisposing factors

The mean score for cognition and attitude was significantly higher among those with positive help-seeking intentions (mean = 9.78, SD = 1.79) compared to those with negative intentions (mean = 9.03, SD = 2.32) (t = -6.57, p < 0.001).

Enabling/hindering factors

A significant difference was found based on household income (χ² = 10.77, p = 0.013), suggesting that lower income groups may exhibit different patterns in help-seeking intentions. Those receiving a subsistence allowance were more likely to have negative help-seeking intentions (χ² = 6.86, p = 0.009). A significant association was observed between family practice physician contracting services and help-seeking intentions (χ² = 75.99, p < 0.001), with a higher proportion of respondents who had signed up for a family practice physician showing positive help-seeking intentions. Additionally, a significant difference was found among those who had contacted or received services from a family practice physician in the past year (χ² = 82.02, p < 0.001), with those having received services more likely to demonstrate positive help-seeking intentions. There was also a significant regional difference in help-seeking intentions (χ² = 30.52, p < 0.001). Significant differences were observed between urban and rural/pastoral respondents (χ² = 38.37, p < 0.001).

Needs factors

A significant relationship was found between limitations in daily activities due to health and help-seeking intentions (χ² = 9.68, p = 0.008). Individuals with the most severe limitations demonstrated the lowest proportion of positive intentions. There was a significant relationship between insomnia symptoms and help-seeking intentions (χ² = 8.48, p = 0.037), indicating that the presence of insomnia may be associated with an individual’s help-seeking intentions. A significant difference was observed in PHQ-9 scores between the two groups (t = 5.72, p < 0.001). Respondents with positive help-seeking intentions had lower PHQ-9 scores (mean = 3.26, SD = 4.39) compared to those with negative intentions (mean = 4.69, SD = 4.79).

Predisposing factors

Age showed a significant association with help-seeking intentions. Compared to individuals aged 12–35, those aged 36–59 were 1.46 times as likely to have positive help-seeking intentions (OR = 1.46, p = 0.040), and those aged 60–89 were 1.68 times as likely (OR = 1.68, p = 0.035). Women were 1.31 times as likely to have positive help-seeking intentions compared to men (OR = 1.31, p = 0.041). Furthermore, compared to illiterate individuals, those with a high school or technical education were 3.16 times as likely to have positive help-seeking intentions (OR = 3.16, p = 0.013). Positive cognition and attitude toward depression are strongly associated with an increased likelihood of seeking help (OR = 1.21, p < 0.001). Higher level of perceived stigma associated with depression was associated with an 8.9% decrease in the likelihood of seeking help (p = 0.020).

Enabling/hindering factors

Households receiving subsistence allowances were less likely to seek help for depression compared to those not receiving such allowances (OR = 0.53, p = 0.008). Individuals whose nearest healthcare facilities were secondary or tertiary hospitals were 1.63 times as likely to seek help for depression compared to those whose nearest facilities were primary care centers (OR = 1.63, p = 0.010). Individuals with family practice physician contracting services were 1.92 times as likely to seek help for depression compared to those without such services (OR = 1.92, p = 0.012). Furthermore, individuals who had contacted or received family practice physician services in the past year were 2.14 times as likely to seek help for depression compared to those who had not (OR = 2.14, p < 0.001). Individuals residing in Nyingchi were 2.37 times as likely to seek help for depression compared to those in the reference region, Shannan (OR = 2.37, p < 0.001). Finally, individuals living in rural or pastoral areas were 2.13 times as likely to seek help for depression compared to those living in urban areas (OR = 2.13, p < 0.001).

Needs factors

surprisingly, individuals with less severe (OR = 3.52, p = 0.004) or no limitations (OR = 2.87, p = 0.011) in daily activities were more inclined to seek help for depression than those who are severely limited. Furthermore, individuals experiencing insomnia symptoms for more than half the days were less likely to seek help compared to those without insomnia symptoms (OR = 0.50, p = 0.043). The depression symptoms, was negatively associated with help-seeking intentions. For each unit increase in the PHQ-9 score, the odds of seeking help decrease by 6.1% (p < 0.001).

Model comparisons revealed a clear hierarchy. The incremental improvement in fit from the baseline Model I to the full Model IV, which encompasses all predisposing, enabling, and needs factors, was statistically significant (p < 0.001) and substantively meaningful. This progression, evidenced by the significant reduction in the − 2 Log Likelihood (-2LL) and the increase in R² values, underscores the necessity of integrating all three factor types for a comprehensive explanation of help-seeking intentions. Among the factor blocks, enabling variables contributed the most to the model’s explanatory power (ΔNagelkerke R² = 0.142), followed by predisposing factors (ΔNagelkerke R² = 0.054), while needs factors had the least impact (ΔNagelkerke R² = 0.031).

The full model (Model IV) demonstrated a good fit to the data. This conclusion is supported by multiple indices: a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test result (χ² [8] = 8.80, p = 0.360) indicated excellent calibration between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. The model explained a moderate proportion of the variance (Nagelkerke R² = 0.26), which is consistent with studies on complex health behaviors. Furthermore, the model showed practical utility with an overall classification accuracy of 70.1%. Notably, its higher specificity (74.9%) compared to sensitivity (64.4%) suggests it is particularly effective at identifying individuals with negative help-seeking intentions, a key group for targeted public health intervention.

Robustness validation

To further validate our main findings, we performed a backward stepwise logistic regression as a robustness check. The results are presented in Supplementary Table S1. The stepwise procedure retained 15 of the original predictors, including nearly all variables found to be significant in our main model (Model IV). The strong concordance between the main model and the stepwise model confirms the stability and robustness of our findings, reinforcing the validity of the identified determinants of help-seeking intentions.

Univariate versus multivariate findings

An important methodological consideration is why several variables—specifically age group, gender, and perceived stigma—were non-significant in univariate tests but emerged as significant predictors in the final multivariate model. This phenomenon often reflects suppression effects or confounding, where the true relationship between a predictor and an outcome is masked by its correlation with other variables in a crude analysis (Vittinghoff et al., 2012). For instance, the effect of age was likely suppressed by its correlation with education and employment status. Once these factors were controlled for in the multivariate model, the independent influence of life experience associated with older age became apparent. Similarly, the effect of gender was clarified once enabling factors like healthcare access were accounted for. These findings underscore the importance of multivariate modeling in disentangling complex predictors and highlight a key strength of the Andersen model [44].

Discussion

Main findings

Our study of 1,374 Xizang individuals reveals critical insights into mental healthcare engagement patterns, with only 45.6% demonstrating positive help-seeking intentions—a figure that drops to just one-third among those meeting clinical depression criteria. The robust performance of our comprehensive Andersen model (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.360; Nagelkerke R²=0.26; specificity = 74.9%) demonstrates that help-seeking intentions are associated with dynamic interplays among predisposing, enabling, and needs factors. Notably, structural enabling factors—particularly family physician contracting (OR = 1.92, 95% CI 1.15–3.21) and proximity to advanced hospitals (OR = 1.63, 95% CI 1.13–2.36)—showed the strongest associations, highlighting the fundamental role of healthcare accessibility in this context. These findings carry important policy implications, suggesting that infrastructure development may be associated with improved service utilization patterns.

The model’s good fit and predictive validity revealed several important patterns: while positive attitudes and lower stigma (predisposing factors) were associated with increased help-seeking intentions, greater clinical need (higher PHQ-9 scores) was associated with decreased likelihood of seeking care (6.1% reduction per unit increase). This discrepancy for the most severely affected individuals, combined with the model’s unexplained variance, underscores the limitations of quantitative approaches. The findings emphasize the need for mixed-methods research to examine the unmeasured cultural and experiential factors that shape help-seeking in this unique context.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings on predisposing factors both align with global trends and reveal dynamics unique to the Xizang context. The greater help-seeking intention among women, older adults, and the more educated is consistent with broad patterns in mental health literature, which often attribute these trends to gender roles [45, 46], life experience [47], and higher health literacy [45, 48]. This further validates the findings of our study and highlights the importance of considering age and gender factors when addressing mental health issues.

Our findings on the powerful role of perceived stigma and attitudes, while consistent with broader literature [34], offer crucial nuances specific to the Xizang context. The reluctance to seek services despite high need parallels patterns in Western ethnic minority studies [49], but the underlying drivers appear different. While nationally a minority, Xizang residents are the demographic majority within their autonomous region. This “local majority” status suggests that help-seeking behaviors are shaped less by external majority-minority power relations and more by intra-community dynamics. Consequently, the robust negative association with stigma likely stems not from external discrimination, but from the fear of devaluation within one’s immediate, predominantly intra-ethnic social circle. Conversely, the powerful positive effect of favorable attitudes toward depression highlights a key opportunity: in such “local majority” contexts, interventions targeting culturally-grounded health literacy and positive belief systems may prove especially effective. This highlights how the interplay of cultural values and community structures can produce help-seeking patterns that differ meaningfully from those of dispersed minority populations.

The paramount importance of enabling factors in our study highlights that structural, economic, and relational elements are critical determinants of help-seeking in Xizang. Economic hardship clearly acts as a barrier, as shown by the negative correlation between receiving subsistence allowances and help-seeking intentions, a finding consistent with studies on resource limitations [50]. In parallel, structural access to what is perceived as higher-quality care is a powerful facilitator; individuals living near secondary or tertiary hospitals, or in regions like Nyingchi with greater tourism-related economic development, demonstrated significantly higher intentions to seek help.

Even more influential than structural access, however, was relational access built on trust. The strong positive association with family physician services underscores that personalized, continuous primary care plays a crucial role in promoting mental health utilization. This aligns with extensive research emphasizing that a trust-based, patient-provider relationship is key to overcoming help-seeking barriers [47, 50–52]. This insight also helps explain one of our most surprising findings: that rural and pastoral residents showed higher help-seeking intentions than their urban counterparts, contradicting general trends [48]. This paradox suggests that in the Xizang context, the closer, more culturally-attuned relationships with local village doctors—who are often embedded community members—outweigh the material advantages of urban medical centers. It underscores that for enabling factors, a healthcare system perceived as accessible and trustworthy is ultimately more important than physical infrastructure alone.

Regarding needs factors, our analysis yielded several interesting findings. First, the relationship between limitations in daily activities and help-seeking intentions was not monotonic; individuals with moderate or no limitations showed higher help-seeking intentions compared to those who were severely limited. Individuals with moderate or no limitations were more likely to seek help, possibly because they retained a higher sense of capacity or self-efficacy to take action on their mental health. Conversely, the striking reluctance of the most severely limited individuals to seek help can be explained by the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation. This theory posits that individuals’ coping actions are guided by their mental representation of a health threat, particularly its most tangible and disruptive consequences [53]. For participants experiencing severe functional limitations, the challenges of performing daily activities are the most salient consequences. Consequently, their self-management efforts are likely prioritized towards these immediate physical needs, while mental health concerns, which may be perceived as secondary, are deferred.

Insomnia symptoms reduced the likelihood of seeking help. One possible reason is that insomnia is a common symptom experienced by many people in daily life, and individuals may initially attribute it to temporary stress or lifestyle factors rather than a serious mental health problem. Finally, for each unit increase in the PHQ-9 score, which measures the severity of depression symptoms, the odds of seeking help decreased by 6.1%. As the severity of depression symptoms increases, individuals may experience more intense feelings of hopelessness and helplessness [50]. Moreover, severe depression may also lead to social withdrawal, where individuals isolate themselves from others and become less likely to reach out for support. The stigma associated with more severe mental illness may also play a role, as individuals with higher PHQ-9 scores may be more reluctant to admit their condition.

Public health and policy implications

This study’s findings provide an evidence-based roadmap for policymakers to address mental health service gaps and reduce barriers to care in Xizang. In response to the growing burden of mental health disorders, while the Chinese government has rolled out several initiatives such as the National Mental Health Work Plan (2015–2020) and the Work Plan for Exploring Innovative Depression Prevention and Treatment Services (2020), Xizang confronts unique implementation challenges due to its geographical remoteness, economic underdevelopment, and healthcare provider shortages [54, 55]. To address the inequalities identified in our research, we propose the following targeted interventions:

Healthcare System Strengthening and Equitable Resource Allocation. Our findings indicate significant geographical disparities: proximity to secondary/tertiary hospitals and residing in certain regions like Nyingchi were associated with higher help-seeking intentions. To bridge this urban-rural and inter-regional divide, government investment is urgently needed to expand specialized mental health services beyond urban centers. We recommend establishing mental health units in county-level hospitals and optimizing the layout of medical resources to ensure equitable access for all residents, regardless of their location.

Family Physician System Enhancement as a Gateway to Care. The strong positive association between family physician services and help-seeking intentions was a key finding Therefore, policy should focus on accelerating the implementation and quality improvement of the family physician contracting system. This includes providing adequate training for primary care teams to identify and manage depression, offering incentives for providing mental health services, and promoting public trust in family doctors as the first point of contact for mental health concerns.

Targeted Campaigns for Stigma Reduction and Health Education. Our model confirmed that stigma is a major barrier, while positive attitudes are a key facilitator. Furthermore, we identified specific demographic groups with lower help-seeking intentions, including men and individuals with less education. Therefore, we recommend launching culturally-appropriate mental health awareness campaigns using Tibetan-language media and community leaders. These initiatives must go beyond general awareness and specifically target these vulnerable groups to effectively reduce stigma and address health inequalities.

Integrating Mental Health into Social Welfare to Address Socioeconomic Barriers. The finding that subsistence allowance recipients were less likely to seek help reveals how poverty compounds mental health disparities. Policy should focus on integrating mental health support with social welfare programs, including providing subsidized mental health services for low-income populations and embedding mental health screening within existing social assistance frameworks.

Implementation of Systematic Screening Programs. A critical finding was that individuals with more severe depression symptoms (higher PHQ-9 scores) were paradoxically less likely to seek help, highlighting the inadequacy of passive self-identification approaches. We strongly urge the implementation of systematic depression screening in primary care settings. This population-level strategy is crucial for early identification and intervention, ensuring that those most in need receive timely care, particularly given that systematic depression screening can boost diagnosis and treatment rates [56–58].

Strength and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. It employed Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Service Use to the context of help-seeking intentions regarding depression in the Xizang region validated its applicability in a unique cultural and geographical setting. The study focused on the Xizang population, which is relatively under-studied in terms of mental health help-seeking behavior. Given the region’s unique cultural, geographical, and socioeconomic characteristics, understanding the factors influencing help-seeking intentions among Xizang residents is crucial. By filling this research gap, the study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of mental health service utilization across different populations in China and globally. This focus on an under-represented group also has the potential to inform culturally sensitive mental health interventions and policies tailored to the specific needs of the Xizang people.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional nature of our design inherently limits causal inference due to temporal ambiguity bias, as both exposure and outcome variables were assessed simultaneously. While we identified significant associations between various factors and help-seeking intentions, the inability to establish temporal precedence means these relationships should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Future longitudinal studies tracking participants over time would help elucidate the temporal sequencing and potential causal pathways underlying these associations.

An important consideration is the reliance on self-reported data, which may be subject to several forms of bias despite our implementation of quality control measures. Participants may have underreported depressive symptoms due to social desirability bias, particularly given the cultural stigma surrounding mental health issues in this population. Additionally, recall bias could have affected the accuracy of reported symptom duration and help-seeking intentions. Incorporating clinician-rated measures and behavioral observations in future research would help mitigate these limitations.

The geographical concentration of our sampling frame may have introduced selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. While we included participants from three distinct regions of Xizang, certain subgroups such as nomadic pastoralists or residents of extremely remote areas may be underrepresented. The time-bound nature of data collection also means we might have missed transient populations. Expanding future studies to employ more comprehensive sampling strategies across Xizang’s diverse geographical and cultural landscape would enhance the representativeness of findings.

Additionally, our prevalence estimate of depression (15.1%) was lower than the 23.7% reported by a national online survey [9]. This discrepancy is likely attributable to key methodological differences. The previous study’s online convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, whereas our study used a rigorous, multi-stage random sampling with in-person interviews in primarily rural and pastoral regions, deliberately excluding the capital, Lhasa. Furthermore, our use of trained, local Tibetan-speaking staff for face-to-face interviews may have yielded different response patterns (e.g., due to social desirability bias) compared to an anonymous online format. Therefore, our prevalence estimate should be interpreted as a robust finding for the specific populations in our sampling frame, rather than an estimate for the entire Xizang.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing help-seeking intentions regarding depression among Xizang individuals, highlighting the hierarchical significance of enabling, predisposing, and needs factors. The findings underscore the necessity of targeted interventions to address barriers to mental health service utilization. Key strategies include promoting mental health awareness through culturally tailored media and community initiatives, optimizing the allocation of medical resources in remote areas, strengthening the family doctor system through enhanced training and incentives, and implementing systematic depression screening programs for high-risk populations. These measures, grounded in empirical evidence, have the potential to improve mental health outcomes and foster well-being within the Xizang community. Future research should focus on evaluating the effectiveness of these interventions and addressing the diverse needs of specific subgroups to ensure equitable access to mental health services.
