The Oral Microbiome: A Silent Contributor to Systemic Health and Disease National Institutes of Health 2024 Workshop
Authors: Gabriela Riscuta, Anil Wali, Emmanuel F. Mongodin, Mukesh Verma, Marco Cardone, Hye-Sook Kim, Tamara McNealy, Altaf Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreviously considered primarily only in the context of dental diseases, the oral microbiome is now recognized as a contributor to a variety of systemic diseases, including cancer and cardiovascular diseases. This commentary explores the evolving view of the oral cavity as a gateway to the body’s broader physiologic networks, implicating oral microbiome dysbiosis in a spectrum of chronic conditions. Drawing on current evidence, we propose a re-envisioned healthcare model that integrates oral and systemic health and outline research gaps and clinical priorities to harness the oral microbiome for preventive and therapeutic gains, emphasizing cancer-relevant biomarkers and intervention opportunities.

Introduction

On June 25, 2024, the National Cancer Institute (NCI), National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research, National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, and National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health convened a one-day virtual workshop open to the public on the topic: “Oral Microbiome in Health and Systemic Diseases.” The workshop assembled experts from diverse disciplines to discuss current knowledge about interactions among oral bacteria, viruses, fungi, and the host and identify gaps for research on chronic diseases and cancer (1). This commentary will outline important knowledge gaps and challenges identified during the workshop which brought together leaders from several disciplines. Discussions highlighted the need for a better understanding of immune modulation, inflammation, and microbial metabolites of the oral microbiome as potential cancer drivers, acknowledging recent evidence that microbial detection in tumors may be less prevalent than previously thought (2). Emerging questions focus on multiomics microbial risk scores, integrative biomarkers, and longitudinal cohort designs to establish causal roles of the oral microbiome in systemic diseases.

The workshop acknowledged key questions, including the need to identify specific biomarkers for early disease detection, linking oral microbiome dysbiosis to systemic diseases. The workshop also identified the need for improved approaches, analytic and computational tools, and clinical studies and methodologies to analyze the connections between the oral microbiome and systemic diseases. For example, the development of a microbial risk score that integrates multiomics data and relevant metadata using artificial intelligence/machine learning (ML) approaches could offer personalized prevention strategies, particularly for cancer and inflammatory diseases affecting distant sites (such as the heart, lungs, joints, or brain). Future research must address these challenges through large-scale studies, longitudinal cohort designs, and innovative model systems that better reflect human diseases. These efforts will pave the way for effective interventions targeting the oral microbiome for the prevention and mitigation of systemic health risks.

In this commentary, we reflect on the implications of this expanding field, consider unresolved controversies, and propose a future in which oral microbiome science informs integrative models of health and disease. Three scientific sessions were prefaced by introductory remarks from Dr. Robert H. Shoemaker, Division of Cancer Prevention, at NCI, and invited keynote lecture by Dr. George Hajishengallis, University of Pennsylvania.

Rethinking the Role of the Oral Microbiome

The human oral cavity hosts one of the most diverse microbial communities in the body, comprising more than 700 bacterial species alongside fungi, viruses, and archaea (3). For decades, these microbes were primarily associated with local pathologies—dental caries and periodontitis being the canonical examples. Yet mounting evidence now places the oral microbiome at the intersection of systemic diseases, including chronic inflammatory diseases, challenging long-standing silos between dentistry and medicine. This paradigm shift is not merely academic. Dysbiosis within the oral cavity has been linked to cardiovascular disease, lung diseases, diabetes, adverse pregnancy outcomes, rheumatoid arthritis, and even neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer disease (4–6). These associations suggest that the mouth may act as both a mirror and a mediator of systemic inflammation (4–6). Historically, the oral microbiome was viewed through a reductionist lens—specific pathogens, such as Streptococcus mutans or Porphyromonas gingivalis, were blamed for specific diseases. Although this model yielded practical interventions, it obscured the broader ecological dynamics at play. The current model of “polymicrobial synergy and dysbiosis” reframes oral disease as the outcome of disrupted microbial communities interacting with host immunity (7). What if the effects of this dysbiosis reach beyond the oral cavity? Chronic inflammation, transient bacteremia, and microbial metabolites are just a few of the proposed conduits through which the oral microbiome may affect systemic physiology. For instance, P. gingivalis not only disrupts periodontal tissue but also invades endothelial cells, contributing to atherogenesis (8), or can migrate to the lungs to worsen chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (9). Similarly, its ability to express peptidylarginine deiminase (PAD) enzymes links it to the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis (10).

Mechanisms and biomarker opportunities in systemic diseases

The oral microbiome comprises a complex ecosystem of bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses that extend its influence well beyond the oral cavity (11, 12). Dysbiosis—defined as a disruption in microbial community structure and function—has been implicated in multiple systemic diseases through interconnected inflammatory, immune, metabolic, and carcinogenic pathways (2, 12–15). These mechanisms offer opportunities for biomarker development and clinical translation (Table 1).

In oncology, mechanistic evidence increasingly supports a functional role for oral microbiota in carcinogenesis. Dysbiotic microbial communities may promote cancer development through chronic inflammation, immune evasion, genotoxic stress, and production of carcinogenic metabolites (20–26). F. nucleatum has been shown to bind epithelial receptors such as E-cadherin, activating β-catenin, and NF-κB signaling pathways that enhance cellular proliferation and inflammatory signaling (20–22). Pancreatic tumor cell proliferation is enhanced by P. gingivalis, in part, by activation of the Akt signaling cascade (23). P. gingivalis contributes to colorectal cancer progression by activating the hematopoietic NLRP3 inflammasome and MAPK/ERK signaling pathway (24–26).

The oral microbiome represents a promising source of non-invasive biomarkers for disease risk, progression, and therapeutic response. Distinct oral microbial profiles have been associated with systemic diseases including cancer. Increased abundance of F. nucleatum correlates with colorectal cancer risk and prognosis, whereas taxa such as Streptococcaceae, Veillonellaceae, and Lactobacillaceae families are associated with dismal prognosis and immunoresistance in melanoma and lung cancer (27–29). Salivary and plaque-based microbiome profiling offers a scalable, patient-friendly approach for early detection and risk stratification.

Host inflammatory and immune responses provide complementary biomarker opportunities. Salivary cytokines, immunoglobulin profiles, and immune cell signatures reflect host–microbiome interactions and have the potential to help monitor autoimmune disease activity and potentially early cancer detection (30, 31).

Both modifiable behaviors (diet, smoking, alcohol use, oral hygiene, physical activity, and medication exposure) and nonmodifiable factors (race, ethnicity, age, and geography) shape oral microbial composition and resilience (Fig. 1). Diet is a particularly strong determinant; high consumption of processed and ultraprocessed foods is associated with reduced microbial diversity and enrichment of pro-inflammatory taxa, whereas fiber-rich, minimally processed diets support more stable and beneficial communities (32–34). These interactions likely contribute to interindividual variability in disease susceptibility and may partially explain observed health disparities.

Oral microbiome dysbiosis. Host and environmental factors—such as diet, smoking, hygiene, aging, and geography—shape oral microbiome composition and activity. Disruption of this microbial equilibrium causes dysbiosis, triggering localized or systemic inflammation and immune responses that can cascade into systemic pathogenic effects.

Current Gaps: Discussion and Controversies and Future Needs

Despite increasing correlations associating the oral microbiota to a host of systemic diseases, the workshop acknowledged that significant scientific gaps still persist, particularly with regard to causality. Do oral pathogens drive systemic inflammation, or are they passengers in a body already under immunologic duress? Many studies are either cross-sectional or observational, which restrict the ability to draw inferences.

Furthermore, the oral microbiome is not a uniform entity—it varies widely by site (tongue vs. gingiva), by individual, and across time. Most studies rely on single-point cross-sectional sampling or limited taxonomic resolution. There is a need for longitudinal human cohorts and mechanistic studies to clarify the directionality. Leveraging large-scale health surveys, EHRs, and real-world data is critical to accelerating discovery and improving generalizability across diverse populations. Expanding routine oral health and microbiome sampling within healthcare systems will enable integration of oral microbial data with clinical, behavioral, environmental, and nutritional information. Translating microbiome science into public health impact will require population-level strategies to maintain a healthy oral microbiome, including preventive oral care, dietary and nutritional interventions, and targeted therapeutics. The integration of metagenomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics could reveal functional signatures of dysbiosis with greater precision (35).

Although inflammation, immune dysregulation, microbial metabolites, and microbial translocation are central mechanisms linking the oral microbiome to systemic disease, the relative contributions of specific taxa, microbial networks, and host factors remain incompletely defined. The field has shifted from a single-pathogen paradigm toward a polymicrobial synergy and dysbiosis model, emphasizing community-level interactions and functional outputs (36).

Future progress will depend on integrating multiomics approaches including metagenomics, metabolomics, and host transcriptomics within well-designed longitudinal cohorts and interventional studies. Rigorous validation of oral microbial signatures as biomarkers for disease risk and therapeutic response, particularly in oncology, would be beneficial. Addressing these challenges require interdisciplinary collaboration across microbiology, immunology, nutrition, epidemiology, oncology, and clinical medicine, as well as closer integration of dental and medical care.

Concern pertains to clinical translation. Although microbial biomarkers demonstrate potential for the early identification of systemic diseases, including diabetes and cardiovascular conditions, they have not been integrated into standard practice. Microbiome-targeted therapies—probiotics, small-molecule inhibitors, or vaccines—remain in early stages of development. Clinical and public health implications of oral–systemic links are profound. Oral health, long ignored in systemic disease management, may have the potential to become high-impact target for diagnosis or intervention. Periodontal therapy has already been shown to improve glycemic control in a small clinical trial enrolling patients with diabetes (37). A meta-analysis of 34 clinical studies indicates that periodontal therapy is associated with an improvement in glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes, and the effects were sustained at 3, 6, and 12 months after treatment (38).

Periodontal disease is a known risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes. A meta-analysis showed that treatment during pregnancy reduced the risk of preterm birth rate and improved infant birth weight, remaining a topic of ongoing interest among both dentists and obstetricians (39). The debate remains between periodontists and obstetricians about the timing of the treatment.

A more integrated model—in which dentists and physicians share data, co-manage chronic diseases, and develop joint preventive strategies—would accelerate progress.

Conclusion

The oral microbiome is no longer a niche topic in dental research—it is a key player in the broader narrative of human health. Growing evidence linking oral microbial dysbiosis to systemic diseases, including cancer, challenges traditional boundaries between oral and systemic health and calls for integrative models of pathogenesis, diagnosis, and care. Recognizing its role in systemic diseases shifts our understanding of pathogenesis and opens doors to novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. The mouth is not an isolated organ; it is an ecosystem at the interface between the body and the world. As we embrace this perspective, the lines between oral and systemic health blur—for the better. Rigorous evaluation and validation of oral microbial signatures as biomarkers of disease risk, progression, and therapeutic response represent a major translational opportunity, particularly in oncology. Progress in this field will require sustained interdisciplinary collaboration across microbiology, immunology, nutrition, epidemiology, oncology, and clinical medicine, as well as closer integration of dental and medical care. Such efforts have the potential to inform both preventive and therapeutic strategies and to reposition oral health as an integral component of systemic disease management.
