Free boundary regularity and well-posedness of physical solutions to the supercooled Stefan problem
Sebastian Munoz

Abstract
We study the regularity and well-posedness of physical solutions to the supercooled Stefan problem. Assuming only that the initial temperature is integrable, we prove that the free boundary, known to have jump discontinuities as a function of the time variable, is C 1 C^{1} as a function of the space variable, and is C ∞ C^{\infty} in an open set with countable complement, which we describe explicitly. We also prove that, as conjectured in [ 7 ] , the set of positive times when a jump occurs cannot have accumulation points. In addition, we prove that short-time uniqueness of physical solutions implies global uniqueness, which allows us to obtain uniqueness for very general initial data that fall outside the scope of the current well-posedness regime. In particular, we answer two questions left open in [ 19 , 27 ] regarding the global uniqueness of solutions. We proceed by deriving a weighted obstacle problem satisfied by the solutions, which we exploit to establish regularity and non-degeneracy estimates and to classify the free boundary points. We also establish a backward propagation of oscillation property, which allows us to control the occurrence of future jumps in terms of the past oscillation of the solution.



Keywords:
Stefan problem; obstacle problem; McKean-Vlasov equation; physical solutions; backward propagation of oscillation; free boundary; nondegeneracy; blow-up.

Introduction

We study the regularity and well-posedness of the supercooled Stefan problem

\begin{cases}u_{t}-\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}=0&t>0,\;x>\Lambda_{t},\\
\dot{\Lambda}_{t}=\frac{\alpha}{2}u_{x}(\Lambda_{t},t)&t>0,\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\;u(\Lambda_{t},t)=0&x\geq 0,\;t\geq 0,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\alpha>0
,
u_{0}\geq 0
is a probability density, and
\Lambda:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a non-decreasing function.

The problem (
1.1
) models the freezing of supercooled water into ice, with initial temperature
-u_{0}
, in the one-phase setting where the temperature in the solid is held constant, and equal to
0
. Unlike the classical Stefan problem, (
1.1
) is known to be ill-posed and its classical solutions may in general blow up in finite time, leading to discontinuities in the frontier
\Lambda
and instantaneous temperature spikes (see
[
26
]
). Therefore, the problem of defining the solution beyond the blow-up is of central interest.

For this purpose, (
1.1
) can be reformulated in a probabilistic sense. In this viewpoint, one considers a large population of Brownian particles that interact through the moving boundary: as particles hit the freezing threshold, they stop (modeling solidification) and release heat that instantaneously affects the remaining particles. This leads to the McKean-Vlasov equation

\begin{cases}X_{t}=X_{0^{-}}+B_{t}-\Lambda_{t},\\
\tau=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\leq 0\},\\
\Lambda_{t}=\alpha{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq t),\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
B_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion, and
\mathcal{L}(X_{0^{-}})=u_{0}(x)dx
. Beyond the physical setting of the Stefan problem, this type of model has also been studied in the context of contagion in large financial markets (see
[
12
,
13
,
20
,
23
,
24
]
) and integrate-and-fire models in neuroscience (see
[
4
,
8
,
15
]
). An advantage of this formulation is that it allows for the moving front
\Lambda
to have jump discontinuities. We will work with the following equivalent definition, expressed directly in terms of Brownian motion, which is more amenable to our methodology.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a standard Brownian motion
B
on
{\mathbb{R}}
with initial distribution
u_{0}(x)dx
, and a non-decreasing, right-continuous function
\Lambda:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, we say that
\Lambda
is a probabilistic solution to the supercooled Stefan problem (
1.1
) if

\Lambda_{t}=\alpha{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq t),\quad t\geq 0,\;\;\tau=\inf\{t\geq 0:B_{t}\leq\Lambda_{t}\}.

(1.3)

The way to recover the original system (
1.1
) from a probabilistic solution, at least formally, is by letting
u(x,t)
be the density function of the Brownian motion before it stops. Namely, if
\Lambda
is a probabilistic solution, and
u:{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is given by

{\mathbb{P}}(B_{t}\in[a,b],t<\tau)=\int_{a}^{b}u(x,t)dx,\quad\quad a<b,\;\;t>0,

(1.4)

then
u
satisfies (
1.1
), as long as the solution is sufficiently regular. However, in the presence of jump discontinuities of
\Lambda
, the classical formulation breaks down, and the choice of the jump size is not uniquely determined. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\Lambda
be a probabilistic solution to (
1.1
). We say that
\Lambda
is a physical solution if, for every
t\geq 0
,

\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{t^{-}}=\inf\{x>0:{\mathbb{P}}(t\leq\tau,B_{t}\in(\Lambda_{t^{-}},\Lambda_{t^{-}}+x])<\alpha^{-1}x\}.

(1.5)

The notion of physical solution is a natural one, as it can be shown that these are the solutions for which the jump distance is as small as possible (see
[
12
, Prop. 1.2]
). Existence of physical solutions was recently shown by S. Cuchieri, S. Rigger, and S. Svaluto-Ferro in
[
6
, Thm. 6.5]
under very general assumptions, requiring only finite moment for the initial density
u_{0}
(see also
[
8
,
23
,
24
]
). Uniqueness was shown first by F. Delarue, S. Nadtochiy, and M. Shkolnikov in
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
, under the assumption that
u_{0}
is a bounded function that changes monotonocity finitely often on compact sets. More recently, uniqueness was obtained for a broad class of oscillatory initial conditions in
[
27
, Thm. 1.1]
, albeit requiring the hard
L^{\infty}
constraint
u_{0}\leq\alpha^{-1}
. Local uniqueness was shown for very general initial data by S. Ledger and A. Søjmark in
[
19
, Thm. 3.1]
, requiring only a natural assumption for
u_{0}
near
x=0
.

In this paper, we study the regularity of physical solutions and their free boundary, while making no assumptions on the initial density
u_{0}
. Therefore, our regularity results apply equally to the locally monotone regime of
[
7
]
, to the oscillatory regime of
[
27
]
, and to the possibly non-unique solutions of
[
6
]
. We also advance the well-posedness theory considerably, by proving global uniqueness of physical solutions for initial data that fall outside the scope of
[
7
,
27
]
, under only the local condition of
[
19
, Thm. 3.1]
.

Our first main result answers an open question from
[
7
]
, namely whether the time discontinuities of
\Lambda
can have accumulation points. It states that no positive time can be an accumulation point for the jump times.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
u_{0}
is a probability density on
[0,\infty)
, and let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
). Then the set of discontinuities of
\Lambda
is locally finite in
(0,\infty)
.

For the locally monotone solutions of
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
, it is already known that
t=0
cannot be an accumulation point, so Theorem
1.3
implies that the jumps do not accumulate at all. On the other hand, if the initial data is highly oscillatory, as in
[
27
]
, our result says that these oscillations are resolved instantly, so that the jumps can only accumulate at the initial time.

Next, we present our results concerning the regularity of the free boundary. Since the function
\Lambda_{t}
may have infinite speed, and even jump discontinuities, any global regularity statement requires representing the boundary as a function of the space variable. To state the theorem, we define the freezing time
s:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,\infty]
by

s(x)=\inf\{t>0:\Lambda_{t}>x\}.

(1.6)

The freezing time satisfies
s(x)\equiv 0
for
x\leq\Lambda_{0}
, and
s(x)\equiv\infty
for
x\geq\alpha
. For
x\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
, one has
s(x)\in(0,\infty)
, and
s
is a left inverse for
\Lambda
, namely

s(\Lambda_{t})=t,\quad t\in(0,\infty).

Indeed, since
\Lambda
can be shown to be strictly increasing,
s
is continuous. The jump discontinuities of
\Lambda
then correspond to intervals in which
s
is constant. Our first regularity result says that the free boundary is of class
C^{1}
, and it is smooth outside of a countable set.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
u_{0}
is a probability density on
[0,\infty)
, let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), and let
s
be given by (
1.6
). Then
s\in C^{1}((\Lambda_{0},\alpha))
, and there exists an open set
R\subset(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
such that
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)\backslash R
is countable, with
s\in C^{\infty}(R)
.

To give a more precise description of the free boundary, we classify the elements of
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
into regular points and singular points, as follows. Let
J\subset(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
be the set of points located strictly inside a jump, namely

J:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}:\Lambda_{t^{-}}<x<\Lambda_{t}\text{ for some }t\in(0,\infty)\}=\{x\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha):\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}<x<\Lambda_{s(x)}\}.

(1.7)

Definition 1.5
.

Let
x_{0}\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
. We say that
x_{0}
is a regular point if

u(x_{0},s(x_{0})^{-}):=\lim_{t\uparrow s(x_{0})^{-}}u(x_{0},t)=0\quad\quad\text{or}\quad\quad x\in J,

(1.8)

and we say that
x_{0}
is a singular point if

u(x_{0},s(x_{0})^{-})=\alpha^{-1}\quad\quad\text{or}\quad\quad\Lambda_{s(x_{0})^{-}}<\Lambda_{s(x_{0})},\;\;x_{0}\in\{\Lambda_{s(x_{0})^{-}},\Lambda_{s(x_{0})}\}.

(1.9)

Let
R
denote the set of regular points, and
S
denote the set of singular points. If
x_{0}\in R
, we say that
(x_{0},s(x_{0}))
is a regular free boundary point, and if
x_{0}\in S
, we say that
(x_{0},s(x_{0}))
is a singular free boundary point.

The regular free boundary points are therefore those where either
u
vanishes continuously or those located strictly inside a jump. The singular free boundary points are either the endpoints of a jump, or points where
u
jumps down to
0
from the critical value
\alpha^{-1}
. This definition is justified by the next result, which guarantees that the sets
R
and
S
are disjoint, and they exhaust all possible free boundary points, so that
R\cup S=(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume that
u_{0}
is a probability density on
[0,\infty)
, let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), let
u
be given by (
1.4
), and let
s:(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)\to(0,\infty)
be given by (
1.6
). Let
R
be the set of all regular points, and let
S
be the set of all singular points. Then
R\cap S=\emptyset
and
R\cup S=(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
. Moreover, the set
S
is countable, the set
R
is open,
s\in C^{\infty}(R)
, and
u
is smooth up to the regular part of the free boundary,

\quad u\in C^{\infty}(\{(x,t):x\in R,\;t\leq s(x)\}).

(1.10)

Finally, if one lets

U:=R\backslash J=\{x\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha):u(x,s(x)^{-})=0\},

(1.11)

then
U
is open,

U=\{x\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha):s^{\prime}(x)>0\},\quad\Lambda\in C^{\infty}(s(U)),

and

\dot{\Lambda}_{s(x)}=\frac{1}{s^{\prime}(x)}=\frac{\alpha}{2}u_{x}(\Lambda_{s(x)},s(x)),\quad x\in U.

(1.12)

This result provides a thorough classification of the free boundary points, guaranteeing that the solution and its free boundary are smooth outside of an explicitly described countable set.

To place Theorem
1.6
in context, we compare it with the regularity result of
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
, which was proved under the assumption that
u_{0}
is bounded and changes monotonicity finitely often in compact intervals. In that result, the authors showed that for each time
t_{0}
, there are three possibilities: (i) if
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})=0
and
u_{x}(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})<\infty
, then
\Lambda
is
C^{1}
on some interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon)
;
(ii) if
u_{x}(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})=\infty
but
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})<\alpha^{-1}
then
\Lambda
is
C^{1/2}
on
[t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon)
;
(iii) if
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})\geq\alpha^{-1}
, then
\Lambda
is neither
C^{1}
nor
C^{1/2}
at
t_{0}
, and a jump may occur at
t_{0}
.

Theorem
1.6
shows that the intermediate case (ii) never occurs at any positive time
t_{0}
. In fact, one always has
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})\in\{0,\alpha^{-1}\}
at any point of continuity, so the frontier
\Lambda
is automatically smooth near
t_{0}
whenever
\lim_{t\uparrow t_{0}}u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t)<\alpha^{-1}.
Moreover, our result guarantees maximal regularity in a full neighborhood
(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}+\varepsilon)
, rather than a one-sided interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon)
. This upgrade is possible in part thanks to Theorem
1.3
, which rules out the accumulation of jumps to the left of
t_{0}
. We also highlight that Theorem
1.6
is valid without the requirement that
u_{0}
is bounded or locally monotone.

Our last main result is a uniqueness claim.

Theorem 1.7
.

Assume
u_{0}
is a probability density, let
t_{0}>0
, and assume that any two physical solutions to (
1.1
) agree on
[0,t_{0}]
. Then there exists at most one physical solution to (
1.1
).

We obtain this result by showing that physical solutions automatically become locally monotone at any positive time
t_{0}>0
(see Proposition
3.5
), even if
u_{0}
fails to satisfy this assumption. Therefore, uniqueness on
[t_{0},\infty)
follows from the existing well-posedness theory of
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
. We now present two consequences of Theorem
1.7
. The first of these is a uniqueness result under the assumption that, near
x=0
,
u_{0}
does not oscillate or become too flat if it approaches the critical value
\alpha^{-1}
. Local uniqueness under this assumption was proved in
[
19
, Thm. 3.1]
, but the global uniqueness was left as an open question, which is answered by the following corollary.

Corollary 1.8
.

Assume that
u_{0}
is a probability density, and assume that there exist constants
\delta>0
,
c>0
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

u_{0}(x)\leq\alpha^{-1}-cx^{n}\quad\text{for almost every}\;x\in(0,\delta).

Then (
1.1
) has at most one physical solution.

The main novelty of Corollary
1.8
is that it provides
global
uniqueness under only
local
assumptions on
u_{0}
near
x=0
.

To understand this result, it is also important to note that oscillation around the value
\alpha^{-1}
near
x=0
is the critical behavior when it comes to uniqueness. Indeed, if
\text{ess}\limsup_{x\to 0^{+}}u_{0}(x)<\alpha^{-1}
, then Corollary
1.8
automatically gives global uniqueness. On the other hand, if
\text{ess}\liminf_{x\to 0^{+}}u_{0}(x)>\alpha^{-1}
, then the solution jumps at
t=0
, and the physical jump condition (
1.5
) then forces
\text{ess}\liminf_{x\to\Lambda_{0}^{+}}u_{0}(x)\leq\alpha^{-1}
.

As a second application, Theorem
1.7
yields uniqueness for a family of oscillatory initial densities that arise as a critical case of the oscillatory regime of
[
27
, Thm. 1.1]
. Indeed, it was shown in
[
27
, Prop. 1.6]
that the solutions are locally unique, but the global uniqueness was again posed as an open question, addressed by our next result.

Corollary 1.9
.

Assume that
\alpha=1
, and assume that

u_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha_{1},&x\in\bigcup_{n\geq 1}[a_{2n},a_{2n-1}),\\
\alpha_{2},&x\in\bigcup_{n\geq 1}[a_{2n+1},a_{2n}),\end{cases}

where
0<\alpha_{1}<1<\alpha_{2}
,
a_{2n-1}=r^{n-1}a_{1}
,
a_{2n}=pr^{n-1}a_{1}
, and
r=pq
,
p,q\in(0,1)
. Then, for any
\alpha_{2}>1
sufficiently close to 1, the physical solution
\Lambda
of (
1.1
) is unique.

We now describe our methodology, which begins, in Sections
2
and
3
, by analyzing a partial differential equation solved by any probabilistic solution, namely

u_{t}-\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}=(\nu(x)\chi_{u>0})_{t},

(1.13)

where
\nu
is the probability density of the stopped particles, so that
B_{\tau}\sim\nu(x)dx.
If we define
u(\cdot,0^{-}):=u_{0}
, then
\nu
can be described explicitly as

\nu(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\;x\notin(0,\alpha),\\
u(x,t^{-})&\text{if}\;\;\Lambda_{t^{-}}<x<\Lambda_{t}\;\text{for some }\;t\in[0,\infty),\\
\alpha^{-1}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1.14)

Despite the highly irregular source term, and the inevitable discontinuities of
u
across the boundary, one can obtain several useful inequalities. Crucially, we obtain the second-order estimates

u_{t}(x,t)\leq C_{R},\quad u_{xx}(x,t)\chi_{x>\Lambda_{t}}\leq C_{R}\quad(x,t)\in(-\infty,R)\times(R^{-1},\infty),

(1.15)

valid for any probabilistic solution (see Proposition
2.6
and Lemma
2.7
), and as well as the non-degeneracy estimate

u(x,t)\geq c_{R}(x-\Lambda_{t})^{+},\quad(x,t)\in(x,t)\in(-\infty,R)\times(R^{-1},R),

(1.16)

valid for any physical solution, any
(x_{0},t_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)
, and a small constant
c_{R}>0
(see Proposition
3.3
).

Estimates
1.15
and
1.16
are key in our analysis. We remark, for instance, that letting
x\downarrow\Lambda_{t}
in (
1.16
)
formally yields a positive lower bound on the velocity
\dot{\Lambda}_{t}
, which is equivalent to a Lipschitz estimate on the freezing time
s=\Lambda^{-1}
. On the other hand, the one-sided bounds of (
1.15
) allow us to interpret quantities such as
u(\Lambda_{t},t^{-})
or
u_{x}(\Lambda_{t},t)
in the pointwise sense, and, combined with (
1.16
), make it possible to prove Theorem
1.7
by ruling out oscillatory behavior for
u
near the boundary.

The time derivative estimate of (
1.15
) is inspired by a similar, new bound for the classical Stefan problem, which was recently obtained by A. Figalli, X. Ros-Oton, and J. Serra in
[
14
, Prop. 3.4]
. We note that our derivation of this bound does not rely on the one-dimensional setting, and does not need
u
to be continuous. In the context of the supercooled Stefan problem, the weak formulation of (
1.13
), for general target measures
\nu
, was first developed in
[
17
,
5
]
to obtain existence of weak solutions in arbitrary dimensions (see also
[
11
]
).

In Section
4
, we show that jumps cannot accumulate, with the first ingredient being a backward propagation of oscillation property. That is, we prove that, given two time levels
t_{*}>t_{0}>0
, any sign changes of
u_{x}
at time
t_{*}
can be transported backward along continuous curves to corresponding sign changes of
u_{x}(\cdot,t_{0})
(see Lemma
4.6
). This part of our proof is a refined version of the construction in
[
7
, Lem 4.1]
. The main improvement is that we ensure that our curves can be defined globally on
[t_{0},t_{1}]
without ever hitting the boundary or escaping to
+\infty
. This allows us to quantify the oscillations at
t_{*}
purely in terms of the oscillations at
t_{0}
, and to use the globally defined curves as barriers for a recursive argument.

The second ingredient is the fact that whenever
\Lambda
jumps at some time
t_{*}>t_{0}
, the physicality condition (
1.5
) forces one additional oscillation of
u
near the boundary at time
t_{*}
(see Lemma
4.7
). These oscillations can then be propagated back to the fixed time
t_{0}
, which allows us to estimate the number of future jumps in terms of the number of past oscillations at
t_{0}
. Since we showed that the latter is locally finite, this allows us to rule out the accumulation of jumps and prove Theorem
1.3
.

In Section
5
, we prove the main regularity results for the solution and its free boundary. For this purpose, we utilize the potential

w(x,t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}u(x,s)ds,

(1.17)

which satisfies the parabolic obstacle problem

w_{t}-\frac{1}{2}w_{xx}=-\nu(x)\chi_{w>0}.

(1.18)

Taking advantage of the fact that jump times do not accumulate, one sees that, near any free boundary point that lies outside of a jump,
\nu\equiv\alpha^{-1}
. In that case, (
1.18
) becomes the standard obstacle problem with constant source, which allows us to perform a classical blow-up analysis on these non-jump points (see
[
2
,
3
]
), to achieve a precise understanding of the singular points, and to prove Theorem
1.6
. Finally, we leverage the information obtained from the blow-up analysis, as well as the second-order estimates (
1.15
), to show that the freezing time is of class
C^{1}
, proving Theorem
1.4
.

We note that the transformation (
1.17
) was first used in
[
17
]
, where the authors exploited the properties of the potential
w
, in the higher-dimensional supercooled Stefan problem, to establish, among other things, the lower-semicontinuity of the freezing time
s
(for the classical Stefan problem, this type of transformation dates back to
[
10
]
). Furthermore, the idea of obtaining higher regularity results for the free boundary by leveraging the regularity theory of the parabolic obstacle problem for
w
was first suggested in
[
17
, Sec. 4.3]
.

2
Analysis of probabilistic solutions and their associated obstacle problem

Throughout the paper, we will tacitly assume that
u_{0}
is a probability density on
[0,\infty)
. The results of this section are valid for all probabilistic solutions, as they do not make use of the physical jump condition (
1.5
).

2.1
The equation satisfied by
u

We begin with a basic estimate on
u
and its time integral, which, in particular, shows that
u
is well-defined as a density.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a probabilistic solution to (
1.1
), and let
u
be given by (
1.4
). Then
u
is a classical solution to

u_{t}-\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}=0,\quad(x,t)\in\Omega:=\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty):x>\Lambda_{t}\}=\{u>0\},

Moreover, there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

u(x,t)\leq Ct^{-1/2},\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty),

(2.1)

and

\int_{0}^{\infty}u(x,s)ds\leq 2x,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(2.2)

Proof.

Letting
\tau_{0}=\inf\{t:B_{t}\leq 0\}
, we note that
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
, because
\Lambda\geq 0
. Hence, for any
[a,b]\subset[0,\infty)
,

{\mathbb{P}}(B_{t}\in[a,b],t<\tau)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(B_{t}\in[a,b],t<\tau_{0})=\int_{a}^{b}v(x,t)dx,

(2.3)

where
v
is the solution to

v_{t}-\frac{1}{2}v_{xx}=0,\quad v(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad v(0,t)=0,\quad(x,t)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty).

This shows that
u
is well-defined, and

u(x,t)\leq v(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(G(x-y,t)-G(x+y,t))u_{0}(y)dy,\quad x>0,\quad t>0,

(2.4)

where

G(x,t)=(2\pi t)^{-1/2}\exp(-x^2/{2t}).

(2.5)

In particular, this readily implies (
2.1
). Furthermore, by a direct computation, (
2.4
) yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}u(x,t)dt\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}(G(x-y,t)-G(x+y,t))u_{0}(y)dydt=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\min(x,y)u_{0}(y)dy\leq 2x.

(2.6)

which shows (
2.2
). Note that, since
\Lambda
is non-decreasing and right continuous, it is upper semicontinuous. Therefore, the set
\Omega
is open. Letting
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, and noting that
\varphi(B_{\tau},\tau)=\varphi(B_{0},0)\equiv 0
we have, by Itô’s formula and the compact support of
\varphi
(see
[
25
, Ch. 3, Cor. 3.6; Ch. 4, Thm. 3.3]
),

0={\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{\tau},\tau))={\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{0},0))+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}(B_{s},s)+\varphi_{t}(B_{s},s)\right)ds\right)\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}(B_{s},s)+\varphi_{t}(B_{s},s)\right)\chi_{s<\tau}ds\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}(B_{s},s)+\varphi_{t}(B_{s},s)\right),s<\tau\right)ds\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{t}\right)u(x,s)dxds,

(2.7)

where (
1.4
) was used in the last equality. This shows that
u
solves the heat equation in the distributional (and therefore classical) sense in
\Omega
. The fact that
u
vanishes outside of
\Omega
follows directly from (
1.4
) and the definition of
\tau
. Hence, by the strong maximum principle,
\Omega=\{u>0\}
.
∎

The next lemma concerns the regularity of the past limit
u(x,t_{0}^{-})
, for
x>\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}
, which will be of particular importance when
\Lambda_{t_{0}}>\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}
. It also identifies

as a concrete probability distribution. The limit at
x=\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}
is more delicate, and will be treated later (see Lemma
2.8
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Lambda
be a probabilistic solution to (
1.1
), let
u
be given by (
1.4
), and let
t_{0}>0
. Then for
x\in(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},\infty)
, the limit

u(x,t_{0}^{-}):=\lim_{t\uparrow t_{0}}u(x,t)

exists. The function
u(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})
is analytic in space, and defines a strictly positive, smooth extension of
u
in
(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},\infty)\times(0,t_{0}]
. Moreover, we have

{\mathbb{P}}(B_{t_{0}}\in[a,b],t_{0}\leq\tau)=\int_{a}^{b}u(x,t_{0}^{-})dx,\quad\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}\leq a<b.

(2.8)

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, for any
\delta>0
, the function
u
is bounded and satisfies the heat equation in the cylinder
[\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}+\delta,\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}+\delta^{-1}]\times(t_{0}/2,t_{0})
. Hence, by the interior Schauder estimates (see
[
21
, Thm. 4.9]
), for any
k>1
,
u
is uniformly bounded in
C^{k}([\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}+2\delta,\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}+(2\delta)^{-1}]\times(t_{0}/4,t_{0}))
, which implies the smooth extension claim. The fact that
u(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})
is analytic is then a standard fact about classical solutions to the heat equation (see
[
22
, Ch. VI, Thm. 1]
). The strict positivity follows from the strong maximum principle for the heat equation. Finally, letting
t\uparrow t_{0}
in (
1.4
), and using the local boundedness of
u
, we obtain

\int_{a}^{b}u(x,t_{0}^{-})dx=\lim_{t\uparrow t_{0}}{\mathbb{P}}(B_{t}\in[a,b],t<\tau)=\lim_{t\uparrow t_{0}}{\mathbb{P}}(B_{t_{0}}\in[a,b],t<\tau)={\mathbb{P}}(B_{t_{0}}\in[a,b],t_{0}\leq\tau),

where the continuity (in probability) of Brownian motion was used in the second equality, and the monotone convergence theorem was used in the third equality.
∎

Recalling that the definition of
\nu
was given in (
1.14
), we now make the basic observation that
\nu
is a probability density, and the freezing time
s
is continuous,

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Lambda
be a probabilistic solution to (
1.1
), let
s
be given by (
1.6
), and let
\nu
be given by (
1.14
). Then
\Lambda
is strictly increasing,
s
is continuous, and

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\nu(x)dx=1.

(2.9)

Proof.

We have, for
h>0
, recalling (
1.4
),

\alpha^{-1}(\Lambda_{t+h}-\Lambda_{t})={\mathbb{P}}(t<\tau\leq t+h)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\inf_{s\in(0,h]}(B_{t+s}-\Lambda_{t+s})\leq 0,t<\tau\right)\\
\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\inf_{s\in(0,h]}B_{t+s}\leq\Lambda_{t},t<\tau\right)=\int_{\Lambda_{t}}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\inf_{s\in(0,h]}B_{s}\leq\Lambda_{t}\Bigr|B_{0}=x\right)u(x,t)dx>0,

(2.10)

where in the last inequality we used the strict positivity of
u
in
\{\Lambda_{t}<x\}
and the standard reflection principle for Brownian motion (see
[
9
, Exm. 8.4.1]
). The continuity of
s(x)=\inf\{t:x<\Lambda_{t}\}
then follows from the strict monotonicity of
\Lambda
.

Now, letting
A=\{t\in[0,\infty):\Lambda_{t}=\Lambda_{t^{-}}\},
observe that
s^{-1}(A)=\{x\in[0,\alpha):\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}=\Lambda_{s(x)}\}
, and
\Lambda_{s(x)}=x
for all
x\in s^{-1}(A)
. Therefore, by change of variables (see
[
25
, Prop. 4.9]
), we have

\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\nu(x)dx=\alpha^{-1}\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}dx=\alpha^{-1}\int_{\Lambda_{t}=\Lambda_{t^{-}}}d\Lambda_{t}.

(2.11)

On the other hand, noting that
\Lambda
has countably many discontinuities, we obtain from (
2.8
) that

\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}>\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\nu(x)dx=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}\int_{\Lambda_{t^{-}}}^{\Lambda_{t}}u(x,t^{-})dx=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}{\mathbb{P}}(B_{t}\in[\Lambda_{t^{-}},\Lambda_{t}],\tau\geq t)=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}{\mathbb{P}}(\tau=t)\\
=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}\alpha^{-1}(\Lambda_{t}-\Lambda_{t^{-}})=\alpha^{-1}\int_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}d\Lambda_{t}.

(2.12)

Adding (
2.11
) and (
2.12
), we conclude that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\nu(x)dx=\alpha^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Lambda_{t}=1.

(2.13)

∎

We now derive the equation solved by
u
in the whole space.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\Lambda
be a probabilistic solution to (
1.1
), and let
u
be given by (
1.4
). The function
u
satisfies, in the distributional sense,

\begin{cases}u_{t}-\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}=(\nu(x)\chi_{u>0})_{t}&x\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\chi_{x>\Lambda_{0}}&x\in{\mathbb{R}}.\end{cases}

(2.14)

Namely, for every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\times[0,\infty))
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\varphi_{t}(u-\nu\chi_{u>0})+\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}u\right)dxdt+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(u_{0}(x)-\nu(x))\varphi(x,0)dx=0.

(2.15)

In particular,
u
is a global subsolution to the heat equation in
{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)
, and
u\in L^{2}_{\emph{loc}}((0,\infty):H^{1}_{\emph{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}))
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\times[0,\infty))
. Using Itô’s formula as in (
2.7
),

{\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{\tau},\tau))={\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{0},0))+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}(B_{s},s)+\varphi_{t}(B_{s},s)\right)ds\right)\\
=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u_{0}(x)\varphi(x,0)dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{t}\right)u(x,t)dxdt.

(2.16)

Since
B_{\tau}=\Lambda_{\tau}
whenever
\Lambda_{\tau}=\Lambda_{\tau^{-}}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{\tau},\tau))={\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(\Lambda_{\tau},\tau),\Lambda_{\tau}=\Lambda_{\tau^{-}})+{\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{\tau},\tau),\Lambda_{\tau}>\Lambda_{\tau^{-}}).

(2.17)

Now, observing that
\alpha^{-1}\Lambda_{t}={\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq t)
is the cumulative distribution function of
\tau
, it follows that

{\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(\Lambda_{\tau},\tau),\Lambda_{\tau}=\Lambda_{\tau^{-}})=\int_{\Lambda_{s}=\Lambda_{s^{-}}}\varphi(\Lambda_{s},s)d(\alpha^{-1}\Lambda_{s}).

(2.18)

Therefore, changing variables as in (
2.11
), we have

\int_{\Lambda_{s}=\Lambda_{s^{-}}}\varphi(\Lambda_{s},s)d(\alpha^{-1}\Lambda_{s})=\alpha^{-1}\int_{{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}}\varphi(x,s(x))dx\\
=\alpha^{-1}\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\int_{0}^{s(x)}\varphi_{t}dtdx+\alpha^{-1}\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\varphi(x,0)dx\\
=\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{t}\nu\chi_{u>0}dtdx+\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\varphi(x,0)\nu(x)dx,

(2.19)

where the integrals are understood to be over
x\in(0,\alpha)
. Thus, we infer from (
2.18
) and (
2.19
) that

{\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(\Lambda_{\tau},\tau),\Lambda_{\tau}=\Lambda_{\tau^{-}})=\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{t}\nu\chi_{u>0}dtdx+\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}=\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\varphi(x,0)\nu(x)dx.

(2.20)

On the other hand, since the set of discontinuities of
\Lambda
is countable, using (
2.8
) and recalling the convention
u(\cdot,0^{-}):=u_{0}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{\tau},\tau),\Lambda_{\tau}>\Lambda_{\tau^{-}})=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}{\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{\tau},\tau),\tau=t)=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}{\mathbb{E}}(\chi_{B_{t}\in[\Lambda_{t^{-}},\Lambda_{t}]}\varphi(B_{t},t),\tau\geq t)\\
=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}\int_{\Lambda_{t^{-}}}^{\Lambda_{t}}\varphi(x,t)u(x,t^{-})dx=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}\int_{\Lambda_{t^{-}}}^{\Lambda_{t}}\varphi(x,t)\nu(x)dx\\
=\sum_{\Lambda_{t}>\Lambda_{t^{-}}}\int_{\Lambda_{t^{-}}}^{\Lambda_{t}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{t}\nu\chi_{u>0}dtdx+\int_{\Lambda_{t^{-}}}^{\Lambda_{t}}\varphi(x,0)\nu(x)dtdx\\
=\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}>\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{t}\nu\chi_{u>0}dtdx+\int_{\Lambda_{s(x)}>\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}}\varphi(x,0)\nu(x)dx.

(2.21)

In view of (
2.17
), (
2.20
), and (
2.21
), using the fact that that
\varphi
is compactly supported and
\nu
is supported in
[0,\alpha]
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}(\varphi(B_{\tau},\tau))=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\varphi_{t}(x,t)\nu(x)\chi_{u>0}dxdt+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\varphi(x,0)\nu(x)dx.

(2.22)

The equality (
2.15
) thus follows from (
2.16
). Finally, the fact that

is subcaloric follows readily from the fact that the set
\{u(t)>0\}
is decreasing in time. Namely,

u_{t}-\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}=(\nu\chi_{u>0})_{t}\leq 0,

and the
L^{2}_{\text{loc}}((0,\infty);H^{1}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}))
bounds on

then follow from this inequality by standard energy estimates.
∎

2.2
The weighted obstacle problem

In view of Lemma

w(x,t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}u(x,s)ds,\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty),

(2.23)

is well defined. We show now that

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be a probabilistic solution to (

), let

be given by (

), and let

be given by (

(2.24)

Furthermore,

\begin{cases}w_{t}-\frac{1}{2}w_{xx}=-\nu(x)\chi_{w(x,t)>0}\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty),\\
w\geq 0,\,\,w_{t}\leq 0,\,\,\{w>0\}=\{w_{t}<0\}.\end{cases}

(2.25)

Proof.

The fact that
w_{t}\leq 0
and

follows directly from the definition. Moreover, we infer from (

) that, for

w(x,t)\leq w(x,0)\leq 2x\leq 2R,\;\;x\leq R,\;\;t\in[0,\infty).

To see that

to

The precise justification of this formal computation proceeds as follows. Let

on

, and
\zeta\equiv 0
on
[2,\infty)
. Then, noting that
\chi_{w>0}=\chi_{u>0}
, and

\zeta_{R}\psi\equiv\psi
for sufficiently large

, (
2.15
) yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\psi u-\varphi_{t}\nu\chi_{w>0}+\zeta_{R}^{\prime}\Psi u+\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}u\right)dxdt=0.

(2.28)

Since
\varphi
is compactly supported and
\nu(x)\chi_{w>0}(x,\cdot)\equiv 0
for

, we have, for sufficiently large

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{t}\nu\chi_{w>0}dtdx=\int_{\Lambda_{0}}^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{t}\nu\chi_{w>0}dtdx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\psi\nu\chi_{w>0})dtdx+\int_{\Lambda_{0}}^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta_{R}^{\prime}\Psi\nu\chi_{w>0}dtdx,

\psi

. Next, we have, for sufficiently large

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{xx}udtdx=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{xx}w_{t}dtdx\\
=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{xx}wdtdx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta_{R}^{\prime}\Psi_{xx}wdtdx-\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\varphi_{xx}w(x,t)dx\right)\Biggr\rvert_{t=0}^{t=\infty}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{xx}wdxdt+o(1)

as

\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta_{R}^{\prime}\Psi_{xx}wdtdx\right|

Finally, if

\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{\prime}_{R}\Psi udtdx\right|\leq C\left|\int_{A}\sup_{t\in[R,2R]}u(x,t)dx\right|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{R}}\left|A\right|=o(1)

as

), (
2.30
), (

which yields (

2.3
Second-order estimates for

We now derive the second-order bounds on the solution that will allow us to prove the non-degeneracy and regularity results. Given
(x_{0},t_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)
and

, we define the parabolic cylinders

Q_{r}(x_{0},t_{0}):=(x_{0}-r,x_{0}+r)\times(t_{0}-r^{2},t_{0}+r^{2}),\quad Q_{r}^{-}(x_{0},t_{0}):=(x_{0}-r,x_{0}+r)\times(t_{0}-r^{2},t_{0}].

(2.34)

We begin with an upper bound on the time derivative.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be a probabilistic solution to (
1.1
), let
u
be given by (
1.4
), and let

be given by (

is locally bounded above in
{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)

and, for every

\|(u_{t})^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}((-\infty,R)\times(R^{-2},\infty))}\leq C_{R}\quad R\geq 1.

Proof.

Let
(x_{0},t_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)
,
r=\frac{t_{0}}{10}
,
D=Q_{6r}(x_{0},t_{0})
,

, and let

z(x,t)=\frac{u(x,t+h)-u(x,t)}{h}.

In
\{u>0\}

z_{t}-\frac{1}{2}z_{xx}=h^{-1}(\nu\chi_{u(t+h)>0})_{t}\leq 0,

so
z
is subcaloric in
\{u>0\}\cap D
. This implies, in particular, that
z^{+}
is subcaloric in
\{u>0\}\cap D
. Given that
s
is continuous, the set

A=\{(x,t)\in D:s(x)<t+h\}=\{(x,t)\in D:u(x,t+h)=0\}^{\circ}

is an open neighborhood of
\{u=0\}\cap D
, disjoint from
\{u(t+h)>0\}
. Additionally, for

z^{+}(x,t)=h^{-1}(u(x,t+h)-u(x,t))^{+}=h^{-1}(-u(x,t))^{+}=0
, so trivially
z^{+}
is subcaloric in

. Noting that
D
is the union of the overlapping open sets
A\cup(\{u>0\}\cap D\}=D
, in both of which

is subcaloric, we conclude that
z^{+}
is subcaloric in

[

), writing
Q_{r}:=Q_{r}(x_{0},t_{0})

\|z^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}\leq C\left(\fint_{Q_{2r}}(z^{+})^{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\leq C|Q_{2r}|^{-1}\text{sup}_{\theta>0}\theta|\{|z|>\theta\}\cap Q_{2r}|.

(2.36)

We note that in the last inequality we used the embedding

by a standard interpolation argument. But

, where

so, by the Calderón-Zygmund estimate for the heat equation (see
[
14

,
[
16

\text{sup}_{\theta>0}\theta|\{|z|>\theta\}\cap Q_{2r}|\leq\text{sup}_{\theta>0}\theta|\{|v_{t}|+|v_{xx}|>\theta\}\cap Q_{2r}|\leq C(r^{-2}\|v\|_{L^{1}(Q_{3r})}+\|v_{t}-v_{xx}/2\|_{L^{1}(Q_{3r})}).

(2.37)

On the other hand, since

and

v\leq 0,\quad v_{t}-v_{xx}/2=-\nu(x)(\chi_{w(t+h)>0}-\chi_{w(t)>0})\geq 0.

By testing against a smooth, compactly supported bump function

\|v_{t}-v_{xx}/2\|_{L^{1}(Q_{3r})}\leq\left|\int(v_{t}-v_{xx}/2)\zeta\right|=\left|\int(\zeta_{t}+\zeta_{xx}/2)v\right|\leq\frac{C}{r^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{1}(Q_{4r})}.

(2.38)

Similarly, letting
\zeta

Q_{5r}
and equal to

\|v\|_{L^{1}(Q_{4r})}\leq\left|\int v(x,t)\zeta(x,t)dxdt\right|=\left|h^{-1}\int(w(x,t+h)-w(x,t))\zeta(x,t)dxdt\right|\\

(2.39)

Thus, we conclude from (

), (
2.37
), (
2.38
), (
2.39
), and (
2.2
) that

\|z^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}\leq Cr^{-7}\|w\|_{L^{1}(Q_{6r})}\leq Cr^{-4}(|x_{0}|+r).

The result follows by letting

Lemma 2.7

, such that

u_{xx}(x,t)\leq C_{R},\quad u_{x}\geq-C_{R},\quad(x,t)\in((-\infty,R)\times(R^{-2},\infty))\cap\{u>0\}.

(2.40)

Proof.

The upper bound on
u_{xx}
follows immediately from Proposition
2.6
, in view of the fact that
u_{xx}=2u_{t}
in
\{u>0\}
. On the other hand, since
(x+1,t)\in\{u>0\}
whenever
(x,t)\in\{u>0\}
, the mean value theorem implies that
u(x+1,t)-u(x,t)=u_{x}(y,t)
for some
y\in(x,x+1)
, so that

-u_{x}(x,t)=u_{x}(y,t)-u_{x}(x,t)+u(x,t)-u(x+1,t)\leq\max_{z\in[x,x+1]}(u_{xx}(z,t))+u(x,t)\leq C_{R}.

∎

As a second application of Proposition
3.3
, we can now make sense of the limiting value
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})
, for any
t_{0}>0
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\Lambda
be a probabilistic solution to (
1.1
), let
u
be given by (
1.4
), and let
t_{0}>0
. The limit

\lim_{t\uparrow t_{0}}u(x,t)=:u(x,t_{0}^{-})

(2.41)

exists for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and defines a continuous extension of
u
in
[\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},\infty)\times(0,t_{0}]
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.6
,
u_{t}
is locally bounded above, so the limit (
2.41
) exists for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In view of Lemma
2.2
, it suffices now to prove that

is right continuous at

, and that the convergence in (

) holds locally uniformly in

. In fact, by the upper bound on
u_{t}
, assuming the continuity of

, the locally uniform convergence follows automatically by Dini’s theorem. Hence, the problem is reduced to showing that

\lim_{x\downarrow a}u(x,t_{0}^{-})=u(a,t_{0}^{-}).

For this purpose, observe that, by Lemma
2.3
,
u(x,t_{0}-\rho)>0
for

and small

. Hence, by Lemma
2.7

and letting

, we have, for

and some constant

independent of

,

u(a,t_{0}^{-})\leq u(x,t_{0}^{-})+C(x-a).

(2.42)

On the other hand, by definition of

, given

u(a,t_{0}^{-})\geq u(a,{\overline{t}})-\varepsilon

(2.43)

for any

sufficiently close to

. Fixing one such

u(a,{\overline{t}})>0
, the smoothness of

in

implies that there exists

, depending on

and
\varepsilon
, such that

u(a,{\overline{t}})\geq u(x,{\overline{t}})-\varepsilon.

(2.44)

whenever

. But using the upper bound on

, we have

u(x,{\overline{t}})\geq u(x,t_{0}^{-})-C(t_{0}-{\overline{t}})\geq u(x,t_{0}^{-})-C\varepsilon.

(2.45)

Hence, by (
2.42
),(
2.43
), (
2.44
), and (
2.45
), we conclude that

u(x,t_{0}^{-})+C\varepsilon\geq u(a,t_{0}^{-})\geq u(x,t_{0}^{-})-C\varepsilon,\quad x\in(a,a+\delta).

(2.46)

∎

3
Nondegeneracy estimates for physical solutions and applications

In the present section, we will exploit the estimates of Section
2
, combined with the physical jump condition
1.5
, to obtain nondegeneracy results for physical solutions. As applications, we prove that the freezing time
s
is locally Lipschitz continuous, as well as Theorem
1.7
.
We begin with an elementary consequence of (
1.5
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), and let
u
be as in (
1.4
). Let
t_{0}>0
, and assume that
\Lambda_{t_{0}}>\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}
. Then
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})\geq\alpha^{-1}
and
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}},t_{0}^{-})\in(0,\alpha^{-1}]
. Moreover, there exists
x\in(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},\Lambda_{t_{0}})
such that
u_{x}(x,t_{0}^{-})<0
.

Proof.

These claims follows directly from (
2.8
), Lemmas
2.2
and
2.8
, and the definition of physical solution. Indeed, for
x>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\geq t,B_{t}\in(\Lambda_{t^{-}},\Lambda_{t^{-}}+x])=\int_{\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}}^{\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}+x}u(x,t_{0}^{-})dx,

(3.1)

so a value
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})<\alpha^{-1}
would contradict the physical jump condition (
1.5
), which proves that

u(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},t_{0}^{-})\geq\alpha^{-1}.

(3.2)

On the other hand, we have

\int_{\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}}^{\Lambda_{t_{0}}}u(x,t^{-})dx={\mathbb{P}}(\tau=t_{0})=\alpha^{-1}(\Lambda_{t_{0}}-\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}}).

(3.3)

Thus, (
1.5
) implies that

u(\Lambda_{t_{0}},t^{-})=\lim_{x\downarrow\Lambda_{t_{0}}}\frac{1}{x}\int_{\Lambda_{t_{0}}}^{\Lambda_{t_{0}}+x}u(x,t^{-})dx\leq\alpha^{-1}.

(3.4)

Note also that
u(\Lambda_{t_{0}},t^{-})>0
, by Lemma
2.2
and the strong maximum principle.
Finally, if we had
u_{x}(x,t_{0}^{-})\geq 0
for
x\in(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},\Lambda_{t_{0}})
, (
3.2
) and (
3.4
) would imply that
u_{x}(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})\equiv 0
on
(\Lambda_{t_{0}^{-}},\Lambda_{t_{0}})
. This is a contradiction to the fact that, by Lemma
2.2
,
u(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})
is analytic.
∎

We now obtain a local lower bound for the density of stopped particles.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(B,\Lambda_{t})
be a physical solution, let
u
be defined by (
1.4
), and let
\nu
be defined by (
1.14
). The function
x\mapsto\nu(x)
is locally bounded away from
0
in
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
, and let
t_{0}=s(x_{0})>0
.
By Lemma
2.7
, there exists
C_{1}>1
, such that

u_{x}(x,t)\geq-C_{1},\quad(x,t)\in((x_{0}-10\alpha,x_{0}+10\alpha)\times(t_{0}/10,\infty))\cap\{u>0\}.

(3.5)

Assume, by contradiction, that there exists a sequence
x_{n}\to x_{0}
such that
\nu(x_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Recalling that

J=\{x\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha):\Lambda_{t^{-}}<x<\Lambda_{t}\text{ for some }t\in(0,\infty)\},

(3.6)

we have, by definition,
\nu(x)=\alpha^{-1}
for
x\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)\backslash J
. Therefore, we may assume that
x_{n}\in J
, namely that

\nu(x_{n})=u(x_{n},s(x_{n})^{-}),\quad\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}}<\Lambda_{s(x_{n})},\quad x_{n}\in(\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}},\Lambda_{s(x_{n})}).

But, by Lemma
3.1
,
u(\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}},s(x_{n})^{-})\geq\alpha^{-1}
, so that, by (
3.5
), for sufficiently large
n
,

\nu(x_{n})=u(x_{n},s(x_{n})^{-})\geq u(\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}},s(x_{n})^{-})-C_{1}(x_{n}-\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}})\geq\alpha^{-1}-C_{1}(x_{n}-\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}}),

(3.7)

which, since
\nu(x_{n})\to 0
, implies that

\Lambda_{s(x_{n})}-\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}}\geq x_{n}-\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}}\geq\frac{1}{2\alpha C_{1}},

for
n
sufficiently large. Thus, since
x_{n}\in(\Lambda_{s(x_{n})^{-}},\Lambda_{s(x_{n})})
and
x_{n}\to x_{0}
, we infer that
\Lambda_{s(x_{0})^{-}}<\Lambda_{s(x_{0})}
and, for
n
sufficiently large,

s(x_{n})=s(x_{0}),\quad x_{n}\in(\Lambda_{s(x_{0})^{-}},\Lambda_{s(x_{0})}),\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\nu(x_{n})=u(x_{n},s(x_{0})^{-}).

But, this implies that
\nu(x_{n})\to 0=u(x_{0},s(x_{0})^{-})
, a contradiction to Lemmas
2.2
,
2.8
and
3.1
.
∎

We now derive the main non-degeneracy estimate for physical solutions.

Proposition 3.3

(Linear non-degeneracy)
.

Assume that
(x_{0},t_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)
, and let
r\in(0,t_{0}/10)
. There exists a small constant
c>0
such that

u(x,t)\geq c(x-\Lambda_{t})^{+},\quad(x,t)\in Q_{r}(x_{0},t_{0}).

(3.8)

Proof.

By a compactness argument, it is enough to show that the statement holds for sufficiently small
r
. If
(x_{0},t_{0})
is not a free boundary point, the conclusion follows trivially by the fact that the set
\{u>0\}
is open and
x-\Lambda_{t}\leq 0=u(x,t)
whenever
(x,t)\in\{u=0\}^{\circ}
. Thus, we may assume that
x_{0}\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
,
t_{0}=s(x_{0})
, and
r<\min(\alpha-x_{0},x_{0}-\Lambda_{0})/10
. By Lemma
3.2
, there exists
c_{0}>0
such that

\nu(x)\geq c_{0},\quad x\in(x_{0}-2r,x_{0}+2r).

(3.9)

We now fix
(x_{1},t_{1})\in Q_{r}(x_{0},t_{0})
, and define, for
c_{1},c_{2}>0
to be chosen later,

z(x,t)=u(x,t)-c_{1}(x-\Lambda_{t})+c_{2}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{2}((x-x_{1})^{2}+t_{1}-t)-w(x,t)\right),\quad(x,t)\in D:=Q_{r}^{-}(x_{1},t_{1}).

We have, in view of (
2.25
), (
3.9
) and the monotonicity of
\Lambda
, in the distributional sense,

z_{t}-\frac{1}{2}z_{xx}=c_{1}\dot{\Lambda}_{t}+c_{2}(\nu(x)-c_{0})\geq 0,\quad(x,t)\in Q_{2r}(x_{0},t_{0})\cap\{u>0\}.

(3.10)

Recalling (
1.14
), Lemmas
2.2
and
2.8
, and the continuity of
w
, we see from (
3.9
) that if
c_{1}
is sufficiently small, depending on
c_{0}
and
\alpha
but not on
(x_{1},t_{1})
, then

\liminf_{(x,t)\in D,\;\text{dist}((x,t),\{u=0\})\to 0}z(x,t)\geq 0.

(3.11)

Requiring
c_{1}<\frac{c_{0}r^{2}}{64(r+|x_{0}|)}c_{2}
, we may fix
0<\rho\ll r
small enough (depending on
r
,
c_{0}
, and the local
C^{1,1}_{x}\cap C^{0,1}_{t}
norm of
w
, but not on
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, or
(x_{1},t_{1})
), such that

z\geq-c_{2}\frac{c_{0}r^{2}}{16}-c_{2}C\rho^{2}+c_{2}\frac{c_{0}r^{2}}{4}\geq c_{2}\frac{c_{0}r^{2}}{8},\;\;(x,t)\in\left(\partial_{p}D+Q_{\rho}(0,0))\cap(\{u=0\}+Q_{\rho}(0,0)\right).

(3.12)

On the other hand, if
(x,t)\in Q_{2r}(x_{0},t_{0})\backslash(\{u=0\}+Q_{\rho}(0,0))
, then
u(x,t)\geq\delta
for some
\delta>0
that depends on
\rho
and
u
, but not on
(x_{1},t_{1})
. We therefore have

z(x,t)\geq\delta-C(c_{1}+c_{2})\geq\delta/2,\quad(x,t)\in(D+Q_{\rho}(0,0))\backslash(\{u=0\}+Q_{\rho}(0,0)),

(3.13)

for sufficiently small
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, depending on
\delta
,
r
,
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{2r}(x_{0},t_{0}))}
, and
|x_{0}|
, but not on
(x_{1},t_{1})
. Note that
z\geq 0
in
\{u=0\}
and, in view of (
3.10
),
z
is supercaloric in
Q_{2r}(x_{0},t_{0})\cap\{u>0\}.
Thus (for instance, by mollification followed by the classical maximum principle), (
3.11
), (
3.12
), and (
3.13
) imply that
z\geq 0
almost everywhere in
D
. Recalling that
\Lambda
is upper semi-continuous, it follows that
z
is upper semi-continuous in
\{u>0\}
, and therefore
z(x_{1},t_{1})\geq 0
, which yields

u(x_{1},t_{1})\geq c_{1}(x_{1}-\Lambda_{t_{1}})+c_{2}w(x_{1},t_{1})\geq c_{1}(x_{1}-\Lambda_{t_{1}}).

Since the choice of
(x_{1},t_{1})\in Q_{r}(x_{0},t_{0})
was arbitrary, this concludes the proof.
∎

As an application, we can already show that the free boundary is locally Lipschitz continuous.

Corollary 3.4
.

The freezing time
s:(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)\to(0,\infty)
is locally Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
and let
\Lambda_{0}+\delta<x_{1}<x_{2}<\alpha-\delta
. Assume that
r:=|x_{1}-x_{2}|<\delta/4
and
s(x_{2})\geq s(x_{1})>\delta/4.
Then, by Proposition
3.3
, there exists a constant
c>0
, depending on
\delta
but not on
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, such that for
t\in(s(x_{1}),s(x_{2}))
,

u(x_{2}+r,t)\geq c(x_{2}+r-\Lambda_{t})^{+}\geq c(x_{2}+r-\Lambda_{s(x_{2})^{-}})^{+}\geq cr.

Thus since
w(x_{1},s(x_{1}))=w_{x}(x_{1},s(x_{1}))=0
and, by Proposition
2.5
,
|w_{xx}|
is bounded,

cr(s(x_{2})-s(x_{1}))\leq\int_{s(x_{1})}^{s(x_{2})}u(x_{2}+r,s)ds=\int_{s(x_{1})}^{s(x_{2})}-w_{t}(x_{2}+r,s)ds\leq w(x_{2}+r,s(x_{1}))\leq Cr^{2},

that is,

|s(x_{2})-s(x_{1})|\leq\frac{C}{c}|x_{2}-x_{1}|.

∎

As a second application of the non-degeneracy estimate, we now prove that, for positive times
t>0
, any physical solution falls under the well-posedness regime of
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
. Indeed, the existence, uniqueness, and regularity results in that work were obtained under the extra assumption that the initial density
u_{0}
changes monotonicity at most finitely often in compact sets. We show that any physical solution automatically satisfies this local monotonicity property at any time level
t>0
, even if the initial data does not.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), and let
u
be given by (
1.4
). Then, for every
t>0
, the function
u(\cdot,t^{-})
changes monotonicity at most finitely often on compact subsets of
[\Lambda_{t^{-}},\infty)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.4
,
u\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}((0,\infty);H^{1}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}})).
Therefore, in particular, for almost every
t_{1}>0
,
u(\cdot,t_{1})
is in
H^{1}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}})
and is therefore continuous. This implies, for almost every
t_{1}>0
,

\lim_{x\downarrow\Lambda_{t_{1}^{-}}}u(x,t_{1})=0.

(3.14)

By Lemmas
2.8
and
3.1
, it follows that
\Lambda_{t_{1}}=\Lambda_{t_{1}^{-}}
and
u(\cdot,t_{1})=u(\cdot,t_{1}^{-})
for any such
t_{1}
. Furthermore, in view of Lemma
2.7
and Proposition
3.3
,
\lim_{x\downarrow\Lambda_{t_{1}}}u_{x}(x,t_{1})
exists, and is strictly positive. On the other hand, since
u
solves the heat equation in
\{u>0\}=\{(x,t):x>\Lambda_{t}\}
,
u(\cdot,t_{1})
is analytic in
(\Lambda_{t_{1}},\infty)
. From the strict monotonicity near the boundary and the interior analyticity, we infer that
u(\cdot,t_{1})=u(\cdot,t_{1}^{-})
changes monotonicity at most finitely often on compact subsets of
[\Lambda_{t},\infty)
. By
[
7
, Lem 4.1]
, this monotonicity property propagates in time and is therefore valid for
u(\cdot,t^{-})
, for every
t\geq t_{1}
. Since
t_{1}
may be chosen to be arbitrarily small, we conclude that the property is valid for all
t>0
.
∎

Finally, we may now prove that, for physical solutions, local uniqueness implies global uniqueness.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Assume
\Lambda^{1}
and
\Lambda^{2}
are two physical solutions of (
1.1
), and assume that
\Lambda^{1}_{t_{0}-}=\Lambda^{2}_{t_{0}-}
for some
t_{0}>0
. Letting
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be the corresponding functions given by (
1.4
), we then have
u_{1}(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})=u_{2}(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})
. In addition, by Proposition
3.5
,
u_{1}(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})=u_{2}(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})
changes monotonicity at most finitely many times on compact subsets of
[\Lambda^{1}_{t_{0}^{-}},\infty)=[\Lambda^{2}_{t_{0}^{-}},\infty)
. Therefore, by
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
,
\Lambda^{1}_{t}=\Lambda^{2}_{t}
for
t\geq t_{0}
.
∎

4
Jump times for physical solutions and backward propagation of oscillation

This section is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
1.3
. Namely, we will prove that, for any physical solution
\Lambda
, no positive time
t_{1}
can be an accumulation point for the set of discontinuities of
\Lambda
. We first observe that, in view of the non-degeneracy estimates of the previous section, the jumps cannot accumulate to the right of
t_{1}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), and let
u
be given by (
1.4
). There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\Lambda\in C^{1}((t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon))
,
u\in C({\mathbb{R}}\times(t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon))
, and

\dot{\Lambda}_{t}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\lim_{x\downarrow\Lambda_{t}}u_{x}(x,t)=:\frac{\alpha}{2}u_{x}(\Lambda_{t},t)>0,\quad t\in(t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon).

(4.1)

Proof.

By Proposition
3.5
, for every
t>0
,
u(\cdot,t^{-})
changes monotonicity at most finitely many times on compact subsets of
(\Lambda_{t},\infty)
. The result then follows directly by applying
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
to the unique physical solution on
[t_{0},\infty)
with initial density
u(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})/\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u(\cdot,t_{0}^{-})
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

For the benefit of the reader, we observe that, formally, (
4.1
) follows by differentiating with respect to time the identity

\Lambda_{t}=\alpha{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq t)=\alpha\left(1-\int_{\Lambda_{t}}^{\infty}u(x,t)dx\right),

(4.2)

while using the fact that
u(\Lambda_{t},t)=0
and
u
solves the heat equation in
\{x>\Lambda_{t}\}
.

The bulk of this section will then be spent showing that jump times cannot accumulate to the left of any
t_{1}>0
. The strategy will be to relate the appearance of jumps of
\Lambda
with oscillatory behavior of
u
in past times. For this purpose, we begin by citing the following result from
[
7
, Lem. 4.2]
, which gives a local description of the zero set of
u_{x}
in the set
\{u>0\}
, and is valid for any local solution of the heat equation.

Lemma 4.3

(Local description of the set of critical points for the heat equation)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), and let
u
be given by (
1.4
), and let
(x,t)\in\{u>0\}
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
be the smallest integer such that
\partial^{k+1}_{x}u(x,t)\neq 0
. Then there exists a neighborhood
Q_{\delta}(x_{0},t_{0})
in which the zero set of
u_{x}
is the union of
k
curves.
2\lfloor k/2\rfloor
curves are given by the graphs (in the
(t,x)
–coordinate system) of continuous functions on
[t-\delta,t]
that are
C^{\infty}
on
[t-\delta,t)
. In the
(x,t)
–coordinate system, these curves form
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
strictly convex smooth functions with graphs contained in the negative half-space. If
k
is odd, there is one more curve given, in the
(t,x)
coordinates, by the graph of a
C^{\infty}
function on
[t-\delta,t+\delta]
that, in particular, crosses the
x
axis.

We now show that, for any
t_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, any
t_{1}>t_{0}
sufficiently close to
t_{0}
, and any point
(x_{1},t_{1})
such that
u(x_{1},t_{1})>0
and
u_{x}(x_{1},t_{1})=0
, the point
(x_{1},t_{1})
can be connected back to
\overline{\{u(\cdot,t_{0})>0\}}
with a continuous curve that remains contained in
\{u>0\}\cap\{u_{x}=0\}
for
t\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
. In addition, this curve may be chosen so that
u_{x}\neq 0
in either a left-neighborhood or a right-neighborhood of the curve.

Lemma 4.4

(Local construction of zero curves)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), and let
u
be given by (
1.4
). Let
t_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, and let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
\Lambda\in C^{1}((t_{0},t_{0}+\varepsilon))
, and

for

. Let
x_{1}\in(\Lambda_{t_{1}},\infty)
be such that
u_{x}(x_{1},t_{1})=0
. There exist continuous curves
z^{-},z^{+}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to(0,\infty)
, such that

(a)

z^{\pm}(t_{1})=x_{1}
, and
z^{-}(t)\leq z^{+}(t)
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.

(b)

z^{\pm}(t)\in(\Lambda_{t},\infty)
and
u_{x}(t,z^{\pm}(t))=0
whenever
t\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
.

(c)

For every
t\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
, there exists a parabolic neighborhood

Q_{\delta}^{-}(z^{\pm}(t),t)\subset\{(t,x)\in(t_{0},t_{1}]\times(0,\infty):\Lambda_{t}<x\}

(4.3)

on which
u_{x}(t,x)\neq 0
whenever
\pm x>\pm z^{\pm}(t)
.

The curves
z^{\pm}=z^{\pm}_{(t_{1},x_{1})}
are uniquely determined by
t_{1}
and
x_{1}
. Furthermore, two curves
z^{-}_{(t_{1},x_{1})}
and
z^{-}_{(t_{2},x_{2})}
(or two curves
z^{+}_{(t_{1},x_{1})}
and
z^{+}_{(t_{2},x_{2})}
) do not intersect at any
t\in[t_{0},\min(t_{1},t_{2})]
unless they agree in all of
[t_{0},\min(t_{1},t_{2})]
.

Proof.

This result is based on part of the proof of

p_{2}(x,t)=\begin{cases}\alpha^{-1}(x^{+})^{2}&\text{if }u(x_{0},t_{0}^{-})=0,\\
-\alpha^{-1}t&\text{if }u(x_{0},t_{0}^{-})=\alpha^{-1}.\end{cases}

(5.3)

The points
x_{0}\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)\backslash\overline{J}
such that
u(x_{0},s(x_{0})^{-})=0
form an open subset
\tilde{U}
of
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)\backslash\overline{J}
with countable complement, and we have

s\in C^{\infty}(\tilde{U}),\quad\Lambda\in C^{\infty}(\tilde{U}),

with
s^{\prime}>0
in
U
.

Proof.

Since
x\notin\overline{J}
, (
1.14
) and Proposition
2.5
imply that, in some neighborhood of
(x_{0},t_{0})
,
w
solves

\begin{cases}w_{t}-\frac{1}{2}w_{xx}=-\alpha^{-1}\chi_{w>0}&\\
w_{t}\leq 0,\;\{w>0\}=\{w_{t}<0\}.\end{cases}

(5.4)

Therefore, the classical theory for the parabolic obstacle problem (see
[
1
, Prop. 5.5, Thm. 3.9]
) implies that (
5.2
) holds for some uniquely determined
p_{2}
, where

p_{2}=\alpha^{-1}(x^{+})^{2}\quad\text{or}\quad p_{2}=\alpha^{-1}(x^{-})^{2}\quad\text{ or}\quad p_{2}=\alpha^{-1}(-mt+(1-m)x^{2}),\quad m\in[0,1]

(5.5)

So to prove (
5.3
), we only need to show that, if
p_{2}
is of the third type in (
5.5
), then necessarily
\alpha^{-1}m=u(x_{0},t_{0}^{-})
and
m=1
, and that the second possibility in (
5.5
) cannot occur. Note first that (
5.2
) implies the expansion

w(x,t)=\alpha^{-1}(m(t_{0}-t)+(1-m)(x-x_{0})^{2})+o((t_{0}-t)+|x-x_{0}|^{2}),\quad t\leq t_{0}.

(5.6)

Therefore, in particular, letting
x=x_{0}
we have the one-sided expansion

w(x_{0},t)=\alpha^{-1}m(t_{0}-t)+o((t_{0}-t)),\quad t\leq t_{0},

as
t\uparrow t_{0}
. Recalling that
w_{t}=-u
, L’Hôpital’s rule and Lemma
2.8
therefore yield

u(x_{0},t_{0}^{-})=\alpha^{-1}m.

On the other hand, if we assume that
m<1
, then (
5.6
) implies

w(x,t_{0})=\alpha^{-1}(1-m)(x-x_{0})^{2}+o(|x-x_{0}|^{2}).

But this would mean that
u(x,s(x_{0}))>0
for
0<|x-x_{0}|\ll 1
, which is clearly not possible by the geometry of the free boundary (in fact,
u(x,s(x_{0}))=0
for
x<x_{0}
). Thus
m=1
, and
u(x_{0},t_{0}^{-})=\alpha^{-1}
whenever
p_{2}
has the third form in (
5.5
). By a similar argument, we rule out the possibility that
p_{2}=\alpha^{-1}(x^{-})^{2}
, since it would imply that
u(x,s(x_{0}))>0
for
0<x_{0}-x\ll 1
.

We have shown that
\tilde{U}
equals the set of points
x_{0}\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)\backslash\overline{J}
such that the blow-up
p_{2}
has the first form in (
5.3
). Thus, the fact that
\tilde{U}
is open and that
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}(s(\tilde{U}))
is a classical result about the parabolic obstacle problem (see, for instance,
[
1
, Lem 4.2]
,
[
3
, Thm. II]
). On the other hand, by Lemma
4.1
and Proposition
3.3
, for any
x_{0}\in\tilde{U}
, and some small constant
c_{0}>0
,

\dot{\Lambda}_{s(x_{0})}=\lim_{t\downarrow s(x_{0})}\dot{\Lambda}_{t}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\lim_{t\downarrow s(x_{0})}u_{x}(\Lambda_{t},t)\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}c_{0}>0,

(5.7)

which implies that
s=\Lambda^{-1}
is also smooth in
\tilde{U}
.
∎

We now show that the regular and singular points, as given by Definition
1.5
, exhaust the free boundary. We also show that, thanks to Theorem
1.3
, then the set
\partial J
(in the subspace topology of
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
) consists exclusively of the endpoints of individual jumps.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
) and let
s
be given by (
1.6
). Let
R
and
S
be, respectively, the regular and singular points. Then
R\cap S=\emptyset
and
R\cup S=(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
. Moreover, if
\overline{J}
denotes the closure of
J
relative to
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
, then

\overline{J}=\{x\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha):\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}}<\Lambda_{s(x)},\quad x\in[\Lambda_{s(x)^{-}},\Lambda_{s(x)}]\}.

(5.8)

Proof.

The fact that
R\cap S=\emptyset
follows directly from Lemmas
2.2
,
2.8
and
3.1
. Assume that
x_{0}\in\partial J
. By Theorem
1.3
, jump times form a discrete set, so we must have
\Lambda_{s(x_{0})^{-}}<\Lambda_{s(x_{0})}
and
x_{0}\in\{\Lambda_{s(x_{0})^{-}},\Lambda_{s(x_{0})}\}
. This proves that (
5.8
) holds, and, by definition of singular points,
\partial J\subset S
. On the other hand, Proposition
5.1
shows that
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)\backslash{\partial J}\subset R\cup S
.
∎

We can now prove the first regularity result.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

By definition, the set
J
is open, and
s^{\prime}(x)=0
in
J
, so we immediately obtain

s\in C^{\infty}(J),

(5.9)

and, by Lemma
2.2
,

u\in C^{\infty}(\{(x,t):x\in J,\;t\leq s(x)\}).

(5.10)

By Lemma
3.1
and (
5.8
), we have
U\cap\overline{J}=\emptyset
. This proves that
U=\tilde{U}
, where
\tilde{U}
is as in Proposition
5.1
. Thus,
U
is open,
s\in C^{\infty}(U)
,
\Lambda\in C^{\infty}(s(U))
and
s^{\prime}>0
on
U
. Since
R=U\cup J
, we therefore obtain from (
5.9
) that
s\in C^{\infty}(R)
. We now claim that

u\in C^{\infty}(\{(x,t):x\in U,\;t\leq s(x)\}).

(5.11)

By the smoothness of
\Lambda_{t}
, this is equivalent to showing that, for
x_{0}\in U
and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,
z(x,t)=u(x+\Lambda_{t},t)
satisfies

z\in C^{\infty}(\overline{V_{\varepsilon}}),\quad V_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,t):|t-s(x_{0})|<\varepsilon,\;0\leq x<\varepsilon\}.

(5.12)

But, by (
2.14
) and the definition of
U
,
z\in C(V_{2\varepsilon})
solves, in the classical sense,

\begin{cases}z_{t}-\frac{1}{2}z_{xx}-\dot{\Lambda}_{t}z_{x}=0,&(x,t)\in V_{2\varepsilon},\\
z(0,t)=0&t\in(s(x_{0})-2\varepsilon,s(x_{0})+2\varepsilon),\end{cases}

(5.13)

so (
5.12
) follows by the boundary Schauder estimates, using again the fact that
\Lambda
is smooth near
s(x_{0})
(see
[
21
, Thm 4.22]
). Thus, in view of (
5.10
) and (
5.11
), we have completed the proof of (
1.10
). The first equality in (
1.12
) is simply the chain rule applied to the identity
\Lambda_{s(x)}=x
, and the second equality follows, for instance, by differentiating (
4.2
) and using the smoothness of
u
up to the boundary given by (
5.11
). Finally, by Lemma
4.1
, for every singular point
x_{0}\in S
there exist
\delta=\delta(x_{0})>0
such that
(x_{0},x_{0}+\delta({x_{0}}))
contained in
R
. Hence, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are at most
\lceil n\alpha\rceil
singular points
x_{0}
with
\delta(x_{0})\geq\frac{1}{n}
, which proves that the set
S
is countable.
∎

Our final objective will be to establish global
C^{1}
regularity. We begin by showing that
s
is differentiable at any singular point that is not an endpoint of a jump.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), and let
s
be given by (
1.6
). Assume that
x_{0}\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
is a singular point, and assume that
x_{0}\notin\overline{J}
. Then
s
is twice differentiable at
x_{0}
, and
s^{\prime}(x_{0})=s^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})=0
.

Proof.

Let
t_{0}=s(x_{0})
, and let
w_{r}
be as in Proposition
5.1
so that,
r\downarrow 0
,

w_{r}(x,t)\to p_{2}(t)=-\alpha^{-1}t\quad\text{locally uniformly in}\;{\mathbb{R}}\times(-\infty,0].

We then have the expansion

w(x,t)=-\alpha^{-1}(t_{0}-t)+o(|t-t_{0}|^{2}+|x-x_{0}|^{2}),\quad t\leq t_{0},

and setting
t=s(x)
yields

s(x)=s(x_{0})+o(|x-x_{0}|^{2}),\quad x\leq x_{0}

(5.14)

In view of Proposition
2.5
,
w_{r}
is bounded in
C^{1,1}_{x}\cap C^{0,1}_{t}
, and therefore, up to a subsequence, there exists
w_{0}\in C({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}})
such that

w_{r}(x,t)\to w_{0}(x,t)\quad\text{ locally uniformly in}\quad{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}.

Moreover,
w_{0}\in C^{1,1}_{x}\cap C^{0,1}_{t}
, with
w_{0}\geq 0
,
(w_{0})_{t}\leq 0
, and
w_{0}(x,0)=p_{2}(0)\equiv 0
. This implies that
w_{0}(x)=p_{2}(t)^{+}
, independently of the subsequence, and the convergence holds for the entire family
\{w_{r}\}
. Note that, for sufficiently small
r
,
w_{r}
solves the obstacle problem

(w_{r})_{t}-\frac{1}{2}(w_{r})_{xx}=-\alpha^{-1}\chi_{w_{r}>0},\quad(x,t)\in Q_{2}.

Therefore, by a standard maximum principle argument (see
[
3
, Lem. 5.1]
), one has, for any
(x_{1},t_{1})\in\overline{\{w_{r}>0\}}
and any
\rho\in(0,1)
, the non-degeneracy property

\text{sup}_{(x,t)\in Q_{\rho}^{-}(x_{1},t_{1})}w_{r}\geq c\rho^{2},

for some constant
c>0
depending only on
\alpha
. Thus, since
w_{r}\to p_{2}^{+}=0
uniformly in
(-2,2)\times[0,2]
, we must have, for any
\delta>0
, and
r
sufficiently small, depending on
\delta
,

w_{r}\equiv 0\quad\text{ on }[-1,1]\times[\delta,1].

Translating the statement back to
w
, and recalling that
w_{t}\leq 0
, this means that

w\equiv 0\quad\text{ on }\quad[x_{0}-r,x_{0}-r]\times[t_{0}+\delta r^{2},\infty)

for
r
sufficiently small. Thus, letting
x>x_{0}
and
r=x-x_{0}
, we conclude that

0<s(x)-s(x_{0})<\delta|x-x_{0}|^{2}

for
|x-x_{0}|
sufficiently small. Since
\delta
can be taken arbitrarily small, this says precisely that (
5.14
) holds for
x>x_{0}
.
∎

Next, we show that, along regular points, the speed of the frontier
\Lambda
tends to
+\infty
near a singular point.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\Lambda
be a physical solution to (
1.1
), let
x_{0}\in(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
be a singular point, and assume that
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of regular points such that
x_{n}\to x_{0}
as
n\to\infty
. Then
s^{\prime}(x_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Since
s^{\prime}(x)=0
for all
x\in J
, we may assume that
u(x_{n},s(x_{n})^{-})=u(x_{n},s(x_{n}))=0
for all
n
. Assume first that
x_{n}<x_{0}
for all
n
, so that
s(x_{n})<s(x_{0})
. By Definition
1.5
and Lemma
3.1
,
m:=u(x_{0},t_{0}^{-})>0
. Therefore, by Proposition
2.6
, for
n
sufficiently large, we have

u(x_{0},s(x_{n}))\geq u(x_{0},s(x_{0})^{-})-C(s(x_{0})-s(x_{n}))\geq\frac{m}{2}.

On the other hand, by Lemma
2.7
and Theorem
1.6
,

u(x_{0},s(x_{n}))\leq u(x_{n},s(x_{n}))+u_{x}(x_{n},s(x_{n}))(x_{n}-x_{0})+\frac{C}{2}(x_{n}-x_{0})^{2}=\frac{2}{\alpha s^{\prime}(x_{n})}(x_{0}-x_{n})+\frac{C}{2}(x_{0}-x_{n})^{2}

(5.15)

Thus, we obtain

\frac{2}{\alpha s^{\prime}(x_{n})}\geq\frac{m}{2(x_{0}-x_{n})}-\frac{C}{2}(x_{0}-x_{n})\to+\infty\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

which shows that
s^{\prime}(x_{n})\to 0
.

Next, assume that
x_{n}>x_{0}
for all
n
. Then, since
u(x_{n},s(x_{n})^{-})=0
for all
n
, we must have
\Lambda_{s(x_{0})}=x_{0}
and
u(x_{0}+\delta,s(x_{0}))>0
for
\delta>0
. By Lemmas
2.7
and
2.2
, if
\delta>0
is sufficiently small,

u(x_{0}+\delta,s(x_{0}))=u(x_{0}+\delta,s(x_{0})^{-})\geq u(x_{0},s(x_{0})^{-})-C\delta\geq\frac{m}{2}.

(5.16)

On the other hand,
x_{n}\in(x_{0},x_{0}+\delta)
for sufficiently large
n
, and as in (
5.15
) we have

u(x_{0}+\delta,s(x_{n}))\leq\frac{2}{\alpha s^{\prime}(x_{n})}\delta+\frac{C}{2}\delta^{2}.

(5.17)

Thus, using (
5.16
) and (
5.17
) and letting
n\to\infty
, we obtain

\frac{m}{2}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha\limsup_{n\to\infty}s^{\prime}(x_{n})}\delta+\frac{C}{2}\delta^{2}.

Since
\delta
can be taken arbitrarily small, we infer that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}s^{\prime}(x_{n})=0
.
∎

In view of the preceding lemmas, we may now prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

The
C^{\infty}
claim in the set
R
follows from Theorem
1.6
, so we only need to prove that
s
is globally of class
C^{1}
. We first claim that
s
is everywhere differentiable in
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
. By Theorem
1.6
and Lemma
5.3
, it suffices to show that
s
is differentiable at every singular point
x_{0}\in\overline{J}
. Given that
J
consists of regular points, it follows that
x_{0}\in\partial J
, which, by Lemma
5.2
, means that
\Lambda_{s(x_{0})^{-}}<\Lambda_{s(x_{0})}
and
x_{0}\in\{\Lambda_{s(x_{0})^{-}},\Lambda_{s(x_{0})}\}
. Furthermore, by Theorem
1.3
, there must exist a small
\delta>0
such that
(x_{0}-\delta,x_{0}+\delta)\cap\partial{J}=\{x_{0}\}
. But then, in view of Proposition
5.1
and Lemmas
5.3
and
5.4
,
s
is differentiable in
(x_{0}-\delta,x_{0}+\delta)\backslash\{x_{0}\}
, and
\lim_{x\to x_{0}}s^{\prime}(x)=0
. By L’Hôpital’s rule, we infer that
s
is differentiable at
x_{0}
, and
s^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
. This shows that
s
is everywhere differentiable and, in fact,
s^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
for every singular point
x_{0}
. By Theorem
1.6
,
s^{\prime}
is continuous at any regular point. Finally, by another application of Lemma
5.4
, we conclude that
s^{\prime}
is continuous at every singular point, and therefore in all of
(\Lambda_{0},\alpha)
.
∎
