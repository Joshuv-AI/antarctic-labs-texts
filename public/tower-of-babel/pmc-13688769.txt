A non–inferiority randomized controlled trial comparing nebulized fentanyl and intravenous morphine for acute pain management in the emergency department
Authors: Pitchapar Preungprasob, Rapeeporn Rojsaengroeng, Chirakit Hengrasmee, Pornsiri Kanokkarnjana, Yaowapa Changya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntravenous (IV) morphine is the standard treatment for acute pain in emergency department (ED), but is limited by invasiveness and adverse effects. Nebulized fentanyl is a noninvasive alternative. This study aimed to assess the efficacy of nebulized fentanyl compared with IV morphine for acute pain relief 15 min after administration. Secondary outcomes included pain score trends, adverse effects, and rescue analgesia requirements at 30, 45, and 60 min.MethodsWe conducted a double-blind, randomized controlled trial in patients aged 18 to 65 years who had severe pain (numeric rating scale(NRS) score ≥ 7) originating from headaches, musculoskeletal conditions, or cancer. Patients with abdominal pain, unstable vital signs, and chronic opioid use were excluded. Participants were randomly assigned to received either nebulized fentanyl (4 mcg/kg) or IV morphine (0.1 mg/kg). Pain scores, adverse events, and rescue analgesia were recorded at 15, 30, 45, and 60 min by emergency medicine residents who were blinded to treatment allocation. Non–inferiority margin was established if the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the mean NRS difference did not exceed 3 points.ResultsSeventy-two patients were enrolled, with comparable baseline characteristics and NRS scores (nebulized fentanyl: 7.94 ± 1.09; IV: 8.14 ± 0.90). Both groups showed significant pain reduction at 15 min (nebulized fentanyl: 5.56 ± 1.66; IV: 5.67 ± 1.85; p < 0.001), with no significant difference in mean NRS reduction (p = 0.83). At all measured time points (15, 30, 45, and 60 minutes), the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the mean difference in pain reduction remained below the predefined non–inferiority margin of 3.ConclusionsNebulized fentanyl is a non-inferior, non-invasive alternative to IV morphine for severe acute pain in the ED. However, this finding applies predominantly to stable adults aged 18 to 65 years with musculoskeletal pain. It demonstrated a comparable short-term efficacy and preliminary safety profile within a 60-minute observation window. However, given the small sample size and restrictive exclusion criteria of this study, further larger-scale trials are required to fully establish its safety profile, particularly in broader and higher-risk patient populations.”Clinical trial numberTCTR20240624001. Introduction

Acute pain is a prevalent clinical issue, affecting approximately 70% of patients receiving emergency medical services and representing a common presentation in emergency departments (EDs) [1]. Inadequate acute pain management is associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including increased psychological distress (such as anxiety and depression), impaired occupational functioning, and disrupted sleep patterns [2]. The World Health Organization and European Society for Emergency Medicine recommend the administration of strong opioids in cases of moderate–to–severe acute pain [1, 3]. Morphine remains the most widely used opioid in this context and is administered through intramuscular (IM), subcutaneous (SC), and Intravenous (IV) injections [1]. IV morphine has an onset of action within 5–10 minutes(min) with analgesic effects peaking around 20 min and lasting 4–5 hours(hr). Despite its efficacy, IV morphine has limitations, including the invasive nature of its administration, the time required for IV- line insertion, and an IV insertion failure rate in adults of 12–26% [4–7]. Adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, constipation, dizziness, sedation, and euphoria further complicate its clinical use [1, 6, 8]. Emerging evidence suggests that nebulized fentanyl is a promising alternative for the management of acute pain [9, 10]. A potent synthetic opioid approximately 100 times stronger than morphine, fentanyl has a more favorable side effect profile and a rapid onset of action (1–2 min via IV and 7–8 min via IM) with effects lasting 30–60 min [11, 12]. Nebulized and intranasal fentanyl have demonstrated effective analgesia for cancer pain within 15 min of administration [9]; pharmacokinetic studies have shown that inhaled fentanyl can achieve serum concentrations comparable to those of IV fentanyl, with even faster peak plasma levels [10]. Additionally, Singh et al. [13] found that nebulized fentanyl, although slower in onset than IV, provided a longer duration of pain relief in postoperative patients. In pediatric populations with limb trauma, nebulized fentanyl at a dosage of 4 mcg/kg significantly reduced pain scores and was shown to be a convenient, non–invasive, and effective alternative to IV morphine [14, 15]. Despite these findings, the application of nebulized fentanyl in adult ED patients who experience various forms of acute pain remains unexplored. Given its noninvasive nature, rapid onset, and potentially reduced risk of adverse effects, further investigation is warranted. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of nebulized fentanyl in the management of acute pain in adults in the ED, contributing to the development of improved patient–centered analgesic protocols.

The primary objective of this study was to determine the efficacy of nebulized fentanyl compared with IV morphine in relieving acute severe pain in ED patients 15 min after treatment. The secondary objectives were to compare pain scores at 30, 45, and 60 min as well as the incidence of adverse effects and the requirement for extra rescue pain therapy.

Methods

Study design and settings

This study was a double–blind, non–inferiority, randomized controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio conducted at a single urban teaching hospital in Bangkok, Thailand during July – Dec, 2024.

Participants

The study included patients aged 18–65 years with acute severe pain presenting to the ED with acute severe pain (numeric rating scale(NRS) score ≥ 7) that started within the previous 7 days. To quantify pain intensity, we used the 11-point NRS, ranging from 0 to 10. The NRS was selected because it is a validated, reliable, and highly sensitive tool for evaluating acute pain within the emergency department setting [16, 17]. At each time point (baseline, 15, 30, 45, and 60 minutes), trained emergency medicine residents asked patients to verbally report their current pain level using the NRS, and the self-reported value was recorded. Patients with unstable vital signs (including systolic blood pressure (SBP) <90 or >180 mmHg, heart rate (HR) <50 or >150 bpm, respiratory rate (RR) <12 or > 30 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation < 95%, actual body weight < 40 kg or >115 kg), a history of chronic opioid use > 10 days, certain comorbidities (including asthma, severe liver dysfunction or cirrhosis, and chronic kidney disease defined as CrCl < 30 mL/min (18), chief complaints of abdominal pain, an allergy to opioids, nasal conditions affecting nebulization, contagious respiratory infections, or life–threatening conditions requiring emergent intervention, who were pregnant or breastfeeding, those having used opioids within the last 4 hours, or those who were unable to provide pain scores (because of conditions such as an altered mental status with a Glasgow Coma Scale < 15 or communication barriers) were excluded. The patients presenting with abdominal pain were excluded from the study due to ongoing concerns that opioids may mask clinical signs or worsen conditions such as biliary colic, thereby influencing clinical decision–making [18]. This uncertainty is amplified in low – to middle–income countries, where limited access to advanced diagnostic imaging necessitates greater reliance on physical examination, potentially reinforcing reluctance to administer early analgesia [19, 20].

Eligibility criteria

Patients aged 18–65 years who presented to the emergency department with acute pain of ≤7 days’ duration. Only patients experiencing severe pain were included, defined as a score of 7–10 on the NRS.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had hemodynamic instability, defined as a systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or >180 mmHg, heart rate < 50 or > 150 beats per minute, respiratory rate < 12 or > 30 breaths per minute, or oxygen saturation < 95%. Patients with body weight < 40 kg or >115 kg were also excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included the presence or suspicion of significant comorbidities such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), severe hepatic impairment or cirrhosis, or chronic kidney disease with creatinine clearance (CrCl) <30 mL/min. Patients with a history of analgesic use within the previous 4 hours or long–term opioid use exceeding 10 days, including tramadol and/or codeine, were excluded.

Further exclusions included patients presenting with abdominal pain, those unable to undergo pain assessment due to altered mental status (Glasgow Coma Scale < 15) or communication barriers, and individuals with a known allergy to opioids (morphine or fentanyl), ondansetron, or naloxone. Patients with nasal trauma, abnormal nasal function or deformity, nasal masses (e.g., nasopharyngeal carcinoma), active epistaxis, or infectious rhinitis were also excluded. In addition, patients who were unable to tolerate nebulization, such as those with allergies to plastics or claustrophobia, were not eligible. Patients with transmissible infections potentially spread by nebulization, including COVID–19, influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), or tuberculosis, were excluded. Finally, pregnant or breastfeeding women and patients presenting with life–threatening emergencies requiring immediate intervention, such as ST–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or aortic dissection, were excluded from the study.

Study protocols

After the attending physician had independently decided to administer analgesia, the researchers informed the patient of their allocated treatment group, either verbally or in writing, depending on the patient’s condition. Information provided included trial purpose, randomization, interventions (nebulized fentanyl or IV morphine), and monitoring details.

The patients were randomly assigned to two groups. NRS, vital signs, Glasgow Coma Score, and oxygen saturation were assessed and recorded at baseline (0 minutes), and subsequently at 15, 30, 45, and 60 min after initiating the analgesic drug. The occurrence of adverse effects, such as nausea, vomiting, hypotension, bradycardia, decreased RR (<12 breaths per minute), and decreased level of consciousness, was constantly monitored by on–duty residents who were unaware of the group assignment.

Rescue therapy

After the interventions, on–duty residents recorded pain assessments. The treating emergency residents and patients were blinded to the drugs administered. If the patient did not achieve sufficient pain relief (defined as an NRS score > 4) at 30 minutes after the intervention, the treating emergency physician provided a rescue dose of 1 mg IV morphine. Both the patients and the assessing residents remained blinded to the initial treatment group. The subsequent pain scores at 45 and 60 minutes reflected the post-rescue outcomes for these patients. Study termination occurred upon patient withdrawal or the development of severe adverse effects such as respiratory depression, bradycardia, hypotension, or anaphylaxis.

Definitions

Administration of analgesic medication by Nebulization

Nebulization is the process of generating aerosol droplets from a liquid medication using a nebulizer device enclosed within a chamber that holds the solution around a narrow tube connected to ambient air. When compressed air or oxygen is rapidly passed through the tube, a negative pressure is created in the outgoing air stream. The high–pressure external air pushes the liquid into the air stream, causing it to impact a baffle placed in the same line, which breaks the liquid into a fine mist of aerosol droplets. These droplets can then carry small particles into the lower respiratory tract. Nebulizers are widely used for administering bronchodilator medications, which are typically mixed with normal saline, to treat patients with bronchospasm conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The aerosol mist also helps moisturize the respiratory tract, softening mucus and facilitating its clearance [18].

Pain

Pain is defined as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage or inflammation [20]. Acute pain constitutes approximately 80% of all pain and is characterized by sudden onset, clear localization, identifiable cause, and defined duration. It is usually temporary, lasting 0–7 days up to less than 6 months, typically resulting from tissue injury or pathology that resolves once healing occurs. Most cases arise secondary to pathological disease damaging tissues [21]. Pain consists of three components: pain stimuli (noxious stimuli), pain receptors (nociceptors), and pain transmission pathways (pain impulse pathways).

Pathophysiological mechanisms of pain are classified as follows:

1. Nociceptive Pain: This type of pain results from tissue damage or inflammation activating nociceptors located throughout the body’s sensory organs. These nociceptors are sensitive to stimuli and transmit signals via neurons to the brain, which interprets them as pain. Nociceptive pain is further divided into three subtypes:

(1.1) Somatic Pain: arising from skin and subcutaneous tissue

(1.2) Deep somatic pain refers to pain originating from tissues located deeper than the skin surface, such as muscles, bones, or joints.

(1.3) Visceral pain originates from internal organs within the abdominal cavity and is often poorly localized, for example, pain arising from the stomach or intestines [21].

2. Neuropathic pain: Defined as pain resulting from damage to nervous tissue, with pathological origins in either the central or peripheral nervous system. The characteristics of neuropathic pain include burning, hot sensations, sensations of pins and needles, and pain upon touch (allodynia) [21].

Pain assessment

Refers to the measurement of pain intensity based on the patient’s subjective experience using NRS. This tool uses a scale from 0 to 10, where ‘0’ represents ‘no pain’ and ‘10’ represents the ‘worst pain imaginable’. At each time point, the assessor verbally asks the patient to rate their current pain level, and the self-reported number is recorded [1, 21]. Pain management refers to interventions aimed at eliminating, reducing, alleviating, and controlling pain, as well as mitigating its associated adverse effects [20]. Pain severity refers to the degree of suffering or intensity of pain experienced, ranging from mild to severe levels [20].

Randomization and masking

After obtaining ethical approval, the researchers trained emergency physicians on the study protocol. Patients were randomly assigned by a computer into two groups (nebulized fentanyl + IV normal saline solution (NSS) vs. nebulized NSS+IV morphine) using a 1:1 allocation ratio. The randomized numbers were kept on a computer and were only opened by the researchers before the interventions.

The study-maintained blinding through a double–dummy design and other procedural measures, detailed as follows:

- Patients were randomized to receive either nebulized fentanyl plus IV NSS or IV morphine plus nebulized NSS. This ensured that all participants received both nebulized and IV administrations, but only one contained the active drug, maintaining the blind for both delivery modes.

- A designated nurse, who was not involved in outcome assessment, prepared the medication according to the randomization list, so that the emergency medicine residents assessing outcomes were unaware of the treatment allocation.

- An emergency physician prescribed the treatment, while the other emergency medicine resident, who was responsible for recording pain scores, vital signs and adverse events was blinded to the study group. They did not know which treatment the patient received.

Medication preparations

A nurse prepared fentanyl (4 mcg/kg mixed with 4 mL NSS for nebulization) and morphine solutions (0.1 mg/kg mixed with 10 mL NSS for IV administration). In our study, a nebulized fentanyl dose of 4 mcg/kg was adopted to balance efficacy with safety. While alternative regimens like 1.5 or 3 mcg/kg exist, the low systemic bioavailability of inhaled fentanyl (~20%) warrants a higher dose to treat severe pain. Extrapolating the 4 mcg/kg dose from pediatric trials to adults is physiologically sound, as this lower bioavailability remains consistent across age groups [22]. After obtaining written informed consent, patients were randomized to receive either nebulized fentanyl (4 mcg/kg) plus an IV NSS or IV morphine (0.1 mg/kg) plus nebulized NSS. This double–dummy design ensured blinding of patients and healthcare providers to the treatment allocation. Both nebulized and IV administrations used identical–appearing active and placebo preparations to maintain blinding.

Nebulized fentanyl was administered for 8 min using a standard Venti mask with a 4 mL nebulization chamber at a constant oxygen flow rate of 8–10 L/min. After completion of nebulization, the patient’s vital signs and NRS scores were recorded at 15 min after the end of nebulization and subsequently at 30, 45, and 60–minute intervals until the end of the study by on–duty emergency residents who were blinded to the treatment groups. Further complaints of pain with an NRS ≥ 4 after 30 min and rescue therapy (1 mg IV morphine) were provided by the emergency physicians treating the patient.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this randomized controlled trial was calculated to evaluate the non–inferiority of nebulized fentanyl compared with intravenous morphine for reducing moderate to severe acute pain in the emergency department. The primary outcome was the mean pain intensity measured using the NRS at 15 minutes after drug administration. The calculation was based on comparing the mean difference in pain reduction between the experimental and control groups.

The sample size estimation was performed using a formula for comparison of two means in a non–inferiority trial. In this calculation, n₁ represents the sample size in the experimental group and n₂ represents the sample size in the control group, with the allocation ratio (k = n₁/n₂) set at 1, indicating equal group sizes. The significance level was set at α = 0.05, corresponding to a standard normal deviate of Z₁−α = 1.645, and the statistical power was set at 90%, corresponding to Z₁−β = 1.28.

Minimum Clinically Important Difference (MCID): In emergency department acute pain management, the established MCID for the NRS typically ranges between 1.3 and 2.4 points [16]. Setting the margin at 3.0 points aligns with standard regulatory guidance (e.g., USA Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency non-inferiority frameworks), which allows a margin slightly above the MCID for patient-observed outcomes if the active comparator has a high magnitude of effect. The expected mean change in pain score in the experimental group (μ₁) was assumed to be 3.09, while the expected mean change in the control group (μ₂) was 2.0. The non–inferiority margin (δ) was set at 3.0, and the variance (σ2) of the differences in pain score changes was assumed to be 2.5. Both Intent-to-Treat (ITT) and Per-Protocol (PP) analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the non-inferiority conclusion. The ITT population included all randomized participants who received at least one dose of the assigned intervention, while the PP population excluded participants with major protocol violations or rescue therapy before the primary endpoint. Non-inferiority was considered confirmed only if both the ITT and PP analyses demonstrated that the upper bound of the two-sided 95% CI did not exceed the predefined margin of 3.

These parameters were derived from the study by Furyk et al. titled “Nebulized fentanyl versus intravenous morphine in children with suspected limb fractures in the emergency department: a randomized clinical trial.” Although the initial parameters were informed by pediatric data, direct adult equivalents support our assumptions. Specifically, Farahmand et al. demonstrated in an adult ED that 4 mcg/kg of nebulized fentanyl was both safe and non-inferior to 0.1 mg/kg of IV morphine for severe acute pain, confirming that the dosing parameters and pharmacokinetics are appropriately extrapolated to the adult population.

Based on the sample size calculation using these parameters, a minimum of 30 participants per group was required. To compensate for potential dropout or loss to follow–up, an additional 20% was added to the calculated sample size. Consequently, the final sample size was 36 participants in the experimental group and 36 participants in the control group, resulting in a total sample size of at least 72 participants for this study.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis for this study was conducted in two parts based on data type. Categorical variables such as sex, comorbidities, education level, frequency distributions, and percentages were calculated and differences between the nebulized fentanyl and IV morphine groups were assessed using the chi–squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test when expected cell frequencies were 5 or more. For sparse data with expected frequencies below 5, such as rare adverse events and rescue therapy rates, Fisher’s exact test was applied to ensure statistical accuracy. For continuous variables such as age and weight, means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges were presented depending on the data distribution. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t–test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Continuous variables were assessed for normality. Student’s t-test was used to compare normally distributed data, such as pain scores and vital signs, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to non-normally distributed variables, such as age and weight.

The primary analysis focused on comparing the difference from baseline NRS at 15 min posttreatment, focusing on non–inferiority using Student’s t–test. Non–inferiority was considered if the upper bound of the 95% CI for the mean difference in the NRS score did not exceed 3(4,8,10). Secondary comparisons for adverse event rates and the need for rescue therapy at 30, 45, and 60 min were performed between groups using chi–square or Fisher’s exact tests, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software: Release 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

A total of 115 patients aged 18–65 years with NRS scores of > 7 were initially considered. Following the exclusion of patients with abdominal pain (n = 25), unstable vital signs (n = 1), reactive airway disease (n = 2), chronic liver or kidney disease (n = 4), communication barriers (n = 1), pregnancy (n = 4), morphine allergy (n = 1), recent opioid use within 4 hours (n = 3), and those who declined to participate (n = 2), 72 patients were enrolled (Fig. 1).

Demographics and comorbidities were not significantly different between the two groups (Table 1). The median age was 42 years (interquartile range (IQR): 26.50–54) in the nebulized fentanyl group and 45.50 years (IQR: 30.50–52.50) in the IV morphine group. Females comprised the majority of participants in both groups (19 patients (52.80%) in the nebulized fentanyl group and 21 (58.30%) in the IV morphine group). Musculoskeletal pain was the most common cause of pain reported.

Baseline characteristics

Primary outcome

The baseline NRS in the nebulized fentanyl group was 7.90 ± 1.10, and that in the IV morphine group was 8.10 ± 0.90. At 15 min, the NRSs in the nebulized fentanyl and the IV morphine groups were 5.56 ± 1.66 and 5.67 ± 1.85, respectively, both showing a significant reduction from baseline (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The upper limit of the 95% CI for the mean difference in NRS reduction did not exceed 3 at 15 min (Table 2), supporting the non–inferiority of nebulized fentanyl to IV morphine for acute pain management (Fig. 2).

Secondary outcomes

Both nebulized fentanyl and IV morphine significantly reduced the NRS (p < 0.001) at each measured interval (30, 45, and 60 min). There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of changes from baseline at any time point (Table 2). No significant differences were observed in changes in HR, RR, or Oxygen saturation over time between groups; although minor differences in SBP were noted, they were not clinically significant, indicating comparable hemodynamic stability and respiratory safety between the two treatments (Supplement 1–5).

Adverse events and rescue therapy

Adverse events were minimal and occurred only in the IV morphine group. Three patients experienced mild adverse events: one reported nasal irritation, which resolved without intervention, likely caused by oxygen and NSS nebulization; one experienced vomiting, an opioid side effect, which was effectively managed with IV ondansetron (8 mg) according to the protocol; and one developed a rash attributed to a rapid IV injection of morphine, which was not associated with clinical anaphylaxis and resolved following treatment with IV chlorpheniramine (10 mg). None of the events had any residual clinical consequences (Table 3). No serious adverse events such as anaphylaxis or opioid toxicity were observed in either group. Rescue therapy with IV morphine (1 mg) was required in two patients from each group. (Table 4)

Discussion

Our findings show that nebulized fentanyl provided pain relief in the ED comparable to that provided by IV morphine, supporting its noninferiority as an alternative to IV morphine. Our findings align with those of Farahmand et al. [21] who performed a double–blind randomized clinical trial (RCT) comparing nebulized fentanyl (4 mcg/kg) versus IV morphine (0.1 mg/kg) in 90 patients presenting to the ED with moderate–to–severe limb trauma pain (mean initial NRS = 8), where pain relief in both groups at 5 and 10 min was similar [23]. While earlier studies like Farahmand et al. recorded pain scores at 5 and 10 minutes, our data collection began at 15 minutes to account for the 8-minute nebulization delivery time and the double-dummy blinding protocol. This interval ensured the complete administration of the active medication and allowed the assessment of true post-treatment pain reduction without interrupting patient care. However, since our study initiated data recording at 15 min post–administration, we cannot say whether a similar treatment response occurred at the 5–minute mark. Consistent with the results of the previous study, no adverse events were observed. Additionally, the need for rescue therapy in both groups was comparable with that reported by Farahmand et al. Deaton et al. [22] examined a double–blind RCT between nebulized fentanyl (2 mcg/kg) and IV morphine (0.1 mg/kg) in patients with abdominal pain (mean baseline NRS = 8.4), finding that pain relief was comparable at 10 min with a duration of action of up to 40 min, and fewer rescue doses were needed. This is in contrast to the results of our study, which used a dose of 4 mcg/kg nebulized fentanyl. Physiologically, nebulized fentanyl has a lower systemic bioavailability (~20%) compared to IV administration, requiring higher doses (4–5 mcg/kg) to achieve comparable analgesic effects. In our study, a nebulized fentanyl dose of 4 mcg/kg was adopted to balance analgesic efficacy with safety considerations.Furthermore, while Deaton et al. evaluated abdominal pain, we excluded this patient population. In many low- to middle-income clinical environments where advanced diagnostic imaging may not be immediately available, physicians must rely heavily on serial physical examinations. This context justifies a more conservative approach to early opioid analgesia in abdominal cases to avoid masking surgical signs. While previous studies have evaluated nebulized fentanyl in both adult and pediatric populations, this study offers important local validation. Rather than introducing a globally novel therapeutic agent, our study contextualizes and validates this analgesic protocol specifically within a Southeast Asian urban tertiary teaching hospital setting. Thus, our findings support the practical implementation of nebulized fentanyl as a viable, non-invasive alternative to IV morphine when intravenous access is difficult or when painful IV cannulation should be avoided.

The use of nebulized fentanyl in the ED may offer a practical alternative to IV morphine when a painful IV cannulation should be avoided. The claim that nebulized fentanyl is a good alternative for difficult IV access is a logical inference based on the fact that nebulization is non-invasive. In accordance with the CONSORT extension for non-inferiority trials, our interpretation of nebulized fentanyl’s non-inferiority balances its clinical utility against its physiological limits. While the non-invasive route prevents painful IV insertion and has a favorable preliminary safety profile, its lower bioavailability means higher doses (4 mcg/kg) are required. Thus, it serves as a valid alternative to IV morphine specifically when avoiding invasive access is prioritized. However, further research with larger sample sizes should explore the efficacy of nebulized fentanyl compared to IV morphine in managing pain in the ED to evaluate whether analgesic onset occurs within 5 min. In addition, future research should evaluate long–term clinical outcomes, safety, and satisfaction of patients and healthcare providers with this treatment.

Limitations

A limitation of our study is that the non–inferiority margin was not directly derived from the minimum clinically important difference (MCID). Instead, it was informed by regulatory guidance, historical data on the active control, and clinical judgment regarding an acceptable loss of efficacy. Although this approach is consistent with recommended practice, we acknowledge that it may not fully reflect an MCID–based threshold and this should be considered when interpreting our findings.

The relatively small sample size and single center design may have limited the generalizability and external validity of our findings. While our study observed no severe adverse events in the nebulized fentanyl group, the safety findings must be interpreted with caution. The small sample size (n = 36 per group) and the short 60-minute follow-up period limit our ability to detect rare complications or delayed adverse effects. Furthermore, because we excluded patients with significant comorbidities, unstable vital signs, or those older than 65 years, these preliminary safety outcomes cannot be generalized to more medically complex populations. Pain assessment is subjective and can be influenced by individual patient perceptions or distractions, introducing potential bias. However, assessors were blinded to the intervention. The findings were specific to patients with severe pain (NRS 7–10) and excluded patients with abdominal pain, altered mental states (such as intoxication, confusion, or suspected traumatic brain injury), or hemodynamic instability, affecting their applicability to these populations. In addition, patient satisfaction should be further investigated, as the administration of nebulized fentanyl may cause claustrophobia, discomfort, or irritation. Additional adverse events specific to nebulizing opioids include bronchospasm, bitter or metallic taste, and dry mouth [24]. Moreover, the use of nebulized fentanyl typically requires connection to an oxygen or air pipeline, which may affect the practicality of its use. Therefore, it is important to assess the feasibility and ease of administration from the perspective of healthcare providers in emergency departments across various healthcare settings, including both large and small hospitals, as well as facilities in urban and rural areas. The one–hour follow–up period in this study limited the ability to evaluate the long–term efficacy and safety of the intervention. Although a 60–minute observation period is consistent with common emergency department discharge timelines, it may not adequately capture sustained analgesic effects or the potential need for additional dosing. A longer follow–up duration of at least 4–6 hours would provide more comprehensive insights into extended pain relief, the need for medication adjustment at home, and other post–discharge outcomes.

Conclusions

Nebulized fentanyl is a non-inferior, non-invasive alternative to IV morphine for severe acute pain in the ED. However, this finding applies predominantly to stable adults aged 18 to 65 years with musculoskeletal pain. It demonstrated a comparable short-term efficacy and preliminary safety profile within a 60-minute observation window. However, given the small sample size and restrictive exclusion criteria of this study, further larger-scale trials are required to fully establish its safety profile, particularly in broader and higher-risk patient populations.
