Network Centrality Metrics Based on Unrestricted Paths, Walks and Cycles Compared to Standard Centrality Metrics
Juuso Luhtala, Vesa Kuikka, Kimmo Kaski

Abstract
A key issue with standard network measures of closeness and betweenness centrality is that they rely on the shortest paths between nodes within the network structure, whereas the degree centrality only reveals the immediate neighborhood of a node. Furthermore, many measures found in the literature do not accurately represent the physical or probabilistic characteristics of nodal centrality, network flow, and other salient properties. For example, recurrent spreading in a network is often overlooked by these metrics. Standard centrality measures have limitations, being optimal for one application but not for others. Here, we present new metrics based on our influence spreading model to characterize network structure for various network science applications. These probabilistic measures account for all feasible walks and cycles in the network. We compare our new metrics with the standard metrics in terms of the node rankings given by different centrality measures, by examining scatter plots, and by using the Pearson correlation coefficient. In the influence spreading model, we define the in-centrality measure to characterize how central a node is as a target of influence by other nodes and the out-centrality measure to characterize how central a node is as a source of influence on other nodes. Our results show that the influence spreading betweenness centrality reveals the importance of alternative routes while maintaining similarity to standard betweenness centrality.

Introduction

Network science is a rapidly expanding field of research that focuses on investigating the structure and dynamics of interdependent phenomena in complex systems of various scientific disciplines. This approach has been applied especially in studies of social science and communication networks, but also in studies of biological networks and its numerous subfields. In these fields of research, various modeling and other computational methodologies have become important in characterizing and analyzing the properties of these networks. The methods used in network science comprise a range of approaches, including statistical techniques, visualization methods, and various metrics that describe the structure, properties, and processes in networks. An important area of research focuses on clusters and communities in networks, where numerous methods for detecting communities have been proposed. In addition, basic tools, such as network centrality metrics, help measure the importance of individual nodes within the network structure.

In this article, we will frequently refer to paths, walks, and cycles. Based on
4
and
9
, we define a walk on a network as an alternating sequence of nodes and edges, where every node is incident to both the edges that come before and after it in the sequence, and a path on a network is a walk where all nodes and edges are distinct. It is important to emphasize that all paths are walks, but all walks are not paths. A cycle is a walk where edges are required to be distinct, and the only repeated nodes are the first and the last node of the walk
4
.

It should be noted that standard centrality measures have limitations, as they may be optimal for one application but suboptimal for another. Network Structure and Dynamics

Here we highlight key characteristics of networks with new metrics that describe their structures, focusing on nodes and edges
33
. Additionally, we describe network flow as a spreading process of non-exclusive events within the network structure.

In the influence spreading model, weights are assigned to both nodes and edges. In this probabilistic framework, the weight of an edge represents the probability of influence or information passing through that edge, while the weight of a node indicates the probability of the node changing its state and enabling the passage of influence or information. These two properties can also be modeled as distinct features.

In the present study, the edges are considered to be directed, unidirectional, or bidirectional. For the bidirectional edges, it is possible to assign different weights for each direction. Undirected edges can be modeled by setting the same edge weight to each direction in a bidirectional edge. Then removing an edge or node from the network structure is equivalent to setting its weight to zero.

One of the most important features of our model is that cycles (or loops)
27
are allowed in the network structure without restrictions. Moreover, these cycles can exist within other cycles and bidirectional links can be included in cycles, allowing flow to occur in both directions. An example of this is brain networks, where the cyclic propagation of signals can take place.

The definitions of standard network centrality measures, such as closeness centrality and betweenness centrality, count only geodesic or shortest paths between two nodes in the network. This is a limitation because it overlooks other paths and walks that can significantly influence various centrality properties and flow characteristics in the network. Our proposed centrality metrics address this issue by considering all possible walks that are shorter than a maximum walk length, which is defined as a model parameter. When the edge probabilities are low, due to decay effects, the model accounts for all relevant walks with the accuracy of the computations.

Centrality measures inherently imply specific patterns of flow in networks
8
. This is true even for degree centrality, unless the network graph is used solely for visualization purposes. Two main extreme types of processes occur in networks: spreading along self-avoiding walks and spreading along node-state-independent walks. In the case of self-avoiding walks, each node can only be visited once, whereas in the case of node-state-independent walks, there are no such restrictions. Evidently, cycles cannot appear on self-avoiding walks.

Network centrality metrics and community detection methods have been developed both for local and global network structures
38
. Here the term global refers to approaches that take into account the entire network. In many cases, global methods address the limitations found in standard metrics or other measures proposed in the literature. Many of them are based on existing techniques, enhance them with improvements or modifications, or use combinations of standard metrics
38
;
36
;
30
.

Applications

In this section, we highlight some representative applications where our proposed metrics play a significant role. This list is not exhaustive, as network centrality metrics are crucial in most network analysis studies.

Social network analysis can help us find influential leaders in a social group, trace the source of new ideas and innovations, and assess the speed of information spreading
10
. In social network analysis, an important application is the detection of the source or sources of rumor, misinformation, and disinformation
42
. The characteristics of influence spreading involve complex social contagion mechanisms, where multiple interactions are necessary for an opinion or behavior change to occur
12
. In contrast, information or persuasive comments may require just a single interaction to propagate the news. It is important to note that we use the term
complex social contagion
to refer to Damon Centola’s concept of
complex contagion

12
, where multiple influences are needed for an individual to change their opinion or behavior. In section
5.1
, we describe in detail a different meaning for the term
complex contagion
in the context of the influence spreading model.

Modeling the spread of epidemics has become a significant area of research due to the COVID-19 pandemic. New approaches have been developed to describe both the spread and containment of the virus. Detailed modeling at the organizational level has allowed for targeted isolation of specific units within organizations and has enabled the investigation of how individuals in different contact networks contribute to spreading the virus
33
. Commonly used metrics to summarize model outcomes include network centrality, betweenness, and the basic reproduction number.

Communication networks are usually evaluated using various metrics that indicate their connectivity and reliability. In this context, circular propagation is not relevant, which allows for the use of self-avoiding walk models. When investigating cyber threats and attacks, it is important to model at both the enterprise and cross-enterprise levels. The use of various technical attack techniques and multi-layer approaches highlights the necessity for detailed modeling of these environments
34
.

Related Work

Centrality measures based on shortest paths make simplistic assumptions of how information, traffic or other network processes flow through a network
18
;
8

Krackhardt also noted that a high standard betweenness centrality value can indicate that a node with high betweenness has access to a variety of non-redundant sources of information.

The relationship between degree centrality and closeness centrality was recently studied in
17
. The authors found a non-linear relationship between degree centrality and closeness centrality. Degree, closeness, betweenness, eigenvector, and Katz centralities were mathematically examined with respect to whether these measures fulfill the authors’ three basic requirements for centrality measures in
35
. The correlation between community-aware centrality measures and classical centrality measures was studied in
39

Freeman discusses the notion that the center of a star-shaped network or the hub of a wheel-shaped network is structurally more central than any other node in any network of similar size
20
. However, this is more of a convincing argument than an exact mathematical definition. A comprehensive survey of centrality measures in complex networks gives a nice summary and overview of centrality measures
41
. The survey highlights how degree centrality is a centrality measure based on local information, whereas closeness centrality and betweenness centrality make use of global knowledge of the network. Bovet and Makse give an overview of centrality measures based on whether they are based on shortest paths, walks, random walks, or non-backtracking walks
9
. They note that degree centrality can actually be understood as the number of walks of length one starting from a node.

In a foundational work, Friedkin proposed a process model of social influence from which three centrality measures were defined

Influence Spreading Model and New Centrality Metrics

In this section, we will introduce the influence spreading model, present a motivating example of how it works, and introduce new metrics defined consistently by the influence spreading model.

5.1
Influence Spreading Model

Our influence spreading model, previously published in
33
, provides a detailed description of network structure and non-conserving probabilistic influence flow within that structure. The edges in the network can be directed or bidirectional, and both nodes and edges are assigned individual weights or probabilities for transmitting influence. The model supports both circular and recurrent spreading throughout the network. Additionally, a version of the model has been developed to implement only self-avoiding walks, meaning that each node can appear only once on a given walk. Furthermore, the model can account for breakthrough effects, enabling us to describe scenarios that lie between these two extreme cases.

The influence spreading model allows for two distinct types of spreading, namely complex contagion and simple contagion. Complex contagion allows unrestricted self-intersecting walks and cycles, with the only limitation for walks being a specified maximum walk length, and the target node cannot self-intersect. However, simple contagion does not allow for self-intersecting walks or cycles. In complex contagion, the focus is on the first arrival or the first influence on a target node. Before reaching the target node, the walks are limited by the specified maximum length. The calculation for each walk ends when it reaches the target node. In this article, we focus only on complex contagion since we wish to study centrality measures related to unrestricted paths, walks, and cycles and compare them to standard centrality measures.

The probability of spreading from node
s
to node
t
is calculated by considering all possible walks that are shorter than or equal to a specified maximum allowed walk length
L_{max}
. We assume that the spreading process starts with probability one from the initial node
s
. It is also important to emphasize that the influence spreading model is an analytical model and not a simulation model. Due to the analytical nature of the model, its probability outputs are exact within the limitations of floating-point number calculations.

The complete logic of calculating influence spreading probabilities was originally presented in
32
. Depending on the context, the original article often used the term path interchangeably to mean either a path or a walk as defined in Section
1
. In this article, we will aim to use more exact terminology that aligns with existing graph theory terminology.

The full influence spreading model specifies a time-dependent component in addition to edge and node probabilities
32
. In the full model, this time-dependent probability component approaches one as time grows unbounded. In this article, we assume that the time component probability equals one. By setting the time component probability to one, we can have a clearer focus on how the influence spreading model’s centrality measures behave as the edge probability is varied.

In
32
, a detailed algorithmic description was given on how different walks and their probabilities are combined. Next, we will explain the logic of the walk combination process and the algorithm. Understanding how the influence spreading probability is calculated when walks are only restricted by the maximum allowed length of the walk is crucial to understanding the results that are presented in Section
6

5.3
New Centrality Metrics

The output of the influence spreading model is represented as a matrix, known as the influence spreading matrix. The elements of this matrix describe the directed probabilities of influence spreading from a source node
i
to a target node
j
for all pairs of
N\times N
nodes within a network consisting of
N
nodes. The elements of the matrix reflect the probabilities of directed spreading or connectivity between nodes in the network
V
, and it is used to analyze the structure and flow processes of the network. Importantly, models other than our influence spreading model can also create this probability matrix. Using the influence spreading matrix
M
, we define our new out-centrality
C_{out}
and in-centrality
C_{in}
as follows

C_{out}(i)=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\in V\setminus\{i\}}M(i,j),\quad i=1,...N

(5)

and

C_{in}(j)=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i\in V\setminus\{j\}}M(i,j),\quad j=1,...,N.

(6)

We assume that the spreading process begins with the probability of one from the initial node. When spreading occurs from branching paths or walks, it can happen in multiple directions, indicating the likelihood of first arrival at the end nodes. By ignoring the diagonal elements of the influence spreading matrix (a node’s influence on itself is always one), we can define the out-centrality and in-centrality values. To normalize these values to one (or express them as percentages), we use the factor
1/(N-1)
on the right side of equations (
5
) and (
6
). Out-centrality measures how much a node influences other nodes on average, and in-centrality measures how much a node is influenced by other nodes on average.

The influence spreading model also defines its own betweenness centrality measure. We refer to this betweenness as ISM betweenness centrality or ISM betweenness, which is defined with the help of the concept of cohesion. The cohesion
\mathcal{C}
of the network
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of nodes and
E
is the set of edges in the network, is defined as

\mathcal{C}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,e\in V\\
s\neq e\end{subarray}}M(s,e).

The cohesion
\mathcal{C}_{v}
of the network without node
v
and its removed incident edges is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{v}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,e\in V\setminus\{v\}\\
s\neq e\end{subarray}}M^{*}(s,e),

where
M^{*}
is the influence spreading matrix calculated from the network without node
v
and its incident edges. With these definitions, we define ISM betweenness centrality
b_{ISM}(v)
of a node
v
as

b_{ISM}(v)=\frac{\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}_{v}}{\mathcal{C}}.

(7)

It is important to emphasize the way the ISM betweenness

is calculated. When the ISM betweenness value of node

is calculated, we remove the node and its incident edges from the network, and then the logic of the influence spreading algorithm
1

by examining how much the cohesion
\mathcal{C}
of the network changes when node

acts as a particularly important bridge in the network, its removal can even lead to the network being disconnected into two separate components.

Results

In this section, we will compare the metrics defined by the influence spreading model to standard centrality metrics. Furthermore, we will further examine the behavior of the centrality metrics defined by the influence spreading model.

The influence spreading model defines its own betweenness centrality (
7
) and also new centrality metrics out-centrality (
5
) and in-centrality (
6
). The compared standard centrality metrics are betweenness centrality (
3
), degree centrality (
1
) and closeness centrality (
2
).

The influence spreading model uses probabilities as edge weights. In our experiments, the edge probability is varied between experiments, but all edges of the network always have the same probability. However, the standard betweenness centrality and the closeness centrality require a distance to be defined between the nodes. We interpret the shortest path between the nodes as the path with the fewest edges (as if the distance between the nodes were one). If edge probabilities are not the same for all edge weights, then a systematic way of mapping probabilities to distances is needed. Appendix
A
explores one possible way of mapping edge probabilities to edge distances. However, in our experiments, the shortest paths are calculated with the assumption that all edge distances are equal. Therefore, calculating the shortest path between nodes reduces to finding the path with the fewest edges.

We frequently characterize nodes as peripheral or central. Borgatti and Everett proposed formalizations for the concept of core/peripheral structure in networks
7
. One of the formalizations they proposed was a discrete model consisting of a core and a periphery. The core was defined as a cohesive subgraph in which nodes are connected to each other in a maximal sense, and the periphery consists of nodes that are loosely connected to the core but do not share the maximal cohesion with the core. Yanchenko and Sengupta explain the core as consisting of nodes that are densely connected to each other, and some of them also have connections to peripheral nodes, whereas peripheral nodes are only sparsely connected to each other
45
. We use the term peripheral nodes in the sense and meaning given by Yanchenko and Sengupta, and central nodes are interpreted as belonging to the core.

In Sections
6.1
and
6.2
, we will have a detailed individual node-level examination of the behavior of the influence spreading model and its centrality metrics on two small networks. Using small networks allows us to examine and explain the behavior of the influence spreading model and its centrality metrics with node-level detail. In Section
6.3
, we examine the collective network-level behavior of the influence spreading model and its centrality metrics on larger
1000
node random graphs/networks. Finally, in Section
6.4
, we show a few counterexamples where some of the phenomena we have found do not appear. All experiments in Sections
6.1
,
6.2
, and
6.3
use the maximum allowed walk length
L_{max}=20
. In Section

, the effect of using shorter or longer
L_{max}
is discussed.

6.3
Network-Level Analysis of the Influence Spreading Model in Random Networks

In this section, we examine the behavior of the influence spreading model on larger randomly generated networks and compare the results to standard centrality metrics. We use scatter plots and Pearson correlation coefficients in our comparisons. The sample Pearson correlation coefficient
r_{xy}
for a sequence of observation pairs
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
is defined as

r_{xy}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\bar{x})(y_{i}-\bar{y})}{\sqrt{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}\right)}},

where
\bar{x}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
and
\bar{y}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}

28
.

We have used stochastic network models to generate the random networks used in this section. The stochastic network models used are Erdős-Rényi random graph model
15
, Watts-Strogatz small world model
44
and Barabási-Albert preferential attachment model
2
. Erdős-Rényi model comes from the mathematical tradition of random graph theory, whereas Watts-Strogatz and Barabási-Albert models are innovations of network science. These three random network models each capture a different type of network with their characteristic properties and provide an aggregate representation of many individual networks that share these properties. The characteristic properties of the random networks produced by these stochastic network models are very well known, and they have been extensively studied in the literature. Therefore, these three random network models provide varied, yet controlled conditions to assess the influence spreading model and its centrality metrics compared to standard centrality metrics.

We also note that we performed the same or similar experiments on the well-known Les Misérables network
29
and on another random network generated by using the extension of the Barabási-Albert model proposed by Holme and Kim
25
. However, the Les Misérables network data set is quite small with 77 nodes and 254 edges, while similar phenomena appeared in the results as in our chosen three random networks. Therefore, the inclusion of the Les Misérables network was considered redundant. Furthermore, the random network produced by the method proposed by Holme and Kim displayed results similar to Barabási-Albert random network. This is not entirely surprising, since the essential difference between these random network models is that the method of Holme and Kim can create high clustering, whereas the Barabási-Albert model cannot.

The model widely known as the Erdős-Rényi model (or less widely known as the Erdős-Rényi-Gilbert model) has two formulations
15
;
23
. In the first formulation, the number of nodes and edges is fixed, and then a graph is randomly selected out of all possible graphs. In the second formulation, the number of nodes is fixed, and edges are created between all pairs of nodes independently of all other edges with probability
p
. In this study, we use the second formulation and denote a random Erdős-Rényi network with
\text{ER}(n,p)
, where
n
is the number of nodes, and
p
is the independent edge creation probability.

The Erdős-Rényi model has been thoroughly studied and its properties are well known
37
. The Erdős-Rényi model creates networks with low clustering, and its degree distribution has been shown to be the Poisson distribution.

The Watts-Strogatz model uses a regular ring lattice of (fixed number of)
n
nodes and
k
neighbors for each node as the starting point, after which edges are rewired with probability
p

44
. Thus, with probability
p
, a node is connected to a uniformly randomly selected non-neighbor (duplicate edges are not allowed) and with probability
1-p
the edge remains unchanged. With this method of network construction, Watts and Strogatz aimed to develop a mixture of order and randomness to create more realistic networks. The parameter
p
controls the amount of randomness. The Watts-Strogatz model has the ability to create high clustering and short average path lengths between nodes. Due to the short average path lengths, it is also called the small-world model. The exact degree distribution of the Watts-Strogatz model is derived in
1
. When
p=0
, the degree distribution is constant
k
. When
p
grows, the degree distribution begins to resemble the shape of the degree distribution of the Erdős-Rényi random graph
1
i.e. the degree distribution becomes approximately Poisson distributed. We denote a network produced by the Watts-Strogatz model by
\text{WS}(n,k,p)
.

The Barabási-Albert model addresses some of the limitations of Erdős-Rényi model and Watts-Strogatz model
2
. First, the Barabási-Albert model does not assume a fixed number of nodes, and instead, the number of nodes is allowed to grow. Second, Barabási-Albert model does not create edges between nodes with equal probability since a new edge is more likely to be connected to a node that already has a high degree. This is called preferential attachment, where high-degree nodes are more likely to receive more neighbors when the network size grows. The Barabási-Albert model begins from an initial graph and then continues adding nodes using preferential attachment until the desired network size has been reached. We denote the Barabási-Albert network with
\text{BA}(n,m)
, where
n
is the desired number of nodes, and
m
is the number of edges to be attached from a new node to existing nodes. The degree distribution of the Barabási-Albert model is scale-free.

Many real-world networks display high clustering and non-Poissonian degree distributions
37
. Scale-free (i.e. power-law) degree distributions have been proposed to be a common phenomenon for many real networks
1
. However, the prevalence of scale-free degree distributions in real networks has also been recently disputed in
11
, where it is actually found that scale-free degree distributions are rare and the log-normal distribution provides an adequate modeling fit instead of a power-law distribution. A short overview on the prevalence of scale-free degree distributions in real networks can be found in
26
. In light of this differing research on what degree distributions characterize real networks, we argue that testing the behavior of the influence spreading model on random networks that display either Poissonian or scale-free degree distributions is especially justified.

7
Discussion

The influence spreading model serves as a versatile framework to investigate properties of various complex networks as it also allows for directed unidirectional or bidirectional edges and any edge probabilities to be used. In this article, however, we have studied undirected networks, where all edge probabilities are assumed to be equal. This setting has given us a controlled environment, where the effect of the edge probability can be captured more easily. We have also developed an understanding of how the influence spreading model behaves when the edge probability is varied.

All experiments performed in Sections
6.1
,
6.2
, and
6.3
were performed using the maximum allowed walk length
L_{max}=20
. We also performed all these experiments for shorter and longer maximum allowed walk lengths. The effect of the maximum length of allowed walk is that the reversing ranking behavior in the in-centrality occurs earlier if walks are allowed to be longer than the length of
20
, and reversing occurs later if walks are only allowed to be shorter than the length of
20
. In general, a longer
L_{max}
increases the probability of influencing others, as it allows more walks within the network. In
32
, the effect of varying the maximum allowed walk length was discussed in detail (the article used the term path instead of walk while retaining the meaning of walk as defined in this article).

In this study, we have only presented the complex contagion version of the influence spreading model. For random networks produced by the Erdős-Rényi, Watts-Strogatz, and Barabási-Albert models, the complex contagion process results in in-centrality and out-centrality values being positively correlated for low edge probabilities. As the edge probability is increased, eventually this relationship between in-centrality and out-centrality completely reverses, where there exists a negative correlation between these two measures. It is important to emphasize that simple contagion cannot create this phenomenon since cycles are not allowed, and therefore there are the same number of paths from a source node to a target node as there are from the target node to the source node. In complex contagion processes, cycles act as an amplifier. In social networks, this amplification effect could be interpreted as an echo chamber for ideas.

We have also seen how in-centrality and out-centrality characterize the nodes of the network when everything else is assumed to be equal, i.e. when the edges of the network are undirected and when the edge probabilities are the same for all edges of the network. In this case, the network topology determines the in-centrality and out-centrality values of the nodes. We have seen how peripheral nodes tend to be the least influential nodes (lower out-centrality values), whereas more central nodes have more influence (higher out-centrality values). This phenomenon seems to persist as edge weight increases. We have also seen how, for low edge probabilities, peripheral nodes are influenced the least due to lower in-centrality values, whereas more central nodes are being influenced the most because of higher in-centrality values. However, as the edge weight increases, at some point the situation reverses itself, and peripheral nodes become the most influenced nodes, and the most central nodes become the least influenced nodes. In real social networks, for example, it is plausible to think that the peripheral nodes are the least influential nodes, whereas the most central nodes are the most influential nodes. Additionally, in real social networks, nodes can be so isolated that they are influenced very little by the rest of the network due to their isolation, whereas more central nodes are influenced by at least their immediate neighborhood. The in-centrality and out-centrality defined by the influence spreading model produce behavior that aligns well with the aforementioned behavior of a real social network.

The proposed ISM betweenness centrality has been shown to be capable of revealing what standard betweenness centrality cannot. For example, the ISM betweenness can highlight the importance of alternative routes in a network. It also emphasizes nodes that act as important bridges in a network. However, the ISM betweenness centrality has a great computational cost. Calculating the ISM betweenness centrality requires that the influence spreading algorithm be run again for every single node, since removing a different node from the network creates a new network. The ISM betweenness centrality reveals more of the network topology than the standard betweenness centrality, but this advantage comes at a greater computational cost.

8
Conclusion

Many standard centrality measures, such as closeness and betweenness centrality, are based on shortest paths in the network. The influence spreading model defines centrality metrics based on unrestricted paths, walks, and cycles. We have seen how there are similarities between the centrality metrics defined by the influence spreading model and standard centrality metrics, especially for low edge probabilities. We have also seen how the centrality metrics defined by the influence spreading model can reveal information that centrality metrics based on shortest paths cannot. The novelty presented in this article is the detailed examination of the behavior of ISM betweenness centrality, out-centrality, and in-centrality in comparison to standard centrality metrics.

We have demonstrated that ISM betweenness centrality can reveal the importance of alternative routes while maintaining similarity to standard betweenness centrality. We have also demonstrated that out-centrality can detect central nodes as influential nodes and in-centrality can detect peripheral nodes as the most influenced nodes when all else is equal (network edges are undirected and all edge probabilities are equal). These results give validity to out-centrality and in-centrality measures, since we achieve results that are logical. Therefore, we can infer that when studied networks are made more realistic with directed edges and different edge probabilities, we will see even clearer differences between the roles of nodes when our influence-based centrality metrics are used.

Appendix A
Appendix

The influence spreading model uses probabilities as edge weights. However, standard betweenness centrality and closeness centrality require a distance to be defined between nodes. In real-world networks, there is some sort of relationship between the influence spreading probability and the distance between nodes. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, social distancing was encouraged in order to reduce the probability of infection. Therefore, there is a need to map edge probabilities to edge distances in a systematic way. This is especially true if the edge probabilities are not the same for all edges.

One possible interpretation of a shortest path in a graph which has probabilities as edge weights is to interpret the path with the highest probability as if it were the shortest path. If we denote
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m}
as the edge probabilities in a graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of nodes,
E
is the set of edges between nodes, and
m=|E|
is the number of edges, then our task is to find a path between source node
s
and target node
t
that maximizes the product
p_{(s,a)}\cdots p_{(b,t)}
, where
(s,a),\dots,(b,t)\in E
are the edges and
s,a,\ldots,b,t\in V
are the nodes on the path that maximizes the probability of influence between nodes
s
and
t
.

Maximizing the product
p_{(s,a)}\cdots p_{(b,t)}
is equivalent to maximizing the logarithmic sum

\log p_{(s,a)}+\ldots+\log p_{(b,t)}

which is equivalent to minimizing

-\log p_{(s,a)}-\ldots-\log p_{(b,t)}

which is again equivalent to minimizing

\log\frac{1}{p_{(s,a)}}+\ldots+\log\frac{1}{p_{(b,t)}}.

In the previous formulation, we assumed that the shortest path contains at least one intermediary node. If that is not the case, then naturally the shortest path between
s
and
t
is the edge
(s,t)
.

When
0<p_{i}\leq 1
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, then
\log(1/p_{i})\geq 0
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. Now,
\log(1/p_{i})
, for all
1\leq i\leq m
, can be used as a nonnegative distance between nodes, because as probability
p
increases the distance
\log(1/p)
decreases, i.e.
\log(1/p)
is a strictly decreasing function of
p
. Furthermore, we can now use Dijkstra’s algorithm for finding shortest paths in the network, since edge weights are non-negative
14
. In this case, Dijkstra’s algorithm picks the edges with the lowest distance
\log(1/p_{i})
. Therefore, with this transformation the path
i
with the lowest value
\log(1/p_{i})
is chosen i.e. the highest probability path is chosen for the shortest path. In the special case, where
p_{i}=1
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, then the problem reduces to finding the path with the fewest edges.
