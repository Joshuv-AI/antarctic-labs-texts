Precision Targeting of Human M2 Macrophages by Magnetic Nanoparticles Functionalized with an in-House Antibody
Authors: Chloe Bazile, Julie Bordenave, Saber Amri, Flavien Raynal, Laetitia Ligat, Manon Farcé, Henri Coste, Pascal Clerc, Olivier Sandre, Corinne Bousquet, Vera Pancaldi, Stéphane Mornet, Veronique Gigoux, Mary Poupot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, particularly due to resistance induced by tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)—exhibiting a M2-like phenotype—within the tumor microenvironment. The specific targeting of pro-tumoral M2 TAMs constitutes thus a major challenge in anticancer therapies but current strategies lack specificity. We produced and patented a monoclonal antibody, called mAb6-25, that specifically targets M2-like macrophages (M2M) but not M1-like macrophages (M1M) or monocytes. Here, we investigated the conjugation of this antibody on magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) as a potential nanoplatform for specific M2 TAM targeting.MethodsMagnetic iron oxide multicore nanoflowers (NF) were functionalized with a fluorophore allowing their detection and the mAb6-25 for M2 TAM targeting. The M2M targeting by MNP-mAb6-25 was determined, comparatively to M1M, in 2D and 3D in vitro co-culture models with cancer cells by flow cytometry and confocal microscopy analysis, while MNP-mAb6-25 uptake was evaluated by NMR relaxometry. The in vivo M2 TAM targeting was analyzed in a xenograft mouse model of NSCLC by IVIS optical imaging.ResultsWe demonstrated that the MNP-mAb6-25 nanoplatform preferentially binds M2M compared to M1M (13-fold higher uptake in M2M) or cancer cells in monoculture as well as in 2D and 3D co-culture models mixing M2M or M1M with A549 NSCLC cells. Moreover, MNP-mAb6-25 internalized and accumulated in the lysosomes of M2M. The efficacy of intravenously injected MNP-mAb6-25 to target NSCLC sub-cutaneous xenografted models containing M2M was subsequently demonstrated.ConclusionThis study highlights the potential of MNP-mAb6-25 as a nanoplatform for selective targeting TAM-containing NSCLC tumors. This nanotechnology tool may lead to the development of new applications: one involving the imaging-based detection of pro-tumoral M2 TAMs by MRI, and the other involving their potential depletion through the application of localized magnetic fields, paving the way for imaging-guided diagnosis and further therapeutic evaluation.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the leading cause of cancer death. Despite recent therapeutic advances, such as immunotherapy or targeted therapy, many patients develop resistance, leading to relapse. Tumor resistance to treatments and the immune system is not only related to intrinsic properties of tumor cells but also to other cellular and acellular components in the tumor microenvironment (TME). These components, forming a dynamic network, are represented by normal and tumoral tissue-resident cells with a large proportion of recruited and resident immune cells. Among these cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) represent 30 to 50% of the tumor mass and are correlated with poor prognosis in NSCLC.1–3 These pro-tumor TAM, harboring a type 2 phenotype, contribute largely to the tumor progression4,5 and tumor resistance.6–8 Indeed, if at the beginning of the tumor development, infiltrated and resident macrophages are able to eliminate cancer cells, they may switch to a pro-tumoral M2 phenotype at a later stage.9 Macrophages are very plastic cells characterized by a rapid switch of phenotype from an M1-like inflammatory/antitumoral phenotype to an M2-like immunosuppressive/pro-tumoral phenotype, depending on the cellular and molecular microenvironment of the tumor.9

In this context, targeting TAMs is therefore a major challenge, to improve treatment efficacy and lead to patient remission. Several strategies have been proposed to reduce the pro-tumoral properties of TAMs, either by inhibiting their recruitment or their reprogramming, blocking their functions or depleting them.10,11 Some are based on the use of cytokines, small molecules or nanoparticles targeting metabolic signal pathways, others consider immune-stimulating or -blocking antibodies. However, none of these strategies specifically target the pro-tumoral TAMs. Indeed, many of them also target the myeloid cells including monocytes, M1 macrophages or dendritic cells, thus causing adverse effects.

We previously produced and patented a monoclonal antibody, termed mAb6-25, that exclusively recognized human M2-derived macrophages (M2M) but not monocytes or M1-derived macrophages (M1M) (Patent 2018: WO2018020000A1). The mAb6-25 interacts specifically with M2M through the folate receptor beta (FR-β), which is a membrane protein exclusively expressed by the embryonic cells and macrophages with an M2 phenotype.12 Moreover, we previously showed that the FR-β was expressed by pro-tumor TAM in Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and promoted the survival of leukemic cells and their proliferation in lymph nodes of patients with aggressive CLL (Domagala M, submitted). We also demonstrated that mAb6-25 specifically targets TAM on biopsies from different types of tumors (WO2018020000A1). Interestingly, after interacting with the FR-β at the surface of M2M or TAM, the mAb6-25 is internalized without causing their death or any modification of their phenotype (unpublished data).

Recent advances in nanotechnology have provided a wide range of organic, inorganic and composite nanoplatforms to revolutionize the domain of health, more particularly in oncology with the development of imaging agents and/or nanomedicines. Currently, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPION) represent one of the most promising strategies to elaborate new theranostic nanoplatforms combining imaging properties with therapeutic activity. SPION are highly biocompatible and biodegradable, gradually degrading over weeks and facilitating iron recycling.13 Moreover, the major advantage of such magnetic nanoparticles (MNP) is that they can combine a targeting strategy with various therapies, by functionalizing them with a biomolecule which targets an antigen, a protein or a receptor overexpressed in cancer, allowing their delivery and accumulation preferentially into the tumor and the metastasis.14 Their potential to target cells was not only explored for cancer cells, but also for immune populations within the tumor microenvironment. Indeed, previous studies reported the successful use of functionalized MNP with ligands targeting conventional receptors such as CD206 or CD204 to label and detect TAMs in vitro or in vivo.15,16 However, these markers are not specific for pro-tumoral M2M macrophages, they can also be expressed by other myeloid cells. We therefore aimed to evaluate a new imaging agent for oncology based on TAM targeting, termed MNP-mAb6-25, which combines the M2M-targeting property of the mAb6-25 with the potential magnetic resonance (MR) imaging properties of magnetic nanoparticles (MNP). In the present study, we investigated the targeting efficacy of the mAb6-25 antibody conjugated to MNP labelled with fluorophores, allowing their fluorescent detection in vitro and in vivo. To establish the proof of concept of an efficient M2M targeting within the tumor, we chose NSCLC as a cancer model because of the high level of M2M present in its microenvironment.1–3 Our strategy, schematized in Figure S1, first consist in evaluating the ability of the targeted MNP, termed MNP-mAb6-25, to specifically target the M2M, but not the M1M, in 2D mono- and co-culture with NSCLC cells as well as in 3D M2M/NSCLC cells spheroids. Then, given that the mAb6-25 recognizes the human FR-β but not the murine form, the in vivo efficiency of MNP-mAb6-25 in a NSCLC xenograft mouse model containing human M2-like macrophages was evaluated by first in vivo optical imaging observations.

Materials and Methods

MNP Synthesis, Functionalization and Conjugation

Materials

All reagents for the MNP synthesis, ie iron (II) chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl2∙4H2O, 98%), iron (III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3∙6H2O, >97%), iron (III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3∙9H2O, >98%), diethylene glycol (DEG, 99%), N-methyldiethanolamine (NMDEA, 99%), sodium hydroxide micropellets (NaOH, 98%), nitric acid fuming (HNO3, 69%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St Quentin Fallavier, France) and used without further purification. Sulfo-Cyanine5 NHS ester (sulfo-Cy5-NHS) and sulfo-Cyanine7 NHS ester (sulfo-Cy7-NHS) were obtained from Lumiprobe GmbH (Hannover, Germany). 2-iminothiolane was from Pierce Thermo (Courtaboeuf, France) and maleimide-PEG-SVA (succinimidyl valerate) 2000 g/mol was supplied by Laysan Bio-Gannet Biochem (Arab, AL, US). Sterile ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ.cm was produced by Labostar system (EVOQUA water technologies, Nanterre France) equipped with an UV lamp.

MNP Synthesis and Conjugation

Magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, referred as MNP or nanoflowers (NF), were synthesized via the polyol route, as previously described.17,18 Briefly, FeCl3·6H2O and FeCl2·4H2O were dissolved in a liquid mixture of DEG (diethylene glycol) and NMDEA (N-methyldiethanolamine) in a 1:1 ratio. In parallel, sodium hydroxide was dissolved in a polyol solution. Both solutions were purged with an inert gas (N2 or Ar), then mixed and heated up to 220°C, leading to the formation of magnetite Fe3O4 nanoparticles. Then, the nanoparticles were washed several times. Subsequently, the nanoparticles were oxidized to maghemite (γ-Fe2O3) by adding a ferric nitrate solution and heating them at 80°C. After isolation of the nanoparticles, a 2M HNO3 solution was added, enabling magnetic sedimentation. Finally, the nanoflowers were washed several times with ethyl acetate and redispersed in an aqueous medium. All the physico-chemical characterizations regarding the relationship between the nanoflower structure and the magnetic properties have recently been described in depth in previous published studies.17

The PEGylation of the nanoflowers was carried out according to a protocol similar to that published in a previous study.19 The detailed procedure will be described in a forthcoming publication due to a pending patent. The PEG corona contains amine end functional groups useful for further fluorescent labelling and bioconjugation steps. Fluorophores (sulfo-Cy5-NHS for in vitro or sulfo-Cy7-NHS for in vivo experiments) were grafted to amine groups by incubation with MNPs (at an iron oxide weight concentration of 3.2 g/L) in 10 mM HEPES pH 7.8 buffer for 4 h within a nominal ratio of 200 fluorophores/MNP. MNPs were washed from free fluorophores under magnetic separation using µ Columns (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) with degassed 10 mM HEPES pH 7.8 buffer.

The conjugation of antibodies (mAb6-25 or scramble IgG1 corresponding to isotypic control antibody (CI)) to MNPs was performed as a procedure previously described on PEGylated gold NPs (Arraud et al, 2014). Firstly, a 1 mg/mL mAb solution was incubated with a 100-fold molar excess of 2-iminothiolane for 15 min in a 50 mM degassed borate buffer, pH 8. In these conditions, one thiol (SH) group was exposed on average per antibody molecule, as determined by Ellman’s assay. The excess of Traut’s reagent was eliminated by five cycles of ultrafiltration with degassed 25 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 3.4 mM EDTA, pH 7.2 buffer through 3 kDa cutoff Amicon® Ultra-0.5 mL centrifugal filters (Millipore) at 4,000 g for 15 min. At the same time, MNPs were functionalized by a heterobifunctional PEG linker combining a maleimide group at one end of the PEG chain and a succinimidyl valerate group at the other. A large excess of maleimide-PEG-SVA (100 mg) was directly added on 4 mL of MNP dispersion (6 mg/mL of iron oxide) and left to incubate for 2 h. MNPs were cleared of free PEG cross linkers under magnetic separation using µ Columns (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) with degassed 25 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 3.4 mM EDTA, pH 7.2 buffer. Right after that, the maleimide functionalized MNPs were mixed with SH-modified mAbs at a ratio of 1 to 8 mAbs per MNPs and incubated overnight at 4°C. Bioconjugated MNPs were washed with 1× PBS under magnetic separation using µ Columns and stored at 4°C in low adhesion Eppendorf tubes. The final concentration MNPs was determined by optical density measurement at 350 nm using an experimentally measured extinction coefficient value of 2960 M−1.cm−1. To do so, a volume of 10 µL of iron oxide dispersion was added in 990 µL 5M HCl (dissolution occurred at 70°C in a water bath for 10 min) and diluted again ten times with 5 M HCl into UV-vis quartz cuvette. Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis analysis indicated that all mAbs added were conjugated on MNP due to the large excess of maleimide groups exposed on their surface.

MNP Characterizations

The size of the nanoparticle core was analyzed by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). Nanoparticles samples were prepared by depositing a drop (10 µL) of nanoparticles suspension on carbon coated copper grids (Lacey/thin double carbon film Cu-300LD, 300 mesh, Pacific Grid Tech, San Francisco, CA). The excess of the droplet was aspired with a paper filter to leave a thin liquid film on the TEM grid. TEM images were obtained using a Jeol JEM-1400+ instrument operated at 120 kV, and digital micrographs were obtained with a Smart Orius 1000 Gatan camera. NP size distribution and diameter were obtained by manually measuring 300 NPs of each batch with ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

Nanoparticle characterization was also performed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) in backscattering mode (165°) with a Vasco™ instrument (Cordouan Technologies™, Pessac, France) to determine the hydrodynamic intensity average size (Zave) and polydispersity index (PDI) defined as the ratio of the 2nd order coefficient to the square of the 1st order coefficient in the Cumulant series analysis of the autocorrelation curves. DLS measurements were carried out in 1× PBS buffer, except for non-PEGylated nanoflowers, which were measured in Milli-Q water. In practice, five runs of 40 s duration were acquired, the Z-average diameter (Zave) and PDI were averaged and a standard deviation was calculated from the statistics of the five runs.

The ζ-potential of the nanoparticles was measured in 10 mM HEPES buffer at pH 7.4 using a WALLIS™ analyzer based on laser Doppler electrophoresis (LDE) technology (Cordouan Technologies™, Pessac, France).

Cells

A549 cells and A549-GFP were obtained from the Cell Biolabs (USA). These cells were cultured in complete RPMI1640 medium (RPMI 1640 (Gibco, USA), 10% SVF (invitrogen, USA), 100 µg/mL Penicilline-streptomycine (Sigma Aldrich, USA) at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Monocytes-Derived Macrophages

Monocytes were isolated from healthy donors. Peripheral blood samples from healthy volunteer donors were provided by the French Blood Establishment (Établissement Français du Sang, EFS). The use of anonymized donor samples complied with the applicable French regulations and EFS procedures; therefore, no additional ethics committee approval was required. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected after separation by Ficoll (Ficoll Paque Plus, GE healthcare, USA) gradient centrifugation. Monocytes were isolated from PBMC by positive selection, using the anti-CD14 MicroBeads (Miltenyi, Germany), in accordance with manufacturer’s instructions. To obtain M1 or M2-like macrophages, monocytes were activated respectively with 50 ng/mL of Granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, Premium Grade, Miltenyi, Germany) or 25 ng/mL CSF-1 (Premium Grade, Miltenyi, Germany), at day 0 and day 3. At day 6, half of the medium was replaced by fresh medium with a final concentration of 10 ng/mL of interferon‑γ γ (IFN-γ, Premium Grade, Miltenyi, Germany) to generate M1 macrophages (M1M), or a final concentration of 25 ng/mL of interleukin-4 (IL-4, Premium Grade, Miltenyi, Germany) and IL-13 (Premium Grade, Miltenyi, Germany) to generate M2 macrophages (M2M). For M1M, 18 h after IFN-γ addition, 10 ng/mL of lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Sigma Aldrich, USA) was added in the culture.

Analysis of Nanoparticles Cytotoxicity

10,000 monocytes/well were seeded in a 96-well plate. After the polarization, they were treated with increased concentrations (0 to 128 γ-Fe2O3/mL) of MNP-mAb6-25, MNP-CI or MNP-NV in complete RPMI medium for 48 or 72 h. The percentage of viability was measured with the Cell Meter Cell Viability Assay kit *Green fluorescence* (AAT Bioquest, USA) by spectrofluorometry and normalized to cells without MNPs.

2D Co-Culture Model

After polarization, M1 or M2 macrophages (M1M or M2M) were detached with Accutase (Cell Detachment Solution, Bio Legend, USA) at 37°C for 20 min, and then labeled with CellTracker Orange (CMTMR, Thermofischer, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A549 were detached with Trypsin (Gibco, USA) and then labeled with CellTrace Violet (CTV, Thermofischer, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A549 were seeded alone or in co-culture with M1M or M2M at a ratio of 1:1 (0,25 × 106 total cells) in 1.5 mL of complete medium into 12 wells plate and cultured for 7 days at 37°C in 5% CO2.

For the microscopy, A549-GFP were used instead of A549 cells labeled with CTV.

3D Spheroid Models

Macrophages were labeled with CellTracker Orange (CMTMR, Thermofischer, USA), as described in 2D co-cultures. A549 labelled with CellTrace Violet or expressing GFP were seeded alone or with M1M or M2M at a ratio of 1:1 (5,000 total cells/well) in a final volume of 200 µL/well in complete medium with 1,25% of MethoCult H4230 (StemCell, USA) into 96-well ultra-low attachment U-bottom plates (Nunclon sphera, Thermofischer, USA). To help the spheroid formation, plates were centrifuged for 10 min at 215 g, then incubated for at least 3 days at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Cancer Cell Proliferation

Cancer cell proliferation was measured by 2 techniques. First, after 3, 5, and 7 days of culture, cells were counted by trypan blue assay on a Malassez counting chamber. For the 3D model, spheroids were pooled and dissociated by Accutase (Cell Detachment Solution, Bio Legend, USA) before the counting on a Malassez counting chamber. The cancer cell number was determined with the multiplication of the percentage of CTV+ or CFSE+ cells obtained by flow cytometry with the count number. Secondly, cancer cells were stained with the CellTrace Violet proliferation kit (Thermofischer, USA) or the CellTrace CFSE (Carboxyfluorescein Succinimidyl Ester) proliferation kit (Thermofischer, USA), before spheroid formation. The proliferation was assessed by the dilution of the CellTrace Violet (CTV) or of CFSE measured by the Fortessa flow cytometer. The cancer cell number was determined with the multiplication of the percentage of CTV+/CMTMR− or CFSE+/CMTMR− cancer cells obtained by flow cytometry and the count number.

Analysis of Nanoparticles Uptake by Flow Cytometry

For the kinetic, M1M or M2M or co-culture models were incubated for various time periods with MNP-mAb6-25 ([γ-Fe2O3] = 8 μg/mL for M1M or M2M and [γ-Fe2O3] = 16 μg/mL for co-culture), at 37°C in complete RPMI medium. For specificity, cells were incubated with increased concentrations of MNP-mAb6-25, MNP-CI, MNP-NV ([γ-Fe2O3] =8 to 32 μg/mL) for 24 or 72 h. Then, cells are washed 2 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and incubated during 20 min on a rocker plate at 37°C with Accutase (BioLegend, USA), SVF is added to stop the reaction. The cells were collected, centrifuged 5 min at 1500 rpm and resuspended in 200 μL of PBS, 2% of SVF containing 0.8 µg/mL 7-AAD (Sony Biotechnology, USA) to measure the cell viability.

For the 3D model, each model is sampled and grouped by condition in a 1.5 mL eppendorf, then washed twice and dissociated with Accutase (BioLegend, USA) for 30 min. Then SVF is added and the cells are centrifuged 5 min at 1500 rpm. The cells were resuspended in 200 μL of PBS, 2% of SVF containing 0.8 µg/mL 7-AAD (Sony Biotechnology, USA) to measure cell viability. Cell viability was finally analyzed by flow cytometry, on LSRII BD Fortessa or Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, France) and the results were analyzed with FlowJo (BD Biosciences, France).

Quantification of MNP Uptake by Relaxometry

1×106 M2M was polarized into a 6-well plate in complete RPMI medium. M2M were incubated with MNP-mAb6-25 ([γ-Fe2O3] = 16 μg/mL) in complete RPMI medium for 24 h or 72 h, at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, washed twice with PBS, and incubated during 20 min on a rocker plate at 37°C with Accutase (BioLegend, USA), SVF is added to stop the reaction, then all is collected and centrifuged 5 min at 1500 rpm. The amount of internalized MNPs was determined through NMR relaxometry, as previously described.20

MNP-mAb6-25 Subcellular Localization by Confocal Microscopy

0.25 x 106 M2M/compartment were polarized into 4-compartment Cellview culture dishes (Greiner Bio-One). Cells were incubated with MNP-mAb6-25 ([γ-Fe2O3] = 16 μg/mL) in complete RPMI medium for 24 h or 72 h. The cells were then incubated for 15 minutes with 10 nM LysoTracker Green DND-26 (excitation: 488 nm, Life technologies) MNP-mAb6-25 (excitation: 633 nm) and Lysotraker co-localization was analyzed using a LSM780 confocal microscope (Zeiss) and quantified with ImageJ software.

MNP-mAb6-25 Localization by TEM

7.5 × 106 M2M were polarized into petri dishes (Falcon). Cells were incubated with MNP-mAb6-25 ([γ-Fe2O3] = 16 μg/mL) in complete RPMI medium for 72 h. Then, cells were fixed using a final concentration of 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M Sorensen buffer (pH 7.2; EMS, EMS11600-05), added to the culture medium in a volume-to-volume ratio for 15 minutes at room temperature. Following this primary fixation, the cells underwent secondary fixation with the same solution (2.5% glutaraldehyde, 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M Sorensen buffer, pH 7.2) for 1 h 30 min at room temperature and were then post-fixed for 1 h at room temperature with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) and 1.5% potassium ferricyanide K3Fe(CN) 6 in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer. Cells were then scraped off, pelleted, concentrated in agarose, and treated for 1 h with 2% aqueous uranyl acetate. The samples were then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and embedded in Epon. After 48 h of polymerization at 60 °C, ultrathin sections (80 nm thick) were mounted onto 200 mesh Formvar-carbon-coated copper grids. Finally, sections were stained with Uranyless and lead citrate. Grids were examined using a transmission electron microscope (Jeol JEM-1400, JEOL Inc., Pea-body, MA, USA) operated at 80 kV, and images were acquired with a digital camera (Gatan Rio 9, Gatan Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA).

Analysis of M2 Markers Expression by Flow Cytometry

After detachment and dissociation, cells were resuspended in flow cytometry buffer (2% SVF in PBS) containing 2,5 µg/mL of Human BD Fc BlockTM (BD Pharmigen, France) and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Then, cells were stained with antibodies: anti-CD163-BUV395 (BD Biosciences, USA), anti-CD204-PC7 (Sony Biotechnology, USA), anti-CD206-BV510 (Sony Biotechnology, USA), anti-CD209-PerCP Cy5.5 (BD Biosciences, USA), anti-CD86-BV605 (Sony Biotechnology, USA), anti-CD64-APC Cy7 (BioLegend, USA), anti-FolR-β-CF633 (Biotum, USA), at 5 µg/mL, for 20 min at 4°C. Cells were then stained with Annexin-V-FITC according to manufacturer protocol and resuspended in Annexin-V buffer containing 0.8 µg/mL 7-AAD (Sony Biotechnology, USA) to measure the cell viability. All the results were acquired on a Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, France) and the data were analyzed with Flow Logic 700.2A (Inivai Technologies, Australia).

Cytokines Measurement

Cytokines (TGF-β1, CCL2, IL-8, M-CSF, IL-10 and IL-6) were measured by flow cytometry according to the LegendPlex supplier protocol (BioLegend, USA).

Confocal Microscopy Studies

3D Imaging with Antibody

Spheroids were incubated with anti-FR-β coupled with CF633 (Mix-n-StainTM CFTM633 Antibody Labeling Kit, Clinisciences, France), anti-CD206 coupled with CF405S (Mix-n-StainTM CFTM405S Antibody Labeling Kit, Clinisciences, France), anti-CD163 coupled with CF405S (Mix-n-StainTM CFTM405S Antibody Labeling Kit, Clinisciences, France), anti-CSF-1 coupled with CF405S (Mix-n-StainTM CFTM405S Antibody Labeling Kit, Clinisciences, France) at 4 µg/mL between 6 h and 72 h . The spheroids were then fixed directly in the wells with 4% PFA (PFA 16% Solution EM grade, Electron Microscopy Sciences, USA) during 4 h at room temperature. Then the spheroids were cleared with RapiClear 1,49 (SunJin Lab, Nikon, France) at least 24 h between 2 coverslips with two iSpacer of 0.25 mm (Nikon, Japon). Acquisition of images was performed with a LSM880 or LSM980 confocal microscope with 25 × objective (Zeiss, Germany).

2D and 3D Imaging with MNP

2D co-cultured models were made into 4-compartment Cellview culture dishes (Greiner Bio-One). 2D or 3D models were incubated with MNP-mAb6-25 or MNP-CI or MNP-NV ([γ-Fe2O3] = 16 μg/mL) in complete RPMI medium for 72 h. For 2D, cells were washed twice with PBS and then fixed with 4% PFA (PFA 16% Solution EM grade, Electron Microscopy Sciences, USA) during 30 minutes at room temperature. SlowFadeTM Glass Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen, USA) was added to recover the well. For 3D, after the incubation with MNP ([γ-Fe2O3] = 32 μg/mL), the spheroids were fixed with 4% PFA (PFA 16% Solution EM grade, Electron Microscopy Sciences, USA) during 4 h at room temperature. Then, the spheroids were cleared with RapiClear 1,49 (SunJin Lab, Nikon, France) at least 24 h between 2 coverslips with two iSpacer of 0.25 mm (Nikon, Japon). Acquisition of images was performed with a LSM980 confocal microscope with 40× objective for 2D cultures or 25× objective for 3D cultures (Zeiss, Germany).

3D Modeling and in silico Analysis

3D Modeling with Imaris

Confocal microscopy images were analyzed by IMARIS software (version 10.2, Oxford Instruments). Spots were defined according to a diameter of 8 µm for M2M and 9 µm for antibody labelling. Then the percentage of M2M labelled with anti-CD206, anti-CD163, anti-FR-β was determined according to the distance between spots, with a maximum distance of 6 µm. Finally, the volume was calculated with the surface tool used on the fluorescence of GFP A549.

In silico Analysis

Multichannel 3D fluorescence images of spheroids were acquired in.czi format, each composed of 60 to 93 z-stacks and three fluorescent channels: one for CMTMR+ macrophages (red) and two channels of interest (blue and white), corresponding to distinct antibodies (CD163 or CD206, and FR-β, respectively). Image analysis was performed using Fiji (ImageJ v1.54p) with the 3D Objects Counter plugin (v2.0) for object detection and quantification. CNMTR+ macrophages, blue, and white objects were segmented independently using the 3D Objects Counter plugin. A minimum object size threshold of 5 voxels was applied to exclude background noise and small artifacts. Two different analyses approaches were used, differing in pre-processing and thresholding methods:

- Automatic thresholding approach: Prior to segmentation, gamma correction was applied to the blue channel to enhance contrast and improve signal clarity. Gamma values were optimized individually for each spheroid. Segmentation was then performed using the default intensity threshold computed automatically by Fiji. This threshold is calculated using the Max Entropy method,21 which determines a global threshold that maximizes the combined entropy of foreground and background pixel distributions across the 3D image histogram.

- Empirical thresholding approach: No gamma correction was applied. Instead, segmentation thresholds were manually defined based on visual inspection and adjusted independently for each fluorescence channel to best capture relevant signals. After segmentation, the 3D centroid coordinates of each object were extracted. To evaluate antibody uptake by macrophages, 3D Euclidean distances were computed between each macrophage centroid and all blue and white object centroids. Macrophage objects located within 6 µm of either a blue or white centroid were considered positive for the corresponding marker. Macrophages located within 6 µm of both blue and white centroids were classified as double-positive.

The automatic thresholding, which included gamma correction and default intensity thresholds, determined that the average number of M2M per spheroid was 393 ± 73, with 12 ± 6% of CD206/FR-β and 5 ± 3% CD163/FR-β co-labelled M2M. In contrast, the empirical thresholding method, based on manual adjustment of signal intensity and without gamma correction, resulted in a higher estimated number of M2M (757 ± 122) per spheroid and higher numbers of CD206/FR-β (16 ± 4%) and CD163/FR-β (21 ± 10%) co-labelled M2M. These findings reinforce the reliability of M2M quantification with labeling strategies. While both segmentation methods are justifiable, the choice of strategy can influence results due to their respective strengths and limitations. The empirical method tends to be more inclusive, detecting more cells by capturing low-intensity signals, whereas the automatic method applies stricter criteria, resulting in a more consistent but more stringent selection.

In vivo Studies

NSG mice were obtained from Charles River, bred and housed at the CREFRE (Toulouse). All animal experimental were performed following relevant named institutional and national guidelines and regulations, so protocols were approved by the institutional Animal Care and Use Ethical Committee of the UMS006 and Region Midi-Pyrenees (APAFIS#46085-2023112714074561) and were performed in strict accordance with the European Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

5 million M2M and 5 million A549-GFP cells were injected with Matrigel (1:3 by volume, Corning) via subcutaneous injection into the flank of the mouse (n = 11 for A549-GFP and n = 19 for A549-GFP and M2M). The mice were monitored weekly. After three weeks, MNP-mAb6-25 and MNP-CI nanoparticles were injected intravenously in the mouse tail, at a concentration of 2.5 mg/kg. Nanoparticle targeting was analysed by IVIS for 3 days. The In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS Spectrum) was used to scan the mice and/or their isolated organs to localize the fluorescent signal of the MNP-mAb6-25. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and then sacrificed by cervical dislocation. For the detection of the fluorescence, wavelengths used were 745 nm for the excitation and 800 nm for the emission. The images were captured using the Living Images software. The quantification was performed by comparing the Total Radiant efficiency of the selected regions for each condition.

Statistical Analysis

Results are expressed as the mean ± SEM of at least two independent experiments. Normality and lognormality tests were performed on the data. Then statistical analysis was performed using one or two-way ANOVA tests or Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test depending on the normality using Graph Pad Prism 10.1. Differences were considered significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Synthesis and Characterization of MNP Targeting M2 Macrophages (MNP-mAb6-25)

We developed pro-tumoral M2M-targeting MNP termed MNP-mAb6-25 and constituted by a magnetic iron oxide (γ-Fe2O3) multicore nanoflower (NF) coated with a PEG shell, to make them biocompatible and stealthier, and functionalized with the in-house monoclonal antibody mAb6-25 through a spacer arm by a Michael reaction (Figure 1A). The MNP-mAb6-25 were also labeled with a fluorophore, cyanine 5 or cyanine 7, to allow their detection by fluorescence for in vitro (flow cytometry and microscopy) or in vivo experiment (bioimaging on murine model). Control MNP functionalized (MNP-CI) or not (MNP-NV) with the isotypic control antibody were also synthesized. The physico-chemical properties of the different nanoparticles were characterized by measuring the core diameter, the hydrodynamic diameter in PBS and the zeta potential at pH 7.4. TEM analysis showed that the iron oxide core of the MNP was 19,5 ± 2,7 nm (Figure 1B). The functionalization with the antibody (isotypic control or mAb6-25 antibodies) led to increase the hydrodynamic diameter and the negative surface charge of the MNP. Indeed, the hydrodynamic diameter and surface charge of the MNP-mAb6-25 were respectively 61.4 nm and –9.7 ± 0.6 mV and those of MNP-CI were 74.5 nm and –12.7 ± 0.8 mV compared with 45.2 nm and –0.4 ± 0.9 mV for the non-vectorized MNP-NV (Figure 1C, D and Table S1). Specific Uptake of MNP-mAb6-25 by M2M

In order to study MNP-mAb6-25 uptake by the different types of macrophages, we generated M1 and M2 macrophages (termed M1M and M2M) thanks to the in vitro culture for seven days of purified monocytes from blood samples of healthy donors in the presence of appropriate cytokines to induce M2M or M1M. We also confirmed that mAb6-25 specifically bound to M2-derived macrophages but not M1-derived macrophages or monocytes, as previously described (Figure S2) (patent WO2018020000A1). We first analyzed the cytotoxicity of MNP-mAb6-25 in M2M and M1M and showed that the MNP-mAb6-25 did not present any cytotoxicity up to 64 μg γ-Fe2O3/mL on both M1M and M2M (Figure S3).

As the density of antibody conjugation to the MNP can affect the MNP ability to target M2M, an exhausting screening of MNP vectorized with different mAb6-25 (1 to 8 antibodies/MNP) was performed to identify the optimal vectorization able to induce a specific uptake by M2M, expressing the FR-β, comparatively to M1M that did not express this receptor. As shown in Figure S4, the uptake of MNP-mAb6-25 by M2M increased with the mAb6-25 grafting density at the surface of the MNP, up to 5 antibodies per MNP (Figures S4A–C). We also compared the MNP-mAb6-25 uptake between M2M and M1M and showed that the ratio M2M/M1M increased with the mAb6-25 grafting density at the surface of the MNP, up to 5 antibodies per MNP (Figure S4D). However, the MNP-mAb6-25 grafted with 3 antibodies presents a high efficacy of M2M targeting vs M1M and the lower variability in the uptake and the ratio M2M/M1M. Even if the best results were obtained with 5 antibodies, the condition using 3 is a good compromise to reduce mAb6-25 consumption and cost while maintaining a good level of binding on M2M for further experiments. We then analyzed in-depth the uptake of MNP-mAb6-25 grafted with 3 antibodies per MNP in M2M and in M1M. The kinetics of fluorescent MNP-mAb6-25 uptake was analyzed by flow cytometry on the two types of macrophages. The results showed that the amount of MNP-mAb6-25 taken up by M2M and M1M increased over the incubation time reaching a plateau above 48 h of incubation (Figure 2A). Moreover, the uptake of MNP-mAb6-25 was significantly higher (13-fold increase at 48 h) and more rapid in M2M than in M1M, indicating that MNP-mAb6-25 specifically targeted the M2M. Moreover, M2M took up higher amount of fluorescent MNP-mAb6-25 as well as free fluorescent mAb6-25 than control MNP-NV and MNP-CI, as evidenced by the shift of the peak of MFI (mean of fluorescence intensity) toward higher fluorescence intensities (Figure 2B and S5). In contrast, weak MFI was observed in M1M incubated with MNP-mAb6-25, MNP-CI or MNP-NV as well as with free fluorescent mAb6-25 (Figure 2B and S5). Furthermore, the quantification of cell-associated fluorescence by flow cytometry, analyzed after 24 h of incubation, showed that the amount of MNP-mAb6-25 taken up by M2M increased with the nanoparticle’s concentration (Figure 2C). This quantification also showed that MNP-mAb6-25 uptake by M2M is more important than control MNP-CI and MNP-NV, on one hand, and that MNP-mAb6-25 uptake is higher in M2M than in M1M at the same nanoparticle’s concentrations (Figure 2C). All together, these results indicate that M2M took up higher quantity of MNP-mAb6-25 than control MNPs and that M2M uptakes higher quantity of MNP-mAb6-25 than M1M, strongly demonstrating the high specificity of MNP-mAb6-25 to target M2M. This specific targeting of M2M was also observed by confocal microscopy showing that the MNP-mAb6-25 internalized in M2M but not in M1M (Figure 2D and S6). The absolute amount of MNP-mAb6-25 taken up by M2M was also determined by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) compared to control MNPs. The relaxometry results showed that the MNP-mAb6-25 uptake by M2M was respectively 2.4- or 7.4-fold higher than MNP-CI or MNP-NV, through longitudinal T1 and transversal T2 relaxation time measurements (Figure 2E). Subsequent characterization of the intracellular location of MNP-mAb6-25 in M2M was studied by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which demonstrated that MNP-mAb6-25 were mainly localized in the lysosomes of M2M, after 72 h of incubation (Figure 2F). Confocal microscopy study confirmed the accumulation of MNP-mAb6-25 in the lysosomes of M2M (Figure 2G). The analysis of MNP-mAb6-25 colocalization with the Lysotracker indicated that 72.6 ± 4.08% and 96.4 ± 0.73% of MNP-mAb6-25 taken up by M2M localized within the lysosomes after 24 or 72 h of incubation, respectively (Figure 2H).

MNP-mAb6-25 Specifically Target M2M in Cancer Cells/M2M 2D Co-Culture Models

We then investigated the efficacy of MNP-mAb6-25 to target the M2M in 2D co-culture models mixing CMTMR-stained M2M and CTV-labelled A549 cancer cells (ratio 1:1). This cell line was chosen regarding its M2-recruiting cytokine profile.22

The kinetics of the MNP-mAb6-25 uptake, analyzed by flow cytometry, indicated that the number of nanoparticles taken up by M2M increased over the time of incubation (Figure 3A). Moreover, the MNP-mAb6-25 uptake was significantly higher and more rapid in M2M co-cultured with A549 compared to cancer cells alone. More precisely, the MNP-mAb6-25 uptake was respectively 13,292 ± 1,738 more important in M2M compared to A549 after 72 h of incubation. The flow cytometry quantification also confirmed the high specificity of MNP-mAb6-25 to target M2M since control MNPs, ie MNP-NV and MNP-CI, were taken up in very low amounts by M2M in the 2D co-culture (Figure 3B). Moreover, the specificity of MNP-mAb6-25 for M2M was reinforced by the very small amount of MNP-mAb6-25 uptake by M1M (Figure 3B) or A549 cancer cells (Figure S7A) in monoculture or in the 2D co-culture. Confocal microscopy studies of 2D co-cultures confirmed that MNP-mAb6-25 accumulated in M2M but not in A549, and that neither MNP-NV nor MNP-CI were detectable by microscopy which correlates with flow cytometry results (Figure 3C). MNP-mAb6-25 Specifically Target M2M in Cancer Cells/M2M Spheroids

To better approximate in vivo conditions, we extended these analyses to multicellular spheroids composed of cancer cells and macrophages. We first developed a robust 3D lung cancer model for studying macrophages behavior and TAM targeting, by optimizing the cellular density for spheroid formation using A549 lung cancer cells. 1,000 to 10,000 A549 cancer cells were used to form the spheroids in ultra-low-attachment U-shaped bottom plates. The cell counting and the measurements of spheroids area for 4 days showed that spheroids seeded with 2,500 cells present the best spheroids growth (Figure S8). 3D spheroids mixing A549 cancer cells with primary myeloid cells (ratio 1:1) - either M2M, M1M or monocytes derived from purified monocytes from blood samples of healthy donors – were then formed to analyze the impact of myeloid cells on spheroids growth. The counting of cancer cells and the analysis of spheroids area showed that the spheroids growth increased significantly and more importantly in the presence of M2M than in absence of M2M, highlighting the pro-tumoral role of M2M in promoting cancer cell proliferation within these conditions of spheroids culture (Figure 4A). Importantly, the analysis of M2 markers as well as the secreted cytokines showed that M2M co-cultured cancer cells in 2D or 3D models kept their differentiation status, compared to M2M monoculture (Figure S9A and S9B). Indeed, high levels of M2 markers were expressed at the surface of these M2M, including CD163, CD206, and FR-β, in addition to the secretion of high amounts of immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β1, CCL2, Il-8 and Il-10. We performed similar experiments with 3D spheroids containing A549 and M1M. Surprisingly, M1M, known to present anti-tumoral activity, stimulated the proliferation of cancer cells in A549/M1M spheroids (Figure S10A and S10B). We thus analyzed the expression of M2M markers on these cells and showed a significant rise in the expression of CD206, CD204, and FR-β and a decrease in CD86, indicating that M1M switched toward an M2-like phenotype (Figure S10C). Additionally, we also observed an increase in the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines in A549/M1M spheroids, including TGF-β1, CCL2, Il-8, as shown in A549/M2M spheroids (Figures S10D and S9B). These results confirmed the switch of M1M toward M2M phenotype and explained the pro-tumoral activity of these depolarized M1M on A549 cancer cells (Figure S10A). Interestingly, the presence of monocytes did not modify the spheroids growth, compared to A549 spheroids and did not polarize into M2 macrophages during co-culture, as shown by the lack of M2 markers CD204 and FR-β even after 7 days (Figures S10A and S11). Furthermore, we analyzed the localization of M2M in the A549/M2M spheroids using several antibodies recognizing receptors expressed at the M2M surface including the mAb6-25 that targets the FR-β. To do so, M2M were stained with the intracellular CMTMR fluorescent probe, mixed with A549-GFP cancer cells to form spheroids and grown for 7 days. The spheroids were then fixed and labelled with fluorescent antibodies recognizing CD206, CSF1-R, CD163 and FR-β (Figures 4B and S12). The results showed that M2M macrophages were distributed throughout the spheroid and co-labelled by the mAb6-25 and the anti-CD206, anti-CSF1-R or anti-CD163 (Figures 4B and S12A). The colocalization profiles also revealed the overlap of the CD206, FR-β with CMTMR M2M-staining, without any overlap with A549-GFP cancer cells (Figure 4B). These results were confirmed by the in-silico 3D models of the labeled spheroids using Imaris software and modeling software (Figures S12B and C). A 3D representation of the entire spheroids was elaborated from the z-stack confocal images and the quantification of M2M population within the spheroids was determined at 5 ± 3% to 21 ± 10% of total cells depending on the M2 markers (CD206/FR-β or CD163/FR-β) used in the analysis and the in-silico segmentation strategies (automatic or empirical thresholding) (Figures S12B and C). Comparison of segmentation approaches showed consistent quantification of FR-β+ cells between automatic and empirical thresholding. In contrast, CD163 cells detection was greatly affected by the thresholding strategy, with higher proportions obtained using empirical thresholds. This difference likely reflects the more diffuse and lower-contrast staining pattern of CD163, which is not as well captured by default automated thresholding parameters. All together, these results validated that M2M/A549 multicellular models constituted a suitable model to investigate strategies to target and detect pro-tumoral M2M.

We then investigated the efficacy of MNP-mAb6-25 to target the M2M in this 3D co-culture models, ie multicellular spheroids mixing CMTMR-stained M2M and CTV-labelled A549 cancer cells (ratio 1:1) grown for 3 days then incubated with MNP-mAb6-25. The kinetics of the MNP-mAb6-25 uptake, analyzed by flow cytometry, indicated that the amount of nanoparticles taken up by M2M increased over the time of incubation (Figure 4C). Moreover, the MNP-mAb6-25 uptake was significantly higher and more rapid in M2M compared to cancer cells. More precisely, the MNP-mAb6-25 uptake was respectively 7714 ± 351 more important in M2M compared to A549 (Figure S7B). The flow cytometry quantification also confirmed the high specificity of MNP-mAb6-25 towards M2M since control MNPs, ie MNP-NV and MNP-CI, were taken up in very low amounts by M2M in 3D models (Figure 4D). Moreover, the specificity of MNP-mAb6-25 for M2M was reinforced by the very small amount of MNP-mAb6-25 uptake by M1M alone or in the 3D models (Figure 4D). Confocal microscopy studies were also performed on 3D M2M/A549 spheroids (Figure 4E) and validated the high specificity of MNP-mAb6-25 towards M2M in these multicellular 3D models. Indeed, whereas MNP-mAb6-25 colocalized with CMTMR-labelled M2M, they were not detected in GFP-labelled A549. Similarly, control MNP-NV and MNP-CI were not or poorly detected in M2M or cancer cells. The specificity of the MNP-mAb6-25 for M2M was reinforced by its low detection in M1M/A549 spheroids compared to M2M/A549 spheroids (Figures S13 and 4E). The analysis of confocal microscopy images using IMARIS software, that measure the distance between the spots labelled by the fluorescent MNP and CMTMR-M2M, validated a high colocalization of MNP-mAb6-25 with CMTMR-labeled M2M (~60%) in M2M/A549 spheroids, compared to control MNP-NV or MNP-CI (~30%) (Figure S14). In contrast, the IMARIS analysis showed a weak colocalization between MNP-mAb6-25 and CMTMR-labeled M1M in M1M/A549 spheroids (Figure S13). Similarly, MNP-NV and MNP-CI were not or poorly colocalized with M1M or cancer cells, in M1M/A549 spheroids (Figures S13 and S14). All together, the results obtained from flow cytometry and confocal microscopy studies validated the high selectivity of MNP-mAb6-25 to target the M2M 3D multicellular spheroids.

MNP-mAb6-25 Uptake in Xenograft Mice Model

We next conducted in vivo studies to investigate the capacity of MNP-mAb6-25 to target a NSCLC tumor containing M2M. To do this, given that the mAb6-25 recognizes only the human form of FR-β, NSG mice were subcutaneously injected with a mix of A549 cancer cells and human M2M with a 1:1 ratio. After 3 weeks, the tumor had grown to a volume of around 130 mm3. The Cy7-labelled MNP-CI or MNP-mAb6-25 were injected intravenously in the mice tail and their tumor uptake was analyzed by IVIS Spectrum measuring the MNP fluorescence intensity (Figure 5). Seventy‑two hours after injection, the amount of MNP-mAb6-25 detected within the A549/M2M tumor was higher than control MNP-CI (Figure 5A left and B), suggesting that MNP conjugation with mAb6-25 enhanced the MNP efficacy to target the tumor containing M2M (Figure 5C). We also observed that MNP-mAB6-25 accumulated in the liver (Figure 5A-right) similarly than control MNP; such liver accumulation may reflect the MNP clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, in agreement with physico-chemical properties – notably their size - of MNP-mAb6-25. However, a remarkable higher uptake of MNP-mAb6-25 by the A549/M2M tumors than A549 tumors and liver was observed, supporting an efficient M2 TAM targeting in A549/M2M tumors by MNP-mAB6-25 (Figure 5A). These results strongly highlight the potential of MNP-mAb6-25 to target and detect tumors containing pro-tumoral M2M expressing the FR-β. Discussion

In many cancers and more particularly in NSCLC, TAM play a key role in treatments and immune attacks resistance by protecting tumor cells.23 Targeting them is therefore a major challenge in lung cancer. Several studies including Phase I clinical trials have highlighted the targeting of these TAM, particularly with the anti-CSF-1R antibody that showed encouraging results on the tumor regression but caused adverse effects.24–26 Specific targeting of pro-tumoral TAM offers a promising strategy to overcome this limitation but remains challenging. Magnetic nanoparticles are increasingly being studied in cancerology thanks to their properties allowing the development of innovative contrast and/or therapeutic agents. Here, we investigated the M2M targeting of PEGylated magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles endowed with remarkably high transverse relaxivity thanks to their nanoflower morphology giving them cooperative magnetic behavior within highly crystalline magnetic multidomains.27 These MNP were conjugated with an in-house monoclonal antibody mAb6-25, that targets the folate receptor beta (FR-β) specifically expressed by the type 2 macrophages (MNP-mAb6-25). Taking account of their physisco-chemical properties (size, surface charge, PEG coating…), MNP-mAb6-25 should be primarily cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system, resulting in predominant accumulation in the liver and spleen rather than renal excretion.28

We first showed that MNP-mAb6-25 specifically targeted M2M compared to M1M, internalized and accumulated in the M2M lysosomes without inducing cytotoxicity. Such internalization and lysosomal accumulation of MNP targeting-receptor at the cell surface has been already described mainly in cancer cells as, for example, MNP targeting the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR).29 However, to our knowledge, few studies described nanoparticles targeting M2M or TAM. MNP targeting the CD206 mannose receptor have been developed for TAM imaging.15 In this study, confocal microscopy analysis showed that MNP functionalization with mannose specifically targeted the M2M in 2D cultures, comparatively to M1 macrophages, as well as in 4T1 mouse mammary tumor. They showed a colocalization in the tumor of these MNP-mannose with the CD206 positive cells. The MNP uptake by M2M was also confirmed using colocalization studies with anti-CD163 and anti-CD68 M2M markers. However, other cell types including immature dendritic cells can express the CD206; thus, MNP targeting CD206 did not exclusively target the pro-tumoral M2M. Another study described polyacide lactique-co-glycolique (PLGA) nanoparticles decorated with the peptide Y-Leg that targets TAM and presented a subcellular localization in the lysosome of J774A.1 macrophage cell line after their internalization, correlating with our results.30 However, the study did not demonstrate the specific M2 targeting towards M1. Moreover, this targeting is not really specific to primary M2M. To highlight the high specificity of the MNP-mAb6-25 to target pro-tumor macrophages, we developed a robust 3D multicellular tumor model mixing the NSCLC cancer cell line (A549) with M2M-derived purified monocytes from blood samples of healthy donors, that recapitulate in vivo conditions, notably the ability of M2M to promote cancer cells proliferation. We then demonstrated that MNP-mAb6-25 specifically targeted the M2M in these multicellular spheroids as well as in 2D co-culture M2M/A549 models, by flow cytometry and confocal microscopy. Moreover, control MNPs, MNP-NV and MNP-CI, that were not functionalized with mAb6-25, were less efficiently taken up by M2M in these 2D and 3D M2M/A549 models. Additionally, control MNPs and MNP-mAb6-25 were poorly taken up by M1M and cancer cells in monoculture, 2D and 3D co-culture models.

The specific targeting of pro-tumoral M2M by MNP-mAb6-25 was then validated in vivo using an immunodeficient mouse model elaborated with the grafting of a mixture of human lung cancer cells (A549) and human M2M-derived purified monocytes from blood samples of healthy donors. A stronger fluorescent signal was observed in tumors carrying human M2M having received fluorescent MNP-mAb6-25 compared to control MNP-CI, strongly suggesting that MNP-mAb6-25 may be an efficient imaging tool to target and track pro-tumoral M2M in NSCLC models. Few studies showed the potential of in vivo TAM targeting by magnetic nanoparticles for imaging. For example, MNP targeting CD206 was demonstrated to accumulate at the tumor site, by MRI or NIR light.15 However, in this study, the conventional markers used for MNP targeting, CD206, is also expressed on other cell types such as dendritic cells; thus, such targeted MNP may not specifically target pro-tumoral TAM. Another study also conducted in a mouse model of breast cancer, demonstrates the potential of M2M-targeting MNP for tumor imaging31 using a M2 peptide for the targeting; despite the encouraging results, the unknown nature of the target remains a critical missing piece.

In conclusion, this study provides an integrated view from the synthesis to detection and imaging performance, highlighting the potential of MNP-mAb6-25 for M2M-containing tumor imaging. The 6–25 antibody that specifically targets M2M was successfully conjugated to PEGylated magnetic nanoflowers and tested in 2D models, 3D multicellular tumor spheroids and in vivo mice bearing M2M/NSCLC tumors. Moreover, MNP-mAb6-25 recognizing the FR-β, that is exclusively expressed on M2 macrophages in human adults, will be more specific and precise compared to MNP targeted with antibodies directed against conventional markers such as CD206, CD204, CD163, or CSF-1R, which are expressed on several myeloid cell types. This specificity allows for more reliable identification of TAM involved in the tumor progression and cancer inflammation, in addition to providing an analytical tool for phenotypic or predictive monitoring, as M2 pro-tumoral TAM are associated with a poor prognosis in NSCLC. Beyond the specific monitoring of pro-tumoral TAM, nanoparticles targeting specifically TAMs hold promise for clinical applications in cancer therapy. Further studies evaluating MNP-mAb6-25 as TAM depleting agent by magnetic hyperthermia may offer new therapeutic opportunities to remodel NSCLC microenvironment into a more immunopermissive one and sensitize NSCLC to chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy. Furthermore, by selectively delivering therapeutic agents to TAMs, these TAM-targeting nanoparticles may also promote macrophage reprogramming from a pro-tumoral to an anti-tumoral phenotype. Moreover, beyond therapeutic applications, MNP-mAb6-25 may also represent a valuable tool for NSCLC diagnosis as an MRI contrast agent, and patient stratification on M2 TAMs abundance in the tumors, thereby allowing for tailored clinical follow-up and monitoring of treatment responses paving the way for a fully integrated theranostic strategy combining targeted IONP-based therapy with molecular imaging.
