CT-guided three-dimensional reconstruction with predictive modeling for personalized esophageal anastomosis in type C esophageal atresia
Authors: Vassiliy Lozovoy, Zhenis Sakuov, Assylzhan Yerekeshov, Dastan Rustemov, Ruslan Bilal, Temirlan Zhangaskanov, Zariat Shatkenova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnastomotic stricture remains the most common and clinically significant complication following primary repair of type C esophageal atresia. Despite various technical modifications, postoperative narrowing at the anastomotic site continues to occur. We describe an approach incorporating preoperative three-dimensional CT reconstruction to enable individualized planning of the esophageal anastomosis.Aims and ObjectivesTo evaluate the feasibility, safety, and early clinical outcomes of a modified esophageal anastomosis guided by preoperative radiological and CT-based measurements in neonates with type C esophageal atresia.Materials and MethodsThis single-center case series included five consecutive neonates who underwent primary repair between January 2022 and December 2023. Preoperative CT was performed to assess associated anomalies. Multiplanar and 3D reconstructions were used to measure the diameters of the proximal esophageal segment and the distal esophagus at the fistula level. Based on these parameters, a geometry-based calculation was applied preoperatively to determine the optimal length and configuration of the distal esophageal incision to achieve a wider anastomosis.ResultsOn postoperative day 7, contrast esophagography demonstrated a mean anastomotic diameter of 4.71 ± 0.43 mm. One patient developed an anastomotic leak requiring re-thoracotomy and repair. No cases of tracheoesophageal fistula recurrence were observed. During 12 months of follow-up, no patients developed clinically significant anastomotic stricture requiring gastrostomy or repeated dilatation.ConclusionCT-based three-dimensional reconstruction combined with predictive modeling enables individualized incision planning and formation of a wider anastomosis. This approach may reduce postoperative strictures and support patient-specific management of type C esophageal atresia.

Introduction

Esophageal atresia (EA) is a congenital condition defined by disrupted esophageal continuity, which may be accompanied by a tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF) (1). Esophageal atresia with a TEF (Gross type C) is the most common variant of this congenital malformation. Anastomotic stricture (AS) is the most common complication following repair of esophageal atresia type C (2) and frequently necessitates repeated postoperative bougienage procedures (3). The development of stricture is multifactorial, with surgical technique and anastomotic configuration being major contributing factors (4). Traditional circular end-to-end anastomoses are prone to contraction during healing, resulting in luminal narrowing. Although various technical modifications have been described, most do not allow precise adaptation to individual patient anatomy.

Diagnosis of esophageal atresia is based on the clinical presentation and confirmed by contrast radiography of the upper esophageal pouch. The routine use of computed tomography (CT) for preoperative evaluation of esophageal atresia is not recommended, but it is useful in the presence of associated pathologies, such as congenital heart defects, vascular anomalies, and other associated anomalies. In addition, 3D CT imaging can determine the level of the tracheoesophageal fistula and the distance between the atretic segments.

Using three-dimensional CT reconstruction, we determined the diameters of the proximal (D1) and distal (D2) esophageal pouches and used these anatomical parameters to define the planned anastomotic configuration (D3). Based on these patient-specific data, we developed a modified anastomotic technique guided by predictive mathematical modeling, aiming to achieve a wider lumen and potentially reduce the risk of postoperative stricture formation.

Materials and methods

The study was approved by the local ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all patients. This prospective, single-center observational study was conducted at the University Medical Center “Mother and Child Center” in Astana, Kazakhstan, between January 2022 and December 2023. Throughout this period, 25 newborns with esophageal atresia and a distal TEF were treated. Five patients underwent preoperative computed tomography because of associated anomalies. Specifically, a CT scan was performed in one patient for tracheal stenosis and in four patients as part of an evaluation for suspected congenital heart disease (Table 1).

Preoperative imaging and measurements

Computed tomography was performed using a multislice CT scanner Aquilion Prime SP TSX-303B (Canon, Japan). Patients were positioned in the supine position to ensure stable and standardized imaging conditions. Images were reconstructed with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm and a reconstruction interval of 0.1 mm. Multiplanar reformatted images were generated interactively on a dedicated workstation (Vitrea Advanced Visualization) using the Lung Airway Analysis mode with the “airway lumen” (airway sinus) setting, allowing full 360-degree rotation. After CT imaging, three-dimensional reconstruction was carried out, allowing measurement of the diameters of the proximal (D1) and distal (D2) atretic esophageal segments (Figure 1). These two parameters (D1 and D2) were subsequently used for mathematical prediction of the optimal anastomotic configuration.

CT-based 3D model of the trachea and esophagus. D1, the diameter of the proximal esophageal segment; D2, the diameter of the distal esophageal segment.

CT examinations were performed using pediatric low-dose protocols in accordance with the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle. Radiation dose parameters ranged from CTDIvol 1.16–14.0 mGy and DLP 15.92–45.0 mGy·cm. The variability in radiation dose reflected differences in patient body weight (1.4–3.78 kg) and the use of intravenous contrast-enhanced CT in selected patients when clinically indicated.

Relevance of mathematical modeling

In the surgical treatment of esophageal atresia, the formation of an esophago-esophageal anastomosis between segments of different diameters remains one of the key technical challenges. Mismatch between the circumferences of the anastomotic edges may lead to local redistribution of tension and may contribute to anastomotic leakage and subsequent cicatricial stricture formation. In clinical practice, incisions on the distal esophageal segment are used to compensate for differences in diameter between the proximal and distal segments. However, the depth and length of these incisions are usually determined empirically, which reduces the reproducibility of the technique and increases dependence on the individual experience of the surgeon. Therefore, in the present study, a geometric model was developed to estimate the incision depth required to compensate for the difference in circumference between the proximal and distal esophageal segments based on objective anatomical measurements obtained from CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction.

Surgical technique

The neonate was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position with the right arm elevated. Under general anesthesia, a right thoracotomy was performed through the fourth intercostal space. The surgical field was accessed via an extrapleural approach. The azygos vein (preserved) and the TEF were identified, after which the fistula was ligated and divided.

Following adequate mobilization of both the proximal and distal esophageal segments, the proximal pouch was opened using a transverse incision. The first distal esophageal incision (L1) was standardized at 5 mm in all patients and was planned preoperatively. The length of the second incision (L2) was then calculated preoperatively using the predictive formula based on the measured D1 and D2 values. In cases where the distal lumen was considered sufficiently enlarged after the initial 5-mm incision, no additional L2 incision was required. The calculated incision lengths were measured using a sterile millimeter ruler. Both L1 and L2 were marked on the distal esophageal segment according to the preoperative calculations before performing the incisions.

This approach allowed enlargement of the distal esophageal lumen, facilitating the creation of a wider anastomosis in more than one plane (Figure 2). The anastomosis was constructed using interrupted absorbable sutures (polydioxanone 5-0), placed to minimize circumferential tension. A nasogastric tube was passed across the anastomosis. The incision was closed in layers following the placement of an intercostal drainage tube in all cases.

Postoperative evaluation

All patients received standardized postoperative care, including nasogastric decompression, administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, and parenteral nutrition until enteral feeding was established. All patients received postoperative mechanical ventilatory support during the early postoperative period according to our institutional protocol for EA repair. Postoperative anastomotic diameter (D3) was assessed on postoperative day 7 using contrast esophagography. Measurements were performed by the operating pediatric surgeon using digital imaging software. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the mean value was used for analysis (Figure 3). In the absence of anastomotic complications, the chest drain was subsequently removed. Anastomotic leakage was defined as extravasation of contrast material, whereas stricture and recurrence of tracheoesophageal fistula were diagnosed based on clinical symptoms, including dysphagia, regurgitation, and recurrent respiratory infections, and confirmed by radiological imaging.

Measurement of the esophageal anastomotic diameter (D3).

Follow-up

All patients were followed prospectively for at least 12 months after surgery. The primary outcome was the postoperative anastomotic diameter (D3). Secondary outcomes included anastomotic leakage, recurrent TEF, anastomotic stricture requiring esophageal dilatation or gastrostomy, and clinically evident chest wall deformity.

Results

Five neonates with esophageal atresia type C were included in this case series. All five patients had associated congenital anomalies; however, none had a confirmed syndromic diagnosis (Table 1). Prenatal diagnosis of EA was established in two patients. In two additional patients, the diagnosis was not suspected antenatally. One patient had prenatal polyhydramnios without a definitive diagnosis of EA before birth. Discussion

Anastomotic stricture (AS) is the most common postoperative complication following type C esophageal atresia repair and may require repeated dilatation procedures (5). Its development is multifactorial: pre-operative factors such as gap length and prematurity, intraoperative factors including anastomotic tension and suture technique, and postoperative issues like anastomotic leak and gastroesophageal reflux all contribute (4). Importantly, the anastomotic configuration itself has a major impact. Traditional circular end-to-end anastomosis tends to contract substantially during the healing process, reducing the luminal diameter and predisposing to stricture. Consequently, various modifications of the anastomotic technique have been explored to minimize this complication (6).

P. Catalano and colleagues (2011) presented a technique for performing an oblique esophageal anastomosis to prevent the development of AS following surgical repair of EA. A retrospective review of 16 patients (14 neonates with type III and 2 with type IV esophageal atresia according to the Ladd-Gross classification) demonstrated that this method, which creates a wider suture line not confined to a single plane, effectively minimizes the risk of stricture formation. The study concluded that the oblique anastomosis technique is a safe and effective alternative to the conventional end-to-end esophageal anastomosis (4).

In the study by Mohamed Oulad Saiad, a novel surgical technique was evaluated, designed to minimize the risk of AS following esophageal atresia repair. A retrospective analysis of 49 patients was conducted, comparing outcomes between those who underwent a wave-shaped anastomosis and those who received a traditional end-to-end anastomosis. The results showed that none of the patients in the first group developed strictures, whereas eight patients in the second group did, representing a statistically significant difference (p = 0.046). The technique is characterized by a wide, multi-planar anastomosis that creates a large low-tension zone, promoting better healing and reducing the likelihood of stricture formation. The authors suggested that further studies are required to confirm these findings (7).

Another article introduced a new technique for esophageal anastomosis aimed at minimizing the risk of AS formation in patients with EA. The method involves creating a wide anastomosis with a suture line not confined to a single plane, which allows for uniform tension distribution and reduces the likelihood of narrowing during the healing process. Over a 10-year period, this method was applied in 11 cases, with only one patient developing an AS, which was successfully managed with a single dilatation (8).

In a different study, a novel surgical technique for primary repair of EA was presented, intended to prevent AS formation. The authors, Mehmet Melek and Ufuk Cobanoglu, described a method involving a plus-shaped (+) incision at the proximal end of the atretic esophagus, which allows for an expanded anastomotic area. This technique was applied to 11 patients with EA and tracheoesophageal fistula between 2005 and 2009. The results demonstrated that none of the patients required dilatation for AS and none experienced symptomatic dysphagia during a four-year follow-up period. The authors concluded that this method minimizes stricture formation and provides an effective approach for esophageal reconstruction in infants (9).

Although routine CT imaging is not generally recommended for diagnosing esophageal atresia in newborns (10), it can help identify the level of the distal tracheoesophageal fistula and reduce intraoperative difficulties during its dissection, as well as reveal additional anomalies such as a right-sided aortic arch, vertebral anomalies, pulmonary abnormalities, and other congenital anomalies (11). To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has reported on tailoring the type of anastomosis in esophageal atresia to an individual patient based on CT 3D modeling, even though current technological advancements make this approach feasible.

It should be emphasized that several previously described modifications of esophageal anastomosis also incorporate distal esophageal incisions to enlarge the lumen. However, in most of these techniques, the depth and length of the incisions are determined intraoperatively and rely heavily on the surgeon's subjective assessment and experience. In contrast, the key advantage of the proposed approach is the ability to define incision parameters preoperatively through mathematical modeling based on three-dimensional CT reconstruction. This strategy provides a more objective method of surgical planning and supports the creation of a tailored, patient-specific anastomosis that accounts for individual anatomical variability. However, a notable limitation of CT-based planning is the associated radiation exposure, which is of particular concern in the neonatal population and should be carefully considered when selecting patients for this approach.

In our series, a CT-based 3D reconstruction allowed successful esophageal anastomosis in all five patients. The present data demonstrate that the anastomotic diameter (D3) consistently exceeded the distal esophageal diameter (D2), with a mean increase of 29.3%. This finding suggests that the applied surgical approach allows for effective enlargement of the distal segment and the formation of a relatively wide anastomosis, which is hypothesized to contribute to a lower risk of postoperative stricture formation. During the 12-month follow-up period, none of the patients developed dysphagia, and none required esophageal dilatation.

One patient developed an anastomotic leak requiring reoperation. Although this patient had significant associated pathology, including congenital laryngeal stenosis and prolonged respiratory compromise, the potential contribution of the modified anastomotic technique cannot be completely excluded. Enlargement of the distal esophagus through additional incisions may compromise local vascular supply or increase tension at the anastomotic margin, thereby increasing the risk of leakage. Furthermore, it is possible that a conventional anastomosis without distal esophageal enlargement would have resulted in a different balance between leakage risk and subsequent stricture formation. Given the small sample size, the observed leak should be interpreted cautiously, and larger comparative studies are required to determine the safety profile of this technique.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence, computer vision, and predictive surgical analytics are facilitating personalized operative planning and intraoperative decision-making. While the present study does not utilize machine learning algorithms, it applies a patient-specific quantitative planning strategy based on three-dimensional anatomical reconstruction. Such approaches may represent an early step toward future AI-assisted surgical planning systems in neonatal surgery. Furthermore, advances in computer vision and thoracoscopic image analysis may eventually enable real-time intraoperative measurements, potentially reducing dependence on preoperative CT imaging and further supporting personalized esophageal reconstruction strategies (12, 13).

The present study was designed as a case series and did not include a concurrent control group. Therefore, the absence of anastomotic strictures during follow-up should be interpreted cautiously. Published studies have reported anastomotic stricture rates ranging from 30% to 50% following conventional repair of type C esophageal atresia. While no strictures were observed in our cohort, the small sample size and uncontrolled study design do not allow conclusions regarding superiority of the proposed technique. Larger comparative studies are needed to validate these preliminary findings.

Limitations

The proposed mathematical model has several limitations: It does not account for tissue heterogeneity of the esophageal wall.It does not include biomechanical parameters (elastic modulus, stress distribution).It is mainly applicable to primary esophago-esophageal anastomosis.Several limitations should be acknowledged regarding D3 assessment. Preoperative diameters (D1 and D2) were obtained using CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction, whereas postoperative diameter (D3) was measured using contrast esophagography. These imaging modalities differ in spatial resolution and measurement characteristics. Furthermore, esophagographic measurements may be influenced by the degree of luminal distension, contrast volume, patient positioning, and respiratory motion at the time of image acquisition. Although each D3 measurement was performed three times and averaged, no formal inter-observer or intra-observer reliability analysis was conducted.

Conclusion

Integration of CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction with predictive mathematical modeling enables individualized planning of the anastomotic incision and facilitates construction of a wider anastomosis. This approach may reduce the incidence of postoperative strictures, potentially decrease the need for repeated esophageal bougienage, and could provide a patient-specific strategy for the surgical correction of type C esophageal atresia.
