Magnetic Microcapsules with Carbon Nanotubes-Fe3O4 for Enhanced Solar Thermal Energy Storage
Authors: Mingjie Huang, Li Yin, Haoyang Ren, Yi Zhang, Yongxing Liang, Sen Liao, Yingheng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study successfully fabricated multifunctional n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 phase-change microcapsules with enhanced photothermal conversion and thermal energy storage capabilities. The microcapsules were constructed via a Pickering emulsion-templating method combined with in situ interfacial precipitation, exhibiting a well-defined core–shell structure, a uniform spherical morphology, and a narrow size distribution. The incorporation of the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite not only endowed the microcapsules with superparamagnetic behavior but also significantly improved their thermal conductivity and broadband light absorption, yielding a high photothermal conversion efficiency of 63.85% under solar irradiation. Furthermore, the microcapsules achieved a latent heat of 131.2 J/g with an encapsulation efficiency exceeding 54.5% and demonstrated thermal reliability and cycling stability over 400 repeated thermal cycles. This work provides a feasible strategy for designing multifunctional phase-change materials that integrate magnetic responsiveness, efficient solar-to-thermal conversion, and high-capacity energy storage, thereby demonstrating promising potential for applications in solar thermal harvesting and intelligent thermal management.

Introduction

With the accelerating global transition toward sustainable development, solar energy has become a cornerstone of renewable energy strategies due to its abundance, cleanliness, and universality. However, its intermittent and fluctuating nature continues to restrict its large-scale and stable utilization. To mitigate this limitation, phase-change materials (PCMs) have been widely investigated for solar thermal energy storage (TES), owing to their ability to absorb and release large amounts of latent heat during reversible phase transitions, thereby facilitating effective thermal management and continuous energy supply. −

Despite their advantages, conventional PCMssuch as paraffin, fatty acids, and polyethylene glycolstill suffer from low intrinsic thermal conductivity, leakage during melting, and poor long-term cycling stability, which significantly hinder their practical deployment. − To overcome these challenges, microencapsulation technology has been introduced as a reliable approach to physically confine PCMs within core–shell microstructures. This design effectively prevents liquid leakage, improves shape stability, and enhances heat transfer owing to the enlarged interfacial area. The performance of such systems is contingent on the shell composition: inorganic shells (e.g., CaCO3, SiO2, Al2O3, TiO2, CuO) typically offer superior thermal stability and mechanical integrity. Among these, CaCO3 has attracted particular interest due to its low cost, environmental benignity, and chemical durability, rendering it suitable for medium-temperature TES applications. To date, numerous CaCO3-based PCM microcapsules have been designed and studied, including CaCO3/n-octadecane, CaCO3/n-tetradecane, CaCO3/stearic acid, and related systems. However, an excessively thick or brittle shell may compromise the latent heat storage density, leading to a trade-off between encapsulation efficiency and energy storage capacity.

In parallel, the integration of photothermal conversion materials into PCM microcapsules has emerged as an advanced route to enhance solar energy utilization. Nanomaterials such as Fe3O4 nanoparticles and carbon nanotubes­(CNTs) exhibit strong light absorption and photothermal conversion capabilities due to localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) and broadband solar absorption. Fe3O4 offers additional magnetic responsiveness and high photothermal efficiency, while CNTs provide outstanding thermal conductivity and mechanical strength. Nevertheless, previously reported Fe3O4- or CNT-based PCM systems often suffer from nanoparticle aggregation, interfacial mismatch, and limited synergistic coupling between thermal and magnetic domains, resulting in reduced thermal conductivity and poor stability. −

To address these limitations, this work presents novel n-eicosane-based phase-change microcapsules featuring a CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 hybrid shell. The rationally designed composite shell integrates the structural robustness of CaCO3 with the exceptional light absorption and high thermal conductivity of CNTs, as well as the magnetic responsiveness of Fe3O4 nanoparticles. This multifunctional architecture leverages the complementary properties of each component to enhance the overall photothermal energy-storage performance of the microcapsules. Because of its multifunctional characteristics, the proposed system shows great potential for solar thermal energy storage, building temperature regulation, and intelligent thermal management applications.

Experiment

Synthesis of Magnetic Load Materials

The synthesis procedure for the CNTs-Fe3O4 magnetic composite is illustrated in Figure a. Briefly, 0.5 g of carboxylated multiwalled carbon nanotubes (C-MWCNTs) was dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water and sonicated for 1 h. Subsequently, 1 g of FeCl3·4H2O and 2.7 g of FeCl3·6H2O were sequentially introduced into the suspension, which was then mechanically stirred in a water bath at 70 °C for 30 min. While maintaining the temperature, ammonia solution was gradually added to adjust the pH to 10.0. Following pH adjustment, 0.61 g of sodium dodecylbenzenesulfonate (SDBS) was incorporated to enhance the surface electronegativity of Fe3O4 nanoparticles and suppress agglomeration. The resulting precipitate was magnetically separated and washed three times with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol, respectively. Finally, the product was dried under vacuum at 70 °C for 5 h to yield the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite powder.

Synthetic strategy and reaction mechanism of n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules.

CNTs-Fe3O4 Composites Undergo Coupling Treatment

To prevent significant agglomeration of the as-synthesized CNTs-Fe3O4 composite, a coupling treatment was performed using KH-560. First, 1 g of the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite was dispersed in 10 mL of ethanol and ultrasonicated for 30 min to achieve a uniform dispersion. Meanwhile, the KH-560 silane coupling agent was hydrolyzed by mixing 20 mg of liquid KH-560 with 180 mg of deionized water and 1.8 mL of anhydrous ethanol. Acetic acid was added dropwise to adjust the pH to 4–5, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min to complete hydrolysis. This hydrolyzed KH-560 solution was then added dropwise to the composite dispersion, followed by stirring at 60 °C for 6 h. Finally, the product was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with ethanol, and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for subsequent use.

Synthesis of Magnetic Microcapsules

Phase-change microcapsules with an n-eicosane core and a CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 composite shell were synthesized via a precipitation reaction using the Pickering emulsion-template method. Briefly, 5.65 g of n-eicosane and 0.61 g of SDBS were dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water and stirred at 55 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 1.47 g of CaCl2 (dissolved in 80 mL of water) was added dropwise to the mixture over 3 h while maintaining the temperature at 55 °C. After the formation of the oil-in-water emulsion, 1.07 g of Na2CO3 in 50 mL of deionized water was added dropwise, and stirring was continued at 55 °C for 4 h. In parallel, 1 g of CNTs-Fe3O4 was dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water by sonication for 1 h. Aliquots of 10, 15, and 20 mL of this dispersion were then introduced into the emulsion and stirred for 2 h to obtain samples with varying CNTs-Fe3O4 content. Finally, the products were collected by centrifugation and dried in an oven at 70 °C for 6 h.

Results and Discussion

Synthetic Strategy and Microstructural Analysis

Figure illustrates the synthesis mechanism of the n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 composite microcapsules. Drawing upon previous studies of CaCO3-based microcapsules, an aqueous phase is employed as the continuous phase to construct an oil-in-water emulsion system for interfacial self-assembly and precipitation. In this system, the rapid reaction between Ca2+ and CO3 2– at the oil–water interface , leads to the formation of a loose and irregular CaCO3 shell encapsulating the phase change material (PCM) core. , Driven by the Pickering stabilization effect and physicochemical adsorption, the CNT-Fe3O4 nanoparticles are uniformly anchored onto the porous CaCO3 surface, thereby creating a dense hybrid shell.

Figures and present SEM images of the CaCO3-based phase-change microcapsules with and without the CNTs-Fe3O4 composites. The magnetic samples were prepared with four different CNTs-Fe3O4 loadings to evaluate the influence of composite content on shell morphology. As shown in Figures a and a, the pure CaCO3 microcapsules without CNTs-Fe3O4 exhibit smooth surfaces and a uniform spherical morphology, as further evidenced by several fractured particles that reveal the internal phase change core. This confirms the formation of CaCO3-based core–shell microcapsules via interfacial precipitation. Upon incorporation of CNTs-Fe3O4, the microcapsules (Figure b–d) maintain their spherical shape and structural integrity, indicating that the introduction of the composite does not disrupt shell formation. The CaCO3 shell effectively encapsulates the n-eicosane PCM core, preventing leakage during the solid–liquid phase transition associated with heat absorption and release. However, with an increasing CNTs-Fe3O4 content, the surface morphology becomes progressively rougher and more textured. This suggests that a larger quantity of CNTs-Fe3O4 nanoparticles adheres to the CaCO3 surface through interfacial adsorption and physical anchoring. This morphological evolution is more distinctly visualized in Figure b–d, where numerous CNTs form interconnected conductive networks across the shell surface.

The size distribution of the microcapsule samples was determined via SEM image analysis, as shown in Figure S2. All microcapsule samples exhibited high monodispersity with relatively narrow particle size distributions. The average particle size of n-eicosane@CaCO3 was approximately 1.62 μm. As the CNTs-Fe3O4 content increased to 3%, the average particle size of the microcapsules slightly increased to 2.09 μm, accompanied by a slight broadening of the size distribution range. This phenomenon is mainly attributed to the successful anchoring of the CNTs-Fe3O4 nanoparticles onto the surface of the CaCO3 shell, resulting in a slight increase in the shell thickness of the microcapsules. Overall, all samples maintained their intact spherical morphology and narrow particle size distributions, indicating that the introduction of the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite did not disrupt the structural integrity of the microcapsules.

Phase and Chemical Composition Analysis

The magnetic phase-change microcapsules and their constituents were analyzed using XRD, FTIR spectroscopy, XPS, and EDX. Figure a presents comparative Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3@n-eicosane microcapsules, n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules, and n-eicosane. As depicted in Figure a, a set of absorption peaks associated with the n-eicosane core and the CaCO3 shell was observed in the FTIR spectra of all microcapsule samples. Additionally, a characteristic absorption band for the Fe–O bond stretching vibration was identified at 594 cm–1, confirming that the incorporation of the CNT-Fe3O4 composite did not disrupt the microcapsule structure. This result indicates the successful encapsulation of the n-eicosane core by the CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 shell. The absorption bands of the CNTs in the magnetic microcapsule samples are not prominent, owing to the intrinsically weak infrared vibration signals of the CNTs.

(a) FT-IR patterns and (b) XRD spectra of pure n-eicosane, CaCO3, n-eicosane@CaCO3 microcapsules, and n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 magnetic microcapsules.

Figure b shows a comparison of the XRD patterns of the n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules and the CaCO3@n-eicosane microcapsules. The diffraction peaks corresponding to n-eicosane, CaCO3, and CNTs-Fe3O4 can all be identified in the patterns. Combined with the XRD results from Figure S3 (not shown here), this further indicates that the crystallinity of n-eicosane remains unaffected after encapsulation within the calcium carbonate and CNTs-Fe3O4 shell. This suggests that the n-eicosane core material within the microcapsules maintains its phase-change energy storage capability. To investigate the influence of the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite on the crystal structure of the shell, we compared the XRD patterns of samples doped with 1%, 2%, and 3% CNTs-Fe3O4 (Figure c). By referencing the standard card for calcium carbonate (PDF no. 83-0578), it can be observed that all PCM microcapsule samples exhibit diffraction peaks corresponding to calcite-type CaCO3, indicating that the introduction of the CNTs-Fe3O4 does not alter the crystal structure of the CaCO3 shell.

Furthermore, the CNTs-Fe3O4 magnetic additive exhibits a set of diffraction peaks at 2θ = 35.56°, 43.19°, and 62.82°, which correspond to the (311), (400), and (440) crystal planes of Fe3O4, respectively, as confirmed by the standard PDF card. This verifies the formation of Fe3O4 nanoparticles with an inverse spinel structure on the CNT surface. The weak diffraction peak at 26.16° is attributed to the (002) crystal plane of the CNTs. It is worth noting that in Figure b, the sample with 1% CNTs-Fe3O4 addition exhibits only weak diffraction signals, whereas increasing the doping amount to 3% significantly enhances the corresponding diffraction peak intensity. This demonstrates that the diffraction signal intensity is positively correlated with the amount of CNTs-Fe3O4 added, further confirming the presence of crystalline CNTs-Fe3O4 in the samples. This structural characteristic endows the material with a strong solar absorption capacity, thereby improving the photothermal conversion performance of the material.

Owing to the relatively low loading of CNTs-Fe3O4 in the magnetic microcapsules, no distinct diffraction or absorption peaks corresponding to Fe3O4 were detected in the XRD and FTIR analyses. To further verify the successful incorporation and spatial distribution of the magnetic and carbonaceous components, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were performed.

As shown in Figure a, the EDX elemental mapping of the pristine n-eicosane@CaCO3 microcapsules reveals a uniform distribution of Ca and O within well-defined spherical structures, which is consistent with the formation of pure CaCO3 shells. In contrast, Figure b,c presents the EDX mappings of the 2% and 3% n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules, respectively. Fe, Ca, and O elements are homogeneously distributed throughout the shell region. The presence of Fe confirms the successful deposition of CNTs-Fe3O4 nanoparticles onto the CaCO3 surface, indicating the formation of a CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 composite shell with a uniform elemental distribution.

EDX patterns and elemental mapping images of the (a) n-eicosane@CaCO3 sample, (b) 2% n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 sample, and (c) 3% n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 sample.

The surface chemical composition and bonding states were further investigated by XPS. The wide-scan and high-resolution spectra in Figure a–e reveal the presence of four characteristic elementsCa, C, O, and Feproviding direct evidence for the incorporation of Fe into the microcapsule shell. Figure a shows the high-resolution XPS core-level spectra and their peak fitting results for the phase-change microcapsules. The C 1s spectrum (Figure b) can be deconvoluted into three peaks corresponding to the CO3 2– (289.5 eV), C–O (285.9 eV), and C–C (284.8 eV) bonds. The O 1s spectrum (Figure c) displays three peaks at 532.0, 531.6, and 530.8 eV, which are attributed to O–C, CO3 2–, and O–Fe bonds, respectively. The Ca 2p spectrum (Figure e) shows two characteristic peaks at binding energies of 350.6 and 347.1 eV. Furthermore, the high-resolution Fe 2p spectrum (Figure d) can be fitted with two spin–orbit doublets, Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2, further confirming that the chemical state of Fe is consistent with the presence of CNTs-Fe3O4. Together, the EDX and XPS analyses conclusively demonstrate that the Fe3O4 nanoparticles and CNTs are successfully integrated into the CaCO3 shell, forming a hybrid inorganic-carbon composite shell that provides both magnetic and conductive functionalities to the phase-change microcapsules.

Thermal Degradation and Latent-Heat-Storage/Release Behaviors

The thermal degradation behavior of the n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 composite microcapsules was characterized by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), using pure n-eicosane and CaCO3@n-eicosane microcapsules as controls. The resulting TGA and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) curves are shown in Figure a,b, respectively. Pure n-eicosane exhibits a single-step degradation profile within the range of 160–300 °C, corresponding to the volatilization and decomposition of the organic phase. Virtually no residue remains after decomposition, indicating its inherently low thermal stability. However, within the temperature range of 0–1000 °C, the prepared n-eicosane microcapsules with a CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 hierarchical shell exhibited a distinct three-stage thermal decomposition behavior. Clearly, during the initial stage from 0 to 300 °C, the primary weight loss is attributed to the evaporation of the encapsulated n-eicosane core. This is followed by a minor second-stage weight loss within the 300–550 °C range, resulting from the premature oxidation of the CNTs catalyzed by Fe3O4, along with the decomposition of the residual SDBS surfactant. In the high-temperature region from 550 to 1000 °C, the deep oxidative decomposition of the CaCO3 shell and residual substances occurs under the catalysis of Fe3O4. This additional weight loss in the final stage leads to the reduction in residual mass, indicating that Fe3O4 has a catalytic effect on the decomposition of CaCO3.

(a)­TGA and (b) DTG thermograms of pure n-eicosane and composite microcapsule samples.

Upon the incorporation of the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite, the thermal degradation pathway undergoes a significant change. The residual masses for the samples with 1%, 2%, and 3% CNTs-Fe3O4 are approximately 45%, 35%, and 25%, respectively, exhibiting a decreasing trend with increasing additive content. This reduction is primarily attributed to the catalytic activity of Fe3O4, which promotes the oxidative cleavage of the organic components into volatile products such as CO2 and H2O. Although the CNTs inherently possess high thermal stability, their partial oxidation under the catalysis of Fe3O4 contributes to additional gas evolution and mass loss. The synergistic interaction between the CNTs and the Fe3O4 not only alters the degradation mechanism but also improves the overall thermal endurance of the microcapsules. As can be seen in Figure b, the temperatures corresponding to the maximum decomposition rates for the pure n-eicosane, the CaCO3 microcapsules, and the CNTs-Fe3O4@CaCO3 microcapsules with 1–3% CNTs-Fe3O4 are 228.4 °C, 646.3 °C, 720.8 °C, 729.8 °C, and 748.1 °C, respectively. This demonstrates that the microencapsulation technology employed in this study, which encapsulates n-eicosane within a CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 shell, effectively retards the volatilization of the n-eicosane core material at elevated temperatures and enhances its thermal stability, thereby expanding the usable temperature range for CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3-based phase-change thermal energy storage materials.

Thermal Conductivity of the Composite MicroPCMs

Thermal conductivity is an important thermal property index of phase-change materials, which can directly reflect the heat storage and release rates. Figure c shows the thermal conductivities of n-eicosane, CaCO3 microcapsules, and n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 composite microcapsules with different shell-to-core ratios. Compared with that of the CaCO3@n-eicosane microcapsules, the thermal conductivity of the 1%–3% n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 composite microcapsules increased by 16.1%, 17.3%, and 22.2%, respectively. This enhancement can be attributed to the combined contributions of the components within the CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 shell during the encapsulation of n-eicosane: the CNTs provide the primary heat transfer capability, Fe3O4 improves their dispersion and interfacial heat transfer, and the CaCO3 matrix acts as a supporting framework that integrates these components and encapsulates the core. Through the synergistic action of these components, an efficient three-dimensional thermal conduction network is constructed within the microcapsule shell, thereby overcoming the key bottleneck of the low thermal conductivity inherent in conventional phase-change microcapsules.

Phase-Change Behaviors and Thermal Cycle Stability

The thermal energy storage and release performance are crucial criteria for evaluating the potential of phase-change materials in practical applications. To elucidate the phase transition behavior and thermal cycling stability of the n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 phase-change microcapsules with an n-eicosane core, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis was conducted. Among the various performance parameters for microencapsulated phase-change thermal storage materials, the phase change enthalpy, encapsulation efficiency, and thermal conductivity are particularly critical. The encapsulation efficiency and energy storage efficiency of these microcapsules can be calculated using the following equations, respectively 1 ER=ΔHm.microcapsuleΔHm.pcm×100% 2 EE=ΔHm.microcapsule+ΔHc.microcapsuleΔHm.pcm+ΔHc.pcm×100%

Pure n-eicosane exhibits dual exothermic peaks during crystallization, a feature that is preserved in the microencapsulated samples, indicating that microencapsulation within a CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 composite shell does not alter the crystallization behavior of the core material. The appearance of this dual-peak crystallization profile is attributed to the formation of a metastable rotator phase during the crystallization of n-eicosane, resulting from conformationally disordered molecules and intrachain defects. This mechanism is consistent with previous reports in the literature. , It is noteworthy that both the pure n-eicosane and the microencapsulated samples display only a single endothermic peak during melting, and their phase transition temperatures (melting temperature, T m, and crystallization temperature, T c) are highly comparable. This further confirms that the microencapsulation process and the incorporation of the composite shell do not significantly affect the phase-transition behavior of the core material.

The latent heats of phase change (ΔH m and ΔH c) of pure n-eicosane are 240.8 J/g and 240.6 J/g, respectively. The melting and crystallization enthalpies of the 1–3% magnetic microcapsules are listed in Table . With the addition of the 3% CNTs-Fe3O4 composite, the prepared composite microcapsules exhibit phase change latent heats (ΔH m and ΔH c) of 131.2 J/g and 125.6 J/g, respectively, fulfilling the requirements for photothermal energy storage in efficient solar energy utilization. The slightly reduced T m values in the microcapsules indicate that the inorganic shell facilitates enhanced heat transfer via its high specific surface area, thereby promoting earlier melting.

The thermal reliability of the n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules was evaluated over 400 consecutive DSC thermal cycles (Figure d). The melting and crystallization curves from the first and 400th cycles remain highly consistent, demonstrating excellent reversibility and a stable latent heat storage capacity throughout the repeated phase transitions. Both the phase change temperatures and enthalpies exhibited only minor variations (with the enthalpy fluctuation being a mere ±0.85 J·g–1), as shown in Figure S4, confirming that the incorporation of CNTs-Fe3O4 composite materials does not adversely affect the thermal cycling stability.

Thermal Conditioning

To evaluate the thermal regulation performance of the phase-change microcapsules, this study employed infrared thermal imaging to examine the phase transition behavior of an optimal composite sample, namely, the 3% n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules (designated as S3). The samples were placed on a constant-temperature hot stage, and a series of thermal images were captured to record surface temperature evolution. For comparison, three reference samples were used: S0 (pure n-eicosane), S1 (pure CaCO3 microspheres), and S2 (CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microspheres without the phase-change material).

Figure a presents the obtained infrared thermal images and the corresponding temperature–time curves. During heating, sample S0 (pure n-eicosane) rapidly changes color from dark blue to cyan, after which the color transition ceases, reaching a maximum temperature of only 42.6 °C. During cooling, this color change trend reverses. Sample S1 (pure CaCO3) shifts from dark blue to cyan during heating and from cyan back to dark blue during cooling. This behavior indicates that S1 heats up rapidly until it reaches the temperature of the hot stage, followed by equally rapid cooling to room temperature. The thermal images of sample S2 (CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microspheres) show a rapid color transition from dark blue to cyan and then to red during heating. The reverse color evolution is observed during cooling. This phenomenon demonstrates the rapid thermal response of the CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microspheres.

(a) Representative infrared thermographic images of (S0) pure n-eicosane, (S1) CaCO3, (S3) CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 spheres, and (S4) 3 wt % n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules recorded during heating and cooling processes; (b) heating and (c) cooling time for CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 composite microspheres and phase-change microcapsules.

In contrast, the n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules (S3) exhibited a markedly slower and more gradual color transition. This delayed temperature increase indicates that the phase-change core absorbed a significant amount of latent heat during melting, thereby suppressing the temperature rise of the shell and surface. During cooling, the crystallization of the core gradually released the stored latent heat, thereby maintaining a higher surface temperature over a prolonged period. This thermal buffering capability is a hallmark of efficient phase-change energy storage systems.

The corresponding temperature–time profiles (Figure b,c) further corroborate this thermal buffering effect. Two distinct temperature plateaus are observed in the microencapsulated sample: a melting plateau between 49.8 and 51.2 °C during heating, and a crystallization plateau from 42.3 to 39.3 °C during cooling. These plateaus correspond to the endothermic melting and exothermic crystallization of the n-eicosane core, respectively. In contrast, the control samples (S1 and S2) exhibit no such plateaus, as their temperature changes are governed solely by sensible heat, without any contribution from latent heat storage or release.

Photothermal Conversion

The light absorption capacity of the n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules was investigated by using a UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer to assess their solar photothermal conversion potential, as shown in Figure a. Pure n-eicosane exhibits relatively poor light absorption, reaching a maximum of only 13.4%. In contrast, the magnetic microcapsule samples with 2% and 3% CNTs-Fe3O4 loading demonstrate significantly enhanced performance, achieving maximum absorption rates of up to 95% across a broad wavelength range from 200 to 2500 nm. These results confirm the superior light absorption capability of the n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 magnetic microcapsules.

For comparison, n-eicosane, n-eicosane@CaCO3 microcapsules, and n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 magnetic microcapsules with 1%, 2% and 3% loading were tested under identical conditions using the experimental setup shown in Figure S1A; a xenon arc lamp was employed as a simulated sunlight source with an irradiance intensity of 1000 mW/cm2 to investigate the photothermal energy conversion and storage capabilities of the magnetic composite microcapsules and the calcium carbonate microcapsules. Both n-eicosane and the composite microcapsule samples exhibited a gradual temperature increase with increasing exposure time. Upon simulated solar irradiation, their temperatures rose and subsequently decreased after the light source was removed. The photothermal conversion curves are shown in Figure b. Under irradiation at an intensity of 1000 mW/cm2 for 522 s, the temperature of pure n-eicosane increased by only 8.8 °C, confirming that its intrinsic photothermal conversion capability is negligible. In contrast, under the same conditions, the temperature of the 3% n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 magnetic microcapsules rose to 59.53 °C. Compared to the n-eicosane and n-eicosane@CaCO3 microcapsules, the composite microcapsules exhibited a superior temperature ramp rate, indicating a higher solar photothermal conversion efficiency. This result is attributed to the incorporation of the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite, which absorbs sunlight more effectively than pure n-eicosane and the CaCO3 shell to convert it into thermal energy. This is attributed to the Fe3O4 nanoparticles acting as solar radiation absorbers with complementary spectral absorption characteristics, while the CNTs possess a broader light absorption range and a superior photothermal conversion capability. The strong light absorption and conversion characteristics of the CNTs, combined with the full-spectrum absorption properties of the Fe3O4 nanoparticles, can significantly enhance the overall solar absorption performance and photothermal conversion efficiency of the phase-change material. Consequently, the magnetic composite microcapsules exhibit superior capability in converting sunlight into thermal energy. Magnetic Analysis

The magnetic properties of n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 phase-change microcapsules with varying mass fractions were characterized at room temperature, and the resulting hysteresis loops are depicted in Figure f. The saturation magnetization values were measured to be 1.83 emu/g, 3.88 emu/g, and 5.47 emu/g for the microcapsules with 1%, 2%, and 3% CNTs-Fe3O4, respectively. As shown in the digital photograph in Figure b, when the magnetic microcapsules were dispersed in deionized water and a magnet was placed on the right side of the beaker, the sample was rapidly attracted to the magnet. These results confirm that the incorporation of the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite endows the microcapsules with superparamagnetic properties and that their magnetic response can be systematically enhanced by increasing the concentration of the Fe3O4 nanoparticles in the Pickering emulsion system.

This indicates that the magnetic microcapsules prepared in this study possess strong magnetothermal conversion capability. Notably, the alternating magnetic field produces an accelerating effect on the solar photothermal energy conversion and storage process. The synergistic combination of magnetic field excitation and light irradiation offers an innovative solution to the drawbacks of low thermal utilization efficiency and the single functionality of conventional phase-change materials, thereby providing valuable research insights for the development of multifunctional phase-change thermal storage microcapsule systems.

Conclusions

In this study, n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 microcapsules with enhanced photothermal conversion and thermal energy storage capabilities were successfully fabricated via a Pickering emulsion-templating method coupled with in situ interfacial precipitation. The resulting microcapsules feature a well-defined core–shell structure, a uniform spherical morphology, and a narrow size distribution, which collectively ensure structural integrity and reproducibility. All samples exhibited superparamagnetic behavior, confirming the successful incorporation of CNTs-Fe3O4 into the composite shell.

Under optimized conditions, the microcapsules achieved a high latent heat storage capacity of 131.2 J/g, with an encapsulation efficiency exceeding 54.5%. The incorporation of the CNTs-Fe3O4 composite not only improved the emulsion stability during synthesis but also improved the thermal conductivity and broadband light absorption of the system, thereby leading to a substantial increase in the solar photothermal conversion efficiency and the overall energy storage performance. Furthermore, the hybrid CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 shell imparted excellent thermal stability and cycling durability to the microcapsules, maintaining consistent phase-change behavior and latent heat capacity over prolonged thermal cycling. This robust performance ensures reliable long-term thermal energy storage and release across a wide operating temperature range. As shown in Table , the thermal performance enhancement of the microcapsules developed in this study is favorably compared with that of similar microcapsules from previous studies. , In summary, this work presents an effective strategy for constructing multifunctional phase-change microcapsules that integrate magnetic responsiveness, efficient photothermal conversion, and high-capacity thermal energy storage. The developed n-eicosane@CNTs-Fe3O4/CaCO3 system constitutes a promising platform for advanced applications in solar thermal harvesting, intelligent thermal management, and next-generation energy-storage technologies.
