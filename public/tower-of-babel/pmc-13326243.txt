Comparison of the accuracy and efficiency of robot-assisted implant placement using trephine versus conventional serial drilling: an in vitro study
Authors: Ning Zhu, Qinghua Liu, Yuchen Chang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to compare the accuracy and efficiency of implant-site preparation and placement between conventional serial drilling and trephine drilling, with assistance from an implant robot, on varying bone surface inclinations.MethodsA total of 80 implants were placed in a standard polyurethane model using the two drilling methods (conventional serial drilling and trephine drilling) with four different bone surface inclinations (0°, 15°, 30°, 45°), resulting in eight sub-groups. The implant robot was utilized for implant-site preparation and placement. The global coronal deviation, global apical deviation, angular deviation, and implant placement time were recorded as outcome measures.ResultsIn line with the experimental objectives, implants were placed using conventional serial drilling and trephine drilling techniques under conditions of different bone surface inclinations. While no significant difference was observed in implant accuracy, the trephine drilling method showed significantly higher implant efficiency.ConclusionsUnder the present in vitro conditions, implant robot-assisted trephine drilling for implant-site preparation did not compromise implant precision compared with conventional serial drilling and improved implant efficiency. In addition, it may offer the practical advantage of harvesting autogenous bone material.Keywords: Dental implant, Trephine drill, Dental implant robotic system, Accuracy, Efficiency

Background

Tooth loss is frequently associated with alveolar bone defects. A systematic review found that within six months of tooth loss, horizontal ridge resorption ranges from 29 to 63%, while vertical ridge resorption is between 11 and 22% [1], necessitating bone augmentation during dental implant surgery [2, 3]. Among the different materials used for bone augmentation, the gold standard is autogenous bone due to its osteogenic, osteoinductive, and osteoconductive properties [4, 5].

The use of a trephine drill, a drill bur commonly employed clinically for bone extraction and biopsy [6], to harvest autogenous bone from the implant site avoids the need for an additional donor site, while also providing an intact cylindrical bone graft for augmentation [7, 8]. Furthermore, a series of recent in vivo [9] and in vitro studies [10, 11] have reported additional benefits associated with the use of trephines for implant-site preparation. Compared to the conventional serial drilling technique, trephine requires fewer drills and a simplified process, with negligible differences in heat generation and subsequent bone regeneration [11].

However, compared to the conventional sequential drilling protocol, the exclusive use of a trephine drill for implant-site preparation lacks the guidance provided by a pilot drill. The absence of initial guidance predisposes the drill to slip on the bone surface, reducing the accuracy of the implant. This problem is exacerbated when using the trephine drill on more oblique bone surfaces, as the drill tends to make initial contact with the bone surface on one side of the annular cutting edge before other areas, which results in the drill generating a force parallel to the bone surface, thereby causing lateral sliding on the bone surface. There have been numerous reports of concern about reduced precision when using drills in these challenging topographies [9, 12, 13]. Consequently, this limitation restricts the broader application of trephine drills in implant surgery, particularly during freehand surgical procedures or when utilized by clinicians with limited experience.

To date, robotic computer-assisted implant surgery (r-CAIS) has gained widespread use in clinical dental implant surgeries. Through integration of dynamic navigation with real-time feedback and precise robotic arm control [14, 15], surgical implant robots provide superior positional feedback capabilities [16] and enable real-time adjustments during surgical procedures [17]. Thus, compared to freehand placement, static computer-assisted implant surgery (s-CAIS) and dynamic computer-assisted implant surgery (d-CAIS), robotic systems can achieve greater precision in implant placement [18–22]. Their refined control of the drill and adjustment functionality can mitigate the inherent tendency of trephine drills to slide along the bone surface interface during implant-site preparation. Nevertheless, the robotic system requires relocation after each drill change, which significantly lengthens the overall duration of surgery [18, 23–25] and consequently increases patient discomfort. The use of trephine drills for implant-site preparation can reduce the number of required drills, thereby streamlining the procedure and partially offsetting the extended operative duration associated with surgical implant robotics.

Thus, robotic-assisted implant-site preparation using trephine drills has the potential to mitigate the limitations of both techniques. The robotic-assisted approach can enhance procedural efficiency without compromising the accuracy of implant placement, while simultaneously acquiring intact cylindrical autogenous bone grafts for augmentation. However, there has been limited systematic investigation of this technique, with only a single case report by Li et al. to date [9]. The reliability and accuracy of this technique require validation through standardized models and larger sample sizes. To our knowledge, no systematic studies have addressed these issues.

This study compares the accuracy and efficiency of r-CAIS-assisted conventional serial drilling and r-CAIS-assisted trephine in the preparation of implant sites under standardized in vitro model conditions, to determine whether r-CAIS-assisted trephine drills can achieve precision and procedural efficiency comparable to those of traditional serial drilling while simultaneously harvesting autogenous bone for augmentation purposes.

The null hypothesis was that there is no difference in the accuracy and efficiency of implant placement between preparation using conventional serial drilling and trephine drilling on varying bone surface inclinations.

Methods

The Remebot implant robot (Baihui Weikang Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) utilized during surgery comprises a robotic arm, an optical tracking system, a system integration cart, and surgical navigation software. A total of 80 placement tasks were conducted. Each task involved the robotic arm guiding the drill to prepare the implant-site and place an implant (3.3 × 10 mm Bone Level Tapered Roxolid SLA, Straumann) in the polyurethane model. The implant-site preparation method required categorizing the tasks into two groups: the serial drilling and trephine groups. The serial drilling group used conventional sequential drills (BLT drill set for Ø3.3 mm implant, Straumann), consisting of five drills and an implant: a 2.0 mm triangular drill, 2.2- and 2.8-mm pilot drills, a 3.3 mm profile drill, a 3.3 mm tap drill, and a 3.3 × 10 mm implant (Fig. 1a). The trephine group used three drills and an implant: a trephine with an outer diameter of 3.0 mm (Trepane229 020, Hager & Meisinger), a 3.3 mm profile drill, a 3.3 mm tap drill, and a 3.3 × 10 mm implant (Fig. 1b). Each group was further subdivided into four sub-groups based on the angle between the implant and the bone surface (0°, 15°, 30°, 45°) (Fig. 1c, d, e, f), with each sub-group completing 10 placement tasks.

In this study, solid rigid polyurethane (#1522–05; Sawbones, Pacific Research Lab, Vashon, Washington, United States) with a density of 40 PCF (0.64 g/cc) was used to construct an in vitro standard model. All polyurethane blocks used in this experiment were manufactured in the same batch by the same supplier and exhibited identical density and material properties. Ten solid rigid polyurethane blocks (9 × 6.5 × 4 cm3) were used, each serving as a block. On each block, eight implant sites were preplanned with a minimum inter-site distance of 5 mm and a minimum distance of 5 mm from the block edges, and were spatially labeled 1 to 8 in a fixed order. The eight treatment combinations (two drilling methods × four surface inclinations) were assigned to the eight sites on each block using a random permutation of the numbers 1 to 8 generated with statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics, v27.0, IBM Corp.). Specifically, a list L of the eight treatment labels (Serial-0°, Serial-15°, Serial-30°, Serial-45°, Trephine-0°, Trephine-15°, Trephine-30°, Trephine-45°) was fixed, and a random permutation R1 = (r1, r2, …, r8) was used to assign the i-th label in L to the site numbered ri. A second independent random permutation R2 = (s1, s2, …, s8) determined the implantation sequence on each block, with the site numbered s1 operated first and site s8 last. This process was repeated independently for all ten polyurethane blocks, ensuring that each block contained all eight treatment combinations exactly once and that the execution order was randomized across sites and methods.

The operational process comprised three main phases: preoperative preparation, implantation procedure, and accuracy verification (Fig. 2).

Workflow of the experiment

Preoperative preparation

A solid rigid polyurethane cubic block with a density of 40 PCF measuring 9 × 6.5 × 4 cm3 was prepared. A trapezoid-shaped groove was designed on one side of the cubic block (Fig. 3a) to secure the markers for the surgical robot. Each marker was equipped with an identification plate black and white grids (Fig. 3b) to assist the surgical robot with intraoperative real-time spatial positioning. Additionally, seven ceramic beads were embedded in the preset groove using adhesive (Baihui Weikang Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) to record their specific positions. This allowed for the superimposition of preoperative and postoperative images using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), enabling accurate determination of implant placement error. The assembled standard model (Fig. 3c) was registered using an optical tracking system to capture the relative positions of the identification plate and ceramic beads. The collected data were saved as a model registration file. All standard models were scanned using CBCT (NewTom GiANO, Verona, Italy) with a voxel size of 0.2 mm. The data were saved for implant planning in digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format. The model registration file and CBCT scan data were imported into the Remebot system. The spatial implant positions were planned on three-dimensional (3D) images, ensuring a minimum implant spacing of 5 mm. The angles between the planned implant long axes and the normal to model surface were set to 0°, 15°, 30°, and 45° to simulate implant placement on bone surfaces with varying inclinations. The implant positions planned within the Remebot system were defined as the reference standard for the assessment of postoperative implant placement accuracy.

Implantation procedure

The standard model was positioned beneath the implant robot’s camera, with the angle between the identification plate and the camera adjusted to maintain parallel alignment. The camera tracked the identification plate to determine the model’s position in 3D space, completing the model loading process. The robotic arm was then moved above the model, and its coordinated information was recorded using the navigation software to complete the robotic arm registration. Following the experimental design, the required drills were loaded into the Remebot system. The robotic arm was positioned in the surgical area, and automatic calibration was completed. A well-trained operator was responsible for all preoperative preparations, model positioning, robotic arm registration, and intraoperative drill exchanges. Under the operator’s supervision, the implant robot autonomously performed the implant-site preparation and placement (Fig. 4a, b, c). To minimize heat generation during osteotomy while approximating clinical conditions, standardized thermal control measures were applied throughout all implant site preparations. Drilling speeds were strictly set according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In the serial drilling group, the 2.0 mm triangular drill and the 2.2 mm and 2.8 mm pilot drills were operated at 800 rpm, the 3.3 mm profile drill at 300 rpm, and the 3.3 mm tap drill at 15 rpm. In the trephine group, the 3.0 mm trephine drill was operated at 800 rpm, followed by the same profile and tap drill speeds. Continuous external irrigation with pre-cooled sterile saline (5 °C) was delivered throughout the drilling process. To further reduce thermal accumulation and facilitate debris clearance, the robotic system was set to operate in an intermittent drilling mode for the 2.0 mm triangular drill, the 2.2 mm and 2.8 mm pilot drills, and the 3.0 mm trephine drill, executing a controlled advance of 3 mm followed by a 1 mm retraction in a stepwise motion. Drilling force was not subjectively controlled by the operator but was automatically regulated by the robotic system based on real-time mechanical feedback from the robotic arm, ensuring a standardized and consistent drilling process across all procedures. Implant insertion torque was recorded automatically by the implant motor during final implant placement, and all implants achieved a final insertion torque of greater than 35 N·cm.

A designated person used a digital stopwatch to record the total time from the first drill’s contact with the model to the completion of implant placement.

Accuracy verification

To verify implant accuracy, the standard model with the placed implants (Fig. 5a) was scanned by CBCT under the same preoperative conditions, generating corresponding DICOM format files. The postoperative images were imported into the Remebot system, and registration was performed using the seven ceramic beads on the model to superimpose the preoperative and postoperative images (Fig. 5b, c). Remebot then proceeded to calculate seven positional deviations between the actual and planned implant positions (Fig. 5d). Three key variables were evaluated to assess implant placement accuracy: (1) global coronal deviation, defined as the three-dimensional linear distance between the center of the implant platform in the postoperative and planned positions; (2) global apical deviation, defined as the three-dimensional linear distance between the implant apex in the postoperative and planned positions; and (3) angular deviation, defined as the spatial angle between the long axes of the postoperative and planned implants. Linear deviations were measured in millimeters, and angular deviation was measured in degrees. These parameters are widely recognized as core metrics in studies evaluating implant placement accuracy [22, 26–28].

Verification of implant accuracy. a The standard model with the implants in position. b, c Superimposition of the preoperative and postoperative CBCT images, d Deviations between the implant’s planned (green) and actual (red) positions (Coronal: ① global coronal deviation, ② lateral coronal deviation, ③ vertical coronal deviation; Apical: ④ global apical deviation, ⑤ lateral apical deviation, ⑥ vertical apical deviation; Axial: ⑦ angular deviation)

Regarding the reliability of the measurements, the accuracy verification process in this experiment was fully automated using the Remebot system to ensure objectivity. The reliability of this system for automated implant accuracy assessment has been corroborated by multiple previous studies [26–28]. Furthermore, the entire process involved no manual intervention, manual landmark identification, or subjective judgment; consequently, no intra-observer or inter-observer measurement variability was introduced, rendering repeated reliability test unnecessary.

The experimental data was imported into statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics, v27.0, IBM Corp) for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including the mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and 95% confidence interval, were calculated for global coronal, apical, and angular deviations across the eight sub-groups. The significance level was set at 0.05, and the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate the variable normality in each group. An independent samples t-test was conducted for variables following a normal distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test was used for non-normally distributed variables to determine significant differences in implant accuracy between the serial drilling and trephine groups under different bone surface inclinations.

Results

In line with the experimental objectives, implants were placed using the conventional serial drilling and trephine drilling techniques under different bone surface inclinations (0°, 15°, 30°, 45°). Ten implants were placed for each method under each inclination, resulting in eight sub-groups and 80 implants in total. All implants achieved a placement torque greater than 35 N·cm.

The statistical results of global coronal deviation, global apical deviation, and angular deviation of each sub-group are presented in Tables 1, 2, 3, and 4 and Fig. 6. Overall, the mean deviations across all subgroups ranged from 0.24 mm to 0.50 mm for global coronal deviation, 0.27 mm to 0.59 mm for global apical deviation, and 0.39° to 0.71° for angular deviation, indicating clinically acceptable accuracy for all implant placements.

Discussion

Using an in vitro standard model and with the assistance of a dental implant robot, this study evaluated the effects of two implant-site preparation methods—conventional serial drilling and trephine drilling—on implant placement accuracy and efficiency under different bone surface inclinations. The results showed no significant differences in implant accuracy between the two methods. However, due to the reduced number of drills required for trephine drilling, its preparation time was significantly shorter than that of conventional serial drilling.

These findings provide substantive evidence supporting our original conjecture. The r-CAIS technique offers real-time intraoperative feedback, counteracting the tendency of trephines to slip on steep bony surfaces. Conversely, utilizing trephine drills for implant-site preparation reduces the number of drills required during surgery, thus eliminating the additional operative time associated with relocation of the robotic system following drill changes. Consequently, the integration of robot-assisted trephine drilling circumvents the limitations associated with the individual techniques, thereby significantly improving the efficiency of implantation without compromising its accuracy and also providing autogenous bone for bone augmentation.

In this study, the comparison deviation of implants placed using the trephine drill for implant-site preparation showed a mean (± SD) global coronal deviation of 0.40 ± 0.14 mm, a mean (± SD) global apical deviation of 0.41 ± 0.15 mm and a mean (± SD) angular deviation of 0.61 ± 0.23°. To our knowledge, prior to this study, only one case report by Li et al. [9] had reported the accuracy outcomes of robot-assisted implant-site preparation using a trephine drill. Their global coronal and apical deviation were close to those observed in this study, while the angular deviation was slightly higher. The lower angular deviation in this study may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the case report by Li et al. included only one clinical case, rendering their results statistically insignificant. Secondly, compared to the clinical environment in the study by Li et al., the standard model in this study had a simpler structure, avoiding complications such as intraoral fluids, blood, head positioning, limited mouth opening, and visual obstructions [20], thereby creating a more controlled environment for the implant robot. Additionally, Chen et al. [29] highlighted that density differences between the buccal and palatal bones, which were hard to simulate in homogeneous in vitro models, can cause slippage of the robotic arm of the implant robot during implant-site preparation and increase errors.

In the conventional serial drilling group of this study, the comparison deviation of implants showed a mean (± SD) global coronal deviation of 0.40 ± 0.17 mm, a mean (± SD) global apical deviation of 0.47 ± 0.17 mm and a mean (± SD) angular deviation of 0.55 ± 0.28°. This result is slightly higher than the accuracy reported in two other in vitro studies [29, 30] that also utilized robot-assisted conventional serial drilling techniques. This discrepancy in accuracy is likely attributable to differences in the materials, specifications of the implants, and the types of robotic system used, as illustrated in Table 7. In this study, we utilized polyurethane blocks of 40 PCF density. Polyurethane, approved by the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) as a standard material for the testing of instruments and oral implants, has been widely used in recent in vitro studies of oral implants [31–33]. Polyurethane provides standardized bone models with consistent density and elastic modulus for testing [34]. In contrast, the two in vitro studies described above employed materials with higher densities than those used in the present study, which may explain the improved accuracy observed in our results [30, 35]. Furthermore, the 3.3 × 10 mm implant used in this study is relatively small compared to those used in the other two studies. While the influence of implant size on placement accuracy remains a subject of debate [29, 36], for conventional serial drilling protocols, the use of a smaller-diameter implant requires fewer sequential drills, which may partially account for the relatively higher placement precision observed in this experiment. Lastly, the robotic system employed in this study differed from those of the other two studies. This discrepancy may be attributable to varying levels of deviation. However, Bai et al. [37] conducted animal studies and found lower global coronal and apical deviation and higher angular deviation compared to this study. This difference may be due to factors such as the distinct oral environment and bone density in canines compared to the in vitro model used here. Overall, the accuracy of the data obtained from this study aligns with previous studies.

The four bone surface inclinations (0°, 15°, 30°, and 45°) were selected to simulate clinically relevant implant conditions. Post-extraction alveolar ridge resorption frequently results in inclined bone surfaces, and implant placement on such surfaces to maximize residual bone volume is common in clinical practice. In addition, in edentulous jaw rehabilitation, implants are often intentionally tilted to avoid injury to the inferior alveolar nerve or perforation of the maxillary sinus. In this study, 0° represents an ideal flat surface, while 15° to 45° simulate progressively increasing clinical inclinations, enabling evaluation of the accuracy and stability of robot-assisted trephine drilling under varying anatomical conditions.

As discussed in the Introduction section, the inclination of the bone surface is crucial in trephine drilling due to its unique annular cutting edge. Previous studies have suggested that trephine drills are prone to slip on steep bone surface inclinations, potentially reducing the implant accuracy [12]. Li et al. [9] also noted that in clinical cases, the performance of trephine drills in situations involving steep or irregular bone surfaces required further investigation. In the present study, we observed even at a large bone surface inclination of 45°, the trephine maintained acceptable implant accuracy (global coronal deviation = 0.45 ± 0.11 mm, global apical deviation = 0.50 ± 0.13 mm, angular deviation = 0.66 ± 0.18°) and showed no significant difference in comparison to the conventional drilling group.

Previous studies have consistently indicated that novices require a certain learning curve to achieve proficiency in d-CAIS technology, and that the operator’s experience level is a critical factor influencing both implant efficiency and the accuracy of implant placement [38–41]. In contrast, no significant learning curve has been observed in the accuracy of implant placement when novices use r-CAIS technology, suggesting that this approach seems to mitigate the disparity in accuracy among dentists with different experience levels [25]. In this study, the robotic arm autonomously completes the entire implant process, eliminating the need for the operator to hold the handpiece, significantly reducing the training requirements, and minimizing the influence of the surgeon’s skills and reaction time on the outcome of the surgery. Notably, before this study, the operator had no prior experience with implant robots in clinical and in vitro studies. After a brief training period, the operator could proficiently operate the implant robot and achieve consistent accuracy data. Consistent with previous studies [25], the results of this study confirm that r-CAIS technology exhibits a shortened learning period and minimizes the influence of the clinician’s experience level on accuracy of the implant.

The total sample size of 80 implants and the group size of 10 implants in this study were determined based on previously published in vitro studies in this field [26, 29, 42]. This sample size may have been underpowered to detect small differences in accuracy parameters. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences in accuracy between the two drilling protocols should be interpreted with caution and cannot be directly interpreted as evidence of equivalence.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as an in vitro study conducted using homogeneous polyurethane blocks, this investigation does not replicate the complexities of the clinical environment. Polyurethane is isotropic and homogeneous, whereas human cortical bone is anisotropic and heterogeneous, with properties varying by location, density, and microstructure. The model also does not account for soft tissue presence, patient movement, intraoral fluids, and anatomical variability such as differences in cortical and trabecular bone architecture. These factors may affect both the accuracy and procedural efficiency of implant placement in real-world clinical practice. Second, this study utilized a single robotic system, one implant system, and a single implant dimension. Therefore, the extent to which these findings can be generalized to other robotic systems, implant designs, or implant dimensions remains uncertain. Third, although trephine drilling enables simultaneous harvesting of autogenous bone, the present study did not evaluate the quality, quantity, or cellular viability of the harvested bone cores. In addition, thermal effects and potential microdamage to the surrounding bone during osteotomy were not assessed. Fourth, despite the robotic system’s capacity to reduce operator-dependent variability, the impact of the learning curve, particularly on workflow efficiency and calibration accuracy, was not systematically examined. Operator experience may still influence surgical performance and outcomes, even in robot-assisted procedures.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study using polyurethane models and the Remebot robotic system, this study’s findings indicate that implant robot-assisted trephine drilling for implant-site preparation does not compromise implant precision compared to conventional serial drilling and improves implant efficiency. In addition, trephine drilling may provide the practical advantage of harvesting autogenous bone material; however, this potential benefit, including its application in bone augmentation or biopsy harvesting, requires further validation in future in vivo studies.
