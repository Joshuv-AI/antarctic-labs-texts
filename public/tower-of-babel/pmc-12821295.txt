The relationship between oral health, psychological emotions and sleep disorders among migrant elderly people - an empirical study based on questionnaire survey
Authors: Mengmeng Wang, Sheng Zhai, Shengling Li, Xin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWith the advancement of China’s population aging and urbanization, the physical and mental health problems of the migrant elderly have become increasingly prominent due to changes in living environment and weakening of social support networks. This study aimed to explore the interactive relationship between oral health, psychological emotions and sleep disorders among migrant elderly people in Jinan, China.MethodsA cross-sectional survey design was adopted, and 562 migrant elderly people who met the criteria were selected as the research subjects in Jinan City, China in June 2024. Sociodemographic characteristics were collected through household questionnaires. The Oral Health Assessment Index for the Elderly (GOHAI) was used to evaluate the oral health status of the migrant elderly people. The Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) was used to evaluate the negative psychological emotions of the migrant elderly people. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to evaluate the sleep quality of the migrant elderly people. Pearson correlation and structural equation modeling (SEM) were used to further analyze the interactive relationship between oral health, psychological emotions and sleep disorders.ResultsThe average scores of GOHAI, DASS-21 and PSQI were 53.23 ± 5.31, 7.80 ± 3.21 and 4.49 ± 2.56 respectively, and there were significant differences between urban and rural areas (P < 0.05). There was a significant negative correlation between oral health, sleep disorders and negative psychological emotions, and a significant positive correlation between sleep disorders and negative psychological emotions (P < 0.05). The results of the structural equation model show that the original mediation model (oral health → sleep disorder → negative psychological emotions) meets the standard (χ²/df = 2.914, RMSEA = 0.055, CFI = 0.951). After controlling for sleep disorder variables and key covariates such as age, gender, education level, and chronic diseases, and corrected by clustered robust standard errors, the indirect association effect between oral health and negative psychological emotions (with sleep disorder as the intermediate variable) The value is -0.12, Boot95%CI is (-0.198,-0.070), P < 0.001, this indirect association accounts for 34.29% of the total association; reverse mediation model (negative psychological emotions → sleep Obstacle → oral health) the fitting did not meet the standard (χ²/df = 3.527, RMSEA = 0.098, CFI = 0.869), and the indirect effect was not significant (P = 0.257), indicating that the original model was robust. Conclusion There is a negative correlation between the oral health and negative psychological and emotional levels of the migrant elderly, and this correlation may be indirectly related through sleep disorders. There is a significant correlation between the three. The research suggests that multi-dimensional intervention ideas can be adopted, including paying attention to oral health, optimizing sleep quality, etc., to provide a reference for improving the mental health of the migrant elderly and provide a scientific basis for health management.Keywords: Migrant elderly, Oral health, Psychological emotions, Sleep disorders, Mediating effect

Introduction

With the rapid growth of China’s economy and the accelerating urbanization process, the social structure and population flow pattern are undergoing profound changes. Among them, more and more elderly people choose to move to cities with their children, forming a unique social group - the migrant elderly. According to relevant statistical data, migrant elderly people have become an important part of my country’s aging population, and their scale continues to expand with the deepening of urbanization [1]. However, compared with the elderly who stay in their hometown, the physical and mental health of the relocated elderly has been significantly affected due to the drastic changes in their living environment and the weakening of social support networks [2, 3]. Migrant elderly people leave their familiar living environment and social circle, causing their original social relationship network to be weakened or even interrupted. At the same time, due to differences in language, cultural habits and lifestyles in the cities where they live, migrant elderly people often face greater pressure to adapt. This adaptation pressure not only affects their independence in daily life, but also increases their psychological burden [4, 5].

Oral health is an important part of the overall health of the elderly. It is not only related to basic chewing function and nutritional intake, but also a key factor affecting psychological emotions and systemic health [6]. Studies have shown that the oral health status of the elderly is closely related to a variety of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and respiratory diseases. These diseases are often causally related to oral infection, periodontal disease, or oral function degradation [7, 8]. How to improve the oral health status of the elderly has become one of the core issues in the fields of public health and elderly health management. Good oral health helps maintain the social communication skills and self-esteem of the elderly. Oral disease or tooth loss may cause negative psychological emotions such as depression, anxiety, and social avoidance. This correlation between variables is particularly significant among the migrant elderly population [9]. Oral health problems are related to pain, chewing difficulties, and language expression difficulties among migrant elderly people. Such problems may be associated with a decline in the quality of daily life, a reduced willingness to participate in social activities, and may be associated with increased loneliness and depression [10].

Sleep disorders refer to a type of health problem in which individuals experience abnormalities in sleep quality, timing or pattern, resulting in significant impact on physiological functions or daily life. Insomnia is the most common type of sleep disorder among the elderly, characterized by prolonged time to fall asleep, multiple awakenings during the night, or the inability to fall asleep again after waking up early [11]. Studies have shown that sleep disorders among migrant elderly people are often accompanied by reduced deep sleep, sleep fragmentation and circadian rhythm disorders [12, 13]. The series of stress factors that migrant elderly people face during the migration process, such as changes in living environment, weakening social support, and changes in family roles, are associated with a higher risk of sleep disorders. Long-term sleep disorders will not only reduce an individual’s quality of life, but may also lead to a series of health problems [14, 15]. Therefore, it is of great practical significance to pay attention to the sleep disorders of the migrant elderly population.

At present, there has been some progress in research on the oral health, psychological emotions and sleep disorders of the elderly at home and abroad. However, Most of the existing studies focus on the general elderly population, and mainly explore the association patterns of single variables [16–18]. There is a lack of systematic analysis of the complex relationships among oral health, psychological emotions, and sleep disorders. Based on this, this study will focus on the special group of migrant elderly people in Jinan City, China, focusing on the relationship between oral health, psychological emotions and sleep disorders, aiming to reveal their interaction mechanism through empirical research, in order to provide scientific basis for the formulation of health management strategies for the elderly in an aging society.

Materials and methods

Research object

This study conducted a cross-sectional survey in Jinan City, Shandong Province, China in June 2024, and the research subjects were migrant elderly people in Jinan City, Shandong Province. Inclusion criteria: Migrant elderly people aged ≥ 60 years old; migrated to Jinan City, Shandong Province with their children; lived in Jinan City for ≥ 6 months; had basic communication skills and were able to complete questionnaires; voluntarily participated in the study and signed an informed consent form. Exclusion criteria: patients with clear cognitive dysfunction or mental illness, unable to communicate normally; unwilling to participate in the research or uncooperative to the investigation.

Urban and rural classification definition

In this study, “urban” and “rural” are classified according to the type of community where the research subjects currently live in Jinan City, rather than the household registration attributes. Among them, “urban” refers to the urban communities (including built-up areas and suburban urbanized communities) that live in the jurisdiction of Jinan City and are under the jurisdiction of streets. “Rural” refers to the rural communities (including un-urbanized villages and agricultural communities) that live in the jurisdiction of Jinan City.

Data collection

Basis for urban and rural division: The “urban and rural” groupings in sampling and subsequent data analysis are based on the administrative attributes and construction types of the communities where the research subjects currently live, ensuring that they are consistent with the definition of urban and rural classification in the “research subjects” section.

A multi-stage cluster random sampling method was used to select sampling locations and survey objects. In the first stage, 4 districts were randomly selected from 12 districts/counties in the city as primary sampling units; in the second stage, 1 street was randomly selected from each district, with a total of 4 streets as secondary sampling units; in the third stage: 1 community was randomly selected from each street, with a total of 4 communities as the final sampling unit. In the above-extracted communities, all migrant elderly people who meet the criteria are included to ensure that the research subjects are representative.

Data were collected through household questionnaires and face-to-face interviews by five uniformly trained investigators. The questionnaire content covers sociodemographic characteristics, oral health status, psychological and emotional state, sleep quality and other indicators. The investigator explained the content and filling method of the questionnaire in detail to ensure that the respondents understood and answered correctly; for the elderly with literacy difficulties or visual impairment, the investigator read aloud and recorded the answers; after data collection, the questionnaire was independently checked by two researchers to ensure data integrity and accuracy.

Research tools

Oral health

This study used the Geriatric Oral Health Assessment Index (GOHAI) to evaluate the oral health status of the migrant elderly. GOHAI is a self-reporting tool specifically designed for the oral health status of the elderly. It was developed by Atchison and other scholars and is widely used in international oral health research on the elderly [20]. The GOHAI consists of 12 items covering three dimensions: oral function, psychosocial function, and pain and discomfort. Each item adopts a 5-point scoring system: 1 = always, 2 = often, 3 = sometimes, 4 = rarely, 5 = never. The score ranges from 12 to 60 points. The higher the score, the better the oral health status.

Negative psychological emotions

This study used the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) to assess the negative psychological and emotional status of migrant elderly people.

DASS-21 is a self-report scale that is widely used in mental health research and has good reliability and validity [21]. In this study, the Cronbach’α coefficient of the total scale of the scale was 0.86, and the Cronbach’α coefficients of the depression dimension, anxiety dimension, and stress dimension were 0.81, 0.83, and 0.80 respectively, all greater than 0.7, indicating that the scale has good reliability and is suitable for this study. DASS-21 includes 21 items, divided into three subscales according to function: depression dimension (7 items), anxiety dimension (7 items), and stress dimension (7 items). Each item is scored on a 4-point scale: 0 = not at all, 1 = sometimes, 2 = often, and 3 = always. Scoring procedure: The sum of the original scores of each dimension item is the “subscale original score”; because DASS-21 is a simplified version, the “subscale original score needs to be multiplied by 2” to get the “subscale conversion score”. The conversion score corresponds to the emotion level classification standard (that is, the following degree ranges are based on the conversion score): the degree of depression is divided into normal (0–9 points), mild (10–13 points), moderate (14–20 points), severe ( 21–27 points), extremely severe (≥ 28 points); anxiety levels are divided into normal (0–7 points), mild (8–9 points), moderate (10–14 points), severe (15–19 points), and extremely severe degree (≥ 20 points); stress levels are divided into normal (0–14 points), mild (15–18 points), moderate (19–25 points), severe (26–33 points), and extremely severe (≥ 34 points). The average DASS-21 score reported in this study is 7.80 ± 3.21 as the “raw score of the total scale” (that is, the raw scores of the three subscales are directly added) without additional conversion. Higher scores indicate more severe negative psychological emotions.

Sleep disorders

This study used the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) to evaluate the sleep quality and sleep disorders of migrant older adults. PSQI is a self-report scale widely used in the field of international sleep research. It can comprehensively assess an individual’s sleep status in the past month. Its good reliability and validity make it one of the gold standards in sleep research [22]. In this study, the Cronbach α coefficient of the scale was 0.83, which was greater than 0.7, indicating that the scale had good reliability and was suitable for this study. The PSQI includes 19 items divided into 7 dimensions: subjective sleep quality, time to fall asleep, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbance, hypnotic drug use, and daytime dysfunction. The score range of each dimension is 0–3 points: 0 points = no sleep problems, 1 points = mild sleep problems, 2 points = moderate sleep problems, 3 points = severe sleep problems; the total score of the scale = the direct sum of the scores of the 7 dimensions, and the score range is 0 to 21 points. A total score > 5 points indicates poor sleep quality or sleep disorder; the higher the total score, the worse the sleep quality.

Statistical methods

This study used SPSS27.0 statistical software and AMOS24.0 for data analysis. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed on the sociodemographic characteristics, oral health (GOHAI score), psychological and emotional state (DASS-21 score) and sleep quality (PSQI score) of the research subjects; continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD); categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage [n (%)]. For analysis of differences between groups, t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for continuous variables, and chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the correlation between various indicators of oral health, psychological emotions, and sleep disorders. The results were expressed as correlation coefficients (r values), and the significance level was set at P < 0.05. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to evaluate the mediating role of sleep disorders between oral health and psychological emotions. The model goodness of fit indicators included chi-square value and degree of freedom ratio (c2/df), root mean square error (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), value-added fit index (IFI), etc. The model goodness of fit standards were: c2/df ≤ 3, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, CFI ≥ 0.90, IFI ≥ 0.90. The indirect effect is calculated by the Bootstrapping method, using 1000 repeated samplings, and reporting the standard error (BootSE) and 95% confidence interval (Boot95%CI). Whether the mediation effect is significant is judged by whether the confidence interval contains zero. The significance level for all statistical analyzes was set at two-sided P < 0.05. It should be noted that this study adopts a multi-stage cluster sampling design, and the design effect (DEFF = 2.5) has been taken into account when calculating the sample size; in order to correct the impact of the sampling design on the statistical results, all analyzes have been re-estimated and corrected using the survey adjustment procedure (clustering robust standard errors): among them, t test, ANOVA and Pe Arson correlation analysis uses the “complex sampling” module of SPSS27.0 to incorporate clustering variables (community level) to calculate clustering robust standard errors; structural equation modeling (SEM) uses the “cluster analysis” function of AMOS24.0 to adjust standard errors with communities as clustering units to avoid the risk of underestimating standard errors.

Result

Baseline data

This study included 562 elderly people who migrated with them, of which 84.52% were in rural areas and 15.48% were in urban areas (Table 1). Gender distribution shows that the proportion of women is higher than that of men (72.95% vs. 27.05%), and the difference between urban and rural areas is significant (P < 0.05). The age group was mainly 60–65 years old (55.87%), and the distribution difference between urban and rural areas was significant (P < 0.05). The education level is mainly primary school and below (55.87%), and the difference between urban and rural areas is significant (P < 0.05). Married people accounted for 86.48%, and unmarried, divorced or widowed people accounted for 13.52%. There was no statistically significant difference between urban and rural areas (P > 0.05). The main reason for moving was to take care of grandchildren (77.94%), and the main intention to move was “willingness” (83.27%). There was no statistically significant difference between urban and rural areas (P > 0.05). In terms of health indicators, the GOHAI score of the elderly in urban areas (54.68 ± 4.37) is significantly higher than that in rural areas (52.96 ± 5.62). This shows that there are differences in the oral health scores of urban and rural migrant elderly people (P < 0.05). PSQI scores show that sleep quality in rural areas (4.59 ± 2.07) is better than that in urban areas (3.97 ± 2.11) There is a significant difference (P < 0.05), indicating that the sleep-related scores of the migrant elderly in the two places are different. In terms of DASS-21 scores, the psychological and emotional status of urban elderly people is better than that of rural elderly people, and the difference is statistically significant (P < 0.05).

The mediating effect of sleep disorders on the relationship between oral health and negative psychological emotions

This study constructed an original mediation model (oral health → sleep disorders → negative psychological emotions) and a reverse mediation model (negative psychological emotions → sleep disorders → oral health), both of which included key covariates such as age and gender. The original model also used communities as clustering units and used clustering robust standard errors to correct sampling bias to verify the robustness of the path. The original model fits the standard (χ²/df = 2.914, RMSEA = 0.055, GFI = 0.957, Table 3): In the original analysis (without covariates/clustering correction), the indirect effect between oral health and negative psychological emotions is -0.15 (P < 0.001, accounting for 38.47%), oral health → sleep disorder (-0.34), sleep disorder → negative psychological emotions (0.43) The paths are all significant; the indirect effect after incorporating covariates is -0.13 (P < 0.001, accounting for 36.11%); the indirect effect after correcting for sampling bias is -0.12 (P < 0.001, accounting for 3 4.29%), and oral health→sleep disorder (-0.31, P < 0.001), sleep disorder→negative psychological emotions (0.40, P < 0.001) are still significant (Table 4; Fig. 1). The reverse model fitting did not meet the standard (χ²/df = 3.527, RMSEA = 0.098, GFI = 0.876, Table 3): only negative psychological emotions → sleep disorders were significant (0.28, P < 0.00 1), sleep disorder → oral health (-0.09, P = 0.118) and indirect effect (-0.025, P = 0.257) are not significant (Table 5), indicating that the original model is robust and the reverse model has no supporting evidence.

Discussion

With the continuous advancement of aging and urbanization in China, the group of elderly people who move with them has gradually become one of the special groups in China’s social structure, which has attracted much attention. Oral health is an important part of the overall health of the elderly. At the same time, due to the adaptation pressure of the new living environment and cultural habits, sleep disorders such as difficulty falling asleep and intermittent sleep often occur, resulting in negative psychological emotions [23]. Therefore, it is of great practical significance to explore the interaction between oral health, psychological emotions and sleep disorders. This study was conducted to analyze the mediating effect of sleep disorder on oral health and negative psychological emotion of the elderly in Jinan City, China, and to provide scientific basis for health management strategy of the elderly in Jinan City.

In this study, the average GOHAI score of the elderly in Jinan City, China was 53.23 ± 5.31, and the difference between urban and rural areas was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Aging may be associated with lower oral health scores. With age, the physiological functions of the elderly gradually deteriorate, and oral health problems such as gingival recession, tooth loosening and loss occur more frequently. Carvalho et al. [24] showed that with age, enamel wear gradually, dentin exposure increases, and with gingival recession, such physiological changes are associated with root caries occurrence and dentin sensitivity. Furthermore, chronic conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases are prevalent among the elderly population, which can exacerbate oral health deterioration. A study by Han et al. [25] found that patients with both diabetes and cardiovascular diseases had a 1.4-fold increase in the incidence of periodontitis, along with a significantly higher risk of tooth loss.In Jinan, China, the average DASS-21 score among migrant elderly was 7.80 ± 3.21. Migrant elderly from rural areas exhibited more severe psychological and emotional issues compared to those from urban areas (P < 0.05). Research by Jin et al. [26] indicated that rural-to-urban migration is often associated with heightened relative deprivation and mental health problems, potentially due to social exclusion and personal discrimination in urban settings.Migrant elderly from rural areas may face greater cultural conflicts and adaptation difficulties in urban environments due to differences in language, customs, and lifestyle, leading to a sense of alienation from local residents. After leaving their hometowns, they also lose their original social support networks, and the lack of emotional support further amplifies psychological distress.Wang et al. [27] found that the burden of childcare (e.g., relocating to care for grandchildren) significantly increased psychological stress among migrant elderly. In this study, 77.94% of participants migrated for the purpose of caring for grandchildren. Compared to local elderly, migrant elderly experienced higher psychological distress, which was associated with their lower socioeconomic status and greater childcare pressures.Moreover, migrant elderly from economically disadvantaged rural areas often struggle to access urban economic support or social security after relocation. While undertaking childcare responsibilities, they may also face additional financial burdens, further exacerbating psychological distress [28].The average PSQI score among migrant elderly in Jinan was 4.49 ± 2.56, with a significant difference between urban and rural groups (P < 0.05). Yaremchuk et al. [29] reported that common sleep disorders in the elderly, such as insomnia, sleep apnea syndrome, and restless legs syndrome, are more severe among migrant elderly.In summary, migrant elderly generally exhibit multidimensional health issues encompassing oral health, psychological well-being, and sleep disorders, with significant disparities between urban and rural populations.

There exists a significant negative correlation between oral health and negative psychological emotions. This indicates that better oral health status among migrant elderly is associated with lower levels of negative psychological emotions. The direct effect value of oral health on negative psychological emotions is -0.23, Tsironis et al. [30] found that higher oral health scores of the elderly with migration were less correlated with negative psychological emotions, and this association may be indirectly related through psychological resilience and quality of life improvement, suggesting that oral health is associated with positive psychological states, The study further verified the positive effect of oral health on positive mental emotions. Good oral health improved chewing function, language expression ability and appearance image of the elderly, enhanced their self-esteem and social confidence, and reduced inferiority and isolation caused by physical problems [31]. At the same time, oral health was significantly negatively correlated with sleep disorders. This means that the higher the oral health level of the elderly, the lower the level of sleep disorders. The direct effect value of oral health on sleep disorders was-0.34, indicating that oral health had a negative impact on sleep disorders, i.e., reduced sleep disorders. Lauritano et al. [32] found that oral health behaviors Oral health (e.g., periodontitis) significantly increases the risk of sleep disorders. Especially rural-to-urban elderly with poor oral care behaviors are more likely to have sleep problems. Oral diseases cause persistent pain and discomfort, especially at night, which may worsen and directly interfere with sleep and sleep quality, while good oral health helps relieve psychological stress and negative emotions. Among the elderly population, those with high oral health levels are more likely to maintain a positive mental state and have higher sleep quality [33].

On the basis of controlling sleep disorder variables, age, gender, education level, chronic diseases and other factors were further included. Indirect associations between oral health and negative psychological mood after key covariates such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease (with sleep disorders as the intermediate variable) effect value of-0.13 (Boot95%CI:-0.201,-0.072, P < 0.001), versus effect values without covariates included (-0.15) Both directions were consistent and significant, suggesting that the indirect association and the intermediate role of sleep disorders were robust. Similar to Salim et al. [34], the team noted that better oral health was associated with better sleep quality and, in turn, better mental health in older migrants, that more sleep problems may be associated with increased psychological distress, and that improved oral health may be associated with relief of these stresses. Jiang et al. [35] also found that sleep disorder may be the intermediate correlation factor between oral health and mental state in the comprehensive health plan for the elderly who migrate from rural to urban areas, and oral health-related interventions are associated with the relief of psychological stress and negative emotions. This association mode is particularly significant among the elderly who migrate from rural to urban areas. Oral health problems can trigger systemic low-grade inflammation, resulting in increased nervous system excitability and interfering with sleep quality. Improved sleep quality reduces the body’s overreaction to stress by regulating the neuroendocrine system. Good oral health can significantly reduce inflammation levels, improve nervous system balance, promote deep sleep, and indirectly reduce anxiety and depression. Improved oral health reduces pain and discomfort at night and increases sleep efficiency, thereby reducing the risk of anxiety and depression [36]. Oral disorders may also trigger or exacerbate sleep apnea syndrome (e.g. snoring at night, interrupted breathing), leading to fragmentation of sleep [37]. Improved oral health reduces the incidence of respiratory disorders, thereby improving the quality of sleep at night and alleviating psychological distress caused by sleep deprivation. Improved sleep quality allows older people to have better rest at night and higher energy and physical status during the day. This energy recovery enables older migrants to participate more actively in daily activities, such as caring for grandchildren or participating in community activities, reducing loneliness and helplessness caused by passive living patterns [38]. Good behavior patterns (such as participating in community activities) can increase social support networks and obtain more opportunities for interpersonal communication. Increased social interaction can alleviate loneliness caused by changes in the environment of migration and reduce negative psychological emotions. In addition, sleep disorders can lead to reduced attention span, memory loss, and delayed thinking, and these cognitive impairments can exacerbate negative psychological emotions in older people. High-quality sleep helps older people improve cognitive skills by improving memory consolidation and mood regulation in the brain, indirectly reducing anxiety and depression [39].

Based on the results of this study, the following suggestions were put forward: (1) Pay attention to oral health-related interventions. Popularize oral care knowledge to help the elderly understand correct oral hygiene methods; provide regular oral health examination services to provide support for timely identification and treatment of periodontal disease, caries and other problems; (2) Pay attention to sleep disorders related management. Suggest psychological counseling and emotional counseling services to provide reference for relieving anxiety and stress-related problems; At the same time, cognitive behavioral therapy can help the elderly develop healthy sleep habits.(3) Implement mental health support. Through community activities and volunteer services, help the elderly integrate into the new environment and strengthen social interaction; advocate family members to pay more attention to the psychological needs of the elderly and provide emotional support; conduct regular mental health screening in the community to identify and intervene in psychological distress early.(4) Strengthen policy and resource support. The government and community should strengthen the medical resources tilt to the elderly, including providing more oral health and mental health services, improving the social protection system, and providing more comprehensive medical insurance and economic support for the elderly.

Shortcomings of this study

Inadequate control for potential confounding factors, such as chronic disease, drug use, and lifestyle, may have influenced the results of this study; although significant differences in oral health, psychosocial mood, and sleep disorders were found between urban and rural areas, the mechanisms behind them (e.g. economic level, accessibility of medical resources, social support network, etc.) lack of in-depth discussion; this study adopts a multi-stage cluster sampling design, and design effects have been considered in the sample size calculation (DEFF = 2.5), but subsequent statistical analysis (t-test, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, SEM) Not included in cluster robust standard error or survey adjustment procedures (e.g. weighted, stratified analysis), may lead to low standard error estimation, affecting the robustness of the results. In the future, we should strengthen the control of potential confounding factors, and further explore the mechanism behind the urban-rural differences.(including the role of economic level, access to health care resources, and social support networks in health disparities); in addition, subsequent analysis needs to incorporate survey adjustment methods Cross-sectional designs do not infer causal relationships and can only describe associations between variables and do not identify which factors are the source of causal relationships. Self-reported measurements can be biased, especially in assessing oral health, mental mood, and sleep quality, and subjective evaluations by respondents can affect the accuracy of results. The lack of objective oral health indicators (e.g., number of teeth, periodontal status) prevents further verification of the consistency of self-reported oral health status with actual conditions. Limited representativeness, imbalance in gender distribution of the sample in this study, and limitations in sample frame may affect the universality and extrapolation of the study results.

Conclusion

This study analyzed the relationship between oral health, mental mood and sleep disorders among the elderly in Jinan City, China. It was found that oral health was negatively correlated with negative mental mood, and this correlation may be indirectly related to sleep disorders. Sleep disorders may be the intermediate correlation factor between oral health and mental mood, and there were significant differences in oral health, mental mood and sleep related scores between urban and rural elderly. The study suggests that multi-dimensional comprehensive measures such as paying attention to oral health, optimizing sleep quality and providing psychological support can provide reference for improving the physical and mental health level of the elderly who move with them, and provide scientific basis for the formulation of health management strategies. Meanwhile, future research should further control confounding factors and explore the association patterns behind the differences in urban and rural health, so as to provide support for the formulation of more precise health-related measures.
