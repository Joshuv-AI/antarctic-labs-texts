Dietary habits, nutritional supplement use, and adherence to national dietary guidelines in patients with psoriasis
Authors: Helene Meyer Martin, Charlotte Näslund-Koch, Marianne Bengtson Løvendorf, Stig Egil Bojesen, Camilla J. Kobylecki, Signe Vedel-Krogh, Lone Skov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory disease with a well-established association with obesity. However, the role of diet remains unclear. This study examined dietary habits in patients with psoriasis and compared adherence to national dietary guidelines with matched healthy controls. We included 466 patients with psoriasis from the BIOSKIN cohort and 1,029 healthy controls from the Copenhagen General Population Study. According to a Food Frequency Questionnaire, 53% of the patients reported a “healthy and varied diet,” and 39% “no specific diet”. Approximately 10% practiced intermittent fasting, while smaller proportions followed a Mediterranean diet (4%), a ketogenic/low-carb, high-fat diet (3%), or an anti-inflammatory diet (2%). Overall, 90% showed high or intermediate adherence to dietary guidelines. Patients with mild psoriasis adhered more often to guidelines than those with moderate-to-severe disease (33% vs. 17%, p = 0.001). No significant difference in dietary adherence was observed between patients with psoriasis and matched healthy controls (p = 0.79). In conclusion, patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis have less healthy dietary patterns compared to those with mild disease. When comparing all patients with psoriasis to healthy controls, no difference in adherence to dietary guidelines was found. Due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, the causal relationship between diet and psoriasis remains unclear.

Introduction

Psoriasis is an inflammatory skin disease associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities1. Obesity is a causal risk factor for developing psoriasis2,3 and randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that weight loss improves the severity of psoriasis4. However, as diet is closely linked to body weight, it remains unknown whether the diet itself affects psoriasis, or whether it is merely body weight that matters. A healthy diet has been shown to be of great importance in preventing and managing cardiometabolic diseases5. An umbrella review of meta-analyses of observational studies and RCTs, with data from more than 12,800,000 participants, found that adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a reduced risk of overall mortality, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes6. Similarly, RCTs (n = 4291) have shown that low-carbohydrate ketogenic diets effectively reduce cardiovascular risk factors including glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides in patients with type 2 diabetes7. Although the relationship between diet and psoriasis is less well studied8, and evidence to suggest optimal diet is lacking, many patients with psoriasis request dietary counselling as part of their treatment9. According to a survey answered by 1,206 patients from the National Psoriasis Foundation in the U.S., 80% had tried to modify their diet due to their psoriasis. In another web-based survey of 448 patients with psoriasis, 85% reported being either “motivated” or “potentially motivated” to make lifestyle changes, particularly in relation to diet and physical activity10. Nevertheless, only a minority of patients receive nutritional advice11. It is imperative for healthcare professionals to understand the dietary patterns of patients with psoriasis in order to best support these patients. We aimed to describe dietary patterns in patients with psoriasis and compare adherence to general dietary guidelines between patients with psoriasis and matched healthy controls in a Danish cohort study. We hypothesized that patients with psoriasis would have a higher degree of non-adherence to dietary guidelines than healthy controls, and that this is more pronounced in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis.

Method

Study population

This is a cross-sectional study using data from ‘The Copenhagen Translational Skin Immunology Biobank and Research Program’ (BIOSKIN)12, an ongoing observational cohort of patients with inflammatory skin diseases. Patients with psoriasis were enrolled from February 2022 to July 2024 at the outpatient clinic at the Department of Dermatology and Allergy, Copenhagen University Hospital - Herlev and Gentofte, or from private dermatological clinics in Copenhagen. Anthropometric measurements, information about anti-psoriatic treatment, and blood samples were collected. All patients also completed a comprehensive questionnaire regarding lifestyle and socioeconomic parameters, such as smoking habits, alcohol intake, educational level, information about comorbidities, and filled out a Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) during the visit. At the same time, the severity of psoriasis was assessed by a healthcare professional using Body Surface Area (BSA) and Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI). Since many patients in our outpatient clinic are well-treated, moderate-to-severe psoriasis was defined using systemic treatment (conventional or biologicals) or having a PASI, DLQI, or BSA ≥ 1013.

Healthy control group

To compare adherence to national dietary guidelines between patients with psoriasis and healthy controls, we included a matched control group from the Copenhagen General Population Study (CGPS). CGPS is a prospective cohort study of the general population in Denmark, including individuals aged 20 to 100, consecutively and randomly enrolled from 2003 to 2015 to best reflect the Danish adult population14. All participants completed a self-reported Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) developed in collaboration with nutritional specialists14 and similar to the FFQ in BIOSKIN. Participants from the CGPS (n = 1029) were matched 1:2 with patients with psoriasis from the BIOSKIN cohort (n = 438) based on age, sex, BMI, and smoking status. Only individuals without psoriasis were included as healthy controls, and individuals with missing data on the FFQ were excluded.

Ethical considerations

BIOSKIN is approved by the Scientific Ethical Committee of the Capital Region, Denmark (H-21032986) and conducted according to the Helsinki Declaration. All patients received oral and written information and provided written consent prior to inclusion in the study12.

All participants in the CGPS also provided a written informed consent prior to inclusion14. The CGPS is approved by the Scientific Ethical Committee of the Capital Region, Denmark (#H-KF-01-144/01) and conducted accordingly to the Helsinki Declaration.

FFQ and adherence to national dietary guidelines

All patients completed a self-reported FFQ examining dietary habits, weekly intake of specific food products, and use of nutritional supplements (Table S1). Only patients who had completed the entire FFQ were included in this study and proportion of missing data are presented in the supplementary (Table S2). To evaluate the overall dietary habits of the participants, questions about intake of saturated and unsaturated fats, as well as participants’ weekly consumption of food products such as fruits, vegetables, fish, fast food, and sugary beverages, were used to assess their adherence to national dietary guidelines. The Danish Health Authority recommends a plant-rich and varied diet, eating plenty of vegetables, fruits, legumes, and fish, along with whole-grain foods, choosing plant-based oils and low-fat dairy products15. It is also recommended to limit intake of sugar, salt, and fatty foods, and choosing water as the main source of hydration. Questions from the FFQ were categorised into three groups (A, B, and C) to determine if participants’ dietary intake aligned with national dietary recommendations, as done in other studies14,16,17. Category A addresses macronutrients, including fats (both saturated and unsaturated). Agreements to guidelines were defined as consuming fruit and vegetables at least 3 times a week and using unsaturated fats for bread and cooking. Category B focused on the consumption of both healthy and unhealthy foods, and agreement to the guidelines was defined as a weekly intake of at least 3 servings of fish (three times or more) and less than 0.5 L of sugar-sweetened beverages a week. Finally, category C assessed the intake of salt, based on questions regarding the weekly consumption of fast food, and 0–1 servings per week was considered in agreement with guidelines. The participants’ dietary pattern was classified into being either high, intermediate, or low adherence to national dietary guidelines. High adherence was defined as agreement in all three categories (A, B, and C) or full agreement in category A but one question in B or C in disagreement. Low adherence was characterised by disagreement in three or more of the questions in group A, while the remaining participants were classified as having intermediate adherence to guidelines.

Blood samples

Metabolic markers such as plasma HbA1c, non-fasting triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and total cholesterol were collected during the visit. Additionally, plasma 25-hydroxyvitamin D and vitamin B12 levels were measured. The collected blood samples were analysed with standard hospital assays at the Department of Clinical Biochemistry, Copenhagen University Hospital - Herlev and Gentofte, Denmark.

Covariates

Information about age, sex, and ethnicity was obtained at the visit, together with anthropometric measurement including BMI and waist and hip circumference. Data on lifestyle factors were collected from the self-reported questionnaire. Smoking was registered as current, former, or never. Alcohol intake was categorised into the following groups: 0–1 units, 2–7 units, 8–15 units, 16–21 units, or > 21 units per week. Educational level was classified into three categories: high school or less, high school + 1–4 years, or high school + > 4 years. Information regarding comorbidities such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidaemia was also obtained from the self-reported questionnaire.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA/NOW SE 18.5. A P-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Baseline characteristics were summarised using descriptive analyses, using Chi-squared test, t-test, Fisher’s Exact test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test to examine any difference between patients with mild or moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Continuous variables were presented as means or medians, depending on the data distribution and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. A Chi-squared test or a Fisher’s Exact test was used to determine any difference related to participants’ dietary habits and adherence to guidelines. A logistic regression analysis was used to examine the relationship between disease severity (mild/moderate-to-severe) and adherence to national dietary guidelines in the BIOSKIN cohort. Analyses were presented as a crude model and a multivariable adjusted model including BMI (≥ 25/<25 kg/m2), sex (female/male), and educational level (high school or less/high school + 1–4 years/high school + > 4 years). To compare patients with psoriasis to healthy controls, 1,234 individuals from the CGPS cohort were matched with 438 patients from the BIOSKIN cohort. Due to missing data from the FFQ, 1,029 healthy controls from the CGPS were included in the final analyses. There were no missing data on the matched variables. To compare adherence between patients with psoriasis from BIOSKIN with healthy controls from the CGPS, a logistic regression analysis was conducted. Since matching was done using age, sex, BMI, and smoking status only a crude model was presented.

Results

Demographics

A total of 466 patients with psoriasis were included from the BIOSKIN cohort. The mean age of the patients was 49.6 years (SD ± 15.7), and 43.8% of the patients were women (Table 1). The mean BMI was 28.3 kg/m2 (SD ± 6.5), and 66.7% of the patients were overweight or obese. The majority of the population (79%) received systemic therapies, including both conventional treatments and biologics. Notably, 17.4% were treated exclusively with topical therapies, while 3.4% did not receive any treatment. The median PASI was 2.5 (range 0-40.8), and similar values were found for BSA (median 2, range 0–80) and DLQI (median 2, range 0–26). Patients diagnosed with moderate-to-severe psoriasis exhibited a slightly lower PASI compared to those with mild disease, indicating highly effective treatment of this group. Regarding metabolic comorbidities, 36.3% had hypertension, 35.2% had dyslipidemia, and 8.4% had type 2 diabetes mellitus. 26% were currently smoking, and 21% had a high educational level attending high school + > 4 years. When comparing patients with moderate-to-severe disease to those with mild psoriasis, patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis had a lower educational level (43.7% vs. 26.0% had attended high school or less, p = 0.005 across all three groups) and DLQI (1 vs. 3, p = 0.006), higher BMI (29 kg/m2 vs. 26 kg/m2, p = 0.001), higher prevalence of current smokers (28.7%, vs. 17.5% p = 0.02), and a higher prevalence of hypertension (38.2% vs. 28.9% p = 0.041), type 2 diabetes mellitus (4.1% vs. 9.5% vs. 4.1%, p = 0.044) and dyslipidemia (37.7% vs. 25.8%, p = 0.029).

Dietary habits of patients with psoriasis

In response to the FFQ, 53% of patients indicated that they adhered to a “healthy and varied diet”, while 39% did “not follow any specific diet” (Fig. 1A). Around 10% practiced intermittent fasting and only a smaller proportion followed other diets such as Mediterranean (4%), Ketogenic/Low Carb High Fat (3%), or an anti-inflammatory diet (2%). Patients with mild psoriasis differed significantly from patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis, with more patients with mild disease following either a vegetarian diet (10.2% vs. 2.7%, p = 0.001), eating a “healthy and varied diet” (63.9% vs. 49.9%, p = 0.014), or eating organic (27.8% vs. 15.5%, p = 0.005). Significantly more patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis did not follow any specific diet compared to patients with mild disease (43.6% vs. 25.8%, p = 0.001) (Fig. 1B). Regarding daily nutritional supplements, 37% of all patients used multivitamins, 41% used vitamin D supplements, and 23% used omega 3 supplements (Fig. 1C).

Half of all patients reported eating wholegrains and vegetables daily, and 73% marked eating fast food less than once a week. Furthermore, 53% reported eating fish once a week or several times weekly, and 73% reported only drinking 0–1 glasses of sugary beverages weekly. However, more than half of the patients also reported consuming sweets on multiple occasions throughout the week, with a substantial number reporting a daily consumption (Figure S1). Only 16% (n = 76) reported that specific food products triggered their psoriasis, such as alcohol, sugar, and processed food, as shown in Fig. 2A. Even fewer (n = 33, 7%) experienced that specific food products improved their psoriasis, specifically vegetables, healthy food, water, and fish (Fig. 2B). Around 10% responded that they had some sort of food allergy.

Adherence to National dietary guidelines

When comparing patients’ dietary habits with guidelines from the Danish National Health Authorities, 90% showed a high or intermediate adherence. Notably, a higher proportion of patients with mild psoriasis showed a high adherence to guidelines compared to patients with moderate-to-severe disease (33% vs. 17%, p = 0.001). Also, in a multivariable adjusted logistic regression analysis, we found an association between adherence to dietary guidelines and disease severity (P for trend = 0.002) (Fig. 3). Patients in the intermediate adherence group had an odds ratio (OR) of 2.2 (95% Confidence Interval (CI) 1.32–3.79) for having moderate-to-severe psoriasis, while patients in the low adherence group had an even greater risk of having moderate-to-severe psoriasis with an OR of 3.15 (95% CI 1.10–8.99) compared with patients in the high adherence groups. When comparing patients with psoriasis to healthy controls, a higher proportion of healthy controls demonstrated high adherence to dietary guidelines compared to patients with psoriasis (26.4% vs. 21.2%, p = 0.002). However, a higher proportion of healthy controls also showed low adherence to dietary guidelines compared to patients with psoriasis (13.9% vs. 9.6%, p = 0.002). Similar patterns were observed in the logistic regression analysis, and overall, there was no association between adherence to dietary guidelines and psoriasis (p for trend = 0.79) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Our findings show that patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis generally adhere to a less healthy diet compared to those with mild disease. However, when comparing all patients with psoriasis (regardless of severity) to healthy controls, no significant difference in adherence to a healthy diet was observed.

Compared to previous studies, our study offers the most comprehensive analysis of dietary habits among patients with psoriasis, including a detailed stratification by disease severity and a comparison with healthy controls matched on age, sex, BMI, and smoking status. In addition to describing patients’ dietary habits, we compared their intake of various macro- and micronutrients with the Danish national dietary guidelines18, which are considered indicative of a healthy diet. Furthermore, we examined differences in adherence to these guidelines according to psoriasis severity, as well as between patients with psoriasis and healthy controls.

Mild and moderate-to-severe psoriasis represent two clinically distinct groups. Patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis more commonly present with unfavourable lifestyle factors and metabolic comorbidities compared to those with mild disease, which has been demonstrated in several previous studies1,19 and is further supported by our findings. Multiple factors could underlie this difference. Firstly, patients with psoriasis have inflammation not only in the skin but also systemically. A recent study involving over 100,000 individuals demonstrated that low-grade systemic inflammation is an independent risk factor for developing psoriasis, particularly among those with moderate-to-severe disease20. Low-grade systemic inflammation is also associated with metabolic comorbidities21. Furthermore, evidence suggests shared genetic factors between psoriasis and cardiometabolic diseases, potentially mediated by systemic inflammation22.

The impact of diet on systemic low-grade inflammation remains unclear. Both micro- and macronutrients can have either anti- or pro-inflammatory effects. For instance, Tabung et al. explored the influence of various food groups on pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), and tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-alpha23. This empirical dietary inflammatory index was later used in another study by Bridgman et al., examining 85,185 women from the Nurses’ Health Study II. The authors found that pro-inflammatory dietary patterns were not associated with an increased risk of developing psoriasis24. Conversely, the Mediterranean diet - often considered anti-inflammatory - has shown potential benefits in psoriasis in both observational studies and RCTs25,26.

The cross-sectional nature of this study, like many previous studies, raises the possibility of reverse causation. Patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis may consume less healthy diets as a coping mechanism for living with a chronic condition. Preparing healthy meals at home requires time and energy, which may be limited for individuals with severe psoriatic disease. Additionally, patients with psoriasis - especially those with more severe forms - are at increased risk of mental health disorders such as depression and anxiety27, which can further complicate adherence to a healthy diet.

However, our findings, that patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis consume a less healthy diet than those with mild disease could perhaps also indicate that dietary habits may influence disease severity. This is consistent with findings on obesity, where higher body weight is associated with more severe psoriasis2. The critical question is whether diet itself directly affects psoriasis severity, or if the observed effect is primarily due to body weight. Notably, we observed an association between adherence to dietary guidelines and psoriasis severity, even after adjusting for BMI. This suggests that diet itself, beyond weight control, may influence psoriasis outcomes. Other factors such as depression, stress, physical activity, and alcohol consumption may influence both diet and disease severity. Although we adjusted for key confounders in the regression analyses, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded17.In contrast to some previous studies, we found no significant difference in adherence to dietary guidelines between patients with psoriasis and healthy controls. For instance, a case-control study with 71 patients with psoriasis and 71 healthy controls found that patients with psoriasis were less likely to follow a Mediterranean Diet28. However, the controls were only matched on age and sex, with the most important aspect -body weight - being neglected. A large general population study, also from Denmark, found that low adherence to dietary guidelines was associated with having psoriasis, also after adjusting for multiple confounders17. However, in this study, psoriasis was defined using ICD codes and thus mainly capturing moderate-to-severe psoriasis, and not including mild psoriasis as in our study. This further supports the notion that mild and moderate-to-severe psoriasis may constitute separate phenotypic expressions within the same disease spectrum. The study of lifestyle factors, including diet, is inherently challenging due to potential confounding. People who follow a healthy diet often also engage in regular physical activity, avoid smoking and excessive alcohol intake, attain a higher educational level, and experience lower levels of psychosocial stress. One could speculate that it is the total pro-inflammatory burden in the body that matters. This theory is supported by a study examining Life’s Essential 8 (LE8)29. The LE8 is a set of eight key health behaviours and factors that have been identified by the American Heart Association as being critical for the promotion of cardiovascular health and overall well-being. The eight factors include a healthy diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep, body weight, blood lipid levels, blood glucose, and blood pressure. In this cross-sectional study of 12,744 participants from the NHANES database, a clear association was observed between psoriasis and low LE8, indicating an unhealthy lifestyle29. To date, only a few RCTs have been conducted to investigate the potential of diet as an intervention for the management of psoriasis severity. The highest level of evidence is currently derived from a study which have examined the effects of a hypocaloric diet with a corresponding weight loss in patients with psoriasis8. Some limitations of our study should be acknowledged. As previously discussed, the cross-sectional, case-control design precludes any causal interpretations between diet and psoriasis. Even though we found that patients with moderate-to-severe exhibited a less healthy dietary pattern compared to patients with mild psoriasis, causal relationships cannot be determined. Additionally, our patient population was from a resource-rich area, which may have attenuated the differences observed between patients and healthy controls. However, our healthy controls may also represent a healthier subset of the general population due to participation bias, which could potentially counterbalance this effect. Furthermore, dietary data were collected using a short FFQ. As with all self-reported measures, this approach is subject to limitations, such as recall bias and social desirability bias30. Nevertheless, a similar short FFQ has been validated against a 198-item FFQ, demonstrating that the short version accurately reflects overall dietary patterns31.

Since most patients in our outpatient clinic are well-treated, measures like PASI, BSA, and DLQI likely underestimate the lifetime severity of the disease. Therefore, disease severity was also classified based on the use of systemic treatment. Another limitation of our study concerns the temporal mismatch between cohorts, as CGPS data were collected approximately 10–20 years earlier than the BIOSKIN data. Secular changes in diet, public health awareness, and lifestyle behaviours over this period may have influenced group comparability. If similar data were collected from healthy controls today, they might exhibit a generally healthier dietary pattern, meaning that the true difference between patients with psoriasis and healthy individuals could be even greater than observed in our study. This could potentially lead to a Type II error. Lastly, some variables, particularly blood biomarkers, contain missing data. These variables are presented descriptively in Table 1 but were not included in the main analyses. Therefore, the missing data are not expected to have influenced the overall findings of the study.

In conclusion, in this study of 466 patients with psoriasis and 1,029 healthy controls, we found that patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis eat less healthy than patients with mild disease. No significant difference in adherence to dietary guidelines was observed between patients with psoriasis and healthy controls matched on age, sex, BMI, and smoking status. Further RCTs are needed to provide clear, evidence-based guidelines on diet for patients with psoriasis.
