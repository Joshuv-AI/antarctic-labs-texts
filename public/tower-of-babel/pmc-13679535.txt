Effect of Alveolar Ridge Inclination on Dynamic Navigation Accuracy of Implant Surgery: A Retrospective Clinical Study
Authors: Xuechun Jiang, Ran Zhang, Huiyun Jiang, Ni Zeng, Huimin Nie, Wenjuan Zhou, Zhonghao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the accuracy of dynamic navigation-guided dental implantation system across different alveolar ridge inclinations.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective study included 34 patients (120 implants) who underwent dynamic navigation-assisted dental implantation between January 2021 and December 2025. Deviations at the entry point of the initial drill and pilot drill were recorded to analyze the influence of alveolar ridge inclination on surgical accuracy. Preoperative and postoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans were automatically superimposed using specialized software to calculate deviations between planned and actual implant positions. Angular, entry point, and apex deviations were measured in both two-dimensional and three-dimensional measurements.ResultsThe adjusted GEE analysis showed no significant overall difference in initial drill deviation among the three inclination groups (P=0.062), whereas a significant overall difference was observed in implant entry-point deviation (P=0.023). None of the pairwise comparisons remained statistically significant after FDR correction. In the continuous-angle analysis, ridge angle was significantly associated with initial drill deviation after FDR correction.ConclusionAlveolar ridge inclination was associated with differences in initial drill deviation in dynamic navigation-assisted implant surgery. Patients with high alveolar ridge inclinations exhibit lower accuracy compared with those with low inclinations.Keywords: alveolar ridge inclination, dynamic navigation, dental implants, implant accuracy

Introduction

With the continuous innovation and development of digital technologies, Static Computer-Assisted Implant Surgery (sCAIS), Dynamic Computer-Assisted Implant Surgery (dCAIS), and Robotic-Assisted Implant Surgery (RAIS) have been extensively adopted in the field of implant dentistry.1,2 Compared with sCAIS, dynamic computer-assisted surgery enables clinicians to achieve real-time monitoring of the alveolar osteotomy procedure and implement immediate, precise intraoperative adjustments tailored to the patient’s individual anatomical variations, endowing it with a broader applicability in implant surgical practice.3,4

Although digital technologies have markedly enhanced the accuracy of dental implant placement,5–7 certain errors remain unavoidable in clinical practice. Some literatures indicate that dynamic computer-assisted surgery may be influenced by multiple factors, including registration methods, surgical procedures, and alveolar bone density.8–10 Among these variables, the buccolingual inclination of the alveolar ridge has been reported to be associated with positional deviation at the implant apical level.11,12 Song et al13 conducted a retrospective study involving 314 dental implants placed using sCAIS, dCAIS, or RAIS. The results indicated that when the alveolar bone inclination at the implant site exceeded 45°, both apical and angular deviation increased significantly, with more pronounced discrepancies detected at the molar regions. Therefore, for patients with severe alveolar ridge inclination, more rigorous surgical techniques are required.

However, to date, most studies investigating factors influencing dCAIS have focused on registration and alignment protocols, implant site distribution, alveolar bone density, and clinician proficiency.14,15 Although Song et al13 investigated the influence of alveolar bone inclination in a multifactorial cohort study, the association between alveolar ridge inclination and implant placement accuracy has not been specifically evaluated in dCAIS. In the present study, alveolar ridge inclination was considered the primary exposure, while drilling-related and implant positional deviations were defined as the outcomes. Extraction status and jaw location were considered potential confounding factors. The study is aimed to categorize different alveolar bone morphologies according to the buccolingual inclination, and to evaluate the risk factors affecting the accuracy of dCAIS.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Yantai Stomatological Hospital (approval number: 202403102). All participants enrolled were comprehensively informed verbally and in writing of the purpose of data utilization, research procedures, and the usage range of their anonymized clinical records and CBCT imaging data prior to the initiation of the study. Every patient voluntarily signed standardized written informed consent forms approved by our institutional ethics committee, granting permission for their medical data to be used for this dental implant-related observational research. All personal identifiable information such as full names, resident registration numbers, contact information and residential addresses was permanently erased from the research dataset to strictly protect patient privacy. A total of 34 patients were enrolled and screened between January 2021 and December 2025. This study was conducted in accordance with the 2013 revised Declaration of Helsinki and complied with the STROBE reporting guidelines (Figure 1).

Flowchart of implant enrollment.

Patient Selection

Participants were included if they met the following criteria: (1) received implant placement with the aid of a dynamic navigation system; (2) had complete medical records and available preoperative and postoperative CBCT data; and (3) underwent implant placement in sites without prior bone augmentation.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe systemic diseases that contraindicated implant surgery; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of osteoporosis; (3) intraoperative manual adjustment of implant position due to objective factors; and (4) Intraoperative registration error of the navigation system > 0.5mm.

Classification of Alveolar Ridge Inclination

Based on preoperative imaging evaluation of alveolar ridge morphology at the implant site, all implant sites were classified according to the inclination of the alveolar ridge. The measurement protocol was performed as follows: the long axis of the implant was defined as line L1, which intersects the alveolar bone wall at point A. Lines L2 and L3 were established as the extensions of the implant boundaries on the labial (buccal) and lingual (palatal) sides adjacent to the inclined bone wall, intersecting the bone wall at points B and C respectively. Lines AB and AC were constructed, and the angles formed between AB/AC and L1 measured as angleαand Angleβ (Figure 2).

Lines L2 and L3 represent the extensions of the implant margins on the labial/buccal and lingual/palatal sides adjacent to the inclined bone wall, intersecting the bone wall at points B and C, respectively. Lines AB and AC were constructed, angle α was defined as the angle between AB and L1, and angle β as the angle between AC and L1. (b) Measurement protocol for immediate implant sites: identical to that for non-immediate implant sites.

Baseline characteristics, including implant parameters, implantation site, and surgical protocol, was documented. All alveolar ridge inclination measurements were performed independently by the same examiner. Each implant site was measured three times on separate occasions, and the average of the three measurements was used as the final value for statistical analysis.

Surgical Procedures

All patients underwent routine preoperative data acquisition and virtual implant planning in accordance with a prosthetically driven protocol. Intraoperatively, the entire procedure from osteotomy site localization to final implant placement was performed under dynamic navigation (Dcarer Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) guidance. Postoperative CBCT (NewTom VGi, Bologna, Italy, 90kV, 8mA, 8 s) was routinely obtained for all patients (Figure 4).

Workflow of dynamic computer-assisted implant surgery. (a–f) Preoperative virtual tooth arrangement and implant planning. (g and h) Intraoperative implant placement procedure. (i) Postoperative panoramic image.

Accuracy Assessment

Initial Drill Deviation

During dynamic computer-assisted implant surgery, real-time data were captured by the navigation software. The deviation was automatically recorded when the initial drill was lifted after completion of implant site positioning (Figure 5a).

Intraoperative assessment of the initial drill and pilot drill deviations. (a) Measurement of the initial drill deviation. (b) Measurement of the pilot drill deviation.

Pilot Drill Buccolingual Deviation

Real-time deviations in three sectional planes were documented by the navigation software when the pilot drill was lifted intraoperatively, the buccolingual deviation at the implant entry and apex point were measured on the buccolingual cross-section, defined as the linear distance between the planned and actual entry points at the implant platform (Figure 5b).

Final Implant Position Accuracy

Postoperative CBCT data were imported into the Dcarer® (Digital-Health Care Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) accuracy analysis system. The software automatically performed registration between the preoperative virtual planning data and postoperative CBCT data based on anatomical landmarks. The planned and actual implant positions were identified, and the corresponding deviations were calculated. Implant accuracy was evaluated using the following parameters: entry deviation, apical deviation, angular deviation, horizontal buccolingual and mesiodistal deviations at the entry point, horizontal buccolingual and mesiodistal deviations at the apex, entry depth, and apical depth (Figure 6).

Measurement of overall implant position accuracy. (a) Virtually planned implant position. (b) Parameters employed to evaluate deviations between the planned and actual implant positions. ① entry deviation; ② apex deviation; ③ angular deviation; ④ entry horizontal buccolingual deviation; ⑤ entry horizontal mesiodistal deviation; ⑥ apex horizontal buccolingual deviation; ⑦ apex horizontal mesiodistal deviation; ⑧ entry depth; ⑨ apex depth.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed by a single researcher using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., New York, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Given the presence of multiple potential confounding factors in the experiment, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were applied for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Because multiple implants could be nested within the same patient, patient ID was specified as the clustering variable, an exchangeable working correlation structure was used, and robust standard errors were applied to account for within-patient correlation. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. In addition to the categorical analysis, ridge inclination was modeled as a continuous variable, with effect estimates expressed per 10° increase in measured ridge angle.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 120 implants from 34 patients were enrolled in accordance with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The implants were distributed as follows: 41 implants in the low-inclination group, 43 implants in the moderate-inclination group, and 36 implants in the high-inclination group. The follow-up period ranged from 6 months to 5 years. Up to the last follow-up, the survival rate of all implants were 100%, with no mechanical or biological complications observed. Basic information, including implant characteristics, implantation sites, and surgical protocols, were recorded (Table 1). All implants were selected preoperatively according to the prosthetic requirements and available alveolar bone volume. Detailed implant and crestal characteristics are presented in Table S1.

Initial Drill Deviation

The generalized estimating equations demonstrated no significant differences in initial drill deviation among the three groups (P > 0.05), with median values of 0.700 (0.590,0.950) mm in the high-inclination group, 0.600 (0.410,0.950) mm in the moderate-inclination group, and 0.530 (0.400,0.740) mm in the low-inclination group (Figure 7). Although the unadjusted pairwise comparison between the high- and low-inclination groups yielded a nominally significant difference, this association did not remain statistically significant after FDR correction (adjusted P = 0.459) (Table S2).

Box-plots of the initial drill deviation in three groups. The central line represents the median, the box represents the interquartile range, the whiskers extend to values within 1.5 times the interquartile range, and points beyond the whiskers represent outliers.

In the complementary continuous-angle GEE analysis, each 10° increase in the measured ridge angle was associated with a 0.03-mm decrease in initial drill deviation (β = −0.03, 95% CI: −0.05 to −0.01; P < 0.001; FDR-adjusted P = 0.010) (Figure S1).

Pilot Drill Deviation

The adjusted GEE analysis showed no statistically significant overall differences among the three inclination groups in pilot-drill buccolingual deviation at the entry point (P = 0.263), buccolingual deviation at the apex (P = 0.170), or angular deviation (P = 0.560). Furthermore, none of the corresponding pairwise comparisons remained statistically significant after FDR correction (Figure 8).

Final Implant Position Accuracy

The generalized estimating equations demonstrated significant intergroup differences in implant entry deviation (P = 0.023). The median values was 1.802 (1.420,2.340) mm in the high-inclination group, 1.380 (0.870,2.171) mm in the moderate-inclination group, and 1.050 (0.760,1.671) mm in the low-inclination group. However, none of the subsequent pairwise comparisons remained statistically significant after FDR correction.

No statistically significant overall differences were observed among the three inclination groups for apical deviation (P = 0.069) or angular deviation (P = 0.802). Similarly, no significant overall group differences were detected for the two-dimensional buccolingual, mesiodistal, or depth deviations at either the implant entry point or apex (all P > 0.05). None of the pairwise comparisons for these outcomes remained statistically significant after FDR correction (Figures 9 and 10).

Discussion

The present retrospective study investigated the accuracy of dynamic navigation–assisted dental implant placement in relation to alveolar ridge inclination, and further evaluated the influence of surgical sites and immediate implantation on positional deviation. Under strict dynamic navigation guidance, favorable overall accuracy was achieved in all groups, with a 100% implant survival rate during the follow-up period. These findings confirm that dynamic navigation is a reliable and predictable modality for implant therapy, consistent with previous studies reporting high accuracy and low complication rates for computer-assisted implant surgery.16

Among the included 120 implants, the global deviation at the implant entry point was 1.430 (0.945,2.207) mm, the global deviation at the apex was 1.550 (0.965,2.226) mm, and the angular deviation was 3.000° (1.570°,4.651°). Although the present study reported median values with IQR instead of mean values, the overall magnitude of deviation was generally comparable to that documented in previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses,17 which reported mean entry deviations of approximately 1.020 mm, mean apical deviation of 1.330 mm, and mean angular deviation of 3.590°. Taken together, these findings support the reliability of dynamic navigation workflows in routine clinical practice.

A key finding of this study was that alveolar ridge inclination was associated with implant accuracy, especially at the implant entry level. A significant overall inter-group difference was observed in entry deviation among the low-, moderate-, and high-inclination groups; however, none of the subsequent pairwise comparisons remained statistically significant after FDR correction.

Notably, despite the real-time tracking and visual guidance provided by dynamic navigation systems, the current results indicated that alveolar ridge inclination may still be associated with initial positioning precision, which may subsequently influence the definitive implant position. In the continuous-angle analysis, smaller measured ridge angles, representing more pronounced ridge inclination, were associated with greater initial drill deviation. This observation suggests that ridge morphology may influence the early stage of the drilling trajectory, although the underlying mechanism remains to be clarified.

A possible explanation for these observations is the asymmetric resistance exerted by the inclined alveolar bone wall during osteotomy. Specifically, uneven mechanical resistance on the drill bit across the buccolingual or mesiodistal planes may potentially induce passive deviation toward the side of the bone wall with lower resistance. Although the dynamic navigation system enables real-time tracking of drill position, such mechanical factors may not be completely compensated for by navigation guidance. Especially in the initial positioning stage, minor deviation may potentially increase as drilling depth progresses, ultimately affecting final implant accuracy. However, this proposed mechanism was not directly investigated in the present study and should therefore be regarded as a possible explanation rather than a demonstrated mechanism. This interpretation aligns with evidence from a clinical study, which reported that alveolar bone inclination may affect the accuracy of digitally assisted implant placement.13

Several studies and clinical applications have confirmed that the accuracy of dynamic navigation-guided implant placement is influenced by multiple factors.18,19 Ma F et al20 compared the precision of dynamic navigation systems in immediate versus delayed implantation and reported no significant difference. The present study also found no statistically significant association with implantation timing; however, the limited sample size and marked imbalance in implantation timing mean that this finding should be interpreted cautiously and cannot be considered evidence of equivalence or absence of an effect. Beyond implantation timing, the present study further explored the effect of implant position on navigational accuracy; however, no statistically significant difference in accuracy was observed between implants placed in the maxilla and mandible. This nonsignificant finding should likewise be interpreted cautiously and does not establish that jaw position has no effect on implant placement accuracy. Nevertheless, the literature regarding maxillary-mandibular accuracy differences remains inconsistent. A systematic review and meta-analysis21 reported a significant difference in angular deviation between the maxilla and mandible in implant surgery, while other studies22 failed to replicate this finding in dynamic computer-assisted implantation, which is in line with our observations. This discrepancy may be attributed to confounding variables including surgeon expertise, implant design, and Kennedy classification,23 all of which may modify navigational accuracy.

As a single-center retrospective study, this research has limitations: 1. The angle used to classify alveolar crest inclination may be affected by the relationship among implant diameter, crestal width, and implant macro-design. In the present study, all implant diameters were selected preoperatively based on prosthetic requirements and available alveolar bone volume. Nevertheless, this potential influence should be considered when interpreting the results.2. Although jaw location and immediate versus non-immediate implantation status were included as covariates in the multivariable GEE models, residual confounding from unmeasured or incompletely controlled factors cannot be excluded. 3. No a priori sample-size calculation was performed because this retrospective study included all eligible cases available during the predefined study period. To address this gap, future multi-center prospective studies with stratification by bone density and site region, along with multivariable analysis, are needed to better understand the independent effect of ridge inclination and its interaction with navigation workflow and drilling strategies, providing more rigorous evidence for clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, alveolar ridge inclination was associated with selected accuracy outcomes in dynamic computer-assisted implant surgery. A significant overall difference in implant entry-point deviation was observed among the inclination groups, whereas the overall comparison for initial drill deviation was not statistically significant. In addition, the continuous-angle analysis demonstrated an association between ridge angle and initial drill deviation after adjustment for multiple testing. For sites with pronounced ridge inclination, clinicians should carefully assess three-dimensional ridge morphology on preoperative CBCT images and optimize the implant position during digital planning. Because bone recontouring and specific staged drilling strategies were not directly evaluated in the present study, their potential benefits require further prospective investigation.
