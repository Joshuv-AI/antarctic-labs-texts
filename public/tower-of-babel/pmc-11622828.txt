Why Are Men More Restrained in the Intensive Care Unit?
Authors: Lucy J. Modra, Andrew Casamento
Published: 2024

Many previous studies have reported that women are less likely than men to receive vital organ support in the intensive care unit (ICU) (1–5). This raises the possibility that other aspects of treatment provided in the ICU may also vary according to patient sex. In a study reported in this issue of AnnalsATS, Mehta and colleagues (pp. 1751–1758) examined sex differences in the use of eight best-practice supportive treatments for mechanically ventilated patients, including thromboembolic prophylaxis, stress ulcer prophylaxis, spontaneous breathing trials, and the minimization of sedation and physical restraints (6).

Their retrospective observational study of more than 19,000 patients (37.5% women) from eight ICUs in Ontario, Canada, revealed that most supportive treatments were provided equitably to men and women. However, men were more likely than women to receive continuous sedative infusions and physical restraint during their periods of mechanical ventilation. Men had higher incidence of delirium and were more likely to have periods of either agitation or deep sedation while being ventilated.

This study addresses an important gap in the literature. Although previous studies have examined key interventions provided to critically ill men compared with women, few have examined the supportive care of mechanically ventilated patients. The former examined systemic differences at key decision-making nodes in the treatment of critically ill patients, for example, the decision to admit a patient to the ICU or to provide a risky but potentially lifesaving intervention such as intubation or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. In contrast, the treatments examined by Mehta and colleagues (6) are established best practices that should be provided to most mechanically ventilated patients, with few exceptions (7). In this way, Mehta and colleagues examined sex differences in embedded processes of care in the ICU.

Unlike major interventions such as vital organ support, the provision of these supportive treatments is unlikely to be affected by orders limiting medical treatment. This is important when considering sex differences in treatment, as previous studies demonstrated that women are more likely to have orders limiting medical treatment than men (8, 9). However, an order limiting medical treatment is unlikely to proscribe the provision of supportive care for a patient already receiving mechanical ventilation, so this factor is unlikely to confound the findings of the present study.

It is reassuring that Mehta and colleagues (6) report no sex differences in the provision of most best-practice supportive care interventions, including deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis, stress ulcer prophylaxis, and early mobilization. This suggests gender equity in many processes of care for mechanically ventilated patients.

However, their study also reveals two important differences between women and men receiving mechanical ventilation in the ICU.

First, men had a higher incidence of delirium than women. This observation is consistent with the findings of previous studies suggesting that men are at higher risk of postoperative delirium than women, with the notable exception of cardiac surgery (10, 11). The increased rate of delirium among men may be partly attributed to alcohol or drug withdrawal, as both alcohol and drug use disorders are more common in men than women (12, 13).

Second, men were more likely to receive continuous sedative infusions and mechanical restraint than women. This may be consistent with an overall approach of providing more “aggressive” treatment to men. In this instance, more aggressive treatment equates to lower quality care, as best-practice guidelines recommend minimizing the use of sedation and restraint.

As the authors highlight, there are many possible explanations for these differences. They may be attributed to biologically based sex differences in the metabolism of sedative and psychotropic medication or gendered differences in patient behavior when delirious or distressed. There is some evidence that men and women manifest different phenotypes of delirium, with men more likely to develop hyperactive delirium with motor symptoms and women more likely to develop hypoactive delirium (14). Given that hyperactive delirium is more easily recognized than hypoactive delirium, it is also possible that delirium is simply underdiagnosed in women.

In addition, clinicians may respond differently to agitated behavior in women and men. A recent study of Australian ICU patients showed that rapid response activations for episodes of aggression or violence in critically ill patients were far more likely for male patients than female patients (15). Therefore, it is possible that clinicians believe that male patients need to be more deeply sedated or restrained than female patients to maintain the safety of both ICU patients and their caregivers. Conversely, clinicians may find it less acceptable to impose physical restraint on a female patient than a male patient, providing a relative protective effect for female patients. Either way, it will be important to develop safe and acceptable strategies to facilitate sedation and restraint minimization in mechanically ventilated men.

An interesting question raised by this study is the direction of possible causal association between increased delirium and increased use of sedation and physical restraint in men compared with women. Do men receive more continuous sedative infusions and restraint than women because they are more delirious, or are men more delirious because they are receiving more sedation and physical restraint? Both directions of effect seem plausible. As the authors note, questions of causality are beyond the scope of their retrospective observational study.

In the same way that a patient’s degree of agitation could contribute to the use of continuous sedative infusions and physical restraint, it is possible that agitation contributed to the clinicians’ decision to intubate the patient in the first place. Therefore, the finding that critically ill men had higher rates of agitation than women may have implications beyond the cohort of mechanically ventilated patients examined in this study. Specifically, this finding may help explain the observation in previous studies that men were more likely than women to receive mechanical ventilation, whereas women were more likely to receive noninvasive ventilation (1, 4). Against this theory, Mehta and colleagues’ (6) study revealed that a higher percentage of women than men were intubated primarily for altered conscious state. However, even among patients intubated primarily for another reason, most commonly respiratory failure, agitation and the ability to comply with less invasive treatments can contribute to the decision to intubate. Certainly, a patient who can tolerate noninvasive ventilation and vascular access procedures without sedation is far less likely to be intubated than a patient who cannot tolerate these interventions.

As acknowledged by the authors, there are limitations to this study. Beyond adjusted ICU mortality, there are limited data pertaining to patient outcomes. To better understand the implications of the reported sex differences in the use of sedation and physical restraint, it would be helpful to understand the sex-specific rates of complications such as line dislodgement, post-traumatic stress disorder, and other neuropsychological outcomes in ICU survivors. Furthermore, there were no data on the relative doses of sedatives provided to women and men. This is an important consideration, particularly given sex differences in efficacy of opioids, and the correlation between opioid dose and incidence of delirium (16, 17).

Notwithstanding these limitations, Mehta and colleagues’ (6) comprehensive study examines a range of evidence-based supportive treatments in a broad cross-section of ICU patients, providing an important contribution to our understanding of sex differences in the treatment of critically ill patients.
