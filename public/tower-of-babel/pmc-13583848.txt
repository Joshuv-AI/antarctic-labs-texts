Patient and caregiver lived experiences and mental health service engagement during first-episode psychosis in Uganda: a longitudinal mixed-methods study protocol
Authors: Prisca Oroma, Andrew Sentoogo Ssemata, Wilber Ssembajjwe, Rita Auma, Sophia Balinga, Blessed Tabitha Aujo, Andrea Kaggwa Kaddu, Mary Ampiire, Wilson Winstons Muhwezi, Emmanuel Kiiza Mwesiga, Etheldreda Mpungu Nakimuli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionEngagement with mental health services during first-episode psychosis is critical for symptom control, quality of life and relapse prevention. However, disengagement rates remain high in Uganda, with severe consequences for patients and caregivers. This study protocol describes a mixed-methods investigation that aims to examine the relationship between patient and caregiver lived experiences and mental health service engagement during first-episode psychosis.Methods and analysisThe mixed-methods study will recruit 82 patients with first-episode psychosis and their primary caregivers from Butabika National Referral Mental Hospital in Kampala, Uganda. Inclusion criteria are being aged 18–60 years, having received antipsychotic medication for fewer than 12 weeks and living in the Greater Kampala Metropolitan Area, with a consenting caregiver. Caregivers must be adults (aged ≥18 years) providing full-time care for at least 6 months prior to enrolment. Patients with substance use disorders will be excluded. Qualitative data on the lived experiences of patients and caregivers will be collected using the draw-write-and-tell method, while quantitative data on service engagement and associated factors will be collected using semistructured questionnaires. The quantitative component is exploratory and is intended to identify candidate predictors of engagement for testing in future adequately powered studies. The data will be analysed using Stata V.18, and participants will be reimbursed for their time.Ethics and disseminationEthical clearance has been obtained from the School of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (SOMREC) (reference: Mak-SOMREC-2024-1002) of Makerere University and institutional approval from Butabika National Referral Mental Hospital. All participants will provide informed consent prior to participation. Data will be de-identified and securely stored, with results disseminated through peer-reviewed academic publications, conferences and community stakeholder workshops.Keywords: Caregivers, Schizophrenia & psychotic disorders, Quality Improvement, Patient Care Management, Africa South of the Sahara, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Background

Early engagement with mental healthcare services during the first episode of psychosis is consistently associated with better symptomatic and functional outcomes, including improved quality of life (QOL), reduced disability and lower mortality.1 2 However, continued engagement with mental healthcare services after first contact is often discontinuous, with cycles of disengagement and re-entry that effectively extend the duration of untreated or inadequately treated illness.3–5 In Uganda, these challenges are particularly pronounced. Early pathways to care are frequently shaped by caregivers who initially pursue alternative and complementary therapies before reaching formal services.2 6 7 Patients commonly present late and severely unwell, but even after inpatient stabilisation, disengagement with mental health services is as high as 70%, with high rates of failure to return for follow-up and measurable excess mortality among those who disengage.2 6 The benefits of early psychosis care are only realised when patients and families can enter and remain in care early enough and long enough for those treatments to change long-term trajectories.

Although early intervention in psychosis has been extensively studied in high-income settings, evidence from low- and middle-income countries remains limited. A recent systematic review found that people with first-episode psychosis in low- and middle-income countries often experience delays in receiving treatment, with poorer outcomes and higher mortality, while evidence on early intervention models in these settings remains sparse.8 The limited available evidence suggests that early psychosis care in low- and middle-income countries may require adaptation to local service realities, including collaboration with traditional and informal care systems, involvement of non-specialist providers, use of mobile technologies, attention to medication side effects and family engagement.8 In Uganda, where specialist mental health services are highly centralised and pathways to care often involve family decision-making and alternative treatment providers, understanding how patients and caregivers experience care is essential for improving continuity after first contact.

What remains unclear is why engagement is so unstable in the Ugandan first-episode psychosis context and which levers are most actionable for improving continuity of care. Existing studies have described disengagement rates and associated outcomes,2 6 but their predictors have limited generalisability. The mechanisms by which engagement is produced or lost remain unclear. In particular, the role of patient and caregiver lived experience has not been sufficiently examined. Recent syntheses of lived experiences during first-episode psychosis have identified common thematic domains, but they have largely excluded low-income countries and have not examined how these experiences map onto concrete engagement outcomes (discharge follow-up, retention and re-engagement).6 9–11 It is unclear how patients and their caregivers interpret symptoms, experience stigma, build trust with help systems or how early encounters with both alternative and formal providers shape subsequent choices. As a result, the field lacks a context-sensitive account of how the lived experience of patients and caregivers shapes engagement during first-episode psychosis in Uganda. What remains insufficiently understood is how lived experience, family involvement, symptom interpretation, stigma, alternative care-seeking and early encounters with formal services shape continued engagement after discharge from hospital-based first-episode psychosis care in Uganda. This study addresses that gap by combining longitudinal quantitative assessment of engagement with in-depth qualitative exploration of patient and caregiver experiences.

Aims

This study aims to address this gap by examining patient and caregiver lived experiences during first-episode psychosis and their relationship to engagement with mental health services in Uganda. Specifically, we aim to (1) explore patient and caregiver lived experiences during first-episode psychosis in Uganda; (2) examine mental healthcare services engagement during first-episode psychosis in Uganda; (3) explore associations between duration of untreated psychosis (DUP), prior alternative or complementary care, internalised stigma, antipsychotic side-effect burden and caregiver-treating-team contact and subsequent service engagement as hypothesis-generating analyses for future adequately powered studies and (4) integrate the quantitative and longitudinal qualitative findings to identify plausible mechanisms and targets for future intervention development. Understanding these experiences will inform the design of culturally appropriate interventions to improve early engagement, reduce the DUP and enhance clinical and functional outcomes for both patients and caregivers. The quantitative component is exploratory and hypothesis-generating, identifying candidate predictors rather than estimating causal effects.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study will employ a prospective longitudinal mixed-methods design to examine how patient and caregiver lived experiences influence mental health service engagement during first-episode psychosis. This is an exploratory study that seeks to generate an in-depth understanding of patient and caregiver experiences while examining how service engagement evolves over time following first-episode psychosis in Uganda, an area for which limited longitudinal evidence currently exists.

A convergent mixed-methods approach is appropriate as service engagement is shaped by both measurable clinical and service-related factors and contextual, experiential and relational processes that are best captured qualitatively.12 13 Quantitative and qualitative data will be collected from patients and their caregivers at three time points: at enrolment in care (baseline) and at 3-month and 6-month follow-up clinic visits. The quantitative and qualitative components will be integrated at the interpretation stage to generate complementary insights into patterns and processes of service engagement. The longitudinal design will allow examination of changes in experiences and patterns of service engagement over time and how these evolve during early contact with mental health services. This approach is consistent with mixed-methods guidance on integrating qualitative and quantitative evidence to generate a more complete understanding of complex health service phenomena.13 14

Study setting and site

The study will be conducted at Butabika National Referral Mental Hospital located approximately 13 km east of Kampala, Uganda. Butabika is Uganda’s main national referral psychiatric hospital and plays a central role in the delivery of specialist mental health services, training and policy direction. The hospital receives patients from across the country and serves as the primary referral centre for individuals experiencing their first-episode psychosis. The research team has prior experience recruiting and following patients with first-episode psychosis at this site, supporting the feasibility of longitudinal follow-up.2 5–7 15–20 As a national referral facility, Butabika provides an appropriate setting for exploring patient and caregiver lived experiences of mental healthcare engagement within Uganda’s public mental health system.

Participants

Participants will be recruited from patients attending the Psychosis Clinic and the convalescent inpatient wards at Butabika National Referral Mental Hospital prior to discharge following admission for first-episode psychosis. The convalescent wards admit patients who have moved beyond the acute phase of illness and are clinically stable enough to participate in consent and baseline assessment procedures. Following discharge, participants will be followed through the hospital’s outpatient psychosis clinic and scheduled review visits. The study will therefore recruit patients during inpatient stabilisation and assess subsequent engagement with outpatient mental health services after discharge. Enrolment is open to all patients reporting to the clinic and diagnosed with first-episode psychosis. Eligible patients will be identified during routine clinical care and approached by trained research staff for study participation. Caregivers of participating patients will also be invited to take part in the study, contingent on patient consent. Recruitment commenced in 2025 and is expected to continue until June 2026, with longitudinal follow-up conducted at 3-month and 6-month post-enrolment.

Participant eligibility criteria

Patients will be eligible for inclusion if they meet all of the following criteria: (a) are aged 18–60 years; (b) have a diagnosis of a psychotic disorder confirmed using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI), a validated structured diagnostic interview21; (c) are experiencing first-episode psychosis, defined as the first admission for a psychotic disorder; (d) have received antipsychotic medication for less than 12 weeks at the time of enrolment22 23; (e) are admitted to the convalescent wards of Butabika Hospital; (f) reside within the Greater Kampala Metropolitan Area, to facilitate follow-up and (g) have a caregiver willing to participate in the study. Patients with a current substance use disorder as determined by MINI will be excluded. This exclusion is intended to reduce clinical heterogeneity and allow focused examination of mental health service engagement trajectories among individuals with first-episode psychosis.24

Caregivers will be eligible for inclusion if they (a) are the primary caregiver responsible for the participating patient; (b) are aged 18 years or older; (c) have lived with the patient full-time for at least 6 months prior to hospital admission and (d) have provided written informed consent to participate.

Study procedure for the quantitative component

Sampling and recruitment

Butabika National Referral Mental Hospital is the national psychiatric referral hospital.18 Given this context, the study will recruit a consecutive sample of eligible patients presenting to the psychosis clinic and admitted to the convalescent wards during the recruitment period. Members of the research team with clinical experience in managing patients with first-episode psychosis will be trained in study procedures. In collaboration with the hospital records team, the research team will screen admission registers to identify patients who meet the eligibility criteria. Eligible patients will be approached during their admission to receive information about the study, undergo eligibility confirmation and provide written informed consent. Following patient consent, caregivers will be contacted to assess their willingness to participate. Caregivers who consent will be invited to complete baseline assessments, typically on or near the patient’s day of discharge.

Baseline assessments:

At enrolment (baseline), quantitative data will be collected from patients and caregivers to document sociodemographic, clinical and psychosocial characteristics using standardised measures (table 1). These baseline variables will serve as potential predictors of subsequent mental healthcare service engagement.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

After discharge, study staff will liaise with the hospital records department to track attendance at scheduled outpatient review visits. Mental healthcare service engagement will be assessed using the Service Engagement Scale (SES), a clinician-rated measure of patient and caregiver engagement with mental health services.25 Participants who attend their scheduled review visits will be assessed using the SES during routine clinic encounters. Participants who fail to attend a scheduled review within 1 week of the appointment date will be assigned an SES score of 0 for that time point, indicating non-engagement. Follow-up assessments will be conducted at 3-month and 6-month post-enrolment, regardless of whether participants have attended their scheduled clinic visits, to ensure complete longitudinal data capture. Given evidence that family contact with early psychosis services may influence patient engagement, caregiver-treating-team contact will be assessed as a potential predictor of service engagement. This will be measured using a structured contact log documenting whether the caregiver attended discharge planning, accompanied the patient to follow-up visits, received telephone contact from the treating team or initiated contact with the team.

Sample size justification

As this is an exploratory longitudinal mixed-methods study, the quantitative sample size is primarily based on feasibility, expected patient flow at Butabika National Referral Mental Hospital and the need to generate sufficiently precise estimates of service engagement over the 6-month follow-up period. Based on prior recruitment experience with first-episode psychosis at the study site, we estimate that 82 eligible patient-caregiver dyads can be recruited within the study period. The quantitative recruitment target is based primarily on feasibility and expected patient flow at Butabika National Referral Mental Hospital. Previous recruitment experience indicates that approximately 82 eligible patient-caregiver dyads can be enrolled during the study period. The study is not powered for confirmatory regression or causal inference. If 82 patients provide paired baseline and 6-month SES data, a two-sided test with alpha=0.05 and 90% power would detect a standardised within-person mean change of approximately 0.36. This corresponds to approximately 2.8 points on the raw 0–42 SES only if the SD of the within-person change score is 7.7 points (equivalent to 0.55 on the mean-item scale). Because loss to follow-up will reduce the number of complete pairs, the observed precision may be lower. The proportion retained at 6 months will be reported with a 95% CI, but the study is not presented as powered to estimate candidate-factor associations.

This sample size is considered adequate to detect meaningful differences in mental healthcare service engagement over time. This sample will allow estimation of the proportion of participants retained in care at 6 months with acceptable precision and will provide sufficient data for exploratory regression models examining associations between baseline clinical, psychosocial and service-related factors and subsequent engagement. The study is not powered to estimate candidate-factor associations definitively. Any such analyses are exploratory and intended to identify signals and mechanisms for testing in future adequately powered studies. The quantitative findings will be interpreted alongside longitudinal qualitative data to identify potentially actionable predictors and mechanisms of engagement for future intervention development.

Operational definition of mental healthcare service engagement

For this study, mental healthcare service engagement refers to the extent to which a patient and caregiver remain actively connected to formal mental healthcare following first contact for first-episode psychosis. Engagement will include attendance at scheduled post-discharge review visits, availability for follow-up, collaboration with the treating team, help-seeking when symptoms or crises occur, treatment adherence and ongoing caregiver involvement in care. Quantitatively, engagement will be assessed using the SES at routine clinic visits and at 3-month and 6-month follow-up. Non-attendance will be defined as the failure to attend a scheduled outpatient review visit within 7 days of the appointment date without documented alternative contact, rescheduling or clinical follow-up. Re-engagement will be defined as renewed contact with the mental health service after a missed appointment or period of non-attendance.

Primary quantitative outcome

The primary outcome is mental healthcare service engagement, measured using the SES.25 The SES is a 14-item clinician-rated instrument assessing four domains of engagement: availability, collaboration, help-seeking and treatment adherence. Raw SES total scores range from 1 to 42, with higher raw scores indicating greater difficulty engaging with services. For ease of interpretation, we will report both the raw SES score and, where appropriate, a reversed engagement score calculated as 42 minus the raw SES score, with higher reversed scores indicating stronger engagement. A raw SES score will not be assigned solely based on non-attendance. Where the information required to complete the clinician-rated SES is unavailable, the score will be recorded as missing for that time point, and attendance will be reported separately.

Secondary quantitative outcomes

Secondary outcomes include disability-related indicators (functional impairment, QOL and medication side effects), mortality (all-cause mortality and suicide), hospital readmission and treatment adherence.

Quantitative predictor variables

Predictor variables include DUP, use of alternative and complementary therapies, diagnostic category, stigma, symptom profile and severity, physical and metabolic health indicators, medication side effects and sociodemographic characteristics such as age and sex.

Qualitative component

The qualitative component will employ a longitudinal qualitative design informed by Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis to explore patient and caregiver lived experiences during first-episode psychosis and how these experiences shape engagement with mental healthcare services over time. A participatory visual method, specifically draw-write-and-tell, will be combined with in-depth interviews to generate rich, participant-led reflection on illness onset, care-seeking pathways, experiences of treatment, stigma, family relationships and recovery.26

Draw-write-and-tell technique

This is a participatory visual method that complements traditional qualitative approaches by enabling participants to express experiences, emotions and meanings that may be difficult to articulate verbally.26 27 The draw-write-and-tell method is particularly suited to first-episode psychosis contexts, as the technique facilitates reflection on personal and socially situated experiences and supports the exploration of sensitive topics such as illness perceptions, care-seeking trajectories and treatment experiences. The method enables participants to externalise experiences through visual representation and short written reflections, which are then explored through narrative interviews. Combining visual outputs (drawings and written narratives) with narrative interviews allows participants to guide the discussion, facilitates participant-led meaning-making and supports in-depth exploration of subjective experiences. A longitudinal qualitative design will allow examination of how illness perceptions, care-seeking experiences and service engagement evolve over time, particularly during the critical early treatment period.

Participant selection

Participants for the qualitative component will be purposively selected from individuals enrolled in the quantitative cohort. A maximum variation sampling strategy will be employed to capture a diverse range of experiences across gender, age group, diagnosis, DUP and level of service engagement. Approximately 20 patients with first-episode psychosis will be recruited at baseline. This sample size is informed by the concept of information power, whereby a smaller sample is appropriate when the study aim is focused, the sample is specific and in-depth longitudinal data are generated. The longitudinal design, involving repeated interviews, is expected to yield substantial analytic depth at the individual case level. In addition, a purposive subsample of caregivers (approximately 15–20) will be recruited to participate in longitudinal in-depth interviews to explore caregiving experiences and perspectives on patient engagement with care. Sampling will remain iterative. Recruitment will continue until sufficient variation is achieved to address the research objectives. This strategy will allow the study to capture a broad range of experiences related to illness onset, care-seeking pathways and engagement with mental health services, thereby enhancing the depth, richness and analytical value of the qualitative data.

Data collection procedures

Baseline data collection

At baseline, participants will be provided with a blank A3 sheet of paper and drawing materials (crayons, pencils or pens) and invited to draw and/or write about their experiences during the onset and early phase of psychosis, including how they understood what was happening, how they sought help and what engaging with services has meant to them. Participants will be informed that artistic ability is not required and that they may express themselves in any format they prefer. The emphasis will be placed on meaning with no expectation of artistic ability. Following the drawing or writing exercise, participants will take part in an unstructured, in-depth interview. The interview will begin with an open-ended invitation such as ‘Can you tell me about what you have drawn/written and what it means to you?’ The participant’s narrative will guide the discussion. Interviews will explore what living with first-episode psychosis means to them; their recollections of illness onset and early experiences; pathways to care and how they arrived at the mental healthcare facility; experiences of receiving care at the facility and key challenges or struggles encountered and their hopes and fears for the future. This participant-led approach ensures that the meanings attached to visual outputs remain grounded in participants’ own interpretations. Caregiver interviews will follow a semistructured format exploring recognition of symptoms, help-seeking decisions, caregiving burden, perceptions of services and factors influencing sustained engagement. Interviews will be conducted in the participant’s preferred language, audio-recorded with consent and last approximately 60–90 min. Field notes will be documented to capture contextual observations and researcher reflections.

Longitudinal follow-up

Participants will be provided with rough sketchbooks and encouraged to continue drawing or writing about their experiences at home between study visits. These materials will serve as reflective tools and prompts for subsequent interviews. Patient and caregiver participants will be re-interviewed at 3 and 6 months following the baseline interviews. During these interviews, participants will be invited to reflect on any drawings or writings produced since the previous visit and to discuss changes in their experiences, perceptions of care and engagement with mental health services over time. Follow-up interviews will explore changes over time in illness perceptions, experiences of treatment and service engagement, adherence and discontinuation, social support and stigma, functional recovery and future outlook. This process will support exploration of evolving illness narratives and trajectories of engagement.

This longitudinal approach will enable the study to capture evolving experiences and meanings associated with illness and service engagement during the early course of psychosis. The qualitative findings will provide contextualised, in-depth insights into how patients and caregivers understand first-episode psychosis, navigate care pathways and experience mental health services. When integrated with quantitative findings, this component will strengthen interpretation of service engagement outcomes and inform the development of more responsive and patient-centred mental health interventions.

Retention strategy

We will use various strategies to reduce loss to follow-up, a significant problem in this population.2 First, telephone follow-ups by research assistants will remind discharged patients of interviews.28 Furthermore, including family members will improve retention, as family involvement strongly predicts a return to care.6

Data analysis

Quantitative data analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants will be summarised using means and SDs (or medians and IQRs for skewed distributions) for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Potential confounders will be identified a priori, including age, sex and welfare status, and will be adjusted for in the main outcome analysis. The primary outcome is mental healthcare service engagement, measured using the SES, calculated as the mean score across the 3-month and 6-month follow-up visits. Secondary outcomes include functional impairment, QOL (assessed using the WHO QOL-Brief version), medication adherence, readmission and all-cause mortality. Covariates will include sociodemographic, clinical, welfare, stigma, economic and functional variables. Change over time will be described directly as the mean within-person change from baseline to 3 months, from 3 to 6 months and from baseline to 6 months, each with a 95% CI, alongside the number and proportion of participants whose scores improve, worsen or remain unchanged between visits. Individual trajectories will be plotted against the group median, and engagement will be cross-tabulated by attendance status so that the characteristics of those lost to follow-up are visible. Each pre-specified factor will be examined in a separate regression model adjusted for age, sex, welfare status and baseline symptom severity, with estimates reported as effect sizes with 95% CIs. Exploratory candidate-factor analyses will be conducted one factor at a time, without an omnibus model or data-driven variable selection. All analyses will be conducted using Stata V.18, and statistical significance will be set at p<0.05.

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative data, including visual artefacts (drawings and written reflections), interview transcripts and field notes, will be analysed using Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA), an approach suited to examining how people make sense of major life experiences, including illness, recovery, care-seeking and identity disruption.29 30 IPA is appropriate for this study because first-episode psychosis is a profound and often disorienting experience for both patients and caregivers, and the study seeks to understand how participants interpret symptoms, services, caregiving, stigma and recovery over time.

Audio recordings will be transcribed verbatim and translated into English where necessary. Transcripts will be checked against audio files for accuracy.

Data will be managed and coded using NVivo. Visual artefacts will be scanned, catalogued and linked to the corresponding interview transcript using participant identifiers. The analysis will focus on participants’ own explanations of their drawings and written reflections; visual materials will not be interpreted independently of participant narratives.

Analysis will proceed in four stages. First, each transcript and associated visual material will be read repeatedly to develop an idiographic understanding of the participant’s account. Second, exploratory notes will be developed on experiential claims, meanings, emotions and contextual features. Third, emergent themes will be generated within each case. Fourth, cross-case analysis will examine convergences and divergences across patients and caregivers and across baseline, 3-month and 6-month interviews. Longitudinal case summaries will be developed to examine how illness meanings, service experiences and engagement trajectories evolve over time.

Rigour and trustworthiness

Analyst triangulation will be employed, with at least two researchers independently reviewing selected transcripts and visual materials. Reflexive team discussions will be conducted to examine interpretive assumptions. An audit trail will be maintained documenting analytic decisions. Visual artefacts will not be interpreted independently of participants’ narratives; meaning attribution will remain grounded in participants’ explanations.

Triangulation of quantitative and qualitative data

A convergent mixed-methods approach will be adopted to integrate quantitative and qualitative findings. Quantitative data on mental healthcare service engagement and covariates will be triangulated with qualitative themes to develop a comprehensive understanding of patient and caregiver experiences. This approach will allow identification of patterns and mechanisms underlying engagement. This understanding allows new theoretical propositions about engagement to be developed for future implementation studies and interventions in mental health service delivery.

Data management plan and quality control

Quantitative data

Data will be captured electronically using Open Data Kit, ensuring real-time uploading and secure storage on a password-protected server. Each participant’s assessments will be linked to a unique identifier to consolidate all data within the same folder. Datasets will be anonymised prior to analysis and securely backed up on encrypted institutional servers. Access will be restricted to authorised members of the core research team. Any data shared beyond the study team will be de-identified and shared only in accordance with ethical approvals, participant consent and institutional data governance procedures. Access to electronic data will be restricted to the core research team, all of whom will have received training in study procedures and completed certifications in ethical research conduct. Data integrity will be ensured through regular checks for completeness, consistency and accuracy.

Qualitative data

All interviews and draw-write-and-tell sessions will be audio-recorded using encrypted digital recorders. Audio files will be transferred daily to a secure, password-protected server and backed up on a second encrypted drive. Drawings and written materials from draw-write-and-tell exercises will be scanned and stored as PDF or image files in the same secure folder linked to the participant’s unique identifier.

Audio recordings will be transcribed verbatim, with transcripts checked for accuracy by a second team member. Drawings/writings will be coded and catalogued systematically, ensuring they are linked to the correct participant while maintaining anonymity. Identifiable information will be removed from transcripts and images prior to analysis.

Access and oversight

Only core research team members will have access to the qualitative and quantitative data. All team members will have training in ethical research conduct, including confidentiality and secure data handling. Any paper-based notes collected during interviews will be stored in locked cabinets, accessible only to authorised personnel.

Quality assurance

To maintain rigour, all qualitative coding will be conducted in duplicate, with discrepancies resolved through team discussion. Regular audit checks will verify that transcripts, drawings and recordings are complete, anonymised and stored according to protocol. This ensures integrity, confidentiality and reproducibility of both quantitative and qualitative data.

Study governance, stakeholder engagement and community oversight

A governance council will oversee all study activities to ensure ethical, safe and contextually appropriate conduct alongside the research team. The council will be led by people with lived experience from Heart to Heart Spaces (https://hearttoheartspaces.com/), a Ugandan mental health community providing safe spaces for individuals to share their experiences, coping mechanisms and recovery strategies. The council will review the study protocol and data collection tools prior to study initiation and observe the piloting of procedures to suggest refinements aimed at improving data quality, participant engagement and retention. Heart to Heart Spaces has been involved in the design of the protocol and will continue to advise on participant engagement and the ethical conduct of the study. The council will ensure the study aligns with community perspectives, promoting accountability, transparency and ethical responsiveness throughout the research process.

Assessing the capacity to consent and informed consent

The University of California, San Diego Brief Assessment of Capacity to Consent (UBACC) instrument will assess whether participants have understood the consent process, including the risks and benefits of the study.15 It is a 10-item scale comprising 3 factors that evaluate understanding, appreciation and reasoning. Scores of less than 14.5 on three occasions indicate that the participant has not understood and will not be enrolled. The UBACC has been translated into Luganda and previously used in this setting.15

Given the vulnerability of individuals experiencing first-episode psychosis, capacity to provide informed consent will be assessed in collaboration with the clinical team prior to participation. Participation will occur only when individuals are clinically stable and able to provide informed consent. Clinical stability will be determined by the treating clinical team and will refer to patients who are no longer acutely agitated or severely disorganised, are able to participate in a conversation about the study and demonstrate capacity to provide informed consent using the UBACC. Interviews will be conducted in private spaces within the facility to ensure confidentiality.

Given the length of the quantitative and qualitative assessments, participants will be offered breaks during all study procedures, including quantitative assessments, draw-write-and-tell activities and interviews. Assessments may be paused, shortened, rescheduled or discontinued if a participant becomes tired, distressed or clinically unstable. Participants may decline to answer any question and may withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their care. If distress or risk is identified, the session will be paused, and the participant will be referred to the attending clinical team for appropriate support.

Confidentiality and access to data

All study data, including quantitative assessments, qualitative interview recordings and participant drawings/writings, will be securely stored on password-protected servers and linked to unique participant IDs. Only the core research team will have access and all identifiers will be removed from datasets used for analysis. No individual-level data will be included in publications or presentations.

Dissemination policy

Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications, conference presentations and community reports. Patients, caregivers and the Heart to Heart Spaces community will be involved in sharing results in formats accessible to service users. The study team will retain full independence in deciding the content and timing of publications, without involvement of any external sponsor.

Data availability

Deidentified quantitative and qualitative data will be made available to researchers on reasonable request, in line with ethical approvals and participant consent. Qualitative drawings and writings will be shared in a de-identified form to protect participant confidentiality.

Patient and public involvement

The study was developed in collaboration with Heart to Heart Spaces, a mental health community of lived-experience experts, who contributed to the study design and will serve on the governance committee to protect patient interests. They will also support participant follow-up and dissemination of study findings to ensure that outcomes are relevant and accessible to service users. A subset of patient and caregiver participants will be invited to provide feedback on preliminary qualitative findings to enhance interpretive validity and ensure that themes accurately reflect lived experiences. At the dissemination stage, findings will be shared with patient and caregiver groups through stakeholder meetings at the study site. A plain-language summary of findings will be developed and made available to participants and community stakeholders. The research team will also engage with Heart to Heart Spaces to support broader dissemination and translation of findings into practice.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval has been obtained from the School of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (SOMREC) of Makerere University (reference Mak-SOMREC-2024-1002) and administrative approval from the Butabika Hospital Institutional Review Board. Potential participants will have the opportunity to ask questions before providing written informed consent, demonstrating an understanding of the study procedures. Participants who are unable to provide informed consent due to acute illness will be approached only after they have regained the capacity to consent, in accordance with approved ethical procedures.

Findings from this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed academic publications, conference presentations, stakeholder meetings, policy briefs and feedback sessions with participating communities and health facilities. The results are expected to inform improvements in mental health service delivery for individuals experiencing first-episode psychosis in Uganda.

Discussion

This longitudinal mixed-methods study will explore patient and caregiver lived experiences and their relationship to engagement with mental healthcare services during first-episode psychosis in Uganda. By combining quantitative assessment of service engagement with participatory qualitative methods, including the draw-write-and-tell technique, the study will provide nuanced insights into factors influencing engagement, such as stigma, alternative treatment-seeking and systemic barriers.

Engaging both patients and caregivers and incorporating lived-expert perspectives through Heart to Heart Spaces strengthens the study’s relevance and aims to reduce attrition, a common challenge in first-episode psychosis research. Triangulation of qualitative and quantitative data will support a comprehensive understanding of patterns of disengagement and re-engagement, enabling identification of actionable strategies to enhance early intervention, continuity of care and patient-centred decision-making.

While the study is limited by its single-site design and the exclusion of patients with comorbid substance use disorders, these measures help focus on mental healthcare service engagement in a defined population and reduce clinical heterogeneity. Findings from this study will inform culturally relevant interventions to improve service access, treatment adherence and early support for patients and caregivers. Other potential limitations include the single-site design, which may limit generalisability beyond the Greater Kampala area, and the exclusion of patients with comorbid substance use disorders, which may affect the representativeness of findings. Nevertheless, embedding input from people with lived experience and systematic follow-up strategies aims to minimise attrition and enhance the validity and relevance of the study findings.

Implications

This study will generate evidence on how patients and caregivers experience first-episode psychosis and how these experiences shape engagement with mental health services during the critical early treatment period. By integrating longitudinal quantitative assessment with participatory qualitative inquiry, the study will identify clinical, relational, social and service-related factors that may support or undermine continuity of care after hospital discharge. The findings will inform the development of culturally responsive, patient-centred and caregiver-centred strategies to strengthen early engagement, reduce disengagement and improve continuity of care for people experiencing first-episode psychosis in Uganda and similar low-resource mental health systems.
