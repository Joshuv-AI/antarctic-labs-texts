Investigating a role for NSAIDs in the pathophysiology of vaccine-associated myocarditis

Buoninfante et al.1 provide a comprehensive overview of possible mechanisms involved in the development of myocarditis following COVID-19 vaccination. They also suggest next steps to further understand the pathophysiology of this disease; however, one common factor has been overlooked.

As myocarditis most often occurs in adolescent and young adult males, medications have not played a prominent role in this investigation as medication use in this age group is uncommon; however, there is one notable exception: use of antipyretics. The second dose of the primary vaccination has been shown to be associated with a higher risk of developing myocarditis compared to the first dose. It is also known that the second dose is more symptomatic, and therefore, it is more likely that one might use an antipyretic to control fever, headache, myalgia, etc. Similarly, myocarditis is more common following the first dose of the smallpox vaccine, which, conversely, is usually more symptomatic than the second dose2. Yet, investigating these drugs as possible suspects is problematic. One of the obstacles is that most case reports, while discussing symptoms at presentation for myocarditis (usually three or more days after vaccination), generally fail to mention symptoms that directly followed vaccination—and in that same vein—fail to report whether antipyretics were used or not3.

The authors of the present review discuss how “myocarditis animal models could provide valuable information”, with mention of the coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) animal model in particular. As it turns out, potentially relevant research has already been conducted in this model of myocarditis, where several non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)—ibuprofen, aspirin, and indomethacin—were investigated. NSAIDs are a class of drugs that reduce pain, inflammation, fever, with aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, celecoxib, and diclofenac being some common types used in the US. Findings from these studies demonstrated that administration of NSAIDs early during the course of CVB34,5 and CVB46 infection in mice exacerbated myocarditis and increased mortality, while those administered later had no effects7 or lesser deleterious effects8. Of course, many patients with post-vaccine myocarditis have been treated with NSAIDs—as in the past, children who developed Reye syndrome often subsequently received aspirin— but this does not override its potential role as a trigger for this disease. While structurally quite different, the NSAIDs studied to date—aspirin, ibuprofen and indomethacin—all similarly inhibit the activity of COX enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2). By inhibiting COX enzymes, these NSAIDs also reduce the production of prostaglandins; however, further animal research is required to determine if this common mechanism can aggravate myocarditis.

Further support for these animal models comes from research examining data from the World Health Organization pharmacovigilance database. In a 2022 study, Nyugen et al.9 identified classes of drugs most associated with myocarditis. Vaccines ranked second—though this data did not include the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines—and salicylates ranked fourth. Unfortunately, what has not been explored is whether there is potential synergism between the two. In fact, the data to effectively answer this question has not been consistently collected. Synergism, however, is a critical factor; aspirin alone does not cause Reye syndrome, nor would it be likely to occur following an acute viral infection alone, such as influenza B or chickenpox; however, the combination of the two, in rare cases, results in encephalopathy with fatty liver.

By continuing to neglect recording and reporting on antipyretic use, the role these drugs may play in the development of rare adverse events cannot be determined. This is no different from the discovery of aspirin’s role in the development of Reye syndrome. It was often overlooked as it was a commonly used drug, and this syndrome was a rare occurrence. However, early on aspirin was suspected to be a factor in the development of this syndrome10,11; yet, only by systematically collecting data in each case and establishing whether aspirin was used or not, and if so, at what dose and frequency, and then comparing it to a matched control population, was an association discovered— although it took nearly two decades before such studies were finally carried out. Sadly, for antipyretics today, this standard is not being met. Some of the most commonly used drugs in this population need to be ruled out as a factor in this disease. While indomethacin would not be commonly used in these cases, ibuprofen use in contrast could be frequent. Furthermore, while acetaminophen (which is not an NSAID) has rarely been associated with cases of myocarditis12, it should not be discounted when investigating potential risk factors associated with this novel clinical entity.

Many epidemiological studies have been conducted to determine if the COVID mRNA vaccines increase the risk of myocarditis, but imagine if the vast majority of patients were missing data on whether they have received the vaccine or not—how could a link possibly be established? This is the position we are in with NSAIDs, but also non-NSAID antipyretics, such as paracetamol and metamizole. It is critical that data on usage is collected so that we can determine whether these drugs play a role in myocarditis following mRNA vaccination, as well as COVID-19 infection. Finally, in agreement with the authors, further investigations using myocarditis animal models would be helpful, in particular, looking at whether early administration of NSAIDs may exacerbate myocarditis or other adverse events.
