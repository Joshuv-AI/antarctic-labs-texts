Plasma SQSTM1/p62 act as a biomarker for steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head
Authors: Wenyuan Hou, Peng Peng, Fangjun Xiao, Jiaqing Tian, Xianshun He, Shun Lu, Huan Xiao, Mincong He, Qiushi Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutophagy is closely associated with the onset and progression of steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SIONFH). SQSTM1/p62 is an important indicator of autophagic activity. The aim of this study was to investigate the role of SQSTM1/p62 in the development of SIONFH. From May 2021 through November 2021, 36 patients diagnosed with SIONFH and 36 healthy controls were recruited for this study. Evaluations included imaging and pathologic assessment of clinical bone tissue, location and level of SQSTM1/p62 expression, plasma SQSTM1/p62 levels, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. We observed that the expression level of SQSTM1/p62 in bone samples decreased with the Association Research Circulation Osseous (ARCO) phase. Plasma SQSTM1/p62 levels were significantly higher in the SIONFH group compared to healthy controls. Plasma SQSTM1/p62 levels were higher in pre-crash patients than in post-crash patients, and lower plasma SQSTM1/p62 levels were associated with elevated ARCO stage. Plasma SQSTM1/p62 may represent a potential biomarker for different stages during SIONFH. Lower plasma SQSTM1/p62 levels indicate an advanced stage of SIONFH. This study provides new clues for early diagnosis of SIONFH.Keywords: Steroid-induced necrosis of the femoral head, SQSTM1/p62, Autophagy, CollapseSubject terms: Diagnostic markers, Predictive markers

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of femoral head (ONFH) is caused by interruption or impairment of the blood supply to the femoral head, resulting in the death of bone cells and bone marrow components, and leading to alteration of the femoral head structure and collapse of the femoral head, which in turn leads to joint pain and dysfunction1,2. The number of ONFH patients worldwide is increasing every year3–5. An estimated 20,000 new cases of osteonecrosis are diagnosed annually in the United States, while in China, the number of non-traumatic ONFH patients reaches 8.12 million6. The exact etiology of ONFH is unknown, and steroid use is a common causative factor, accounting for 51% of ONFH cases7. At present, the specific pathogenesis of steroid-induced ONFH (SIONFH) has not been fully elucidated to prevent the occurrence of SIONFH or to target it for early treatment, so it is important to explore the specific pathogenesis.

There is growing evidence that dysregulated autophagy is associated with the development of SIONFH8,9. Autophagy is a self-protective mechanism to stabilize the internal environment in response to various stressors such as oxidative stress, hypoxia, and starvation, which are cellular cycling processes10,11. As one of the forms of programmed cell death, it engulfs and degrades damaged organelles12. Under pathological conditions, excessive autophagy promotes cell death in normal tissues13. Current research suggests that autophagy is also a fundamental process in cardiovascular homeostasis, health and aging14,15. To the best of our knowledge, little is known about the role of autophagy in SIONFH, despite its protective role in various diseases. Exploring and unraveling potential autophagy-related genes and autophagy levels in SIONFH, as well as functional links to molecular processes related to bone pathophysiology, may provide potential biomarkers for assessing and monitoring disease risk and prognosis.

SQSTM1/p62 (hereafter called p62) can participate in the process of autophagosome membrane formation through binding of its own structural domain to ubiquitinated substrates, autophagosomes, and is degraded as an autophagic substrate16. p62 levels are often used as a biological indicator to assess autophagic processes, with lower levels indicating stronger autophagy17. To date, p62 has been widely used as a biomarker for a variety of chronic diseases, cancers, and cardiovascular diseases, among many others18,19. However, the possible involvement of p62 in the collapse of SIONFH remains unexplored. Here, we hypothesized that plasma p62 might be associated with disease progression in SIONFH. In the present study, we determined the correlation between plasma p62 concentrations and clinical features at different stages of the disease to explore its role in SIONFH.

Materials and methods

Study population

36 patients diagnosed with SIONFH were admitted to the Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine between May and December 2021. Additionally, 36 healthy individuals served as control subjects. Diagnosis of SIONFH was based on medical history, physical examination, and X-ray and MRI findings. Patients had been receiving 2 g of prednisone or an equivalent steroid for a minimum of 3 months, with diagnosis occurring within the preceding 2 years20. All patients were classified according to the ARCO staging system6. Inclusion criteria comprised: (a) Patients classified as stage II, III, or IV according to the ARCO staging; (b) Adult patients aged between 18 and 65 years. Exclusion criteria included: (a) Patients with concurrent large joint pathologies; (b) Patients with chronic comorbidities such as osteoporosis, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, etc. Healthy control subjects had no history of hip pain or steroid use and presented normal anteroposterior pelvic and frog leg radiographs.

Sample collection

Approximately 5 ml of peripheral venous blood was collected from fasting subjects in both groups, centrifuged at 1000×g for 15 min after natural coagulation at 4 °C, and serum was extracted and stored at − 80 °C. Femoral head specimens were obtained post-total hip arthroplasty (THA). Samples were collected from the subchondral necrotic area or healthy bone, approximately 1–3 mm below the cartilage, for analysis.

H&E staining of bone tissue

Bone specimens from necrotic areas of both groups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution at room temperature for 24 h. Sequential decalcification, dehydration, and paraffin embedding were performed. 5-μm sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a microscope (BX53, Olympus Corporation, Japan). Five randomly chosen high-magnification fields were used to count bone gaps, with the percentage of voids indicating the total apoptotic cell rate. Percentage of voids = (number of voids/number of bone gaps) × 100%.

Immunohistochemistry of p62

Paraffin section (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized, hydrated, antigen-repaired, and immunohistochemically stained for p62 expression using the En Vision two-step staining technique. Staining results were observed and captured under a light microscope (3DHISTEC, Pannoramic MIDI). Image J software was used for quantitative analysis, with the percentage of positive areas serving as a quantitative index of p62 and comparison.

Immunofluorescence of p62

Following dewaxing, antigen repair, and serum closure of 5 um thick sections, primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times, sections were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature and stained with DAPI nuclear stain. Image J software was utilized to calculate the p62 positive cell rate. Positive cell rate = (number of positive cells/total number of cells) × 100%.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for p62

Serum p62 protein concentration was determined via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using a kit from Cusabio, Wuhan, Hubei Province, China, following kit instructions.

Micro CT scan

Bone specimens were scanned using NEMO Micro CT. Avatar 3.0 software was used to observe changes in femoral head shape, quantitative analysis of bone microstructure, and 3D reconstruction.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated independently at least three times. SPSS 23.0 statistical software was employed for data analysis. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Independent samples t-test and X2 test were used for continuous and categorical data, respectively, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics of cases in both groups

A total of 36 patients were recruited in the SIONFH group with a mean age of 38.19 ± 12.73 years. In the control group, 36 patients were recruited with a mean age of 43.15 ± 11.57 years. No age difference was observed (Student’s t-test, P > 0.05).There were 14 males and 19 females in the SIONFH group, and 12 males and 14 females in the control group. The number of patients with stage II ONFH was 15, stage III 14 and stage IV 7, respectively.

Radiographic and pathologic evaluation of bone tissue

Early radiographs of femoral head necrosis were not obvious, but as the condition progresses, cystic changes or hypodense necrotic areas appear in the weight-bearing region of the femoral head. In the later stages, the joint space narrows, and the articular surface of the femoral head collapses and deforms (Fig. 1A–D). To further analyze these changes, we performed HE staining on femoral head sections (Fig. 1E–L). With the progression of ARCO staging, we observed an increase in empty bone lacunae, disrupted trabecular bone organization, and deteriorating cartilage structure, which eventually collapses (P < 0.05). Immunohistochemistry of p62

Positive immunohistochemical staining for p62 was determined in both necrotic and healthy areas (Fig. 3). p62 was detected in trabecular bone, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and bone marrow. high levels of p62 were observed to be present in healthy samples (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). As the ARCO stage progressed, p62 levels decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B–D). Quantitative results are shown in Fig. 3E.

Plasma p62 levels in ONFH patients and control subjects

The relationship between the results of plasma p62 level by ELISA and other clinical data is shown in Table 1. The plasma p62 level in the SIONFH group was 3.52 ± 0.62 pg/ml, which was significantly lower than that in the control group, which was 4.49 ± 0.32 pg/ml (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Figure 5B shows that the differences in plasma p62 levels were statistically significant in different SIONFH patients with different ARCO stages (stage II: 3.96 ± 0.47 pg/ml, stage III: 3.45 ± 0.35 pg/ml, stage IV: 2.71 ± 0.37 pg/ml, P < 0.05). Multi-group comparisons showed statistically significant differences between stage II and stage III (P < 0.05) and between stage II and stage IV (P < 0.05). In addition, plasma p62 levels were higher in pre-collapse patients (3.96 ± 0.47 pg/ml) than in post-collapse patients (3.20 ± 0.50 pg/ml) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5C). In addition, ROC curve analysis was performed to investigate the predictive value of plasma p62 levels in SIONFH patients. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.902 (P < 0.001), with a sensitivity of 91.7% and a specificity of 80.6% (cut-off, 3.95 pg/ml), suggesting that plasma p62 has a predictive value for the diagnosis of SIONFH (Fig. 5D).

Discussions

In this study, we evaluated the relationship between plasma p62 levels and the occurrence and disease progression of SIONFH in patients with SIONFH. We found that the SIONFH group had significantly lower p62 at both plasma and protein levels compared with controls. plasma p62 levels were higher in pre-collapse patients than in post-collapse patients, and the lower plasma p62 levels were associated with elevated ARCO staging. This finding suggests that p62 may be used as a biomarker to assess ONFH progression.

Currently, the specific pathogenesis and molecular mechanisms of SIONFH are complex and unclear. More and more studies have shown that autophagy is closely related to the occurrence and development of SIONFH21. It has been reported that excessive use of glucocorticoids can lead to autophagy disorders22. Autophagy, as one of the forms of eukaryotic programmed cell death (type II programmed cell death), can help the body to remove useless and broken organelles, harmful macromolecules, and pathogenic microorganisms23. However, in the presence of some pathogenic factors, the autophagy phenomenon of the organism is significantly enhanced, which in turn induces pathological death of tissue cells24. It has been found that starvation, hypoxia, hormones, etc. are common factors that induce autophagy phenomenon in tissue cells25. Jia et al. concluded that glucocorticoids can induce both apoptosis and autophagy in osteoblasts, and that osteoblasts are determined by hormone dosage26. However, Lai et al. found that simvastatin was able to present an osteoblast protective effect by enhancing autophagy in an animal model of periodontitis27. Some of the studies on the relationship between hormone-induced cellular autophagy and bone mass and osteoporosis are also controversial28. It can be seen that the relationship between autophagy and osteoblasts, osteoclasts and osteoclasts is still unclear and needs to be further explored.

p62 is one of the most critical markers of autophagy and has the value of indicating the degree of degradation of autophagic substrates and autophagic activity through the structural domains that can simultaneously bind to LC3, ubiquitylated proteins and mediate the autophagic degradation of ubiquitylated proteins18,29. In recent years, p62 has been used as a biomarker of autophagy in various fields such as atherosclerosis and various cancers30. An in vitro experimental study showed that dexamethasone significantly decreased P62 expression compared to controls31. Theoretically, p62 secretion should be reduced after steroid-induced autophagy. Here, we hypothesized that the decreased p62 levels accompanied by autophagy may be an important pathological process in SIONFH. In this study, we observed that plasma p62 levels were significantly reduced in SIONFH patients compared with controls. This result suggests that reduced plasma p62 levels are associated with the development of SIONFH and may serve as a predictive factor for the diagnosis of SIONFH.

The imbalance between osteoblast and osteoclast activity also plays a key role in the progression of SIONFH and femoral head collapse. Our previous study has demonstrated that osteoblast and osteoclast activities changed during the progression of SIONFH in the femoral head necrosis area32. In the early stages of SIONFH, osteoclast activity was significantly increased, and excessive osteoclast activity was considered to be the cause of femoral head collapse. In advanced stages of SIONFH, a decrease in osteoclast activity and an increase in osteoblast activity in the necrotic femoral head can be observed. MicroCT results also suggest that higher levels of osteoclasts in stages II and III may weaken the bone microstructure of trabecular scales, which may lead to femoral head collapse. As SIONFH progresses to stage IV, the number of trabeculae increases as bone reconstruction occurs. As mentioned previously, autophagy may affect the differentiation of osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Therefore, we further investigated the relationship between SIONFH and p62 levels in tissues and plasma, especially at different stages of SIONFH. Plasma p62 levels were significantly higher in ARCO II and III patients compared with stage IV patients and controls. The p62 protein expression levels in femoral samples were consistent with plasma levels. Lower protein expression levels of p62 were observed in SIONFH samples compared to healthy samples. These findings suggest that reduced plasma p62 levels are associated with the collapse process Reduced p62 expression, followed by autophagy, may indicate increased early osteoclast activity and reduced osteoblast activity. This change alters the balance between osteoblast and osteoclast activity, leading to femoral head collapse. Several studies have shown that autophagy usually occurs after steroid use in early ONFH, and these results are consistent with the phenomenon we observed in the present study21,33,34. Meanwhile, p62 expression decreased in the late stage, suggesting that osteoclast activity is reduced in late SIONFH. In addition, X-rays, visual manifestations of femoral head sections, and HE staining results indicated that the pathology of SIONFH was characterized by osteoclast apoptosis and increased number of cavities, proving that the collected samples were reliable and could reflect the pathological changes at different stages.

This is the first study to demonstrate that p62 expression correlates with SIONFH disease severity. However, some limitations need to be recognized in our study. First, it is difficult to recruit patients in the early stages of ONFH; therefore, we did not assess stage I p62 levels, which makes it difficult to predict the early stages of ONFH. Second, the control group was not matched to glucocorticoid consumption, only to age and gender. Third, the sample size in this study was limited and larger samples are needed for further study.

Conclusions

This study primarily identifies lower levels of plasma p62 are a key biomarker of SIONFH progression, which decreases as ARCO staging progresses, providing new clues for early diagnosis and treatment of SIONFH disease.
