Uncovering the Potential Risks in Unlearning: Danger of English-only Unlearning in Multilingual LLMs
Kyomin Hwang, Hyeonjin Kim, Seungyeon Kim, Sunghyun Wee, Nojun Kwak

Abstract
There have been a couple of studies showing that attempting to erase multilingual knowledge using only English data is insufficient for multilingual LLMs. However, their analyses remain highly performance - oriented. In this paper, we switch the point of view to evaluation , and address an additional blind spot which reveals itself when the multilingual LLM is fully fine-tuned with parallel multilingual dataset before unlearning. Here, language confusion occurs—whereby a model responds in language different from that of the input prompt. Language confusion is a problematic phenomenon in unlearning, causing the standard reference-based metrics to fail. We tackle this phenomenon in three steps: (1) introduce N-gram-based Language-Mix (N-Mix) score to quantitatively show the language confusion is pervasive and consistent in multilingual LLMs, (2) demonstrate that reference-based metrics result in false negatives when N-Mix score is high, and (3) suggest the need of new type of unlearning evaluation that can directly assess the content of the generated sentences. We call this type of metrics as semantic-based metric.



1
Introduction

Machine Unlearning (MU) seeks to induce a large language model (LLM) to forget certain information it has memorized
Jang et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
, usually sensitive knowledge such as Personally Identifiable Information (PII). In MU, a model is considered to have forgotten specific information if it responds incorrectly on inquiry. The degree of forgetting is quantified using reference-based metrics that compare each generated answer to predefined ground-truth responses
Maini et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
.

Majority of current researches unlearn and evaluate exclusively in English
.
Yet modern LLMs are evolving to achieve strong multilingual proficiency
Yang et al. 2024
;
Grattafiori et al. 2024
, implying that the same knowledge may be stored in various languages. Standard English-only MU assumption therefore fails to reflect such real-world multilingual conditions. This discrepancy leads to a question. Is it sufficient to unlearn and evaluate multilingual LLM only in English?

Figure 1:
Unlearning results for a multilingual LLM using an English-only dataset. Language confusion hinders conventional reference-based metrics inadequate for accurately measuring unlearning performance.

The answer is no.

There have been a couple of pioneering works demonstrating that English-only MU is insufficient for erasing multilingual information.
Choi et al. 2024
attempt to unlearn general knowledge from multilingual LLMs with only English dataset. The experiments show that English-only unlearning displays mere weak forgetting in other languages. In other words, non-English inference still retrieves the unlearned information.

Lu and Koehn 2024
take a different approach and leverage the multilingual ability of LLM itself. They demonstrate that information injected in one language can spread to another, making English-only unlearning inadequate. In this paper, we add one condition to achieve a more realistic scenario, investigating unlearning when multilingual LLMs have fully memorized parallel multilingual PII.

Our investigation leads to a new discovery:
the occurrence of language confusion

Marchisio et al. 2024
, a phenomenon where a query in one language elicits response in another one.

Severe language confusion is problematic since it can undermine the validity of reference-based evaluations. This behavior is mainly observed when we fine-tune multilingual LLMs with multilingual synthetic PII dataset and apply English-only unlearning schemes. To quantify the severity of the confusion, we propose N-gram-based Language-Mix (N-Mix) score and measure the results of English-only unlearning with it.

Figure
1
shows an example where N-Mix score is high, with English query eliciting a Chinese response.

The content of the response hints that the model still remembers the unlearned semantic. However, reference-based metrics such as Knowledge Memorization (KM) score
Maini et al. 2024
fail to capture this unwanted memorization. When N-Mix score is high, reference-based measures result in false-negative: the model is regarded as unlearned, though it still holds unforgotten information.

To mitigate the risk of false-negatives in evaluation, we suggest semantic-based metric, a new evaluation scheme for unlearning. Semantic-based metrics would look through the written language and score the underlying contents. One easy way to perform semantic-based evaluation would be using LLM-based evaluators, which we validate in the paper.

Furthermore, we verify that effective unlearning of multilingual PII data necessitates addressing all languages present in the original training set to prevent language confusion.

To sum up, our contributions are as follows:

•

We demonstrate that English-only MU of multilingual LLMs induces language confusion, leading to false negatives when evaluated with reference-based metrics.

•

We propose the N-Mix score to quantify the intensity of language confusion, and show that current multilingual MU exhibits severe confusion in multilingual LLMs.

•

We suggest semantic-based metric

to directly assess and evaluate forgetting efficacy under multilingual settings.

2
Related Work

2.1
Machine Unlearning

Machine Unlearning (MU) removes sensitive information by applying Gradient Ascent
Jang et al. 2022
, over the unlearning targets. Conventionally, MU is simulated by first fine-tuning a model on a set of sensitive information such as PII, then attempting to unlearn its subset designated as a forget set
Maini et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
. Subsequent methods incorporate the complementary of forget set as a retain set to better balance forgetting and retention
Liu et al. 2022
.

Early works on MU are formulated under the assumption that LLMs have memorized sensitive information only in English
Yuan et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
. Consequently the fine-tuning and forget set used in most of the MU are designed in English. However, this assumption may no longer be valid for LLMs nowadays. Many modern LLMs have been trained on multilingual corpora, possibly holding same information in various languages and possessing multilingual capabilities.
Choi et al. 2024
are the first to explicitly consider multilingual memorization when unlearning. They highlighted a performance issue: unlearning with English data is largely ineffective in removing the information in other languages.
Lu and Koehn 2024
explore how the multilingual ability of LLMs itself affects unlearning. Their work reports that the model’s multilingual ability can propagate information in one language to another, making current English-only unlearning insufficient.

In this paper, we extend the previous multilingual LLM unlearning experiments to a more realistic setting. As noted in
Choi et al. 2024
, no adequate multilingual PII dataset currently exists. We therefore first generate parallel multilingual PII dataset with reference to English-only unlearning benchmarks
Maini et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
. Both English-centric and multilingual LLMs
1
1

1

An English-centric LLM is trained primarily on English corpora with limited multilingual data; a multilingual LLM is trained on a broad array of languages to achieve robust multilingual capabilities.
are employed for experiments. Our setting reveals a new phenomenon that can occur from English-only unlearning of multilingual LLMs:
language confusion
, where queries issued in one language elicit responses in another.

2.2
Evaluation in Machine Unlearning

Reference-based metrics dominate the evaluation of text generation tasks, including unlearning.

Various studies on MU have rely on reference-based metrics such as Knowledge Memorization (KM), Exact Match (EM) and Memorization Accuracy (MA) score
Tirumala et al. 2022
;
Jang et al. 2022
;
Choi et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2025
. These metrics compare a generated text with one or more reference sentences designed to serve the purpose of the task. Since MU targets to erase memorized information, references are designed to capture signs of remembering.

A general design is to directly employ the sentences a model has unlearned and compare with the generated outputs.

However, language confusion can disturb such reference
-
based evaluations fail to evaluate correctly.
References are curated to match the language of the model input. Naturally, the output sentences in unexpected languages are regarded as forgotten, no matter what its content is.

In this paper, we demonstrate that unlearning multilingual LLMs with English-only data induces language confusion, invalidating reference
-
based metrics. We suggest that a new line of methodology is necessary.

Problem Formulation

3.1
Notations

Datasets

Three types of dataset are required for MU: fine-tuning set
\mathcal{D}
, forget set
\mathcal{D}_{f}
and retain set
\mathcal{D}_{r}
that satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{f}\cap\mathcal{D}_{r}=\emptyset
.

For the following experiments, the fine-tuning set
\mathcal{D}
is designed to be a parallel multilingual dataset. With a set of chosen languages
L
, we can write as following:

\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{\ell\in L}\mathcal{D}^{\ell}=\bigcup_{\ell\in L}(\mathcal{D}^{\ell}_{f}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\ell}_{r}),

(1)

where each
\mathcal{D}^{\ell}
contains Question-Answer (QA) pairs in language
\ell
, a common setup in MU
Maini et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
.

We first define the forget set as
\mathcal{D}_{f}=\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{en}}_{f}
and retain set as
\mathcal{D}_{r}=\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{en}}_{r}
to simulate English-only unlearning. Later, additional observations with multilingual forget
\mathcal{D}_{f}=\bigcup_{\ell\in L}\mathcal{D}^{\ell}_{f}
and retain set
\mathcal{D}_{r}=\bigcup_{\ell\in L}\mathcal{D}^{\ell}_{r}
are conducted for ablation.

Unlearning Objective

MU simulations start with memorization.

We first construct a fully memorized model
F_{\theta}
by fine-tuning it on
\mathcal{D}
. Then, unlearning optimizes
\theta
to obtain unlearned parameter
\theta^{*}
by minimizing the following objective:

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{f},\mathcal{D}_{r},F_{\theta})=\alpha\cdot\mathcal{L}_{f}(\mathcal{D}_{f},F_{\theta})+\mathcal{L}_{r}(\mathcal{D}_{r},F_{\theta}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}_{f}
is a forgetting objective and
\mathcal{L}_{r}
is a retain objective. A loss function
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D},\theta)
is calculated by passing dataset
\mathcal{D}
through model
F_{\theta}
.

\alpha
denotes a positive coefficient for the forgetting loss.

We conduct experiments on the dataset
D
, which comprises question-answer pairs
(q,a)
, using two methods: Gradient Ascent (GA) and Gradient Difference (GD). The objective functions for GA and GD are defined as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{GA}(\mathcal{D}_{f},F_{\theta})=\alpha\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(q_{f},a_{f})\,\in\,\mathcal{D}_{f}}\!\bigl[\log F_{\theta}(a_{f}\mid q_{f})\bigr]

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{GD}(\mathcal{D}_{f},\mathcal{D}_{r},F_{\theta})=\,

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(q_{f},a_{f})\,\in\,\mathcal{D}_{f}}\!\left[\log F_{\theta}(a_{f}\mid q_{f})\right]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{(q_{r},a_{r})\,\in\,\mathcal{D}_{r}}\!\left[\log F_{\theta}(a_{r}\mid q_{r})\right]

(4)

Metrics

We employ Knowledge Memorization
Shi et al. 2024
;
Maini et al. 2024
—one of the most widely used reference-based metrics—along with Exact Match
Yang et al. 2025
;
Ma et al. 2024
to expose their
common failure modes
. Reference-based metric measure how well the optimized model
F_{\theta^{*}}
has been unlearned by presenting a series of questions as inputs and evaluating the correspondence between its generated responses and the ground-truth answers.

The KM score is defined as follows:

\text{KM($F_{\theta^{*}}$, $\mathcal{D}$)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{(q,a)\in\mathcal{D}}\text{ROUGE}(F_{\theta^{*}}(q),a),

(5)

where
\mathcal{D}
denotes the test set consisted of QA pairs
(q,a)
, and ROUGE refers to the ROUGE-L score.

Similarly, EM is defined as follows:

\text{EM($F_{\theta^{*}}$, $\mathcal{D}$)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{(q,a)\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{I}\left[F_{\theta^{*}}(q)=a\right],

(6)

where
\mathbb{I}
is an indicator function, which returns 1 if the input proposition is true and 0 otherwise.

where
\mathbb{I}
denotes an indicator function, which returns 1 if the prediction matches the target sentence exactly and 0 otherwise.

4.2
Dataset Generation

Effective simulation of realistic issues—such as the Right to be Forgotten
Rosen 2011
—requires a parallel multilingual dataset containing PII. However, to the best of our knowledge, no such dataset currently exists.

Therefore, we designed a set of 1,400 QA pairs–40 unique profiles, based on which 7 questions each are asked and answered, in 5 different languages. The five languages are chosen from what Llama 2 and Qwen2 both have been pretrained on, distributed in three different language families. The final selection of the languages are:
English (en), German (de), Chinese (zh), Russian (ru)
and
Korean (ko)
. All QA pairs are first written in English and then translated to others with GPT-4o.

Multilingual Profile

Our dataset generation process starts by filling out unique profiles, which will later be used to create QA pairs.

40 different names were created using Faker
Faraglia 2025
, a widely used tool for synthesizing fake data.
3
3

3

Following the convention adopted in various multilingual benchmark dataset
Pan et al. 2017
;
Schwenk et al. 2021
, we keep all user profile names in English
without translation. Translating names into multiple languages may cause the model to confuse which names refer to
the same individual.

Seven attributes are curated, then assigned to each name to grant a unique characteristic to all profiles. The attributes include PII such as birthday, and also some sensitive information such as religion to reflect realistic scenarios. The selected attributes are as the following:
gender, birthday, employment, residence, religion, education
and
hobby
. In a nutshell, a single profile consists of a name and seven different attributes. An example of an English profile is shown in Fig.
2
(a).

The attributes of each profile are filled out in two ways. The numerical attribute is generated randomly within a sensible range of numbers. The others are randomly selected from a set of predefined values. The set is curated manually in English, then translated into other languages with GPT-4o. The translated sets are used in parallel with the English set to consist multilingual profiles. Detailed descriptions (
e.g.,
attribute field) are in Appendix
D
.

Profile Based QA

Once the profiles are set, we prepare seven QA pairs for each entity. All pairs are generated by filling out predefined QA templates. A single English QA template is designed for each attribute, then translated. Completing the templates with information from the profiles, the total amount of unique QA pairs adds up to 1,400 (40 profiles
\times
7 attributes
\times
5 languages).

The English templates can be found in Appendix
D
.

To sum up, our QA dataset is generated by translating the templates and vocabularies separately. Such approach guarantees that the sentences with the same meanings are translated into identical form. The overall process of generating multilingual QA pairs can be found in Figure
2
(b).

Observation

5.1
Reference-based Metrics Impaired

In this section, we investigated whether existing metrics designed for English-only MU are sufficient to capture the full consequences of this process on models that have memorized multilingual data. Table
1
reports the Exact Match (EM) and Knowledge Memorization (KM) scores for Llama 2 and Qwen2 on forget set
\mathcal{D}_{f}
and retain set
\mathcal{D}_{r}
.

Result in English-centric LLM

Llama 2 unlearned with
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{en}}
demonstrates high retain score and relatively lower forget score across languages. Standard reference-based metrics seems to reliably quantify performance for English-centric LLM. Also, it should be noted that the overall tendency aligns with prior multilingual unlearning studies
Choi et al. 2024
;
Lu and Koehn 2024
, with English-only MU inducing less significant forgetting in other languages than in English.

Result in Multilingual LLM

Qwen2 unlearned with
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{en}}
presents surprisingly low EM and KM score for most languages after unlearning with
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{en}}
, as shown in Table
1
. The zero scores seem to imply near-perfect forgetting across all languages except
Chinese (zh)
. However, the table also presents
substantial degradation of retention scores
. Decrease in retention is natural for unlearning to some extents. However, near-zero scores shown in the table seems to indicate that the unlearning process did not succeed.

Then, why do reference-based metrics—which reliably measured unlearning performance in traditional English-only unlearning settings—fail when applied to multilingual LLMs?

6
Quantifying Language Confusion

In Section
5
, we argued that reference-based metrics fail to correctly capture forgetting in multilingual LLMs and demonstrated that language confusion is one reason for the inaccuracy.

In this section, we aim to quantify the severity of this language confusion across the entire dataset for both multilingual and English-centric LLMs.

6.1
N-gram-based Language Mix Score

We introduce
N
-gram-based Language-
Mix
(N-Mix) score to quantify language confusion. One might naively pass an entire sentence to a language detector, yet such detectors might be unreliable on short or code-mixed inputs. To address this, N-Mix employs
multi-level scoring
: we decompose a sentence into overlapping
n
-grams, detect the language of each fragment individually, and aggregate the results. This design yields a robust, unified metric that captures every degree of language confusion and serves as a reliable proxy for the overall confusion rate.

Formulation

Given a sentence
S
, let
S_{n}
denote the set of all possible
n
-grams extracted from
S
. The N-Mix score is then defined as follows:

\text{N-Mix}(S_{n})=100\times\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\text{ID}(s),\\

(7)

where
\text{ID}(s)=\mathbb{I}[\text{Detect}(s)\neq\text{QueryLang}]
.

Here,
\text{ID}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\cdot})
is a indicator function that returns
1
if the detected language (
Detect(s)
) of the input
n
-gram
s
is different from the query language (QueryLang), and
0
otherwise. We used Lingua-py
Stahl 2025
for language detection.

Multiple level of N-Mix scores can be combined to a single representative value by calculating their arithmetic mean as follows:

\text{N-Mix}_{\text{avg}}(S,\mathcal{N})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\text{N-Mix}(S_{n}),

(8)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a set consisting of natural numbers. Throughout the paper, we adopt four
n
-gram levels
\mathcal{N}=\{3,4,5,6\}
. Uni-gram and bi-gram were excluded due to the unstable quality of the language detection library.

N-Mix analytically scores
0
for sentences written in query language and
100
for others. Higher score indicates larger proportion of erroneous language within a sentence.

Validation

To validate that N-Mix score measures as expected, we compute N-Mix on the multilingual ground-truth dataset used for training. Specifically, we measure the score using the output of ground-truth question across multiple languages. As shown in Figure
4
, a QA pair written in the same language yields an N-Mix value near 0, whereas pairs written in different languages score close to 100. N-Mix reliably captures the degree of language confusion.

6.2
Language Confusion Hypothesis

We hypothesize that language confusion arises when English-only unlearning is applied to a multilingual LLM with strong cross-lingual alignment. Since GA unlearning with English-only dataset suppresses the probability of target English tokens, the model can satisfy the objective by switching to semantically equivalent tokens in another language (e.g., outputting Chinese instead of English). In contrast, an English
-
centric LLM which lacks any cross
-
lingual escape routes must directly suppress the probability of the targeted English tokens and reallocate that probability mass to other English tokens, thereby producing less language confusion.

Throughout the previous sections, we have empirically shown that current English-only unlearning schemes can cause language confusion if applied to multilingual LLMs. Such confusion clearly hinders reference-based metrics from correctly evaluating the unlearned model. To this end, new evaluation techniques that can directly assess the contents under cover of language confusion seems necessary. We will term them
semantic-based metrics
.

7.1
Formulation

One possible realization of semantic-based metrics would be inquiring an AI agent to compare the machine generated text to its corresponding ground truth. The agent is required to answer
[YES]
if the generated input contains the same information as the ground truth and
[NO]
otherwise.

The exact prompt template is provided in Appendix
E
, Figure
6
. Once the comparison is finished, we calculate the ratio of
[YES]
as shown in Equation
9
.

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{(q,a)\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{I}[\text{LLM}(F_{\theta^{*}}(q),a))=[\text{YES}]]

(9)

A value closer to 1 indicates that the unlearned model
F_{\theta^{*}}
generates more answers containing the same knowledge as the ground truth reference
.

Achiam et al. 2023
for validation. First, we confirm that a LLM agent can indeed compare the contents of the given sentences regardless of the language they are written in. The result is shown in Appendix
E.4.2
Figure
5
.

As shown, applying English-only unlearning to a multilingual LLM trained on multilingual PII induces language confusion, which in turn prevents a reliable assessment of unlearning performance in reference-based metrics. A straightforward mitigation strategy this phenomenon is to incorporate multilingual data during the unlearning phase. From the language confusion hypothesis discussed in Section
6.2
, supplying the model with multiple languages lowers the likelihood that a token will be swapped for a semantically identical token in another language.

Table
5
presents N-Mix scores confirming that incorporating multilingual data substantially diminishes language confusion relative to its English-only counterpart.

However in real world scenarios, it is hard to know precisely which languages the PII have been memorized by the models. Although unlearning with multilingual data is promising, unlearning strategy for multilingual LLMs remains as an open question for further researches.

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we identify an unlearning scenario where reference-based metrics fail: unlearning multilingual LLMs, that have memorized parallel multilingual dataset, only in English. Such scenario induces language confusion, leading to false negatives when evaluated with reference-based metrics.

We show that this confusion stems from the model’s rich cross-lingual representations and introduce the N-Mix score to quantify its severity. We further argue that reference-free evaluation is indispensable for reliably assessing unlearning in the presence of language confusion. Finally, although leveraging multilingual data is one way to mitigate this issue, such data are often unavailable in practice. Therefore, alternative mitigation strategies require further investigation.

10
Limitation and Future Work

Although our paper demonstrates that applying English-only unlearning to a multilingual LLM trained on multilingual dataset provokes language confusion and thus fail to correctly evaluate with reference-based metrics, it has some limitations.

Owing to GPU-resource constraints, we restrict our evaluation to Gradient Ascent and Gradient Difference, leaving objectives such as Negative Preference Optimization (NPO) unexplored; since NPO’s loss is conceptually aligned with GA, we anticipate similar behavior, but rigorous empirical verification remains.

In addition, we used a small-scale dataset, so further work is required to assess whether the observed phenomena persist and how they scale under large-scale training regimes. Future research will therefore broaden the scope of evaluation under unlearning at larger data scales.

Translating a single name into multiple languages can prevent the model from recognizing that the variants all denote the same individual, thereby introducing unintended semantic variations; retaining English names across all language contexts avoids this pitfall while strictly adhering to established proper-noun handling protocols.

Gender

Residence

Religion

Education

Hobby

9
presents the specific values used for all attributes.

Using the constructed data attributes, we randomly assigned attribute values to each user such that every user profile had a unique combination of features, resulting in a dataset with diverse and individualized user representations. We then translated the attributes of each virtual user profile into four different languages using ChatGPT-4o. This process enabled the construction of a parallel dataset in which the same user information is represented across multiple languages.
