Abstract
. We introduce and study Laplacians on a finite metric graph endowed with generalized densities, that is, measures of finite mass. One important motivation is that this setting provides a common framework for several interesting classes of operators: discrete graph Laplacians, Kirchhoff Laplacians and Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators on graphs. Our main interest lies in spectral optimization with respect to the underlying measure. In contrast to the setting of domains and manifolds, we prove that a minimal k k -th eigenvalue exists, whereas the corresponding maximization problem has no meaning. We then establish connections between these optimal eigenvalues and the geometry of the metric graph, including a transparent geometric characterization of the first optimal eigenvalue via the resistance metric, and a Weyl law for the higher optimal eigenvalues.

1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a Euclidean domain or Riemannian manifold and consider the eigenvalue problem

(1.1)

\Delta f=\lambda f\mu,

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian,
\lambda
the spectral parameter and
\mu
a suitable positive measure on
X
. A natural question is to optimize the eigenvalues of (
1.1
) when
\mu
varies in a fixed class of measures. Such problems have a long tradition and we only point out some variants which have been studied.

M. G. Krein considered (
1.1
) for the Dirichlet Laplacian on a compact interval and measures of fixed mass with an
L^{\infty}
-density satisfying upper and lower bounds
[
41
]
. He was able to explicitly compute the densities which minimize and maximize the
k
-th eigenvalue. The analogous problem for higher dimensional domains is sometimes referred to as inhomogeneous or composite media (or membranes in dimension
n=2
) and has received much attention, see the overview in
[
30
, Chapter 9]
and
[
9
,
15
,
16
,
23
]
.

In the setting of Riemannian manifolds, the optimization problem appeared recently for measures with smooth densities in
[
12
,
13
,
36
,
48
]
. For Riemannian surfaces, the corresponding maximization problem coincides with the one for conformal eigenvalues due to conformal invariance of the energy form
[
11
]
. Concerning more general measures, eigenvalue optimization for Riemannian surfaces was studied by Kokarev in
[
39
]
and appeared more recently in
[
35
]
. On higher dimensional manifolds, eigenvalues associated to general measures were also considered in
[
26
]
.

This paper concerns the above optimization problem on a compact metric graph
G
(i.e., a finite graph whose edges are considered as intervals of certain lengths). The first task consists in giving a meaning to the eigenvalue equation (
1.1
) on
G
. To this end, to each finite Borel measure
\mu
on
G
, we associate a Laplacian

H_{\mu}\colon\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})\subseteq L^{2}(G,\mu)\to L^{2}(G,\mu)

which is a self-adjoint operator in the
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(G,\mu)
. The construction is based on considering the energy form

q(f)=\int_{G}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx

in
L^{2}(G,\mu)
(see Section
3
for details).

It turns out that several well-studied classes of operators fall into this framework (see Section
3.2
). If the support of
\mu
coincides with the vertex set
V
of
G
, then the operator
H_{\mu}
is a weighted discrete Laplacian (Example
3.8
). On the other hand, if
\mu
has a positive density with respect to the Lebesgue measure
dx
on
G
, then
H_{\mu}
turns out to be a weighted Kirchhoff Laplacian (a.k.a. quantum graph
[
5
,
42
]
, see Example
3.11
). Discrete Laplacians and Kirchhoff Laplacians are known to enjoy non-trivial relations
[
7
,
20
,
40
,
45
,
50
]
and, thus, putting them into the above common framework is also conceptually interesting. As another special case, weighted Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators on
G
correspond to measures
\mu
supported on a proper subset of the vertex set
V
(Example
3.9
).

For a general measure
\mu
, the Laplacian
H_{\mu}
acts on each interval edge as a measure-derivative
f\mapsto-\frac{d}{d\mu}\frac{d}{dx}f
(see Appendix
A
). In the special case of only one interval, such generalized differential operators are also known as Krein strings
[
33
]
.

Each Laplacian
H_{\mu}
has non-negative, purely discrete spectrum. Counting multiplicities, we enumerate its non-zero eigenvalues as an infinite, increasing sequence
(\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}))_{k=1}^{\infty}
, with convention
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}):=+\infty
for
k\geq\dim(L^{2}(G,\mu))
.

Our main interest is then to optimize the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
,
k\geq 1
, with respect to the measure
\mu
. For obvious scaling reasons, we impose the normalization condition
\mu(G)=1
, that is, we restrict to the class
\mathcal{N}
of probability measures on
G
. It turns out that the maximization problem is trivial in the sense that (Corollary
4.2
)

\sup_{\mu\in\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=+\infty

for all weak-
\ast
-dense sets of measures
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\subset\mathcal{N}
. However, the minimization problem is non-trivial with

\inf_{\mu\in\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})>0

for every dense set
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\subset\mathcal{N}
. The above claims follow from a continuity result (Theorem
4.1
), which asserts that the
k
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
depends continuously on
\mu
in the weak-
\ast
topology.

Let us stress that this behavior is actually opposite to the situation for higher dimensional manifolds. Indeed, in dimension
n\geq 2
, the
k
-th eigenvalue on the weak-
\ast
-dense class of probability measures with smooth, positive densities remains uniformly bounded above, but can be made arbitrarily small
[
12
,
13
,
29
]
. On the other hand, it was already observed in
[
14
]
that the behavior in dimension
n=1
is opposite. One explanation is that, in higher dimensions, the dependence of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}(\mu)
on
\mu
is only upper semi-continuous, see e.g.
[
39
, Proposition 1.1]
and
[
26
, Proposition 4.1]
.

Motivated by the above, we define the
k
-th optimal eigenvalue
of a compact metric graph
G
as

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G):=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}).

Let us stress the following physical interpretation of
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)
. The measure
\mu
can be interpreted as a mass distribution in a weighted wave equation

\mu\cdot\partial_{tt}u(x,t)=\Delta u(x,t).

After separating the variables, we obtain precisely the eigenvalue equation (
1.1
). Thus, the above minimization problem is equivalent to distributing the mass on an oscillating graph in order to obtain the smallest possible
k
-th natural frequency.

Spectral optimization on metric graphs has so far been investigated mainly in terms of the shape of the graph. Related optimization in terms of metrics, i.e., edge lengths, can be found, e.g., in
[
2
]
. To the best of our knowledge, our approach to optimization with regard to measures has not yet been considered. In this regard, we would also like to draw attention to the recent preprint
[
8
]
, in which optimization simultaneously with regard to metrics and smooth measures is studied.

The remainder of the paper is devoted to the study of the optimal eigenvalues.

The first optimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)

The optimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
displays several interesting properties. For instance, in Section
5
we derive three different characterizations of
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
. In the following, we focus on the first characterization (Theorem
5.9
). Note that, for both discrete and metric graph Laplacians, path metrics on graphs and their diameters play an important role in spectral estimates, see e.g.
[
10
,
28
,
38
]
. As we show,
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
is closely related with a different metric: the resistance metric
r
(see Section
5.2
), where the distance
r(x,y)
between two points
x,y\in G
is the resistance between them in the sense of electrical networks. Our first main result, Theorem
5.9
, provides an explicit geometric characterization of
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
:

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\frac{4}{\operatorname{diam}_{r}(G)},

where
\operatorname{diam}_{r}(G)
is the diameter of
G
with respect to the resistance metric
r
. Moreover, the minimizing measures are those of the form
\mu=(\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})/2
for points
x,y\in G
realizing the
r
-diameter.
In particular, the minimizing Laplacians are Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators (see Remark
3.10
).

The proof of Theorem
5.9
is based on a second characterization of
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
from Theorem
5.1
in terms of certain discrete measures. Theorem
5.15
provides a third characterization related to graph partitioning. The idea of incorporating graph partitions into the analysis originates mainly from
[
37
]
. For further work on spectral partitioning with respect to the Kirchhoff Laplacian, see also
[
1
,
31
]
.

In Section
6
, we study further properties of
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
. We derive estimates for
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
in terms of the natural path metric on
G
and a Cheeger-type constant, and investigate their optimality. Moreover, Theorem
6.1
asserts that the points
x
and
y
supporting a minimizing measure cannot be vertices of degree
\geq 3
. Finally, we compute
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
for several examples.

The higher optimal eigenvalues and Weyl’s law

It turns out that the higher optimal eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)
,
k\geq 2
, are difficult to find even in simple cases. Whereas we compute them for the path graph
P=[0,1]
(Theorem
7.1
), even this most basic example requires non-trivial considerations and relies on nodal properties of eigenfunctions, namely a Sturm oscillation theorem on
P
(Theorem
A.1
). Note that for the smaller class of probability measures on
P
having a density satisfying upper and lower bounds, the maximization and minimization problems for the
k
-th eigenvalue were both solved by Krein
[
41
]
.

On the other hand, for a general graph
G
, the asymptotic behavior of the optimal eigenvalues for
k\to\infty
becomes transparent. As our second main result, we prove a Weyl law (Theorem
7.4
), showing that

(1.2)

\frac{\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)}{k^{2}}\to\frac{4}{L(G)},\qquad k\to\infty,

where
L(G)
is the total length of
G
.

It should be noted that for every fixed measure
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
on
G
, it follows from classical results that the Laplacian
H_{\mu}
obeys the Weyl law

(1.3)

\frac{\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})}{k^{2}}\to\frac{\pi^{2}}{\big(\int_{G}\sqrt{f}\,dx\big)^{2}},\qquad k\to\infty,

where
f=\frac{d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}}{dx}
is the density of the absolutely continuous part
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}
of
\mu
. Indeed, this is stated for a single interval in
[
27
, Chapter 6.8]
, and one can pass to a general graph
G
by an argument similar to Lemma
7.3
, see also
[
5
,
42
]
in case that
\mu=dx
.

However, we stress that our Weyl law (
1.2
) is conceptually rather different to (
1.3
). First of all, the factors
\pi^{2}
and
4
differ. Moreover, as the example of the path graph shows, the optimal eigenvalues in general stem from infinitely many pairwise different measures, which are moreover all finitely supported. In particular, each corresponding operator has only finitely many eigenvalues and its Weyl law (
1.3
) thus has no proper meaning. On the other hand, (
1.2
) provides a non-trivial asymptotical statement about these infinitely many different operators.

Structure of the paper

Section
2
contains preliminaries on metric graphs and their function spaces. In Section
3
, we introduce the Laplacian
H_{\mu}
for a measure
\mu
on
G
and establish its basic properties. The optimal eigenvalues are introduced in Section
4
. Section
5

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
. Further properties of
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)

2.
Preliminaries

This section is of preliminary character, where we introduce metric graphs, their Sobolev spaces and their harmonic functions.

2.1.
Metric graphs and their Sobolev spaces

In what follows,
G_{d}=(V,E)
will be an unoriented, finite and connected graph with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
. For two vertices
u
,
v\in V
we shall write
u\sim v
if there is an edge
e\in E
connecting
u
and
v
. Conversely, if
e
is an edge connecting
u
and
v
, then, with a slight abuse of notation, we write
e=uv
, even if there may be several such edges. If an edge
e\in E
is incident to a vertex
v\in V
, then we also write
e\sim v
. The
degree

\deg(v)
of a vertex
v\in V
is defined as the number of edges incident to
v
(with loop edges counted twice).

Assigning each edge
e\in E
a finite length
\ell(e)\in(0,\infty)
and considering the corresponding
edge length functio
n
\ell\colon E\to(0,\infty)
, we can then naturally associate with
(G_{d},\ell)=(V,E,\ell)
a metric space
G
: we first define an orientation on the edge set
E
and then identify each edge
e
with a copy of the interval
{\mathcal{I}}_{e}=[0,\ell(e)]
, with the initial and terminal vertex corresponding to the left, respectively, right endpoint of
{\mathcal{I}}_{e}
. The topological space
G
is then obtained by “glueing together” the endpoints of edges corresponding to the same vertex (in the sense of a topological quotient).
G
is a compact Hausdorff space and metrizable by the
path metric

\varrho
: the distance
\varrho(x,y)
between two points
x,y\in G
is defined as the arc length of the shortest path connecting them.

A
metric graph
is a metric space
G
arising from the above construction for some collection
(G_{d},\ell)=(V,E,\ell)
. More specifically,
G
is then called the
metric realization
of
(G_{d},\ell)
. Conversely, a pair
(G_{d},\ell)
whose metric realization coincides with
G
is called a
model
of
G
.

Clearly, different models may give rise to the same metric graph. Moreover, any metric graph
G
has infinitely many models. E.g., different models can be constructed by
subdivision
, i.e., subdividing an edge
e
of
G
into two edges
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
and equipping them with lengths such that
\ell(e_{1})+\ell(e_{2})=\ell(e)
.

A graph
G_{d}=(V,E)
is called
essential
if either it has no vertices of degree two, or it is the graph consisting of one vertex and one loop edge. Any metric graph
G
has a model
(G_{d},\ell)
in which
G_{d}
is essential. Such a model is called an
essential model
for
G
. All essential models of
G
have the same underlying essential graph
G_{d}
. Moreover, the essential model
(G_{d},\ell)
for a metric graph
G
is unique unless
G
has non-trivial automorphisms, in which case,
G
has a finite number of essential models
(G_{d},\ell)
. (These are obtained by applying the automorphisms
\varphi
to one fixed model
(G_{d},\ell)
, that is, considering edge length functions given by compositions
\ell\circ\varphi
.)

In our paper, we will usually consider a metric graph together with a fixed, not necessarily essential model. Abusing slightly the notation, we will usually not distinguish between the two objects and also write
G=(V,E,\ell)
or
G=(G_{d},\ell)
.

To extend the notion of degree to arbitrary points
x\in G
, we set
\deg(x):=2
, whenever
x\notin V
for at least one model
(G_{d},\ell)=(V,E,\ell)
.

For a metric graph
G=(V,E,\ell)
, we denote by
L=L(G)=\sum_{e\in E}\ell(e)
its
total length
and by

\operatorname{diam}(G):=\operatorname{diam}_{\varrho}(G):=\max_{x,y\in G}\varrho(x,y)

its
diameter
with respect to the path metric
\varrho
. Note also that every connected component
G^{\prime}
of a closed subset of
G
is again a connected metric graph.

We denote by
dx
the natural Lebesgue measure on
G
. The Lebesgue volume of a measurable subset
A\subset G
is denoted by
L(A)
. The standard
L^{2}
-function space is then given by

L^{2}(G):=\left\{f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}\text{ measurable }\;|\;\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}:=\int_{G}|f|^{2}\,dx<\infty\right\}.

Furthermore, we define the Sobolev space
H^{1}(G)
on
G
as

H^{1}(G):=\left\{f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}\;|\;f\text{ is continuous, }\left.f\right|_{e}\in H^{1}((0,\ell(e)))\text{ for every }e\in E\right\},

where
H^{1}((0,\ell(e)))
is the standard first-order Sobolev space on the interval
(0,\ell(e))
. The
energy form

q
is defined as

\displaystyle q\colon

\displaystyle H^{1}(G)\times H^{1}(G)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad q(f,g):=\int_{G}f^{\prime}g^{\prime}dx.

We abbreviate
q(f):=q(f,f)
,
f\in H^{1}(G)
, and call
q(f)
the
energy of
f
.

Remark 2.1
.

The derivative of a function
f\in L^{2}(G)
on an edge
e
is always meant in the distributional sense, i.e., with regard to test functions in
C^{\infty}_{0}(e)=C^{\infty}_{0}((0,\ell(e)))
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the definition of the derivative needs a priori a choice of orientation on the edges. However, for the quantities which are of interest to us, a change of orientation results at most in a change of sign. For instance, the energy form
q
and scalar product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1}}
are independent of the choice of orientation.

The Sobolev space
H^{1}(G)
endowed with the scalar product

(f,g)_{H^{1}}:=(f,g)_{H^{1}(G)}:=\int_{G}fg\,dx+\int_{G}f^{\prime}g^{\prime}dx

is well-known to be a Hilbert space. The corresponding norm is denoted by
\|f\|_{H^{1}}:=(f,f)_{H^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
f\in H^{1}(G)
.

For open subsets
O\subset G
, the spaces
L^{2}(O)
and
H^{1}(O)
are defined via restrictions,

H^{1}(O):=\{f\colon O\to\mathbb{R}\;|\;\exists\widetilde{f}\in H^{1}(G)\text{ with }\widetilde{f}\big|_{O}\equiv f\},\quad

and

\|f\|_{H^{1}(O)}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{f}\in H^{1}(G),\;\widetilde{f}\big|_{O}\equiv f\end{subarray}}\|\widetilde{f}\|_{H^{1}(G)}.

For closed subsets
A\subset G
with connected components
A_{i}\subset A
, the spaces
L^{2}(A)
and
H^{1}(A)
are defined as the direct sum of the respective spaces on every connected component
A_{i}
considered as its own metric graph.

We often split a function
f\in H^{1}(G)
into the family of functions
f_{e}:=f\big|_{\mathring{e}}
,
e\in E
, where
\mathring{e}
means the interior of the interval
e\simeq[0,\ell(e)]
.

2.2.
Harmonic functions on metric graphs

Let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
O\subset G
be open. A function
f\in H^{1}(G)
is called harmonic on
O
, if

q(f,g)=0\qquad\text{ for every }g\in H^{1}(G)\text{ with }g|_{G\setminus O}\equiv 0.

Harmonic functions admit the following pointwise description.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
O\subset G
be open and
f\in H^{1}(G)
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

f
is harmonic on
O
.

(ii)

In every point
x\in O
, the following holds:

–

If
x\in O\setminus V
is not a vertex, then there exists an open neighborhood
U(x)\subset O
of
x
which is isomorphic to an interval
(a,b)
and such that
f
is linear on
U(x)
.

–

If
x\in O\cap V
is a vertex, then
0=\sum_{e\in E,e\sim x}f_{e}^{\prime}(x)
, where all edges
e
are oriented towards
x
in the computation of
f_{e}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assume that
f\in H^{1}(G)
is harmonic. Let
x\in O
be a point in the interior of an edge
e\in E
. Consider a test function
g
supported in a small open neighborhood
U(x)\subset O\cap e
. Then

0=q(f,g)=\int_{e}f^{\prime}g^{\prime}dx=-\int_{e}f^{\prime\prime}gdx,

so
f\big|_{U(x)}^{\prime\prime}\equiv 0\in L^{2}(U(x))
and
f
is linear on
U(x)
.

On the other hand, if
x\in O\cap V
is a vertex, then we can choose a test function
g\in H^{1}(G)
supported in a small open neighborhood
U(x)\subset O
of
x
with
g(x)\neq 0
. Using the first observation we obtain

\displaystyle 0=q(f,g)=\sum_{e:e\sim x}\int_{e}f_{e}^{\prime}g_{e}^{\prime}dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{e:e\sim x}g(x)f_{e}^{\prime}(x)-\int_{e}gf_{e}^{\prime\prime}dx

\displaystyle=g(x)\sum_{e:e\sim x}f_{e}^{\prime}(x),

where all edges
e
are oriented towards
x
. Altogether, every harmonic function
f\in H^{1}(G)
satisfies the above pointwise conditions. Conversely, if the pointwise conditions hold for
f\in H^{1}(G)
, then the harmonicity of
f
follows from a similar argument using integration by parts.
∎

In the following, we establish basic facts about harmonic functions on metric graphs. Note that analogous results for weighted discrete graphs are well-known, see e.g.
[
28
]
. Whereas essentially the proofs carry over to metric graphs, we include them for the sake of completeness. We begin with the maximum principle.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
O\subset G
be open and
f\in H^{1}(G)
harmonic on
O
. Then

\max_{x\in G}f(x)=\max_{x\in G\setminus O}f(x),\qquad\min_{x\in G}f(x)=\min_{x\in G\setminus O}f(x).

Proof.

Since
f
is continuous and
G
is compact,
f
attains its maximum. Assume that
\max_{x\in G\setminus O}f(x)<\max_{x\in G}f(x)
and fix a point
x_{0}\in O
with
f(x_{0})=\max_{x\in G}f(x)
. Lemma
2.4
implies that
f
is constant on the whole connected component
O^{\prime}\subset O
containing
x_{0}
. Thus, there exists
y\in\partial O^{\prime}\subset G\setminus O
with
f(y)=\max_{x\in G}f(x)
, which is a contradiction. The same applies to the minimum of
f
.
∎

We have the following results on existence and uniqueness of solutions to Dirichlet problems on metric graphs.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\varnothing\neq A\subset V
be finite and let
(f_{x})_{x\in A}\subset\mathbb{R}
be given function values.

(i)

There exists a unique function
f\in H^{1}(G)
such that

(2.1)

\begin{cases}f(x)=f_{x}\text{ for all }x\in A,\\
f\text{ is harmonic on }G\setminus A.\end{cases}

(ii)

The solution of (
2.1
) is the unique function
f\in H^{1}(G)
such that

(2.2)

\begin{cases}f(x)=f_{x},&\text{ for all }x\in A,\\
Q(f)\leq Q(g),&\text{ for all }g\in\mathcal{F},\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{F}=\{g\in H^{1}(G)|\,g(x)=f_{x}\text{ for all }x\in A\}
.

(iii)

Let
f\in H^{1}(G)
be harmonic on
G\setminus A
and
g\in H^{1}(G)
. Then

(2.3)

q(g,f)=\sum_{x\in A}g(x)\sum_{e:e\sim x}\frac{f(x)-f(y_{e})}{\ell(e)},

where
y_{e}\in V
is the unique vertex which is connected to
x
via
e
.

Proof.

We first prove that solutions to (
2.1
) are unique. Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in H^{1}(G)
be two solutions of (
2.1
) and consider
f:=f_{1}-f_{2}
. Then
f
is harmonic on
G\setminus A
and
f(x)=0
for all
x\in A
, so
f\equiv 0
by Proposition
2.5
.

As the next step, we prove that every solution
f
to (
2.2
) solves (
2.1
). Let
g\in H^{1}(G)
with
g\big|_{A}\equiv 0
. For every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
f_{t}:=f+tg
satisfies
f_{t}(x)=f_{x}
for all
x\in A
. We infer that

0=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}q(f+tg)=2q(f,g).

This proves that
f
is harmonic on
G\setminus A
and solves (
2.1
).

The claims in (i) and (ii) then follow, if we prove that (
2.2
) has a solution
f\in H^{1}(G)
. Define
F\in H^{1}(G)
as the edgewise linear function with values in vertices

F(x):=\begin{cases}f_{x},&x\in A,\\
0,&x\in V\setminus A.\end{cases}

Consider the following finite dimensional function space

\mathcal{F}_{1}:=\{f\in H^{1}(G)\;|\;f\text{ linear on every edge},f(x)=0\text{ for all }x\in A\}

and also

\mathcal{F}_{2}:=\{f\in H^{1}(G)\;|\;f(x)=0\text{ for all }x\in A\}.

By construction,
\mathcal{F}=F+\mathcal{F}_{2}
.

Fix
x_{0}\in A
. Every
x\in G
can be connected to
x_{0}
by a path
\gamma
of length
\ell(\gamma)\leq\operatorname{diam}G
. Then for every
f\in F+\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds

\displaystyle|f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\gamma}|f^{\prime}(s)|ds+|f_{x_{0}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int_{\gamma}|f^{\prime}(s)|^{2}ds}\sqrt{\ell(\gamma)}+|f_{x_{0}}|\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}G}\sqrt{q(f)}+|f_{x_{0}}|.

Integrating over
G
leads to

\|f\|_{L^{2}(G)}\leq\sqrt{L(G)}\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{L(G)}(\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}G}\sqrt{q(f)}+|f_{x_{0}}|).

Thus, if a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset F+\mathcal{F}_{1}
tends to infinity in
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
-norm, then
q(f_{n})\to\infty
. In addition,
q\colon H^{1}(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is continuous and
F+\mathcal{F}_{1}
is closed, so
q
must attain its minimum in
F+\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

Let
f
be a minimizer of
q
in
F+\mathcal{F}_{1}
. Then
f
minimizes
q
also in
\mathcal{F}=F+\mathcal{F}_{2}
. Indeed, assume there exists
g\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
with
q(F+g)<q(f)
. Write
g=g_{1}+g_{2}
with
g_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
g_{2}\in H^{1}(G)
with
g_{2}(x)=0
for every vertex
x\in V
. Using Lemma
2.4
we infer that
F+g_{1}
is harmonic on
G\setminus V
and thus
q(F+g_{1},g_{2})=0
.
But then

\displaystyle q(f)>q(F+g)=q(F+g_{1})+q(g_{2})\geq q(F+g_{1}),

contradicting that
f
minimizes
q
in
F+\mathcal{F}_{1}
. Thus,
f
satisfies (
2.2
).

It remains to prove the equality in (
2.3
). Let
f,g\in H^{1}(G)
be as above. Lemma
2.4
shows that
f
is linear (or harmonic) on every edge. We decompose
g=g_{1}+g_{2}
with
g_{1}
linear on every edge,
g_{1}(x)=g(x)
for every
x\in A
, and
g_{1}(x)=0
for every
x\in V\setminus A
. Then
g_{2}\big|_{A}\equiv 0
and we calculate

\displaystyle q(g,f)=q(g_{1},f)=\sum_{e\in E}\int_{e}g_{1}^{\prime}f^{\prime}dx=\sum_{x\in A}g(x)\sum_{e:e\sim x}\frac{f(x)-f(y_{e})}{\ell(e)},

completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\varnothing\neq A\subset G
be a closed subset and
f\in H^{1}(G)
. Then there exists a unique function
\widetilde{f}\in H^{1}(G)
such that

(2.4)

\begin{cases}\widetilde{f}|_{A}=f|_{A},\\
\widetilde{f}\text{ is harmonic on }G\setminus A.\end{cases}

Proof.

Consider the connected components
O_{i}
,
i\in I
, of
G\setminus A
. Since the topological boundary
\partial O_{i}
is finite, applying Proposition
2.6
to
\overline{O}_{i}=O\cup\partial O_{i}
, we find a unique function
f_{i}\in H^{1}(\overline{O}_{i})
with
f_{i}=f
on
\partial O_{i}
. Glueing together the functions
f_{i}
on
O_{i}
,
i\in I
, with
f
on
A
, we arrive at a unique function
\widetilde{f}
with the above properties.
∎

When considering harmonic functions, we will often simplify complicated graphs using the following proposition, which corresponds to parallel series in physics.

Definition 2.8

(Pumpkins)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. An
n
-pumpkin
is a metric graph consisting of two vertices joined by
n
distinct edges.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
. A
[n_{1},\dots,n_{m}]
-pumpkin chain
is a metric graph with
m+1
vertices
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
and
n_{1}+n_{2}+\dots+n_{m}
edges, out of which
n_{i}
join
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,m
.

Proposition 2.9

(Parallel series for pumpkin subgraphs)
.

Let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph and
f\in H^{1}(G)
harmonic on an open subset
O\subset G
.
Suppose
\overline{O}
contains two vertices
x_{1},x_{2}
and
n
edges
e_{1},\dots e_{n}\subset O
between
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.

Let
G^{\prime}
be the graph obtained by replacing the
n
edges by a new edge
e
connecting
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
with length
\ell^{\prime}(e)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(e_{i})^{-1}\right)^{-1}
. Denote by
\Phi\colon G\to G^{\prime}
the natural surjective map, obtained by linearly dilating every edge
e_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, onto
e
.

Then there exists a unique function
g
on
G^{\prime}
with
g\circ\Phi=f
. Moreover,
g
is harmonic on
\Phi(O)
and
q(g)=q(f)
.

Proof.

By construction, we can identify
G^{\prime}\setminus e
with
G\setminus(e_{1}\cup\dots\cup e_{n})
. Let
g\in H^{1}(G^{\prime})
be the unique function on
G^{\prime}
such that
g=f
on
G^{\prime}\setminus e
and
g
is linear on
e
. Since
f
is linear on each edge
e_{i}
by Lemma
2.4
, we obtain that
g\circ\Phi=f
, which proves existence of
g
. Uniqueness follows by a similar argument.

The harmonicity of
g
on
\Phi(O)
follows from Lemma
2.4
and the fact that

g_{e}^{\prime}(x_{1})=\frac{f(x_{2})-f(x_{1})}{\ell^{\prime}(e)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f(x_{2})-f(x_{1})}{\ell(e_{i})}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{e_{i}}^{\prime}(x_{1}).

To compare the energies, note first that

\displaystyle q(g|_{e})=\frac{|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|^{2}}{\ell^{\prime}(e)}

\displaystyle=|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\ell(e_{i})}=q(f|_{e_{1}\cup\dots\cup e_{n}}).

Since
g=f
on
G^{\prime}\setminus e\cong G\setminus(e_{1}\cup\dots\cup e_{n})
, we conclude that
q(g)=q(f)
.
∎

3.
Laplacians on metric graphs with measures

In the following, we discuss how to associate a Laplacian to a graph equipped with a measure. Throughout this section, we let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph.

3.1.
Measures and Sobolev spaces on
G

Let

\mathcal{M}:=\left\{\mu\text{ Borel measure on }G\;|\;\mu(G)=\int_{G}d\mu<\infty\right\}

be the set of all Borel measures on
G
having finite mass and

\mathcal{N}:=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}\;|\;\mu(G)=1\right\}\subset\mathcal{M}

the subset consisting of all probability measures. The support of a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
\operatorname{supp}\mu
.

Note that we can identify
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
with the positive linear functional
\varphi_{\mu}\colon C(G)\to\mathbb{R}
,
f\mapsto\int_{G}fd\mu
. This allows to view
\mathcal{M}
as a subset of the dual
(C(G)^{\ast},\|\cdot\|_{C(G)^{\ast}})
of
(C(G),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
. In the following, we endow
\mathcal{M}
with the weak-
\ast
topology. By the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem, we have
\|\mu\|_{C(G)^{\ast}}=\mu(G)
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. In particular, by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem,
\mathcal{N}
with the above topology is compact.

A first observation is the following inequality.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a metric graph and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. Suppose
f\in H^{1}(G)
has at least one zero. Then

(3.1)

q(f)\operatorname{diam}(G)\mu(G)\geq\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\mu(G)\geq\int_{G}|f|^{2}d\mu,

where
\operatorname{diam}(G)=\operatorname{diam}_{\varrho}(G)
is the diameter of
G
with respect to the path metric
\varrho
.

Proof.

The second inequality is obvious. To prove the first inequality, fix
x_{0}\in G
with
|f(x_{0})|=\|f\|_{\infty}
. Let
\gamma\subset G
be a shortest path connecting
x_{0}
with an arbitrary zero of
f
. Since
\gamma
has length smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(G)
, we obtain

\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}=|f(x_{0})|^{2}=\left|\int_{\gamma}f^{\prime}(s)ds\right|^{2}\leq\operatorname{diam}(G)\int_{G}|f^{\prime}|^{2}dx=\operatorname{diam}(G)q(f).\qed

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, we let
L^{2}(G,\mu)
be the corresponding
L^{2}
-space consisting of (equivalence classes of) measurable functions
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}:=\int_{G}|f|^{2}d\mu<\infty.

Furthermore, on the Sobolev space
H^{1}(G)
we define the scalar product

(f,g)_{H^{1},\mu}:=\int_{G}fg\,d\mu+\int_{G}f^{\prime}g^{\prime}dx=(f,g)_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}+q(f,g).

The corresponding norm is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1},\mu}
.

The next lemma ensures that these different norms on
H^{1}(G)
are all equivalent:

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider a metric graph
G
.

(i)

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then

(3.2)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{1}(G)}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu_{1})}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{2}(G)}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu_{2})}+\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}(G)q(f)}

for all
f\in H^{1}(G)
.

(ii)

There exists a uniform constant
C>0
such that for all
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
the estimate

\frac{1}{C}\|f\|_{H^{1},\mu}\leq\|f\|_{H^{1}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1},\mu},\qquad f\in H^{1}(G),

holds.

(iii)

(H^{1}(G),(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1},\mu})
is a Hilbert space for all
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Upon replacing measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
by their normalizations
\widetilde{\mu}=\mu/\mu(G)
, it suffices to prove the estimate (
3.2
) for
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{N}
. Then every function
f\in H^{1}(G)
can be decomposed as
f=c\mathbbm{1}_{G}+f_{0}
with

c=\int_{G}f\,d\mu_{2}\text{ and }f_{0}\in H^{1}(G)\text{ such that }\int_{G}f_{0}\,d\mu_{2}=0.

Since
f_{0}
must have at least one zero,
\|f_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{q(f)\operatorname{diam}(G)}
by Lemma
3.1
. Then

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\leq|c|+\|f_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\int_{G}|f|\,d\mu_{2}+\sqrt{q(f)\operatorname{diam}(G)}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu_{2})}+\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}(G)q(f)}.

Choosing
\mu_{2}
as the Lebesgue measure
dx
, one readily obtains the two-sided estimate in (ii). Finally, since
(H^{1}(G),(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1}})
is a Hilbert space and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1},\mu}
are equivalent, the same holds for
(H^{1}(G),(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1},\mu})
.
∎

In the following, we want to consider
H^{1}(G)
as a subspace of
L^{2}(G,\mu)
. If
\operatorname{supp}\mu\neq G
, two functions
f,g\in H^{1}(G)
can differ on
G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu
while the equality
f=g
still holds in
L^{2}(G,\mu)
. To remedy this, we define the closed subspace

H^{1}(G,\mu):=\{f\in H^{1}(G)\;|\;f\text{ is harmonic on }G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu\}\subset H^{1}(G).

Equipped with
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1},\mu}
, this space becomes a Hilbert space. If
\operatorname{supp}\mu=G
, then
H^{1}(G,\mu)=H^{1}(G)
as vector spaces.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. The orthogonal projection from
(H^{1}(G),(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1},\mu})
onto the closed subspace
H^{1}(G,\mu)\subset H^{1}(G)
is denoted by

\mathcal{P}_{\mu}:H^{1}(G)\to H^{1}(G,\mu).

Note that the image
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}(f)
of a function
f\in H^{1}(G)
is precisely the unique solution of the problem (
2.4
) for
A=\operatorname{supp}\mu
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
H^{1}_{0}(G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu):=\{f\in H^{1}(G)\;|\;f\big|_{\operatorname{supp}\mu}\equiv 0\}
. Then

H^{1}(G)=H^{1}(G,\mu)\oplus H^{1}_{0}(G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu)

is an orthogonal decomposition regarding the scalar product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H^{1},\mu}
. Moreover, the decomposition is orthogonal with respect to
q(\cdot)
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}
, meaning that

q(f,g)=\int_{G}fg\,d\mu=0

for all
f\in H^{1}(G,\mu)
and
g\in H^{1}_{0}(G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu)
.

Proof.

The orthogonality in the
L^{2}(G,\mu)
–scalar product is a direct consequence of the definition of
H^{1}_{0}(G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu)
. The orthogonality regarding the energy form follows from the definition of harmonicity on
G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu
.
∎

Note that the natural map
f\mapsto[f]
, sending every continuous function
f
to the corresponding equivalence class in
L^{2}(G,\mu)
, is injective from
H^{1}(G,\mu)
to
L^{2}(G,\mu)
. In particular, we have a natural inclusion
H^{1}(G,\mu)\subset L^{2}(G,\mu)
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. Then the embeddings

(H^{1}(G,\mu),\|\cdot\|_{H^{1},\mu})\hookrightarrow(C(G),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})

and

(H^{1}(G,\mu),\|\cdot\|_{H^{1},\mu})\hookrightarrow(L^{2}(G,\mu),\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu)})

are compact.

Proof.

The compactness of the embedding
(H^{1}(G),\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}})\hookrightarrow(C(G),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 8.8]
for the statement for intervals). Since the norms
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1},\mu}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
are equivalent by Lemma
3.2
, the first claim follows.

Now consider the map
\iota
sending each function
f\in(C(G),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
to its corresponding equivalence class
[f]\in(L^{2}(G,\mu),\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu)})
. Clearly
\iota
is bounded and thus composing this map with the compact embedding
(H^{1}(G,\mu),\|\cdot\|_{H^{1},\mu})\hookrightarrow(C(G),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, we obtain a compact operator. Since this operator is also injective (due to the definition of
H^{1}(G,\mu)
), it is in fact a compact embedding.
∎

3.2.
Laplacians on metric graphs with measures

Viewing
H^{1}(G,\mu)
as a subspace of
L^{2}(G,\mu)
, we consider
q\big|_{H^{1}(G,\mu)}
as a quadratic form in
L^{2}(G,\mu)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The quadratic form
q\big|_{H^{1}(G,\mu)}
is a densely defined, closed and non-negative form in
L^{2}(G,\mu)
.

Proof.

It is clear that
q\geq 0
. To show that
H^{1}(G,\mu)
is dense in
L^{2}(G,\mu)
it suffices to show the statement only on
\operatorname{supp}\mu
, i.e., we may assume that
\operatorname{supp}\mu=G
. But
C(G)\subset L^{2}(G)
is dense in
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}
-norm by Lusin’s theorem, and
H^{1}(G)\subset C(G)
is dense in
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-norm by the Stone-Weierstrass theorem, so the claim holds. Moreover,
q\big|_{H^{1}(G,\mu)}
is closed by Lemma
3.2
and the fact that
H^{1}(G,\mu)\subset H^{1}(G)
is a closed subspace.
∎

Consequently,
q
generates a non-negative, self-adjoint operator

\displaystyle H_{\mu}\colon\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})\subset L^{2}(G,\mu)\longrightarrow L^{2}(G,\mu)

defined by

\displaystyle f\in\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})\text{ and }H_{\mu}f=g\,\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle f\in H^{1}(G,\mu)\text{ and }q(f,\varphi)=\int_{G}g\varphi\,d\mu\text{ for all }\varphi\in H^{1}(G,\mu).

Using the orthogonal decomposition in Lemma
3.4
, one can prove that the equality

(3.3)

q(f,\varphi)=\int_{G}H_{\mu}f\varphi\,d\mu

holds more strongly for all
f\in\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})
and
\varphi\in H^{1}(G)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. Then
H_{\mu}
has discrete spectrum and
\ker(H_{\mu})=\operatorname{span}(\mathbbm{1}_{G})
.

Proof.

Discreteness of the spectrum is equivalent to the compactness of the embedding
H^{1}(G,\mu)\to L^{2}(G,\mu)
, which was proved in Lemma
3.5
. We have
f\in\ker H_{\mu}
exactly when
f^{\prime}\equiv 0
. Since
G
is connected, this holds exactly for constant
f
.
∎

The class of Laplacians
H_{\mu}
,
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, contains the following examples.

Example 3.8

(Weighted discrete Laplacians)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
be a measure of the form

\mu=\sum_{v\in V}m(v)\delta_{v}

where
\delta_{v}
denotes the Dirac measure at
v\in V
and
m\colon V\to(0,\infty)
is a weight function. Since
\operatorname{supp}\mu=V
, the corresponding
L^{2}
-space is finite dimensional and coincides with the space
\ell^{2}(V,m)=\{f\colon V\to\mathbb{R}\}
equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{m}=\Big(\sum_{v\in V}|f(v)|^{2}m(v)\Big)^{1/2},\qquad\text{for }f\colon V\to\mathbb{R}.

Under this identification, the operator
H_{\mu}
has domain
\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})=\ell^{2}(V,m)
and acts on
f\in\ell^{2}(V,m)
as a
weighted discrete Laplacian
given by

(3.4)

(H_{\mu}f)(v)=\frac{1}{m(v)}\sum_{w\sim v}\sum_{e=vw}\frac{1}{\ell(e)}(f(v)-f(w)),\qquad v\in V.

Example 3.9

(Weighted Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators on graphs)
.

For a subset
B\subset V
and a function
m\colon B\to(0,\infty)
, consider the measure

\mu=\sum_{b\in B}m(b)\delta_{b}.

The
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(G,\mu)
corresponds to
\ell^{2}(B,m)=\{f\colon B\to\mathbb{R}\}
with the norm

\|f\|_{m}=\Big(\sum_{b\in B}|f(b)|^{2}m(b)\Big)^{1/2},\qquad\text{for }f\colon B\to\mathbb{R}.

The operator
H_{\mu}
has domain
\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})=\ell^{2}(B,m)
. By Proposition
2.6
, every function
f\in\ell^{2}(B,m)
has a unique extension
\tilde{f}\in H^{1}(G)
such that
f=\tilde{f}
on
B
and
\tilde{f}
is harmonic on
G\setminus B
. In fact,
\tilde{f}
is linear on every edge
e
and satisfies
\sum_{w\sim v}\sum_{e=vw}\frac{1}{\ell(e)}(\tilde{f}(v)-\tilde{f}(w))=0
at every vertex
v\notin B
. As follows from Proposition
2.6
, the image
H_{\mu}f
is then given by

(H_{\mu}f)(v)=\frac{1}{m(v)}\sum_{w\sim v}\sum_{e\in vw}\frac{1}{\ell(e)}(\tilde{f}(v)-\tilde{f}(w)),\qquad v\in B.

Operators of this type are called
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators on graphs
in the literature. Their eigenvalues are often referred to as
Steklov eigenvalues
. If
B=V
, then
H_{\mu}
is a weighted discrete Laplacian from Example
3.8
.

Remark 3.10
.

In Example
3.9
, we can actually consider a general finite subset
B\subset G
. Namely, the condition
B\subset V
always holds after choosing a model which contains
B
in its vertex set
V
(which can be done by suitably subdividing edges).

Example 3.11

(Weighted Kirchhoff Laplacians)
.

Fix a strictly positive function
m\in L^{1}(G)
. Consider the measure
\mu=m(x)\,dx
and the corresponding Laplacian
H_{\mu}\colon\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})\subset L^{2}(G,\mu)\to L^{2}(G,\mu)
. Introduce also the space

H^{2}(G\setminus V,\mu)=\bigoplus_{e\in E}\Big\{f_{e}\in\mathrm{AC}([0,\ell(e)])\colon f_{e}^{\prime}\in AC[0,\ell(e)],\,\frac{f_{e}^{\prime\prime}}{m}\in L^{2}((0,\ell(e)),m\,dx_{e})\Big\}

where
\operatorname{AC}[0,\ell(e)]
denotes the space of absolutely continuous functions and
dx_{e}
the Lebesgue measure on an interval edge
[0,\ell(e)]
,
e\in E
. Then the operator
H_{\mu}
acts edgewise as the weighted second derivative

H_{\mu}f(x)=-\frac{1}{m(x)}f^{\prime\prime}(x)

on the domain

\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})=\{f=(f_{e})_{e\in E}\in H^{2}(G\setminus V,\mu)|\,\text{ $f$ satisfies \eqref{eq:kirchhoff} at every vertex $v\in V$}\},

where the so-called
Kirchhoff conditions
at a vertex
v\in V
are given by

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f\ \text{is continuous at}\ v,\\[2.84526pt]
\sum\limits_{e\sim v}f_{e}^{\prime}(v)=0,\end{cases}

and we sum over all edges
e
incident to
v
, which are all oriented towards
v
.

For
\mu=dx
, the operator
H_{\mu}=H_{dx}
is the standard Kirchhoff Laplacian on
G
.

Example 3.12

(Limits of shrinking manifolds)
.

Consider a measure on
G
of the form

\mu=\sum_{e\in E}\mu_{e}dx_{e}+\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{v}\delta_{v}

for coefficients
(\mu_{e})_{e\in E},(\mu_{v})_{v\in V}\subset(0,\infty)
.

The corresponding Laplacian
H_{\mu}\colon\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})\subset L^{2}(G,\mu)\to L^{2}(G,\mu)
has domain

\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})=\{f\in L^{2}(G,\mu)|\,\text{$f$ is continuous and }f_{e}\in H^{2}((0,\ell(e))\text{ for all edges $e$}\},

where
H^{2}((a,b))
is the second order Sobolev space on an interval
(a,b)
.

Moreover,
H_{\mu}
maps
f\in\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})
to the function
H_{\mu}f\in L^{2}(G,\mu)
given by

(H_{\mu}f)(x)=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{\mu_{e}}f^{\prime\prime}(x),&\text{if $x$ lies in the interior of $e\in E$}\\
\frac{1}{\mu_{x}}\sum_{e\sim x}f_{e}^{\prime}(x),&\text{if $x$ is a vertex}\end{cases}

where we sum over the edges
e
incident to
x
, which are all oriented towards
x
.

Such Laplacians appear as limits of Laplacians on shrinking manifolds, if different parts of the manifolds shrink at a specific relative speed, see
[
21
, Section 7]
and
[
47
, Section 7.2.3]
.

4.
Optimal eigenvalues on a metric graph

Throughout this section, let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph. Consider a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
and let
H_{\mu}
be the associated Laplacian acting in
L^{2}(G,\mu)
. By Lemma
3.7
, the spectrum of
H_{\mu}
consists of an increasing sequence of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, which we enumerate, counting multiplicities, as

\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu})<\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})\leq\lambda_{2}(H_{\mu})\leq\dots.

If
\dim(L^{2}(G,\mu))<\infty
or, equivalently, the support of
\mu
is finite, then this sequence is finite; otherwise, the sequence is infinite. For convenience, we set
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}):=\infty
if
k\geq\dim(L^{2}(G,\mu))
and index the sequence over
k
in
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
in both cases.

We have already seen that
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu})=0
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
(Lemma
3.7
). For
k\geq 1
, we have the variational characterizations

\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset H^{1}(G,\mu)\\
\dim F=k+1\end{subarray}}\max\limits_{f\in F\setminus\{0\}}\;R_{\mu}(f),

where
R_{\mu}(f):={q(f)}/{\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G;\mu)}}
is the Rayleigh quotient of
f\in H^{1}(G,\mu)
, and

\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{0},\dots,f_{k-1}\in H^{1}(G,\mu)\end{subarray}}\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}(G,\mu)\setminus\{0\}\\
f\perp_{\mu}f_{0},\dots,f_{k-1}\end{subarray}}R_{\mu}(f).

In particular, the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})
equals

(4.1)

\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}(G,\mu)\\
f\neq 0,\;f\perp_{\mu}\mathbbm{1}\end{subarray}}R_{\mu}(f)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}(G)\\
f\neq 0,\;f\perp_{\mu}\mathbbm{1}\end{subarray}}R_{\mu}(f),

where the second equality follows from Lemma
3.4
.

In the following, we are interested in maximizing and minimizing the
k
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
with respect to the measure
\mu
. The following continuity result provides the basis of our study.

Theorem 4.1

(Spectral continuity)
.

Let
(\mu_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}
be a sequence of measures converging to a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
in the weak-
\ast
topology. Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then:

(i)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
in
[0,\infty]
.

(ii)

Assume that
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})<\infty
, so that
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})<\infty
for all large
m
by (i). Let
v^{0}_{m},\dots,v^{k}_{m}
be orthonormal eigenfunctions of
H_{\mu_{m}}
for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu_{m}}),\dots,\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})
. Then, upon passing to a subsequence,
v^{0}_{m},\dots,v^{k}_{m}
converge uniformly on
G
to orthonormal eigenfunctions
v^{0},\dots,v^{k}
of
H_{\mu}
for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}),\dots,\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
.

We stress that the convergence in Theorem
4.1
(i) holds in
[0,\infty]
, that is, we allow that
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=+\infty
or
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})=+\infty
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
k
. The base case
k=0
is trivial by Lemma
3.7
. Suppose the statement is true for
k-1
. We first assume that
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})<\infty
. Then the support of
\mu
must contain at least
(k+1)
points. By the weak-
\ast
convergence, the same must hold for the measure
\mu_{m}
if
m
is large. In particular, we must have
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})<\infty
for all large
m
.

Fix
\mu_{m}
-orthonormal eigenfunctions
v^{0}_{m},\dots,v^{k-1}_{m}
of
H_{\mu_{m}}
for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu_{m}}),\dots,\lambda_{k-1}(H_{\mu_{m}})
. By the induction hypothesis, passing to a subsequence, we may assume that the functions
v^{j}_{m}
converge uniformly on
G
to
\mu
-orthonormal eigenfunctions
v^{j}
of
H_{\mu}
for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}),\dots\lambda_{k-1}(H_{\mu})
.

We first show that

(4.2)

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})\leq\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}).

Choose a function
v\in H^{1}(G,\mu)
with
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=R_{\mu}(v)
and
v\perp_{\mu}v^{j}
for all
j<k
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the function

v_{m}:=\mathcal{P}_{\mu_{m}}(v)-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\int_{G}v\cdot v_{m}^{j}d\mu_{m}\right)v_{m}^{j}

belongs to
H^{1}(G,\mu_{m})
and satisfies
v_{m}\perp_{\mu_{m}}v_{m}^{j}
for all
j<k
. In addition,

q(v_{m}^{j},v_{m}^{i})=\lambda_{i}(H_{\mu_{m}})\delta_{i,j}

and thus, using (
3.3
),

q(v_{m})=q(\mathcal{P}_{\mu_{m}}(v))-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\int_{G}v\cdot v_{m}^{j}d\mu_{m}\right)^{2}\lambda_{j}(H_{\mu_{m}})\leq q(\mathcal{P}_{\mu_{m}}(v)).

Since
\mu_{m}
converges to
\mu
in the weak-
\ast
topology and
v_{m}^{j}
converges to
v^{j}
uniformly, we have, as
m\to\infty
,

\int_{G}v\cdot v_{m}^{j}d\mu_{m}\to\int_{G}v\cdot v^{j}d\mu=0\qquad\text{ for all }j<k.

Moreover, with
\mathcal{P}_{\mu_{m}}=\operatorname{id}
in
L^{2}(G,\mu_{m})
and
q(\mathcal{P}_{\mu_{m}}(\cdot))\leq q(\cdot)
, we get

\int_{G}v_{m}^{2}\,d\mu_{m}\to\int_{G}v^{2}\,d\mu,\qquad R_{\mu_{m}}(v_{m})\leq\frac{q(v)}{\int_{G}v_{m}^{2}\,d\mu_{m}}\to R_{\mu}(v)

as
m\to\infty
. In particular,

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})\leq\limsup_{m\to\infty}R_{\mu_{m}}(v_{m})\leq R_{\mu}(v)=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})

and (
4.2
) is proved.

Next we prove that, up to restricting to a suitable subsequence, we also have

(4.3)

\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})\leq\liminf_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}}).

Since a sequence
(a_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
converges to
a\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if every subsequence of
(a_{n})_{n}
has a subsequence on which the convergence holds, applying the above to arbitrary subsequences of
\mu_{m}
, this then implies that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Then (
4.2
) implies that

q(v_{m}^{k})=R_{\mu_{m}}(v_{m}^{k})=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})\leq\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})+\varepsilon

for all large
m
. In particular, the sequence
(v_{m}^{k})_{m}
is bounded in
(H^{1}(G),\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}})
by Lemma
3.2
. Applying Lemma
3.5
, up to restricting to a subsequence, we can assume that
(v_{m}^{k})_{m}
converges uniformly to a function
v^{k}\in C(G)
.
Using the definition of weak-
\ast
topology, one readily obtains that

1=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{G}\left(v_{m}^{k}\right)^{2}\,d\mu_{m}=\int_{G}\left(v^{k}\right)^{2}\,d\mu

and that, since
v_{m}^{j}
converges uniformly to
v^{j}
for all
j<k
,

\qquad 0=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{G}v_{m}^{k}v_{m}^{j}\,d\mu_{m}=\int_{G}v^{k}v^{j}\,d\mu.

We also have that
(v_{m}^{k})_{m}
converges to
v^{k}
in
L^{2}(G)=L^{2}(G,dx)
. Moreover, since the quadratic form
q
is closed in
L^{2}(G)
, it is lower semi-continuous in
L^{2}(G)
(see, e.g.,
[
17
, Theorem 1.2.1]
). Thus, we conclude that
v^{k}
belongs to the form domain
\operatorname{dom}(q)=H^{1}(G)
and

q(v^{k})\leq\liminf_{m\to\infty}q(v_{m}^{k}).

Combining the above statement, we obtain that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\liminf_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})=\liminf_{m\to\infty}\frac{q(v_{m}^{k})}{\int_{G}\left(v_{m}^{k}\right)^{2}\,d\mu_{m}}\geq\frac{q(v^{k})}{\int_{G}\left(v^{k}\right)^{2}\,d\mu}=R_{\mu}(v^{k}).\end{split}

Since
(v^{0},\dots,v^{k})
are mutually orthogonal, by the min-max-principle it follows that
R_{\mu}(v^{k})\geq R_{\mu}(\mathcal{P}_{\mu}(v^{k}))\geq\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
and hence (
4.3
) holds. This finishes the proof of the claim in (i).

The mutual orthogonality of
(v^{0},\dots,v^{k})
as well as
\|v^{k}\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}=1
have already been proved. It remains to show that
v^{k}\in H^{1}(G,\mu)
is an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
. Using the above and taking into account that
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}v^{k}
is orthogonal to
v^{0},\dots,v^{k-1}
in
L^{2}(G,\mu)
, we already have

R_{\mu}(v^{k})\leq\liminf_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})\leq\frac{q(\mathcal{P}_{\mu}v^{k})}{\int_{G}(\mathcal{P}_{\mu}v^{k})^{2}d\mu}\leq\frac{q(v^{k})}{\int_{G}\left(v^{k}\right)^{2}d\mu}=R_{\mu}(v^{k}).

Thus
R_{\mu}(v^{k})=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
. Further, the last inequality shows that
q(\mathcal{P}_{\mu}v^{k})=q(v^{k})
. Since
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}v^{k}=v^{k}
on
\operatorname{supp}\mu
, we get
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}v^{k}\equiv v^{k}
and
v^{k}\in H^{1}(G,\mu)
by Proposition
2.6
. Thus
H_{\mu}v^{k}=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})v^{k}
, and
v^{k}
is in fact a eigenfunction for
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
.

To complete the proof, we treat the case that
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=+\infty
or, equivalently,
\dim L^{2}(G,\mu)\leq k
. Assume there exists a sequence
(\mu_{m})_{m}\subset\mathcal{M}
converging to
\mu
with
\liminf_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})<+\infty
. By the above argument, for a subsequence, the eigenfunctions
v_{m}^{0},\dots,v_{m}^{k}
converge uniformly to mutually orthogonal functions
v^{0},\dots,v^{k}\in L^{2}(G,\mu)
. But this contradicts the fact that
\dim L^{2}(G,\mu)\leq k
.
∎

In order to prevent scaling problems, we restrict to our optimization problems to measures in
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{M}
. It turns out that the question of maximizing the
k
-th eigenvalue has a trivial answer:

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
be a dense subset of
\mathcal{N}
. Then

\sup_{\mu\in\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=+\infty\qquad\text{for all $k\geq 1$}.

Proof.

By density,
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
contains a sequence
(\mu_{m})_{m}
converging to a Dirac measure
\mu=\delta_{x}
. But then
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{m}})\to\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=+\infty
for
m\to\infty
by Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Note that in Corollary
4.2
, we can choose
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
as the set of probability measures with finite support, or having the form
\mu=fdx
for a smooth function
f>0
.

On the other hand, the minimum of
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
over
\mathcal{N}
exists by Theorem
4.1
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
G
be a metric graph and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The
k
-the optimal eigenvalue
of
G
is defined by

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G):=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}).

As is clear from Lemma
3.7
, we have
\lambda_{0}^{\min}(G)=0
and
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)>0
for all
k\geq 1
.

Remark 4.4
.

Multiplying all edge lengths in
G
by
c>0
, we obtain another metric graph
G_{c}
of total length
L(G_{c})=cL(G)
. It is not hard to see that

(4.5)

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)=c\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G_{c}).

Thus, one can assume that
G
has total length
L(G)=1
when studying
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)
.

5.
The first optimal eigenvalue

In this section, we derive three different characterizations of the first optimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
: in terms of two-point-measures, resistance metrics and graph partitions, respectively.

5.1.
Characterization via two-point-measures

As it turns out, the first eigenvalue is minimized by a measure supported on two points. This reduces the analysis of the optimal eigenvalue to rather special measures.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G
be a metric graph. Then there exists a measure
\mu
of the form

(5.1)

\mu=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{y}

for two points
x\neq y\in G
such that

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu}).

Moreover, every measure
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
with
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})
is of the form (
5.1
) for some points
x\neq y\in G
.

Before proving Theorem
5.1
, we introduce the following notation. For two points
x\neq y\in G
, define the measure
\mu_{x,y}\in\mathcal{N}
as
\mu_{x,y}:=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{y}
and the function
f_{x,y}\in H^{1}(G,\mu)
as the unique solution of the equations

(5.2)

\begin{cases}f(x)=1,\\
f(y)=-1,\\
f\text{ harmonic}\text{ on }G\setminus\{x,y\}.\end{cases}

Then the function
f_{x,y}
is the unique
\mu_{x,y}
-normalized eigenfunction (up to the sign) of the Laplacian
H_{\mu_{x,y}}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu_{x,y}})
. In terms of these notions, Theorem
5.1
asserts that

(5.3)

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\min_{x\neq y\in G}\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu_{x,y}})=\min_{x\neq y\in G}q(f_{x,y}).

In order to prove Theorem
5.1
, we first provide two lemmas.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
and let
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function such that
\int_{G}f\,d\mu=0
. Then:

(i)

\|f\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}^{2}\leq|\max f||\min f|
, where
\max f
and
\min f
denote the maximum and minimum of
f
, respectively.

(ii)

There exist
x,y\in G
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that the measure
\nu=\alpha\delta_{x}+(1-\alpha)\delta_{y}
satisfies

\int_{G}|f|^{2}\,d\nu=\int_{G}|f|^{2}\,d\mu\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{G}f\,d\nu=0.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claims in case that
\|f\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}=1
. Then

\int_{G}(f-\min f)^{2}d\mu=\int_{G}f^{2}d\mu-2\int_{G}f\min fd\mu+\int_{G}(\min f)^{2}d\mu=1+(\min f)^{2}.

Since
f(x)-\min f\leq\max f-\min f
for
x\in G
, we further obtain the estimate

\displaystyle 1+(\min f)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{G}(f-\min f)^{2}d\mu\leq(\max f-\min f)\int_{G}f-\min fd\mu

\displaystyle=(\max f-\min f)(-\min f)=-\min f\max f+(\min f)^{2},

which proves the inequality in (i).

In order to prove the claim in (ii), note first that
\max f>0>\min f
since
\int_{G}f\,d\mu=0
. Combining the inequality in (i) with the continuity of
f
, we infer that there exist points
x,y\in G
with
f(x)f(y)=-1
. Setting
\alpha:=\frac{1}{f(x)^{2}+1}<1
, we get

\int_{G}fd\nu=\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)=\frac{f(x)}{f(x)^{2}+1}+\frac{f(x)^{2}f(y)}{f(x)^{2}+1}=0

and

\int_{G}|f|^{2}d\nu=\alpha f(x)^{2}+(1-\alpha)f(y)^{2}=\frac{f(x)^{2}}{f(x)^{2}+1}+\frac{f(y)^{2}f(x)^{2}}{f(x)^{2}+1}=1,

which concludes the proof.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

Further analysis of the above proof shows that Lemma
5.2
generalizes from metric graphs to connected compact Hausdorff spaces.

Lemma 5.4
.

Consider a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
with
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
. Let
f
be an eigenfunction for the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})
and let
\nu
be the measure from Lemma
5.2
. Then
\operatorname{supp}\mu=\operatorname{supp}\nu=\{x,y\}
.

Proof.

Since
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)<\infty
, the support of
\mu
must contain at least two points. Thus, it suffices to prove that
\operatorname{supp}\mu\subseteq\operatorname{supp}\nu
.

Assuming the opposite, let
x\in G
be a point which lies in
\operatorname{supp}\mu
, but not in
\operatorname{supp}\nu
. Then there exists a small open neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
\nu(U)=0
and
0<\mu(U)<1
(here we use that
\operatorname{supp}\mu\neq\{x\}
).

We claim that there exists a point
s\in U\cap\operatorname{supp}\mu
such that
f(s)\neq 0
. Indeed, if
f\equiv 0
on
U\cap\operatorname{supp}\mu
, then the measure
\hat{\mu}\in\mathcal{N}
defined as

\hat{\mu}(A)=\frac{1}{\mu(G\setminus U)}\mu(A\setminus U),\qquad\text{$A\subset G$ measurable},

would satisfy
\mu(G\setminus U)\|f\|^{2}_{\hat{\mu}}=\|f\|^{2}_{\mu}
and

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(H_{\hat{\mu}})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in H^{1}(G)\\
g\perp_{\hat{\mu}}\mathbbm{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{q(g)}{\|g\|_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}}\leq\frac{q(f)}{\|f\|_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}}<\frac{q(f)}{\|f\|_{\mu}^{2}}=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G),

a contradiction.

Fix a test function
\varphi\geq 0
in
H^{1}(G)
with
\varphi(s)>0
and which is supported on a small neighborhood
W
of
s
, such that
f(w)\neq 0
for all
w\in W
. Taking into account (
3.3
) and the fact that
s\in\operatorname{supp}\mu
, we obtain that

q(f,\varphi)=\int_{G}\varphi H_{\mu}f\,d\mu=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})\int_{W}\varphi f\,d\mu\neq 0.

We finish the proof by showing that, simultaneously,

q(f,\varphi)=0,

which provides the desired contradiction. Since
\varphi
is supported on
G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\nu
by construction, it is enough to prove that
f
is harmonic on
G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\nu
. Consider the projection map
\mathcal{P}_{\nu}\colon H^{1}(G)\to H^{1}(G,\nu)
for the orthogonal decomposition
H^{1}(G)=H^{1}(G,\nu)\oplus H^{1}_{0}(G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\nu)
. Taking into account the properties of
\nu
in Lemma
5.2
and Proposition
2.6
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})

\displaystyle=\frac{q(f)}{\|f\|_{\mu}^{2}}=\frac{q(f)}{\|f\|_{\nu}^{2}}=\frac{q(f)}{\|\mathcal{P}_{\nu}(f)\|_{\nu}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q(\mathcal{P}_{\nu}f)}{\|\mathcal{P}_{\nu}(f)\|_{\nu}^{2}}\geq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in H^{1}(G,\nu)\\
g\perp_{\nu}\mathbbm{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{q(g)}{\|g\|_{\nu}^{2}}=\lambda_{1}(H_{\nu})\geq\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G).

These inequalities show that
q(f)=q(\mathcal{P}_{\nu}(f))
. Applying Proposition
2.6
once again, we conclude that
f
is harmonic on
G\setminus\operatorname{supp}\nu
, finishing the proof.

∎

After these preparations, we are in position to prove Theorem
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
and fix an eigenfunction
f
for the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})
. Consider the discrete measure
\nu=\alpha\delta_{x}+(1-\alpha)\delta_{y}
constructed in Lemma
5.2
. We will prove that

(5.4)

\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})\geq\lambda_{1}(H_{\nu})\geq\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu_{x,y}}).

The first inequality in (
5.4
) is a consequence of the properties of
\nu
, that is,

\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\frac{q(f)}{\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}}=\frac{q(f)}{\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\nu)}}\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in H^{1}(G)\setminus\{0\}\\
g\perp_{\nu}\mathbbm{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{q(g)}{\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\nu)}}=\lambda_{1}(H_{\nu}),

where we used (
4.1
). Consider the family of measures
\nu_{\beta}:=\beta\delta_{x}+(1-\beta)\delta_{y}
for
\beta\in(0,1)
. We prove that

(5.5)

\lambda_{1}(H_{\nu_{\beta}})\geq\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu_{x,y}})

for all
\beta
, which in particular implies the second inequality in (
5.4
). Fix a
\nu_{\beta}
-normalized eigenfunction
f_{\beta}\in H^{1}(G,\nu_{\beta})
for
\lambda_{1}(H_{\nu_{\beta}})
. Since
f_{\beta}\perp_{\nu_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{G}
, we infer

f_{\beta}(y)=\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}f_{\beta}(x).

Using this equality in the equation
\|f_{\beta}\|_{L^{2}(G,\mu_{\alpha})}=1
leads to

f_{\beta}(x)=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}.

By Proposition
2.6
,
f_{\beta}
is now uniquely determined by
\beta
(up to the sign). Taking
g\in H^{1}(G)
as the unique function which is harmonic on
G\setminus\{x,y\}
and satisfies
g(x)=1
and
g(y)=0
, we obtain the equality
f_{\beta}-f_{\beta}(y)\mathbbm{1}=(f_{\beta}(x)-f_{\beta}(y))g
. This means that the minimum

\min_{\beta}\lambda_{1}(H_{\nu_{\beta}})=\min_{\beta}q(f_{\beta})=\min_{\beta}(f_{\beta}(x)-f_{\beta}(y))^{2}q(g)=q(g)\min_{\beta}\left|\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}+\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}}\right|^{2}

is uniquely attained for
\beta=1/2
, so that (
5.5
) follows.

Having established (
5.4
), we prove the above claims as follows. Let
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
be a measure with
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\lambda_{1}^{\min}
. Then (
5.4
) implies that
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu_{x,y}})=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\lambda_{1}^{\min}
. On the other hand, by Lemma
5.4
, the support of
\mu
equals
\operatorname{supp}\mu=\operatorname{supp}\nu=\{x,y\}
, so that
\mu=\nu_{\beta}
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
. Since the inequality in (
5.5
) is strict for
\beta\neq 1/2
, it follows that
\mu=\mu_{x,y}
, completing the proof.
∎

Theorem
5.1
implies another characterization of
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
using the space
H^{1}(G)
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
G
be a metric graph. Then

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}(G)\\
\max f=1,\,\min f=-1\end{subarray}}q(f).

Proof.

Consider first a function
f\in H^{1}(G)
such that
f(a)=1=\max f
and
f(b)=-1=\min f
for some
a\neq b\in G
. Then Proposition
2.6
implies that

q(f)\geq q(f_{a,b})=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu_{a,b}})\geq\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G).

On the other hand, let
\mu=\mu_{x,y}
be a minimizing measure from Theorem
5.1
. Then the corresponding eigenfunction
f_{x,y}\in H^{1}(G,\mu)
satisfies
\max f=f(x)=1
and
\min f=f(y)=-1
by Proposition
2.5
. Moreover,
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=q(f_{x,y})
. Combining these observations, the claim follows.
∎

For later use, we also collect the following auxiliary fact.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mu=\mu_{x,y}
be a minimizing measure for
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
. Then

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=q(f_{x,y})=2\sum_{e=xz}\frac{1-f_{x,y}(z)}{\ell(e)}=2\sum_{e=yz}\frac{1+f_{x,y}(z)}{\ell(e)},

where in this representation we consider a model
(V,E,\ell)
of
G
with
x,y\in V
.

Proof.

Since
f_{x,y}
is harmonic on
G\setminus\{x,y\}
, Proposition
2.6
leads to

\displaystyle 0=q(f_{x,y},\mathbbm{1}_{G})=\sum_{e=xz}\frac{1-f_{x,y}(z)}{\ell(e)}+\sum_{e=yz}\frac{-1-f_{x,y}(z)}{\ell(e)},

which proves the last of the above equalities. Applying Proposition
2.6
once again,

\displaystyle q(f_{x,y})=\sum_{e=xz}\frac{1-f_{x,y}(z)}{\ell(e)}+\sum_{e=yz}\frac{1+f_{x,y}(z)}{\ell(e)},

yielding the remaining equalities.
∎

5.2.
Characterization via resistance

Let
G
be a metric graph. For points
x,y\in G
, there exists a unique solution
f\in H^{1}(G)
to the equations

(5.6)

\begin{cases}\Delta f=\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}\\
f(y)=0\end{cases}

where
\Delta
is the distributional Laplacian on
H^{1}(G)
, i.e., the first equation in (
5.6
) is understood as

q(f,g)=g(x)-g(y)\qquad\text{for all $g\in H^{1}(G)$}.

The
(effective) resistance

r(x,y)
between
x
and
y
is defined as

r(x,y)=f(x).

The reciprocal
1/r(x,y)
is sometimes called the
(effective) conductance
between
x
and
y
.

In the literature, one can find many equivalent definitions of the resistance and in this regard we refer to
[
32
, Theorem 3.2]
. For instance,

(5.7)

r(x,y)=1/\min\{q(f)|\,f\in H^{1}(G),f(x)=1,f(y)=0\}.

We need also the following representation in terms of the function
f_{x,y}
introduced in (
5.2
). Taking into account Proposition
2.6
and Lemma
5.6
, it follows that the unique solution to (
5.6
) is given by
f=\frac{2}{q(f_{x,y})}(f_{x,y}+\mathbbm{1}_{G})
. In particular, the resistance
r(x,y)
can be written as

(5.8)

r(x,y)=\frac{2}{q(f_{x,y})}(f_{x,y}(x)+1)=\frac{4}{q(f_{x,y})}.

It is well-known that the resistance
(x,y)\in G\times G\mapsto r(x,y)
is a metric on
G
(e.g.,
[
32
, Lemma 3.5]
). It is comparable to the path metric
\varrho
in the sense that (see, e.g.,
[
32
, Lemma 3.52]
)

(5.9)

r(x,y)\leq\varrho(x,y)\qquad\text{for all $x,y\in G$}.

Moreover, the metrics
r
and
\varrho
coincide if and only if
G
is a tree. For general graphs, the next lemma controls the resistance
r
using the square of the path metric
\varrho
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
G
be a metric graph with total length
L(G)
. Then

r(x,y)\geq\frac{\varrho(x,y)^{2}}{L(B_{\varrho}(x,\varrho(x,y))}\geq\frac{\varrho(x,y)^{2}}{L(G)}\qquad\text{for all $x,y\in G$}.

Here,
B_{\varrho}(x,t)
denotes the ball in the path metric
\varrho
of radius
t
around
x\in G
, and
L(B_{\varrho}(x,t))
its Lebesgue volume.

Proof.

By (
5.7
), the estimate is equivalent to the inequality

\min\{q(f)|f\in H^{1}(G)\text{ with $f(x)=1$ and $f(y)=0$}\}\leq\frac{L(B_{\varrho}(x,\varrho(x,y))}{\varrho(x,y)^{2}}.

The latter follows immediately by considering the function
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(s)=\max\{1-\frac{\varrho(x,s)}{\varrho(x,y)},0\},\qquad s\in G.

The derivative
f^{\prime}
is supported on
B_{\varrho}(x,\varrho(x,y))
, where it satisfies
|f^{\prime}|=1/\varrho(x,y)
almost everywhere. Estimating
q(f)
accordingly, we complete the proof.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

The metrics
\varrho
and
r
are actually bilipschitz equivalent with

r(x,y)\leq\varrho(x,y)\leq 2\#(E)\,r(x,y)\qquad\text{for all $x,y\in G$}.

This follows from (
5.9
) combined with Lemma
5.7
and the elementary estimate
L(B_{\varrho}(x,t))\leq 2\#(E)t
, which is valid for
x\in G
and
t>0
.

The next theorem characterizes
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
in terms of the resistance metric
r
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
G
be a metric graph and
\operatorname{diam}_{r}(G)
the diameter of
G
with respect to the resistance metric
r
, i.e., the maximum of
r\colon G\times G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Then

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\frac{4}{\operatorname{diam}_{r}(G)}.

Moreover, the measures
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
with
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
are precisely the measures
\mu_{x,y}
for points
x,y\in G
with
r(x,y)=\operatorname{diam}_{r}(G)
.

If
G
is a tree, then

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\frac{4}{\operatorname{diam}_{r}(G)}=\frac{4}{\operatorname{diam}_{\varrho}(G)}.

Proof.

Combining (
5.3
) with the representation (
5.8
) for
r
, we infer that

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\min_{x\neq y}\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu_{x,y}})=\min_{x\neq y}q(f_{x,y})=\min_{x\neq y}\frac{4}{r(x,y)}=\frac{4}{\operatorname{diam}_{r}(G)}.

The claim concerning trees follows from the fact that
r=\varrho
for trees.
∎

5.3.
Characterization via spectral partitioning

As it turns out, there is an interesting connection between the first optimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{\min}
and spectral partitioning on graphs.
We begin with a variant of optimal eigenvalues which takes into account a Dirichlet condition.

Throughout this section, let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph. Given a closed, non-empty subset
A\subset G
, we
denote by

H^{1}_{0}(G,A):=\{f\in H^{1}(G)\;|\;f|_{A}\equiv 0\}

the space of
H^{1}
-functions vanishing in
A
. For a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(G\setminus A)
, that is, a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
with
\mu(A)=0
, we introduce the Sobolev space

\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(G,A,\mu):=\{f\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A)\;|\;f\text{ harmonic on }G\setminus(\operatorname{supp}\mu\cup A)\},

which is a dense subspace of the
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(G,\mu)
. In the above context, we also refer to
A
as the
Dirichlet set
.

It turns out that the restriction of the energy form
q
to
H^{1}_{0}(G,A,\mu)
is closed. Replacing
H^{1}(G,\mu)
by
H^{1}_{0}(G,A,\mu)
in the construction of the Laplacian
H_{\mu}
leads to a self-adjoint operator

H_{\mu}^{D}\colon\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu}^{D})\subset L^{2}(G,\mu)\to L^{2}(G,\mu)

in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(G,\mu)
. We call
H_{\mu}^{D}
the
Laplacian for
\mu
with Dirichlet condition at
A
, or simply the
Dirichlet Laplacian for
\mu
.

The operator
H_{\mu}^{D}
is non-negative and has purely discrete spectrum. Taking into account multiplicities, we enumerate its eigenvalues as

\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})\leq\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu}^{D})\leq\lambda_{2}(H_{\mu}^{D})\leq\dots.

Note that the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})
is strictly positive and admits the variational characterization

(5.10)

\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A,\mu)\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{q(f)}{\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A)\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{q(f)}{\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}}.

We are interested in the following Dirichlet variant of optimal eigenvalues.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
A
be a closed, non-empty subset in a metric graph
G
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the
k
-the optimal Dirichlet eigenvalue with respect to
A
is defined by

\lambda_{k}^{\min,D}:=\lambda_{k}^{\min,D}(G,A):=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}(G\setminus A)}\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}^{D}),

where
\mathcal{N}(G\setminus A):=\{\mu\in\mathcal{N}|\,\mu(A)=0\}
.

We focus on the smallest optimal Dirichlet eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}
. A first estimate is provided by the following variant of Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
G
be a metric graph and
\emptyset\neq A\subset G
a closed subset. Then

(5.11)

\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A)\geq\frac{1}{\operatorname{diam}(G)},

where
\operatorname{diam}(G)=\operatorname{diam}_{\varrho}(G)
is the diameter of
G
with respect to the path metric
\varrho
.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
3.1
and the characterization of
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu})
in (
5.10
).
∎

The next lemma reduces the study of
\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A)
to the case where
G\setminus A
is connected. For a subset
A\subset G
as above, the closures of all connected components
U_{i}
,
i\in I
, of
G\setminus A
form a countable family
(\overline{U}_{i})_{i\in I}
of metric subgraphs of
G
.

Lemma 5.12
.

Notations as above,

\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A)=\min_{i\in I}\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(\overline{U_{i}},\partial U_{i}),

where
\partial U_{i}=\overline{U_{i}}\cap A
denotes the boundary of
U_{i}
in
A
.

Proof.

First we prove that

\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A)=\inf_{i\in I}\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(\overline{U_{i}},\partial U_{i}).

The inequality „
\leq
“ is trivial, since every measure on a connected component
\overline{U_{i}}
yields a measure on
G
. To prove the reverse inequality, take a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{N}(G\setminus A)
and consider the restrictions
\mu_{i}=\mu|_{\overline{U_{i}}}
for
i\in I
. Then
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})
is an eigenvalue for the Laplacian
H_{\mu_{i}}^{D}
for every component
\overline{U_{i}}
on which the corresponding eigenfunction
f\in L^{2}(G,\mu)
is non-vanishing. Fixing such a component
\overline{U_{i}}
yields

\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})\geq\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu_{i}}^{D})\geq\frac{1}{\mu(\overline{U_{i}})}\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(\overline{U_{i}},\partial U_{i}).

Since
\mu(\overline{U_{i}})\leq 1
, the desired estimate follows.

Let
C>0
be arbitrary. Assume there exists a countable infinite set
J\subset I
with
\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(\overline{U_{i}},\partial U_{i})<C
for every
i\in J
. Applying Lemma
5.11
, we obtain

L(G)\geq\sum_{i\in J}L(\overline{U_{i}})\geq\sum_{i\in J}\operatorname{diam}(\overline{U_{i}})\geq\sum_{i\in J}\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{\min D}(\overline{U_{i}},\partial U_{i})}\geq\sum_{i\in J}\frac{1}{C}=\infty.

This contradiction shows that the infimum is in fact a minimum.
∎

The following result provides an analog of Theorem
5.1
for optimal Dirichlet eigenvalues.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
G
be a metric graph and
\emptyset\neq A\subset G
a closed subset. Then there exists a point
x\in G\setminus A
such that

\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A)=\lambda_{0}(H_{\delta_{x}}^{D}).

Moreover, every measure
\mu\in\mathcal{N}(G\setminus A)
with
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})=\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A)
is a Dirac measure
\mu=\delta_{x}
for some
x\in G\setminus A
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.12
, it suffices to treat the case where
A
is finite and
G\setminus A
is connected. Fixing a suitable model of
G
, we may also assume that all points of
A
belong to the vertex set
V
. Using the min-max principle, we obtain that

\displaystyle\lambda^{\min,D}_{0}(G,A)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}(G\setminus A)}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A)\\
\|f\|_{\infty}=1\end{subarray}}\frac{q(f)}{\int_{G}|f|^{2}d\mu}\geq\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}(G\setminus A)}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A)\\
\|f\|_{\infty}=1\end{subarray}}q(f)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A)\\
\|f\|_{\infty}=1\end{subarray}}q(f)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A)\\
f\geq 0,\,\max f=1\end{subarray}}q(f).

For every
f\geq 0
in
H^{1}_{0}(G,A)
with
\max f=1
, fix a point
x_{f}\in G
with
f(x_{f})=1
. Consider the unique function
\widetilde{f}\in H^{1}(G)
such that

\begin{cases}\widetilde{f}(x)=f(x),&\text{ if }x\in V\cup\{x_{f}\},\\
f\text{ harmonic },&\text{ on }G\setminus(V\cup\{x_{f}\}).\end{cases}

We have
q(\widetilde{f})\leq q(f)
by Proposition
2.6
and
\widetilde{f}\in H_{0}^{1}(G,A)
, since
A\subset V
. Setting
F:=\{\widetilde{f}\;|\;f\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A),\,f\geq 0,\,\max f=1\}
, the above leads to

\lambda^{\min,D}_{0}(G,A)\geq\inf_{h\in F}q(h).

Consider a sequence
(\widetilde{f}_{n})_{n}\subset F
such that
q(\widetilde{f}_{n})\to\inf_{h\in F}q(h)
for
n\to\infty
. Since
0\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}\leq 1
and
G
is compact, we may suppose that the values
\widetilde{f}_{n}(v)
for
v\in V
have limits
\alpha_{v}\in\mathbb{R}
, and moreover the corresponding points
x_{f_{n}}\in G
converge to a point
x_{0}\in G
. We would like to define a function
g\in H^{1}(G)
by requiring that

\begin{cases}g(v)=\alpha_{v},&\text{for all $v\in V$},\\
g(x_{0})=1,\\
g\text{ harmonic },&\text{ on }G\setminus(V\cup\{x_{0}\}).\end{cases}

In order to prove that
g
is well-defined, we have to verify that, if
x_{0}=u
is a vertex, then we also have
\alpha_{u}=1
. However, this follows from the estimate

\displaystyle|\alpha_{u}-1|^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}|\widetilde{f}_{n}(u)-\widetilde{f}_{n}(x_{f_{n}})|^{2}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\varrho(u,x_{f_{n}})q(\widetilde{f}_{n})=0.

By construction, the function
g
belongs to
H^{1}_{0}(G,A)
. Using that the energy of an edgewise linear function
h\in H^{1}(G)
equals
q(h)=\sum_{e=vw}(h(v)-h(w))^{2}/\ell(e)
, one can further verify that
\lim_{n\to\infty}q(\widetilde{f}_{n})\geq q(g)
.
Setting
\mu=\delta_{x_{0}}
, we then obtain that

\lambda^{\min,D}_{0}(G,A)\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}q(\widetilde{f}_{n})\geq q(g)=\frac{q(g)}{\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}}\geq\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})\geq\lambda^{\min,D}_{0}(G,A)

and the first statement is proved.

Assume that
\mu\in\mathcal{N}(G\setminus A)
satisfies
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})=\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A)
. Let
f
be a non-negative eigenfunction for
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})
with
\max_{x\in G}f(x)=1
. With Proposition
2.5
, we can choose
x\in\operatorname{supp}\mu\setminus A
with
f(x)=1
. Further let
g\in H^{1}_{0}(G,A)
be the unique solution to the equations

\begin{cases}g(x)=1,\\
g\equiv 0\text{ on $A$},\\
g\text{ harmonic on }G\setminus(A\cup\{x\}).\end{cases}

Due to Proposition
2.6
, we have

\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A)=\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu}^{D})=\frac{q(f)}{\int_{G}|f|^{2}d\mu}\geq q(g)=\lambda_{0}(H_{\delta_{x}}^{D})\geq\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G,A).

This equality means that
g=f
and
\int_{G}|f|^{2}d\mu=1
. In particular,
f=1

\mu
-almost everywhere. But
f=g
takes it maximum only at
x
due to Proposition
2.5
. It follows that
\mu=\delta_{x}
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 5.14
.

The infimum in the definition of
\lambda_{k}^{\min,D}
is in fact attained for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This can be shown by arguments similar to the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Our next objective is to introduce a notion of graph partitions adapted to our context. A (not necessarily exhaustive)
weighted
n
-partition
of a metric graph
G
is a collection

\mathcal{P}=\big\{(G_{1},\omega_{1}),\dots,(G_{n},\omega_{n})\big\}

where
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
are closed, non-empty and connected subsets of
G
with mutually disjoint interiors, and
\omega_{1},\dots\omega_{n}
are strictly positive reals with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}=1
.
Note that we only use the terminology partition informally, since the sets
G_{i}
may intersect on their boundaries, and their union might be a proper subset of
G
.
The
set of weighted
n
-partitions of a metric graph
G
is denoted by
\mathfrak{P}_{n}(G)
.

Given a weighted partition
\mathcal{P}\in\mathfrak{P}_{n}(G)
, every closed set
G_{i}
in
\mathcal{P}
is itself naturally a metric graph. We endow
G_{i}
with the Dirichlet set
\partial G_{i}
, that is, the topological boundary of
G_{i}
in
G
. Then
\mathcal{P}
has an inherent
Dirichlet energy
given by

\Lambda(\mathcal{P}):=\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\frac{1}{\omega_{i}}\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G_{i},\partial G_{i}).

Minimizing the energy with respect to all partitions leads to

\mathcal{L}_{n}(G):=\inf_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathfrak{P}_{n}}\Lambda(\mathcal{P}).

Finding a minimizing partition, i.e., a partition
\mathcal{P}\in\mathfrak{P}_{n}(G)
with
\Lambda(\mathcal{P})=\mathcal{L}_{n}(G)
, is a hard problem in general. However, for partitions consisting only of
n=2
pieces, we have the following connection to the first optimal eigenvalue.

Theorem 5.15
.

Let
G
be a metric graph. Then

\mathcal{L}_{2}(G)=\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G).

Moreover, if
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
is a minimizing measure for
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
and
f\in L^{2}(G,\mu)
is an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})
, then the nodal domains

\displaystyle N^{+}=\overline{\{x\in G\;|\;f(x)>0\}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad N^{-}=\overline{\{x\in G\;|\;f(x)<0\}}

together with the weights
\omega_{+}=\omega_{-}=\frac{1}{2}
form a minimizing
2
-partition for
\mathcal{L}_{2}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
\mu=\mu_{x,y}
be a minimizing measure from Theorem
5.1
, and
f=f_{x,y}
the corresponding eigenfunction. Then the nodal domains
N^{+}
and
N^{-}
are closed, non-empty and have disjoint interiors. Their connectivity can be deduced from the min-max principle. That is, enumerate the connected components of
N^{+}
and
N^{-}
as
N_{1}^{+},\dots,N_{p}^{+}
and
N_{1}^{-},\dots,N_{q}^{-}
, respectively, with
x\in N^{+}_{1}
and
y\in N^{-}_{1}
. Then by Corollary
5.5
,

\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=q(f)\geq q(f|_{N^{+}_{1}\cup N^{-}_{1}})\geq\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu}),

so
f
is constant on each connected component of
G\setminus(N^{+}_{1}\cup N^{-}_{1})
. Due to the continuity of
f
, this means that
f\equiv 0
on
G\setminus(N^{+}_{1}\cup N^{-}_{1})
, and thus
p=q=1
.

It follows that
\mathcal{P}=\{(N^{+},1/2),(N^{-},1/2)\}
is a weighted
2
-partition. Note that the restriction
f|_{N^{+}}
is an eigenfunction for the operator
H_{\delta_{x}}^{D}
on
N^{+}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})/2
. The same holds for
\delta_{y}
on
N^{-}
. Thus,

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})\geq\Lambda(\mathcal{P})\geq\mathcal{L}_{2}(G).

Conversely, let
\mathcal{P}=\{(G_{1},\omega_{1}),(G_{2},\omega_{2})\}
be an arbitrary weighted
2
-partition. By Lemma
5.13
, we can find points
x_{i}\in G_{i}
,
i=1,2
, such that the measure
\mu_{i}:=\delta_{x_{i}}
minimizes the first Dirichlet eigenvalue on
G_{i}
. Set
\mu:=\omega_{1}\mu_{1}+\omega_{2}\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{N}
. Fix a
\mu_{i}
-normalized, non-negative eigenfunction
f_{i}\in H_{0}^{1}(G_{i},\partial G_{i})
for
\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu_{i}}^{D})
on
G_{i}
. We extend
f_{i}
,
i=1,2
, by zero to all of
G
and consider the function

f:=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}}f_{1}-\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}}f_{2}\in H^{1}(G).

By construction,
f\perp_{\mu}\mathbbm{1}_{G}
and
\|f\|_{\mu}=1
, so that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)\leq\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})\leq q(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}\int_{G_{1}}|f_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}dx+\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}\int_{G_{2}}|f_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\omega_{2}(\frac{1}{\omega_{1}}\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu_{1}}^{D}))+\omega_{1}(\frac{1}{\omega_{2}}\lambda_{0}(H_{\mu_{2}}^{D}))\leq\Lambda(\mathcal{P}).

Since
\mathcal{P}\in\mathfrak{P}_{2}(G)
was arbitrary, we have proved the statement.
∎

The analog of Theorem
5.15
for higher optimal eigenvalues fails. For example,
\lambda_{2}^{\min}(C)<\mathcal{L}_{3}(C)
for the circle
C
. Nevertheless, we pose the following conjecture, which constitutes a weaker version of Theorem
5.15
for
k\geq 1
.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(G_{i},\omega_{i})_{i=0,\dots,k}
be a minimizing partition for
\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(G)
and
x_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,k
, be points on
G
such that
\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}(G_{i},\partial G_{i})
is realized by the Dirac measure
\delta_{x_{i}}
. Then the measure

\mu=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\omega_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}

is minimizing, i.e.,

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}).

In particular, there is a minimizing measure for
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)
supported on
k+1
points.

We close the section with an overview of all equivalent characterizations.

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
G
be a metric graph. Then:

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\min\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})=\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}q(f_{x,y})=\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}(G)\\
\max f=1\\
\min f=-1\end{subarray}}q(f)=\dfrac{4}{\operatorname{diam}_{r}(G)}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(G).

6.
Further properties of the first optimal eigenvalue

In what follows, we discuss further properties of the first optimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{\min}
. Throughout this section, let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph.

6.1.
Basic properties

The next result further specifies the minimizing measures for
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
. Recall that
\deg(s)
denotes the degree of a point
s\in G
, with
\deg(s):=2
for all
s\in G\setminus V
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
be a measure with
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})
and write
\mu
as
\mu=\mu_{x,y}=(\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})/2
for two points
x,y\in G
. Then
\deg(x),\deg(y)\in\{1,2\}
, i.e. the two points lie at the end of a pending edge or in the interior of an edge.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
, the minimizing measure
\mu
is of the form
\mu=\mu_{x,y}
for some points
x,y\in G
. Upon refining the model, we may assume that
x
and
y
are vertices.

Assume that
\deg(x)>2
and let
f=f_{x,y}
. First contract every edge, where
f
is constant. The new graph
G^{\prime}
has a bigger first optimal eigenvalue by Lemma
6.3
. But we have
q(f)=q(f|_{G^{\prime}})
, so the first optimal eigenvalue in fact coincides by Corollary
5.5
. This also shows that
\mu_{x,y}
is a minimizing measure for
G^{\prime}
. Thus, we may assume that
G=G^{\prime}
, that is,
f
is non-constant on every edge.

Since the function
f
is linear and non-constant on edges, all its level sets
\{z\in G\;|\;f(z)=c\}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
, are finite. Denote the values, which are taken by
f
in vertices, by
a_{1}=1=f(x)>a_{2}>\dots>a_{n}=-1=f(y)
. We assume that for each
i
, the level set
V_{i}:=\{z\in G|f(z)=a_{i}\}
consists only of vertices. This can always be achieved by refining the model. In particular,
V=\bigsqcup_{i}V_{i}
.

We construct a new graph
\widetilde{G}
by glueing together all vertices
v\in V_{i}
for each set
V_{i}
. Then
\widetilde{G}
is a pumpkin chain with vertices
\widetilde{w}_{1},\dots\widetilde{w}_{n}
corresponding to the values
a_{1},\dots a_{n}
. The points
x
and
y
can be identified with the endpoints
\widetilde{w}_{1}
and
\widetilde{w}_{n}
of
\widetilde{G}
, so that the pumpkin chain
\widetilde{G}
starts with a
\deg(x)
-pumpkin and ends with a
\deg(y)
-pumpkin.

The glueing induces a natural surjective map
\Phi\colon G\to\widetilde{G}
. By construction, the function
f
is the pullback
f=\widetilde{f}\circ\Phi
of a function
\widetilde{f}\in H^{1}(\widetilde{G})
with
q(f)=q(\widetilde{f})
. On the other hand, for every function
g\in H^{1}(\widetilde{G})
, the pullback
g\circ\Phi\in H^{1}(G)
has the same energy, maximum and minimum. By Corollary
5.5
, we infer

q(\widetilde{f})\geq\lambda_{1}^{\min}(\widetilde{G})\geq\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=q(f)=q(\widetilde{f}).

Applying Proposition
2.9
, we can collapse every pumpkin in
\widetilde{G}
except the first one into a path of suitable length, to obtain a graph
\widehat{G}
with the following property: for the corresponding surjective mapping
\Psi\colon\widetilde{G}\to\widehat{G}
, there exists a unique function
\widehat{f}
with
\widehat{f}\circ\Psi=\widetilde{f}
and
q(\widehat{f})=q(\widetilde{f})
. The same argument as above implies that

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(\widehat{G})=q(\widehat{f}).

We proceed by calculating the energy
q(\widehat{f})
. The graph
\widehat{G}
contains a
\deg(x)
-pumpkin, having
x
as its left endpoint and a vertex
z
as its right endpoint. Either
z=y
, and
\widehat{G}
consist only of the pumpkin, or it contains an additional edge
e_{y}
attached at
z
, having
y
as its other endpoint. Let
\ell_{1}\geq\dots\geq\ell_{\deg(x)}>0
be the lengths of the edges
e_{1},\dots,e_{\deg(x)}
in the pumpkin at
x
and let
\ell_{0}
be the length of the remaining graph. If the edge
e_{y}
is absent, we have
\ell_{0}=0
. Further set

S:=\sum_{i=2}^{\deg(x)}\frac{1}{\ell_{i}}.

In terms of these quantities, the energy of
\widehat{f}
equals

q(\widehat{f})=4\,\frac{S+\frac{1}{\ell_{1}}}{\ell_{0}S+\frac{\ell_{0}}{\ell_{1}}+1}.

This follows from the fact that, by Corollary
5.5
and Proposition
2.6
, the function
\widehat{f}
is harmonic on
\widehat{G}\setminus\{x,y\}
with
f(x)=1
,
f(y)=-1
, and thus takes the values

f(x)=1,\qquad f(y)=-1,\qquad f(z)=\frac{\ell_{0}S+\frac{\ell_{0}}{\ell_{1}}-1}{\ell_{0}S+\frac{\ell_{0}}{\ell_{1}}+1},

in the vertices
x,y
and
z
of
\widehat{G}
.

Using the assumption that
\deg(x)\geq 3
, we then construct a function
\widehat{g}\in H^{1}(\widehat{G})
with smaller energy than
\widehat{f}
. Note first that

(6.1)

\ell_{1}S=\frac{\ell_{1}}{\ell_{2}}+\dots+\frac{\ell_{1}}{\ell_{\deg(x)}}\geq\deg(x)-1\geq 2.

This permits us to define a point in the interior of the edge
e_{1}=[0,\ell_{1}]
emanating at
x
as
x^{\prime}:=\frac{\ell_{1}S-1}{2S}
.
Let
\widehat{g}:=f_{x^{\prime},y}\in H^{1}(\widehat{G})
be the harmonic function on
\widehat{G}\setminus\{x^{\prime},y\}
defined in (
5.2
). We use Proposition
2.9
to calculate the energy of
\widehat{g}
. First collapse the edges
e_{2},\dots,e_{\deg x}
to a new edge of length
S^{-1}
. Then the new graph is a
[2,1]
-pumpkin chain between the vertices
x^{\prime},z
and
y
. By again collapsing the
2
-pumpkin, we get a new edge of length
\frac{\ell_{1}S+1}{4S}
. The resulting graph is a path graph of length
\frac{\ell_{1}S+1}{4S}+\ell_{0}
and thus
\widehat{g}
has energy

q(\widehat{g})=\frac{16S}{(4\ell_{0}+\ell_{1})S+1}.

Using that
\ell_{1}S\neq 1
by (
6.1
), it is straightforward to deduce that

q(\widehat{g})<q(\widehat{f})=\lambda_{1}^{\min}(\widehat{G}),

which is a contradiction by Corollary
5.5
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

The idea of reducing a general metric graph to a pumpkin chain via glueing appeared also in
[
4
]
, where it was used in context with eigenvalue estimates (see, e.g.
[
4
, Proof of Lemma 5.1]
). Proof techniques involving changes to the geometry of a metric graph are sometimes also called graph surgery methods.

We omit the proof of the following elementary fact.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph. Shortening an edge
e\in E
, that is, decreasing the length
\ell(e)
, increases the first optimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{\min}
. Moreover, contracting an edge
e\in E
, that is, collapsing it into one vertex, also increases
\lambda_{1}^{\min}
.

6.2.
Estimates via diameter

Theorem
5.9
characterizes the optimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
in terms of the resistance metric
r
. If one considers instead the path metric
\varrho
and its diameter
\operatorname{diam}(G)=\operatorname{diam}_{\varrho}(G)
, the following estimate holds.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
G
be a metric graph. Then

(6.2)

\frac{4L(G)}{\operatorname{diam}(G)^{2}}\geq\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)\geq\frac{4}{\operatorname{diam}(G)}.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
5.9
and the inequalities for the metrics
\varrho
and
r
in (
5.9
) and Lemma
5.7
.
∎

Remark 6.5
.

By the above,
\lambda_{1}(H_{\mu})\geq\frac{4}{\operatorname{diam}(G)}
for every measure
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
. Taking
\mu
with
\operatorname{supp}\mu=V
, we recover the estimate for weighted discrete Laplacians from
[
43
, Corollary 3.7]
(see also Example
3.8
).

In particular,
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
can be controlled by the diameter and length of a graph. It turns out that upper bounds in terms of only one of these quantities are impossible.

Denote by
\mathscr{G}_{L}
the class of metric graphs
G
having total length
L(G)=L
. Similarly, let
\mathscr{G}^{\prime}_{L}
be the class of metric graphs
G
with diameter
\operatorname{diam}(G)=L
.

Corollary 6.6
.

Let
L>0
. Then

\sup_{G\in\mathscr{G}_{L}}\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\sup_{G\in\mathscr{G}^{\prime}_{L}}\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=+\infty.

Proof.

Concerning
\mathscr{G}_{L}
, consider the equilateral
n
-star consisting of
n
edges of length
L/n
glued at a central vertex. The diameter in the path metric
\varrho
is
2L/n
. Thus, by Corollary
6.4
, the first optimal eigenvalue tends to
+\infty
for
n\to\infty
.

In
\mathscr{G}_{L}^{\prime}
, consider the equilateral
[n\times n]
-pumpkin chain
W_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
(that is, consisting of
n
pumpkins with
n
edges each), with constant length function
\ell\equiv\frac{L}{n}
(see Definition
2.8
). Then
\operatorname{diam}(W_{n})=L
and
L(W_{n})=Ln
. Let
x,y\in G
be points realizing the
r
-diameter
\operatorname{diam}_{r}(W_{n})=r(x,y)
. We can assume that each of the points
x
and
y
belongs either to the first or last pumpkin. Due to Proposition
2.9
one can replace each of the
(n-2)
pumpkins in the middle by a path of length
\frac{L}{n^{2}}
, without changing the resistance
r(x,y)
of
x
and
y
. The new graph
W_{n}^{\prime}
has diameter
\operatorname{diam}(W^{\prime}_{n})=\frac{2L}{n}+\frac{(n-2)L}{n^{2}}
, so

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(W_{n})\geq\lambda_{1}^{\min}(W_{n}^{\prime})\geq\frac{4}{\frac{2L}{n}+\frac{(n-2)L}{n^{2}}}\to\infty,\qquad\text{ for }n\to\infty,

by Theorem
5.9
and Corollary
6.4
.
∎

The lower bound in (
6.2
) is sharp, as equality holds for the path
P_{L}=[0,L]
. It turns out that
P_{L}
also minimizes
\lambda_{1}^{\min}
on some classes of metric graphs.

Corollary 6.7
.

The path graph
P_{L}=[0,L]
minimizes the first optimal eigenvalue in both the classes
\mathscr{G}_{L}
and
\mathscr{G}^{\prime}_{L}
, that is,

\min_{G\in\mathscr{G}_{L}}\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\min_{G\in\mathscr{G}^{\prime}_{L}}\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\lambda_{1}^{\min}(P_{L})=\frac{4}{L}.

Moreover,
P_{L}
is the unique minimizer in
\mathscr{G}_{L}
, whereas other minimizers exist in
\mathscr{G}^{\prime}_{L}
.

Proof.

Since
P_{L}
is a tree,
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(P_{L})=4/\operatorname{diam}(P_{L})=4/L
by Theorem
5.9
. Since
\operatorname{diam}(G)<L
for every other graph
G\in\mathscr{G}_{L}
, the path graph
P_{L}
is the unique minimizer in
\mathscr{G}_{L}
. On the other hand,
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(T)=4/L
holds for every metric tree of diameter
L
, so all of them minimize
\lambda_{1}^{\min}
in
\mathscr{G}_{L}^{\prime}
.
∎

6.3.
An upper estimate

Theorem
5.15
indicates that the first optimal eigenvalue is related to graph partitioning. In the following, we elaborate on this connection by proving an upper estimate for
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
in terms of a Cheeger-type constant.

Let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph. For a set of vertices
Y\subset V
, we define its
combinatorial boundary
as

\delta Y=\{e\in E\;|\;e\text{ connects a vertex in }Y\text{ and in }V\setminus Y\}

and the
volume of the combinatorial boundary
as

|\delta Y|:=\sum_{e\in\delta Y}\frac{1}{\ell(e)}.

This leads to the following upper bound for the first optimal eigenvalue.

Lemma 6.8
.

Consider a subset
Y\subset V
with
\emptyset\subsetneq Y\subsetneq V
. Then

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)\leq 4|\delta Y|.

Proof.

Consider the edgewise linear function
f\in H^{1}(G)
which satisfies
f\equiv 1
on
Y
and
f\equiv-1
on
V\setminus W
. Then
q(f)=4|\delta Y|
. Applying Corollary
5.5
, we arrive at the claimed estimate.
∎

We introduce a
Cheeger-type constant
of a metric graph
G
as

h(G):=\min\Big\{\min_{\emptyset\subsetneq Y\subsetneq V}|\delta Y|,\,\min_{e_{1}\neq e_{2}\in E}\frac{1}{\ell(e_{1})}+\frac{1}{\ell(e_{2})},\,\min_{e\in E}\frac{4}{\ell(e)}\Big\}.

Note that this expression depends on the choice of the model of
G
. To be more precise, we define
h(G)
as the above expression for one (equivalently, any) essential model of
G
(see Section
2.1
). In considerations involving
h(G)
, we will always work with an essential model of
G
. Since removing vertices of degree
2
from a model decreases the above expression, this corresponds to taking the minimal constant among all models.

Remark 6.9
.

Comparing
h(G)
to established notions of Cheeger constants for graphs, its shape might be a bit surprising. Indeed, the conceptual idea behind Cheeger constants is to partition a graph into two subsets of the same volume and with smallest possible boundary. However, our definition of
h(G)
seemingly does not take into account the volume of subsets. This is due to the shape of the minimizing measure
\mu=\mu_{x,y}=(\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})/2
, which gives the same volume
1/2
to any subset containing only one of the points
x
and
y
.

It turns out that
h(G)
has the following simpler representation.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
G
be a metric graph. Then

h(G)=\min\Big\{\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e\text{ bridge}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\ell(e)},\min_{e_{1}\neq e_{2}\in E}\frac{1}{\ell(e_{1})}+\frac{1}{\ell(e_{2})},\min_{e\in E}\frac{4}{\ell(e)}\Big\},

where a bridge of the metric graph
G
is an edge
e
such that
G\setminus e
is disconnected.

Proof.

Assume that
Y\subset V
satisfies
h(G)=|\delta Y|
and
|\delta Y|<\min_{e\neq f\in E}\frac{1}{\ell(e)}+\frac{1}{\ell(f)}
. Then
\delta Y
only consists of exactly one edge
e
. If we remove
e
from
G
, then there is no connection between vertices in
Y
and
V\setminus Y
, thus
e
is a bridge.
∎

As the main result of this section, we estimate
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)
in terms of
h(G)
.

Proposition 6.11
.

Let
G
be a metric graph. Then

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)\leq 4h(G).

Proof.

Given an edge
e\in E
, denote by
\varphi_{e}
the unique
H^{1}
-function with
\varphi_{e}\equiv 0
on
G\setminus e
, with
\varphi_{e}(x)=1
for the midpoint
x
of
e
, and which is linear on both components of
e\setminus\{x\}
. A direct calculation shows that
q(\varphi_{e})=4/\ell(e)
.
Given two edges
e\neq f
, it follows from Corollary
5.5
that

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)\leq q(\varphi_{e}-\varphi_{f})=q(\varphi_{e})+q(\varphi_{f})=\frac{4}{\ell(e)}+\frac{4}{\ell(f)}.

Breaking an edge
e\in E
into two edges at the midpoint of
e
and applying the above argument, we infer that
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)\leq 16/\ell(e)
for all edges
e
. Comparing with Lemma
6.8
, we conclude.
∎

The estimate in Proposition
6.11
is sharp, as equality holds for the path graph
P=[0,1]
. Additional examples for the sharpness are provided in the next section.

6.4.
Examples

In this section, we calculate the first optimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{\min}
for several graphs. For metric trees,
\lambda_{1}^{\min}
was described in Theorem
5.9
. Motivated by this, in the following we focus on graphs containing many cycles.
Our key tool is the equality
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G)=\min_{x\neq y\in G}q(f_{x,y})
from (
5.3
).

Example 6.12

(Complete graph)
.

Let
K_{n}
,
n\geq 4
, be the equilateral complete graph with
n
vertices and constant edge lengths
\ell(e):=\ell:=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}
. In particular,
K_{n}
has total length
L(K_{n})=1
. By (
5.3
), we can calculate
\lambda_{1}^{\min}(K_{n})
by minimizing the energy of the function
f_{x,y}
over all
x,y\in G
. By Theorem
6.1
, it suffices to consider points
x
and
y
which lie in the interior of edges.

Assume that
x\in e_{1}
and
y\in e_{2}
for two disjoint edges
e_{1}=uv
and
e_{2}=wz
(i.e.,
\{u,v\}\cap\{w,z\}=\varnothing
). Identify
x
with the point
0<a<\ell
in the interval
e_{1}=[0,\ell]
and
y
with the point
0<b<\ell
in the interval
e_{1}=[0,\ell]
. By symmetry, the function
f=f_{x,y}
takes the same value on all vertices
\hat{v}\in V\setminus\{u,v,w,z\}
. Fixing a vertex
\hat{v}\in V\setminus\{u,v,w,z\}
, the harmonicity conditions in the vertices
u,v,w,z
and
\hat{v}
can be written as

\begin{cases}\ell f(x)+a\big(f(w)+f(z)+(n-4)f(\hat{v})\big)=(\ell+(n-2)a)f(u)\\[5.69054pt]
\ell f(x)+(\ell-a)\big(f(w)+f(z)+(n-4)f(\hat{v})\big)=(\ell+(n-2)(\ell-a))f(v)\\[5.69054pt]
\ell f(y)+b\big(f(u)+f(v)+(n-4)f(\hat{v})\big)=(\ell+(n-2)b)f(w)\\[5.69054pt]
\ell f(y)+(\ell-b)\big(f(u)+f(v)+(n-4)f(\hat{v})\big)=(\ell+(n-2)(\ell-b))f(z)\\[5.69054pt]
f(u)+f(v)+f(w)+f(z)=4f(\hat{v}).\end{cases}

These equations allow to determine the function
f
. Minimizing the corresponding energy and applying (
5.3
), we calculate

\min_{x\in e_{1},\,y\in e_{2}}q(f_{x,y})=\frac{4n^{2}\left(n-1\right)}{n+2},

where the minimum is achieved for
x
and
y
being the edge midpoints. In a similar way, one can show that, if one places points
x
and
y
on the same edge or on two adjacent edges, then the corresponding energy is bigger. Thus,

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(K_{n})=\frac{4n^{2}\left(n-1\right)}{n+2}

and the minimizing measures are the measures
\mu_{x,y}
where
x
and
y
are the midpoints of disjoint edges. Note that in this case also
\varrho(x,y)=\frac{4}{(n-1)n}=\operatorname{diam}_{\varrho}K_{n}
.

The Cheeger-type constant of
K_{n}
is given by

h(K_{n})=n(n-1).

The estimate in Proposition
6.11
is asymptotically sharp for
K_{n}
as
n\to\infty
.

Example 6.13

(Pumpkin graph)
.

Let
G_{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be the equilateral
n
-pumpkin, that is, the metric graph consisting of two vertices
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, joined by
n
edges of the same length
\ell:=1/n
. Proceeding as in Example
6.12
, consider two points
x,y\in G\setminus V
. We distinguish two cases:

Assume first that
x
and
y
belong to the same edge
e
. Set
a:=\varrho(x_{0},x)
and
b:=\varrho(x_{1},y)
, so that
\varrho(x,y)=1/n-a-b
.
As is readily verified, the harmonic function
f=f_{x,y}
takes the values

f(x_{0})=\frac{n(n-1)(b-a)+1}{n(n-1)(a+b)+1},\qquad f(x_{1})=\frac{n(n-1)(b-a)-1}{n(n-1)(a+b)+1},

in the vertices
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
. Its energy is given by

q(f)=\frac{4n^{2}}{\big(n(n-1)(a+b)+1\big)\big(1-n(a+b)\big)},

which becomes minimal precisely when
a+b=\frac{n-2}{2n(n-1)}
. In this case, it equals

q(f)=16(n-1).

Now assume that
x
and
y
belong to different edges
e_{x}\neq e_{y}
, respectively. Set
a:=\varrho(x_{0},x)
and
b:=\varrho(x_{0},y)
. The harmonic function
f=f_{x,y}
is then given by the function values

f(x_{0})=\frac{(a-b)(1-a(n-1)-b(n-1))}{a^{2}(n-1)+b^{2}(n-1)+2ab-a-b}

and

f(x_{1})=\frac{(a-b)(-2-n^{2}(a+b)+n(a+b+1))}{n(a^{2}(n-1)+b^{2}(n-1)+2ab-a-b)}.

Its energy equals

q(f)=\frac{4}{a^{2}(1-n)+a+b^{2}(1-n)+b-2ab},

which becomes minimal for
a=b=1/(2n)
. In this case, we obtain
q(f)=8n
.

We conclude that

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(G_{n})=8n

and that the minimizing measures are the measure
\mu_{x,y}
, where
x
and
y
are midpoints of two distinct edges. Note that in this case
\operatorname{diam}_{\varrho}(G)=\varrho(x,y)
. Moreover, the estimate from Proposition
6.11
in terms of
h(G_{n})=2n
is sharp.

Example 6.14

(Butterfly graph, a.k.a.
{[}n,n{]}
-pumpkin chain)
.

For
n\geq 2
, consider the butterfly graph
B_{n}
, that is the graph consisting of three vertices
x_{0},x_{1}
and
c
, and having
n
edges each from
c
to
x_{0}
and from
c
to
x_{1}
, all of the same length
\ell=\frac{1}{2n}
. Using similar arguments as in the previous two examples, one can calculate that

\lambda_{1}^{\min}(B_{n})=16(n-1).

The minimizing measures are precisely the measures
\mu_{x,y}
where
x
lies on an edge at
x_{0}
, and
y
on an edge at
x_{1}
, with positions
\varrho(x_{0},x)=\varrho(x_{1},y)=\frac{n-2}{4n(n-1)}
. Note that
\varrho(x,y)=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}<\operatorname{diam}(B_{n})=\frac{1}{n}
for
n\geq 3
.

The optimal Cheeger constant is given by
h(B_{n})=4n
, so that Proposition
6.11
is asymptotically sharp for
n\to\infty
.

7.
Weyl’s law for optimal eigenvalues

In this section, we calculate the optimal eigenvalues of the path graph
P=[0,1]
and derive a Weyl’s law for optimal eigenvalues on general metric graphs.

7.1.
Optimal eigenvalues of the path graph

In the following, we view the interval
[0,1]
as a metric graph
P
consisting of two vertices joined by an edge of length one, and call
P
the path graph (of length one).

Theorem 7.1
.

The optimal eigenvalues of the path graph
P=[0,1]
are

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(P)=4k^{2},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Moreover, the unique measure
\mu_{k}\in\mathcal{N}
with
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(P)=\lambda_{k}\big(H_{\mu_{k}}\big)
is

(7.1)

\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{2k}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2k}\delta_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{k}\delta_{\frac{j}{k}}\in\mathcal{N}.

In the proof of Theorem
7.1
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

Consider the path graph
P_{L}=[0,L]
of length
L>0
. For the Dirichlet sets
A=\{L\}
and
A^{\prime}=\{0,L\}
, the smallest optimal eigenvalues are given by

\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}\big(P_{L},A\big)=\frac{1}{L},\qquad\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}\big(P_{L},A^{\prime}\big)=\frac{4}{L}.

The unique minimizing measures are given by
\mu=\delta_{0}
and
\mu^{\prime}=\delta_{L/2}
, respectively.

Proof.

To prove the first claim, note that every minimizing measure for
\lambda_{0}^{\min,D}
is a Dirac measure due to Lemma
5.13
. Since
\lambda_{0}(H_{\delta_{x}}^{D})=1/(L-x)
for every
x\in[0,L)
, the claim follows. Similarly, to prove the second claim, we need to minimize
\lambda_{0}(H^{D}_{\delta_{x}})
over all measures
\delta_{x}
for
x\in(0,L)
. A simple calculation shows that
\lambda_{0}(H^{D}_{\delta_{x}})=\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{L-x}
, which becomes minimal precisely for
x=\frac{L}{2}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

The statement is trivial for
k=0
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the vertex set
V=\{\frac{j}{k}|\,j=0,\dots,k\}
and join the vertices
\frac{j}{k}
and
\frac{j+1}{k}
by an edge of length
\frac{1}{k}
for all
j
, which gives a model of
P
. By Example
3.8
, the operator
H_{\mu_{k}}
for the measure
\mu_{k}
from (
7.1
) is a discrete Laplacian. In fact,
H_{\mu_{k}}=2k^{2}H_{\rm{norm}}
, where
H_{\rm{norm}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{V}
is the normalized Laplacian acting on functions
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
as

(H_{\rm{norm}}f)(v)=\frac{1}{\deg(v)}\sum_{w\sim v}(f(v)-f(w)),\qquad v\in V.

It is well-known that
\lambda_{k}(H_{\rm{norm}})=2
(e.g.,
[
10
, Example 1.4]
) and we conclude that

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(P)\leq\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu_{k}})=4k^{2}.

It remains to prove that
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(H_{\mu})\geq 4k^{2}
. Fix a minimizing measure
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
. By the Sturm oscillation theorem
A.1
, the
\mu
-normalized eigenfunction
f
for
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
has precisely
k
zeros
0<x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}<1
. Set
x_{0}:=0
,
x_{k+1}:=1
and consider the intervals
I_{i}:=(x_{i},x_{i+1})
,
i=1,\dots,k
. Then

\frac{\int_{I_{i}}|f^{\prime}|^{2}dx}{\int_{I_{i}}|f|^{2}d\mu}=\frac{\int_{P}f^{\prime}\cdot(f|_{I_{i}})^{\prime}dx}{\int_{I_{i}}|f|^{2}d\mu}=\frac{\int_{P}H_{\mu}f\cdot f|_{I_{i}}d\mu}{\int_{I_{i}}|f|^{2}d\mu}=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})\frac{\int_{I_{i}}|f|^{2}d\mu}{\int_{I_{i}}|f|^{2}d\mu}=\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu}),

where
f|_{I_{i}}
is extended by zero to
P
. Lemma
7.2
implies that

4k^{2}\geq\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})=\frac{\int_{I_{i}}|f^{\prime}|^{2}dx}{\int_{I_{i}}|f|^{2}d\mu}\geq\max_{i}\left(\frac{1}{\mu(I_{0})|I_{0}|},\frac{1}{\mu(I_{k})|I_{k}|},\frac{4}{\mu(I_{i})|I_{i}|}\right),

where
I_{i}:=(x_{i+1}-x_{i})
. Define
s_{i}=\mu(I_{i})+|I_{i}|
,
i=0,\dots,k
. Then

s_{i}^{2}\geq 4\mu(I_{i})|I_{i}|.

Moreover, one of the inequalities

s_{0}>\frac{1}{k},s_{k}>\frac{1}{k}\text{ and }s_{i}>\frac{2}{k}\quad\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,k-1,

must fail. Indeed, otherwise we have the contradiction

2\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{k}>\frac{1}{k}+\frac{1}{k}+(k-1)\frac{2}{k}=2.

If
s_{0}\leq\frac{1}{k}
or
s_{k}\leq\frac{1}{k}
, then

\mu(I_{i})|I_{i}|\leq\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{4}\leq\frac{1}{4k^{2}},\quad\text{ for }i=0\text{ or }i=k.

If
s_{i}\leq\frac{2}{k}
for some
i=1,\dots,k-1
, then

\mu(I_{i})|I_{i}|\leq\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{4}\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

In both cases, we obtain that

4k^{2}\geq\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})\geq\max_{i}\left(\frac{1}{\mu(I_{0})|I_{0}|},\frac{1}{\mu(I_{k})|I_{k}|},\frac{4}{\mu(I_{i})|I_{i}|}\right)\geq 4k^{2}

and thus
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(P)=4k^{2}
. Moreover, by the above estimates, this implies that

s_{0}=\frac{1}{k},\;s_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\text{ and }s_{i}=\frac{2}{k}\quad\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,k-1,

and

\mu(I_{i})=|I_{i}|=s_{i}/2,\qquad\text{ for all }i.

By Lemma
7.2
, the unique minimizing measure on each interval
I_{i}
is a Dirac measure. Combining the above, we get that
\mu
coincides with
\mu_{k}
defined in (
7.1
).
∎

7.2.
Weyl’s law

In the following, we derive a Weyl’s law for the optimal eigenvalues of a metric graph. The result is based on the following lemma.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
G=(V,E,\ell)
be a metric graph with optimal eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)\geq\frac{4(k-(\#E-1))^{2}}{L(G)}

for all
k\geq\#E-1
and

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)\leq\frac{4\big(k+2\#E-\#V\big)^{2}}{L(G)}

for all
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

Our strategy is to compare
G
to a path graph by first “cutting”
G
into intervals and then ”glueing” them again to a path graph
P_{L}=[0,L]=[0,L(G)]
.

More precisely, we cut the graph
G
into intervals
[0,\ell(e)]
,
e\in E
, and consider their disjoint union
\widetilde{G}:=\bigcup_{e\in E}[0,\ell(e)]
. Let
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
be a measure on
G
of the form
\mu=mdx
for some function
m>0
. Restricting
\mu
to each interval edge
e\in E
, we obtain a measure
\widetilde{\mu}
on
\widetilde{G}
.

We compare the associated Laplacians
H_{\mu}
and
H_{\tilde{\mu}}
. By the properties of
\mu
, one can naturally identify
L^{2}(G,\mu)\cong L^{2}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{\mu})
. Moreover, the operators have form domains
H^{1}(G,\mu)=H^{1}(G)
and
H^{1}(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{\mu})=H^{1}(\widetilde{G})=\bigoplus_{e\in E}H^{1}([0,\ell(e)])
, respectively. We can actually view
H^{1}(G)
as a subspace of
H^{1}(\widetilde{G})
, consisting of those functions
f\in H^{1}(\widetilde{G})
with
f(a)=f(b)
, whenever
a
and
b
are endpoints of intervals which corresponded to the same vertex in
G
. Since every vertex
v\in V
gives rise to
\deg(v)-1
such conditions, the co-dimension of
H^{1}(G)
in
H^{1}(\widetilde{G})
is precisely
\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)-1=2\#E-\#V
. The quadratic forms of
H_{\mu}
and
H_{\tilde{\mu}}
coincide on
H^{1}(G)\subset H^{1}(\widetilde{G})
. Thus, the standard interlacing principle yields that

\lambda_{k}(H_{\widetilde{\mu}})\leq\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})\leq\lambda_{k+2\#E-\#V}(H_{\widetilde{\mu}}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Minimizing over all measures
\mu
and applying a density argument, we obtain

(7.2)

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(\widetilde{G})\leq\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)\leq\lambda_{k+2\#E-\#V}^{\min}(\widetilde{G}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

On the other hand, we can glue the intervals in
\widetilde{G}
to a path graph
P_{L}=[0,L]
with
\#E
edges. Applied to
P_{L}
, the inequalities (
7.2
) read as

(7.3)

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(\widetilde{G})\leq\lambda_{k}^{\min}(P_{L})\leq\lambda_{k+\#E-1}^{\min}(\widetilde{G}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

The claimed estimates then follow from Theorem
7.1
(see also Remark
4.4
).
∎

Theorem 7.4

(Weyl’s law)
.

Let
G
be a metric graph and let
N^{\min}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be the counting function for optimal eigenvalues, defined by

N^{\min}(\lambda):=\#\big\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}|\,\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)\leq\lambda\big\}.

Then

(7.4)

\frac{N^{\min}(\lambda)}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\sim\frac{\sqrt{L(G)}}{2},\qquad\lambda\to\infty,

and

(7.5)

\lambda_{k}^{\min}(G)\sim\frac{4k^{2}}{L(G)},\qquad k\to\infty.

Proof.

These asymptotics follow immediately from Lemma
7.3
.
∎

Appendix A
A Sturm oscillation theorem

We prove the following Sturm oscillation theorem on the path graph
P=[0,1]
.

Theorem A.1

(Sturm oscillation theorem for general measures)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
be a measure on the path graph
P=[0,1]
and let
f
be an eigenfunction of
H_{\mu}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
. Then
f
has precisely
k
zeros and all of them lie in
(0,1)
.

Since
P
coincides with the interval
P=[0,1]
, the operator
H_{\mu}
is a Sturm–Liouville operator with measure-valued coefficients. The study of such operators has a long tradition
[
22
,
33
]
. In particular, different versions of Sturm oscillation and comparison theorems have been obtained in this setting, see e.g.
[
24
,
25
,
44
,
49
]
. On the other hand, we were not able to find the above variant, which is needed in the proof of Theorem
7.1
, in the literature.

Consider a bounded interval
(a,b)
and a locally finite Borel measure
\varrho
on
(a,b)
. Let
\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}((a,b),\varrho)
be the space of functions
f
such that there exists a function
h\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}((a,b),\varrho)
with

f(y)=f(x)+\int_{[x,y)}h\,d\varrho

for all
x<y
. Every function
f\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}((a,b),\varrho)
is left-continuous and we set

\frac{d}{d\varrho}f:=h.

We are interested in the differential expression

\tau=-\frac{d}{d\varrho}\frac{d}{dx}f,

which is well-defined for
f
belonging to the domain

\mathcal{D}_{\tau}=\big\{f\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}((a,b),dx)|\,f^{\prime}\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}((a,b),\varrho)\big\}.

More precisely, the condition
f^{\prime}\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}((a,b),\varrho)
means that
f^{\prime}
has a representative in
\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}((a,b),\varrho)
, which is moreover unique by left-continuity. When referring to
f^{\prime}
for some
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\tau}
, we mean this particular representative.

We need the following result on uniqueness of solutions (e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Lemma A.2
.

Fix a point
c\in(a,b)
and let
\alpha,\beta,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists a unique solution
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\tau}
of the equation
\tau f=\lambda f
satisfying

f(c)=\alpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad f^{\prime}(c)=\beta.

Consider the path graph
P=[0,1]
and let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. The corresponding Laplacian
H_{\mu}\colon\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})\to L^{2}(G,\mu)
relates to the differential expression
\tau
for
\varrho:=\mu|_{(0,1)}
. More precisely, we have
\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})\subset\mathcal{D}_{\tau}
and

\tau f=-\frac{d}{d\mu}\frac{d}{dx}f=H_{\mu}f\quad\text{in }L^{1}((0,1),\mu)

for every
f\in\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})
. To see this, let
c<x\in(0,1)
be two points in which
f
is differentiable. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, take
\phi_{n}\in H^{1}(G)
as the test function with
\varphi_{n}\equiv 1
on
[c,x-1/n]
, with
\varphi_{n}\equiv 0
on
[0,c-1/n]\cup[x,1]
and which is linear everywhere else on
P=[0,1]
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{[c,x)}(H_{\mu}f)d\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}(H_{\mu}f)\phi_{n}d\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}q(f,\phi_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}n\int_{c-1/n}^{c}f^{\prime}(s)ds-n\int_{x-1/n}^{x}f^{\prime}(s)ds=f^{\prime}(c)-f^{\prime}(x).

A similar argument shows that the right and left derivatives in the endpoints of
P=[0,1]
exist for all
f\in\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})
and equal

(A.1)

f_{+}^{\prime}(0)=-\mu(\{0\})(H_{\mu}f)(0),\qquad f_{-}^{\prime}(1)=\mu(\{1\})(H_{\mu}f)(1).

Here, the respective right hand side is zero, whenever
\mu(\{0\})
or
\mu(\{1\})=0
.

Remark A.3
.

The above generalizes to an arbitrary metric graph
G
and measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. Namely, for
f\in\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})
, on every interval edge
(0,\ell(e))
,
e\in E
, we have

H_{\mu}f=-\frac{d}{d\mu}\frac{d}{dx}f\qquad\text{in $L^{2}((0,\ell(e)),\mu)$},

where the restriction
f|_{(0,\ell(e))}
belongs to the respective domain
\mathcal{D}_{\tau_{e}}
. Moreover,

\sum_{e\sim v}f_{e}^{\prime}(v)=-\mu(\{v\})(H_{\mu}f)(v),\qquad\text{for all $v\in V$},

where all edges
e\sim v
are oriented pointing away from
v
.

From this perspective, the Laplacian
H_{\mu}
may be viewed as a graph variant of a Krein string operator. Note that such operators have already been studied before on star graphs in
[
18
,
46
]
. Under additional assumptions on the measure
\mu
, our operators also coincide with those for general graphs in
[
34
]
.

Proposition A.4
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. Then all eigenvalues of
H_{\mu}
are simple.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that every solution
f\in\operatorname{dom}(H_{\mu})
of
H_{\mu}f=\lambda f
with
f(0)=0
vanishes everywhere on
P=[0,1]
. Taking into account (
A.1
), such a function
f
necessarily satisfies
f_{+}^{\prime}(0)=0
. Since the equation
H_{\mu}f=\lambda f
is linear of second order, the initial conditions
f(0)=f_{+}^{\prime}(0)=0
should already imply that
f\equiv 0
.
More formally, this can be deduced from Lemma
A.2
. Extend
\mu
and
f
by zero to a measure
\nu
and function
g\in\operatorname{dom}(H_{\nu})
on
J=(-1,1)
. Then
-\frac{d}{d\nu}\frac{d}{dx}g=\lambda g
on
J
and
g(0)=g^{\prime}(0)=0
, so it follows that
g\equiv 0
and
f\equiv 0
.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
and let
f\in H^{1}(P,\mu)
be an eigenfunction for the
k
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
. Then
f
has at most
k
zeros and all its zeros lie in
(0,1)
.

Proof.

We already know that
f(0),f(1)\neq 0
by the proof of Proposition
A.4
. Suppose that
f
has at least
l>k
zeros
0<x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{l}<1
. Set also
x_{0}:=0
and
x_{l+1}:=1
. For
i=0,\dots,l
, let
f_{i}\in H^{1}(G)
be the function obtained by restricting
f
to
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
and extending to
[0,1]
by zero. These
l+1
functions are all non-trivial, by Lemma
A.2
, and
\mu
-orthogonal by construction. The same holds for their projections
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}(f_{i})
,
i=0,\dots,l
. By the Min-Max-principle and Proposition
2.6
,

\lambda_{l}\leq\max_{f\in\operatorname{span}\{f_{0},\dots,f_{l}\}}\frac{q(\mathcal{P}_{\mu}(f)))}{\|\mathcal{P}_{\mu}(f)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}}\leq\max_{i}\frac{q(f_{i})}{\|f_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}}=\max_{i}\frac{\lambda_{k}\|f_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}}{\|f_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G,\mu)}}=\lambda_{k}.

We obtain that
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{l}
, in contradiction to Proposition
A.4
. ∎

If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
has a positive
L^{1}
-density, i.e.
\mu=rdx
for
r\in L^{1}(0,1)
with
r>0
, then

\tau=-\frac{1}{r}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}.

Moreover, the Laplacian
H_{\mu}
coincides with the standard self-adjoint Sturm–Liouville operator in
L^{2}((0,1),\mu)
associated with the Neumann boundary conditions

f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime}(1)=0.

We have the following classical version of the Sturm oscillation and comparison theorem (e.g.,
[
3
, Theorem 6.1.1 and Theorem 6.1.2]
).

Proposition A.6
.

Consider the Laplacian
H_{\mu}
for
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
with positive
L^{1}
-density.

(i)

Let
f
be an eigenfunction for the
k
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
. Then
f
has precisely
k
zeros
x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{k}
and all of them lie in
(0,1)
.

(ii)

Additionally, let
g
be an eigenfunction for an eigenvalue
\lambda_{l}(H_{\mu})>\lambda_{k}(H_{\mu})
. Then
g
has at least one zero in every interval
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
for
i=0,\dots,k
, where
x_{0}:=0
and
x_{k+1}:=1
by convention.

Using the above ingredients, we can prove Theorem
A.1
.

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

We proceed by induction over
k
. The case
k=0
is clear. Fix eigenfunctions
f_{0},\dots,f_{k}
for the first
k+1
eigenvalues
\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{k}
of
H_{\mu}
and suppose that the claim holds for
f_{0},\dots,f_{k-1}
. Choose a sequence of measures
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with positive
L^{1}
-densities converging to
\mu
. Since all eigenvalues are simple by Proposition
A.4
, applying Theorem
4.1
, we find eigenfunctions
f_{n,0},\dots,f_{n,k}
of
H_{\mu_{n}}
which converge uniformly to
f_{0},\dots,f_{k}
as
n\to\infty
. By Proposition
A.6
, each eigenfunction
f_{n,l}
has precisely
l
zeros
x_{1}(f_{n,l})<x_{2}(f_{n,l})<\dots<x_{l}(f_{n,l})
. We may suppose that each zero
x_{j}(f_{n,l})
converges to a point
x_{j}(f_{l}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{j}(f_{n,l})
. Since
f_{n,l}\to f_{l}
uniformly, each limit
x_{k}(f_{l})
is a zero of
f_{l}
and
x_{1}(f_{l})\leq x_{2}(f_{l})\leq\dots\leq x_{l}(f_{l})
. Moreover,
x_{j}(f_{l})\neq 0,1
by Lemma
A.5
.

It suffices to prove that the limiting zeros
x_{j}(f_{k})
for
f_{k}
are all different, that is,
x_{j}(f_{k})<x_{j+1}(f_{k})
for all
j=1,\dots,k-1
. The result then follows by Lemma
A.5
.

We first show that three consecutive zeros cannot collapse, that is, we cannot have
x_{j}(f_{k})=x_{j+1}(f_{k})=x_{j+2}(f_{k})
. In such a case, using the interlacing property in Proposition
A.6
for
f_{n,k}
and
f_{n,k-1}
, we would have two coinciding limiting zeros
x_{i}(f_{k-1})=x_{i+1}(f_{k-1})
of
f_{k-1}
. Applying induction, this is impossible.

It remains to outrule that
x_{j-1}(f_{k})<x_{j}(f_{k})=x_{j+1}(f_{k})<x_{j+2}(f_{k})
for some
j=1,\dots,k
(with convention
x_{0}(f_{k}):=0
and
x_{k+1}(f_{k}):=1
). Since in this case the nodal domain
(x_{j}(f_{n,k}),x_{j+1}(f_{n,k}))
of
f_{n,k}
has collapsed onto
x_{j}(f_{k})=x_{j+1}(f_{k})
, the eigenfunction
f_{k}
does not change sign on
I_{j}:=[x_{j-1}(f_{k}),x_{j+2}(f_{k})]
. Supposing that
f_{k}\geq 0
on
I_{j}
, the differential equation
H_{\mu}f_{k}=\lambda_{k}f_{k}
implies that
f_{k}^{\prime}
is monotonically decreasing and thus
f_{k}
is concave on
I_{j}
. Using that
f_{k}(x_{j})=0
, we obtain that then
f_{k}\equiv 0
on
I_{j}
, which implies that
f_{k}\equiv 0
on
[0,1]
by Lemma
A.2
. This contradiction finishes the proof.
∎
