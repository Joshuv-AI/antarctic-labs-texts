Practical guide to implementing pre-emptive pharmacogenetic screening in routine pediatric oncology care
Authors: Emma C Bernsen, Lidwien M Hanff, Theodorus W Kouwenberg, Melanie M Hagleitner, Bastiaan B J Tops, Nienke Biesot, Jayne Y Hehir-Kwa, Alwin D R Huitema, Meta H M Diekstra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInterindividual variability in drug response is a challenge in pediatric oncology, where the risk of treatment-related toxicity is exacerbated by narrow therapeutic windows and combination therapies. Pharmacogenetics (PGx) aims to reduce this variability by linking genetic variants to differences in drug pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and toxicity. Although evidence-based pharmacogenetic guidelines are available covering over 100 gene-drug pairs, systematic use of pre-emptive PGx screening is not yet embedded in routine pediatric oncology care. In a recent study, we demonstrated the relevance of pre-emptive PGx screening in pediatric oncology by showing that 16% of 1,151 patients were eligible for genotype-based drug dose or treatment modifications. Building on these findings, we implemented pre-emptive PGx screening into clinical practice of pediatric oncology in the Netherlands. Successful implementation required addressing challenges, including adaptation of existing infrastructure, interpretation of pharmacogenetic results for a pediatric population, integration into electronic health records, and education of clinical staff. We repurposed diagnostic germline whole genome sequencing (WGS) for individual genetic profiling and applied existing pharmacogenetic guidelines to guide drug dosing and treatment decisions. Here, we describe our implementation strategy for pre-emptive PGx screening in pediatric oncology and share insights to support other centers aiming to integrate PGx screening into routine care.KEYWORDS: pediatric oncology, implementation, pharmacogenetics, pharmacogenomics, pre-emptive pharmacogenetic screening

Introduction

Children with cancer constitute a vulnerable population frequently exposed to polypharmacy and high-risk drugs. In this population, interindividual variability in drug response poses a challenge, where narrow therapeutic windows and combination therapies increase the risk of treatment-related toxicity [1]. Pharmacogenetics (PGx) aims to reduce such variability by linking genetic variants to differences in drug pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and toxicity [2]. Over the past two decades, expanding evidence has led to the development of PGx guidelines that provide dosing and treatment recommendations for over 100 gene-drug pairs [3,4]. These standardized recommendations have been established through several international associations, including the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC), the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group (DPWG), the Canadian Pharmacogenomics Network for Drug Safety (CPNDS), and the French National Network of Pharmacogenetics (RNPGx) [3–10]. Despite the availability of these guidelines, PGx testing in most pediatric oncology centers remains limited to thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT), nudix hydrolase 15 (NUDT15), and dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPYD). Beyond these gene-drug pairs, PGx testing is predominantly applied reactively, following an unexpected adverse drug event or treatment failure in a patient. This is concerning given that approximately 16% of pediatric oncology patients may directly benefit from pre-emptive dose adjustments or alternative therapies based on existing PGx guidelines [11]. Moreover, pre-emptive PGx screening has been associated with a reduction in clinically relevant adverse drug reactions of 30% and decreased drug-related healthcare costs [2,12,13].

At our center, the routine use of paired tumor/normal whole-genome sequencing (WGS) for diagnostic purposes presented a unique opportunity to leverage existing genomic data for broader pre-emptive PGx screening [14,15]. However, transitioning from reactive to pre-emptive PGx screening requires more than the availability of guidelines and genetic data [16–20]. Implementation in pediatric oncology introduces specific challenges related to the interpretation of adult-derived PGx recommendations to the pediatric setting, integration of PGx analysis and variant calling tools into existing bioinformatics infrastructure, and education of multidisciplinary healthcare teams [19]. Therefore, in this paper, we describe our approach to implementing pre-emptive PGx screening into routine pediatric oncology practice in the Netherlands. We outline the key steps of the implementation process, the considerations that shaped our workflow design, and the solutions applied to integrate PGx screening into clinical practice. By sharing our experience, we aim to provide a practical framework for clinicians, pharmacists, and healthcare institutions seeking to implement pre-emptive PGx screening into their clinical practices (Figure S1 and Table S1).

Implementation strategy: four phases

Baseline practice in the Princess Máxima Center for Pediatric Oncology

At baseline, pre-emptive PGx testing at the Princess Máxima Center consisted of routine TPMT and NUDT15 genotyping, performed in-house to guide thiopurine dosing, and DPYD genotyping, performed externally to guide fluoropyrimidine dosing. To expand beyond this limited and partly externally performed pre-emptive PGx approach toward broader panel-based screening covering additional gene-drug pairs, a multidisciplinary pharmacogenetics working group was established. After defining baseline PGx practice, including the reusability of diagnostic germline WGS data for pre-emptive PGx screening, the implementation was structured into four phases (Figure 1): (1) selection of PGx guidelines and gene panel, (2) identification of implementation barriers, (3) design and implementation of a WGS-based PGx workflow, and (4) embedding PGx screening into routine care through education and communication. Each phase is described in detail below.

Phase 1. Selection of pharmacogenetic guidelines and gene panel

The first step in the implementation process was to identify which PGx guidelines and gene-drug recommendations could form the basis of pre-emptive PGx screening [16,18]. Among the available resources, we selected CPIC guidelines and DPWG recommendations, as these largely align and the DPWG recommendations are integrated into our clinical decision support (CDS) system for medication monitoring [21,22]. To determine which genes to be included in pre-emptive PGx screening, a multidisciplinary team of pharmacists and pediatric oncologists systematically reviewed available evidence-based CPIC guidelines and DPWG gene-drug recommendations that are of relevance to pediatric oncology [23,24]. In total, 28 CPIC guidelines and 103 DPWG gene-drug recommendations were evaluated [21,25–38]. As previously described in detail [11], gene-drug pairs were excluded if the associated drugs were not used in pediatric oncology (e.g., Cytochrome P450 family 2 subfamily C member 9 (CYP2C9) and statins) or not prescribed in the Netherlands (e.g., warfarin). This resulted in the exclusion of 13 CPIC guidelines and 69 DPWG recommendations. Ultimately, 15 CPIC guidelines and 34 DPWG gene-drug recommendations were selected and the potential pre-emptive PGx panel comprised of 13 genes (Table 1).

Clinical-related barriers

A key clinical barrier is the limited applicability of existing PGx guidelines to pediatric oncology. Most CPIC and DPWG recommendations are derived from adult studies, creating uncertainty regarding their clinical use in pediatric patients due to ontogeny. This challenge is compounded by inconsistencies between PGx information in drug labels, international guidelines, and national recommendations, which reduces clinical confidence and usability. In addition, interpretation of PGx results may be complicated by drug-drug-gene interactions and phenoconversion [42,43]. Concomitant medications may alter enzyme activity and thereby modify the predicted genotype-based phenotype, while disease-related factors such as inflammation or organ dysfunction may also affect enzyme activity independent of genotype. Incorporating these dynamic clinical factors into PGx interpretation remains challenging, particularly in pediatric oncology patients who often receive complex multidrug regimens.

Additional clinical barriers included limited PGx expertise among clinicians and pharmacists, challenges in interpreting and communicating PGx results to patients and caregivers, and concerns related to data sharing and patient privacy, all of which have been reported to impede adoption of PGx in routine care [40,41,44,45].

Infrastructure-related barriers

From an infrastructure perspective, several barriers were identified. First, standardized bioinformatics workflows were required to reliably extract PGx variants from diagnostic WGS data and translate these into clinically interpretable phenotypes. Second, interoperability between laboratory software generating PGx reports and hospital information systems was limited. Automated integration of PGx results into the electronic health record (EHR) and linkage to clinical decision support (CDS) systems were lacking, hindering timely and effective use of PGx information in clinical practice.

Practical-related barriers

The use of diagnostic WGS introduced additional practical barriers relevant to implementation. As WGS is embedded within a diagnostic setting, turnaround times may limit the availability of PGx results for drugs that are initiated immediately after suspicion of malignancy, such as rasburicase or allopurinol.

Phase 3. Designing and implementing the pre-emptive pharmacogenetic screening workflow

Building on the barriers identified in Phase 2, we designed and implemented a pre-emptive PGx screening workflow tailored to the pediatric oncology setting. Implementation decisions focused on leveraging existing diagnostic infrastructure, ensuring reliable genotype-to-phenotype translation and integrating clinical potentially actionable recommendations into routine care. The final implementation workflow is summarized in Figure 2 and detailed below.

Final workflow for pre-emptive pharmacogenetic screening in routine pediatric oncology care. The figure depicts the end-to-end workflow for pre-emptive pharmacogenetic (PGx) screening from diagnostic sequencing to clinical decision-making. Germline whole genome sequencing (WGS) data generated during routine patient diagnostics are used as input for pharmacogenetic analysis. Variant call format (VCF) files are processed using PharmCAT to translate genetic variants into pharmacogenetic phenotypes. A pharmacogenetic report is generated and clinically validated by a pharmacist before documentation of phenotypes into the electronic health record (EHR). Phenotypes are recorded by the pharmacist as contraindications and checked with the current medication. Recording the contraindications function as clinical decision support (CDS), triggering automated alerts at the time of prescribing. When an alert is generated, the prescriber and pharmacist consult to interpret the PGx result and consider potential modifications to drug choice or dosing. The workflow highlights the roles of the diagnostics laboratory, bioinformaticians, pharmacy, and routine clinical care in the implementation of pre-emptive PGx screening.

Leveraging germline whole-genome sequencing for pre-emptive PGx screening

Given that tumor/normal WGS was already routinely performed in our center for diagnostics, this platform was leveraged for pre-emptive PGx screening. Repurposing existing germline WGS data minimized the need for additional testing while enabling comprehensive and standardized genetic profiling. To ensure accurate genotype and phenotype assignment, a bioinformatics workflow was developed for PGx variant extraction and interpretation. Several tools for PGx genotype calling and phenotype assignment from sequencing data are currently available, including Astrolabe, Aldy, Stargazer, (B)Cyrius, PharmCAT, and StellarPGx [46–50]. Based on compatibility with WGS data, alignment with CPIC guidelines and DPWG recommendations and the ability to translate genotypes to PGx phenotypes, PharmCAT was selected for clinical implementation. The selection of this annotation tool for WGS-based PGx has been reported separately [51].

Bioinformatics pipeline and validation

PharmCAT was imputed into the bioinformatics WGS pipeline within a high-performance computing (HPC) environment, building on the institute’s standardized WGS data processing workflows [15]. Germline Variant Call Format (VCF) files generated during diagnostic sequencing were processed by PharmCAT. PharmCAT was applied in accordance with the developers’ recommendations to annotate star-allele diplotypes and assign corresponding PGx phenotypes [52]. PharmCAT validation was performed using reference samples and previously characterized genotypes to ensure concordance and reproducibility [51]. The analytical validation of this annotation tool for WGS-based PGx has been reported separately [51].

The workflow was designed to generate PGx results within approximately 2 weeks after diagnosis, with the aim of timely integration into clinical decision-making. Although the turnaround time of 2 weeks is sufficient for most oncologic treatments, it limits the clinical utility of pre-emptive PGx screening for rasburicase (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase G6PD) or allopurinol (ATP-dependent transporter of the ATP-binding cassette ABCG2) [53]. Implementation of PGx screening using WGS also introduced technical challenges related to variant detection and phenotype assignment. For human leukocyte antigen (HLA), mitochondrially encoded 12S ribosomal RNA (MT-RNR1), and cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6), limitations in variant calling, phasing, or functional annotation may result in indeterminate or incomplete PGx phenotypes. These genes present analytical challenges: HLA genes are highly polymorphic and structurally complex, MT-RNR1 is encoded in the mitochondrial genome and requires dedicated approaches for accurate variant detection aside from the WGS analysis we perform, and CYP2D6 contains copy number variations (CNVs) that, using our standard WGS pipeline, leads to uncertain genotype and phenotype results of CYP2D6 [54–56]. Consequently, G6PD and ABCG2 were excluded due to turnaround time constraints, while HLA, MT-RNR1, and CYP2D6 were excluded because of current technical limitations in reliable variant detection and phenotype assignment from WGS data.

Framework for pediatric-specific interpretation of PGx results

In parallel with the development and validation of the bioinformatics pipeline, existing PGx guidelines were systematically reviewed. To support the clinical use of existing PGx guidelines in pediatric oncology, we developed a structured framework to assess their applicability to this population. Rather than generating novel recommendations, this framework evaluated CPIC and DPWG guidelines for relevance in pediatric oncology practice. The assessment focused on (i) the evidence linking genetic variation to pharmacokinetics and/or clinical outcomes (i.e., efficacy, toxicity), (ii) the drug’s indication in pediatric oncology, (iii) concordance between adult and pediatric dosing strategies, and (iv) the potential influence of ontogeny on genotype-phenotype relationships (Table 3).

Based on this structured appraisal, pediatric-oriented prescribing guidance was formulated that aligns with existing guidelines while explicitly highlighting contexts in which extrapolation of PGx recommendations requires caution. Where appropriate, contextual qualifiers were added to acknowledge factors such as phenoconversion, limited pediatric evidence, or disease-specific treatment goals in oncology. In case of drug-drug-gene interactions, patients with multiple concomitant medications and/or multiple actionable PGx phenotypes will be evaluated on an individual case basis through multidisciplinary consultation to ensure appropriate interpretation and application of PGx recommendations within the context of the complete treatment regimen.

These considerations are summarized in Table 3, with additional clarifications provided in Table S2. These qualifiers serve to guide clinical interpretation rather than to supersede existing guideline recommendations. The resulting guidance forms the basis of the PGx recommendations presented in the clinical portal (Figure S2). We predominantly follow recommendations from DPWG as these are integrated in our CDS, triggering automated alerts with DPWG recommendation text at the time of drug prescribing. When no DPWG recommendations are available, we will follow the CPIC guidelines.

PGx test results are translated into a standardized clinical report, provided as a structured PDF file and integrated into the electronic health record system (EHR). The report includes genotype and phenotype assignments, star-allele diplotypes and functional annotation of alleles and metadata describing the analysis. This report is validated by a pharmacist before integration of relevant phenotypes into the EHR. Phenotypes are documented as contraindications and function as a clinical decision support (CDS) system, triggering automated alerts at the time of prescribing. While PGx reports can be incorporated into the electronic health record, two major interoperability gaps remain. First, not all laboratory and pharmacy software systems are directly connected, meaning that PGx results often still require manual transfer or reentry between platforms. Second, the EHR does not yet support prescribing advice and automated clinical decision support for all relevant gene-drug pairs. As a consequence, clinicians may need to rely on additional manual steps, such as consulting external recommendations or contacting the laboratory or pharmacy for clarification. Further development of standardized interfaces and broader implementation of PGx-enabled decision support within the EHR will be essential to achieve fully automated and scalable PGx-guided prescribing.

Data exchange with external healthcare providers is facilitated through the Dutch national health information exchange infrastructure, Landelijk Schakelpunt (LSP) [63]. The LSP enables secure electronic exchange of essential medical data between healthcare providers, including information on the availability of patient records.

Phase 4. Embedding pre-emptive pharmacogenetic screening into routine care of pediatric oncology

To support sustained clinical adoption of pre-emptive PGx screening, targeted educational activities were established alongside the technical implementation. These measures aimed to ensure that PGx results are consistently interpreted, appropriately applied in clinical decision-making, and maintained over time.

Education and communication

An educational program was developed for healthcare providers involved in pediatric oncology care and PGx testing. Educational sessions were delivered as multidisciplinary teaching rounds and workshops, involving pediatric oncologists, nurses, pharmacists, bioinformaticians, and laboratory staff. These sessions focused on the principles of PGx, interpretation of PGx reports and application of prescribing recommendations in pediatric oncology. In addition to live education, communication strategies were implemented to inform clinical teams about the introduction of pre-emptive PGx screening and associated changes to routine care. This included dissemination of written guidance and workflow descriptions through the clinical portal.

Maintenance and updating of pharmacogenetic information

To ensure the continued validity and clinical relevance of PGx recommendations, a formal maintenance process was established. The multidisciplinary pharmacogenetics working group conducts a regular review of implemented guidelines, variant-calling procedures, and quality assurance processes. Updates to CPIC or DPWG guidelines, as well as changes in variant interpretation or bioinformatics pipelines, are assessed for clinical relevance and incorporated into the implementation workflow as needed [64]. In addition, mechanisms were put in place to provide timely notifications to clinicians when clinically relevant updates occur, supporting ongoing safe and evidence-based use of PGx information in routine pediatric oncology care.

Costs

Costs are frequently cited as a limiting factor for the implementation of PGx screening in routine clinical care. Relevant cost components include genetic testing, data storage, bioinformatics infrastructure, software, and personnel, as well as reimbursement by health insurance providers. In our setting, pre-emptive PGx screening was implemented by leveraging diagnostic WGS that was already part of routine pediatric oncology care. The consumable and sequencing costs for germline WGS are approximately €700–800 per sample. Importantly, these costs reflect sequencing of normal tissue only and do not include downstream bioinformatics analysis or interpretation. When both tumor and normal tissue are sequenced for diagnostic purposes, total sequencing costs are substantially higher; however, for PGx screening, only the germline dataset is required. By reusing existing germline WGS data, additional laboratory costs for PGx testing were avoided. Incremental costs were primarily related to bioinformatics processing, data storage, software implementation, and maintenance of the PGx pipeline. These costs were incorporated within existing institutional infrastructure and workflows, limiting the need for additional investment. At present, reimbursement for PGx testing varies and may depend on national healthcare policies and insurance coverage. In the Dutch healthcare system, the use of diagnostic WGS data for secondary analyses, such as PGx screening, offers a cost-efficient approach to implementation. Future studies evaluating the cost-effectiveness of pre-emptive PGx screening in pediatric oncology, including potential reductions in drug-related toxicity and treatment interruptions, will be important to further inform reimbursement strategies and broader adoption.

Impact for patients

Although this study focused on the technical and organizational implementation of pre-emptive PGx screening, its integration into routine pediatric oncology care has important implications for patients and families. Pre-emptive PGx testing generates results that will influence both current and future pharmacotherapy of patients, requiring clear explanation of their clinical relevance, limitations, and potential long-term value. In contrast to reactive single-gene testing, panel-based PGx screening may identify variants that are not immediately actionable but could become relevant later in life, securing patients with lifelong PGx-based dosing opportunities. Implementation therefore necessitates structured patient education and counseling to ensure adequate understanding of PGx results and their implications. Families will be informed about how PGx results are used in prescribing decisions, how they are stored within the electronic health record, and how they may be shared with external healthcare providers. The use of a pharmacogenetic “DNA medication card” may further empower patients by enabling continuity of PGx-informed care beyond the treating center; however, this also requires appropriate explanation regarding interpretation and responsible use [65]. Although the direct impact of pre-emptive PGx screening on patient experience, comprehension, and shared decision-making was not formally evaluated in this study, these aspects represent important components of sustainable implementation.

Future perspectives

This study describes the design and implementation of a workflow for pre-emptive PGx screening in pediatric oncology using diagnostic WGS, and the embedding of this workflow into routine clinical care. While this represents an important step toward personalized medicine in pediatric oncology, several next steps are required to further strengthen and expand its clinical impact.

Evaluation of implementation and research opportunities

Although the present study focused on constructing and embedding an implementation workflow, formal evaluation of implementation outcomes (e.g., feasibility, sustainability, and acceptability) and clinical outcomes (e.g., toxicity, treatment modifications, and costs) remains an important next step. Beyond evaluation of clinical utility, systematic pre-emptive PGx screening in combination with diagnostic WGS creates important research opportunities. The availability of standardized germline PGx data for a broad pediatric oncology population enables prospective assessment of genotype–phenotype relationships and facilitates more robust analyses of treatment-related toxicity. As PGx information will be available for each patient irrespective of clinical events, this approach inherently generates internal comparison groups, thereby strengthening causal inference in observational analyses. Furthermore, integration of PGx data with detailed treatment protocols and toxicity registries may support identification of novel gene-drug associations and refinement of existing dosing recommendations in pediatric oncology.

Evidence development, guideline refinement and expanding PGx gene test panel

A key future perspective is the continued expansion of pediatric-specific pharmacogenetic evidence. Although existing CPIC and DPWG recommendations formed the backbone of the implementation described here, a substantial proportion of potentially relevant gene-drug pairs remains insufficiently supported by clinical data, and emerging evidence is likely to modify existing recommendations. Prospective clinical and translational studies, including evaluation of enzyme activity and treatment-related toxicity, are needed to inform future guideline development.

Maintaining the clinical relevance of PGx results over time is essential. As new genetic variants and gene-drug associations are identified, workflows for guideline updates, variant reinterpretation, and clinical communication will require ongoing attention. Long-term accessibility of PGx results, for example, through the “DNA medication card,” may facilitate reuse of germline data during follow-up care and beyond pediatric oncology [65]. In parallel, ongoing improvements in sequencing technologies, bioinformatics pipelines, and variant annotation resources are expected to enhance coverage of clinically relevant PGx variants and reduce the number of indeterminate phenotypes. Integration of emerging computational tools and curated reference databases may further support reliable reinterpretation of PGx results as knowledge evolves.

For certain genes, additional methodological considerations remain necessary. For example, accurate CYP2D6 phenotype assignment from WGS data requires specialized bioinformatic tools capable of resolving structural variation and copy-number changes. Although CYP2D6 was not included in the current clinical implementation workflow, several approaches have demonstrated that clinically relevant CYP2D6 alleles and predicted metabolic phenotypes can be inferred from sequencing data. Given the clinical importance of CYP2D6 for the metabolism of many drugs, evaluation and validation of dedicated CYP2D6 genotyping tools is planned in the near future to enable incorporation of CYP2D6 into routine pre-emptive PGx screening. Similarly, HLA and MT-RNR1 variants require alternative analytical approaches to overcome current limitations in variant detection and annotation. Continued development and validation of these technical solutions will support future integration of these clinically relevant PGx markers into routine pediatric oncology workflows.

Integration into clinical systems and standard care

Further embedding of PGx screening into routine pediatric oncology care will require continued alignment with national and international clinical frameworks. Integration of pediatric PGx recommendations into DPWG medication surveillance activities and national pediatric formularies, such as the Dutch Kinderformularium that provides dosing information for children, may improve consistency and clinical uptake. In addition, incorporating PGx information into disease-specific treatment and supportive care protocols could further normalize its use at the point of care. From a technical perspective, future implementation efforts should focus on improving interoperability between laboratory systems, electronic health records, and clinical decision support platforms to enable automated, real-time PGx guidance at the point of prescribing. In addition, strategies to reduce turnaround times for high-urgency medications, such as rapid genotyping workflows or hybrid testing approaches, will be essential to maximize the clinical impact of pre-emptive PGx screening in acute treatment settings.

Conclusion

This paper describes the implementation of pre-emptive pharmacogenetic screening in routine pediatric oncology care and highlights key clinical, infrastructural, and practical considerations encountered during this process. We demonstrate that existing CPIC guidelines and DPWG recommendations can serve as a robust foundation for pre-emptive PGx screening in pediatric oncology when carefully adapted to the pediatric context. By detailing our implementation workflow, we aim to provide practical guidance for other centers seeking to integrate pharmacogenetics into clinical practice. Continued development of pediatric-specific pharmacogenetic evidence and guidelines, together with incorporation of newly identified genetic variants, will further enhance the role of pre-emptive PGx screening in precision medicine for pediatric oncology.
