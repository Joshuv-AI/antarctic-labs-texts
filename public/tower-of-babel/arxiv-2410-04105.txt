Hydrodynamic equations and near critical large deviations of active lattice gases
Luke Neville

Abstract
Using a path integral approach, we derive and study the hydrodynamic equations and large deviation functions for three active lattice gases. After a review of the path integral for master equations, we first look at a one dimensional model of motility induced phase separation (MIPS), re-deriving the large deviation function that was previously found through a mapping to the ABC model. After extracting the deterministic hydrodynamic equations from the large deviation function, we analyse them perturbatively near the MIPS critical point using a weakly non-linear analysis. Doing this we show that they reduce to equilibrium Model B very close to criticality, with non-equilibrium, or Active Model B terms emerging as we leave the critical region. The same type of weakly non-linear analysis is then applied to the full large deviation function, and we show that the near critical stationary probability distribution is given by the exponential of a ϕ 4 \phi^{4} free energy, as expected in ordinary equilibrium phase separation. Similar calculations are then done for the two other lattice gases one which is another MIPS model, and another which models flocking, and in both cases we find analogous results.

Given these difficulties it seems necessary to find models that can be coarse grained in a mathematically controlled way, and a class that seems to fill this requirement is the diffusive lattice gas

,
27
,

. These stochastic models originated in the mathematical physics literature

,

Although perhaps more artificial than traditional particle based simulations, they are valuable because their microscopic dynamics means that they become effectively deterministic in the limit of small lattice spacing, with their average behaviour following a set ‘exact’ hydrodynamic equations. As such, these diffusive lattice gases can undergo phase transitions even in one dimension

\mathcal{P}[\text{traj}]\asymp\exp[-\mathcal{A}/\epsilon],

,

,

,

,

,

,

In this paper we use these methods to take the two lattice models of active matter introduced in Ref.
[
37
]
and connect them with other, more standard hydrodynamic theories of active matter
[
3
]
. For, say, the model of MIPS, this is required because the hydrodynamic equations derive in Ref.
[
37
]
govern two fields, the density and the magnetisation, whereas standard field theories of MIPS like active model B use only one
[
15
]
. These two fields appear naturally in the derivation of the hydrodynamic equations from the lattice gas, coming from the two types of particle in the lattice gas. In usual studies of MIPS it is argued that all fields apart from the density may be adiabatically eliminated
[
21
]
, however for the lattice gas hydrodynamics the magnetisation evolves on the same time scale and so cannot. In this work we take inspiration from standard gradient expansions
[
20
,
54

to show that this statement, although true in general, does not hold near the MIPS critical point where only the density is critically slowed. As such we are able to apply a weakly non-linear analysis to show that the full lattice gas hydrodynamics can be mapped onto active model B near the critical point. In fact, we show to leading order in a reduced temperature type parameter, that the dynamics become perfectly equilibrium-like near the critical point, with non-equilibrium terms emerging as one leaves the critical region. After doing this calculation on the deterministic hydrodynamic equations, we then include fluctuations by applying the same weakly non-linear theory to the full LDF. This allows us to show that the near critical stationary probability distribution is the exponential of a

[
37

, finding analogous results in both cases.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: in Sec.

2.1
Path integral

With lattice gases introduced, we would now like to derive hydrodynamic equations and LDFs describing their macroscopic behaviour. Although not necessary, it is easiest to do this using the path integral because it naturally gives the trajectory probabilities. There are various path integral representations of master equations, perhaps most famous being that of Doi and Peliti
[
60
,
61
,
18
,
59
]
, which works by converting the master equation to second quantised form and using coherent states. Not only is this algebraically cumbersome, it can be difficult to map Doi-Peliti variables to physical particle densities
[
62
]
. As such, we instead use a more direct method
[
63
,
64
]
, similar to the approach introduced in Refs.
[
65
,
66
]
.

As in all path integrals, the key object of interest is the evolution operator taking the probability at time zero to the probability at time
T
. Similar to quantum mechanics, this is given by the exponential of the Hamiltonian,
\exp[\mathcal{H}T]
, and as shown in
A
, this has the path integral representation

\int\mathcal{D}[n_{i},\hat{n}_{i}]\exp[-\int^{T}_{0}\mathrm{d}t\ \left(\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i}\partial_{t}n_{i}\right)-\mathcal{H}(\hat{n}_{i},n_{i})],

(7)

where
\hat{n}
is the conjugate ‘momentum’, taking the place of
-\partial_{n}
in the Hamiltonian. In this representation, the general form of the Hamiltonian is even clearer, with

\mathcal{H}(n_{i},\hat{n}_{i})=\sum_{i}\left(e^{\sum_{i}\Delta n_{i}\hat{n}_{i}}-1\right)r_{i}(\{n\}).

(8)

This path integral fully captures the microscopic dynamics of the lattice gas, but to study the large scale, hydrodynamic behaviour, we must coarse grain the particle numbers
n_{i}
, into a smooth density field
\rho(x)

[
50
,
26
]
.

2.2
Hydrodynamic limit

As the lattice spacing,
\epsilon
, tends to zero, we expect the discrete particle numbers
n_{i}
to converge to a smooth hydrodynamic density profile
\rho(x=i\epsilon)
, which only varies on a macroscopic scale. To convert between these two levels of description, one may be tempted to take the discrete path integral (
7
), and directly replace
n_{i}
with
\rho(x)
everywhere. This ‘mean-field’ approximation sometimes works, but, as shown in section four of Ref.
[
36
]
and Ref.
[
62
]
, generally gives incorrect hydrodynamic equations. The failure arises because this naive smoothing does not account for fluctuations/ correlations that may exist in small mesoscopic regions around each lattice site. Such correlations are captured by being more careful and considering the local equilibrium structure of the lattice gas
[
26
,
27
,
66
]
.

To this end, we will first write the Hamiltonian as the sum of a Hamiltonian density,
\mathcal{H}=\sum_{i}H_{i}(\{n_{i},\hat{n}_{i}\})
, which is given by the term in the summation of (
8
). We then divide the lattice up into mesoscopic boxes, of linear dimension
\delta
centred on the lattice sites, where
\delta
is much larger than the lattice spacing,
\epsilon
, but much smaller than the system size
[
50
]
. This lets us write the Hamiltonian as sum over boxes

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{x}\frac{1}{|B_{\delta}(x)|}\sum_{i\in B_{\delta}(x)}H(\{\hat{n}_{i},n_{i}\}).

(9)

where
|B_{\delta}(x)|
is the number of sites in the box and must be included to avoid over counting.

The assumption of local equilibrium then states that each of these boxes rapidly relaxes to a local equilibrium state that is near constant in each box, but which evolves slowly over the macroscopic, hydrodynamic scale
[
50
,
67
,
31
]
. The average number of each particles in each box is then equal to the slowly varying density
\rho(x,t)
, and the empirical averages over each box are assumed to converge to the average over the (as yet unknown) local equilibrium measure. Mathematically this means

\frac{1}{|B_{\delta}(x)|}\sum_{i\in B_{\delta}(x)}H(\{\hat{n}_{i},n_{i}\})\rightarrow\langle H(\{\hat{n}_{i},n_{i}\})\rangle,

(10)

where the angle brackets mean an average over local equilibrium. In general, we expect some form of local equilibrium to exist when the particles are so rapidly mixed in each box, that each particle can explore the entire mesoscopic box over a microscopically short time, while only leaving the box after a macroscopic time
[
30
]
. This requirement holds for the diffusively dominated dynamics studied in this work, and for the models we study the local equilibrium has been rigorously established
[
37
,
42
]
.

Assuming a slowly varying density profile, we exchange the sum over sites with an integral, yielding

\mathcal{H}(\hat{\rho},\rho)=\epsilon^{-d}\int\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}\ \langle H(\{\hat{n}_{i},n_{i}\})\rangle,

(11)

where we have also relabelled
\hat{n}
to
\hat{\rho}
, and used
d
for the spatial dimension. Here we have also assumed that the conjugate field can be directly smoothed which we can only justify by comparing our results with other work
[
68
]
. In general we must also perform some extra steps like Taylor expanding the fields around each site, but this is better seen through example.

Substituting these results into (
7
) yields the hydrodynamic path integral governing the stochastic evolution of the density field
\rho

\begin{split}&\mathcal{P}[\rho(\textbf{r},t)]=\int\mathcal{D}[\rho,\hat{\rho}]e^{-\mathcal{A}/\epsilon^{d}},\quad\mathcal{A}=\int\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}\ \hat{\rho}\partial_{t}\rho-\mathcal{H}(\hat{\rho},\rho).\end{split}

(12)

The factor of
\epsilon^{-d}
in the exponent means that, as
\epsilon
tends to zero, the probability of observing a single trajectory takes large deviation form,
\mathcal{P}\asymp\exp[-\mathcal{A}/\epsilon^{d}]
. We thus identify the action
\mathcal{A}
as the LDF for a single trajectory.

2.3
Hydrodynamics for diffusion

Our discussion thus far has been rather formal, so we now demonstrate the approach using the one dimensional diffusion model introduced earlier. Using the conjugate variable, the Hamiltonian in
Eq.

6
is re-written as

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{i}\left(e^{\hat{n}_{i+1}-\hat{n}_{i}}+e^{\hat{n}_{i-1}-\hat{n}_{i}}-2\right)n_{i}.

(13)

We then have to take the average over local equilibrium,. Because this Hamiltonian is linear in the occupation number,
n_{i}
, it turns out we do not need to know the exact equilibrium measure as
\langle n_{i}\rangle=\rho(x)
by definition. However, as we need a similar result later, let us note that the local equilibrium measure is known to be product Poissonian
[
53
]
, i.e.

\mu_{\text{eqm}}=\prod_{i}\frac{\rho_{i}^{n_{i}}e^{-\rho_{i}}}{n_{i}!},

(14)

which follows quite naturally from the fact that we can have an arbitrary number of particles per site, and that they are non-interacting. Performing the average over
\mathcal{H}
we arrive at the same expression with
n
swapped for
\rho
. Assuming the density is smooth we then exchange the sum for an integral and Taylor expand the fields to
O(\epsilon)
about each site, yielding

\mathcal{H}=\epsilon\int\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}\ \rho\partial_{x}^{2}\hat{\rho}+\rho(\partial_{x}\hat{\rho})^{2}+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(15)

Substituting this Hamiltonian into the action, the final (optional) steps are to diffusively re-scale time according to
\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\epsilon^{-2}\mathrm{d}t
and perform some integrations by parts. Doing this we arrive at the known LDF for non-interacting diffusing particles
[
50
]

\mathcal{A}=\int\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}\int\mathrm{d}t\ \hat{\rho}(\partial_{t}\rho-\partial_{x}^{2}\rho)-\rho(\partial_{x}\hat{\rho})^{2}.

(16)

Because this LDF is quadratic in
\hat{\rho}
, stochastic hydrodynamic equations can be extracted using the Martin–Siggia–Rose–Janssen–De Dominicis (MSRJD) formalism
[
69
,
70
,
71
]
, yielding

\partial_{t}\rho=\partial_{x}^{2}\rho+\partial_{x}\xi,

(17)

where
\xi
is a Gaussian noise with variance

\langle\xi(x,t)\xi(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2\epsilon\rho\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(18)

The scaling of the noise with the lattice spacing means that the deterministic hydrodynamic equations become exact in the limit of zero lattice spacing, and this will remain true for all the lattice gases we study.

The Hamiltonian (

[

]

\mu_{\text{eqm}}=\prod_{i,\alpha}{(\rho_{i}^{\alpha})}^{n_{i}^{\alpha}}{(1-\rho_{i}^{\alpha})}^{(1-n^{\alpha}_{i})}.

(20)

\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\epsilon^{-2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\gamma\rho[1-\cosh 2\hat{m}]+\gamma m\sinh 2\hat{m}.

]

3.1
Deterministic behaviour

Before analysing fluctuations, it is instructive to look at the deterministic behaviour. Because this LDF is not Gaussian, we cannot directly use the MSRJD formalism of section
2.3
to extract the deterministic equations. Instead, we note that in the small lattice spacing limit, the path integral may be evaluated using the saddle point method
[
50
]
. Extremising the LDF, the relevant saddle point trajectories solve Hamilton’s equations

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\hat{\rho}},\quad\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\rho},\\
\partial_{t}m=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\hat{m}},\quad\partial_{t}\hat{m}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta m},\end{split}

(22)

where the conjugate variables act like momenta
[
50
]
. Although complicated, to find the deterministic hydrodynamic equations we only need the trivial solution
\hat{\rho}=\hat{m}=0
, corresponding to noiseless, deterministic motion
[
18
,
74
,
75
,
76
,
55
]
.

Substituting these into Hamilton’s equations we arrive at a set of coupled PDEs for the fields
\rho,m
:

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}\rho+\lambda\partial_{x}m(1-\rho)=D\partial_{x}^{2}\rho,\\
\partial_{t}m+\lambda\partial_{x}\rho(1-\rho)=D\partial_{x}^{2}m-2\gamma m,\end{gathered}

(23)

which are the hydrodynamic equations previously derived by more conventional methods in Ref.
[
37
]
. In that work, simulations of these equations were also compared to those of the underlying lattice gas, and excellent agreement was found when the lattice spacing was on the order of
10^{-3}
.

Following these authors, we simplify these equations by re-scaling space-time as
x\rightarrow\sqrt{D/\gamma}x
,
t\rightarrow t/\gamma
, arriving at

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}\rho+\text{Pe}\partial_{x}(m(1-\rho))=\partial_{x}^{2}\rho,\\
\partial_{t}m+\text{Pe}\partial_{x}(\rho(1-\rho))=\partial_{x}^{2}m-2m,\end{gathered}

(24)

where the Péclet number, comparing the rate of propulsion and diffusion, is
\text{Pe}=\lambda/\sqrt{D\gamma}
. Using these re-scaled equations, Kourbane-Housenne et al.
[
37
]
also calculated the spinodal and binodal densities shown in in
Fig.

3
, finding them to be in quantitative agreement with simulations of the underlying lattice gas.

3.3
Near critical probability distribution

Having seen how the deterministic equations compare to (active) model B, we now analyse the effect of fluctuations, which are important for all finite, but non-zero lattice spacings. We will limit ourselves to extremely small lattice spacings where, as the noise scales like
\epsilon
(c.f. section
2.3
), the noise is weak. Importantly, the noise remains weak even near the critical point
[
39
]
, which is quite different from the situation considered in ordinary statistical mechanics, where fluctuations are magnified near the critical point
[
12
,
41
,
40
]
.

Our approach will use the techniques of weak-noise theory
[
82
,
83
]
, with the aim to perturbatively calculate the stationary probability distribution near the critical point. In weak-noise theory the probability of observing a given configuration is given by the probability of the most likely state, multiplied by the transition probability of moving between the most-likely state and the desired configuration in an infinite amount of time
[
82
,
50
,
64
]
,i.e.

\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{C}]\asymp\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{C}_{0}]\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{C}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}],

(37)

where
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is the most likely configuration.

This transition probability is readily computed using the path integral (
12
) by conditioning the paths on the desired initial and final configurations. As mentioned in section
3.1
, the path integral is dominated by the saddle point trajectories which solve Hamilton’s equations (
22
). However, unlike the deterministic case, we will be interested in fluctuations where
\hat{\rho},\hat{m}
may not be zero
[
74
]
. The initial condition for these equations is the most likely configuration which must be a fixed point of Hamilton’s equations (
22
).

The general form of any LDF, and therefore Hamilton’s equations are such that any fixed point must have
\hat{\rho},\hat{m}=0
, i.e. the fixed-point must be noise-less
[
64
]
. Because the Hamiltonian is conserved by the dynamics, we find

\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{C}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}]\asymp\exp[-\frac{\sqrt{D}}{\sqrt{\gamma}\epsilon}\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}\ \hat{\rho}\partial_{t}\rho+\hat{m}\partial_{t}m],

(38)

with
\hat{\rho},\hat{m}
evaluated along the saddle path connecting configurations
\mathcal{C}_{0}
and
\mathcal{C}
. The pre-factor
\sqrt{D/\gamma}
comes from converting to the dimensionless units of
Eq.

24
, which handily sets all other coefficients in the LDF to unity
[
68
]
. Unfortunately this simplification does not make solving (
22
) any easier, and similar saddle equations have only been solved in a handful of cases (see Refs.
[
84
,
28
,
63
]
for interesting examples).

and
O(r^{2})
can be summed into a single set, which upon returning to the original space-time variables are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi=\partial_{x}^{2}\phi,

(56a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m=\partial_{x}^{2}m+Arm-6B\rho_{0}m\phi-2Bm^{3}.

(56b)

These equations do not derive from a single free energy, and so initially one may think that the dynamics are not in an equilibrium class. However, the density perturbation is decoupled from the magnetisation and diffusively relaxes, while the magnetisation is still critically slowed. Therefore over long times we may assume that the density perturbation relaxes to zero. Substituting this into the magnetisation equation we find the dynamics of
m
take model A form, with

\partial_{t}m=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta m},

(57)

and a free energy

\mathcal{F}=\int\mathrm{d}\textbf{r}\ -\frac{Ar}{2}m^{2}+\frac{B}{2}m^{4}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}m)^{2}.

(58)

In other words, the free energy is still Ising type over long time scales, however the dynamics are different because there is no conservation law.

6
Discussion

Starting from the master equations describing the stochastic dynamics we have derived hydrodynamic equations, and the corresponding large deviation functions describing the coarse grained behaviour of three active lattice gases. Using this hydrodynamic description we located their critical points and performed a series of weakly non-linear calculations, using them to argue that all models considered lay in mean-field kinetic Ising universality classes, model B for MIPS, and model A for flocking
[
80
]
.

These calculations were first done on the deterministic hydrodynamic equations, but were extended to include fluctuations by perturbatively solving the full saddle-point equations near the critical point. Doing this we found that the stationary probability distributions were given by the negative exponential of a free energy, just as in equilibrium, meaning that detailed balance is effectively restored asymptotically close to the critical point.

In the case of MIPS we also continued the expansion to higher order, showing that the two-field hydrodynamics reduced to active model B, the standard field theory of motility induced phase separation
[
15
,
3
]
, as we left the critical region. This implies that non-equilibrium effects do become important as one departs from the critical point, however they are irrelevant at criticality. This agrees with a number of numerical studies on the MIPS critical point, of which most conclude that it is in the Ising universality class
[
89
,
90
,
91
,
92
]
.

In this work we focused on one dimensional systems, but the methods used here could also be used in higher dimensional systems. Unfortunately, the LDFs become extremely cumbersome, as a new species must be introduced for every lattice direction. However, it is certainly worth investigating this further, as numerical studies of two dimensional lattice models of MIPS have found some possible differences between models on square and triangular lattices
[
92
,
89
]
. Moreover, the active terms become more interesting in dimensions two or higher, with there being two independent non-equilibrium terms in the density current, rather than the one in one dimension
[
15
]
. It would also be interesting to make these lattice gases thermodynamically consistent by explicitly accounting for the chemical fuel used in self-propulsion
[
93
,
94
]
, which would give insight into the earlier phenomenological approaches which started with the continuum equations
[
93
]
. We leave all this for future work.

Appendix A
Path integral derivation

The path integral representation is derived here for single species on one site, but the derivation is identical for multiple sites. To put the master equation in path integral form we use linearity to evolve the probability forwards by a small amount of time
\delta t
,
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\delta t)=\exp[\mathcal{H}\delta t]\mathcal{P}_{n}(0)
.

\displaystyle e^{\mathcal{H}\delta t}\mathcal{P}_{n}(0)

\displaystyle\approx[1+\mathcal{H}\delta t]\mathcal{P}_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=[1+\delta t(e^{-\Delta n\partial_{n}}-1)r(n)]\mathcal{P}_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{0}}[1+\delta t(e^{-\Delta n_{0}\partial_{n}}-1)\delta_{n,{n_{0}}}r(n_{0})]\mathcal{P}_{n_{0}}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{0}}\int_{-\mathrm{i}\pi}^{\mathrm{i}\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{n}_{0}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}[1+\delta t(e^{-\Delta n_{0}\partial_{n}}-1)e^{-\hat{n}_{0}(n-n_{0})}r(n_{0})]\mathcal{P}_{n_{0}}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{0}}\int_{-\mathrm{i}\pi}^{\mathrm{i}\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{n}_{0}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}[1+\delta t(e^{\Delta n_{0}\hat{n}_{0}}-1)e^{-\hat{n}_{0}(n-n_{0})}r(n_{0})]\mathcal{P}_{n_{0}}(0)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{n_{0}}\int_{-\mathrm{i}\pi}^{\mathrm{i}\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{n}_{0}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\exp[-\hat{n}_{0}(n-n_{0})+\delta t(e^{\hat{n}_{0}\Delta n}-1)r(n_{0})]\mathcal{P}_{n_{0}}(0),

where in the last line we have re-exponentiated the result, which is correct to
O(\delta t)

[
95
]
. By combining many of these evolutions we get the probability at a time
T

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(T)=\int\mathcal{D}[n,\hat{n}]e^{-\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}_{n}(0),

(59a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\sum_{t=0}^{t=T-\delta t}\hat{n}_{t}\left(n_{t+\delta t}-n_{t}\right)-\delta t\left(e^{\Delta n\hat{n}_{t}}-1\right)r\left(n_{t}\right),

(59b)

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}[n,\hat{n}]=\lim_{\delta t\rightarrow 0}\prod_{t=0}^{T-\delta t}\sum_{n(t)=0}^{N_{max}}\int_{-\mathrm{i}\pi}^{\mathrm{i}\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{n}(t)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}.

(59c)

It is tempting to take the continuum limit and convert the forward differences to time derivatives. However,
n
is always an integer valued quantity and a derivative does not exist. With this fact in mind we will write the path integral in continuous notation although it should be understood in its discrete form. In continuum form, the action is

\mathcal{S}=\int\mathrm{d}t\ \hat{n}\partial_{t}n-\mathcal{H}(\hat{n},n),

(60)

which is the standard form from classical mechanics if
\hat{n}
is the conjugate ‘momentum’ to
n
, obeying canonical commutation relations
[n,\hat{n}]=1
. The way we have discretised time also means that the continuum Langevin equations from this integral must be interpreted in the Itô sense
[
96
]
.

Using this path integral the probability of a single trajectory through configuration space is given by

\mathcal{P}[\text{traj}]=\int\mathcal{D}[\hat{n}]e^{-\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}(t=0),

(61)

where only
\hat{n}
is integrated over, while the value of
n
is specified at each time step.

Appendix B
Current form of the LDF

Macroscopic fluctuation theory considers the joint fluctuations of the density
\rho
, conserved current
j
, and flux
K

[
50
]
, however our LDFs are written in terms of the conjugate field
\hat{\rho}
. To demonstrate how to convert between the two we use the following LDF describing diffusion combined with particle creation and destruction at rates
b
and
d
respectively

\mathcal{A}=\int\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}x\ \hat{\rho}[\partial_{t}\rho-D\partial_{x}^{2}\rho]-D\rho(\partial_{x}\hat{\rho})^{2}-b(e^{\hat{\rho}}-1)-d(e^{-\hat{\rho}}-1)\rho.

(62)

To put this in current form we demand that the density satisfies the exact equation

\partial_{t}\rho=-\partial_{x}j+K,

(63)

where
\rho
,
j
, and
K
are fluctuating quantities representing the density, conserved, and un-conserved currents. Let us now break apart the LDF into two pieces coming from the conserved and non-conserved current. For the conserved current alone we have an

\mathcal{A}=\int\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}x\ \hat{\rho}[\partial_{t}{\rho}-D\partial_{x}^{2}\rho]-D\rho(\partial_{x}\hat{\rho})^{2}.

(64)

We now replace
\partial_{t}\rho
with
-\partial_{x}j
and integrate by parts, giving the result

\mathcal{A}_{j}=\int\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}x\ j\partial_{x}\hat{\rho}+D(\partial_{x}\rho)(\partial_{x}\hat{\rho})-D\rho(\partial_{x}\hat{\rho})^{2}.

(65)

We would now like to perform the path integral over
\hat{\rho}
, and this can be done exactly, giving

\mathcal{A}_{j}=\int\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}x\ \frac{(j+D\partial_{x}\rho)^{2}}{4D\rho}.

(66)

This quantifies how the conserved current
j
fluctuates around it’s average value
-D\partial_{x}\rho
. We now turn to the non-conserved current, with an LDF

\mathcal{A}=\int\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}x\ \hat{\rho}\partial_{t}{\rho}-b(e^{\hat{\rho}}-1)-d(e^{-\hat{\rho}}-1)\rho.

(67)

Following similar methods to before we replace
\partial_{t}\rho
with
K
and perform the path integral over
\hat{\rho}
. The
\hat{\rho}
path integral cannot be performed exactly but can be done using a saddle point method. The result is that
\hat{\rho}
is given by

e^{\hat{\rho}}=\frac{K+\sqrt{K^{2}+4db\rho}}{2b}.

(68)

This is substituted into the LDF to give

\mathcal{A}_{K}=\int\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}x\ b+d\rho-\sqrt{K^{2}+4bd\rho}+K\log\frac{\sqrt{K^{2}+4bd\rho}+K}{2b}.

(69)

The total probability for a given path is then

\mathcal{P}\asymp\delta(\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}j-K)e^{-\mathcal{I}(\rho,j,K)/\epsilon}

(70)

where the large deviation functional is

\mathcal{I}(\rho,j,K)=\mathcal{A}_{j}+\mathcal{A}_{K},

(71)

and the functional delta function ensures that the exact equation (
63
) is satisfied. The LDF in terms of
\rho
and
\hat{\rho}
can be recovered by introducing a functional representation of the delta function and extremising the action over
K
and
j

[
50
]
.
