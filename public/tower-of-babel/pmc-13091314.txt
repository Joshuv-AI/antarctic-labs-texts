Women oncologists across 18 Latin American countries: A multinational survey of professional barriers, leadership, and pay equity

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen oncologists in Latin America play an essential role in cancer care but face persistent challenges related to leadership representation, career advancement, salary equity, and work–life balance. This study aimed to analyze the professional experiences and perceived barriers among women oncologists across Latin America.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted using an online survey distributed through professional networks between October 2023 and March 2024. A total of 350 women oncologists from 18 Latin American countries participated. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and cross-tabulations with IBM SPSS version 27.ResultsSeventy-seven percent of respondents reported needing to work harder than male colleagues to gain professional recognition. Motherhood was perceived as a barrier to career progression by 60.9% of participants, and 51% reported reducing their working hours after having children. Between 30% and 40% reported experiencing maternity-related discrimination. While 60.3% perceived their salary as equitable compared with male counterparts, 24.6% disagreed. High levels of work-related stress were reported by 67.7% of respondents. Women's representation in leadership positions remained limited overall, averaging 28.7% across institutions, although proportions ranged from approximately 30% to 35% in some specialties.ConclusionWomen oncologists in Latin America continue to report perceived barriers related to leadership opportunities, career progression, and work–life balance. This multinational survey provides an important regional baseline for understanding gender-related professional dynamics and highlights the need for institutional strategies that promote gender equity and leadership development in oncology across the region.

Introduction

The gender composition of the medical profession has changed substantially worldwide, including in Latin America. Over recent decades, female participation in medical education has increased significantly. For example, in Mexico, female enrollment in medical schools rose from 11% in 1970 to nearly 50% by 1998, reflecting a broader regional trend toward greater female representation in medicine. However, this increase in educational participation has not always translated into equal professional opportunities in the medical workforce. [1]

Oncology, a critical specialty for public health, continues to face gender equity challenges. Despite progress in female participation, women oncologists often encounter barriers affecting professional recognition, access to leadership roles, research opportunities, and career advancement [2], [3]. These disparities may influence not only the professional development of physicians but also the organization and functioning of oncology services [4].

Gender equity in medicine has been associated with improvements in professional environments and healthcare delivery. Diverse medical teams can contribute to better communication with patients and improved clinical decision-making [5], [6]. Nevertheless, international reports from organizations such as the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) continue to highlight persistent gender gaps in leadership representation, salary equity, and career advancement opportunities for women oncologists [7], [8]. Additionally, the disproportionate burden of family responsibilities remains a significant challenge affecting professional development and work–life balance [9].

While several international initiatives have promoted mentorship programs and gender equity policies within oncology organizations [9], [10], the situation in Latin America remains influenced by complex sociocultural, economic, and institutional factors that may limit women's access to leadership opportunities [11]. These challenges are also associated with higher levels of burnout among women physicians, often related to heavy workloads, administrative responsibilities, and difficulties balancing professional and family roles [12].

In this context, understanding the professional experiences of women oncologists in Latin America is essential to identify structural barriers and opportunities for improvement. Despite the growing participation of women in medicine, empirical evidence describing their professional experiences within oncology across the region remains limited. This study presents the results of a multinational survey of 350 women oncologists from 18 Latin American countries, aiming to analyze perceived professional challenges and contribute to the discussion on gender equity in oncology systems in the region.

Methods

Study design and participants

This descriptive cross-sectional study was based on an online survey conducted between October 2023 and March 2024 among women oncologists practicing in Latin America. A total of 350 participants from 18 countries completed the survey.

The questionnaire included 52 items addressing demographic characteristics, professional context, leadership opportunities, perceived gender-related barriers, the impact of motherhood on career development, and work–life balance.

Recruitment and dissemination

The survey was disseminated through professional oncology networks across Latin America, including professional societies, institutional mailing lists, social media platforms, and WhatsApp groups used by oncology professionals. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were provided. The questionnaire remained open for approximately six months.

Languages and survey development

The survey was developed in Spanish and made available to participants across Latin America.

The survey instrument was specifically developed for this study and did not undergo formal psychometric validation. Although the questionnaire was reviewed by members of the research team and oncology professionals within the Latin American Association of Women in Oncology (ALAMO), the absence of formal validation may affect measurement reliability and comparability across settings. Future studies should consider formal pilot testing and psychometric validation of the survey instrument.

Data collection and analysis

Responses were collected anonymously and analyzed using IBM SPSS software (version 27). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the data, and cross-tabulations were performed to explore patterns across countries and oncology specialties. As the study was primarily exploratory and descriptive, inferential statistical tests were not prioritized. Future studies with larger and more balanced samples may allow more robust statistical comparisons across specialties, countries, and demographic groups. Results are presented following STROBE recommendations for reporting observational studies.

Handling of missing data

Incomplete responses were included in descriptive analyses when data were available for the corresponding question. Item non-response rates were reported where relevant. No statistical imputation of missing data was performed.

Selection bias and representativeness

Participation in the survey was voluntary and recruitment relied on professional networks and social media dissemination. As a result, the sample may overrepresent individuals who are more actively engaged in professional organizations or academic activities. In addition, the heterogeneous distribution of respondents across countries and specialties may limit the generalizability of the findings to all women oncologists in Latin America. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Sex and gender considerations (SAGER guidelines)

This study focused on oncology professionals who self-identified as women. Gender was treated as a self-reported identity variable used as an eligibility criterion for participation in the survey. Therefore, the findings reflect the perspectives of women oncologists and are not intended to represent other gender identities.

Perceived gender inequality

Overall, 77% of respondents reported feeling that they needed to work harder than their male colleagues to achieve comparable professional recognition. While 60.3% perceived salary equity with male counterparts performing similar roles, 24.6% reported disparities in compensation. In addition, 26.3% indicated experiencing barriers when applying for leadership positions.

Impact of motherhood on career development

In our survey, 51% of the female oncologists reduced their working hours after becoming mothers, and 60.9% considered that this situation negatively affected their professional development. However, 71% of the participants reported not having experienced direct discrimination due to motherhood when applying for leadership roles.

Mental and occupational health

Work-related stress and burnout were commonly reported among respondents. Overall, 67.7% of participants indicated experiencing work-related stress during their professional practice.

Female representation in leadership roles

Female representation in leadership positions varied across countries. Brazil showed the highest proportion of women in leadership roles (40%), followed by Bolivia and the Dominican Republic (both 37%). In Paraguay (32%), Peru (33%), and Chile (30%), representation remained substantial but lower. In contrast, Mexico and Argentina reported lower proportions (23% and 25%, respectively).

Access to leadership positions

A substantial proportion of respondents reported having held leadership roles. The highest proportions were observed in the Dominican Republic (74%), Bolivia (72%), and Peru (72%). Paraguay and Chile showed similar rates (71% and 70%, respectively), while Mexico and Argentina reported slightly lower figs. (62% and 63%).

Impact of motherhood on professional careers

Motherhood influenced professional trajectories in several countries. In Brazil, 65.7% of respondents reported reducing their working hours after becoming mothers, followed by Peru (63.1%), Chile (50%), and Bolivia (35%). Reported experiences of maternity-related discrimination were highest in Brazil and the Dominican Republic (38%), while in Mexico, Argentina, and Colombia the reported rates ranged between 31% and 35%.

Perception of salary equity

Most respondents in Brazil, Bolivia, Chile, Peru, Paraguay, and Uruguay (approximately 60%) reported perceiving salary equity with male colleagues. Slightly lower proportions were observed in Mexico and Argentina (55%), while Colombia and the Dominican Republic reported values close to 58%.

Discussion

This multinational survey provides insight into the self-reported professional experiences of women oncologists across Latin America, highlighting perceived challenges related to leadership opportunities, career progression, salary equity, and the impact of motherhood on professional development. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the professional environment in oncology and the factors that may influence gender equity within the field in the region. To our knowledge, few studies have examined these issues from a multinational perspective within Latin American oncology communities.

Several important patterns emerge from the results. A large proportion of respondents reported working primarily in the public sector (65.7%), which may reflect a search for job stability or structural barriers affecting access to private sector opportunities. In addition, only 20.8% reported dedicating more than half of their professional time to academic activities, suggesting potential challenges in balancing clinical practice with academic development.

Perceived gender inequities remain an important concern. Seventy-seven percent of respondents reported feeling that they must work harder than their male colleagues to obtain comparable professional recognition. Although 60.3% perceived salary equity with male counterparts in similar roles, a considerable proportion still reported perceived disparities in professional opportunities.

Motherhood appears to influence career trajectories for many respondents. Approximately 51% reported reducing their working hours after becoming mothers, and 60.9% perceived that motherhood negatively affected their professional development. At the same time, 71% did not report experiencing direct discrimination when applying for leadership roles, suggesting that barriers may operate through more subtle structural mechanisms rather than explicit exclusion.

Mental health concerns were also widely reported, with 67.7% of respondents indicating work-related stress or burnout symptoms. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting higher levels of burnout among physicians working in high-demand specialties such as oncology. Professional workload, administrative responsibilities, and challenges related to work–life balance may contribute to these outcomes, particularly among women physicians managing both professional and family responsibilities. [14]

Leadership representation remains another relevant issue. A majority of respondents (70.3%) indicated that greater female representation in leadership roles is needed, and 84.9% expressed a preference for female leadership when professional qualifications are equivalent. Nevertheless, 26.3% reported experiencing gender-related barriers when applying for leadership positions.

These findings are consistent with previous international research indicating that female physicians frequently encounter structural barriers limiting access to positions of leadership and influence within healthcare systems [7]. Salary disparities in medicine have also been widely documented and continue to disproportionately affect women [15]. Globally, female physicians earn less than their male counterparts even after adjusting for specialty, experience, and workload. Read et al. reported that women physicians earn approximately 80% of male physicians' salaries, with differences reaching up to $50,000 annually despite comparable professional profiles [16].

Disparities in leadership opportunities have also been described. Women are often more hesitant to apply for leadership roles, frequently feeling the need to meet all requirements before applying, whereas men may apply despite not meeting all criteria. This dynamic highlights the importance of initiatives that strengthen leadership training and professional confidence among female physicians [17], [18].

Specialty-specific dynamics may also influence gender representation. For example, analyses of female representation in radiation oncology in the United States have shown only modest growth in recent years, with increases of approximately 0.4% between 2016 and 2020. Demanding training pathways and limited mentorship opportunities have been suggested as contributing factors [19], [20].

Mentorship and professional development programs have demonstrated promising results in addressing gender disparities. Initiatives such as the Women's Empowerment and Leadership Initiative (WELI) have been associated with increased confidence and greater willingness among participants to pursue leadership positions [20], [21]Other studies suggest that mentorship networks and leadership training, including the participation of male allies, can significantly support professional advancement and diversity within medical specialties. [22], [23], [24].

Burnout represents another important challenge in medicine and appears to disproportionately affect women. Female physicians report higher levels of burnout than their male counterparts, often related to heavy workloads and difficulties in balancing professional and family responsibilities [25]. Institutional policies supporting work-life balance, mental health resources, parental leave, and childcare may therefore play an important role in addressing these challenges [26].

Despite these obstacles, women continue to make significant progress across multiple medical specialties. For example, women in neurosurgery have increasingly contributed to the development of the specialty and serve as important role models for younger generations of physicians [27]. Nevertheless, structural barriers continue to limit women's participation in leadership and advanced academic roles within oncology [28]. Support networks, mentoring initiatives, and institutional strategies promoting equity remain essential for strengthening female leadership in medicine [29], [30].

Regional differences may also influence professional experiences. Analyses focusing on Latin America suggest that career development for many oncology professionals may reach a plateau due to the combined impact of motherhood and gender-related factors. Women in oncology specialties continue to face limited leadership opportunities, scarce mentoring, work overload, wage inequity, and persistent challenges in balancing family and professional responsibilities. Strengthening mentoring networks, leadership development programs, and institutional policies promoting gender equity will therefore be essential to advancing gender equality in oncology across the region [30].

Recommendations

The analysis of cross-tabulations revealed differences in leadership opportunities, professional authority, and experiences related to workplace and maternity-related challenges across countries and oncology specialties. These findings were discussed with representatives from 18 countries during the 3rd Women in Oncology Forum held in Bolivia in March 2024 in the context of International Women's Day. Based on these discussions, several strategies to promote gender equity in oncology were identified.

Gender equity policies

The findings suggest the importance of institutional and policy-level strategies aimed at promoting gender equity in oncology. Potential initiatives include improving salary transparency, addressing wage gaps, and supporting women's access to leadership positions through mentoring and professional development programs.

Institutional support for motherhood

Institutions may consider policies that facilitate work–life balance, including flexible schedules, childcare support, and parental leave policies that allow reintegration into professional activities without negative career consequences.

Mental and emotional well-being

Given the high levels of reported work-related stress, institutional programs promoting mental wellbeing, psychological support, and healthier work environments may contribute to improving professional sustainability.

Leadership training

Leadership development initiatives, mentoring networks, and professional support structures may help increase women's participation in leadership and decision-making roles within oncology institutions.

Conclusions

This study provides one of the first multinational assessments of the professional experiences of women oncologists across Latin America. The findings highlight persistent perceived barriers related to leadership opportunities, career progression, and the impact of motherhood, while also reflecting the growing presence and influence of women in oncology across the region.

By capturing perspectives from professionals in 18 countries, this study establishes an important regional baseline for understanding gender-related dynamics in oncology. Advancing gender equity and leadership opportunities for women physicians will be essential for strengthening more inclusive and resilient oncology systems across Latin America.

Data protection

No identifiable personal information (such as names, email addresses, or IP addresses) was collected as part of the survey. Responses were recorded anonymously and analyzed in aggregate form to ensure participant confidentiality.

The questionnaire was distributed through professional networks and participation was open; therefore, duplicate participation could not be completely excluded. However, the survey targeted oncology professionals and relied on professional self-identification within the questionnaire.

Survey data were stored in a secure, password-protected electronic database accessible only to the study investigators. The dataset will be retained for research verification purposes for a limited period and may be deleted upon request from the corresponding author once the study is completed and published.

Informed consent

Before accessing the questionnaire, participants were presented with an introductory information screen describing the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the anonymous handling of responses. The survey targeted oncology professionals in Latin America and did not collect identifiable personal data.

The questionnaire was disseminated through professional networks, including oncology WhatsApp groups, social media channels, and professional mailing lists, and remained open for approximately six months. Participation was entirely voluntary, and no incentives were provided.

Participants were informed that they could choose not to participate or discontinue the survey at any time without any consequences. Proceeding to the questionnaire was considered as provision of electronic informed consent.

Ethics approval

This study consisted of an anonymous, voluntary online survey targeting oncology professionals across Latin America. The survey did not involve patients, clinical interventions, or the collection of identifiable personal data.

The questionnaire and study objectives were reviewed internally by the leadership and scientific coordination of the Latin American Association of Women in Oncology (ALAMO) prior to distribution.

Given the minimal-risk nature of the study and the anonymous collection of professional opinions, formal institutional review board approval was not deemed necessary according to commonly accepted standards for survey-based research involving healthcare professionals.
