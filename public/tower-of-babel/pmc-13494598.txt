Εndogenous metabolomic responses in circadian disrupted night shift working populations: a scoping review
Authors: Behzad Heibati, Paige Lacy, Konstantinos C Makris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNight shift work (NSW) has been known for its linkage with circadian disruption, while the NSW carcinogenic potential is well documented. However, there has been little research on the NSW-associated biological pathways and small molecules that may be implicated with the early stages of carcinogenicity. Metabolomics platforms are novel exposomics tools that act as intermediate biological layers of information linking NSW schedules with the downstream endogenous response in humans. To this extent, we conducted a scoping review to answer the question: how does NSW impact the endogenous human metabolome using metabolomics tools? Thus, this work critically reviewed human studies on the association between NSW and the associated endogenous biological response. Online databases were screened, and 13 original publications were included in the analysis; eight studies were conducted in occupational settings, and the remaining studies were well-controlled laboratory experiments simulating NSW. This work summarized the consistent reporting of the most frequently reported metabolites and associated biological pathways enriched in actual NSW, or in simulated NSW conditions of occupational and experimental laboratory studies, respectively. Further research is warranted to better understand the complex metabolic changes associated with NSW, informing strategies to mitigate early-stage carcinogenic effects, ultimately improving health and well-being of shift workers.Keywords: Night shift work, Metabolomics, Exposome, Occupational settings, Metabolic pathways

Introduction

Night shift work (NSW) is common in modern society and is characterized by work schedules that occur during the usual biological night, leading to disruption of the endogenous circadian system.1 Such schedules, including those involving overnight or transmeridian work, alter normal exposure to the environmental light–dark cycle and can result in circadian misalignment. Growing evidence links this disruption to adverse health effects, including an elevated risk of cancer.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Given that approximately 20% to 30% of the workforce in industrialized nations are engaged in non-standard working schedules,7 understanding the biological factors contributing to carcinogenesis is crucial; however, biological mechanisms underlying the stimulated endogenous response and downstream biological processes due to external stressors, such as NSW, are not yet fully understood.

The mechanistic evidence of NSW carcinogenicity in humans mostly come from consistent effects on circadian disruption, such as reduced melatonin production and estrogen levels (in females only) (IARC, 2020). While some theories suggest that psychosocial stress resulting from disruptions to social rhythms may play a role,8 there are also indications that disturbances to the normal sleep-wake cycle (circadian rhythm) following NSW would be implicated with neuroendocrine and cardiometabolic stress, as well as with disrupted sleep patterns.9 Such disruptions may alter immune functioning and induce cellular stress.10,11 Consistent mechanistic evidence of immunosuppression, chronic inflammation, and cell proliferation, including suggestive evidence of alterations in estrogen homeostasis led to the overall classification of NSW as being probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A).1 Mechanistic evidence comes primarily from animal studies, while evidence from epidemiological or intervention studies remains limited.12 Controlled experimental studies of circadian disruption/misalignment and sleep deprivation, including simulated night shift protocols, forced desynchrony designs, and acute or chronic sleep restriction experiments have consistently reported alterations in metabolic pathways related to glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, amino acid metabolism, and oxidative stress.13, 14, 15

Omics platforms, such as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics may be particularly attractive tools towards deciphering mechanistic evidence of NSW effects on cancer. Among these -omics platforms, metabolomics appears as exceptionally attractive for its ability to provide a detailed snapshot of cellular processes in the body,16 as a dynamic response to NSW schedules and types. Metabolomics platforms would be best to study circadian desynchronization phenomena in humans adhering to NSW systematic schedules. Metabolic alterations can reflect disruptions in circadian-regulated pathways involved in energy metabolism, lipid signaling, redox balance, inflammation, and one-carbon metabolism - processes that are central to genomic stability, immune surveillance and hormone regulation, all implicated in carcinogenesis disease process.17,18 Pathway enrichment provides a higher-level, systems biology perspective, helping researchers to move from lists of altered metabolites to a more integrated understanding of which metabolic processes are disrupted and how these might relate to disease mechanisms or environmental exposures.19

Eurhythmic light-dark alterations and their influence on the central clock functioning and its cross talk with peripheral clock systems, such as the liver clock may be perturbed by NSW.1 The most recent International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) monograph on NSW1 did not report on the use of metabolomics platforms, but focused instead on the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) characteristics of select metabolites (e.g., glucose-6-phosphate) or circadian disruption markers, such as disruption in melatonin secretion in experimental studies. Building on the wealth of NSW studies reported in the corresponding monograph (IARC, 2020), a more recent scoping review mapped research in the field of NSW and its association with oxidative stress/damage and inflammation processes; NSW was overall associated with select biomarkers of oxidative stress/damage and tissue chronic inflammation, albeit with mixed results.4

To our knowledge, there is no comprehensive mapping of the potentially carcinogenic NSW effects on the endogenous biological response of night shift workers by unraveling the biodynamics of the targeted/untargeted human metabolome profile. Towards this goal, we conducted a scoping review to (i) map the global literature of human metabolomics studies of NSW in human studies; (ii) describe the endogenous response associated with NSW in human studies deploying metabolomics platforms; and (iii) describe the characteristics of metabolomics platforms used to generate signatures of the human metabolome, including data processing, feature annotation, and metabolite identification stages.

Methods

This scoping review protocol was drafted using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis Protocols extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR).20 We formulated the following specific research question: "How does NSW impact the endogenous human metabolome using metabolomics tools?" according to the ''Participants", ''Exposure", ''Comparator", and ''Outcomes" (PECO) framework. The Population (P) included adult workers exposed to different work schedules. The Exposure (E) was defined as NSW or rotating shift schedules associated with circadian disruption. The Comparator (C) consisted of workers with standard daytime schedules or those not exposed to shift work. The Outcomes (O) focused on metabolomic alterations and circadian disruptions, including changes in metabolite concentrations, or disrupted metabolic pathways identified using bioanalytical platforms, such as mass spectrometry (MS) or nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The main database searched was PubMed from January 2000 until January 7, 2026, using the MeSH terms: [metabolomics] AND [night shift]. Studies involving humans of all ages were included, and only studies in English were assessed. The search strategy was prepared by the research team and further refined through team discussion. The following information was recorded from each study: study design, sample size, shift work features, sleep deprivation characteristics, sampling time, biospecimen type, features/metabolites, main outcomes, and statistically significant metabolites. Enrichment and pathway analyses were also recorded to gain insights into the underlying biological mechanisms.

Studies were excluded if they were non-original articles (e.g., reviews, editorials), non-human studies, or did not involve metabolomics as an analytical platform. Applying these criteria, a total of 35 studies were initially identified, of which 22 were excluded. The remaining 13 studies met the inclusion criteria, and they were included in the final analysis. Selected references were imported into Covidence, an electronic platform for systematic reviews and meta-analysis.21 To increase consistency, two reviewers (BH and KCM) screened the selected publications, discussed the results, and amended the screening and data extraction procedure before beginning screening titles and abstracts and then full text. Two reviewers (BH and KCM) independently extracted data from each eligible article in Excel to populate fields in Table 1. Disagreements on data extraction or classification were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers.

Results

Study selection

We identified 35 human studies of NSW that applied metabolomics platforms for full text review (Fig. 1). Of those, 13 studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria, and they were subject to data extraction after removal of duplicates and exclusion of ineligible hits (Fig. 1). The most common reason for exclusion was not meeting the criteria of applying metabolomics platforms (n = 12), non-original articles (n = 2) or non-human studies (n = 8). We grouped the studies by setting (occupational or simulated NSW), and summarized the type of settings, populations, study designs for each setting group, the NSW type and characteristics, along with the metabolomics platforms, the related metabolites and enriched biological pathways (Table 1). Out of these 13 articles, eight took place in an occupational setting, while the rest five studies were conducted under simulated NSW conditions in laboratory settings or in a cohort setting (Table 1).

Flowchart of article selection process in this scoping review on the application of metabolomics platforms in human studies of night shift work.

Experimental studies of simulated NSW

The studies consistently identified several key metabolites and pathways disrupted by simulated NSW (Table 2). Common findings included alterations in glycerophospholipid metabolism, with altered metabolites such as phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LysoPE), lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC), and choline being notably affected. Changes in amino acid metabolism, particularly for arginine and proline were frequently observed. Additionally, disruptions in rhythmic metabolites associated with food metabolism (e.g., glucose metabolism/ insulin regulation, lipid /glycerophospholipid metabolism, amino acids and other pathways) were highlighted, along with changes in glycolysis, ketone metabolism, and redox metabolism pathways. These consistent disruptions across simulated NSW studies are associated with changes in lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and circadian rhythm.

For example, Cogswell et al. conducted a cross-sectional, in-laboratory, study simulating NSW in 16 healthy young adults (mean age: 22.4 years).22 Participants were initially acclimatized under laboratory conditions (9 h sleep opportunity/night), followed by a randomized cross-over protocol with 9-h adequate sleep and 5 h insufficient sleep conditions, each lasting 5 days. The main study outcomes were the dim light melatonin onset (DLMO) or offset (DLMOff). The study explored different sleep conditions (adequate and insufficient sleep) and varying food intake conditions, as well as light exposure. Blood samples were collected every 2 h during both measurement periods under similar conditions of minimal activity, constant posture, hourly snacks and exposure to dim light. A total of 4209 compounds were derived from 26,301 metabolomics features; main metabolites included those related to glycerophospholipid metabolism, such as choline, PE, PC, LysoPE, and LysoPC. They identified glycerophospholipid metabolism (KEGG pathway entry hsa00564) as the only significant (FDR corrected P = 0.018) pathway associated with the annotated compounds in the DLMOff biomarker model. There were five KEGG metabolite hits (PE, PC, LysoPE, LysoPC, and Choline) annotated within the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway. These five KEGG metabolite hits corresponded to 19 annotated HMDB compound IDs from the DLMOff biomarker model consisting of choline, 2 PEs, 9 PCs, 3 LysoPEs, and 4 LysoPCs.

Similarly, Kervezee et al. performed a study in nine healthy young adults (8 males, 1 female) to investigate metabolic responses under simulated NSW conditions.23 Participants underwent sleep deprivation during the night portion of their circadian cycle, by engaging into a four day long simulated night shift protocol, in which the habitual sleep-wake cycle was delayed by 10 h. Targeted metabolomic profiling on plasma samples was done every 2 h at baseline day and then at the end of NSW simulated 24-h period. Most rhythmic metabolites during simulated NSW were out of synchrony with the body’s internal clock, indicating unique metabolic signatures of circadian misalignment. A significant (P < 0.0001) overlap was detected between the metabolites that were identified as significantly rhythmic at baseline and during NSW with 32 metabolites being classified as rhythmic in both conditions. The 19 metabolites that were rhythmic at baseline, but not during the night shift condition predominantly consisted of lipids (6 lysophospholipids, 3 phosphatidylcholines, and 1 sphingolipid). Using linear mixed-effects models, they identified 27 metabolites with significantly different levels during night shift compared with baseline: levels were significantly increased for 7 metabolites (4 lysophospholipids, hippuric acid, spermidine, and threonine) and significantly decreased for 20 metabolites [6 acylcarnitines, 4 organic acids, 3 amino acids, 3 biogenic amines, 2 sphingolipids, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) and choline], with the direction of change being consistent across subjects.

In the study by Chen et al., 14 males and 18 females were subject to a short-term (24 h) sleep deprivation scheme and donated fasting blood samples prior to (day 1) and following (days 2 and 3) the treatment.24 They examined the impact of short-term sleep deprivation over 3 days on metabolites related to central carbon metabolism, lipid metabolism, and immune function. Sleep deprivation caused marked molecular changes (46.4% transcript genes, 59.3% proteins, and 55.6% metabolites) that were partially reversed by day 3. The immune system, in particular neutrophil-mediated processes associated with plasma superoxidase dismutase-1 and S100A8 gene expression, was markedly affected. Sleep deprivation decreased melatonin levels and increased immune cells (leukocytes, neutrophils, monocytes), inflammatory factors, and C-reactive protein. Metabolites affected by the NSW schedule included orotic acid and TCA-cycle pyruvate, with enriched pathways such as central carbon metabolism in cancer and pyruvate metabolism, as well as arginine and proline metabolism.

Skeene et al. performed a 7 days in-laboratory experiment under controlled conditions (constant ambient temperature of 21 °C, constant light <50 lx during scheduled wakefulness).25 The study consisted of a baseline 24 h period, followed by assignment to either 3 days simulated day-shift schedule (sleep opportunity: 22:00–06:00) or, after a transition nap (sleep opportunity: 14:00–18:00), to a 3-days simulated NSW schedule (sleep opportunity: 10:00–18:00). During the simulated NSW periods, breakfast, lunch, and dinner were provided at 1.5, 7, and 13.5 h of scheduled wakefulness, respectively. The 3 days simulated shift schedule was followed by a 24 h constant routine protocol, which involved controlled environmental conditions with fixed semi recumbent posture, hourly identical snacks, and sustained wakefulness to investigate rhythms free of exogenous factors. The study concluded with a recovery day. During the 24 h constant routine, blood samples were taken at intervals of 1–3 h. The samples were used for targeted LC/MS metabolomics and cortisol and PER3 assays. Blood samples were taken hourly during the baseline day (18:30–21:30) and during the constant routine (18:30–01:30) for melatonin assays. In the 7-day long study, rhythms in certain metabolites, including glycerophospholipids and LysoPCs, and amino acids such as ornithine, arginine, isoleucine, and proline, including acylcarnitines were related to food metabolism that followed sleep/wake and feeding/fasting cycles, rather than patterns of the central pacemaker.

McDermott et al. examined mechanisms linking circadian misalignment (simulated NSW) to metabolic risk by profiling peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy adults under a tightly controlled laboratory protocol.26 Young adults (n = 14, range of 22–34 years) were assigned to a 3-day simulated day shift or a 3-day simulated night shift treatment; immediately afterwards, participants underwent a 24-hour constant routine (awake, dim light, fixed posture, evenly distributed intake) with serial blood sampling. The team performed shotgun/ liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) proteomics on PBMCs (analyzed subset n = 6 per condition) and integrated these data with previously collected targeted plasma metabolomics from the same individuals/time points using a network approach (GENIE3).

The integrated metabolomics–proteomics analysis demonstrated circadian rhythm alterations after simulated night shift work, particularly affecting metabolites and pathways involved in glucose metabolism, insulin regulation, and inflammation.26 Circulating glucose rhythms displayed an approximately 8.5-hour phase shift after night shift, while insulin showed altered rhythmicity during NSW. Glycine was identified as a key metabolite associated with inflammatory and insulin-regulatory network signatures. They quantified ∼1388 proteins and showed that circadian misalignment profoundly altered the temporal coordination between metabolic and immune pathways, supporting a model in which internal desynchrony develops between central circadian regulation of insulin secretion and peripheral regulation of insulin sensitivity and inflammatory processes.26

Occupational NSW studies

These studies consistently identified several key metabolites and pathways disrupted by NSW (Table 2). Alterations in fatty acid metabolism, particularly involving medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines, and changes in amino acid metabolism (arginine and glutamine levels) were observed, being suggestive of oxidative stress and tissue damage mechanisms (Table 2). Inflammatory markers, like glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) were also noted, along with disruptions in organic acids (alpha-aminoadipic acid, butyric acid, succinic acid, and p-hydroxyhippuric acid) and acylcarnitines. These consistent disruptions across studies highlight significant NSW impacts on endogenous response, affecting oxidative stress, lipid processing, energy metabolism, and systemic inflammatory responses.

For instance, Harding et al. studied the influence of NSW-rotating car factory shift workers, collecting 196 plasma samples from 49 males, both at the onset and end of night shifts (22:00–06:00) and day shifts (06:00–14:00).27 Participants followed a five-day work week from Monday to Friday, followed by two days off on Saturday and Sunday. Blood samples from the participants on two separate days were collected. Sample collection occurred during an early morning shift in either the 2nd or 3rd week of the early morning shift rotation. On alternate days, the sample collection took place during a night shift, again in the 2nd or 3rd week of the night shift rotation. Each participant had venipuncture blood samples taken at the beginning and end of each work shift on both sample collection days, resulting in a total of up to four blood samples per participant. Night shift work was associated with changes in concentrations of arginine (geometric mean ratio [GMR] 2.30, 95% CI: 1.25–4.23), glutamine (GMR 2.22, 95% CI: 1.53–3.24), kynurenine (GMR 3.22, 95% CI: 1.05–9.87), lysophosphatidylcholine 18:2 (LysoPC18:2) (GMR 1.86, 95% CI: 1.11–3.11), lysophosphatidylcholine 20:3 (LysoPC20:3) (GMR 2.48, 95% CI: 1.05–5.83), phosphatidylcholine diacyl 34:2 (PCaa34:2) (GMR 2.27, 95% CI: 1.16–4.44), and phosphatidylcholine acyl-alkyl 38:5 (PCae38:5) (GMR 1.66, 95% CI: 1.02–2.68). Key pathways affected by NSW included amino acid metabolism, specifically alterations in arginine and glutamine levels; additional disruptions were noted in kynurenine pathways, suggesting changes in stress response mechanisms. Lipid metabolism was also affected, as indicated by changes in LysoPC and PC lipid concentrations, which would impact cell membrane composition and signaling.

Another NSW study on nurses had 26 of them with no NSW schedule, 43 on NSW, and 22 with former NSW.28 Blood samples were drawn in the morning, at the end of the night shift for night shifters (7:15–7:45 a.m.) and at the beginning of the working day for day shifters (8:30–9:00 a.m.). Authors measured 188 metabolites, including amino acids, biogenic amines, acylcarnitines, sphingolipids, and glycerophospholipids, utilizing LC-MS/MS and FIA-MS/MS. Lower levels of 21 glycerophospholipids and 6 sphingolipids were observed when comparing current vs. never NSW nurses; however, higher levels of serotonin (+171%, 95% CI: 49%–393%), aspartic acid (+156%, 95% CI: 40%–365%), and taurine (+182%, 95% CI: 68%–375%) were observed in current NSW vs. never NSW nurses. Years worked in night shifts did not seem to affect metabolite levels. A random-forest algorithm confirmed taurine and aspartic acid among the most important variables in discriminating current NSW vs. never NSW. Authors identified fatty acid metabolism and glycerophospholipid metabolism as affected by NSW schedules, highlighting the significant alterations in lipid processing and energy metabolism due to the irregular work schedules.

Rotter et al. recruited 100 shift and non-shift female workers, including 68 nurses working both day and night shifts for up to five consecutive days and collected 3640 urine samples. A total of 424 first morning urine samples were analyzed using a targeted metabolomics approach. The shift work group (n = 75 nurses) included nurses working both day and night shifts. To account for potential hormonal changes over time (such as the menstrual cycle), a four-week pause was scheduled between day and night shift assessments. The non-shift working group (n = 25 females) consisted of nurses working exclusively during the day shift. During day shifts, nurses in both groups worked up to four consecutive days (Monday–Thursday), while night shifts extended up to five consecutive days (Monday–Friday). For day shift nurses, the core working hours were from 6 a.m. to 2 pm. (shift working group) and 8 a.m. to 4 pm. (non-shift working group), while the night shift spanned from 9 pm. to 6 a.m. In this study, 31 metabolites were significantly (false discovery rate < 0.05) associated with NSW in nurses.29 One metabolite (acylcarnitine C10:2) significantly decreased in night shift with all three creatinine normalization methods. Furthermore, 11 and 4 metabolites significantly associated with NSW in early and late chronotype classes were observed, respectively. Increased levels of medium- and long chain acylcarnitines indicate a strong impairment of fatty acid oxidation. The enriched biological pathways associated with NSW were primarily focused on disturbances in fatty acid oxidation due to perturbed fatty acid metabolism. Specifically, the disruption was highlighted by increased levels of medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines.

Bizzarri et al. examined a cohort of workers (n = 2020) from the Netherlands Lifelines cohort, comprising 1010 non-shift workers and 1010 NSW workers. Night shift workers were defined as individuals who worked at least 3 h between 00:00 and 05:00, either in a rotating or permanent schedule, and logged > 12 h per week. Non-shift workers, on the other hand, were participants who never engaged in shift work (i.e., no early morning, evening, or night shifts) and maintained regular work hours during the three months preceding the blood draw (starting work after 06:00 and finishing before 21:00). Authors observed changes in several metabolic markers in male NSW compared with non-shift workers, but no such changes in females.30 The markers were positively associated with the largest effect sizes in male night shift workers included glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA, metabolite ratio = 0.219, P = 2.3 × 10−4) and triglycerides to total lipids ratio in small VLDL (estimate = 0.208, P = 5.1 × 10−4). The largest negative effect sizes in male night shift workers included cholesteryl esters to total lipids ratio in small VLDL (metabolite ratio = −0.22, P = 2.2 × 10−4) and ‘cholesterol to total lipids ratio in small VLDL’ (metabolite ratio = −0.219, P = 2.5 × 10−4). Glycoprotein acetyls which are markers of low-grade chronic inflammation were associated with NSW. Significant changes were also observed in the metabolism of triglycerides and cholesterol esters within various sizes of VLDL particles.

The impact of NSW on the 24 h urinary metabolome of police officers undergoing rotating NSW (n = 11) was also studied.31 Participants underwent two 24 h laboratory visits, conducted before and after working seven consecutive nights. Participants provided urine samples before and after sleep periods and every 2–3 h during wake periods. When needed, participants could provide additional urine samples outside these scheduled time points. The authors measured 79 metabolites, including 20 acylcarnitines, 22 amino acids, 17 biogenic amines, and 17 organic acids, as well as glucose, choline, and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO).31 They found that 43 metabolites (54%) were classified as rhythmic during the day schedule, compared with 26 (33%) during the night schedule, indicating a significant reduction in the number of rhythmic metabolites. Acylcarnitines showed a slight variation in the number of rhythmic metabolites between day and night (P = 0.157). In contrast, amino acids, biogenic amines, and organic acids showed a significant difference in rhythmicity. Both amino acids and biogenic amines showed a significantly lower number of rhythmic metabolites during the night compared to the day (P < 0.0001). Similarly, organic acids demonstrate a significant variation, with fewer rhythmic metabolites during the night (P = 0.006). Other pathways affected by NSW were those involving alterations in organic acids and acylcarnitines.

Heibati et al. investigated the potential for NSW chrono-metabolism of benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene and xylenes (BTEX) and associated leukemogenic effects among gasoline-station workers in Plovdiv, Bulgaria (n = 71).32 Each participant followed both a 12-hour day-shift schedule and a 12-hour night-shift work schedule and provided four time-stamped urine samples: (S1) baseline morning urine (∼07:00) on the first day shift after two days off; (S2) end-of-day-shift urine (∼19:00) after the second consecutive day shift; (S3) start-of-night-shift urine (∼19:00) after a two-day off-work period; and (S4) end-of-night-shift urine (∼07:00) after two consecutive night shifts. In parallel, personal BTEX exposures were measured over 12 h shifts using wearable passive samplers, while urinary BTEX (parent compounds) and metabolites (mandelic acid and trans, trans-muconic acid, t,t-MA) were quantified using mass spectrometry. To capture downstream biological responses, the authors measured urinary 8‑hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and profiled the urinary metabolome using untargeted GC–MS. Despite similar personal airborne benzene levels across shift types (median ∼3.05 µg/m³ for day shifts vs ∼2.92 µg/m³ for night shifts), NSW was associated with consistently lower end-of-shift urinary concentrations of several parent BTEX compounds (notably creatinine-adjusted urinary benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and both xylene metrics), while urinary t,t-MA showed a positive association with NSW, suggesting chrono-modulated differences in benzene metabolism across work schedules. Metabolomics analyses identified a small set of metabolites associated with NSW and a larger set associated with 8-OHdG; notably, 4-hydroxybenzeneacetic acid was significantly associated with both NSW and 8-OHdG, supporting a potential “meet-in-the-middle” signature linking shift schedule to oxidative stress biology. Pathway analyses highlighted glycine/serine/threonine metabolism (along with other pathways such as ascorbate/aldarate metabolism) as among the most impacted pathways, consistent with perturbations in one-carbon/antioxidant and related metabolic processes.

Ungur et al. investigated whether blood metabolomic signatures can discriminate burnout severity and NSW–related stress among healthcare professionals in Romania.33 In this retrospective occupational study, they enrolled 97 medical professionals (physicians and nurses) from hospitals in the Transylvania region, who completed the Maslach Burnout Inventory–Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS) and were classified as high vs low burnout across the three MBI domains, emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalization (DP), and personal accomplishment (PA), and also by day vs night work. Blood samples were analyzed using UHPLC-QTOF-ESI+-MS with untargeted and semi-targeted metabolomics. Across the blood metabolome (the paper reports identification/selection of ∼99 metabolites for downstream statistical analysis), burnout-linked biology was dominated by (i) steroid/hormone metabolism (cortisol/cortisone and related steroids), (ii) energy/lipid transport pathways, including acylcarnitines, and (iii) neurotransmitter-related pathways (catecholamines) and tryptophan-derived metabolites, consistent with an integrated stress–circadian disruption signal that differs by work schedule and burnout strata.

In a companion analysis on the same study population, Ungur et al. evaluated whether urine metabolomic patterns would discriminate burnout levels and NSW effects among healthcare professionals.34 They administered the MBI-HSS instrument to 64 physicians and nurses (surveyed Sept–Oct 2023) and classified participants by day work (n = 25) vs night work (n = 39) and high vs low burnout (with “middle + high” grouped as high). Urine was collected the morning after interview (∼6 a.m., before breakfast), preserved and profiled by UHPLC-QTOF-ESI+-MS using untargeted + semi-targeted metabolomics profiling. After feature filtering/alignment, the authors report 79 identified metabolites used for multivariate regression modeling plus ROC/AUC biomarker ranking and pathway enrichment/network analysis. Key findings emphasized (a) night vs day differences e.g., higher melatonin (and related metabolites), cortisone, leucyl-threonine, retinyl linoleate and lower noradrenaline/adrenaline and some acylcarnitines in night shift work. While melatonin is typically suppressed in night shift workers elevated melatonin observed in this study may be related to delayed/shifted melatonin release.35 The pathway interpretation again converged on steroid metabolism, acylcarnitine/energetic metabolism, and catecholamine plus tryptophan (serotonin/melatonin) metabolism as major axes linked to NSW and burnout.

Discussion

This scoping review allows for the better understanding of the biological perturbations in human metabolome signatures upon systematic exposures to NSW schedules in occupational health studies and in well-controlled lab experiments that simulate NSW. Circadian disruption has been implicated with the carcinogenic effects of NSW using circadian markers, like melatonin and/or cortisol,1 yet the biological processes and mechanisms governing the association between NSW and cancer in humans remains to be elucidated. This scoping review revealed important insights into metabolic changes associated with NSW that may contribute to cancer-related biological pathways, based on studies conducted in both occupational and simulated NSW experimental studies (Fig. 2). Most occupational health studies did not apply an enrichment/pathway analysis using results from their metabolomics platform. Only two such studies reported enrichment analyses, identifying tryptophan, glutathione, glycine, and serine metabolism as affected by NSW, including changes in protein biosynthesis, immune regulation27 or changes in cellular energy metabolism and regulation (e.g., changes in cell membrane carrier OCTN2).29 All simulated NSW experimental studies conducted enrichment/pathway analyses along with their metabolomics platforms. In effect, simulated NSW experimental studies highlighted the prevalence of pathways, such as glycerophospholipid/lysophospholipid metabolism, including arginine-proline metabolism.22, 23, 24, 25,32, 33, 34

Night shift work studies in humans and metabolomic evidence of carcinogenesis: altered metabolic pathways.

These pathways indicate significant adaptations in lipid management, protein synthesis, and nitrogen processing, demonstrating how the body adjusts to the metabolic challenges posed by NSW. Blood and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were the only human biospecimen matrices used to perform metabolomics profiling in the simulated NSW experimental studies, while either urine or blood samples were used in occupational studies. Liquid chromatography coupled with MS was the main bioanalytical instrumentation used to generate metabolomics datasets, plus one study using NMR. Interestingly, all occupational health studies used various types of targeted metabolomics platforms, while some simulated NSW experimental studies reported the application of untargeted metabolomics platforms.

In the occupational health studies, the most frequently associated metabolites with NSW were those belonging to glycerophospholipids, acylcarnitines, and amino acids, like taurine (Table 2).22, 23, 24, 25,32, 33, 34 In simulated NSW experimental studies involving simulated NSW, the most frequently associated metabolites with NSW were amino acids, acylcarnitines, and sphingolipids (Table 2). These studies used targeted metabolomics to explore how simulated NSW influences lipid metabolism, energy production, and stress responses. Furthermore, the studies highlighted early biological changes associated with NSW, including alterations in glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, serotonin, aspartic acid, and taurine.28 These changes could have implications for cellular energy metabolism and regulation, potentially leading to metabolic disorders. It appears that NSW-associated circadian disruption in central and peripheral clocks manifests itself in distinct physiological and cellular perturbations that in addition to disrupting melatonin production and metabolism, other metabolic pathways are affected too, potentially leading to chronic oxidative tissue damage and chronic inflammation (Fig. 2).

Simulated NSW experimental studies (disrupting sleep patterns for different number of hours or repeated patterns) affected circadian rhythm markers, such as the DLMO or DLMOoff,22 or disrupted the circadian rhythmicity of select metabolites23,25 as part of their distinct metabolomics signatures. Most frequently reported metabolites associated with changes in circadian rhythm and/or endogenous response because of simulated NSW were choline, glycerophospholipids, amino acids and acylcarnitines.22, 23, 24, 25,32, 33, 34 NSW affected the oxidative system due to altered taurine levels, but lipid metabolism was the predominant metabolic alteration observed in these studies. This shift in lipid metabolism appears to be a more consistent and significant finding across both occupational and simulated NSW experimental studies.

Alterations in lipid and amino acid metabolism have been consistently implicated in biological processes relevant to tumor development. Findings largely based on preclinical and mechanistic studies showed that dysregulated lipid metabolism can support increased cellular proliferation through enhanced membrane biosynthesis and altered lipid signaling, while also influencing inflammatory and immune-related pathways.36,37 Similarly, changes in amino acid metabolism may affect tumor-associated processes by modulating energy production, redox balance, and immune cell function. Experimental studies in cancer models have demonstrated that such metabolic reprogramming can facilitate tumor growth, metastatic behavior, and immune evasion, based on cellular and animal model studies.38,39 In the context of NSW, metabolomic alterations observed across studies may therefore reflect circadian disruption-driven metabolic changes that influence pathways associated with carcinogenic risk, rather than direct carcinogenic effects.

Across studies of NSW and simulated circadian disruption, a recurrent pattern emerges in metabolic pathways that are also mechanistically implicated in carcinogenesis. Perturbations in central carbon metabolism, including the TCA cycle and glucose–insulin regulation reflect a shift toward metabolic inflexibility and elevated glucose/insulin–IGF signalling, both linked to mitogenic drive and cancer risk; this aligns with authoritative evaluations that classify NSW as probably carcinogenic and with reviews connecting hyperinsulinaemia/IGF signalling to tumour promotion.1,40 Disruptions in mitochondrial β‑oxidation highlighted by widespread alterations in acylcarnitines across blood and urine, indicated impaired fatty‑acid utilization and mitochondrial stress, conditions known to elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, while promoting DNA damage and oncogenic signaling.41 Night shift‑related changes in lipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism, particularly elevations or remodeling of PCs/lysoPCs and free fatty acids, mirror tumour‑associated lipidomic signatures that support membrane biogenesis, oncoinflammatory signaling, and ferroptosis resistance in cancer cells.42 Altered sphingolipid profiles, such as increased sphingomyelins, point toward dysregulation of the ceramide–sphingosine/S1P axis, a fundamental pathway to apoptosis control and frequently hijacked to favour survival and therapy resistance in cancer.43 Disruptions in amino‑acid and urea‑cycle metabolism, including changes in tryptophan–kynurenine, arginine–proline, and phenylalanine pathways, suggest modulation of immune tolerance (via IDO/TDO→kynurenine/AhR),44 nitric‑oxide signalling, and one‑carbon/biogenic amine metabolism interfaces tightly tied to tumour immune evasion and proliferative capacity.45 Disturbances in glutathione and redox metabolism further reflect altered oxidative‑stress handling, a well‑established feature of tumour progression and treatment resistance.46 Night‑shift perturbations of nucleotide metabolism (e.g., orotate/orotic acid) also intersect with a core, pan‑cancer dependency on augmented nucleotide biosynthesis that fuels proliferation, immune evasion, and resistance.47 In addition, pronounced alterations in steroidogenesis, catecholamine production, and the melatonin–serotonin axis highlights endocrine and circadian disruption, particularly desynchronized cortisol dynamics and suppressed nocturnal melatonin signaling, which are considered key mechanisms linking circadian misalignment to hormone‑dependent cancer risk and broader oncogenic processes.48,49 Taken together, the metabolic signatures observed across these human studies converge on pathways, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid remodeling, immune‑metabolic rewiring, oxidative stress, and hormonal/circadian imbalance, that overlap strongly with recognized hallmarks of cancer biology, supporting the plausibility of NSW as a metabolic and endocrine environment conducive to carcinogenesis; this synthesis is consistent with IARC’s Group 2A classification of night‑shift work and related mechanistic evidence.

This scoping review has some limitations. First, occupational studies predominantly used targeted metabolomics platforms, which focus on predefined metabolites and pathways, potentially missing broader metabolic changes detectable through untargeted approaches. Second, in laboratory-based sleep-deprivation or simulated NSW studies, changes in meal timing and dietary schedules during experimental protocols may have influenced the measured metabolomic profiles, potentially confounding the effects attributed to circadian disruption or sleep loss. While some studies attempted to standardize caloric intake or meal composition, residual dietary effects cannot be fully excluded.

This work carries important implications for the mechanistic understanding of NSW effects on carcinogenicity process. The human metabolome acts as an intermediate biological layer of information between NSW and the health outcome(s) of interest. Exposomics tools, such as metabolomics could shed light on the perturbations of endogenous response upon external NSW stimulation. Equally important to study would be the temporal dynamics of the NSW-induced circadian disruption process. Such exposomic studies would shed light on the mechanistic evidence better informing our understanding of NSW-driven circadian disruption phenomena.

This work advances our understanding of how systematic NSW schedules and their systematic patterns evolving over time may be associated with alterations in the human metabolome, reflecting circadian disruption and metabolic perturbations earlier implicated with carcinogenesis, such as oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, etc. By integrating evidence from occupational and well-controlled laboratory experiments simulating NSW, this review highlighted how circadian misalignment influences the dynamic equilibrium between external stressors (e.g., nutrient intake, work habits, and sleep patterns) and the regulation of various metabolites across different cell systems, tissues, and organs. Experimental studies in laboratory settings have shown NSW-driven dysregulation and disruption of homeostasis in circadian and metabolic systems. These findings reveal how disruptions in the synchronization between biological clocks and NSW schedules, often leading to biological perturbations, such as altered melatonin production and changes in estrogen levels. Such perturbations may influence biological processes implicated with higher cancer risk. The circadian clock maintains a 24 h equilibrium of balancing the diurnally repeating cycle of nutrient intake and expenditure, and its endogenous response. When disruptions in the interfacial synchronization between biological clock-regulated processes and that of NSW diurnal schedule occur, then these would manifest into a perturbed disease phenotypic response.50

Conclusions

With the help of metabolomics platforms, this review mapped important metabolites and cancer-associated biological pathways in NSW studies conducted either in occupational settings or in simulated NSW laboratory settings. Given that NSW is intrinsically linked to chronic circadian disruption, these metabolic alterations may represent key mediators connecting circadian misalignment to cancer risk. This work underscores the need for further research to understand the complex metabolic changes associated with NSW. Such understanding could inform strategies to mitigate the early-stage carcinogenic effects of NSW, ultimately improving health and well-being of shift workers. It also highlights the potential of metabolomics as a powerful tool for studying the carcinogenic pathogenesis of NSW, by providing a comprehensive view of metabolic changes that could serve as biomarkers for early detection and prevention of shift work-related health disorders.
