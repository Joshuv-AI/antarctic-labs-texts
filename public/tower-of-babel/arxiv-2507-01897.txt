Electron-phonon vertex correction effect in superconducting H 3 S
Shashi B. Mishra, Hitoshi Mori, Elena R. Margine

Abstract
The Migdal-Eliashberg (ME) formalism provides a reliable framework for describing phonon-mediated superconductivity in the adiabatic regime, where the electronic Fermi energy exceeds the characteristic phonon energy. In this work, we go beyond this limit by incorporating first-order vertex corrections to the electron-phonon (e-ph) interaction within the Eliashberg formalism and assess their impact on the superconducting properties of H 3 S and Pb using first-principles calculations. For H 3 S, where the adiabatic assumption breaks down, we find that vertex corrections to the e-ph coupling are substantial. When combined with phonon anharmonicity and the energy dependence of the electronic density of states, the predicted critical temperature T c T_{\rm c} is in very good agreement with experimental observations. In contrast, for elemental Pb, where the adiabatic approximation remains valid, vertex corrections have a negligible effect, and the calculated T c T_{\rm c} and superconducting gap closely match the predictions of the standard ME formalism. These findings demonstrate the importance of non-adiabatic corrections in strongly coupled high- T c T_{\rm c} hydrides and establish a robust first-principles framework for accurately predicting superconducting properties across different regimes.

by incorporating the Green’s function approach developed by Migdal for describing the electron-phonon (e-ph) interaction in the normal metallic state

Nevertheless, ME theory relies on Migdal’s theorem

, which assumes that the dynamics of electrons and ions happen on two well-separated energy scales, in accordance with the adiabatic Born-Oppenheimer approximation
Born and Oppenheimer 1927
. Migdal argued that vertex corrections to the bare e-ph scattering diagram can be neglected when the parameter
\lambda(\hbar\omega_{0}/\varepsilon_{\rm F})
is small, where
\lambda
is the effective e-ph coupling constant,
\omega_{0}
is the characteristic phonon frequency, and
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
is the Fermi energy. Specifically, it was shown that each additional term in the perturbative expansion of the e-ph vertex is suppressed by at least a factor of the order
\hbar\omega_{0}/\varepsilon_{\rm F}\sim\sqrt{m/M}\sim 10^{-2}
, where
m
and
M
are the electron and ion mass. The condition
\lambda(\hbar\omega_{0}/\varepsilon_{\rm F})\ll
1 is satisfied in most metallic systems since the adiabatic parameter
\hbar\omega_{0}/\varepsilon_{\rm F}\ll
0, given that the electronic energy scale is of the order of several eV, whereas phonon energies are generally only a fraction of an eV.

;
Kostur, Vladimir N. and Mitrović, Božidar 1993

This perturbative scheme is expected to remain applicable as long as

;

In recent years, there has been growing interest in understanding the impact of higher-order e-ph processes on key materials properties (e.g., spectral functions, band-gap renormalization, polaron formation, carrier mobility, or superconducting pairing) but studies that move beyond leading-order approximations remain rare

;
Schrodi et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2020
;

;

;

;

;

;
Lihm and Poncé 2025
. This scarcity is primarily due to the rapid increase in computational complexity, as even incorporating second-order scattering events is highly nontrivial. Nonetheless, promising progress has been made, with several recent studies achieving such treatments fully from first principles

;
Bianco and Errea 2023
;
Houtput et al. 2025
. On the superconductivity front,
current approaches to non-adiabatic effects within the Eliashberg framework have relied on simplifying approximations. For example, in the anisotropic case, model Hamiltonians for the electronic structure, combined with an isotropic Einstein phonon spectrum and a single e-ph parameter, have been employed to solve the non-adiabatic Eliashberg equations

. In the isotropic case, besides replacing the momentum dependence in all kernels with Fermi-surface averages, further simplifications have been introduced, such as factorizing the lowest-order vertex correction kernel into a product of two e-ph interaction kernels

;
Durajski and Szczesniak 2016
;
Schrodi et al. 2020
.

In this work, we develop and implement a first-principles formalism to compute second-order e-ph vertex corrections within the isotropic Eliashberg framework
Eliashberg 1960
;
Eliashberg 1961
, extending beyond the conventional ME approximation
Migdal 1958
;
Allen and Mitrović 1983
.
The main challenge in implementing this general treatment lies in the significantly increased computational cost, driven by two key factors. First, evaluating the vertex-corrected Eliashberg spectral function requires the computation of four distinct e-ph matrix elements associated with two phonons. Second, incorporating the resulting vertex-corrected e-ph interaction kernel into the Eliashberg equations introduces additional summations over Matsubara frequencies and integrations over electronic energies. Our methodology is implemented in the
E
PW code and supports both the full-bandwidth (
FBW
) and Fermi-surface restricted (
FSR
) approaches

;
Lee et al. 2023
;
Lucrezi et al. 2024
. The
FBW
scheme retains the full energy dependence of the density of states (DOS), enabling the inclusion of e-ph scattering processes away from the Fermi level (
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
), while

assumes a constant DOS near
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
.

To demonstrate the capabilities of our non-adiabatic methodology, we investigate the effect of vertex corrections on the superconducting gap and critical temperature (
T_{\rm c}
) of H
3
S. This high-

superconductor serves as a prime example of a system where the adiabatic limit breaks down

;
Gor’kov and Kresin 2018
. At 200 GPa, a flat-band feature near
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
gives rise to a van Hove singularities (vHs), leading to a small effective Fermi energy. Moreover, the characteristic phonon energy in H
3
S exceeds 200 meV, yielding an adiabatic parameter of
\hbar\omega_{0}/\varepsilon_{\rm F}\approx
5
Gor’kov and Kresin 2018
. We find that the e-ph coupling constant with vertex corrections (
\lambda^{\rm V}
) remains a significant fraction of the adiabatic coupling strength (
\lambda
), highlighting the persistence of strong coupling beyond the adiabatic regime in systems where the adiabatic limit breaks down, as is the case for many recently studied high-
T_{\rm c}
hydrides
Gor’kov and Kresin 2018
;
Pickett 2023
;
Hilleke and Zurek 2022
.

Apart from vertex corrections, another critical factor in H
3
S is the breakdown of the harmonic approximation, driven by hydrogen’s low mass and significant quantum fluctuations

;
Sano et al. 2016
;
Taureau et al. 2024
. These anharmonic effects renormalize phonon frequencies, particularly hydrogen-related modes, leading to strong phonon hardening and a consequent reduction in both the e-ph coupling and critical temperature. To examine the interplay between anharmonic and non-adiabatic effects, we compute the e-ph interactions and superconducting properties of H
3
S by solving the Eliashberg equations in both the adiabatic and non-adiabatic regimes, with and without anharmonic phonons. Our results show that when both anharmonic and non-adiabatic effects are considered together, the resulting
T_{\rm c}
falls within the experimental range.

Finally, we analyze the impact of vertex corrections in Pb, where the adiabatic limit is generally considered valid. In this case, including vertex corrections results in a
T_{\rm c}
nearly identical to that predicted within the adiabatic framework, confirming their negligible effect in systems where the Migdal approximation holds.

.

II
Theory

In this section, we review the theoretical background underlying phonon-mediated superconductivity, focusing on ME formalism and its extension to include the lowest-order vertex corrections. To solve the Eliashberg equations, we employ the
FBW
approach, which accounts for the full energy dependence of the electronic DOS. This is essential for accurately capturing the effects of sharp structures in the DOS, such as vHs, and allows for calculations with both fixed and self-consistently updated chemical potential (
\mu_{\rm F}
). We also present simplified expressions based on the constant DOS
FSR
approach.

II.1
Electron self-energy with electron-phonon vertex corrections

Within the Nambu formalism
Nambu 1960
, the Hamiltonian of an electron-phonon interacting system can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{e}+\hat{H}_{ph}+\hat{H}_{e\text{-}e}+\hat{H}_{e\text{-}ph},

(1)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}\hat{\Psi}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{\Psi}_{n\mathbf{k}}

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{ph}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\!\left(\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{e-e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{nm}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\!\left(\hat{\Psi}_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{\Psi}_{n\mathbf{k}}\right)\!\!\left(\hat{\Psi}_{m-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{\Psi}_{n-\mathbf{k}}\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{e\text{-}ph}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{p}}}\sum_{nm\nu}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}g_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{q}\nu}\!\left(\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+\hat{a}_{-\mathbf{q}\nu}^{\dagger}\right)\!\Psi_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\hat{\tau}_{3}\Psi_{n\mathbf{k}}.

(5)

Here,
\hat{\tau}_{i}

(i=0,1,2,3)
are the Pauli matrices. Each of the terms is described below.

The first term represents the electron Hamiltonian, where
\hat{\Psi}_{n\mathbf{k}}
is a two-component electron field operator

\hat{\Psi}_{n\mathbf{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{n\mathbf{k}\uparrow}\\
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix},\,\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{k}\uparrow}\,\,\hat{c}_{n-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix},

(6)

with
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{k}\sigma}
and
\hat{c}_{n\mathbf{k}\sigma}
are the creation and annihilation operators for an electronic state with momentum
\mathbf{k}
, band index
n
, spin
\sigma
, and energy
\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}
. The second term describes the phonon Hamiltonian, where
\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
is the frequency of a phonon mode with momentum
\mathbf{q}
and branch index
\nu
, and
a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
and
a_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
are the bosonic creation and annihilation operators.
The anharmonic terms, when relevant, are incorporated into Eq. (
3
) through additional contributions derived from the self-consistent phonon theory, which replaces the true double-well potential with an effective temperature-dependent harmonic potential that minimizes the free energy, as discussed in detail in Ref.
Zacharias et al. 2023
.
The third term accounts for the Coulomb contribution, where
W_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}
are the scattering matrix elements between electrons.

The fourth term describes the e-ph interaction, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a), where
N_{p}
denotes the number of unit cells in the Born-von Kármán (BvK) supercell. This term corresponds to the conventional linear coupling between an electron and a phonon, commonly referred to as the Fan-Migdal term. Higher-order contributions to
\hat{H}_{e-ph}
, known as nonlinear e-ph interactions, could also influence materials properties

;
Dee et al. 2020
;
Bianco and Errea 2023
;
Houtput et al. 2025
;
Zappacosta et al. 2025
;
Lihm and Poncé 2025
;
Lihm and Poncé 2025
, but are not considered in this work.
The linear e-ph interaction term is governed by the matrix element
g_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{q}\nu}
, which quantifies the scattering between electronic states
n\mathbf{k}
and
m\mathbf{k}\!+\!\mathbf{q}
mediated by a phonon with wavevector
\mathbf{q}
and branch index
\nu
. This matrix element is defined as
Giustino et al. 2007
;
Giustino 2017

\displaystyle{}g_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{q}\nu}

\displaystyle=\bra{u_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}}\Delta_{\mathbf{q}\nu}v^{\rm KS}\ket{u_{n\mathbf{k}}}_{\rm uc},

(7)

where
u_{n\mathbf{k}}
is the Bloch-periodic part of the Kohn-Sham (KS) electron wavefunction, and the integral is performed over the unit cell (uc). The derivative of the self-consistent potential is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbf{q}\nu}v^{\rm KS}

\displaystyle\equiv e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot{\bf r}}\Delta_{\mathbf{q}\nu}V^{\rm KS}

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{\kappa\alpha{\bf R}_{p}}\!\!e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot({\bf r}-{\bf R}_{p})}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2M_{\kappa}\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}}e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf{q})\frac{\partial V^{\rm KS}({\bf r})}{\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}}.

(8)

Here,
\kappa
labels atoms in the unit cell with
M_{\kappa}
as the atomic mass, and
\alpha
denotes Cartesian directions. The index
p
labels unit cells with BvK boundary conditions,
\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}
is the position of atom
\kappa
in the unit cell
p
,
\bf{R}_{p}
is the lattice vector identifying the unit cell
p
, and
e_{\kappa\alpha,\nu}(\mathbf{q})
is the eigenvector corresponding to atom
\kappa
in the Cartesian direction
\alpha
for a collective phonon mode
\mathbf{q}\nu
.
The differential
\partial V^{\rm KS}({\bf r})/\partial\tau_{\kappa\alpha p}
is computed using density functional perturbation theory (DFPT)
Baroni et al. 2001
. In the presence of anharmonicity, the phonon modes are no longer exact eigenmodes of a harmonic potential, making
\Delta_{\mathbf{q}\nu}v^{\rm KS}
formally ill-defined. To incorporate anharmonic effects within Eq. (
II.1
), we retain the harmonic DFPT-computed potential derivatives while replacing the phonon eigenvectors and frequencies with their anharmonically renormalized counterparts, obtained by diagonalizing the dynamical matrix obtained from the special displacement method
Zacharias et al. 2023

Using Nambu’s notation, we define the finite-temperature electron Green’s function as a
2\times 2
matrix
Nambu 1960
:

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\langle\hat{T}_{\tau}\hat{\Psi}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{k}}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\!\begin{pmatrix}\langle\hat{T}_{\tau}\hat{c}_{n\mathbf{k}\uparrow}(\tau)\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{k}\uparrow}(0)\rangle&\langle\hat{T}_{\tau}\hat{c}_{n\mathbf{k}\uparrow}(\tau)\hat{c}_{n-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}(0)\rangle\\
\langle\hat{T}_{\tau}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}(\tau)\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{k}\uparrow}(0)\rangle&\langle\hat{T}_{\tau}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}(\tau)\hat{c}_{n-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}(0)\rangle\end{pmatrix},

(9)

where
\hat{T}_{\tau}
denotes Wick’s time-ordering operator in imaginary time, and
\langle\cdots\rangle
indicates the grand-canonical thermodynamic average. The diagonal elements correspond to the normal Green’s functions for spin-up and spin-down electrons, describing single-particle electronic excitations. The off-diagonal elements are the anomalous Green’s functions, introduced by Gor’kov
Gor’Kov 1958
, which describe superconducting Cooper pairs. An important feature of the Nambu-Gor’kov formalism is that, in this matrix representation, the familiar Feynman-Dyson rules of many-body perturbation theory remain valid
Schrieffer 1999
.

The study of the superconducting state involves the determination
of the matrix Green’s function in Eq. (
II.1
). In Matsubara space, this matrix Green’s function obeys the Dyson equation
Migdal 1958
;
Mahan 1993
:

\left[\hat{G}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j})\right]^{-1}=\left[\hat{G}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{0}(i\omega_{j})\right]^{-1}-\,\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j}),

(10)

where
\hat{G}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{0}(i\omega_{j})
is the non-interacting Green’s function

\left[\hat{G}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{0}(i\omega_{j})\right]^{-1}=i\omega_{j}\hat{\tau}_{0}-(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}-\mu_{\rm F})\hat{\tau}_{3},

(11)

and
\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j})
is the electron self-energy including contributions arising from the e-ph and Coulomb interactions

\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j})=\hat{\Sigma}^{\rm ep}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j})+\hat{\Sigma}^{\rm c}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j}).

(12)

In these expressions,
i\omega_{j}=i(2j+1)\pi k_{\rm B}T
denotes the fermionic Matsubara frequency, where
j
is an integer,
T
is the temperature, and
k_{\rm B}
is the Boltzmann constant.

Following the standard practice, the Coulomb part of the electron self-energy is defined within the
GW
approximation

;
Hybertsen and Louie 1986
as

\hat{\Sigma}^{\rm c}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j})=-k_{\rm B}T\sum_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}W_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3},

(13)

where
W_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})
is the dynamically screened Coulomb interaction. Assuming a static screened Coulomb interaction, Eq. (
13

\hat{\Sigma}^{\rm c}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j})=-k_{\rm B}T\sum_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}W_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}^{\rm od}_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3},

(14)

where only the off-diagonal components of the Green’s function
\hat{G}^{\rm od}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j})
are retained to avoid double counting Coulomb effects, since the one-particle eigenenergy
\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}
in
\hat{G}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{0}(i\omega_{j})
already includes part of the Coulomb interaction

.

Recent methodological advancements have enabled a fully
ab initio
treatment of Coulomb interactions within Eliashberg theory

;
Sanna et al. 2018
;
Davydov et al. 2020
;
Pellegrini et al. 2023
;

;

. However, explicitly incorporating the full Coulomb kernel

in Eq. (

) remains computationally challenging. As a result, this interaction is typically approximated using the semi-empirical Morel-Anderson
\mu_{\rm c}^{*}
parameter

;

, defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{c}^{*}=\frac{\mu_{c}}{1+\mu_{c}\,\rm{ln}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{el}}{\hbar\omega_{0}}\right)},

(15)

where
\mu_{c}
is the Fermi-surface averaged Coulomb interaction,
\omega_{0}
is a characteristic phonon frequency, and
\varepsilon_{el}
is the electronic bandwidth. Within the Eliashberg formalism, the corresponding phonon energy scale is given by the Matsubara frequency cutoff

, thus

must be replaced by
\mu^{*}_{\rm E}
, which are related via

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu^{*}_{\rm E}}=\frac{1}{\mu_{c}^{*}}+\rm{ln}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}}{\omega_{c}}\right).

This approximation is used in the current study.

For the phonon part, the central approximation consists of retaining only the first-order e-ph scattering diagram, shown in Fig.

;

;

The electron self-energy
\hat{\Sigma}^{\rm ep}_{n\bf{k}}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{\rm ep}_{n\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{j})=\Sigma_{n\bf{k}}^{\mathrm{(A)}}(i\omega_{j})+\Sigma_{n\bf{k}}^{\mathrm{(NA)}}(i\omega_{j}).

(17)

The adiabatic self-energy
\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{(A)}}(i\omega_{j})
, corresponding to the first-order Feynman diagram in Fig.
1
, is expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{(A)}}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=-k_{\rm B}T\sum_{m\mathbf{q}}\sum_{\nu j^{\prime}}|g_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{q}\nu}|^{2}D_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3},

where

Since

Following the standard practice

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}{(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime}})^{2}+\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{2}}.

We define the anisotropic e-ph interaction kernel as

\displaystyle\lambda_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{2\omega}{(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime}})^{2}+\omega^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\alpha^{2}F_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\omega),

(20)

where the anisotropic Eliashberg spectral function is given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}F_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=N_{\mathrm{F}}\sum_{\nu}|g_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{q}\nu}|^{2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}),

(21)

with
N_{\mathrm{F}}
as the DOS per spin at
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
. Substituting Eqs. (
19
)
-
(
21
) into the self-energy expression, Eq. (
II.1
) takes the compact form

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{(A)}}(i\omega_{j})\!

\displaystyle=k_{\rm B}T\sum_{m\mathbf{q}j^{\prime}}\!\!\frac{\lambda_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})}{N_{\mathrm{F}}}\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}.

(22)

The non-adiabatic self-energy
\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{(NA)}}(i\omega_{j})
, corresponding to the second-order Feynman diagram in Fig.
1
(b), is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{(NA)}}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=(k_{\rm B}T)^{2}\sum_{mlp}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{\nu\mu}\sum_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}g_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{q}\nu}g_{l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}^{\mathbf{p}\mu}g_{p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p},l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}}^{-\mathbf{q}\mu}g_{n\mathbf{k},p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}^{-\mathbf{p}\mu}D_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})D_{\mathbf{p}\mu}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}-i\omega_{j}+i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}.

(23)

Here, each matrix element represents an e-ph scattering vertex, associated with either phonon absorption (
\mathbf{q}\nu
or
\mathbf{p}\mu
) or phonon emission (
-\mathbf{q}\nu
or
-\mathbf{p}\mu
). The expression captures a two-phonon scattering process involving four distinct interactions, each requiring explicit evaluation of the corresponding matrix element.

Analogous to the adiabatic case, we substitute the phonon propagators with their bare counterparts and introduce the anisotropic first-order vertex correction to the e-ph coupling via a double spectral representation

\displaystyle\lambda^{\rm V}_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p},p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\frac{2\omega}{(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime}})^{2}+\omega^{2}}\times\frac{2\omega^{\prime}}{(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})^{2}+\omega^{\prime}{{}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\alpha^{2}F^{\rm V}_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p},p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}),

(24)

where the anisotropic first-order vertex correction to the Eliashberg spectral function is expressed as follows

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}F^{\rm V}_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p},p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=N_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\sum_{\nu\mu}g_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{q}\nu}g_{l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}^{\mathbf{p}\mu}g_{p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p},l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}}^{-\mathbf{q}\mu}g_{n\mathbf{k},p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}^{-\mathbf{p}\mu}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu})\delta(\omega^{\prime}-\omega_{\mathbf{p}\mu}).

(25)

Using Eqs. (
II.1
) and (
25
), the non-adiabatic electron self-energy can be expressed in a compact form as

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{(NA)}}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{(k_{\rm B}T)^{2}}{N_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\sum_{mlp}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{p}}\sum_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}-i\omega_{j}+i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}_{p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}(i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\lambda^{\rm V}_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p},p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}).

(26)

II.2
Isotropic approximation to electron self-energy

Accounting for the anisotropy in the non-adiabatic self-energy is computationally demanding, as Eq. (
II.1
) involves Brillouin zone integrals over two crystal momenta. To date, the most advanced calculations of superconducting properties with anisotropic vertex corrections have relied on several approximations, which include using model Hamiltonians for electronic states, an isotropic Einstein phonon spectrum, and a single e-ph coupling parameter
Schrodi et al. 2020
. However, for most materials, except for a few notable layered systems such as MgB
2
-type superconductors
Szabó et al. 2001
;
Margine and Giustino 2013
;
Tomassetti et al. 2024
, graphite intercalation compounds
Margine et al. 2016
;
Mishra et al. 2024
, and transition metal dichalcogenides
Heil et al. 2017
;
Das et al. 2023
, the effect of this full momentum dependence is weak and an isotropic treatment can be employed
Davydov et al. 2020
;
Pellegrini and Sanna 2024
. This approximation consists of replacing the
\mathbf{k}
-dependent quantities with their Fermi-surface averages. A detailed derivation is provided in Sec. II of the Supplementary Information
SI
.

Within the isotropic approximation, the adiabatic self-energy takes the following form

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{(A)}}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{N_{\mathrm{F}}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}(\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3},

(27)

where
\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})
is the isotropic e-ph kernel, expressed as

\displaystyle\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{2\omega}{(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime}})^{2}+\omega^{2}}\,\alpha^{2}F(\omega).

(28)

The kernel is an even function of the Matsubara frequency and attains its maximum at
\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime}}=0

\displaystyle\lambda=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\alpha^{2}F(\omega)}{\omega},

(29)

where
\lambda
denotes the isotropic e-ph coupling strength.
The isotropic Eliashberg spectral function
\alpha^{2}F(\omega)
represents the double Fermi-surface average of
\alpha^{2}F_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\omega)
and is given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}F(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{F}}}\sum_{nm}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})\delta(\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\sum_{\nu}|g_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{q}\nu}|^{2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}),

(30)

Following similar steps, the isotropic non-adiabatic self-energy associated with the lowest-order vertex correction can be simplified as

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{(NA)}}(i\omega_{j})=\frac{(k_{\rm B}T)^{2}}{N_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}\lambda^{\rm V}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime})N(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 2.84544pt\times\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}(\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}-i\omega_{j}+i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3}\hat{G}(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\hat{\tau}_{3},

(31)

where the isotropic vertex-corrected e-ph kernel

\displaystyle\lambda^{\rm V}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{2\omega}{(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime}})^{2}+\omega^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\frac{2\omega^{\prime}}{(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})^{2}+\omega^{\prime}{{}^{2}}}\alpha^{2}F^{\rm V}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).

(32)

The vertex-corrected kernel attains its maximum at
i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}}=0\text{ and }i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}=0
, and reduces to

\displaystyle\lambda^{\rm V}

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\frac{\alpha^{2}F^{\rm V}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}{\omega\,\omega^{\prime}}.

(33)

Here,
\lambda^{\rm V}
is called the vertex-corrected e-ph coupling strength and is a dimensionless measure of the average e-ph coupling strength arising from the non-adiabatic vertex corrections.
The isotropic vertex-corrected Eliashberg spectral function
\alpha^{2}F^{\rm V}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is obtained by performing the Fermi-surface average as follows

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}F^{\rm V}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C(0)}\sum_{nmlp}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}\mathbf{p}}\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})\delta(\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})\delta(\varepsilon_{l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})\delta(\varepsilon_{p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})\alpha^{2}F^{\rm V}_{n\mathbf{k},m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p},l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p},p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).

(34)

Here, the normalization factor
C(0)
corresponds to the total weight of all four-fold combinations of states at
\varepsilon_{\rm F}

\displaystyle C(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{nmlp}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}\mathbf{p}}\delta(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})\delta(\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\delta(\varepsilon_{l\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F})\delta(\varepsilon_{p\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}}-\varepsilon_{\rm F}).

(35)

Computing Eq. (
34
) has remained a longstanding challenge, even in the isotropic framework, due to the requirement of evaluating four e-ph matrix elements involving two different phonons. To date, additional approximations have been employed, such as estimating the density factor
C(0)
based on the free-electron model
Freericks et al. 1997
;
Durajski and Szczesniak 2016
, and expressing the vertex-corrected e-ph term
\lambda^{\rm V}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})
as a product of two e-ph vertices
\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})

Freericks et al. 1997
;
Schrodi et al. 2020
. In this work, we compute this term without relying on these simplifications. We exploit the symmetry under phonon exchange
(\mathbf{q},\nu,\omega)\leftrightarrow(\mathbf{p},\mu,\omega^{\prime})
to reduce the computational cost. This symmetry, evident from the Feynman diagrams in Fig.
1
(b), enables us to express
\alpha^{2}F^{\rm V}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
in terms of the real part of the four e–ph matrix elements product. A detailed derivation is provided in Sec. III of the Supplementary Information
SI
.

II.3
Isotropic non-adiabatic full-bandwidth Eliashberg equations

To obtain the Eliashberg equations, the standard procedure is to express the total self-energy on the basis of Pauli matrices in terms of three scalar functions
Carbotte 1990
;
Scalapino et al. 1966
;
Allen and Mitrović 1983
:

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}(i\omega_{j})=i\omega_{j}\left[1-Z(i\omega_{j})\right]\hat{\tau}_{0}+\chi(i\omega_{j})\hat{\tau}_{3}+\phi(i\omega_{j})\hat{\tau}_{1},

(36)

where
Z(i\omega_{j})
is the mass renormalization function,
\chi(i\omega_{j})
is the energy shift, and
\phi(i\omega_{j})
is the order parameter. The quantity
\Delta(i\omega_{j})=\phi(i\omega_{j})/Z(i\omega_{j})
defines the superconducting gap function. Through the Dyson equation (
10
), the calculation of the Green’s function
\hat{G}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j^{\prime}})
is reduced to solving three coupled equations for
Z(i\omega_{j})
,
\chi(i\omega_{j})
, and
\phi(i\omega_{j})
. A detailed derivation is given in Sec. IV of Supplemental Information
SI
.
We refer to this final set as the isotropic non-adiabatic full-bandwidth (
NA-FBW
) Eliashberg equations and are given by

\displaystyle Z(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{N_{\mathrm{F}}\omega_{j}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{(k_{\rm B}T)^{2}}{N_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,\omega_{j}}\sum_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}\lambda^{\rm V}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon)N(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\left[\gamma^{T}(\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime}})P^{Z}(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon,i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}-i\omega_{j}+i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\gamma(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\right],

(37)

\displaystyle\chi(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=-\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{N_{\mathrm{F}}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(k_{\rm B}T)^{2}}{N_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\sum_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}\lambda^{\rm V}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon)N(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\left[\gamma^{T}(\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime}})P^{\chi}(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon,i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}-i\omega_{j}+i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\gamma(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\right],

(38)

\displaystyle\phi(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{N_{\mathrm{F}}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\left[\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})-\mu_{\rm E}^{*}\right]\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\hskip 1.42262pt+\frac{(k_{\rm B}T)^{2}}{N_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\sum_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}}\lambda^{\rm V}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon)N(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\times\left[\gamma^{T}(\varepsilon^{\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime}})P^{\phi}(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon,i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}-i\omega_{j}+i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\gamma(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\right],

(39)

\displaystyle N_{e}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}N(\varepsilon^{\prime})\left[1-2k_{\rm B}T\sum_{j^{\prime}}\frac{\varepsilon-\mu_{\rm F}+\chi(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})}{\Theta(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j^{\prime}})}\right].

(40)

In Eq. (
40
),
N_{e}
represents the electron number that determines the chemical potential which is updated self-consistently
Marsiglio and Carbotte 2008
;
Lee et al. 2023
;
Lucrezi et al. 2024
. This is referred to as
FBW
+
\mu
, while calculations performed with a fixed
\mu_{\rm F}=\varepsilon_{\rm F}
are denoted simply as
FBW

Lee et al. 2023
;
Lucrezi et al. 2024
. The Coulomb interaction enters the Eliashberg expression only through Eq. (
39
), where it is approximated by the semi-empirical pseudopotential
\mu_{\rm E}^{*}
introduced earlier. The summation over the Matsubara frequencies in Eqs. (
37
)
-
(
40
) formally extends from
-\infty
to
+\infty
, however, in practical calculations, it is evaluated over positive Matsubara frequencies only and truncated at a cutoff frequency, as detailed in Sec. V of the Supplementary Information
SI
.

Above, we follow Ref.
Schrodi et al. 2020
and introduce the pseudo-vector
\gamma(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
as

\displaystyle\gamma(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\\
\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\\
\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix},

(41)

and its transpose is given by

\displaystyle\gamma^{T}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix},

(42)

along with the matrices
P^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
,
P^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
, and
P^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
defined as follows

\displaystyle P^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\!\begin{pmatrix}-\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&-\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\\
\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&0\\
-\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&0&\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix}\!,

(43)

\displaystyle P^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\!\begin{pmatrix}-\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&-\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&0\\
-\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&-\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\\
0&-\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&-\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix}\!,

(44)

\displaystyle P^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\!\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&0&-\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\\
0&\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\\
-\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})&-\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix}.

(45)

The elements of
\gamma(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
,
P^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
,
P^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
, and
P^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
are given by

\displaystyle\gamma^{Z}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{j}Z(i\omega_{j})}{\Theta(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})},

(46)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\chi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon-\mu_{\rm F}+\chi(i\omega_{j})}{\Theta(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})},

(47)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi(i\omega_{j})}{\Theta(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})},

(48)

with

\displaystyle\Theta(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\left[\omega_{j}Z(i\omega_{j})\right]^{2}+\left[\varepsilon-\mu_{\rm F}+\chi(i\omega_{j})\right]^{2}+\left[\phi(i\omega_{j})\right]^{2}.

(49)

II.4
Isotropic non-adiabatic Fermi-surface restricted Eliashberg equations

We can further simplify the isotropic
NA-FBW
Eliashberg equations by adopting the constant DOS approximation, such that
N(\varepsilon)\rightarrow N_{\mathrm{F}}
. Assuming an infinite electronic bandwidth at half-filling, the energy integral can be evaluated analytically, as detailed in Sec. VI of the Supplementary Information
SI
, leading to the isotropic non-adiabatic Fermi-surface restricted (
NA-FSR
) Eliashberg equations

\displaystyle Z(i\omega_{j})=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\pi k_{\rm B}T}{\omega_{j}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\tilde{\gamma}^{Z}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi^{3}(k_{\rm B}T)^{2}N_{\mathrm{F}}}{\omega_{j}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}}\lambda^{V}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle\times\left[\tilde{\gamma}^{T}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\tilde{P}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}-i\omega_{j}+i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\tilde{\gamma}(i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\right],

(50)

\displaystyle\Delta(i\omega_{j})Z(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\pi k_{\rm B}T\sum_{j^{\prime}}\left[\lambda(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}})-\mu_{\rm E}^{*}\right]\tilde{\gamma}^{\phi}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle+\pi^{3}(k_{\rm B}T)^{2}N_{\mathrm{F}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}}\lambda^{\rm V}(i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime}},i\omega_{j}-i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle\times\left[\tilde{\gamma}^{T}(i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\tilde{P}^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}}-i\omega_{j}+i\omega_{j^{\prime}})\tilde{\gamma}(i\omega_{j^{\prime\prime}})\right].

(51)

The pseudo-vector
\tilde{\gamma}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
and its transpose
\tilde{\gamma}^{T}(\varepsilon,i\omega_{j})
, along with the matrices
\tilde{P}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})
and
\tilde{P}^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})
are expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(i\omega_{j})=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\gamma}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})\\
\gamma^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix},\hskip 1.42271pt\tilde{\gamma}^{T}(i\omega_{j})=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})&\gamma^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix},

(52)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\tilde{\gamma}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})&-\tilde{\gamma}^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})\\
-\tilde{\gamma}^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})&\tilde{\gamma}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix},

(53)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\gamma}^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})&-\tilde{\gamma}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})\\
-\tilde{\gamma}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})&-\tilde{\gamma}^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})\end{pmatrix},

(54)

where we define

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{\omega}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{j}}{\sqrt{\omega_{j}^{2}+\Delta^{2}(i\omega_{j})}},

(55a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{\Delta}(i\omega_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta(i\omega_{j})}{\sqrt{\omega_{j}^{2}+\Delta^{2}(i\omega_{j})}},

(55b)

with
\Delta(i\omega_{j})=\phi(i\omega_{j})/Z(i\omega_{j})
being the superconducting gap function.

IV.1
Electronic structure and phonon dispersion of H
3
S

At 200 GPa, H
3
S crystallizes in the high-symmetry
Im\bar{3}m
phase with a lattice parameter of 3.089 Å, consistent with previous theoretical studies
Duan et al. 2014
;
Bernstein et al. 2015
;
Errea et al. 2015
.
The band structure, shown in Fig.

\varepsilon_{\rm F}

-
1.75 eV and 2.50 eV, as well as a sharp vHs peak near
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
. This vHs significantly enhances the e-ph coupling strength, which is a key factor in the high
T_{\rm c}
of H

S
Sano et al. 2016
;
Quan and Pickett 2016
;
Akashi 2020
;
Thomsen and Goesten 2024

\varepsilon_{\rm F}

Akashi et al. 2015

Additionally, the width of the vHs effectively defines a low Fermi energy scale (
\sim

and raising questions about the validity of Migdal’s approximation in this system

;
Gor’kov and Kresin 2018

Figure

The phonon spectrum exhibits a clear separation between low-frequency acoustic modes (below 75 meV), mainly arising from sulfur vibrations, and high-frequency optical modes (above 75 meV), primarily involving hydrogen. This separation is a direct consequence of the large mass difference between S and H atoms.

Accurately capturing these anharmonic effects is essential for a reliable description of vibrational dynamics and superconducting properties of H

IV.3
Superconducting gap of H

The superconducting properties of H

Several studies on H

IV.4
Electronic structure and phonon dispersion of Pb

To further test our hypothesis regarding the breakdown of the Migdal approximation and the significance of e-ph vertex corrections, we analyze elemental Pb, a prototypical conventional superconductor.
The electronic structure of Pb, shown in Fig.
5
, features a broad band, with a nearly flat DOS around
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
and sharp features located approximately 2 eV below and above it.
The phonon spectrum extends up to
\sim
9 meV and exhibits a pronounced Kohn anomaly near the W-point in the BZ, which is attributed to strong Fermi-surface nesting
Aynajian et al. 2008
.

Although Migdal’s adiabatic condition (
\hbar\omega_{0}/\varepsilon_{\rm F}
) is well satisfied in Pb due to the large separation between the electronic and phononic energy scales, the observed Kohn anomaly suggests the presence of nonlinear e-ph interactions
Freericks et al. 1997
, which are not captured by the standard treatment. These considerations motivate the inclusion of non-adiabatic e-ph vertex effects in our analysis, even for nominally conventional superconductors such as Pb.

V
Summary

In this work, we revisit the formulation of the lowest-order vertex corrections within the Eliashberg formalism and develop a fully
ab initio
approach to evaluate the vertex-corrected Eliashberg spectral function and the corresponding e-ph coupling strength. This methodology, implemented in the EPW code
Poncé et al. 2016
;
Lee et al. 2023
, enables us to move beyond model Hamiltonians and apply the formalism to realistic materials such as H
3
S and Pb.
To solve the isotropic Eliashberg equations, we employ three approaches: the
FSR
,
FBW
with a fixed
\mu_{\rm F}
, and
FBW
with a self-consistently updated
\mu_{\rm F}
.
For H
3
S, calculations include both harmonic and anharmonic phonons using a Coulomb pseudopotential
\mu_{\rm E}^{*}
= 0.16, while for Pb, we consider harmonic phonons with
\mu_{\rm E}^{*}
= 0.10.
In H
3
S, which exhibits strong e-ph coupling, incorporating vertex corrections leads to a significant reduction in both
\lambda
and
T_{\rm c}
, signaling a breakdown of the Migdal approximation.
When effects from phonon anharmonicity and variation in the electronic DOS are taken into account, the predicted
T_{\rm c}
agrees well with experimental measurements, resolving long-standing discrepancies between theory and experiment.
By contrast, for elemental Pb, a prototypical conventional superconductor, vertex corrections have a negligible impact on both
\lambda
and
T_{\rm c}
, consistent with the expectations of Migdal–Eliashberg theory.
Overall, our findings demonstrate that vertex corrections are essential for accurately describing superconductivity in materials with strong non-adiabaticity, low carrier density, or sharp features in the DOS, such as high-
T_{\rm c}
hydrides, while validating their negligible influence in conventional adiabatic superconductors. The computational framework established here provides a rigorous and predictive first-principles methodology for extending the applicability of Eliashberg theory beyond the conventional Migdal limit.
