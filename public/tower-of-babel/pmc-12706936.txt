Effect of diode laser irradiation on push-out bond strength of fiber post to root dentin using two different resin cements: an in-vitro study
Authors: Reza Tayefeh Davalloo, Fereshteh Naser Alavi, Seyedeh Maryam Tavangar, Faride Darabi, Narges Simdar, Zeynab Moghaddami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to assess the effect of diode laser irradiation on pushout bond strength (PBS) of fiber post to root dentin using different resin cements.MethodsThis in vitro experimental study utilized 36 extracted single-rooted mandibular premolars. The samples underwent decoronation, root canal treatment, and post space preparation. Teeth were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 9 each): ((I) etch-and-rinse (ER) adhesive system and conventional resin cement, (II) diode laser irradiation (for simulated antimicrobial disinfection) + ER cement, (III) self-adhesive (SA) cement, and (IV) diode laser irradiation + SA cement. After 10,000 thermocycles (5–55 °C), PBS was measured using a universal testing machine (Zwick/Roll, Ulm, Germany) at 0.5 mm/min until failure. Cement-dentin interface morphology was qualitatively assessed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM; MIRA3 TESCAN, Czech Republic) on one specimen per group. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Bonferroni post-hoc tests (α = 0.05).ResultsSignificant differences in PBS were observed among groups (P < 0.001). Mean ± SD PBS values were: ER cement, 5.61 ± 1.47 MPa; diode laser + ER cement, 5.75 ± 0.88 MPa; SA cement, 7.75 ± 0.55 MPa; diode laser + SA cement, 7.60 ± 0.97 MPa. Pairwise comparisons showed SA cement groups had significantly higher PBS than ER cement groups (P < 0.001 for both). Diode laser irradiation had no significant effect within cement types (P > 0.05).ConclusionResin cement type significantly influenced PBS, with SA cement outperforming ER cement. Diode laser irradiation had no significant effect on PBS.

Background

Restoration of endodontically treated teeth (ETT) presents clinical challenges due to substantial loss of coronal structure, rendering them prone to fracture under functional loads [1, 2]. Intracanal fiber posts are commonly used to retain coronal restorations, with treatment success hinging on effective coronal sealing and equitable distribution of occlusal stresses [3–5]. However, the primary determinant of post retention is the bond strength between the fiber post, resin cement, and root dentin; inadequate adhesion promotes crack propagation at the interface, culminating in debonding and catastrophic failure [6–8].

Fiber posts, when luted with adhesive resin cements, enable formation of a monolithic post-cement-dentin complex, yet the cement-dentin interface remains the weakest link, accounting for most clinical failures [9, 10]. Root dentin poses unique bonding hurdles compared to coronal dentin, including fewer dentinal tubules, tubular sclerosis, irregular geometry inducing polymerization stresses, and limited access for preparation—all contributing to inferior bond strengths [11, 12]. Endodontic procedures, such as intracanal disinfection, may further compromise this interface [13, 14].

Resin cements outperform other types in enhancing post retention and fracture resistance, with cement type directly influencing bond quality [15, 16]. Debonding at the dentin interface is the predominant failure mode for fiber posts in ETT [17], underscoring the need to evaluate cement-dentin adhesion [18]. Prior studies on etch-and-rinse (ER) versus self-adhesive (SA) resin cements yield conflicting results: some report superior PBS with ER systems due to effective smear layer removal and deeper hybrid layer formation [18, 19], while others favor SA cements for simplified application and comparable performance [20, 21].

Persistent root canal infections, despite chemomechanical debridement, drive treatment failures, as conventional irrigants exhibit limited intratubular penetration relative to bacterial invasion depth [22, 23]. Compared to other laser systems, diode laser consumes very little energy, is low-cost, and helps to protect the environment [24]. In addition to root canal disinfection, diode laser is also used to desensitize coronal dentin. Shalaby et al. [25] showed that diode laser irradiation reduced coronal dentin hypersensitivity without affecting the resin bond strength. Alonaizan et al. [26] reported that diode laser, unlike sandblasting with 50-µm aluminum oxide particles, reduced the pushout bond strength (PBS). However, Borges et al. [27] indicated that diode laser did not significantly affect the PBS. Regarding the effect of diode laser on root dentin, Farmakis et al. [28] demonstrated that short-pulse diode laser irradiation had a positive effect on the quality of intracanal bonding, but ultra short-pulse irradiation had negative effects on the quality of bond. Given the routine clinical use of diode laser for intracanal disinfection and the potential for thermal or structural modifications to root dentin that could influence adhesive bonding, this study specifically examined whether such irradiation—prior to fiber post cementation—affects PBS, hypothesizing no significant impact based on mixed prior evidence.

The bond strength of different resin cements to fiber posts and root canal dentin has been previously evaluated with conflicting results [7]. Nozari et al. [18] showed that the bond strength of fiber posts to root dentin by using an etch-and-rinse (ER) resin cement was higher than that by using a self-adhesive cement. Conversely, Angnanon et al. [20] indicated that the bond strength of fiber posts to canal dentin was higher by using a self-adhesive cement than an ER resin cement. Considering the existing controversy regarding different cements and the effect of diode laser on PBS of fiber post to root dentin, the present study aimed to assess the effect of diode laser irradiation on the PBS of fiber post to root dentin using different resin cements. The null hypotheses of the study were: (1) type of resin cement has no significant effect on PBS of fiber post to root dentin, and (2) diode laser irradiation has no significant effect on PBS of fiber post to root dentin.

Methods

This in vitro experimental study utilized 36 extracted single-rooted mandibular premolars with no root caries, similar dimensions, and a minimum root canal length of 14 mm. These teeth were extracted due to periodontal diseases as part of orthodontic treatment. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GUMS.REC.1404.051).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated to be a minimum of 9 specimens in each group using G*Power software version 3.1 and independent t-test for the comparison of two means assuming an effect size (d) of 1.47 according to the mean and standard deviation values extracted from a previous study [11], alpha = 0.05, and study power of 80%.

Specimen preparation

The teeth were stored in 0.5% chloramine T solution for disinfection. Subsequently, they were decoronated at 2 mm above the cervical region using a diamond disc (Komet, Germany). Following this, they were manually instrumented using the step-back technique. The working length of each canal was determined by placing a file inside the canal and observing the tip at the apex, then subtracting 1 mm. Using the initial file, the initial 1–2 mm expansion was performed by reaming, and expansion was continued from the master apical file #35 to file #60 using the step-back technique; saline was used for irrigation. Thereafter, obturation was performed using gutta-percha (Meta Biomed) and sealer (Meta Biomed). The teeth were stored in distilled water for 24 h post-treatment to allow sealer setting. Subsequently, two-thirds of the canal were emptied using #1 and #2 peeso-reamers (Thomas, France) to accommodate #1 fiber post (Angelus, Brazil). The teeth were then randomly assigned to 4 groups (n = 9) using a table of random numbers generated by Excel software:

Group 1 (bonding agent + ER cement): The root canals were rinsed with water and air-dried. The canals were etched with 37% phosphoric acid (Bisco, USA) for 15 s, then rinsed for 15 s and dried with paper points. A two-component dual-cure bonding agent (All Bond, Bisco, USA) was mixed in equal proportions and applied with a microbrush into the canal; excess material was removed with a paper point, and a gentle air stream was applied. Before placement, the posts were washed with ethanol and then dried. Then, one layer of the bonding agent was applied on them and air-thinned. Light-curing was performed for 10 s. The intensity of the curing light was controlled with a radiometer before use. Then, an ER cement (Top Cem, Huge, China) was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and inserted into the canal using a file (the cement should be inserted into the canal within 60 s after mixing). After dipping the post into the cement, it was placed into the canal with gentle finger pressure. Excess cement was removed using a microbrush 30 to 60 s after being placed in the canal. Light curing was performed for 20 s at an intensity of 1000 mW/cm2. Chemical polymerization continued for 420 s [11].

Group 2 (laser + bonding agent + ER cement): Diode laser irradiation was performed before cementing the fiber post. The canals were irradiated with diode laser (Poolsar, Navid Asayesh, Iran) with 980 nm wavelength, 2.5 W power, and a 200-µm optical fiber tip in continuous-wave mode. The laser optical fiber was placed along the entire length of the root canal, and five cycles of irradiation were performed from the apical to the cervical region in spiral movements at a speed of 2 mm/s for 20 s each (total active exposure time: 100 s), separated by 10-second rest periods for root cooling after each cycle. Then, the fiber posts were cemented with ER cement (Top Cem, Huge, China) as in the first group according to the manufacturer’s instructions [29].

Group 3 [self-adhesive (SA) cement]: The canals were rinsed with water and dried with paper points until they were damp (care was taken not to over-dry the dentin). The posts were washed with ethanol and dried. SA cement (PermaCem, DMG, Germany) was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and delivered into the canal with a file (within 60 s). After being dipped in cement, the posts were placed in the canal with gentle pressure and excess cement was removed 30 to 60 s after placement. Light curing was performed for 20 s at an intensity of 1000 mW/cm2, and chemical polymerization continued for 420 s.

Group 4 (laser + SA cement): First, diode laser irradiation with the same characteristics as in the second group was performed in the root canal, and then the fiber posts were cemented with SA cement (PermaCem, DMG, Germany) as in the third group according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Thermocycling

After 24 h of storage of all specimens in distilled water at 37 °C, they were subjected to 10,000 thermal cycles (between 5 °C and 55 °C, with a dwell time of 20 s, and a transfer time of 10 s) in a thermocycler (Nemo Mecatronica, Mashhad, Iran) [30]. Then, each root was sectioned horizontally and perpendicularly to the longitudinal axis of the tooth into transverse sections with 1 mm thickness from the middle region of the root using a microtome to examine the PBS.

PBS test

The 1-mm-thick transverse sections were embedded in acrylic resin and secured in a universal testing machine (Zwick/Roll, Ulm, Germany). A cylindrical stainless-steel plunger (1 mm diameter) was positioned centrally on the post surface within the canal lumen, ensuring contact only with the post and avoiding the surrounding dentin (plunger diameter calibrated to be 1 mm smaller than the post diameter to isolate the cement-dentin interface and prevent structural damage to the tooth). A compressive load was applied perpendicularly from the apical toward the coronal direction at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min until failure occurred (Fig. 1). Failure was identified by a sudden drop in the force-displacement curve generated by the device software. The maximum force to dislodgement (N) was recorded for each section. The bond strength and bonding area were calculated using the formula = 2πrh A (r: post radius, h: section thickness). The PBS in megapascals (MPa) was calculated by dividing the maximum breaking force by the lateral surface area of the post [31].

Assessment of bonding quality at the cement-dentin interface (SEM)

In order to assess the morphology of the cement-dentin interface, one additional specimen was prepared in each group. One-millimeter sections were made from the middle part of the root, washed with deionized water, ultrasonicated for 10 min, mounted on metal rods, and gold sputter coated. Then, images were obtained by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; MIRA3 TESCAN, Czech Republic) with 15 kW voltage, 14.48 mm working distance, and x100 magnification. Note that this qualitative evaluation was performed on one specimen per group to illustrate representative interface features.

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were analyzed using Python version 3.11, the Pandas (data management), SciPy, Stats (statistical tests) and Matplotlib (graph drawing) libraries. First, the data were imported from the Excel file into the Python environment and then divided into groups based on cement type and diode laser irradiation. For each group, the mean and standard deviation of PBS (in MPa) were calculated. Considering the normal distribution of data as shown by the Shapiro-Wilk test (P > 0.05) and homogeneity of the variances as confirmed by the Levene test (P > 0.05), one-way ANOVA and modified Bonferroni test were used. the intensity of the between-group effect was also calculated using the eta ratio (Partial Eta Squared) to determine the contribution of the group factor to the total variance.

SEM results

Figure 2 shows SEM photomicrographs of the dentin-cement interface in the study groups. SEM images of the cement-dentin interface of the bonded specimens showed that in the ER cement group (Fig. 1A, B), the cement adaptation and integrity appeared to be better and more favorable, and the bonding interface was more homogenous. While in the SA cement groups (Fig. 1C, D), there were more obvious discontinuities at the cement-dentin interface. The morphology of the canal dentin substrate was similar in the laser and non-laser groups, and there was no observable signs or thick deposits at the dentinal tubule openings.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of diode laser irradiation on the PBS of fiber posts to root dentin using ER and SA resin cements. The results demonstrated that ER cement produced significantly higher PBS values (7.744 ± 0.547 MPa without laser; 7.598 ± 0.971 MPa with laser) compared to SA cement (5.604 ± 1.468 MPa without laser; 5.478 ± 0.880 MPa with laser). Diode laser irradiation had no significant effect on PBS within either cement type. Thus, the null first hypothesis that resin cement type has no significant effect on PBS was rejected and the second hypothesis that diode laser irradiation has no significant effect on PBS of fiber post to root dentin was accepted.

These findings align with several studies reporting superior PBS with ER systems over SA cements for fiber post cementation. For instance, Theodor et al. [32] and Kececi et al. [19] found that ER adhesives created deeper hybrid layers through effective dentin demineralization, enhancing micromechanical retention. In contrast, SA cements often leave residual smear layers, limiting hybrid layer formation and penetration [33]. After 10,000 thermal cycles, this difference was pronounced, as SA cements’ hydrophilic components may promote water sorption and hydrolytic degradation over time [32, 34].

Qualitative SEM analysis of one specimen per group supported these results, showing more continuous cement-dentin interfaces with ER cements compared to discontinuities observed with SA cements. However, these observations are preliminary due to the limited number of specimens examined and the subjective nature of SEM imaging.

Regarding diode laser irradiation, the lack of effect on PBS is consistent with reports that 980-nm diode lasers primarily provide antimicrobial benefits without altering dentin structure or smear layer removal when used alone [24, 31, 35, 36]. SEM images revealed no morphological differences in canal dentin between laser-treated and untreated groups, further indicating minimal thermal impact under the applied parameters (2.5 W, continuous wave, 20 s per cycle). This contrasts with studies using pulsed modes or combined irrigants, where lasers enhanced bonding [28, 37]. Recent investigations reinforce this neutrality for diode lasers in continuous mode. For example, a 2025 study on post space conditioning found that diode laser activation did not significantly improve PBS of fiber posts cemented with SA cement (p >0.05), though sealer type (e.g., MTA Fillapex) had a greater influence. Similarly, a 2024 pull-out bond strength evaluation reported a significant increase with 1 W continuous-wave 980 nm irradiation (90.04 ± 30.45 MPa vs. 62.95 ± 22.09 MPa, p = 0.021), but noted alignment with prior push-out data showing no effect. These variations may stem from test methodology (pull-out vs. push-out) or lower power settings, highlighting the need for standardized protocols [38, 39].

In contrast, other laser types like Er, Cr: YSGG have shown more consistent enhancements. A 2024 in vitro study using Er, Cr: YSGG (1.25–1.75 W) on root dentin significantly boosted PBS in the apical third (p < 0.001) by removing smear layers and opening tubules, with mixed failure modes indicating stronger retention and no thermal risks. Such findings suggest diode lasers may be better suited for disinfection rather than conditioning, while Er, Cr: YSGG could offer dual benefits in clinical scenarios requiring improved adhesion [40].

Clinically, prioritizing ER cements could reduce debonding risks in endodontically treated teeth, particularly under cyclic loading. The absence of adverse laser effects supports its routine use for antimicrobial efficacy pre-cementation, potentially lowering retreatment rates without compromising retention.

Although conducted in vitro—without the biological, chemical, and physical challenges of the oral environment—this study serves as valuable preliminary research. Our methodology demonstrated that conventional resin cements with adhesives yield stronger long-term bonds to root dentin compared to SA cements. However, using resin cements from different manufacturers (Huge Dental for ER and DMG for SA) may introduce variability in composition and performance, potentially limiting direct comparability; future studies employing products from a single manufacturer would address this. Diode laser irradiation, using the parameters tested here, had no adverse effect on PBS while providing established antimicrobial benefits for root canal disinfection [13, 30, 31]. Thus, it could be a compatible adjunct in protocols prior to fiber post cementation. Nonetheless, further clinical studies are needed to confirm these findings under real-world conditions and evaluate long-term outcomes. Limitations of this in vitro study include reliance on thermocycling to simulate intraoral conditions, which inadequately replicates temperature fluctuations, pH changes, and occlusal forces that may influence cement performance over time. Additionally, the effects of diode laser irradiation on root dentin and fiber post bond strength warrant investigation using varied parameters (e.g., power, wavelength, and irradiation cycles), as well as in different root regions. Finally, while SEM offered illustrative insights into interface integrity, the examination of only one specimen per group restricts detection of inter-specimen variability and precludes statistical analysis; larger-scale SEM evaluations are recommended.

Conclusion

Despite these constraints, the results suggest ER cements as preferable for optimizing long-term fiber post retention. Diode laser irradiation appears safe for root canal disinfection prior to cementation without compromising PBS. Future research should employ larger samples, clinical simulations, and fracture mode assessments to validate these findings. Additionally, comparative trials across laser wavelengths and irrigation adjuncts could clarify optimal protocols for enhancing PBS in diverse clinical contexts.
