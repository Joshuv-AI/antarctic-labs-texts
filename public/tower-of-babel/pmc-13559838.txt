Respiratory pandemic risk in the Anthropocene: A One Health framework and GISRS+ agenda
Authors: Jae Min Lee, Hye Ok Kim, Youn-Jung Kim, Manish Kumar Singh, Sung Soo Kim, Seung Geun Yeo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent epidemics and pandemics caused by respiratory viruses, alongside the animal panzootic spread of highly pathogenic avian influenza A(H5Nx), have become a structural feature of the Anthropocene, yet responses remain largely reactive. This review integrates findings from WHO's Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System (GISRS) and related surveillance data (2000–2024), epidemiological studies of influenza A virus, SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, and H5Nx, and One Health literature. We examine major groups of respiratory viruses and identify mismatches between risk and surveillance by focusing on spillover potential from animal hosts, human-to-human transmission and its controllability, and Anthropocene characteristics that increase epidemic risk. The analysis indicated that SARS-related coronaviruses and influenza A viruses, particularly H5Nx, are among the leading candidates based on currently available evidence because they have large reservoirs in animal hosts and spillover to humans is highly probable. The previous presymptomatic spread of SARS-CoV-2 and recent mammalian adaptation in H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b highlight limitations of the traditional symptom-based and pathogen-specific surveillance system. Spillover events tend to occur in tropical and subtropical regions in low- and middle-income countries, but most genomic surveillance is in high-income countries. We propose interventions that address the upstream, midstream, downstream processes of epidemics. Upstream interventions are primary prevention measures related to land use, livestock, wildlife, and urban environments; midstream interventions are GISRS+-based pathogen-agnostic genomic and metagenomic early warning systems triggered by One Health; and downstream interventions include vaccines, antivirals, non-pharmaceutical interventions, and engineering with equity-centred global governance and sustainable financing.Keywords: Respiratory pandemics, Influenza A(H5Nx), SARS-related coronaviruses, Anthropocene, One Health

Introduction

Over the past two decades, the world has experienced a rapid succession of major respiratory events, including regional epidemics (SARS-CoV-1 in 2002, MERS-CoV in 2012) and global pandemics (A(H1N1)pdm09 in 2009, SARS-CoV-2 in 2019). In parallel, clade 2·3·4·4b H5N1 of the highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) virus has evolved from causing large outbreaks in birds to becoming a sustained panzootic threat that has infected diverse mammalian species worldwide, including Antarctica [1]. Each epidemic led healthcare systems to renew their commitments, increased attention by the media and funding for research, and ambitious pledges by governments to strengthen their preparedness. Yet these efforts have mostly waned once the immediate danger subsided, so that current surveillance systems and structural reforms are incomplete or fragmented. This recurrent “panic–neglect” cycle has weakened our collective defense against future pandemics [2].

The threat posed by respiratory viruses in the 21st century is the culmination of evolutionary and epidemiological processes from the past century. The 1918 influenza A(H1N1) pandemic, which affected an estimated one-third of the global population and caused at least 50 million deaths, was the most devastating public health event of recent times, and probably occurred after several years of viral adaptation in human or swine hosts [3], [4]. More recent events, including the apparent laboratory- or vaccine-associated re-emergence of the H1N1 virus in 1977, demonstrates that respiratory pandemics occur due to a combination of natural selection, ecological opportunity, and anthropogenic interventions (Fig. 1A) [5]. At the same time, analyses of emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) indicated that more than 60% of these diseases are zoonoses, predominantly from wildlife, and that high-risk regions in low-latitude “hotspots” are associated with rapid changes in land use, urbanisation, population density, livestock systems, population mobility, and use of wildlife (Fig. 1B, C) [6], [7], [8]. Thus, the patterns of these diverse events—whether epidemics, pandemics, or panzootics—caused by SARS-CoV-1, A(H1N1)pdm09, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, and the current H5Nx viruses resemble the patterns of the 1918 pandemic and the 1977 H1N1 event [4], [5]. This suggests that the risk of respiratory pandemics is an intrinsic part of the socio-ecological fabric of the Anthropocene.

The impact of recent pandemics has extended beyond infection and mortality, in that they have led to widespread disruptions of many health systems and societies. A rapid increase in the number of patients during an epidemic adversely affects routine care, elective procedures, cancer screening, immunisation programmes, and management of chronic diseases, all of which contributed to an increased indirect mortality [9], [10]. Non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), such as lockdowns, closure of schools and workplaces, and restrictions of mobility, have disrupted education, careers, and social networks, and also threatened the status of patients with mental-health problems. In the USA alone, the combined economic and health losses from the COVID-19 epidemic were estimated to be about US$16 trillion [11], [12]. Thus, these events should not be viewed as isolated events caused by pathogens, but as syndemics, in which an epidemic interacts with multiple chronic diseases and social factors (Fig. 1D).

This review of major respiratory events from 2000 to 2024—encompassing human pandemics (A(H1N1)pdm09 and SARS-CoV-2), regional epidemics (SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV), and animal panzootics with sporadic zoonotic spillover (H5Nx)—has three primary aims. The first aim is to synthesise the temporal, spatial, and host-range patterns of diseases caused by SARS-CoV-1, A(H1N1)pdm09, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, and the H5Nx viruses using the most recent evidence on spillover pressures and the limitations of existing global surveillance. The second aim is to reinterpret the patterns of these diseases by considering human–animal–environment interactions to develop a qualitative risk framework for respiratory epidemics during the Anthropocene. The third aim is to build on this framework by proposing principles for the future, in which the existing Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System (GISRS) of the WHO evolves into a pathogen-agnostic GISRS+ platform that integrates surveillance of multiple pathogens with interventions that decrease the risk and increase equity-centred governance. Distinct from broader One Health reviews, we focus on diseases caused by respiratory viruses and explicitly compare the risk of pandemics and measures that can be used to control the influenza A virus (including H5Nx) and SARS-related coronaviruses during the Anthropocene.

Previous reviews have predominantly addressed respiratory pandemics through isolated lenses—focusing either on the molecular evolution of specific pathogens or on generalized One Health concepts. However, a critical gap remains in translating these ecological and evolutionary insights into actionable global surveillance strategies. The primary contribution of this Review is to bridge this gap. By directly integrating upstream Anthropocene drivers with the established WHO GISRS infrastructure, we present a unified framework that shifts the paradigm from reactive, pathogen-specific responses to proactive, pathogen-agnostic interventions.

Methods for literature search and evidence synthesis

Although structured as a narrative review, we employed a defined literature search strategy to ensure a comprehensive and transparent synthesis. We searched the PubMed database, WHO official repositories, and GISAID for peer-reviewed articles, epidemiological reports, and genomic data published between January 2000 and March 2024. The search terms included combinations of “respiratory pandemics,”“One Health,”“influenza A,”“H5Nx,”“SARS-related coronaviruses,”“genomic surveillance,” and “Anthropocene.” Articles were included if they provided empirical epidemiological data on spillover events, evaluated global surveillance systems, or analyzed the environmental drivers of zoonotic emergence. Non-English articles and highly localized clinical case reports lacking global surveillance implications were excluded. Data extraction focused on the temporal and spatial trends of virus emergence and systemic gaps in surveillance. Accordingly, the multidimensional framework and the GISRS+ agenda proposed in this review are based on a conceptual interpretation of the synthesized empirical evidence.

Global burden of respiratory virus diseases

Using 2019 estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) showed that age-standardised mortality (deaths per 100,000) from lower respiratory infections (LRIs) was greatest in sub-Saharan Africa, south Asia, and parts of south-east Asia, and less in the high-income regions of western Europe, North America, and east Asia (Fig. 2A) [13], [14]. This pattern can be attributed to differences in population age structure; availability of vaccinations, antibiotics, primary care, and oxygen-therapy; and the burdens of poverty, undernutrition, and chronic cardiopulmonary disease. Longitudinal WHO data on influenza-associated respiratory mortality (2000−2023) also showed persistently higher burdens in Africa and Oceania, and disruptions around 2020 (during the COVID-19 pandemic) demonstrated that a novel pathogen can amplify weaknesses in existing health systems and increase mortality due to LRIs (Fig. 2B). The risk of LRI mortality therefore disproportionately affects structurally disadvantaged populations.

(B) Age-standardised influenza-associated respiratory mortality per 100,000 in different WHO regions from 2000 to 2023, showing persistently high levels in Africa and Oceania.

Thus, the seasonal influenza A and B viruses and the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) have predictable effects on morbidity and mortality. Data from the WHO and national surveillance systems indicated that seasonal influenza causes approximately 290,000 to 650,000 respiratory deaths annually, mostly in older adults and those with cardiovascular or chronic respiratory diseases [13], [15]. Hospitalisation and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from these viruses are greatest among children younger than 5 years, pregnant women, and those who are immunocompromised. RSV is also a leading cause of severe LRI among children younger than 5 years, and was estimated to cause about 33 million episodes of acute LRI, 3·2 million hospitalisations, and 60,000 to 120,000 deaths annually [16]. These seasonal viruses thus form a baseline of mortality, hospitalisations, and high DALYs, and sporadic pandemics caused by other novel respiratory viruses can greatly increase the overall burden.

Since 2000, data on the emergence of three novel coronaviruses (whether resulting in regional epidemics or a full pandemic) demonstrated that they amplified the effects of other respiratory pathogens. SARS-CoV-1 (2002−2003) affected just over 8000 individuals and led to about 770 deaths in 29 countries (case-fatality ratio [CFR]: ∼9·6%), and was spread along airline networks and among health-care facilities; because infectiousness peaked largely after symptom onset, the epidemic was contained using the classical public health measures of case isolation, contact tracing, and intensified surveillance [17], [18]. MERS-CoV (2012) affected several thousand patients and led to more than 900 deaths, mainly in the Middle East (CFR: 34–36%). This outbreak was caused by repeated zoonotic spillover from dromedary camels and nosocomial amplification, rather than sustained human-to-human transmission [19], [20]. By contrast, SARS-CoV-2 was first reported in Wuhan, China in late 2019, and it spread to all WHO regions within a few months. Initial studies estimated the basic reproduction number (R₀) was about 2·0 to 3·0, and an analysis of 72,314 patients in China estimated the overall CFR was about 2·3%, with a much higher fatality rate among older adults and people with cardiovascular disease [21], [22], [23]. Because infectiousness of SARS-CoV-2 begins several days before symptom onset and many secondary cases develop during this presymptomatic phase, symptom-based surveillance alone was insufficient. SARS-CoV-2 therefore infected millions of people, caused millions of deaths, and also led to a sharp global increase in LRI-related excess mortality [14], [24].

In parallel, the broader group of highly pathogenic avian influenza H5Nx viruses has undergone a substantial shift in habitat, driven most notably by specific H5N1 lineages. Since the first human H5N1 infections in Hong Kong (1997), H5N1-lineage viruses have caused intermittent large outbreaks in poultry and wild birds throughout Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Africa, and sporadic human infections suggest this virus has the potential to cause a pandemic [25]. The emergence of the H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4 (especially the 2.3.4.4b lineage) marked a new phase in this panzootic. This clade was initially detected in east Asia, and then spread along migratory bird flyways and via the global poultry trade to Europe, Africa, and the Americas. By the early 2020s, this virus was responsible for infections in wild birds, domestic poultry, and mammals in many countries, even Antarctica, indicating it was an animal panzootic [1]. Recent outbreaks have occurred in foxes, mink, marine mammals (seals and sea lions), and in large dairy cattle herds of North America, and there is evidence of mammal-to-mammal transmission [26]. Molecular analyses have repeatedly identified mutations, such as PB2 E627K, D701N, and T271A in the Polymerase Basic Protein 2 (PB2) of this virus. These are canonical markers of mammalian adaptation and were present in samples from mammals and wild birds [27], [28]. Human H5Nx infections remain rare, and are typically caused by exposure to infected poultry or contaminated environments, and there is no evidence of sustained human-to-human transmission. Nonetheless, recurrent spillovers from avian and mammalian hosts suggest that human-adapted variants may evolve. The current H5Nx panzootic illustrates how high-density livestock production, changes in land-use, and climate change can alter the geographic range and host spectrum of respiratory pathogens.

Candidate pathogens for the next respiratory pandemic and qualitative risk assessment

To accurately assess these intersecting challenges within our multidimensional framework, it is crucial to clearly distinguish the stages of epidemic progression and their associated terminology. In this context, the viral agent itself acts as the hazard. Spillover denotes the initial probability of emergence—specifically, the cross-species transmission from an animal host to a human. Following this emergence, a pathogen's pandemic potential (or threat) is defined by the probability of it achieving sustained human-to-human transmission. However, the ultimate pandemic risk (the expected global impact) is not determined by the pathogen alone; it is profoundly amplified by the socio-ecological and health system vulnerabilities structural to the Anthropocene. Our systemic capacity to anticipate, detect, and mitigate this risk constitutes our global preparedness.

During the Anthropocene, the substantial background burden of LRIs from seasonal influenza and RSV is increasingly overlaid by episodic emergence of diseases caused by viruses such as SARS-CoV-1, A(H1N1)pdm09, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, and H5Nx. These combined threats have made respiratory pandemics a major threat to health and societal systems. A useful framework to assess the risk of a human pandemic uses a five-stage process of pathogen emergence (Fig. 3A): circulation confined to animals (stage 1), sporadic animal-to-human transmission (stage 2), limited human-to-human transmission (stage 3), sustained circulation in humans and animals (stage 4), and exclusive circulation among humans as a seasonal or endemic pathogen (stage 5) [29]. For respiratory viruses, these stages correspond to circulation in animal reservoirs, such as wild birds, poultry, bats, or camels (stage 1); spillover at farms and live-animal markets (stage 2); local clusters in isolated households, workplaces, and healthcare facilities (stage 3); wider dissemination in the community and internationally (stage 4); and endemic human transmission (stage 5). The transition from one stage to the next depends on the pathogen load and mutation rate in the reservoirs, the frequency of human exposure, the speed of adaptation to a new host, and the capacity of surveillance, clinical care, and public health systems to prevent the spread of disease [30], [31].

These are affected by the dynamics of the pathogen reservoir, frequency of contacts, within-host adaptations, and public health responses. The controllability of an outbreak cannot be inferred from the basic reproduction number (R₀) alone, because transmission that occurs before symptom onset or from asymptomatic infections (θ) must also be considered. When θ is low, pathogens with relatively high R₀ can potentially be contained by rapid isolation of cases, contact tracing, and travel restrictions. Conversely, for infections such as influenza, in which a substantial proportion of transmission occurs during the presymptomatic phase or from clinically unrecognized mild infections, classical case-based measures are rarely sufficient; population-level interventions, including vaccination, antivirals, and structured NPIs, are necessary [18]. An independent assessment of the 2009 A(H1N1)pdm09 pandemic concluded that, despite the modest virulence of this virus and the availability of pre-existing influenza surveillance and vaccine platforms, the WHO pandemic alert system, surveillance and information-sharing, access to vaccines and antivirals, and risk communication were inadequate [32]. Taken together, these results suggest that three key variables are related to the emergence of a pandemic: (i) frequency of spillover from human–animal interactions; (ii) epidemiological parameters, such as R₀, timing of infectiousness, and the proportion of asymptomatic transmission; and (iii) the efficacy of surveillance, health-care, and policy systems. SpillOver models operationalise the first variable by scoring newly detected animal viruses using traits associated with the risk of human infection, and highlights lineages with high spillover potential [33]. The second variable is exemplified by the COVID-19 pandemic, in which a substantial fraction of transmission occurred during the asymptomatic phase, making it problematic to implement case isolation and contact tracing. The third variable is related to the characteristics of the Anthropocene: changes in climate and land-use, loss of biodiversity, increased urbanisation and global mobility; these changes have altered the interactions among humans, animals, and the environment, and increased the risk for the emergence of a respiratory pandemic by a novel pathogen [24], [34].

The influenza A viruses (especially H5Nx) and SARS-related coronaviruses appear to be leading candidates for the next respiratory pandemic. The influenza A virus was responsible for previous pandemics, demonstrating it has a high potential for spillover and efficient human-to-human transmission; this virus can also infect a broad range of animals that form stable reservoirs, and it has a segmented genome enabling reassortment. High-density poultry production and globalised livestock and food chains increase the risk of dissemination of the influenza A virus, and this virus is among the riskiest viruses in SpillOver models [33]. Within this genus, H5Nx viruses have high pathogenicity and a very broad host spectrum, indicating a greater risk for development of sustained human-to-human transmission [35]. The SARS-related coronaviruses (sarbecoviruses) also have a high risk of spillover because of their extensive genetic diversity in horseshoe bats, history of spillover into humans or intermediate hosts, and the capacity for high transmissibility and global spread, as in SARS-CoV-2. The high similarity (>96%) of the genomes of the early Wuhan SARS-CoV-2 isolates and a bat-derived sarbecovirus underscores the significance of this reservoir and its potential for re-emergence [36]. Once a virus achieves sustained human-to-human transmission and appreciable presymptomatic spread, symptom-based detection and isolation are insufficient interventions (Fig. 3B) [24].

By contrast, the seasonal influenza A viruses (H1N1 and H3N2), influenza B virus, and RSV are now adapted to humans and pose risk mainly through their seasonal outbreaks and interactions with other respiratory viruses. Long-term surveillance has identified fewer than expected co-infections because of the discordant seasonality between the influenza and common cold viruses (such as rhinoviruses). This suggests that population-level virus–virus interactions can affect virus dissemination and the need for strategies that improve seasonal forecasting, selection of appropriate vaccines, and planning for surges rather than classical control of emergence [37]. Finally, practical risk is greatest in locations where there is a high risk of spillover and a weak health infrastructure: low and middle income countries (LMICs) in tropical and subtropical regions, rapidly expanding urban and agricultural frontiers, and regions with intensive livestock farming. Global analysis of EIDs showed that spillover and locations with rapid dissemination are in low-latitude regions with high biodiversity, rapid changes in land use, population growth, and intensive livestock production. The risk of an epidemic is greater when ecological conditions that favour an epidemic co-occur with a weak health infrastructure [6], [38]. Paradoxically, high-income countries ecological conditions less favourable for an epidemic and more surveillance resources, and they also perform more research on their own populations [7]. This asymmetry, in which “resources are scarcest where risk is greatest”, underscores the need to redirect preventive interventions to regions with greater risk.

Next-generation surveillance of respiratory pathogens: GISRS+, One Health, and equity

Since 1952, the GISRS has provided the backbone for the global monitoring and responses to seasonal and pandemic influenza and HPAI via its network of National Influenza Centres, WHO reference laboratories, and the Pandemic Influenza Preparedness (PIP) framework, which established protocols for sharing information about viruses, technology transfer, and equitable allocation of vaccines and diagnostics [32], [39]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, this infrastructure facilitated extensive diagnostic screening, and GISAID, which was initially designed to address influenza outbreaks, became a global hub for near real-time sharing of millions of SARS-CoV-2 sequences [40], [41]. While the WHO has recently proposed a GISRS+ roadmap that extends this traditional human-centric system to RSV, SARS-CoV-2, and other emerging respiratory viruses [42], the expanded GISRS+ model proposed in this review integrates entirely new One Health components into this architecture. Specifically, we propose that GISRS+ act as an interoperable intelligence hub that explicitly links existing national human clinical surveillance and GISAID data with previously siloed animal-health systems (e.g., agricultural and wildlife monitoring) and environmental surveillance programmes (such as wastewater-based epidemiology). Rather than proposing an entirely new infrastructure, the risk-based strategy proposed here builds directly upon the proven successes and collaborative networks of the existing WHO GISRS and PIP frameworks. It uses the expanded GISRS+ as a foundation to develop an intelligent system that explicitly prioritises high-risk pathogens, such as H5Nx viruses and SARS-related coronaviruses [32], [41].

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the limitations of vertical and pathogen-specific surveillance, and the feasibility of integrated surveillance of multiple respiratory pathogens [43]. In many countries, sentinel sites and laboratories designed for influenza-like illness (ILI) and SARS infection (SARSI) were repurposed for multiplex RT-PCR for testing of multiple pathogens, and this enabled concurrent assessment of seasonal patterns and the risk in different populations for multiple viruses [44]. Next-generation systems should coordinate real-time genomic surveillance with diagnostics to enable early detection of new variants and transmission pathways, and should then integrate this information with clinical and epidemiological data [45]. However, the previous genomic surveillance of SARS-CoV-2 was also impacted by inequities: many LMICs had limited sequencing capacity and delays in reporting [46]. High-risk populations, such as informal workers and residents of long-term care facilities, were under-represented in sentinel systems, underscoring the need for a system with improved standardisation, greater equity, sustainable financing, and larger capacity [43]. The current influenza surveillance system should therefore be expanded so that it can perform more assays, and transform influenza-like illness (ILI) and severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) sentinel networks into pathogen-agnostic early-warning systems that consider diagnostic, genomic, clinical, and epidemiological data. These systems should be designed to detect high-risk pathogens, particularly influenza A viruses and sarbecoviruses, in regions where Anthropocene changes have most altered the environment (Fig. 4A).

Integrated GISRS+ respiratory surveillance and the geographic mismatch of risk and surveillance. (A) GISRS+ consists of integrated national and regional systems that collect diagnostic, genomic, and epidemiological data from ILI/SARI sentinel sites, respiratory labs and national influenza centres (e.g., multiplex RT-PCR, WGS), animal and wildlife surveillance (e.g., H5Nx, SARS-related CoVs), and surveillance of the environment and wastewater, culminating in a large, pathogen-agnostic system for the standardised sharing of data and early warning of an outbreak. (B) Geographic mismatch of risk and surveillance, in which many tropical and subtropical regions have the highest risk of pathogen emergence, but the lowest levels genomic and One Health surveillance.

Preparedness for respiratory pandemics also requires detection of early signals of risk, such as novel interactions among humans, animals, and the environment, to prevent widespread human transmission. Large recent H5N1 outbreaks in harbour and grey seals off the New England coast showed that marine mammals can act as evolutionary amplifiers, in which novel mutations enable adaptation of a virus to mammals [47]. Routine sampling and genomic analysis of migratory bird flyways, regions with intensive livestock, and marine mammals could provide an early warning for high-risk influenza viruses. Environmental surveillance, particularly wastewater-based epidemiology (WBE), is a complementary system that can identify asymptomatic infections in medically underserved regions. For example, WBE in the UK detected multiple lineages of SARS-CoV-2 RNA in airport and aircraft wastewater, demonstrating that potential cross-border transmission of pathogens and introduction of new variants can be monitored without tracking individual travellers [48]. These data should be integrated within a unified One Health intelligence system.

The proposed system consists of the real-time integration of genomic, diagnostic, and clinical data from humans, animals, and the environment on shared platforms for assessment of risk and decision-making. This aligns with recent calls to include genomic surveillance of infectious diseases and antimicrobial resistance in the public-health infrastructure [45]. Yet global EID analyses show that regions with spillover and early amplification mostly occur in tropical and subtropical LMICs due to their high biodiversity, rapid changes in land use, high population growth, and intensive livestock production [6], [38]. In contrast, most surveillance resources and genomic data are from high-income countries [7], [43], [46]. An independent panel reviewing the response to COVID-19 therefore recommended redefining pandemic preparedness as a global public good and creating new financing and governance mechanisms with the sharing of burdens to improve surveillance and responses in LMICs [49]. Next-generation surveillance systems should emphasize equity while developing integrated diagnostic, genomic, and epidemiological systems that increase capacity while protecting data sovereignty in high-risk and underserved regions by linking these systems to One Health and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance (Fig. 4B) [43], [45].

Next-generation strategies to improve preparedness for respiratory pandemics: early warning, response, prevention, and governance

The preparedness for respiratory pandemics must shift from reactive crisis management to anticipatory and system-level strategies that combine prevention measures, intelligent early warnings, precise responses, and equity-centred governance. Experiences with outbreaks of the SARS-CoV-2 and H5Nx viruses show that pathogen-specific and reactive surveillance are inadequate [49], [50]. Next-generation systems should instead operate as multilayered intelligence platforms that integrate data on the interactions among humans, animals, and the environment with genomic and epidemiological data [45]. Clinical surveillance should evolve into a precision public health measure. This should include routine multiplex respiratory panels for detection of influenza viruses, SARS-CoV-2, RSV, and other key viruses at ILI/SARI sentinel sites to enable early recognition of off-season epidemics and atypical age-distribution patterns. Targeted surveillance that considers real-time genomic data in high-risk settings (e.g., intensive care units and long-term care facilities) can be used as a “last line of defence” for detecting new variants or novel pathogens [44], [45]. One Health-based animal and environmental signals should be used to guide public health actions using pre-agreed protocols. These protocols should include detection of mutations that enable adaptation to mammals in H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b and other emerging H5Nx viruses, or unusual mortality in poultry, wild birds, or mammals. Detection of such mutations should serve as a critical trigger for intensified human surveillance in affected areas, and abnormal increases in viral load or evidence of new lineages from WBE should be integrated with clinical and epidemiological data to enable rapid risk assessment and proportionate public-health action [35], [47], [48].

Decentralised genomic networks and metagenomic detection must become core components of this new system. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) should be deployed rapidly when there are clusters of unexplained pneumonia, mass mortality events in animals, or atypical severe respiratory diseases. These networks can be built in diverse regions to prevent the “blind spots” that occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, when low sequencing coverage and long reporting delays in many LMICs hampered variant detection [45], [46]. Adding metagenomic approaches that are not restricted to predefined panels, can provide a broad “catch-all” layer for detecting new pathogens [51]. The integration of genomic and metagenomic networks with clinical, animal, and environmental surveillance data can provide an intelligent early-warning system for detecting new pathogens and employing interventions to prevent widespread dissemination in human populations.

The combined use of an early warning system with vaccines, antivirals, and NPIs has the potential to save many lives. Proactive deployment of vaccines and therapeutics is also needed. These interventions should move beyond variant-specific vaccines to universal and pan-pathogen vaccines that target the broader sarbecovirus clade or all subtypes of the influenza A virus to provide broad and durable protection that is resistant to antigenic change [52]. Programmes for early use of oral antivirals should be strengthened and coupled with real-time surveillance of resistance in high-risk pathogens, particularly H5Nx and related viruses, to preserve therapeutic effectiveness. NPIs should be redesigned as precision tools that reduce transmission while preserving social and economic activity. In light of the high costs of widespread lockdowns, future NPIs should prioritise indoor air quality and ventilation standards to prevent infections, and transform buildings into tools of infection control by use of modern ventilation, filtration, and disinfection systems [53]. Using the risk matrix described above, deployment of NPIs and infrastructure investments should be prioritised for highly vulnerable regions and populations. This integrated One Health surveillance strategy increases the efficiency of financial investments by pooling resources and reducing duplicative efforts across the human, animal, and environmental health sectors. Furthermore, it promotes equity among populations by ensuring that vulnerable regions and LMICs gain timely, shared access to critical early-warning data and diagnostic capacity.

Primary prevention must also be recognised as a structural pillar of respiratory security. If vaccines and therapeutics are for “fire-fighting”, then reducing upstream risks associated with the Anthropocene, such as changes in climate, land-use, and livestock production, are for “fire-prevention”. Biosecurity governance at ecological interfaces and in livestock systems should be redesigned because intensive industrial farming can facilitate the introduction, spread, and evolution of pathogenic viruses. The risk of spillover can be decreased by regulation of stocking densities, strengthened biosecurity and biocontainment measures, and phased closure of wildlife trade and live-animal markets [54]. In parallel, halting deforestation and restoring degraded ecosystems is a way to restore the natural barriers that prevent the spread of pathogens because these barriers reduce the contact of wildlife with humans and decrease the development of zoonotic hotspots [55]. The structural resilience of urban and community spaces should be improved by reducing overcrowded housing, standardising air quality and ventilation design in public transport and indoor public spaces, and building a robust remote work and education infrastructure that can function during crises. Multiple analyses indicated that the annual costs of curbing deforestation, regulating the wildlife trade, and strengthening One Health-based surveillance are very small compared to the economic losses and mortality caused by pandemics. Collectively, the implementation of primary prevention measures, intelligent early warning systems, targeted responses, and equity-centred governance can address upstream, midstream, and downstream processes that improve preparedness for respiratory pandemics (Fig. 5).

Strategic framework to improve preparedness for respiratory pandemics at the upstream, midstream, and downstream levels. Upstream risk reduction consists of improved management of land use and livestock, regulations that address wildlife-trade and high-risk interactions, and resilient urban design and indoor ventilation. Midstream risk reduction consists of an intelligent early warning system, in which GISRS+ provides surveillance of multiple pathogens, genomics and metagenomics data for pathogen detection, and One Health data from animals and wastewater is linked to human data. Downstream risk reduction consists of targeted responses and mitigation, such as vaccines, early oral antivirals, precision NPIs, and engineering and structural changes (ventilation/HVAC). All three domains rely on equitable global governance and financing (WHO Pandemic Agreement, Pandemic Fund, PABS, increased capacity of LMICs).

Finally, preparedness for a respiratory pandemic should be treated as a global public good that is essential for human survival, rather than a discretionary national policy. The COVID-19 pandemic showed that fragmented and country-centred responses were insufficient to curb international spread and they also undermined equity in global health security. There is need for an international pandemic treaty that is centred on the WHO Pandemic Agreement because International Health Regulations (IHR) have limitations in the provision of early warnings, information sharing, and equitable responses. Instead, a new legal instrument should cover prevention, preparedness, and response that requires information sharing and fair allocation of resources [56]. Treaty-level commitments must be translated into concrete procedures for a pathogen access and benefit-sharing (PABS) system, a platform for research into future pathogens, predictive integrated surveillance (“pathogen Surv-Radar”), and decentralised but quality-assured manufacturing networks [57]. Links with the World Bank Pandemic Fund, CEPI, Gavi, and related initiatives will enable the development of an effective global shield for protection from respiratory pandemics. Financing should shift from the traditional crisis-triggered aid towards risk-sharing and more efficient investments. A shift from short-term, project-based support to medium- and long-term funding that is predictable is required to sustain upstream prevention and routine surveillance. Laboratory networks, surveillance systems, and community infrastructures established for AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria should be designed as multi-use assets that support disease-specific services and pandemic preparedness, and that also improve financial efficiency and resilience [58]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many LMICs repurposed staff, laboratories, and data systems designed for AIDS, TB, and malaria. This initially reduced programme services, but the lasting gains in diagnostic and surveillance capacity demonstrated the benefit of these modifications.

Conclusions

Recent health crises — most notably the COVID-19 pandemic and the continuing panzootic of HPAI H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b viruses — show that respiratory pandemics are now closely associated with the Anthropocene, because they are driven by more intensive livestock production, rapid changes in land use, and increased global connectivity. SARS-related coronaviruses and H5Nx viruses are among the leading candidates based on currently available evidence, and reactive, pathogen-specific responses are no longer sufficient responses. Instead, protection from respiratory pathogens requires three strategic shifts. First, traditional surveillance must be transformed into intelligent surveillance that is pathogen-agnostic. Thus, GISRS should evolve into GISRS+, a system that integrates multiplex diagnostics, real-time genomic and metagenomic data, and One Health indicators to detect known high-risk pathogens and unknown pathogens. Second, the global governance system must be equitable, with appropriate and consistent financing, prompt sharing of data about pathogens, and improved surveillance and responses in LMICs. Third, crisis management and reactive responses must give way to primary prevention, restoration of ecosystems, increasing the biosecurity of livestock, regulation of wildlife trade, and improved indoor air-quality and ventilation standards. These changes consider prevention of respiratory epidemics as an essential global public good, and that decisions made now that affect surveillance, prevention, and equitable resource allocation will shape global resilience to respiratory pandemics in the future.

This review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, given its narrative nature, the qualitative pathogen ranking presented here involves considerable uncertainty and should not be interpreted as a definitive predictive model. Second, our proposed framework relies on global data that are subject to incomplete surveillance and regional reporting biases, which may skew risk assessments. Finally, while the proposed GISRS+ model offers a comprehensive theoretical architecture, it currently lacks quantitative validation in real-world settings. Future empirical studies and pilot implementations are necessary to test and refine the operational efficacy of this integrated One Health surveillance system.
