Cost-Effectiveness and Budget Impact of Cardioform versus Amplatzer and Medical Therapy Alone for Patent Foramen Ovale Closure: A UK Payer Perspective
Authors: James R Bentham, Jet Neervoort, Timon Louwsma, Lambertus F Wolters, Julie Lyon, Erik J Landaas, Mitesh Nakum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study evaluated the cost-effectiveness and budget impact of two patent foramen ovale (PFO) closure devices and medical management for the prevention of recurrent PFO-associated strokes in the UK.Patients and MethodsWe used a Markov model to simulate 1000 individuals with a history of PFO-associated stroke over a five-year time horizon. Treatment options included GORE® CARDIOFORM Septal Occluder (GSO Device), Amplatzer™ Talisman PFO Occluder (ATO Device), and Medical Therapy Alone. Effectiveness data were drawn from the REDUCE and RESPECT trials and prior cost-effectiveness analyses (CEAs). No head-to-head clinical trial comparing the ATO and GSO Device was identified; therefore, comparative clinical effectiveness estimates were derived from a matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC). Costs were assessed from a UK payer perspective (2023 GBP). Outcomes included quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), prevented strokes, incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), and budget impact.ResultsGSO Device was estimated to be cost-saving and generate 24.66 additional QALYs versus ATO Device (dominant ICER) over five years and in a cohort of 1000 patients. Compared with Medical Therapy Alone, it provided 406.66 additional QALYs at an ICER of £14,343/QALY. It also prevented 27.86 and 67.05 strokes versus ATO Device and Medical Therapy Alone, respectively.ConclusionThe use of GSO Device is a cost-effective option for the prevention of secondary PFO-associated strokes, with favorable estimated clinical outcomes. Future studies providing direct comparative evidence between GSO and ATO could further inform the relative clinical and cost-effectiveness of available PFO closure devices.

Introduction

Each year, 100,000 strokes occur in the United Kingdom (UK),1 resulting in 38,000 stroke-related deaths,2,3 with ischemic stroke comprising 85% of these cases.4 Among 1.4 million survivors in the UK,1 26% will have another stroke within five years.3 In addition, stroke incidence in the UK is expected to rise 60% from 2015 to 2035, causing the economic burden to increase from £26 billion in 2015 to £75 billion in 2035.5

Approximately 25–40% of ischemic strokes are classified as cryptogenic, an entity largely consisting of embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS) cases.6 Patent foramen ovale (PFO), an incomplete closure of the opening between the atria, present in about 25% of the general population, has been frequently identified as a possible cause of ESUS, as the opening can potentially enable an embolus to enter the systemic circulation and subsequently reach the brain.7–12 The PFO-Associated Stroke Causal Likelihood (PASCAL) risk stratification system and the Risk of Paradoxical Embolism (RoPE) score are used to estimate the likelihood that a stroke is causally related to a PFO (PFO-associated stroke).13,14 PFO-associated stroke, therefore, represents a distinct clinical entity within the broader category of cryptogenic stroke.13 This study focuses specifically on patients with PFO-associated stroke.

PFO-associated stroke management usually involves Medical Treatment (MT) Alone or in combination with percutaneous PFO closure by a transcatheter device. For patients aged 18 to 60 with a history of ischemic cryptogenic stroke and PFO anatomy, the National Clinical Guideline for Stroke in the UK and Ireland recommends percutaneous PFO closure to prevent recurrent strokes in this patient population.15 Multiple meta-analyses further support the clinical effectiveness of PFO closure over MT Alone.16,17 The current uptake of PFO closure in England was assessed by Willits et al,18 who reported 973 PFO closure procedures for the secondary prevention of stroke or TIA across 20 hospitals between October 2014 and August 2017. This demonstrates that PFO closure is routinely performed in the UK and highlights the relevance of assessing its clinical and economic implications.

The leading PFO closure devices available in the UK market are the GORE® CARDIOFORM Septal Occluder (GSO Device) from W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc., Amplatzer™ Talisman PFO Occluder (ATO Device) from Abbott, and the Occlutech® Figulla Flex II Occluder (OFO Device) from Occlutech GmbH. GSO Device and ATO Device are both FDA-approved and have demonstrated efficacy vs MT Alone in the REDUCE and RESPECT trials.19,20

In contrast, the OFO Device is not FDA-approved and has been assessed primarily in real-world and observational studies until now.21,22 The results of the ongoing OCCLUFLEX trial (NCT05069558)—which compares the OFO Device to GSO Device and ATO Device—are expected in 2026.23 In the absence of a head-to-head comparison of GSO Device and ATO Device, a matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) on the REDUCE and RESPECT trial data provided insights into the relative clinical effectiveness of these devices. The MAIC showed a non-significant trend favoring the GSO Device due to lower recurrent stroke count and fewer serious adverse events (SAEs).24 Specific attention was given to atrial fibrillation, which was found to occur less in patients treated with GSO Device compared to ATO Device (odds ratio (OR): 0.68), though this difference was not found to be statistically significant.24

The cost-effectiveness of PFO closure (both in general and for GSO Device and ATO Device vs MT alone) is well-documented.25–28 Previous studies comparing ATO and GSO Devices were manufacturer-supported.24,27,29,30 In the US, GSO Device shows greater cost-effectiveness than the ATO, yielding better patient outcomes and cost savings.29 However, similar data are absent in the UK context. Health economic data are crucial for decision-makers working within limited budgets to evaluate financial implications and guide resource allocation.

This study aims to address this gap by evaluating the comparative cost-effectiveness of GSO Device versus ATO Device, MT Alone, and the OFO Device for secondary PFO-associated stroke prevention in the UK. A secondary objective was to assess the budget impact of increased GSO Device use among eligible patients.

Materials and Methods

Comparator Inclusion

This study aimed to compare the performance of three PFO closure devices, which collectively hold the majority of market share: GSO Device, ATO Device, and OFO Device,18 as well as MT Alone. MT Alone consisted of antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation therapy (warfarin), aligning with the REDUCE and RESPECT trials.19,31 Data on GSO Device and ATO Device devices are frequently and well-reported, making their inclusion in a comparative economic evaluation feasible.19,24–27,30 To ensure inclusion of all three relevant devices currently available on the UK market, an additional targeted literature review (TLR) was conducted, focusing on OFO Device, to provide a comprehensive overview of available evidence. Methods and results of the TLR are available in Supplementary Material 1 and Supplementary Table S1. Due to the absence of published randomized controlled trial (RCT) data and missing safety data across other study types, it was not feasible to include OFO Device as a comparator in our economic evaluation.

Study Design

A cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) was conducted in Microsoft Excel to compare the clinical and economic value of the GSO Device versus the ATO Device and of the GSO Device versus MT Alone for patients with PFO-associated strokes. The CEA compared the costs and health outcomes of the three strategies from a UK payer perspective. A Markov model was used to simulate a cohort of individuals with a history of PFO-associated stroke who were assumed to have comparable baseline characteristics to individuals registered in the REDUCE trial.19 Data from the REDUCE and RESPECT trials informed the effectiveness of the three strategies,19,31 together with previous CEAs.27,30 To minimize confounding from differences in trial populations, we used a published MAIC to assess the relative effectiveness and safety outcomes for the GSO and ATO devices,24 as no direct head-to-head comparison exists. The ISPOR CHEERS 2022 checklist and NICE economic evaluation guidance were integrated into our model’s development and reporting methods.32,33 The CHEERS 2022 checklist is available in Supplementary Material 2 and Supplementary Table S2.

Model Structure

We built an eight-state Markov model based on previously published CEAs on PFO closure.25–27,30 The model structure is shown in Figure 1. After experiencing a PFO-associated stroke and receiving MT Alone, GSO Device plus MT, or ATO Device plus MT, patients enter the model in the stable post-index stroke health state and can transition to other states in subsequent model cycles. These include ischemic attack (TIA) and recurrent stroke, accompanied by corresponding post-event health states, as well as death. Recurrent strokes, in this context, refer to clinical ischemic strokes as defined in the REDUCE and RESPECT trials.19,20 Transitions from TIA, post-TIA, recurrent stroke, and post-stroke to a stable state were not feasible; after a recurrent stroke or TIA, patients move to post-stroke or post-TIA states in the next cycle. Half-cycle correction was applied to account for transitions occurring throughout each model cycle. A portion of patients become disabled after a stroke, needing nursing home care. These patients enter a “tunnel” stroke state in the disabled post-stroke category, which leads either back to the same state or to death. Patients in the disabled post-stroke state cannot transition elsewhere except to the tunnel or death, the latter being an absorbing state. We used annual transition probabilities reported by Amarenco et al to inform our model’s transitions.34 Given our 3-month cycle length, we converted these annual probabilities to three-monthly probabilities (see Supplementary Material 3). Background mortality rates were applied across all health states, and we excluded additional post-surgery mortality risk from the model to prevent exaggerated mortality rates, since the REDUCE and RESPECT trials indicated that reported deaths were unrelated to the device, procedure, or MT. Utility values for all health states are listed in Table 1. For details about the origin of health-related quality of life data, see Supplementary Material 4.

The model applied stable health-state utility values of 0.80 and 0.88 for patients receiving MT Alone and PFO closure, respectively. The incremental utility benefit associated with PFO closure was informed by studies suggesting improved QoL following PFO closure, independently of recurrent stroke reduction.26,38,39 Specifically, Mirzada et al38 reported significantly better physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, vitality, and general health among patients undergoing PFO closure compared with non-closure patients (p<0.05). Based on this evidence and following the methodology of Tirschwell et al,26 a conservative 10% utility improvement after PFO closure was assumed.

We included SAEs that occurred after device placement and assigned them to patients at the end of the first cycle of the model. Patients who received GSO Device had an estimated 3.63% chance of an SAE according to the REDUCE trial, while those treated with the ATO device had a 4.21% chance of an SAE based on the RESPECT trial.19,20 Additionally, we excluded SAEs related to MT, as MT was included across all treatment arms and thus their inclusion would not affect the comparative results. Detailed information about these SAEs and their associated costs can be found in Supplementary Material 5.

Cost Inputs

We included direct healthcare costs in our model, covering hospitalization, diagnostic imaging, MT, institutionalization, and PFO closure procedures to reflect a UK payer perspective. Data on PFO closure included device costs, device placement costs, and follow-up check costs. To accurately estimate the costs of diagnostic tests for PFO-associated stroke, we used NHS Reference Costs.40,41 Additionally, we included costs of acute treatment and hospitalization, aggregating cost figures from multiple hospital units.41,42 Post-cryptogenic ischemic stroke costs were extracted from a previous model.30 We inflated all costs to 2023 UK£ using the GDP deflator provided by HM Treasury, as recommended by NHS England.43 We assumed negligible costs for the post-TIA state, a model assumption we validated by discussions with key opinion leaders. Analytical Methods

Base Case Model

The base case adopted a UK payer perspective and employed a five-year time horizon, consistent with previous evaluations of GSO, and to limit additional uncertainty associated with extrapolating treatment effects over longer periods.27,30 We simulated a cohort of 1000 individuals with a starting age of 45 years from the UK with a history of PFO-associated stroke, aligning with previous models and clinical trials.19,27,30 We discounted both costs and health outcomes with an annual rate of 3.5% and adopted a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of £20,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY), as recommended by NICE.33

Outcomes

Health outcomes were measured in QALYs, life years (LY), and strokes prevented. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated by combining QALY with cost data and LY outcomes with cost data for competing treatments. We deemed a treatment cost-effective compared to its comparator when the ICER was below the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold; lower ICERs indicated greater cost-effectiveness. Moreover, we calculated the incremental net monetary benefit (NMB) to express the value of an intervention in monetary terms using a known WTP per QALY. We included the NMB framework because it offers advantages, including transparent comparison of multiple strategies, the ability to consider various WTP thresholds, and greater stability compared to incremental cost-effectiveness ratios, especially when differences in effectiveness are minor. A positive NMB suggests that the ICER is below the WTP threshold, indicating good value for money. Higher NMB values were preferred because they indicated greater health improvements at lower cost.

Scenario and Sensitivity Analyses

To ensure the robustness of our model and estimate the certainty around our outcomes, we conducted both a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) and a deterministic sensitivity analysis (DSA). In the PSA, we simultaneously varied 100 model parameters across 1000 iterations. For the DSA, we independently varied 101 input parameters from their base-case values to compare GSO Device versus ATO Device and GSO Device versus MT Alone. In both sensitivity analyses, we varied model parameters within appropriate ranges derived from the standard errors of each variable. When measures of uncertainty were unavailable, we established the range estimates as ±20% of the point estimate for cost data, age data, and transition probabilities, and ±5% for utility values.27,30

Additionally, we conducted scenario analyses to test critical model assumptions and assess the robustness of the base-case ICER to key parameters within the model. 21 scenarios were examined, ranging from adjusting a single parameter to simultaneously modifying multiple parameters. For example, we incorporated indirect costs to adopt a societal perspective, encompassing productivity losses from stroke or TIA (See Supplementary Material 8 and Supplementary Figure S8-1 to S8-3). We also analyzed the effect of different WTP thresholds or age-dependent stroke risk with a higher starting age on the NMB. An overview of all scenarios is available in Supplementary Material 9 (Supplementary Table S9-1).

Budget Impact Analysis

Scenarios

Budget impact analyses (BIAs) assessed healthcare expenditure changes when increasing GSO use for preventing recurrent stroke in patients with index PFO-associated stroke from a UK payer perspective. Two BIAs compared different scenarios, considering population size, eligibility, market share, and accumulating annual costs for new patients.

The first analysis evaluated the financial impact of shifting market shares by increasing the use of GSO device in combination with MT, while reducing the use of ATO Device. This analysis compared a scenario in which GSO Device in combination with MT is used in 23.7% and ATO Device in 13.0% of the population (new environment) to a scenario reflecting the current environment, where ATO Device is utilized in 23.7% and GSO Device in 13.0% of cases.

The second analysis estimated the budgetary impact of using the GSO as the PFO-closure device in combination with MT. This analysis compared a scenario in which only GSO Device is used for PFO closure (new environment), covering 36.7% of the total population, with the current climate, in which both GSO Device and ATO Device are used, covering 13.0% and 23.7% of the population, respectively. In both scenarios, a similar proportion (63.3%) of the total population is treated with MT Alone.

Input Data

To represent the current treatment landscape, we used the most recent market shares for treatments for PFO-associated stroke in the UK. The market shares of GSO Device and ATO Device were reweighted to encompass the entire market share of PFO closures, as these treatments have sufficient data available for modelling.18 In the current environment, 63.3% of patients were treated with MT Alone, 13.0% with GSO Device, and 23.7% with ATO Device.

Model Structure

To generate the BIA results, we employed the Markov structure of the CEA. We modeled a real-life scenario using a population of 4811 patients. This size was determined by combining several factors: the annual PFO-associated stroke incidence in the UK (100,000 cases),1 the percentage of index strokes (71%),44 the percentage of ischemic strokes (85%),1 the percentage of patients younger than 60 years and thus eligible for PFO-closure (25%),45 the percentage of cryptogenic strokes in patients younger than 60 (48%),46 and the PFO-attributable fraction in patients younger than 65 (66.43%).14,47 In our BIA, we assumed a consistent yearly inflow of 4811 patients.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this analysis was the budget impact from a payer perspective, assessed annually and cumulatively over 1 to 5 years. This impact was estimated by calculating the reimbursement the payer paid for medical services provided. Additionally, the number of strokes was reported as a secondary outcome for the compared scenarios.

Results

GSO Device versus ATO Device

Key results for GSO Device versus both comparators are shown in Figure 2A and B, Table 3 and Supplementary Material 10 (Supplementary Table S10). In a simulated cohort of 1000 patients over a five-year time horizon, GSO Device was estimated to be cost-saving and associated with 24.66 additional QALYs versus the ATO Device (dominant ICER). The NMB was £850,415, indicating that the GSO Device was cost-effective at a WTP threshold of £20,000. Scenario Analyses

21 scenario analyses were performed to assess how changes in key assumptions and parameters affect the model results. When comparing GSO Device and ATO device, these analyses consistently supported the original findings: GSO Device remained cost-effective relative to ATO Device across all scenarios. Compared to MT Alone, two of the 21 scenarios resulted in a negative NMB, indicating that the GSO Device was not cost-effective at a WTP of £20,000 per QALY when the model assumed a shorter time horizon and equal utility values for stable health states between MT alone and PFO closure.

The scenario analyses covered various assumptions, but here we focus on two—10-year and lifetime (until patients reach 100 years of age; 55-year) horizons—that illustrate their impact on cost-effectiveness and reflect real-world situations. These scenarios demonstrated a substantial increase in NMB for GSO Device, as shown in Figures 2A and B. Specifically, the NMB for GSO Device versus ATO Device increased to £2,951,086 at ten years and £20,060,396 over a lifetime horizon. Similarly, for GSO Device versus MT Alone, the NMB increased to £12,222,909 at ten years and £60,480,163 over a lifetime horizon. These results highlight the significant economic advantage of adopting GSO Device treatment over extended periods. Moreover, when applying extended time horizons alongside the increased risk of stroke and mortality with age, GSO Device’s cost-effectiveness further increased. An overview of all scenario analysis results is available in Supplementary Material 9 (Supplementary Table S9-2).

At a WTP threshold of £20,000 per QALY, GSO Device was the cost-effective treatment option in 81.2% of simulations compared to the ATO Device. Using GSO Device for All PFO Closures

The second BIA evaluated the difference in expenditure for UK payers over a five-year time horizon, comparing two scenarios: 1) using only GSO Device as the PFO-closure device in combination with MT (new environment), and 2) using both GSO Device and ATO Device for PFO closures (current environment). In both scenarios, 63.3% of the population received MT Alone. Table 4 presents the key results of the BIA. Over a five-year time horizon with an annual incidence of 4811 new PFO-associated strokes in patients younger than 60 years, the total costs for UK payers were £82.04 million in the current environment and £81.18 million when only using GSO Device plus MT for PFO closures, resulting in a net budget impact of -£0.86 million. PFO closure was the only cost category negatively impacting the budget, but it remained cost-saving in diagnostics, hospital and treatment, and long-term care.

Over a five-year period, treating patients according to current practice was estimated to result in a total of 1037 recurrent strokes. In contrast, when we modeled all patients eligible for PFO closure to be treated with GSO Device in combination with MT, the number of recurrent strokes was estimated to be 943. Therefore, more frequent use of GSO for PFO closure could prevent nearly 94 recurrent strokes over the five years, along with associated resource use.

Discussion

From the UK payer perspective, percutaneous PFO closure using GSO Device for the prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke in patients with PFO-associated stroke proved more cost-effective in comparison to ATO Device and MT Alone. The base case analysis demonstrated GSO Device to be dominant over ATO Device, with an additional 24.66 QALYs at lower costs. Compared to MT Alone, GSO Device was cost-effective with an ICER of £14,343 per QALY. Based on the PSA, there was an 81.2% probability of GSO Device remaining cost-effective over ATO Device and a 77.7% probability of being cost-effective over MT alone at a WTP threshold of £20,000/QALY.

Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

The CEAC from the PSA was cut off at £150,000, as the curve stabilized around a WTP threshold of £100,000, indicating that GSO Device was almost certainly the cost-effective treatment option. The PSA demonstrated GSO Device as the preferred option over ATO Device across a wide range of thresholds, supporting GSO Device as a valuable PFO closure device. ATO Device was not cost-effective compared to the other interventions, primarily because of differences in efficacy rather than costs: GSO devices were more expensive, and device placement costs were equal between GSO Device and ATO Device. Additionally, the CEAC indicated that at WTP thresholds below £15,000 per QALY, MT Alone is most likely the most cost-effective option, based on a cohort of 1000 patients over a five-year time horizon. The high upfront costs of PFO closure mainly drive this result. Nevertheless, GSO Device resulted in significant health improvements by preventing strokes, which outweighed the additional costs at higher WTP thresholds and longer time horizons.

GSO Device was projected to save LYs, prevent strokes, and reduce hospital days compared to both ATO Device and MT Alone. Consequently, the quality of life for patients who received GSO Device, as well as their caregivers, is generally higher. These results were driven by the MAIC-derived hazard ratio for recurrent stroke (HR 0.46; 95% CI 0.15–1.43), which indicated a numerical reduction in recurrent stroke events but did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference. These benefits are further reflected in cost savings, as fewer patients will require subsequent treatment for recurrent strokes or SAEs, including atrial fibrillation, related to the PFO closure procedure or device. However, while our model incorporates the 25 device- and procedure-related SAEs from the RESPECT trial,20,26 it excludes rare clinical complications such as cardiac erosion, and factors beyond the model’s scope may influence the cost-effectiveness of any of our comparators. Furthermore, even though we used a comprehensive SAE analysis, models inherently simplify reality and cannot account for every scenario.

The cost-effectiveness results were sensitive to the assumed long-term QoL benefit associated with PFO closure. While available evidence suggests potential improvements in QoL following PFO closure,38,39 the magnitude and durability of this benefit remain uncertain. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that assuming equal utility values between PFO closure and MT Alone substantially reduced cost-effectiveness, highlighting that the incremental QALY benefit in the model was significantly driven by this utility assumption rather than differences in survival.

GSO Device is estimated to significantly reduce hospital stays compared to both ATO Device and MT Alone, thereby enhancing patient health outcomes and optimizing healthcare resource utilization. This reduction in hospital days could be beneficial for the UK healthcare system, which is under pressure from limited resources and high demand.48 Consequently, our outcomes provide valuable insights to aid treatment decisions and ensure effective resource allocation, ultimately alleviating the substantial burden on healthcare providers.

The impact of using GSO Device is expected to be noticeable in the short term, given the favorable NNT values. Specifically, over a five-year time horizon, the NNT to prevent one stroke with GSO Device is 36 compared to ATO Device and 15 compared to MT Alone, and the NNT to prevent one hospital day using GSO Device is six compared to ATO Device and three compared to MT Alone. For the BIA, we estimated the total annual number of candidates for PFO closure in the UK to be 4811.1,14,44–47

Differences in device design may contribute to differences in procedural characteristics and potential long-term outcomes between PFO closure devices. The GSO Device uses a PTFE-covered structure with two discs designed to facilitate septal apposition and tissue integration, whereas the ATO Device uses a nitinol wire-mesh framework. These differences have been proposed to influence factors such as endothelialization, closure rates, and device-related complications.49,50 In contrast, wire mesh frame devices are generally stiffer and rely on endothelialization for closure. Wire-mesh frame devices have also been associated with cardiac erosion.51 The GSO Device is reported to release less nickel compared to ATO,52 potentially benefiting nickel-sensitive patients.53 These studies highlight how the differences in device architecture can result in greater clinical outcomes. Potential differences in rare device-related complications, such as cardiac erosion, were not included in the current model due to limited comparative data and may represent an area for future research.

Patient characteristics in the model were aligned with those in the REDUCE trial, but in clinical practice, stroke risk varies substantially by factors such as RoPE score, age, shunt size, and atrial septal aneurysm.47 This means that stroke risk may be higher or lower in specific subgroups. For example, in a sample of 402 patients, recurrent stroke or TIA occurred more frequently in patients with a RoPE score <7 compared to those with a score ≥ 7 (HR 3.21, p=0.03).54

Sensitivity and Scenario Analyses

We conducted a one-way DSA to understand the impact of individual parameter uncertainties on the NMB estimates. When comparing GSO Device to ATO Device, the analysis revealed that the effectiveness of GSO Device compared to ATO Device had a major impact on the model results, as it translates into favorable quality of life values for patients. Additionally, device costs were an important factor in the model. The results highlight the importance of the selected time horizon, a point further underscored by scenario analyses using different time horizons and their influence on cost-effectiveness outcomes. When comparing GSO Device to MT Alone, the DSA highlighted the strong influence of the utility value for stable patients on model outcomes. Additionally, it accentuated the importance of upfront PFO closure costs. This indicates that improved health outcomes after GSO Device compared to MT, as well as high upfront device costs, significantly influenced the model results.

21 scenario analyses strengthened the robustness of the model findings, as GSO remained cost-effective in all simulated scenarios compared to ATO and in 19 of 21 compared to MT Alone. The scenarios that resulted in a negative NMB accentuate the impact of a shorter time horizon and utility values on the model outcomes, as reflected in the DSA results.

While the base case used a five-year horizon, longer horizons demonstrated that long-term savings outweigh the higher initial cost of GSO. Extending the horizon from five to ten years increased NMB from £2.3 million to £12.2 million for GSO versus MT, while the NNT to prevent one stroke decreased from 15 to 7, and the NNT to prevent one hospital day decreased from 3 to 1. Compared with ATO, the NNT to prevent one stroke declined from 36 to 17, and the NNT to prevent one hospital day declined from 6 to 3.

Budget-Impact Analyses

This study assessed the financial impact of increasing the use of GSO Device for PFO closure through two BIAs. The first examined the effect of switching market share between GSO Device and ATO Device for secondary stroke prevention in patients eligible for PFO closure over five years. In the current treatment landscape, 63.3% of patients receive MT alone, while 36.7% undergo PFO closure (35.5% receive GSO Device and 64.5% receive ATO Device). Adjusting market shares to favor GSO Device resulted in net savings of £370,000, primarily driven by preventing approximately 42 recurrent strokes and their associated costs in a simulated cohort of 1000 patients. While performing a PFO closure procedure with GSO Device has higher upfront costs, its improved safety profile and recurrent stroke prevention offset these initial expenses, yielding long-term economic benefits.

The second BIA explored a scenario in which all patients undergoing PFO closure (36.7%) were treated exclusively with GSO Device, leading to an even greater budget impact saving of £860,000 for UK payers over five years. The only cost increase in this scenario was the one-time expense of PFO closure in the first year, including the device, placement procedure, and related SAEs. However, GSO Device was associated with lower costs across all other categories—diagnostics, hospital and treatment costs, and long-term care—resulting in substantial cost savings over time. These savings were driven by its superior effectiveness in preventing recurrent strokes compared to ATO Device, with an estimated total of 94 strokes prevented over five years, ultimately reducing hospitalizations due to recurrent stroke and TIA and long-term care requirements. These findings highlight the potential to improve PFO closure strategies for better clinical and economic results, emphasizing the need for further research on real-world cost-effectiveness and long-term benefits.

Future Research and Implications

As the use of PFO closure devices continues to expand, future research should address remaining uncertainties, including the lack of direct comparative evidence between devices and limited data on long-term clinical outcomes, safety, and HRQoL following PFO closure. Additional real-world evidence and direct comparative studies between GSO and ATO Devices will be valuable to further inform their relative clinical and economic value.

Although this study did not focus on cardiac erosion, a recent real-world evaluation of GSO Devices using the MAUDE database reported no cardiac erosion incidents from product launch through April 2024. In contrast, rare instances of erosion have been reported with other occluders, such as ATO Device.55 There are also a few case reports documenting erosion events with OFO Devices, including a late-onset cardiac tamponade caused by an erosion 5 years after OFO Device implantation,56 and a separate series reporting cardiac erosions after closure with the same device.57 Further collection and validation of long-term safety data are needed to determine whether device-related complications should be incorporated into future cost-effectiveness analyses.

Our findings may help inform decision-making by UK payers and healthcare providers regarding the potential value of PFO closure strategies for patients with PFO-associated stroke. While uncertainty remains around specific model inputs, these results provide additional evidence to support considerations of the clinical and economic value of PFO closure and should be interpreted alongside current clinical guidelines, local expertise, patient selection criteria, and emerging comparative evidence when informing commissioning and treatment decisions.

Strengths and Limitations

Regarding our CEA, novelty is among its main strengths, as this is the first UK-based study to compare two PFO closure devices and to include new scenarios.27,30 These encompassed a societal perspective, the application of similar utility values for stroke patients treated with PFO closure versus MT alone, the increasing risk of stroke and mortality with age, and the use of unadjusted transition probabilities for the ATO Device arm instead of adjusted probabilities calculated from the comparative evidence in the MAIC.24 Lastly, the model incorporated insights from previous CEAs comparing the economic value of GSO Device or ATO Device to MT Alone, along with evaluations from national HTA bodies to enhance its robustness.27,30 These included, for instance, stroke severity as it can lead to disability, caregiver disutilities, and long-term institutionalization in a nursing home. Additionally, robust estimates of SAE-related expenses after PFO closure in a UK context were derived from a previous CEA, based on the SAEs reported in the RESPECT study.20

The CEA has six main limitations. Given that the incidence of TIA, stroke, stroke-related mortality, and SAEs was limited during the REDUCE trial, it was not possible to utilize individual patient data to calculate more robust values for transition probabilities and the incidence of safety outcomes. To address this, we relied on transition probabilities and incidence data derived from available models and literature.19,20,24,28,30,34

Another limitation is our reliance on a published matched-adjusted indirect treatment comparison, given the absence of RCTs directly comparing ATO Device and GSO Device. This MAIC used the REDUCE and RESPECT trial data and objectively compared each of the devices to MT Alone. Thus, the limitations outlined by Kasner et al24 may, by extension, apply to this study and the comparative evidence used in this model should be interpreted with caution. This included reducing the sample size of the REDUCE trial cohort after matching it to the RESPECT trial. Future studies, directly comparing the ATO and GSO device would be desirable to confirm the findings of the MAIC. Nevertheless, this study addressed this uncertainty in the PSA, mitigating its impact on the results.

Furthermore, the favorable base-case results versus ATO are dependent on the MAIC-derived point estimate for recurrent stroke reduction, which did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference, assumptions regarding long-term utility improvements following PFO closure compared to MT Alone. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted in the context of uncertainty surrounding the comparative effectiveness estimates and QoL assumptions, rather than as evidence of substantial differences in survival between treatment strategies.

Another limitation relates to the time horizon of five years and the starting age of 45, which may not fully capture the long-term consequences of PFO closure and medical therapy over patients’ lifetimes. These base-case assumptions were selected to align with previous evaluations of the GSO Device, the characteristics of patients enrolled in the REDUCE and RESPECT trials, and the available clinical follow-up data. The potential impact of longer time horizons was explored in scenario analyses and resulted in more cost-effective results, indicating that a five-year time horizon may potentially underestimate the value of GSO Device.

Lastly, the economic model developed in this study used data from controlled clinical settings and focuses on a sub-population that specifically fits the criteria for PFO-associated stroke in the UK. While using these tailored data strengthens the internal validity of the model, it also limits generalizability. The results may not fully reflect outcomes in broader, real-world populations—either outside the UK or in patients with differing demographics, comorbidities, or stroke characteristics—and rehospitalization rates or other outcomes may vary accordingly.

Despite these limitations, the study offers a robust model incorporating accurate information on the clinical practice of recurrent stroke prevention in patients who were diagnosed with a PFO. In the absence of head-to-head studies comparing GSO Device and ATO Device, this model offers the best available evidence by enabling a comparative CEA between different treatment options for preventing recurrent strokes in patients with a PFO.

To develop a comprehensive and transparent model, we aimed to include the three main PFO closure devices in this study; however, due to the lack of appropriate data for OFO Device, it could not be incorporated into the analysis. The absence of available information on this device, with neither RCTs performed nor safety data for the device being publicly available, not only precluded OFO Device from being included in indirect treatment comparisons but also could raise valid concerns for healthcare providers and patients about the use of this device.

The inclusion of a BIA shows the full potential of increasing GSO use. Nevertheless, it’s not without its limitations. First, multiple sources were used to estimate the population eligible for PFO closure, which may have affected the precision of our population estimates. However, using multiple sources helped mitigate potential biases from individual sources. Second, due to limited data, it was not possible to include all available treatments on the market. Nevertheless, by employing reweighted market shares and a Markov model, we were able to provide a thorough assessment of the financial implications of adopting GSO exclusively in combination with MT. This methodological approach ensured that our analysis is robust and reflective of real-world conditions.

Conclusion

The results of the comparative economic evaluation suggest that the use of GSO Device may represent a cost-effective option for the prevention of secondary PFO-associated strokes compared with ATO Device and MT Alone, while also demonstrating a favorable incremental budget impact. The CEA and BIA demonstrated the upfront costs associated with PFO closure and suggested potential long-term resource savings associated with preventing recurrent strokes.

The analyses were based on the best available comparative evidence and incorporated uncertainty around key clinical inputs. Future direct comparative evidence will further inform the relative clinical and cost-effectiveness of available PFO closure strategies and support decision-making regarding their use in clinical practice.
