DLO-Splatting: Tracking Deformable Linear Objects Using 3D Gaussian Splatting
Holly Dinkel, Marcel Büsching, Alberta Longhini, Brian Coltin, Trey Smith, Danica Kragic, Mårten Björkman, Timothy Bretl

Abstract
This work presents DLO-Splatting, an algorithm for estimating the 3D shape of Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs) from multi-view RGB images and gripper state information through prediction-update filtering. The DLO-Splatting algorithm uses a position-based dynamics model with shape smoothness and rigidity dampening corrections to predict the object shape. Optimization with a 3D Gaussian Splatting-based rendering loss iteratively renders and refines the prediction to align it with the visual observations in the update step. Initial experiments demonstrate promising results in a knot tying scenario, which is challenging for existing vision-only methods.

III
The DLO-Splatting Algorithm

The DLO-Splatting algorithm estimates the 3D state
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}^{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}
of a DLO represented by
N
nodes over time given an initial state
\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{0}
, the actions applied to the DLO
\bm{\mathrm{a}}^{t}
, and visual observations consisting of RGB images
\mathcal{I}^{t}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
, where
H
and
W
are the camera resolution, from
K
cameras with camera perspective projection matrices
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 4}
. The DLO-Splatting algorithm estimates the 3D state of a DLO using a prediction-update framework akin to Bayesian filtering. First, the DLO state is predicted using position-based dynamics (Section
III-A
). This state is iteratively updated using 3D Gaussian Splatting-based rendering (Section
III-B
).

III-A

Prediction with Position-Based Dynamics

Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) derived from physics first-principles are used to predict the next state of the rope given the current estimated state and a gripper action. The position of the grasped node,
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{g}^{t}
, is computed as the closest node in the set of nodes describing the shape of the rope,
\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}
, to the position of the center of the gripper,
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{g}^{t}
, as

\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{g}^{t}=\argmin_{i}\|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}^{t}-\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{g}^{t}\|.

(1)

The predicted position of the rope given gripper action
\bm{\mathrm{a}}^{t}=\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{g}^{t}-\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{g}^{t-1}
is integrated through Verlet velocity integration of each node in
\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t}
for time step
\Delta t
according to

\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t+1}=\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t}+\left(\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t}-\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t-1}\right)\Delta t+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{t}\Delta t^{2},

(2)

where each
\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{i}^{t}\in\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{t}
for
\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}
are the summed external forces acting on node
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}^{t}
including gravity
\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{i,g}^{t}
, the normal force
\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{i,N}^{t}
, and friction
\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{i,f}^{t}
. Given the constant mass of each node
m_{i}
as the total mass of the object
m
divided by the number of nodes
N
, gravitational constant
g
, and friction coefficient
\mu_{f}
, the external forces acting on node
i
are

\begin{matrix}\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{i,g}^{t}=\left[0,0,-m_{i}g\right]\\
\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{i,N}^{t}=\left[0,0,m_{i}gc_{i}^{t}\right]\\
\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{i,f}^{t}=-\mu_{f}m_{i}g\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{i,xy}^{t}\end{matrix}

(3)

where binary
c_{i}^{t}
indicates whether node
i
is in contact with the manipulation plane, i.e.,
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i,z}^{t}\leq 0
, and
\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{i,xy}^{t}
is the velocity of node
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}^{t}
if
c_{i}^{t}=1
. Furthermore,
\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{t}=\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{g}^{t}+\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{N}^{t}+\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{f}^{t}
.

The rope length is constrained to maintain segment lengths by applying constraint forces,
\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{i}^{t}\in\bm{\mathrm{N}}^{t}
, as

\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t}=\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t}+\bm{\mathrm{\Lambda}}^{t}\bm{\mathrm{N}}^{t}.

(4)

The parameter
\lambda_{i}^{t}\in\bm{\mathrm{\Lambda}}^{t}
is used to satisfy the constraint on segment length,
L
, given by

\lambda_{i}^{t}=\frac{L-\|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1}^{t}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}^{t}\|}{2},

(5)

and the constraint direction is

n_{i}^{t}=\frac{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1}^{t}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}^{t}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1}^{t}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}^{t}\|}.

(6)

When the grasped node moves to the new gripper position, the position correction
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{g}^{t+1}=\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{g}^{t}+\bm{\mathrm{a}}^{t}\Delta t
must also be propagated through the rope. After applying Verlet velocity integration, the positions of nodes along the rope are adjusted to keep the segment lengths between adjacent nodes constant. Given lengths
\bm{\mathrm{l}}^{t+1}
,

l^{t+1}_{i}=\|\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{t+1}_{i+1}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{t+1}_{i}\|,

(7)

and desired length
L
, length corrections,
\Delta\bm{\mathrm{l}}^{t+1}
are computed for each nonzero length in
\bm{\mathrm{l}}^{t+1}
as

\Delta l^{t+1}_{i}=L-l^{t+1}_{i}\times\frac{\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{t+1}_{i+1}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{t+1}_{i}}{l^{t+1}_{i}}

(8)

and applied to the positions of the nodes on the segment as

\begin{matrix}\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{t+1}_{i}\leftarrow\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{t+1}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta l^{t+1}_{i}\\
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{t+1}_{i+1}\leftarrow\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{t+1}_{i+1}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta l^{t+1}_{i}\end{matrix}.

(9)

The predicted nodes are finally
\bm{\mathrm{X}}_{\text{PBD}}^{t}=\left[\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}^{t},\cdots,\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{N}^{t}\right]^{\intercal}
.

III-B

Update with 3D Gaussian Splatting-Based Rendering

Fig. 2:

The DLO-Splatting algorithm is compared to TrackDLO on qualitative tracking during a cross move commonly performed in knot tying. During this move, one tip of the DLO is moved through a loop to create an additional crossing in the topology. After eight seconds, both DLO-Splatting and TrackDLO failed to estimate the correct topology of the DLO, however DLO-Splatting succeeds in tracking the grasped tip.

In 3D Gaussian Splatting, the scene is represented as a set of
M
3D Gaussian distributions, where each distribution
G_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{\mu}}_{j},\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j},o_{j})
is parameterized by its position
\bm{\mathrm{\mu}}_{j}
, covariance
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
, color
\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}
, and opacity
o_{j}

[
42
]
. The 3D Gaussians are distributed on the centerline of the DLO, such that each segment contains
d
equally spaced Gaussians.
This representation establishes a direct coupling between the 3D Gaussians and the DLO’s nodes, allowing the Gaussians positions to deform along with the rope. To capture the shape of the rope, we model the size of all Gaussian with covariance matrix
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}=\sigma\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{3\times 3}
where
\sigma
corresponds to the diameter of the rope, resulting in equal-sized spherical Gaussians. Stochastic gradient descent optimizes the Gaussian parameters using the
\mathcal{L}_{obs}
rendering loss, defined by

\mathcal{L}_{obs}=\|\mathcal{I}^{t}_{k}-\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{t}_{k}\|_{2},

(10)

between ground truth image
\mathcal{I}^{t}_{k}
and rendered image
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{t}_{k}
. Rendering
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{k}^{t}
projects the 3D Gaussians onto the image plane of perspective
k
using
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{k}
as

\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{t}_{k}=h(\bm{\mathrm{X}}_{\text{PBD}}^{t},\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{k})=\left[\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{u}},G_{j})\right]_{\forall\bm{\mathrm{u}}\in\mathcal{I}_{k}^{t}}.

(11)

The Gaussians are ordered by their distance to the image plane (
G_{0}
is nearest) which allows for their aggregation using
\alpha
-blending. The value of blending factor
\alpha_{j}
is estimated for each Gaussian by multiplying its opacity by its Gaussian function,

\alpha_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})=o_{j}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\mathrm{u}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j}\right)^{\intercal}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime})^{-1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{u}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j}\right)\right),

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{j}
are the 2D projections of the Gaussian parameters onto the image plane. Rendering evaluates for each
\bm{\mathrm{u}}\in\mathcal{I}_{k}^{t}
the rendering function given by

\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})=\sum_{j\in M}\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}\alpha_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})\prod_{l=0}^{j-1}(1-\alpha_{l}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})).

(13)

For the state update, DLO-Splatting adapts this static Gaussian Splatting formulation to allow for fast optimization of dynamic rope states. The key difference is DLO-Splatting only models the appearance of the rope with Gaussians, while the geometry of the rope is modeled with the node chain
\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{t}
as shown in Figure (
1
). The rendering loss in Eq.
10
can be used to iteratively update the node positions as

\hat{\bm{X}}^{t+1}=\bm{X}^{t+1}_{\text{GS}}=\bm{X}^{t+1}_{\text{PBD}}+\Delta\bm{X}^{t+1},

(14)

\text{where}\hskip 10.00002pt\Delta\bm{X}^{t+1}=\argmin_{\Delta\bm{X}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{obs}}\left(\bm{X}^{t+1}_{\text{PBD}}+\Delta\bm{X}\right)

(15)

is optimized using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD). The original Gaussian Splatting implementation uses direction-dependent spherical harmonics as color representation, enabling the modeling of non-Lambertian effects such as reflections. Since DLOs do not exhibit these visual effects, DLO-Splatting only models their RGB colors instead. The initial RGB color values are the averages of the initial segmentation of the rope. Representing the object thickness with spherical Gaussians also allows for ignoring Gaussian rotations during deformation, simplifying optimization. This work uses
gsplat
as the rasterizer for Gaussian Splatting rendering for improved speed and memory efficiency
[
47
]
.

V
Conclusion

This work introduced DLO-Splatting, an in-progress method for estimating the state of a DLO relying on physics and rendering. The DLO-Splatting algorithm is inspired by recent advancements in cloth and DLO state estimation, aiming to handle especially challenging scenarios where vision-only methods often fail. Current limitations include difficulty modeling and constraining self-intersections, a slow update rate of 1 Hz, and reduced performance during occlusions—particularly from the gripper. To address these challenges in the prediction step, future work could use a higher-fidelity simulation to model deformation
[
52
]
or sample physics prediction at a much higher rate to improve resolution of physics constraints. To reduce errors caused by visual artifacts and occlusions, future work may incorporate a Gaussian Splatting-based representation of the entire scene into the rendering process
[
29
]
. Additionally, the current implementation uses object masking during the update step, and future work could use more recent DLO instance segmentation methods to track multiple objects simultaneously
[
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
]
. While DLO-Splatting is still early in development, it demonstrates a promising direction for state estimation in tasks involving complex DLO geometries and dynamics—such as knots—where parts of the object are self-colliding and heavily occluded, making purely vision-based tracking unreliable.
