TmKayak functions as an effector of Jun N-terminal kinase pathway to modulate immune responses in Tenebrio molitor
Authors: Ho Am Jang, Maryam Ali Mohammadie Kojour, Keunho Yun, Su Hyeon Hwang, Sungsin Jo, Sang Mok Jung, Chul-Hyun Kim, Yong Seok Lee, Yeon Soo Han, Yong Hun Jo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling pathway plays a critical role in insect stress responses and immunity; however, its downstream transcriptional mechanisms remain poorly understood in coleopteran species. The kayak gene, a Fos homolog that functions as a JNK-responsive transcription factor, has been well studied in Drosophila melanogaster, but its role in beetles remains unexplored. In this study, we examined the functional role of TmKayak in the immune response of Tenebrio molitor using RNA interference. Upon TmKayak knockdown, we assessed the expression patterns of 15 antimicrobial peptide (AMP) genes in four immune-relevant tissues including fat body, gut, Malpighian tubules, and integument following three distinct microorganisms injection with Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, or Candida albicans. In all three injection models, E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans, a noticeable reduction in viability was observed in larvae injected with dsTmkayak compared to the dsEGFP control group. Notably, quantitative real-time PCR analysis revealed that TmKayak knockdown resulted in broad suppression of AMP expression, particularly in response to E. coli and S. aureus. In contrast, a relatively mild regulatory effect was observed following injection with C. albicans. Collectively, these findings identify TmKayak as a key transcriptional effector of the JNK pathway and contribute to a deeper understanding of innate immune regulation in T. molitor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51995-w.

Introduction

Insects depend solely on innate immune mechanisms to combat microbial threats, which are influenced by evolutionary limitations, as well as physiological and ecological considerations, including brief lifespans, restricted energy resources, and regular exposure to various environmental circumstances1,2. Lacking adaptive immunity—an advanced characteristic of vertebrate immune systems—insects have depended on the enhancement and specialization of innate immune processes to identify and respond to infection3. Conserved signaling pathways, including Toll, immune deficiency (IMD), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and Janus kinases (Jak-STAT), have been refined as a result of this evolutionary trajectory4,5. These pathways coordinate rapid and strong responses to bacterial, fungal, and viral threats. The insect innate immune system provides sufficient protection through the production of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), phagocytosis, melanization, and other cellular responses, highlighting its evolutionary success in sustaining host survival across diverse ecological niches6–9.

A major outcome of immune pathway activation is the induction of AMPs, which are small cationic peptides that directly target and neutralize invading pathogens by disrupting their membranes or interfering with essential cellular processes10. Tissue-specific AMPs are produced in the fat body, gut epithelium, and hemocytes, and are secreted into the hemolymph to provide systemic or localized immune protection11. These immune responses are initiated by the activation of signaling pathways such as Toll, IMD, JNK, and Jak-STAT. The JNK pathway is particularly notable for its multifunctional roles in insect physiology and immunity. Initially characterized in insects for its involvement in stress responses, wound healing, and apoptosis, JNK signaling has also been implicated in immune modulation, particularly under severe infection or tissue damage12,13. Despite these critical functions, the transcriptional mechanisms downstream of the JNK pathway, particularly those leading to AMP induction, remain insufficiently characterized. This is particularly true in non-dipteran insects, such as coleopterans, where the identity and role of JNK-associated transcription factors are still poorly understood.

The kayak gene encodes a transcription factor homologous to the vertebrate Fos and is a well-characterized downstream component of the JNK signaling pathway in Drosophila melanogaster14,15. Kayak forms a part of the AP-1 transcription factor complex, which is assembled through dimerization with Jun-like proteins, and regulates the transcription of target genes in response to developmental signals15,16. Acting downstream of the JNK pathway, kayak functions as a critical transcriptional effector that mediates JNK-dependent gene regulation. It is activated by phosphorylation events triggered by kinase cascades, allowing context-dependent regulation of target genes across diverse physiological conditions13. Despite extensive studies in dipteran models, the function of kayak in other insect orders, particularly in coleopterans, remains poorly understood.

In species such as Tenebrio molitor, a holometabolous beetle with an increasingly established role as an immune model, the involvement of kayak in host defense has not been clearly defined. Although core immune pathways, such as Toll and IMD, have been functionally characterized in T. molitor17–24, little is known about the role of JNK in the regulation of immune effector genes, including AMPs. This knowledge gap hinders a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms by which JNK-mediated transcriptional responses are regulated in beetles and how these mechanisms diverge from those characterized in dipteran models.

In this study, we investigated whether the putative JNK downstream transcription factor Tmkayak contributes to immune responses in the yellow mealworm T. molitor. RNA interference (RNAi) was employed to selectively silence Tmkayak expression, and its impact on the transcriptional regulation of 15 AMP genes was evaluated. Expression profiling was conducted in four immune-relevant tissues: fat body (systemic immunity), gut (oral defense), Malpighian tubules (excretory and immune functions), and integument (barrier and cuticular immunity). The spatial specificity of Tmkayak-dependent immune regulation within the host was assessed by examining multiple tissues. To explore pathogen-specific responses, T. molitor larvae were challenged with three distinct microorganisms: Escherichia coli (Gram-negative bacterium), Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive bacterium), and Candida albicans (fungus), each representing a different class of immune challenge. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was used to evaluate AMP gene expression at 24 h post-infection, a time point previously established to represent a representative phase of the immune response in T. molitor.

The tissue- and pathogen-specific roles of Tmkayak in the regulation of AMP gene expression were investigated using this integrative approach. The findings of this study are expected to provide novel insights into the transcriptional mechanisms underlying JNK pathway-mediated immunity in coleopteran insects and contribute to a broader understanding of innate immune regulation beyond dipteran models.

Results

Evolutionary and structural features of the Tmkayak transcription factor

Genomic analysis revealed that TmKayak exists in three isoforms: transcript variants X1, X2, and X3. Domain analysis of the Bzip_Fos structure revealed conserved features characteristic of the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) domain, which mediates both DNA binding and protein dimerization. The dimer interface, highlighted in red, represents the critical region through which two Fos proteins interact to form a stable transcription factor complex (Supplementary Figure S1). This conserved bZIP domain was identified in all three isoforms of TmKayak: X1, X2, and X3. To explore the evolutionary relationship of TmKayak, multiple sequence alignments were conducted with its homologs (Supplementary Figures S2, S3, and S4). Multiple sequence alignment revealed conserved regions shared across species that were consistently retained in all three TmKayak isoforms, X1, X2, and X3. Sequence similarity was highest between TmKayak isoX1 and ZmKayak isoX1 (91%), followed by TcKayak isoB (89%) and ClKayak-like isoX1 (50%) (Supplementary Figure S2C). Phylogenetic analysis (Supplementary Figure S2B) placed TmKayak-isoX1 near its coleopteran counterparts ZmKayak isoX1 and TcKayak isoB. TmKayak isoX2 showed 90% sequence similarity to ZmKayak isoX2, 86% similarity to TcKayak isoC, and 50% similarity to ClKayak-like isoX3 (Supplementary Figure S3C). In phylogenetic trees (Supplementary Figure S3B), TmKayak isoX2 clustered with ZmKayak isoX2 and TcKayak isoC. TmKayak isoX3 showed 91% sequence similarity to ZmKayak isoX3, 88% sequence similarity to TcKayak isoA, and 51% sequence similarity to VpKayak isoX3, VvKayak isoX3, and VsKayak isoX2 (Supplementary Figure S4C). In phylogenetic trees (Supplementary Figure S4B), TmKayak isoX3 clustered with ZmKayak isoX3 and TcKayak isoA. Kayak sequences from Lepidoptera, Diptera, Hemiptera, and Hymenoptera grouped into distinct branches, indicating order-specific divergence. The EsKayak-like isoX1 and X2 protein from the decapod crustacean Eriocheir sinensis segregated as an outgroup, supporting its distant evolutionary relationship.

Expression patterns of Tmkayak throughout developmental stages and in distinct tissues

Developmental and tissue-specific expression patterns of Tmkayak mRNA were analyzed using qPCR (Supplementary Figure S5). Tmkayak mRNA expression was detected across all developmental stages and tissues, with the highest expression observed in the two-day-old adult stage (Supplementary Figure S5A). During development, Tmkayak expression gradually increased from the egg stage to the pupal stage, peaked on day one of pupation, and slightly decreased in the late pupal stage. The expression levels were upregulated in adults compared to those in the late pupal stages, indicating reactivation during the pupal-to-adult transition.

The tissue-specific distribution of Tmkayak mRNA in larvae and adults is shown in Supplementary Figure S5B and S5C, respectively. In larvae, qPCR analysis revealed that Tmkayak was most abundantly expressed in the fat body and integument, with transcript levels approximately 2- to 5-fold higher than those in other tissues (Supplementary Figure S5B). In contrast, Tmkayak expression in adults was predominantly detected in the fat bodies and testis, with transcript levels approximately 5-fold higher than those observed in the Malpighian tubules, where the expression was the lowest (Supplementary Figure S5C). These results support the stage-dependent and tissue-specific roles of Tmkayak in T. molitor.

Tissue-specific temporal expression of Tmkayak following immune challenge

Following the microbial challenge, the transcriptional response of Tmkayak to different pathogens was investigated by measuring its expression levels at multiple time points and in several immune-related tissues. Accordingly, the mRNA expression levels of Tmkayak were quantitatively measured using qPCR at five time points (3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h post-infection) in various immune-related tissues, including the gut, fat body, integument, and Malpighian tubules. These measurements were performed following a microbial challenge with E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans to evaluate the temporal and tissue-specific transcriptional responses of Tmkayak to different classes of pathogens (Fig. 1). PBS (pH 7) was used as a mock control to account for injection-related injury and is represented by red dashed lines in the corresponding figure. Following E. coli infection, Tmkayak expression was elevated in both the fat body and the gut, with sustained induction in the fat body across all time points and transient upregulation in the gut at 3, 9, and 12 h (Fig. 1A and D). Upon challenge with S. aureus, Tmkayak transcripts significantly increased in the fat body at 3, 9, and 12 h, and elevated expression was observed in the integument at 3 and 12 h (Fig. 1A and C). Meanwhile, in the fat body under C. albicans infection, Tmkayak expression was observed at 3, 6, 9, and 12 h, but not at 24 h, with relatively lower transcript levels than those observed in E. coli and S. aureus infections (Fig. 1A). In response to C. albicans infection, Tmkayak was expressed at elevated levels in Malpighian tubules at all measured time points, except at 3 h, showing the highest induction at 12 h (Fig. 1B). Together, these results demonstrate that Tmkayak was transcriptionally activated in response to diverse microbial infections and was inducible in the immune-related tissues of T. molitor.

Validation of Tmkayak knockdown efficiency and its impact on survival

To elucidate the functional involvement of Tmkayak in the survival of T. molitor larvae during microbial infection, RNAi was used to suppress Tmkayak expression. Larvae were injected with double-stranded RNA targeting Tmkayak (dsTmkayak) and subsequently challenged with one of three pathogens: the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli, the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus, or the fungal pathogen C. albicans (Fig. 2). As a negative control, a separate group of larvae was injected with dsRNA against the enhanced green fluorescent protein (dsEGFP), which does not target any endogenous T. molitor genes, thereby accounting for the potential non-specific effects of dsRNA injection and manipulation. The efficiency of gene silencing was confirmed by qRT-PCR analysis conducted 5 days post-injection. The results demonstrated that larvae treated with dsTmkayak exhibited a substantial reduction (approximately 87%) in Tmkayak transcript levels compared to the dsEGFP control group, indicating that RNAi treatment effectively knocked down the target gene (Supplementary Figure S6).

Following the microbial challenge, the survival of dsTmkayak- and dsEGFP-treated T. molitor larvae was monitored daily over 10 days. In all three infection models, E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans, a noticeable reduction in viability was observed in larvae injected with dsTmkayak compared to the dsEGFP control group, indicating that the knockdown of Tmkayak adversely affected larval survival, regardless of the pathogen type.

Larvae challenged with C. albicans exhibited the most pronounced decrease in survival. Significant mortality was observed as early as day three post-infection, and by the end of the experimental period, survival had decreased by approximately 57% compared to the mock control group (Fig. 2D) or E. coli and S. aureus infections, moderate but consistent declines in survival were observed in the Tmkayak-silenced group, with final survival rates reduced by approximately 37% and 46%, respectively, compared to those in the mock control group (Fig. 2B and C). These results suggest that Tmkayak plays a broad role in mediating host resistance against diverse microbial infections, with a particularly critical function in the antifungal defense against C. albicans.

Collectively, these findings indicate that Tmkayak plays an essential role in the immune defense of T. molitor larvae against a broad range of microbial pathogens. The consistent reduction in survival across all infection models following the Tmkayak knockdown suggests that this gene contributes to the general host resistance mechanisms. Notably, the highest mortality rate was observed in C. albicans infection, highlighting a critical function of Tmkayak in antifungal immunity. Although E. coli and S. aureus infections also resulted in decreased survival in Tmkayak-silenced larvae, the effect was comparatively moderate, suggesting that although Tmkayak contributes to antibacterial defense, its role may be especially vital in response to fungal challenges.

Tissue-specific effects of Tmkayak RNAi on antimicrobial peptide gene expression

To gain deeper insight into the role of Tmkayak in regulating local immune responses, the expression profiles of 15 AMP genes were examined in four immune-related tissues, fat bodies, gut, Malpighian tubules, and integument, of Tmkayak-silenced T. molitor larvae 24 h post-infection with E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans (Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6). Tissue-specific qPCR analysis was performed to assess whether RNAi-mediated suppression of Tmkayak altered AMP gene expression compared to dsEGFP-injected controls.

In the integument (Fig. 6), which constitutes the outermost protective barrier against invading pathogens, Tmkayak knockdown in the E. coli infection significantly downregulated 10 AMP genes: TmTene4, TmDef-like, TmColeA, TmColeB, TmColeC, TmAtt1a, TmAtt1b, TmAtt2, TmTLP1, and TmTLP2. In the S. aureus infection, the following 11 AMP genes were downregulated: TmTene1, TmTene2, TmTene4, TmDef-like, TmColeA, TmColeB, TmColeC, TmAtt1a, TmAtt1b, TmAtt2, and TmTLP1. In contrast, only five AMP genes, TmDef, TmColeC, TmAtt1b, TmAtt2, and TmTLP1, showed reduced expression after C. albicans infection. Pathogen-specific differences in AMP gene downregulation were observed, with E. coli triggering the most extensive suppression and C. albicans showing a milder effect. These results indicate that Tmkayak contributed to antimicrobial gene regulation at the cuticular interface, reinforcing its role in barrier-based immune responses in a pathogen-dependent manner.

Overall, Tmkayak acts as a key transcriptional regulator of AMP genes in T. molitor, with tissue- and pathogen-specific roles. Its knockdown led to broad AMP suppression, particularly in the E. coli and S. aureus infections, indicating its prominent role in antibacterial defense. The relatively mild response to C. albicans suggested that Tmkayak modulated immune responses in a context-dependent manner.

Discussion

The JNK signaling pathway plays pleiotropic roles in insects by regulating stress responses, development, and immune functions in a context-dependent manner. In this study, we focused on Tmkayak, a Fos-like transcription factor that acts downstream of the JNK pathway, and explored its role in immune regulation in T. molitor. Our findings demonstrate that Tmkayak was expressed in a developmentally regulated and tissue-specific manner and that its expression was further modulated by microbial infection. Notably, the functional knockdown of Tmkayak resulted in altered AMP gene expression and increased mortality in response to bacterial and fungal challenges, with the magnitude and specificity of these effects varying across different tissues and pathogens. These results underscore the importance of investigating Tmkayak function within the spatial context of tissue type and developmental stage, as its immunological role appeared to be shaped by local physiological conditions, rather than a uniform systemic response.

Our findings demonstrate that Tmkayak exhibited dynamic and developmentally regulated expression in T. molitor, with transcript levels increasing from the egg to the pupal stages and peaking at early pupation (Supplementary Figure S5A). This suggests their role in metamorphic processes, such as cuticle remodeling and cellular differentiation. Interestingly, this expression pattern resembles that of kayak (D-Fos) in D. melanogaster, which, as a part of the AP-1 complex, is essential for thorax closure during pupal development via JNK signaling25,26. These findings imply that Tmkayak may play a similar role in tissue remodeling during metamorphosis in T. molitor. Tissue-specific analysis further highlighted the multifaceted role of Tmkayak. In larvae, high expression in the fat body and integument (Supplementary Figure S5B) suggests a role in metabolic regulation or cuticle maintenance, both of which are critical for larval growth and defense27,28. The fat body is a central immune and metabolic organ, and the integument serves as a primary physical barrier; Tmkayak may thus be implicated in cuticle integrity or immune readiness. In adults, expression of Tmkayak in the gut (Supplementary Figure S5C) suggests its involvement in epithelial maintenance and immune defense. Similar functions have been reported in D. melanogaster, where AP-1 (Fos/Jun) promotes intestinal stem cell proliferation and gut regeneration through the JNK and EGFR signaling pathways, particularly in response to stress or damage29. High Tmkayak expression in the testis indicates its possible role in reproductive development. In Drosophila, JNK/AP-1 signaling is essential for somatic cyst cell differentiation and germline development during spermatogenesis. The disruption of this pathway impairs the germline architecture and germline stem cell maintenance under stress, underscoring the critical role of JNK signaling in testicular function30. The involvement of the JNK signaling pathway in testis development has been clearly demonstrated, whereas the involvement of the JNK signaling pathway in immune/stress responses in the testis remains to be determined.

Tmkayak expression exhibited tissue- and pathogen-specific patterns, highlighting its potential role in the immune response of T. molitor (Fig. 1). Notably, the fat body showed strong and sustained induction across all pathogens, especially after E. coli and S. aureus infections (Fig. 1A), indicating its central role in systemic immunity, consistent with previous studies on fat body-mediated antimicrobial responses31,32. The gut responded specifically to E. coli (Fig. 1D), whereas the integument showed selective induction upon S. aureus challenge (Fig. 1C), suggesting localized responses in barrier tissues. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies in D. melanogaster, where epithelial cells in the gut and cuticle produce AMPs in response to microbial stimuli. In the gut, these responses help maintain immune homeostasis by producing AMPs and reactive oxygen species (ROS)33. The integument serves not only as a structural barrier, but also as an immunologically active tissue. In insects, the integument comprises an outer cuticle and an underlying layer of epidermal cells responsible for cuticle production. Epidermal cells activate the IMD signaling pathway upon injury or infection, leading to the localized expression of AMPs34,35. Collectively, these results highlight the ability of barrier tissues to mount tissue-specific immune responses against distinct classes of pathogens. A striking pattern was observed in Malpighian tubules, where Tmkayak was strongly induced by C. albicans infection (Fig. 1B), peaking at 12 h. This supports the recent findings that Malpighian tubules contribute to immune regulation in addition to excretion36. These patterns suggest that Tmkayak is involved in localized immune responses in a pathogen-specific and tissue-dependent manner.

Sequence alignment of TmKayak mRNA isoforms was carried out. The conservation of the amino acids of the coding region was highly preserved among the different isoforms and the bZIP domain was in-frame in all of them, indicating that transcriptional functions are conserved. The differences were restricted to short sequences of the amino acid that are the result of different splicing events and are not indicative of significant variations of the transcription factor functions. Since the RNAi target region is conserved in all isoforms, all TmKayak transcripts were probably simultaneously knock-down in this experiment.

Functional suppression of Tmkayak resulted in broad immunological defects across multiple tissues in T. molitor, highlighting its critical role in larval survival and AMP gene regulation. Survival analyses (Fig. 2) revealed that Tmkayak knockdown significantly increased larval mortality in response to E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans infections, with the most pronounced effect observed after fungal challenge. As a complementary approach to the survival assay, tissue-specific AMP gene expression profiling was performed following Tmkayak silencing. The results demonstrate that AMP expression was differentially regulated across immune tissues, depending on the type of pathogen, indicating a central role of Tmkayak in spatially coordinated immune responses. In the fat body, the main site of systemic humoral immunity, Tmkayak knockdown led to significant downregulation of a wide range of AMP genes, especially in E. coli and S. aureus infections (Fig. 3). This suggests that Tmkayak plays a central role in the regulation of systemic antibacterial responses. The observed suppression of multiple AMP classes, including Defensin, Coleoptericins, and Attacins, aligns with the established role of JNK signaling in regulating immune gene expression in insects37. Although microbial infection in this study was induced systemically via hemocoel injection, significant AMP suppression was observed in the gut following Tmkayak knockdown, particularly after E. coli and S. aureus challenge (Fig. 4). This suggests that Tmkayak may contribute to immune regulation not only in systemic tissues, but also in mucosal barriers. Similar tissue-specific AMP responses have been observed, in which gut epithelial cells produce AMPs in response to microbial exposure38. However, because the gut is not the primary site of infection in this model, it remains unclear whether the observed AMP suppression reflects a direct local role of Tmkayak or is secondary to systemic immune signaling. Further studies employing oral infection models are required to clarify its function in gut-specific immunity. Interestingly, the Malpighian tubules, which are traditionally associated with excretion and osmoregulation, also exhibited strong AMP downregulation following Tmkayak knockdown (Fig. 5). Notably, certain responses, such as the suppression of AMPs in response to E. coli and S. aureus, were uniquely observed in the tubules, suggesting a specialized antimicrobial regulatory role in this tissue. This aligns with previous findings that Malpighian tubules can function as immune-responsive organs, independent of the fat body36,39. In the integument, which serves as the outermost physical and immunological barrier, RNAi-mediated knockdown of Tmkayak led to significant repression of multiple AMP genes, particularly following challenge with E. coli and S. aureus, indicating that Tmkayak contributed to cuticle-associated antibacterial immunity (Fig. 6). In contrast, its effect was less pronounced in C. albicans infections, implying that antifungal defense in the integument may be mediated by immune mechanisms distinct from those utilized during antibacterial infection. Integuments can mount alternative immune responses against fungal invasion, notably through melanization40,41. These findings suggest that humoral and non-transcriptional defense mechanisms play important roles in peripheral immune barriers, such as the integument. Furthermore, it has been previously reported that Kayak can function independently of Jun42. It is therefore hypothesised that the TmKayak gene might be involved in regulating AP-1-dependent as well as independent gene expression in the larvae of T. molitor, especially upon fungal infection.

The predominant tissue-specific regulation of AMPs suggests that Tmkayak likely functions within localized signaling environments, enabling the fine-tuning of immune responses at primary pathogen entry sites. Collectively, these findings establish Tmkayak as a regulator of innate immunity in T. molitor, functioning through tissue-specific mechanisms and immune responses triggered differently by distinct pathogens. Its involvement in regulating a broad array of AMP genes across immune-relevant tissues, along with its impact on host survival, suggests a functional parallel with the AP-1 transcription factor Kayak (dFos) in D. melanogaster. In S2 cells, Kayak is required for the JNK pathway-mediated induction of AMPs in response to Gram-negative bacterial challenge43. In Drosophila, the JNK pathway is rapidly and transiently induced by LPS, and contributes to early immune responses rather than sustained AMP gene expression. The Drosophila Fos homologue, dFos (kayak) has been shown to be primarily involved in stress responses, epithelial cell remodeling, and developmental processes44,45. In these processes kayak contributes to gene expression that is only marginally overlapping with the regulated gene set of NF-κB; for example kayak has been associated with cytoskeleton organization at sites of wound-induced injury44,45. In contrast, we demonstrate here the central role of the coleopteran Kayak homologue, TmKayak, in mediating immune gene expression in T. molitor. TmKayak promotes expression of defense gene(s) in most tissues tested and RNAi-mediated silencing of TmKayak imparts significant reductions in host survival following infection with Gram-positive or Gram-negative bacteria. Notably, as observed for other immune gene regulatory pathways, several AMPs are induced by different pathogens to variable extents. Also, certain defense genes are upregulated to greater extents in particular tissues. These features reveal a higher level of complexity in immune gene regulation in T. molitor than has been described for Drosophila. These findings suggest that TmKayak functions in a manner that is different to that of dFos, and point to the existence of a more context-dependent, spatially-organized immune signaling pathway in coleopteran insects.

Collectively, our results suggest that the Kayak immunity-signaling pathway may have evolved new functions or evolved into distinct signaling modules in coleopteran insects, which support a more evolved and context-dependent and spatially organized immunity. To advance our understanding of Tmkayak-mediated immune regulation, further investigations are warranted to delineate downstream regulatory networks. In particular, future studies should explore the potential crosstalk between Tmkayak and the Toll, IMD, and JNK signaling pathways, as well as its interaction with tissue-specific transcription factors that are likely involved in the spatial specificity of antimicrobial gene expression.

Materials and methods

Insect rearing

T. molitor larvae were reared in incubators maintained at 25 ± 1 ℃ and a humidity of 60 ± 5% in the dark. Larvae in the 10th to 12th instar, reared on sterilized wheat bran for 15 min at 121 °C, were utilized in the experiments.

Preparation of bacterial pathogens for immune challenge

The immunological challenge tests used the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli K12, the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus RN4220, and the fungus C. albicans AUMC 13529. E. coli and S. aureus were incubated overnight in Luria-Bertani broth at 37 °C, whereas C. albicans was cultivated in Sabouraud dextrose broth at 37 °C. Bacterial cells were collected, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.0), and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 15 min. The samples were subsequently suspended in PBS, and the optical density of the suspension at 600 nm was quantified using a spectrophotometer (Eppendorf, Germany). The densities of E. coli and S. aureus were adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/µL, whereas that of C. albicans was adjusted to 5 × 104 cells/µL for immune challenge tests.

Total RNA extraction, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, and expression of Tmkayak

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR were routinely performed to assess the mRNA expression levels of Tmkayak at various developmental stages of T. molitor, across different tissues, and in response to pathogens (E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans). Total RNA was extracted from various developmental stages (eggs, young instar larvae (tenth to twelfth instar), late instar larvae (nineteenth to twentieth instar), pre-pupae, 1–7-day-old pupae, and 1–5-day-old adults), as well as from diverse tissues (integument, hemocytes, gut, fat body, and Malpighian tubules from both late instar larvae and 5-day-old adults; ovary and testis from 5-day-old adults) of T. molitor using the Clear-S total RNA extraction kit (Invirustech, Gwangju, South Korea), adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol. Two micrograms of extracted RNA were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Oligo(dT)12–18 primer. The reverse transcription reactions were conducted with a MyGenie96 Thermal Block (Bioneer, Korea) and AccuPower RT PreMix (Bioneer, Korea), according to the manufacturer’s instructions under the specified conditions: 72 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 1 h, and 94 °C for 5 min. cDNA was preserved at − 20 °C before analysis.

To examine the impact of microbial infection on Tmkayak expression, the 12th to 15th instar larvae (n = 20) of T. molitor were subjected to infection with 1 × 106 cells/µL of E. coli and S. aureus, and 5 × 104 cells/µL of C. albicans. The larvae in the control group were injected with PBS. The hemocytes, gut, Malpighian tubules, integument, and fat body were collected at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h after microbial inoculation. The relative expression level of Tmkayak mRNA was assessed via qPCR utilizing AccuPower® 2X Greenstar qPCR Master Mix (Bioneer, Korea), with synthesized cDNAs serving as templates and Tmkayak-specific primers designed through the Primer 3 plus program (http://primer3plus.com/cgi-bin/dev/primer3plus.cgi) (Tmkayak_qPCR_Fw and Tmkayak_qPCR_Rv) (Supplementary Table). The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, succeeded by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing and extension at 60 °C for 30 s. qPCR analysis was conducted using an AriaMx Real-Time PCR System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and data analysis was performed using AriaMx Real-Time PCR software. The ribosomal protein L27a-encoding gene of T. molitor (TmL27a) was used as an internal control. The mRNA expression levels were assessed utilizing the 2−∆∆Ct technique. The data are expressed as mean ± standard error derived from three biological replicates.

Double-stranded RNA synthesis and injection

The double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) fragment of Tmkayak was synthesized by PCR-amplifying the Tmkayak fragment using gene-specific primers including a T7 promoter region at the 5′ end (Supplementary Table). Primers were formulated using the Snapdragon program (http://www.flyrnai.org/cgi-bin/RNAi_find_primers.pl) to avoid cross-silencing effects. The fragments were amplified utilizing AccuPower® Pfu PCR PreMix under these conditions: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s (denaturation), 53 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 30 s (extension); concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were purified using the AccuPrep PCR purification kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea), and dsRNA was generated using the EZTM T7 high-yield in vitro transcription kit (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The dsRNA product was purified by precipitation with 5 M ammonium acetate and 99% ethanol and then measured using an Epoch spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, VT, USA). The generated dsRNA was preserved at − 20 °C until use.

To confirm the knockdown of Tmkayak, 1 µg/µL of synthesized dsTmkayak was administered into early instar larvae (10th to 12th instars; n = 30) utilizing disposable capillary needles connected to a micro-applicator (Picospiritzer III Micro Dispense System; Parker Hannifin, NH, USA). dsEGFP served as a negative control.

Survival analysis of larvae following microbial challenge and target gene knockdown

To investigate Tmkayak in the immunological response of T. molitor, dsTmkayak (1 µg/µl) was initially administered to early instar larvae (instars 10–12th; n = 30) via disposable needles attached to a micro-applicator (Picospritzer III Micro Dispense System; Parker Hannifin, Hollis, NH, USA). An equivalent quantity of dsEGFP was administered to larvae as a negative control. The efficacy of Tmkayak knockdown was assessed using qRT-PCR, and more than 87% knockdown was achieved five days post-injection. The Tmkayak-silenced and dsEGFP-injected larvae were subjected to challenges with E. coli (1 × 106 cells/µL), S. aureus (1 × 106 cells/µL), or C. albicans (5 × 104 cells/µL) in triplicate trials. Larvae were observed for 10 days, and the number of surviving larvae was recorded. The survival rates of Tmkayak-silenced larvae were compared with those of control larvae. Kaplan–Meier plots were used for pertinent analyses.

Analysis of AMP gene expression following RNAi-mediated silencing of the target gene

To elucidate the role of Tmkayak in innate immunity, the effect of Tmkayak knockdown on the expression levels of 15 T. molitor AMP-encoding genes was assessed following microbial challenge. The expression of Tmkayak in larvae was suppressed by RNA interference. Tmkayak knockdown larvae were infected with E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans. The dsEGFP-treated larvae served as the negative control, whereas PBS-treated larvae functioned as the infection control. The larvae were homogenized 24 h post-injection. Total RNA was isolated from the homogenized samples and subsequently subjected to cDNA synthesis, as previously described. The expression levels of 15 AMP genes were determined: TmTenecin-1 (TmTene1), TmTenecin-2 (TmTene2), TmTenecin-3 (TmTene3), TmTenecin-4 (TmTene4), TmAttacin-1a (TmAtt1a), TmAttacin-1b (TmAtt1b), TmAttacin-2 (TmAtt2), TmDefensin (TmDef), TmDefensin-like (TmDef-like), TmColeoptericin-A (TmColeA), TmColeoptericin-B (TmColeB), TmColeoptericin-C (TmColeC), TmCecropin-2 (TmCec2), TmThaumatin-like protein-1 (TmTLP1), and TmThaumatin-like protein-2 (TmTLP2). AMP primers were used for qPCR analysis (Supplementary Table).

Statistical analysis

All tests were conducted in triplicate. Data were subjected to t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Tukey’s multiple range test was used to assess differences across groups (p < 0.05).
