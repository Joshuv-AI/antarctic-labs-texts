Severe Nonischemic Dilated Cardiomyopathy Associated With Multi-Ingredient Herbal Supplement Use
Authors: Khaled Bani Yaseen, Ahmad Abdelfattah, Hanin N. Lataifeh, Wassim Abuozeid, Noreen Mirza, Ahmad W. Haddad, Addi Suleiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHerbal supplements marketed for cognitive and cardiovascular enhancement are widely used, yet safety data remain scarce. Supplement-associated cardiomyopathy is a rare but life-threatening complication that remains underrecognized.Case SummaryA 39-year-old previously healthy man developed progressive dyspnea, and bilateral leg edema 2 months after initiating a multi-ingredient herbal supplement. Echocardiography revealed severe left ventricular dysfunction (ejection fraction 10%-15%). Comprehensive evaluation excluded ischemic, infectious, autoimmune, infiltrative, and toxicologic causes. Despite maximal guideline-directed therapy, the patient required orthotopic heart transplantation. Genetic testing was not obtained, representing a workup limitation.DiscussionMultiple supplement ingredients lacked published cardiovascular safety data. The temporal association between supplement initiation and rapid progression to biventricular failure, in the absence of any identifiable alternative etiology, raises concern for supplement-associated cardiotoxicity. Clinicians should obtain thorough supplement histories in unexplained heart failure.Take-Home MessagesThis case underscores the cardiotoxic risk of multi-ingredient herbal supplements and the need for heightened clinical vigilance.

History of Presentation

A 39-year-old man presented with 1 month of progressive exertional dyspnea, fatigue, and bilateral lower-extremity edema. He had been previously healthy and exercised regularly with heavy weightlifting and endurance training for approximately 1 hour daily but recently became dyspneic after only 20 minutes of activity. He denied chest pain, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, syncope, fever, cough, or recent viral illness. Two months before symptom onset, he had initiated an over-the-counter multi-ingredient herbal supplement marketed for cardiovascular and cognitive wellness. No previous cardiac imaging or workup had been performed, and no alternative supplements were used in the preceding years.Take-Home Messages•Multi-ingredient herbal supplements can cause severe nonischemic cardiomyopathy and should be considered in unexplained new-onset heart failure.•Early referral for mechanical circulatory support or transplantation may be life-saving in supplement-induced cardiomyopathy presenting with advanced heart failure.

On admission, temperature was 36.8 °C, heart rate 105 beats/min, blood pressure 107/71 mm Hg, respiratory rate 18 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air. Physical examination demonstrated jugular venous distension with positive hepatojugular reflux, bibasilar crackles, a holosystolic apical murmur radiating to the axilla, and mild bilateral pitting edema.

Past Medical History

The patient had no previous medical history, established cardiac diagnoses, or previous cardiovascular workup. He was born in Haiti and had immigrated to the United States 17 years earlier. He denied tobacco use, reported occasional alcohol consumption, and had discontinued marijuana use 5 years previously. Family history was negative for cardiopulmonary disease, sudden cardiac death, and inherited cardiomyopathy syndromes.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included acute myocarditis, ischemic cardiomyopathy, toxin- or supplement-induced cardiomyopathy, infiltrative cardiomyopathy (amyloidosis, sarcoidosis), Chagas cardiomyopathy, viral cardiomyopathy, autoimmune cardiomyopathy, and idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated creatinine 1.5 mg/dL, alanine aminotransferase 100 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 60 U/L, B-type natriuretic peptide 1,855 pg/mL, and normal serial high-sensitivity troponins. HbA1c, low-density lipoprotein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, and thyroid-stimulating hormone were unremarkable. Antinuclear antibody and anti–double-stranded DNA antibodies were negative. Serologic testing for Trypanosoma cruzi, Lyme disease, and Coxsackievirus was negative. Urine toxicology screening was negative for cocaine, cannabinoids, and opioids.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated severe biventricular dilation and dysfunction with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 9% and right ventricular ejection fraction 17%. No late gadolinium enhancement consistent with infiltrative disease, previous myocardial infarction, or myocarditis was identified.

Right heart catheterization revealed severe hemodynamic compromise: cardiac index 1.3 L/min/m2, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 35 mm Hg, and pulmonary artery pressure 56/46 mm Hg.

Limitation of Diagnostic Workup

Genetic testing for dilated cardiomyopathy–associated variants (eg, TTN, LMNA, MYH7, SCN5A) was not obtained during this hospitalization. Although the temporal association with herbal supplement use and the absence of any identifiable alternative etiology support a toxic mechanism, an underlying genetic predisposition cannot be fully excluded. This represents an acknowledged limitation of this case report and should be pursued in future similar presentations.

Management

The patient was admitted for acute decompensated systolic heart failure and treated with intravenous furosemide. Guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction was initiated with sacubitril/valsartan, eplerenone, metoprolol succinate, empagliflozin, and furosemide. Colchicine was started empirically for possible inflammatory myocardial injury without clinical improvement.

Because of progression to cardiogenic shock, metoprolol and losartan were discontinued, and inotropic support with milrinone and dobutamine was initiated. He developed nonsustained ventricular tachycardia and was discharged with a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator (LifeVest) pending advanced heart failure evaluation.

One month later, he was readmitted following 3 LifeVest shocks. Repeat right heart catheterization confirmed persistent severe hemodynamic compromise (cardiac index 1.5 L/min/m2, elevated filling pressures). He subsequently developed torsades de pointes degenerating to ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest; return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after 2 cardiopulmonary resuscitation cycles, and amiodarone infusion was initiated. Persistent cardiogenic shock required Impella 5.5 support, escalating vasopressor and inotropic therapy, aggressive diuresis, and ultimately venoarterial–extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) for worsening renal failure and low cardiac output. He was placed on the heart transplant list.

Outcome and Follow-Up

A suitable donor was identified, and the patient underwent orthotopic heart transplantation. Postoperatively, Impella and VA-ECMO support were successfully removed. He demonstrated stable hemodynamics, improved renal function, and recovery without major complications, and was discharged on standard immunosuppressive and supportive therapy.

Discussion

Dilated cardiomyopathy is characterized by ventricular enlargement and impaired systolic function in the absence of significant coronary artery disease or abnormal loading conditions. Etiologies include genetic abnormalities, myocarditis, toxic exposures, endocrine disease, autoimmune disorders, and drug-related cardiotoxicity. Current guidelines recommend comprehensive evaluation for reversible causes, particularly in younger patients with nonischemic cardiomyopathy.1

Herbal and dietary supplements are increasingly marketed for cardiovascular and cognitive wellness despite limited regulatory oversight and incomplete safety data. Although many supplements are perceived as harmless because they are “natural,” multi-ingredient formulations may contain numerous biologically active compounds with unpredictable pharmacologic effects, variable concentrations, and potential interactions with cardiovascular medications.2

Several components of the patient’s supplement have demonstrated cardiovascular effects in experimental or limited clinical studies. Curcumin, the active component of turmeric (Curcuma longa), has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties and may improve endothelial function; however, clinical evidence remains heterogeneous, and curcumin has been associated with drug interactions and rare hepatotoxicity.3, 4, 5 Mimosa pudica has demonstrated angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitory and antihypertensive effects in experimental models, but human cardiovascular data are absent.6 Sea moss and bladderwrack contain omega-3 fatty acids and polysaccharides, although direct myocardial effects remain undocumented.7,8

Critically, several ingredients—patchouli, carpenter’s bush, fitweed, and gullyroot—have no published cardiovascular safety data. Their effects on myocardial electrophysiology, contractility, or toxicity are entirely unknown. In multi-ingredient supplements, synergistic or unpredictable interactions between compounds may further complicate safety assessment.9

A key question raised by this case is whether the degree of LV and left atrial remodeling is consistent with a 2-month process. The severity of LV dilation and left atrial remodeling observed—although typically associated with longstanding disease—may develop more rapidly in the setting of acute, severe toxic myocardial injury, particularly in younger patients with preserved baseline cardiac reserve.10 Acute toxic cardiomyopathy—especially from agents with direct myocardial or mitochondrial toxicity—can produce rapid and severe biventricular dysfunction, including secondary mitral annular dilation from acutely elevated end-diastolic volumes.10 The absence of any previous cardiac history or imaging in this previously athletic man, the no-prior-workup baseline, and the linear temporal association with supplement initiation are consistent with a predominantly acute process. We acknowledge that an underlying subclinical genetic cardiomyopathy cannot be excluded without genetic testing, which was not obtained—a recognized limitation of this case.

Current heart failure guidelines recognize toxin-induced cardiomyopathy as a potentially reversible etiology, yet herbal supplements remain understudied compared with alcohol, chemotherapy, or recreational drugs.1 This case therefore represents a gap in current evidence-based guideline coverage.

Conclusions

This case highlights severe nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy temporally associated with the use of a multi-ingredient herbal supplement in a previously healthy young man. Although direct causality cannot be established and genetic predisposition cannot be excluded, the absence of any identifiable alternative etiology and the clear temporal pattern underscore the importance of considering herbal supplements as possible contributors to unexplained cardiac dysfunction. Clinicians should obtain a comprehensive supplement history, pursue genetic testing where feasible, and maintain vigilance for atypical presentations. Further research is needed to characterize the cardiotoxic potential of multi-ingredient herbal formulations.
