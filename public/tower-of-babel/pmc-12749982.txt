Feature centric based deep learning approach for music mood recognition with HuBERT transformer model
Authors: Yaoyu Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, music mood classification is explored using advanced deep learning and transformer-based models to accurately predict the emotional content of music. Music plays a crucial role in human life, influencing emotions, behaviors, and mental states. Accurately classifying the mood of music is essential for applications such as music recommendation systems, emotional intelligence in AI, and mental health monitoring. With the growing impact of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in emotion recognition, sentiment analysis has become a vital research area, contributing to fields like social media monitoring, customer experience analysis, and personalized content delivery. We employ a state-of-the-art transformer-based model (HuBERT) in comparison with deep learning models (ConvFormer, LSTM) and pre-trained models (YAMNet) to evaluate their effectiveness in music mood classification. The study is conducted on a publicly available dataset containing five mood labels such as Aggressive, Happy, Dramatic, Sad, and Romantic with 500 audio files per category. To analyze and extract meaningful patterns from the dataset, advanced different five audio features such as Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), and Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) are utilized. To ensure robust model evaluation, an 80–20 holdout split is applied. Our experimental results indicate that HuBERT achieves the highest classification accuracy of 95%, outperforming both traditional deep learning models and pre-trained architecture. This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of machine learning approaches for music mood classification, demonstrating the potential of transformer-based models in enhancing AI-driven sentiment analysis. The findings contribute to the development of intelligent music recommendation systems, emotional AI applications, and advancements in affective computing.

Introduction

In this digital era, the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning has significantly transformed various domains, including sentiment analysis and affective computing1. The ability to analyze and interpret human emotions through computational models has led to groundbreaking applications in healthcare, social media analysis, customer feedback processing, and music recommendation systems2. Among these, music mood classification has gained immense importance, as it enables the automatic identification of emotional content in songs, enhancing user experiences in streaming platforms, personalized playlists, and therapeutic applications3.

Sentiment analysis, a subfield of natural language processing (NLP), has traditionally focused on understanding human emotions from text-based data sources such as reviews, tweets, and articles4. However, with the increasing consumption of multimedia content, researchers have extended sentiment analysis to audio and music lyrics, allowing machines to interpret the emotional aspects of songs5. Music is a powerful medium that evokes a wide range of emotions, and classifying its mood requires analyzing both acoustic signals, wearable devices6, EEG-based signals and lyrical content7. This presents a significant challenge due to the complex interaction between melody, rhythm, instrumentation, and textual meaning8.

The growth of AI has led to the emergence of deep learning-based models, which have outperformed traditional machine learning methods in sentiment detection, personality detection and emotion recognition tasks9. Early approaches in music mood classification relied on statistical models, handcrafted features, and classical machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests (RF), and k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)10. However, these models struggled to generalize across diverse datasets due to their dependence on manually extracted features11. The introduction of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) revolutionized the field by enabling end-to-end feature learning directly from raw audio signals12.

With the increasing complexity of multimodal sentiment analysis, transformer-based architectures have gained prominence due to their ability to process sequential data efficiently13. Transformers such as BERT, RoBERTa, and Vision Transformers (ViTs) have shown remarkable performance in text, image, and audio-based emotion classification tasks14. Unlike traditional deep learning models, transformers leverage self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies and contextual relationships within music lyrics and acoustic features15. This has led to significant improvements in music mood classification accuracy, making transformers a promising approach for future research16.

In this research study, we aim to explore the effectiveness of deep learning, and transformer-based models in music mood classification. We utilize a comprehensive dataset based on five labels that include both audio and lyrical features, extracted using techniques such as Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC), Chromagram Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), waveforms, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Mel-spectral contrast analysis. Our study investigates different architectures, including LSTMs, and transformers, comparing their classification performance across multiple mood categories. Furthermore, we analyze the strengths and limitations of each model to propose optimized frameworks for emotion recognition in music.

Related work

Deep learning-based music mood classification has become of primary interest among research and has been developed mostly through utilizing lyrics as a main input for sentiment analysis17. Deep learning architectures, feature extraction techniques, multimodal methods have been proposed to increase classification accuracy by integrating some of them18. An integrated, multi-modal model of lyrics and audio signals is presented which indeed is shown to be effective at combining textual and acoustic features for mood classification. The results indicate that deep learning performances are almost an order of magnitude better than traditional machine learning techniques in this domain19. Like the above, an ensemble learns based lyric classification approach has also been implemented on sentiment-based classification tasks using specific XGBoost and grid search20. Latent semantic analysis does seem to have some role to play in lyric-based mood classification and that performance can be further improved when latent semantic analysis is used with deep learning approaches21. Building on this, we extend the idea by coupling a listener model that would combine lyrics and audio signals with a multi-channel architecture for a more precise emotion detection22. With that said, pre-trained word embeddings such as GloVe were utilized to improve lyric-based mood classification, where we extracted features using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Our results support that pre-trained word vectors can significantly improve classification rate; thus, deep learning models are trained to recognize emotions in lyrics better23. In another study the connections between deep neural networks and music mood classification were emphasized using a dataset of 18,000 songs using an end-to-end deep learning model24. Alternatively, a sentence level deep learning approach has been suggested, where lyrics are sentence segmented to structured data to gain better granularity25. Further attempts have been made by sentiment based preprocessing methods using SentiWordNet to extract sentiment features in the song lyrics and the outcome shows that the machine learning algorithms do benefit from sentiment aware feature extraction26.

Then automatic mood classification techniques further evolved by combining sentiment lexicons with machine learning classifiers and opened a way for robust/music search engines based on sentiment aware model27. While lyric based models by themselves do not completely reflect the mood of the song, a study found out that combining lyric based models with audio-based classifiers improves the classification accuracy28. Further, classification performance using recent advancements of the transformer models. Self-attention mechanisms can capture contextual nuances of both lyrics and audio features in transformer architectures and a multi modern music mood classification system based on transformer architectures surpasses traditional model, bi-directional GRU29. CNNs can also be integrated with Vision Transformers (ViTs) into speech emotion recognition systems and lead to an improved classification accuracy by utilizing CNNs for local feature extraction, and ViTs to capture global relationship30. Gathering comparative studies in emotion detection in text imply that transformer-based models, being fine-tuned on emotion datasets have significantly outperformed traditional machine learning models, based on their superior context understanding and semantic processing capabilities31.

Fused by multimodal emotion recognition frameworks using pre-trained transformers across multiple text, speech, and video inputs, the maximum accuracy approaches are achieved (to fuse data from disparate modalities)32. Good results have been obtained in sentiment analysis from noisy and unstructured text using transformer-based sentiment analysis models, on social media datasets33. In addition, the transformer-based methods were used to build depression detection model and applied linguistic, sentiment, emotion and topic features to improve the classification accuracy using textual representation, which showed that transformers have the potential in mental health monitoring through textual analysis34. An emotion recognition framework is developed, which is based on masked learning techniques to provide more focus on relevant parts of input data to enhance the performance of emotion classification, especially in scenarios with incomplete or noisy data35. This serves as a state-of-the-art review on multimodal emotion recognition using transformers, proving to be a key resource for the research community in the same domain36. To illustrate that transformers can process as well as integrate different data modalities for improving classification accuracy, the authors have implemented an automatic music mood classification system based on a multi modal framework37. In addition, a review of literature on transformer models in machine learning emphasizes the application of emotion recognition and showed superiority of these models compared to the traditional models in detecting sentiment and mood in various sentiment and mood classification tasks38. Since the deep learning and transformer-based models are growing in music mood classification, these advancements demonstrate that the multimodal approaches are crucial to geometric sentiment analysis in music39. In recent years, transformer-based models have proved to be effective when applying them to mood and emotion classification tasks. Also, they are especially good at dealing with complex datasets over multiple modalities, by having the ability to capture contextual and sequential information40.

Recent studies have explored emotion recognition across music and speech using deep learning and hybrid approaches. One work applied pre-trained CNN models such as AlexNet and VGG16 on chroma spectrograms to extract deep visual features for music emotion recognition, achieving up to 89.2% accuracy with VGG16 on the Fc7 layer, highlighting the effectiveness of deep feature representations in this domain41. Another study extended this line of research by focusing on EEG-based emotion recognition during music listening, where spectrograms of brain signals were analyzed using transfer learning with AlexNet and VGG16, showing promising results in classifying mood states such as happy, sad, relaxed, and angry42. Complementing these efforts, a hybrid framework for speech emotion recognition combined acoustic features (e.g., MFCC, RMS, zero-crossing rate) with deep features extracted from CNNs, and further refined them through feature selection, achieving state-of-the-art performance across multiple benchmark datasets43. Together, these studies demonstrate the growing role of deep and hybrid feature extraction methods in advancing emotion recognition tasks across music, brain signals, and speech.

Based on analysis of existing literature, prior work on using various approaches for music mood recognition has mostly relied on supervised feature learning and limited contextual representation. To fill this gap, the study emphasizes the novelty of applying HuBERT, whose architecture is specifically designed to capture fine-grained audio patterns and long-range dependencies through self-supervised pretraining. The aspects of its application to music mood recognition that are novel compared to existing transformer-based approaches include the exploitation of hierarchical acoustic representations and context-aware embeddings, which allow the model to learn mood-specific cues embedded in temporal and spectral domains more effectively. This contribution offers a distinct advancement over earlier transformer-driven methods.

Research proposed methodology

The research implements a structured approach to transform audio data before performing machine learning and deep learning model applications, as shown in Fig. 1. Noise reduction forms the initial step in which audio data processing begins through multiple operations that include digital conversion along with trimming sounds to a standard length and padding them when needed. The process of feature extraction depends on Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), and Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) to extract essential frequency combined with temporal characteristics44. Deep learning models consisting of ConvFormer, LSTM, and YAMNet receive the extracted features for processing various complex audio representations that contain sequential dependencies. The model’s performance is measured through established evaluation metrics accuracy as well as precision and recall and F1-score to conduct a reliable assessment of this approach. The pipeline functions with high efficiency and precision to classify audio which makes it suitable across different real-world applications45. The method for preparing audio data for machine learning application requires a standard sequence of preprocessing followed by feature extraction and model training and subsequent evaluation steps. Comparison based models

This section investigates the various models of deep learning in music mood classification such as LSTM, ConvFormer and YAMNet. Local patterns of an audio signal are learned via spectrograms and evaluated models, which are extensively used for feature extraction from spectrograms. Since music time-series data has sequential dependency52. These models are based on features extraction and feature extraction capabilities, accuracy, as the best among these models is identified for music sentiment analysis. LSTM which was designed particularly to address vanishing gradient challenges in sequential information. Technology finds broad applications in speech recognition systems and both emotion analysis and audio event detection tasks. LSTM cells manage extended dependencies through their memory cells combined with gated operational elements53. Each LSTM cell includes three major components: Forgot Gate to Dispose Items and Input Gate to Record Data and Cell State Update to Store Information and Output Gate to Shape Final Outputs.

Moreover, YAMNet was developed as a pre-trained model using MobileNetV1 architecture to handle audio classification through Mel spectrograms54. YAMNet is a deep learning model based on MobileNet, a pre-trained embedding from a large-scale dataset is used in audio classification. First, it converts audio signals into log-Mel spectrogram representations, that are the input to a series of depth wise separable convolutional layers. The hierarchical features of spectrogram are extracted by these layers and include temporal and spectral patterns. Then the model applies global average pooling, which reduces the dimensionality while preserving most of the information, and a fully connected layer that maps the features described to already predefined audio classes. For multi classification, YAMNet utilizes SoftMax activation function, such that it outputs probability scores for each category. The pre-training of the model also ensures that it can generalize well across different sound domains making it a powerful tool for transfer learning in audio-based applications55.

Additionally, ConvFormer represents a blended system which unites CNN with Transformer frameworks to extract local and global relationships from audio information. The short-range frequency features of data are processed by CNNs, and Transformers analyze temporal dependencies over longer durations through their operation56. ConvFormer applies a convolutional stem followed by multiple transformer encoder layers with three components: Convolutional feature extraction and multi-head self-attention and transformer encoder block. This model unites CNN and Transformer components to reach peak performance and processes both neighborhood spectrum characteristics along with extended time sequence connections57.

Results and discussion

Music mood classification is a challenging and developing task, still a technique needs to be developed to analyze such complicated acoustic patterns. As deep learning (DL) models can extract higher level features from a raw audio signal, traditional machine learning approaches have been gradually being replaced by DL models. To improve classification accuracy and build a solid means to mood classification, this research aims to integrate a feature driven scheme with modern deep learning methods.

Descriptive analysis based on features

The basics of mood recognition are constructed on top of the descriptive analysis of the various Audio features such as MFCC, STFT, Mel-Spectrogram, Waveform and RMSE.

Waveform

The waveforms for each mood class—Aggressive, Dramatic, Happy, Romantic, and Sad—reveal significant differences in amplitude variation, energy distribution, and temporal characteristics, which provide valuable insights for mood classification, shown in Fig. 4. The Aggressive waveform exhibits high amplitude spikes with periodic sharp peaks, indicating strong percussive beats and high-energy music patterns. This suggests that aggressive music is characterized by strong rhythmic structures, high intensity, and abrupt sound changes, often found in rock, metal, or high-tempo electronic genres. The Dramatic waveform, on the other hand, shows fluctuating amplitude variations with a mix of high and low energy, reflecting the dynamic and emotional intensity of dramatic compositions. These characteristics are common in orchestral, cinematic, or suspenseful tracks, where variations in loudness contribute to dramatic expression.Fig. 4Waveform analysis across all mood labels, illustrating the temporal amplitude variations of the audio signals.

Waveform analysis across all mood labels, illustrating the temporal amplitude variations of the audio signals.

The Happy Waveform displays moderate amplitude variations with consistent energy throughout, suggesting a steady rhythm and uplifting harmonies. Happy music often contains repetitive, high-energy beats with smooth transitions, commonly found in pop, dance, and upbeat genres. The Romantic waveform, in contrast, features relatively lower amplitude fluctuations with smoother transitions, indicating softer instrumentation and a more relaxed energy flow. Romantic music often relies on melodic progression, harmonization, and subtle dynamic shifts to evoke emotions of warmth and affection. Lastly, the Sad waveform presents irregular yet controlled amplitude fluctuations, with less pronounced spikes, indicating softer, more sustained sounds with gradual decays. Sad music typically has slower tempos, lower pitch ranges, and smoother transitions, contributing to a melancholic or reflective mood. The lower energy variation in sad waveforms aligns with the emotional depth associated with this mood. By analyzing waveform structures, deep learning models can better differentiate rhythmic intensity, energy flow, and harmonic structure, leading to more accurate mood classification.

In addition to technical contributions, it is essential to address ethical and practical considerations for real-world deployment of music mood recognition systems. A major shortcoming is genre bias whish highlight the most AI models in music are based on data that are biased towards Western and well-known genres, which might systematically marginalize non-Western and less common musical traditions, thus, seriously jeopardizing the fairness and generalization of the model59. Moreover, considering the importance of cultural context for mood interpretation, cultural bias is also a key issue as emotion perception to music differs between cultures as Western listeners, for example, are more accurate to show anger and joy from Western classical music than from Chinese traditional music60. One way to address these difficulties may be to include cross-cultural baselines such as Global Mood that cover a wide range of linguistic and cultural contexts to collect culture-specific distributions of mood descriptions61.

Far beyond cultural equity, ethical deployment also includes data ownership and creative rights. AI systems trained with copyrighted music without adequate permissions risks of consent and fair use, while unacknowledged imitation of an artistic style risks damaging artist integrity62. Transparent Dataset Curation, Fair Licensing Practices, Inclusiveness for Marginalized Genres are all important mechanisms that contribute to promoting responsible AI. Beyond that, transparency can be increased by the application of explainable AI (XAI) methods to mood recognition models, and this facilitates the understanding and acceptance of the decisions taken63. In general, such potential limitations are required to make sure AI-powered music mood recognition is a fair, culturally aware and well-ethical process in real-world apps.

Conclusion

This study investigated music mood classification by comparing machine learning, deep learning, pre-trained, and transformer-based models to accurately detect emotional content in music. Given the significant role of AI and sentiment analysis in emotion recognition, accurate mood classification is essential for applications such as music recommendation systems, emotional AI, and therapeutic interventions. We evaluated CNN, LSTM, YAMNet, and HuBERT on a public dataset with five mood labels. The findings demonstrated that HuBERT achieved the highest accuracy of 95%, outperforming conventional deep learning models in capturing long-range dependencies and patterns. Future research can address the limitations of the current study by expanding the dataset to include a wider range of musical genres and multilingual lyrics, which will enhance the model’s generalizability. Another direction is the integration of multimodal data, such as combining audio features with lyrical content, to capture a broader spectrum of mood-related signals. Ensemble modeling with CNNs, LSTMs, and transformers can also be explored to overcome the constraints of relying on a single architecture. Furthermore, incorporating explainable AI (XAI) methods more deeply into the framework can directly address the limitation of model interpretability observed in transformer-based systems, making the results more transparent and actionable.
