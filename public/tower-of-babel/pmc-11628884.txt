Genome‐wide association study uncovers pea candidate genes and metabolic pathways involved in rust resistance
Authors: Salvador Osuna‐Caballero, Diego Rubiales, Nicolas Rispail
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPea (Pisum sativum L.) is an important temperate legume crop providing plant‐based proteins for food and feed worldwide. Pea yield can be limited by several biotic stresses, among which rust represents a major limiting factor in many temperate and subtropical regions. Some efforts have been made to assess the natural variation in pea resistance to rust, but its efficient exploitation in breeding is limited since the resistance loci identified so far are scarce and their responsible gene(s) unknown. To overcome this knowledge gap, a comprehensive genome‐wide association study (GWAS) has been performed on pea rust, caused by Uromyces pisi, to uncover genetic loci associated with resistance. Utilizing a diverse collection of 320 pea accessions, we evaluated phenotypic responses to two rust isolates using both traditional methods and advanced image‐based phenotyping. We detected 95 significant trait‐marker associations using a set of 26,045 Diversity Arrays Technology‐sequencing polymorphic markers. Our in silico analysis identified 62 candidate genes putatively involved in rust resistance, grouped into different functional categories such as gene expression regulation, vesicle trafficking, cell wall biosynthesis, and hormonal signaling. This research highlights the potential of GWAS to identify molecular markers associated with resistance and candidate genes against pea rust, offering new targets for precision breeding. By integrating our findings into current breeding programs, we can facilitate the development of pea varieties with improved resistance to rust, contributing to sustainable agricultural practices and food security. This study sets the stage for future functional genomic analyses and the application of genomic selection approaches to enhance disease resistance in peas.

INTRODUCTION

Pea (Pisum sativum L. 2n = 14) is one of the principal legume crops grown globally, ranking as the second most produced pulse in the world after dry bean (FAOSTAT, 2022). This crop is a vital source of plant‐based protein for both animal feed and human food, offering significant health benefits (Clemente & Olias, 2017; Tulbek et al., 2016). Additionally, pea plays a crucial role in agriculture sustainability through nitrogen fixation that enhances soil fertility and structure, making it a valuable component of crop rotation or intercropping systems, particularly in conjunction with cereals (Gungaabayar et al., 2023; Skoufogianni et al., 2019).

Pea crop is prone to various diseases and pests, which are principal factors limiting their yield (Rubiales et al., 2023, 2015). Rust disease poses a substantial threat in many pea‐growing regions worldwide, especially in temperate and subtropical areas where conditions are favorable for its causal agents (Osuna‐Caballero et al., 2024). Pea rust is caused by two biotrophic fungi: Uromyces viciae‐fabae (Pers. de Bary), which is the primary rust pathogen in warm and humid climates, affecting leading pea‐producing countries such as India and China; and Uromyces pisi (Pers.) (Wint.), which is more prevalent in temperate zones, especially in Mediterranean countries (Emeran et al., 2008). It can lead to significant yield reductions—up to 50% for Uromyces viciae‐fabae and 30% for U. pisi infections (Barilli, Moral et al., 2012; Singh et al., 2023). Such impacts on yield and seed quality underscore the necessity for focused breeding and management strategies to effectively counteract rust disease in pea crop.

Rust control with fungicides is effective (Emeran et al., 2011) but it requires repeated treatments which is hardly economical for low‐input field crops like pea, and it raised important environmental issues. To address this latter issue, the possibility to control pea rust by inducing systemic acquired resistance (Barilli, Sillero, Fernández‐Aparicio et al., 2009) or by applying natural compounds has been evaluated. Despite showing promises, the available formulations are only partly effective, and their long‐term environmental impacts remain unverified (Barilli, Rubiales et al., 2017, 2012, 2022). Accordingly, the deployment of rust‐resistant pea genotypes appeared as the most desirable control method. This requires putting further emphasis on the development of rust‐resistant cultivars. In this line, large pea germplasm collections have been evaluated for resistance to U. pisi, disclosing variable levels of incomplete resistance, although no complete resistance has been described so far (Barilli, Sillero, & Rubiales, 2009; Osuna‐Caballero et al., 2022). Partial resistance (PR) is characterized by a non‐hypersensitive response and reduced disease severity (DS). This mechanism, frequently detected in legumes against rust, is the common source of rust resistance in peas (Rubiales et al., 2011). In addition to PR, hypersensitive resistance (HR), often monogenic in origin, has been observed in lentil and faba beans against U. viciae‐fabae (Adhikari et al., 2021; Avila et al., 2003; Negussie et al., 2012; Rubiales et al., 2013). Incomplete and late‐acting HR have also been recently described in pea offering new opportunities for rust resistance breeding in pea (Osuna‐Caballero et al., 2022). The limited number of resistance sources available for pea offering only PR urge breeders to further exploit the large diversity of pea germplasm which can be aided by the implementation of image‐based analysis systems (Osuna‐Caballero, Olivoto, Jiménez‐Vaquero et al., 2023).

Little is known so far about the genetic architecture controlling U. pisi resistance in peas. An initial study on a segregating F2 population obtained from a cross between Pisum fulvum and P. sativum subsp. sativum identified the quantitative trait locus (QTL) Up1 explaining 63% of the phenotypic variance on linkage group (LG) III (chromosome 5) (Barilli et al., 2010). However, this QTL was not identified on the subsequent analysis of the corresponding recombinant inbred line (RIL) which instead detected two QTLs, UpDSII and UpDSIV on LG II (chromosome 6) and IV (chromosome 4), explaining 24% and 20% of the phenotypic variance, respectively. These results suggest that U. pisi resistance in pea is controlled by two to three QTLs, although further studies on additional pea population or germplasm are necessary to clarify its genetic architecture. The phenotypic information available in pea germplasm, combined with new high‐throughput phenotyping systems focused on rust (Gallego‐Sánchez et al., 2020; Osuna‐Caballero, Olivoto, Jiménez‐Vaquero et al., 2023), and the availability of high‐quality reference pea genomes (Kreplak et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022), enables more in‐depth genomic studies. Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have emerged as a powerful approach to dissect the complex genetic architecture underpinnings quantitative traits. By scanning the genome for polymorphisms correlated with phenotypic variance, GWAS has facilitated the identification of both single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and Diversity Arrays Technology‐sequencing (DArT‐seq)‐derived markers that are intricately linked with key traits and uncover potential candidate genes across numerous legume species (Susmitha et al., 2023). Specifically, recent GWASs in peas have been useful to identify favorable alleles for agronomic traits or disease resistance (Alemu et al., 2022; Leprévost et al., 2023), but there is still a lack of such studies for rust disease.

In the present study, we bring new sources of resistance against two U. pisi isolates by employing both traditional screening methods and innovative image‐based methodologies across an extensive germplasm collection of 320 pea accessions, representative of global diversity. Moreover, we explored the genetic architecture of PR to rust infection through GWAS as a preliminary step toward more efficient precision breeding. To achieve this, the phenotypic response to U. pisi of a wide collection of pea accessions was integrated with previously generated DArT‐seq polymorphic markers, revealing significant marker‐trait associations (MTAs). Mapping these significantly associated markers on pea reference genome also uncovered several candidate genes untapping previously uncharted metabolic pathways potentially implicated in rust response.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant and fungal material

Plant material consisted of a diverse core collection of 320 Pisum spp. accessions of worldwide origin gathered previously at Institute for Sustainable Agriculture (IAS), Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Científicas (CSIC), to ensure an extensive range of characteristics in terms of phenotype and their genetic makeup (Rispail et al., 2023). The core collection encompasses a diverse range of genetic material, including wild relatives (16%), landraces (61%), cultivars (17%), and breeding lines (3%) collected from all the continents, except Antarctica. It is a representation of the Pisum genera, capturing the genetic diversity across the three main species—P. sativum, P. fulvum, and Pisum abyssinicum—as well as the P. sativum subspecies, namely, sativum, arvense, jomardi, elatius, and humile. It is also a representation of all Pisum morphotypes including semi‐leafless genotypes. In addition, the collection includes accessions that have already demonstrated their potential for resistance to several pea diseases including rust (accessions IFPI3260, PI347352, PI347343, PI347353, and PI347389) (Barilli, Sillero, & Rubiales, 2009).

To assess the levels of resistance to rust contained in the collection, two highly virulent isolates of U. pisi that specifically affect pea, UpCo‐01 and UpKeS‐05, were used (Barilli, Moral et al., 2012). These two isolates were preserved at IAS, CSIC.

Phenotyping

Three series of experiments were conducted to assess the rust resistance components of the pea core collection. One series of experiments took place under field conditions over three cropping seasons, involving adult plants, while the other two experiments were conducted under controlled conditions (CC) using seedlings infected with the U. pisi isolate UpCo‐01 or UpKeS‐05. Phenotyping of the IAS pea core collection in response to the rust isolate U. pisi UpCo‐01 under field and controlled conditions have been previously reported in Osuna‐Caballero et al. (2022). We briefly summarize the experimental conditions.

For the field experiment, the pea collection was evaluated over three consecutive crop seasons from 2018 to 2020 in Cordoba, Spain, as described in Osuna‐Caballero et al. (2022). Briefly, 10 seeds per accession were sown in 1‐m rows, spaced 0.7 m apart, in a 19 × 19 alpha lattice design with three replicates including controls. Mature plants were artificially inoculated with the U. pisi isolate UpCo‐01 in March each season to ensure a homogeneous progression across trials. Due to the experimental design dimensions (n = 1083 each season), rust symptoms were evaluated only once 30 days after inoculation (dai) by estimating DS—assessed as the percentage of rust symptoms (i.e., pustules) covering the experimental unit.

The response of the pea collection to the same U. pisi isolate (UpCo‐01) was also evaluated at seedling stage under CC. In this CC experiment, 2‐week‐old plants (three‐leaf stage) per genotype were inoculated with UpCo‐01, incubated for 24 h in 100% relative humidity in complete darkness, and thereafter incubated in a growth chamber at 20°C with a 12 h photoperiod. The experiment followed a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replicates per genotype and was repeated twice independently, leading to the evaluation of six plants per genotype. Disease evaluation was performed daily from 7 to 13 dai that covered the period from the emergence of the first pustules on the leaf surface to the end of the first infection cycle (i.e., pustule sporulation). Evaluation was performed by visually counting the number of pustules (infection frequency [IF]) per centimeter square. Then these daily values were integrated into three disease progression parameters including the area under disease progress curve (AUDPC), the latency period (LP50), and the monocyclic disease progress rate (MDPr). Additionally, DS and infection type (IT; Stakman et al., 1962) were also visually evaluated at 13 dai. As pustule counting is highly time consuming and error‐prone, one leaflet of the third leaf or the corresponding stipule of each plant was photographed at 600 dots per inch resolution. Pustule counting was then performed on these high‐quality RGB (red, green, blue) pictures. The complete description of this CC experiment is presented in Osuna‐Caballero et al. (2022).

To validate the response of the pea collection to rust, a second CC experiment was performed challenging the collection with another virulent rust isolate of U. pisi. For this second CC experiment, three 2‐week‐old plants per genotype were inoculated with the UpKeS‐05 isolate. As previously, the experiment followed an RCBD and was repeated thrice leading to the evaluation of nine plants per genotype. All CC evaluations were performed in the same growth chambers set with the same conditions of temperature, relative humidity, and photoperiod. In addition, the condition and experimental procedure used for the sowing and growing of the plants before inoculation, the inoculation with the rust isolate UpKeS‐05, and incubation of the inoculated plants until evaluation were the same as this first CC experiment to ensure consistency of the results between both CC experiments. To facilitate and fasten the evaluation of rust symptoms, rust pustule counting was performed with a recently described image‐based analysis system (Osuna‐Caballero, Olivoto, Jiménez‐Vaquero et al., 2023). For this, a leaflet from the third leaf of each plant, or its corresponding stipule in case of semi‐leafless genotypes, was detached and placed on a Petri dish filled with a water–agar mixture (1:10 v:v), at 7 dai, when the rust colonies were already established (Figure S1). This method, detailed in Barilli et al. (2022), was devised to preserve leaf viability while facilitating the documentation of rust progression via RGB imaging. Subsequent analysis of daily photographs of these Petri dishes, spanning from 7 to 13 dai, was conducted in R software version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2021) using the pliman package (Olivoto et al., 2022) with a previously developed and freely available specialized R script (https://zenodo.org/records/7991462; Osuna‐Caballero, Olivoto, Rubiales et al., 2023). This analysis allows the automatic quantification of IF, DS, and pustule size (PS, mm2) and their integration into the corresponding disease progression parameters (AUDPC, LP50, and MDPr). While this approach does not influence pustule development on the leaf surface, it eases and fastens assessment of disease parameters with a prominent level of concordance with traditional visual evaluations (Osuna‐Caballero, Olivoto, Jiménez‐Vaquero et al., 2023).

Phenotypic data analysis

The statistical analysis of the three phenotypic datasets (field, CCUpCo‐01, and CCUpKeS‐05) was conducted using R 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2021) and the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) for mixed models. The linear model with interaction effect used to analyze the field data from multi‐environment trials was applied using the genotype effect as fixed factor, while the remaining sources of variance, such as environment, genotype‐by‐environment interaction, and replicated nested block in environments, were included as random effects variables. Genotyping

The process of DNA extraction, DArTseq protocol, and data analysis were previously detailed in Rispail et al. (2023). In summary, DNA was extracted from pooled leaves or stipules of 20 seedlings per accession and, after quality controls, subjected to high‐density Pea DArTSeq 1.0 array analysis using the DArT‐Seq technology at Diversity Arrays Technology Pty Ltd. as described by Barilli et al. (2018). Data cleaning was performed to remove markers with low quality or lacking polymorphism, following the recommendations of Pavan et al. (2020). Therefore, silico‐DArT markers with heterozygosity >10%, minimum allele frequency < 5%, and >20% missing data were removed from the analysis. As a result, 26,045 polymorphic Silico‐DArT markers were retrieved. The filtered markers were then mapped onto the two P. sativum reference genome sequences (Kreplak et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022).

Genome‐wide association mapping

The 26,045 DArT markers were used for association mapping with rust scoring assessed on the pea core collection. In this study, a single‐trait GWAS was conducted using two different models: the single locus mixed linear model (MLM) and the multi‐locus Bayesian information and linkage disequilibrium iteratively nested keyway (BLINK) (Huang et al., 2019). Both models were performed within GAPIT 3.0 (Wang & Zhang, 2021) and have different assumptions: MLM allows for the identification of individual markers associated with the trait, while BLINK considers multiple markers simultaneously, considering the joint effects of nearby markers. Therefore, the analysis can capture both major and minor genetic effects contributing to the rust response variation.

The Bayesian information criterion (BIC)‐based model selection procedure was used to determine the optimum number of principal components (PCs) required to efficiently control the population structure of the collection. Additionally, the Astle kinship matrix was used to estimate relatedness among individuals, further accounting for potential genetic correlations (Astle & Balding, 2009).

To select the significant MTA, two different threshold levels were applied (Bonferroni corrected logarithm of the odds [LOD] threshold and false discovery rate [FDR]). The Bonferroni corrected LOD threshold, calculated based on the number of markers (26,045), offers a more stringent criterion to minimize false positives. In contrast, the FDR method proposed by Storey and Tibshirani (2003) provides a less restrictive threshold, allowing for the identification of potentially interesting markers that might be missed by the Bonferroni correction. FDR limit was set for each model and trait combination to ensure less than one false positive association with the R package qvalue (Storey & Bass, 2022).

To visualize the results, Manhattan plots were generated, displaying the −log10(p‐value) for each marker along the seven chromosomes, highlighting regions where significant associations occur. Quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were also performed to assess the overall distribution of p‐values and evaluate whether any genomic inflation or deflation occurred through the calculation of lambda (λ) using QQperm R package (Petrovski & Wang, 2016). All models with genomic inflation (λ) below 0.8 or higher than 1.2 were not considered. Manhattan and Q–Q plots were generated using CMplot R package (Lilin‐Yin, 2023).

As a result of the polycyclic evolution of the disease in the field, DS in the three field seasons was higher than in CC. While the three field trials and their estimated average (DS_field) had similar mean values and ranges, the 2018 experiment displayed a lower H 2, accompanied by a pronounced positive skewness of 1.72. This suggests that most pea accessions exhibited reduced DS in 2018 experiment which is further corroborated by its lower coefficient of variation (CV%) relative to the subsequent two field trials. Under CC, most accessions demonstrated moderate levels of symptoms but with well‐formed pustules (IT = 4). The AUDPC and MDPr for IF (AUDPC_IF and MDPr_IF) presented similar levels of heritability (H 2 = 0.77 and H 2 = 0.73, respectively), although they varied in their descriptive statistics; the distribution of the MDPr_IF is closer to a normal distribution than AUDPC_IF, as inferred from their skewness. The CV also underscored greater disparities between these traits, evident from a higher phenotypic range observed for AUDPC_IF (173.07) than for MDPr_IF (16.13).

All accessions also displayed rust symptoms in response to the isolate UpKeS‐05 under CC (Table 1). In addition, the results obtained in response to this U. pisi isolate followed a similar trend as the one observed for the UpCo‐01 under CC (Table 1). As observed in response to UpCo‐01, the accession PI273209 displayed necrotic spots reflecting a low IT and a late‐acting incomplete hypersensitive response to rust in response to UpKeS‐05 (Figure S2). Similarly, AUDPC exhibited greater skewness and a broader variability compared to MDPr. For this isolate, however, heritability of most traits was lower, indicating a distinct or lower virulence pattern. In both CC experiments, LP50 was the trait with the lowest heritability and coefficients of variation, reflecting the low influence of genetic component in LP50 variations. Therefore, LP50 is not considered a selectable trait for breeding perspectives in pea rust.

In general, the accuracy values—interpreted as the square root of H 2—consistently exceeded 0.7, confirming the reliability of the experimental measurements. On the other hand, strong positive (>0.50) and negative (<−0.50) skewness was detected for most traits confirming the need to transform raw values to achieve a normal distribution before fitting the mixed models and predicting the estimated means (BLUEs) for each trait.

The first two PCs, accounted for over 50% of the total trait variability. Dim1 differentiated traits based on their influence on rust symptoms. Traits positively contributing to rust symptoms clustered on the right, while those inversely related to DS, such as the LP50, are positioned on the left, although with a lower contribution to the variability. By contrast, Dim2 effectively delineated traits based on the rust isolate (UpCo‐01 and UpKeS‐05). Traits assessed for UpCo‐01 under both field and CC were situated at the top of the biplot, while those associated with UpKeS‐05 isolate are anchored at the bottom.

A total of 95 significant MTAs combining the results obtained for each trait and GWAS model (Figure 2; Table S1). The BLINK model identified 70 markers linked to pea rust of which 49 were unique to this model, while the MLM model detected 46 associated markers of which 25 were unique. Notably, 21 associated markers were common to more than one trait or model, while one marker was identified from all three datasets (Figure S4).

In general, the BLINK model yielded a higher number of significant MTAs with lower p‐value and higher LOD (Table 2). The total phenotypic variation explained (PVE) by the MTA for each trait varied also greatly depending on the GWAS model. As the BLINK model identified more significant MTAs for each trait, their corresponding PVEsum was higher (Table 2). With this model, the highest number of MTAs was identified for DS_field, with a total of 13 markers explaining 86.2% of the total phenotypic variance, while the MLM model only uncovers three MTAs for this trait, accounting for 10.2% of the phenotypic variance (Table 2). By contrast, the MLM method uncovered seven MTAs with AUDPC for CCUpCo‐01, while BLINK only uncovered five MTAs for this trait, although these MTAs explained around 70% of PVEsum.

It is worth noting that the sum of the phenotypic variation explained by the MTAs for each trait was closely related to their level of heritability (Figure S5), except DS2020.

Therefore, Table 3 shows candidate genes directly related to disease resistance pathways or including MTAs with a PVE >5%. It is interesting to note that several of these candidate genes contained more than one significant associated marker, underscoring their importance in contributing to the phenotypic variation of the trait. A total of 10 candidate genes were detected on chromosome 1. Among them, two genes, 127087512 and 127115771, each marked by two associated markers, were related to vesicle trafficking within cells. Two additional genes, 127115471 and 127137201, were related to wax and cell wall biosynthesis. Interestingly, one uncharacterized gene, marked by three associated markers, was related to BFA1 assembly, which is required for ATP synthesis in Arabidopsis thaliana.

On chromosome 2, seven candidate genes were detected. Four of these genes (127120834, 127121528, 127118910, and 127117580) are related to carbohydrates and amino acid metabolism. Two genes, 127117481 encoding an F‐box protein and 127118667 encoding a MADS‐box transcription factor, were related to defense response to stresses in plants (Table 3), while the last identified candidate gene was engaged in cell trafficking.

On chromosome 3, nine candidate genes were detected. Among them, two genes, 127132076 and 127127305, encoded protein kinases related to plant development, stress responses, and hormone signaling. Two genes, 127130208 and 127127894, were also associated with vesicle trafficking. Additionally, two genes were related to secondary metabolism, while another one was related to auxin homeostasis similarly to gene 127080599 from chromosome 1.

From the three genes detected on chromosome 4, two genes (127138368 and 127076644) encoded proteins related with the secondary metabolism and cell wall biosynthesis, while the last one, gene 127138740, putatively encodes a haloacid dehalogenase‐like hydrolase.

Chromosome 5 harbored the highest number of candidate genes with 18 genes identified from 23 MTAs. A notable hotspot in chromosome 5 consisted of three closely spaced markers within a 1.2 Mb window, located within genes 127082537, 127087291, and 127087323. Interestingly, two of these genes, 127082537 and 127087323, were related to mobile elements in A. thaliana, while the third one, together with genes 127087766 and 127084564, was related to DNA transcription regulation. As in chromosomes 1 and 4, several genes related with cell wall biosynthesis and maintenance were detected on chromosome 5. Several genes related to transport and signaling, and with fatty acid or secondary metabolism were also detected on this chromosome.

Of the five candidate genes identified on chromosome 6, three were related to DNA formation and transcription. The last two genes were related to inositol phosphate metabolism and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling, respectively.

Chromosome 7 harbors 11 genes in the vicinity of 13 associated markers. Among them, the function of three genes was not known in peas, but the ortholog of two of them, 127105234 and 127105763, have been related to tissue development in A. thaliana. Similarly, to the other chromosomes, several candidate genes related with the regulation of DNA transcription regulation (127108155 and 127105708), the amino acid metabolism (127103253), the vesicle trafficking (127102730), and the response to stresses (127105022, 127104606 and 127104295) were identified in this chromosome.

DISCUSSION

Rust is a worldwide distributed disease that severely affects pea crop yield (Rubiales et al., 2015). In warm and humid areas, the main causative pathogen is U. viciae‐fabae, while U. pisi is responsible for pea rust in more temperate regions (Singh et al., 2023). Complete resistance to rust has not yet been identified in pea, with PR being the primary source of genetic resistance available so far to control this disease in this species (Osuna‐Caballero et al., 2024). In this study, we applied a GWAS approach to identify regions of the pea genome associated with traits linked to the PR response. To achieve this, a collection of 320 pea accessions was evaluated under field and CC in response to two rust isolates (UpCo‐01 or UpKeS‐05).

The evaluation method and phenotypic response of the collection against the rust isolate UpCo‐01 were previously described in Osuna‐Caballero et al. (2022). Here, we describe the phenotypic response of the same collection against the rust isolate UpKeS‐05 obtained using the methodology described in Osuna‐Caballero, Olivoto, Jiménez‐Vaquero et al. (2023). While seedling inoculation and incubation were performed with the same experimental protocol and in the same growth chamber, the evaluation was performed with the recently described image‐based evaluation system proposed by Osuna‐Caballero, Olivoto, Jiménez‐Vaquero et al. (2023) which imply placing detached leaflet of each plant on water–agar plates after establishment of the rust colonies. Preliminary experiments comparing intact and detached leaves assays detected no significant differences in the intensity and velocity of pustule development between assays (Barilli et al., 2022). In addition, a high concordance correlation coefficient (ccc = 0.962) was observed between the image‐based scoring and visual assessment (Osuna‐Caballero, Olivoto, Jiménez‐Vaquero et al., 2023) which ensures the comparability of CC experiments. Comparison of the responses of the collection to UpCo‐01 and UpKeS‐05 under CC revealed some commonalities and differences. Similarly, to the situation with UpCo‐01, complete resistance to UpKeS‐05 was not detected in the collection. In addition, the reduced number of well‐developed pustules and low IT detected on the accession PI273209 in response to either isolate suggest that this accession exhibits a late HR response against both UpCo‐01 and UpKeS‐05 isolates (Figure S2). Disease ratings were nonetheless lower in response to UpKeS‐05 than to UpCo‐01 and the response to each isolate were clearly differentiated on the PCA suggesting that the collection responded differentially to these isolates. As mild to moderate relationship was also observed for most traits between isolates, these differences are more likely revealing differences in the overall virulence level of the isolates, although the existence of U. pisi races in pea could not be ruled out.

Previous studies performed on the pea core collection demonstrated a rapid linkage disequilibrium decay and a broad distribution of the polymorphic Silico‐DArT markers throughout the pea genome (Rispail et al., 2023). The collection was found to be structured into six groups consistent with the taxonomy of the Pisum species and subspecies, although a significant portion of the pea population resulted admixed in phylogenetic terms (Rispail et al., 2023). Accordingly, BIC selection models indicated that population structure was efficiently controlled in the GWAS models by the Astle kinship matrix alone and did not require the addition of the initial PCs to the models (Table S2). This was previously demonstrated on GWASs performed on plant populations with a high percentage of admixture (Kaur et al., 2023). The BLINK model improves statistical power and enhances the robustness of association signals compared to MLM (Huang et al., 2019). Furthermore, the extensive coverage of the DArT markers across the pea genome is ideal for multi‐locus methods like BLINK, making it a frequently used model in GWAS to analyze complex traits in legumes (Susmitha et al., 2023; Tibbs Cortes et al., 2021). Accordingly, this method detected the highest number of markers significantly associated with rust disease development traits.

The two GWAS models applied detected a total of 95 significant MTAs to one or more of the 19 rust disease traits, among which 21 markers were detected by both methods which will be instrumental for future breeding for rust resistance in pea. This is the first GWAS targeting rust resistance in pea, while similar studies addressing rust disease in legumes remain scarce. Most previous studies were based on the MLM method, while only one study on soybean (Glycine max)–rust (Phakopsora pachyrhizi) applied the BLINK method (Martins et al., 2022; Montejo Domínguez et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022; Xiong et al., 2023). The number of significant marker‐trait associated depended on the number and distribution of the available markers. Accordingly, 100 and 129 MTAs were identified in response to rust in soybean and common bean in studies with more than 20,000 genome‐wide markers (Wu et al., 2022; Xiong et al., 2023), while only seven DS associated markers were identified in response to U. pisi in grass pea (Lathyrus sativus L.) from a marker set of 5651 SNPs (Martins et al., 2022). Interestingly, aligning these seven grass pea markers onto pea reference genomes allowed the identification of 19 candidates genes, among which five have similar functions to those detected in the present study (Martins et al., 2022).

Significant MTAs were detected on each pea chromosome, although chromosome 5, corresponding to the LG 3, harbors the highest number of MTAs. Previous studies on the genetic architecture of U. pisi resistance in pea identified three QTLs, Up1, UpDSII, and UpDSIV, from segregating F2 and RIL populations (Barilli et al., 2018, 2010). Up1 identified on LG 3 by Barilli et al. (2010) explained a total phenotypic variance of 63% in the biparental F2 population between P. fulvum (resistant) and P. sativum (susceptible). These results have not been corroborated in this work, where the associated markers detected on chromosome 5 are far away from the Up1 flanking random amplified polymorphic DNA markers OPY111316 and OPV171078. This confirms the results on the RIL derived from the initial F2 population that did not detect QTL in this region (Barilli et al., 2018). Instead, this study detected two new QTLs, UpDSII and UpDSIV, on LGs 2 and 4, corresponding to chromosomes 6 and 4, respectively. The QTL UpDSII explained an average of 24% of the phenotypic variation in the field and 28% under CC. Similarly to our results, the markers surrounding UpDSII explained 16% of the phenotypic variation in the field and 20% under CC for the same UpCo‐01 isolate. On the other hand, the QTL UpDSIV explained 20% of the phenotypic variation both in the field and under CC according to the results of Barilli et al. (2018). Markers colocalized near UpDSIV in this study explained 26% of the phenotypic variation in the field. However, in the present study, none of the markers significantly associated with UpCo‐01 severity was found on chromosome 4. These discrepancies may be due to the origin of the biparental population, that arose from interspecific crossing between P. fulvum and P. sativum, in counterpart of the GWAS population carrying greater genetic diversity.

Examining the surrounding regions of the 95 associated markers detected in the present study allowed the identification of 62 candidate genes with potential function in rust resistance. According to their annotation, these candidate genes participate in a variety of functions, including primary and secondary metabolism, cell wall synthesis, cell trafficking, DNA transcription regulation and defense response. While no typical nucleotide binding‐leucine‐rich repeat resistance genes have been detected in the vicinity of the associated markers, several genes encoding well‐known defense genes have been identified which would be interesting targets for further functional genomic studies. This includes three of the identified genes encoded protein kinases including a PBS1‐like protein kinase, a calcium‐dependent protein kinase (CDPK3), and cysteine‐rich receptor‐like kinase (CRK8). Previous studies reported the crucial role of PBS1 in bacterial and viral resistance in A. thaliana and soybean (Pottinger et al., 2020). PBS1 was also shown to play a role in herbivore resistance in interaction with CDPK3 (Desaki et al., 2023; Miyamoto et al., 2019), hinting the involvement of both genes in a common defense pathway. Interestingly, one marker significantly associated with pea rust resistance was located at 8.27 kb of the receptor‐like kinase CRK8 that has been previously shown to play a crucial role in rust resistance in wheat (Gu et al., 2020; Kamel et al., 2023). In addition to these kinases, this study identified an ortholog of an F‐box protein CPR1/CPR30 (127117481) (Kandasamy et al., 2008), that acts, between other functions, as a negative regulator of salicylic acid‐dependent resistance (R) proteins like SNC1 (Gou et al., 2012), and a dynamin‐related protein 1E (127127894) whose knockout mutation was found in A. thaliana to induce a stronger hypersensitive response to powdery mildew (Leibman‐Markus et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2006).

Apart from these defense genes, many of the detected genes are related with vesicle formation and cell trafficking, cell wall and wax biosynthesis, secondary metabolite synthesis, hormonal response, and transcription regulation which is in accordance with the expected PR mechanisms detected in the pea core collection. Therefore, our study opens new opportunities to characterize further the genetic basis of resistance to rust in pea.

Altogether, this GWAS study shed light on the complex genetic architecture underpinning PR to rust in pea. In addition to the detection of 95 new molecular markers associated with PR, this study detected 62 candidate genes that contain or were intricately linked to these markers, which offer several promising targets to improve our understanding of rust resistance in pea and future plant breeding strategies. These genes span a diverse array of biological functions providing the basis for further gene functional mapping. The identification of several candidate genes known to participate in rust resistance in cereal (i.e., wax genes and CRK8) suggests that at least some of them might be the responsible gene underlying the detected QTL, confirming the usefulness of GWASs to uncover new resistance QTL and genes. Therefore, this study opens the possibility to characterize further, via gene expression studies or TILLING, the function of these candidate genes to explore their implication in rust disease in pea. The significantly associated DArT‐seq markers also provide a basis for future marker‐assisted selection and the development of efficient genomic prediction models for rust resistance. Therefore, this study contributes to improving our understanding of the genetic makeup of rust resistance in peas. It also opens new avenues for precision breeding for rust resistance improvement and enhancement of crop resilience and productivity.
