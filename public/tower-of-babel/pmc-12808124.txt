Comparative tolerance and phytoremediation potential of four Lagerstroemia indica cultivars under cadmium stress
Authors: Jing Li, Yilong Li, Doudou Zi, Feifei Zhai, Shurong Deng, Yunxing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWoody ornamentals hold significant promise for phytoremediation. However, the cadmium (Cd) tolerance and accumulation ability of the commonly planted Lagerstroemia indica, have not been insufficiently studied. This study evaluated four L. indica cultivars (‘Red Rocket’, ‘Pink Velour’, ‘Dynamite’, and ‘Black Diamond’) under soil Cd stress (0, 10, 50, and 100 mg·kg− 1) in an 80-day pot experiment. Significant intraspecific variation in Cd tolerance was observed, reflected in distinct growth and Cd accumulation strategies. ‘Dynamite’ showed a hormetic growth response at 10 mg·kg− 1 Cd but suffered complete mortality at 100 mg·kg− 1 Cd, indicating low tolerance and limited phytoextraction potential. In contrast, ‘Red Rocket’ and ‘Black Diamond’ demonstrated a consistent phytostabilization strategy. They maintained stable growth (tolerance index, TI > 1.0) at the highest Cd level (100 mg·kg− 1), characterized by effective root Cd retention with high root bioconcentration factors (BCFs > 1.0) and low translocation factors (TFs < 1.0). ‘Pink Velour’ displayed a plastic response. It showed a balanced root-shoot distribution (TF > 1.0) at 50 mg·kg− 1 Cd, but under high stress (100 mg·kg− 1), it showed pronounced root sequestration (TF < 1.0). This confirms phytostabilization as its primary tolerance mechanism. These cultivar-specific strategies highlight the potential of selecting specific L. indica cultivars for the phytomanagement of Cd-contaminated soils.Keywords: Woody ornamentals, Lagerstroemia indica, Cadmium tolerance, Cadmium accumulation, Phytoextraction, PhytostabilizationSubject terms: Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Soil contamination by cadmium (Cd), characterized by high chemical persistence and significant biological toxicity, poses severe ecological and public health risks in China1. The widespread contamination is primarily driven by anthropogenic activities such as industrial mining, smelting, and intensive agriculture, which have significantly increased Cd mobilization into the environment2,3. Industrial emissions, particularly through atmospheric deposition, are widely recognized as a predominant source of Cd input in many contaminated areas4–8. The resulting Cd exposure threatens both ecosystems and human health. In plants, chronic exposure to trace amounts (0.2–2.0 mg·kg− 1 ) can disrupt nutrient balance, induce chlorosis, and alter root architecture9. In animals and humans, Cd primarily accumulates in the liver and kidneys, leading to organ dysfunction10,11.

Phytoremediation has emerged as a sustainable strategy to address this issue by using plants to extract and stabilize heavy metals. However, the efficacy of this technology is fundamentally determined by the physiological and molecular mechanisms for Cd uptake, translocation, and detoxification12. Cd entry into plants is primarily facilitated by transporters of essential metals13,14. In roots, Cd2+ competes for uptake through transporters such as the iron transporter IRT1 and the manganese transporter NRAMP515,16. Following absorption, Cd is translocated from roots to shoots by the xylem, a process driven by transpiration, which is a critical determinant of phytoextraction efficiency17,18. This process is regulated by pivotal transporters. Specifically, HMA proteins mediate xylem loading, facilitating Cd translocation to shoots. In contrast, CAX and HMA3 transporters sequester Cd into root vacuoles, which limits its upward movement14,15.

Among other mechanisms, Cd exerts its phytotoxicity once inside the plant by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inhibiting crucial processes like photosynthesis19–21. To mitigate Cd toxicity, plants have thus evolved a multi-layered defense system22. This includes initial exclusion through root exudates and binding to cell wall components. Subsequently, intracellular chelation occurs by ligands such as glutathione (GSH) and phytochelatins (PCs). Ultimately, Cd is sequestered into vacuoles by transporters including HMA3 and CAX23–25. The balance between these uptake, translocation, and detoxification strategies ultimately determines Cd accumulation patterns and phytoremediation efficacy. The efficiency of these physiological and molecular processes directly influences a plant’s suitability for phytoremediation.

Despite more than 400 hyperaccumulator plant species being identified to date26,27, recognized Cd hyperaccumulators (such as Solanum nigrum and Sedum plumbizincicola) remain limited28–30. Furthermore, most are herbaceous species with inherent limitations such as low biomass and shallow root systems, which restrict their efficiency and practical application31. In contrast, woody plants—with greater biomass, deeper root zones, and higher transpiration rates—represent promising candidates for enhanced metal extraction and accumulation32–34. Recent screening reveals substantial interspecific variation in Cd tolerance among ornamental woody species, ranging from the stimulated growth in Euryops pectinatus to the significant inhibition in Gardenia jasminoides35, raising the question of whether such contrasting strategies exist at the cultivar level. Among them, landscape woody ornamentals offer a strategic advantage by not only extracting soil contaminants but also revitalizing the visual quality of degraded environments, as highlighted in recent phytoremediation screening studies. Woody species such as Salix and Populus exemplify this multi-functionality: they have demonstrated significant potential for phytoremediation by exhibiting a notable ability to extract soil Cd. Beyond their remediation efficacy, their rapid growth and distinct forms can quickly provide visual structure and greenery, thereby contributing to the ecological and aesthetic revitalization of contaminated sites16,36–38.

Building on the multi-functionality of woody plants, research is increasingly targeting species that are valued primarily for their ornamental appeal. As a representative landscape woody ornamental, Lagerstroemia indica (Lythraceae) has emerged as a promising candidate. Preliminary research indicates that certain cultivars can accumulate high levels of Cd in their leaves, highlighting their phytoextraction potential5. However, despite these promising findings, the tolerance and accumulation mechanisms of L. indica for Cd remain incompletely understood. The well-documented variation in metal uptake efficiency among cultivars makes comprehensive screening of specific germplasms essential to identify superior candidates5.

In this study, we evaluated the Cd tolerance and accumulation capacities of four L. indica cultivars (i.e., Lagerstroemia indica ‘Red Rocket’ (‘Red Rocket’), Lagerstroemia indica ‘Pink Velour’ (‘Pink Velour’), Lagerstroemia indica ‘Dynamite’ (‘Dynamite’), and Lagerstroemia indica ‘Black Diamond’ (‘Black Diamond’)) through controlled pot experiments with Cd-contaminated soil. This study aimed to compare Cd accumulation patterns among cultivars, elucidate the cultivar-specific mechanisms underlying differential Cd tolerance, and identify superior cultivars for phytoremediation applications. Our findings offer fundamental insights into Cd handling strategies in this ornamental species and provide a basis for selecting effective genotypes for soil remediation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental design

Healthy seeds of four L. indica cultivars (‘Red Rocket’, ‘Pink Velour’, ‘Dynamite’, and ‘Black Diamond’) were obtained from a commercial seed supplier in China and germinated in trays containing a 2:1 (v/v) mixture of peat and vermiculite5. Germination and seedling growth were conducted under controlled greenhouse conditions for 20–30 days. Specifically, the pots were maintained in a greenhouse at day/night temperature of 22–25℃, with relative humidity of 60–75%, and photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 300–500 µmol·m-2·s− 1 under a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod39,40. Seedlings with 4–5 true leaves were selected for transplantation. Test soil (0–20 cm depth) was collected from an uncontaminated site at the South Campus of Henan Polytechnic University (35°11’12"N, 113°15’27"E, Jiaozuo, China). The soil had the following properties: pH 8.37, total N 471.10 mg·kg− 1 , available P 2.85 mg·kg− 1 , available K 145.89 mg·kg− 1 , and organic matter 10.30 g·kg− 1 . Air-dried soil (< 2 mm) was homogenized and spiked with CdCl2·2.5H2O to establish four contamination levels: 0 (control), 10, 50, and 100 mg Cd·kg− 1 dry soil. Following 14-day equilibration at field capacity, soils were packed into 9 cm diameter plastic pots. Uniform seedlings were transplanted (one per pot, 4–6 replicates per treatment) and watered to maintain soil moisture. After 80 days of Cd exposure, plants were harvested.

Growth, Biomass, and root morphometric measurements

Plants were harvested after 80 days of growth. During the Cd treatment period, growth parameters, including shoot height, and the length and width of the third pair of fully expanded leaves were measured periodically, with four to six seedlings per Cd concentration serving as biological replicates. At harvest, plants were carefully removed from the pot. The root systems were gently washed free of soil over a 0.5 mm sieve to retain lateral roots less than 1 mm in diameter. The intact roots were then spread flat in a water-filled tray and scanned at 600 dpi using a GXY-A Root Analysis System39. All plant tissues were subjected to enzyme inactivation at 105 °C for 30 min, followed by oven-dried at 70℃ until a constant weight was achieved for biomass determination39. Finally, the dried samples were ground into a homogeneous powder using a plant grinder for subsequent analysis.

Determination of Cd contents

The HNO3-HClO4 digestion method (4:1, v: v) was used for plant Cd analysis. Approximately 0.1 g of dried, finely ground plant material was digested with 20 ml acid mixture on a hot plate. The solution was heated until reduced to 1–2 ml, cooled to room temperature, filtered through a membrane, and diluted to 10 ml with deionized water. For soil Cd analysis, an aqua regia-hydrogen peroxide digestion method was employed. Approximately 0.25 g of oven-dried soil (passed through a 0.25 mm sieve) was digested with 20 ml of 50% aqua regia (HCl: HNO3 = 3:1, v: v) on a hot plate. After cooling, the digestate was filtered and diluted to 10 ml in a volumetric flask41. Cd concentrations in both plant and soil digests were quantified using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS-300, PerkinElmer Inc., USA).

Calculation of Cd tolerance and accumulation indices

The tolerance index (TI), bioaccumulation factor (BCF), and translocation factor (TF) of the four L. indica cultivars were calculated as follows: TI = Shoot or root dry weight of Cd-treated plant / Shoot or root dry weight of control plant; BCF = Cd concentration in shoot or root / Cd concentration in soil; and TF = Cd concentration in shoot / Cd concentration in root5,42,43.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Excel 2016 and SPSS 23.0. Significant differences among treatments (p < 0.05) were determined by Duncan’s multiple range test.

Results

Seedling growth parameters

After 80 days of Cd stress, plant growth responses were quantified by measuring shoot height, leaf length, leaf width, and whole-plant biomass. Under 10 mg·kg-1 Cd stress, ‘Dynamite’ was the only cultivar that displayed a stimulatory growth response, with significant increases in shoot height and leaf width compared to the control (Fig. 1a, c). The other three cultivars remained comparable to the control across all measured growth parameters (Fig. 1). When Cd concentration was raised to 50 mg·kg− 1 , ‘Dynamite’ showed a significant increase in shoot height increment, while that of ‘Red Rocket’ maintained an increasing trend (Fig. 1a). The effect on ‘Dynamite’ remained the most pronounced among all cultivars. At the highest concentration of 100 mg·kg− 1 Cd, the divergence in cultivar tolerance became even more pronounced. ‘Dynamite’ suffered complete mortality. Conversely, ‘Black Diamond’ and ‘Pink Velour’ not only survived but displayed significant growth promotion in shoot height and leaf dimensions (Fig. 1a,b, c).

Under 10 mg·kg-1 Cd stress, ‘Dynamite’ demonstrated both shoot and root TIs above 1.0. Discussion

Cultivar-Specific growth responses under Cd stress

Cd is a toxic element that generally inhibits plant growth and disrupts physiological processes, as evidenced in sunflowers (Helianthus annuus) where Cd stress significantly reduced plant height, root length, and chlorophyll content44,45. However, the existence of cultivar-specific tolerance mechanisms can lead to divergent responses, a phenomenon observed in our study across four L. indica cultivars (Fig. 1) and further exemplified by other ornamental species. For instance, while Gardenia jasminoides growth was inhibited at 20–40 mg·kg− 1 Cd, Euryops pectinatus exhibited stimulated shoot growth at concentrations below 40 mg·kg− 1 Cd35. Notably, this growth disparity was underpinned by divergent antioxidant strategies, with E. pectinatus sustaining high enzyme activity to minimize oxidative damage, in contrast to the weak response and significant damage seen in G. jasminoides35. This contrast underscores the pivotal role of the antioxidant system in determining Cd tolerance outcomes. Consistent with the importance of this mechanism, the cultivar in the study ‘Dynamite’ demonstrated a classic hormetic response under 10 mg·kg− 1 Cd stress, with significant increases in plant height, leaf width, and biomass, with shoot and root tolerance indices (TIs) reaching 2.04 and 3.00, respectively (Figs. 1, 2). We propose that this growth stimulation is linked to the activation of its antioxidant defense system. Low-dose Cd stress may have triggered a beneficial level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, enhancing the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), as reported in other species exhibiting hormesis46–49. This adaptive response might reduce basal oxidative damage, contributing to the observed growth promotion. The ability of ‘Dynamite’ to maintain this delicate ROS balance likely underlies its superior performance under low Cd stress, a conserved adaptive mechanism seen across diverse plant species, from herbaceous Brassica juncea L50 to woody Lonicera japonica Thunb51,52.

In contrast, ‘Red Rocket’, ‘Pink Velour’, and ‘Black Diamond’ maintained stable growth under the same 10 mg·kg− 1 Cd treatment, showing neither significant inhibition nor the stimulatory hormesis seen in ‘Dynamite’ (Fig. 1). A notable pattern emerged for ‘Pink Velour’ and ‘Black Diamond’, which displayed root TIs greater than 1.0 with shoot TIs less than 1.0 (Fig. 2). This specific response points towards a root-sequestration strategy, where Cd is preferentially retained and immobilized in the root system to restrict its translocation to and damage of the aerial parts. We hypothesize that this stable performance under Cd stress is underpinned by a highly efficient, possibly constitutive, root-based defense system. This system likely involves elevated basal levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants like glutathione (GSH) and ascorbate (AsA) to mitigate oxidative stress, coupled with metal chelators such as phytochelatins (PCs) for intracellular detoxification. Together, these mechanisms may pre-adapt these cultivars to metal stress without necessitating major physiological adjustments46,53. The physiological basis for these divergent growth responses may lie in the balance between Cd-induced oxidative stress and the plant’s antioxidant capacity. At low levels, ROS are recognized as important signaling molecules that can contribute to enhanced plant growth, whereas excessive accumulation causes oxidative damage to cellular structures. When Cd concentration increased to 50 mg·kg− 1, all cultivars still demonstrated strong tolerance, maintaining shoot and root TIs above 1.0 without visible growth inhibition (Fig. 2). Notably, ‘Dynamite’ showed a significant increase in plant height and biomass, and ‘Red Rocket’ also exhibited a growth increment (Fig. 1a, d). This resilience contrasts sharply with the progressive growth inhibition typically observed in conventional species such as Triticum aestivum54 and Medicago sativa55 under comparable Cd stress, underscoring the superior and inherent Cd resistance of L. indica.

At the highest Cd concentration of 100 mg·kg− 1, cultivar differences became even more pronounced. ‘Dynamite’ suffered complete mortality, indicating a catastrophic failure of its detoxification mechanisms. This failure plausibly involved irreversible enzyme inactivation via Cd-sulfhydryl binding, severe membrane lipid peroxidation (potentially indicated by elevated MDA), and a collapse of the antioxidant system, surpassing the threshold for cellular survival. The response of ‘Dynamite’ parallels that of the Cd-sensitive Gardenia jasminoides, which also exhibited a collapsed antioxidant defense under high Cd stress35. In contrast, ‘Pink Velour’ and ‘Black Diamond’ not only survived but thrived, with biomass significantly exceeding the control (Fig. 1d) and both root and shoot TIs remaining above 1.0 (Fig. 2). The root TI of ‘Black Diamond’ reached 2.23, highlighting a superior capacity for Cd immobilization in root tissues. This remarkable maintenance of growth under severe metal stress is consistent with reports from other tolerant ornamental shrubs such as Hydrangea macrophylla and Hibiscus syriacus19,43, suggesting that root plasticity and efficient internal detoxification may be conserved tolerance strategies among tolerant woody species. The ability of these cultivars to retain high biomass under elevated Cd stress confirms that biomass maintenance is a key tolerance mechanism28. Therefore, the exceptional Cd tolerance of ‘Pink Velour’ and ‘Black Diamond’ not only distinguishes them from other L. indica cultivars but also highlights their potential for the application in phytoremediation. The observed disparities in shoot growth and biomass response prompted our investigation into whether below-ground architectural modifications underpin these cultivar-specific tolerance phenotypes.

Cultivar-Specific root architectural responses under Cd stress

The divergent shoot growth responses among L. indica cultivars under Cd stress were underpinned by distinct root architectural adaptations. As the primary interface for metal perception, roots undergo strategic structural modifications that have been demonstrated to directly influence Cd uptake, xylem loading, and subsequent accumulation in aerial parts16,56. The observed inter-cultivar variation in root morphological responses underscores the critical role of genetic background in shaping these adaptations. It is important to note that the relationship is complex: while a greater root surface area can increase interfacial metal contact, the ultimate outcome for shoot biomass depends on the integration of uptake with efficient root-internal detoxification mechanisms57. At the lower Cd concentration (10 mg·kg− 1), a pronounced divergence in root strategy emerged (Table 1). ‘Red Rocket’, ‘Pink Velour’ and ‘Black Diamond’ maintained root growth comparable to the control, indicating a constitutive tolerance that does not require major morphological adjustment. In contrast, ‘Dynamite’ displayed a significant hormetic stimulation of its root architecture (Table 1). This morphological response may be driven by a Cd-induced reconfiguration of phytohormone signaling pathways, such as auxin and cytokinin6,58, leading to extensive root expansion59. The expansion in ‘Dynamite’ may be driven by a Cd-induced reconfiguration of its hormonal network, promoting lateral root formation. This improved root architecture could enhance tolerance not necessarily by reducing uptake, but by increasing the root system’s capacity for metal chelation and detoxification in the apoplast or symplast, thereby restricting translocation to the shoots56,60.

Under moderate Cd stress (50 mg·kg− 1), both ‘Dynamite’ and ‘Red Rocket’ showed remarkable enhancement in root growth (Table 1), indicating an active acclimatization strategy61. This allocation of resources to root expansion likely served to sustain nutrient and water uptake under stress, thereby stabilizing shoot growth. However, as this also increases the interface for Cd interception, their tolerance ultimately depends on coupling this morphological plasticity with robust internal detoxification mechanisms. In woody plants like poplar, this is achieved through mechanisms such as vacuolar sequestration and the upregulation of genes involved in cell wall lignification62,63. At the highest Cd level (100 mg·kg− 1), the superior tolerance of ‘Pink Velour’ and ‘Black Diamond’ was clearly manifested in their significant increases in root length, surface area, and volume (Table 1). This strategic root expansion under duress enhances soil exploration for resource acquisition while simultaneously providing a larger sink for metal sequestration, thereby mitigating translocation to and toxicity in shoots. Such plasticity represents a conserved adaptive mechanism against metal toxicity, as evidenced in other heavy-metal stressed plants64. The strong correlation between this pronounced root plasticity and their high TIs (Fig. 2) underscores that root architectural remodeling is a fundamental component of the superior Cd resistance in these cultivars, a phenomenon also observed in metal hyperaccumulators like Cannabis sativa L65,66 and Sedum alfredii66. Collectively, the observed differences in root morphology and architecture across cultivars highlight the fundamental role of root plasticity in driving these distinct adaptive strategies.

Cultivar-Specific differences in Cd tolerance and phytoremediation potential

The divergent growth and root morphological responses among cultivars were paralleled by distinct strategies for Cd accumulation and partitioning. As expected, tissue Cd concentrations generally increased with rising external Cd levels (Table 2), consistent with the dose-dependent uptake pattern widely observed in plants67,68. However, the distribution between organs varied significantly, revealing a prevalent strategy of root sequestration, which was most pronounced in ‘Red Rocket’ and ‘Black Diamond’. This phenomenon, consistent with findings in other species69–71, suggests a robust mechanism for limiting Cd translocation that likely involves strong binding to root cell walls and efficient vacuolar sequestration. A comparative study on woody species elucidates the mechanistic basis of root sequestration35. The highly tolerant Euryops pectinatus detoxified Cd via vacuolar compartmentalization and organo-chelation, a strategy aligned with HMA3/MTP transporter activity, whereas Gardenia jasminoides relied on cell wall immobilization. This evidence confirms that the root sequestration we observed involves a complex interplay between these symplastic and apoplastic pathways. The observed pattern of low translocation but high root bioconcentration may be explained by efficient vacuolar sequestration. This process is presumably facilitated by the coordinated action of transporters such as HMA3 and MTPs. Consequently, Cd is effectively immobilized in the roots, preventing its transfer to and damage of the aerial tissues72,73.

In contrast, ‘Dynamite’ showed a unique and concentration-dependent accumulation pattern. Under moderate Cd stress(50 mg·kg− 1), it accumulated more Cd in the shoots than in the roots (Table 2), resembling hyperaccumulators such as Phytolacca americana L.74. This suggests the activation of an efficient root-to-shoot translocation mechanism, which relies on enhanced cadmium loading into the xylem. This process is typically mediated by xylem-loading transporters, such as HMA2 and HMA4 in other plants28,75. However, the complete mortality of ‘Dynamite’ at the highest concentration (100 mg·kg− 1) indicates that this translocation mechanism was coupled with an inadequate internal detoxification capacity in the shoots. The substantial energy expenditure for root expansion and Cd transport may have compromised the shoot’s capacity to synthesize metal-chelating ligands. It is plausible that a deficiency in one of the primary classes of such ligands, phytochelatins (PCs), left incoming Cd ions un-sequestered. Consequently, the disruption of cellular homeostasis led to severe membrane lipid peroxidation and irreversible enzyme inactivation, culminating in cell death68,76,77.

Analysis of bioconcentration (BCF) and translocation (TF) factors further elucidated these strategies (Table 3). As external Cd levels rose, both shoot and root BCF values generally declined across all cultivars, suggesting a potential saturation of Cd uptake mechanisms or activation of exclusion strategies under higher stress78,79. Cultivars such as ‘Red Rocket’ and ‘Black Diamond’ consistently maintained TFs < 1.0 and high root BCFs confirming a phytostabilization phenotype centered on root immobilization5. ‘Dynamite’, with its TF > 1.0 under moderate Cd stress (50 mg·kg− 1), demonstrated a transient shoot-accumulation capacity that proved non-viable. The Cd accumulation pattern in ‘Pink Velour’ highlights a unique, plastic adaptive strategy. While its TF approached 1.0 under moderate stress (50 mg·kg− 1), indicating a balanced distribution, its most notable response was under high Cd stress (100 mg·kg− 1), where root Cd content surged to a substantial 177.67 mg·kg− 1, demonstrating a clear preference for root storage. This shift underscores that its primary and most effective tolerance mechanism under severe stress is efficient root storage, which aligns it more closely with a phytostabilization strategy than with phytoextraction. This physiological plasticity in partitioning may be regulated by a Cd-induced reconfiguration of phytohormone signaling pathways31,57,66, potentially reallocating resources between growth and tolerance under stress.

Therefore, the distinct patterns of Cd accumulation and translocation directly determined the differential Cd tolerance and phytoremediation potential observed among the L. indica cultivars. Based on this mechanism, ‘Red Rocket’ and ‘Black Diamond’ emerge as promising candidates for phytostabilization due to their consistent root-sequestration strategy. The superior tolerance and robust root-based metal immobilization, particularly of ‘Black Diamond’, position it as the standout cultivar. In contrast, the high Cd sensitivity of ‘Dynamite’ disqualifies it for practical use. The phenotypic plasticity of ‘Pink Velour’ in Cd partitioning, while a distinct physiological trait, ultimately reinforces its role in tolerance through root storage rather than in a targeted, high-efficiency remediation strategy like phytoextraction.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that intraspecific variation in L. indica provides a spectrum of adaptive mechanisms to Cd stress, primarily centered on the plasticity of root architecture and the efficiency of Cd partitioning. Our findings identify ‘Black Diamond’ and ‘Red Rocket’ as superior candidates for phytostabilization, thus providing promising germplasm resources for phytoremediation. Furthermore, the findings clarify the physiological basis for selecting ornamental cultivars, which relies on root sequestration capacity and translocation patterns. Further investigation is needed to validate these mechanisms under field conditions and to elucidate the molecular pathways governing root architecture and Cd partitioning.
