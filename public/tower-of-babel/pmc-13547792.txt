Dual Thiol Drug–Induced Insulin Autoimmune Syndrome in a Non-Asian Patient Carrying HLA-DRB1*04:06: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Mohammad Adi, Doniyor Umarov, Zairullo Oqboyev, Aseel Smerat, Ahmed Ashraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInsulin autoimmune syndrome (IAS, Hirata’s disease) is a rare cause of endogenous hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia classically linked to high-titer insulin autoantibodies and exposure to sulfhydryl (–SH)–containing drugs. IAS is strongly HLA-associated and is most frequently reported in East Asian populations. We report a case in which simultaneous exposure to two thiol-containing agents unmasked IAS in a non-Asian patient carrying HLA-DRB1*04:06.Case presentationA 60-year-old woman developed recurrent autonomic and neuroglycopenic episodes 2–4 hours after meals. Home medications included captopril and over-the-counter α-lipoic acid (ALA). During a symptomatic episode her plasma glucose was 38 mg/dL with markedly elevated serum insulin, C-peptide and proinsulin; mixed-meal testing reproduced a postprandial glucose peak followed by profound hypoglycemia accompanied by paradoxical hyperinsulinemia. Insulin autoantibodies were strongly positive (>150 U/mL) and HLA genotyping revealed DRB1*04:06. A supervised fast was non-diagnostic for insulinoma. Management comprised immediate discontinuation of both captopril and ALA (captopril replaced with losartan) and dietary measures (frequent small low-carbohydrate meals). Hypoglycemic episodes abated within two weeks and resolved by six weeks; fasting glucose, insulin and IAA titers had substantially improved by three months.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first reported case of IAS temporally associated with concurrent captopril and ALA exposure in a non-Asian carrier of HLA-DRB1*04:06. The case highlights the need to review both prescription drugs and supplements when investigating hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, that IAS can occur outside typical ethnic groups when permissive HLA alleles are present, and that prompt withdrawal of offending agents with conservative measures often leads to spontaneous remission.Keywords: insulin autoimmune syndrome, thiol-containing drugs, HLA-DRB1*04:06, postprandial hypoglycemia, drug-induced hyperinsulinemia

Introduction

Insulin autoimmune syndrome (IAS, Hirata’s disease) is a rare autoimmune cause of endogenous hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia. 1 It presents as recurrent hypoglycemia (often postprandial) despite extremely high serum insulin levels and high-titer insulin autoantibodies.1,2 First described in Japan in 1970, IAS remains most frequently reported in Asian populations (especially Japan), although sporadic cases have been documented worldwide. 3 Many cases are drug-induced; commonly implicated medications contain sulfhydryl (–SH) groups such as methimazole, α-lipoic acid (ALA), captopril and tiopronin.2,3 Biochemically, IAS presents with non-suppressible hyperinsulinemia and disproportionately elevated C-peptide and proinsulin levels, often producing an insulin-to-C-peptide molar ratio >1 due to antibody-bound insulin. Diagnosis is confirmed by high-titer insulin autoantibodies in the context of endogenous hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia.1,4

IAS has a well-established HLA association. Over 90% of Japanese IAS patients carry HLA-DRB104:06, whereas reported Caucasian series often involve HLA-DRB104:03.3,5 The pathogenesis involves formation of high - capacity/low-affinity insulin–antibody complexes, which initially bind secreted insulin and later release it unpredictably, causing glycemic swings. Clinically, dietary management with frequent small low-carbohydrate meals is advised to blunt insulin surges, and immediate withdrawal of any offending drug is critical. Most patients improve spontaneously within weeks to months after trigger removal, although severe cases may require medications such as corticosteroids, acarbose or even plasmapheresis.1,4

Multiple elements of our case are notably distinctive. The patient had simultaneous exposure to two thiol-containing medications - captopril and ALA - an unusual scenario given that IAS is typically linked to a single triggering agent. She also carried the HLA-DRB1*04:06 allele, strongly associated with IAS in Asian populations but rarely identified in non-Asian individuals.2,3,5 Her presentation was limited to postprandial hypoglycemia with a pronounced mixed-meal response and markedly elevated insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin levels, reflecting a classic yet exceptionally robust biochemical profile. The prompt resolution of symptoms and decline in insulin autoantibody titers following drug discontinuation underscore the reversibility of the condition when identified early. 4 Taken together, the dual environmental triggers, uncommon genetic background, and dramatic but fully reversible clinical course render this case a rare addition to the IAS literature.

Case Presentation

A 60-year-old woman presented to the endocrinology clinic with a 3-month history of recurrent episodes of tremors, profuse sweating, palpitations, and mental confusion. These episodes occurred 2-4 hours after meals and were reliably relieved by carbohydrate intake. Her past medical history was significant for essential hypertension and pre-diabetes (HbA1c 6.2% one year prior). Her home medications included captopril 25 mg twice daily, amlodipine 5 mg daily, and over-the-counter α-lipoic acid (ALA) 600 mg twice daily, which she had started for mild peripheral neuropathy six months prior to symptom onset. The patient’s self-identified ancestry was Caucasian, with no known East Asian heritage in her family background.

Physical examination revealed a well-appearing female with a body mass index of 32 kg/m2. Vital signs were stable. Neurological and cardiopulmonary examinations were unremarkable. There was no stigmata of insulin injection.

During a symptomatic episode in the emergency department, her point-of-care blood glucose was 38 mg/dL. Laboratory confirmation and further workup were initiated. The key initial and dynamic test results are summarized in Table 1.

Detailed Laboratory Investigations

A supervised 72-hour fast was terminated at 24 hours as the patient remained euglycemic and ketonemic, ruling out an insulinoma. Given the strong post-prandial nature of her symptoms, a mixed-meal tolerance test (MMTT) was performed. The results were dramatic and diagnostic for a disorder of reactive hypoglycemia (Figure 1).

Mixed-Meal Tolerance Test (MMTT) Demonstrating Reactive Hypoglycemia and Paradoxical Hyperinsulinemia. Description of Figure 1: This line graph shows plasma glucose (mg/dL, blue line, left Y-axis) and serum insulin (µIU/mL, red line, right Y-axis) over 300 minutes following a standardized mixed meal. Glucose peaks at 60 minutes (210 mg/dL) and then precipitously declines to a nadir of 45 mg/dL at 180 minutes, coinciding with the patient's typical symptoms. Notably, insulin levels remain extraordinarily elevated (>150 µIU/mL) throughout the entire test, even as glucose falls to hypoglycemic levels, demonstrating a profound failure of insulin suppression.

Given the biochemical profile of non-suppressible hyperinsulinemia, the negative fast, and the lack of exogenous insulin use, IAS was suspected. This was confirmed by the strongly positive insulin autoantibody (IAA) test. HLA genotyping revealed the presence of the susceptible HLA-DRB1*04:06 allele.

The primary intervention was the immediate discontinuation of both thiol-containing agents: captopril and ALA. Captopril was switched to losartan. The patient was counseled on a strict dietary regimen consisting of frequent, small, low-carbohydrate, high-protein meals to minimize large insulin secretory bursts.

The patient’s hypoglycemic episodes decreased in frequency and severity within two weeks and resolved completely by six weeks. Follow-up laboratory testing at 3 months showed remarkable improvement (Figure 2).

Discussion

IAS is a rare cause of endogenous hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, classically presenting with spontaneous hypoglycemia, marked hyperinsulinemia, and high-titer insulin autoantibodies in individuals with no prior exposure to exogenous insulin.2,3 IAS is well-recognized in Japan and other East Asian regions—accounting for up to one-third of spontaneous hypoglycemia cases in Japan—yet it remains distinctly uncommon in Western countries. 3 Numerous studies have shown that IAS is strongly associated with exposure to sulfhydryl-containing drugs, which may disrupt insulin’s disulfide bonds and increase its immunogenicity. Pharmacovigilance analyses and case-series reviews have identified more than 40 medications linked to IAS across over 250 published cases, with antithyroid drugs, α-lipoic acid (ALA), clopidogrel, tiopronin, captopril, and other thiol-bearing compounds among the most frequent triggers.1,6 More than 90% of affected individuals report prior use of a thiol agent or a medication metabolized into a thiol. 1 This strong environmental association underscores the importance of a thorough medication and supplement review when evaluating unexplained hypoglycemia.1,6

IAS is also strongly linked to HLA class II alleles. More than 90% of Japanese patients carry HLA-DRB104:06, which is thought to enhance presentation of thiol-modified insulin peptides, potentially due to a serine residue at position β37 of the DRβ chain. In contrast, only about 1% of Caucasians carry HLA-DRB1*04:06; IAS cases in Western populations are instead most commonly associated with HLA-DRB104:03.6,7 Reports of European IAS cases triggered by ALA have shown that among six affected individuals, five carried HLA-DRB104:03 and one carried DRB104:06. 5 Additional published cases involving 2 Italian women with ALA-induced IAS also demonstrated HLA-DRB1*04:03 as the predominant allele. Overall, fewer than 60 non-Asian IAS cases have been reported in the literature. When these occur, they are most often associated with ALA or other thiol-containing supplements and show a clear predominance of the HLA-DRB1*04:03 allele.3,6-8

Although our patient had no known East Asian ancestry, she was found to carry HLA-DRB1*04:06, an allele that is strongly associated with IAS in Asian populations yet rarely reported in other ethnic groups. Only isolated non-Asian cases in the literature have documented this allele, suggesting that while rare, HLA-DRB1*04:06–associated IAS can occur outside its typical geographic and ethnic context. It remains unclear whether this allele confers the same degree of susceptibility in non-Asian individuals, but its presence raises the possibility that certain immunogenetic backgrounds may facilitate the formation of high-capacity, low-affinity insulin–antibody complexes in the setting of thiol-drug exposure. 5 We emphasize that the presence of HLA-DRB1*04:06 represents a genetic association with IAS susceptibility rather than establishing causation, and the functional significance of this allele in non-Asian populations requires further investigation.

This case shows a rare IAS presentation with dual thiol-containing triggers. Whereas most reported cases involve a single offending drug (such as methimazole, ALA, clopidogrel, captopril), our patient combined captopril and ALA exposures. Both agents are well-documented IAS inducers: antithyroid drugs and ALA are among the most frequently reported triggers (ALA appears in ∼14–15% of cases), and captopril, a sulfhydryl ACE inhibitor, has also been implicated in multiple case reports. 6 In IAS, sulfhydryl groups are thought to alter endogenous insulin’s structure, rendering it more immunogenic by reducing disulfide bonds within the insulin molecule. 7 This mechanistic link helps explain why many IAS cases involve thiol drugs: they can induce high-capacity, low-affinity insulin–antibody complexes that sequester insulin and later release it unpredictably, causing profound glycemic swings. In our patient, the combined exposure to two such drugs may have contributed to the dramatic biochemical profile, although we acknowledge that this remains speculative. Indeed, systematic analyses confirm that thiol-containing medications dominate IAS triggers, and that captopril, methimazole, and clopidogrel rank among the strongest signals for inducing IAS.6,7 While many IAS patients take multiple medications, introducing some uncertainty in identifying the causal agent, published case series and pharmacovigilance analyses typically attribute IAS to a single drug per case. Known thiol-containing triggers include methimazole, captopril, and ALA, each documented individually in prior reports. Although it is conceivable that simultaneous or sequential exposure to captopril and ALA could have a cumulative immunogenic effect on insulin, any synergistic mechanism remains speculative and has not been demonstrated. Rather than definitively establishing synergy, this case provides a temporal association between dual exposure and IAS onset. In the literature, drug-induced IAS generally implicates one agent at a time, and to our knowledge, no prior cases have described IAS triggered by concurrent use of captopril and ALA, making this dual-exposure scenario unprecedented.1,5,6 We present the possibility of synergistic or additive effects as a hypothesis that warrants further investigation rather than a proven mechanism.

IAS typically manifests as postprandial hypoglycemia due to the unpredictable release of antibody-bound insulin. Biochemically, it is characterized by markedly elevated insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin levels, often with an insulin:C-peptide molar ratio greater than one. Although extreme insulin and antibody elevations are striking, similar robust profiles have been reported in prior cases. The condition is generally reversible, with most drug-induced IAS patients improving within weeks to months after withdrawal of the triggering agent and implementation of dietary measures. Severe or persistent cases may require immunosuppression or plasmapheresis, but such interventions are uncommon.1,2,7

This case report has several strengths, including the comprehensive biochemical workup with MMTT demonstrating classic reactive hypoglycemia and paradoxical hyperinsulinemia, detailed HLA genotyping confirming the presence of HLA-DRB1*04:06, and complete longitudinal follow-up data documenting clinical and biochemical resolution after drug discontinuation. The documentation of dual thiol-containing drug exposure adds to the emerging literature on drug-induced IAS.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. As a single case report, causality cannot be definitively established; the temporal association between drug exposure and IAS onset, while compelling, does not prove causation. The proposed synergistic mechanism between captopril and ALA remains speculative and is based on a single observation rather than experimental evidence. Additionally, the findings may not be generalizable to broader populations, and the functional significance of HLA-DRB1*04:06 in non-Asian individuals requires further investigation. Finally, the lack of functional immunological studies limits our mechanistic understanding of the drug-allele interaction in this patient.

Conclusion

This case expands the clinical spectrum of IAS by highlighting how multiple environmental and genetic factors can intersect to unmask the syndrome. The simultaneous use of two independent thiol drugs (captopril and ALA) appeared to have potentially contributed to triggering IAS in a predisposed individual; however, this observation remains speculative and requires further investigation. Additionally, the presence of the Asian-associated HLA-DRB1*04:06 allele in a non-Asian patient emphasizes that clinicians should not discount IAS based on ethnicity alone, while recognizing that HLA allele carriage represents a susceptibility factor rather than a causative determinant. Practitioners should maintain a high index of suspicion for IAS in any patient with unexplained hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia – especially the postprandial type – and should inquire specifically about medications and supplements containing sulfhydryl groups. Early recognition can spare patients unnecessary invasive evaluations and enable curative intervention (drug withdrawal and dietary counseling). Ultimately, this case reinforces that IAS, though rare, is an important reversible cause of hypoglycemia that must be considered in the appropriate clinical context.
