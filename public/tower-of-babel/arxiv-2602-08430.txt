Understanding and Optimizing Attention-Based Sparse Matching for Diverse Local Features
Qiang Wang

Abstract
We revisit the problem of training attention-based sparse image matching models for various local features. We first identify one critical design choice that has been previously overlooked, which significantly impacts the performance of the LightGlue model. We then investigate the role of detectors and descriptors within the transformer-based matching framework, finding that detectors, rather than descriptors, are often the primary cause for performance difference. Finally, we propose a novel approach to fine-tune existing image matching models using keypoints from a diverse set of detectors, resulting in a universal, detector-agnostic model. When deployed as a zero-shot matcher for novel detectors, the resulting model achieves or exceeds the accuracy of models specifically trained for those features. Our findings offer valuable insights for the deployment of transformer-based matching models and the future design of local features.

Upon revisiting the training of existing matching models, we identified that the presence of nearby keypoints—introduced by multi-scale extraction or the absence of non-maximum suppression—can significantly degrade the matching performance.
Our analysis not only facilitates the training of LightGlue models for binary features such as ORB but also isolates the individual impact of detectors and descriptors on the matching performance.
For non-binary features, the matching differences of transformer-based matching models are mostly caused by detectors. For binary features, the descriptors contribute more significantly for final performance.

Their model trained with SuperPoint keypoints can work for SIFT detectors, but the performance when applied to other features is unclear.

We show that most existing descriptors can work well across different detectors under attention-based matching framework, as long as the matching model is trained with such detectors.

We further propose one method to fine-tune existing image matching models towards universal detector-agnostic ones with fixed descriptors. It’s achieved by incorporating additional correspondence supervision from various local detectors during the fine-tuning process. The fine-tuned model demonstrates improved generalization performance when applied to novel detectors. It show similar performance compared to carefully-tuned specialist matching models. Our de-coupled paradigm also enables powerful matching performance for binary features. Our zero-shot matcher can run successfully on the challenging IMC 2021 dataset with ORB keypoints. To summarize, the main contributions of the paper are:

•

We revisit the deployment of learning-based sparse image matching models for diverse local features and emphasize the critical importance of removing nearby keypoints.

•

The impact of detectors and descriptors within attention-based matching frameworks can be decoupled, revealing their respective roles in overall matching performance.

•

We fine-tune existing models to achieve generic, zero-shot, detector-agnostic matching, demonstrating consistent improvements across all evaluated features.

•

Our findings offer new insights into optimizing the training and deployment of specialized LightGlue models for novel feature types.

Related work

Sparse image matching methods
first detect local features using hand-designed or learned heuristics, represented as 2D locations
[

,
46
,

Deep learning methods have been employed to learn local detectors

Image correspondences are established by nearest neighbor (NN) or ratio test (RT) between the descriptors.
Nearest neighbor-based matching methods face the issue of false-positive and missed matches, as they rely solely on feature appearance without considering the 2D locations of the keypoints
[

15

. Outlier detection and filtering are often followed to remove suspicious matches

Deep image matchers
are primarily inspired by the pioneering work of SuperGlue
[
48
,
73
,
72
]
. The SuperGlue model aggregates both appearance and geometric information with position encoding. Information between pairs of images is shared through cross-attention layers
[
63
]
. This approach consistently boosts performance when trained on various features
[

,

,
35
,
45
,
8

. Subsequent works aim to enhance SuperGlue by incorporating cluster information
[

. Recent studies show that the SuperGlue model still demonstrates superior performance on various tasks

.

[
56
]
and discard Sinkhorn algorithm for match assignment. Their open-sourced glue-factory repo
[
24
]
enables easier training of LightGlue models for novel features.

Decoupling the feature detector and descriptor
is not novel. It has been explored for both hand-crafted features
[
25
,
54
]
and learned features
[
5
,
36
,
39
,
59
]
. While some works emphasize the advantages of joint learning
[
17
,
19
]
,

argue that decoupled learning is superior. However, the decoupling of the detector and descriptor is rarely addressed when using attention-based transformer models
[
53
]
, partially due to the poor generalization of SuperGlue models to other features
[

,
55
,
12

Generalization of deep image matching models
to new features has rarely been discussed since it was commonly believed that the SuperGlue models are feature-dependent. The SuperGlue model trained for SuperPoint will fail when fed in R2D2 descriptors
[
44
,
53
]
. This is expected since the domain gap between different descriptors can be large.
As an early attempt to build detector-agnostic image matching model,
53
propose detector-oblivious network for matching.
A detector-oblivious description network is fed with the keypoint locations and image to replace local descriptors.
Some preliminary results are shown, which successfully apply such model trained on SuperPoint keypoints to SIFT keypoints.
They also include a simple baseline, which naively extracts SuperPoint descriptor from R2D2 keypoint location and feeds it into SuperGlue model trained for SuperPoint features. However, such baseline brings significant performance drop on MegaDepth dataset. We revisit this baseline with careful inspection and find that several implementation details can significantly impact performance. By addressing these issues, we obtain a strong zero-shot image matching model for novel detectors without modifying the network architecture or re-training from scratch.

Always remove nearby keypoints

During training SuperGlue model, ground truth correspondences are generated by first computing the reprojection matrix between all detected keypoints using the ground truth homography or pose and depth. Correspondences are cells with a reprojection error that is a minimum along both rows and columns, and that is lower than given threshold: 3, 5, and 3 pixels for homographies, indoor, and outdoor matching respectively
[
48
]
. For SuperPoint or DISK which performs detection at single scale and performs non-maximum-suppression, SuperGlue works pretty well
[
33
]
.

For features that might generate nearby keypoints at adjacent locations as shown in
Fig.

1
, establishing ground truth matches using the criteria above can be problematic. On one hand, R2D2 and ORB extract keypoints at multi-scales, which may produce keypoints at very close locations on different scales. Currently the correspondences for features across different scales are not handled explicitly.

Figure 2
:
LightGlue matching results for SiLK with and without NMS are shown. Without NMS, SiLK show cluttered keypoints, many of which the LightGlue model fails to match.

On the other hand, recent local features such as Dedode
[
20
]
and SiLK
[
23
]
show strong matching results with MNN at the cost of many keypoints (10K-30K), but
the official implementations of Dedode and SiLK comes without non-maximum suppression (NMS)
[
21
]
. When integrating Dedode with Kornia
[
45
]
, the code related to NMS have been completely removed. Due to the in-accurate depth estimation on MegaDepth dataset, the correspondences for near-by keypoints can be wrong.

To verify the impact of NMS and multi-scale extraction, we train four LightGlue models: Dedode/SiLK feature with NMS enabled, R2D2/ORB with single-scale extraction.
The results for two-view pose estimation task on Megadepth-1500 dataset are shown in
Table

.

Our model for Dedode trained with NMS is consistently better than official model in Kornia, with 4.1%/3.3% absolute gain of AUC@5°. The same trend holds for SiLK features as well, with NMS during training and test, we obtain 9.9% absolute gain. For R2D2, we obtain similar accuracy when the training and inference configurations are aligned. Notable performance drops (4%
\sim
6.1%) occur when the inference configuration differs from the training setup for R2D2, indicating inherent bias of the matching models.

We visualize the matching results of the LightGlue model on SiLK features in
Fig.

2
, with and without NMS. Without NMS, the SiLK features contain many repeatable but closely spaced keypoints. However, the LightGlue model fails to match many of these potential correspondences. When NMS is applied, the same LightGlue model successfully recovers most of the matches.
Interestingly, this behavior contrasts with the original SiLK paper, which recommends disabling NMS when using nearest-neighbor matching
.

For ORB features, the default training of LightGlue model with multi-scale extraction fails to converge. Simply turning on single-scale extraction makes the training stabilized. For LightGlue+ORB features, the AUC@5°is 45.7, while nearest-neighbor matcher obtains only 10.1, suggesting LightGlue can bring significant boost for binary features since it can leverage the context to mitigate the deficiencies of less discriminative descriptors. Compared to non-binary features, LightGlue models still perform worse. In the following section, we identify the individual contributions of detector and descriptor for the performance gap.

In the following section, we present a method to fine-tune existing matching models towards stronger detector-agnostic models. It not only boosts performance for all off-the-shelf models, but also closes the gap between them.

Towards detector-agnostic matching model

Previous works
[
53
]
attribute the representation power of the descriptor as the bottleneck of generalization and replace the existing descriptor with a learned network. However, for transformer-based models, we argue that most existing feature descriptors are discriminative enough since the models can use cross attention and context for matching.
We attribute the performance gap with above model to the bias of local detectors during training rather than descriptors. The keypoints for training are all extracted using one specific detector.
To verify the idea that existing descriptors are not the issue,
we fine-tuned the LightGlue model with descriptors extracted by multiple detectors, while keeping the descriptor network weights frozen, as shown in
Fig.

5
.

Three LightGlue models
[
33
]
for SuperPoint, DISK, and ALIKED are tuned on the MegaDepth dataset
[
32
]
, which is also used for initial training. We extract keypoints for all three features and sample the same descriptors for all keypoints.
Each model is fine-tuned for 10 epochs. The initial learning rate is set at 5e-5 for the first 5 epochs. The learning rate is exponentially reduced to 6e-5 after 10 epochs.
The fine-tuning process for each model takes about 1.5 days.

The relative pose estimation accuracy on the MegaDepth-1500 dataset using SuperPoint, DISK, and ALIKED after fine tuning are shown in
Fig.

For example, matching SuperPoint keypoints with DISK descriptors obtains 58.7% under AUC@5°, compared to 67.0% with SuperPoint descriptors. After fine-tuning, the performance gap is largely closed, with the AUC@5°of 65.7%. The AUC is comparable for the same detectors no matter what descriptors or matchers are used.
The results indicate that the detectors, not the descriptors, are the key factor that determine the accuracy of transformer-based matching methods.

Relative pose estimation

For relative pose estimation task, the MegaDepth-1500 dataset is chosen following previous work

Evaluation setup
: The long side of the image is resized to 1600 and at most 2,048 keypoints are extracted for all detectors. The essential matrix is computed with LO-RANSAC

For R2D2, we extract only with single scale. We use the official Dedode (v1) weights since it’s the one that Kornia trained the LightGlue model for.
The pose error is computed as the maximum angular error in rotation and translation. The AUC at 5°, 10°, and 20°are reported.

6
Conclusion

We revisit the application of transformer-based image matching models on different local features. A simple yet effective approach is introduced to remove nearby keypoints, leading to a significant impact on performance. To better understand the roles of detectors and descriptors, we conduct a decoupled analysis within the attention-based image matching framework. Furthermore, we propose a novel fine-tuning method to adapt existing specialized models into detector-agnostic models. Experimental results on public benchmarks across various tasks demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. We believe our work provides valuable insights for developing improved sparse image matching models and local features.

7.2
Limitations

The performance of our model is limited by capacity of descriptor and detector, as we don’t fine-tune the weights of local features.
For now, we need datasets with multi-view posed images such as MegaDepth and MegaScenes
[
61
]
for fine-tuning, which are challenging to collect.
