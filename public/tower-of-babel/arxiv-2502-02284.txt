Connections on a principal Lie groupoid bundle and representations upto homotopy

Abstract
A Lie groupoid principal [ 𝕏 : X 1 ⇉ X 0 ] [\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}] bundle is a surjective submersion π : P → M \pi\colon P\to M with an action of 𝕏 \mathbb{X} on P P with certain additional conditions. This paper offers a suitable definition for the notion of a connection on such bundles. Although every Lie groupoid 𝕏 \mathbb{X} has its associated Lie algebroid A := 1 ∗ ​ ker ⁡ d ​ s → X 0 A:=1^{*}\ker ds\to X_{0} , it does not admit a natural action on its Lie algebroid. There is no natural action of 𝕏 \mathbb{X} on T ​ P TP either. Choosing a connection ℍ ⊂ T ​ X 1 \mathbb{H}\subset TX_{1} on the Lie groupoid 𝕏 , \mathbb{X}, and considering its induced action up to homotopy of 𝕏 \mathbb{X} on graded vector bundle T ​ X 0 ⊕ A , TX_{0}\oplus A, we prove the existence of a short exact sequence of diffeological groupoids over the discrete category M M (with appropriate graded vector space structures on the fibres) for the [ 𝕏 : X 1 ⇉ X 0 ] [\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}] bundle π : P → M . \pi\colon P\to M. We introduce a notion of connection on [ 𝕏 : X 1 ⇉ X 0 ] [\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}] bundle π : P → M , \pi\colon P\to M, and show that such a connection ω \omega splits the sequence. Finally, we show that a connection pair ( ω , ℍ ) (\omega,\mathbb{H}) on [ 𝕏 : X 1 ⇉ X 0 ] [\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}] bundle π : P → M \pi\colon P\to M is isomorphic to any other connection pair.

Principal Lie groupoid bundle over a manifold

We start by recalling the basic definitions of Lie groupoid and principal Lie groupoid bundles.
Let
\mathfrak{M}an
be the category of smooth, Hausdorff, second countable manifolds.

Definition 1

(Lie groupoid).

A
Lie groupoid
is a groupoid internal to the category of smooth manifolds
\mathfrak{M}an
, along with the source and target maps being submersions.

Explicitly, a Lie groupoid is a groupoid with both the object and morphism sets to be smooth manifolds along with smooth structure maps. Moreover, the source (hence, the target) map is required to be a submersion. We denote a Lie groupoid by
[\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}]
or simply by
\mathbb{X}
. The source and target maps will typically be denoted by
s
and
t.
The composition map will be denoted as
m\colon X_{1}\times_{s,t}X_{1}\to X_{1}
when we need to specify it explicitly; otherwise we write
m(g,h)
as
gh.
Associated to a Lie groupoid
[\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}]
we have its
tangent grouopid

[T\mathbb{X}:TX_{1}\rightrightarrows TX_{0}]
with composition maps are differentials of those of
[\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}].
In particular
dm\bigl((g,\beta),(h,\alpha)\bigr)
will be denoted as
(gh,\beta\alpha):

dm\bigl((g,\beta),(h,\alpha)\bigr):=(gh,\beta\alpha).

Remark 1
.

For our purpose, we consider our smooth manifolds in

{\mathfrak{M}an}
to be Hausdorff and second countable. However, the choice is not completely unambiguous. For example, in foliation theory, as it turns out, the holonomy groupoids are not Hausdorff; thus, Hausdorff condition is dropped. Even the object manifold is occasionally assumed to be Hausdorff, whereas the morphism manifold is not

Remark 2
.

Observe that the category of smooth manifolds is not closed under fibre product or pull-back. However, the submersion condition on the source and target maps of a Lie groupoid
[\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}]
ensures that the set
X_{1}\times_{X_{0}}X_{1}
is indeed a smooth manifold.

It is often interesting to investigate the Lie groupoids enjoying some additional properties. For instance, if the source, target maps of a Lie groupoid
[\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}]
are étale, then the Lie Groupoid
[X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}]
is called
étale

(s,t)\colon X_{1}\rightarrow X_{0}\times X_{0}

In the following passage, we provide some common examples of Lie groupoids.

Example 1

(A manifold as Lie groupoid).

A smooth manifold
M
defines a Lie groupoid
[M\rightrightarrows M]
with both source and target maps are identity maps. This Lie groupoid is called the
discrete groupoid
associated with the manifold
M

Example 2

(Pair groupoid).

For every manifold
M

M
. So, source and target maps are the second and first projections, respectively, and the composition map is given as:

(m^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime})\circ(m^{\prime},m)=(m^{\prime\prime},m).

Example 3

Let
\mathcal{U}=\bigl\{U_{i}\bigr\}_{i\in I}
be an open cover of a manifold
M
. Let
\bigsqcup\limits_{i\in I}U_{i}
be the disjoint union of the cover, and
\bigsqcup\limits_{i,j\in I}U_{i}\cap U_{j}
the disjoint union of the double intersections of the same. Evidently we have a Lie groupoid

with the respective source-target maps

,

with the composition
(j,k,x)\circ(i,j,x)=(i,k,x).
This Lie Groupoid

Example 4

(Lie group as a Lie groupoid).

A Lie group

[G\rightrightarrows*]
with the object manifold being the singleton
0-

Example 5

(Action groupoid of a Lie group).

Given a smooth right action of a Lie group

[P\times G\rightrightarrows P].
The structure maps are respectively given by

Example 6

(Vector bundle as a Lie groupoid).

Let

[E\rightrightarrows M]
with source and target maps

4
Diffeological spaces

Although smooth manifolds and smooth maps have a rich history and classical status, they are often unsuitable in certain circumstances. For instance, the fibre product of two smooth manifolds fails to be a smooth manifold, and likewise, for the orbit space of the action of a Lie group on a smooth manifold. However, it is sometimes necessary to have some notion of “smoothness” on such spaces. The “Diffelogical spaces provide the required framework”. One can consult
[
23
]
for a more detailed exposition. This paper will only focus on the absolute necessity and leave out the technical details.

Definition 11
.

A
diffeological space

(X,D_{X})
is a set
X
with a collection of maps
\{U\rightarrow X\}:=D_{X}
, where each
U
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n
satisfying the following three axioms:

1.

(covering) Every constant map is in
D_{X}
.

2.

(smooth compatability) If
p:U\rightarrow X
is in
D_{X}
and
f:V\rightarrow U
is a smooth map, where
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open, then
p\circ f\colon V\rightarrow X
is also in
D_{X}
.

3.

(local) If
U=\bigcup\limits_{i\in I}
is an open cover for
U
and
p:U\rightarrow X
is s map such that
p\restriction_{U}:U_{i}\rightarrow X
is in
D_{X}
, then
p
lies in
D_{X}
.

The elements of
D_{X}
are called
plots
.
A map
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
is called
a smooth map between diffeological spaces

(X,D_{X})\rightarrow(Y,D_{Y})
if
for any plot
p:U\rightarrow X
, the composite map
f\circ p:U\rightarrow Y
is in
D_{Y}
. We denote the category of diffeological spaces and smooth maps by
{\mathfrak{Diffeo}}
.

Example 30
.

Any
n
-dimensional smooth manifold
M
is a diffeological space with a plot given by a usual smooth map
U\to M,
for an open subset
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.
Also, a smooth map between two such diffeological manifolds is the same as a smooth map between smooth manifolds. Thus, the category of smooth manifolds embeds into the category
{\mathfrak{Diffeo}}
in a natural way:
\mathfrak{Man}\subset\mathfrak{Diffeo}.

Example 31

(Pullback diffeology).

Suppose
f:Y\rightarrow X
is a set map and
D_{X}
a diffeology on
X
. Then, one can pullback the diffeology
D_{X}
to give a diffeological structure for
X
. A map
p:U\rightarrow Y
is a plot if and only if the composite map
f\circ p:U\rightarrow X
lies in
D_{X}
. This gives a diffeological structure on
Y
, called
p
ullback diffeology and denoted as
f^{*}D_{X}
. Note that
f\colon(Y,f^{*}D_{X})\rightarrow(X,D_{X})
is a smooth map.

Example 32

(Subspace diffeology).

A subset
Y\subset X
of a diffeological space
(X,D_{X})
inherits a natural diffeological structure by the pullback diffeology along the inclusion map
i:Y\rightarrow X;
that is, the plots of subspace diffeology are
f:U\rightarrow X\in D_{X}
such that
f(U)\subseteq Y
.

Example 33

(Product diffeology).

Let
(X,D_{X})
and
(Y,D_{Y})
be two diffeological spaces. Then, the product set
X\times Y
has a natural diffeology on it given by the product of diffeologies
D_{X}\times D_{Y}=\{(f,g)\mid f\in D_{X}\,\text{and}\,g\in D_{Y}\}
.

Example 34

(Fibre product diffeology).

One of the biggest advantages of working with the category of diffeological spaces and smooth maps is that
{\mathfrak{Diffeo}}
admits fibre products. Suppose
f:(X,D_{X})\rightarrow(Z,D_{Z})
and
g:(Y,D_{Y})\rightarrow(Z,D_{Z})
are smooth maps between diffeological spaces, then the fibre product set
X\times_{Z}Y
has a natural diffeology inherited as a subspace diffeology of the product diffeology. Explicitly,
X\times_{Z}Y
is a diffeological space with the diffeology
D_{X}\times_{D_{Z}}D_{Y}=\{(f_{1},g_{1})\mid f\circ f_{1}=g\circ g_{1},f_{1}\in D_{X},g_{1}\in D_{Y}\}.

Example 35

(Pushforward diffelogy).

Let
(X,D_{X})
be a diffeological space and
f:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
be a set map. There is a natural way of obtaining a diffeological structure on
X^{\prime}
in the following manner. A map
p:U\rightarrow X^{\prime}
is a plot if and only if, for every
u\in U
, there exists an open neighbourhood
V
of
u
and a plot
q:V\rightarrow X
in
D_{X}
such that
p\restriction_{V}=f\circ q
. Typically, the pushforward diffeology along a map
f:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
is denoted by
f_{*}(D_{X})
.

Example 36

(Quotient diffeology).

Let
(X,D_{X})
be a diffeological space and
\sim
be an equivalence relation on
X
. Then pushforward along the canonical quotient map
q_{X}\colon X\rightarrow\dfrac{X}{\sim}
induces a diffeology on
\dfrac{X}{\sim},
called the
quotient diffeology
. Hence, a map
f:U\rightarrow\dfrac{X}{\sim}
is a plot if and only if for every
u\in U
, there exists an open neighbourhood
V
of
u
and a plot
g:V\rightarrow X
such that
f\restriction_{V}=q_{X}\circ g.

A smooth, surjective map
f:(X,D_{X})\rightarrow(Y,D_{Y})
is called a
subduction
if
the diffeology
D_{Y}
coincides with the pushforward diffeology
f_{*}(D_{X})
.
In particular, the canonical quotient map
q_{X}:X\rightarrow\dfrac{X}{\sim}
in
example

36
is a subduction.

Definition 12

(Definition 8.3 in
[
23
]
).

A
diffeological groupoid
is a groupoid
X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}
, internal to the category of diffeological spaces.

Explicitly, the sets
X_{1},X_{0}
are diffeological spaces along with the structure maps
s,t,m,I,u
all smooth in a diffeological groupoid
[\mathbb{X}\colon X_{1}\rightrightarrows X_{0}]
. Recently, the abelianization of Lie and diffeological groupoids has been investigated in
[
42
]
.

Remark 13
.

Observe that a diffeological groupoid’s source and target maps automatically become subductions.

Generalization of the examples of Lie groupoids given between
example

1
–
example

5

to diffeological groupoids is straightforward.

Definition 13

(Definition 4.5 in
[
15
]
).

Let
(X,D_{X})
be a diffeological space. A diffeological vector space over
(X,D_{X})
is a diffeological space
(V,D_{V})
along with a smooth map
\pi:(V,D_{V})\rightarrow(X,D_{X})
satisfying following conditions:

–

\pi^{-1}(x)
is a vector space for each
x\in X
.

–

The vector space addition map
+:(V\times_{X}V,D_{V\times_{X}V})\rightarrow(V,D_{V})
is a smooth map.

–

scalar multiplication map
*:(R\times V)\rightarrow V
is a smooth map.

–

zero section of
\pi:V\rightarrow X
is a smooth map.

The notion of diffeological vector space over a diffeological space has been studied in
[
15
]
, and the same object with the name
diffeological vector pseudo bundle
has appeared in
[
33
]
. Any ordinary smooth vector bundle is an example of a diffeological vector space, and some other nontrivial examples can be found in
[
33
]
. Also, in
section

5
, we will see that associated with a Lie groupoid principal bundle is a sequence of diffeological groupoids with vector space structure.
