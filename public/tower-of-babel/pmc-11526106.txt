Influence of self-reported physical exercise on musculoskeletal pain among Chinese medical students
Authors: Lianshuai Zheng, Xu Chen, Xueyuan Rong, Yao Meng, Lina Jiao, Dongmei Yan, Jizheng Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
From January to April 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic affected eastern China, leading medical students to adopt home protection measures that significantly altered their physical activity and lifestyle habits. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of musculoskeletal pain (MSP) among medical students during home protection and to analyze the influence of demographic factors, physical exercise habits during normal school life, home protection data on MSP. This research provides a foundation for developing interventions to reduce MSP among medical students. A cross-sectional survey was conducted at Nanjing Medical University in Jiangsu Province from January to April 2020, utilizing the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ) to measure MSP. Multivariate logistic regression identified factors influencing MSP. A total of 859 students participated, with a 40.2% prevalence of MSP during home protection. Multivariate analysis showed that female sex (OR: 1.648; 95% CI: 1.157–2.347), irregular physical exercise during school (OR: 1.648; 95% CI: 1.157–2.347), and less than or equal to six hours of sleep per day during home protection (OR: 1.474; 95% CI: 1.020–2.131) were significant risk factors. The high prevalence of MSP underscores the need for interventions that focus on gender-specific risks, promote regular physical exercise, and ensure adequate sleep.

Introduction

In December 2019, the COVID-19 pandemic emerged in China1. The disease was classified as a Category B infectious disease under the Law of the People’s Republic of China on the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases but was managed as a Category A disease at the time2. Disrupting transmission routes became critical, and home protection measures were widely implemented, with most medical students in eastern China observing strict home protection between January and April 20203,4.

Musculoskeletal pain (MSP) is a common condition among medical students and a significant public health concern. It negatively impacts quality of life and contributes to disability, work incapacity, and increased healthcare costs5. To date, a number of surveys on the prevalence of MSP in whole-body regions (overall prevalence within seven days prior to the survey) among medical students had been conducted globally. Chinese medical students showed 46.9% (in 2005)6, Malaysian medical students 45.7% (in 2010)7, Pakistani medical students 38.9% (in 2016)8, Brazilian medical students 74.9% (in 2017)9, Bahraini medical students 77.6% (in 2017)10, Saudi medical students 48.4% (in 2018)11, Indian medical students 50% (in 2019)12, Jordanian medical students 58.9% (in 2021)13, Syrian medical students 54% (in 2022)14, Bangladeshi medical students 52.1% (in 2022)15, and Chinese medical students 41.4% (in 2022)16. A multicenter study found that Swiss health profession students in their final year had a higher prevalence of MSP, particularly in the lower back and neck, than the general population17. Compared to students from other disciplines, medical students are exposed to more MSP risk factors due to the extended study hours required to meet professional demands18. The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated this issue19. During the pandemic, in-person classes were replaced by distance learning. Several studies have reported that distance learning significantly increased MSP in the neck and shoulders, as factors such as poor posture, suboptimal ergonomics, prolonged electronic device use, and insufficient breaks contributed to pain in these regions20–24. However, few studies have explored the overall prevalence of MSP across all body regions, limiting the development of comprehensive preventive strategies25,26. Therefore, whether the implementation of home protection policies had led to an increase in the overall prevalence of MSP required further study.

Previous research has linked MSP in medical students to obesity, physical inactivity, poor posture, and prolonged use of electronic devices8,9,11,27,28. The role of physical activity in preventing MSP remains contentious11,18, with discrepancies in findings potentially arising from a focus on the intensity and frequency of physical activity rather than its type or regularity. Studies on physical activity during leisure time, such as walking or gardening, have shown it to be protective against MSP among dental workers29, but few have examined the effect of regular leisure-time physical exercise habits (defined as planned, structured, and sustained physical activity with a certain intensity and frequency) on MSP in medical students. During home protection, the lifestyle habits of medical students, including physical exercise, electronic device use, posture, and sleep duration, changed markedly30. However, there was limited research on the overall prevalence and risk factors for MSP among medical students during home protection. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the overall prevalence of MSP among medical students during home protection and to analyze the influence of demographic factors, physical exercise habits during normal school life and home protection data on MSP. The findings will contribute to the development of interventions to reduce MSP in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among medical students at Nanjing Medical University in Jiangsu Province, Eastern China, from January to April 2020. Data were collected through a self-designed structured questionnaire based on literature review and expert consultation. A pre-survey was conducted among 100 participants before entering the formal survey (pre-survey data were not included in the final analysis), and the questionnaire was modified to improve its intelligibility and reduce defects. The survey was conducted online. The questionnaire was managed through the online platform (www.wjx.cn) and filled out by scanning the WeChat QR code. Participants were provided with a brief explanation of the study’s purpose in the first part of the online questionnaire, and informed consent was obtained electronically. Participants were required to provide consent before continuing the questionnaire; those who declined were withdrawn from the survey. To prevent duplicate responses, each IP address was limited to one submission. The questionnaire was distributed by trained school counselors who offered detailed explanations regarding the study’s purpose, procedures, and completion instructions. All eligible students were encouraged to participate. The survey was voluntary, anonymous, and participants could withdraw at any time.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) absence of serious physical illness; (2) no history of chronic musculoskeletal pain disorders; and (3) consent to participate. Exclusion criteria were: (1) inability to understand the study’s purpose or process; (2) history of mental disorders; (3) already started clinical internship; and (4) lack of consent. The minimum sample size was calculated using the formula n = z2*p*(1-p))/d2, where z = 1.96 (95% confidence interval (CI)), d = 5% (margin of error) and p is the prevalence of MSP. Due to the lack of previous relevant studies at the study site and to obtain the maximum sample size, we considered p = 50%.31 This yielded a minimum sample size of 385.Considering the efficiency of the questionnaire and in order to reduce sampling error, the sample size was expanded as much as possible. A total of 900 questionnaires were collected, of which 41 were excluded due to logical errors. In the final analysis, 859 valid responses were included, yielding an effective response rate of 95.4%.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire comprised four sections: demographic factors, physical exercise habits during normal school life, home protection data, and MSP assessment. Demographic factors included sex, age (in years), academic year, major, residence, and body mass index (BMI). BMI was calculated from self-reported weight (kg)/height squared (m²), and categorized as follows: <18.5 (underweight), 18.5–23.9 (normal), and ≥ 24 (overweight or obese)32,33.

The physical exercise habits during normal school life section assessed exercise patterns, intensity, and frequency. Physical exercise patterns were assessed by asking, “During your leisure time in school, do you actively engage in regular physical exercise?” (defined as planned, structured, and sustained physical activity with a certain intensity and frequency). Physical exercise intensity was assessed by asking, “What is your usual intensity of physical exercise during leisure time at school?” with response options including “light intensity” (e.g., walking), “moderate intensity” (e.g., jogging, rope skipping), and “vigorous intensity” (e.g., basketball, soccer). The intensity of physical exercise was classified according to the Rating of Perceived Exertion Scale (Borg’s RPE (6–20) Scale)34. Physical exercise frequency was assessed by asking, “How often do you engage in physical exercise of 30 minutes or more per week during leisure time at school?” with response options: “≤1 time per week,” “2–4 times per week,” and “≥5 times per week.”

The home protection section included questions on the duration of home protection (in days), reductions in physical exercise intensity and frequency, daily sitting time (hours), use of a backrest while sitting, daily electronic device use (hours), and daily sleep duration. Reductions in exercise intensity and frequency were assessed through two questions: “Has your exercise intensity decreased during home protection?” and “Has your exercise frequency decreased during home protection?“. The questionnaire clarified that physical exercise refers to proactive and planned physical activity (e.g., biking, walking, jumping rope, swimming) as opposed to necessary activities (e.g., working, studying, reading, dressing).

MSP was assessed using the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ)35, validated for assessing pain in nine regions: neck, shoulders, upper back, elbows, hands/wrists, lower back, hips/buttocks, knees, and ankles/feet. As the study focused on MSP during home protection (no more than three months), data on MSP occurring in the seven days prior to the survey were collected to ensure comparability with other studies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 19.0. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables, revealing that all continuous variables were non-normally distributed. As a result, continuous variables were reported as the median (M) and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test was used to evaluate differences between categorical variables, while the Mann-Whitney U test was employed for comparisons of continuous variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors and control for confounding variables. A significance level of α = 0.05 was set for all statistical tests.

Ethics declarations and consent to participate

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Lianyungang Maternal and Child Health Hospital (LYG-MER2021035). Electronic written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The median duration of home protection was 35 days (IQR: 10 days). Prevalence of MSP

The overall prevalence of MSP in this study was 40.2% (n = 345; 95% CI: 36.9-43.4%). The most commonly affected regions were the neck (32.9%, n = 283), shoulders (15.6%, n = 134), and lower back (11.2%, n = 96), followed by the hips/buttocks (7.5%, n = 64), upper back (7.0%, n = 60), knees (3.8%, n = 33), hands/wrists (3%, n = 26), ankles/feet (2.3%, n = 20), and elbows (1.9%, n = 16) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this study, the overall prevalence of MSP (across all body regions) within seven days prior to the survey among medical students during home protection was 40.2% (n = 345; 95% CI: 36.9-43.4%), similar to findings from studies conducted in 2005 and 2022, which reported overall prevalence rates of 46.9% and 41.4% (within seven days prior to the survey), respectively6,16, during normal school life among Chinese medical students. Studies conducted among physiotherapy students in Sicily and medical students at Al-Balqa Applied University, who had implemented home protection policies for 6–12 and 3 months during the COVID-19 pandemic, reported MSP prevalence rates of 73.1% and 75.9%, respectively25,26. Similarly, a study of medical students at a public university in southern Brazil and another in the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at Cukurova University (Adana, Turkey) found MSP prevalence rates of 74.9% and 75.8% during normal school life prior to the pandemic9,36. These findings indicate that there was no significant change in the overall prevalence of MSP within seven days prior to the survey during home protection. However, medical curricula and student lifestyles vary significantly between countries, making it difficult to directly compare MSP prevalence. Continuous monitoring of MSP prevalence and associated risk factors within the same country, or even at the same university, is crucial for developing effective prevention policies.

Sex was identified as a significant risk factor for MSP in this study (OR: 1.391, 95% CI: 1.006–1.922), with women having a 39.1% higher risk of MSP than men, consistent with the results of most studies8,9,11,37. Notably, some studies have found that men are at higher risk for MSP in certain regions of the body, such as the lower back. For example, a study of 1,163 medical students from five medical schools in Saudi Arabia found that men had a higher risk of lower back pain than women38. These differences highlight that overall prevalence of MSP and its risk factors was quite different with that of one specific body region.

This study found that regular physical exercise during normal school life was more effective in preventing MSP than the intensity or frequency of exercise. Individuals who engaged in irregular physical exercise had a significantly higher risk of MSP (OR: 1.648; 95% CI: 1.157–2.347) compared to those who exercised regularly. A study at Mekelle University in Ethiopia, involving 422 participants, found that regular physical exercise reduced the risk of neck and shoulder pain18. In this study, 73.9% of participants reported a reduction in physical exercise intensity, and 71.5% reported a reduction in physical exercise frequency compared to what they did in their normal school life prior to home protection. Changes in exercise intensity or frequency during the home protection period (25–45 days) were not identified as risk factors for MSP, a finding supported by similar studies11,39.

Most prior research on the correlation between physical exercise and MSP has focused on the intensity, frequency, or total duration of exercise, often yielding inconsistent results11,39–42. A careful review of these studies reveals that researchers have largely overlooked the importance of evenly distributing exercise across the week. However, studies examining MSP risk factors among workers in various occupations have shown that regular, planned physical exercise during leisure time effectively prevents MSP43–45. This study was the first to confirm that this holds for Chinese medical students, consistent with findings from a study of 145 medical students in South Africa46. Consequently, we recommend that medical students engage in regular physical exercise during their leisure time, and medical schools should develop policies to support this.

Sedentary behavior is very common in normal school life and has been identified in many studies as a risk factor for MSP among college students47,48. Medical students were required to sit for long periods of time for lectures, reading, and writing due to the constraints of the school administration system, mode of delivery (mainly classroom teaching), and curriculum, resulting in certain ergonomic hazards (prolonged holding of one posture, poor sitting posture, and ill-fitting chairs) that adversely influence the musculoskeletal system18,46. In addition, extensive use of electronic devices for teaching and leisure purposes further exacerbates the problem23.

Leisure time physical activity (physical exercise) was beneficial to musculoskeletal health and mental health, contributes to the prevention of MSP49–51, and appears to reduce the harm associated with sedentary behavior. A recent Mendelian randomization study revealed that regular physical exercise may reduce the occurrence of MSP associated with sedentary behavior (passive) by promoting mental health52, as certain mental health conditions (e.g., depression) were known to contribute to the occurrence of MSP53. However, direct evidence of a causal relationship between sedentary behavior, regular physical exercise, and MSP had not been found.

Less than or equal to six hours of sleep per day during home protection was also identified as a significant risk factor for MSP (OR: 1.474; 95% CI: 1.020–2.131), with a 47.4% higher risk compared to individuals who received 6–8 h of sleep per day. Although the relationship between sleep quality and MSP is well established, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear54. Some studies suggest that sleep deprivation triggers the release of inflammatory factors, disrupting the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and reducing dopamine secretion, which may lead to MSP55. Other studies propose that sleep deprivation increases pain sensitivity, reduces the desire to exercise, and exacerbates psychological issues such as anxiety, depression, and fear of pain, which can reinforce MSP56,57.

While this study offers valuable insights, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any conclusions about causality, highlighting the need for longitudinal studies. Second, physical exercise was measured by a self-designed questionnaire and collected by self-report, which may be subject to recall bias. In the future, validated questionnaires or objective measurement methods are needed to confirm the results of this study. Third, this study did not control for variables such as the duration of different intensities of physical exercise, the exact number of times of physical exercise performed per week, and changes in posture, which may have an impact on MSP. Future studies should address these limitations. Finally, this study was conducted in a single medical university in Jiangsu Province, Eastern China, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Caution should be exercised in generalizing the findings to different regions or countries. Future research should expand the geographical scope and sample size to enhance the applicability of the results. Despite these limitations, this study contributes to the understanding of MSP risk factors for medical students and offers potential solutions for its prevention.

Conclusions

The overall prevalence of MSP among medical students during home protection is high, with the neck and shoulders being the most commonly affected areas. Urgent intervention measures are needed to reduce MSP, particularly among female students. Regular physical exercise during school life and ensuring 6–8 h of sleep per day appear to be key factors in reducing the risk of MSP.
