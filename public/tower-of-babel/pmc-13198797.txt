An ex vivo appositional growth model to study periosteal activity and associated BMP signaling
Authors: Gavin Chen, Jillian Scott, Ana Garcia Castineiras, Laura Gamer, Vicki Rosen, Emily R Moore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe periosteum is an osteogenic tissue containing periosteal stem and progenitor cells (PSPCs) that form and repair bones. Much is known about how the periosteum regulates fracture repair, but its key role in bone accrual during postnatal growth and adult load-induced formation remains a significant knowledge gap. The periosteal activity that drives bone formation and repair can be studied using existing mouse models, but traditional in vivo techniques are not feasible for investigating appositional growth, or widening of the bones, in young mouse pups. We therefore developed an ex vivo appositional growth model that combines existing in vitro fluid flow approaches and a custom support to secure mouse femur explants in a culture system. We observed that femurs widen after daily exposure to fluid flow, and this coincides with expansion of the periosteum and activation of BMP signaling, which is essential for periosteal osteogenesis. We further validated our system by recapitulating an in vivo thin-bone phenotype in mice that exhibit a lack of periosteal activity due to disrupted BMP signaling. Using our device, we were able to partially recover the thin-bone phenotype by supplementing culture media with recombinant BMP2. This ex vivo appositional growth model is an important step in obtaining a more holistic understanding of how the periosteum and its resident PSPCs drive bone formation and repair. Furthermore, we anticipate this apparatus can be used to interrogate the periosteum’s osteogenic capabilities and motivate future bone anabolism treatments for skeletal diseases and the prevention of fractures or enhancement of repair.Keywords: Periosteum, bone apposition, load-induced bone formation, BMP signaling, BMP2, periosteal stem and progenitor cells, osteogenesis

Introduction

The periosteum, a thin tissue covering the outer surface of bones, contains unique niche factors and houses periosteal stem and progenitor cells (PSPCs) that regulate its structure and activity.1 The periosteum’s essential role in bone fracture repair is well-established.1–3 Equally critical is its role in accrual of bone to prevent fracture incidence, which occurs initially during postnatal growth and can be supplemented in adulthood through load-induced bone formation. During appositional growth, bones widen as they lengthen to establish a force-balance relationship that enables the skeleton to withstand daily loading, and bone mass acquired during growth has been identified as an indicator of fracture risk.4,5 The concept of bone apposition was first conceived when a surgeon observed that rotating feed with and without red plant dye resulted in alternating red and unstained layers reminiscent of tree rings in the cross section of pig bones.6 Juvenile appositional growth is recapitulated in load-induced bone formation in adults, where the mature skeleton experiences heightened loads that require new bone to re-equilibrate the force-balance. The periosteum is highly active during growth and load-induced bone formation. Activity is demonstrated by periosteal expansion, where width of the periosteum increases as resident PSPCs proliferate and differentiate to deposit new bone matrix at the periosteum-bone interface via intramembranous ossification.7,8 When sufficient apposition has been reached to satisfy force-balance requirements, the periosteum becomes thinner and quiescent until re-activated by external stimulation, such as heightened loading. Removal of the periosteum completely abolishes bone apposition and repair, and disruption to PSPCs drastically impedes these biological processes.1,9–13

Despite the importance of periosteal activity in bone growth and homeostasis, our understanding of this highly osteogenic tissue is largely limited to the context of fracture repair. During repair, the periosteum expands again and resident PSPCs give rise to cells that heal the injury via endochondral ossification.13,14 These same PSPCs have been shown to participate in appositional growth, load-induced formation, and repair, suggesting niche factors unique to each biological process drive cell fate decisions.9,15 However, advances in characterizing PSPCs and their contribution to repair have not provided insight into how the periosteum regulates intramembranous ossification in appositional growth and load-induced formation.

One uniting factor central to PSPC activation, proliferation, and differentiation is BMP2-mediated BMP signaling. Our group recently reported that removal of BMP2 from skeletal lineage cells uniquely disrupts bone apposition and repair.3,16 Specifically, knockout (KO) mice develop normally but exhibit thinner limbs by 2 weeks of age. The thin bones spontaneously fracture because the optimal force-balance ratio is disrupted, and these mice are subsequently unable to initiate repair due to lack of periosteal BMP2. Load-induced bone formation has not been characterized in this model because the bones are too fragile for traditional ulnar/tibial loading experiments. A better understanding of how the periosteum directs bone apposition requires new experimental tools to investigate periosteal activity, PSPC fate decisions, and cell signaling interactions.

Here, we developed an ex vivo appositional growth model to study periosteal activity and BMP signaling. Briefly, mouse femurs were secured in custom supports in explant culture media and exposed to daily fluid flow using a platform rocker. Femur explants exposed to fluid flow demonstrated periosteal expansion, an increase in width, and activated BMP signaling, which are characteristics indicative of bone apposition. More importantly, we were able to recapitulate the thin-bone phenotype observed in our BMP2 KO mice, and partially restore bone apposition in KO femurs by supplementing culture media with recombinant BMP2. This model enables us to better understand the osteogenic capabilities of the periosteum and test potential therapeutics for skeletal diseases related to deficient bone accrual.

Materials and methods

Animals

Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl mice were generated in the Rosen Lab and model characteristics and genotyping are previously described.3,16 Experiments were performed in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Harvard Medical Area IACUC. Mice were housed and cared for in accordance with IACUC standards in an AAALAC-accredited facility. Mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation and cervical dislocation as secondary confirmation in accordance with IACUC standards.

Femur explant isolation and rocking apparatus setup

Dissected femurs were cultured in explant media, which was modified from a prior publication: DMEM (Gibco, 11885-084) supplemented with 40 U/mL PenStrep (Gibco, 15140-122) + 50 mg/mL L-Ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, A4544) + 50 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, 194678) + 100 mM β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, G9422) + 20% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, A7906).17 Reagents were combined and filter sterilized (Thermo-Fisher, 126-0020) prior to use in culture, and the media was changed every other day. Femurs were carefully dissected from 1-week-old mice with intact epiphyses. In cold PBS under a dissecting microscope, the majority of muscle and connective tissue were removed using fine forceps and a scalpel in a cutting motion parallel to the bone surface. The right femur was assigned to the loaded, or “rock” group, and the left femur served as a static contralateral control. This comparison was necessary to capture rocking-induced changes in apposition since starting length and width varied between individual pups. Both femurs were placed into femur supports (Figure 1A, Figure S1, Progressive Engineering Technologies, LLC) in 8-well rectangular plates so the femoral head was pointing upward. The supports are hand-machined from 316 stainless steel, a material selected for its anti-corrosive and anti-bacterial properties, its ability to withstand frequent autoclaving and lengthy exposure to incubator conditions, and its weight so the supports do not move with rocking. Femurs placed in the supports are fully submerged in culture media and elevated above the plate surface so fluid completely surrounds and can pass uninterrupted over the midshaft (Figure 1A). Explant culture media was added to the dish and femurs were incubated for 24 h to acclimate before exposure to rocking. The dish was placed onto a Rocker II Platform Rocker (Boekel Scientific, 260350) and exposed to 1 h of rocking per day (Figure 1B). Static explants were placed in the same incubator as rocker explants but were not exposed to rocking. Explants were collected after 3 or 5 d of daily rocking for histological analysis or mRNA extraction.

Micro-CT and measurements of explant structural properties

For initial measurements to confirm appositional growth, femurs were imaged daily and length and width were measured manually using epiphyseal landmarks (Figure 1C). Explants were scanned using a high-resolution desktop micro-tomographic imaging system (μCT35, Scanco Medical AG). Scans were acquired using a 7 μm isotropic voxel size, 55 kVp peak X-ray tube intensity, and 8 W X-ray tube current. Image acquisition and analysis protocols were conducted in accordance with guidelines for the assessment of bone microstructure in rodents.18 Length was determined by generating 3D reconstructions of the scans and measuring from the femoral head to the base of the distal epiphysis using Scanco Evaluation Program. The midshaft midpoint was identified as half of the total length and width was measured at the midpoint. Structural measurements were conducted on the segment spanning 1 mm distal to 1 mm proximal of the midshaft midpoint. Scanco Evaluation Program scripts were used to measure bone volume (BV) over total volume (TV) fraction (BV/TV, %), cortical thickness (mm), and marrow volume (mm3).

Histology and staining

Dissected femurs were fixed in 10% formalin overnight, decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA (VWR, 75800-470) for 1 week, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 10 μm. Slides were rehydrated and stained using an H&E kit (Abcam, ab245880), Toluidine Blue O solution (Sigma-Aldrich, 198161), or Weigert’s Iron Hematoxylin A (VWR, 26044-05), Weigert’s Iron Hematoxylin B (VWR, 26044-15), and Van Gieson’s Solution (VWR, 26046-05).19 For immunohistochemistry, the following primary antibodies were used: BMP2 (Invitrogen, PA5-85956, 1:500) and pSMAD1/5/8 (MilliporeSigma, AB3848-I, 1:250). Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, and antigen retrieval was performed with Target Retrieval Solution (Dako, S1699) at 95°C for 20 min or 65°C for 1 h. The sections were incubated with Dual Endogenous Enzyme Block (Dako, S200380) for 30 min at room temperature to suppress endogenous alkaline phosphatase and peroxidase enzymes. Blocking was performed in 0.5% TNB buffer (Akoya Biosciences, FP1020) for 1 h. The sections were then incubated in primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C and incubated in goat anti-rabbit biotin secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, B2770) at a dilution of 1:2000 in blocking solution. Amplification was performed per manufacturer instructions using the TSA Biotin System Kit (Akoya Biosciences, NEL700A001). Slides were incubated in Streptavidin Alexa Fluor 555 conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, S32355) at a dilution of 1:2000 in blocking solution and mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P36931). Images were collected at 20X magnification using a Keyence BZ-X710 microscope and its associated software.

RT-qPCR

To collect periosteal tissue from the explants, fine forceps and a scalpel were used to peel the periosteum from the bone surface under a dissecting microscope in cold PBS, as previously described.20 Stripped periosteum was immediately transferred to TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, 15596018) on ice. RNA was isolated using an RNeasy Kit (Qiagen, 74106). RNA was converted to cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher, 4368814). qPCR was performed using Faststart universal SYBR Green (Sigma-Aldrich, 4913850001) and a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosciences). mRNA values were normalized to GAPDH as an internal control. Explants exposed to rocking are often expressed as a fold change in relation to static controls normalized to a value of “1.” Primer sequences are available upon request.

Statistical analysis and study design

Researchers were blinded to all data analysis. Differences between control and experimental groups were determined using a 2-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA where appropriate. Values are reported as mean ± SEM, with p < .05 considered statistically significant. The sample size was selected to achieve a power of at least 80% and specific sample numbers for each experiment are detailed in the figure captions. Samples from female and male mice were combined for analysis because we did not detect any differences due to sex. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism.

Results

Device conception and design

Our initial interest in studying bone apposition arose from attempts to understand the thin-bone phenotype present in Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl mice. Apposition is difficult to observe in vivo because it occurs rapidly in the first few weeks after birth, when mice are physically small and sensitive to traditional experimental techniques designed for larger, older mice, such as frequent injections and applied loading. To avoid these technical limitations, we explored ex vivo culture systems that would allow us to directly observe bone widening in a more controlled environment. In our initial attempts, bones cultured under static conditions successfully lengthened over time, but failed to grow in width regardless of the source of the bone (femur, humerus, etc.), age of the mouse, and various culture conditions that were tested. We noted that the rate of limb bone apposition increases significantly around the time mouse pups begin walking, and bones are known to widen in response to loading. We hypothesized that apposition could be promoted in bone explants by introducing a loading component, so we developed an ex vivo appositional growth model inspired by a rocker system used to apply fluid shear during osteogenic cell culture.21–23 In this system, cells are seeded on rectangular plates positioned on a variable-speed platform rocker in an incubator. The fluid passes over the cells in an oscillatory, linear fashion as the platform tilts, and any fluid flow-induced osteogenic changes can be observed in cells exposed to 1 h of rocking.24 We adapted this setup for femur explants using supports to secure the femurs in place so fluid flow exposure was consistent between samples (Figure 1A). This design enables us to provide a consistent force profile across samples and avoid specimen contamination.

Bone apposition and periosteal activity in femur explants exposed to rocking

We then examined whether our rocker apparatus could promote bone apposition in explants. Femurs were dissected from 1-wk-old mice, since this age is representative of when rapid bone apposition initiates in vivo, and exposed to daily rocking (Figure 1B). Consistent with our prior attempts at explant culture, femurs under static conditions did not widen after a week in culture. However, femurs exposed to 1 h of daily rocking were significantly wider when compared to their static contralateral controls (Figure 1D and E). Changes in length were not impacted by rocking (Figure 1E). We compared explants cultured for a week to bones that grew in vivo and were dissected from 2-wk-old mice. After 7 d in culture, static and rock explant width was similar to 1-wk-old (explant day 0) and 2-wk-old femurs grown in vivo, respectively (Figure S2). Explants exhibited more areas of woven bone-like structure compared to the compact bone seen in 2-wk-old femurs, but structural features were otherwise comparable. Explants were viable after 1 wk in culture and suitable for histological examination (Figure 1C), with the rock group showing significant periosteal expansion (Figure 1F). The periosteum normally expands during apposition and load-induced bone formation, so this result suggests periosteal activity is activated/enhanced in femur explants when oscillatory fluid flow is applied. Indeed, markers for periosteal cell mechanotransduction are elevated in periosteum collected from rock explants compared to static controls (Figure 2A). Rock explant periosteum also had increased expression of Bmp2 and downstream targets of BMP signaling (Figure 2B), which positively correlates with high periosteal activity. These findings suggest our force profile is sufficient to induce periosteal expansion and our rocking apparatus is an effective model for appositional growth.

Validation using an in vivo bone apposition model

We next tested whether our apparatus would allow us to observe changes in the thin-bone phenotype characteristic of Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl mice.16 Femurs in mutant mice are significantly thinner compared to Bmp2fl/fl controls at 2 wk of age, despite having similar width at 1 wk of age. We dissected femurs from these mice at 1 wk of age and applied our week-long protocol to mimic this timeframe. Explants from control mice widened in comparison to static controls, but this behavior was lost in the mutant explants (Figure 2C-D). No significant differences in length (Figure 2C) were observed between control and mutant explants, which is also consistent with the in vivo model. We then evaluated bone structural properties using micro-CT. Cross sections of the midshaft midpoint further demonstrate that control explants widen with rocking, but mutant explant width is comparable to static controls (Figure 2E). Previous histological analysis suggested Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl mice have thicker cortical bone layers and smaller marrow volumes compared to control mice.16 We quantified these properties using the micro-CT scans and confirmed the cortical bone layer is thicker and the marrow volume is smaller in mutants (Figure 1F). These alterations result in a higher bone volume fraction (BV/TV) in mutant femurs since the whole bone is thinner.

The Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl thin-bone phenotype is the result of a lack of periosteal activity and BMP signaling, so we examined these aspects in our ex vivo model.16 First, we evaluated expansion of the periosteum to assess activity. The periosteum of control femurs expands when rocking is applied, but the thickness of the periosteum is not affected by flow-induced load in the mutant explants (Figure 3A). Control femurs have a basal level of BMP signaling in the periosteum and bone under static conditions. pSMAD1/5/8 expression increases after 3 d of daily rocking, suggesting BMP signaling is activated in response to loading (Figure 3B). Similar to in vivo observations, mutant explants have little to no pSMAD1/5/8 expression regardless of whether rocking is introduced. Collectively, our data are consistent with in vivo observations and confirm we can analyze the thin-bone phenotype associated with periosteal inactivity using our new rocker system.

Interrogating BMP2 in periosteal osteogenesis

BMP2 was previously thought to have no impact on load-induced bone formation, but our pSMAD1/5/8 results suggest it is involved in apposition (Figure 3B).25 We interrogated this potential role using our model. With rocking, control explants exhibited an increase in BMP2 mRNA (Figure 2B) and protein expression (Figure 3C) in the periosteum. mRNA expression of transcriptional targets associated with BMP signaling activation (Id1, Id3) also increased with exposure to loading (Figure 2B) These findings further highlight the importance of BMP2-mediated BMP signaling in periosteal osteogenesis, and suggest a new function in load-induced activation of the periosteum.

BMP2 is a secreted protein that can be stored in the extracellular tissue matrix. Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl mice therefore lack access to BMP2 that readily engages in cell-cell interactions upon release, as well as matrix-bound BMP2 that is stored for future use. We have wondered whether the thin-bone and failure to initiate fracture repair phenotypes observed in Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl mice could be remedied by re-introducing BMP2.16 Local administration of recombinant BMP2 to young mice has several technical limitations, so we attempted to address this question using our appositional growth model. Explant culture media was supplemented with 100 ng/mL BMP2 and rocking was applied as previously described (Figure 1B). Control explants and the length of all explants were unaffected by BMP2 supplementation (Figure 4A and B, Figure S3). Mutant explants treated with BMP2 exhibited more porous cortical bone compared to untreated groups (Figure 4A). Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl static explants treated with BMP2 remained significantly thinner than control explants. However, mutant explants exposed to rocking widened with BMP2 treatment and the resulting width was comparable to that of control explants (Figure 4A and B). Interestingly, mutant explants exposed to rocking and treated with BMP2 exhibited a cortical thickness comparable to control explants (Figure 4C). This correction was not sufficient to address the increased BV/TV observed in mutants because the marrow volume remained significantly smaller compared to controls (Figure 4C). Overall, treatment (rescue) with BMP2 appeared to partially correct the thin-bone phenotype when loading was applied.

Examining periosteal gene targets for bone apposition

We next evaluated whether periosteum from explants could be used for transcriptional analysis after a week in culture. The periosteum was stripped as previously described, and we were able to extract high-quality mRNA from our samples. We then evaluated genes associated with BMP signaling (Bmp2, Id1, Id3, and Osx), osteogenic lineage commitment of PSPCs (Osx), and bone apposition (Satb2, Fgfr3, Nrp2, and Wnt16).20,26–30 In control explants, expression of every gene increased when rocking was applied, and this increase was further enhanced in many of the genes when BMP2 was supplemented in the media (Figure 5). Gene expression was unchanged in the mutant explants that were not treated with BMP2. In mutant explants treated with BMP2, expression of almost every gene was moderately increased, with Satb2 being the only gene that approached a level comparable to control explants. These results further validate the experimental utility of our femur explants system, and confirm that our apparatus can be used to evaluate transcriptional changes associated with bone apposition.8

Discussion

Here, we describe a novel ex vivo system and use it to study bone apposition, a key but poorly understood function of the periosteum. Our approach addresses existing limitations with studying bone apposition and periosteal activity in vivo. Briefly, we modified existing explant culture and fluid flow-induced loading systems to activate appositional growth in femurs of young mice. We confirmed that our apparatus and culture conditions stimulated periosteal expansion and activated BMP signaling, which ultimately resulted in widening of the bone explants. We also demonstrated that culture media can be supplemented to test potential therapeutics for promoting bone formation, and explants can be processed for histological and transcriptional analysis, making our ex vivo appositional growth model a useful tool for investigating bone apposition and associated periosteal activity.

A current major knowledge gap is understanding how the periosteum “chooses” between activating for bone apposition (osteoblast-mediated) or fracture repair (chondrocyte-mediated). Recent efforts have focused on characterizing PSPC populations, but findings suggest the same PSPCs participate in apposition, load-induced formation, and fracture repair.1,9,10,12,15,31 Another possibility is that bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) uniquely contribute to fracture repair, but it has been shown that PSPCs overwhelmingly drive callus formation, mineralization, and remodeling, so it is unlikely that BMSC behavior is a key influence.13,14 We speculate that factors that influence the periosteal niche—including mechanical inputs—impact transcriptional outputs and cell signaling that determine PSPC fate decisions. Clarifying how the periosteum can be reactivated to promote apposition during load-induced bone formation is likely to provide important insights for developing bone anabolic treatments targeting elderly patients.32 Age-related decreases in bone formation have been linked to decreased osteogenic activity of PSPCs, rather than the number of available PSPCs or number/potency of BMSCs.33–35 Our system enables the study of phenotypes that cannot be tested using standard in vivo loading techniques, such as tibial or ulnar loading. We anticipate that using our ex vivo appositional growth model will provide novel insights and a more comprehensive understanding of PSPC and whole periosteum behavior.

While conducting our validation studies, we discovered some unexpected findings regarding mechanical stimulation and periosteal BMP signaling. BMP2 was previously thought to not be involved in load-induced bone formation, but our results indicate BMP2 expression increases (Figures 2C and 3C) and BMP signaling is activated (Figures 2C, 3B, and 5) in response to loading.25 Further studies are required to determine if BMP signaling is involved in PSPC mechanotransduction, or if its activation is a consequence of mechanotransduction to facilitate PSPC proliferation and/or osteogenic differentiation. Although BMP2/BMP signaling is necessary for periosteal activation, BMP2 treatment does not appear to enhance bone formation under normal conditions. This was demonstrated in control explants, which exhibited no changes in bone width or structural properties when treated with recombinant BMP2 (Figure 4, Figure S3). BMP2 treatment did enhance bone apposition in Prx1Cre;Bmp2fl/fl explants, resulting in a partial recovery of the thin-bone phenotype (Figure 4).16 We observed changes after a week of BMP2 supplementation, so a full recovery could be possible with a longer treatment regimen. Interestingly, this recovery only occurred when rocking was applied, further suggesting a role for BMP2/BMP signaling in load-induced bone formation and PSPC mechanotransduction. Of the genes associated with apposition, Satb2 expression was uniquely recovered with BMP2 supplementation. Satb2 mutations are associated with thin bones, low bone mass, and spontaneous fractures in patients.36 This gene is considered a transcriptional regulator of skeletal development and growth, and has been linked to BMP2.26,37 More work is required to characterize the role of Satb2 in periosteal activity, but our results suggest that it is a promising focus for future studies on appositional growth.

There are limitations to consider for future uses of this apparatus. First, we only confirmed explant viability up to a week in culture, so our system may not be useful for longer-term studies. Second, we noted that explants appeared to be more robust when FBS was included in culture media, but we selected BSA to facilitate BMP signaling experiments. Other groups report that partially submerging explants in media facilitates viability and growth by introducing oxygen.38,39 The femur support design can accommodate partial submergence by adjusting the media volume, but this will likely impact the fluid flow profile. Third, we did not formally characterize the force profile applied to the explant. We took measures to ensure fluid flow was consistent between samples, including using rectangular dishes to promote more linear flow, locking the variable speed on the platform rocker, and positioning the explants identically within the custom femur supports and plate. In addition, fluid flow is not the most physiologically relevant way to load limb bones but is a confirmed method to stimulate osteogenesis.24 Ultimately, we demonstrated that the applied force is sufficient to induce bone apposition in a way that is consistent across samples, device users, and experimental repeats. Like any in vitro and ex vivo tool, this apparatus is meant to provide insights for future in vivo investigation.38 Regardless of the limitations, this work contributes to advancing our understanding of the periosteum and how its osteogenic power can be harnessed to treat bone disease and enhance repair.
