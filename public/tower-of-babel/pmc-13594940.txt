The storage condition and duration differentially affect the internal quality and oxidative stability of eggs from aged laying hens
Authors: Sarah Maria Pires Camargo, Helder Freitas de Oliveira, Júlia Marixara Sousa da Silva, Karolyne Moura Nascimento, Deibity Alves Cordeiro, Frederico Lopes da Silva, Renata Rodrigues Gomes, Aline Mondini Calil Racanicci, José Henrique Stringhini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The extension of laying cycles has increased the importance of understanding how eggs produced by older hens respond to storage conditions. This study evaluated the effects of hen age, temperature, and storage duration on internal egg quality, albumen viscosity, and oxidative stability. Eggs from laying hens aged 31, 62, and 88 weeks were maintained under refrigeration or at room temperature and evaluated at the initial assessment and after 10, 20, and 30 days of storage. Internal quality was assessed using the yolk index (YI), albumen index (AI), and Haugh unit (HU). Albumen viscosity, yolk lipid oxidation, and protein oxidation were also determined, consisting of the same three laying hen ages and two storage condition, but considering only two storage periods (1 and 30 days). Storage duration and temperature significantly affected all quality parameters evaluated. Refrigerated eggs maintained relatively high YI, AI, and HU values throughout storage, whereas storage at ambient temperature accelerated quality deterioration. After 30 days, eggs stored at ambient temperature showed marked reductions in internal quality compared to refrigerated eggs. Descriptive patterns suggested a decrease in albumen viscosity during storage, particularly at room temperature, with an apparently greater decline in eggs from older hens. Lipid oxidation increased during storage and was more pronounced in eggs kept at ambient temperature. Refrigeration preserved higher concentrations of free thiol groups in both the albumen and yolk, indicating greater protein stability during storage. It is concluded that storage duration affecting egg quality deterioration, while refrigeration effectively preserved their physical, functional, and oxidative characteristics. Eggs produced by older laying hens are more susceptible to storage-related quality losses, highlighting the importance of refrigerated storage during extended laying cycles.

Introduction

Eggs are among the most widely consumed animal-derived foods because of their high nutritional value, affordability, and versatility. They provide high-quality proteins, essential fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals, making them important components of human nutrition across different populations. In addition to direct consumption, eggs represent a valuable raw material for the food industry because of their unique functional properties, including emulsification, foaming, gelation, and water-binding capacity, which support their extensive use in a wide range of processed food products (Réhault-Godbert and Nys, 2019; Puglisi and Fernandez, 2022; Caffa et al., 2025).

Changes in consumer lifestyles, urbanization, and the increasing demand for convenient foods have expanded the egg-processing sector and stimulated the consumption of liquid, frozen, and dried egg products. Consequently, maintaining egg quality throughout storage and distribution has become increasingly important not only for consumer acceptance but also for preserving the technological functionality required for industrial applications (Agregán et al., 2023; Gautron et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2022).

Maintaining internal egg quality during storage remains a major challenge for both the table egg market and the egg-processing industry. Because eggs continue to undergo physicochemical changes after oviposition, preserving their structural and functional characteristics is essential to ensure consumer acceptance, technological functionality, and product shelf-life. In this context, storage conditions play a central role in determining the rate at which quality deterioration occurs. Studies have demonstrated that water vapor and carbon dioxide are continuously lost through eggshell pores during storage, promoting increases in albumen pH and progressive alterations in albumen and yolk integrity (Mohammed et al., 2022; Madrigal-Portilla et al., 2023; Salahuddin et al., 2024).

As storage progresses, deterioration extends beyond physical changes and involves biochemical processes that may further compromise egg quality. Eggs displayed or stored under ambient conditions are often exposed to temperatures that accelerate quality loss, particularly in warm storage condition. In addition to reducing albumen viscosity and weakening yolk structure, oxidative reactions may contribute to the degradation of internal egg components. Protein oxidation can affect albumen integrity, whereas lipid oxidation may impair yolk stability, collectively reducing freshness and shelf-life (Obianwuna et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). Therefore, evaluating both physical and oxidative indicators is important for a comprehensive assessment of egg quality during storage.

In addition to storage conditions, hen age is an important factor influencing egg quality and shelf-life potential. As laying hens age, eggs generally become larger and are characterized by thinner shells and increased shell porosity. These structural changes may facilitate moisture and gas exchange with the external storage condition, potentially accelerating the physicochemical processes associated with quality deterioration during storage (Oliveira et al., 2020; Camargo et al., 2021; Şekeroğlu et al., 2024). Although the effects of storage conditions on egg quality have been extensively investigated, limited information is available on how eggs from hens in extended laying cycles respond to prolonged storage under different storage condition, particularly with respect to internal quality, albumen functionality, and oxidative stability.

On the basis of these considerations, it was hypothesized that eggs produced by hens at advanced ages would exhibit increased susceptibility to quality deterioration during storage, characterized by greater decreases in internal quality, albumen functionality, and oxidative stability. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of hen age, storage temperature, and storage duration on the physical and functional characteristics of eggs, including internal quality traits, albumen viscosity, and markers of lipid and protein oxidation.

Materials and methods

Location and ethical approval

The experiment was conducted at the Experimental Poultry Facility of the Poultry Science Sector, Department of Animal Science, School of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Science, Federal University of Goiás (UFG), Goiânia, Goiás, Brazil. Ethical approval was not required because the study involved only eggs and did not include the use or handling of live animals.

Experimental design

A total of 288 eggs from commercial Hy-Line laying hens at 31, 62, or 88 weeks of age were used to evaluate albumen index, yolk index, and Haugh unit. This study used animals from the same commercial farm, representing three flocks of different ages managed under the same standardized storage condition and nutritional programs, in accordance with breed guidelines. A cage rearing system was used, and eggs were collected via an automatic conveyor belt. The experiment was conducted in a completely randomized design using a 3 × 2 × 4 factorial arrangement, with three laying hen ages (31, 62, or 88 weeks), two storage condition (ambient temperature or refrigerated), and four storage periods (1, 10, 20, or 30 days). Four replicates of three eggs each were used per treatment, resulting in 24 treatments. For viscosity, lipid oxidation (TBARS), and protein oxidation (thiol groups), a subset of 144 eggs was evaluated using a completely randomized design in a 3 × 2 × 2 factorial arrangement, consisting of the same three laying hen ages and two storage condition, but considering only two storage periods (1 and 30 days). For these analyses, four replicates of three eggs each were used per treatment, resulting in 12 treatments.

Storage conditions and environmental monitoring

The eggs were stored in previously identified commercial cardboard packaging and kept at ambient temperature or in a refrigerator. All refrigerated eggs were stored in a single refrigerator, whereas eggs assigned to room-temperature storage were maintained in the same room. For the initial assessment, eggs assigned to room-temperature storage were evaluated on the day of lay, whereas refrigerated eggs were evaluated after 24 h of refrigeration. Subsequent evaluations were performed at the same experimental periods for both storage conditions (10, 20, and 30 days). The air temperature and relative humidity were recorded once daily with a digital thermometer. Under ambient storage conditions, the mean minimum and maximum temperatures were 25.2 and 29.7°C, respectively. Under refrigerated storage conditions, the mean minimum and maximum temperatures were 4.6 and 11.5°C, respectively. The humidity was stable in the refrigerated storage condition, with an average value of 82.3%. In the natural storage condition however, it fluctuated, with an average value of 66.7%.

Internal egg quality evaluation

Three eggs from each experimental unit were used to evaluate internal egg quality. The following parameters were determined: yolk index (YI), albumen index (AI), Haugh unit (HU), and albumen viscosity.

At each storage period (48 hours eggs and after 10, 20, and 30 d of storage), the eggs were individually identified and weighed via a precision electronic balance (BEL Engineering®, accuracy ± 0.001 g). Eggs were then manually broken onto a flat, leveled glass surface, and yolk and albumen heights (mm) were measured via a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo®, Japan). These measurements were subsequently used to calculate the YI and AI.

The albumen and yolk heights (mm) were measured with a micrometre. Yolk index was determined by the ratio between yolk height and yolk diameter. Albumen index was determined by the ratio between albumen height and the average of the two measured albumen diameters.

Haugh units (HUs) were calculated according to the equation proposed by Haugh, as described by Silva (2004):HU=100×log⁡(H−1.7×P0.37+7.57)where HU is the albumen height (mm) and P is the egg weight (g).

Yolk and albumen diameters were measured with a digital calliper (±0.01 mm). Albumen diameter was measured in two widths (smaller and larger), obtaining the average between these two values expressed in millimeters (mm) to determine the albumen index by Oliveira et al. (2021).

The albumen viscosity was determined via a rotational viscometer (Visco Tester 6 L/R, Haake®, Germany). Eleven consecutive measurements were recorded at 20-s intervals to ensure instrument stabilization, using a L2 spindle at a rotational speed of 6 rpm, and samples at room temperature (26°C).

Oxidative stability assessment

Lipid and protein oxidation were evaluated on days 1 and 30 of storage. Four replicates—each consisting of three eggs per treatment—analyzed in triplicate using lyophilized samples, totaling 12 treatments. Lipid oxidation was assessed by measuring thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), whereas protein oxidation was determined by quantifying free thiol groups (-SH). Together, these analyses provided complementary indicators of oxidative deterioration in yolk lipids and albumen proteins during storage.

Lipid oxidation (TBARS)

Lipid oxidation was determined by measuring thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) according to the method described by Vyncke (1970), as modified by Sørensen and Jørgensen (1996). The results are expressed as μmol malondialdehyde (MDA)/kg yolk sample. A standard curve was prepared using tetraethoxypropane (TEP; Sigma‒Aldrich®, St. Louis, MO, USA) diluted in 7.5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) containing 0.1% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and 0.1% propyl gallate. Lyophilized yolk samples were rehydrated with Milli-Q water before analysis. Aliquots were reacted with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reagent in a boiling water bath for 40 min, followed by cooling and spectrophotometric determination at 532 and 600 nm. TBARS concentrations were calculated via a standard curve (R² = 0.9993).

Protein oxidation (free thiol groups)

Protein oxidation was evaluated by determining free thiol groups (-SH) via 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB; Sigma‒Aldrich®, St. Louis, MO, USA), according to Ellman (1959), with adaptations described by Jongberg et al. (2019). The results are expressed as μmol malondialdehyde (MDA)/kg of dried yolk.

Lyophilized yolk and albumen samples were rehydrated with Milli-Q water, homogenized in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), heat-treated, centrifuged, and filtered prior to analysis. Protein concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 280 nm using a standard curve prepared from BSA. Free thiol groups were quantified by reaction with DTNB, which produces a yellow-colored compound proportional to the concentration of sulfhydryl groups present in the sample. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm via a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Gehaka®, São Paulo, Brazil). Thiol concentrations were determined from a standard curve prepared with L-cysteine (R² = 0.9812), with lower thiol concentrations indicating greater protein oxidation.

Statistical analysis

The data were screened for normality and homogeneity of variance via the Shapiro‒Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Potentially outlying observations were screened through boxplot inspection and externally studentized residuals obtained from the fitted models, with an absolute studentized residual greater than 3 (|ri| > 3) used as the predefined threshold. Flagged observations were examined for possible data-entry, analytical, or procedural errors; no observations were excluded from the analyses. Variables meeting the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) via a three-way factorial arrangement consisting of hen age, storage temperature, and storage duration, including all two-way and three-way interactions.

For albumen index, yolk index, and Haugh unit, data were analyzed using a 3 × 2 × 4 factorial arrangement, consisting of three hen ages (31, 62, and 88 weeks), two storage temperatures (ambient and refrigerated), and four storage periods (1, 10, 20, and 30 days). For albumen viscosity and oxidative stability variables (TBARS and thiol groups), data were analyzed using a 3 × 2 × 2 factorial arrangement, consisting of the same three hen ages and two storage temperatures, but considering only the initial and final storage periods (1 and 30 days). All two-way and three-way interactions were included in the respective models.

The models included the fixed effects of hen age, storage environment, storage time, and all two- and three-way interactions. When significant effects were detected, the means were compared via Tukey’s test. Statistical analyses were performed via R software (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), and P ≤ 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

For YI, the storage temperature markedly affected quality preservation. Eggs stored under refrigeration maintained higher YI values throughout the experimental period, whereas those stored under ambient conditions exhibited progressive decreases. After 30 d of storage, YI decreased from 0.46 to 0.19 in eggs stored on the room temperature, whereas refrigerated eggs-maintained values close to 0.42. Hen age also influenced YI, with eggs from 62- and 88-wk-old hens generally exhibiting lower values than eggs from 31-wk-old hens during storage.

A similar pattern was observed for the AI. Albumen quality decreased with increasing storage duration in all the treatments, but refrigeration substantially reduced the magnitude of this decline. Eggs stored under ambient conditions presented marked reductions in AI after 10 d of storage, reaching values as low as 0.02 at 20 and 30 d, whereas refrigerated eggs maintained AI values between 0.08 and 0.09 throughout the same period. In addition, eggs from older hens generally presented lower AI values than those from younger hens did.

The storage duration and temperature also markedly affected the HU values. Fresh eggs presented HU values above 92 regardless of hen age; however, substantial reductions occurred during storage under ambient conditions. After 30 d, the HU values ranged from 13.09 to 38.53 for the room temperature eggs, whereas the values for the refrigerated eggs were between 75.75 and 81.83. The greatest decline was observed in eggs from 88-wk-old hens stored under ambient conditions, while refrigeration effectively preserved HU regardless of hen age.

Protein oxidation was assessed through the concentration of free thiol groups in the albumen and yolk (Figure 5, Figure 6). In albumen, storage conditions markedly influenced thiol concentrations over time. The thiol concentrations of the refrigerated eggs increased after 30 d of storage, whereas those of the eggs stored under ambient conditions slightly decreased. Consequently, albumen from refrigerated eggs maintained higher thiol concentrations throughout storage than eggs stored under ambient conditions.

Hen age also affected the albumen thiol concentration. Eggs from 88-wk-old hens presented lower thiol concentrations after storage than eggs from younger hens did, whereas albumen from eggs produced by 31- and 62-wk-old hens maintained comparatively higher values over time.

A similar pattern was observed in the yolk samples. Compared with ambient storage, refrigerated storage resulted in higher thiol concentrations throughout the experimental period. In addition, eggs from 88-wk-old hens presented the greatest reduction in yolk thiol concentration during storage, whereas eggs from 31- and 62-wk-old hens presented smaller changes over time.

Discussion

The internal quality traits evaluated in the present study were markedly influenced by both hen age and storage conditions. Advancing hen age has been consistently associated with changes in egg composition and structure, including increased egg weight, altered yolk-to-albumin proportions, and reductions in shell quality, which may increase susceptibility to quality deterioration during storage (Sokołowicz et al., 2018; Camargo et al., 2021; Tainika et al., 2024; Oliveira et al., 2026). Similarly, storage duration and temperature are recognized as major determinants of egg quality preservation, with prolonged storage and ambient temperatures promoting greater losses in freshness and internal quality than refrigerated conditions do (Lee et al., 2016; Northcutt et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2024). These findings are consistent with the results observed in the present study, in which the yolk index, albumen index, and Haugh unit progressively decreased during storage, particularly under ambient conditions and in eggs produced by older hens.

The reduction in these quality parameters can be attributed primarily to the physicochemical changes that occur after oviposition. Water vapor and carbon dioxide are continuously lost through eggshell pores, increasing the albumen pH and promoting albumen thinning. Consequently, water migrates from the albumen to the yolk, weakening the vitelline membrane and reducing yolk integrity, which is reflected in the lower yolk index, albumen index, and Haugh unit values. Refrigerated storage slowed these deterioration processes, preserving albumen consistency and yolk structure throughout the storage period. Similar responses have been reported by Kim et al. (2022), Sokołowicz et al. (2023), and Eregama et al. (2024), who demonstrated that refrigeration effectively maintains internal egg quality by reducing the rate of physicochemical changes associated with storage.

Albumen viscosity is an important functional property of eggs and is closely associated with the integrity of the ovomucin-lysozyme complex, which is largely responsible for the gel-like structure of thick albumen in fresh eggs (Kemps et al., 2010; Jiang et al., 2022; Wan et al., 2024). During storage, physicochemical changes such as carbon dioxide loss, increased albumen pH, and progressive disruption of this complex promote albumen thinning and reduce viscosity. The results observed in the present study support this mechanism, as viscosity progressively decreased with increasing storage duration, particularly under ambient conditions. Similar responses have been reported in previous studies evaluating egg quality during storage, which demonstrated that elevated temperatures accelerate albumen degradation and the loss of its functional properties (Vlčková et al., 2019; Wlaźlak et al., 2024).

Although inferential comparisons could not be performed, the lower viscosity observed in the samples evaluated after 30 d was qualitatively consistent with albumen thinning during storage. The comparatively higher viscosity of the refrigerated samples from 31- and 62-wk-old hens was also compatible with the expected retardation of albumen deterioration at lower storage temperatures. Nevertheless, because each treatment combination was represented by a single observation, treatment effects could not be distinguished from sample-specific variation. Therefore, these profiles should be interpreted only as exploratory evidence and not as confirmation of effects associated with storage environment, storage duration, or hen age.

In addition to changes in viscosity, storage influences the oxidative stability of egg components. Lipid oxidation occurs primarily in the yolk due to its high concentration of unsaturated fatty acids, whereas protein oxidation affects both yolk and albumen proteins, potentially altering their structural and functional properties (Silvério et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2023a; Deng et al., 2025). Although shell eggs possess natural antioxidant defenses, including phosphovitin, ovotransferrin, tocopherols, and carotenoid compounds, prolonged storage and elevated temperatures favor oxidative reactions (Romero et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2023b). The oxidative responses observed in the present study indicate that the biochemical stability of egg components during storage depends on the interplay between hen age, storage condition, and storage duration. Higher concentrations of free thiol groups are indicative of lower albumen protein oxidation, and their preservation under refrigeration is consistent with the greater oxidative stability reported under low-temperature storage (Jongberg et al., 2019). Lipid oxidation also showed an age-dependent response to storage. Under refrigeration, TBARS concentrations increased in eggs from 31-week-old hens, remained stable in those from 62-week-old hens, and decreased in eggs from 88-week-old hens. Moreover, the initially higher TBARS concentrations observed in eggs from 88-week-old hens and the consistently lower values in those from 62-week-old hens suggest that oxidative responses during storage cannot be attributed solely to storage temperature. Although no direct association between lipid and protein oxidation was assessed in the present study, mechanisms described in the literature indicate that these processes may be biologically interconnected, as reactive species generated during lipid peroxidation can promote secondary oxidative modifications in proteins (Domínguez et al., 2019; Oliveira et al., 2021; Geng et al., 2023).

Some limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Each hen age was represented by a single commercial flock, and each storage condition was represented by a single storage unit. Therefore, potential flock- and storage-unit-specific effects cannot be fully separated from the effects attributed to hen age and storage condition, respectively. Although management and storage conditions were standardized and monitored throughout the experiment, these findings should be interpreted within this experimental context. Future studies incorporating independent flocks within each age group and replicated storage units would strengthen the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusion

Refrigerated storage was associated with better preservation of internal egg quality during the 30-d storage period compared with room-temperature storage. Hen age and storage duration also influenced egg quality traits, with their effects varying according to the evaluated response. Overall, the findings reinforce the importance of refrigeration for maintaining egg quality during prolonged storage, including eggs produced by hens in extended laying cycles.
