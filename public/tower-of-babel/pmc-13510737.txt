Metal-free redox active covalent organic frameworks for electrocatalytic oxygen reduction reaction
Authors: Bikash Mishra, Surajit Samui, Sourav Ghosh, Supriti Dutta, Chowdhury Ismat Nurani, Monojit Roy, Debashis Adhikari, Ranjit Thapa, Ramendra Sundar Dey, Pradip Pachfule
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing efficient, metal-free covalent organic framework (COF) electrocatalysts is a promising strategy for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) due to their structural tunability and stability. Herein, we report two redox-active donor–acceptor COFs, TAPA-DHTD and TTT-DHTD, incorporating a thiophene-based dithiophenedione (DHTD) unit. Among them, TTT-DHTD exhibits superior ORR performance with a half-wave potential (E1/2) of 0.82 V vs RHE, approaching that of 20% Pt/C (0.85 V) and surpassing most reported metal-free COFs. It also demonstrates excellent methanol tolerance and long-term durability, maintaining activity over 120 hours. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that the donor–acceptor framework optimizes the p-band center, enhances charge separation, and improves adsorption of oxygenated intermediates, thereby accelerating ORR kinetics. This work provides a rational strategy for designing durable, high-performance metal-free electrocatalysts for sustainable energy applications.

INTRODUCTION

Advanced techniques for sustainable energy storage and conversion have garnered widespread interest across academia and industry due to the rapid depletion of fossil fuel resources and escalating environmental concerns. Indeed, among these pivotal technologies, the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) serves as a critical half-reaction in metal–air batteries (MABs) and fuel cells (1, 2). During device operation, ORR takes place at the cathode, where molecular oxygen is electrochemically reduced. However, efficiently activating and cleaving the O=O bond, which has a high bond dissociation energy of 498 kJ mol−1, remains a substantial challenge and contributes to the inherently sluggish ORR kinetics (3). To enhance the efficiency of ORR, noble metals as well as non-noble metal-based alloys, single-atom catalysts (SACs), chalcogenides, etc. have been widely employed, leveraging their strong affinity for oxygen intermediates and superior catalytic activity (4–7). However, their high cost, limited availability, inadequate long-term stability, and restricted functionality under harsh conditions hinder widespread adoption (8, 9). In response, considerable research has focused on developing alternatives based on metal or nanoparticle-doped heteroatom-doped carbon materials (10). Despite notable progress, these hybrid systems still deal with inherent challenges related to structural degradation of the carbon support, limited long-term durability arising from both metal leaching/agglomeration and support instability, and a relatively narrow functional scope primarily dictated by the incorporated metal species (11). These challenges have prompted an urgent need for the development of robust, metal-free, porous catalysts that combine chemical stability with high electrocatalytic activity and multifunctionality.

Among the various classes of porous materials, covalent organic frameworks (COFs) have emerged as an exceptionally promising platform as electrocatalysts. COFs are crystalline porous polymers constructed from organic building blocks linked via strong covalent bonds. Their intrinsic modularity, exceptionally high surface areas, and precisely tunable structures have rendered them highly attractive for a wide range of applications, including gas storage and adsorption, molecular separation, optoelectronics, and heterogeneous catalysis (12–17). Although several COFs incorporating precious metals have been reported and have shown promising activity, concerns remain about their stability and sustainability for long-term applications. In this context, the unique ability of COFs to integrate functional moieties into periodically ordered architectures through atomic-level design presents a compelling opportunity for the rational development of efficient, stable, and cost-effective metal-free electrocatalysts. This structural versatility makes COFs especially promising for enhancing the ORR - a key process in electrochemical energy conversion technologies. Notably, Yao and co-workers first reported a metal-free bisthiophene-sulfur-based COF, JUC-528, which exhibited better ORR performance compared to its monothiophene analogue, JUC-527 (18). However, this improvement still results in JUC-528 delivering a half-wave potential of only 0.70 V vs. RHE, which remains considerably lower than that of state-of-the-art ORR catalysts (e.g., Pt/C). Despite these advances, reports on metal-free COFs exhibiting high ORR catalytic activity remain relatively scarce (19, 20). This limitation is largely attributed to their typically low half-wave potentials (E1/2), which reflect higher overpotentials required to drive the reaction and, consequently, lower catalytic efficiency. As a result, there is an urgent need for comprehensive design strategies and mechanistic insights to guide the development of highly active, metal-free COF electrocatalysts for ORR.

To address this critical gap, herein, we demonstrate the development of a metal-free, redox-active, dithiophenedione-based donor–acceptor COF, TTT-DHTD, synthesised from 2,4,6-tris(4-aminophenyl)-1,3,5-triazine (TTT, electron acceptor) and 4,8-dioxo-4,8-dihydrobenzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’]dithiophene-2,6-dicarbaldehyde (DHTD, electron donor). The triazine unit was selected as an electron-deficient acceptor moiety because of its strong electronegativity and high nitrogen content, which can generate electron-deficient carbon centers favorable for oxygen adsorption and activation. In contrast, the thiophene-based dithiophenedione (DHTD) unit was incorporated as an electron-rich donor and redox-active building block to enhance π-electron delocalization and charge-transfer efficiency within the framework. Furthermore, the sulfur-containing thiophene moieties introduce additional redox activity through n–π* transitions and modulate the local electronic environment around the catalytic sites, helping align the oxidation and reduction sites both spatially and energetically within the framework (21). The synergistic integration of triazine and thiophene units within a donor–acceptor architecture therefore promotes efficient charge separation, optimizes the adsorption of oxygen intermediates, and facilitates ORR kinetics through the generation of dual active sites comprising triazine-N and thiophene-C centers. The resulting TTT-DHTD COF exhibits a remarkably high half-wave potential (E1/2) of 0.82 V vs. RHE, corresponding to a substantially reduced overpotential and enhanced ORR activity compared to previously reported metal-free COFs (22–26). This enhanced performance arises from dual active sites (triazine-N and thiophene-C), generated by the rational donor–acceptor architecture of TTT and DHTD units. In TTT-DHTD COF, the DHTD unit not only provides strong electron-donating capability but also introduces thiophene moieties that contribute to additional redox activity through n–π* transitions, helping align the oxidation and reduction sites both spatially and energetically within the framework (21). To rationalize the function of redox-active DHTD moiety, quinone group-free thieno[3,2-b]thiophene-2,5-dicarbaldehyde (DTDA) based TTT-DTDA COF has been synthesized and correlated with other COFs to demonstrate the functionality-activity relationship. Theoretical calculations reveal that the donor–acceptor configuration in the TTT–DHTD COF promotes efficient charge separation and strengthens interactions with oxygen intermediates during the ORR. The combination of electron-accepting TTT and electron-donating DHTD units creates multiple active sites with markedly reduced overpotentials, while the most active nitrogen site exhibits a pronounced p-state density near the Fermi level and an elevated p-band center (Ep), features that correlate strongly with enhanced ORR activity.

Further, to systematically evaluate the impact of donor–acceptor modulation, another COF, TAPA-DHTD, was also synthesized by pairing the same electron-donating 4,8-dioxo-4,8-dihydrobenzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’]dithiophene-2,6-dicarbaldehyde (DHTD) unit with an electron-rich N1,N1-bis(4-aminophenyl)benzene-1,4-diamine (TAPA) linker. Unlike TTT-DHTD, this framework exhibited comparatively lower ORR activity, highlighting the critical role of triazine-based electron-accepting centers in boosting catalytic performance.

RESULTS

Considering the advantageous properties of donor–acceptor COFs and analyzing the impact of electronic modulations in the COF backbone on electrocatalysis, we synthesized a series of electronically distinct COFs as potential electrocatalysts for the ORR. Two redox-active COFs (TAPA-DHTD and TTT-DHTD) and their non-redox-active analogue (TTT-DTDA) were successfully synthesized via solvothermal condensation reactions. Typically, TAPA-DHTD COF was prepared via a condensation reaction between N1,N1-bis(4-aminophenyl)benzene-1,4-diamine (TAPA) and 4,8-dioxo-4,8-dihydrobenzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’]dithiophene-2,6-dicarbaldehyde (DHTD) in a mixed solvent system of N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc) and mesitylene (fig. S1). The reaction mixture was sealed in a Pyrex tube and heated at 120°C for 3 days. Upon completion, the resulting product was isolated by thorough washing with acetone, methanol, tetrahydrofuran, and hexane, yielding a black, powdery solid. Similarly, TTT-DHTD COF was synthesized via a Schiff-base condensation reaction between 4,4′,4″-(1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triyl)trianiline (TTT) and DHTD in a solvent mixture of mesitylene and 1,4-dioxane, under identical conditions at 120°C for 3 days, affording a brown precipitate (Fig. 1A, fig. S2) (27). Last, TTT-DTDA COF was prepared solvothermally using TTT and thieno[3,2-b]thiophene-2,5-dicarbaldehyde (DTDA) under solvothermal conditions, producing a reddish-brown solid (fig. S3) (28).

Powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) analyses were conducted using Cu Kα radiation to assess the crystallinity of the synthesized COFs. The TAPA-DHTD COF exhibited a distinct diffraction peak at 2θ = 2.53° corresponding to the (100) plane, along with a minor reflection at 5.30° (200), indicative of a well-ordered crystalline framework (Fig. 1B). In comparison, the TTT-DHTD COF displayed a prominent (100) reflection at 2θ = 2.36°, accompanied by additional peaks at 4.91° (200), 6.41° (31¯ 0), and 8.39° (42¯0), suggesting a high degree of structural order. In addition, the peak at ∼25° in the PXRD patterns of both COFs reflects π-π stacking in the frameworks (Fig. 1C). To corroborate the experimental PXRD results, theoretical structural models for both COFs were constructed considering different stacking arrangements, including AA, AB, and ABC modes (fig. S11). Structural optimization was performed using the Forcite module, followed by simulation of the corresponding PXRD patterns and Pawley refinement. A systematic comparison between simulated and experimental PXRD profiles revealed that the AA-stacked model provided the best agreement with the experimental diffraction patterns, particularly in peak positions and relative intensities. In contrast, the AB- and ABC-stacked models showed less satisfactory matches. Furthermore, Pawley refinement of the AA-stacked structures afforded lower residual values, confirming that AA stacking is the most probable packing arrangement for both COFs and is consistent with the experimentally observed diffraction patterns.

To confirm the structural integrity of the synthesized frameworks, solid-state 13C cross-polarization magic angle spinning (CP/MAS) NMR spectroscopy was conducted. For the TAPA-DHTD and TTT-DHTD COF, a resonance signal observed at 147 ppm is indicative of successful imine bond formation, while a peak at 170 ppm corresponds to the carbonyl (C=O) group of the DHTD linker (Fig. 2A). Further, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analyses supported these findings. Both COFs exhibit a characteristic peak at 1660 cm−1, associated with the C=O stretching vibration of the quinone moieties. Additionally, the presence of peaks at 1568 cm−1 for TTT-DHTD COF and at 1578 cm−1 for TAPA-DHTD COF corresponds to C=N stretching vibrations, signifying the successful formation of imine linkages via Schiff base condensation (fig. S4).

To evaluate the porosity and surface characteristics of the synthesized COFs, nitrogen sorption isotherms were carried out at 77 K after thorough degassing under vacuum for 24 hours. The TAPA-DHTD COF exhibited a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area of 520 m2 g−1 and a Langmuir surface area of 4535 m2 g-1, displaying a type IV isotherm profile (Fig. 2B and fig. S5B). The TTT-DHTD COF, in comparison, showed a BET surface area of 915 m2 g−1 and a Langmuir surface area of 2591 m2 g−1, corresponding to a type IV isotherm (Fig. 2C and fig. S5A). The enhanced surface area TTT-DHTD COF could be attributed to the planarity of the triazine core (TTT) that ensures high crystallinity and perfectly aligned layer-on-layer stacking compared to the non-planar, propeller-like geometry of the TAPA unit which leads to offset layer orientation and restricted pore accessibility (29). Theoretical pore size distribution calculations indicated pore sizes of 3.3 nm for TAPA-DHTD and 3.7 nm for TTT-DHTD, which closely match the experimentally observed values, confirming the well-developed porosity and structural integrity of both frameworks (Fig. 2B and 2C; inset). The discussion fig. S6 provides the characterization of TTT-DTDA COF, showing that it has high crystallinity and a porous structure.

The periodic framework structures of TAPA-DHTD and TTT-DHTD COFs were further characterized using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (fig. S7). TAPA-DHTD exhibited a well-defined honeycomb-like porous morphology, confirming the formation of a highly ordered and periodic framework. To evaluate chemical stability under conditions relevant to electrocatalysis, both COFs (30 mg each) were immersed in water and 3 M KOH for 24 hours. Powder x-ray diffraction analysis revealed that TTT-DHTD retained its crystallinity completely in water and exhibited only a marginal decrease in peak intensity in alkaline medium (fig. S8B). Likewise, TAPA-DHTD preserved its framework with only a negligible loss of crystallinity, collectively demonstrating the high structural robustness of the COFs in aqueous and strongly basic environments (fig. S8A). To evaluate the thermal stability of the synthesized COFs, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed under a nitrogen atmosphere. Both COFs demonstrated excellent thermal stability, maintaining structural integrity up to 320°C under a nitrogen atmosphere (fig. S9). The electrical conductivity of the COF samples was measured using the two-probe technique. The calculated conductivities were found to be 1.6 × 10−5 S.cm−1 and 4.24 × 10−5 S.cm−1 for TTT-DHTD and TAPA-DHTD, respectively (fig. S10). Slightly lower conductivity in the case of TTT-DHTD COF compared to TAPA-DHTD can be attributed to its highly porous network (30). To verify the presence of any metal impurities, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) analysis of the TTT–DHTD COF was performed. The results indicate that no detectable metal impurities are present in the sample, except for a negligible amount of lithium (Li), which is well below levels that could contribute to the ORR activity (fig. S24).

Evaluation of electrochemical oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) activity of COFs

Owing to their donor–acceptor frameworks, extended π-conjugation, and abundant redox-active sites, the synthesized COFs were evaluated as potential electrocatalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) in alkaline media. All experimental potentials were converted against the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). Initially, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed using a rotating disk electrode (RDE) under an argon atmosphere in 0.1 M KOH electrolyte under static condition. TTT-DHTD COF exhibited two reversible peaks originating from the redox-active triazine units and quinone moiety present in the DHTD linker, respectively (Fig. 3A).

However, the CV study revealed the efficient oxygen reduction capability of the TTT-DHTD COF in the presence of oxygen-saturated electrolyte, as indicated by a cathodic peak appearing at 0.79 V. Similarly, electron-enriched triphenyl amine monomer-based TAPA-DHTD COF exhibited one reversible redox peak owing to the presence of a keto unit in argon-saturated electrolyte (Fig. 3B). Generation of a cathodic peak in the presence of oxygen-saturated electrolyte also confirmed the ORR activity of TAPA-DHTD COF. To further elucidate the influence of redox-active benzodithiophenedione moieties, the TTT-DTDA COF was also examined, which exhibited a pronounced cathodic peak in oxygen-saturated electrolyte, indicative of notable ORR activity (Fig. 3C). Further assessment of the oxygen reduction activity of these COFs was performed by employing a rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE). Linear sweep voltammetry was performed at 10 mVs−1 at 1600 rpm in the presence of oxygen (Fig. 3D). TTT-DHTD COF exhibited better onset potential and higher current density as compared to TAPA-DHTD COF and TTT-DTDA COF. The kinetic aspect of the onset potential for ORR of an electrocatalyst strongly depends on the facile adsorption of the oxygen molecule through the intermolecular transfer of electrons between the adsorbate and the adsorbent site of the electrocatalyst, thereby initiating the oxygen reduction process by overcoming the thermodynamic potential barrier (31). Superior onset potential towards the ORR process for TTT-DHTD COF can be attributed to the presence of a strong electron-accepting triazine moiety and redox-active dithiophene-4,8-dione moiety. Initial adsorption of the oxygen molecule can propagate through facile transfer of an antibonding π*2px electron from the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of the oxygen molecule to the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the electropositive carbon center adjacent to a strongly electronegative triazine moiety or keto moiety (32). The presence of these dual active sites in TTT-DHTD COF strongly facilitates the ORR process by polarizing the O=O bond. Interestingly, the onset potential (0.92 V) and half-wave potential (0.82 V) as exhibited by our metal-free TTT-DHTD COF catalyst are superior to the TAPA-DHTD COF and TTT-DTDA COF (Fig. 3E), and comparable to the half-wave potential (0.85 V) of the state-of-the-art 20% Pt/C catalyst (fig. S12).

To gain insights about the kinetic aspects of the ORR process, rotating ring disk electrode experiments were conducted at different rotational speeds (625–2500 rpm) for TTT-DHTD, TAPA-DHTD, and TTT-DTDA COFs (Fig. 3F, fig. S14, A and B). A linear relationship in Koutecky-Levich plot demonstrated that the ORR process follows first-order kinetics as the reduction current increases linearly with an increase in rotational speed due to enhanced O2 concentration owing to convectional flow (figs. S13 and S14, C and D) (33). Among all COFs, TTT-DHTD COF exhibited the maximum number of electrons being transferred and produced the least amount of hydrogen peroxide as undesired product (fig. S15), demonstrating higher selectivity and specificity towards the four-electron pathway. To better understand the kinetic feasibility of the ORR process, Tafel slopes were determined for all COFs. Among all COFs, TTT-DHTD COF showcased the lowest Tafel slope value (63 mV dec−1) as compared to TAPA-DHTD COF (78 mV dec−1) and TTT-DTDA COF (91 mV dec−1), which confirms a kinetically faster ORR process for TTT-DHTD COF (Fig. 3G). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements also indicated that the lower charge transfer resistance of TTT-DHTD facilitated interfacial charge transfer and promoted oxygen reduction to a greater extent than TAPA-DHTD COF (fig. S16). Electrochemical active surface area, as proportional to double layer capacitance value, demonstrates the specifically exposed active area of the catalyst available for the charge transfer process (34). To evaluate the double-layer capacitance (Cdl), cyclic voltammetry was performed at different scan rates in the non-Faradaic potential window range (fig. S17). TTT-DHTD COF exhibited a double-layer capacitance value of 4.95 mF cm−2, which is nearly four times that of TTT-DTDA COF (1.23 mF cm−2 and TAPA-DHTD COF (1.21 mF cm−2). Enhanced Cdl value for TTT-DHTD COF can be attributed to exposed core polarity generated on electropositive carbon sites due to the presence of electron-extracting triazine and polar keto groups. From these analyses, it could be concluded that the synergistic modulation of electron density through the electron-accepting triazine unit and polarizable benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’] dithiophene-4,8-dione unit strongly modifies the polarity of active sites, fostering the kinetic adsorption of oxygen molecules followed by subsequent reduction to water. Absence of any of these factors (triazine unit in TAPA-DHTD or benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’] dithiophene-4,8-dione units in TTT-DTDA) in other COFs results in slower activation of the oxygen molecule, thereby decelerating the overall ORR activity.

Prolonged catalytic ORR activity of the electrocatalyst is highly essential for real-life fuel cell applications. In this regard, the long-term stability of the electrocatalyst was assessed by chronoamperometry under dynamic conditions. TTT-DHTD COF catalyst retained nearly 97% of its initial current density even after 120 hours (Fig. 3H), surpassing the state-of-the-art 20% Pt/C catalyst, whose current density declined to 75% after 20 hours of operation under similar conditions (fig. S18), confirming the robustness of the TTT-DHTD catalyst for the ORR process.

Moreover, the onset potential and half-wave potentials for TTT-DHTD COF were compared with other recently reported excellent metal-free COFs (Fig. 3I), showcasing one of the highest activities towards the electrochemical ORR process (18, 19, 23, 26, 35–44). Methanol tolerance test of an electrocatalyst is highly beneficial for direct methanol fuel cell application, as the interference of methanol can drastically inhibit the ORR process through poisoning the catalyst (45). Further, to analyse the impact of methanol on ORR activity, a chronoamperometry stability test of the electrocatalyst in the presence of 1 M methanol has been executed (fig. S19). TTT-DHTD COF exhibited excellent methanol tolerance, retaining similar current density even after the addition of methanol. Whereas the current density falls sharply for the standard Pt/C catalyst due to catalyst poisoning. The CV responses of TTT-DHTD COF before and after the methanol tolerance test remain identical (fig. S20). The XPS, FT-IR, PXRD and SEM analyses of the recovered TTT–DHTD COF ink, conducted before and after the long-term stability test, confirmed that the structural integrity, morphology, and elemental composition of the material remain unchanged. Furthermore, the binding energies corresponding to the various functional groups in the catalyst are preserved without any noticeable shifts (figs. S21 to S23). A comprehensive comparison of the catalytic performance of the TTT–DHTD COF with previously reported state-of-the-art metal-free ORR catalysts is presented in table S2. These results collectively underscore the potential of TTT-DHTD COF as a highly stable and methanol-tolerant, metal-free electrocatalyst, making it a promising candidate for Zn-air battery (ZAB) applications.

Benefiting from its excellent electrocatalytic ORR activity, TTT-DHTD COF was further employed in a Zn-air battery application. The configuration of the aqueous Zn-air battery is shown in Fig. 3J. The Zn-air battery was assembled using TTT-DHTD as the air cathode and a Zn plate as the anode. TTT-DHTD exhibited a current density of 46 mA cm−2 at a voltage of 1 V and a peak power density of 79 mW cm−2 at 0.62 V (Fig. 3K), which corresponds to the superior ORR activity of our developed metal-free catalysts. Zn-air battery exhibited excellent rate performance, as confirmed by discharge curves obtained at different current densities (fig. S25A). The fabricated Zn-air battery exhibited an open-circuit voltage of 1.33 V (Fig. 3L). One LED panel consisting of 25 LEDs can be lit up using two Zn-air batteries connected in a series (Fig. 3L, inset). The assembled Zn-air battery also exhibited considerable stability during continuous discharge at 10 mA cm−2 over 15 hours (fig. S25B). Such precise engineering of donor-acceptor and redox-active moieties into COF materials offers an excellent opportunity for sustainable energy conversion.

Elucidation of the mechanistic pathway of ORR

The electrochemical stimulus-driven oxygen reduction process is kinetically sluggish and proceeds via a two-electron or four-electron pathway, producing H2O2 and H2O, respectively. However, challenges associated with selectivity of the final product are strongly dependent on various factors: thermodynamic, kinetic, catalyst reactivity, and reaction environment. Thermodynamically, the ORR process favours the four-electron pathway mediated complete reduction to water (E = 1.23 V vs. RHE) as compared to the two-electron pathway mediated production of H2O2 (E = 0.695 V vs. RHE). From a kinetic aspect, faster scission of O-O bond readily generates subsequent intermediates such as *OOH, *O and *OH to produce water via 4e− pathway as compared to slower O-O bond cleavage process leading to the formation of H2O2 (Fig. 4C). However, the important factors that contribute notably towards the selectivity of the product are the nature of the catalyst to oxygen binding affinity. Previous reports suggest that when the binding affinity of HOO* with the active site is weak, it rapidly detaches from the catalyst surface upon protonation to undergo two-electron pathways. However, the moderate binding affinity of *OOH with the catalyst active site, following the Sabatier principle, drives the reaction towards the 4e− pathway for the formation of *O intermediate to form water in the next step. Apart from these, the pH of the reaction medium and the ions present in the electrolyte also regulate the reaction pathway. Although the RRDE experiment thoroughly confirms the four-electron reaction pathway, it is pertinent to investigate the mechanistic pathway and the nature of the active site to correlate the catalyst structure-activity relationships for these COFs.

To unravel the mechanistic origin of the enhanced ORR performance of TTT-DHTD, DFT calculations together with in situ Raman spectroscopy were employed to identify the catalytically active sites and monitor the formation and transformation of key reaction intermediates under operating conditions. The calculated Raman spectra were found to be in good agreement with the experimental Raman spectra, supporting the experimental observations (Fig. 4, A and B). At open circuit potential, the catalyst TTT-DHTD COF exhibited its characteristic Raman spectra (Fig. 4B), where any characteristic peaks pertaining to the ORR intermediates were not observed. However, as the potential increases, multiple characteristic peaks emerge in the spectrum. We believe that the initial diffusion of an oxygen molecule, followed by its binding to the catalyst surface, initiates the reduction process. Subsequent transfer of electron leads to formation of superoxo (*O2-) species as evident through a broad Raman stretching frequency at 1000–1040 cm−1. Further protonation and electron transfer lead to the formation of peroxo species (*OOH), as confirmed by a broad stretching band at 695–725 cm−1. Generally, incorporation of nitrogen owing to high electronegativity leads to electron delocalization, resulting in favouring the oxygen adsorption via side-on (Yeager model) in contrast to the conventional end-on mode (Pauling model) (4). For instance, Xiang et al. have already discussed the role of the C=N bond in oxygen activation via forming a four-membered intermediate (46). Similarly, Wei et al. demonstrated that oxygen species adsorbed on the C-N moiety generated a broad Raman spectrum between 1100–1200 cm−1 (47–49). It is interesting to note that the Raman spectra of TTT-DHTD COF show two peaks at around 1135 cm−1 and 1510 cm−1, respectively, indicating adsorption of the ORR intermediates (*O2, *OOH) on the imine bond (47). Theoretical Raman spectra also shows similar peaks 1160 cm−1 and 1503 cm−1 for *O, 1483 cm−1 for *O2, and 1137 cm−1 and 1554 cm−1 for *OOH (Fig. 4A). The excellent agreement between the experimental and theoretical Raman analyses confirms that the imine linkage serves as an active adsorption site for ORR intermediates, while the donor–acceptor architecture of the TTT-DHTD framework facilitates efficient charge redistribution across the conjugated backbone, thereby promoting oxygen adsorption and activation and ultimately enhancing ORR kinetics.

To identify the most catalytically active sites for the oxygen reduction reaction in three distinct COFs—TTT-DTDA, TAPA-DHTD, and TTT-DHTD—a systematic theoretical investigation was carried out using density functional theory (DFT) within the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) model. We evaluated seven potential adsorption sites in TTT-DTDA (Fig. 5A), four in TAPA-DHTD (Fig. 5B), and nine in TTT-DHTD (Fig. 5C). For each site, the complete four-electron ORR pathway was simulated, and the corresponding overpotentials (η) were calculated and summarized in table S3. Among the investigated sites, the nitrogen center at Site 1 in TTT-DTDA, the nitrogen at Site 2 in TAPA-DHTD, and Sites 1 (N) and 7 (C) in TTT-DHTD exhibited the lowest overpotentials, clearly indicating the excellent catalytic activity of TTT-DHTD relative to the other two COFs. Consistently, the Raman spectrum displayed distinct absorption peaks at approximately 1130 cm−1 and 1510 cm−1. Computed free energy diagrams revealed that the most active sites were Site 1 of TTT-DTDA (Fig. 5D), Site 2 of TAPA-DHTD (Fig. 5E), and Sites 1 and 7 of TTT-DHTD (Fig. 5, F and L).

Interestingly, nitrogen-containing sites consistently outperformed carbon-based sites in TTT-DTDA, TAPA-DHTD, and TTT-DHTD COFs (Fig. 5J). To understand the origin of this enhanced activity in TTT-DHTD, we analysed the projected density of states (PDOS) for the most active nitrogen sites across the three frameworks. A clear trend was observed, with a systematic increase in p-state density near the Fermi level and an upward shift of the p-band center (Ep) from TTT–DTDA to TAPA–DHTD to TTT–DHTD, suggesting a progressive enhancement in ORR activity along this series (Fig. 5, G to I).

To further elucidate the role of electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups in modulating catalytic activity, we examined the correlation between Bader charge and overpotential (Fig. 5K). While the intrinsic charge distribution across active sites remained largely unchanged, the incorporation of -C=O containing DHTD units led to a notable decrease in overpotentials. This observation underscores the critical influence of the DHTD moiety in tailoring the electronic environment of the active sites. Most notably, the synergistic combination of acceptor TTT and donor DHTD units in TTT–DHTD yielded multiple active sites with overpotentials below 1.0 eV. Among these, Site 7 displayed the lowest overpotential (0.47 eV) and the highest Bader charge (0.23 e) among all carbon-based sites across the three COFs, highlighting the effectiveness of the donor–acceptor strategy in optimizing ORR performance through electronic structure modulation. The synergy between the electron-accepting TTT and electron-donating DHTD is reflected in the improved activity at C sites near the quinone group, as evidenced by changes in Bader charge, lower overpotential, and more favorable *OH and *OOH binding (fig. S26). We have also considered the two-layer stacking effect in TTT-DTDA and TTT-DHTD, which confirms the superior activity of TTT-DHTD in this configuration (fig. S27). Taken together, the combined in situ Raman and DFT analyses unambiguously confirm that the triazine-based TTT-DHTD exhibits outstanding ORR performance (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, two redox-active metal-free COFs, TTT-DHTD and TAPA-DHTD, were synthesized as efficient electrocatalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction. Notably, TTT-DHTD COF delivered a magnificent half-wave potential (E1/2) of 0.82 V vs. RHE, approaching that of the benchmark Pt/C (0.85 V) catalyst, owing to the synergetic interplay of triazine and dithiophene moieties present in the vicinity. This represents one of the highest reported E1/2 values among metal-free COF-based electrocatalysts (0.82 V) to date. Moreover, the TTT-DHTD COF demonstrated remarkable long-term stability, outperforming the commercial 20% Pt/C catalyst by maintaining a higher current density for up to 120 hours of continuous operation. These findings establish the robustness and promising potential of redox-active donor–acceptor COFs as next-generation metal-free electrocatalysts for sustainable energy applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis of TAPA-DHTD COF

In a Pyrex tube, N1,N1-bis(4-aminophenyl)benzene-1,4-diamine (TAPA, 10.51 mg, 0.036 mmol), and 4,8-dioxo-4,8-dihydrobenzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’]dithiophene-2,6-dicarbaldehyde (DHTD, 15 mg, 0.054 mmol) were combined with 0.8 mL of mesitylene, 1.2 mL of DMAc, and 0.05 mL of 6 M aqueous acetic acid. The mixture was sonicated for 10 minutes to ensure a uniform dispersion. The tube was then rapidly cooled using liquid nitrogen (77 K) and subjected to three freeze-pump-thaw cycles to degas the contents. After sealing the tube under vacuum, it was heated at 120°C for 3 days. A black precipitate formed, which was collected via centrifugation, washed successively with anhydrous methanol, tetrahydrofuran and anhydrous acetone. The collected powder was purified by Soxhlet extraction with methanol (12 hours at 90°C), followed by drying at 80°C for 2 hours, yielding a black, pure TAPA-DHTD powder. (Isolated yield = ∼81%).

Synthesis of TTT-DHTD COF

TTT-DHTD was synthesized by the reported procedure (27). A pyrex tube was charged with 4,4′,4″-(1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triyl)trianiline (TTT, 53 mg, 0.15 mmol) and 4,8-dioxo-4,8-dihydrobenzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’]dithiophene-2,6-dicarbaldehyde (DHTD, 62 mg, 0.225 mmol), 1.0 mL of mesitylene, 2.0 mL of dioxane and 0.5 mL of 6 M aqueous acetic acid. To achieve homogeneous dispersion, the contents of the tube were sonicated for 15 minutes. The tube was then flash-frozen at 77 K (liquid N2 bath) and degassed by three freeze-pump-thaw cycles. The tube was sealed under vacuum and heated to 120°C for 3 days. A brown-colored precipitate was collected by filtration, washed with acetone, and finally with methanol and THF. The collected powder was then purified by Soxhlet extraction with methanol (12 hours) at 95°C and subsequently dried under atmospheric conditions to yield a brown-colored, pure TTT-DHTD COF (Isolated yield = ∼85%).

Electrochemical measurements

The CHI 760E electrochemical workstation was used to perform cyclic voltammetry (CV). A Metrohm multi-channel Autolab (M204) electrochemical workstation was used to conduct rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) analysis using a GC disk (0.196 cm2) and a Pt ring (0.041 cm2). All other electrochemical measurements were performed in a three-electrode cell with RRDE as the working electrode, platinum foil as the counter electrode, and Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) as the reference electrode. The electrochemical oxygen reduction reaction was conducted in an alkaline medium using 0.1 M KOH. The electrolyte was continuously purged with argon or oxygen for 30 minutes prior to the experiment.
